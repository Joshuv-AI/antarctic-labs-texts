Abstract
. Wasserstein gradient flow provides a general framework for minimizing an energy functional J J over the space of probability measures on a Riemannian manifold ( M , g ) (M,g) . 1.
Introduction

Many problems in statistics, physics, and machine-learning can be formulated as a finding a minimizer

(1)

\displaystyle\rho_{*}\in\argmin_{\rho\,\in P_{ac}(M)}J(\rho),\qquad J:P_{ac}(M)\to\mathbb{R}

for an energy
J
on the space of probability measures
P_{ac}(M)
over a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with finite second moments and density relative to the Riemannian volume form. For sufficiently smooth energies
J
it is often natural to study the static optimization problem (
1
) by analyzing the dynamics of
Wasserstein gradient flow
on
J
. That is, given an initial condition
\rho_{0}
, one seeks a solution to the dissipative PDE

(2)

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\mathrm{div}_{g}\left(\rho_{t}\nabla_{g}\frac{\delta J}{\delta\rho}(\rho_{t})\right),

in which the right hand side is the steepest descent direction at
\rho_{t}
for the energy
J
with respect to the Wasserstein-2 metric. In (
2
),
\mathrm{div}_{g},\nabla_{g}
are the Riemannian divergence, gradient and
\frac{\delta J}{\delta\rho}(\rho_{t})
is the
first variation
of the functional
J
evaluated at
\rho_{t}
(see
[
7
]
for a precise definition). Examples of well-known Wasserstein gradient flows for various energy functionals
J
include both PDEs and SPDEs such as the heat equation and McKean-Vlasov equations as well as important algorithms in statistics and machine learning such as score-matching and Langevin dynamics.

Both for the purposes of theoretical analyses of Wasserstein gradient flows and to solve (
2
) numerically, a number of time discretizations
Wasserstein gradient flow (
2
) have been proposed. One such prescription commonly employed in practice is to fix a finite step size
\eta>0
and consider the forward-Euler scheme

(3)

\rho^{(n+1)}=\rho^{(n)}-\eta\,\nabla_{g}\cdot(\rho^{(n)}v^{(n)}),\qquad v^{(n)}=\nabla_{g}\frac{\delta J}{\delta\rho}(\rho^{(n)}),

which updates the density
\rho^{(n)}
using the instantaneous velocity
v^{(n)}
. This strategy is easy to implement numerically after approximating
\rho^{(n)}
by a sum of delta functions. However, it has significant deficiencies:

(1)

The iterate
\rho^{(n+1)}
may no longer belong to
P_{ac}(M)
: it is neither guaranteed to be positive nor to have unit mass.

(2)

The update
-\eta\,\nabla_{g}\cdot(\rho^{(n)}\nabla_{g}\delta J(\rho^{(n)}))
merely represents an additive perturbation, not a proper push-forward (i.e. of a transport map
\nabla\phi_{\sharp}\rho^{(n)}
along a Wasserstein geodesic).

(3)

The forward-Euler scheme is stable only for sufficiently small
\eta
.

(4)

Forward-Euler
does not
guarantee the energy-dissipation relation
J(\rho^{n+1})\leq J(\rho^{n})
.

In addition to these limitations, a body of work (e.g.
[
29
]
) has identified major regularity issues in forward-Euler schemes (
3
) even in the regime of small step-size (cf §
3
). In contrast, the seminal work Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto (JKO)
[
16
]
initiated the study of the implicit-Euler discretization of (
2
) given by

(4)

\rho_{k+1}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta}=\argmin_{\rho\in P_{ac}(M)}\,\,J(\rho)+\frac{1}{2\eta}W_{g}^{2}(\rho_{k}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta},\rho)\,\,.

The update (
4
) is a proximal-point problem in the squared Wasserstein-2 metric
[
23
,
17
]

W_{g}^{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
, where by definition (see
[
2
]
):

\displaystyle W_{g}^{2}(\mu,\nu):=

\displaystyle\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{M\times M}d_{g}^{2}(x,y)\gamma(dx,dy),

d_{g}(x,y)
denotes the Riemannian distance between a pair of points
x,y\in M
and the infimum is over all couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
, the set of all
\gamma\in P_{ac}(M\times M)
with marginals
\int_{M}\gamma(x,dy)=\mu(x)
and
\int_{M}\gamma(dx,y)=\nu(y)
denoted by
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
.

Definition 1

(Order-
k
Integrator for the JKO Scheme.)
.

Suppose
J:P_{ac}\to\mathbb{R}
is an energy functional for which the JKO Scheme is well-posed and stable with piecewise interpolation
\rho_{\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor}^{\mathrm{JKO}}
. A curve
(\rho^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\subset P_{ac}
is called an order-
k
integrator for the JKO scheme if for any
T>0

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{2}(\rho^{\prime}_{t},\rho_{\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta})=O_{T}(\eta^{k}).

A key result in
[
2
]
is that the proximal point scheme (
4
), which is often called the JKO scheme, is a first order integrator of the Wasserstein gradient flow of
J
.

Theorem 1

(
[
2
]
Theorem 4.0.4, 11.2.1)
.

Suppose
J
is proper, l.s.c., coercive, and
\lambda
-geodesically convex along generalized geodesics. Then the JKO Scheme is well-posed and stable, with piecewise interpolation
\rho_{\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor}^{\mathrm{JKO}}
. Moreover, the Wasserstein gradient flow
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{t}\nabla\frac{\delta J}{\delta\rho}(\rho_{t})\right),

exists, is unique, and satisfies for any
T>0

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta})=O_{T}(\eta).

Theorem
1
is usually interpreted in the forward direction: the JKO Scheme approximates the Wasserstein gradient flow with
O_{T}(\eta)
accuracy. In reverse, in the sense of Definition
1
, the Wasserstein gradient flow
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is itself an order-1 integrator for the JKO Scheme.

In this work, we take this first-order consistency estimate as a black-box: we assume that the energy functional
J
satisfies the hypotheses guaranteeing the existence of the gradient flow, the well-posedness of the JKO Scheme, and the stability and energy-dissipation estimates of
[
2
]
(e.g. the assumptions of Theorem 11.2.1). Under these conditions, the JKO Scheme provides an
O_{T}(\eta)
approximation to the continuous flow. Our main result builds on this first-order relationship by identifying a second-order modified flow matching the JKO iterates to
O_{T}(\eta^{2})
.

In contrast to the forward-Euler discretization (
3
), the JKO scheme (
4
) exhibits many desirable theoretical properties:

(1)

For
J
a
\lambda
-convex energy functional in
(P_{ac},W_{2})
with
\lambda>0
the JKO scheme is
unconditionally-stable
: for any
\eta>0
the scheme produces a sequence
(\rho_{k}^{{}_{\mathrm{JKO},\eta}})_{k}\subset P_{2}(M)
that converges in the Wasserstein metric to the unique minimizer of
J
.

(2)

The JKO scheme (
4
) exhibits discrete energy dissipation:

J(\rho_{k+1}^{\mathrm{JKO,\eta}})+\frac{1}{2\eta}W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{k+1}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta},\rho_{k}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta})\leq J(\rho_{k}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta}).

(3)

The iterates of the JKO scheme converges as
\eta\rightarrow 0
to the unique solution of the associated Wasserstein gradient flow for a broad class of
J
(see e.g. Theorem 5.1 in
[
16
]
, Theorem 4.0.4 in
[
2
]
, and Theorem 2 in
[
26
]
.

While (
4
) is a canonical method for analyzing the optimization problem (
1
), it is not constructive since it requires solving at each iteration an infinite-dimensional optimization problem. Indeed, (
4
) admits exact solutions only in special cases such as mean and covariance updates for the linear Fokker-Planck equation
[
14
]
and Gaussian convolution steps for the heat-equation
[
5
,
10
]
.

It is therefore unclear how the geometry of
P_{ac}(M)
and the local structure of
J
interact to determine the JKO updates
\rho_{k}^{{}_{\mathrm{JKO},\eta}}
beyond the fact that they are order
\eta
approximations to Wasserstein gradient flow (Theorem
1
). The central result of the present article is to provide additional insights on the structure of JKO iterates by computing the second order
implicit bias
of the JKO scheme with respect to the Wasserstein gradient flow (
2
).

1.1.
Main Result: Implicit Bias of JKO

Our main result Theorem
2
identifies, for any sufficiently smooth energy
J:P_{ac}(M)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and step size
\eta>0
a modified energy
J^{\eta}:P_{ac}(M)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
so that Wasserstein gradient flow on
J^{\eta}
matches the JKO scheme updates on
J
to order
\eta^{2}
, providing a richer understanding of the JKO scheme than Theorem
1
. To state our main result we recall the following

Definition 2

(Metric Slope)
.

Let
J:P_{ac}(M)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\infty
be an energy on
({P}_{ac}(M),W_{g})
. The
metric slope
at a point
\rho\in{P}_{ac}(M)
is the quantity

|\partial J(\rho)|=\left(\int_{M}\left\lVert\nabla_{g}\frac{\delta J}{\delta\rho}\right\rVert_{g}^{2}\rho(dx)\right)^{1/2}.

The squared-metric slope is a natural quantity in the analysis of Wasserstein gradient flows
[
2
,
28
]
, as it quantifies the rate of energy dissipation
\frac{d}{dt}J(\rho_{t})=-|\partial J(\rho_{t})|^{2}
, an equality commonly known as the
energy dissipation equality
for a purely dissipative gradient flow.

Theorem 2

(The Implicit Bias of the JKO Scheme)
.

Fix an energy
J:P_{2,ac}(M)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\infty
, a step size
\eta
and denote by
\rho_{k}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta}
the
k
-th iterate of the JKO scheme on
J
with step size
\eta.
Suppose that:

(A1)

J
is proper and l.s.c. in
(P_{2},W_{2})
.

(A2)

For each
k\geq 0
write
\rho(k\eta):=\rho_{k}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta}
. Suppose the
W_{2}
-gradient flow

\partial_{t}\rho^{\mathrm{GF}}_{t}+\nabla\cdot(-\nabla\psi(\rho_{t}^{\mathrm{GF}})\rho_{t}^{\mathrm{GF}})=0,\qquad\psi=\frac{\delta J}{\delta\rho}

admits classical solution
(\rho^{\mathrm{GF}}_{t})\in C_{t,x}^{2}
on
t/\eta\in[n,(n+1)]
which is
O_{T}(\eta)
from
\rho_{k}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta}
(Theorem
1
). Further, suppose
\rho_{t}
satisfies fast decay to zero at infinity so integration by parts
\int\varphi\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-\int\varphi\,\mathrm{div}(\rho_{t}v_{t})=\int\langle\nabla\varphi,v_{t}\rangle\rho_{t}
is valid on
M
.

(A3)

Suppose that in a neighborhood of the curve
(\rho_{t}:t\in[n\eta,(n+1)\eta])
that (a)
J
is twice Fréchet differentiable (b)
J
has a smooth variation
\psi(\rho_{t}):=\frac{\delta J}{\delta\rho}\in C^{1,2}_{t,x}
; and (c)
\nabla\psi(\rho),\,\partial_{t}\nabla\psi(\rho)\in L^{2}(\rho_{t})

Define the functional
J^{\eta}:P_{ac}(M)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

(5)

J^{\eta}(\rho):=J(\rho)-\frac{\eta}{4}\left|\partial J(\rho)\right|^{2}.

Denoted by
\rho_{t}^{\eta}
the Wasserstein gradient flow
\rho_{t}^{\eta}
on
J^{\eta}
. Then for any
T>0

(6)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}W_{2}\left(\rho_{t}^{\eta},\rho_{\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor}^{\mathrm{JKO},\eta}\right)=O_{T}(\eta^{2}).

Theorem
2
, is a local, second-order result. It reverses the usual direction of the analysis: instead of showing the JKO Scheme approximates a given flow, it identifies a modified energy
J^{\eta}
whose Wasserstein gradient flow coincides with the discrete JKO updates to
O_{T}(\eta^{2})
. This construction only requires local smoothness and well-posedness assumptions on
J
and on the continuous and discrete flows; it does not explicitly require
\lambda
-geodesic convexity.

Although
\lambda
-convexity (with properness, l.s.c., and coercivity) is not needed for this second-order analysis, it is a standard sufficient condition which
[
2
]
guarantees existence, uniqueness, stability, and first-order consistency of both the Wasserstein gradient flow and JKO scheme (Theorem
1
).

At a high level, Theorem
2
shows that the behavior of the backward-Euler scheme, to second order in the step size, amounts to
subtracting
from the energy
J
its squared metric slope with a weight depending on the step-size. The resulting Wasserstein gradient flow is more conservative (“sticky”) in regions of high metric slope for
J
. Intuitively, this offers stability relative to routines such as forward-Euler discretizations which are prone to overshoot minima and quickly leave regions of
P_{ac}(M)
where
J
is
sharp

[
27
,
3
]
.

We prove Theorem
2
in Section
B
. Before doing so, we offer a number of perspectives and examples of this implicit regularization in the discussion below. In particular:

•

In Section

we give examples of the implicit bias of JKO for many of the canonical energy functionals minimized with Wasserstein gradient flow. As we shall see below, the implicit regularization from Theorem
2
recovers a variety of interesting divergences / regularizers.

•

In Section
1.3
we discuss how Theorem
2
recovers previous results on the implicit bias of Euclidean gradient descent
[

]
.

•

In Section

we provide a novel generalization of the results in

from gradient flows on flat a Euclidean to gradient flows on general Riemannian manifolds.

•

In Section

we state the implicit bias of JKO on the free-energy functional, finding the bias to correspond to two symmetric operators acting on the energy and entropy. We find that the implicit bias of the potential energy generates a kinetic energy term, while the implicit bias of the entropy generates
quantum drift-diffusion
(See, e.g.
[

•

In Section

Illustrative Examples

Below we catalog the implicit bias

Potential Energy.

Hence,

\displaystyle H^{\eta}(\rho)=\frac{\eta}{4}\int_{M}\lVert\nabla E(x)\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\rho(dx)

This corresponds to the Dirichlet energy of the potential

Entropy.
The entropy functional, its first variation, and its Wasserstein gradient are given by

\displaystyle J(\rho)=\int_{M}\rho(dx)\log\rho(x),\quad\frac{\delta J}{\delta\rho}=\log\rho+1,\quad\nabla_{g}\frac{\delta J}{\delta\rho}=\nabla_{g}\log\rho

The gradient-flow on

corresponds to the heat equation
\partial_{t}\rho=\Delta_{g}\rho

(8)

\displaystyle H^{\eta}(\rho)=\frac{\eta}{2}\int_{M}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lVert\nabla_{g}\rho(x)\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\rho}\,dx,

or equivalently the trace of the Fisher information matrix

[

(9)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{4}\int_{M}\lVert\nabla_{g}\log\rho(x)\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\rho(dx)=\frac{\eta}{4}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\lVert\nabla_{g}\log\rho\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

1.3.
Recovering the Backward Euler Implicit Bias for Euclidean GD

Theorem
2
generalizes well-known results on the implicit bias of gradient descent
[
27
,
3
]
. To explain this, let us restrict to the case when
M=\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the standard Euclidean metric. Given a smooth objective function
E:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the gradient flow

\dot{x}_{t}=-\nabla E(x_{t})

can be discretized via either a forward or backward Euler scheme:

(Forward Euler)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-\eta\nabla E(x_{k})

(Backward Euler)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{x}\left\{E(x)+\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\lVert x-x_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\right\}

A fundamental result about the implicit bias of these discretizations is the following

Theorem 3

(
[
27
,
3
]
)
.

Let
x_{k}^{GD,\eta}
be the iterates of either the forward or backward Euler schemes above. Define

E^{\eta}(x)=\begin{cases}E(x)+\frac{\eta}{4}\left\lVert\nabla E(x)\right\rVert^{2},&\quad\text{Forward Euler}\\
E(x)-\frac{\eta}{4}\left\lVert\nabla E(x)\right\rVert^{2},&\quad\text{Backward Euler}\end{cases}

and write

(11)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{t}^{\eta}=-\nabla E^{\eta}(x_{t}^{\eta})

for the gradient flow on
E^{\eta}
starting at the same initial condition as the gradient flow for
E
. Then for any
T>0
and all
\eta
sufficiently small

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert x_{t}^{\eta}-x_{\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor}^{GD,\eta}\right\rVert=O_{T}(\eta^{2}).

Remark 1
.

The articles
[

,
3
]
focus on the case of gradient descent (i.e. the forward Euler scheme) but their proofs carry over almost verbatim to analyzing the backward Euler scheme as well.

[

,

]
compute this asymptotic bias of gradient descent relative to gradient flow using backward error analysis (BEA)

. Their result can be recovered from our Theorem

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{t}\nabla\frac{\delta J^{\eta}}{\delta\rho}(\rho_{t})\right)=-\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{t}(-\nabla E+\frac{\eta}{4}\nabla\left\lVert\nabla E\right\rVert_{2}^{2}),\right)

1.4.
Implicit Bias of Riemannian Gradient Descent

Fix a smooth, complete Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
of dimension
d<\infty
and a smooth energy
E:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. In this section we present a novel expression for the implicit bias of both the forward and backward Euler discretizations

x_{k+1}=\begin{cases}\exp_{x_{k}}\left(-\eta\nabla_{g}E(x_{k})\right),&\quad\text{explicit Euler}\\
\argmin_{x\in M}\left\{E(x)+\frac{1}{2\eta}d_{g}(x,x_{k})^{2}\right\},&\quad\text{implicit Euler}\end{cases},

of the continuous time gradient flow
\dot{x}_{t}=-\nabla_{g}E(x_{t}).
Specifically, we will give an independent derivation of the following result

Proposition 1

(Riemannian Gradient Descent Bias)
.

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth, complete Riemannian manifold, and let
E:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be in
C^{3}(M)
. For
\eta>0
define
E^{\eta}:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

E^{\eta}(x)=\begin{cases}E(x)+\frac{\eta}{4}\left\lVert\nabla_{g}E(x)\right\rVert^{2},&\quad\text{forward Euler}\\
E(x)-\frac{\eta}{4}\left\lVert\nabla_{g}E(x)\right\rVert^{2},&\quad\text{backward Euler}\end{cases}.

Write
x_{t}^{\eta}
for the gradient flow on
E^{\eta}
and
x_{k}^{GD,\eta}
for either the forward or backward Euler discretization of
x_{t}^{\eta}
with
x_{0}^{\eta}=x_{0}^{GD,\eta}
. Then for every
T>0
and all
\eta
we have

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}d_{g}(x_{t}^{\eta},x_{\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor}^{GD,\eta})=O_{T}(\eta^{2}).

We provide a short proof of Proposition
1
at the end of this section. While formally our arguments can be extended to infinite dimensional Riemannian manifolds such at
P_{ac}(M)
equipped with the Wasserstein-2 metric, precise derivations in infinite dimension require more care. Before turning to the proof, we make a few remarks:

(Novelty)
To our knowledge Proposition
1
is the first generalization of
[
27
,
3
]
to general Riemannian manifolds.

(Implicit Bias as Geodesic Acceleration)
Note that

\nabla_{g}E^{\eta}-\nabla_{g}E=\pm\frac{\eta}{2}\mathrm{Hess}_{g}E[\nabla_{g}E],

where
\mathrm{Hess}_{g}E
is the Riemannian Hessian. This expression reflects that at order
\eta
the implicit bias of forward and backward Euler is to accelerate and decelerate, respectively, gradient flow on
E
in directions where the gradient
\nabla_{g}E
is rapidly changing, as measured by the Hessian of the objective. Equivalently,

(12)

\displaystyle\nabla_{g}E^{\eta}-\nabla_{g}E=\pm\frac{\eta}{4}\left(\mathcal{L}\big\lVert\dot{\theta}\big\rVert_{g}^{2}\right)\bigg|_{\dot{\theta}=\dot{x}_{t}}=\pm\frac{\eta}{2}\nabla_{\nabla_{g}E}\nabla_{g}E,

where
\mathcal{L}=g^{-1}(\partial_{x_{k}}-D_{t}\partial_{\dot{x}_{k}})
is the Riemannian Euler-Lagrange operator. Applying the operator
\mathcal{L}
to the kinetic energy
\frac{1}{2}||\dot{\theta}||^{2}
returns the geodesic acceleration
\nabla_{\dot{\theta}}\dot{\theta}
. Thus, the implicit bias of forward and backward Euler discretization for Riemannian gradient flow is to accelerate or slow down in directions of large geodesic acceleration.

The form of the implicit regularization in the Riemannian case, when written in local coordinates, offers an interesting parallel to the Euclidean case
[
27

(13)

1.5.
Implicit Bias of JKO on the Free-Energy Functional and Langevin Dynamics

An important example of an objective for Wasserstein gradient flow is the

\displaystyle J(\rho)=\int E(x)\rho(dx)+\beta^{-1}\int\rho(dx)\log\rho(x),

\displaystyle H^{\eta}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{4}\int\left\lVert\nabla E+\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho(dx)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{4}\int\lVert\nabla E\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\rho(dx)+\frac{\eta}{2\beta}\int\langle\nabla E,\nabla\log\rho\rangle\,\rho(dx)+\frac{\eta}{4\beta^{2}}\int\lVert\nabla\log\rho\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\rho(dx)

Remarkably, the Riemannian gradient of

Proposition 2

Appendix A
Other Background

Backward Error-Analysis
(BEA)
[
25
,
6
]
is a classical theoretical framework for analyzing the numerical error of integration schemes for differential equations. In particular, let
V\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and suppose one considers the following ordinary differential equation for
t\in[0,1]
and its integral solution (flow-map)

(37)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}(t)=V_{\theta(t)},\quad\Phi_{V}^{t}(\theta):\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

and define a discrete integrator
\hat{\Phi}_{V}^{\eta}

[
6
]
to be a function smooth in the step-size
\eta>0
satisfying
\hat{\Phi}_{V}^{0}=\mathrm{id}
and
\frac{d}{d\eta}\hat{\Phi}_{V}^{\eta}\mid_{\eta=0}\,(p)=V_{p}
. An integrator is consistent with the flow to order
p
if for sufficiently small
\eta
if there exists a constant
C>0
so that

(38)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\Phi}_{V}^{\eta}(\theta)-\Phi_{V}^{\eta}(\theta)\right\rVert\leq C\,\eta^{p+1}

In BEA, one supposes access to solution to the discretized integration scheme
\hat{\Phi}_{V}^{\eta}(\theta)
up to a time
(n+1)\eta=t_{n+1}
from an initial condition at
n\eta=t_{n}
. For a given order
p
, BEA asks whether there exists a modified vector field
\tilde{V}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
perturbing
V
which ensures the continuous flow and discrete integrator coincide:

(39)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\Phi}_{V}^{\eta}(\theta)-\Phi_{\tilde{V}}^{\eta}(\theta)\right\rVert\leq C\,\eta^{p+1}

Since
\hat{\Phi}_{V}^{\eta}(\theta)\sim\Phi_{V}^{\eta}(\theta)
differ to order
\eta^{2}
, any integrator is consistent to order one. While this is the case for gradient-descent and gradient-flow, for any finite
\eta
the two differ beyond order one, with
[
3
]
identifying that the modified flow
\tilde{V}_{\theta(t_{n}+\eta)}
coinciding with gradient-descent to order two is given by

(40)

\displaystyle\tilde{V}:={V}+\eta G:=-\nabla_{\theta}J-\frac{\eta}{4}\nabla_{\theta}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}J\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+O(\eta^{2})

Thus, while gradient-flow follows
-\nabla_{\theta}J
, forward-Euler gradient-descent behaves like the modified continuous flow
\tilde{V}_{\theta(t_{n}+\eta)}
, revealing an implicit bias towards flatter minima.

Implicit Bias of the JKO Scheme

Before we state the next lemma, let us recall Grönwall’s inequality, which states that for a differentiable
\phi:[0,\eta]\to\mathbb{R}
which satisfies
\dot{\phi}(t)\leq c\phi(t)
for all
t\in[0,\eta]
, then
\phi(t)\leq e^{ct}\phi(0)
for all
t
.

Lemma 1

(Stability Lemma)
.

Suppose we have two densities
\rho_{t}^{\prime},\,\,\rho_{t}
satisfying the continuity equation from the same initial datum
\rho_{0}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}^{\prime}=-\nabla\cdot(\rho^{\prime}_{t}v_{t}),\quad\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}w_{t})

Suppose that
v_{t},w_{t}
satisfy for
t\in[0,\eta]
both
L^{2}(\rho_{t})
closeness a.e.
t
and that
w_{t}
(or
v_{t}
) are
L
-Lipschitz

\displaystyle\lVert v_{t}-w_{t}\lVert_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}\leq C\eta^{k},\quad\lVert w_{t}(x)-w_{t}(y)\rVert_{2}\leq L\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}

Then, for any
t_{0}\in[0,\eta]
one has the bound

\displaystyle W_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\leq\left(W_{2}(\rho_{t_{0}},\rho_{t_{0}}^{\prime})+\frac{C\eta^{k}}{L}\right)e^{L(t-t_{0})}-\frac{C\eta^{k}}{L}

Proof.

From Theorem 8.4.7
[
2
]
, we have from the generic differentiability of the Wasserstein-p distance that for
\rho_{t}
absolutely continuous and
v_{t}(x)\in T_{x}{P}_{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
its tangent that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t},\sigma)\,\,=2\iint\langle x-y,v_{t}(x)\rangle d\gamma.

From
[
2
]
one has for our pair of measures
\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime}
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\leq\iint\langle v_{t}(x)-w_{t}(y),x-y\rangle d\gamma_{t}.

The right hand side of the preceding expression can be rewritten

\displaystyle\iint\langle v_{t}(x)-w_{t}(y)+w_{t}(x)-w_{t}(x),x-y\rangle d\gamma_{t}.

By Cauchy-Schwarz we thus obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\iint\langle v_{t}(x)-w_{t}(x),x-y\rangle d\gamma_{t}+\iint\langle w_{t}(x)-w_{t}(y),x-y\rangle d\gamma_{t}

\displaystyle\leq W_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\left(\left(\int\lVert v_{t}(x)-w_{t}(x)\rVert_{2}^{2}d\rho_{t}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\iint\lVert w_{t}(x)-w_{t}(y)\rVert_{2}^{2}d\gamma_{t}\right)^{1/2}\right)

Using that
w_{t}(x)
is
L
-Lipschitz we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq W_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\left(\lVert v_{t}-w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}+L\left(\iint\lVert x-y\rVert_{2}^{2}d\gamma_{t}\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=W_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\left(\lVert v_{t}-w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}+LW_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\right)

Thus, the time-change of the Wasserstein distance is bounded above by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\leq C\eta^{k}W_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})+LW_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime}).

Thus, letting
\phi(t):=W_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\phi(t)^{2}=\phi\dot{\phi}\leq C\eta^{k}\,\phi(t)+L\phi(t)^{2}.

Supposing the non-degenerate case where
\{t>0:W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})>0\}=(0,1]
we have

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}\leq C\eta^{k}+L\phi

Since this differential equation is solved, for
C\eta^{k},\,L>0
, by

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\left(\phi(t_{0})+\frac{C\eta^{k}}{L}\right)e^{L(t-t_{0})}-\frac{C\eta^{k}}{L}

we find the following linear differential inequality by application of Grönwall’s

\displaystyle W_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\leq\left(W_{2}(\rho_{t_{0}},\rho_{t_{0}}^{\prime})+\frac{C\eta^{k}}{L}\right)e^{L(t-t_{0})}-\frac{C\eta^{k}}{L}.

∎

Corollary 1

(Order of 2-Wasserstein Distance Between Order-k Integrators for step size
\eta
.)
.

Suppose one defines a flow satisfying the conditions of Lemma
1
with an order-

When

If we consider the mean-field interacting particle energy functional

B.1.2.

D.1.
Background on Optimization over Riemannian Manifolds.

Let
M
denote a Riemannian manifold. For a point

we denote the tangent space of
M
at
p

T_{p}M
with cotangent space

. Let the Riemannian metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\mid_{T_{\theta}M}:=g(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the norm on
T_{p}M

g_{p}=\sum_{ij}g_{ij}(p)\,\,dx^{i}\mid_{p}\otimes\,dx^{j}\mid_{p}
. Consider a smooth loss function
J:M\to\mathbb{R}
over the manifold. Riemannian optimization concerns finding a minimum
p^{\star}
for the problem

(71)

\displaystyle p^{\star}:=\argmin_{p\in M}J(p)

Riemannian gradient descent and Riemannian gradient flow arise as two natural approaches arise for this problem. In both cases, one relies on the
Riemannian gradient
,
\nabla_{\!g}J\in T_{p}M
, which is the vector uniquely defined by
DJ(p)[V]=g(V,\nabla_{\!g}J)
for the differential
DJ(p)[V]
the linear map defined by
DJ(p)[V]=(J\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(0)
for a smooth curve
\gamma\in M
with
\gamma(0)=p,\,\,\gamma^{\prime}(0)=V
. In Riemannian gradient flow, one minimizes
J
through the following ordinary differential equation on

(72)

\displaystyle\dot{p}=-\nabla_{\!g}J(p)

While Riemannian gradient flow is guaranteed to remain on
M
, unlike in the case of Euclidean gradient descent, the discretization of
72
given by
p^{(k+1)}=p^{(k)}-\eta\nabla_{\!g}J
generally fails to remain. To formalize a discretization which ensures feasibility, for any
p,q\in M
denote the Riemannian geodesic distance between the points by
d_{M}(p,q)
by the infimal length of a curve
\gamma\in C^{\infty}[0,1]
parametrized by arc length which satisfies
\gamma(0)=p
and
\gamma(1)=q
.

(73)

\displaystyle d(p,q)\,\,:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in C^{\infty}[0,1],\\
\gamma(0)=p,\,\gamma(1)=q\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{1}g(\dot{\gamma}(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))ds

In

\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and solving the following proximal-point implicit step for iterations

(74)

\displaystyle p^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\argmin_{p\in M}J(p)+\frac{1}{2\eta}d_{M}(p,p^{(k)})

Which ensures that the sequence of iterates

. An equivalent

. Let

\displaystyle p^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\mathrm{exp}_{p^{(k)}}\left(-\eta\nabla_{\!g}J(p)\right)

D.2.
Derivation of the Forward-Euler Case

As Riemannian gradient descent offers explicit updates, demonstrating its implicit bias follows a similar recipe to that of gradient descent over the Euclidean manifold
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Suppose that we are given a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, where

is a smooth manifold and

denotes its Riemannian metric. In particular, as standard, for

where each

forms a basis of the dual space

. The Riemannian metric

\nabla_{\!g}J:\quad DJ(X)[V]=g(V,\nabla_{\!g}J(X))\qquad\forall\,(V,X)\in T_{p}M

Focusing on the basis vectors

\displaystyle DJ(X)[\partial_{j}]=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial J}{\partial x^{i}}(X)dx^{i}[\partial_{j}]=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial J}{\partial x^{i}}(X)\delta_{ij}=\frac{\partial J}{\partial x^{j}}

\displaystyle g_{i,j}\nabla_{\!g}J(X)^{i}=\frac{\partial J}{\partial x^{j}}\implies\nabla_{\!g}J(X)^{i}=\sum_{j}g^{ij}\frac{\partial J}{\partial x^{j}}

\displaystyle\dot{X}=-\nabla_{\!g}J(X)

\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
, are given as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left(\partial_{j}g_{mi}+\partial_{i}g_{mj}-\partial_{m}g_{ij}\right)

so that we have

\displaystyle\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left(\partial_{j}g_{mi}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}+\partial_{i}g_{mj}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}-\partial_{m}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\right)

where contraction against the
ij
indices implies the symmetry of the terms

\displaystyle\partial_{j}g_{mi}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}=\partial_{i}g_{mj}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}

so the above expression becomes

\displaystyle\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}=g^{km}\left[\partial_{i}g_{mj}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{m}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\right]

Returning to
(\nabla\dot{X})^{ki}\dot{X}_{i}
and applying
g^{km}g_{mj}=\delta^{k}_{j}
we find

\displaystyle(\nabla\dot{X})^{ki}\dot{X}_{i}=g^{km}g_{mj}(\nabla\dot{X})^{ji}\dot{X}_{i}=g^{km}g_{mj}\ddot{X}^{j}

So the implicit regularization may be expressed as

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}+(\nabla\dot{X})^{ki}\dot{X}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left[\partial_{i}g_{mj}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{m}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}+g_{mj}\ddot{X}^{j}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left[\partial_{i}g_{mj}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}+g_{mj}\ddot{X}^{j}-\frac{\partial}{\partial X^{m}}\left(\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left[\frac{\partial g_{mj}}{\partial X^{i}}\frac{dX^{i}}{dt}\dot{X}^{j}+g_{mj}\frac{d}{dt}\dot{X}^{j}-\frac{\partial}{\partial X^{m}}\left(\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left[\frac{d}{dt}\left(g_{mj}\dot{X}^{j}\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial X^{m}}\left(\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial X^{m}}\left(\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\right)-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{X}^{m}}\left(\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial X^{m}}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{X}^{m}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}g^{km}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial X^{m}}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{X}^{m}}\right)\mathcal{L}

Where
(\partial_{X^{m}}-\frac{d}{dt}\partial_{\dot{X}^{m}})
denotes the Euler-Lagrange operator on the
m
-th component and
g^{km}
is the contravariant metric raise, as in standard Riemannian descent on
M
. The flow is given by a descent in the EL operator on the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}
which denotes the kinetic energy with respect to a Riemannian metric
g_{ij}
. Since we have that
\dot{\theta}(t)\simeq_{o(\eta)}\dot{X}(t)
, it holds that for the kinetic energy

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{4}g_{ij}\dot{\theta}^{i}\dot{\theta}^{j}=\frac{\eta}{4}g_{ij}(\dot{X}^{i}+O(\eta))(\dot{X}^{j}+O(\eta))=\frac{\eta}{4}g_{ij}\dot{X}^{i}\dot{X}^{j}+O(\eta^{2})

thus the corrected flow may be entirely given in the modified variable
\theta
as

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\!g}J(\theta)

\displaystyle=-g^{km}\partial_{m}J=\dot{X}

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}^{k}

\displaystyle=-g^{km}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{m}}J(\theta)-\frac{\eta}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{m}}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{m}}\right)\mathcal{L}\right)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}^{k}

\displaystyle=-g^{km}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{m}}J(\theta)-\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{m}}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{m}}\right)\left[\frac{\eta}{4}g_{ij}\dot{\theta}^{i}\dot{\theta}^{j}\right]\right)

Since a potential energy or loss function is independently defined only in the parameters
\theta
as
J:M\to\mathbb{R}
we have that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{m}}J(\theta)=0

Thus, one may extend the Euler-Lagrange operator as

\displaystyle-g^{km}\left(\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{m}}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{m}}\right)J(\theta)-\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{m}}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{\theta}^{m}}\right)\left[\frac{\eta}{4}g_{ij}\dot{\theta}^{i}\dot{\theta}^{j}\right]\right).

We have
(\partial_{\theta^{m}}-\frac{d}{dt}\partial_{\dot{\theta}^{m}})=E_{m}
is the Euler-Lagrange operator and a step-size dependent Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}^{\eta}=\frac{\eta}{4}g_{ij}\dot{\theta}^{i}\dot{\theta}^{j}-J(\theta)
. Here, we distribute the signs to maintain the standard notation that
\mathcal{L}=T-V
for
T
and
V
representing kinetic and the potential energies. To order
\eta^{2}
, one then has that the modified continuous flow of Riemannian gradient descent is as an application of the Euler-Lagrange operator on
\mathcal{L}^{\eta}
:

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}^{k}=\,g^{km}\,\underbrace{\Bigl(\partial_{\theta^{m}}-\tfrac{d}{dt}\,\partial_{\dot{\theta}^{m}}\Bigr)}_{E_{m}}\Bigl[\underbrace{\frac{\eta}{4}\,g_{ij}\dot{\theta}^{i}\dot{\theta}^{j}\;-\;J(\theta)}_{\mathcal{L}^{\eta}}\Bigr]\;+\;O(\eta^{2}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Q

A\prec 0
