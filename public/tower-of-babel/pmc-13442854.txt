Improving medical waste treatment in the Republic of Korea through diversification of operating conditions in sterilization and crushing facilities
Authors: Yoon-Soo Park, Min-jung Kim, Young-Yeul Kang, Tae-Wan Jeon, Sunmin Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to evaluate the biological safety of steam sterilization and crushing facilities under different operating conditions by assessing sterilization efficacy and the operational environment, and to propose strategies for expanding facility installation through diversification of treatment conditions. Sterilization tests were conducted at a currently operating steam sterilization and crushing facility in the Republic of Korea under both the current legal condition (121℃ / 30 min / 1 bar gauge(1 atm gauge)) and an optimal treatment condition (138 ℃ / 10 min / 2.5 bar gauge(3.5 atm absolute)), using five microbial species recommended by the WHO. Under both conditions, four microbial species exhibited a 99.9999% inactivation rate, and no fungi were detected. These results satisfied Level 3 of the WHO’s four-level inactivation standard for medical waste treatment. To confirm the operational safety of the facility, the pH of the wastewater generated during the process was measured, and the average value was 7.59. The moisture content of the sterilized and crushed waste was 38.1% under the legal condition and 26.5% under the optimal condition, meeting the legal standard of below 50%. Analysis of indoor air quality before and after facility operation showed that concentrations of PM10, PM2.5, formaldehyde, and total airborne bacteria remained similar, while the concentration of total VOCs increased after operation. Based on these results, this study proposes a measure to install more facilities by diversifying interim treatment methods, in consideration of the conditions currently stipulated by the Wastes Control Act in the Republic of Korea, and the possibility of introducing technologically advanced equipment in the future.Keywords: Medical waste, Indicator microorganism, Sterilization and crushingSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Health care, Microbiology

Introduction

Medical waste generated from medical institutions was managed by the Ministry of Health and Welfare’s Medical Service Act to control infection. The Ministry of Health and Welfare regarded medical wastes as extracted tissues, and created rules for their disposal and managed them until August 2000. Since then, as the materials were more controlled and managed as wastes, the Ministry of Environment took over the management of the materials, and they began to be controlled under the Wastes Control Act. Accordingly, separate management of wastes generated by medical institutions was required. To this end, while the term of infectious wastes was created under Article 2 (4) of the Wastes Control Act, medical wastes began to be controlled under a more rigorous system1. Medical waste is generated from medical activities, which require thorough management in consideration of its infectious nature to the public’s health and the environment. Due to its infectious nature, medical waste is handled by dedicated containers, vehicles, and incineration facilities. Furthermore, the Korea Environment Corporation has created an RFID control system to strictly monitor and manage medical wastes from discharge to disposal2. Such medical waste has continuously increased due to the aging population and the expansion of medical services3, 4, 5. The amount of medical waste generated has recently increased by approximately 13% from about 200,000 tons (as of 2015) to around 229,000 tons (as of 2022)6. It is also expected to continuously increase, considering the current rate of increase of generated medical waste3.

In order to stably manage this ever-increasing medical waste, three legal regulations have been relaxed in the Republic of Korea. First, disposable diapers with a low risk of infection in medical institutions, including hospitals, were excluded from medical waste categories to manage the amount of waste generated. Before the revision, disposable diapers consumed by both patients with infectious and non-infectious diseases in medical institutions were considered medical waste and were subject to dedicated incineration. However, after the revision, disposable diapers consumed by patients with non-infectious diseases have been excluded from the medical waste categories, and have been incinerated as general waste to reduce the amount of medical waste generated7. Second, the restriction on the installation of waste treatment facilities within the relative protection zone of schools was relaxed to expand the treatment facilities. The relative protection zone refers to an area excluding an absolute protection zone within an area in a radius of 200 m of straight-line distance from the school. Before the revision, the installation of waste treatment facilities within an area in a radius of 200 m of school sites was restricted. However, after the revision, it became possible to install a facility for sterilization and crushing within the site in order to alleviate the accumulation of medical waste8, 9. Third, to cope with an emergency in which a large amount of medical waste is generated due to the rapid spread of infectious diseases and the facility’s capacity is exceeded to handle the waste, the regulation was amended to allow low-hazard medical waste to be disposed of at a designated waste incinerator in a place of business. In general, medical waste is required to be disposed of at a dedicated incinerator for medical waste due to concerns on infection spread. However, to handle the situation in which the capacity of a dedicated medical waste incinerator reaches its limit due to a pandemic, the system has been relaxed to allow general medical waste with relatively low risk to be disposed of at designated waste incinerators10.

Although these legal regulations have been relaxed to stably dispose of medical waste, there are still concerns about the stable management of medical waste. First, the capacity of dedicated incinerators for medical waste has reached its limit. In 2022, the Republic of Korea was operating a total of 13 dedicated incinerators for medical waste6. The total annual capacity of these incinerators was 239,309 tons, and the amount of medical waste incinerated was 246,181 tons, which exceeded 102% of the permitted capacity6. The problem of the insufficient capacity of dedicated incinerators is expected to become more serious in the future based on the increasing trend of medical waste generation. However, the opposition from residents and local governments has made it difficult to expand and build new incinerators11. Second, there are only three dedicated incinerators for medical waste in the Seoul metropolitan area. In 2022, 53% of medical waste generation was found in the area, but there are only three incinerators in the area among a total of 13 dedicated incinerators nationwide, and their incineration capacity accounts for 25% of the total. Such an imbalance in the distribution of dedicated incinerators has led to the situation where medical waste is forced to travel long distances to be disposed of, and in this process, the risk of accidents during the transportation of infectious waste may increase12. Third, there are limitations to regulations on the interim treatment of medical waste in the Republic of Korea. The WHO, the Basel Convention, the United States, the United Kingdom, and other countries have currently suggested various interim treatment methods of medical waste, such as thermal, chemical, and other methods. However, in the Republic of Korea, only thermal treatment methods are allowed, and other options are not allowed. Additionally, the environmental conditions (e.g., time, temperature, and pressure) for treatment are also stipulated13. To alleviate the medical waste disposal problem under these situations, an alternative has been considered - an interim treatment of medical waste generated from large general hospitals, which are the main source of medical waste, at facilities for sterilization and crushing, and then disposal of it in general incinerators instead of dedicated incinerators14.

In order to propose a stable medical waste treatment plan, this study first investigated the amount of medical waste generated and treated to understand the current status of medical waste in the Republic of Korea, and examined the related legal system. Next, the facility for steam sterilization and crushing selected in this study was targeted for biological safety and operational safety testing per treatment condition (i.e., current legal condition, and optimal treatment condition) through microbial sterilization confirmation tests and environmental investigation during operation. Based on these results, this study confirmed the possibility of diversifying operating conditions to facilitate the installation of facilities for sterilization and crushing in hospital sites, and finally proposed a plan for diversifying the interim treatment method.

Materials and methods

For the purpose of proposing a safe treatment plan for medical waste, this study was conducted in four stages. In the first stage, we investigated the generation and treatment status of medical waste in Korea, as well as the regulatory framework governing facilities for sterilization and crushing. In the second stage, we selected equipment operation conditions for the sterilization test and safety test, five representative species for the sterilization test and sterilization criteria for confirming sterilization levels, and items and points for indoor air quality analysis. In the third stage, we pre-tested the vials appropriate for sterilization verification of the facility for steam sterilization and crushing, which is the research target, and produced species in the selected vials. In the fourth stage, we targeted the facility for steam sterilization and crushing for sterility and safety testing. The sterilization test aimed to confirm the sterilization effects of the target species per sterilization condition. The safety of the operating environment was confirmed by investigating the pH of wastewater, the moisture content of sterilized and crushed wastes, and the indoor air quality before and after operation. The experiments in Stages 2 through 4 were conducted at an operational steam sterilization and crushing facility located within a 245-bed general hospital in Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea. Finally, we attempted to examine the possibility of introducing diverse facilities for sterilization and crushing in the future based on the findings of this study. Current status of medical waste

As for the amount of medical waste generated and treated in the Republic of Korea, we compiled the data on National Waste Generation and Treatment Status from the Korean Statistical Information Service of Statistics Korea and the Korea Environment Corporation. Furthermore, we reviewed the current status and problems of medical waste based on reports from relevant research organizations, related papers, and media reports. Then, we conducted field visits and interviews with medical waste generators and dedicated incinerators, and held discussions with relevant experts from the government, industry, and academia.

Conditions and procedures for sterilization and safety tests

We divided the operating conditions (i.e., temperature, time, and pressure) for sterilization and safety tests in this study into current legal conditions and optimal treatment conditions. First, the current legal condition indicates following the operating conditions stipulated in the Wastes Control Act of the Republic of Korea. Appendix 9 of the Enforcement Rules of the Wastes Control Act specifies the conditions for maintaining the performance of the facility for steam sterilization and crushing. It states, “A facility that heats to high temperature after infiltrating moisture with steam must ensure that there should be waste for more than 30 minutes in a sterilization chamber with a minimum of 121 degrees Celsius and a minimum of 1 atmosphere gauge.” The first condition for the sterilization test, i.e., the current legal condition, was set as 121 degrees Celsius, 30 min, and 1 bar gauge(1 atm gauge) based on the Enforcement Rules. A facility for steam sterilization and crushing installed in a hospital in a metropolitan area has already treated actual general medical waste generated by the hospital in line with the legal condition. Therefore, this current legal condition did not require any adjustment to the equipment controller. Through this process, we placed vials, which were produced by using the five microorganisms selected for this research, in the indicator inlet for the sterilization test to confirm sterility.

Next, the optimal treatment conditions refer to modified conditions designed to improve the efficiency of existing treatment processes, assuming that technologically advanced methods will be introduced into the Wastes Control Act in the future. The facility for steam sterilization and crushing used in this study was manufactured by French Company E, and the initial operating condition of this facility was set as 138 degrees, 10 min, and 2.5 bar gauge(3.5 atm absolute). However, the current operating conditions of the facility for steam sterilization and crushing in the Republic of Korea are set as 121 degrees, 30 min, and 1 bar gauge(1 atm gauge), according to Appendix 9 of the Wastes Control Act. Although facilities for sterilization and crushing with various efficient treatment conditions have been manufactured and used in other countries, it is difficult to introduce equipment with various operating conditions due to the conditions stipulated under the Wastes Control Act in the Republic of Korea. Therefore, this study attempted to assume that equipment with various operating conditions would be introduced, and to confirm the sterilization effect by targeting general medical waste generated in a general hospital under those operating conditions. In this sense, the optimal treatment condition in this study is the recommended operating condition of the facility for steam sterilization and crushing, and we performed a test by setting the conditions as 138 degrees, 10 min, and 2.5 bar gauge(3.5 atm absolute). Similar to the test conditions according to the current criteria, we placed vials, which were produced by using the five microorganisms selected for this research, in the biological indicator port of the sterilizer to confirm sterilization.

Selection of representative species and sterilization criteria for sterilization test

Representative species and sterilization criteria for the sterilization test were selected by referring to domestic and international data, after considering the conditions that can be handled in the laboratory and applicable to the equipment. The overseas data for selecting representative species were based on WHO guidelines and STAATT data from the United States, and the domestic data were based on data from the Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency15, 16, STAATT, 1988; Guidelines for Efficacy Testing of Veterinary Disinfectants, 2018). Furthermore, the criteria for selecting sterilization criteria for medical waste were based on WHO guidelines16. The guidelines previously recommended an exposure to 121 degrees for more than 30 min, but if various factors (time, pressure, and process) are considered, more effective treatment can be achieved. Therefore, the guidelines recommend initial testing and periodic inspection based on equipment characteristics and treatment conditions to set effective sterilization conditions for medical waste. The steps to inactivate microorganisms are also divided into four phases, of which phase three(6 log₁₀ reduction of bacterial spore) is recommended for the appropriate treatment of medical waste. The microorganisms that fall under these three stages are bacteria, fungi and lipophilic and hydrophilic viruses, parasites and mycobacteria, Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores, and Bacillus atrophaeus spores. Furthermore, an inactivation criterion of at least 6 log₁₀ reduction was established for each type of microorganism, including bacteria, fungi, lipophilic and hydrophilic viruses, parasites, and mycobacteria. On the other hand, Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores, and Bacillus atrophaeus spores were suggested to remove when their inactivation criteria exceed 4 log₁₀. Additionally, the Guidelines for Efficacy Testing of Veterinary Disinfectants suggested such species as Salmonella typhimurium, Clostridium perfringens, and Mycobacterium fortuitum, as indicators for sterilization verification. In this study, we utilized domestic and international data and attempted to select representative species that can be realistically applied to facilities for sterilization and crushing. Biological indicator vial condition setting for sterilization test

Currently, Geobacillus stearothermophilus is the only standard indicator microorganism stipulated for the sterility verification of steam sterilization and crushing facilities under the Korean waste process test standards. However, to evaluate the biological safety of potential diversified sterilization conditions in the future, this study verified the sterilization efficacy of five microbial species, including the legally required indicator.

While commercial G. stearothermophilus indicators are standard in Korean facilities, custom biological indicator vials were fabricated to quantify the reduction rates of all five test species. The biological indicator vials were designed to satisfy two criteria: (1) sufficient steam permeability for microbial contact and (2) prevention of microbial leakage. Prior to use, the vials, caps, and cotton canvas carriers were sterilized. Each indicator was prepared by inoculating 1 mL of microbial suspension (≥ 1.0 × 106 CFU/g or PFU/g) onto a carrier.

To ensure the reproducibility and robustness of the results, three independent biological replicates were performed for each of the two operating conditions: the current legal condition (121 °C·30 min·1 bar gauge(1 atm gauge)) and the optimal treatment condition (138 °C·10 min·2.5 bar gauge(3.5 atm absolute)). Safety assessment of wastewater, sterilized and crushed wastes, and indoor air quality

For the safety test of the facility for steam sterilization and crushing, we confirmed the pH of the wastewater generated during equipment operation, the moisture content of the sterilized and crushed wastes, and the concentration of indoor air quality before and after the operation. Under the current Wastes Control Act of the Republic of Korea, there is no separate management criteria for wastewater generated from facilities for steam sterilization and crushing, and the wastewater generated after operation is transported to the wastewater treatment plant in a hospital for treatment. Therefore, we analyzed the pH of the wastewater before it was moved to the wastewater treatment plant to determine its harmfulness. The analysis was performed according to the hydrogen ion concentration - glass electrode method of the Waste Process Test Standard, and the harmfulness was determined based on Appendix 13 “Permissible Discharge Limits for Water Pollutant” of the Enforcement Rules of the Water Environment Conservation Act. Next, we analyzed the moisture content of the sterilized and crushed wastes. The current regulations require that the sterilized and crushed wastes discharged from the facility for steam sterilization and crushing after operation should be incinerated in an industrial waste incinerator, then landfilled. Since the sterilized and crushed wastes are currently managed under the Enforcement Rules of the Wastes Control Act, we evaluated the safety through the difference in sterilized and crushed wastes according to different operating conditions of the facility. The moisture content of the wastes was analyzed in accordance with the moisture and solids-gravimetric method of the Waste Process Test Standard, and the safety was evaluated by satisfying the criteria of 50% or less specified in Appendix 9 of the Enforcement Rules of the Wastes Control Act.

Finally, we attempted to evaluate the safety by analyzing the indoor air quality around the facility before and after operation. The current Wastes Control Act does not provide a separate management standard for steam generated from a facility for steam sterilization and crushing. However, the Enforcement Rules of the Indoor Air Quality Control Act specified that the indoor air quality should be controlled based on the maintenance and recommended standards. The indoor air quality maintenance standard is a standard that managers of multi-use facilities must follow; if it is exceeded, administrative penalties will be imposed. Multi-use facilities can be categorized into five types of facilities with similar sizes and usages, such as train station waiting rooms, museums, medical institutions, indoor parking lots, and indoor business facilities. Additionally, the maintenance standard items encompass particulate matter (PM 10 and PM 2.5), carbon dioxide, formaldehyde, total airborne bacteria, and carbon monoxide. On the other hand, the indoor air quality recommended standard is a recommended standard for maintaining pollutant concentrations below a certain level, and there is no administrative punishment. Multi-use facilities in the recommended standard are similar to those in the maintenance standard, but they are classified into three groups according to purposes and sizes, such as underground stations, medical institutions, and indoor parking lots. The items of the recommended standard include nitrogen dioxide, radon, total volatile organic compounds, and fungi. In this study, PM 10, PM 2.5, carbon dioxide, formaldehyde, and total airborne bacteria were selected from the maintenance standard, because they are expected to be generated after the operation of facility for steam sterilization and crushing. Furthermore, total volatile organic compounds were selected from the recommended standard. The measurement and analysis method and equipment for each item are as follows. Particulate matter (PM 10 and PM 2.5) was analyzed using the gravimetric method after sample collection with a particulate matter sampler (KMS-4100). Formaldehyde samples were collected using a 2,4-DNPH cartridge, and analyzed with HPLC, and total airborne bacteria samples were collected in the medium according to the collision method (MAS-100 Eco), and then were analyzed. Finally, total volatile organic compounds were analyzed by TD/GC-FID after adsorption on Tenax tubes according to the solid adsorption and thermal desorption.

In this study, we collected and analyzed samples at three points for indoor air quality measurement. Specifically, the top of the facility for steam sterilization and crushing was set as Point 1, the bottom of the facility was set as Point 2, and the entrance and exit of the site with the facility were set as Point 3. Furthermore, we determined the measurement conditions as follows: background concentration measurement, current legal condition after operation, and optimal treatment condition after operation.

Results and discussion

Medical waste control system and status

Definition and classification of medical waste

Before 2000, medical waste in the Republic of Korea was managed by the Ministry of Health and Welfare under the concept of ‘extracted tissues.’ Based on the rules of extraction material disposal, storage, transport and disposal methods, designation of extraction material disposal companies, and other details required for the management of the materials were stipulated. However, in August 2000, the ‘extracted tissues’ controlled by the Ministry of Health and Welfare started to be controlled under the Ministry of Environment’s Wastes Control Act2. Wastes in the Republic of Korea are categorized into household and industrial wastes under the Wastes Control Act. Household wastes refer to wastes other than industrial wastes, and industrial wastes refer to wastes discharged from designated places of business, and other places of business generating other designated wastes. Current medical waste belongs to designated waste among industrial wastes. In the Wastes Control Act, “medical wastes” are defined as “substances causing harm to human bodies among the wastes discharged from public health and medical institutions, veterinary clinics, testing and inspection institutions and other similar institutions.” Medical waste can be classified into three main categories: isolated medical waste, hazardous medical waste, and general medical waste. In detail, the hazardous medical waste is categorized into the following eight types: organ waste, pathological waste, sharps waste, biochemical waste, and blood-contaminated waste10.

Status of medical waste generation and disposal

As a result of examining the amount of medical waste generated per type, medical wastes have tended to increase continuously since 2016. However, the amount of medical waste generated in 2020 was 195,351 tons, which is approximately 17.14% lower than the amount generated in 2019 (235,754 tons)6. Such a decrease is assumed to be due to a sharp drop in the number of visitors to medical institutions due to the COVID-19 outbreak in 2020. Furthermore, in October 2019, the Republic of Korea excluded disposable diapers consumed by patients with non-infectious diseases from medical waste categories. Such factors are assumed to contribute to a temporary decrease in medical waste generation17. General medical waste accounted for the largest portion (more than 60%) among the eight types of medical waste during the period from 2017 to 2022. This general medical waste includes cotton, bandages, gauze, disposable diapers, sanitary pads, disposable syringes, and infusion sets that may contain blood, body fluids, secretions, and excrement. In the Republic of Korea, general medical waste must be sterilized and crushed according to the specific standards and methods for waste disposal, which are specified in Appendix 5 of the Enforcement Rules of the Wastes Control Act. Furthermore, the treated waste after sterilization and crushing is regarded as general industrial waste, and incinerated. In other words, the general medical waste, which accounts for the majority of medical wastes, is sterilized and crushed within a hospital site, and is labelled as a general industrial waste, leading to a decrease in the total amount of medical waste generation.

Additionally, medical waste is required to be disposed of by waste generators or outsourced to a waste disposal company. This treatment is broadly categorized into self-treatment and outsourced treatment. The former encompasses incineration and other methods, whereas the latter includes incineration, sterilization and crushing, and recycling. Such medical waste must be disposed of in dedicated incineration facilities and facilities for sterilization and crushing. Isolated medical waste, organ waste, biochemical waste, medical waste with substances that can flow down (e.g., blood and body fluid secretions), and medical waste disposed of by intermediate or final disposers shall be incinerated. Other medical waste can be incinerated or sterilized and crushed. Incineration is the predominant treatment method, with its share increasing from 92.9% in 2017 to 97.5% in 2022.

Currently, more than 50% of medical waste in the Republic of Korea is generated within the Seoul Metropolitan Area. However, the majority of dedicated medical waste incinerators are situated in regions outside the Seoul Metropolitan Area6. Furthermore, although the operational capacity of these dedicated incinerators has reached its critical threshold, the establishment of new facilities or the expansion of existing ones remains highly challenging due to social and environmental constraints18.

Sterilization and safety tests per operating condition of a facility for sterilization and crushing

Sterilization test under current legal condition

As a result of the sterilization test under the operating conditions (121 degrees/30 minutes/1 bar gauge(1 atm gauge)) of the facility for steam sterilization and crushing stipulated in the current Act, Bacillus atrophaeus showed a sterilization effect of 99.9999% at the first, second, and third results. The WHO guidelines for medical waste indicate the inactivation standard for medical waste treatment. As for Bacillus atrophaeus spores, the guidelines recommended removing more than 4 log₁₀ for safe sterilization. In this study, we confirmed the sterilization effect of 6 log₁₀, which is 99.9999%, indicating that it sufficiently met the criteria set by the WHO. The WHO guidelines also recommended sterilization of 4 log₁₀ or greater for Salmonella typhimurium, and Mycobacterium fortuitum. Both of these microorganisms were reduced to 6 log₁₀, while showing a sterilization effect of 99.9999%, indicating that the criteria suggested by the WHO was satisfied. In the case of Aspergillus niger, which belongs to fungi, the pre-treatment results showed confluent growth, recorded as Too Numerous To Count (TNTC). This confirms that the initial concentration was sufficiently high to validate the microbial challenge. Post-treatment, no fungal colonies were observed, indicating that the counts were below the limit of detection (< 1.0 × 101 CFU/g). Consequently, a sterilization efficacy of > 99.9999% was achieved. This result satisfies Level 3 of the WHO inactivation standards for medical waste treatment, which requires at least a 6 log₁₀ reduction for fungi. Furthermore, Bacteriophage, which belongs to viruses, showed a 99.9999% sterilization effectiveness at the first, second, and third results. The WHO guidelines suggest that all viruses should be removed by 6 log₁₀ or greater, and the sterilization effect of 99.9999% was confirmed in this study, meeting the WHO standard.

We concluded the following based on such findings. Currently, steam sterilization and crushing facilities in the Republic of Korea use only one species - Geobacillus stearothermophilus – for a sterilization testing. This species is presumed to have been included in regulatory standards based on the assumption that effective sterilization of Geobacillus species—known for their spore-forming ability and high thermal resistance—would also ensure the inactivation of other microorganisms. The findings of this study indicated that the sterilization effect was higher than 99.9999%, not only for spore-forming Bacillus species, but also for other microorganisms. Based on the findings, if there are more diverse interim treatment methods using a facility for steam sterilization and crushing, it is assumed that various species can be utilized as indicator microorganisms for the sterilization test. Sterilization test under optimal treatment conditions

As a result of the sterilization test under an optimal treatment condition (138 degrees/10 minutes/2.5 bar gauge(3.5 atm absolute)), Bacillus atrophaeus indicated a sterilization effect of 99.9999% at the first, second, and third results, confirming the decrease of more than 6 log₁₀, which is the criteria suggested by the WHO. Salmonella typhimurium, and Mycobacterium fortuitum also showed a sterilization effect of 99.9999% at the first, second, and third results, while meeting the WHO criteria of 6 log₁₀ or higher. In the case of Aspergillus niger, the pre-treatment results showed confluent growth, recorded as Too Numerous To Count (TNTC), confirming a sufficient initial microbial challenge. Post-treatment, no colonies were observed in any of the replicates, indicating that the counts were below the limit of detection (< 1.0 × 101 CFU/g). Consequently, a sterilization efficacy of > 99.9999% was achieved, satisfying the WHO Level 3 inactivation standard which requires at least a 6 log₁₀ reduction for fungi. Bacteriophage, which is a virus infecting bacteria, also showed a sterilization effect of 99.9999% at the first, second, and third results, meeting the WHO criteria.

We concluded the following based on such findings. First, it is possible to operate a facility for steam sterilization and crushing with a new operation method through technological advancement in the Republic of Korea. The conditions for a facility for steam sterilization and crushing that can be operated in the country are currently limited to 121 degrees and 30 min, which were set in the past. Such a limitation has made it impossible to introduce a technologically advanced facility for steam sterilization and crushing, even though it is developed in Korea or abroad. As a result of the sterilization test in this study, all microorganisms were confirmed to be safely sterilized even under the conditions of technological advancement. In particular, even Bacillus, which has the highest thermal resistance due to its ability to form spores, was found to be 99.9999% sterilized. These results reveal that it is assumed to be possible to introduce facilities with various conditions, such as high treatment efficiencies through shortened operating hours after reflecting advanced technologies. Furthermore, a higher throughput by shortening the operating hours will lead to a higher operation efficiency of the facility, which may serve as a factor for more installation of facilities for sterilization and crushing in hospitals. Second, it is possible to apply various microbial species as indicator organisms for a facility for steam sterilization and crushing in the Republic of Korea. Microorganisms of the genus Bacillus were set as indicator organisms to confirm sterilization in the Republic of Korea, and if they were reduced by more than 99.99%, the sterilization was assumed to be achieved. However, as confirmed earlier, five organisms showed sterilization effects under the current legal operating condition, and the optimal treatment condition. Thus, various microorganisms may be adopted as indicator organisms in future sterilization test. The results of the sterilization test under optimal treatment condition are shown in Tables 4 and 5.

Safety assessment of the facility for sterilization and crushing under different operating condition

Analysis of wastewater pH and moisture content of sterilized and crushed wastes

In the Republic of Korea, there is no regulation on the pH of wastewater generated in the treatment, installation, and inspection of facilities for steam sterilization and crushing. It was found that the wastewater generated after treatment in the currently operating facility for steam sterilization and crushing is transported to a wastewater treatment plant located in a hospital and is treated appropriately. However, in this study, we attempted to confirm the pH of the wastewater before moving to the wastewater treatment plant, and to review the safety of the wastewater generated after operating the facility. The pH analysis results were evaluated for potential environmental hazard according to Appendix 13 Permissible Discharge Limits for Water Pollutants, of the Enforcement Rules of the Water Environment Conservation Act. The rules state that ‘wastewater beyond a pH range of 5.8–8.6 should be treated.’ However, as a result of confirming it in this study, the average pH of wastewater generated after operating the treatment facility under the current legal condition was 8.54, and that under the optimal treatment condition was 6.63. These results indicate that special wastewater treatment according to the Water Environment Conservation Act is not required, and the wastewater generated after operating the facility is assumed to be non-hazardous (Table 6).

In contrast, at the top of the facility (Point 1) and the bottom of the facility (Point 2) sections of the equipment, both formaldehyde and TVOC concentrations exhibited an increasing trend. In particular, TVOC concentrations increased by approximately 4–7 times compared to the background levels under both experimental conditions. The medical waste treated in this study consisted of actual general medical waste from a hospital, which was primarily composed of plastic materials.

Previous studies have demonstrated that VOC emissions from plastics increase with rising temperature. Modeling results have also indicated that VOC emission rates from plastic materials increase as temperature rises. These studies suggest that chemical compounds migrate from the interior of polymers to the surface and subsequently diffuse into the surrounding air. Such VOC transport may influence the formation and concentration of secondary pollutants in indoor air, as supported by modeling results. Furthermore, predictive modeling has indicated that secondary pollutants, such as formaldehyde, may increase simultaneously under elevated temperature conditions19. Another study reported that VOC emissions increased when the ambient temperature around plastic products rose20. Additionally, research on substances emitted from PVC samples showed that only trace amounts of phenol, 2-ethylhexanol, and hydrocarbons were emitted at 25 °C, whereas emissions increased sharply with increasing temperature21.

In this study, the relationship between temperature changes of plastic components in general medical waste and the increase in formaldehyde and TVOC concentrations was not experimentally investigated. However, based on the aforementioned literature, it can be reasonably inferred that the elevated concentrations of formaldehyde and TVOCs observed in this study may be attributed to temperature-induced emissions from plastic components in the general medical waste.

Finally, the total airborne bacteria (TAB) concentrations measured during the operation of the sterilization and crushing facility under both the legal and optimal conditions were higher than the background levels at all three measurement points; however, none of the measured values exceeded the Korean indoor air quality standards (800 CFU/㎥). Given that the facility utilizes high-temperature steam for sterilization, the observed results are interpreted as being potentially influenced by the increase in indoor air temperature during equipment operation. This interpretation aligns with existing literature suggesting a correlation between rising indoor temperatures and increased microbial concentrations22, 23, 24. Notably, while Point 3 showed the most significant increase, this location was situated near the entrance/exit of the facility room. It is assumed to have been affected by external factors, such as the movement of operators or the inflow of outside air during facility operation. Furthermore, the environmental conditions in Korea during August, characterized by high temperature and humidity (rainy season), are thought to have influenced the results. Existing research regarding the seasonal effects on bioaerosol concentrations supports this assessment24, 25.

Based on these results, as for the particulate matter, including PM 10 and PM 2.5, the background concentrations were higher before the equipment was operated. Furthermore, the trend was the same - an increase under all three conditions and at all three points. Although both PM 10 and PM 2.5 were found to be below the national standard, further research is needed to additionally identify the reasons for the higher background concentrations. As for total volatile organic compounds, and formaldehyde, the legal condition and the optimal treatment condition, which had higher temperature conditions than background conditions, showed higher concentrations. Further studies are needed to reconfirm the correlation between plastics and temperature in general medical waste, which is the target sample of this study.

Similarly, additional investigation across different seasons is deemed necessary to fully elucidate the correlation between total airborne bacteria concentrations and seasonal variations. Indoor air quality measurements are generally sensitive to the surrounding environment; in this study, data were collected in August during the rainy season, characterized by high precipitation (100 mm) and high humidity (average 75%). These specific environmental conditions may have influenced the measurement outcomes. Consequently, further research incorporating various seasons and meteorological factors is required to ensure the stable and safe management of medical waste sterilization and crushing facilities in the future.

Study limitations

This study was conducted at a single steam sterilization and crushing facility operating within a general hospital in the Republic of Korea. Due to the fact that this was the only facility legally operating in Korea at the time of the study, expanding the research to multiple sites was not feasible. Furthermore, because the facility was actively processing medical waste from actual patient treatments on a daily basis, the monitoring period and the number of operational cycles available for research were inevitably limited.

Regarding the wastewater assessment, this study restricted the analysis to pH levels for several methodological reasons. First, as previously mentioned, the Wastes Control Act, the primary regulation for medical waste in the Republic of Korea, does not currently specify separate management items or mandatory evaluation criteria for wastewater generated from medical waste treatment facilities. Second, under the current regulatory framework, wastewater produced after the sterilization process is integrated with other hospital effluents and subsequently treated through the hospital’s on-site wastewater treatment plant. Third, since the wastewater originates from the process of sterilizing waste following patient treatment, it was judged unlikely to contain specific hazardous pollutants typical of general industrial wastewater. Consequently, pH was selected as the most fundamental parameter among the wastewater analysis items managed in Korea to identify the basic characteristics of the effluent.

The authors acknowledge that the findings from a single facility with limited operational cycles may not be universally applicable to all medical waste treatment contexts. Nevertheless, as steam sterilization and crushing is one of the most promising methods for expanding medical waste treatment capacity in Korea, this study serves as a pioneering effort to improve operational regulations. The results provide valuable foundational data for future large-scale research and policy development.

Conclusions

The total amount of medical waste generated in the Republic of Korea in 2022 was 229,502 tons, which has continuously increased every year, and increased approximately 1.35 times compared to the amount generated in 2014 (169,926 tons). Among the various treatment methods for medical waste, incineration at dedicated facilities accounted for the majority. Reliance on incineration has grown, comprising 92.3% of treatment in 2016 and 96.8% in 2020. However, the capacity of dedicated incinerators is insufficient to handle the larger amount of waste generated. Furthermore, it is difficult to expand or create new dedicated incinerators due to opposition from local residents and local governments. To address these problems, there have been plans to expand the installation of facilities by diversifying interim treatment methods for medical waste.

In the Republic of Korea, the facility for sterilization and crushing, which is an interim treatment method for medical waste, is limited to thermal methods and only steam, microwave, and heat pipes are allowed. However, other chemical, biological, and other methods are not allowed. Additionally, environmental conditions (i.e., temperature, time, and pressure) are regulated for each condition, which hinders the import of technologically advanced equipment. Next, each condition requires a sterilization test using spore-forming bacteria. However, other countries allow diverse methods, including thermal, chemical, and biological methods as interim treatment methods for medical waste. Furthermore, the environmental conditions (i.e., temperature, time, and pressure) and microbial species are determined for each method, but if they show equivalent efficacy, they can be used with the approval of the department in charge. As such, the interim treatment methods for medical waste in the Republic of Korea are considerably regulated compared to other countries’ interim treatment methods, hindering the application of various interim treatment methods with technology advancement in the Republic of Korea.

This study compared the treatment efficacy of a medical waste steam sterilization and crushing facility under current legal conditions and optimal treatment conditions.

The results showed that all five indicator microorganisms Bacillus atrophaeus, Salmonella typhimurium, Mycobacterium fortuitum, Aspergillus niger, and Bacteriophage achieved a 99.9999% (6 log10) reduction under both the legal (121 °C, 30 min, 1 bar gauge(1 atm gauge)) and advanced (138 °C, 10 min, 2.5 bar gauge(3.5 atm absolute)) conditions. These findings satisfy the WHO Level 3 inactivation standards for medical waste.

Regarding safety assessments, wastewater pH (8.54 and 6.63) met the effluent standards of the Water Environment Conservation Act (pH 5.8 ~ 8.6). The moisture content of the residues (38.06% and 26.45%) also complied with the Wastes Control Act. Although the concentrations of TVOC and formaldehyde increased after operation, these results should be interpreted in correlation with the temperature rise of the plastics within the general medical waste. Similarly, the increase in TAB concentration appeared to be temperature-dependent and should be interpreted in correlation with both the thermal elevation resulting from equipment operation and the prevailing hot and humid seasonal influences in Korea during the study period.

While these findings provide foundational data for policy improvement in Korea, further research is required to evaluate broader indicator microbes beyond spores and to monitor seasonal air quality variations to ensure the stable management of future facilities. In preparation for the future introduction of steam sterilization and crushing equipment with diversified operating conditions, additional studies on indoor air quality across various seasonal and environmental parameters are deemed necessary to ensure effective air quality management in operational spaces.

As this study was conducted at a single facility over a constrained research period with a limited number of operational trials, there are limitations to the universal applicability of these findings across all facilities. Nonetheless, the results provide essential foundational data that can serve as a reference for the expanded installation of such facilities in various hospitals, thereby facilitating the diversification of medical waste treatment methods in the Republic of Korea. Furthermore, these findings hold significant practical importance in achieving national goals for reducing the continuously increasing volume of medical waste generation.
