Purely extraosseous epidural cavernous hemangioma: a rare diagnostic entity. Illustrative case
Authors: Mokshal Porwal, Maaria Chaudhry, Nassim Stegamat, Cody Woodhouse, Shahed Elhamdani, Chen Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDExtraosseous epidural cavernous hemangiomas are rare spinal lesions. While often asymptomatic, they can present in patients with myelopathy-/radiculopathy-like symptoms. These lesions can be caused by venous pooling secondary to negative pressure within the intrathoracic cavity. Diagnosis is often challenging and largely relies on radiographic imaging, where lesions are commonly found in the thoracic spine region and present as a homogeneous contrast-enhancing signal intensity on MRI T1-weighted sequences and appear as hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences. Although usually indolent, these lesions can cause compression or can rupture, causing hyperacute neurological decline.OBSERVATIONSThe authors describe the case of a 77-year-old woman with a history of breast cancer and melanoma presenting with progressive low back pain and new urinary incontinence. Initial lumbar MRI was unremarkable, but thoracic MRI revealed a 2-cm dorsal epidural lesion at the T7 level. Minimally invasive laminectomy achieved gross-total resection and pathological analysis confirmed a cavernous hemangioma. On the 1-month follow-up, the patient reported complete remediation of symptoms.LESSONSThis case highlights the importance of including extraosseous epidural cavernous hemangioma in the differential diagnosis for spinal lesions, even in patients with nonspecific symptoms. Early recognition requires considering spinal imaging beyond the symptomatic region. Prompt resection is recommended to prevent worsening symptomatology and acute neurological decline. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25637

Vertebral hemangiomas are benign, vascular lesions often found within the spinal intramedullary space.1 While the majority of hemangiomas occur intracranially, vertebral hemangiomas tend to grow within marrow spaces, with the more aggressive subtypes occasionally encompassing the entire vertebral body.1 Vertebral hemangiomas with no osseous involvement are an extremely rare subset of hemangiomas thought to comprise a small percentage of all extradural lesions, with less than a couple hundred reports in the literature.2 Most vertebral hemangiomas are asymptomatic, but an estimated 1% of lesions that are symptomatic often present similar to disc herniation.1 Accurate preoperative diagnosis of epidural hemangiomas can be challenging because they are often mistaken for a variety of more commonly occurring lesions, such as schwannomas, meningiomas, or arachnoid cysts.3 Unlike these other lesions, the risk of rupture with epidural hemangiomas is higher, which may lead to acute neurological decline from mass effect and local irritation. Risk factors for rupture can include lesion hypermobility and pregnancy but are not entirely known.4 Therefore, epidural hemangiomas can present more acutely and might necessitate more urgent surgical intervention than their other more benign counterparts. We present a case of a purely extraosseous epidural cavernous hemangioma in a patient presenting with nonfocal, chronic symptoms of back pain and urinary incontinence. Additionally, we provide a comprehensive review of the literature to further highlight diagnostic and treatment paradigms.

Illustrative Case

A 77-year-old female patient with a medical history of breast cancer and melanoma visited her primary care doctor with the chief complaint of progressively worsening back pain and new-onset urinary incontinence. She first noticed the low back pain several months before presentation and denied any trauma or inciting factors preceding the onset of her symptoms. The patient remained functionally independent; however, due to her persistent symptoms, lumbar MRI was completed by her primary care physician, which showed no acute abnormalities. The patient then presented to the emergency department for continued episodes of symptoms for which the neurosurgery department was consulted. The patient presented with no focal deficits, and motor and sensory examination was intact. After neurosurgical evaluation, thoracic MRI was ordered for further investigation, which revealed a 2-cm contrast-enhancing cystic dorsal epidural lesion at the level of T7 (Fig. 1). The patient was subsequently admitted for an oncological workup. A CT study of the patient’s chest, abdomen, and pelvis and nuclear medicine bone imaging revealed no evident metastatic lesions. Given the isolated spinal lesion and patient’s symptoms, she was taken for a T7 minimally invasive spinal laminectomy and mass resection. During resection, the mass was noted to be purely extraosseous and epidural, and gross-total resection (GTR) was achieved. The patient tolerated surgery well and was discharged home 2 days postoperation. The patient reported significant improvement in her urinary incontinence and back pain 1 month after surgery. Final pathological analysis confirmed cavernous hemangioma. The patient continued to do well at the 4-month postoperative follow-up; her neurological examination remained intact, and back pain and urinary incontinence improved. Discussion

We describe the case of a patient with a rare thoracic epidural hemangioma who presented with symptoms of back pain and urinary incontinence. The patient had no weakness or focal symptoms, leading to a unique diagnostic challenge and unexpected surgical findings.

The first reported case of a purely epidural hemangioma was by Globus and Doshay in 1929.5 Radiographically, these lesions present as a homogeneous contrasting-enhancing signal intensity on T1-weighted images and appear as hyperintense on T2-weighted images.3 Purely epidural hemangiomas are found more often in the thoracic region, possibly due to the large number of vertebrae and intervertebral space. Venous pooling secondary to negative pressure within the intrathoracic cavity may also predispose patients to form hemangiomas.6

The preoperative differential diagnosis for cavernous epidural hemangiomas includes angiomyolipoma, neurofibroma, metastasis, and schwannoma, and imaging may not be enough to distinguish between these lesions. In a study by Zhang et al.,7 preoperative diagnosis based on radiographic findings correctly identified purely epidural hemangiomas only 36% of the time. Therefore, it can be challenging for imaging alone to accurately diagnose cavernous epidural hemangiomas, and intraoperative histopathological confirmation may be needed for a formal diagnosis.

A literature review was conducted using PubMed. The search included the words “cavernous,” “epidural,” and “hemangioma” and yielded 48 results as of November 2024. Of these, 38 papers discussed strictly extraosseous epidural hemangiomas. Most of the studies were case reports and varied widely in country of origin. The age at diagnosis ranged from 6 to 75 years. The majority of lesions in these studies and case reports were found in the thoracic region. Most lesions were discovered in the dorsal aspect of the spinal cord. Symptoms at diagnosis were mainly chronic; however, there were a few documented cases of acute symptoms secondary to spontaneous hemorrhage. The exact symptomatology at presentation was highly variable. Recurrent lesions after GTR were rare.3 A summary of the literature review is outlined in Table 1. Comprehensive review of the literature for cavernous epidural hemangiomas of the spine

LE = lower extremity; STR = subtotal resection; UE = upper extremity.

Surgical intervention is both diagnostic and therapeutic for most symptomatic epidural hemangiomas. The recommended treatment for epidural cavernous hemangiomas is GTR. Reoperation for any remaining lesion is possible; however, the presence of surgical site scarring and dural adhesions from the original operation makes reoperation risky. In cases of subtotal resection, targeted radiation therapy has frequently been used. Preoperative embolization to reduce the risk of large-volume intraoperative hemorrhage during resection may be considered. This should be considered in frail or older patients in whom significant blood loss may be less tolerated. We elected for a minimally invasive decompression and resection to minimize tissue disruption and improve healing. Since imaging is not generally diagnostic for epidural hemangiomas, preoperative embolization was not pursued given ambiguity and the small size of the lesion. However, we were prepared to convert to open decompression and exploration if required intraoperatively. Intraoperative neuromonitoring was utilized to allow for additional safety during the minimally invasive approach.

Delaying resection of epidural cavernous hemangiomas is risky, as symptoms may rapidly progress due to microhemorrhages and compression. In a study conducted by Feng et al.,8 the acute symptoms of epidural hemangiomas were thought to be caused by microhemorrhages within the lesion, leading to abrupt compression of the thecal sac. Increased neovascularization (secondary to increased estrogen), venous buildup, or infection can all provoke rupture of an untreated cavernous hemangioma.9,10 Sudden-onset rupture with debilitating physical examination findings (sudden loss of bowel or bladder incontinence, paraplegia, loss of sensation, etc.) should prompt urgent surgical evacuation of the hematoma.

Observations

Our patient presented with low back pain and urinary incontinence. On initial outpatient evaluation, lumbar MRI was completed and negative for any abnormalities. Because there are no focal neurological symptoms, it may be easy to dismiss these lesions that appear in the cervical and thoracic regions. On evaluation by the neurosurgery team, we determined that the patient’s unexplained urinary incontinence may localize further up the spine given her concurrent back pain. Thoracic imaging was ordered and revealed the abnormality that led to surgical decision-making that may have been missed otherwise.

Lessons

This case highlights that the location for these rare entities is generally in the thoracic spine. As expected, these lesions avidly enhance with contrast. Our case highlights that epidural hemangiomas are important to include in the diagnostic workup for cervical and thoracic lesions in atypical back pain. Expedited diagnostic workup and excision are important, as these lesions may be highly vascular and can present with acute symptoms due to hemorrhage.

The exact incidence of purely epidural cavernous hemangiomas, their rate of growth, and their recurrence rate remain unknown. More importantly, it is unclear what proportion of cavernous hemangiomas acutely rupture and resolve asymptomatically. Therefore, further studies investigating what percentage of epidural cavernous hemangiomas asymptomatically resolve are needed.
