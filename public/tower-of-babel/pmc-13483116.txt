Machine learning-based modeling for precise runoff forecasts in hydrological systems
Authors: Ali Aldrees, Bilal Siddiq, Muhammad Faisal Javed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate daily runoff forecasting can provide detailed, data-driven decisions on normal rainfall, flooding, and drought. Therefore, skillful prediction of daily runoff in a region is a challenging task in hydrology. Most studies rely on standalone models, integrated models, or manually tuned hyperparameters, which hardly handle seasonal data. Therefore, this study attempted to integrate standalone models (Extreme Gradient Boosting, Adaptive Boosting, and Gene Expression Programming) with an advanced technique (Particle Swarm Optimization) for daily runoff prediction. Optimized models; PSO-XGB, PSO-Adb, and PSO-GEP were employed using nine years of rainfall data. The Performance of hybrid models was compared to that of classic GEP, XGB & Adb algorithms as the benchmarks. Models performance were evaluated using RMSE, MAE, NSE, R2, PBIAS, and the a-20 index. Among all methods, PSO-XGB achieved the highest accuracy (R2 = 0.96), outperforming hybrid and standalone models. Regarding the novelty approach, a hypothetical user-friendly Software Interface (SI) was developed for hybrid models to deliver its use for real scenario engineering purposes. Additionally, the study also derived a GEP-based equation for practical runoff magnitude.

Introduction

Rainfall is indeed a crucial hydrological element with significant importance in the water resource sector. However, owing to extreme climate alteration, the runoff pattern and associated hydrological cycle have been adversely changed by human activities1. Therefore, the runoff pattern happens to be non-stationary and non-linear in nature2,3. And the mechanisms of runoff, atmospheric temperature and conditions, precipitation, and evaporation are all influencing factors in the analysis of runoff prediction4. In recent years, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has offered several benefits, such as predicting future events, analysing large and complex datasets quickly, and producing results that are ultimately more affordable and accurate. Various algorithms have emerged as powerful tools for various forecasting, including surface flow renowned techniques like the standalone Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Adaptative boost (Adb), Genetic Programming (GP), Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS), etc., have been employed recently in the runoff modeling process. Studies showed that modeling the relationship between rainfall (R) and runoff (R), where daily discharge is simulated at different rainfall depths (10, 20, and 40 cm) using ML techniques5,6. This literature concluded that the proposed model ML techniques have performed well in capturing maximum runoff. An additional study highlighted the potential of the hybridized ANN technique as an effective approach for modeling seasonal discharge in hydrology7. Similarly, another study compared the Linear Genetic Programming (LGP) with the ANN model for stream flow prediction. LGP performance efficiency was quite higher than the conventional ANN technique8. Similarly, another advanced ML, the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), known for its high accuracy, robustness to overfitting and one of the highly potential MLs for non-linear and high dimensional dataset9. Another study also used XGB for prediction purposes; it has outperformed rival models in the study, such as ANN, SVM, and RF10. The Adaptive Gradient Boost (Adb) algorithm helps in integrating misclassified samples and multiple weak learners into a robust ensemble way. A literature has employed Adb for rainfall-runoff prediction to encounter flood disasters, integrating with PSO significantly improved the rainfall forecasting11.

However, even extreme standalone models rely deeply on hyperparameters that need manual tuning for excellent modelling, and sometimes such standalone or even hybrid ML require large parameters to fine-tune, which results in overfitting and sometimes under fitting problem. More and more studies now approach ensemble and hybrid algorithms that integrate conventional models to fine-tune hyperparameters, eliminate outliers, and deliver high performance. Further, the hybridization of a model through different hybrid techniques can lead to more accurate and robust predictions in various applications, including water quality assessment12–14. A study showed an increase of 40% and 35% regarding prediction while comparing the WT-ANN (Wavelet Transform-Artificial Neural Network) model, which significantly outperformed the basic feedforward neural network (FFNN) model. The same study showed that Wavelet Gene Expression Programming (W-GEP) models outperformed standalone Gene Expression Programming (GEP) models for all nine inputs in runoff forecasting, with unexpected outcomes15. Another study highlighted the combined performance of GEP-GA (Gene Expression Programming-Genetic Algorithm) compared to GP indicates that the GEP-GA approach outperformed the GP in multiple attempts16. A case study utilizes three models: Time-Varying Filter and Empirical Mode Decomposition (TVF-EMD), Variational Model Decomposition (VMD), ANN, and Complete Ensemble Empirical Mode (CEEMDAN) to optimizes runoff forecasting at Wanzhou station. The findings concluded that all hybrid techniques achieved the best results compared to standalone models17. These methods leverage the strengths of multiple individual models by aggregating their predictions, resulting in more accurate and robust results compared to using a single model18. The Swarm Particle Optimization (PSO) can be used as an integration, an effective approach in non-linear Machine learning techniques and working in high-dimensional and dynamic environments, which avoids manual tuning and determine close-optimal auto parameters19. A research shown that integrating Swarm Particle Optimization (PSO) with feed-forward neural networks (FFNN) and gene expression models (GEP) improved the prediction efficiency of water quality20. Similarly, the PSO was used in another study21 to improve SVR. Likewise, another research demonstrated the tuning of applicable models via PSO, such as gradient boosting (PSO-GR), random forest (PSO-RF), support vector machine (PSO-SVR), and artificial neural network (PSO-ANN) models. These improved models efficiently break down unprocessed inputs into high-frequency elements and supply pertinent variables for modelling performance22. Based on the excellent performance of PSO, a recent study23 also reported the high forecasting effectiveness of critical water parameters, such as Total Dissolved Solids and Dissolved Oxygen, after integrating with the PSO algorithm.

The published literature executed GEP, XGB, and Adb largely on a wide range of hydrological studies, motivating professionals to be used on practical purposes. Until recently, limited studies have shown the enhanced accuracy of GEP, XGB, and Adb for the purpose of runoff forecasting. Further, this study attempts to integrate GEP, Adb, and XGB algorithms with the PSO model to improve the model’s accuracy. In summary, the key objectives of this study are: (1) To apply proposed hybrid models on the rainfall dataset received from the Halden watershed located in Trabzon province, Turkey. (2) To evaluate the runoff forecasting models by fitting the correlation between actual and predicted values. (3) Tuning of hyperparameters of integrated models via PSO. (4) Assessing and comparing the performance of each developed model with standalone models. (5) To propose a hypothetical User Interface Tool that could be developed for engineering purposes. (6) Lastly, to propose an equation for rainfall multiple-day prediction based on the GEP model.

Gene expression program (GEP)

The activity of thousands of genes within a cell or tissue can be measured simultaneously using the potent molecular biology approach. The first stage in using the GEP model in this work is creating the expression trees (ETs/chromosome number) that are utilized to address analytical problems29. It often involves measuring expression levels of thousands of genes across a relatively small number of samples. Swarm-based optimization using PSO

Kennedy and Eberhart initially introduced the PSO, drawing inspiration from the collective behavior of fish schools and flocks of birds32. ‘Swarm’ describes a population of possible solutions that are analyzed in parallel to maximize the model parameter, known as particles. The core concepts of PSO include: (1) Particles: moving through the solution, (2) Position: provides a solution of particles, (3) Velocity: Step size and direction for the next iteration, (4) Best Position:

Best position achieved by the particle in the search space, 5. Global Position: The best position by a particle in the swarm algorithm. The swarm consists of multiple particles that move through the solution space at specific velocities, each retaining a memory of its best-found position. Equation (2) presents the particle velocity and best position, and its global best position, while Eq. (3) updates each particle position based on the newly calculated velocity. These formulas work together to direct the iterative process of finding the ideal model parameters33. The overall flow of the PSO methodology is illustrated in Fig. 8. Next, the fitness of each particle was evaluated by training the filtered dataset using the applied machine learning models and calculating the prediction errors for rainfall and runoff dataset. Each particle then updated its best position by selecting the set of hyperparameters that produced the best performance with in the rage, Afterward, the gobal best position was determined by selecting the optimal hyperparameters set for each model among all the best particle positions, The velocities and positions of the particles were subsequently updated using PSO-based equations to guide the hyperparameters toward improved modelling performance. These steps were interactively repeated form the fitness evaluation through the position updates until the maximum number of iterations was reached, Finally, among all the optimized models, the XGB-PSO hyperparameters produced the most accurate predictions for the wastewater parameters, followed by GEP-PSO and GEP-PSO which also delivered strong performance results.

Data Set: training and testing phases

Overfitting can be avoided by properly classifying the dataset into training and testing sets. This ensures that the model learns generic patterns instead of learning the training data. In this type of division, the experimental results are compared to the projected values. If the dataset contains highly diverse or imbalanced categories, the split may be adjusted to ensure that each subset represents the full variety of the data. Two common splits are 70 by 30 (70% for training and 30% for testing) and 80 by 20 (80% for training and 20% for testing). The current study’s authors chose an 80:20 ratio. Approaches such as k cross-validation, regularization, early stopping, noise and missing values, and careful tuning of hyperparameters were likely applied to enhance model robustness, prevent overfitting, and prevent performance degradation on test datasets. Statistical measures

A model verification involves evaluating whether the model has been implemented accurately according to its intended design and whether it performs as expected under tough conditions. The statistical metrics employed in this study are: R2, RMSE, MAE, PBIAS, NSE, and the a20-index. According to published articles, a good correlation between experimental and predicted values is indicated by an R2 ≥ 0.836. The RMSE and MAE measure variance between predicted and actual values. Values ranging from 0.01 to 0.20 are considered satisfactory and vice versa. RMSE and MAE quantify prediction errors, with MAE being more suitable for skewed or noisy data, while the RMSE is preferred for normalized datasets. NSE measures prediction error relative to the variance of observed values, ranging from –∞ to 1, with 1 indicating a perfect match. The a20-index evaluates model reliability when R2 values are similar, reflecting the proportion of predictions within ± 20% of the observed values. Lower error metrics and a higher a-20 index, and an elevated R2 value collectively indicate superior model accuracy and predictive performance37. Another numerical metric, Percent Bias (PBIAS) is used in modelling to indicate whether a model analytically over- or undercalculates predictive values38. A positive PBIAS value indicates an overestimated value, while a negative magnitude shows an underestimated value. Depending upon different thresholds, such as ≤ ± 5, ≤ ± 5 x ≤ ± 10, ≤ ± 10 x ≤ ± 15, > ± 15 differentiate into very good, good, acceptable, and Limited. On the other side, a PBIAS of value closer to zero shows excellent prediction39. These Numerical indices serve as objective tools for assessing a model’s predictive capability, stability, and generalization performance40. 10-fold cross-validation (CV) approach

A reliable method for evaluating machine learning (ML) model performance, particularly on unseen data. This method splits the dataset into k equal folds, training the model on k–1 folds and testing on the remaining one, repeating this process k times. This technique helps mitigate overfitting, reduces sampling bias, and improves model accuracy, as confirmed by statistical indicators. A study suggests that 10-fold offers a good balance between computational efficiency and reliable model assessment41. To achieve this, a model must generalize well, avoiding overfitting and noise in the data. Overfitting occurs when a model learns both the true patterns and random noise, leading to poor performance on unseen data42. Data quality evaluation

To ensure the reliability of input data for machine learning forecasting, a through data quality control process was carried out. Missing values in the hydrological time series were handled using linear interpolation or nearest neighbour imputation, depending on the extent and pattern of the gaps. Consistency checks were performed by comparing rainfall and runoff records to identify possible anomalies or recording errors. In addition, the homogeneity of the time series was evaluated using Pettitts test and the Standard Normal Homogeneity Test (SNHT) to detect any abrupt changes in data trends.

Auto correlation function (ACF) & partial ACF

After the pre-processing stage, the raw data may still contain trends and non-linearity can lead to misleading the model. Inconsistencies in rainfall patterns, climate change, rainfall measurement devices, soil infiltration, groundwater recharge, evaporation rates and building structures are some of the factors that often contribute to delays in data collection. Mostly rainfall comprises lag, and for accurate time series modelling, the optimal lag is highly important to identify. The reason behind the lag is that mostly rainfall time series, the data is not necessarily linear. Further, without recognizing the optimal lag, the model performs poorly and results in unacceptable efficiency. Thus, the ACF measure correlation between the rainfall timeseries dataset to highlight optimal values while the PACF tool removes the impact of lags and produces fine results. Forecasting evaluation of developed models (PSO-XGb/Adb/GEP)

The predictive performance of optimized hybrid (PSO-XGB/Adb/GEP) and standalone/benchmark models (XGB, Adb & GEP) during runoff forecasting is shown in Table 3; Fig. 13. To assess the runoff predictive quantity, the PSO-XGB model R2 (0.97, 0.95 & 0.95) and all other statistical measures, such as RMSE, a-20 index, NSE, and PBIAS, delivered excellent performance among all rival models against 1st, 2nd, and 3rd day forecasting in the training and testing phase. After PSO-XGB, the PSO-Adb has provided the best outcomes in the regression analysis, R2 = 0.95, 94 and 0.93, and other numerical indicators during predictive days. While PSO-GEP and standalone bench models yielded very good and acceptable outputs in all statistical measures, as shown in Table 3.

Thus, the PSO-XGB, PSO-Adb, and PSO-GEP maintained their superior accuracy in all three forecasting days as compared to the standalone algorithm. Actually, the superior performance of PSO-XGB over PSO-Adb and PSO-GEP is attributed to the difference in boosting mechanisms. Also, this optimized model is capable of automatically capturing complex variable interactions (runoff response vs. dry existing conditions), effectively handling discontinuities in the input and output relationship and presenting threshold-driven behaviors. In rainfall-runoff forecasting, streamflow data typically characterized by imbalanced flow distributions and noisy peak discharge observations, increasing the risk of overfitting. PSO-XGB mitigates this issue by optimizing a regularized gradient-boosting objective using various derivatives, while PSO identifies optimal hyperparameter configurations. This enhances generalization performance and improves predictive stability, particularly under rainfall measurement uncertainty and extreme hydrological variability. Similarly, the PSO-GEP attempts to derive an explicit analytical expression linking rainfall inputs to runoff outputs. Rainfall data usually complex threshold effects, soil moisture, and interaction between previous and current rainfall conditions. Representing such dynamics using a single symbolic expression can limit GEP model flexibility45. Additionally, with the help of PSO integration, it captures auto tuning in the case of learning rate, ensemble tree, maximum depth, etc., resulting in minimum overfitting during runoff forecasting in the modelling of PSO-XGB/Adb44,46.

Limitations and future studies

The thorough construction and optimization of each model in this work shows excellent real-world applicability through improved prediction accuracy and explanatory power. (1) The 9-year dataset is too small for highly precise forecasting, requiring larger datasets. (2) Relying only on rainfall and runoff input dataset without considering other key parameters such as sub catchments, slope of each catchment, climatic criteria, land use type, etc. (3) Regarding models, the XGBoost, though powerful, is computationally demanding, memory-intensive, and less interpretable than simpler models. Similarly, the Adboost may underfit with overly simple weak learners or overfit with complex ones, and poor encoding can restrict the representation of complex functions. While the GEP performance depends heavily on data quality, missing or inaccurate data can significantly reduce prediction accuracy50. (4) When applied to real-world runoff forecasting systems, a model might struggle with unseen conditions, reducing its reliability for operational decision-making52.

Future research possibilities might improve hybrid extreme models by concentrating on important elements associated with runoff/rainfall, such as sub catchments, slope of each catchment, land use type, etc. By adding these essential components, researchers may improve the modeling findings even further producing forecasts for runoff prediction. Further, exploring different hybris models such as BO-XGB, BO-Adb, BO-RF, and BO-GEP may also provide high modelling performance in runoff. rainfall prediction.
