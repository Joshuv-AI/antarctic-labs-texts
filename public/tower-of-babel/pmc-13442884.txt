Intensifying precipitation extremes and shortened snow persistence reshape hydroclimatic risk in a Himalayan mountain basin
Authors: Vinay Kumar Gaddam, Aishwarya Ray, Charanlal Maloth, Shankar Karuppannan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study addresses the limited understanding of how interacting atmospheric extremes and cryospheric changes jointly influence infrastructure risk in high-mountain basins, where most assessments consider these processes independently. The objective of this study is to quantify the coupled impacts of climate extremes and snow cover dynamics on infrastructure exposure in the Baspa Basin, Western Himalaya, using an integrated basin-scale framework. The results show a significant warming trend (~ 0.34 °C per decade) and an increase in rainfall extremes (RX1day increase of ~ 10–20%), accompanied by a decline in snow cover duration (− 4 to − 7 days per decade), particularly at mid-elevations. Spatial analysis indicates that more than 60% of infrastructure is located in areas where increasing rainfall extremes coincide with reduced snow persistence, suggesting emerging compound hydroclimatic risk. These patterns suggest a shift toward increased rainfall-driven runoff and enhanced hydroclimatic variability. However, the results are subject to uncertainties associated with climate data resolution, snow cover estimation, and the assumption of static infrastructure distribution. By integrating atmospheric and cryospheric indicators with exposure analysis, this study provides a novel framework for assessing compound hydroclimatic risk, which may support climate-resilient infrastructure planning in high-mountain regions.Keywords: Climate extremes, Cryosphere–hydrology interactions, Himalayan basin, Snow cover duration, Infrastructure exposureSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

Mountain river basins are among the most climate-sensitive environments due to strong altitudinal gradients, complex terrain–atmosphere interactions, and reliance on seasonal snow storage for hydrological regulation1–3. In the Himalaya, these systems are undergoing rapid transformation driven by warming trends, changing precipitation regimes, and accelerating cryospheric decline4–6. At the same time, settlements, transport corridors, and hydropower infrastructure are increasingly concentrated along narrow valley floors, where exposure to hydro-geomorphic hazards is inherently high7,8. This convergence of climatic sensitivity and spatially constrained development is likely to increase vulnerability to extreme hydroclimatic events9.

A defining feature of climate change in mountain regions is elevation-dependent warming (EDW), wherein warming rates vary with altitude and are often amplified at higher elevations. This phenomenon is influenced by processes such as snow–albedo feedback, atmospheric moisture changes, and variations in cloud cover and lapse rates10. In transitional elevation zones, where temperatures frequently fluctuate around the freezing threshold, even modest warming can substantially reduce snow persistence. Such changes weaken the role of seasonal snowpack as a temporary water reservoir, altering runoff timing, increasing flow variability, and enhancing sensitivity to short-duration rainfall events11,12.

Concurrently, precipitation regimes across the Himalaya are exhibiting increasing variability, with evidence indicating intensification of short-duration extreme rainfall events despite spatially heterogeneous trends in mean annual precipitation13,14. In steep mountain terrain, such extremes may exceed geomorphic thresholds under extreme conditions, triggering flash floods, debris flows, and landslides15,16. The co-occurrence of intensified precipitation and reduced snow buffering has important implications for runoff generation, sediment transport, and slope stability, particularly in basins where anthropogenic modifications have altered natural drainage and terrain conditions17.

Within this context, the cryosphere plays a critical role in mediating these processes by regulating the seasonal redistribution of water through snow accumulation and melt. However, satellite observations across the Himalaya indicate widespread reductions in snow cover duration, upward migration of the seasonal snowline, and increasing occurrence of rain-on-snow conditions in mid-elevation zones18,19. These changes reduce the buffering capacity of mountain catchments and increase the proportion of direct rainfall contribution to runoff, thereby enhancing hydroclimatic intermittency and peak flow variability20.

Despite these advances, existing studies predominantly examine climate extremes, cryospheric change, and hydrological response in isolation, with limited attention to their combined effects on infrastructure exposure and risk21. This limitation restricts the identification of compound hazard pathways, particularly in rapidly developing mountain basins where climate-driven changes interact with expanding infrastructure and land-use transformation. A more integrated framework is therefore needed to assess how intensifying atmospheric extremes and cryospheric decline jointly influence exposure patterns and hydroclimatic risk.

The Baspa Basin in the Western Himalaya provides an appropriate setting for such an analysis due to its steep elevation gradient, mixed snow–rain hydrological regime, and ongoing infrastructure development. The basin is influenced by both western disturbances and monsoon systems, resulting in pronounced seasonal variability and complex hydroclimatic interactions. Increasing settlement expansion, road construction, and hydropower development within valley-confined terrain further enhance exposure to climate-driven hazards. Accordingly, this study develops an integrated climate–cryosphere–exposure framework to quantify hydroclimatic change in the Baspa Basin. The specific objectives are to:

Study area

The Baspa Basin is located in the Kinnaur district of Himachal Pradesh, India, within the northwestern Himalaya, and forms a major right-bank tributary of the Sutlej River. The basin extends between approximately 31.1°–31.6° N and 78.3°–78.8° E, covering an area of ~ 1,100 km2. Elevation ranges from about 1,800 m to over 6,000 m a.s.l., with nearly half of the basin situated above 4,000 m, reflecting pronounced altitudinal variability (Figs. 1a, 1b). The region experiences a dual climatic influence from mid-latitude western disturbances and the Indian Summer Monsoon, resulting in strong seasonal variability. Winters are characterized by snowfall at higher elevations, while monsoon precipitation during July–September contributes substantially to annual runoff. Orographic effects and complex terrain lead to significant spatial heterogeneity in temperature and precipitation. The basin is characterized by steep slopes (Fig. 1c;22), deeply incised valleys, and active geomorphic processes. Settlements, transportation corridors, and hydropower infrastructure are predominantly concentrated along narrow valley floors and river corridors. This terrain-constrained development increases exposure to hydroclimatic hazards, particularly in zones influenced by intense rainfall and snow variability.

Data and methodology

Climate datasets

Daily maximum temperature (Tmax), minimum temperature (Tmin), and precipitation data for 1981–2023 were obtained from the ERA5-Land reanalysis dataset23,24. The data were clipped to the basin boundary derived from a digital elevation model (DEM) and spatially aggregated to represent basin-scale conditions. ERA5-Land has been widely used in high-mountain regions and demonstrates strong agreement with in situ observations, with temperature correlation coefficients typically exceeding 0.80–0.85 and RMSE values below ~ 1.5 °C25. Standard quality-control procedures were applied to identify outliers and physically implausible values26. Missing data were negligible and were not gap-filled to avoid introducing artificial trends. Snow cover and cryosphere analysis

Snow cover dynamics were examined using MODIS MOD10A1 and MYD10A1 daily snow products at 500 m spatial resolution for the period 2000–2023. Because consistent satellite-based snow observations are unavailable prior to 2000, cryospheric trend analysis was restricted to 2000–2023. Climate–snow interaction analyses were conducted exclusively within this overlapping window to ensure temporal consistency and prevent artificial correlations arising from unequal time spans. Cloud-contaminated pixels were minimized using quality assurance flags, temporal compositing, and logical filtering procedures. Daily snow classifications were aggregated seasonally and annually, and snow cover duration (SCD) was calculated as the total number of snow-covered days per grid cell per year. Elevation bands at 500 m intervals were derived from the DEM to assess elevation-dependent snow persistence, enabling detection of differential sensitivity across altitudinal zones10. Validation studies across Himalayan terrain indicate that MODIS snow products achieve overall classification accuracies of approximately 85–92% under clear-sky conditions, with uncertainties primarily associated with cloud contamination and terrain shadow effects30,31. Elevation-stratified analyses in Himalayan basins confirm that appropriately processed and gap-filled MODIS series reliably capture seasonal snow persistence and snowline migration patterns32,33, supporting their suitability for long-term cryospheric trend detection. It is important to note that snow cover analysis is limited to the satellite era (2000–2023), whereas climate extreme indices were evaluated for the longer period 1981–2023. To maintain temporal consistency and avoid artificial correlations arising from unequal record lengths, all climate–cryosphere interaction analyses were restricted to the common overlapping period (2000–2023). Trend magnitudes reported for snow cover duration therefore represent changes during the MODIS observation period only.

Spatial exposure and vulnerability mapping

The Hazard Exposure Index (HEI) is developed to quantify the spatial co-occurrence of climatic and terrain-related factors influencing hydroclimatic exposure. It represents a relative measure of exposure based on the interaction between extreme rainfall intensity, topographic sensitivity, proximity to river channels, and changes in snow cover duration. The index is designed to identify areas where multiple stressors converge, rather than to provide a comprehensive risk assessment, as socio-economic vulnerability and adaptive capacity are not explicitly incorporated.

Spatial exposure was assessed within a GIS framework by integrating climate trend outputs with terrain and infrastructure datasets. Urban settlements, road networks, hydropower installations, slope gradients, drainage networks, and land-use layers were harmonized to a common spatial resolution and coordinate system. Slope gradient derived from the DEM was used as a proxy for geomorphic sensitivity, as steep slopes are strongly associated with rainfall-induced landslide initiation34. Proximity to river channels was incorporated to represent potential flood exposure.

The index was constructed using standardized raster layers representing RX1day trend magnitude, slope gradient, distance to river channel, and snow cover duration decline. Final HEI values were classified into low, moderate, and high exposure categories using the Jenks natural breaks method. This approach enables identification of zones where intensification of extreme rainfall coincides with reduced snow persistence and concentrated infrastructure, supporting basin-scale environmental monitoring and climate adaptation planning.

Results

Temperature extremes

Analysis of ETCCDI temperature indices indicates robust warming across the Baspa Basin during 1981–2023 (Table 3; Fig. 2). Both maximum and minimum temperature extremes exhibit increasing trends; however, indices associated with minimum temperature display stronger rates of change, indicating asymmetric warming and a systematic contraction of cold extremes. Basin-averaged trends show that warm days (TX90p) increased by approximately 2.8% per decade, while warm nights (TN90p) increased more rapidly at 3.6% per decade; both trends are statistically significant (p < 0.05). In contrast, cold days (TX10p) and cold nights (TN10p) exhibit significant declining trends, reflecting a narrowing of the lower tail of the temperature distribution.

Precipitation extremes and variability

Unlike temperature, total annual precipitation over the Baspa Basin does not exhibit a statistically significant long-term monotonic trend during 1981–2023. However, indices characterizing precipitation extremes and intra-annual variability reveal consistent and statistically robust changes (Table 4; Fig. 3). Threshold-based metrics indicate increasing heavy rainfall frequency across substantial portions of the basin. The number of days with precipitation ≥ 20 mm (R20mm) increased by approximately 0.7 days per decade (p < 0.05), while R10mm shows a positive but spatially heterogeneous trend. These results suggest that although mean precipitation remains variable, the frequency of high-intensity rainfall events is increasing40.

Intensity-based indices display a stronger signal of change. Annual maximum 1-day precipitation (RX1day) increased by approximately 4.5 mm per decade (p < 0.05), and maximum consecutive 5-day precipitation (RX5day) increased by approximately 6.8 mm per decade (p < 0.05). Spatial analysis indicates that more than 70% of grid cells exhibit increasing RX1day trends, with statistically significant clusters concentrated in the central and lower valley sectors (Table 4). These valley-floor zones coincide with areas of concentrated settlement and infrastructure, thereby amplifying hazard exposure. The intensification of short-duration rainfall events is consistent with thermodynamic expectations under warming conditions and aligns with prior documentation of increasing convective precipitation extremes across the Western Himalaya13,41.

Hydroclimatic persistence indices further indicate increasing variability in rainfall distribution. Consecutive dry days (CDD) increased by approximately 6 days per decade (p < 0.05), particularly during pre-monsoon and post-monsoon periods. In contrast, trends in consecutive wet days (CWD) are weak and spatially inconsistent. This divergence suggests a transition toward enhanced rainfall intermittency rather than sustained wet conditions. The concurrent increase in CDD and RX1day reflects a redistribution of precipitation into fewer but more intense events, increasing the probability of threshold exceedance in steep terrain. Prolonged dry intervals may reduce soil cohesion and vegetation cover, while subsequent high-intensity rainfall events enhance rapid runoff generation and slope instability. The combined temporal evolution of CDD and RX1day (Fig. 4) therefore indicates growing hydroclimatic intermittency and the emergence of compound risk conditions within the basin. The temporal variability of RX1day and CDD indicates pronounced interannual fluctuations, with a slight increasing tendency in both extreme precipitation intensity and dry spell duration. This concurrent behavior suggests enhanced hydroclimatic intermittency, characterized by the co-occurrence of intensified rainfall extremes and prolonged dry periods.

Changes in snow cover dynamics

Sen’s slope analysis for the MODIS observation period (2000–2023) indicates statistically significant declines in snow cover duration (SCD) across much of the Baspa Basin, particularly within the 2,500–4,500 m elevation bands (− 4 to − 7 days per decade; p < 0.05) (Table 5; Fig. 4). The strongest rates of reduction are observed between 3,500 and 4,500 m, identifying this mid-elevation transition belt as the zone of maximum cryospheric sensitivity. Spatially, these elevations correspond to areas where winter temperatures frequently fluctuate around the freezing threshold, making seasonal snow persistence highly responsive to modest warming. Lower elevation zones (< 2,500 m) also exhibit decreasing SCD, reflecting an increasing proportion of winter precipitation occurring as rainfall rather than snowfall. In contrast, elevations above 4,500 m show weaker or statistically insignificant trends, indicating partial thermal buffering at the highest altitudes where mean winter temperatures remain well below freezing. Snow cover trend analysis is restricted to the MODIS satellite observation period (2000–2023) because consistent spatial snow datasets are not available prior to 2000.

Integrated climate–cryosphere–urban risk interaction

The integrated assessment reveals a compound hazard pathway linking atmospheric extreme intensification, cryospheric decline, and concentrated infrastructure exposure within the Baspa Basin. Spatial overlay analysis indicates that statistically significant increases in RX1day (+ 4.5 mm per decade, p < 0.05) coincide with mid-elevation belts exhibiting rapid declines in snow cover duration (− 4.8 days per decade, p < 0.05). These co-located signals identify transitional elevation zones (3,000–4,500 m) where warming-driven reductions in snow persistence intersect with intensifying short-duration rainfall extremes. This pattern is consistent with emerging tendencies toward increased rainfall contribution to runoff, inferred from shortened snow persistence and intensifying precipitation extremes. Reduced snow persistence decreases transient water storage, advancing melt timing and increasing the proportion of direct rainfall contribution during monsoon events. When extreme precipitation occurs in this altered hydrological context, runoff generation becomes more immediate and discharge flashiness increases. Enhanced peak flows in steep, valley-confined terrain elevate the likelihood of channel instability, bank erosion, debris mobilization, and slope failure.

Infrastructure exposure analysis indicates that approximately 62% of settlements and hydropower installations located within the 3,000–4,500 m elevation band fall inside zones exhibiting both significant RX1day intensification and shortened snow persistence. These infrastructure-dense corridors therefore represent priority compound-risk hotspots. The interaction between intensified rainfall forcing and reduced cryospheric buffering constitutes a hydrogeomorphic amplification mechanism, wherein atmospheric and cryospheric changes reinforce one another to increase threshold exceedance probability. The observed spatial co-occurrence of statistically significant precipitation intensification and snow cover decline within developed valley sectors represents a physically consistent compound hazard pathway rather than independent statistical trends. Climate extremes in this context function as systemic risk multipliers, operating through coupled atmospheric–hydrological–geomorphic processes that disproportionately affect infrastructure concentrated along river corridors. This integrated synthesis demonstrates that emerging urban vulnerability in the basin cannot be attributed to single hazards alone but arises from the interaction of non-stationary climate extremes with evolving cryospheric and land-use conditions. The identification of compound hazard pathways is based on spatial co-occurrence of statistically significant atmospheric and cryospheric trends rather than event-based attribution. While the analysis does not model individual flood or landslide events, the overlapping directional signals provide physically consistent evidence of increasing compound risk potential. The interaction between atmospheric warming, cryospheric decline, and intensified precipitation can be conceptualized as a cascading climate–cryosphere–hydrology risk pathway (Fig. 6), where reduced snow persistence diminishes seasonal storage capacity while extreme rainfall events increase instantaneous runoff generation, collectively amplifying hydro-geomorphic hazards and infrastructure exposure in valley-confined mountain basins.

Urban and infrastructure exposure

Spatial exposure analysis indicates that settlements and critical infrastructure within the Baspa Basin are disproportionately concentrated in zones characterized by elevated compound hazard potential (Table 6; Fig. 7). Approximately 38% of urban settlements and more than 50% of hydropower installations are located within areas classified as high exposure, where statistically significant precipitation intensification coincides with steep slopes, confined valley morphology, and close proximity to active river channels. An additional 30–40% of assets fall within moderate-exposure zones, indicating that a substantial proportion of development lies within terrain sensitive to extreme hydroclimatic forcing. The road network exhibits particularly high susceptibility, with nearly 46% of mapped road segments traversing high-exposure sectors characterized by steep gradients and documented landslide propensity. These transport corridors frequently experience disruption during high-intensity rainfall events, compounding accessibility constraints and delaying emergency response. Hydropower infrastructure is subject to compounded operational and structural risks arising from elevated peak discharge, increased sediment transport, and slope instability within upstream catchments43. Intensification of short-duration rainfall, when combined with reduced snow buffering capacity, enhances sediment mobilization and increases the likelihood of intake blockage, reservoir siltation, and structural stress.

Rather than isolated hazard occurrences, these patterns reflect a systemic interaction between evolving hydroclimatic forcing and pre-existing geomorphic and land-use constraints. Climate extremes in this context operate as amplifiers of baseline geomorphic sensitivity, increasing threshold exceedance probability in already vulnerable terrain. Results and discussion

This study advances understanding of Himalayan hydroclimatic change by integrating extreme climate indices, satellite-derived cryospheric dynamics, and infrastructure exposure within a unified basin-scale framework. Unlike previous studies that assess precipitation or snow processes independently, this work evaluates their interaction and spatial co-occurrence, providing a more comprehensive perspective on compound climate risk in high-mountain environments. The findings align with established evidence of elevation-dependent warming (EDW), where snow–albedo feedback enhances warming at transitional elevations. The observed warming rate (~ 0.34 °C decade⁻1; p < 0.01) is consistent with recent studies across the Western Himalaya, indicating accelerated high-elevation warming. Future warming is projected across the Himalaya44.

A key feature of the basin response is the stronger increase in warm nights compared to warm days, suggesting asymmetric thermal amplification. This pattern is likely driven by reduced nocturnal radiative cooling, increased atmospheric moisture, and changes in cloud cover. Mid-elevation zones (3,000–4,500 m) emerge as particularly sensitive regions, where temperatures frequently fluctuate around the freezing threshold, making snow persistence highly responsive to small thermal changes. Similar elevation-dependent sensitivity has been documented in other mountain systems, including the European Alps and Tibetan Plateau, supporting the broader applicability of these findings.

Precipitation analysis reveals intensification of short-duration rainfall extremes without a corresponding increase in total annual precipitation. This indicates a redistribution of rainfall into fewer but more intense events, consistent with thermodynamic increases in atmospheric moisture capacity under warming conditions. In steep Himalayan terrain, such intensification can exceed geomorphic thresholds, triggering flash floods and landslides even without significant changes in mean rainfall. The concurrent increase in consecutive dry days further highlights growing hydroclimatic intermittency, increasing the likelihood of extreme runoff following prolonged dry periods. Cryospheric changes reinforce these atmospheric signals. Reduced snow cover duration at mid-elevations diminishes seasonal water storage and advances melt timing, increasing the contribution of rainfall to runoff during monsoon periods. The upward migration of the seasonal snowline is consistent with regional observations of snow-to-rain transitions in the Western Himalaya. When combined with intensified precipitation extremes, reduced snow buffering enhances peak discharge, sediment transport, and slope instability. These findings demonstrate that atmospheric and cryospheric processes interact to amplify hydroclimatic extremes, rather than acting independently. Anthropogenic land-use changes further increase basin sensitivity. Infrastructure expansion, including hydropower development and road construction, alters slope stability, drainage pathways, and geomorphic resilience. In the Baspa Basin, infrastructure is concentrated within mid-elevation zones that also exhibit strong climatic changes, increasing exposure to compound hazards. These interacting processes highlight the importance of integrated climate–cryosphere assessments for understanding hydroclimatic variability in mountain basins.

Hydrological implications

Declining snow cover duration (~ 5–7 days per decade) corresponds to an estimated 10–15% reduction in seasonal snow persistence during 2000–2023. This reduces the basin’s natural water storage capacity and advances melt timing, thereby shortening the buffering period between precipitation and runoff. Concurrent increases in short-duration precipitation extremes (RX1day ~ 4.5 mm per decade) suggest that rainfall contributes more directly to runoff generation, leading to higher peak flows and reduced lag times45. Although direct discharge data are unavailable, the combined trends in precipitation and snow dynamics provide physically consistent evidence of increasing rainfall-driven hydrological responses. Hydrological implications are inferred from observed climate–cryosphere interactions rather than directly simulated using hydrological models.

Spatial variability of basin response

Hydroclimatic responses exhibit strong spatial heterogeneity driven by elevation, slope, and atmospheric exposure. Mid-elevation zones (3,000–4,500 m) represent hotspots of coupled climate–cryosphere change, while higher elevations (> 4,500 m) maintain relatively stable snow persistence due to consistently low temperatures. Lower valley regions, characterized by steep terrain and concentrated infrastructure, show stronger alignment with increasing precipitation extremes. These patterns highlight the role of topography and localized energy balance in shaping basin-scale responses. Although detailed sub-basin analysis was beyond the scope of this study, the results indicate that hydroclimatic sensitivity varies significantly across the basin.

Climate extremes as risk multipliers

The results demonstrate that climate extremes amplify existing topographic and land-use vulnerabilities. Intensified rainfall extremes combined with reduced snow persistence decrease hydrological buffering and promote rapid runoff generation. In valley-confined areas with dense infrastructure, this increases flood magnitude and landslide susceptibility. The rise in consecutive dry days, followed by intense rainfall, reflects increasing hydroclimatic variability, where dry conditions may weaken soil cohesion and vegetation stability, enhancing susceptibility to slope failure during subsequent rainfall events. These compound dynamics underscore the importance of extreme-event metrics over mean climate indicators for risk assessment in mountain regions. Similar patterns of intensified precipitation extremes and declining snow persistence have been reported across other Himalayan basins, suggesting that the observed changes are part of a broader regional transition46.

Implications for applied climatology

This study contributes to applied climatology by linking extreme climate indices with spatial exposure patterns in a high-mountain setting. While urban climatology has traditionally focused on lowland environments, mountain settlements face unique challenges associated with elevation-dependent warming, snow–rain transitions, and terrain constraints. By integrating ETCCDI indices with cryospheric and infrastructure data, the study provides a framework for translating climate diagnostics into actionable risk insights. For instance, increases in warm nights influence snowpack stability and freeze–thaw cycles, affecting slope integrity. Similarly, intensification of RX1day and RX5day has direct implications for infrastructure design, early warning systems, and hydropower operations. The basin-scale approach adopted here demonstrates how applied climatology can bridge regional climate trends and local adaptation needs47.

Implications for adaptation planning

The concentration of infrastructure within high-exposure zones highlights the need for climate-resilient planning. Design standards based on historical climate conditions are increasingly inadequate under non-stationary climate regimes. Incorporating extreme-event projections into infrastructure design, slope stabilization, and drainage planning is essential for reducing future risk. Risk-informed land-use planning is particularly important in valley regions where expansion options are limited. Measures such as controlled development, zoning regulations, and nature-based solutions (e.g., slope revegetation and riparian buffers) can reduce vulnerability. Integration of cryospheric changes into water resource management is also critical, as reduced snow persistence alters seasonal water availability for hydropower and downstream users, mainly for disaster risk reduction goals48.

Uncertainty and data limitations

Uncertainty arises primarily from the use of reanalysis and satellite datasets in a complex mountain environment with limited ground observations. ERA5-Land may underestimate localized extreme precipitation due to spatial smoothing, with potential biases of 10–20% in steep terrain. Similarly, MODIS snow products are affected by cloud cover and terrain shadow, although they provide consistent spatial coverage with reported accuracies of 85–92%. These uncertainties may influence the magnitude of detected trends but are unlikely to affect their direction or statistical significance. Therefore, results are interpreted as robust indicators of relative hydroclimatic change rather than precise local estimates. Future studies incorporating multiple datasets and in situ validation would improve confidence in high-mountain climate assessments.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, reanalysis data may smooth localized extremes, potentially underestimating high-intensity precipitation events. Second, MODIS-derived snow data are subject to cloud contamination despite correction procedures. Third, hydrological interpretations are inferred due to the absence of long-term discharge data, limiting direct validation of runoff changes. Fourth, the exposure analysis does not include socio-economic vulnerability or adaptive capacity, and therefore represents exposure rather than risk. Future research should incorporate high-resolution observations, multi-dataset comparisons, and integrated hydrological modeling to better quantify climate impacts. Inclusion of socio-economic factors would enable more comprehensive risk assessments.

Toward climate services and resilience

The findings highlight the need for climate services that incorporate extreme-event metrics and cryospheric indicators into operational decision-making. Indices such as RX1day and RX5day can improve hazard monitoring and early warning systems for floods and landslides. Nature-based solutions, including vegetation-based slope stabilization and restoration of drainage networks, can complement engineered interventions by reducing runoff and enhancing resilience. Strengthening institutional capacity to interpret and apply climate data is essential for effective adaptation. The integrated framework developed in this study is transferable to other high-mountain basins undergoing similar hydroclimatic transitions and provides a foundation for climate-resilient planning.

Conclusions

This study indicates statistically significant warming (~ 0.34 °C per decade), intensification of precipitation extremes, and shortened snow persistence in the Baspa Basin. Mid-elevation zones (3,000–4,500 m) exhibit the highest sensitivity to these changes. The spatial overlap between increasing extreme precipitation and reduced snow persistence suggests a growing potential for enhanced hydroclimatic variability and compound hazard conditions. While these findings are derived from basin-scale indicators rather than direct hydrological observations, they provide consistent evidence suggesting a tendency toward increased rainfall contribution to runoff and reduced seasonal buffering capacity. The findings should be interpreted considering uncertainties associated with reanalysis-based precipitation estimates, MODIS snow retrievals, and temporally static infrastructure datasets. Future studies integrating hydrological simulations and dynamic infrastructure datasets may further improve basin-scale risk assessment.
