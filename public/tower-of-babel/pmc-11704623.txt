A statistical approach for systematic identification of transition cells from scRNA-seq data
Authors: Yuanxin Wang, Merve Dede, Vakul Mohanty, Jinzhuang Dou, Ziyi Li, Ken Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryDecoding cellular state transitions is crucial for understanding complex biological processes in development and disease. While recent advancements in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) offer insights into cellular trajectories, existing tools primarily study expressional rather than regulatory state shifts. We present CellTran, a statistical approach utilizing paired-gene expression correlations to detect transition cells from scRNA-seq data without explicitly resolving gene regulatory networks. Applying our approach to various contexts, including tissue regeneration, embryonic development, preinvasive lesions, and humoral responses post-vaccination, reveals transition cells and their distinct gene expression profiles. Our study sheds light on the underlying molecular mechanisms driving cellular state transitions, enhancing our ability to identify therapeutic targets for disease interventions.

Introduction

In the complexity of life, change is not just inevitable, it is essential. Decoding the journey of a cell as it transitions from one type or state to another is important for our understanding of life’s complexity. To ensure survival, cells need to be able to effectively respond to changes in their environment. This process, which is fundamental to the evolution, development, and adaptability of multicellular organisms, involves the emergence of transition cells, unique entities that bridge different cell types and states. The concepts of transition cells and cell transition dynamics have been widely illustrated by the renowned Waddington’s landscape.1,2 In Waddington’s landscape, cells with specific biological functions and stable configurations reside at the valleys.1 During developmental processes, some cells can become transition cells, climbing over ridges and relocating into other valleys2 (Figure 1A). Unlike stable cells, transition cells are on a path of change, undertaking vital roles in several important processes such as differentiation,3,4,5 de-differentiation,6 or transdifferentiation.7,8 These transformations underlie critical biological phenomena, including developmental processes such as hematopoiesis,9 events that require response to injury (e.g., tissue regeneration,10 responses to infection), or in malignant processes such as carcinogenesis.11 Therefore, accurate identification of transition cells is crucial not only for mapping the intricate landscape of developmental biology but also for devising effective therapeutic intervention strategies to aid in disease onset and progression.Figure 1Identifying transition cells based on gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients(A) Stable cells and transition cells are separated through their intrinsic gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients (GPPCCs). At the molecular level, transition cells are characterized by unique patterns of gene expression and gene regulatory networks (GRNs), substantiating the dynamic nature of cellular transitions, serving as valuable biomarkers and potential targets for therapeutic interventions. Dysregulated cellular transitions can lead to various diseases, further underlining their essential role in maintaining cellular and organismal health.12,13 Therefore, studying gene expression profiles of transition cells opens a window to observe these fundamental molecular processes and to construct computational models that reveal biological roadmaps of these journeys.14

Despite the promise of transition cells, our ability to accurately identify and study these transition cells has been limited due to technical constraints and a lack of comprehensive datasets.15 Traditional methods of comparing gene expression profiles employ molecular biology tools such as qPCR for single genes and bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). These approaches are limited in accurately characterizing transcriptional dynamics due to the lack of whole transcriptome coverage and cellular heterogeneity. To overcome these limitations, single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) is widely used to measure gene expression profiles of thousands of individual cells from heterogeneous samples in a single experiment, providing measurements on cell-to-cell transcriptional variations, revealing heterogeneous and rare cell populations and enabling the observation of continuous transition processes.16,17

To characterize cell state transitions, computational tools such as Monocle18 and Slingshot19 have been developed. These methods infer the pseudotime of each cell by ordering cells in embedding spaces.19,20 Although they require no prior knowledge, the transitions characterized are based mainly on transcriptional similarity, instead of underlying regulatory circuits that generate transcriptomic trajectories. However, RNA velocity, another analytical method for studying developmental processes, estimates transition probabilities through an analysis of full splicing dynamics.21 While RNA velocity provides a snapshot of an individual cell’s motion at the time of measurement, it is sensitive to technical noise in the scRNA-seq data,22,23 and it does not directly inform changes in cell fate (i.e., non-stochastic changes not just in gene expression profiles but also in regulatory and transcriptional elements, leading fundamentally to new cellular phenotypes and functions during developmental processes).24

Due to the difficulty of finding reliable markers,17,25 state-transitioning cell identification methods such as CellRank and MuTrans were developed. CellRank23 combines RNA velocity and transcriptional similarity to define transcriptional macrostates and to calculate transition probabilities of individual cells along specific developmental trajectories. MuTrans26 identifies attractor basins corresponding to stable cellular states with distinct biological functions and finds transition cells based on transition entropy. None of the existing tools can identify transition cells from a gene regulatory perspective: those based on trajectory and RNA velocity inference do not model underlying GRNs, and those explicitly constructing GRNs often have limited scalability or accuracies.17,27

To address the gaps, we leverage concepts from the field of systems biology. In systems biology, differential equations are widely used to describe dynamic processes in living cells. Assuming that the behavior of a cell can be approximated as a dynamic system with explicit regulatory rules, we can model gene expression rates as functions of expression levels of regulatory genes. Once we estimate model parameters via wet lab experiments or data fitting, we can venture into finding numerical solutions and study the stability of equilibrium points.27 However, this seemingly straightforward approach has proven to be computationally complex and expensive, and it is complicated further by scalability issues. Consequently, it can only accommodate model systems involving a limited number of genes.28,29

Recognizing all of these challenges, we propose an alternative path forward. Instead of deciphering the whole regulatory process and its underlying regulators, we propose the calculation of a transition index for each cell by leveraging correlation structures between gene pairs. Although correlation relations between gene pairs have been studied during cell fate dynamics, identifying transition cells from scRNA-seq data is still challenging in practice. A previous study30 defined a critical transition index based on changes between cell-cell correlations and gene-gene correlations from a single-cell qPCR assay. This index, calculated using the qPCR expressions of around 20 core genes, is not optimal for scRNA-seq data, which contains around 20,000 genes with sparse, noisy expression measurements. Another study31 proposed an analytical pipeline, aimed at identifying transition cells passing through tipping points that are relatively transient, occurring within seconds or minutes. Recent studies,13,26,32 however, suggest cellular state transitions can be rather gradual, occurring from hours to days. To identify gradually transitioning cells, we model gene expressions using continuous differential equations and prove mathematically that correlation structures between gene pairs can describe the tendency that a cell is going through a gradual state transition (Figure 1B). With the transition index calculated for each cell, transition cells during a developmental process can be systematically identified from scRNA-seq profiles, enabling the discovery of genes that can be exploited for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy of complex diseases such as cancer.

Results

Evaluation of transition index using simulation datasets

To test the accuracy and robustness of our transition index, we evaluated its performance on simulation datasets generated using SERGIO, which models gene expressions at the single-cell level based on GRNs. We first simulated a three-steady-state linear transitioning process with 5,000 stable cells in each steady state, 1,000 cells transitioning from state 1 to state 2, and 1,000 cells transitioning from state 2 to state 3 with low technical noise (see STAR Methods). As shown in Figure 1C, stable cells from different steady states form different clusters in the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), while transition cells not only link the major stable clusters but they also distribute across stable cell clusters. This is probably because gene expressions do not switch abruptly. Conventional methods such as Monocle3 and Slingshot usually consider transition cells as cells at branching points. This can lead to us missing transition cells that initiate transition processes, limiting our studies into the initial drivers of state transitions (Figure S1A). As cellular state transitions can initiate from multiple stable cells, transition cells at initial steps allocate sparsely in the stable cell clusters due to biological and technical noise. Due to their scattered distribution and dissimilarity to the majority of transition cells in the gene expression space, it is challenging for methods studying cell-to-cell transitions, such as RNA velocity, to accurately infer the transition probability of these cells (Figure S1B). By modeling the behavior of a cell as a dynamical system, CellTran can successfully identify these transition cells based on transition index (Figure 1D; Table S1A).

As doublets sharing transcriptomics of two distinct cellular states may confound the analysis and interpretation of scRNA-seq data,33 we tested the robustness of CellTran against doublets. We randomly selected 1,000 stable cells from both steady state 1 and steady state 2 and simulated doublets by adding the gene expression levels of randomly paired cells (Figure 1E). We found that the transition indices of these simulated doublets were similar to those of the original stable cells (Figure 1F). Thus, CellTran appears clearly robust against the presence of the doublets.

To compare CellTran with existing methods such as CellRank and MuTrans, we calculated the transition probability based on non-terminal probability for CellRank and entropy for MuTrans, and we evaluated the performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (PRAUC) (see STAR Methods). We tested different numbers of macrostates and attractors ranging from 2 to 5. For the best models in terms of AUROC and PRAUC, CellRank classified a small subset of stable cells as terminal state cells and failed to separate transition cells and stable cells (Figures S1C and S1D), while MuTrans identified cells around the attractor-switching points as transition cells, missing those at early and late transition stages (Figures S1E and S1F). CellTran achieves a better performance than the best CellRank and MuTrans models (Table S1B). In addition to a linear transitioning structure with a low noise level, we also evaluated the performance of CellTran in bifurcating and trifurcation structures with both low and high noise levels (see STAR Methods). As shown in Table S1B, CellTran performs substantially better than CellRank and MuTrans. Additionally, we compared CellTran with an approach that determines transition cells based on RNA velocity length. Both CellTran and RNA velocity length can identify transition cells bridging between two stable cell clusters, while for those transition cells whose gene expressions are similar to stable cells, CellTran performed better (Figures S1G–S1K).

We then tested the robustness and sensitivity of CellTran. We decreased the percentage of transition cells from about 13% to 7% and 1%, and we simulated gene expression data with 50%, 70%, and 90% dropout rates (see STAR Methods). With fewer transition cells, the transition index calculated based on a smaller cell neighborhood achieves good performance in terms of AUROC and PRAUC (Table S1A). This is because the strengths and connections of regulatory networks can change during cellular state transitions,34,35 while CellTran requests gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients (GPPCCs) to be calculated from cells that have similar regulatory profiles. To satisfy the assumption of CellTran, a small number of neighboring cells should be used for calculating the transition index. However, expression data with dropout requires a large cell neighborhood containing sufficient observations for a high AUROC and PRAUC, especially when the dropout rate reaches 90% (Table S1A).

Identifying transition cells in mouse skeletal muscle regeneration scRNA-seq data

To validate our findings derived mathematically in the STAR Methods, we applied our analysis framework to a mouse muscle cell regeneration scRNA-seq dataset. Muscle regeneration is an important process as it underlies recovery in response to muscle injury, restoring damage to myofibers, and has implications for various muscle-related disorders. For adults, muscle stem cells (MuSCs) are in a quiescent state at the periphery of muscle myofibers in normal conditions. In response to injury, Pax7hi quiescent MuSCs will activate, marked by the expression of Myf5, initiating a cascade of events that involve differentiation into MyoD+ myoblasts and subsequent transitions into Moyghi commitment or Mymxhi fusion states. Importantly, cells in short-lived commitment and fusion stages, together with activated MuSCs are recognized as transition cells that drive this critical regenerative process.25,36 McKellar et al. integrated 88 publicly available single-cell and single-nucleus RNA-seq datasets in a study to enable the detection of infrequent and transient cell populations.25 We performed our analysis using this comprehensive datasets and their original cell-type labels (Figure 2A). We calculated GPPCCs and corresponding transition indices (Figure 2B) as described in the STAR Methods. Strikingly, our results indicate that the empirical cumulative distribution function’s (eCDF’s) slope of Pearson’s correlations are different between transition cells and stable cells (Figure 2C), and the transition indices for cells in the transition state are significantly higher than those of cells in stable states (Figure 2D) (Wilcoxon test; p < 0.01). We identified a cutoff of 0.25 from the distributions of the calculated transition index via the Gaussian mixture model (GMM). Cells with a transition index higher than the cutoff are labeled as transition cells.

We also compared CellTran with CellRank and MuTrans using the dataset. The AUROC and PRAUC for CellTran is 0.83 and 0.7 when using 200 neighboring cells and the top 50 most variable genes for each cluster with clustering resolution equal to 0.5 (see STAR Methods). The AUROC and PRAUC for the best CellRank model is 0.76 and 0.65 and for the best MuTrans model is 0.61 and 0.28 (Table S1D). CellTran performs better than CellRank and MuTrans (Figures 2E and 2F).

To test the sensitivity of CellTran regarding the choice of neighboring cells and genes, we calculated the transition index based on various numbers of nearest neighbors, ranging from 50 to 300, and top variable genes, ranging from 50 to 200 (see STAR Methods). As multiple states can be involved in the muscle regeneration process, we clustered cells at various resolutions and computed transition indices using cluster-specific top variable genes. As shown in Table S1D, CellTran is not sensitive to the number of top genes. A neighboring size containing 200–300 cells results in high AUROC and PRAUC.

Revealing transition cells with cycling progenitors in endocrinogenesis

Next, we tested the capabilities of CellTran in identifying transition cells confounded with proliferations in the mouse pancreas endocrinogenesis process. During mouse embryonic development, pancreas endocrine cells are generated from two distinct transitions. At the first transition stage (embryonic day [E]9.0–12.5), only newly generated glucagon-producing α cells are observed, whereas at the secondary stage (E12.5–15.5), all endocrines, including glucagon-producing α cells, insulin-producing β cells, somatostatin-producing δ cells and ghrelin-producing ε cells, are generated. During both transitions, bipotent ductal cells within the pancreatic epithelium first transit into Ngn3low endocrine progenitor-precursors (EPs). These Ngn3low EPs then raise the expression level of Ngn3 and transit into pre-endocrines. The pre-endocrines finally differentiate into different subtypes of endocrines.37 Here, we focused on the secondary transition and used samples from E15.5 for the analysis.38

Based on the top 50 most variable genes (see STAR Methods), we found that cells with high transition indices belonging to ductal, Ngn3high EP, pre-endocrine, and endocrine cells (Figures S3A, S3D, and S3E). Some of these cells also have high cell-cycle scores, especially G2M scores (Figures S3B and S3C). When we regressed out the cell-cycle scores and recalculated transition indices without including cell-cycle-related genes, the transition indices of the pre-endocrine and endocrine cells with high transition indices and low cycling scores remained high (Figures S3F and S3G), while the transition indices of those cycling ductal and Ngn3high EP cells dropped. Thus, regressing out cell-cycling effects and excluding cycling genes can delineate effects related to proliferation from other transitional processes such as endocrinogenesis.

Comparison and consistency with RNA velocity in mouse lung alveolar regeneration datasets

CellTran was developed to identify cellular state transitions based on GRNs and to quantify the transition probability through GPPCCs. To compare CellTran with RNA velocity, an approach providing transition information of individual cells, we further analyzed mouse lung alveolar regeneration datasets. Strunz et al. generated two time-resolved scRNA-seq datasets to study the lung regeneration process: whole-lung scRNA-seq and high-resolution scRNA-seq. In the high-resolution dataset, epithelial cells are enriched using magnetic-activated cell sorting and are collected from more time points compared with that of the whole-lung dataset.39 It has been shown that alveolar type-2 pneumocytes (AT2) cells become activated, act as alveolar stem cells, and differentiate into alveolar type-1 pneumocytes (AT1) cells during injury.40

As shown by Strunz et al., in the whole-lung dataset, the majority activated AT2 cells and nearly half of the Krt8+ cells have large RNA velocities, indicating the transition from activated AT2 cells to Krt8+ cells, and finally to AT1 cells.39 CellTran also observes high transition indices in activated AT2 cells (Figure S2). By separating transition cells from stable cells using the GMM, we found about 50% activated AT2 cells, 19% Krt8+ cells, and 7% AT2 cells are transition cells. Since there are only 38 AT1 cells in the dataset, we do not have enough observations to calculate their transition index. In the high-resolution dataset, we observe a similar trend as we did in the whole-lung dataset: about 56% activated AT2 cells, 24% Krt8+ cells, and 15% AT2 cells are transition cells. As the high-resolution dataset includes the full regeneration process of AT1 cells,39 we also observe 62% transitioning AT1 cells.

Transition cells in pancreatic preinvasive lesions are heterogeneous and have distinct gene expression profiles

We next applied CellTran on a pancreatic preinvasive lesions dataset to investigate the molecular processes during cancer development. Given that pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive cancer with poor prognoses, early detection remains pivotal for potential curative interventions if diagnosed early.41 Identifying transition cells within preinvasive pancreatic lesions takes on critical significance because it offers an opportunity to reveal the cellular dynamics that underlie the progression from benign state to malignancy. Schlesinger et al.42 used mouse models to perform a time course scRNA-seq during the progression from acinar metaplasia to tumor formation, investigating the early molecular processes giving rise to PDAC. Briefly, they used Ptf1a-CreER; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice as control and Kras+/LSL-G12D; Ptf1a-CreER; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice in the experimental group and injected tamoxifen to induce preinvasive lesions. Pancreas samples were collected at six time points (17 days, 6 weeks, 3 months, 5 months, 9 months, and 15 months post-tamoxifen injection [PTI]) for sequencing. Acinar cells first underwent ductal metaplasia, and metaplastic cells were observed beginning at 3 months PTI. Pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) lesions formed afterward, and samples at 15 months PTI were invasive PDAC.

To study the metaplastic cells during disease progression, we calculated a transition index for each cell at different time points (Figure 3A), and we separated them into high transition index cells and low transition index cells using the GMM (Figure 3B). Groups of cells existing at 3 months PTI clustered into the high transition index group (Figures 3A, 3C, and S4A), suggesting that these cells were potentially undergoing transitions to lesion development. To further characterize these cells and better understand their roles at early events of preinvasive lesions, we reclustered the transcriptomic profiles of these transition cells (see Figure 3D; STAR Methods). High-transition index cells at 3 months PTI have heterogeneous transcriptomic profiles, indicating various underlying molecular processes (Figures 3E–3H and S4B; Table S2). We then explored the events preceding the accumulation of PanIN lesions. At 6 weeks PTI, the transition index exhibited a significant increase compared to controls (Wilcoxon test; Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < 0.01) (Figure S4L). We then separated stable and transition acinar cells at 6 weeks PTI and identified a total of 561 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; Wilcoxon test; Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < 0.01, avg_log2FC [fold change] > 1) when comparing transition cells at 6 weeks PTI to the control group. Noteworthy among these were 407 genes unique for transition cells (Tables S3A and S3B), which were not observed when comparing all cells at 6 weeks PTI with controls. Importantly, this subset of 407 genes was found to be enriched in signaling pathways linked to PDAC tumorigenesis, including Myc Targets,43 p53 pathways,44 and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) signaling via nuclear factor κB45 (Table S3C). Additionally, our analysis revealed the upregulation of genes within transition cells compared to stable cells at 6 weeks PTI. These genes included CD47, recognized as a “don’t eat me signal,”46 and Sox4, implicated in the promotion of cancer development.47 This observed upregulation highlights the dynamic alterations inherent in transition cells, implicating potential drivers of cellular transformations as they transition into more critical states.

Collectively, these results underscore the significance of our efforts to identify transition cells within preinvasive pancreatic lesions using our transition index. Based on CellTran, we not only identified transition cells within these lesions but we also uncovered the distinctive gene expression profiles that characterize these significant intermediary states.

Transition cells reflect the effectiveness of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines

To further examine the applicability of CellTran, we explored its potential in investigating physiological responses to external stimuli. To this end, we leveraged CellTran to analyze scRNA-seq data obtained from humoral responses against SARS-CoV-2 vaccines. Nouailles et al.48 vaccinated hamsters with a mock vaccine, attenuated sCPD9 mutant SARS-CoV-2, Ad2-spike adenovirus vector vaccine, and BNT162b2 mRNA vaccine followed by boosters (see STAR Methods). They then challenged the hamsters with SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant 14 days after boosters. On day 2 post-challenge, blood samples were collected for scRNA-seq.48 In an effort to investigate the cellular dynamics underlying the immune responses triggered by SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, we calculated transition indices of the immune cells in this dataset using CellTran. We identified a subcluster of B cells having higher transition indices compared to other B cells (Figure 4B), and we observed that this group of B cells came mainly from vaccinated hamsters (Figure S5B). To further determine the relevance of these B cells in humoral responses, we investigated the gene expression profiles of these cells. Notably, because these Prdm1+, Tnfrsf17+ cells upregulated Tnfrsf13b and downregulated Pax5 (Figures 4E and S5A), we interpreted these cells being transitioned toward pre-plasmablasts48 after the challenge. Moreover, we observed a subcluster of classical monocytes with higher transition indices compared to the rest of the classical monocyte population (Figure 4C), although it is important to note that the majority of these classical monocytes were derived from mock-mock vaccinated hamsters (used as controls) (Figure S5B). Notably, the upregulation of inflammation-related genes such as Ecm1 and Tagln2 (Table S4B) within the transition cells from these mock vaccinated mice49,50 (Figure 4F) corroborates the observation that inflammation-induced damage occurs in the absence of vaccination.51 Moreover, for platelets, there appeared to be transition cells from mostly vaccinated hamsters (Figure S5B) that are sparsely distributed across the whole cluster of platelets (Figure 4D). Discussion

Recent advances in scRNA-seq technologies have enabled a high-resolution measurement of cellular dynamics during developmental processes. However, the current analytical tools are insufficient to study transcriptional dynamics and state transitions from a gene-regulatory perspective at the cellular state level. Here, we presented an alternative framework. By assuming the existence of underlying deterministic gene regulatory relations, we define a transition index based on gene pairwise correlation structures to identify transition cells. Our approach does not use technology-specific features and can be applied to analyze scRNA-seq data produced by various technologies including but not limited to 10x Chromium data.

Although RNA velocity methods, such as scVelo38 and CellDance,55 have made significant contributions to mapping developmental trajectories by inferring cellular transition velocities through splicing dynamics, there remain challenges in accurately detecting transition cells, especially in cases where transcriptomic profiles closely resemble those of stable cells (Figures S1G–S1K). CellRank and MuTrans have aimed to study dynamic developmental processes through the characterization of cellular state transitions. They depend heavily on definitions of cell-to-cell transcriptomic similarity. Our approach, however, aimed to characterize regulatory changes as the basis for cell state transitions. By modeling gene expression dynamics using stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and associating transitional states with distributions of GPPCCs, CellTran can successfully identify transition cells of various transition structures in the input datasets. The transition indices calculated using CellTran are sensitive to the number of neighbors but are not that sensitive to the number of variable genes. In practice, 200–300 neighboring cells and 50–100 top genes usually achieve a good trade-off between resolution and estimation accuracy (Table S1D).

During our efforts in characterizing transition cells within pancreatic preinvasive lesions, we noted cell state heterogeneity at the same time point, which suggested potential divergent cell fates at a later time point (Figures 3E–3H). In studying the immune system responses against SARS-CoV-2, transitioning B cells and platelets are observed in vaccinated mice, while transitioning classical monocytes are observed in untreated mice. However, their transition directions varied post-SARS-CoV-2 exposure, which highlights the efficacy of the vaccine. Notably, the transition cells within platelets were sparsely distributed instead of aggregating together and forming a subcluster (Figure 4D). In such cases, these transition cells are less likely to be captured by CellRank or MuTrans due to the similarity of their gene expression profiles with their neighbors in stable states. Unlike the existing methods, our approach leverages the gene regulatory relations to detect these cells and therefore can offer insights into subtle changes during developmental processes.

Our model offers the advantage of not requiring regulatory relations to be defined by a specific mathematical form. This flexibility allows for the calculation of transition indices using broader biological concepts such as metagenes or regulatory modules. Analyzing cellular transitions based on these extracted signatures enables a multifaceted exploration of developmental processes, offering insights from various perspectives. For instance, different cancer types or subtypes usually follow unique developmental trajectories, yet they may show overlaps in metagenes and/or regulatory modules due to the inherent biological characteristics of cancer cells.56,57 Identifying and characterizing transition cells based on the regulatory relations among metagenes and regulatory modules can help us reveal cancer type-/subtype-independent signals for predicting survival time and treatment responses.

Transition cells serve as vital indicators of cellular state changes during important developmental processes. When integrated with metadata and clinical data such as cell-type annotations and disease stages, these transitional cells offer deep insights into the underlying physiology and etiology of complex diseases. Detecting transition cells in normal or premalignant tissues not only uncovers potential avenues for early and targeted interventions but it also enables the discovery of genes with dynamic expression profiles during transitions that could play essential roles in disease progression. In the context of primary tumors, the identification of dysregulated genes within transition cells enhances prognostic accuracy and informs the design of tailored therapeutic strategies. Although not all of these genes may serve necessarily as markers for eventual cellular states, they undoubtedly play significant roles during the orchestrated process of state transitions. By further reconstructing regulatory networks based on the characteristics of transition cells and comparing them against those of stable cells, we can have a comprehensive picture of molecular mechanisms that underscores developmental and disease-related processes. This knowledge empowers us to identify specific targets for medical interventions that would counteract unfavorable transitions and enhance favorable clinical outcomes.

Limitations of the study

Our study employs a straightforward approach using Pearson’s correlation structures to identify transition cells. However, it is important to acknowledge limitations inherent in this methodology. CellTran aims at detecting transition cells that are near regulatory equilibrium states. It relies on having a robust and sensitive estimation of pairwise gene expression correlation coefficients from cells with similar regulatory profiles. Consequently, the cellular state transitions that CellTran can effectively detect are those that occur gradually and are observable in a sufficient number of cells. Another assumption of our approach is that an adequate change in the number of regulatory relationships accompanies the transition of a cell from a stable state to a transition state. In cases where changes in regulatory relations are limited to only a few genes, CellTran faces challenges in distinguishing whether these changes represent noise or genuine signals of transition. Under such circumstances, the empirical distribution of Pearson’s correlation coefficients between stable and transition cells will resemble each other closely. This similarity results in the inferred transition indices for these cells that do not exhibit a multimodal distribution. Consequently, using a GMM to differentiate between transition and stable cells becomes problematic due to the lack of distinct separation in the data. This limitation underscores the need for the careful interpretation of results in scenarios where the cellular transitions are subtle or involve minimal changes in gene regulatory relationships.

Data simulation

The simulation data is generated using SERGIO, which simulates stochastic single-cell gene expressions based on gene regulatory networks.58 SERGIO can simulate both stable cells and transition cells that differentiate as a result of the expression changes of master regulators. We used internal datasets obtained from SERGIO, which are sampled from real regulatory networks in E.Coli., to simulate single-cell gene expressions and relative labels as the ground truth for evaluations. To test the applicability of CellTran, we simulated gene expression data with different transitioning structures (linear, bifurcating, and trifurcation), technical noise levels (low: noise_params = 0.2, high: noise_params = 0.5), dropout rate (50%, 70%, and 90%), and transition cells percentages (∼1%, 7%, and 13%). For linear and bifurcating structures, we used De-noised_100G_3T_300cPerT_dynamics_8_DS8, which contains 100 genes and three steady states. For linear structure, we set the transition probability to be 0.08 for both transitions from steady state 1 to state 2 and state 2 to state 3. For a bifurcating structure, we set the transition probability to be 0.08 for transitions from steady state 1 to state 2 and state 3. For the trifurcation transitioning structure, we used De-noised_100G_4T_300cPerT_dynamics_10_DS5, which contains 100 genes and four steady states. We set transition probability to be 0.08 for transitions from steady state 1 to state 2, 3, and 4. For gene expressions with dropout events, we randomly selected low- and moderate-expression genes59 (whose mean expression levels were below the medium) and set their expressions to 0 at dropout rates.

In summary, based on the bifurcation theory, we found that gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients (GPPCC)s were indicative of the behavior of the cells: in transition cells, GPPCCs approach −1 or 1, whereas in stable cells they are closer to 0 (Figure 1A). Identifying transition cells based on gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients

To detect transition cells based on the mathematical derivation above, we developed an analytical workflow which contains several steps (Figure 1B).

Data preprocessing

After getting the cell-gene count matrix from scRNA-seq data, we removed low quality cells such as dying cells and doublets based on QC metrics,61 and we then normalized count matrix by the total expression levels.62 As all the datasets we used are from previous studies (see STAR Methods), we processed the data as suggested by the original publications. To find gene expression values affected by dropout,59 in our analysis we used R scLink package (version 1.0.1). Briefly, this method uses a Gamma-Normal mixture distribution fYj(y)=λjGamma(y;αj,βj)+(1−λj)Normal(y;μj,σj) to model the expression of each gene. If the expression of a gene is accurately measured, it would follow a normal distribution. Otherwise, it follows a Gamma distribution. For each gene in each cell, a non-detection probability is estimated based on λj. We treated gene expressions with high λj as dropouts and excluded them from calculating GPPCCs.63

For each cell, we selected the cell together with its nearest neighbors in principle Component Analysis (PCA) dimensions based on cosine distance to calculate its gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients, assuming cells with similar expression profiles have similar overall underlying gene regulatory relations. Since the correlation structures we calculated contained every possible combination of genes, to reduce computational complexity while gaining a good detection power, we used top genes with the largest expression variance (selected using var function in R (version 4.2.1)). In our analysis, we first clustered cells based on all gene expressions using Seurat v4.4.0 and selected top variable genes within each cluster for the calculation. We then used sclink_cor function in R scLink package (version 1.0.1) with default parameters (dthre = 0.9)63 to calculate Pearson’s correlation coefficients excluding gene expression values affected by dropout.

Transition index calculation

To summarize the distribution of gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients into a single metric that is comparable for different cells, we defined atransitionindex=∑i=0N1argmaxxDmax<|ρi|<argminxDmaxN,where 1 is a 0–1 indicator function (1(A)=1 if condition A is true, and 1(A)=0 otherwise), ρi is the GPPCC for each gene pair, and N is the total number of gene pairs included in the calculation, to quantify the probability that a cell is a transition cell. The transition index we defined is inspired by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic. We assume there are both transition cells and stable cells captured by scRNA-seq in a developmental process. We first find the archetype of transition cell and stable cell by using a modified Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic. We define D=F1(x)−F2(x), where x is the absolute value of gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients of a cell and F(·) stands for the empirical cumulative distribution functions (eCDF), representing the distribution difference of GPPCCs between two cells. As proven mathematically, there are more gene pairs whose absolute value of Pearson’s correlation coefficients are close to 1 in transition cells than in stable cells. Thus, based on the distribution difference, by finding the maximum difference among all cells, Dmax=max(supxD), we identify archetypal stable cells and transition cells. As illustrated in Figure 1B, for an archetypal transition cell, the number of gene pairs accumulates comparatively slow when the absolute value of coefficient is small and fast when the value is larger than a threshold (argmaxx(Dmax)). To count and compare the number of gene pairs exceeding such a threshold in each cell, we summarize the percentage of gene pairs whose absolute value of Pearson’s correlation coefficients are between argmaxx(Dmax) and argminx(Dmax). Considering there may be noise in measuring the Pearson’s correlation coefficients (e.g., only few observations used for the calculation due to dropout issues), we use data specific argminx(Dmax), which is usually lose to 1 (as the upper boundary).

Numbers of nearest neighbors selection

Since CellTran calculates gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients based on the expression profiles of cells with similar regulatory profiles, we need to select a proper number of neighbors for each cell to do the calculation. This number choice is empirically determined from the data, to achieve a good tradeoff between temporal resolution and estimation accuracy. If the cell states are going through rapid changes, it would be advantageous to select fewer cells to achieve a good temporal resolution. However, it is also advantageous to select more cells to reduce standard error. In CellTran, the neighboring cells are selected by using the shared nearest neighbors (SNN) method in the principal component analysis (PCA) space, which is advantageous over the tSNE and UMAP methods that heavily reduce the dimensions for visualization purposes. This neighbor-finding method is also popularly used by other toolkits for single cell genomics such as Seurat.64 In our analysis, we used FindClusters function in R Seurat package (version 4.4.0). To calculate the transition index for most of the cells, we tried different numbers of nearest neighbors that are less than the majority community size of cells detected. For myogenic dataset, we calculated transition index using 50, 100, 200 and 300 neighboring cells, and compare the performance of these selections. As this dataset contains many clusters whose population size are less than 300, if we choose to calculate transition index using 300 neighboring cells based on the highest AUROC and PRAUC, it’s likely for the cells in these clusters having different regulatory relations to their 300 neighbors, which violates the assumption of CellTran. Thus, we cannot calculate transition indices of these cells. To get the transition information for most of the cells, we chose to use 200 neighboring cells for the calculation and comparison with CellRank and MuTrus. For endocrinogenesis dataset, we used 200 neighboring cells. For lung regeneration datasets, we used 300 neighbors for whole-lung dataset and 200 neighbors for high-resolution dataset. In our PDAC and COVID applications, we used 300 nearest neighbors.

Gene selections for calculating pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients

We calculate transition index for each cell based on gene pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients. In the simulation datasets, since the total number of genes is 100, we used all the genes for the calculation. A typical real-world scRNA-seq, however, measures the expression of ∼30k genes. To make the calculation feasible, we need to select a subset of genes. If the study focuses on transitions along specific pathway(s), genes within such pathway(s) should be included in the analysis. Otherwise, genes with largest variance are selected. Based on our simulation results, dropouts can affect the performance of CellTran. As the overall dropout rate for scRNA-seq data is about 90%, we select top most variable genes for the calculation due to the mean-variance relationship of scRNA-seq data. These genes usually have high mean expression levels, which is less likely to have dropouts compared to low-expression genes. The large variance of these genes also makes it possible for us to observe the expression dynamics during cellular state transitions. To capture different expression patterns in different potential stable states, we first cluster cells into different clusters using the FindClusters function in Seurat. We then find the most variable genes in each cluster using the var function in R, based on which we calculate transition indices. The resolution for clustering and number of top genes used in calculating transition index are also data dependent. More genes give us more observations but also have higher risk of including a large number of dropout events. In the experiment using myogenic data, we calculated transition index using top 50, 100, 150 and 200 variable genes with clustering resolution equaling to 0.5, 0.8 and 1, and compared their performance. For endocrinogenesis dataset, we used top 50 variable genes with 0.4 clustering resolution. For muscle and lung regeneration datasets, we used top 50 genes with 0.5 clustering resolution. In the PDAC dataset, we used top 50 genes with 0.5 clustering resolution, and for COVID dataset, we used top 100 genes with 0.5 clustering resolution.

Comparison with RNA velocity

To compare CellTran with RNA velocity methods, we generated a synthetic dataset using SERGIO. We simulated a linear transitioning process with two stable states: 5000 stable cells from stable state 1, 5000 stable cells from stable state 2, and 1000 cells transitioning from stable state 1 to stable state. We calculated the RNA velocity for each cell using the scVelo package, and obtained the velocity length using the scv.tl.velocity_confidence() function. We used RNA velocity lengths to estimate the probability of transitioning, and calculated AUROC and PRAUC to evaluate the performance.

Performance assessment and benchmarking

To numerically represent the probability of each cell to be a transition cell, different methods used different metrics. For CellTran, transition index was used to reflect the probability. While for CellRank, we treated macrostates as stable cellular states and calculated the probability of each cell to be a transition cell as the probability not being in a macrostate. MuTrans analogizes cells as particles locating at attractors or saddle points of multiple potential wells and used transition entropy to describe the probability of a cell to be a transition cell. Since for some real-world datasets, we don’t have information about the spliced and unspliced RNA counts, we used CytoTRACEKernel for CellRank, which does not require the counts for both spliced and unspliced RNA. We calculated the transition index, non-terminal probability, and entropy for these methods and evaluated the performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (PRAUC).

Analysis of transition cells’ heterogeneity in PDAC dataset early metaplastic cells

To investigate acinar metaplasia processes, an early stage of pancreatic cancer, we used the top 50 most variable genes to calculate GPPCCs and transition indices for acinar cells and metaplastic cells. Metaplastic cells were then extracted based on the cell type annotations in the original publication. We then used Gaussian mixture model (GMM) to separate cells into transition cells, and stable cells. We assigned cells clustered into the low transition index group with the probability larger than 80% as stable cells, and cells clustered into the high transition index group with the probability larger than 80% as transition cells. To study the early phase of metaplasia process, transition cells at 3 months PTI are extracted and reclustered using SNN (shared nearest neighbors) with resolution equaling to 1 (Figure 4D) (Seurat version 4.2.1). Marker genes of metaplastic gastric cells, senescent cells and CNV-positive PDAC cells were obtained from the original publication.42

Analysis of transition cells’ differentially expressed gene in PDAC dataset pre-PanIN acinar cells

Acinar cells at 6 weeks PTI were extracted based on the cell type annotations in the original publication.42 We then used GMM to separate acinar cells at 6 weeks PTI into transition cells and stable cells. We performed DEGs analysis by comparing all cells and transition cells at 6 weeks PTI with acinar cells at the control time point. Differentially expressed genes with p_val_adj<0.01 and avg_log2FC > 1 for all cells and transition cells were compared and gene list enrichment analysis was performed using enrichr MSigDB Hallmark database (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/).

Identifying and characterizing transition cells in SARS-CoV-2 vaccination data

The blood sample scRNA-seq data was downloaded in the form of.rds file containing metadata such as cell type annotations and vaccine treatments. We calculated transition indices and identified transition cells using R (v4.0.0). DEGs analysis was performed to find altered gene expressions comparing transition cells and cells in stable states for each cell type. Due to the small number of DEGs whose avg_log2FC > 1 for B cells and monocytes, we only performed gene list enrichment analysis on DEGs downregulated in the transitioning platelets (Wilcoxon test; adjusted p-value <0.01, avg_log2FC < −1) using enrichr Reactome database (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/).
