Wzi modulates immune evasion of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae in a CPS-dependent and independent manner
Authors: Sha Wei, Tingting Xu, Quanhang Xiang, Jialong Liu, Dongliang Li, Jiexue Zhang, Lili Huang, Jinquan Liu, Junlin Liu, Yanru Chen, Qingqing Du, Kai Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKP) poses a significant public health threat due to its ability to cause severe invasive infections with higher mortality than classical strains. Understanding its pathogenesis is essential for devising effective treatment strategies. We analyze shared virulence-associated genes from three hvKP transposon mutagenesis libraries and identify wzi as a key virulence determinant of hvKP. We demonstrate that Wzi deficiency reduces CPS production in K. pneumoniae, differing from its function as a CPS anchor in Escherichia coli. Importantly, Wzi exerts a pivotal role in K. pneumoniae pathogenicity in vitro and in vivo through CPS-dependent and -independent mechanisms. It promotes hvKP survival in the host by suppressing IFN-γ-related cytokine secretion during early infection via CPS-dependent pathways. Wzi and CPS independently trigger sustained neutrophil recruitment through CXCL1 upregulation, resulting in pulmonary barrier damage and enhanced bacterial systemic dissemination for encapsulated K. pneumoniae. Wzi further enables K. pneumoniae to evade neutrophil-mediated clearance in a CPS-dependent manner. Notably, wzi sequence polymorphisms significantly influenced serum resistance independent of CPS, with hvKP-associated alleles conferring the highest resistance. Our findings collectively highlight Wzi as a CPS-dependent and -independent virulence factor that mediates immune evasion and tissue invasion of hvKP, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for combatting hvKP infections.KEYWORDS: Klebsiella pneumoniae, hypervirulence, Wzi, CPS, innate immunity

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is an encapsulated gram-negative bacterium and a major cause of pneumonia, urinary tract infections, liver abscesses, and sepsis [1–3]. In recent decades, K. pneumoniae has become a significant global concern, primarily due to its increased antibiotic resistance and hypervirulence [4,5]. Classical K. pneumoniae (cKP) is a group of K. pneumoniae that lacks hypercapsule, macromolecular exopolysaccharide, or excessive siderophores, and rarely causes diseases in otherwise healthy individuals (except urinary tract infection) even if it is multidrug-resistant (MDR) [6]. In contrast, hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKP) produces hypercapsules and excessive siderophores, which are frequently associated with a hypermucoid phenotype called hypermucoviscosity (hmv). Of greater concern, hvKP induces severe infections in both immunocompromised and healthy individuals, resulting in a high mortality [6].

Among the identified virulence factors of K. pneumoniae, capsular polysaccharide (CPS) is a crucial protective structure against the host’s innate immunity [7,8]. K. pneumoniae produces over 100 types of capsules, each structurally different in the repeating polysaccharide units of CPS [9]. Epidemiological data show that hvKP isolates are strongly associated with capsule types of K1, K2, K5, K16, K28, K54, K57, and KN1 [10,11]. K1 and K2 are the predominant types among them, accounting for approximately 70% of all reported hvKP cases worldwide [12,13]. Traditionally, hvKP isolates are categorized as hmv by the string test, i.e. their colony stretches more than five millimeters [14]. The relationship between hypercapsule, hmv, and hypervirulence has been demonstrated in numerous studies. Despite some conflicting reports [15,16], in most cases the three phenotypes correspond well to each other, e.g. mutations in rmpA [17], rcsAB [18], and kvrAB [19] result in reduced capsule production, mucoviscosity, and virulence.

Capsule production depends on the Group I or Wzx/Wzy pathway in K. pneumoniae, with a set of genes in the capsule synthesis locus (K-locus) responsible for the process [20]. The core capsule biosynthetic machinery consists of genes at the 5’ end of the CPS region encoding proteins involved in CPS translocation and processing at the bacterial surface; the variable region in the middle encoding the capsule-specific sugar synthesis, processing and export proteins, and the core assembly components; and genes at the 3’ end encoding glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, UDP-glucose dehydrogenase, and mannose-related GDP-D-mannose synthesis [20]. After polymer synthesis initiated in the cytoplasm, the polymers are flipped across the inner membrane, polymerized, and transferred to the outer membrane [21,22]. Finally, the capsule is anchored to the cell surface, probably by Wzi, as found in Escherichia coli [23]. Deficiency of Wzi in E. coli shows a significant reduction in surface-associated capsule and an increase in cell-free polymer [24]. However, such a function of Wzi has not yet been confirmed in K. pneumoniae. Additionally, the actual function of Wzi in K. pneumoniae during infection is largely unknown. A recent study using Tn-seq showed that wzi contributes to the fitness of K. pneumoniae in the lungs without experimental validation [25], suggesting that this gene may be involved in the pathogenicity of K. pneumoniae. Nucleotide diversity has been observed among the Wzi genes in K. pneumoniae, and unique alleles are associated with specific K-types [26], indicating that this gene may be under selection pressure. However, whether the sequence polymorphism in Wzi has impact on the hmv and/or virulence during K. pneumonia infection remains unclear.

In this study, we determined the contribution of Wzi to K. pneumoniae CPS production and pathogenicity. Our results showed that the Δwzi mutant reduced K. pneumoniae CPS production and sedimentation-based mucoviscosity but retained the hmv phenotype (positive string test), indicating that Wzi functions differently in CPS and hmv. Wzi deficiency significantly decreased the virulence of K. pneumoniae in vitro and in vivo in a CPS-independent and -dependent manner. Additionally, we found that the sequence polymorphism of wzi significantly affected bacterial resistance to serum killing, with wzi of hvKP conferring the highest resistance. Our findings highlight the dual role of Wzi in CPS production and as an independent virulence factor in helping K. pneumoniae against host clearance.

Materials and methods

Construction of in-frame deletion mutant and complementation for wzi

To evaluate the in situ function of wzi, the gene was knocked out in ATCC 43816 using the two-plasmid system pCasKP-pSGKP, as previously reported [27]. Briefly, approximately 500-bp flanking regions of the target gene were amplified and ligated by overlapping PCR to serve as the donor template. Spacer DNA was cloned into the pSGKP vector and transformed into E. coli DH5α, followed by plasmid extraction. Both the pSGKP-spacer plasmid and the donor template DNA were electroporated into pCasKP-harboring ATCC 43816. The deletion of wzi was confirmed by PCR and sequencing. The plasmids pCasKP and pSGKP-spacer were eliminated by inoculating the knockout mutant into antibiotic-free LB broth at 37°C with shaking overnight. The wcaJ mutant (∆wcaJ) was stored in our lab [28] and the wzi/wcaJ double mutant was constructed using ∆wzi as the template, with primers designed in our previous study [28].

To construct the complementary strain, a 1667-bp fragment containing the putative promoter region and the ORF of Wzi was amplified with primers Comp F/R and cloned into the HindIII and BamHI sites of pACYC184. The resulting plasmid was then electrotransferred into Δwzi to obtain the complementary strain Δwzi-comp. The primers used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Polysaccharide preparation and electrophoresis

Extracts of K. pneumoniae polysaccharides from whole-cell lysates were prepared as previously reported [29,30]. Cells were pelleted, resuspended in lysis buffer [60 mM Tris, pH 8.0; 10 mM MgCl2; 50 μM CaCl2; 500 U/mL DNase and RNase; and 3 mg/mL lysozyme], and incubated at 37°C for 1 h, followed by vortexing and three liquid nitrogen/37°C freeze–thaw cycles. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was added to a final concentration of 0.5%, followed by incubation at 37°C for an additional 30 min. The samples were then processed with boiling and proteinase K treatment as previously described [30]. Equal amounts of cell lysates were loaded onto SDS-PAGE. Samples were separated on 4–12% MeilunGel Bis-Tris gels and stained overnight with alcian blue as previously described [31].

Mice and model of lung infection

C57BL/6 mice used for subsequent experiments were ordered from GemPharmatech (Suzhou, China). The mice were bred and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, and 5–7 weeks old male mice were used in the experiments. Mice were randomly allocated to cages, and investigators were blinded to group assignments throughout the experiment. The cages were then randomly assigned to either the control or K. pneumoniae-infected group. Lung infection was conducted as previously described [32]. Briefly, bacteria in the stationary phase (overnight culture) were subcultured and grown at 37°C with agitation until they reached the mid-log phase. Bacteria were subsequently harvested by centrifugation (20 min, 2500 × g, 24°C), resuspended in PBS, and adjusted to 1 × 105 CFU per 30 μL by plating 10-fold serial dilutions onto LB plates. Mice were anesthetized, and 30 μL of the bacterial suspensions were inoculated intranasally. Infected mice were monitored every 4–8 h and euthanized upon reaching behavioral and/or pathophysiological humane endpoints. Survival was monitored for 7 days.

Animal experiments were complied with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals as mandated by the State Council of the People’s Republic of China and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee in Jinan University [protocol# 20,200,402-20]. Humane endpoints-including weight loss >20%, severe lethargy, inability to access food or water, and dyspnea-were strictly observed. Mice reaching these endpoints were first anesthetized with 5% isoflurane in an induction chamber. Once deep anesthesia was confirmed by loss of the pedal reflex, they were promptly euthanized via cervical dislocation performed by trained staff. Death was verified by the cessation of heartbeat and respiration. We have adhered to ARRIVE guidelines. The sample sizes for each group were determined based on established protocols, and no formal power calculation was performed.

Bacterial load determination and histology

For bacterial load determination, mice were treated as described above and euthanized at indicated time points by cervical dislocation or anesthetic overdose. Lungs, spleens, and livers were aseptically excised, weighed, and placed in cold PBS. After mechanical homogenization, 10-fold dilution series of organ homogenates were prepared and plated on LB agar plates. Colonies were counted after overnight culture, and the bacterial load was calculated as CFU per gram of tissue. To collect lungs for histology, the trachea was surgically exposed and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for overnight fixation. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of the sections was performed according to standard protocols and examined.

RNA sequencing and immune infiltration analysis

The collected lungs of K. pneumoniae infected and placebo treatment mice were frozen in liquid nitrogen and sent through blue ice to APExBIO for RNA sequencing. In brief, total RNAs from lung samples were extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were measured using a Nanodrop 2000c spectrophotometer. Library construction and sequencing were performed by the APExBIO Company. Immune Cell Abundance Identifier (ImmuCellAI) was used for immune infiltration analysis and analyzed using CHI BIOTECH CO., LTD.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Mouse lungs were aseptically excised and placed in cold PBS containing cocktails. After mechanical homogenization and centrifugation, the supernatant was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. The concentration of total protein was tested by the BCA protein assay kit (GLPBIO, Montclair, America), and the concentrations of IFN-γ, IL-12, IFNα, and CXCL1 were measured by ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ELISA kits (Elabscience) included mouse IFN-γ ELISA kit (E-EL-M0048); mouse IL-12 ELISA kit (E-EL-M3062); mouse IFN-α ELISA kit (E-EL-M3054); mouse CXCL1 ELISA kit (E-EL-M0018).

Flow cytometry

The trachea was cannulated with an 18-gauge angiocatheter, and the lungs were lavaged four times with 0.5 mL of ice-cold PBS. Cells were enumerated using a Cell Counter (CountStar IC1000, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the cells were incubated with combinations of monoclonal antibodies for 15 min on ice in the dark, followed by washing with PBS. The following fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies (BioLegend) were used: CD45-FITC (clone 30-F11), CD11c-PE/Cy7 (clone N418), Siglect F-Bv421 (clone S17007L), CD64-PE (clone X54-5/7.1), and Ly6G-APC (clone 1A8). Cells were stained with 7-AAD Viability Staining Solution (BioLegend).

Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity

Quantification of MPO activity in organs was performed using MPO Activity Assay Kit from Elabscience (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). After infection, the lungs were weighed and used for MPO quantification according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Isolation of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Bone marrow harvest was performed as described previously [33]. Briefly, male and female mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and the legs were dissected. Using an aseptic technique, bone marrow was harvested from the tibia and femur after removal of the surrounding muscle. The joints were cut with a scalpel, and the exposed bone marrow was washed out of the ends of the bones using a 25-gauge needle and a 10-mL syringe filled with PBS. Clumps were gently disaggregated with a needleless syringe and passed through a 70-μm cell strainer. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 250 × g for 5 min at room temperature to pellet the cells. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in DMEM with 10% FBS. Cells were differentiated at 1 × 106 cells/well in a 12-well plate supplemented with 25 ng/mL macrophage colony stimulating factor for 7 days.

Cell infection assay

Mid-log phase bacteria were harvested by centrifugation (5 min, 6000 rpm, 24°C), resuspended in sterile PBS and adjusted to 5 × 108 colony forming units (CFU)/mL. BMDMs were then inoculated with K. pneumoniae strains at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50. Plates were incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. After 1 h of incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and cultured in RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS and gentamicin (50 μg/mL). To determine the intracellular bacterial load, cells were washed three times with PBS and lysed with 0.5% saponin (Sigma-Aldrich, New jersey, USA) in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Serial dilutions were plated on LB agar to quantify the number of intracellular bacteria. All experiments were performed in triplicate on at least three independent occasions.

The human blood samples used in this study were obtained from three healthy volunteers at Shenzhen People’s Hospital with prior approval from the Ethics Committee of the Shenzhen People’s Hospital from April to May 2022. Isolation of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs)

PMNs were isolated using Polymorphprep (Serumwerk Bernburg AG, Bernburg, Germany) as previously described [34]. First, 5 mL of blood or bone marrow was layered over 5 mL of Polymorphprep™ and centrifuged at 500 × g for 35 min at 22°C. The PMN layers were collected, and the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline. After washing, the cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and used immediately.

Neutrophil killing

Neutrophil cultures were incubated in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS. K. pneumoniae WT, Δwzi, ΔwcaJ and ΔwcaJ/wzi were incubated with neutrophils without opsonization at a MOI of 1. At 0 h, 2 h, and 3 h post infection, 100 μL of the mixed solutions were collected, subjected to serial dilution, and plated for bacterial counting. The assay was triplicated with neutrophils isolated from three different volunteers.

qRT-PCR

BMDMs were infected with K. pneumoniae at a MOI of 10:1. Total RNA was isolated from infected cells at 2 and 4 h post infection using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RNA samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA using the TransScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qRT-PCR was performed using the Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Total volume of each reaction mixture was 20 μL, containing 10 μL of SYBR qPCR Master Mix, 0.5 μL of each upstream and downstream primer, 1 μL of cDNA template, and 8.5 μL of ddH2O. The following reaction procedure was used: 95°C for 10 min, then 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 40 s. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The relative expression values of selected genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to the expression levels of the GAPDH gene. Primers for qRT-PCR are listed in Table 1.

Transwell migration assay

The transwell migration assay was performed as described previously [35]. Briefly, 500 μL cell solution containing 5 × 105 neutrophils was added to the membrane of the 12-well transwell insert (8 μm pore size) and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 10 min. Gently add 1 mL of migration buffer containing CXCL1 200 ng/mL to the well directly below the transwell insert and incubate the plate at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 4 h. Gently removed transwell inserts with tweezers and migrated cells in the lower chamber medium were quantified by microscope and Cell Counter (CountStar IC1000, Shanghai, China).

Overexpression of wzi variants

To overexpress wzi variants associated with different serotypes (including K1, K2, K57, K28, K47, and K64) in ATCC 43816 Δwzi and K64 Δwzi, a 1431-bp fragment of the full-length wzi gene was amplified and cloned into the XbaI and PaeI sites of pBAD33-Cm and pBAD33-Cm/Rif. The resulting plasmid was then electrotransferred into Δwzi mutants, respectively. The primers used in this study are listed in Table 1.

C3b deposition assay

Bacterial culture (OD600 = 0.5, approximately 5 × 108 bacteria/ml) was incubated with 30% normal human serum (NHS) in a microtiter plate for 30 min at 37°C shaking. PBS was served as a negative control. Following incubation, the bacteria were washed twice with PBST and then incubated with an anti-C3b primary antibody (Proteintech, 21337–1-AP; 1:3000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. After another PBST wash, the bacteria were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. The bacteria were then washed three times with PBST. To develop the signal, a fresh tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added, and the reaction was stopped with 2 N sulfuric acid after 2–5 min. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 600 nm.

Opsonized phagocytosis assay

K. pneumoniae was grown to mid-log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.4–0.6), harvested by centrifugation at 6,000 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in cold PBS. The bacterial suspension was opsonized by incubation with 10% NHS for 30 min. Meanwhile, THP-1 cells were differentiated into 12-well culture plates. The opsonized bacteria were added to the macrophages at a MOI of 50:1. To synchronize the infection, the plate was centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min to settle the bacteria onto the cell monolayer, followed by incubation at 37°C under 5% CO2 for 1 h to allow phagocytosis. After incubation, the macrophages were washed three times with PBS to remove non-adherent and non-internalized bacteria. To eliminate the extracellular bacteria, the cells were treated with 1640 + 10% FBS +100 µg/mL gentamicin for 1 h at 37°C. The cells were then thoroughly washed with PBS to remove the gentamicin antibiotic. Finally, macrophages were lysed with 0.5% saponin in PBS for 10 min. The opsonized bacterial and lysates were serially diluted in PBS and plated on LB agar plates to enumerate the internalized bacteria by colony counting.

Serum susceptibility testing

Serum resistance testing was performed as previously described [36]. Twenty-five microliters of 2.5 × 105 CFU of K. pneumoniae in PBS (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) were inoculated into wells of a 96-well plate containing 75 μL of 100% pooled normal human serum (Sigma-Aldrich, New Jersey, USA). Control serum was inactivated by heating at 56°C for 30 min before bacterial incubation. The plate was then incubated at 37°C. At 0 and 1 h after inoculation, appropriate dilutions were plated on LB agar to enumerate surviving bacteria.

Data analysis

The data were first tested for normality using Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. As the assumptions of parametric tests were not consistently met, non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for comparisons involving more than two time points, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc tests with Holm’s correction for multiple comparisons. Statistical significances were referred as *, **, or *** for p-values < 0.05, <0.01, or <0.001, respectively.

Results

Wzi is involved in CPS production but not hmv phenotype

Hmv has been widely recognized as a key feature of hvKP; however, the correlation of hmv with hv has been challenged recently. One of the reasons is that a few factors involved in hmv and/or hv have been identified in K. pneumoniae. To investigate additional factors, we analyzed results derived from three transposon mutagenesis libraries generated by different methods. A library using the K1 type strain NTUH-K2044 identified 64 genes positively associated with CPS by density-TraDISort method [37]. Another library using a K2 type strain, ATCC 43816 (or KPPR1) identified 44 genes associated with hmv through an ordered transposon library developed by Cartesian-Pooling and Coordinate-Sequencing [38]. The third library, also using ATCC 43816 in a well-established mouse model of pneumonia via InSeq, detected over 300 mutants associated with a fitness defect [39]. We identified two overlapping genes among the three libraries, namely wzi and kvrB, suggesting the critical role of these genes in CPS, hmv and/or hv. The role of kvrB in CPS regulation and virulence has been elucidated well in K. pneumoniae [19], while that of wzi is ambiguous.

To determine the function of wzi in K. pneumoniae, we engineered an in-frame mutant of wzi using ATCC 43816, and a capsule mutant (ΔwcaJ) constructed previously [28] was as the negative control. Deficiency of wzi did not significantly affect the growth of ATCC 43816 in vitro (Figure S1). CPS production was evaluated by uronic acid quantification and SDS-PAGE staining with the polysaccharide-specific dye, alcian blue [29]. The amount of uronic acid detected in the Δwzi mutant decreased to approximately 66.07% of the level produced by the WT, which can be restored by the complementation (Figure 1(a,b)). The negative control ΔwcaJ almost completely lost the uronic acid (Figure 1(a,b)). These results demonstrate that wzi is involved in CPS biosynthesis but not as critical as wcaJ. Wzi is known to anchor CPS on the cell surface in E. coli [24,40]; however, such a function in K. pneumoniae remains unclear. To understand the role of Wzi in anchoring CPS on the cell surface, we measured the amount of cell-associated CPS and cell-free CPS. A marked reduction in cell surface-associated CPS was observed in the Δwzi mutant compared to WT but not in cell-free CPS (Figure 1(b)), suggesting that Wzi affects the production of CPS. This is consistent with the results of uronic acid measurements. However, it remains unclear whether Wzi functions by anchoring CPS to the cell surface as found in E. coli. This is because we cannot determine whether the reduction in cell surface-associated CPS is due to the reduction in total CPS or to direct anchoring.

To further understand whether Wzi has an impact on the hmv phenotype, we measured mucoviscosity using a sedimentation assay. The OD600 ratio of the supernatant to the culture of Δwzi and ΔwcaJ were 80.36% and 50.95% of that of the WT, respectively (Figure 1(c)), suggesting that the deficiency of wzi and wcaJ attenuates mucoviscosity. This is consistent with the reduced level of uronic acid and CPS production (Figure 1(a,b)). However, string test showed that the colonies of Δwzi mutant retained the hmv phenotype, whereas those of ΔwcaJ were not (Figure 1(d)), suggesting that Wzi can affect CPS production but is not critical for hmv.

CPS-driven dichotomous roles of Wzi in K.pneumoniae pathogenesis

Given the role of Wzi in CPS production, we then evaluated its effect on K. pneumoniae virulence with and without CPS using a murine pneumonia model. The Δwzi-infected mice exhibited 85.7% survival over 7 days, whereas all WT-infected mice died (Figure 2(a)), suggesting that Wzi is a key virulence factor of encapsulated K. pneumoniae. Compared to WT, the bacterial load in the lungs of Δwzi mutant infected mice was comparable at 2 and 5 h post infection (hpi) (Figure S2 and 2b) but significantly decreased at 10, 24, and 48 hpi, with approximately 6-, 33-, and 1036-fold reductions, respectively (Figure 2(b)). Notably, the load of Δwzi mutant in the lung significantly reduced at 48 hpi compared to 24 hpi, but that of WT was comparable at 24 and 48 hpi (Figure 2(b)), suggesting that Wzi is important for encapsulated K. pneumoniae survival in the lung. Both WT and Δwzi were detected in the liver and spleen at 24 hpi but undetectable at 10 hpi (Figure 2(c,d)), indicating that K. pneumoniae entered the blood and disseminated between organs after 10 hpi. The load of WT in the liver and spleen increased from 24 to 48 hpi, while no significant changes were detected for the Δwzi mutant (Figure 2(c,d)). Approximately 1.3 × 103 and 2.8 × 103 times lower bacterial loads were found in the liver and spleen of Δwzi-infected mice compared to those of WT at 48 hpi (Figure 2(c,d)), suggesting an essential role of Wzi in encapsulated K. pneumoniae dissemination in vivo.

Since CPS is also critical for K. pneumoniae survival in the host (Figure S3), we generated a noncapsulated ΔwcaJ/wzi double mutant strain to eliminate CPS-mediated effects and evaluate the independent role of Wzi in virulence. In the murine pneumonia model, both the ΔwcaJ and ΔwcaJ/wzi mutants exhibited significantly attenuated virulence and failed to induce lethality, prompting us to assess their bacterial burdens in host tissues. The pulmonary bacterial burdens of ΔwcaJ/wzi-infected mice were comparable to those of the ΔwcaJ mutant at 10 hpi but significantly increased at 24 hpi (Figure 2(e)). Notably, enhanced systemic dissemination was observed in ΔwcaJ/wzi-infected mice, with higher bacterial loads in the liver and spleen compared to ΔwcaJ-infected mice at 24 hpi (Figure 2(f,g)). These findings indicate that Wzi restricts non-capsulated K. pneumoniae dissemination and survival in vivo. Taken together, our results reveal that Wzi exhibits dichotomous roles in K. pneumoniae pathogenesis depending on CPS presence, promoting infection and dissemination in encapsulated strains while exhibiting opposite effects in non-capsulated mutants.

Wzi inhibits IFNγ related cytokines secretion dependent on CPS

To further explore how Wzi affects K. pneumoniae survival in vivo, we examined the secretion of IFN-γ-related cytokines in the lungs infected with WT, Δwzi, ΔwcaJ and ΔwcaJ/wzi mutants. At 10 hpi, the secretion levels of IL-12, IFNα, and IFN-γ in the lung of Δwzi mutant-infected mice were significantly higher compare to 5 hpi, but not in WT-infected mice (Figure 3(a-c)). All mutants (Δwzi, ΔwcaJ and ΔwcaJ/wzi) induced higher secretion of IL-12, IFNα, and IFN-γ than WT at 10 hpi (Figure 3(d-f)), suggesting that both Wzi and CPS inhibit the secretion of IFN-γ-related cytokines at early infection stage. Notably, the cytokine levels induced by ΔwcaJ and ΔwcaJ/wzi mutants were comparable (Figure 3(d-f)), indicating that the Wzi-mediated cytokine suppression is CPS-dependent. Given the established role of IFN-γ-related cytokines in anti-Klebsiella defense, we further confirmed that IFN-γ treatment reduced K. pneumoniae survival in BMDMs (Figure 3(g)). Together, these findings establish that Wzi promotes K. pneumoniae survival in a CPS-dependent manner by inhibiting IFN-γ-related cytokines production.

Wzi and CPS independently induce neutrophil infiltration to alveolar space, while CPS but not Wzi is critical for the resistance to neutrophil-mediated clearance

To understand the mechanism underlying Wzi immunomodulation in K. pneumoniae, we evaluated the impact of Wzi on tissue damage. Pathological analysis of lung sections at 24 hpi showed that Δwzi-infected mice exhibited less severe inflammation and neutrophil infiltration compared to WT (Figure 4(a)), suggesting that Wzi contributes to lung damage caused by encapsulated K. pneumoniae. To identify the immune responses affected by Wzi at early infection stage, RNA-Seq was performed on lung tissues infected with WT or Δwzi mutant at 5 hpi, and the infiltration of immune cells was examined using ImmuCellAI-mouse. Increased neutrophil infiltration was detected in WT infected lungs compared to Δwzi infected lungs (Figure 4(b)). To validate Wzi-induced innate immune cell infiltration, we detected the recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages in the alveolar using flow cytometry and sequential gating [41,42]. Immune cells were identified as CD45+ cells after excluding debris, doublets, and dead cells. Among CD45+ leukocytes, CD11c+SiglecF+CD64+ were identified as alveolar macrophages (AMs), and CD11c– Ly6Gbright cells as neutrophils (Figure S4). All CD45+ leukocytes were AMs at 5 hpi, regardless of whether the mice were infected with WT or Δwzi mutant (Figure S5a-b). Neutrophil infiltration occurred at 10 hpi, at which point the leukocytes were mainly composed of AMs and neutrophils. Compared to WT (6319/10000 single cell), neutrophil infiltration induced by infection with the Δwzi mutant significantly reduced in vivo (2044/10000 single cell) (Figure 4(c)). This is consistent with our in silico analysis (Figure 4(b)), supporting that Wzi is involved in neutrophil recruitment during initial infection by encapsulated K. pneumoniae. As the neutrophils infiltration elevated, the percentage of AMs decreased in WT infection group, but the number of AMs was comparable between WT and Δwzi mutant infected mice (Figure 4(d,e)), further demonstrating that Wzi promotes neutrophil infiltration. Myeloperoxidase (MPO), a member of the heme peroxidase-cyclooxygenase superfamily, is abundantly expressed in neutrophil granulocytes [43]. Intracellular MPO activity correlates well with tissue neutrophil content [44]. MPO activity in encapsulated K. pneumoniae-infected lungs was measured to evaluate the effect of Wzi on neutrophil recruitment. The MPO activity significantly decreased in Δwzi-infected lungs at 10 hpi compared to WT (Figure 4(f)), which is consistent with the results of flow cytometry (Figure 4(c)).

To assess the role of CPS in neutrophil recruitment, we compared neutrophil infiltration induced by ΔwcaJ and Δwzi/wcaJ mutants. The ΔwcaJ mutant-infected mice showed lower MPO activity than WT at 10 and 48 hpi (Figure 4(g,h)), suggesting that CPS is involved in neutrophil infiltration. Notably, the Δwzi/wcaJ dual mutant exhibited even lower MPO activity compared to the ΔwcaJ and Δwzi mutant at 10 and 48 hpi (Figure 4(g,h)), suggesting that CPS and Wzi independently contribute to neutrophil recruitment.

Although Wzi and CPS-induced neutrophil recruitment, the bacterial load in the lung of WT was higher than the Δwzi and ΔwcaJ mutant in vivo. Therefore, we speculated that Wzi and/or CPS promote K. pneumoniae to resist neutrophil killing. As expected, the survival of the Δwzi mutant was significantly decreased after being treated by neutrophils compared to WT (Figure 4(i)). In addition, ΔwcaJ and ΔwcaJ/wzi mutants were more sensitive to neutrophil killing than WT and Δwzi mutant (Figure 4(j)), and comparable survival rate was observed in ΔwcaJ and ΔwcaJ/wzi mutant after neutrophil treatment (Figure 4(j)), suggesting that CPS but not Wzi promotes K. pneumoniae resistance to neutrophil-mediated clearance.

Wzi and CPS induce neutrophil infiltration by promoting CXCL1 expression

We next investigated how Wzi and CPS induce neutrophil infiltration during K. pneumoniae infection. Previous studies have shown that neutrophil recruitment during K. pneumoniae infection depends on the induction of chemokines, such as CXCL1 [45,46], CCL3 [47], and CXCL5 [48]. Therefore, we sought to identify the chemokines involved in Wzi-induced neutrophil infiltration. We found that the transcript levels of CXCL1 significantly decreased in BMDMs treated with the Δwzi mutant compared to WT, while CCL3 and CXCL5 levels were comparable between the two groups (Figure 5(a)), suggesting that Wzi affects CXCL1 expression in vitro. To validate the influence of Wzi on CXCL1 expression in vivo, we measured CXCL1 secretion levels in the lungs of mice after K. pneumoniae infection. We found that K. pneumoniae infection promoted CXCL1 secretion at 3 and 5 hpi (Figure 5(b)). Consistent with the BMDMs results, significantly reduced CXCL1 expression was detected in the lungs of Δwzi-infected mice compared to WT at 5 hpi (Figure 5(b)), indicating that Wzi promotes CXCL1 expression at 5 hpi in vivo. In addition, ΔwcaJ-infected mice also exhibited significantly reduced CXCL1 secretion compared to WT at 5 hpi (Figure 5(c)). The ΔwcaJ/wzi double mutant showed a further reduction in CXCL1 secretion compared to either single mutant (Figure 5(c)), suggesting that CPS and Wzi independently contribute to CXCL1 induction during early infection. Together with the transwell results that CXCL1 promoted neutrophil infiltration (Figure 5(d,e)), we conclude that both CPS and Wzi proteins promote CXCL1 expression and further induce neutrophil recruitment.

Wzi sequence polymorphism affects serum killing resistance in a CPS-independent manner

Sequence polymorphism has been observed among the wzi genes in K. pneumoniae, with unique alleles associated with specific K-types [26]. However, it remains unclear whether the sequence polymorphism of wzi has biological significance for K. pneumoniae pathogenicity. Given that Wzi is located on the cell surface as the interface with the host immune system, Wzi may be under selection resulting in a sequence polymorphism. To understand whether the sequence diversity of wzi confers altered function to K. pneumoniae, we overexpressed various wzi alleles in the Δwzi mutant. Western blots showed that all wzi alleles were successfully expressed in Δwzi mutant, and their expression levels were comparable (Figure S6). Cell-associated CPS and viscosity were measured for Δwzi expressing various wzi alleles. Except for wzi from K47, overexpression of wzi from K1, K2, K28, K57, or K64 restored cell-associated CPS and increased viscosity compared to the Δwzi mutant (Figures 6(a,b)), suggesting that most of wzi alleles are interchangeable for CPS production.

To understand the biological significance of Wzi polymorphism, we examined the effect of different wzi alleles on the resistance to human serum killing. The growth of the WT and Δwzi were comparable in heat-inactivated human serum (Figure S7a). However, the survival of Δwzi in human serum was attenuated compared to that of WT (Figure 6(c)), suggesting that Wzi contributes to the resistance of K. pneumoniae to human serum killing. We next determined whether Wzi-mediated resistance to serum killing is dependent on CPS or not. The growth of the WT, Δwzi, ΔwcaJ and Δwzi/wcaJ mutants was comparable in heat-inactivated human serum (Figure S7b). However, the survival of the Δwzi/wcaJ mutant in human serum was significantly reduced compared to that of the ΔwcaJ mutant (Figure 6(d)), while that of the Δwzi mutant was comparable to that of Δwzi/wcaJ, suggesting that Wzi confers resistance to human serum killing in a CPS-independent manner.

To elucidate the mechanism by which Wzi confers serum resistance, we investigated its impact on complement activation. Complement pathway activation leads to the generation and deposition of C3b on bacterial surfaces [49,50]. We found that the Δwzi mutant exhibited significantly greater C3b deposition than WT (Figure 6(e)), indicating that Wzi reduces C3b deposition in encapsulated K. pneumoniae.

Based on the major influence of the CPS on C3b binding, we further assessed C3b deposition in WT, Δwzi, ΔwcaJ, and Δwzi/wcaJ mutants. The ΔwcaJ mutant showed a marked increase in C3b deposition compared to WT, and the Δwzi/wcaJ double mutant showed an even greater increase than either single mutant (Figure 6(f)). This suggests that Wzi can inhibit C3b deposition independently of CPS, although CPS itself also impedes C3b binding.

Consistent with this, opsonophagocytosis assay revealed that the Δwzi/wcaJ double mutant was more susceptible to phagocytosis than the ΔwcaJ mutant (Figure 6(g)), confirming that Wzi also inhibits macrophage phagocytosis in a CPS-independent manner. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that Wzi enhances K. pneumoniae resistance to serum killing and attenuates phagocytosis by reducing complement activation.

Notably, Wzi alleles of K1, K2 and K57 are highly associated with typical hvKP, and they all significantly increased K. pneumoniae survival in human serum (Figure 6(h)). While Wzi alleles of K28 and K64, frequently associated with cKP, showed comparable survival ratio with Δwzi mutant (Figure 6(h)). The results suggest that the sequence polymorphism of Wzi has an impact on the serum killing resistance of K. pneumoniae. Although Wzi from K47 failed to restore CPS in bacteria (Figure 6(a)), it also promoted K. pneumoniae resistance to serum killing (Figure 6(e)), further demonstrating that Wzi promotes the resistance to serum killing independent of CPS. To assess the robustness of the effect of Wzi sequence polymorphism on serum killing resistance, we engineered an additional Δwzi mutant in the CRKP ST11-K64 strain KP47434, representing the prevalent CRKP clone in China [51]. Overexpressed these Wzi alleles in KP47434Δwzi yielded phenotypes consistent with those observed in the K2 genetic background (Figure S8).

Discussion

As a conserved member of the cps locus, the actual role of Wzi in the virulence of K. pneumoniae has rarely and systematically been discussed, especially in the host immune response, largely because the function of Wzi has traditionally been considered to be CPS-dependent. In this study, we found that Wzi not only functions on CPS production but also acts as a CPS-independent and -dependent virulence factor in K. pneumoniae, suggesting that Wzi functions differently from the other cps genes reported. Our findings challenge the conventional understanding that the function of Wzi is primarily determined by its effect on CPS production.

Here, we determined that Wzi is involved in CPS production in K. pneumoniae, and Wzi deficiency can significantly reduce CPS production, which is consistent with previous reports [37,38]. However, in E. coli and A. baumannii, Wzi deficiency does not affect the CPS production, but causes a decrease in cell-associated CPS and an increase in cell-free CPS in the supernatant [40]. Therefore, Wzi has been suggested to assist in capsule assembly by anchoring it to the membrane as an outer membrane lectin in E. coli [52]. The underlying mechanism of such a diverse function of Wzi in different species warrants further investigation. Additionally, we noted that Wzi deficiency reduced cell-associated CPS but retained the hmv phenotype, and the virulence of Δwzi mutant was significantly attenuated in vitro and in vivo. These results suggest that the amount of CPS, but not hmv, is critical for hypervirulence, raising the concern that the string test may cause false-positive results for the hvKP detection. The sedimentation-based test is an alternative method for quantifying mucoviscosity in liquid cultures. However, discrepancies may arise between the two methods. As observed in this study, the wzi null mutant showed a reduced sedimentation-based hmv phenotype yet a positive string test result. We suppose that the apparent discrepancy between the two methods likely reflects differences in the production and organization of CPS. Loss of Wzi leads to a limited reduction of overall CPS production and impaired surface retention. This limited reduction in total CPS production may not affect the string test result, since additional exopolysaccharides are involved, as previously suggested [15]. The sedimentation assay relies heavily on the retention of hmv cells in the supernatant after centrifugation, and impaired surface retention of CPS may therefore have a significant effect on the results. These findings highlight that the sedimentation-based hmv assay and the string test may not accurately distinguish between the complicated mechanisms underlying the hmv phenotype and CPS production.

We further showed that Wzi has an impact on the pathogenicity of K. pneumoniae, and that Wzi deficiency can significantly increase the survival rate of mice. Our mechanistic study suggests that the role of Wzi in the pathogenicity of K. pneumoniae is partially attributed to the inhibition of IFN-γ secretion at the early infection stage. IFN-γ is important for host defense against pathogens. IFN-γ knockout mice infected with K. pneumoniae resulted in a significantly increased mortality, higher bacteria burden, and defective host immune response [53,54], suggesting that IFN-γ is important for host defense against K. pneumoniae. To our knowledge, the virulence factors responsible for IFN-γ secretion during K. pneumoniae infection have not been reported prior to our study. We further determined that Wzi inhibited the secretion of IFN-γ-related cytokines at 10 hpi and promoted K. pneumoniae growth in the lung dependent on CPS, indicating that CPS is the first virulence factor identified in K. pneumoniae responsible for IFN-γ secretion at the early stage of infection.

Once invading microorganisms reaches a certain threshold beyond the killing capacity of AMs, cytokines, chemokines, and arachidonic metabolites will be secreted by AMs and epithelial cells to recruit neutrophils to aid in pathogen clearance [55]. In general, the early recruitment of neutrophils is critical for bacterial clearance and host survival [56]. In this study, we found that Wzi and CPS can independently induce neutrophil recruitment. Both Δwzi and ΔwcaJ mutant induced lower neutrophil recruitment than the WT during infection, but the bacterial loads in the lungs were significantly reduced compared to the WT at 10 hpi. Consistent with a previous study, increased infiltration of neutrophils with higher bacterial burden was detected in carbapenem-resistant hvKP infected mice compared with cKP [57]. This could be due to these low-virulence strains being more susceptible to neutrophil killing and/or other immune responses, e.g. AMs and pulmonary antimicrobial proteins.

The presence of CPS enhanced K. pneumoniae resistance to neutrophil killing, exacerbated tissue damage, and facilitated bacterial dissemination from the lungs to the spleen and liver. Prolonged or excessive neutrophil recruitment and activation are known to compromise the pulmonary barrier, increasing pathogen invasion into the bloodstream [58], perpetuating inflammation in pneumoseptic K. pneumoniae infection [59–64]. Neutrophils can also act as reservoirs for pathogens and aid systemic dissemination [65]. Thus, we propose that CPS-driven neutrophil recruitment contributes to organ damage, further enabling K. pneumoniae dissemination across organs. This highlights the important role of CPS in K. pneumoniae pathogenesis, and explains the enhanced survival and dissemination capacity of WT strain compared to Δwzi mutant in encapsulated K. pneumoniae (Figure 2(b-d)). Of note, although Wzi promotes neutrophil recruitment, it does not enhance K. pneumoniae resistance to neutrophil killing, unlike CPS. Additionally, the non-capsulated K. pneumoniae mutant ΔwcaJ was more sensitive to neutrophil killing. These findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the enhanced survival and dissemination capacity of the Δwzi/wcaJ mutant compared to ΔwcaJ observed in vivo (Figure 2(e-g)).

Unlike non-capsulated K. pneumoniae, Wzi promotes neutrophil infiltration but simultaneously contributes to resistance to neutrophil killing in encapsulated K. pneumoniae. Given the established role of Wzi in CPS production and surface anchoring in this study, the finding is not paradoxical. Wzi and CPS can independently promote neutrophil infiltration (Figure 4(g,h)). However, it is only CPS, rather than Wzi itself, that contributes to the resistance to neutrophil killing (Figure 4(j)). Furthermore, Wzi was found to inhibit IFNγ secretion in a CPS-dependent manner. Thus, Wzi does not function as an independent virulence factor in the resistance to neutrophil killing and IFNγ secretion inhibition. Instead, the attenuated resistance of the wzi null mutant to neutrophil killing is an indirect consequence of impaired CPS production, rather than a direct effect of Wzi loss itself.

K. pneumoniae induces neutrophil recruitment dependent on the generation of chemokines, e.g. CXCL1 [45,46], CCL3 [47], and CXCL5 [48]. However, the factors by which K. pneumoniae induces neutrophil recruitment and evades neutrophil killing are largely unclear. In this study, we demonstrated that Wzi and CPS independently promoted CXCL1 secretion, driving neutrophil recruitment. This finding aligns with previous reports showing that CPS from Streptococcus pneumoniae and E. coli induces more neutrophil infiltration compared to PBS treated group [66,67].

Although wzi is one of the six conserved genes of the cps locus at its 5’ end, the sequence of polymorphisms of the gene has been noted previously. Different K types are linked to different wzi sequences in K. pneumoniae, which allows wzi as a marker for strain typing [26]. The sequence polymorphisms of wzi suggest that this gene may be under selection, which can be further supported by its location on the membrane. Indeed, as the frontier of contact with the host immune system, Wzi may function independently of CPS as other outer membrane proteins in virulence, e.g. porins, efflux pumps, iron transport systems, and genes involved in allantoin metabolism [68–70]. Of note, K1 and K2 serotypes are highly associated with hypervirulence, which correlates with the specific structures of CPS, e.g. the K1/K2 capsules lack the Man-α2-Man structure or contain sialic acid [71,72]. Here, our study showed that Wzi was involved in the resistance to human serum killing independent of CPS. Intriguingly, Wzi alleles from hypervirulent K locus (K1, K2, and KL57) conferred enhanced resistance to human serum killing compared to those (K28 and K64) from cKP, suggesting that the evolution of wzi may be driven by the host immune system. Our results highlight the multifaceted role of Wzi in the virulence of K. pnuemoniae, not only as one of the core members involved in capsule assembly but also as an outer membrane protein additionally contributing to the hypervirulent of K. pneumoniae.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was determined rather than being based on a formal a priori power calculation. Consequently, the study may be underpowered, and future validation with larger cohorts is warranted. Second, our findings are derived exclusively from a C57BL/6J mouse model. While this strain is a standard model in immunology research, its specific genetic and immunological background limits the direct extrapolation of our results in genetically diverse human populations. The generalizability of our conclusions needs to be confirmed in other mouse strains, such as BALB/c. Third, the functional differences of Wzi alleles from hvKP and cKP were assessed via plasmid-based overexpression in a Δwzi mutant. Although serum killing assays revealed distinct phenotypes, possible variations in expression levels could lead to false-positive results. Conclusive verification of these findings requires future studies employing chromosomal allele replacement. Finally, we did not assess the wzi complementation strain in a mouse infection model, which represents a limitation of this study.

In conclusion, our study systematically investigated the function of Wzi in CPS production, hmv phenotype, and the pathogenicity in K. pnuemoniae resisting against host clearance. The role of Wzi in K. pneumoniae pathogenicity is CPS-dependent and -independent, suggesting that Wzi and CPS coordinate distinct, yet complementary, aspects of immune evasion during K. pneumoniae infection, and supporting the notion that Wzi could be a potential target for anti-infective treatment of K. pneumoniae.
