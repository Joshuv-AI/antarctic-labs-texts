Polysaccharide from Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz. protects against avian pathogenic Escherichia coli-induced intestinal barrier Dysfunction via Suppressing PI3K/Akt-Mediated Claudin-2 upregulation
Authors: Shuijing Chen, Xiaomeng Qu, Jiusheng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) is a leading cause of intestinal barrier dysfunction and diarrhea in chicks, resulting in substantial economic losses to the poultry industry. This study investigated the protective effects and underlying mechanism of polysaccharide from Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz. (PAMK) against APEC-induced intestinal injury using an integrated in vivo (chick) and in vitro (IPEC-J2 cell) approach. In vivo, PAMK intervention significantly reduced necropsy scores, alleviated growth retardation, and improved villus height-to-crypt depth ratios as well as brush border height. PAMK also attenuated the APEC-induced increase in goblet cell counts and upregulated MUC-2 mRNA expression, indicating enhanced chemical barrier function. Furthermore, PAMK suppressed the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and the pore-forming tight junction protein Claudin-2 in the jejunum. In IPEC-J2 cells, PAMK pretreatment inhibited APEC-induced upregulation of TNF-α and Claudin-2 at both mRNA and protein levels, effects that were phenocopied by the PI3K inhibitor LY294002. Mechanistically, PAMK suppressed APEC-induced phosphorylation of Akt and its downstream target GSK-3β, thereby blocking the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β signaling cascade. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that APEC infection increased Claudin-2 protein abundance along the villus apical membrane. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PAMK alleviates APEC-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β pathway to downregulate Claudin-2 expression, suggesting its potential as a dietary supplement to improve gut health in poultry production.Keywords: Polysaccharide from Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz., Avian pathogenic Escherichia coli, Claudin-2, PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β pathway, Tight junction

Introduction

Avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) is a leading cause of colibacillosis in poultry, triggering severe intestinal inflammation, diarrhea, and growth retardation in young chicks, which results in substantial economic losses to the industry (Cao et al., 2025; Nawaz et al., 2024). While antibiotics have been the mainstay of control, the rise of antimicrobial resistance and food safety concerns has created an urgent need for effective, natural alternatives (The Lancet, 2024; Tian et al., 2025).

The intestinal epithelial barrier, maintained by tight junction (TJ) proteins such as claudins, occludin, and zonula occludens (ZO), is a critical defense against enteric pathogens including APEC (Wei et al., 2025; Zha et al., 2025). Among TJ components, Claudin-2 is uniquely pathogenic in context of infection: as a pore-forming protein, its upregulation increases paracellular permeability, contributing directly to "leaky gut" and inflammatory diarrhea (Oami et al., 2024; Venugopal et al., 2019). Although APEC is known to disrupt TJ integrity, the specific mechanisms driving Claudin-2 overexpression in avian models remain unclear. This knowledge gap extends beyond pathogenic infection, as environmental contaminants such as pesticides and microplastics also disrupt tight junctions via pathways like lysosome/ROS/ferroptosis (Shi et al., 2024).

Polysaccharide from Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz. (PAMK), a traditional herbal extract, exhibits recognized anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties (Chen et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025). Preliminary evidence suggests PAMK may alleviate intestinal damage (Kong et al., 2025; Li et al., 2021), yet its precise molecular targets and barrier-protective mechanisms against APEC are undefined.

Recent studies implicate the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β signaling pathway as a central regulator of both intestinal inflammation and TJ dynamics (Huang et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2024). Its activation can promote the expression of pore-forming claudins, including Claudin-2, during enteric infection (Wang et al., 2011). We therefore hypothesized that PAMK protects the intestinal barrier by modulating the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β pathway to suppress Claudin-2 expression.

To test this, we adopted an integrated in vivo-in vitro strategy. Using an APEC-challenged chick model, we first assessed the protective effects of PAMK on overall intestinal health and Claudin-2 expression. We then employed IPEC-J2 porcine intestinal epithelial cells to dissect the underlying signaling mechanism, incorporating pathway-specific pharmacological inhibition. Our findings not only elucidate a novel mechanism of action for PAMK but also strengthen its scientific rationale as a sustainable alternative to antibiotics in poultry production.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang University (Hangzhou, China) (Approval No. ZJU20240476) and were performed in strict accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Materials and reagents

Polysaccharide from Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz. (PAMK, purity >70%) and Astragalus polysaccharide (APS, purity >70%) were obtained from Nantong Feiyu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nantong, China) and Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), respectively. The PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (S1737) was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). RPMI-1640 medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin-streptomycin were supplied by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). TRIzol, HiScript II Q RT SuperMix, Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, and CCK-8 kit were obtained from Vazyme (Nanjing, China). Porcine TNF-α ELISA kit (B0202) was purchased from Dibai Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

Antibodies used included: rabbit anti-Claudin-2 polyclonal antibody (PB0122; Boster, Wuhan, China); phospho-AKT (Ser473) recombinant rabbit monoclonal antibody (ET1607-73; HUABIO, Hangzhou, China); phospho-AKT (Thr308) recombinant rabbit monoclonal antibody (HA722951; HUABIO); phospho-GSK-3β (Ser9) recombinant rabbit monoclonal antibody (ET1607-60; HUABIO); Akt1/2/3 recombinant rabbit monoclonal antibody (ET1609-51; HUABIO); mouse anti-GAPDH monoclonal antibody (60004-1-Ig; Proteintech, Wuhan, China). For immunofluorescence, CoraLite® Plus 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (RGAR002; Proteintech) was used. For Western blotting, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (D110110; BBI Life Sciences, Shanghai, China) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (D110058; BBI Life Sciences) were used. PVDF membrane was obtained from Vazyme (Nanjing, China), and DAB substrate kit (AR1027) from Boster (Wuhan, China).

Bacterial strain and cell line

Avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) strain CVCC3016 was purchased from the China Institute of Veterinary Drug Control (Beijing, China). For chick challenge, bacteria were prepared at 1.68 × 10⁸ CFU/mL in sterile PBS, as previously described (Lu, 2023; Ma et al., 2020; Wang, 2023). The IPEC-J2 cell line (Key Laboratory of Animal Virology, Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, Zhejiang University) was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were used for all experiments.

Animal Experimental Design

Forty 5-day-old White Leghorn chicks (half male and half female) were randomly assigned to four groups: Control, Model, PAMK, and APS (included as a positive control). From day 7 to 13, the PAMK and APS groups received daily oral gavage of 200 mg/kg BW of the respective polysaccharides (Tian, 2021; Xiang et al., 2020); Control and Model groups received saline. On days 11-13, all groups except Control were challenged with 1 mL of APEC suspension (1.68 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) by oral gavage. On day 14, chicks were euthanized for sample collection. Jejunum tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histology or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular assays.

Cell viability and infection model

IPEC-J2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (2 × 10⁵ cells/mL, 100 μL/well). After 24 h, cells were treated with PAMK (0, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800 μg/mL) for 12, 24, or 48 h. CCK-8 reagent (10%) was added, incubated for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage of the control. The highest concentration maintaining viability >90% (200 μg/mL, 24 h) was selected for subsequent experiments.

For infection, cells were seeded into 6-well plates (2 × 10⁵ cells/well). PAMK group was pretreated with 200 μg/mL PAMK for 24 h; inhibitor group was pretreated with 20 μM LY294002 for 2 h as described previously (Qi et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2016). Cells were then challenged with APEC at MOI = 40 for 2 h (infection conditions determined by preliminary experiments (Zhao, 2023)). Experimental groups: Control, Model, PAMK, and LY294002.

Histological and Morphometric Analysis

Fixed jejunal segments were paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Alcian Blue-Periodic Acid Schiff (AB-PAS). Villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), and brush border height were measured from 10 well-oriented villus-crypt units per section using ImageJ at 400 × magnification. VH/CD ratio, lymphocyte percentage in lamina propria, and goblet cell percentage were calculated.

Immunohistochemistry

Jejunal sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking endogenous peroxidase and non-specific binding, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-Claudin-2 antibody (1:200) overnight at 4°C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. DAB was used for visualization, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Integrated optical density (IOD) was analyzed using ImageJ.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent. cDNA was synthesized with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix. qPCR was performed on a C1000 Touch thermal cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using SYBR Green Master Mix. Relative gene expression was calculated by the 2⁻∆∆Ct method with GAPDH as internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min. TNF-α concentrations were measured using a porcine ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm, and concentrations were calculated from a standard curve.

Immunofluorescence

Cells cultured on glass-bottom plates were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 3% BSA. Cells were incubated with rabbit anti-Claudin-2 antibody (1:100) overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted microscope.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA: anti-p-Akt (Ser473, 1:1000), anti-p-Akt (Thr308, 1:1000), anti-p-GSK-3β (Ser9, 1:5000), anti-Akt1/2/3 (1:2000), anti-Claudin-2 (1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (1:5000). After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG (1:3000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized by ECL and quantified using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.4.2. One-way ANOVA with LSD post-hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated three times.

Results

Effects of PAMK on overall health status in APEC-infected chicks

As shown in Fig. 1, APEC infection (model group) significantly decreased body weight (P < 0.01) and increased necropsy scores (P < 0.05) compared to the control group. Although PAMK pretreatment did not fully restore body weight (P > 0.05), it significantly reduced necropsy scores (P < 0.05) and alleviated infection-induced growth retardation. Similar protective effects were observed in the APS group.

PAMK Modulates Intestinal Morphology and Mucosal Integrity

APEC infection induced marked histopathological lesions across all intestinal segments, characterized by villus atrophy, crypt disruption, and inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 2A). These alterations were markedly ameliorated by PAMK or APS intervention. Morphometric analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed that APEC significantly reduced the villus height-to-crypt depth (VH/CD) ratio in the duodenum and jejunum (P < 0.05), and this reduction was significantly ameliorated by both PAMK and APS treatment (P < 0.05). Brush border height (Fig. 2C) followed a similar pattern, with the infection-induced reduction (P < 0.05) significantly ameliorated by polysaccharide treatment (P < 0.05). Lymphocyte counts (Fig. 2D) revealed segment-specific effects. APS significantly reduced lymphocyte infiltration in the jejunum, ileum, and colon (P < 0.05), indicating anti-inflammatory activity. In contrast, PAMK exerted segment-specific effects on lymphocyte populations: decreasing infiltration in the colon (P < 0.05) while increasing counts in the duodenum (P < 0.05). The localized increase in lymphocyte infiltration in the duodenum induced by PAMK may reflect immune recruitment and priming of mucosal immunity, rather than detrimental inflammation.

PAMK Modulates Mucin Secretion and Goblet Cell Response

AB-PAS staining revealed that APEC infection broadly activated goblet cell responses in the intestinal mucosa (Fig. 3A). Compared with the control group, chicks in the model group exhibited markedly increased numbers of AB-PAS-positive cells and deeper staining intensity across the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and colon. Goblet cell counts (Fig. 3B) confirmed that APEC infection significantly increased goblet cell counts in all intestinal segments examined (P < 0.01), suggesting a compensatory secretory response to mucosal insult. Both PAMK and APS intervention effectively attenuated this APEC-induced increase in goblet cell numbers, maintaining mucosal homeostasis. Segment-specific effects were observed: in the duodenum and ileum, PAMK and APS both significantly reduced goblet cell numbers compared with the model group (P < 0.01), restoring them to levels comparable to the control group. Similarly, in the jejunum and colon, both polysaccharides significantly suppressed infection-induced goblet cell proliferation (P < 0.01), with counts returning to control levels (P < 0.05).

Effects of PAMK on mRNA expression of Claudin-2, MUC-2, and inflammatory cytokines in chick jejunum

qPCR analysis revealed that APEC infection significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the jejunum compared with the control group (P < 0.05), which was markedly attenuated by PAMK pretreatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 4B, APEC infection significantly increased the mRNA expression of the pore-forming tight junction protein Claudin-2 (P < 0.05), while PAMK intervention effectively suppressed this upregulation (P < 0.05). Notably, APEC infection significantly reduced the mRNA expression of mucin MUC-2 (P < 0.05), which was restored to control levels following PAMK treatment (P < 0.05), indicating that PAMK enhances chemical barrier function at the transcriptional level. The significant suppression of APEC-induced Claudin-2 mRNA by PAMK in vivo prompted us to further investigate its regulatory mechanisms using an in vitro epithelial model.

Effect of PAMK on IPEC-J2 Cell viability

The cytotoxicity of PAMK on IPEC-J2 cells was assessed by CCK-8 assay after 12, 24, and 48 h of treatment with concentrations ranging from 0 to 800 μg/mL (Fig. 5). At 12 h, PAMK at 100 μg/mL significantly increased cell viability compared to the control group (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed at other concentrations. At 24 h, PAMK at 50-200 μg/mL markedly increased cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner, with no cytotoxicity detected. At 48 h, no significant differences were observed across all concentrations. Based on viability >90%, 200 μg/mL at 24 h was selected for subsequent experiments.

PAMK suppresses APEC-Induced TNF-α and claudin-2 expression in IPEC-J2 Cells

Both PAMK and LY294002 pretreatment suppressed these increases (P < 0.05), with no significant difference between treatment groups for TNF-α (P > 0.05) (Fig. 6A). ELISA revealed that APEC infection increased TNF-α protein secretion (P < 0.05), which was reduced by PAMK and LY294002 (P < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between the two treatment groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 6B). Immunofluorescence staining showed that APEC infection intensified Claudin-2 fluorescence at cell junctions (P < 0.05), an effect attenuated by both PAMK and LY294002 (P < 0.05), with no difference between treatment groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 6C).

PAMK inhibits APEC-induced activation of the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β pathway and claudin-2 protein expression

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which PAMK regulates Claudin-2, we examined the activation of the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β axis. APEC infection significantly triggered the phosphorylation of Akt (Ser473, P < 0.05; Thr308, P < 0.05) and its downstream target GSK-3β (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7A). This activation correlated with a marked upregulation of Claudin-2 protein (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7B). Notably, PAMK pretreatment significantly suppressed the phosphorylation of Akt (Ser473) and GSK-3β, effectively mimicking the effects of the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (no significant differences between PAMK and LY294002 groups, P > 0.05) (Fig. 7A). These findings indicate that PAMK modulates Claudin-2 expression primarily by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β signaling cascade.

Effect of PAMK on claudin-2 localization and distribution in the jejunum

In accordance with the in vitro findings, IHC analysis (Fig. 8B) demonstrated that APEC infection significantly increased Claudin-2 localization along the villus apical membrane (P < 0.05). While PAMK treatment exhibited a moderate numerical reduction in Claudin-2 protein abundance compared to the model group, the difference did not reach statistical significance in the in vivo tissue sections (P > 0.05). This suggests that while PAMK strongly regulates the transcriptional signaling of Claudin-2, the turnover of the established protein matrix in the complex intestinal environment of chicks may require a longer intervention period.

Discussion

The present study establishes that PAMK serves as a multi-target protective agent against APEC-induced intestinal injury in chicks, characterized by the synchronized restoration of the physical, chemical, and immune barriers. Our findings transition from macro-level morphological improvements to the precise orchestration of the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β/Claudin-2 signaling axis, providing a comprehensive mechanistic basis for PAMK as a potent antibiotic alternative (Yang et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2025).

The restoration of intestinal morphology (villus height and brush border height) by PAMK represents the first line of defense recovery. However, a more nuanced finding lies in the rectification of an ineffective compensatory response of the mucosal defense. The paradox observed—where APEC induced goblet cell proliferation yet suppressed MUC-2 transcription—suggests that the host attempts to counteract mucosal insult through rapid recruitment of goblet cells. However, these cells likely remain in an immature state, failing to reach the functional capacity required for robust mucin synthesis (Cen et al., 2025; Gustafsson and Johansson, 2022). PAMK appears to rectify this "quantity-over-quality" imbalance by restoring MUC-2 transcript levels, thereby ensuring the chemical barrier is functionally competent rather than merely numerically increased.

A central breakthrough of this work is identifying the suppression of the pore-forming protein Claudin-2 as a key mechanism for PAMK. Unlike "seal-forming" tight junctions, Claudin-2 facilitates paracellular cation and water leakage, the hallmark of secretory diarrhea (Krug et al., 2012; Luettig et al., 2015). Our in vitro data using the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 demonstrated that the APEC-induced TNF-α/Claudin-2 surge is critically dependent on the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β cascade. By dampening this signaling hyperactivation, PAMK curtails the transcription of Claudin-2, thereby potentially mitigating the "leaky gut" syndrome and clinical diarrhea observed in APEC-infected chicks (Luettig et al., 2016; Zhong et al., 2024).

The observed dissociation between Claudin-2 mRNA suppression and its protein abundance in our in vivo model warrants discussion. While PAMK significantly inhibited Claudin-2 transcription, the protein levels showed only a moderate reduction. This likely reflects the high structural stability and slow turnover of existing tight junction complexes in the complex intestinal environment (Ganapathy et al., 2022; Ikari et al., 2015). Unlike the rapid dynamics of cells in culture, mature intestinal epithelial junctions in vivo are stabilized by an actin-myosin cytoskeleton, making immediate protein-level reversal challenging within a short experimental window. We propose that PAMK exerts a "preventative reinforcement"—by curbing de novo synthesis at the transcriptional level, it prevents further barrier degradation rather than immediately purging existing membrane proteins. The significant downregulation of protein in our IPEC-J2 model confirms that the molecular machinery for protein suppression is functional, but its manifestation in vivo is likely delayed by the turnover kinetics of the established tissue matrix.

The segment-specific effects—enhancing lymphocyte recruitment in the duodenum while suppressing inflammation in the colon—highlight the "bidirectional regulation" typical of traditional Chinese medicine-derived polysaccharides (Peng et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2020). This suggests that PAMK does not simply suppress the immune system but rather "primes" mucosal surveillance while quenching pathological hyper-inflammation (Costes et al., 2019). The growing concern over antimicrobial resistance has intensified the search for effective, safe alternatives to antibiotics (Gupta and Sharma, 2022; O’Connell et al., 2023). Plant-derived polysaccharides offer several advantages, including low toxicity, biodegradability, and multi-target mechanisms of action (Huang et al., 2024a; Ruiqing et al., 2025).

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. First, the transcriptional-protein dissociation of Claudin-2 in vivo necessitates further investigation into post-transcriptional dynamics and protein turnover (Ganapathy et al., 2022). Second, while we observed an ineffective compensatory response in goblet cells, future studies employing maturity markers or secretion assays are required to definitively resolve their functional status. Third, although our barrier protection claims are supported by structural, molecular, and clinical indicators (necropsy scores), the absence of direct permeability assays remains a gap that future research should address to quantify "leak pathway" kinetics. Finally, while the PI3K/Akt/Claudin-2 axis is highly conserved, validating these mechanisms in primary avian epithelial cells will further enhance the translational relevance of PAMK to the poultry industry.

APEC infection disrupts intestinal barrier integrity through a multi-faceted assault, including the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, the exhaustion of functional goblet cells, and the pathological upregulation of the pore-forming protein Claudin-2. PAMK effectively counteracts these alterations by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β signaling cascade, thereby downregulating Claudin-2 transcription and preventing the excessive paracellular leakage of ions and water typically associated with "leaky gut" syndrome (Fig. 9).

This increases paracellular permeability ("leak pathway"), leading to excessive ion and water leakage, clinically manifesting as watery diarrhea. PAMK intervention suppresses Akt (Ser473) and GSK-3β phosphorylation, blocking this signaling axis. This downregulates Claudin-2 expression, attenuates goblet cell hyperplasia, and restores MUC-2 production, preserving intestinal physical and chemical barrier integrity. Abbreviations: PAMK, polysaccharide from Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz.; APEC, avian pathogenic Escherichia coli.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PAMK effectively alleviates APEC-induced intestinal injury in chicks by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β signaling pathway, thereby downregulating Claudin-2 expression, restoring goblet cell function, and enhancing intestinal barrier integrity. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for the potential use of PAMK to improve gut health and reduce antibiotic dependence in poultry production.
