Impact of ex vivo lung perfusion evaluation on donation after circulatory death in lung transplantation
Authors: Mariana Gil-Barturen, Laura Donahoe, Marc de Perrot, Kazuhiro Yasufuku, Andrew Pierre, Elliot Wakeam, Thomas K. Waddell, Shaf Keshavjee, Marcelo Cypel, Jonathan C. Yeung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAs the result of increased warm ischemia time and limited intraoperative evaluation, donation after circulatory death (DCD) donors might be considered greater risk, and use lags behind donation after brain death (DBD). Ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) provides an opportunity to better evaluate DCD lungs, and we sought to examine our outcomes.MethodsA single-center retrospective review of a prospectively collected database was performed for DCD donor lung transplants (LTx) from January 1, 2007, to August 8, 2024. DCD donors were stratified into EVLP (EVLP-DCD) and non-EVLP (non-EVLP-DCD) groups. Donor and recipient demographics, donor quality metrics, post-LTx outcomes, and overall survival were collected.ResultsIn total, 2282 LTx were performed from 2007 to 2024, and 467 (20.5%) were from DCD. EVLP was used in 257 (55%) of DCD. Between EVLP-DCD and non-EVLP-DCD, there was similar primary graft dysfunction grade 3 at 72 hours (14.0% vs 13.3%, P = .735) and no survival difference (P = .28). EVLP-DCD cases had shorter stay in the intensive care unit (median 4 vs 6 days; P < .001) but similar hospital stay (median 22 vs 24 days; P = .162). EVLP-DCD donors had longer time from withdrawal to flush (59 vs 43 minutes; P < .001) and a significantly greater rate of Gram stain–positive cultures (62.6% vs 41.9%; P < .001). The proportion of DCD transplants increased from 6.2% in 2008 to 54.9% in 2025.ConclusionsTransplantation of DCD lungs evaluated by EVLP had comparable outcomes compared with non-EVLP–evaluated DCD lungs and DBD lungs. Liberal use of EVLP evaluation has led to safe and increased use of DCD lungs despite their unpredictably greater risk.

Central Message

In a high-volume ex vivo lung perfusion program, donation after circulatory death lung transplantation after EVLP is associated with outcomes comparable with direct DCD and donation after brain death.

Perspective

The impact of ex vivo lung perfusion in DCD lung transplantation is influenced by center experience, protocol standardization, and procedural volume. High-volume programs with consistent EVLP practices provide important context for interpreting registry-based analyses and may guide strategies to optimize donor lung use.

Donation after circulatory death (DCD) has emerged as an increasingly important source of donor lungs, helping to expand the donor pool amid persistent organ shortages.1 However, DCD lungs have historically been regarded as greater-risk grafts because of the period of warm ischemia that occurs between withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy (WLST) and organ flush, the limited opportunity for intraoperative assessment, and the risk of aspiration, pulmonary edema, and inflammatory “shock lung” after prolonged hypotensive circulatory arrest. As a result, the use of DCD lungs continues to lag behind that of donation after brain death (DBD).

Despite these concerns, studies have demonstrated that outcomes after DCD lung transplantation are comparable with those from DBD donors, with no difference in 1- or 5-year survival rates.2 Although the proportion of DCD donors among all deceased donors has increased to nearly 20% worldwide,3 the fraction of lung transplants using DCD donors remains low, representing less than 5% in the United States.4 This highlights a persistent underuse of DCD lungs despite growing evidence supporting their safety and efficacy.5

Ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) enables functional assessment and reconditioning of marginal or high-risk donor lungs before transplantation.6 In the context of DCD, EVLP provides the unique opportunity to evaluate graft performance after cardiac arrest and to mitigate the uncertainty associated with limited intraoperative evaluation. Although both DCD and EVLP independently have been shown to yield favorable outcomes, the interplay between these 2 approaches remains incompletely characterized. Recent registry analyses from the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) database demonstrated a lower survival among recipients of DCD lungs evaluated on EVLP,7,8 raising questions about the influence of EVLP use, type of EVLP performed, and center-specific practices on these results.

According to International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) registry data, EVLP has been used in less than 20% of lung donors globally,2 reflecting ongoing variability in adoption and likely lower use among DCD donors. In contrast, our center has adopted a liberal EVLP strategy, with more than one half of DCD donor lungs undergoing EVLP evaluation before transplantation on the basis of surgeon decision. As a result, DCD donors accounted for 54.9% of our transplant volume in 2025. This high-use paradigm provides a unique opportunity to evaluate the clinical impact of EVLP use in DCD lung transplantation. In this single-center retrospective study, we evaluated contemporary posttransplant outcomes of DCD lungs assessed with EVLP compared with DCD lungs that did not undergo EVLP and with those receiving DBD lungs.

Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective cohort study using prospectively collected data from the Toronto Lung Transplant Program. All adult patients who underwent lung transplantation between January 1, 2007, and August 8, 2024, were included. The study was approved by the University Health Network Research Ethics Board (REB 25-5082, April 30, 2025).

Recipients of DCD lungs were stratified into 2 groups: those who received DCD lungs after ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP-DCD) and those who received DCD lungs transplanted directly without EVLP evaluation (non-EVLP-DCD). EVLP was performed according to the Toronto EVLP technique, published elsewhere.9 Donor and recipient demographics, intraoperative characteristics, and early posttransplant outcomes were compared between groups. The primary end point was overall survival. Secondary endpoints included intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (LOS), total hospital LOS, and the incidence of primary graft dysfunction (PGD) grade 3 at 72 hours, as defined by the ISHLT criteria, and the need for postoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support. Additional analyses evaluated patterns of EVLP use among transplant surgeons and trends in DCD donor use over the study period.

All candidates listed for lung transplantation during the study period were eligible to receive grafts from both DCD and DBD donors, including lungs evaluated with EVLP. Recipient selection, intraoperative management, and postoperative care were performed according to standard institutional protocols and were consistent across all groups. Donor-recipient matching was performed on the basis of blood type, predicted total lung capacity, and clinical urgency.

Donor and EVLP selection criteria have been previously described by our group.6,9 In summary, high-risk donor lungs were defined by any of the following: partial pressure of oxygen/inspired oxygen fraction (Pao2/Fio2) <300 mm Hg, pulmonary edema, poor compliance, or high-risk clinical history such as suspected aspiration. Donor lungs with established pneumonia, gross gastric aspiration, or extensive mechanical injury were generally declined. DCD lungs were considered for EVLP at the discretion of the transplant surgeon.

Donor Procedures

Donor assessments were determined on the basis of medical history, routine bloodwork, arterial blood gases, radiograph of the chest, computed tomography scan of the chest scan, flexible bronchoscopy, and direct examination of the lungs. The DCD procurement procedure is described elsewhere.10 Abdominal or thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion techniques were not systematically used at our center during the study period and were not included in this analysis. Since 2021, our center has transitioned from traditional ice storage to preservation at 10 °C, in accordance with the results from our institutional clinical trial and multicenter trials11 demonstrating the safety and efficacy of this approach.

EVLP Protocol

We used our previously described and standardized Toronto protocol.9 Ex vivo radiography of the chest and flexible bronchoscopy were routinely conducted at 1 hour and 3 hours of perfusion and repeated as indicated on the basis of lung performance or findings. The decision to accept EVLP-evaluated lungs for transplantation was made after a minimum of 3 hours of assessment. Acceptance criteria included a Pao2/Fio2 ratio >400 mm Hg, stable or improving airway pressures, pulmonary vascular resistance, dynamic compliance, and radiographic appearance.

In December 2022, the hardware platform transitioned from the first-generation Toronto EVLP circuit to the TorEx Lung Perfusion System (Traferox Technologies, Inc). All EVLP procedures were conducted on-site at the transplant hospital (Toronto General Hospital, University Health Network); lungs were not transported while on EVLP. Each procedure was performed by a dedicated team of trained organ perfusion specialists, ensuring consistent application of the standardized institutional protocol.

Posttransplant Care

After the lungs were transplanted, standard of care was provided in both groups, including fluid management, antibiotic prophylaxis, immunosuppression, and surveillance bronchoscopy.12

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate on the basis of data distribution. Variables summarized as means ± standard deviations were compared between groups using the Student t test, whereas variables summarized as medians [interquartile range] were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between survival curves were assessed with the log-rank test. All statistical tests were 2-sided. Data were analyzed using R, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Between January 1, 2007, and August 8, 2024, a total of 2282 lung transplants were performed at our center. Of these, 467 (20.5%) were performed using DCD donors, whereas the remaining 1815 (79.5%) were from DBD donors. Among DCD transplants, 257 (55%) underwent EVLP evaluation before implantation (EVLP-DCD group), whereas 210 (45%) were transplanted directly without EVLP (non-EVLP-DCD group). The proportion of lung transplants performed using DCD donors steadily increased from 6.2% in 2008 to 54.9% in 2025 (Figure 4, A). Among DCD offers evaluated by EVLP, the acceptance rate for transplantation was 61.3%. Overall, 55% of lung transplants performed using DCD lungs at our institution underwent EVLP evaluation before implantation. Notably, EVLP use among DCD lungs increased during the early adoption phase but slightly declined after 2020, even as overall DCD transplantation continued to increase (Figure 4, B and C). WLST-to-Flush Time and EVLP Use

When stratified by WLST-to-flush time, DCD donors with intervals exceeding 60 minutes demonstrated a 14.7% incidence of grade 3 PGD at 72 hours, which was comparable with those with shorter intervals (<60 minutes, 13.4%; P = .826) and irrespective of EVLP use (EVLP-DCD >60 minutes, 15.2% vs EVLP-DCD <60 mins, 13.2%; P = .695).

Among DCD donors with WLST-to-flush times greater than 60 minutes (n = 103, 22.1% of the cohort), 27.2% were transplanted directly without EVLP evaluation. Of the 467 DCD donors, 44 had WLST-to-flush times of 60 to 90 minutes, 24 had times of 90 to 120 minutes, and 35 had times exceeding 120 minutes. Within these strata, 17 grafts were transplanted directly without EVLP in the 60- to 90-minute group, 6 in the 90- to 120-minute group, and 5 in the >120-minute group.

The incidence of grade 3 PGD at 72 hours remained similar across WLST-to-flush time strata in both EVLP (P = .620) and non-EVLP grafts (P = .887; Figure E2, A). Likewise, the proportion of recipients extubated within 72 hours across WLST-to-flush time groups and EVLP use is shown in Figure E2, B. Median ICU LOS and hospital LOS were also comparable across WLST-to-flush time strata. Among non-EVLP grafts, median ICU LOS was 6, 6, 12.5, and 4 days (P = .459), and median hospital LOS was 24, 19, 29.5, and 26 days (P = .192) for WLST-to-flush times <60, 60-90, >90-120, and >120 minutes, respectively. In EVLP grafts, median ICU LOS was 3, 4, 6, and 5 days (P = .476), and median hospital LOS was 21, 20, 23.5, and 26 days (P = .347), respectively.

Discussion

In this study, we observed comparable posttransplant survival outcomes between DCD donor lungs that underwent EVLP assessment and those that proceeded directly to transplantation, with shorter ICU stay in the EVLP group. This stands in contrast to analyses of UNOS registry data,7,8,13,14 which have suggested that the combination of DCD donation and EVLP is associated with inferior survival. Differences in EVLP experience, technique, technology, and practice patterns across centers may partially explain these findings. EVLP performance metrics, technique used, and acceptance criteria are not captured in the UNOS database, making it unclear how grafts were selected for or evaluated during perfusion. Recent analyses of UNOS data from 41 US centers performing EVLP have demonstrated that outcomes after EVLP are strongly influenced by center experience and procedural volume, with significantly worse 1-year survival observed at low-volume EVLP centers; defining high-volume as more than 15 EVLP cases.15 In addition, UNOS analyses have shown that the use of static versus portable EVLP systems varies markedly by region, although no significant differences in outcomes were observed between these approaches.16 Together, these findings underscore the wide heterogeneity in EVLP protocols, experience, and case volumes across US centers compared with our program's standardized approach. At our center, with high EVLP experience, 38.7% of EVLP-assessed lungs were ultimately declined after perfusion, reflecting selection that may not be generalizable to programs with lower EVLP volume, different EVLP techniques, or different thresholds for use. This experience aligns with our institution's report of more than 1000 EVLP cases performed under a standardized protocol, underscoring the importance of volume and consistency in EVLP-based decision-making.17

Both our study and the UNOS analyses are retrospective and therefore susceptible to selection bias. In our high-volume EVLP program, EVLP use was substantial even among surgeons considered “conservative” adopters: within this subgroup, 42.7% of DCD offers were still placed on EVLP, far exceeding US use rates of approximately 15%.2,8 Indeed, although we attempted to identify objective predictors that could determine which DCD lungs could safely bypass EVLP, these efforts were unsuccessful. This limitation appears to stem from the consistently high reliance on EVLP across surgeons, resulting in insufficient contrast in selection behaviors to derive reliable predictors.

The counterintuitive finding that DCD lungs transplanted after EVLP demonstrated a greater prevalence of radiographic infiltrates on donor chest radiography at the time of offer (39.3% vs 14.2%; P < .001) compared with those ultimately declined reflects the limitations of static imaging as a selection tool. The assessment of radiographic infiltrates is inherently subjective, with interobserver variability among procurement surgeons likely contributing to inconsistency in how this variable is recorded. This subjectivity underscores the value of standardized physiological assessment through EVLP.

Interestingly, despite the general concern that WLST-to-flush time is important, there was no difference in WLST-to-flush time between rejected and used lungs after EVLP. Although DCD lungs put on EVLP generally had longer WLST-to-flush times than those directly transplanted, this more likely represents surgeon bias based on the historical concern of long WLST-to-flush times. Nevertheless, 26.5% of DCD lungs with WLST-to-flush times exceeding 60 minutes were transplanted directly without EVLP evaluation. Studies using data reported to the ISHLT registry have similarly shown that a warm ischemic time exceeding 60 minutes (defined as the interval from donor systolic blood pressure <50 mm Hg to cold pulmonary artery flush) did not influence 30-day or 1-year posttransplant survival.18 Collectively, these data suggest that WLST-to-flush time alone may not be a sufficient predictor of graft viability in controlled DCD donors and that physiologic assessment via EVLP provides a more meaningful indicator of posttransplant function.

Our findings must also be interpreted in the context of broader programmatic trends. After the adoption of EVLP, our center's DCD use increased markedly, allowing rapid and safe adoption of use of DCD donors, with DCD donors now representing more than 50% of our total transplant volume, a proportion that far exceeds national averages in both Canada and the United States. Notably, around 10% of DCD lungs transplanted at our center originated from US donor hospitals, highlighting the feasibility of cross-border DCD use and the robustness of our standardized EVLP protocol across different procurement environments.19 Thus, EVLP availability may have contributed to institutional confidence in DCD donor assessment and likely facilitated the absolute expansion of DCD transplantation at our center. As our confidence in DCD lung use grew, some surgeons were able to use EVLP more selectively. This suggests that EVLP functions as a risk-mitigation strategy, potentially lowering thresholds for donor evaluation and acceptance even in cases ultimately transplanted without EVLP. This phenomenon of indirect donor pool expansion has been increasingly described and may explain how availability of EVLP in a centre facilitates growth in transplantation volume without altering post-transplant outcomes.20,21

Temporal trends within our program further support this interpretation. As shown in Figure 4, B, EVLP use for DCD lungs was high during the early adoption phase but slightly declined after 2020, despite a continued increase in overall DCD transplantation. This pattern likely reflects 2 complementary developments. First, increasing experience with DCD over time may have led to greater confidence in directly transplanting DCD without EVLP evaluation. Second, before the implementation of 10 °C static preservation, EVLP was occasionally used for logistical purposes, including extending preservation time to accommodate operating room availability or recipient readiness. After the adoption of 10 °C preservation, which safely allows prolongation of ischemic times,11 this logistical indication largely disappeared. Importantly, these temporal changes in EVLP use were not associated with differences in posttransplant survival. Together, these findings suggest that EVLP use rate reflects maturation and refinement of practice, rather than reduced reliance on EVLP as a clinical tool. The institutional practice of surgeon-level discretion in EVLP use, rather than a uniform mandatory policy, reflects both the confidence established through high-volume experience and the recognition that real-time clinical judgment integrates information beyond fixed criteria.

The substantial gap in EVLP use between our center and US averages (around 15%) reflects not only differences in institutional EVLP infrastructure and volume but also differences in donor selection philosophy. At our center, EVLP is applied liberally to evaluate donors who might otherwise be declined, including those with marginal features such as radiographic infiltrates or prolonged WLST intervals. As with any single-center report, our findings are shaped by the specific infrastructure, expertise, and case volume of our institution, where EVLP has been practiced at scale for nearly 2 decades under a consistent protocol. The high EVLP use rates observed, even among our most selective surgeons, likely reflect institutional infrastructure that is not readily generalizable. Programs adopting EVLP at lower volumes or with less protocol consistency should not assume equivalent outcomes. Conversely, our data provide a reference standard for what outcomes are achievable with robust EVLP programs and underscore the importance of center volume and protocol standardization as determinants of success.

Conclusions

In this single-center experience, lung transplantation using DCD lungs evaluated on EVLP was associated with outcomes comparable with DCD lungs transplanted directly and with lungs from DBD donors. The liberal and standardized application of EVLP enabled safe use of DCD donors that have historically been regarded as greater risk compared with DBD, supporting a substantial expansion of DCD transplantation without compromising early outcomes or survival. Over time, increasing institutional experience and advances in preservation strategies allowed more selective use of EVLP, reinforcing its role as a risk-assessment and use tool (in a similar fashion as high-risk DBD) rather than a determinant of posttransplant outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of experience, protocol standardization, and complementary preservation strategies in optimizing DCD lung transplantation and expanding the donor pool.
