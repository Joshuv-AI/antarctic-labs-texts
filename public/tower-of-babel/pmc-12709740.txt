Determinants of access to maternal healthcare services among women in the Wolaita Zone, Southern Ethiopia: an unmatched case‒control study
Authors: Kassahun Tafesse Hidoto, Kassa Daka Gidebo, Mengistu Meskele Koyra, Maria Luisa Guillen Domínguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal mortality is a significant public health problem, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia. However, evidence on the determinants of access to maternal healthcare services is inadequate in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the determinants of access to maternal healthcare services among women in southern Ethiopia.MethodsWe conducted a community-based unmatched case‒control study between April 15 and May 15, 2023. A sample size of 360 (120 cases and 240 controls) was selected using a systematic random sampling technique. The collected data were entered into EpiData software and exported to SPSS software version 25. The profiles of the participants were explored using descriptive analysis. Binary logistic regressions and multivariate analysis were used.ResultsAccess to maternal healthcare services was greater among women who perceived a need for maternity healthcare (AOR = 7.96 (95% CI = 3.49–18.21)), who were able to seek maternity healthcare (AOR = 6.08 (95% CI = 3.12–15.68)), who were able to reach maternity healthcare (AOR = 4.46 (95% CI = 1.89–10.52)), who were able to pay for maternity healthcare (AOR = 3.41 (95% CI = 1.54–7.56)), and who perceived maternal healthcare services as approachable (AOR = 2.89 (95% CI = 1.32–6.37)) and had greater access to maternal healthcare services than their counterparts.ConclusionHealth sector administrators at the Woreda and Zonal levels better to work on maternal healthcare services advocacy, improving women’s economic capacity, and empowering them in decision making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04105-3.

Background

Maternal mortality is high in many developing countries, mainly due to low access to and poor utilization of maternal health services [1]. Maternal mortality was defined as a woman’s death during pregnancy or within six weeks after pregnancy caused by pregnancy or its associated or aggravated factors, irrespective of the time and site of pregnancy, but not from accidental or incidental deaths [2, 3]. The utilization of maternal healthcare services is low in Ethiopia. For instance, the 2019 Min-Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey (EDHS) reported 48% skilled birth attendants, 74% first antenatal care, 43% fourth antenatal care, and 34% postnatal care [4]. Maternal delays in accessing emergency obstetric care are the major contributing factors to maternal mortality in developing countries [5].

Currently, various strategies and targets are set to reduce maternal mortality. For instance, the coverage of delivery attendance by skilled health professionals should be increased to 90%, and the institutional maternal mortality rate should be reduced to less than 1% in 2020 [5]. A new United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) aims to decrease maternal mortality to 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030 globally [6]. The Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health (MoH) aims to reduce the MMR to 100 and 260 per 100,000 live births in 2025 and reduce it to 45 to 53 per 100,000 live births in 2035 [7].

According to Levesque et al. access to healthcare framework; access to healthcare constructed from five components, namely, perceiving the need for healthcare, seeking healthcare, reaching the needed healthcare, paying for healthcare, and finally engaging in the needed healthcare [8].Improving access is vital to achieving any program’s anticipated outcomes and hence unlocking successful implementation [9]. Thus, access to a healthcare service is essential in health services and health policy [8]. As the WHO describes, access to health care is physical access to health services, financial affordability, and acceptability of the service. In health policy, access functions with specific components include affordability, geographic accessibility, availability, and acceptability of healthcare services [10–12].

Broadly, factors determining access to healthcare can be categorized into service providers’ and receivers’ sides. The providers’ side factors include the accessibility of maternal healthcare services, the quality of community education, antenatal care, and service delivery [13]. On the receiver side, factors include long distances, poor transport facilities, hidden costs, inadequate distribution of facilities, and cultural, social, and economic factors [13]. For instance, a husband’s permission is required among sociocultural factors, and the mother-in-law decides whether women can utilize and access health services [14]. Moreover, service waiting time and direct payments are mixed supply-side and demand-side barriers [15].

In African rural communities, religious beliefs and self-perception of the efficacy of health services by women also determine the utilization of maternal healthcare services [16]. In Tanzania, a cross-sectional facility-based study revealed that women’s socioeconomic status and distance from health facilities were associated with the use of these services [17]. A cross-sectional survey from Kenya showed that 95% of women had to require their husband’s permission, whereas only 28% knew of the services available [18]. In Ghana, women can only use facility-based delivery services if permission is obtained from their husbands [19]. Poor utilization was also included due to a lack of acceptance and knowledge of maternal waiting homes (a maternal healthcare service), long distances, and culturally inappropriate care [20]. Similarly, other studies have reported factors such as sociocultural factors, history of birth complications, antenatal follow-up, knowledge, attitudes, distance, and financial factors [21, 22].

Overall, despite its inadequacy, the pieces of evidence reviewed about determinants of women’s access to maternal healthcare services are highly contextual and inconsistent. In addition, no study has been conducted to assess access to maternal healthcare services with Levesque’s model of access to healthcare. Thus, this study aimed to determine predictors of access to maternal healthcare services that could help health system workers understand and intervene in this issue.

Methods

Study design and period

We performed a community-based unmatched case‒control study between April 15 and May 15, 2023, in the Wolaita Zone, southern Ethiopia.

Study setting

We conducted the study in the Wolaita zone. This zone is one of the administrative zones found in the southern Ethiopia regional state in Ethiopia. It is located 380 km southwest of the capital city, Addis Ababa. The total population of the Wolaita Zone is 2,114,386 based on the projection from a census conducted in 2013 [23]. There are sixteen Woredas and seven town administrations in the zone, which are further subdivided into ‘kebeles’, which is the last administrative structure in the country. The zone has 9 hospitals, 67 health centers, 357 health posts and 22 woreda health offices. There are 3845 health professionals and 2540 supportive staff in the zone.

The health posts are at the kebele level, with 2 in each. A health post consists of two female health extension workers entitled to provide essential health services, including maternal and child healthcare services and house-to-house visits (reported by the zonal health department).

Source and study population

The source population was all women in Wolaita zone woredas who gave birth between April 15, 2022 and April 14, 2023. The study population was all the selected women in Wolaita zone woredas who gave birth between April 15, 2022 and April 14, 2023.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Access to maternal healthcare services was defined as women’s (who on need) utilization of Anternatal care(ANC), delivery care, and postnatal care services from professional healthcare providers in health facilities [24]. All the selected women who gave birth between April 15, 2022 and April 14, 2023 were included. Women who accessed maternal healthcare services for the last gave birth were considered as ‘cases’ in the study and women who did not access for maternal healthcare services(complete antenatal, delivery and postnatal cares) for the last birth given were considered ‘controls’ in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Women who were severely sick (on need intensive medical care), unable to communicate, and/or resided less than six months in the study area during the study period were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

We calculated the sample size using Epi-info7 employing two population proportion formula for unmatched case control study. The assumptions used were 95% confidence level and 80% power. Main factors considered were maternal education [25], Antenatal Care (ANC), knowledge of maternal healthcare service [26], and distance to the nearest health facility. Among these factors maternal education (secondary education) gave the largest sample size. Therefore, we took 16.24% [25], level of exposure (secondary education) in the control group, 2:1 ratio of controls to cases, 2.7 odds ratio, design effect of 1.5, and 10% nonresponse rate the total sample size was 360(120 cases and 240 controls) women who gave birth between April 15, 2022 and April 14, 2023. The assumption for considering 10% nonresponse rate was women may not give consent for time constraint, partner influence, bad consequence of the birth given, and/or personal desire to not disclose her information.

Sampling techniques

To maintain the randomization of study participants and ensure that the study was rigorous at a reasonable level, we employed a multistage sampling technique. The survey was based on the administrative structure set in the country as described in the study setting section above. Among a total of sixteen woredas in the Wolaita Zone, we selected five woredas using a simple random sampling technique. Then, we selected five Kebeles from each of the selected woredas again using a simple random sampling technique, which yielded a total of twenty-five Kebeles to be included in our study. The first household was selected using a simple random sampling technique on the direction set using a coin (representing the left or right direction to begin) for each kebele from administrative office compound as a center location.

The authors selected households within the selected Kebeles employing a systematic random sampling technique. The number of study participants from each Kebeles was based on the proportional distribution calculated using the total sample size of participants and the total number of eligible participants enumerated in the preliminary survey conducted (Fig. 1) (Supplementary file 1 - Fig. 1). The preliminary data collected were used as a sampling frame to measure the interval between households from each Kebeles. Finally, selected households where more than one eligible women, we selected a woman using a simple random sampling technique.

The study participants were identified from the preliminary census conducted at selected study areas. The census was conducted using door-to-door survey in the selected kebeles. In addition, we reviewed health extension workers’ document (registration book) at health posts in the selected kebeles. As described in the study setting above, the health posts are health facilities at a grass-root level to provide basic health services at the community level in Ethiopia. There are two female health extension workers at a given health post responsible to provide basic health services at health post and conduct home-to-home visits for health information dissemination and follow-up.

Data collection tool and techniques

The investigators used a structured and pretested questionnaire developed for from the literature and adapted to the local context [8, 27–30]. The data were collected using a face-to-face interview technique with trained data collectors. We used Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (0.81) as the cutoff point to check the reliability of the study tool. The tool was reviewed by signor researchers in maternal and reproductive health specialty. The tool was prepared in English and then was translated back to the local language ‘Wolaitegna’, then translated back to English by another independent translator to check and its consistency. We prepared the data collection tool with sociodemographic factors, individual factors, women side factors of access and health system side factors of access to maternal health care services. The study participants (cases and controls) who met the inclusion criteria were interviewed at private and convenient place selected at the participant’s home or compound at day time using a face-to-face interviewer administered questionnaire to collect the data.

Data quality control

To maintain the data quality, we selected experienced data collectors and supervisors. We employed training for 2 days about the data collection tool, procedure, and ethical issues for the study participants. We pretested the tool among 5% (6 women) of the cases and 5% (12 women) of the controls in the adjacent woreda of the study area. Supervisors and investigators employed close supervision during the data collection period. To control double interview of a given household, we used a code mark leveled for households already interviewed.

Data processing and analysis

The data were processed by checking for completeness, coding, and entry into Epi Info software version 7. Then, we exported the data into SPSS version 25 software for cleaning, editing, checking for missing values, and analysis. The bivariate analysis was performed between the dependent and independent variables to screen for additional variables to include in the multivariate binary logistic regression model with level of significance, a p value < 0.2 to indicate eligibility. Variables found suitable for dependent variables were included in multivariate binary logistic regression model analysis to identify significant independent predictors of access to maternal healthcare services, with a p value < 0.05. The final model identified was checked for goodness of fit using the Hosmer–Lemehshow test, and the results were significant.

Measurements

The respondents’ knowledge of maternal healthcare and attitudes toward it were also measured with 10-point and 7-point score questions, respectively. The respondent women who had a total score greater than or equal to the mean value were identified. We also measured the predictor variables in the category of women’s side and health system side predictors with different item questions and scores. These included approachability, acceptability, availability, affordability and appropriateness of maternal healthcare service predictors in the category of health system side. On the other hand, we also measured the women’s-side predictors of access to maternal healthcare services, such as the ability to perceive need, seek, reach, pay and engage in maternal healthcare services; each of these variables had 5 score questions, and a value greater than or equal to the mean value was categorized as “yes”, while a value less than the mean value was categorized as “no”. The mean values were used because the data were normally distributed. These predictors of access to maternal healthcare services were adapted from Lévesque’s conceptual framework of access to healthcare services [8].

Results

Most participants, in both the case 63 (52.5%) and control112 (46.7%) groups were aged 25–30 years. The mean age of the overall participants was 28.80 years, with a standard deviation (SD) of 4.04; the mean age for cases was 27.52 years, with a standard deviation of 3.49; and for the controls, it was 29.44 years, with a SD of 4.15. The difference between the cases and controls was statistically significant (x2 square = 10.83, and p value = 0.004) according to the x2 test. We computed the sociodemographic variables to compare participants among cases and controls and revealed that there was a statistically significant difference between cases and controls for most of the variables studied. For instance, we considered educational status (x2 square = 47.47, p value = 0.000) and occupational status (x2 square = 47.47, df = 3 and p value = 0.000). Regarding their occupational status, of the total participants, 50 (41.7%) cases and 147 (61.3%) controls were housewives (Table 1).

Discussion

This study was conducted among recently born women in a rural community based on an unmatched case‒control approach to identify determinants of access to maternal healthcare services. We used the participants who gave birth in the last year to control for recall bias. Our study identified very important determinants of access to maternal healthcare services that are linked with demand-side factors, such as the perceived need for maternal healthcare; the perceived ability to seek, reach, pay and engage for services; and supply-side factors, such as perceived maternal healthcare service, which was approachable by care providers.

Among the women-side factors, women’s perceptions of being able to engage in maternal healthcare services were identified as the most important determinant of access to maternal healthcare services among women. This finding is consistent with previous reports and studies conducted in different parts of the world about access to healthcare services and associated factors [8, 31–34]. This is supported by the fact that self-confidence is key to practicing a need to be identified. This might be because most women feel that obtaining the services needed for maternal healthcare is not easy because of various factors. Women living in developing countries, especially in rural areas, might be affected by a lack of confidence and poor attitudes about the service provided by a health facility; by having few interpersonal relationships with care providers; by feeling shy and worried about their privacy in new environments; and by having more people limit confidence in engaging in maternal healthcare services. Furthermore, this can worsen with conditions such as trust in traditional care providers, negative social influence, lack of physical access to health facilities and financial constraints to incur related costs.

The other women-side factor determining access to maternal healthcare services was women’s perception of the ability to reach a healthcare facility for maternal healthcare services. Those women who can perceive that they are able to access a health facility for care are more likely to access maternal healthcare services than women who are not perceived as able to access such services. This finding is consistent with previous related studies [8, 13, 16, 28, 34–43]. The possible reasons for this are that women who feel that there is a road infrastructure and health facility are not far apart, are confident about obtaining transportation, and are able to incur a greater chance of being planned and arriving on time at health facilities for needed maternal healthcare services. In contrast, women who feel that the health facility is not easy to reach are prepared for other possible options to give birth at home or attend traditional birth attendants.

The study also revealed the perception of the ability to seek care for healthcare from facilities for maternal healthcare services among women as another women-side factor determining access to maternal healthcare services. Those women who are perceived as able to seek healthcare from a health facility for healthcare need are more likely to access maternal healthcare services than women who are not perceived as able to seek such services. The findings of other studies confirm our results [8, 34, 44]. The possible reasons for this are that women who feel that there is road infrastructure, that the health facility is not far from the facility and that they are confident about getting to transportation and able to incur are more likely to be planned and to arrive on time at the health facility for the needed maternal healthcare services. In contrast, women who feel that the health facility is not easy to access are prepared for other possible options to give birth at home or attend traditional birth attendants and are advised not to take care of antenatal or postnatal health care services.

Regarding other factors linked to access to maternal healthcare services, we identified no significant association between cases and controls in the multivariable model; these factors included demographic, social, economic, previous history of sexual and reproductive health, or involvement in financial and supporting interventions. In contrast, most of these factors were found to be associated with earlier studies conducted locally and globally [1, 8, 10, 12, 13, 24, 27, 34, 45–49]. The possible reasons for these differences might be differences in the study area, study period, study population, sample size, and socioeconomic factors between the previous studies and our study.

Strength and limitations

Although the study revealed important determinants of access to maternal healthcare services, it has several limitations. The case‒control study applied in this study cannot establish temporal relationships between cause and effect; thus, it is difficult to determine causal associations. In addition, the lack of literature on access to maternal healthcare with Levesques’ framework was limited to adequately discussing this study’s findings. The study might also be affected by selection bias for cases and controls since we used unmatched case and controls, and since it was based on self-reported responses of participants, social desire influence might also affect the results. Our study was based on home based, homeless women may be excluded that might affect the study findings.

Conclusions

The main determinants of access to maternal healthcare among women are perception of being able to engage in maternal healthcare, perception of seeking the services needed, perception of being able to pay, perception of approachability of the healthcare, perception of ability to reach, and distance from the health facility.

The respective health sector administrators at the Woreda and Zonal levels should work on maternal healthcare service advocacy at the community level and improving women’s economic capacity, empowering them in social decision making. Finally, it is recommended conducting further advanced design studies to generate causal effect evidence on the problem.
