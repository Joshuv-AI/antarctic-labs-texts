Atomic-scale revelation of in situ reverse regulation from particles to clusters in the Ni/La-CeOx catalyst
Authors: Xuan Meng, Lu-Yao Zhang, Yi-Shuang Xu, Kai Xu, Wei-Wei Wang, He-Xu Ye, Jin-Cheng Liu, Feng Yang, Chun-Jiang Jia, Chun-Hua Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubnanometer metal cluster catalysts have garnered significant attention due to their multi-site synergistic catalytic capabilities. However, constrained by classical sintering theory, metal clusters are prone to sintering and growth, which makes it challenging for them to stably exist at high temperatures. Prevailing strategies predominantly focus on the synthesis pathways from atoms to clusters, while overlooking the bidirectional regulation potential of metal particle dynamic reconstruction. This study transcends conventional paradigms by proposing a reverse regulation strategy from particles to clusters. The disadvantage of high temperature is strategically converted into driving forces for particle dispersion through the rational design of catalytic environments and metal-support interactions. Through a comprehensive combination of in situ characterization techniques and theoretical calculations, the interfacial atomic migration pathways and energy evolution during dynamic dispersion are systematically investigated. Furthermore, the formation process and underlying mechanism of stable Ni clusters are elucidated. The optimized 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst exhibits a remarkably high H2 production rate (500 °C, 279.2 mmolH2·gNi−1·min−1) and exceptional thermal stability. This work not only provides a methodology for developing thermally stable cluster catalysts but also fundamentally reveals the mechanism of reverse sintering from the perspective of atomic migration kinetics and thermodynamic steady-state reconstruction.Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Catalyst synthesis, Chemical bonding

Introduction

The structural evolution of active metal is a critical factor influencing the catalytic performance in heterogeneous catalysis1,2. Compared with single-atom catalysts and traditional nanoparticles3,4, subnanometer metal cluster (1−2 nm) materials are preferred due to their capacity for multi-site collaborative catalysis and efficient utilization of support interface effects5,6. However, constrained by classical sintering thermodynamics7, current research predominantly focuses on the conventional synthesis pathway from atoms to clusters8. The catalyst structure is stabilized in a thermodynamically metastable state through the construction of strong coordination or rigid confinement environments9,10. While this passive defense strategy can stabilize clusters under mild conditions8, it fails to fundamentally overcome energy barriers. Consequently, under high-temperature conditions, such clusters inevitably aggregate into larger metal particles11. Notably, high-temperature conditions can also generate abundant reactive oxygen species on the surface of oxide support through the enhanced lattice oxygen migration efficiency and surface atomic migration efficiency12,13. Such highly reactive surfaces of oxide support can strongly interact with the metal phase14, which may enable reverse transformation from particles to clusters. However, current studies on metal particle dispersion are largely confined to specific supports such as Mo2N15,16. The systematic investigations into the reverse dispersion kinetics of metal particles and the migration pathways of interfacial atomic remains insufficient, which directly hinders the rational design of stable cluster catalysts for high-temperature applications.

Utilizing specific catalytic reaction conditions to in situ modulate metal-support interaction (MSI) represents a feasible strategy for achieving the transformation from particles to clusters. As an effective method for generating high-purity COx-free hydrogen, the ammonia decomposition reaction requires a high temperature (> 550 °C) to achieve complete conversion17,18, which precisely facilitates efficient oxygen migration. The strongly reducing atmosphere can also induce the enrichment of oxygen vacancies in reducible oxide supports19. Thus, ammonia decomposition is an ideal reaction for investigating the reverse dispersion of particles. Furthermore, the selection of supports with highly reactive surfaces and excellent tunability is conducive to constructing a strong MSI that surpasses the inherent lattice forces of metal particles20. The exceptional oxygen storage capacity, along with the ability to modulate interactions with active metals by tuning the concentration of oxygen vacancies, renders CeO2 a preferred material21,22. However, the morphology and structure of CeO2 are susceptible to sintering and collapse at high temperatures, which reduces oxygen migration capacity and compromises the anchoring function23. Studies have demonstrated that the incorporation of La can induce lattice distortion in CeO2 support, and consequently enhance its high-temperature stability through the generated surface stress24. Additionally, electron transfer between the metal and the support can further enhance the interaction25. The catalytic environment and the strong MSI effect collectively create conditions for reverse regulation from particles to clusters. Analyzing the atomic-scale reconstruction mechanism and energy evolution law in this dynamic dispersion process is not only expected to establish an effective strategy for constructing high-temperature stable clusters, but also instrumental in advancing the transition of heterogeneous catalysis from static active site construction to dynamic adaptive regulation.

Herein, Ni-based catalysts supported on La-doped CeO2 (10Ni/30La-CeO2, where nLa: nCe = 3: 7) and undoped CeO2 (10Ni/CeO2) were synthesized respectively to catalyze the ammonia decomposition reaction. By means of high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and in situ environmental transmission electron microscopy (ETEM), the structural evolution process of Ni particles transforming into Ni clusters in situ under a high-temperature and reducing atmosphere was clearly elucidated. Combining experimental investigations with theoretical calculations, it was indicated that the in situ formation of stable Ni clusters can be attributed to multiple factors. The surface stress engendered by La doping effectively prevented the collapse of the CeO2 structure at high temperatures. Subsequently, the further enhanced MSI effect and efficient oxygen migration enabled the retention of a greater number of reactive O species on the surface of the support. Ultimately, the formation of strong Ni-O-La and Ni-O-Ce bonding interactions induced the dispersion of Ni particles to achieve a thermodynamic steady state. The successful construction of a stable Ni cluster catalyst has enabled a H2 production rate (500 °C, 279.2 mmolH2·gNi−1·min−1) and exceptional thermal stability. The reverse regulation strategy of inducing particle redispersion successfully transforms the disadvantage of high temperature into a driving force for the transition from particles to clusters. Undoubtedly, this work offers an innovative solution to overcoming the long-standing challenge of high-temperature sintering in catalysts and provides novel insights for the development of high-temperature stable cluster catalysts.

Results

Dynamic structural evolution from particles to clusters. CeO2 nanorods doped with La were prepared via the hydrothermal method. TEM images showed that both CeO2 and 30La-CeO2 exhibited regular nanorod morphologies, with La doping exerting minimal impact on the structural characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 1). Then, Ni was loaded onto the CeO2 and 30La-CeO2 supports utilizing the deposition-precipitation method. To investigate the structural changes of the catalyst, a series of pretreatments were performed. The catalyst, subjected to 12 h of gradient heating treatment with NH3 (for the specific treatment procedure, see Supplementary Fig. 1), was designated as the used catalyst. No significant aggregation was observed in the used 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 2), which suggested that it possessed favorable thermal stability.

The structural characteristics of the catalyst and the state transformations of Ni species under various pretreatment conditions were systematically analyzed by high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and HAADF-STEM. The lattice fringe of the rod-like support was 0.31 nm, which indicated that La doping did not alter the crystal structure of the CeO2 matrix. The corresponding energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping confirmed localized Ni enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 3). No lattice fringes corresponding to La species were observed in the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst, indicating their high dispersion. It was noteworthy that the Ni species on the surface of the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst underwent significant changes at different treatment stages. Firstly, HAADF-STEM analysis revealed a large number of particles with sizes of 5−10 nm on the surface of the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst (Fig. 1a), which was treated with NH3 at 500 °C for 0.5 h. Further, only a limited number of Ni particles along with a substantial amount of Ni clusters were observed after being treated at 600 °C for 1 h (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 4). On this basis, the dispersion of Ni species was markedly improved when the treatment time was extended to 120 h. The metallic Ni particles were no longer detectable on the surface of the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst. A significant number of stable Ni clusters were observed, with an average size of 1−2 nm (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 5). The EDX results (Supplementary Fig. 6), combined with ADF intensity line-profile analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7) obtained from the 30La-CeO2 nanorod edge regions, confirmed the presence of Ni clusters on the surface. In contrast, after being subjected to the same treatments, the structure of the 10Ni/CeO2 catalyst collapsed, and a large number of metallic Ni particles with sizes of 5−10 nm precipitated (Supplementary Fig. 8 and 9). The identification of three distinct states of Ni species, captured through the prolonged treatment time, marked the beginning of in-depth research into reverse dispersion from particles to clusters.

In situ dispersion process of Ni particles. In order to directly observe the formation process of these Ni clusters, in situ aberration-corrected ETEM was employed to characterize the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst26. The nanoparticles gradually contracted when the temperature increased from room temperature to 450 °C under a H2 atmosphere (Fig. 2a, b). Then, the particles completely dispersed upon further increasing the temperature to 600 °C, and the highly dispersed state was retained even after returning to ambient temperature (Fig. 2c, d). Additional in situ ETEM images collected from multiple distinct regions under identical treatment conditions exhibited the same evolution trend of particles to clusters (Supplementary Fig. 10). This finding demonstrated direct evidence for the transition from particles to clusters. However, the particles on the surface of 10Ni/CeO2 catalyst exhibited minimal changes during continuous heating (Supplementary Fig. 11). The subsequent offline EDX elemental analysis of the 10Ni/CeO2 catalyst confirmed that the undispersed particles corresponded to reduced Ni nanoparticles, exhibiting an approximate size of 10 nm (Supplementary Fig. 12). The markedly different evolution of 10Ni/CeO2 and 10Ni/30La-CeO2 under identical conditions clearly demonstrates that electron beam irradiation is not the primary cause of particle dispersion; rather, the reverse sintering is an intrinsic property of the catalyst.

In addition, to elucidate the phase transition and dispersion behavior of Ni species, in situ STEM experiments were conducted under continuous heating conditions at 450 °C and 600 °C, respectively. The 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst initially underwent reduction at 450 °C (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 13), with NiO transforming into metallic Ni, as evidenced by crystal structure analysis (Fig. 2g, h). Upon further heating, both NiO and Ni nanoparticles progressively dispersed, ultimately forming fully dispersed Ni clusters at 600 °C (Fig. 2f). Nevertheless, no dispersion of Ni particles was observed on the surface of the 10Ni/CeO2 catalyst at 600 °C (Supplementary Fig. 14). This distinct contrast powerfully underscores that extended treatment time and elevated temperature alone are not the primary drivers of dispersion. Instead, it is La doping that enables this phenomenon, a conclusion further corroborated by subsequent structural characterizations.

Structural determination of the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to investigate the structure of La-doped CeO2. The analysis revealed only the characteristic diffraction peaks of CeO2, which exhibited a notable downshift (0.54°) upon La doping, indicating the lattice expansion (Fig. 3a). The downshift in the diffraction peak, together with the uniform elemental distribution and the absence of any diffraction peaks or lattice fringes corresponding to other La species, collectively confirmed the complete incorporation of La3+ into the CeO2 lattice. Fourier Transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was utilized to further validate the existence form of the Ni species. Three distinct CO adsorption bands were observed on the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst surface (Fig. 3b). The peaks at 2170 cm−1 and 2165 cm−1 were assigned to CO adsorbed on Ce3+ ions27. The red peaks corresponded to CO bound to Niδ+ species28, while the blue peaks were characteristic of CO adsorbed on metallic Ni0. Quantitative analysis revealed that the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst exhibited a significantly higher proportion of Niδ+ species (51.1%) compared to the 10Ni/CeO2 reference (30.6%), demonstrating the promotional effect of La doping on the stabilization of oxidized Ni sites. The electronic states of Ni species were further investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). While only Ni2+ species were detected in all the as-prepared catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 15), quasi in situ XPS analysis of the used catalysts revealed the coexistence of Niδ+ and Ni0 species (Fig. 3c), indicating partial reduction of NiO during reaction. Notably, the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst exhibited a significantly higher proportion of Niδ+ species (54%) compared to its 10Ni/CeO2 counterpart (28%), a nearly two-fold increase that aligned well with the FTIR results (Fig. 3d). X-ray absorption structure (XAS) analysis further demonstrated that Ni species in the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalysts maintained a stronger NiO-like character across the as-prepared, used, and prolonged-treated states (Fig. 3e, f, Supplementary Fig. 16). It was noteworthy that infrared spectra recorded at −130 °C revealed a continuous increase in the content of Niδ+ species with prolonged treatment time (Fig. 3g, h). This provided direct evidence for the dispersion process of Ni particles.

Investigation of the kinetic mechanism for reverse dispersion from particles to clusters. In situ XRD demonstrated that undoped CeO2 showed progressively sharper diffraction peaks with increasing temperature, indicating the thermal sintering (Fig. 4a), while 30La-CeO2 maintained relatively constant peak intensities and widths (Fig. 4b). Scherrer equation calculations of crystallite size evolution during heating process quantitatively confirmed that La doping effectively inhibited CeO2 particle growth (Fig. 4c). This stabilization effect was further corroborated by BET surface area measurements (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 17). 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst consistently exhibited higher SBET values than 10Ni/CeO2 in both the as-prepared and the used catalysts, demonstrating that doping La was conducive to enhancing structural stability. These findings collectively suggested that doping La into the CeO2 lattice generated surface stress29, which served to suppress CeO2 sintering at high temperatures, thereby providing a structural basis for the dispersion of Ni particles.

In addition to structural factors, the strong MSI effect also played a pivotal role in the system. The temperature-programmed reduction by H2 (H2-TPR) analysis revealed distinct reduction behaviors: 30La-CeO2 exhibited reduction peaks at significantly lower temperatures with greater intensity compared to undoped CeO2 (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 18), demonstrating that La doping substantially enhanced the reducibility of CeO2. This improved reducibility directly correlated with enhanced surface oxygen mobility, which in turn promoted the generation of oxygen vacancies (OVs), a crucial factor governing catalytic performance. Furthermore, the low-temperature reduction peaks of 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst shifted to higher temperatures, indicating a stronger interaction between Ni species and the 30La-CeO2 support. This conclusion was further supported by XRD analysis, which revealed significantly greater peak shifting in 10Ni/30La-CeO2 compared to 10Ni-CeO2, indicating more substantial lattice distortion in the La-doped material (Supplementary Fig. 19). In addition, 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst exhibited a lower binding energy for Ni0 (Fig. 3c), indicating its electron-rich nature. La 3d5/2 shifted towards higher binding energy as evidenced by quasi in situ XPS (Supplementary Fig. 20), indicating decreased charge density of the La species. These results strongly suggested that electrons were transferred from the support to the Ni species. The intensified interactions created optimal conditions for the reverse dispersion process, facilitating the transformation of Ni particles into highly dispersed clusters.

Furthermore, the stable preservation of the 30La-CeO2 structure at high temperatures, coupled with efficient oxygen migration, promoted the formation of surface reactive oxygen species (Oads). These species played a crucial role in triggering the reverse sintering phenomenon (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 21). Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations revealed that the reactive Oads coordinated with either Ce or La atoms on the catalyst surface could establish strong MSI with Ni species, consequently promoting the redispersion of Ni nanoparticles (Fig. 4f). DFT calculations revealed distinct adsorption behaviors depending on the coordination environment of Ni. When Ni atoms formed direct bonds with Ce (Eads = 1.97 eV) or La (Eads = 1.71 eV) atoms in the support, the positive adsorption energies indicated thermodynamic instability. In contrast, Ni coordination with O atoms adjacent to Ce sites exhibited a lower adsorption energy (Eads = −0.26 eV), favoring stable configurations. Notably, undoped CeO2 demonstrated limited thermal stability, as its structure tended to collapse at elevated temperatures. This structural degradation reduced oxygen availability and weakened Ni-O-Ce interactions. Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed that these effects led to a multi-layer Ni distribution on 10Ni/CeO2. On the contrary, 10Ni/30La-CeO2 promoted Ni dispersion. This remarkable difference highlighted the crucial role of La doping in stabilizing the support structure and modulating MSI effects. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that when Ni atoms on the catalyst surface were directly bonded to O atoms in proximity to La, the negative adsorption energy (−0.75 eV) led to the formation of a stable adsorption structure. The exceptional thermal stability of the 30La-CeO2 support at high temperatures facilitated the formation of high-density Ni-O-La/Ce covalent bonding sites. This strong interfacial interaction induced in situ dispersion of Ni particles into clusters. The covalent bonding strength, which exceeded the intrinsic lattice cohesion energy of metallic Ni particles, served as the fundamental driving force for this dispersion phenomenon. Further machine learning force field based molecular dynamics fielded visually demonstrate that Ni particles indeed dispersed under the influence of this strong bonding (Fig. 4f). In addition, it was also found that hydrogen adsorption weakened the interaction between Ni atoms in the clusters and promoted Ni detachment, thereby facilitating the transformation of larger Ni particles into smaller clusters or dispersed species during H2 pretreatment (Supplementary Fig. 22). Through this synergistic mechanism involving multiple factors, we successfully realized the reverse size regulation from aggregated particles to well-dispersed clusters.

Catalytic performance and the catalytic role of La promoter. The successful construction of stabilized Ni cluster catalysts significantly enhanced the atomic utilization efficiency of active metals, leading to a remarkable improvement in catalytic performance. 10Ni/30La-CeO2 with an actual Ni loading of only 7.3% (determined by ICP-MS, Table 1) exhibited a superior NH3 conversion of 61% at 500 °C (GHSV = 30,000 mL·gcat−1h−1, Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 23). The in situ formation process of clusters also triggered a progressive enhancement in initial catalytic activity (Supplementary Fig. 24). Notably, the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst demonstrated exceptional hydrogen production performance, achieving a H2 formation rate of 279.2 mmolH2·gNi−1·min−1 at 500 °C, which was among the highest values for reported non-noble metal catalysts in ammonia decomposition18,30–41 (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst exhibited remarkably superior cycling stability (Supplementary Fig. 25) and high-temperature stability (Fig. 5c), maintaining its performance consistently throughout the stability evaluation over 240 h. HAADF-STEM images showed that its morphology had not yet collapsed, and EDX mapping images confirmed that Ni remained highly dispersed (Supplementary Fig. 26). These results demonstrated that the incorporation of La into CeO2 not only enhanced catalytic performance but also contributed to maintaining its excellent thermal stability. In addition, the apparent activation energy Ea was evaluated within the kinetic range (Supplementary Fig. 27). The Ea of 10Ni/30La-CeO2 (120.3 kJ·mol−1) was comparable to that of 10Ni/CeO2 (125.9 kJ·mol−1), suggesting that doping La did not change the reaction pathway. While the pre-exponential factor A42 increased with La doping, suggesting that the enhanced activity arose from a greater number of active sites. Moreover, the strategy of rare earth doping was also applied to other transition metals (Co and Fe, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 28). The significant enhancement in catalytic performance conclusively demonstrated the efficacy of the La-doping strategy for optimizing transition metal-based catalytic systems.

The incorporation of La can not only modulate the structure of CeO2 and the state of Ni species, but also exert a significant influence on the catalytic mechanism. Ex situ Raman spectroscopy revealed that doping La into CeO2 induced more pronounced lattice distortion, thereby facilitating the formation of abundant surface OVs (Supplementary Fig. 29). A similar conclusion can also be inferred from the Ce 3d XPS spectra (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 30). The content of Ce3+ in 10Ni/30La-CeO2 (43%) was notably higher than that in 10Ni/CeO2 (31%), indicating a significantly greater concentration of OVs. The formation of abundant OVs and the increase in Ce3+ also conversely corroborated the successful doping of La. The role of increased OVs during the ammonia decomposition reaction was investigated through in situ Raman spectroscopy with H2 and NH3 switching. The Raman peaks at 417 cm−1 and 588 cm−1 corresponded to the symmetric vibration mode (F2g) and the defect-induced tensile vibration mode (D) of the CeO2 fluorite cubic structure, respectively. The relative strength ratio of peak D to peak F2g served as an indicator of the oxygen vacancy concentration within the catalyst. Upon switching the test atmosphere from 5% H2/Ar to NH3 at 500 °C, the value of ID/IF2g decreased from 3.6 to 2.7 (Fig. 5e), which indicated that NH3 occupied the surface OVs. The NH3 adsorption capacity of the catalyst was more intuitively assessed through NH3-temperature programmed desorption (NH3-TPD) (Supplementary Fig. 31). Notably, 10Ni/30La-CeO2 demonstrated superior NH3 adsorption capacity, thereby promoting subsequent NH3 activation and dissociation. In addition, the H2 reaction order of 10Ni/30La-CeO2 (‒0.28) was significantly lower in absolute value compared to that of 10Ni/CeO2 (‒0.41), suggesting that La doping effectively alleviated hydrogen poisoning (Supplementary Fig. 32). Similarly, 10Ni/30La-CeO2 consistently displayed a higher retention of surface OH (OOH) species (Fig. 4e), indicating that the generated H species might spill over to the catalyst surface instead of blocking the active sites, thereby enhancing the catalytic activity. Moreover, the electrophilic nature of the catalysts was systematically investigated using CO2-temperature programmed desorption (CO2-TPD) (Fig. 5f). Obviously, the alkalinity of the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst was enhanced, with an increased number of medium-strong alkaline sites on its surface. The enhancement of alkalinity facilitated the transfer of electrons to Ni species, which optimized the position of the d-band center and adjusted the binding energy between the active sites and N atoms, thus promoting the associative desorption of N243. It was important to note that the impact of alkalinity and electron transfer on the electron cloud density did not negate the influence of size on the state of active metals.

To further understand the mechanism of ammonia decomposition, DFT calculations were performed. The enhanced electrophilicity and alkalinity of the La-doped catalyst significantly influenced the adsorption and activation of NH3. The calculated NH3 adsorption energy was ‒0.57 eV on Ni3O/CeO2−x (111) and ‒0.66 eV on Ni3O/LaCeO2−x (111), indicating stronger NH3 binding strength on the La-doped surface (Supplementary Fig. 33–35). Moreover, the activation barriers for N–H bond cleavage at the Ni-slab interface were significantly reduced in the La-doped system. Specifically, the barriers for the three N–H scissions were 1.17, 1.20, and 0.81 eV on Ni3O/LaCeO2−x(111), compared to 1.54, 1.30, and 0.65 eV on the undoped Ni3O/CeO2−x(111). These results confirmed that La doping lowered the activation energy barrier for the elementary step of N–H bond cleavage, thus promoting more efficient NH3 decomposition, which was corroborated by NH3/D2-TPSR (Supplementary Fig. 36). Steady-state turnover frequencies (TOFs), obtained from the calculated steady-state surface coverages, also showed that the onset temperature for NH3 decomposition on LaCeO2−x is about 650 K, which is much lower than that on CeO2−x (Supplementary Fig. 37). Although the widely accepted ammonia decomposition mechanism follows a stepwise dehydrogenation pathway, DFT calculations suggested that N–N coupling between NHx intermediates may occur prior to complete dehydrogenation of NH3 (Supplementary Fig. 38). For example, the energy barrier for the pathway *NH + *NH2 → *NHNH2 is only 0.16 eV. While the subsequent dehydrogenation steps have higher barriers than those of the traditional stepwise dehydrogenation pathway, they remain kinetically accessible under the reaction conditions. This indicates that N–N coupling prior to complete dehydrogenation is kinetically feasible. Although this is not the focus of our study and the observed N–N coupling serves only as a potential addition to the reaction network rather than the main pathway established here, this finding suggests that the ammonia decomposition mechanism may be more complex than a simple stepwise dehydrogenation model. Future studies on the competition between early coupling and stepwise dehydrogenation will help achieve a more comprehensive understanding of this reaction.

Discussion

In summary, this work transcended the conventional technical pathway and successfully realized reverse regulation from particles to clusters via the rational design of the catalytic environments and the MSI effect. A combination of in situ characterization techniques and theoretical calculations elucidated the formation process and underlying mechanism of thermodynamically stable Ni clusters. The incorporation of La into the CeO2 lattice induced surface stress, which effectively inhibited the structural collapse of the support at high temperatures. In addition, the further strengthened MSI effect, coupled with the efficient migration of oxygen species at elevated temperatures, facilitated the retention of more reactive oxygen species on the surface of the support. Finally, the formation of high-density covalent bonding sites of Ni-O-La/Ce induced the in situ dispersion of Ni particles into Ni clusters. The stable Ni cluster catalyst exhibited a high H2 production rate (500 °C, 279.2 mmolH2·gNi−1·min−1) and exceptional thermal stability. It represents the successful implementation of the reverse regulation strategy from particles to clusters in ammonia decomposition, with pioneering significance, which is expected to promote the paradigm shift in heterogeneous catalysis from static active site construction to dynamic adaptive regulation.

Methods

Chemical materials. All of the chemicals used were analytical grade and without any further purification

Preparation of supports. The 30La-CeO2 nanorods were synthesized by the hydrothermal method. Typically, 14.4 g NaOH was dissolved in 40 mL of deionized water in a 100 mL Teflon bottle. Subsequently, a total of 3 mmol of La(NO3)3·9H2O and Ce(NO3)3·3H2O (the molar ratio of La to Ce is 3: 7) were dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water, and then the latter solution was added dropwise to the former solution under vigorous stirring at room temperature. After 15 min, the Teflon bottle was sealed in a stainless autoclave tightly and heated at 100 °C for 24 h in the oven. The resulting precipitates were collected via centrifugation and subsequently washed with deionized water and absolute ethanol. The precipitates were then calcined in a tube furnace at 400 °C for 4 h. The reference CeO2 nanorods were prepared by the same method.

Preparation of x wt.% Ni/30La-CeO2 catalysts. The x wt.% Ni/30La-CeO2 catalysts were prepared via the deposition-precipitation method. Initially, the aforementioned 30La-CeO2 support (0.5 g) was dispersed in 25 mL ultrapure water under continuous stirring. Subsequently, 0.5 mol·L −1 Na2CO3 solution and the corresponding volume of 0.1 mol·L−1 of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O solution were simultaneously added into the 30La-CeO2 solution until the complete addition of the Ni(NO3)2·6H2O solution, ensuring that the final pH of the solution was adjusted to 9. Next, the mixture was aged for 1 h after stirring for 30 min at room temperature, and then the obtained precipitation was washed and filtered with 1 L of ultrapure water. Finally, the catalysts were obtained after drying at 70 °C for 10 h and calcination at 400 °C for 4 h. The reference x wt.% Ni/CeO2 catalysts were prepared using an identical procedure.

Characterization of catalysts. TEM images were taken on a JEOL JEM-1011 instrument operating at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV. HRTEM images were obtained on an FEI Talos F200X microscope under 200 kV. The corresponding elemental mapping was performed on the same instrument under the STEM mode. HAADF-STEM images were conducted using a Spectra 300 (Thermofisher) microscope and FEI Titan Themis G2. HRTEM images of the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst (Fig. 1a) were acquired following the continuous treatment at 450 °C for 2 h and at 500 °C for 0.5 h, respectively. HAADF-STEM images of the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 4) were captured after the gradient heat treatment from 450 °C to 600 °C for 8 h. Additionally, HAADF-STEM images (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 5) were obtained after the 10Ni/30La-CeO2 catalyst was subjected to continuous treatment at 550 °C for 120 h and at 500 °C for 120 h.

In situ ETEM experiment was conducted on an aberration-corrected Titan G2 80−300 ETEM operated at 80 kV in a H2 (50 Pa, 99.99%) atmosphere and a heating control system. For in situ ETEM, NH3 is corrosive, especially at high temperature, and may damage the gas chamber, compromising safety and instrument stability. Above 550 °C, NH3 is nearly fully converted, and the H2 fraction in the system achieves ~75%. Therefore, we conducted the ETEM experiment by using reductive H2 to probe the dynamic structural evolution of Ni. In situ STEM experiment in vacuum was carried out on an FEI Titan Themis G2 at 300 kV with a double spherical-aberration corrector (DCOR) and a high-brightness field-emission gun (X-FEG) with a monochromator installed onto this microscope. All in situ experiments were conducted under relatively low electron-dose conditions (~1.36 × 103 e−Å−2) using a Gatan Oneview CMOS camera at a speed of 25 fps to minimize electron beam damage to the catalysts. To avoid possible long electron beam exposure induced particles dispersion, the beam was on only during the very brief, low-dose exposures used to take the TEM images with a direct electron detector. The as-prepared 10Ni/CeO2 and 10Ni/30La-CeO2 were dropped onto a TEM heating chip (NanoExTMi/v). Prior to experiments, the TEM chips were further cleaned with Ar/O2/SF6 plasma for 5 s. The TEM chips were heated from RT to 600 °C at a heating rate of 1 °C·s − 1 with a beam blank. To observe the temperature range of the variation, each temperature was maintained for about 45–60 min and the images were taken when the sample was stable.

Ex situ and in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) experiments were performed on a PANalytical X’per3 powder diffractometer (Netherlands), operating with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm, 40 kV, 40 mA). The powder samples were placed on a quartz-glass holder without any pretreatment. For the ex situ XRD tests, the collection duration lasted 8.5 min, and the diffraction angles (2θ) ranged from 10° to 90°. The in situ XRD tests were carried out from RT to 600 °C in a flow of 5% H2/Ar mixture (30 mL·min−1) with the same diffraction angles ranging from 10° to 90°. The samples were held inside an Anton Paar XRK900 reactor chamber, and the XRD patterns were collected at 25 °C, 200 °C, 300 °C, 400 °C, 500 °C, and 600 °C, then cooled to 25 °C. For each selected temperature, the data acquisition lasted 30 min. The N2-sorption measurement was operated on a Builder SAA-4200 physisorption instrument at 77 K, and the samples were pretreated at 473 K for 6 h under vacuum before the test. The specific area (SBET) and the pore size distribution for each catalyst were calculated by the BET and the BJH method, respectively.

Temperature-programmed reduction using H2 (H2-TPR) measurements was carried out on a Builder PCSA-1000 adsorption instrument with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). For a typical process, 30 mg samples were preprocessed under air at 300 °C for 30 min before the tests and then purged with pure Ar at RT. After the baseline was stabilized in a 5% H2/Ar atmosphere, the final tests were carried out from RT to 600 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1. Temperature-programmed desorption using NH3 (NH3-TPD) was conducted on an online LC-D200M, TILON mass spectrometer. Prior to testing, 100 mg of fresh samples (20−40 mesh) were pretreated in a 5% H2/Ar gas mixture at 600 °C for 1 h, followed by adsorbing NH3 for 1 h at RT. Next, the samples were exposed to pure Ar for purging, and the temperature range was adjusted from RT to 800 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1 after all of the baselines stabilized, during which the signals of NH3, N2, and H2 were recorded. Temperature-programmed desorption using CO2 (CO2-TPD) was monitored on the same mass spectrometer instrument. The fresh samples (100 mg) were pretreated in a 5% H2/Ar gas mixture at 600 °C for 1 h, followed by adsorbing CO2 for 1 h at RT. Next, the samples were exposed to pure Ar for purging, and then, the temperature range was adjusted from RT to 800 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C· min−1 after all of the baselines stabilized.

Ex situ and quasi in situ XPS were carried out on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB Xi+ XPS spectrometer with Al Kα radiation working at a voltage and current of 15 kV and 20 mA. The vacuum in the analysis chamber was maintained at approximately 10− 9 mbar. The binding energy scales were corrected by setting the C 1 s transition at 284.8 eV for all spectra. For the quasi in situ XPS experiments, the spectra were collected after the samples were pretreated by pure NH3 atmospheres at 600 °C for 1 h. XAS spectra at the Ni K-edge were obtained at the tender energy spectroscopy beamline (BL16U1) in the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF), with an energy range of 2.1−16 keV and a spot size of ~ 670 × 710 μm2.

Ex situ and in situ Raman analyses were performed on a Lab-RAM HR800 spectrometer (HORIBA Jobin Yvon) with laser excitation at 473 nm. For ex situ Raman analysis, the samples were bound on a glass slide, and the spectra were collected at RT under ambient conditions. The scanning range was set from 100–800 cm−1 with a resolution of 2 cm−1. In situ Raman spectra were collected on the same machine with a micro-Raman reaction cell (Xiamen TOPS). The samples were filled in a stainless-steel sample holder, and the experiments were tested from RT to 500 °C in a flow of pure NH3 (10 mL·min−1). In addition, two kinds of gas were induced into the reaction chamber at 500 °C in turn (pure NH3 → 5% H2/Ar→pure NH3 → 5% H2/Ar, 30 mL·min−1) to prove the variation of surface oxygen vacancies. The infrared measurements were conducted in an ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) apparatus with an FTIR spectrometer and a multi-chamber UHV system. The used samples were preprocessed in a pure H2 atmosphere at 600 °C for 30 min, and then CO was introduced at a desired pressure at −130 °C.

Computational details. Machine learning-based molecular dynamics (ML-MD) simulations were performed using GemNet-OC44, a message-passing graph neural network (GNN) framework. A pre-trained model from the Open Catalyst Project (OCP) was employed as the baseline, specifically the GemNet-OC-S2EFS-OC20 + OC22 model45, which was further fine-tuned using our DFT dataset to enhance accuracy on Ni-La-Ce-O systems.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using the VASP 6.3.246,47. Spin-polarized calculations were performed within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional, along with the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method. To accurately describe the localized 4f electronic states of Ce and La, the DFT + U method was employed with an effective U value of 5 eV48. A plane-wave basis set with an energy cutoff of 400 eV was used for all calculations. For geometry optimizations of the large p(4 × 4) slab models of Ni3O/CeO2−x(111) and Ni3O/LaCeO2−x(111), a Γ-point-only k-point mesh was applied to Brillouin zone sampling. Structural optimizations were performed using the conjugate gradient algorithm, with atomic forces converged to below 0.03 eV/Å. Transition states were located using the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method with a force convergence threshold of 0.05 eV/Å49. Catalytic performance tests and kinetics measurements for the ammonia decomposition reaction. The catalytic activity tests for the ammonia decomposition reaction were carried out in a fixed-bed reactor at atmospheric pressure. 50 mg of the sample (20−40 mesh) was diluted with 500 mg quartz sand and packed into the upper part of a quartz tube with an inner diameter of 6 mm. Then, the reaction gas of pure NH3 was introduced with a flow rate of 25 mL·min−1. After the sample was pretreated in pure NH3 at 600 °C for 1 h, the ammonia conversion was measured in the temperature range of 450−600 °C at a step of 50 °C. The concentrations of the gases at the outlet (NH3 and N2) were analyzed online using a gas chromatograph (Ouhua GC 9160, China) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a Porapak Q column. The NH3 conversion was calculated through Eq. 2.

The cycling experiments with eight repeated catalytic cycles were performed to test the durability of the as-prepared sample. The long-term stability of the catalysts was tested at a constant temperature of 550 °C for 120 h, then cooled down to 500 °C for another 120 h (GHSV = 60,000 mL·gcat−1·h−1). The apparent activation energy (Ea) and kinetic orders of NH3, N2, and H2 were all measured by the same apparatus for testing the catalytic performance described above. During the kinetic tests, fewer mass samples were weighed to control the NH3 conversion rates of 5−15% by adjusting the temperature and gas flow rate at the target temperature. The kinetic orders of NH3, N2, and H2 for the catalysts were collected at 500 °C. The catalytic activity was recorded while the concentration of NH3, N2, or H2 in the reaction gas was varied on purpose. The pre-exponential factor (A) was calculated through Eq. 3.
