Electrochemical Determination of the Ionization Energy and the Electron Affinity of Lead Halide Perovskite Thin Films: A Comparison to Ultraviolet Photoelectron Spectroscopy
Authors: Katarina Gugujonovic, Rene Zahrhuber, Thorsten Wagner, Felix Mayr, Elisabeth Leeb, Munise Cobet, Niyazi Serdar Sariciftci, Markus Clark Scharber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable determination of ionization energies (IE) and electron affinities (EA) is essential for understanding energy‐level alignment in solar cells. Here, we benchmark cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical voltage spectroscopy (EVS) for IE and EA determination against ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) for IE and the combination of UPS and photothermal deflection spectroscopy (PDS) for EA. The investigated materials are FA0.8Cs0.2Pb(I1−yBry)3 perovskite thin films with varying bromide fraction y = 0–1. Onset potentials and energies were reproducibly extracted using tangent‐based scripts, avoiding data smoothing and minimizing user bias. The optical band gap scales linearly with bromide fraction. The IE remains approximately constant up to y = 0.4 (EVS) and y = 0.6 (CV), increasing upon further substitution. All investigated methods consistently reveal a decrease in EA with increasing bromide content. Finally, we highlight practical considerations relevant to the application of EC methods and UPS.Keywords: cyclic voltammetry, electrochemical voltage spectroscopy, energy level alignment, mixed‐halide perovskites, onset determination

Introduction

Precise band alignment across the various layers within a solar cell, including the semiconductor, charge‐transport layers, and contacts, is a well‐established prerequisite for high device efficiency. To determine key electronic parameters such as the ionization energy (IE) and electron affinity (EA), several characterization techniques are commonly employed, including ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) for IE and inverse photoemission spectroscopy (IPES) and two‐photon photoemission (2PPE) for EA [1, 4]. The motivation behind this study was to investigate simple electrochemical (EC) approaches, as an accessible alternative to vacuum‐based methods for the characterization of organic–inorganic hybrid perovskites (OIHPs). We focused on cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical voltage spectroscopy (EVS), also known as step potential electrochemical spectroscopy (SPECS), utilizing UPS and photothermal deflection spectroscopy (PDS) for comparative data analysis. PDS was thereby used to determine the optical band gap of the perovskite thin films and, in combination with UPS, to derive the EA by assuming vacuum level alignment. The two EC methods are based on the same principle: the potential applied to the sample is changed over time and the resulting current is measured simultaneously. In CV, the potential changes linearly and continuously, whereas in EVS the potential is changed in steps with a defined hold time (Figure 1a,b). A cyclic voltammogram is presented as a current‐potential plot, from which characteristics such as half‐wave potentials or onsets can be directly determined. EVS data are typically presented as a charge‐per‐potential‐step (ΔQ⋅ΔE−1) versus potential plot with each step represented by a single data point. While CV is a widely used electrochemical method [5, 6], EVS is less common but is considered highly accurate in determining IE and EA [7]. Both methods provide a fast, low‐cost, and accessible alternative to methods that require ultrahigh vacuum. To compare these methods, the determination of the onset potential becomes crucial, as it is directly related to the extracted IE and EA values. Several strategies for onset determination have been reported in the literature, including the use of fixed thresholds [8, 9], smoothing‐based inflection point analysis [10], and tangent extrapolation approaches [11]. The last one is often considered qualitative rather than quantitative, because it often lacks a standardized definition of fitting range and baseline selection, which limits reproducibility [12]. Within this work, we discuss these onset determination methods and explain what boundary conditions we have applied for our tangent‐based analysis script to reproducibly determine the onset values and thus minimize user error. We applied our analysis script on UPS, CV, EVS, and PDS measurements of OIHPs with a sum formula of FA0.8Cs0.2Pb(I1−yBry)3 (FA = formamidinium) with y = 0–1 (see Figure 1d–f). We varied the iodide‐to‐bromide ratio in steps of 0.2 for comparing the reliability and accuracy of the electrochemical and spectroscopic methods across a broad spectrum of band gap energies. Mixed‐halide formamidinium‐cesium perovskites are typical wide band gap materials for the use in perovskite‐organic tandem solar cells [13, 16]. OIHPs are in general well‐known to be relatively unstable against protic solvents, which makes their electrochemical characterization quite challenging. A suitable combination of electrolyte salt and solvent must provide sufficient ionic conductivity while remaining chemically inert with respect to the perovskite layer. Consequently, the electrolyte solution of choice for the characterization of methylammonium (MA) lead iodide may differ from that for a cesium lead halide perovskite, for example. In addition, the electrolyte‐solvent combination must provide a sufficiently large electrochemical stability window, within which the oxidation and reduction onsets of the perovskite lie. Using hydrofluoroether (HFE)‐based solvents, Hasan et al. [17] and Jha et al. [18] were able to perform multiple CV cycles on MAPbI3 [17, 18]. Samu et al. [19] investigated CsPbBr3 and MAPbI3 spectroelectrochemically with tetrabutylammonium (TBA) hexafluorophosphate in dichloromethane (DCM) [19]. Shallcross et al. [20] employed the same electrolyte‐solvent combination for the characterization of a FA‐based perovskite containing both iodide and bromide [20]. In the present work, we investigated FA‐Cs‐based perovskites in a TBAPF6 electrolyte solution in DCM. We determined their redox onset potentials and calculated the IEs and EAs by using the absolute electrode potential (Eabs), which referes to the energetic difference between the vacuum level and the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE). As illustrated in Figure 1c, we assume vacuum‐level alignment across all interfaces, although this condition may not always be fulfilled due to interface dipole formation, band bending, or potential drops [21]. These effects can induce shifts in the vacuum level and should therefore always be kept in mind. A value of +4.75 V [22] was used for Eabs=−ENHEvs.vac, as it is well‐supported by the experimental data from Kötz et al. [23] and Gomer et al. [24]. This value is also in good agreement with the +4.7 V absolute potential adopted by Hansen and Kolb in their seminal study on emersed electrodes [25]. UPS and PDS measurements were performed on the same perovskite compositions to obtain reference values for IE and EA. The aim was to assess the agreement between electrochemically derived and UPS‐based values and thereby estimate the associated deviation. To the best of our knowledge, no direct comparison between electrochemical and vacuum‐based techniques for the determination of the IE and EA of perovskites has been reported before.

Experimental

Materials

The chemicals and materials used in this work are listed in Table S1 in the Supporting Information. All chemicals were used as received.

Perovskite Thin Film Processing

Substrate Preparation

glass|ITO substrates were cut into 2.5 × 2.5 cm pieces for UPS measurements, and 3 × 4 cm pieces for electrochemical characterization. Since the EC measurements require even smaller sample sizes, the substrates were scratched on the back side (three times parallel to the shorter side and once parallel to the longer side) before perovskite deposition and subsequently broken into 8 pieces to ensure identical samples for the various EC measurements. This allowed the samples to be separated by breaking rather than cutting, thereby avoiding additional contamination. For PDS and UV‐Vis measurements, 1 × 1 cm quartz glass substrates were used. All substrates were cleaned using soft tissues soaked in toluene. Tissue residues were removed with pressurized air before the wet‐cleaning procedure in the ultrasonic bath, which included alkaline piranha treatment (0.2 M ammonia solution at 80°C for 15 min, followed by the additon of 2 mL 30% hydrogen peroxide solution and ultrasonication for another 15 min), three rinses with deionized water and one with isopropanol. The cleaned substrates were dried with pressurized air.

Solution Preparation

MeO‐2PACz was dissolved in ethanol (abs.) to obtain a 0.3 mg mL solution. The perovskite precursor solutions were prepared by weighing appropriate amounts of PbI2, PbBr2, FAI, FABr, CsI, and CsBr to achieve a composition corresponding to the sum formula FA0.8Cs0.2Pb(I1−yBry)3, with y=0,0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8, and 1. The salts were dissolved in mixtures of DMF and DMSO. Details on the exact salt amount and solvent ratio for preparing the precursor solutions are provided in the Supporting Information (Table S2). In addition to the mixed precursor solutions, single‐salt solutions (approximately 5 mg mL) containing only one component (PbI2, PbBr2, FAI, FABr, or CsI) were prepared. For the lead halides and CsI, a 1:1 mixture of EtOH and DMF was used as solvent, whereas the FA salts were dissolved in pure EtOH. To the latter, 50 μL of a 5 wt% Nafion solution were added to promote rapid crystallization and more uniform film formation. CsBr could not be dissolved under these conditions and was therefore not investigated. All solutions were prepared in a nitrogen‐filled glovebox and stirred at room temperature overnight.

Film Preparation

The precleaned substrates were treated with oxygen plasma (100 W, 10 min) before use. All spin‐coating and corresponding annealing steps were performed in a nitrogen‐filled glovebox (H2O<0.1ppm;O2<2ppm). Samples were fabricated in the following layer sequence: glass|ITO|MeO‐2PACz|. For the first layer, MeO‐2PACz was deposited onto glass|ITO substrates by spin‐coating, using the parameters listed in Table S3, and subsequently annealed at 100°C for 10 min. Prior to perovskite deposition, the MeO‐2PACz layer was washed with methanol (MeOH) using the same spin‐coating parameters as for the perovskite layer. The spin‐coating program was started, and after 40 s, 200 μL of MeOH were added dropwise within 1 s. Immediately after this first step ended, the perovskite precursor solution was dispensed onto the substrate, and a second spin‐coating step was initiated. Chlorobenzene was added dropwise as anti‐solvent 10–16 s after the start of the second step, depending on the bromide concentration in the precursor solution. A higher bromide concentration required earlier anti‐solvent addition to achieve uniform and smooth films. The optimal dropping time may vary depending on the glovebox conditions. The films were annealed in two steps: first at 65°C for 2 min, followed by at least 10 min at 100°C. After perovskite deposition, the EC substrates were cleaved along the predefined scribe line into pieces of approximately 1.5 × 1 cm. The single‐component precursor solutions were directly drop‐cast onto glass|ITO substrates for CV measurements.

Electrochemistry

All electrochemical experiments were performed in a nitrogen‐filled glovebox by using an Ivium Vertex One EIS potentiostat. For the electrolyte solution, 0.1 M TBAPF6 were dissolved in dichloromethane (DCM). The three‐electrode setup consisted of a Ag/Ag+ reference electrode, a Pt counter electrode, and a glass|ITO|MeO‐2PACz|perovskite substrate as the working electrode. The Ag/Ag+ electrode was prepared using a nonaqueous reference electrode kit purchased from BASi (MF‐2062). The CoralPor(R) tip assembled glass body was filled with a saturated AgNO3 solution in DCM before closing it with the silver wire equipped Teflon cap. DCM was, if necessary, dried by passing it through activated AlOx prior to use. In every electrochemical measurement, the reference electrode was regularly calibrated against a substance with a known redox potential, since its actual potential can deviate significantly from the theoretical value. For calibration, ferrocene was dissolved in the electrolyte solution, using glass|ITO as the working electrode and platinum as the counter electrode. A cyclic voltammogram was recorded from −0.5–1 V vs. Ag/Ag+ at a scan rate of 50 mV s. The half‐wave potential was determined as the mean value of the anodic and cathodic peak potentials, following the standard procedure [26]. Since the half‐wave potential of ferrocene was detemined against the Ag/Ag+ reference electrode, we name it here as . For referring the potential of the reference electrode to NHE, Equation (1) is used. The value for in DCM is calculated by ESCE vs. NHE+EFc/Fc+ vs. SCE, using the potentials from Ref. [27] and Ref. [28].

The ionization energy and electron affinity obtained from electrochemical methods (IEEC and EAEC, respectively) can be further calculated with the onset potentials of the oxidation (Eonset,OX vs. Ag/Ag+) and reduction (Eonset,RED vs. Ag/Ag+), respectively, using Equations (2) and (3) with Eabs taken from Ref. [22] with +4.75 V.

Cyclic Voltammetry

For performing cyclic voltammetry, the potential on the working electrode was varied linearly with a scan speed of 50 mV per second, while the current was detected continuously in steps of 10 mV. When a defined set potential was reached, the scan direction was reversed until the next set potential was reached. If the scan direction is not reversed and the measurement terminates at a defined potential, the technique is referred to as linear sweep voltammetry (LSV).

Electrochemical Voltage Spectroscopy

The applied potential was increased in 10 mV steps, each held for 20 s. For each step, the current was integrated over time to obtain the transported charge. Since the potential steps were of equal size, the charge was plotted as a function of the potential (ΔQ vs. E) rather than ΔQ⋅ΔE−1 vs. E. Since EVS measurements can take up to 67 min, not all samples could be measured on the day of fabrication, but they were analyzed within 2–3 days. No changes were observed in the measurements of films aged for 3 days.

Photoluminescence

For performing photoluminescence (PL) measurements, the samples were excited with a 488 nm laser (Coherent Obis, 5 mW). To prevent excitation light from reaching the detector, long‐pass filters with cut‐on wavelengths of 515 nm and 550 nm were placed in the detection path. The emitted PL was collected using a CCD detector (Andor DV420A‐OE) coupled to a Shamrock 303i monochromator equipped with a 150 lines/mm, 800 nm blaze grating.

Photothermal Deflection Spectroscopy

Photothermal deflection spectroscopy (PDS) is a highly sensitive method for measuring the absorption spectra of thin films. Unlike conventional optical methods, it is largely unaffected by surface roughness, as scattering does not significantly influence the measurement [29, 30].

A schematic representation of the PDS setup including actual data is shown in S1. The measurements were performed using a custom‐built setup consisting of a NKT Photonics SuperK Extreme EXB‐6 supercontinuum white‐light laser as the pump light source, which is connected to an acousto‐optic tunable filter (AOTF, NKT SuperK Select) for wavelength selection. This setup provides pump light in the wavelength range of 450–650 nm and 690–1100 nm, with no output in the intermediate region. An optical chopper is used to modulate the pump light with a frequency of 1.7 Hz. The perovskite thin film samples, deposited on quartz glass|MeO‐2PACz, are placed in a custom‐made stainless steel sample holder. The sample holder is inserted into a 10 mm optical glass cuvette (Hellma OS), filled with the deflection fluid (perfluoro(methyldecalin), Flutec PP9). The modulated pump beam is transferred to the cuvette via an optical fiber and focused on the sample thin film by a set of lenses. A beamsplitter in the pump beam channel is implemented to reflect a fraction of the light onto a calibrated Si photodiode to monitor the pump light power. The heat generated by the absorption of the pump light changes the refractive index of the deflection fluid, which is probed using a fiber‐coupled HeNe laser (Thorlabs Kinesis K‐Cube, 635 nm). The probe beam is guided through the deflection medium parallel to the sample film surface. The deflection is measured on a four‐quadrant photodiode (Thorlabs PDQ80A), which is equipped with a 635 nm band‐pass filter and connected to a position sensing detector (Thorlabs Kinesis K‐Cube). The probe beam deflection and the pump beam power signal are measured on lock‐in amplifiers (Stanford Research Systems SR830 and SR860), while the total photovoltage signal from the four‐quadrant photodiode is measured on a Keithley 2700 digital multimeter. The PDS spectrum is obtained by correcting the beam deflection signal with the pump power and the total photovoltage signal. For obtaining the absolute absorptance (A) spectra, the PDS spectra are scaled to reference absorptance values, determined via transmittance (T) and reflectance (R) spectra measured on a conventional UV‐Vis spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer Lambda 1050). For a more detailed description of the PDS method, the instrument and the data validation, the reader is referred to Ref. [31] and Ref. [29]. The absorption coefficient (α) can be calculated by Equation (4), with x as the perovskite layer thickness. The layer thickness of the perovskite films were measured with a Dektak profilometer. Optical band gaps were determined from Tauc plots of (αhν)2 vs. hν.

UV‐Vis Spectroscopy

T and R spectra of glass|ITO|MeO‐2PACz|perovskite samples were recorded using a Perkin Elmer UV‐Vis‐NIR Lambda 1050 double‐beam spectrometer equipped with a 150 mm integrating sphere. A Ag mirror and a Si wafer sample were used as a reference for the R measurements. A was calculated according to A=1−T−R.

Ultraviolet Photoelectron Spectroscopy (UPS)

UPS measurements were performed on a Theta Probe XPS‐system (Thermofisher, GBR), which features a He discharge lamp as UV source (He I). The pressure in the chamber rose from 1E‐9 mbar to a maximum of 1E‐7 mbar after switching on the He lamp (0.5–0.6 kV, 40 mA). The hemispherical analyzer was set to a low pass energy of 0.5 eV and a dwell time of 50 ms, with 10 scans recorded per measurement. With the lens axis tilted by 50° from the sample normal in the standard horizontal geometry, electrons are collected over an angular range between 20° and 80°. To ensure comparability to the measurements obtained from EC methods, no additional treatments such as sputtering or plasma cleaning were applied prior to the UPS measurements. Two separate loading cycles were performed to analyze a total of six perovskite films (y = 0–1). In each cycle, a gold reference measurement was included to ensure proper calibration. Overall, three consecutive measurement series were carried out. In the first series, each of the three perovskite samples was measured once, together with the gold reference. In the second and third series, the same perovskite samples were remeasured a second and third time, respectively, with measurements performed at different positions on each film to account for possible lateral inhomogeneities. All samples were prepared, stored, transferred, and measured under identical conditions to ensure a consistent sample history across the compositional series. UPS measurements were performed prior to XPS measurements to avoid possible X‐ray‐induced changes in the valence electronic structure. In addition, comparable acquisition parameters and measurement times were used for all samples. Data acquisition as well as the control of the device is performed via a software package (Avantage) provided by the system manufacturer.

UPS spectra are typically plotted on a kinetic energy (Ekin) scale, however, the valence band region is commonly represented as a semi‐logarithmic intensity plot versus binding energy (Eb), obtained by using Equation (5), where hν denotes the HeI photon energy (21.22 eV). This procedure sets the Fermi energy to zero. An example UPS spectrum plotted as intensity versus Ekin on a linear scale and versus Eb on a semi‐logarithmic scale is shown in Figure S2 for clarity.

X‐Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS measurements were performed on a Theta Probe XPS‐system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, GBR, same instrument as used for UPS), which features a monochromated Al‐Kα X‐ray source with an energy of 1486.6 eV. The spot size on the sample surface was 400 μm in diameter. The hemispherical analyzer was set to a pass energy of 40 eV for the recorded high resolution scans at an energy step size of 0.05 eV and 200 eV pass energy and 1 eV for the survey spectra. For charge neutralization on the sample surface the system is equipped with a dual flood gun which simultaneously provides electrons and Ar+ ions with low kinetic energy.

XPS peaks were fitted using the Avantage software provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific. Generally, the data evaluation started with the elimination of the peak background by subtracting a function called smart background, provided by the Avantage software. This function is based on the Shirley background with an additional constraint so that the background cannot be of a greater intensity than the actual data at any point in the region. The integration of the peaks in the XPS scans via the Avantage software was used to determine the compositions of the perovskite samples with different halide ratios. For this, the primary XPS regions recommended by Thermo Fisher Scientific [32] were used. The reference peak for defining the binding energy for all spectra was always the C–C peak at 285 eV.

Defining the Onset

In this section, methods for onset determination in CV, EVS, UPS, and PDS data are discussed. In the following, we explain how the methods ultimately used for data evaluation in this work were selected and what difficulties were encountered with the others. The goal was to obtain reproducible and consistent onset values under identical evaluation conditions within each measurement technique, while accounting for the specific characteristics of the respective datasets (e.g., signal‐to‐noise ratio and baseline behavior), thereby minimizing user‐induced variability.

A rigorous mathematical approach is to determine the point of inflection of a dataset by identifying where the second derivative of the curve equals zero [10]. However, due to inherent noise level of the experimental data, the curves are not smooth enough to directly apply this method. While smoothing could, in principle, enable such an analysis, we refrained from doing so, as it introduces several drawbacks: Smoothing inevitably alters the original data, can shift onset positions depending on the chosen algorithm and parameters, and may obscure genuine fine features while amplifying artifacts [33, 35]. Because the datasets differ in their shape and noise level, applying a smoothing algorithm with a fixed set of parameters would be difficult and potentially misleading. We therefore prefer to work with the pristine experimental data whenever possible.

The threshold method, in which a defined value is set to determine the onset [8, 9], is the most commonly employed procedure in the literature [12]. However, this approach is not universally applicable, as its accuracy strongly depends on the baseline behavior of the signal. For electrochemical measurements, the region preceding the onset is often not constant but increases steadily due to capacitive effects [36]. Combined with the limited number of data points, this makes application of the method to CV and EVS data challenging. For UPS data, there are two reasons why this approach was not applicable for determining the onset energies. First, the signal‐to‐noise ratio varies, particularly when comparing the SECO and VB edge regions. As a consequence, a percentage‐based threshold is not generally applicable. Likewise, the noise level itself does not remain within a constant range, so an absolute threshold value is also unsuitable. For this reason, applying any fixed threshold does not yield reproducible results. Second, applying this method yielded SECO values with higher binding energies than the actual cut‐off. This discrepancy arises from the gradual intensity increase preceding the onset, which should not be defined as the SECO. Such broadening can be attributed to inhomogeneities within the samples [37, 38], and, in some cases, sample inhomogeneities can even lead to pronounced shoulder features, as discussed by Kim et al. [39] and Schultz et al. [40].

A commonly applied approach is to fit a tangent in the linear region of the onset and extrapolate it to the point where it intersects the baseline [12]. For high reproducibility and to minimize user error, the procedure must be defined in detail, however, it is often inadequately described in the literature. Critical questions include: What range of data points is selected for the linear fit? And how is the baseline defined? Defining an appropriate range of data points for fitting can be challenging, as datasets often differ significantly in appearance. For this reason, the tangent method is usually regarded as qualitative, since the user typically selects a portion of the steep increase in the curve that appears linear to the eye. Because our goal was to minimize user error, specific conditions for range selection had to be defined. It should be noted that even when the fitting criteria are well‐defined, a fully automated onset determination is not feasible. Due to variations in signal quality, noise, and baseline behavior, the algorithm can in principle identify false onsets or fit irrelevant regions of the curve. Consequently, a manual double‐check of the result is always required to ensure its physical plausibility. In our tangent approach, a linear regression was applied over a defined number of consecutive data points (Nfit), provided that the coefficient of determination (R 2) met the respective threshold, as illustrated in Figure 2a for an EVS measurement. The onset was defined as the intersection of the extrapolated linear fit with the corresponding baseline, which was defined according to the signal characteristics of each method. For CV, EVS, and PDS measurements, the onset corresponds to the zero crossing of the respective y‐axis quantity (i in CV, ΔQ in EVS, and (αE)2 in PDS). For CV and EVS measurements, we also attempted to fit an additional line to a set of data points in a defined region prior to the onset in order to generate a baseline. However, this approach proved challenging, as in some measurements an appropriate baseline could not be identified due to interfering features (peaks, humps, or shoulders) and capacitive effects in the curves. Due to the very high sensitivity of PDS measurements [29, 30], the baseline typically lies several orders of magnitude below the absorptance values in the band edge region. The determination of the optical band gap values thus remains practically unchanged whether the linear fit is extrapolated to the zero‐y crossing or to a linear fit of the baseline. For fitting the UPS measurements, additional criteria were applied for baseline determination. The baseline was defined using a data‐driven approach based on the region of lowest intensity in the spectrum. Specifically, the global minimum of the intensity was identified, and a symmetric window of 21 consecutive data points (Nbase=21) centered at this minimum was used to calculate the mean intensity I¯. The baseline was then defined as a constant value corresponding to this mean (Figure 2b). The minima were determined separately for the SECO and VB edge regions. This approach ensures a robust and reproducible baseline definition, as it relies on the intrinsic characteristics of the data rather than predefined thresholds. For determination of the VB edge, the spectra were evaluated on a semi‐logarithmic intensity scale, as recommended in the literature for metal halide perovskite thin films [41, 42]. An overview of all fitting parameters used in the tangent‐based analysis is provided in Table 1. In a few instances where a distinct signal was clearly visible in the diagram but an insufficient number of data points was available, the fitting criteria were relaxed by reducing Nfit or R 2 accordingly. In addition to the mathematical definition of the onset, practical phenomena such as electrochemical responses arising from salts not bound to the perovskite crystal, as well as satellite emission lines of He discharge lamps, must be considered. These signals should not be misinterpreted as the actual onset and are therefore discussed in the Results and Discussion section.

Results and Discussion

Six compositions of FA0.8Cs0.2Pb(I1−yBry)3 (y = 0–1.0) thin films were investigated using CV and EVS as an alternative to UPS. To ensure the reliability of the electrochemical measurements, the potential of the Ag/Ag+ reference electrode was calibrated before starting measurements and, for long sessions, several times per day. As demonstrated in Figure S3, the reference electrode potential can shift substantially. Such shifts directly translate into apparent changes in the onset potential and therefore need to be corrected to maintain data consistency among samples. This highlights the importance of regular calibration during extended measurement series. In practice, we observed clogging of the reference electrode frit when insufficiently dried solvents were used, highlighting the need for careful solvent preparation to ensure electrode longevity. Prior to analyzing the electrochemical response, the stability of the perovskite films in contact with the electrolyte solution was evaluated: treating the perovskite films with the electrolyte solution did not cause any visible changes, and the films remained stable during the linear potential sweep, showing signs of decomposition only afterward (see Figure S4). Blank measurements on glass|ITO|MeO‐2PACz remain featureless between −1.61 V and +1.80 V vs. NHE (see Figure S5a). Using chlorobenzene instead of DCM as the solvent in the electrolyte solution narrows the electrochemical window, as demonstrated in the same graph. Perovskite films that were aged for 3 months clearly exhibit signal broadening (Figure S5b), which we presume originates from trap states formed by film degradation. We therefore suggest that films with poor crystallinity and high defect density may display a similar behavior, which can complicate the determination of the onset potential. Figure 3 compares the linear sweeps from CV measurements with the charge‐voltage curves from EVS. Redox processes are consistently observed at earlier potentials along the scanning direction in EVS than in the faster CV potential sweeps. This behavior is well known and can be rationalized electrochemically: the total current comprises a capacitive term that scales linearly with the scan rate and a diffusive (faradaic) term that scales with the square root of the scan rate. Other processes, like kinetically limited redox reactions or deposition of the electrolyte on the electrode, are summarized as the residual current. In cyclic voltammetry, capacitive charging can mask weak faradaic contributions near the thermodynamic threshold at typical scan rates, while ohmic polarization and charge–transfer kinetics further shift discernible features, together yielding apparently delayed onsets in CV compared to step‐based methods [43, 45].

A more detailed evaluation of the linear fits to the electrochemical signals reveals the presence of preonset features, particularly in the oxidative regime. Because such features can lead to misinterpretation of the onset position, their origin was investigated in more detail by recording CVs of the pure precursor components (Figure S6). The resulting onset potentials versus NHE of FAI (0.73 V), CsI (0.94 V), FABr (1.49 V), PbI2 (1.55 V), CsBr and PbBr2 (both > 1.8 V), and metallic lead (0.84 V) are indicated as dashed vertical lines in Figure 3. The boundaries of the electrochemical window (−1.61 V to +1.80 V vs. NHE) are shown in the same figure as dashed red lines. Most of the small features in the i/ΔQ−V curves can be attributed to residual precursor species located in the vicinity of the film or to Pb(0), which is known as a degradation product of PbI2 [46, 47]. We attribute small preonset anodic features at around +1.2 V vs. NHE to the oxidation of nonlattice bromide at/near the perovskite surface forming Br2 or Br3−. This hypothesis is supported by the second and third cycles of the PbBr2 and FABr references (Figure S7), which show a peak at +1.18 V and a shoulder at +1.26 V vs. NHE, respectively. These features are not visible in the first cycle, as the bromide is retained on the electrode in the form of a salt. The photographs in Figure S6b show that the individual precursor films partially dissolve or decompose during the measurement, thereby releasing species such as bromide ions into the solution. In addition to the smaller preonset features, the larger shoulder‐like features likely arise from phase segregation, a well‐known phenomenon in mixed‐halide perovskites [48, 49]. The PL spectra shown in Figure 4 support this interpretation, as samples with y = 0.4–0.8 exhibit emission energies that do not coincide with the absorption band edges, indicating the presence of more than one perovskite phase. This behavior complicates the accurate determination of the onset potential.

To assess the surface composition and possible defect contributions, XPS measurements were performed (Figure S8). The actual surface composition was determined by integration of characteristic peaks (Table S4), enabling the calculation of perovskite stoichiometric indices (Table S5) and corresponding index fractions (Table S6). The Tauc plot derived from the PDS absorption spectra (Figure 5a) indicates an increase in the optical band gap (Eg,opt.) with increasing bromide content (Br/(I + Br)), which is confirmed by a positive Pearson correlation coefficient (r) of +0.99 (Figure S9a). Reported trends of the work function with increasing bromide content are highly inconsistent, with no clear systematic behavior [2, 50, 53]. This phenomena can be attributed to differences in surface termination, defect chemistry, and interface dipoles, which all of them can alter the Fermi level position or induce vacuum level shifts, highlighting that the work function is governed by interfacial electronic structure rather than intrinsic bulk properties. Even sub‐monolayer amounts of adsorbed species can significantly alter the measured kinetic energies and, consequently, the derived electronic surface parameters [2, 21, 54, 55]. As shown in Figures 5b, S11 and S9b, the work function on the surface increases with increasing bromide content (r = +0.90), indicating a shift toward more p‐type semiconducting behavior. This trend may be attributed to the presence of lead vacancies VPb [4, 56, 57]. Pb/(I + Br) ratios in Table S6, all of which fall below the theoretical ideal value of 0.33, indicate a lead deficiency at the surface, supporting the assignment of VPb. Also, fitting of Pb 4f signals obtained from XPS measurements revealed the presence of metallic lead on the surface of the perovskite films (Figure S10). For the evaluation of the valence band edge region in UPS spectra, it is essential to account for additional spectral contributions: A He discharge lamp emits satellite emission lines at photon energies different from the main He I line (21.22 eV), such as HeI (23.09 eV) and He I (23.75 eV). These spectral emission lines appear as shifted replicas of the spectrum excited by the main line [42]. Although the HeI and He I lines exhibit intensities with a factor of 0.4 and 0.1, respectively, relative to the main line [58], their contribution to the UPS signal can be significant, particularly in semi‐logarithmic representations. For valence band edge determination, these features should not be misinterpreted as the onset of the actual spectrum, as shown in Figure 5c. A strong negative correlation of the VB edge position with (Cs+FA) and (Cs+FA)/Pb (Table S6) is observed, with Pearson correlation coefficients r of −0.96 and −0.83, respectively, in agreement with the findings of Barría‐Cáceres et al. [4].

Ionization energies and electron affinities obtained from photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) and electrochemical methods are often compared, yet they are not strictly equivalent due to fundamental differences in the underlying physical processes: PES probes vertical ionization events at the surface and therefore reflects the electronic structure without allowing for structural or dielectric relaxation, whereas electrochemical measurements yield thermodynamically relaxed redox energies that inherently include polarization and solvation effects [60, 61]. In addition, PES is surface‐sensitive and can be influenced by band bending, interfacial dipoles, and surface defects, while electrochemical techniques probe an average response of the bulk material in contact with the electrolyte [21, 60, 62]. Further discrepancies arise from the referencing procedure in electrochemistry, where potentials must be converted to an absolute energy scale, introducing systematic uncertainties depending on the chosen reference electrode and conversion factor [60, 61]. The electrochemical ionization energies derived from the measured potentials using the conversion factor reported by Cardona et al. [22] are in relatively good agreement with the values obtained from UPS measurements; however, the deviation appears to increase with increasing bromide content. Maximum deviations of up to approximately ±0.3 eV were observed when comparing the two electrochemical methods for IE determination with UPS (Figure 6b, Table S9).

The electron affinity was determined by subtracting the UPS‐derived ionization energy from the optical band gap obtained by PDS. The validity of this assumption for perovskite films is discussed in the following. In contrast to organic semiconductors, where the difference between electrochemical and optical band gaps is largely governed by the exciton binding energy and thus typically results in larger electrochemical gaps [61, 62], halide perovskites exhibit a more complex behavior: photogenerated charge carriers are coupled to the polarized crystal lattice, forming large polarons and leading to energetic stabilization [63, 64]. However, because both the exciton binding energy and the polaron stabilization energies are relatively small, transport band gaps, which are defined as the difference between the valence band maximum and conduction band minimum, and optical band gaps in perovskites are expected to be in close agreement, although deviations may arise depending on the measurement technique, material properties, and methodological differences in onset determination. In general, a decrease in EA with increasing bromide content is observed across all methods (Figures 6a and S11). This trend can be rationalized by considering the underlying electronic structure of ABX3 perovskites: The IE is primarily governed by the antibonding hybridization between the B‐site s‐states and the X‐site p‐states. In contrast, the EA, is dominated by B‐site p‐states with minor contributions from the halide orbitals. The B‐site p‐states play a predominant role in shifting the EA: When going from I to Br, the EA decreases due to the reduced halide ionic radius and the resulting wavefunction confinement that affects the lead p‐states [59].

The band gaps derived from electrochemical measurements exhibit a systematic dependence on both the applied technique and the halide composition (Figure 6c). In general, the gap obtained from electrochemical voltage spectroscopy, defined as the difference between the oxidation and reduction onsets, is consistently smaller than the optical gap across the entire compositional series. In contrast, cyclic voltammetry yields gaps that are in good agreement with the optical values at low to moderate bromide contents, while a tendency toward larger gaps is observed at higher bromide fractions. These differences can be rationalized by considering the distinct physical processes underlying the respective techniques. EVS probes quasi‐equilibrium charge accumulation and is therefore particularly sensitive to sub‐bandgap states and tail states, which can shift the apparent onset potentials and lead to an underestimation of the gap. In contrast, CV measurements are more strongly governed by distinct redox processes, resulting in onset potentials that more closely reflect the intrinsic transport levels of the material. At higher bromide contents, however, increased energetic disorder, defect densities, or compositional inhomogeneities may broaden the density of states and shift the redox onsets, leading to an apparent widening of the electrochemical gap. More generally, discrepancies between electrochemical and optical gaps arise because the latter corresponds to the lowest optically allowed transition, whereas electrochemical methods probe relaxed charge carrier states [60, 61, 65].

Conclusion

In this work, we compared the widely‐accessible electrochemical measurement techniques of CV and EVS to the established high vacuum‐based UPS method for the determination of the energy levels of perovskite films, complemented by optical band gap determination via PDS.

A script‐based tangent method enabled reproducible and user‐independent determination of onset energies. Despite limited electrochemical stability, reliable IE and EA values were obtained. CV‐derived band gaps agree well with optical values at low bromide content, whereas EVS systematically underestimates the band gap.

UPS, CV, and EVS differ significantly in their practical applicability and physical probing depth. While UPS provides accurate ionization energies, it requires ultrahigh vacuum and probes only a small sample area. Electrochemical methods offer faster and more accessible alternatives but are influenced by the electrochemical stability window, referencing procedures, and sensitivity to defect states.

Overall, this work establishes electrochemical methods as viable alternatives to UPS for energy level determination in perovskite thin films, while highlighting their limitations and the importance of careful data interpretation.
