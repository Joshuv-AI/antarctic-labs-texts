Increased mortality and Acinetobacter baumannii predominance despite stable healthcare-associated infection rates during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Gülsüm Kaya, Pınar Özkan Oskay, Nesrin Kebapçı Mert, Şeyma Trabzon, Sebahat Aksaray, Zeynep Ergenç, Hasan Ergenç, Cengiz Karacaer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), first identified in Wuhan, China, in December 2019, rapidly evolved into a global pandemic with substantial morbidity and mortality. Severe COVID-19 cases, particularly those with pneumonia requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission, posed significant challenges for healthcare systems. Active surveillance data collected by the infection control team were analyzed for two periods: pre-pandemic (May 2018–February 2020) and pandemic (March 2020–December 2021). The number of ICU patients, patient-days, HAI rates, causative microorganisms, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and mortality rates were compared between periods. Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are defined as infections that are not present at the time of hospital admission but develop during the course of healthcare delivery. The most common HAIs include central line-associated bloodstream infections, catheter-associated urinary tract infections, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and surgical site infections [1]. While HAIs may occur across all hospital settings, their incidence, severity, and clinical impact vary significantly between inpatient wards and intensive care units (ICUs). ICU patients, particularly those requiring mechanical ventilation, invasive monitoring, and prolonged hospitalization, are at substantially higher risk due to critical illness, immune dysfunction, and increased exposure to invasive devices. In contrast, ward-based patients, such as those in pulmonology or general internal medicine units, generally have lower exposure to such risk factors and consequently a lower incidence of device-associated infections.

HAIs are influenced by numerous factors, including patient demographics, frequency of invasive procedures, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and the effectiveness of infection control programs. Recent global estimates indicate that in high-income countries, 7–10 out of every 100 hospitalized patients develop at least one HAI, while in low- and middle-income countries this figure is approximately 15% [2]. These infections contribute to increased morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and substantial economic burden [3]. Beyond individual outcomes, HAIs facilitate the spread of multidrug-resistant organisms within healthcare settings, posing a major public health challenge. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) therefore identify HAI prevention as a priority for patient safety and healthcare quality [4–6].

The COVID-19 pandemic has created unprecedented challenges for global health systems in terms of controlling and preventing HAIs. Increased patient volumes during the pandemic, shortages of healthcare workers, changes in the use of personal protective equipment, and isolation measures have led to a marked increase in HAIs incidence [7]. In particular, significant increases in central line-associated bloodstream infections and ventilator-associated pneumonia rates have been reported during the pandemic period [1, 8, 9]. In the third and fourth quarters of 2020 in the United States, a 46–47 per cent increase in central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI) was reported compared to 2019 [1]. A multicentre study covering 149 intensive care units in Europe has demonstrated a significant increase in the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia in COVID-19 patients [10]. A study reported a significant increase in CLABSI incidence in intensive care units at the beginning of the pandemic period in 21 hospitals in Brazil [11].

Although numerous international studies have evaluated changes in the incidence of HAIs in intensive care units (ICUs) before and during the pandemic, comparative analyses based on single-centre, long-term, and active surveillance data from Turkey are quite limited. Furthermore, there is a notable lack of studies that examine in detail the changes in mortality, the distribution of microorganisms causing HAIs, and antibiotic resistance patterns during the pandemic at the same centre and in the same ICUs. This study aims to fill this important gap in the literature by comparatively analysing the incidence of HAIs developing in the ICUs before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, the causative microorganisms, and changes in resistance rates.

Methodology

Study design and data collection

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Sakarya Yenikent State Hospital (SYDH), a 255-bed secondary-care hospital comprising 50 intensive care unit (ICU) beds. The study population consisted of adult patients admitted to the tertiary anesthesia and resuscitation ICU, secondary ICUs, and mixed ICUs between May 1, 2018 and December 31, 2021. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational studies.

Study periods

Two predefined time periods were analyzed: The pre-pandemic period (PP, May 1, 2018 – February 29, 2020) and the pandemic period (PDP, March 1, 2020 – December 31, 2021). During both periods, active daily surveillance for hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) was performed by the infection control team (ICT). All patients with suspected infection were evaluated by an infection control physician prior to definitive classification of HAI.

Participants

All adult patients aged 18 years or older who were admitted to the ICUs during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Patients younger than 18 years of age were excluded from the analysis.

Definitions

Hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) and colonization were defined according to the surveillance criteria of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [12]. The HAI rate was calculated as the proportion of patients who developed at least one HAI episode among all ICU admissions during the study period, expressed as a percentage. HAI incidence density was calculated as the total number of HAI episodes divided by the cumulative number of patient-days, multiplied by 1,000. Patient-days were defined as the total number of days spent in the ICU by all patients during each respective study period.

Device-associated infection incidence densities were calculated per 1,000 device-days by dividing the number of device-associated infection episodes by the total number of exposure days to the corresponding invasive device and multiplying the result by 1,000. Device-days were defined as the cumulative duration of exposure to a specific invasive device, including ventilator-days for ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), central line-days for central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI), and urinary catheter-days for catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI).

VAE diagnostic criteria

According to the surveillance definitions of the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN), respiratory healthcare-associated infections in mechanically ventilated patients are assessed using the ventilator-associated event (VAE) algorithm rather than a purely clinical definition of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). The VAE framework consists of a tiered approach. It begins with the identification of a ventilator-associated condition (VAC), defined as a period of at least two days of stable or improving oxygenation followed by a sustained worsening, indicated by an increase in daily minimum FiO₂ of ≥ 0.20 or PEEP of ≥ 3 cmH₂O for at least two consecutive days. VAC is followed by infection-related ventilator-associated complication (IVAC), which requires evidence of systemic inflammation (e.g., abnormal temperature or white blood cell count) along with the initiation of a new antimicrobial agent. Finally, possible ventilator-associated pneumonia (PVAP) is defined when IVAC criteria are met in addition to microbiological, histopathological, or respiratory viral evidence of infection. These definitions were applied in accordance with NHSN criteria [12].

Data collection

Data were retrospectively retrieved from electronic medical records and the institutional infection surveillance database. Extracted variables included demographic characteristics, ICU admission details, duration of ICU stay, invasive device exposure duration, microbiological culture results, documented infection episodes, and clinical outcomes.

Infection prevention and control measures

Standard infection prevention and control (IPC) measures were consistently implemented throughout both study periods. These measures comprised the avoidance of unnecessary invasive procedures, strict adherence to aseptic and antiseptic techniques during all medical interventions, timely removal of invasive devices when no longer clinically indicated, and compliance with institutional hand hygiene protocols. Microbiological cultures were obtained in cases of fever, clinical deterioration, or when infection was suspected based on standardized clinical evaluation.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes of the study were HAI rate and HAI incidence density in each study period. Secondary outcomes included device-associated infection incidence densities, distribution of causative microorganisms, and ICU mortality. Infection-related mortality was defined as death occurring during ICU stay in patients diagnosed with at least one HAI episode.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version XX.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Epi Info™ version 7.2.5.0 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, GA, USA). Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Yates-corrected Chi-square test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Data are presented as median [interquartile range] or number (percentage), as appropriate. Univariate logistic regression analysis was initially performed to identify variables associated with in-hospital mortality. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model to determine independent predictors of mortality. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, a Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons in subgroup analyses, and adjusted p-values were interpreted accordingly.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee (02/05/2023; E-71522473-050.01.04-241704-150). Due to the retrospective design of the study, informed consent was waived. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Funding

This study received no external funding.

Results

During the study period, a total of 1,840 patients were followed for 16,572 patient-days in the PP ICU, while 2,119 patients were followed for 12,460 patient-days in the PDP ICU. The overall HAI rate was 3.26% in the PP and 2.73% in the PDP, indicating comparable rates between the two periods (p = 0.333). HAI density was 3.62‰ in the PP and 4.65‰ in the PDP.

Regarding infection types, 28 (46.67%) of HAIs in the PP were bloodstream infections (BSI), 19 (31.67%) were urinary tract infections (UTI), and 13 (21.67%) were pneumonia. In the PDP, these proportions were 31 (53.45%), 21 (36.21%), and 6 (10.34%), respectively. No marked differences were observed between the two periods in terms of infection types (BSI p = 0.461; UTI p = 0.602; pneumonia p = 0.094).

In the distribution of causative microorganisms, HAIs due to Acinetobacter baumannii were markedly higher in the PDP compared with the PP (58.89% vs. 35.00%, p = 0.016). No marked differences were observed between the periods for other pathogens, including Candida spp., Enterobacter spp., Enterococcus spp., Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., coagulase-negative staphylococci, Proteus vulgaris, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus (Table 1). When patients who developed HAIs were evaluated, survivors (n = 36) and non-survivors (n = 82) were compared. Discussion

In our study investigating the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on HAIs rates in the ICU, it was determined that the HAIs rate, BSI, UTI, and pneumonia rates were similar in both periods (p > 0.05), but mortality rates in PDP were significantly higher than in PP, and HAIs caused by A. baumannii was significantly higher (respectively, p = 0.00001; p = 0.016).

HAIs continue to be among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in ICUs today. The COVID-19 pandemic, which affected the entire world and brought life to a virtual standstill, led to a decrease in the number of patients admitted to hospitals; however, it did not result in a significant decrease in the number of patients admitted to intensive care units. In the study conducted by Esin et al., it was observed that the number of patients admitted to the ICU was similar before and during the COVID-19 period (n: 116 and n: 108, respectively) [13]. Our study has similar results; the number of patients hospitalised in the PP and PDP ICUs is similar.

In our study, the HAIs rate was determined to be 3.26% in the PP and 2.73% in the PDP, and no statistically significant difference was observed between the two periods in terms of HAIs frequency (p = 0.333). In the study conducted by Önal ve ark., the HAIs incidence in the ICU was determined to be 16.59 per 1000 patient days during the COVID-19 pandemic period, while it was 13.42 in other intensive care units, and this difference was reported to be not statistically significant (p = 0.107) [14]. However, there are also studies reporting an increase in HAIs rates during the pandemic compared to the pre-pandemic period. Baccolini et al., in their study conducted in the ICU of a teaching and research hospital in Rome, reported that the number of HAIs cases rose from 14 in 2019 to 45 in 2020. The same study showed that hospitalisation in 2020 was a significant risk factor for both HAIs development and catheter-associated HAIs onset (SHR: 2.66; 95% CI: 1.31–5.38 and SHR: 10.0; 95% CI: 1.84–54.41, respectively) [15]. Another study aiming to evaluate the prevalence of HAIs and mortality rates in COVID-19 patients reported that the prevalence of HAIs was 5.9% (n = 71) in patients during the pandemic period and 2.7% (n = 91) in patients before the pandemic period, and that this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001) [16]. These differing outcomes may be related to patient profiles between centres, the effectiveness of infection control measures implemented during the pandemic, the burden on the healthcare system, and differences in healthcare provision between centres.

The increase in the number of patients during the COVID-19 period, together with a reduction in total patient-days, suggests a potential change in ICU length of stay between the two periods. This may reflect earlier discharge, increased in-hospital mortality, or differences in patient turnover during the pandemic. In particular, the higher mortality observed during the pandemic period may have contributed to shorter cumulative exposure time, thereby influencing patient-day–based calculations of infection density and potentially affecting the interpretation of HAI rates.

Our study found that the rates of bloodstream infections, pneumonia, and urinary tract infections were similar between the pre-pandemic and post-pandemic periods (p > 0.05), which is consistent with some studies reported in the literature. Sarı et al. and Solmaz et al. also reported in their studies conducted in Turkey that the rates of bloodstream infections, ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), and catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI) did not show significant differences between the pre-COVID-19 and pandemic periods [16, 17]. Similarly, a large-scale study conducted in intensive care units in South Korea reported a decrease in BSI and VAP incidence during the pandemic period, while CLABSI and CAUTI rates remained unchanged between the two periods [18]. However, some studies conducted in Brazil reported an increase in the incidence of CLABSI during the pandemic while VAP rates remained unchanged [19], and some studies have shown that the incidence of VAP is higher, especially in patients undergoing mechanical ventilation due to COVID-19-related ARDS [20, 21]. It is thought that these heterogeneous results among the studies may be related to differences in patient populations, clinical severity of COVID-19 patients, frequency of invasive procedures, and especially the effectiveness of infection prevention and control practices, which vary between centers.

The observed reduction in VAP during the COVID-19 period may be explained by changes in respiratory care practices implemented in intensive care units. During the pandemic, increased use of protective strategies such as prone positioning, high-flow nasal cannula therapy, and closed in-line suction systems may have contributed to improved airway management and reduced pathogen transmission. In addition, stricter infection prevention precautions adopted for aerosol-generating procedures could have further limited VAP development. These practice modifications, introduced to optimize respiratory support and reduce viral transmission risk, may have inadvertently influenced the distribution of HAIs by decreasing the proportion of VAP cases. These changes may also partly explain the observed reduction in ventilator-associated pneumonia in our cohort.

It has been reported that significant changes have occurred in the rate and distribution of bacterial co-infections during the COVID-19 pandemic, with a marked predominance of Acinetobacter baumannii in intensive care settings [22]. In our study, A. baumannii was the leading pathogen and its frequency increased from 35.00% in the pre-pandemic period to 58.89% during the pandemic (p = 0.016), indicating a substantial shift in the microbiological profile of healthcare-associated infections. A study conducted in China reported that 91.2% of bacterial isolates from COVID-19 patients exhibited carbapenem resistance, underscoring the high burden of antimicrobial resistance in secondary infections. In the same study, A. baumannii was the most frequently isolated pathogen, with multidrug-resistant strains predominating. These findings are particularly relevant in the context of extensive empirical antibiotic use in critically ill patients, despite COVID-19 being a viral infection for which antibacterial therapy is not indicated [23]. Solmaz et al. similarly demonstrated a significant increase in A. baumannii–related infections during the pandemic period (47.9% vs. 20.9%; p < 0.001) [16].

Importantly, the observed increase in mortality in our cohort should also be interpreted in this clinical context. Consistent with the findings of Unterberg et al., higher mortality during the pandemic may reflect greater disease severity at ICU admission and delayed critical care access, particularly in septic and critically ill patients. In our study, although overall HAI rates remained stable, the predominance of multidrug-resistant pathogens such as A. baumannii, together with higher illness severity in non-survivors (as reflected by APACHE II and SOFA scores), likely contributed to worse outcomes. This supports the notion that mortality during the pandemic period was driven more by patient severity and microbiological burden rather than changes in HAI incidence alone. Overall, this pattern supports the endemic nature of A. baumannii in ICU settings in Türkiye, where prolonged hospitalization, invasive device use, and high antimicrobial pressure facilitate its persistence and transmission.

It has been reported that significant changes have occurred in the rate and distribution of bacterial co-infections during the COVID-19 pandemic, with a particular increase in Acinetobacter baumannii [22]. A study conducted in China reported that 91.2% of bacterial isolates from COVID-19 patients exhibited carbapenem resistance, highlighting a substantial burden of antimicrobial resistance in secondary bacterial infections. In the same study, A. baumannii was identified as the most common pathogen associated with co-infections, with multidrug-resistant strains being frequently isolated. These findings are particularly relevant given that COVID-19 is a viral infection for which antibacterial therapy is not indicated; however, empirical and prolonged antibiotic use in critically ill patients may promote the selection and dissemination of resistant organisms, including carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii [23]. Solmaz et al. also reported a significant increase in A. baumannii–related infections during the pandemic compared with the pre-pandemic period (47.9% vs. 20.9%; p < 0.001) [16]. Our findings are consistent with the literature, demonstrating a higher rate of A. baumannii infections during the pandemic period compared with the pre-pandemic period (58.89% vs. 35.00%; p = 0.016). This increase may be attributed to prolonged ICU stays, frequent use of invasive and non-invasive mechanical ventilation, immunosuppressive therapies, and extensive exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics. Overall, this pattern supports the endemic nature of A. baumannii in ICU settings in Türkiye, where prolonged hospitalization, invasive device use, and high antimicrobial pressure facilitate its persistence and transmission.

Although the overall mortality rates among patients with HAIs were comparable between the two periods, our findings suggest that patient-level factors, rather than temporal changes alone, played a more decisive role in outcomes. Specifically, non-survivors were older and had significantly higher APACHE II and SOFA scores, underscoring the critical impact of disease severity on mortality. In line with previous reports, the increased use of invasive devices such as mechanical ventilation and central venous catheters in non-survivors likely reflects both greater clinical instability and a higher risk of device-associated infections, including VAP and CLABSI.

Regarding microbiological findings, the predominance of A. baumannii and other multidrug-resistant pathogens among non-survivors is consistent with the literature highlighting their association with poor prognosis in critically ill patients. Interestingly, Enterococcus spp. was significantly more frequent in survivors, which may be explained by its relatively lower virulence compared to non-fermentative Gram-negative bacteria or differences in infection source and host response. These findings emphasize that not only the presence of HAI but also the causative pathogen profile contributes to clinical outcomes.

Although a shift in the distribution of HAIs—characterized by a decrease in VAP and an increase in catheter-associated infections such as CAUTI and CLABSI—was observed during the pandemic period, this did not translate into a significant difference in overall mortality. This may be attributable to multiple factors, including changes in ICU practices, resource allocation challenges, prolonged device utilization, and disruptions in infection control measures during the COVID-19 pandemic, as widely reported. Additionally, shorter ICU stays during the pandemic period in our cohort may have influenced the observed infection patterns.

COVID-19 is a potentially life-threatening infection, and bacterial infections in patients monitored in the ICUs during the pandemic have been associated with increased morbidity and mortality rates. A study by Unterberg et al. reported that the mortality rate in patients with sepsis was 52% during the pandemic and 33% before the pandemic, and this difference was found to be statistically significant [24]. In the study by Sarı et al., it was shown that mortality rates in patients admitted to the ICUs during the COVID-19 pandemic increased significantly compared to the pre-pandemic period (52% vs. 69%; p < 0.001) [17]. Similarly, another study reported that the mortality rate was 85.9% during the pandemic period, compared to 65.9% in the pre-pandemic period, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05) [16]. In our study, consistent with the literature, the mortality rate during the pandemic period was found to be significantly higher compared to the pre-pandemic period (40.96% vs. 24.94%; p = 0.00001).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its single-center, retrospective design limits generalizability and restricts control over potential confounders. The use of a simple pre–post comparison does not account for temporal trends, seasonal variation, or secular changes in ICU practice; therefore, causal inferences regarding the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic should be interpreted cautiously. Additionally, the absence of data on healthcare staffing and antimicrobial consumption (e.g., defined daily doses per 1,000 patient-days) limits the ability to evaluate system-level pressures and antimicrobial selection dynamics that may have influenced infection epidemiology during the study period.

Conclusion

In this study, although overall HAI rates remained comparable between the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods, a significant increase in mortality was observed during the pandemic, accompanied by a marked rise in A. baumannii-related infections. These findings suggest that adverse outcomes during the pandemic were more closely associated with shifts in pathogen distribution—particularly the predominance of multidrug-resistant A. baumannii and increased clinical severity rather than changes in overall HAI incidence. Therefore, infection prevention strategies during pandemics should not only focus on maintaining standard infection control practices but also prioritize targeted measures against high-risk, multidrug-resistant pathogens such as A. baumannii, alongside strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance systems.
