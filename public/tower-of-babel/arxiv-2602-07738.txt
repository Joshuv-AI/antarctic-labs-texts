Learnable Chernoff Baselines for Inference-Time Alignment
Sunil Madhow, Yuchen Liang, Ness Shroff, Yingbin Liang, Yu-Xiang Wang

Abstract
We study inference-time reward-guided alignment for generative models. Existing methods often rely on either architecture-specific adaptations or computationally costly inference procedures. We introduce Learnable Chernoff Baselines (LCBs) as a method for efficiently and approximately sampling from the exponentially tilted kernels that arise from KL-regularized reward alignment. Using only black-box sampling access to the pretrained model, LCBs implement a form of rejection sampling with adaptively selected acceptance probabilities, which allows fine-grained control over inference-compute scaling. We establish total-variation guarantees to the ideal aligned model, and demonstrate in both continuous and discrete diffusion settings that LCB sampling closely matches ideal rejection sampling while using substantially fewer queries to the pretrained model.

Introduction

As Diffusion Models and other generative systems grow in their ability to produce meaningful facsimiles of human data, we face two central questions: (1)
How can we harness pretrained generative models
to tackle specific downstream tasks that are poorly represented in their training corpora? (2)
How can we guarantee safety
by suppressing harmful or undesirable content while amplifying desirable content? The AI alignment literature aims to address these questions by teaching models to optimize reward functions that encode “good behavior.” These can come from human feedback (for commercial models)
(

Preliminaries

We take a pretrained Diffusion Model (DM) to be a Markov process specified by a set of conditional distributions
\{p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(x_{t}|x_{t+1})\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
together with an initial distribution
p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{T}(x_{T})
over a desired sampling space
\mathcal{X}

(
17

(
2

Independently of the DM, we consider a (possibly non-differentiable) target reward function
r_{0}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[-B_{0},B_{0}]

B_{0}>0

\alpha>0

Having fully specified the joint distribution of

under

, we freely marginalize and condition on any subset of timesteps. From
(

\displaystyle:=e^{v_{t}(x_{t})-v_{t+1}(x_{t+1})}p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(x_{t}|x_{t+1})

\displaystyle p^{*}(x_{T})

\displaystyle:=e^{v_{T}(x_{T})-v_{T+1}}p^{\mathrm{pre}}(x_{T})

(2)

where
v_{t}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[-B,B]

soft-value function

(
25

\displaystyle v_{t}(x_{t})

\displaystyle:=\log\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p^{\mathrm{pre}}(\cdot|x_{t})}[e^{r(x_{0})}],\quad t\in[T]

\displaystyle v_{T+1}

\displaystyle:=\log\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p^{\mathrm{pre}}}[e^{r(x_{0})}]

Equations

2

Step 1:
Obtain
n

, and form the dataset

Step 2:
Form an estimate of the soft-value function,

using the data from Step 1. Also estimate any auxiliary quantities required for sampling.

Step 3:
Sample from the process with tilted kernels
\hat{p}(x_{t}|x_{t+1})\propto e^{\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})}p^{\mathrm{pre}}(x_{t}|x_{t+1})

The costs in Steps 1–2 are one-time (or infrequent) preprocessing costs, whereas Step 3 is paid per generated sample.
Thus, if
C_{\mathrm{pre}}:=\mathrm{Compute}_{1}+\mathrm{Compute}_{2}
denotes the total preprocessing compute and

C_{\mathrm{inf}}:=\mathrm{Compute}_{3}

\mathrm{CostPerSample}(S)\;=\;C_{\mathrm{inf}}\;+\;\frac{C_{\mathrm{pre}}}{S},

This paper therefore focuses primarily on efficiently solving the sampling problem in Step 3, which is nontrivial when
\mathcal{X}
is either continuous or high-dimensional discrete. In order to state fully end-to-end TV bounds, however, we also briefly give a recipe for consistently estimating
\hat{v}_{t}
in Step 2.

Proposal Complexity

Though this section has shown that we can approximately obtain samples from
p^{*}(x_{0})
using rejection sampling, Algorithm
1
requires a large number of proposals from the pretrained model at each iteration. The expected total number of proposals,

1

\mathbb{E}[N]\approx\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}\sim\hat{p}_{t+1}}\left[\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1})}[e^{\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})-B}]}\right].

In general, the only bound on
\mathbb{E}[N]
that can be stated is
\mathbb{E}[N]\leq e^{2B}T
. While the

According to our problem specification

may be a loose upper bound on the reward function

implicitly subsumes a
1/\alpha

To remedy this, we introduce a theory of sampling with baselines, which will ultimately allow us to adaptively set efficient acceptance probabilities.

at level

Suppose there is

e^{-\lambda\tau}M(\lambda)\leq\delta

Then, for
\hat{q}_{t}(x_{t}|x_{t+1})
induced by Algorithm

\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}\sim q_{t+1}}[d_{TV}(\hat{q}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1}),\hat{p}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1}))]\\
\lesssim\frac{\delta^{1-2/\lambda}}{(1-c)^{2}}(M(\lambda)M(-\lambda))^{1/\lambda}.

(6)

Lemma

that is “Chernoff certified” – in the sense that the Chernoff bound reproduces the joint baseline condition (Definition

Such a bound can (a) be used explicitly

.

4.2
Learnable Chernoff Baselines: Learning Baselines from Data

We now use our theory to train joint baselines that target the estimated soft-value function. Like learning the soft-value functions
\{\hat{v}_{t}\}
, learning baselines
\{B_{t+1}\}
corresponds to a single upfront cost that is then amortized by cheaper inferences.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a
user-specified
parameter that selects a tradeoff between sampling accuracy and efficiency. Let us inductively construct a set of
mutually-compatible, joint
baselines for
\{\hat{v}_{t}\}
to address the approximate sampling problem. At time
t
, the goal is to sample
x_{t}|x_{t+1}
, where
x_{t+1}
is a state whose distribution is induced by the approximate sampling process from timesteps
T,\dots,t+1
. Therefore, we wish to produce a joint baseline (see Definition
4.1
) for
(\hat{q}_{t+1},p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t})
, where
\hat{q}_{t+1}
is the marginal distribution of
x_{t+1}
under sampling. All probabilities and expectations in this section are defined with respect to this joint distribution for
(x_{t+1},x_{t})
.

Fix
t\in[T]
. Consider parameterizing the baseline as
B_{t+1}(x_{t+1})=b_{t+1}(x_{t+1})+\tau
, where
b_{t+1}\in H
is a member of a hypothesis class
H
and
\tau
is constant with respect to
x_{t+1}
. By the Chernoff bound, we have that the joint baseline condition can be analyzed as

\displaystyle\textstyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{t+1}\sim\hat{q}_{t+1}\\
x_{t}\sim p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1})\end{subarray}}[\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})-{b}_{t+1}(x_{t+1})\geq\tau]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\lambda\tau}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{t+1}\sim\hat{q}_{t+1}\\
x_{t}\sim p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1})\end{subarray}}[e^{\lambda(\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})-b_{t+1}(x_{t+1}))}];\quad\lambda\geq 0

Treating
\lambda
and
b_{t+1}
as fixed, we see that to ensure that
B_{t+1}
is a baseline, the RHS must be
\leq\delta
. This leads to the
(\lambda,b_{t+1})
-dependent choice:

\textstyle\tau\leftarrow\tau_{\lambda,b}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}(\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda(\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})-b_{t+1}(x_{t+1})}).

In other words, for any
b_{t+1}
, we can select
\tau_{\lambda,b}
in order to make
B^{(\lambda,b)}_{t+1}(x_{t+1}):=b_{t+1}(x_{t+1})+\tau_{\lambda,b}
a joint baseline at level
\delta
, where
\lambda>0
is a free parameter.

Moreover, for any choice of
\lambda\geq 2
and
b_{t+1}
, the baseline
B^{(\lambda,b)}_{t+1}
satisfies the Chernoff condition of the TV-MGF Lemma (Lemma
4.2
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}\sim q_{t+1}}

\displaystyle[d_{TV}\left(\hat{q}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1}),\hat{p}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1})\right)]

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta e^{\frac{2}{\lambda}\log{\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log M(\lambda)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log M(-\lambda)}}

where
M(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})-b_{t+1}(x_{t+1}))}]
. Here and as follows,
\mathbb{E}
denotes the joint expectation.

We propose choosing
\lambda\in(2,\Lambda)
and
b_{t+1}\in H
in order to minimize the right hand side, for some fixed ceiling
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>2}
. Taking logs leads to the objective function

\displaystyle J_{t}(\lambda,b_{t+1}):=

\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})-b_{t+1}(x_{t+1}))}]

\displaystyle\textstyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(b_{t+1}(x_{t+1})-\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t}))}]+\frac{2}{\lambda}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

We can use samples to approximate the expectations and produce
\hat{J}_{t}:(2,\Lambda)\times H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{t}(\lambda,b_{t+1})

\displaystyle:=\textstyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\hat{\mathbb{E}}[e^{\lambda(\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})-b_{t+1}(x_{t+1}))}]

\displaystyle\textstyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\hat{\mathbb{E}}[e^{\lambda(b_{t+1}(x_{t+1})-\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t}))}]+\frac{2}{\lambda}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

In particular, we may safely choose
\hat{\lambda},\hat{b}_{t+1}\in\arg\min_{\lambda\in(2,\Lambda),b\in H}\hat{J}_{t}(\lambda,b)
, and then form
\hat{\tau}_{\hat{\lambda},\hat{b}}=(\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\hat{\mathbb{E}}e^{\hat{\lambda}(\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})-\hat{b}_{t+1}(x_{t+1})})/\hat{\lambda}
using the same data.
This is born out by the following theorem, proved as Corollary
F.2
, in the Appendix, which establishes uniform convergence results for the empirical risk minimizer of
J
.

Proposition 4.5

(Learning the LCB)
.

Given
m
samples
(x^{i}_{t+1},x^{i}_{t})\sim\hat{q}_{t+1}p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}
and the associated
\hat{J}_{t}
, let
\hat{\lambda},\hat{b}_{t+1}\in\arg\min_{\lambda\in(1,\Lambda),b\in H}\hat{J}(\lambda,b)
. Let
J^{*}_{t}
be the minimum value of
J_{t}
(assume realized). Then, with probability
1-\eta
over the data

J_{t}(\hat{\lambda},\hat{b}_{t+1})-J^{*}_{t}\leq 2\epsilon_{0}\ \ \ \textrm{ and }\ \ \ \hat{\tau}_{\hat{\lambda},\hat{b}_{t+1}}\in\tau_{\hat{\lambda},\hat{b}_{t+1}}\pm\epsilon_{0}

where all empirical quantities are estimated with the same samples and
\epsilon_{0}:=e^{4B\Lambda}(2\hat{\mathcal{{R}}}_{m}(H)+\sqrt{\frac{\log 1/\eta}{2m}})
, and
\hat{\mathcal{{R}}}_{m}
is the standard empirical Rademacher complexity
(
31
)
.

Thus, for each
t\in[T]
, we can generate a Learnable Chernoff Baseline (LCB), which is simply a joint baseline
B^{(\lambda,b)}_{t+1}(x_{t+1})=\hat{b}_{t+1}(x_{t+1})+\hat{\tau}_{\hat{\lambda},\hat{b}}+\epsilon_{0}
. Given LCBs
\hat{B}_{T+1}\dots\hat{B}_{1}
for each soft-value function
\hat{v}_{T},\dots\hat{v}_{0}
, we can then generate samples according to Algorithm
2
.

6
Conclusion

We introduced Learnable Chernoff Baselines (LCBs) as a method for performing quick sampling from tilted generative processes.
Our methodology is especially valuable in the ill-conditioned exponential tilt regime induced by KL alignment geometry. Future empirical directions include prompt-conditioned rewards, values and baselines, and to use these trained modules across many downstream tasks. LCBs are limited by soft-value approximation, which is an active area of study in the literature
(
28
)
.

Impact Statement

This paper develops inference-time alignment methods for pretrained generative models. When reward functions reflect socially beneficial objectives (e.g., safety constraints or expert feedback), such methods may improve safety and usefulness by steering generations toward desired behavior without updating model weights. However, these techniques are dual-use: optimizing adversarial or poorly specified rewards can induce pathological outputs, and our experiments suggest the method can efficiently amplify regions of low model support, which could be misused to amplify rare harmful behaviors. Mitigating these risks requires careful reward design, robust evaluation and red-teaming (including against adversarial rewards), and appropriate safeguards in deployment and access.

(exact RS target)

Reverse-time kernels are the learned-value tilts

\hat{p}_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{t+1})\propto e^{\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})}\,p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{t+1}),

(with an analogous definition for
\hat{p}_{T}
). This is the process targeted by
exact
per-step rejection sampling when using
\hat{v}_{t}
.

\hat{q}

Baseline-induced process (approximate sampler)

The process induced by baselined rejection sampling: propose from
p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t+1})
and accept with probability

\min\{1,\exp(\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})-B_{t+1}(x_{t+1}))\}.

The resulting accepted transition defines
\hat{q}_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t+1})
, and marginals are obtained by propagation

\hat{q}_{t}(x_{t})=\int\hat{q}_{t+1}(x_{t+1})\,\hat{q}_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{t+1})\,dx_{t+1}.

Appendix B
Related works

The objective presented in (
1
) and its analysis via soft-value functions (Section
2
) are common starting points for research in safety and alignment, though recent work has challenged the use of KL divergence as regularization
[
21
]
. The conditional kernels
\hat{p}(x_{t}|x_{t+1})
can be targeted both by fine-tuning methods and inference-time methods.

Finetuning approaches include applying PPO to reward plus divergence
[
15
]
, or ignoring the divergence constraint all together
[
7
]
.

To our knowledge, the most classical examples of inference-time alignment procedures are classifier guidance – which steers continuous diffusion processes by means of an external classifier– and classifier-free guidance
[
19
]
, which moves the classifier into the base model and sets a guidance scale. Both are primarily applied to continuous Gaussian diffusion. Classifier guidance corresponds to the special case of our setting where
r(x_{0})=\log p(\texttt{class}|x_{0})
, where
class
is the target class. The principled extension of classifier guidance is to inject
\nabla_{x}\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})
at every step of diffusion.

Recent works use the soft-value formulation to perform guidance in LLMs by scoring prefixes
[
12
,
39
]
. In the diffusion context,
[
28
,
49
]
use SMC techniques to sample from a distribution proportional to
\mathbb{E}[r(x_{0})|x_{t}]p^{\mathrm{pre}}(x_{t}|x_{t+1})
.
[
24
]
seeks to extend such inference-time techniques to deterministic flow models, which is relevant to this work as our work also primarily addresses the case where
p^{\mathrm{pre}}(\cdot|x_{t+1})
is not a Dirac delta. This paper was inspired by
[
43
]
, which sets down an RL-inspired framework for alignment. Unlike existing works, the proposals in this paper (a) provide theoretical guarantees on the sampling distribution (b) scale compute adaptively according to the problem instance, where SMC methods scale inference-time compute by an integer corresponding to the number of particles.

Finally, it has been widely observed that Best-of-N (BoN), which simply draws
N
samples from
p^{\mathrm{pre}}(x_{0})
and picks the sample with the highest reward, is a very difficult baseline to “beat”
[
16
,
20
,
6
]
. Despite its lack of adaptivity to the base diffusion model, BoN is empirically shown to achieve very competitive “win rates” (rate at which BoN output beats unalligned output) at a fixed KL budget
[
6
]
. However, it is not well-known what kind of variational problem BoN is solving, except in simple special cases
[
47
]
, and BoN is known to suffer from “reward hacking” for large
N

[
20
]
. More importantly, like SMC, BoN locks us into at least doubling the cost of inference (with the minimum choice of
N=2
), which may be beatable with more adaptive methods.

Technically, the LCB objective we propose is related to tilted empirical risk minimization
[
26
]
, though we arrived at it independently.

Proposition C.2
.

\tilde{p}_{t}(x_{t})=p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{t+1})e^{\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})}

D.3
TV Analysis

In this section, we present our results about the TV error of sampling with baselines. First, we give the classic decomposition into stepwise error.

Lemma D.4

(TV timestep decomposition, Lemma
4.3
in the main body)
.

d_{TV}(\hat{q}(x_{0}),\hat{p}(x_{0}))\leq d_{TV}(\hat{q}_{T},\hat{p}_{T})+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}\sim\hat{q}_{t+1}(\cdot)}[d_{TV}(\hat{q}(x_{t}|x_{t+1}),\hat{p}(x_{t}|x_{t+1}))]

Proof.

We work by (backward) induction. As our inductive hypothesis, we take

d_{TV}(\hat{q}_{k}(x_{k}),\hat{p}_{k}(x_{k}))\leq d_{TV}(\hat{q}_{T},\hat{p}_{T})+\sum_{t=k}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}\sim\hat{q}_{t+1}}\left[d_{TV}(\hat{q}(x_{t}|x_{t+1}),\hat{p}(x_{t}|x_{t+1}))\right]

The base case at
k=T
is definitional.

Denote by
\hat{q}_{k+1}\hat{Q}_{k}:=\int\hat{q}_{k+1}(x_{k+1})\hat{q}_{k}(x_{k}|x_{k+1})dx_{k+1}
, and likewise define
\hat{p}_{k+1}\hat{P}_{k}
,
\hat{q}_{k+1}\hat{P}_{k}
, and so on.

We have

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\hat{q}_{k+1}\hat{Q}_{k},\hat{p}_{k+1}\hat{P}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\hat{q}_{k+1}\hat{Q}_{k},\hat{q}_{k+1}\hat{P}_{k})+d_{TV}(\hat{q}_{k+1}\hat{P}_{k},\hat{p}_{k+1}\hat{P}_{k}).

The first term is clearly

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{q}_{k+1}}[d_{TV}(\hat{q}(x_{k}|x_{k+1}),\hat{p}(x_{k}|x_{k+1}))].

The second term is bounded by

d_{TV}(\hat{q}_{k+1},\hat{p}_{k+1}),

as applying a transition kernel to two distributions cannot increase their TV distance.

Applying the inductive hypothesis to the second term completes the argument.
∎

The remainder of the section is concerned with bounding the summands. We begin with a simple measure-theoretic lemma.

Lemma D.5
.

Let
Q
and
P
be measures on a space
\mathcal{X}
with
Q\ll P
. Assume there exists a set
E\subset\mathcal{X}
so that
\frac{dQ}{dP}(x)
is constant on
E
. Then

d_{TV}(Q,P)\leq Q(E^{c})+P(E^{c})

Proof.

To begin, observe that

d_{TV}(Q,P)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}|\frac{dQ}{dP}-1|dP=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{E}|\frac{dQ}{dP}-1|dP+\int_{E^{c}}|\frac{dQ}{dP}-1|dP\right).

Let
\kappa:=\frac{dQ}{dP}|_{E}
. Then,

Q(E)=\int_{E}\frac{dQ}{dP}dP=\kappa P(E).

Therefore, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{E}\left|\frac{dQ}{dP}-1\right|dP=

\displaystyle|\kappa-1|\int_{E}dP

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|Q(E)-P(E)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|Q(E^{c})-P(E^{c})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Q(E^{c})+P(E^{c}).

Moreover, by the triangle inequality we have

\int_{E^{c}}|\frac{dQ}{dP}-1|dP\leq\int_{E^{c}}\frac{dQ}{dP}dP+\int_{E^{c}}dP=Q(E^{c})+P(E^{c}).

Therefore

d_{TV}(Q,P)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(Q(E^{c})+P(E^{c})+Q(E^{c})+P(E^{c})\right)=Q(E^{c})+P(E^{c}).

∎

We now argue that under approximate rejection sampling, the ratio condition from the previous lemma is satisfied on the complement of the “Exceedance Region” – the part of the sampling space where the envelope fails.

Lemma D.6
.

Consider a target distribution
p(x)=\tilde{p}(x)/Z_{p}
, where
\tilde{p}
is known but
Z_{p}
is not. Consider a proposal distribution
\mu
and constant
B\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with an exceedance set

F=\{x\ :\frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{\mu(x)B}>1\}

Let
q
be the distribution induced by accepting proposals from
\mu
with probability

a(x)=\min\{1,\frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{\mu(x)B}\}

Then
\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}
is constant on
E:=F^{c}
, and

d_{TV}(q,p)\leq\frac{\mu(F)}{\frac{Z_{p}p(E)}{B}}+p(F)

(9)

Moreover, if
p(x)/\mu(x)\in(1/M,M)
for all
x
, then we have

d_{TV}(q,p)\leq\max\{\frac{MB}{Z_{P}},1\}\mu(F)+M\mu(F)

(10)

Proof.

q
is given as

q(x)=\frac{1\{x\in E\}\times\frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{\mu(x)B}\times\mu(x)+1\{x\notin E\}\times\mu(x)}{Z_{q}}

where

Z_{q}=\int 1\{x\in E\}\times\frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{\mu(x)B}\times\mu(x)+1\{x\notin E\}\times\mu(x)dx

=Z_{p}p(E)/B+\mu(E^{c})\geq Z_{p}p(E)/B

Clearly

is constant on

d_{TV}(q,p)\leq q(F)+p(F)

The first term can be analyzed as

q(F)=\int_{F}\frac{\mu(x)}{Z_{q}}=\frac{\mu(F)}{Z_{q}}\leq\frac{\mu(F)}{\frac{Z_{p}p(E)}{B}}

To prove the the final inequality, we repeat the argument, but instead lower bound

as

\displaystyle Z_{q}

\displaystyle=Z_{p}p(E)/B+\mu(F)

\displaystyle\geq Z_{p}M\mu(E)/BM+\mu(F)

\displaystyle=(1-\mu(F))Z_{p}/BM+\mu(F)

\displaystyle\geq\min\{Z_{p}/BM,1\}

where we use the fact that the average of

and

\min\{1,Z_{p}/BM\}
. We also perform the change of measure

.
∎

Specializing to the setting of the paper, we obtain the “Exceedance Error Lemma.” Below, for any set

D.3.1

Until now,

All cases of interest are subsumed by allowing the Chernoff certificate to hold on an event,

In the following, we define

and the inner expectation is with respect to

, the arbitrary case follows from linearity of expectation. Specifically, by the TV:MGF Lemma, we have shown that, for any

and we may take expectations on either side with respect to an arbitrary

∎

Remark E.5

Theorem E.6

(Proposal complexity of analytic baseline)
.

Consider the (conditional) baseline

for
{v}_{t}
.

F.2
Optimization properties

It is not the case that
J
is globally convex in
\lambda
. However, it still admits a unique minimizer, which can be found using standard 1-d optimization methods. In this discussion,
P_{\lambda}
refers to the exponentially tilted version of the

law,

dP_{\lambda}/dP=e^{\lambda\hat{s}-\psi(\lambda)}
. In our context,

is a joint law over
x_{t+1},x_{t}

Proposition F.3
.

[Uniqueness of
\lambda
minimizer of
J_{\pm}
]
Fix
\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})
and
\hat{b}_{t+1}(x_{t+1})
, and write
\hat{s}:=\hat{v}_{t}-\hat{b}_{t+1}
.
Let

I:=\{\lambda\geq 0:\ \psi(\lambda)<\infty\ \text{and}\ \psi(-\lambda)<\infty\},\qquad\psi(\lambda):=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\hat{s}}].

Assume
\hat{s}
is not almost surely constant,
I\neq\{0\}
, and

\sup_{\lambda\in I}\Big(D(P_{\lambda}\|P)+D(P_{-\lambda}\|P)\Big)\ \geq\ \log(1/\delta),

where
P_{\lambda}
is the exponentially tilted law
dP_{\lambda}\propto e^{\lambda\hat{s}}\,dP
.
Then the objective

J(\lambda)\ :=\ \frac{\psi(\lambda)+\psi(-\lambda)+\log(1/\delta)}{\lambda}

has a unique minimizer
\lambda^{*}\in I
.

Proof.

Let
c=\log{1/\delta}
.
J
is differentiable on
I
, with stationarity condition

\psi^{\prime}(\lambda)/\lambda-\psi(\lambda)/\lambda^{2}-\psi^{\prime}(-\lambda)/\lambda-\psi(-\lambda)/\lambda^{2}-c/\lambda^{2}=0

\iff\lambda\mathbb{E}_{P_{\lambda}}[\hat{s}]-\psi(\lambda)-\lambda\mathbb{E}_{P_{-\lambda}}[\hat{s}]-\psi(-\lambda)=c

\iff D(P_{\lambda}||P)+D(P_{-\lambda}||P)=c

We know that

is a continuous, strictly increasing function: since
\hat{s}
is non-constant, we have

. Also

.

Thus, the critical point can only be attained for only one

. Moreover, the condition that

ensures that the critical point is reached.

We have also seen that,

to the left of the critical point and
J^{\prime}\geq 0
to the right of the critical point. Thus

is unimodal.
∎

In practice,

H.3
LLaDA

Methodology

Our pretrained model is LLaDA
[
32
]
, a masked language model with 8 billion parameters. In masked language diffusion, our space
\mathcal{X}
is the space of sequences of
L
tokens, including a mask token
M\in\mathcal{X}
. In order to output
L
tokens, LLaDA begins with a completely masked sentence
p_{T}=\delta_{M^{L}}
. LLaDA uses bidirectional attention to predict each token (in a block), and retains a random or structured subset of the predicted tokens (which defines
p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t})
).

The pretrained model,
p^{\mathrm{pre}}
, is defined by giving LLaDA the prompt

‘
‘Write a creative story of exactly 3 sentences. Stylistically, it is preferred that the first word of each sentence start with ’A’, ’B’, and ’C’ respectively.”

with
L=32
and sampling its output at high temperature (
1.12
).

We learn soft value functions by finetuning RoBERTa (
distilroberta-base
, 82 million parameters) with a simple prediction head and learned time embedding. We learn a classifier
p_{\theta}(c,x,t)\approx\Pr[r(x_{0})=c|x_{t}=x]
, for
c\in\{0,1/3,2/3,1\}
using multinomial regression, and at any temperature
\alpha
form the soft-value estimate

\displaystyle v_{\theta}(x,t,\alpha)=\log\sum_{c\in\{0,1/3,2/3,1\}}p_{\theta}(c,x,t)e^{c/\alpha}

where
p_{\theta}
is trained on 5000 trajectories sampled from LLaDA.

We now elaborate on the optimization procedure for the baseline function, which is also parameterized as RoBERTa with a time embedding.

In order to mitigate training instability and minimize engineering complexity, the baseline network is pretrained to learn
b_{\phi}(x_{t+1},t)\approx\mathbb{E}[\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})|x_{t+1}]
using data
x_{t+1},x_{t}\sim p^{\mathrm{pre}}(x_{t+1},x_{t})
and an MSE objective. We run Algorithm
3
with
m=350
particles only for setting
\lambda\in[1,6]
. This allows us to mitigate the catastrophic forgetting phenomenon that was encountered in the multivariate Gaussian setting, without resorting to replay buffers or other complicated training techniques. The pretraining of
b_{\phi}(x,t)
uses 2500 trajectories (separately drawn from the data used to train the value function). Training both
\phi
and
\lambda
for a single temperature takes about
15
minutes on an A100 (High Ram) Google Colab notebook.

When performing inference, we clip
B_{t+1}(x_{t+1})=b_{\phi}(x_{t+1},t+1)+\tau_{t+1}
above at
1/\alpha
, which is a uniform upper bound on the soft value function.

Appendix I
Relaxed Coverage Assumption

In Section
4.1
theory, we introduce a worst-case coverage assumption, which is used in Theorem
4.6
, Corollary
4.7
, and Theorem
4.8
. In this Appendix, we weaken it to a condition that can be empirically verified.

As explained in the body of the paper (Section
4.1
), we say a joint baseline
B_{t+1}
has
worst-case coverage at level
c\in(0,1)
(wrt
\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})
) if there exists
c\in(0,1)
such that
\forall x_{t+1}
,
B_{t+1}
satisfies

\Pr_{x_{t}\sim p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1})}[\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})\geq B_{t+1}(x_{t+1})]\leq c

(12)

Importantly, do not require that
c\to 0
in order to prove that sampling error vanishes. It suffices for
c
to be a constant like
\frac{1}{2}
or
\frac{2}{3}
, which is a natural circumstance if
B_{t+1}(x_{t+1})\geq\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1})}[\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})|x_{t+1}]

x_{t+1}
-a.s and
\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})|x_{t+1}
is roughly symmetric. Moreover, we can track the assumption’s effect through our bounds even when
c=1-\epsilon
for some tiny
\epsilon>0
.

Nevertheless, rather than assuming that the condition in (
12
) holds for
x_{t+1}
, it is more realistic to assume that the condition holds
with high probability

\zeta\in(0,1)
with respect to the sampling distribution
\hat{q}_{t+1}(x_{t+1})
. In particular, we define the set of “covered states” at level
c\in(0,1)
to be

\mathcal{C}_{t+1}(c)=\{x_{t+1}\ :\Pr_{x_{t}\sim p^{\mathrm{pre}}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t+1})}\left[\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})\geq B_{t+1}(x_{t+1})\right]\leq c\}

We then say that a baseline has
high-probability coverage at levels
(\zeta,c)\in(0,1)\times(0,1)
if

\Pr_{x_{t+1}\sim\hat{q}_{t+1}}\left[x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{C}_{t+1}(c)\right]\geq 1-\zeta

In other words, for a good set of
x_{t+1}
with measure
1-\zeta
under the sampling distribution,
B_{t+1}(x_{t+1})
upper bounds
\hat{v}_{t}(x_{t})
with probability
c
over next-state proposals
x_{t}
. When
c\in(0,1)
has been fixed, we will omit the dependence of
\mathcal{C}_{t+1}
on
c
.

In the next section of the Appendix, we show that, assuming high-probability coverage at levels
(\zeta,c)
, is sufficient to reproduce the results of Theorem
4.6
(and all of its corollaries), at the cost of an additive
\zeta
term.
