Goltz Syndrome With Large Esophageal Squamous Papillomas—A Unique Cause of Dysphagia Amenable to Endoscopic Treatment
Authors: Pius Ehiremen Ojemolon, Hayalneh Gessessew, Bereket Abraha Molla, Juwairiya Arshi, Raymond Kim, Sonali Sakaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFocal dermal hypoplasia, also referred to as Goltz syndrome, is a rare X-linked dominant multisystem disorder primarily affecting women, with an estimated prevalence of roughly 1 per 1 million individuals. The condition arises from pathogenic variants in the PORCN gene, resulting in disrupted Wnt signaling and subsequent developmental abnormalities involving the skin, skeletal system, and various other tissues. While mucocutaneous papillomas commonly affect the oral cavity and pharynx, distal gastrointestinal manifestations are infrequent. This report describes an exceptionally unusual case of focal dermal hypoplasia with multiple esophageal squamous papillomas causing symptomatic obstruction, which was effectively managed by endoscopic mucosal resection.

INTRODUCTION

Focal dermal hypoplasia (FDH), or Goltz syndrome, is an extremely rare X-linked dominant multisystem genodermatosis caused by pathogenic variants in the PORCN gene, which disrupt Wnt signaling and impair the development of ectodermal and mesodermal tissues (including the skin, skeletal system, and other tissues) formed during the early embryologic process. A large epidemiologic review involving more than 60 million medical records estimates its prevalence at approximately 0.10 per 100,000 individuals.1,2 The condition predominantly affects women, accounting for nearly 90 percent of documented cases, due to male lethality in utero, although mosaic men have been rarely reported.1,2 Clinically, FDH presents with hallmark linear atrophic or hypoplastic skin lesions following Blaschko's lines, fat herniation, and a broad spectrum of skeletal, ocular, dental, and gastrointestinal anomalies.1,2

Mucocutaneous papillomas are well recognized in FDH, occurring most commonly in perioral, ocular, and genital regions, and reflect the underlying dysregulation of epithelial growth in this disorder.1 Involvement of the upper aerodigestive tract is uncommon, and gastrointestinal manifestations, particularly within the esophagus, are rarely reported. Esophageal squamous papillomas associated with FDH are exceedingly rare, with only isolated case reports describing such involvement.3,4 When present, these lesions are typically discovered incidentally during endoscopy, and symptomatic cases are even rarer. When symptoms do occur, they may include dysphagia or chest discomfort.5

Diagnosis of esophageal papillomas relies on endoscopic visualization with histopathologic confirmation. Management depends on the extent of esophageal involvement, presence of symptoms, and associated risks. Proton pump inhibitors can be used for coexisting reflux symptoms, while endoscopic or surgical intervention is reserved for obstructive or symptomatic lesions. Importantly, esophageal papillomas in FDH are typically not considered premalignant.6 Among treatment modalities, endoscopic removal, including endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), often provides the most effective symptomatic relief and functional improvement.7,8

This case highlights an exceptionally rare presentation of FDH involving multiple esophageal squamous papillomas producing symptomatic obstruction with multifocal esophageal involvement rather than diffuse papillomatosis which was managed with a unique therapeutic approach. The patient's history and endoscopic findings emphasize the importance of recognizing unusual gastrointestinal manifestations in this already rare syndrome.

CASE REPORT

A 41-year-old woman with known history of Goltz syndrome and previous right pharyngectomy and right palatine tonsillectomy 2 years before index presentation (with pathology showing benign papillomatous proliferation of tonsillar tissue) presented with progressively worsening dysphagia described as the sensation of food and pills getting stuck in her throat, associated with occasional coughing and choking.

Upper endoscopy showed 2 villous, polypoid, soft, friable esophageal masses noted just proximal to the gastroesophageal junction in the distal esophagus at 34 cm measuring 2 cm and 1 cm, respectively, shown in Figure 1 (the Z-line was regular and was found 35 cm from the incisors); the larger mass was causing partial esophageal luminal obstruction as seen in Figure 2. Another esophageal villous mass lesion measuring 8 mm was noted at 30 cm from the incisors (Figure 3). Multiple biopsies were taken with cold forceps for histology with pathology returning as squamous papilloma.

The patient then underwent repeat upper endoscopy 2 weeks later with successful underwater EMR of the larger mucosal mass in the lower third of the esophagus at the gastroesophageal junction; a total of 25 mm area was resected (Figure 4). Postprocedure, the patient was placed on pureed diet for 1 week then advanced to regular a diet, which she tolerated. She also received pantoprazole 40 mg by mouth twice daily for 1 month. Her dysphagia had significantly improved 1 month postprocedure during follow-up and had resolved during a telehealth visit with her primary care provider 3 months later.

DISCUSSION

Patients with Goltz syndrome frequently exhibit mucocutaneous papillomas involving the oral cavity and pharynx but more distal gastrointestinal involvement is considered uncommon.9,10 In the patient described in this case, a history of papillomatous changes in tonsillar tissue suggests a broader predisposition toward papilloma formation; nonetheless, esophageal involvement remains rare. On extensive literature review, only 10 cases of esophageal papillomas in patients with Goltz syndrome have been previously reported4,6,11–18 since the condition was first described extensively in 1962 by Goltz et al19 while working at the University of Minnesota.

Esophageal squamous papillomas are benign epithelial lesions with very low prevalence only rarely encountered during upper endoscopy. Most reported esophageal squamous papillomas are small, solitary lesions located in the mid to lower esophagus and are incidentally discovered in asymptomatic patients. However, larger lesions or multifocal disease can result in luminal narrowing and symptoms such as dysphagia.18,20 The pathogenesis of esophageal squamous papillomas is incompletely understood but is thought to be multifactorial though most cases are associated with HPV.20 P16 immunostain is very sensitive for HPV infection hence a negative stain has a high negative predictive value for ruling out concurrent HPV infection.21,22 Other proposed risk factors include chronic mucosal irritation from gastroesophageal reflux disease, mechanical trauma, prolonged nasogastric tube use, smoking, and alcohol use.7,20

Endoscopically, the lesions appeared friable, exophytic, and villous, resulting in partial luminal obstruction. Given the patient's progressive dysphagia, multiple lesions, and sizes reaching up to 2 cm, endoscopic resection with histopathologic evaluation was favored over surveillance. There have been previous cases of esophageal papillomatosis treated with EMR, radio-frequency ablation and argon plasma coagulation.4,14,18 In our case, EMR was preferred over radio-frequency ablation and argon plasma coagulation given that our patient's involvement was more focal and mass-like rather than diffuse less raised papillomatosis in which case superficial ablative techniques would be preferred. Underwater EMR was preferred over conventional EMR to optimize visualization and facilitate safe separation of the lesions from the underlying deep muscular layer while maintaining low risk of bleeding, an important consideration in the thin-walled distal esophagus.

Although esophageal squamous papillomas are benign, rare cases concerning for malignant transformation have been reported, particularly in large or multifocal lesions.17,20 In patients with mucocutaneous syndromes such as Goltz syndrome, this case supports complete endoscopic removal followed by surveillance as a reasonable approach for symptomatic individuals. Overall, this case highlights esophageal squamous papilloma as a rare but treatable cause of dysphagia, expands the spectrum of gastrointestinal manifestations associated with Goltz syndrome, and demonstrates the utility of underwater EMR as a safe and effective therapeutic method.
