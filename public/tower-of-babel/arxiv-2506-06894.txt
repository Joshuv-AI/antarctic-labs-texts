Proof of a conjecture by Starr and log-concavity for random commuting permutations
Abdelmalek Abdesselam

Abstract
. We prove a conjecture by Shannon Starr regarding the asymptotics for the number of tuples of commuting permutations with given number of joint orbits. These numbers generalize unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind which count how many single permutations have a given number of cycles. In the case of pairs of permutations, these numbers are related to D’Arcais polynomials and the Nekrasov-Okounkov formula. As a consequence of the above asymptotics, we confirm a log-concavity conjecture in the regime of typical values for the number of joint orbits. As a result of possible indepentent interest in applied mathematics and mathematical physics, we also provide detailed asymptotics, using Mellin transform techniques, for certain multiple series or multivariate Ramanujan sums which are related to ordinary generating functions of Dirichlet convolutions of power laws. Besides these multiple sums asymptotics, our proofs use bivariate saddle point analysis related to the Meinardus theorem in the delicate case of multiple poles for the associated Dirichlet series.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main results

For any
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
, any
n\in\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, and any integer
k
with
0\leq k\leq n
, we define the enumerative quantity
A(\ell,n,k)
as follows. For an ordered
\ell
-tuple
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\ell})

of permutations in the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we define a joint orbit of such a tuple as an orbit in the set
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
for the natural permutation action of the subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
generated by the permutations
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\ell}
. By definition,
A(\ell,n,k)
is the number of tuples
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\ell})
which have exactly
k
joint orbits. Note that when
\ell=1
, i.e., the case of a single permutation, these orbits simply are the cycles of that permutation. For higher
\ell
, these look like discrete
\ell
-dimensional tori inside the set
[n]
(see
[
5
]
and the appendix of
[
27
]
).

For
\ell,n,k
as above, we define the quantity
\mathscr{S}_{\ell}(n,k)
, introduced by Shannon Starr in
[
27
, Conj. 2.3]
, by

\mathscr{S}_{\ell}(n,k):=-\ell\ k\ln k+k\left(\ (\ell-1)\ln n-\ell\ \ln(\ell-1)+\ell+\ln\mathcal{K}_{\ell}\ \right)

where, in keeping with the notation of
[
6
]
,

\mathcal{K}_{\ell}:=(\ell-1)!\ \zeta(2)\zeta(3)\cdots\zeta(\ell)\ ,

in terms of special values of the Riemann zeta function.
Our first main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\ell\geq 4
, there exists a sequence of explicit constants
H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{\ell-1}
, such that for all
s\in(0,1)
, and all sequences
(k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, with

1\leq k_{n}\leq n
that are such that

k_{n}\sim sn^{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}}
, we have the asymptotic equivalence, when
n\rightarrow\infty
,

A(\ell,n,k_{n})\sim\frac{\sqrt{\ell-1}}{2\pi}\times(n-1)!\times\exp\left(\mathscr{S}_{\ell}(n,k_{n})+k_{n}\ \mathscr{E}_{\ell}\left(\frac{k_{n}}{n}\right)\right)\ ,

where

\mathscr{E}_{\ell}(u):=H_{1}u+H_{2}u^{2}+\cdots+H_{\ell-1}u^{\ell-1}\ .

Using bold and very interesting heuristics, Starr discovered the asymptotic formula in the above theorem and formulated it as Conjecture 2.3 in
[
27
]
. His formula, for any
\ell\geq 2
, allowed for logarithmic terms
u^{a}(-\ln u)^{b}
in the correction
\mathscr{E}_{\ell}
to the main quantity
\mathscr{S}_{\ell}
.
However, we found that no logarithms are present as soon as
\ell\geq 4
. The cases
\ell=2
and
\ell=3
are treated separately in the next two theorems, and they indeed involve logarithms. Altogether, Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
, and
1.3
thus provide a complete proof for Starr’s conjecture.
The case of pairs of permutations is the object of the next main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist two explicit constants
I_{1,{\rm log}},I_{1}
, such that for all
s\in(0,1)
, and all sequences
(k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, with

1\leq k_{n}\leq n
that are such that

k_{n}\sim sn^{\frac{1}{2}}
, we have the asymptotic equivalence, when
n\rightarrow\infty
,

A(2,n,k_{n})\sim\frac{1}{2\pi}\times(n-1)!\times\exp\left(\mathscr{S}_{2}(n,k_{n})+k_{n}\ \mathscr{E}_{2}\left(\frac{k_{n}}{n}\right)\right)\ ,

where

\mathscr{E}_{2}(u):=I_{1,{\rm log}}\ u\ln u+I_{1}u\ .

The case of triples of permutations is the object of our third main result.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exist three explicit constants
J_{1},J_{2,{\rm log}},J_{2}
, such that for all
s\in(0,1)
, and all sequences
(k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, with

1\leq k_{n}\leq n
that are such that

k_{n}\sim sn^{\frac{2}{3}}
, we have the asymptotic equivalence, when
n\rightarrow\infty
,

A(3,n,k_{n})\sim\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{2}}\times(n-1)!\times\exp\left(\mathscr{S}_{3}(n,k_{n})+k_{n}\ \mathscr{E}_{3}\left(\frac{k_{n}}{n}\right)\right)\ ,

where

\mathscr{E}_{3}(u):=J_{1}u+J_{2,{\rm log}}\ u^{2}\ln u+J_{2}u\ .

Since some are quite involved, the definitions of the
H,I,J
constants are deferred to the next section §
1.2
. Note that our use of the notation
\sim
,
o(\cdot)
, and
O(\cdot)
conforms to standard practice as in
[
11
]
. In particular, our statements of asymptotic equivalence
f(p)\sim g(p)
, when a parameter
p
goes to some limit, mean that the limit of the ratio
\frac{f(p)}{g(p)}
is
1
. We will also use
\sim
for full asymptotic series expansions.

As a corollary of the previous theorems, we obtain strict log-concavity with respect to
k
of the
A(\ell,n,k)
in the hitherto unexplored regime of typical values of
k
, in the light of the central limit theorem proved in
[
6
]
.

Corollary 1.1
.

For any
\ell\geq 2
, any
s\in(0,1)
, and all sequences
(k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, with

1\leq k_{n}\leq n
that are such that

k_{n}\sim sn^{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}}
, we have

A(\ell,n,k_{n})^{2}>A(\ell,n,k_{n}-1)A(\ell,n,k_{n}+1)\ ,

for
n
large enough.

As oulined in §
1.3
, our proof of the main three theorems is primarily based on bivariate saddle point analysis. It also crucially relies on detailed asymptotics, when
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
, of some remarkable multiple sums already considered in
[
6
]
and denoted by
Z_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}}^{[\ell]}(t)
.
Now letting
\ell
be arbitrary in
\mathbb{N}
, for all
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell})\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell}
, and all
t\in(0,\infty)
, we define these quantities by

Z_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}}^{[\ell]}(t):=\sum_{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell}=1}^{\infty}\delta_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}-1}\ e^{-\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}t}\ .

(1)

The degenerate case
Z_{\varnothing}^{[0]}=e^{-t}
is not excluded from our discussion. The case
Z_{\alpha}^{[1]}(t)={\rm Li}_{1-\alpha}(e^{-t})

is related to polylogarithms. Higher
\ell
cases of these
Z
functions correspond to evaluations at
z=e^{-t}
of ordinary generating functions of multiplicative functions of number theory given by multiple Dirichlet convolution of power laws. These occur in many applications, e.g.,
Z_{1,1}^{[2]}
is the Lambert series studied by Wigert
[
32
]
, and used for proving a mean value theorem for the Riemann zeta function
[
31
, §7.15]
. The particular case
Z_{1,0}^{[2]}
is intimately connected to the Hardy-Ramanujan asymptotics for the partition function
p(n)
.
Our last main result is a reasonably explicit full asymptotic expansion as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
of the
Z
functions for all
\ell
and all complex numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}
, including situations with multiplicities or resonances with the nonpositive integers which account for logarithmic factors.
The next theorem will come as no surprise to analytic number theorists and experts in the Mellin approach to asymptotic expansions (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
and
[
33
]
). We believe it can be useful to applied mathematicians, as well as theoretical and mathematical physicists, to have such asymptotics collected in one place.
By
[z^{k}]f(z)
we will denote the coefficient of
z^{k}
in the power series expansion of
f(z)
at
z=0
. We will also use
{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{\cdots\}
for the indicator function of the enclosed logical statement. As usual,
\Gamma(z)
will denote the Euler gamma function.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any
\ell\geq 0
, and
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell})\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell}
,
as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we have the full asymptotic expansion

Z_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}}^{[\ell]}(t)\sim\sum_{(a,b)}\mathscr{C}_{a,b}^{[\ell]}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell})\ t^{a}(-\ln t)^{b}\ ,

where the sum is over pairs where
a\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{-\alpha_{1},-\alpha_{2},\ldots,-\alpha_{\ell}\}
, and
b\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell\}
, and where

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{a,b}^{[\ell]}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b!}\ \sum_{p,k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}\geq 0}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{b+p+k_{1}+\cdots+k_{\ell}=\ell\}\left([z^{p}]\ z\Gamma(-a+z)\right)

(2)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left([z^{k_{j}}]\ z\zeta(-a+1-\alpha_{j}+z)\right)\ .

The precise meaning of the asymptotic expansion above is that for all
N\geq 0
, the difference between the
Z
function on the left-hand side and the sum on right-hand side restricted to pairs
(a,b)
with
{\rm Re}\ a\leq N
is
o(t^{N})
. This therefore is a complete asymptotic expansion to order
O(t^{\infty})
. As in
[
6
]
, we will use, for
\ell\geq 1
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, the abbreviated notation
Z_{m}^{[\ell]}(t)
for
Z
functions with a descending staircase pattern.
Namely, we let

Z_{m}^{[\ell]}(t):=Z_{m,m-1,\ldots,m-\ell+1}^{[\ell]}(t)\ .

(3)

For the latter functions, we will provide much more explicit asymptotic expansion formulas in §
2
. The most important ones for the proof of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
, and
1.3
are
Z_{\ell}^{[\ell]}
and
Z_{\ell-1}^{[\ell]}
. We will also need leading asymptotics for
Z_{\ell+1}^{[\ell]}
and
Z_{\ell+2}^{[\ell]}
which were already given in
[
6
, §2]
.

1.2.
Definition of the constants featuring in the main theorems

We first address the definition of the constants featuring in Theorem
1.1
, when
\ell\geq 4
.
We define the sequence of constants
(A_{j})_{j\geq 0}
by letting

A_{0}:=1\ ,\qquad A_{1}:=\frac{\zeta(0)}{(\ell-1)\zeta(\ell)}\ ,\qquad A_{2}:=\frac{\zeta(-1)\zeta(0)}{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)\zeta(\ell-1)\zeta(\ell)}\ ,

and then
A_{j}:=0
, for
j\geq 3
.
We also define the sequence of constants
(B_{j})_{j\geq 0}
by letting

B_{0}:=1\ ,\qquad B_{1}:=\frac{\zeta(0)}{(\ell-2)\zeta(\ell)}\ ,\qquad B_{2}:=\frac{\zeta(-1)\zeta(0)}{(\ell-2)(\ell-3)\zeta(\ell-1)\zeta(\ell)}\ ,

as well as

B_{\ell-1}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(-2)\zeta(-1)\zeta(0)}{2\zeta(2)\zeta(3)\zeta(4)}&{\rm if}&\ell=4\ ,\\
\frac{\zeta(-3)\zeta^{\prime}(-2)\zeta(-1)\zeta(0)}{6\zeta(2)\zeta(3)\zeta(4)\zeta(5)}&{\rm if}&\ell=5\ ,\\
0&{\rm if}&\ell\geq 6\ ,\end{array}\right.

and then
B_{j}:=0
, for
j\notin\{0,1,2,\ell-1\}
.

We next define the sequence of constants
(C_{j})_{j\geq 1}
by letting

C_{j}:=\sum_{m\geq 0}B_{m}\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\geq 1}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{m+j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=j-1\}\ A_{j_{1}}\cdots A_{j_{k}}\ ,

for all
j\geq 1
.

Then we introduce the sequence of constants
(D_{p})_{p\geq 1}
defined by letting, for all
p\geq 1
,

D_{p}:=\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\mathbf{m}}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\left\{\sum_{j\geq 2}(j-1)m_{j}=p-1\right\}\times\frac{\left(\sum_{j\geq 2}jm_{j}\right)!}{\prod_{j\geq 2}m_{j}!}\times(-1)^{\sum_{j\geq 1}m_{j}}\times\prod_{j\geq 2}C_{j}^{m_{j}}\ ,

where the sum is over infinite yet almost finite sequences of nonnegative integers
\mathbf{m}=(m_{j})_{j\geq 2}
, i.e., which satisfy
m_{j}=0
for
j
large enough.

We also define the sequence of constants
(E_{j})_{j\geq 1}
by letting

E_{j}:=\sum_{k,j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}\geq 0}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{k+j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=j\}\ A_{k}C_{j_{1}+1}\cdots C_{j_{\ell}+1}\ ,

for all
j\geq 1
.
This in turn allows us to define

F_{j}:=(\ell-1)^{j}\times\sum_{k\geq 1}E_{k}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\geq 1}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=j\}\ D_{j_{1}}\cdots D_{j_{k}}\ ,

for all
j\geq 1
, as well as

G_{j}:=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\geq 1}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=j\}\ F_{j_{1}}\cdots F_{j_{k}}\ ,

also for all
j\geq 1
.
Finally, we can let, for all
j\geq 1
,

H_{j}:=(\ell-1)^{j+1}\ D_{j+1}+G_{j}\ ,

although only the first
\ell-1
values are needed in the statement of Theorem
1.1
.

For
\ell=2
, the needed constants in the statement of Theorem
1.2
are

I_{1,{\rm log}}:=-\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}=\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\ ,

and

I_{1}:=\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(2)}=-\frac{3\ln(2\pi)}{\pi^{2}}\ ,

where we used known special values of the zeta function and its derivative (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Thm. 1.2.4, Cor. 1.3.2, and Cor. 1.3.3]
).

For
\ell=3
, the needed constants in the statement of Theorem
1.3
are

J_{1}:=\frac{2\zeta(0)}{\zeta(3)}=-\frac{1}{\zeta(3)}\ ,

J_{2,{\rm log}}:=-\frac{4\zeta(-1)\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\zeta(3)}\ ,

and

J_{2}:=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)\zeta(3)^{2}}\left[-3\zeta(0)^{2}\zeta(2)-2(\ln 2)\zeta(-1)\zeta(0)\zeta(3)+4\zeta^{\prime}(-1)\zeta(0)\zeta(3)+4\zeta(-1)\zeta^{\prime}(0)\zeta(3)\right]\ .

In the last rather formidable constant, we left the zetas unevaluated since, not only
\zeta(3)
has no simpler expression, but neither does
\zeta^{\prime}(-1)
which is related to the Glaisher-Kinkelin constant.

1.3.
Outline of our approach

For any
\ell\geq 1
, the numbers
A(\ell,n,k)
can be packaged in the bivariate generating function

\mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x,z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{n!}A(\ell,n,k)x^{k}z^{n}\ .

From a result by Bryan and Fulman
[
12
]
(see also
[
5
]
), we have the explicit infinite product representation

\mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x,z)=\prod_{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell-1}=1}^{\infty}\left(1-z^{\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell-1}}\right)^{-x\delta_{1}^{\ell-2}\delta_{2}^{\ell-3}\cdots\delta_{\ell-2}}\ .

(4)

This formula still makes sense and is correct if
\ell=1
, provided one interprets empty products as equal to
1
, and empty tuples as the function
\delta:\varnothing\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with empty graph. In this case, the above reads

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x(x+1)\cdots(x+n-1)}{n!}\ z^{n}=(1-z)^{-x}\ ,

which agrees with the definition of the
A(1,n,k)
as the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind
|s(n,k)|
. The proof of (
4
) in
[
12
]
was in the sense of formal power series in
\mathbb{C}[[x,z]]
, however, as explained in
[
6
, §3]
, the equality holds at the level of analytic functions. Indeed, using the same notations as
[
6
]
, let us define for
|z|<1
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\ell}(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell-1}=1}^{\infty}\delta_{1}^{\ell-2}\delta_{2}^{\ell-3}\cdots\delta_{\ell-2}\ {\rm Log}(1-z^{\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell-1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell}=1}^{\infty}\delta_{1}^{\ell-2}\delta_{2}^{\ell-3}\cdots\delta_{\ell-2}\delta_{\ell-1}^{0}\delta_{\ell}^{-1}\ z^{\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}}\ ,

where
\rm Log
is the principal branch of the complex logarithm. The two series representations for
\mathscr{L}_{\ell}(z)
converge absolutely when
|z|<1
, and therefore the product in (
4
) is an absolutely convergent one, and defines a jointly analytic function of
(x,z)
in the domain
\mathbb{C}\times\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ |\ |z|<1\}
(only the case of real
x
was considered in
[
6
, §3]
, but the same works for any complex value of
x
). Hence,
\mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x,z)
is analytic on the same domain and can be rewritten

\mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x,z)=\exp\left(x\mathscr{L}_{\ell}(z)\right)\ .

As a result, we can extract our coefficients of interest using two Cauchy integrals

\frac{A(\ell,n,k)}{n!}=\oint_{|z|=e^{-t}}\frac{{\rm d}z}{2i\pi z}\ \oint_{|x|=\rho}\frac{{\rm d}x}{2i\pi x}\ \ z^{-n}x^{-k}\ \mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x,z)\ ,

with counterclockwise contours given by circles around the origin with radius
e^{-t}
for the
z
variable, and radius
\rho
for the
x
variable. For the moment,
t
and
\rho
are arbitrary in
(0,\infty)
, but they will have to both be optimized in order to study the above double integral using bivariate saddle point analysis.
Apart from dealing with a double instead of a single integral, we follow the general philosophy explained, e.g., in
[
11
, §5.1]
, namely we will pick the optimal two-dimensional contour in order to minimize the maximum over the contour of the modulus of the integrand. Writing
z=e^{-t+i\theta}
and
x=\rho e^{i\omega}
, with
\theta,\omega\in(-\pi,\pi)
, we consider

\mathscr{M}_{\ell}(t,\rho):=\sup_{\theta,\omega\in(-\pi,\pi)}\left|z^{-n}x^{-k}\ \mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x,z)\right|\ .

We note that

\ln\left|z^{-n}x^{-k}\ \mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x,z)\right|=nt-k\ln\rho+{\rm Re}\left(x\mathscr{L}_{\ell}(z)\right)\ ,

and

{\rm Re}\left(x\mathscr{L}_{\ell}(z)\right)\leq\left|x\mathscr{L}_{\ell}(z)\right|\leq\rho\sum_{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell}=1}^{\infty}\delta_{1}^{\ell-2}\delta_{2}^{\ell-3}\cdots\delta_{\ell-2}\delta_{\ell-1}^{0}\delta_{\ell}^{-1}\ |z|^{\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}}\ .

We thus see that the maximum is reached at
\theta=\omega=0
, and that

\ln\mathscr{M}_{\ell}(t,\rho)=nt-k\ln\rho+\rho Z_{\ell-1}^{[\ell]}(t)\ ,

(5)

where we took advantage of the notation introduced in (
1
) and (
3
).
The optimal
t
and
\rho
are obtained by solving the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ln\mathscr{M}_{\ell}(t,\rho)&=&0\ ,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\ln\mathscr{M}_{\ell}(t,\rho)&=&0\ ,\end{array}\right.

i.e.,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}n&=&\rho Z_{\ell}^{[\ell]}(t)\ ,\\
k&=&\rho Z_{\ell-1}^{[\ell]}(t)\ .\end{array}\right.

We refer to
[
6
, §2]
for the basic properties of the
Z
functions, and in particular their strict positivity and smoothness on
(0,\infty)
, and the relation
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}Z_{\ell-1}^{[\ell]}(t)=-Z_{\ell}^{[\ell]}(t)
used above.
We define the function

h_{\ell}(t):=(\ell-1)\times\frac{Z_{\ell-1}^{[\ell]}(t)}{Z_{\ell}^{[\ell]}(t)}\ ,

(6)

where the
\ell-1
factor is introduced as a matter of convenience, so that
h_{\ell}(t)\sim t
when
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
. We will show in §
2
that
h_{\ell}
is a bijection from
(0,\infty)
onto
(0,\ell-1)
and therefore has a well defined inverse function
h_{\ell}^{-1}:(0,\ell-1)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
.

Now consider a sequence
(k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
as in the statement of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
, and
1.3
. For
n
large enough, we have
0<k<n
because
s\in(0,1)
.
Simple algebra shows that the unique solution
t_{n},\rho_{n}
to the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}n&=&\rho_{n}Z_{\ell}^{[\ell]}(t_{n})\ ,\\
k_{n}&=&\rho_{n}Z_{\ell-1}^{[\ell]}(t_{n})\ ,\end{array}\right.

is obtained by letting

t_{n}:=h_{\ell}^{-1}\left(\frac{(\ell-1)k_{n}}{n}\right)\ ,

(7)

and then

\rho_{n}:=\frac{n}{Z_{\ell}^{[\ell]}(t_{n})}\ ,

(8)

which we will assume for the rest of the proof of the main theorems.
The above choice of contours gives us the multiplicative decomposition

\frac{A(\ell,n,k_{n})}{n!}=\mathscr{M}_{\ell}(t_{n},\rho_{n})\times\mathscr{I}_{n}\ ,

(9)

where the second factor is the integral

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\oint_{|z|=e^{-t_{n}}}\frac{{\rm d}z}{2i\pi z}\ \oint_{|x|=\rho_{n}}\frac{{\rm d}x}{2i\pi x}\ \ \frac{z^{-n}x^{-k_{n}}\ \mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x,z)}{e^{nt_{n}}\rho_{n}^{-k_{n}}\ \mathscr{G}_{\ell}(\rho_{n},e^{-t_{n}})}

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\theta}{2\pi}\ \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\omega}{2\pi}\ \ \mathcal{I}_{n}(\theta,\omega)\ ,

with the last integrand given by

\mathcal{I}_{n}(\theta,\omega):=\frac{e^{-in\theta}e^{-ik_{n}\omega}\exp\left(\rho_{n}e^{i\omega}\mathscr{L}_{\ell}\left(e^{-t_{n}+i\theta}\right)\right)}{\exp\left(\rho_{n}\mathscr{L}_{\ell}\left(e^{-t_{n}}\right)\right)}\ .

(11)

By design, the maximum of
|\mathcal{I}_{n}(\theta,\omega)|
is equal to
1
and is achieved at
\theta=\omega=0
.
Our proof of the main theorems rely on precise asymptotics for the prefactor
\mathscr{M}_{\ell}(t_{n},\rho_{n})
using the results of §
2
, and careful bivariate saddle point analysis for the integral
\mathscr{I}_{n}
provided in §
3
.

1.4.
Related work

The main motivation for the present article is the conjecture by the author
[
4
]
which says that the
A(\ell,n,k)
should be log-concave with respect to
k
. In the particular case
\ell=2
, this conjecture is older and due to Heim and Neuhauser
[
16
, Challenge 3]
.
For
\ell=2
, as explained in
[
5
]
, the equation (
4
) reduces to

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{n!}A(2,n,k)x^{k}z^{n}=\prod_{\delta=1}^{\infty}(1-z^{\delta})^{-x}\ ,

which relates to the D’Arcais polynomials and the Nekrasov-Okounkov formula.
We believe Corollary
1.1
is the first result which establishes the log-concavity for typical values of
k
.

A central problem in analytic number theory is to obtain estimates on partial sums
\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)
where
f(n)
is a function of number-theoretic interest.
Strict partial sum with a sharp cutoff are difficult to analyze. One typically has to modify the definition to
\sum^{\prime}
so the last term counts for half if
x
is an integer.
Such partial sums can also be written
\sum_{n}f(n)\phi(tn)
where
t=\frac{1}{x}
and
\phi(t)={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{0<t\leq 1\}
. Smooth summatory functions like
\phi(t)=e^{-t}
are much nicer and more amenable to the derivation of full asymptotic expansions as in Theorem
1.4
(see the insightful discussion in
[
28
, §3.7]
and
[
18
, p. 33]
). The above sums were also considered, as some renormalization of an a priori divergent series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(n)
, by Ramanujan (see
[
9
]
). The two main techiniques used for deriving asymptotics for these sums are the real variable methods based on the Euler-MacLaurin formula, and the complex variable methods based on the Mellin transform. One could argue that the switch from the former to the latter, as a preferred choice, can be traced back to Riemann’s article on the zeta function
[
26
]
(see also the appendix of
[
14
]
for an English translation).
Although the proofs of Theorem
1.4
and the more explict Proposition
2.1
follow the standard Mellin transform approach
[
15
,
33
]
, we believe they could be useful, given that such asymptotics are needed for many applications (see, e.g.,
[
24
]
).

The main method we use in order to prove Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
, is saddle point analysis. We follow similar steps to those in
[
30
,
13
]
, for example. However, here we need bivariate saddle point analysis. We are in a lucky situation where the two-dimensional contour of integration, which is a torus, does not undergo a change of topology. All we have to do is adjust the radius of each of the two circles involved, and we can thus avoid the more high-powered techniques, like Morse theory, sometimes required for multivariate saddle point problems
[
25
]
.
For
\ell=1
, i.e., Stirling numbers of the first kind, asymptotics have been studied by many authors, see in particular the work of Moser and Wyman
[
23
]
.
However, for
\ell\geq 2
, we are not aware of rigorous asymptotics for the
A(\ell,n,k)
before the present article.
Our bivariate saddle point analysis shares some thematic similarity to the works
[
17
,
21
]
. We note that the symmetrization trick we used for proving the crucial log-convexity lemma below appeared in
[
22
,
20
]
(see also
[
3
]
).

2.
Complete asymptotics and log-convexity for the
Z
functions

Proof of Theorem
1.4
:

We start with the Mellin inversion formula (see
[
29
, Cor 0.14, p. 177]
),

e^{-u}=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\sigma_{\rm ini}-i\infty}^{\sigma_{\rm ini}+i\infty}\Gamma(w)u^{-w}\ {\rm d}w

valid for
u>0
and
\sigma_{\rm ini}>0
. We use it for
u=\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}t
and insert this in the definition of the
Z
functions. Namely, we write for any fixed
t>0
,

Z_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}}^{[\ell]}(t)=\sum_{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell}=1}^{\infty}\delta_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}-1}\times\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\sigma_{\rm ini}-i\infty}^{\sigma_{\rm ini}+i\infty}\Gamma(w)(\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}t)^{-w}\ {\rm d}w\ .

By the complex Stirling asymptotic formula for the Gamma function in the vertical direction (see
[
7
, Cor 1.4.4]
or
[
29
, Cor 0.13, p. 176]
), one has a bound
|\Gamma(w)|\leq Ce^{-c|{\rm Im}w|}
,
for
|{\rm Im}\ w|ge1
,
with constants
C,c>0
depending on the abscissa
\sigma_{\rm ini}
of the initial contour of integration.
Since

\left|\Gamma(w)\ \delta_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1-w}\cdots\delta_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}-1-w}\ t^{-w}\right|\leq C\ \delta_{1}^{({\rm Re}\ \alpha_{1})-1-\sigma_{\rm ini}}\cdots\delta_{\ell}^{({\rm Re}\ \alpha_{\ell})-1-\sigma_{\rm ini}}\ t^{-\sigma_{\rm ini}}\ e^{-c|{\rm Im}w|}\ ,

we get a finite result when integrating over
w
and summing over the
\delta
’s, provided we made sure to pick
\sigma_{\rm ini}>\max(0,{\rm Re}\ \alpha_{1},\ldots,{\rm Re}\ \alpha_{\ell})
, in order for all series to converge.
Then, Fubini’s theorem applies and gives, after commuting the sums and the integral,

\displaystyle Z_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}}^{[\ell]}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\sigma_{\rm ini}-i\infty}^{\sigma_{\rm ini}+i\infty}\sum_{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell}=1}^{\infty}\Gamma(w)\ \delta_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1-w}\cdots\delta_{\ell}^{\alpha_{\ell}-1-w}\ t^{-w}\ {\rm d}w

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\sigma_{\rm ini}-i\infty}^{\sigma_{\rm ini}+i\infty}\Gamma(w)\ \zeta(w+1-\alpha_{1})\cdots\zeta(w+1-\alpha_{\ell})\ t^{-w}\ {\rm d}w\ .

It is known that the Gamma function
\Gamma(w)
is a meromorphic function on the complex plane with simple poles at
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}:=\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}
. The Riemann zeta function also admits analytic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
except for a simple pole at
w=1
. We refer to
[
7
, Ch. 1]
for these standard facts. In the case of the Riemann zeta function, a lot of specific information can also be found in the books
[
8
,
14
,
29
,
31
]
.
The function
w\mapsto t^{-w}=e^{(-\ln t)w}
is entire analytic.
The above integrand therefore is meromorphic with potential poles contained in the discrete subset of
\mathbb{C}
given by
\mathcal{P}:=\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}\cup\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}\}
. We said potential poles, because these could be cancelled by the mysterious zeros of the zeta function, depending on the placement of the points
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}
in the complex plane.
We can now move the contour, to the left, to a final abscissa
\sigma_{\rm fin}<\sigma_{\rm ini}
, while collecting the contributions of the poles crossed via Cauchy’s residue theorem.
We first replace the improper integral
\int_{\sigma_{\rm ini}-i\infty}^{\sigma_{\rm ini}+i\infty}
by
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\sigma_{\rm ini}-iT}^{\sigma_{\rm ini}+iT}
. We apply the residue theorem to the counterclockwise rectangular contour going successively through the points
\sigma_{\rm ini}-iT
,
\sigma_{\rm ini}+iT
,
\sigma_{\rm fin}+iT
,
\sigma_{\rm fin}-iT
and then back to
\sigma_{\rm ini}-iT
.
The contribution of the two segments with
{\rm Im}\ w=\pm T
go to zero, because the previous exponential decay in the vertical direction for
|\Gamma(w)|
can be made uniform in strips
\sigma_{\rm fin}\leq{\rm Re}\ w\leq\sigma_{\rm ini}
with finite width. Moreover, on such strips the zeta function grows uniformly at most polynomially, i.e.,
\exists K,\kappa>0
, such that for all
w
with
\sigma_{\rm fin}\leq{\rm Re}\ w\leq\sigma_{\rm ini}
and
|{\rm Im}\ w|\geq 1
, we have

|\zeta(w)|\leq K|{\rm Im}\ w|^{\kappa}
(see
[
8
, Thm 12.23]

or
[
29
, §3.4]
).
Trivially, one can deduce similar uniform polynomial growth bounds along strips of finite
width for products of shifted zeta functions as features in the above integral representation for
Z
.
As a result, provided the negative real number
\sigma_{\rm fin}
does not belong to the set
\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}\cup\{{\rm Re}\ \alpha_{1},\ldots,{\rm Re}\ \alpha_{\ell}\}
, the moving of the contour is justified and yields

\displaystyle Z_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}}^{[\ell]}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{P}\\
\sigma_{\rm fin}<{\rm Re}\ q<\sigma_{\rm ini}\end{subarray}}{\rm res}_{w=q}\left(\ \Gamma(w)\ \zeta(w+1-\alpha_{1})\cdots\zeta(w+1-\alpha_{\ell})\ e^{(-\ln t)w}\ \right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\sigma_{\rm fin}-i\infty}^{\sigma_{\rm fin}+i\infty}\Gamma(w)\ \zeta(w+1-\alpha_{1})\cdots\zeta(w+1-\alpha_{\ell})\ t^{-w}\ {\rm d}w\ .

By the bounds discussed above, the last integral remainder term is absolutely convergent and is bounded by a constant times
t^{-\sigma_{\rm fin}}
which is
O(t^{N})
if
\sigma_{\rm fin}<-N
.
To compute the residue at
q
we change variables to
z=w-q
and we multiply the function by
z^{\ell+1}
. This converts the operation of taking the residue into that of taking the coefficient of
z^{\ell}
, i.e., computing

[z^{\ell}]\left(\ z\Gamma(q+z)\ z\zeta(q+z+1-\alpha_{1})\cdots z\zeta(q+z+1-\alpha_{\ell})\ t^{-q}\ e^{(-\ln t)z}\ \right)\ .

Expanding the exponential as
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\ln t)^{k}}{k!}
, the function
z\Gamma(q+z)
as a sum over
p\geq 0
, and the factors
z\zeta(q+z+1-\alpha_{j})

as sums over
k_{j}
, with
1\leq j\leq k
, completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
∎

We believe the multiplicative shift by factors of
z
makes the computation of the residues easier, since one is dealing with convergent power series instead of Laurent series. It also makes the range of the summation indices
p,k_{1},k_{2},\ldots
faster to determine. More explicit formulas for these residues can readily be obtained from the following expansions.

Let
u\in\mathbb{C}
. When
u\neq 1
, we immediately get by Taylor expansion, that for all
k\geq 0
,

[z^{k}]\ z\zeta(u+z)={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{k\geq 1\}\times\frac{\zeta^{(k-1)}(u)}{(k-1)!}\ .

(12)

For
u=1
, we have that for all
k\geq 0
,

[z^{k}]\ z\zeta(1+z)=\ {\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{k=0\}+{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{k\geq 1\}\times\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\gamma_{k-1}\ ,

(13)

where
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots
are the Stieltjes constants (see
[
19
, Thm. 3.2.1]
). In particular,
\gamma_{0}=\gamma
, the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

With regards to the gamma function, for
u\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, we get by Taylor expansion that for all
k\geq 0
,

[z^{k}]\ z\Gamma(u+z)=\ {\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{k\geq 1\}\times\frac{\Gamma^{(k-1)}(u)}{(k-1)!}\ .

(14)

For
u=-a
, with
a\in\mathbb{N}
, we use the functional equation for
\Gamma
to write

\displaystyle z\Gamma(u+z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(1+z)}{(u+z)(u+z+1)\cdots(-1+z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{a}}{a!}\times\frac{\Gamma(1+z)}{\left(1-z\right)\left(1-\frac{z}{2}\right)\cdots\left(1-\frac{z}{a}\right)}\ ,

from which we easily get, for all
k\geq 0
,

[z^{k}]\ z\Gamma(u+z)=\frac{(-1)^{a}}{a!}\times\sum_{m,k_{1},\ldots,k_{a}\geq 0}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{m+k_{1}+\cdots+k_{a}=k\}\times\frac{\Gamma^{(m)}(1)}{m!\ 1^{k_{1}}2^{k_{2}}\cdots a^{k_{a}}}\ .

(15)

We will put the above formulas to good use by deriving asymptotics, for the special case
Z_{m}^{[\ell]}
,

, that are more explicit than what Theorem
1.4
delivers.
For this we will need notation for the set

S_{\ell,m}:=\{-m,-m+1,\ldots,-m+\ell-1\}\ .

Proposition 2.1
.

For
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, and as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
, we have the complete asymptotic expansion

Z_{m}^{[\ell]}\sim\sum_{(a,b)\in(\mathbb{N}\cup S_{\ell,m})\times\{0,1\}}\mathscr{C}_{a,b}^{[\ell]}(m,m-1,\ldots,m-\ell+1)\ t^{a}(-\ln t)^{b}\ ,

where the coefficients are given by the following formulas.

If

, then

\mathscr{C}_{a,b}^{[\ell]}(m,m-1,\ldots,m-\ell+1)=\ {\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{b=0\}\times\frac{(-1)^{a}}{a!}\times\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\zeta(-a-m+j)\ .

If
a\in S_{\ell,m}\backslash\mathbb{N}
, then

\mathscr{C}_{a,b}^{[\ell]}(m,m-1,\ldots,m-\ell+1)=\ {\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{b=0\}\times(-a-1)!\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq a+m+1\end{subarray}}^{\ell}\zeta(-a-m+j)\ .

If
a\in\mathbb{N}\cup S_{\ell,m}
, then

\mathscr{C}_{a,1}^{[\ell]}(m,m-1,\ldots,m-\ell+1)=\ \frac{(-1)^{a}}{a!}\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq a+m+1\end{subarray}}^{\ell}\zeta(-a-m+j)\ ,

and

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{a,0}^{[\ell]}(m,m-1,\ldots,m-\ell+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{a}}{a!}\times\left(1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{a}\right)\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq a+m+1\end{subarray}}^{\ell}\zeta(-a-m+j)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\

Proof:

1st case:
Assume
a\in\mathbb{N}\backslash S_{\ell,m}
.
Then the zeta factors in (
2
) are regular at

and the nonvanishing of the coefficient

. Nonnegativity and the constraint

imply that the only contributing possibility is when

and

, i.e.,

\mathscr{C}_{a,b}^{[\ell]}(m,m-1,\ldots,m-\ell+1)={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\{b=0\}\times([z^{0}]z\Gamma(-a+z))\times\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left([z^{1}]z\zeta(-a-m+j+z)\right)\ ,

and the result follows from the use of formulas (
15
) and (

).

2nd case:
Assume

a\in S_{\ell,m}\backslash\mathbb{N}
. This implies
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}

and

. The latter inequalities can be rewritten as

. Since

. Hence

which, given the various constraints at hand, leaves only one possibility, namely,
p=1
,
k_{j}=1
for
j\neq a+m+1
, and

. Note that we must also have

, i.e., no logarithm. Again, using formulas

), (
12
), and (

),
we arrive at the result stated in the proposition.

3rd case:
Assume
a\in\mathbb{N}\cap S_{\ell,m}
.
Now the gamma function is at a pole, and among the zeta factors, exactly one corresponds to a pole, namely, the factor with

.
We must have
k_{j}\geq 1
for

, which results in the cap

This forces
b=0

or
b=1
, i.e., the staircase pattern with no multiplicities among the

’s limits the power of the logarithm to at most 1, instead of the worst-case bound

.

If
b=1

for the other

),

for the coefficient

.

can be

,

, or
(0,0)
.

.

3.
Bivariate saddle point analysis

Picking up the thread from §

(32)

In this section, we will not separate the cases where
\ell\geq 4
and
\ell\in\{2,3\}
.
We will break the domain of integration into two regions: the major arc or two-dimensional patch rather,

\mathcal{D}_{\rm maj}:=\left\{(\theta,\omega)\in(-\pi,\pi)^{2}\ |\ |\theta|\leq t_{n}\ {\rm and}\ |\omega|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}\ ,

and the minor patch

\mathcal{D}_{\rm min}:=(-\pi,\pi)^{2}\backslash\mathcal{D}_{\rm maj}\ .

Proceeding similarly to
[
6
, §3]
, we note that

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\ Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\rho_{n}\sum_{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell=1}}^{\infty}\delta_{1}^{\ell-2}\cdots\delta_{\ell}^{-1}\ e^{-\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}t_{n}}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega+\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}\theta}{2}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\rho_{n}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}p^{\ell-2}\ e^{-pt_{n}}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega+p\theta}{2}\right)\ ,

where we exploited positivity, and only kept tuples of the form

3.1.
Major patch estimates

In this section we will provide estimates for when
(\theta,\omega)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm maj}
.
We define the cutoff

N_{n}:=\left\lfloor\frac{\pi}{2t_{n}}\right\rfloor\ ,

and use it to weaken the lower bound (
33
) to

{\rm Re}\ Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq 2\rho_{n}\sum_{p=1}^{N_{n}}p^{\ell-2}\ e^{-pt_{n}}\sin^{2}\left|\frac{\omega+p\theta}{2}\right|\ .

For
1\leq p\leq N_{n}
, and
|\theta|\leq t_{n}
, we have
|p\theta|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. We also have
|\omega|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
by hypothesis. Hence
\left|\frac{\omega+p\theta}{2}\right|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.
We now use the concavity of the sine function on the interval
[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
, i.e., the bound
\sin u\geq\frac{2u}{\pi}
for
u
in that interval.
This gives

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\ Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\rho_{n}\sum_{p=1}^{N_{n}}p^{\ell-2}e^{-pt_{n}}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\left|\frac{\omega+p\theta}{2}\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2\rho_{n}}{\pi^{2}e^{\frac{\pi}{2}}}\sum_{p=1}^{N_{n}}p^{\ell-2}(\omega+p\theta)^{2}\ ,

where we also used
pt_{n}\leq N_{n}t_{n}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.
By expanding the square, we can rewrite the previous inequality as

{\rm Re}\ Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\frac{2\rho_{n}}{\pi^{2}e^{\frac{\pi}{2}}}\left[u_{n}(\lambda_{n}^{-1}\theta)^{2}+2v_{n}(\lambda_{n}^{-1}\theta)(\mu_{n}^{-1}\omega)+w_{n}(\mu_{n}^{-1}\omega)^{2}\right]\ ,

where we introduced the new quantities

\displaystyle u_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}^{2}\times\sum_{p=1}^{N_{n}}p^{\ell}\ ,

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\mu_{n}\times\sum_{p=1}^{N_{n}}p^{\ell-1}\ ,

\displaystyle w_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mu_{n}^{2}\times\sum_{p=1}^{N_{n}}p^{\ell-2}\ .

For any
m\geq 0
, the elementary inequalities

\int_{0}^{N}x^{m}\ {\rm d}x\leq\sum_{p=1}^{N}p^{m}\leq\int_{1}^{N+1}x^{m}\ {\rm d}x

immediately give the asymptotic equivalence

\sum_{p=1}^{N}p^{m}\sim\frac{N^{m+1}}{m+1}\ ,

when
N\rightarrow\infty
, which we will use for
m=\ell,\ell-1,\ell-2
, together with the trivial
N_{n}\sim\frac{\pi}{2t_{n}}
.
This combined with (
30
) and (
31
) results in the computation of limits:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell(\ell+1)\mathcal{K}_{\ell}}\times\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{\ell+1}=:u_{\infty}\ ,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{K}_{\ell}}\times\sqrt{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell^{3}}}\times\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{\ell}=:v_{\infty}\ ,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}w_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{K}_{\ell}}\times\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{\ell-1}=:w_{\infty}\ .

We note that
u_{\infty}>0
,
w_{\infty}>0
, and also, with some easy algebra,

u_{\infty}w_{\infty}-v_{\infty}^{2}=\frac{1}{\ell^{3}(\ell+1)\mathcal{K}_{\ell}^{2}}\times\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2\ell}>0\ .

This means that the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}u_{\infty}&v_{\infty}\\
v_{\infty}&w_{\infty}\end{pmatrix}

belongs to the cone
{\rm Pos}_{2}

of positive definite real symmetric
2\times 2
matrices.
Since
{\rm Pos}_{2}
is open in the space of real symmetric
2\times 2
matrices, one can pick
\epsilon>0
such that

\begin{pmatrix}u_{\infty}-\epsilon&v_{\infty}\\
v_{\infty}&w_{\infty}-\epsilon\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm Pos}_{2}\ .

Again, since
{\rm Pos}_{2}
is open, and because of the above limits, we have that

\begin{pmatrix}u_{n}-\epsilon&v_{n}\\
v_{n}&w_{n}-\epsilon\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm Pos}_{2}\ ,

for
n
large enough. For such
n
, we have
\forall(\Theta,\Omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

u_{n}\Theta^{2}+2v_{n}\Theta\Omega+w_{n}\Omega^{2}\geq\epsilon(\Theta^{2}+\Omega^{2})\ .

By applying this to
\Theta=\lambda_{n}^{-1}\theta
and
\Omega=\mu_{n}^{-1}\omega
, we get the lower bound

{\rm Re}\ Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\frac{2\rho_{n}}{\pi^{2}e^{\frac{\pi}{2}}}\ \epsilon\left(\lambda_{n}^{-2}\theta^{2}+\mu_{n}^{-2}\omega^{2}\right)\ .

Now pick some
\eta_{\rm maj}>0
such that
\eta_{\rm maj}<\frac{2\rho_{\infty}}{\pi^{2}e^{\frac{\pi}{2}}}\epsilon
. We have established that for
n
large enough,
\forall(\theta,\omega)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm maj}
we have

{\rm Re}\ Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\eta_{\rm maj}\left(\lambda_{n}^{-2}\theta^{2}+\mu_{n}^{-2}\omega^{2}\right)\ .

(34)

3.2.
Minor patch estimates

In this section we will provide estimates for when
(\theta,\omega)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm min}
. We go back to (
33
), use the inequality
p^{\ell-2}\geq 1
and reverse the use of the identity
{\rm Re}(1-e^{iu})=2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{u}{2}\right)
.
We now have the lower bound

{\rm Re}\ Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq 2\rho_{n}\ {\rm Re}\ W_{n}(\theta,\omega)\ ,

where

\displaystyle W_{n}(\theta,\omega)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}e^{-pt_{n}}\left(1-e^{i\omega+ip\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-t_{n}}}{1-e^{-t_{n}}}-e^{i\omega}\times\frac{e^{-t_{n}+i\theta}}{1-e^{-t_{n}+i\theta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{e^{t_{n}}-1}-\frac{e^{i\omega}}{e^{t_{n}-i\theta}-1}\ .

Taking the real part, we see that

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\ W_{n}(\theta,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{e^{t_{n}}-1}-{\rm Re}\left[\frac{e^{i\omega}\left(e^{t_{n}+i\theta}-1\right)}{\left(e^{t_{n}-i\theta}-1\right)\left(e^{t_{n}+i\theta}-1\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{e^{t_{n}}-1}+\frac{X_{n}(\theta,\omega)}{Y_{n}(\theta)}\ ,

where

X_{n}(\theta,\omega):=\cos\omega-e^{t_{n}}\cos(\omega+\theta)\ ,

and

Y_{n}(\theta):=e^{2t_{n}}-2e^{t_{n}}\cos\theta+1>0\ .

By expanding the cosine, and comparing
\cos\theta
to
1
, we can write

\displaystyle X_{n}(\theta,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\omega-e^{t_{n}}\cos\omega\cos\theta+e^{t_{n}}\sin\omega\sin\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-\cos\omega)\left(e^{t_{n}}-1\right)+e^{t_{n}}\cos\omega(1-\cos\theta)+e^{t_{n}}\sin\omega\sin\theta\ .

We note that

\left|\frac{e^{t_{n}}\cos\omega(1-\cos\theta)}{Y_{n}(\theta)}\right|\leq\frac{e^{t_{n}}(1-\cos\theta)}{Y_{n}(\theta)}=\frac{e^{t_{n}}(1-\cos\theta)}{(e^{t_{n}}-1)^{2}+2e^{t_{n}}(1-\cos\theta)}\ .

By dropping the term with the square, in the denominator, we immediately obtain

\left|\frac{e^{t_{n}}\cos\omega(1-\cos\theta)}{Y_{n}(\theta)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\ ,

which results in the key inequality

{\rm Re}\ W_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\frac{1}{e^{t_{n}}-1}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{Y_{n}(\theta)}\left[(-\cos\omega)(e^{t_{n}}-1)+e^{t_{n}}\sin\omega\sin\theta\right]\ .

(35)

1st case:
We assume
\omega\in(-\pi,\pi)
satisfies
|\omega|>\frac{\pi}{2}
. Then,
-\cos\omega\geq 0
, and we simply toss the corresponding term in (
35
). We use
|\sin\omega|\leq 1
in the last term, and thus get the lower bound

{\rm Re}\ W_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\frac{1}{e^{t_{n}}-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{e^{t_{n}}\sin\theta}{Y_{n}(\theta)}\ .

Now consider the calculus exercise of determining the maximum

\max_{-\pi\leq\theta\leq\pi}|f(\theta)|=\max_{0\leq\theta\leq\pi}f(\theta)

for the odd function

f(\theta)=\frac{\sin\theta}{c-\cos\theta}\ ,

where
c>1
is some constant.
The derivative is

f^{\prime}(\theta)=\frac{\cos\theta-1}{(c-\cos\theta)^{2}}\ .

Therefore, the maximum is reached at
\theta=\arccos(c^{-1})
, and we have

\max_{-\pi\leq\theta\leq\pi}|f(\theta)|=\frac{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)^{2}}}{c-\frac{1}{c}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{2}-1}}\ .

We use this for
c=\frac{e^{2t_{n}}+1}{2e^{t_{n}}}>1
, and deduce

\left|\frac{\sin\theta}{Y_{n}(\theta)}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2e^{t_{n}}}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{e^{2t_{n}}+1}{2e^{t_{n}}}\right)^{2}-1}}=\frac{1}{e^{2t_{n}}-1}\ .

Hence, we obtain

{\rm Re}\ W_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\frac{1}{e^{t_{n}}-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{e^{t_{n}}}{e^{2t_{n}}-1}=\frac{1}{e^{2t_{n}}-1}-\frac{1}{2}\sim\frac{1}{2t_{n}}\ .

(36)

2nd case:
We assume
\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)
satisfies
|\theta|>t_{n}
.
For any real numbers
c_{1},c_{2}
one can of course write, for some suitable angle
\tau
,

c_{1}\cos\omega+c_{2}\sin\omega=\sqrt{c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}}\times\cos(\omega-\tau)\geq-\sqrt{c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}}\ .

By applying that to
c_{1}=1-e^{t_{n}}
and
c_{2}=e^{t_{n}}\sin\theta
,
we deduce from (
35
) that

{\rm Re}\ W_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\frac{1}{e^{t_{n}}-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{Y_{n}(\theta)}\sqrt{(e^{t_{n}}-1)^{2}+e^{2t_{n}}\sin^{2}\theta}\ .

We now notice that

\displaystyle\sqrt{(e^{t_{n}}-1)^{2}+e^{2t_{n}}\sin^{2}\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{t_{n}}{2}}\sqrt{e^{-t_{n}}(e^{t_{n}}-1)^{2}+e^{t_{n}}(1-\cos\theta)(1+\cos\theta)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\frac{t_{n}}{2}}\sqrt{(e^{t_{n}}-1)^{2}+2e^{t_{n}}(1-\cos\theta)}\ ,

with the expression in the last square root being an alternate formula for
Y_{n}(\theta)
.
As a result,

{\rm Re}\ W_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\frac{1}{e^{t_{n}}-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{e^{\frac{t_{n}}{2}}}{\sqrt{Y_{n}(\theta)}}\ .

Now for

t_{n}<|\theta|<\pi
we have
\cos\theta\leq\cos t_{n}
and therefore

Y_{n}(\theta)\geq e^{2t_{n}}-2e^{t_{n}}\cos t_{n}+1=2t_{n}^{2}+O(t_{n}^{3})\ ,

by an asymptotic computation done in
[
6
, §4.2]
.
We have thus shown, in this second case, that

{\rm Re}\ W_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\frac{1}{e^{t_{n}}-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{e^{\frac{t_{n}}{2}}}{\sqrt{e^{2t_{n}}-2e^{t_{n}}\cos t_{n}+1}}\sim\frac{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}{t_{n}}\ .

(37)

We now combine both cases and the corresponding lower bounds (
36
) and (
37
), by picking some
\epsilon>0
, such that
\epsilon<1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}<\frac{1}{2}
. For
n
large enough we have

\forall(\theta,\omega)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm min}
,

{\rm Re}\ W_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\epsilon\ t_{n}^{-1}\ .

Finally, pick some
\eta_{\rm min}>0
such that
\eta_{\rm min}<\epsilon\times\frac{[(\ell-1)s]^{\ell-1}}{\mathcal{K}_{\ell}}
.
From the value of
\rho_{\infty}
in (
27
) and the asymptotics (
26
), we easily see that,
for
n
large enough, we have

\forall(\theta,\omega)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm min}
,

{\rm Re}\ Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)\geq\eta_{\rm min}\ n^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\ .

This gives us, for
n
large enough, the upper bound

\left|\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\rm min}}e^{-Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)}\ \frac{{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\omega}{(2\pi)^{2}}\right|\leq\exp\left(-\eta_{\rm min}\ n^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\right)\ .

(38)

3.3.
Proof of the asymptotics for the
\mathscr{I}
integral

We now perform the change of variables
\theta=\lambda_{n}\Theta
, and
\omega=\mu_{n}\Omega
, and write the main integral as

\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\rm maj}}e^{-Q_{n}(\theta,\omega)}\ \frac{{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\omega}{(2\pi)^{2}}=\frac{\lambda_{n}\mu_{n}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{n}(\Theta,\Omega)\ {\rm d}\Theta{\rm d}\Omega\ ,

where

f_{n}(\Theta,\Omega):={\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\left\{|\Theta|\leq t_{n}\lambda_{n}^{-1}\ {\rm and}\ |\Omega|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\mu_{n}^{-1}\right\}\times e^{-Q_{n}(\lambda_{n}\Theta,\mu_{n}\Omega)}\ .

We note that

t_{n}\lambda_{n}^{-1}\sim\sqrt{\ell\mathcal{K}_{\ell}}\times t_{n}^{-\left(\frac{\ell-1}{2}\right)}\rightarrow\infty\ ,

and

\frac{\pi}{2}\mu_{n}^{-1}\sim\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{K}_{\ell}}{\ell-1}}\times t_{n}^{-\left(\frac{\ell-1}{2}\right)}\rightarrow\infty\ ,

because
\ell\geq 2
, by hypothesis.
We also have, thanks to (

\forall(\Theta,\Omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

|f_{n}(\Theta,\Omega)|\leq e^{-{\rm Re}\ Q_{n}(\lambda_{n}\Theta,\mu_{n}\Omega)}\leq\exp\left(-\eta_{\rm maj}(\Theta^{2}+\Omega^{2})\right)

which is integrable, and therefore puts us in a position to use the dominated convergence theorem.

As in
[
6
, §4.3]
, we let
R(w):=e^{w}-1-w-\frac{w^{2}}{2}
, which has the property that
|R(w)|\leq\frac{|w|^{3}}{6}
whenever

is pure imaginary.
We use this for
w=i(\omega+\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{\ell}\theta)

Before going into the derivation of the asymptotics for
\varepsilon_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
, we pause in order to discuss the role of formal power series in asymptotic analysis. If
f(t)
is some function defined in some domain
\mathcal{D}
which has
0
as a limit point, writing

f(t)\sim a_{0}+a_{1}t+a_{2}t^{2}+a_{3}t^{3}+\cdots

is defined, in every textbook on asymptotic analysis, as the statement that for all
N\geq 0
, the remainder
f(t)-(a_{0}+a_{1}t+a_{2}t^{2}+\cdots+a_{N}t^{N})
is
o(t^{N})
as
t
goes to zero. What unfortunately is not often emphasized in textbooks, with the notable exception of the one by de Bruijn
[
11
, §1.6]
,
is that the above statement is best understood as a precise relation
f(t)\sim A(t)
between a true function on the left-hand side, and a formal power series

A(t):=a_{0}+a_{1}t+a_{2}t^{2}+a_{3}t^{3}+\cdots\ \in\ \mathbb{C}[[t]]\ ,

on the right-hand side.
Each of two worlds connected by such a statement has its own natural internal operations. For functions, one defines sum, products, division, composition, etc.
in a pointwise manner for
t\in\mathcal{D}
. For formal power series one has natural analogues, since
\mathbb{C}[[t]]
is a commutative ring, with invertible elements corresponding to formal power series with nonzero constant terms. One also has the operation of substitution (composition) of a formal power series without constant term into another formal power series. A proper handling of this operation mentioned in
[
11
, §1.6]
would need the notion of summable families and a topology on
\mathbb{C}[[t]]
which can be seen as
\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
The most used topology here is the product of discrete topologies on
\mathbb{C}
.
Perhaps the reason for the lack of emphasis on formal power series is that this is too much abstract algebra for analysis majors. Nevertheless, the beauty and computational effectiveness of the calculus of asymptotic power series
reflects the “functorial” nature of relations
f(t)\sim A(t)
between functions and formal power series. For example, if
f(t)\sim A(t)
and
g(t)\sim B(t)
, then
f(t)g(t)\sim A(t)B(t)
where, by definition, the right-hand side is the product in the ring
\mathbb{C}[[t]]
.
The same holds for the other elementary operations, including composition
[
11
, §1.6]
. An exercise which we leave to the reader is that if
f(t)\sim t+a_{2}t^{2}+a_{3}t^{3}+\cdots
then the locally well defined inverse function
f^{-1}
also has an asymptotic expansion
t+b_{2}t+b_{3}t^{3}+\cdots
where the last series is the compositional inverse of the formal power series
t+a_{2}t^{2}+a_{3}t^{3}+\cdots
. Determining this compositional inverse is the problem of reversion, and its explicit solution typically involves sums over tree graphs (see
[
1
,
2
]
and references therein).
Also note that formal power series like
t+a_{2}t^{2}+a_{3}t^{3}+\cdots
form, for the operation of composition, a group one may call the group of unipotent formal diffeomorphisms.

We now put the above principles into practice. Recall from (
6
), (
22
) and (
23
), that

h_{\ell}(t)=\frac{tV(t)}{U(t)}\ .

By plugging the formal power series for

into
\frac{1}{1+x}=1-x+x^{2}-\cdots
, and then taking the product with the formal power series for
V(t)
, we immediately obtain the full asymptotic expansion

h_{\ell}(t)=C_{1}t+C_{2}t^{2}+C_{3}t^{3}+\cdots

where
C_{1}=1
,
C_{2}
,
C_{3}
, etc. are the constants which have been defined in §
1.2
.

Using explicit formulas for the reversion of formal power series, we have
the full asymptotic expansion for the inverse function

h_{\ell}^{-1}(v)=D_{1}v+D_{2}v^{2}+D_{3}v^{3}+\cdots

where
D_{1}=1
,
D_{2}
,
D_{3}
, etc. are the constants which have been defined in §
1.2
. We found it convenient to give a different name for the variable
v
related to
t
by
v=h_{\ell}(t)
.

Proof of the formula for the
D
constants:

Start from Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 from
[
2
]
which express the compositional inverse as a sum of tree-like Feynman diagrams.
These trees have
p+1
vertices of degree
1
: one root and
p
leaves. They also have, for each
j\geq 2
,

m_{j}
(internal) vertices of degree
j+1
. The amplitude of such a tree graph is

\mathcal{A}_{Fey}=v^{p}\times\prod_{j\geq 2}(-j!C_{j})^{m_{j}}\ .

Rather than deal directly with the complicated symmetry factor
\#Aut(E,\mathcal{F})
in the denominator
of
[
2
, Eq. (129)]
, it is better to use
[
2
, Thm. 8]

in order to forget about the bijection
\mathcal{C}
, i.e., use the forgetful map from Feynman diagram structures
[
2
, Def. 12]
to pre-Feynman diagram structures
[
2
, Def. 11]
. Now the symmetry factor in the denominator becomes the more manageable

p!\prod_{j\geq 2}(m_{j}!\times j!^{m_{j}})
, see
[
2
, Prop. 3]
.
However, one still has to sum over Wick contractions
\mathcal{C}
. For each Cayley tree, there are
\prod_{j\geq 2}j!^{m_{j}}

such contractions, while the number of trees is

\frac{(n-2)!}{(1-1)!^{1+p}\prod_{j\geq 2}\left((j+1)-1\right)!^{m_{j}}}

where we made the vertex degrees apparent in the denominator.
Here, the total number of vertices is

n=1+p+\sum_{j\geq 2}m_{j}\ .

Also note that the quantity
\prod_{j\geq 2}j!^{m_{j}}
appears twice in the numerator and twice in the denominator, and therefore cancels and is not visible in the final formulas.
Cleaning up the result of the above argument gives the explicit formula for the
D
constants in §
1.2
.
∎

We use the expansion of

to write

as an asymptotic expansion when

On the other hand, by inserting

\displaystyle-\ln(1+\eta_{n})

\displaystyle-\ln\left[\rho_{n}\times\frac{\mathcal{K}_{\ell}n^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)^{\ell}k_{n}^{\ell}}\right]

\displaystyle\ln\left[\frac{(\ell-1)^{\ell}Z_{\ell}^{[\ell]}(t_{n})}{\mathcal{K}_{\ell}}\times\left(\frac{k_{n}}{n}\right)^{\ell}\right]

\displaystyle\ln\left[U(t_{n})\times\left(\frac{h_{\ell}(t_{n})}{t_{n}}\right)^{\ell}\right]\ .

Using

\frac{h_{\ell}(t_{n})}{t_{n}}\sim C_{1}+C_{2}t_{n}+C_{3}t_{n}^{2}+\cdots\ ,

The

We then insert into the last formula the asymptotic expansion

Hence, by keeping only the first

5.
The case of pairs and triples of permutations

In the absence of an explicit reversion theorem for mixed asymptotic series with logarithms, we do the computation by hand to high enough order, essentially following the method of asymptotic iteration
[
11
, Ch. 2]
.

The
\ell=2
case:

Following similar steps as in the last section, we now have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}-\ln(1+\eta_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{n}^{-1}t_{n}-1+\ln U(t_{n})+2\ln\left[t_{n}^{-1}h_{2}(t_{n})\right]\ ,

where we introduced the sequence
v_{n}:=\frac{k_{n}}{n}=h_{2}(t_{n})
.
From (
18
) and (
20
), we derive

\displaystyle t^{-1}h_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t\ln t+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(2)}t+o(t)}{1+\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t+o(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t\ln t+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(2)}t+o(t)\right)\times\left(1+\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t+o(t)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t\ln t+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)-\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t+o(t)\ .

Hence,

v_{n}=t_{n}-\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t_{n}^{2}\ln t_{n}+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)-\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t_{n}^{2}+o(t_{n}^{2})\ ,

which, turned around, gives

where we used

, to convert

into

Let us introduce the quantity

such that

By expanding

we see that

As a result,

and

\varepsilon_{n}=v_{n}^{-1}t_{n}-1=\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}v_{n}\ln v_{n}+\frac{\zeta(0)-\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(2)}v_{n}+o(v_{n})\ .

We have

\ln U(t_{n})=\ln\left[1+\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t_{n}+o(t_{n})\right]=\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t_{n}+o(t_{n})\ ,

while

\displaystyle\ln\left[t_{n}^{-1}h_{2}(t_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln\left[1-\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t_{n}\ln t_{n}+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)-\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t_{n}+o(t_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t_{n}\ln t_{n}+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)-\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}t_{n}+o(t_{n})\ .

It is also easy to see, by expanding

, that

Putting everything together we get

\Delta_{n}=-\frac{\zeta(0)}{\zeta(2)}v_{n}\ln v_{n}+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(0)}{\zeta(2)}v_{n}+o(v_{n})\ .

The first two terms are what went into the definition of

. The remainder

∎

6.
Log-concavity

We now provide a proof of Corollary
1.1
.
Take a sequence
k_{n}
as in the statements of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
, or
1.3
, whichever is appropriate given the value of
\ell

The shifted sequences
k_{n}-1
and

\Upsilon_{n}:=2\ln A(\ell,n,k_{n})-\ln A(\ell,n,k_{n}-1)-\ln A(\ell,n,k_{n}+1)\ .

It can be written as

\Upsilon_{n}=\ell\ \Upsilon_{n,{\rm main}}+\Upsilon_{n,{\rm err}}\ .

\mathscr{S}_{\ell}
which is not linear in

\Upsilon_{n,{\rm err}}

We now compute

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{n,{\rm main}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2k_{n}\ln k_{n}+(k_{n}-1)\ln(k_{n}-1)+(k_{n}+1)\ln(k_{n}+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{n}\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{k_{n}}\right)\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{k_{n}}\right)+\left(1+\frac{1}{k_{n}}\right)\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{k_{n}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{n}\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{k_{n}}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{k_{n}}-\frac{1}{2k_{n}^{2}}\right)+\left(1+\frac{1}{k_{n}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{k_{n}}-\frac{1}{2k_{n}^{2}}\right)+o\left(\frac{1}{k_{n}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k_{n}}+o\left(\frac{1}{k_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{-1}n^{-\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right)}+o\left(n^{-\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right)}\right)\ .

Now the term
\Upsilon_{n,{\rm err}}
is a linear combination of expressions of the form

k_{n}\left(\frac{k_{n}}{n}\right)^{a}\left(\ln n-\ln k_{n}\right)^{b}

with
a\geq 1
, which expanded becomes a combination of expressions

n^{-a}(\ln n)^{c}k_{n}^{a+1}(\ln k_{n})^{d}\ .

Consider the function
f(k)=k^{a+1}(\ln k)^{d}

and let us compute the following second order finite difference

L(k):=2f(k)-f(k-1)-f(k+1)
,
when
k
is large. We claim that this finite difference is
O(k^{a-1}(\ln k)^{d})
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle L(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2k^{a+1}(\ln k)^{d}-(k-1)^{a+1}\left(\ln k+\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{d}

\displaystyle-(k+1)^{a+1}\left(\ln k+\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k^{a+1}(\ln k)^{d}\left[2-\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{a+1}-\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{a+1}\right]

\displaystyle-(k-1)^{a+1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\binom{d}{j}(\ln k)^{d-j}\left(\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{j}

\displaystyle-(k+1)^{a+1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\binom{d}{j}(\ln k)^{d-j}\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)^{j}\ .

In the top line bracket the constant and linear term in
\frac{1}{k}
cancel.
Hence that line’s contribution is indeed
O(k^{a-1}(\ln k)^{d})
.
The
j\geq 2
terms in the sums also bring
O(k^{-2})
factors multiplying
(k\pm 1)^{a+1}
. In the
j=1
terms, one can expand
\ln(1+x)=x+O(x^{2})
. The
O(x^{2})
part again brings
O(k^{-2})
factors.
This leaves the linear part of
\ln(1+x)
in the
j=1
terms, i.e., the contribution

\frac{1}{k}(\ln k)^{d-1}\times\sum_{i=0}^{a}(k-1)^{a-i}(k+1)^{i}=O(k^{a-1}(\ln k)^{d})\ ,

and the claim is proved.
Now note that

n^{-a}k_{n}^{a-1}\sim s^{a-1}\times n^{-\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right)-\frac{a}{\ell}}=O(n^{-1})\ ,

because
a\geq 1
.
Even after multiplication by powers of logarithms, this remains
o\left(n^{-\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right)}\right)
. In conclusion, after putting everything together, we see that

\Upsilon_{n}=\ell s^{-1}n^{-\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right)}+o\left(n^{-\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right)}\right)>0\ ,

for
n
large,
and the corollary is proved.
∎
