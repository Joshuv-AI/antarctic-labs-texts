Determinants of Stunting, Wasting, and Underweight Among Children Under 5 Years in India: Evidence From 2019 to 2021 Demographic Health Survey
Authors: Rohel Sk, Nasrin Banu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEarly childhood malnutrition is a serious public health issue in India, contributing to stunting, wasting, and underweight among children under 5 years. The study aims to explore the factors influencing these conditions and highlight the region most affected.MethodsThis study uses secondary data collected from National Family Health Survey 2019–21. Multivariate logistic regression has been run to identify the risk factors associated with malnutrition conditions among children under age five including stunting, wasting, and underweight based on Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF) measures. To explore the hot spot zone for stunting, wasting and underweight, cluster analysis has been done applying univariate local Moran's I.ResultsThe study explores a significant proportion of children in India suffering from malnutrition, particularly stunting is more prevalent than of underweight and wasting. Out of 25 indicators, age, sex, birth size of child and the mother's body mass index and current employment status have been identified as key predictors of malnutrition status. The children aged 25 to 36 months are more likely to be stunted, wasted and underweight than the other age groups which is consistent with CIAF with adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 3.92 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.92–7.99, p < 0.01). A sex‐based analysis shows females are less likely to be malnourished than male child which is also evident in CIAF (AOR: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.55–0.80, p < 0.01). The children having normal or larger sized at birth are less likely to suffer from stunting, wasting and underweight and even in CIAF than the small size children at birth. The children of underweight mother are more likely to be malnourished than the children of mother having normal body mass index which is also reported in CIAF (AOR: 1.34; 95% CI: 1.05–1.72, p = 0.02). Additionally, economic status and geographical region significantly impact malnutrition with children with wealthier households faring better which is consistent in CIAF (AOR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.45–0.88 for richest). Univariate local indicators of spatial association cluster analysis reveals that the western, eastern, and central zones are particularly vulnerable to child malnutrition.ConclusionCombating prevailing malnutrition in India requires collective responsibility and collaboration among policymakers, geographers, healthcare professionals, and communities.

Background

Introduction

Healthy citizens are the greatest asset any county can have.—Winston Churchill

Healthy citizens are the greatest asset any county can have.

Good health is very crucial for holistic development of the society. Stunting, wasting, undernourishment and mortality are the key indicators of hunger index measurement for the children under age 5 years. It is very unfortunate that India faced a significant challenge scoring 28.7 and ranking 111th out of 125 countries/regions in the 2023 Global Hunger Index [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that malnutrition contributes 45% of childhood mortality, predominantly affecting middle and low‐income countries. It was predicted that approximately 148.1 million children under the age of five were going to be stunted in 2022 [2]. WHO estimated 45 million were excessively thin for their height, wasting internationally and 462 million were underweight [3, 4]. One in three children under five in south Asia and sub‐Saharan Africa are stunted while India being one of the most populous countries in Asia as well as in the world reported 38% and 15% of children under five is stunted and wasted respectively [5]. Adequate nutrition is essential for child under age 5 years [6]. During this stage child's physical and psychological development is very fast. Stunting, wasting, underweight are the results of undernutrition leading various problems like infant mortality and morbidity, psychological imbalance, hindrance in physical growth [7]. According to United Nations Children's Fund, undernutrition increases the frequency and severity of common infections and increases the likelihood of a child succumbing to these infections and prolongs their recovery time. There is no doubt that undernutrition is a global health issue but it is predominating in developing countries particularly in African and South Asian countries [8].

Early childhood malnutrition is a serious public health concern in India, as it plays a major role in stunting, wasting, and underweight status among children under 5 years. Stunting as measured by low height for age is a worry as it captures chronic malnutrition and appears to have lifelong consequences for growth and learning [9]. Several studies have been conducted to find the causes of this malnutrition which are connected to socio‐economic, maternal and environmental indicators. Health status of a child depends on the economic status of the family. Stunting and underweight are notably prevalent among children in the low‐income families because they are likely to consume unhealthy diets and do not have proper access to healthcare and education facilities [10].

The latest survey conducted by the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) for the years 2019–21 pointed out that children in poor households have restricted access to nutritious food and healthcare. The educated mothers are more informed about the practices of child care leading to better feeding practices and better health seeking behaviors, thus mother's educational level determines the child health status [11]. Maternal health indicators like the maternal body mass index (BMI) and maternal age at birth are associated with child malnutrition. Hence, the duration of child's malnutrition is also depending on age and sex of the child. The likelihood of stunting and wasting is high in children below the age of five and there is also a significant distinction regarding the nutritional situation of boys and girls [12]. Education also plays a role in safeguarding malnutrition because it leads to good hygiene and treatment along with access to clean water, sanitation and healthcare services. Poor hand‐washing habits results increased incidences of infections, diarrhea, which further escalates malnutrition [13]. Breastfeeding practices are also very significant; inadequate breastfeeding or early bottle feeding is also linked to likelihood in prevalence of wasting and being underweight condition of children [14].

The most recent Demographic Health Survey conducted in India during 2019–21 which provides useful estimate and facts regarding the stunting, wasting and underweight in children below 5 years of age. In India notable regional disparities of malnutrition, is determined by socioeconomic and cultural factors. Research evidences that improved governance, healthcare facilities, along with nutrition‐sensitive interventions, are likely to explain comparatively lower proportion of stunted children [15]. The children health issues like underweight, wasting and stunting of 5 years old children in India have become a big concern in the recent past which may pose multiple burdens of nutrition defiance and infection. The survey is a source of primary research data that can be very useful for researchers, policy makers and practitioners who need to find out the root cause of these nutritional issues in the country. It is especially alarming when all three conditions exist simultaneously because such entails a far more comprehensive health complication as compared to those individual ailments, which may predispose children to enduring health complications. Thus, the present study aims to examine the children's nutritional status through a geographic lens and explore the determining factors of the child's malnutrition status in India.

Objectives

The specific objectives of the study are the following:

(1) To identify the regions affected by stunting, wasting and underweight among children;

(2) To explore the determining factors of stunting, wasting and underweight status among the children.

Methods

Study Population

This study is primarily relying on secondary sources of data gathered from the Global Demographic and Health Survey (IAKR7EDT data file) and the National Family Health Survey 2019–21 (NFHS‐5) which is a nationally representative study conducted by the International Institute of Population Sciences and ICF International, USA. The NFHS provides data at the state and union territory levels focusing on the population, health, and nutrition of India. The Indian states and union territories are represented in the NFHS‐5 through the use of a stratified two‐stage sample approach. The main objectives of the NFHS's 2019–21 cycle are to supply vital information on family welfare and health, as well as on emerging concerns in these areas, such as fertility rates, infant and child mortality, maternal and child health. Additionally, it offers insights on a number of recent developments, including the use of emergency contraception, safe injection practices, high‐risk sexual activity, tuberculosis, and non‐communicable diseases. In NFHS‐5, a total of 747,176 reproductive‐age women aged 15 to 49 were surveyed from a sample of 639,699 households.

The goal of the current study is to investigate the factors that contribute to underweight, wasting, and stunting in children under the age of 5 years. In order to achieve this, we have selected a total sample of 198,802 children under five who, ensuring that those who flagged for any discrepancies are removed from the study and that all participants have complete data on relevant nutritional status. Study Variables

Stunting (Height for Age)

Height‐for‐age Z‐scores were used to assess the chronic nutritional status of children under 5 years. This was accomplished by adapting the Child Growth Standards of WHO. According to WHO, stunting refers to low height for age, child's height in terms of the number of standard deviations (SDs) above or below the median height of healthy children in the same age group or in a reference group. We classified children with a measurement of < −2 SD from the median of the reference group as short for their age (stunted).

Wasting (Weight for Height)

Weight‐for‐height Z‐scores were used to assess the acute nutritional status of children under 5 years. This was accomplished by adapting the Child Growth Standards of WHO. According to WHO, wasting is defined as low weight‐for‐height. It usually occurs when a person has not had food of adequate quality and quantity or they have had frequent or prolonged illnesses. Wasting in children is associated with a higher risk of death if not treated properly. We selected children with a measurement of < −2 SD from the median of the reference group as wasted.

Underweight (Weight for Age)

Weight‐for‐age Z‐scores were used to assess the acute nutritional status of children under 5 years. This was accomplished by adapting the Child Growth Standards of WHO. According to WHO, underweight is defined as low weight for age. A child who is underweight may be stunted, wasted or both. We selected children with a measurement of < −2 SD from the median of the reference group as underweight. The response variables were recorded into dichotomies as follows:

Stunted (“0” = No if HAZ ≥ −2 and “1” = Yes if Height Age Z‐score < −2),

Wasted (“0” = No if WHZ ≥ −2 and “1” = Yes if Weight Height Z‐score < −2),

Underweight (“0” = No if WAZ ≥ −2 and “1” = Yes if Weight Age Z‐score < −2).

Risk Factors for Stunting, Wasting, and Underweight

The explanatory variables were classified into three categories i.e., Maternal factors, Children factors and Socioeconomic or community factors. Maternal factors included mother's age, mother's BMI level, mother's breastfeeding status, mother's working status, vitamin A intake in last 6 months, mother's education level, cigarette smoking, antenatal checkup, postnatal checkup, delivery care, place of delivery, delivery at cesarean, diabetes mother, alcoholic mother, thyroid mother and anemic mother etc. Child level factors included age of child in months, child sex and child size at birth. Socioeconomic or community factors included place of residence, sources of drinking water, toilet facility, economic status and geographical region etc. Therefore, in this study 25 independent variables have been taken into consideration for analyzing the risk factors of malnutrition conditions of children aged under than 5 years based on literature survey (Table 1). Statistical Analysis

For selection of determinants associated with stunting, wasting and underweight in children aged 0–59 months, the dependent variable was stated as a binary variable, i.e., category‐I, or indicated as “Not,” is coded as zero (not stunted, not wasted, not underweight [> −2 SD]), and category‐II, or indicated as “Yes,” is coded as one (stunted, wasted, underweight [> −2 SD]).

We have adopted chi square test to check the association between dependent and independent variables, here in this study stunting, wasting and underweight are taken as dependent variable and child level factors, mothers level factors, household level factors and community level factors are considered as independent variables, and these are mentioned very clearly in Table 1 along with their categories and frequencies with percentage.

Before going to analyze regression, we have used variance inflation factor (VIF) to detect the presence of multicollinearity between the variables. The detected variables were removed from the data set.

After getting association between the variable, we have performed multivariate logistic regression to understand the factors associated with undernutrition anthropometric measures and we have performed the Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF). Composite Anthropometric Failure as an indicator of child malnutrition. Strength of association was measured by adjusted odds ratio (AOR), which is the ratio of the probability of an outcome in an exposed group compared to the probability of an outcome in an unexposed group after controlling for other variables in the model. A binary logistic regression was applied to investigate the potential risk factors of three separate dichotomous dependent variables with numerous independent variables by using the stepwise backward elimination approach. The odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated in order to assess the adjusted risk of independent variables and those with less than 0.05 p value were maintained in the final model. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 15.1 (StataCorp, College Station, USA).

Spatial Analysis

For the regional distribution and spatial cluster analysis, SPSS Version 27 (IBM, Armonk, USA) and GeoDa software (Center for Spatial Data Science, Chicago, USA) have been used in the study. For regional distribution of prevalence of stunting, wasting and underweight we have used SPSS and for cluster analysis we have used the GeoDa software. Univariate local Moran's I statistic are computed with the help of GeoDa software, it has been also employed for the spatial autocorrelation graphical report. In this study univariate Moran's I was employed to get the spatial clustering. Boxplot graph has been used for the prevalence of stunting, wasting, and underweight. Moran's I is given by (1) Moras′I=C×∑∑WijZiZi∑Zi2 where Zi is the variable of interest; Wij is the standardized weight matrix and multiplier; C=n/S0. Positive spatial autocorrelation indicates that points with similar attribute values are closely distributed in space whereas negative spatial autocorrelation indicates that closely associated points are dissimilar values ranges from −1 (indicating perfect dispersion) to +1 (perfect correlation).

CIAF

The CIAF is a concept introduced by Svedberg. The whole burden of undernutrition in children was assessed using CIAF [16, 17]. Unlike traditional measures like stunting, wasting, or underweight, which measure different forms of undernutrition independently, the CIAF provides a more comprehensive picture by identifying children who are experiencing multiple forms of anthropometric failure. Weight‐for‐age, height‐for‐age, and weight‐for‐height anthropometric data were collected, and Z‐scores were calculated using WHO growth standards. Children whose Z‐scores were fewer than −2 SD from the median were classified as underweight, wasted, or stunted. After that we have to calculate sample survey weight because without weight adjusted, we can't run the CIAF and CIAF category. The category of undernutrition based on the CIAF is divided into anthropometric failure and no failure (normal) (Table 3). The categories are grouped into: (A) no failure (normal); (B) wasting only; (C) wasting and underweight; (D) wasting, underweight and stunting; (E) underweight and stunting; (F) stunting only; (Y) underweight only. The anthropometric failure is the total amount of undernutrition or sum of category of wasting only (B), wasting and underweight (C), wasting, underweight and stunting (D), underweight and stunting (E), stunting only (F) and underweight only (Y). At the same time, certain anthropometric deficiencies can be identified using the CIAF index [16, 17, 18].

Spatial Clustering of Malnutrition Among Children Under 5 Years

Spatial clustering of malnutrition among children Moran's I is a correlation coefficient that measures the overall spatial autocorrelation between response and predictor variables. Moran's I has been applied to analyses the spatial autocorrelation of malnutrition indicators among the child under five, and the study tried to interpret the results through Moran scatter plot analysis, wherein the plots are divided into four quadrants representing high‐high (HH), low‐low (LL), high‐low (HL), and low‐high (LH) spatial relationship. The findings reveal significant positive spatial autocorrelation across all three indicators stunting, wasting and underweight (Figure 4). The Moran scatter plot indicated that districts with higher prevalence of underweight, stunting and wasting are often geographically clustered with neighboring districts exhibiting similar conditions. The significant clustering has been observed for underweight (Figure 4c) (Moran's I = 0.674) followed by stunting (Figure 4a) (Moran's I = 0.496) and wasting (Figure 4b) (Moran's I = 0.467), suggesting that these health outcomes are influenced by regional socio‐economic and environmental factors rather than being randomly distributed. A significant number of districts fall within the HH quadrant indicating that districts with a high level, conversely, the LL quadrant consistently reflects clusters of districts with low prevalence. In contrast the presence observations in the HL and LH quadrants signifies spatial outliers instances where high prevalence districts are adjacent to low prevalence areas and vice versa, The underweight indicators visualizes a strong concentration of observations within the HH and LL quadrants, suggesting a more pronounced spatial clustering whereas stunting and wasting indicates a relatively wider dispersion across all four quadrants pointing to moderate clustering accompanied by greater spatial heterogeneity. Overall, the Moran Scatter plots demonstrate that child malnutrition is not randomly distributed rather it exhibits clear spatial dependence across districts, characterized by the coexistence of both clustered patterns and localized spatial anomalies.

Risk Factors for Stunting

As shown in Table 4, children aged 0–59 months born to mothers older than 20 years (AOR: 1.29; 95% CI: 0.50–3.30 for mother aged above 40 years) are more likely to be stunted compared to those born to younger mothers (under 20 years old). For children aged 0–59 months, those delivered at the health facility via non‐cesarean section (AOR: 0.93; 95% CI: 0.70–1.24 for those born in government hospital and AOR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.48–1.09 for those born in non‐government hospital) are significantly less likely to be stunted compared with children delivered at home. Mothers of female children report significantly lower rates of stunting (AOR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.56–0.84) than those with male children. Additionally, increasing age of the child was significantly associated with stunting with specific odds ratios: (AOR: 1.24; 95% CI: 0.56–2.77 for the age group 0–12 months), (AOR: 3.06; 95% CI: 1.41–6.63 for the age group 13–24 months children are more chances of being stunted than other age groups) and (AOR: 2.61; 95% CI: 1.20–5.65 for the age group 25–36 months), data for children older than 36 months was deleted because of collinearity. Children who were reported to large and average size by their mothers were significantly less likely to be stunted than those who were reported to be small (AOR: 0.55; 95% CI: 0.38–0.81 and AOR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.45–0.85 for large and average size of children). Moreover, children who are still breastfeeding and who have ever been breastfed are more likely to be stunted than those who have never been breastfed (AOR: 1.14; 95% CI: 0.63–2.06 and AOR: 1.49; 95% CI: 0.84–2.63 for still and ever breastfed respectively). Mothers' education level plays an important role for the stunting of the children those mothers who had higher level of education (AOR: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.55–1.30) were less likely to be stunting than those who had no education. Mothers living in rural areas have higher chances of having stunted children than those in urban areas (AOR: 1.24; 95% CI: 0.90–1.71). Similarly, children under 5 years old whose mother are alcoholic are more likely to be stunted than those with non‐alcoholic mothers (AOR: 1.29; 95% CI: 0.76–2.19 for alcoholic mothers). Children aged 0–59 months whose mothers have severe (AOR: 1.99; 95% CI: 1.13–3.50) or moderate (AOR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.03–1.70) levels of anemia are reported significantly higher chances of being stunted whereas in case of mild (AOR: 1.08; 95% CI: 0.82–1.41) anemia is associated with lower chances of stunted compared to children of non‐anemic mothers. Children aged 0–59 months from middle‐income households (AOR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.39–0.88) and those from the richest households (AOR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.44–0.87) are significantly less likely to be stunted when compared to those from poorest households. Additionally, children aged 0–59 months who have access to improved drinking water are less likely to be stunted than those who have access to unimproved drinking water (AOR: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.65–1.26 for those who had improved water sources). Children aged 0–59 months with access to protected toilet facility are less probable to be stunted than those with unprotected toilet facility (AOR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.75–1.22 for those who had protected toilet facility). Finally, children under 5 years old from western zones (AOR: 1.12; 95% CI: 0.74–1.70) are more likely to be stunted than southern region, whereas others zones like northern, central, north‐eastern and eastern are less likely to be stunted than southern and western regions in the country.

CIAF in Children Aged 0–59 Months

The CIAF, which measures the determinants of undernutrition, reveals a number of important risk and protective factors for children under five. The odds of anthropometric failure are significantly higher for children aged 0–12 months (AOR: 2.19), 13–24 months (AOR: 3.65), and 25–36 months (AOR: 3.92) than for those aged 0–6 months, suggesting a key vulnerability during the supplemental feeding period, hence age is a prominent predictor. Compared to male children, female children had a considerably lower risk of undernutrition (AOR: 0.66), and bigger or medium birth size was linked to a lower risk than small birth size. Children of underweight moms were more likely to suffer from undernutrition (AOR: 1.34, p = 0.02). The significance of maternal care was highlighted by the fact that children whose mothers attended prenatal checkups had a significantly lower risk (AOR: 0.29, p = 0.03), even if breastfeeding status, diarrhea incidence, and maternal age did not exhibit any significant associations. Belonging to wealthier economic categories and giving birth in non‐government hospitals also reduced the risk (AOR: 0.59, p = 0.01), albeit some odds ratios don't seem to match the published p‐values. Regional differences were noticeable, with the western area exhibiting the highest chances (AOR: 1.99, p < 0.01) of undernutrition, and severe anemia greatly raised the risk (AOR: 2.32, p = 0.01). The lack of statistical significance for other characteristics, including as maternal smoking, alcohol use, place of residence, water and sanitation, and delivery mode, suggests that interventions should concentrate on maternal health, early childhood nutrition, and regional disparities. Discussion

Although people of India are living with modern technology and improved medical facilities, they are facing big challenges regarding child and maternal mortality. Not only India, many developing countries are fighting to reduce the child and mothers' mortality as it is a critical public health issue nowadays. In India, malnutrition is a very serious health issue that contributing significantly to morbidity and infant mortality rates. Our study put special focus on twenty‐five factors important for analyzing the child malnutrition.

Our research revealed that a child's age increases the likelihood of stunting. According to several earlier researches [19, 20, 21], children under the age of one are less likely to be malnourished. Children's calorie needs from a variety of foods rise as they get older, but low‐income families sometimes find it difficult to provide for these needs, which stunts growth. Furthermore, moms typically give their attention to their infants' nutritional needs, thus toddlers can get less care from them than they do from newborns.

Siddiqa et al. [19] and Khan et al. [21] suggest that older children's greater food and medical costs may be foregone in order to favor younger siblings. Our research shows that a child's birth weight is negatively correlated with their nutritional health, as smaller birth sizes are associated with stunting, being underweight, and wasting [19, 22, 23]. Maternal poor nutrition during her pregnancy can have an impact on the growth and development of the fetus, leading to small birth sizes.

Both the age of mothers and their children have a significant impact on a child's general development since, as we all know, children's eating habits change as they get older and can lead to a variety of issues like underweight, stunting, and wasting. Our study's findings clearly indicate that rates of stunting, wasting, and underweight increase as children grow older which is similar to the findings of studies by Akombi et al. [24] and Mgongo et al. [25]. Additionally, the age of the mother is also a factor influencing children's stunting and underweight rates.

According to the literature study for the analysis, female children experience lower rates of underweight, wasting, or stunting than their male counterparts [7, 26]. Our study observed similar results. B. Khura et al. [7] and Thurstans et al. [26] opine that boy are more affected by malnutrition than girls because they need more calories for their healthy growth. Furthermore, during the ages of 10–19, boys need more protein, iron, and zinc, even if their body weights are similar to those of girls. This aligns with Indian dietary standards.

Additionally, our study found that children of working mothers tended to be shielded from issues like wasting, stunting, and underweight compared to children of non‐working mothers. This supports previous research indicating that maternal employment generally has a positive impact on children's nutritional status [19, 21, 27]. Working women may be better able to provide a nutritious diet, medical care, and other essentials, which can improve their children's health. Maternal education plays a key role in decreasing child malnutrition [28, 29]. Highly educated women are more likely to ensure timely vaccinations, wholesome diet, and high‐quality healthcare. Studies by M. Siddiqa et al. [19] and Grépin et al. [30] provide convincing evidence that societies with higher levels of education are more effective at decreasing poverty and enhancing population health. Higher educated mothers are more likely to provide their children a better chance of growing up to healthy adults, feed them well whenever possible, and give them access to healthcare with strict adherence to vaccination schedules. These findings are also evident in our study. Surprisingly our findings did not reveal a protective role for the ever and still breastfeeding practices in general against malnutrition. This study reflects the higher risk of anthropometric failure among the children who are ever breastfeeding status significantly, in case of still feeding high chances of anthropometric failure but not statistically significant. There is a general consensus that breastfeed leads to a decrease in the risk of malnutrition condition but several studies revealed that prolonged breastfeeding (more than 12 months) led to increased risk of stunting and severe stunting among Nepalese children [31]. After 6 months, the baby's nutritional needs increase, and breast milk is no longer effective in fulfilling their dietary needs; therefore, complementary diets should be given after 6 months [32]. A very recent study on Ethiopia reveals that there is a notable prevalence of undernutrition in children breastfed until the age of three [33].

Additionally, variables such as the mother's underweight or low BMI, cesarean deliveries, anemia, and alcoholism can significantly impact on the children's nutritional status. Mothers whose weight is low in relation to their height are at a very high risk of stunting, being wasted, and being underweight, which can result in undernourished children and a higher chance of anthropometric failure [34, 35, 36]. Our study also observed the similar results. Mothers with anemia and a history of alcohol consumption are also more likely to be stunted, wasted and underweight than their counterparts to experience anthropometric failure [37, 38, 39]. Similarly, we have found that children of alcoholic mothers are more likely to experience anthropometric failure. A poorer economic standing frequently makes it more difficult to obtain these necessities, which raises the prevalence of childhood malnutrition [10, 29, 40, 41, 42] which is not different from the present study.

A positive result for the Moran's I index for stunting, wasting, and underweight indicate that the district with the highest prevalence of both indicators is spatially clustered, according to the examination of spatial autocorrelation. A separate study by Khan and Mohanty [43] explored significant levels of malnutrition in selected Indian states and regions. The highest concentration of districts affected by stunting is found in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, and Maharashtra. This suggests that these states not only have a high number of malnourished children but that many of their districts are hotspots for stunting. When it comes to wasting, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Jharkhand, and Bihar have the most clustered districts. In a similar vein, districts with a higher underweight prevalence exhibit clustering. Numerous districts in Gujarat, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, and Bihar are thought to be known for having high rates of underweight children. Similarly, the majority of the districts in northeastern India are clustered with better performing districts in terms of stunting, wasting, and underweight, while most districts in southern and northern India show even better outcomes, leading to lower average rates of malnutrition among children under five in these areas. Furthermore, the prevalence of stunting and underweight varies by region, indicating a considerable correlation between the two indicators and geographic location.

Our study aimed to focus on the geographical distribution and variations in children's nutritional status as influenced by spatial factors. The entire western and eastern zones of India, along with parts of central and southern regions exhibit higher rates of child malnutrition. Poverty, poor access to healthcare, and poor sanitation are the primary causes of higher rates of malnutrition in rural areas and certain states in India [10, 20].

Conclusions

Malnutrition remains a global issue even in today's modern and globalized era, primarily due to hunger and poverty. Sustainable Development Goal Target 2.2 aims to eliminate all forms of malnutrition, with a specific focus on achieving the internationally agreed targets for stunting and wasting in children under 5 years of age by 2025, as outlined by the WHO [44]. This problem is particularly concerning in South Asian countries, including India. Current studies indicate that a significant proportion of children in India who are 5 years old suffer from malnutrition, leading to conditions such as stunting, wasting, or being underweight. Among these issues, the prevalence of stunting is reported to be higher than that of underweight and wasting. Approximately 25 indicators were considered in identifying the significant determinants of child malnutrition. Factors such as the child's age, sex, and birth size have been identified as key predictors of malnutrition status. Among maternal variables, the mother's BMI and current employment status also influence the malnutrition status of children. From a socio‐economic perspective, economic status emerges as an important determinant affecting conditions such as stunting, wasting, and underweight among children. The geographical region is a significant predictor of the malnutrition status of children. Further investigation using univariate local Moran's I cluster analysis reveals that the hotspots for stunting are located in the central, western, and eastern zones. Hotspots for wasting children are primarily found in the entire western and eastern zones. Additionally, the hotspots for underweight children are clearly marked in the eastern and western zones, along with parts of central and southern India. This indicates that the western, eastern, and central zones are particularly vulnerable in terms of child malnutrition status.

In such situation where a whole nation is striving to achieve the goal of “no one left behind,” a holistic approach is essential for eradicating hunger, poverty, and child malnutrition. Various factors must be properly addressed, as child health cannot be separated from maternal health, and maternal health is significantly influenced by the socio‐economic environment. Additionally, geographic location also affects these factors. Consequently, all aspects are interconnected and related to one another. A comprehensive policy that tackles these systemic challenges is crucial for eliminating hunger, given that malnutrition is closely linked to poverty. Existing public programs, such as the Public Distribution System, Integrated Child Development Schemes, Poshan Abhiyaans, and Midday Meal Schemes, play a vital role in improving nutrition outcomes for women and children [45]. Moreover, promoting food security, sustainable livelihoods, and providing financial support for low‐income families, as well as educating mothers about nutrition, are key strategies that should be implemented. Specific interventions, including community education programs and nutritional support initiatives like food fortification, can empower vulnerable populations with the knowledge to make healthier dietary choices. The health and well‐being of mothers, along with their access to healthcare, is of utmost importance. India is a vast country, the intensity of problem varies across regions, states and districts. Ultimately, combating malnutrition in India is a collective responsibility that requires collaboration among policymakers, geographers, healthcare professionals, and communities. By focusing on poverty alleviation, enhancing maternal education, and ensuring access to healthcare, we can foster a healthier future for children. Given the severe challenges faced by vulnerable children, swift action is necessary to protect them and ensure they have the opportunity to thrive.
