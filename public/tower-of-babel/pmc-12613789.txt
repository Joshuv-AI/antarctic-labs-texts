Single and joint associations of physical activity and depression with risk of all-cause mortality: a prospective cohort study of NHANES data
Authors: Haoran Chen, Min Wang, Yingyin Li, Xiaowei Xu, Lin Yang, Jiao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression and physical activity (PA) are interrelated elements crucial to human health. However, the evidence regarding the relationship between depression, PA, and mortality remains limited.ObjectiveTo estimate the single and joint associations PA and depression on mortality risk, and further examine whether PA moderates the association between depression and mortality.MethodsData were from the National Health and Nutrition and Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted between 2007 and 2018 and linked to the National Death Index death certificate records. Depression status was determined by the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and PA levels were measured by self-reporting activity (type, metabolic equivalent (MET) and time per week). Kaplan-Meier method and Cox proportional hazards models were applied to explore the single and joint associations between depression and PA with all-cause mortality.ResultsA total of 26,150 participants were enrolled, of whom 9.2% (n = 2412) died during follow-up (median 81 months), 29.9% (n = 7810) met the PA recommendation and 9.2% (n = 2416) suffered from depression (scored ≥ 10 on the PHQ-9). For the single association, depression (HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.16–1.69, serious depression vs. no) was positively associated with mortality, but the association of PA (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.58–0.76, regular PA vs. physically inactive) was inverse. Joint association analyses showed that the association between depression and mortality was influenced by PA, whereas more frequent PA was associated with lower mortality risk irrespective of the level of depression symptoms: compared to participants who were depressed and did not participate in PA, those who did participate in PA, whether depressed or not, had a lower risk of death. Especially, for participants who were moderately depressed and did not PA, both insufficient PA (HR, 0.43; 95% CI, 031-0.61) and regular PA (HR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.24–0.58) significantly reduced the risk of all-cause mortality.ConclusionsOur study found that depression was positively associated with increased mortality risk, while PA was negatively associated with it; moreover, the mortality risk associated with depression worsened with low levels of physical activity. These findings underscore the critical importance of promoting physical activity, especially among individuals with depression, as a potential strategy to mitigate mortality risk. This highlights the need for integrated public health interventions that address both mental health and physical activity to improve overall population health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24820-8.

Background

Depression is the leading cause of disability worldwide [1]. It is estimated that more than 350 million people suffer from depression globally, with 76.4 million years of adjusted life expectancy attributable to depression each year, lounting for 10.3% of the total burden of disease [2], and that this upward trend has accelerated in high socio-demographic index countries, including the United States [3]. Depression is a commonly occurring mental health condition in the United States and it has been affecting nearly 7.2% to 9.2% of the US population, particularly adults, from 2015 to 2020 [4]. Depressive disorders can lead to cognitive and functional decline, cardiovascular disease, chronic pain, and can increase risk of all-cause mortality [5]. The relationship between depression and mortality has been demonstrated by a large number of studies, most of which show that depression, especially major depression, is associated with an increased risk of death [6]. Therefore, it is important to prevent and treat depression [7]. Physical activity (PA) is a cost-effective and accessible intervention and one of the most important measures to improve health for people of all ages [8]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that varying levels of PA can reduce the risk of all-cause mortality [9–11]. A prospective cohort analysis including more than 660,000 participants showed that people who achieved the recommended range of PA had a 31% lower risk of death compared with controls [12]; a study of adults in Europe and the United States have shown a 17% reduction in all-cause mortality when participants engaging in sufficient vigorous physical activity (VPA) [13]. The WHO and American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) have also recommended 150 min or more of moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) per week [14, 15].

Depression is influenced not only by social, psychological, and genetic factors, but also by lifestyle (e.g. PA, diet, sleep, etc.) [16]. There has been a large amount of research evidence to support an inverse correlation between PA and depression, and PA was widely recognized as a promising protective factor for depression [17, 18]. Although PA has not yet been widely used as a treatment [7, 19], we cannot ignore the potential therapeutic effects of PA, which may have similar effects to psychotherapy and medication in alleviating depressive symptoms [20–22]. The single relationship between PA, depression and all-cause mortality has been well explained in previous studies, however, knowledge of the combined associations of different PA types and depression on mortality remained limited [23, 24]. Results from a prospective and population-based cohort examining the joint associations of PA and depression with all-cause mortality showed that low doses of PA increased the risk of all-cause mortality [25]. The study used only metabolic equivalent (MET) to assess the dose of PA, and with a follow-up of 4 years, it may not have been able to capture enough target events. Furthermore, a study based on NHANES data that analyzed the all-cause mortality risk of the joint association of PA and depression would have lost much important information by simply dichotomizing PA and depression and only including participants aged ≥ 50 years [26]. The current relevant experience and evidence were not sufficiently clear, and there were limitations in previous relevant studies. In response to the WHO guidelines [27], and based on current clinical needs, we need to validate the joint associations of PA and depression on mortality risk using larger samples, population cohorts with sufficient follow-up time, and more detailed classification of PA, and explore whether the risk of all-cause mortality due to depression can be modified by PA.

Therefore, the aim of our study is to estimate the single and joint associations of PA and depression on mortality risk, and further examine whether PA modifies the association between depression and mortality.

Methods

Study population

The NHANES was a nationally representative cross-sectional survey with a complex, multi-stage, stratified, clustered probability design, which aimed to assess the health and nutritional status of Americans. The NHANES program was approved by the ethics review board of the National Center for Health Statistics Research and all respondents provided written informed consent. The methods for NHANES data collection were described in detail on the official website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm). The present data (including demographics, examination and questionnaire) were from six cycles of NHANES (2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018). After excluding participants younger than 18 years of age and those with missing data, a total of 26,150 participants were included in the final analysis. A flowchart of the study was presented in Figure S1.

Assessment of depression

The 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) is a brief self-report measure of depression symptoms suitable for use in primary care and research settings with well-established factor structure, reliability, and validity [28]. The PHQ-9 includes nine items and each item is scored ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), which adds up to a range of 0 to 27 total points. The NHANES survey used the PHQ-9 to measure participants’ depressive symptoms over a two-week period. Individuals with a PHQ-9 total score ≥ 10 were considered to have major depression, with a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 88% in a previous study [29]. The total PHQ-9 total scores were further subdivided into intervals of 10–14, 15–19 and ≥ 20 points, representing moderate, moderately severe, and severe depression, respectively [29]. Due to the low prevalence of severe depression, we combined moderately severe depression and severe depression into one category (PHQ-9 scores ≥ 15, severe depression).

Assessment of physical activity

According to several questions from the NHANES questionnaire on “Physical Activity”, we determined the number of days per week and amount of time each day that each individual participated in various PA (MPA, moderate physical activity; VPA, vigorous physical activity). In addition, walking or bicycling for transportation is equal to that of MPA from the definition of metabolic equivalent task (see Table S1 for details).

Based on the 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans [30], participants’ PA types were classified into three groups: inactive group, insufficiently active and sufficiently active. The sufficiently active group was then further categorized based on how often individuals participated in MVPA each week [30]: the weekend warrior group and regular active group (see Table S2 for details).

In accordance with the WHO Analysis Guide (https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/ncds/ncd-surveillance/gpaq-analysis-guide.pdf), PA was converted to minutes of metabolic equivalent (MET) of moderate or vigorous PA per week. MET values varied with the type of exercise, and the NHANES offered the recommended MET values for each PA type (Supplementary Table S1). So the total PA at MET scale was calculated using the following formula: PA (MET-min/week) = MET * weekly frequency * duration of each PA type. And the total MVPA time in a typical week was calculated by multiplying the number of days and total minutes in each day of MPA and VPA.

Assessment of other covariates

The following potential covariates were selected based on previous literatures [31, 32]. Age, gender (male, female), race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black and others), marital status (married, never married, others), education level (less than high school, high school or above), poverty-to-income ratio (PIR: a family poverty index was used to estimate socioeconomic status), smoking (yes, no), drinking (yes, no), body mass index (BMI), sedentary activity were obtained by interviews and physical examinations. The PIR was grouped into three categories: <1, 1-2.9, ≥ 3; the BMI was grouped into four categories: < 18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25-29.9, ≥ 30 kg/m2; and the sedentary activity was divided into three categories (< 240, 240–279, ≥ 280 min) based on the average time per day being sedentary. Other variables related to disease were also included. Participants were considered to have diabetes if they met any of the following criteria [33]: (1) participants gave a ‘Yes’ for any of the following questions: ‘Doctor told you to have diabetes? ’ or ‘Taking insulin now?’ or ‘Take diabetic pills to lower blood sugar?’; (2) the fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL; (3) the glycated hemoglobin A1c (HBA1c) ≥ 6.5%. The hypertension was defined by a self-reported diagnosis from a physician or the systolic pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. And the CVD (cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease) was defined as the presence of a self-reported history of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina/angina pectoris, heart attack or stroke.

Assessment of deaths

Data on all-cause mortality status of NHANES participants as of December 31, 2019 were determined by using a probabilistic match between NHANES and the National Death Index death certificate records (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality-public.htm). The follow-up time was defined as the period of time of the interview date to the date of death or to the end of follow-up.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of participants were described using percentage (%) or mean and their standard deviation (SD). Baseline characteristics were described across different types of depression and difference among those groups were compared by using the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and Chi-Square test for categorical variables.

Single effects of depression and PA on all-cause mortality

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models (multi-Cox) were used to estimate the single effect of depression (depression category or depression score) or PA (PA type or total PA time/MET) on mortality at a categorical or continuous scale, respectively. Meanwhile, Kaplan-Meier method was applied to plot the survival curves associated with depression or PA type. Furthermore, we assessed the nonlinear relationship between depression score, total PA time or total PA MET and mortality by using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots (5th, 50th and 95th percentiles) and 10 scores, 300 min and 1200 min/week was regarded as the reference group, respectively. And the likelihood ratio test was used to assess the non-linearity.

Joint effects of depression and PA on all-cause mortality

To assess the joint associations, we initially assessed the mortality risk in three groups of participants relative to the reference group, which comprised individuals with both depression and physical inactivity. Then we divided participants into 12 distinct subgroups by both depression category and PA type. We evaluated HRs of mortality in each group relative to those with serious depression and inactive by the multi-Cox. We futher examined the joint effects of depression and PA type on mortality in subgroups of different depression categories, with inactive as the reference group. Three models were constructed in this analysis: Model 1 was the crude model; Model 2 was adjusted for age, race and sex; Model 3 was adjusted for all covariates. And the Kaplan-Meier curve was plotted for mortality stratified by the joint depression and PA type.

To explore potential modified effects of selected variables for the association between PA and mortality, exploratory subgroup analyses were performed for participants with depression. Because of the small number of deaths in each subgroup, we combined some subgroups (hypertension, diabetes, CVD) and redefined some variables (age, BMI). We further performed a series of sensitivity analyses to validate the robustness of our results: (1) excluding deaths that occurred in the initial 2 years of follow-up to minimize reverse causation risk; (2) using total PA time or total PA MET instead of PA type with depression to explore joint associations; (3) exploring the dose-response relationship between total PA MET or total PA time and mortality classified by depression.

In our study, schoenfeld’s residuals were used to test the proportional hazards assumption for all covariates, and no violation was observed. Sample weights, clustering, and stratification were used in the data analyses to reflect the design of NHANES. A 2-side P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. SAS (version 9.4) and R (version 4.2.1) software were used for all statistical analysis.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study population

Table 1 presented the characteristics of the study population. Among the 26,150 participants, the all-cause mortality was 9.2% (2412/26150) with a median follow-up time of 81 months, the mean age was 50 years old, 50.6% (n = 13227) were female, 43.5% (11368) were non-Hispanic White, 51.2% (n = 13368) were married, 77.3% (n = 20201) had at least a high school education and most of participants (72.8%) had sedentary time more than 240 min per day. In terms of PA, a total of 7810 (29.9%, weekend warrior + regular) participants met the PA guideline, and 11,603 (44.4%) had insufficient PA. Overall, the demographic characteristics all differed significantly between various depression categories (all the P value < 0.05). In particular, we can observe that participants with more severe depressive symptoms had relatively less PA time and PA MET. Single association between depression or PA and all-cause mortality

In terms of the single effect of depression or PA on mortality, a statistically significant positive relationship was found between the depression (either as a continuous or categorical variable) and all-cause mortality (all the P < 0.001, Table 2): compared with individuals without depression (depression score < 10), the corresponding HRs for individuals with depression score ≥ 10, 10–14 or ≥ 15 were 1.322 (95% CI: 1.163, 1.502), 1.276 (95% CI: 1.088, 1.496) and 1.396 (1.155, 1.688), respectively; and the HR and 95% CI for mortality was 1.030 (1.021,1.039) for each 1 point increase of depression score. However, we noted a significant inverse association between the PA and all-cause mortality (Table 2): participants whose PA type was “insufficient”,”weekend warrior” or “regular” had a lower risk of death compared to participants who were not PA (inactive). In addition, similar results were found for total PA time and total PA MET. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig. 2) showed significant difference among the three depression groups or the four PA type groups (log-rank, P < 0.001), with the highest mortality rate observed in participants with serious depression or physically inactive during the follow-up time.

Joint association between depression and PA and all-cause mortality

Regarding the joint effect of physical activity and depression on all-cause mortality, participants in the other three groups had a lower risk of death after adjusting for covariates compared to participants who were depressed and did not participate in physical activity (all HR < 1, Table S3). In 12 subgroups classified by different combinations of depression category and PA type, Figure S3 showed most of subgroups was associated with lower mortality risk (e.g., no depression and regular PA: HR, 0.23, 95% CI 0.17, 0.29; serious depression and regular PA: HR, 0.17, 95% CI 0.07, 0.41) compared with serious depression and physically inactive as reference. The Table 3 further demonstrated the association between PA type and all-cause mortality based on depression category. When participants without depression were grouped based on PA type, those with PA (insufficient, weekend warrior or regular) all had a lower risk of death relative to individuals without PA, unadjusted or adjusted for other covariates. For participants with depression, more frequent PA also significantly affected the risk of death during the follow-up irrespective of the level of depression symptoms. For example, for participants with serious depression, the PA type of “Weekend warrior” or “Regular” was associated with about 35% (HR 0.654, 95% CI 0.434 to 0.987) and 70% (HR 0.304, 95% CI 0.119 to 0.777) lower mortality risk compared with physically inactive, respectively (Model 3 in Table 3). The Kaplan-Meier survival curves similarly validated the results of the above analysis (Figure S4): at 13.3 years of follow-up, the probability of survival was the lowest for the inactive participants across depression categories, with 83.73% for those who were non-depressed, 80.96% for those who were moderately depressed, and 80.89% for those who were severely depressed, whereas engaging in PA significant improved the risk of death (logrank P < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, based on 2007–2018 NHANES data, we explored the relationship between PA and depression on all-cause mortality individually, as well as the joint analysis of the two on mortality. Our findings highlighted the importance of PA in daily life, particularly for the depressed population, and the benefits of the combined effect with depression on reducing the risk of all-cause mortality.

Consistent with previous studies [25, 26], our findings suggested the mortality risk associated with depression worsened with low levels of PA: for severely depressed populations, all-cause mortality was lower in active than inactive populations (all HRs < 1 in both unadjusted or adjusted models, Table 3), and this beneficial effect was more pronounced in populations that were more frequently active (i.e. PA type of “Regular”). However, the unique aspects of our study were based on a large prospective cohort with a follow-up period of up to 13.3 years, which gave us more chances to obtain robust findings. And we classified PA more precisely based on time, frequency, and MET. Previous studies have mostly only focused more on PA frequency or PA intensity rather than assessing PA levels based on a combination of both. In addition, we also analyzed the association between the types, MET and time of PA and mortality by dividing the population into different subgroups according to the severity of depression, highlighting the role of PA as an association modifier for the association between depression and mortality and revealing relevant interventions that should be taken in depressed populations.

Our multi-categorization of PA helped to prioritize and select appropriate types of PA for people, thereby maximizing the effectiveness of mental health interventions. Previous studies have shown that the relationship between PA and target diseases can be better understood through the use of more detailed exposure coordination methods [34]. In Western countries, adults spend an average of 9 to 10 h a day at work, and the majority of the population is sedentary. In the United States, in particular, data as of 2016 showed that only 26% of men, 19% of women, and 20% of adolescents reported engaging in sufficient PA [35], about 10% of deaths each year are related to insufficient PA [30]. Across diverse careers, choosing the right PA type is key. The weekend warrior PA type, preferred by busy and stressed professionals, provides similar depression-fighting benefits as regular, moderate PA [36]–[37]. Despite the small sample size of the “moderate depression” and “weekend warrior PA” subgroups (n = 66), our study continued to show that the weekend warrior PA type had a statistically significant beneficial effect. Furthermore, the effectiveness of PA in preventing and treating depressive symptoms may be related to the intensity and time of PA [38], so we conducted the dose-response relationship between total PA MET or total PA time and mortality. Consistent with previous experience, there was an inverse dose-response between the total time or MET for PA and whether or not it was analyzed in populations with or without depression (both P for nonlinear < 0.001 and estimated HR < 1). The above results suggested that clinicians should provide individualized PA strategies based on the needs of PA time and PA MET.

This study highlighted the importance of including PA in joint analyses when examining the impact of depression on all-cause mortality. Our study also found an inverse association between PA and depression through cross-sectional analyses (Table S7). The association between PA and depression may be the result of a combination of psychological, neurophysiological and social mechanisms. PA ameliorates depression through a variety of neuromuscular mechanisms, including increased expression of neurotrophic factors, increased availability of serotonin and norepinephrine, modulation of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activity and reduction of systemic inflammation [39], and enhances resilience to oxidative and physiological stress. PA also affects prefrontal cortex (PFC) activity and thus cognitive functioning for beneficial effects on antidepressants [40]. In addition to promoting mechanisms for psychosocial and behavioral aspects such as self-esteem, social support and self-efficacy [41], our findings suggested that the high-level of PA is more effective than the low level of PA, possibly because the low-dose of PA may not be sufficient to stimulate the neurological and hormonal changes associated with the alleviation of depressive symptoms. The associations observed in this study may be explained by more than one mechanism, and in the future our findings could be used in conjunction with these common mechanisms to better guide clinicians or public health workers in designing and implementing interventions for optimal PA types to maximize the antidepressant effects of PA in individuals and reduce all-cause mortality in the population.

Building on previous research [26], our study provided a more comprehensive analysis of potential covariates and subgroup analyses of sedentary activities. Numerous studies have analyzed the relationship between sedentary activity and mortality [42, 43], and our results also indicated a significant association between PA and all-cause mortality in people with sedentary time more than 240 min per day, so we cannot ignore the effect of sedentary activity on all-cause mortality. Some studies have shown that less sedentary time significantly reduces the risk of premature death [44]. Subgroup analyses of the study showed that participants who were sedentary for more than 240 min per day had a greater risk of all-cause mortality, suggesting that sedentary people need to be more active. In addition, subgroup analyses in this study also showed the combined effect of factors such as a history of relevant diseases (CVD, diabetes, hypertension) as well as bad habits (alcohol consumption, smoking) with PA were also significantly associated with all-cause mortality. Patients with CVD could protect cardiac function and improve fitness by promoting PA, which facilitates cardiac rehabilitation and prevents comorbidities such as depression [45]. We also found a significant relationship between different patterns of PA and all-cause mortality in elderly participants with depression (≥ 60 years), which vigorously validated the results of previous studies [46].

The 2020 WHO guidelines highlighted the increasing importance of PA recommendations across diverse populations. The guideline also emphasized the reduction of sedentary behavior, reflecting heightened awareness of its adverse health effects. Despite ongoing debates and the current limitations in high-quality evidence, there was a call among experts for more quantitative recommendations regarding the duration and type of PA [47]. While population-specific variations existed, our findings revealed notable similarities across different groups. Given the rapid pace of contemporary life, our study supported the weekend warrior type as a more practical approach for individuals with demanding schedules. This PA type aligned with the ACSM standards, offering a feasible way for the majority of the public to achieve significant longevity benefits at minimal cost.

In summary, our findings had important new implications for public health workers. However, we acknowledged the possibility of limitations in our study that must be considered: (1) the data were limited to self-reported PA frequency and intensity, which may lead to reporting and recall bias, exaggerating or underestimating individuals’ actual PA levels, while we cannot exclude the possibility of misclassification of some different doses of PA; (2) depression was assessed by self-report using the PHQ-9, which may not be a perfect indicator of depression status; (3) people with major depression may choose not to take part in surveys or health checks leading us to potentially underestimate the actual prevalence of depression. (4) the exclusion of accelerometer data in this study merits note, as such objective tools provide granular insights into PA intensity and patterns—including nuanced distinctions between moderate and vigorous activity—offering a valuable counterpoint to self-reported data, which may suffer from recall bias and overestimation, potentially weakening the precision and reliability of study results. To address this limitation, future research should employ concurrent PA assessment via self-reports and devices like accelerometers. Additionally, integrating multiple depression assessment scales and more objective measurement devices for a more accurate assessment of depression, and further studies with larger samples and higher quality should be carried out to analyze the joint associations of PA with depression and other related factors on all-cause mortality.

Conclusion

The present study suggested both low levels of PA and depression were associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality, and high levels of PA was associated with lower mortality risk irrespective of the intensity of depression. Our findings from NHANES could provide important information for clinicians and public health workers that they should increase education about the potential benefits of PA on psychological disorders such as depression and other related conditions in order to maximize population health. And weekend warrior PA type may already be an effective strategy for ameliorating mortality risk associated with depression.
