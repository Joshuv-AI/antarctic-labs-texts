Clinical and imaging characteristics of phosphaturic mesenchymal tumors: a case series analysis
Authors: Haifeng Hou, Xiaofeng Zhou, Lijun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo characterize the clinical, biochemical, pathological, and multimodal imaging features of phosphaturic mesenchymal tumors (PMTs).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 10 patients with pathologically confirmed PMT. Clinical presentation, laboratory findings, histopathologic and immunohistochemical features, and imaging manifestations across multiple modalities were systematically analyzed.ResultsAll patients presented with long-standing fatigue and bone pain. Preoperative laboratory tests revealed varying degrees of hypophosphatemia and elevated alkaline phosphatase, while postoperative serum phosphate levels gradually normalized. Histopathology demonstrated heterogeneous morphologies. Immunohistochemistry showed consistent SATB2 and SSTR2 positivity in tested cases, whereas the Ki-67 proliferation index ranged from 1% to 15%. On CT, primary lesions appeared as soft-tissue density masses or ground-glass–like nodules, often with irregular calcification and focal bone destruction. MRI demonstrated hypointensity on T1-weighted images, hyperintensity on T2-weighted images, and moderate to marked heterogeneous enhancement. Both [18F]F-OC PET/CT and [18F]F-FDG PET/CT revealed variable degrees of radiotracer uptake in all lesions.ConclusionPMTs are characterized by typical TIO-related clinical and biochemical abnormalities, and SATB2 and SSTR2 are highly sensitive immunohistochemical markers for definitive diagnosis. Whole-body [18F]F-OC PET/CT, targeting somatostatin receptors, serves as a highly specific first-line modality for tumor localization. Dedicated CT and MRI provide essential anatomic characterization for surgical planning, while [18F]F-FDG PET/CT offers complementary, non–tumor-specific metabolic information. [99mTc]Tc-MDP planar bone scintigraphy is useful for assessing the systemic skeletal burden of osteomalacia. This stepwise, multimodal approach improves diagnostic accuracy and supports timely surgical management.

Introduction

Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumor (PMT) is a rare neoplasm and the leading cause of tumor-induced osteomalacia (TIO), a paraneoplastic syndrome marked by hypophosphatemia and defective bone mineralization secondary to excess fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF-23) secretion [1]. Patients usually present with bone pain, muscle weakness, or recurrent fractures. However, these nonspecific symptoms often lead to delayed diagnosis [2].

PMTs can arise in bone or soft tissue and may involve almost any anatomical site. The extremities and craniofacial regions are the most frequently affected locations [3]. Complete surgical excision is curative in most cases, which emphasizes the need for precise preoperative localization. A variety of imaging modalities contribute to this process. Plain radiographs may demonstrate skeletal changes related to osteomalacia. Computed tomography (CT) provides detailed evaluation of lesion morphology and density. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers superior soft tissue contrast and helps to assess tumor extent. Functional and molecular imaging, particularly [68Ga]Ga-based somatostatin receptor (SSTR) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) imaging techniques, has shown high sensitivity for detecting small or occult lesions [4].

Nevertheless, the imaging features of PMTs are heterogeneous and often overlap with those of other mesenchymal neoplasms, making diagnosis challenging [5, 6]. Most available reports are limited to single cases or small series, and systematic characterization is lacking. To address this limitation, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical, pathological, and imaging findings of patients with pathologically confirmed PMTs. This retrospective case series analysis aims to summarize the clinical presentations and imaging characteristics of PMTs. We also reviewed and compared our findings with the existing literature to provide updated insights and practical guidance for improving recognition and diagnostic accuracy.

Clinical data and methods

Clinical data

This retrospective study analyzed the clinical and imaging data of patients with PMT who were diagnosed and treated at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine between August 2021 and June 2025. Pathological confirmation was required for final inclusion. A total of 10 patients (5 males and 5 females) were enrolled, with ages ranging from 28 to 75 years (mean, 56.4 years). The duration of clinical history varied from 5 months to 4 years, with an average of 2.05 ± 1.14 years. Presenting symptoms included unexplained pain in the lumbar spine, back, hips, or lower extremities, often recurrent in nature, accompanied by limited mobility. Early manifestations were nonspecific and typically unresponsive to symptomatic treatment.

Clinical data collected included presenting symptoms, tumor site and origin, lesion size, histopathological findings, and serum phosphate levels before and after treatment. Imaging evaluation comprised CT, MRI, [99mTc]technetium methylene diphosphonate ([99mTc]Tc-MDP) planar bone scintigraphy (BS), as well as [18F]F-AlF-NOTA-octreotide ([18F]F-OC) and [18F]F-fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]F-FDG) PET/CT. Inclusion criteria were: (1) histopathological confirmation of PMT; and (2) availability of at least one imaging modality. Exclusion criteria were: (1) family history of hypophosphatemic osteomalacia; and (2) hypophosphatemic osteomalacia due to alternative causes, such as primary hyperparathyroidism, renal tubular acidosis, or long-term use of adefovir dipivoxil.

Laboratory data, including preoperative and postoperative levels of serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, parathyroid hormone, and 1,25-(OH)2D3, were collected by the treated clinicians.

Imaging examinations

All patients underwent imaging evaluations, including conventional CT, MRI, and nuclear medicine studies (specifically, 3 patients underwent [99mTc]Tc-MDP planar BS, 7 patients underwent [18F]F-OC PET/CT, and 2 patients underwent [18F]F-FDG PET/CT. Additionally, 5 patients received contrast-enhanced CT, and 7 patients underwent contrast-enhanced MRI.

CT scans were performed using Siemens SOMATOM Definition Flash dual-source or Philips Brilliance 64-slice spiral CT systems. Cross-sectional, sagittal, and coronal reconstructions were routinely obtained. MRI examinations were conducted on United Imaging uMR 790 or GE Signa HDxt 3.0T scanners. Sequences included T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), and T2WI with fat suppression. Cross-sectional, coronal, and sagittal reconstructions were performed as needed. Contrast-enhanced scans used gadopentetate dimeglumine (Gd-DTPA) at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg, administered via the median cubital vein.

For [99mTc]Tc-MDP planar BS, whole-body imaging was obtained 3 h after intravenous injection of 925 MBq [99mTc]Tc-MDP using a dual-head camera (Discovery VH; GE Healthcare). [18F]F-OC PET/CT scans were performed on a Siemens Biograph mCT system, 45 min after intravenous injection of 3.7–4.44 MBq/kg of [18F]F-OC. [18F]F-FDG PET/CT scans were also acquired on the Siemens Biograph mCT system, 60 min after intravenous injection of 3.70–5.55 MBq/kg of [18F]F-FDG. Seven patients who underwent [18F]F-OC PET/CT and one patient who underwent [18F]F-FDG PET/CT were scanned from the vertex to the toes, whereas the remaining one patient who underwent [18F]F-FDG PET/CT was imaged from the base of the skull to the mid-thigh.

Data analysis

All imaging studies were independently reviewed by two experienced radiologists or nuclear medicine physicians. They assessed the lesion’s location, size, shape, margin, density or signal intensity, degree of enhancement, tracer uptake and distribution, and its relationship with surrounding tissues. Image interpretation was performed according to standardized protocols. In cases of discrepancies between the two readers, a consensus was reached through discussion. SUV measurements were taken from regions of interest using the appropriate software on the PET/CT images. The lesion locations were determined based on both anatomical landmarks and functional imaging findings.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and paired t-tests were used to compare preoperative and postoperative laboratory values. The assumption of normality for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Lesion locations

The tumors were initially localized in nine patients using functional nuclear medicine imaging (seven by [18F]F-OC PET/CT and two by [18F]F-FDG PET/CT). In one additional patient, the lesion was first detected on a diagnostic CT scan performed to evaluate chronic sinusitis, which revealed an incidental finding of the lesion. A total of 10 lesions were confirmed in 10 patients. Among them, four were located in bone, two in soft tissue, two in the paranasal sinuses, and two in the nasal cavity. The maximum lesion diameter ranged from 1.29 to 4.28 cm, with a mean size of 2.26 ± 1.13 cm.

Laboratory tests

Prior to surgical intervention, all patients exhibited markedly reduced serum phosphorus levels (0.46 ± 0.11 mmol/L; reference range, 0.85–1.51 mmol/L). Serum calcium was largely within the normal range (2.18 ± 0.07 mmol/L; reference range, 2.11–2.52 mmol/L). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was significantly elevated (295.00 ± 143.03 U/L; reference range, 30–120 U/L). Elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels were observed in 8 of 10 patients (119.35 ± 69.83 pg/mL; reference range, 15.0–65.0 pg/mL). Serum 1,25-(OH)₂D concentrations were within normal limits in most patients (22.2 ± 4.28 µg/L; reference range, 20.0–50.0 µg/L), with 7 patients’ levels near the lower limit of normal and 3 patients presenting with subnormal concentrations.

Nine patients underwent surgical resection, and postoperative laboratory follow-up within one month (range, 3 days–1 month) demonstrated a significant increase in serum phosphorus levels (0.94 ± 0.37 mmol/L vs. baseline, p = 0.004). All patients achieved normalization of serum phosphorus during the follow-up period (range, 1–9 months). Serum calcium levels remained stable after surgery (2.19 ± 0.11 mmol/L vs. baseline, p = 0.77). Detailed laboratory results are summarized in Table 1.

Pathological and immunohistochemical findings

Histopathological examination of the ten included cases revealed that PMT lesions were characterized by diffuse infiltration of spindle-shaped cells. The tumor cells were mainly arranged in sheets or nests, with abundant vascularity and proliferation of small vessels within the lesions. In cases involving bone tissue, reactive proliferation of osteocytes and osteoclasts was observed. Mild cellular atypia was present in some patients, but there was no evidence of necrosis or significant mitotic activity.

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed in nine patients (Table 2). SATB2 expression was diffusely positive in all tested cases (9/9). Similarly, SSTR2 was positive in all evaluated cases (6/6). Among the patients tested for both SATB2 and SSTR2, five demonstrated co-expression. CD34 showed positive staining in vascular endothelial cells in most cases (6/8), while CD56 was consistently positive in all tested patients (6/6). In contrast, S-100 (7/7), SOX10 (4/4), Desmin (7/7), and SMA (6/8) were negative in most cases. The Ki-67 proliferation index varied considerably, ranging from 1% to 15%. In addition, focal or weakly positive expression of ERG and β-catenin was detected in some tumors, though their diagnostic significance remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

Imaging findings

PET/CT

[18F]F-OC PET/CT demonstrated increased uptake in all lesions among the 7 patients with PMT (Figs. 1A-D and 2A-C). The SUVmean ranged from 3.04 to 20.77 (mean ± SD, 10.91 ± 6.00), and the SUVmax ranged from 5.47 to 33.58 (18.89 ± 9.14). In comparison, [18F]F-FDG PET/CT demonstrated increased tracer uptake in the lesions of 2 patients (Fig. 3A-C). The SUVmax values were 9.22 and 5.96, with corresponding SUVmean of 6.77 and 3.50. In addition, PET/CT imaging also revealed diffuse skeletal changes throughout the body (Fig. 1E-G) (Table 3).

CT

Among the 10 patients with PMT, 4 had lesions located in bone tissue. These lesions appeared as nodular or rounded, slightly hyperdense masses compared to surrounding tissues, with heterogeneous attenuation, irregular morphology, and ill-defined margins. The mean attenuation values ranged from 300 to 570 HU, with the peripheral regions generally denser than the central areas. In the remaining 6 patients, lesions arose from soft tissues or paranasal sinus and were visualized as abnormal soft-tissue density or discrete soft-tissue masses. Of these, 2 cases demonstrated adjacent bone destruction, 1 case exhibited calcification, and 3 cases showed hypodense components consistent with cystic degeneration or necrosis. Five patients underwent contrast-enhanced CT, which revealed prominent ring-like or striated enhancement in the solid components of the tumors (Table 3).

Systematic evaluation with local CT and whole-body PET/CT demonstrated varying degrees of bone density changes, consistent with osteoporosis and osteomalacia (Fig. 1E). Findings included diffuse reduction in bone mineral density, thinning and rarefaction of trabeculae, multiple vertebral compression deformities, and the presence of both pathological fractures. Fracture distribution included 7 rib fractures, 4 vertebral compression fractures, 4 femoral fractures, 6 pelvic fractures, 3 scapular fractures, and single cases of skull and sternal fractures. Additionally, in two patients with bone lesions, marked osseous destruction surrounding the lesions was observed.

MRI

Nine of the ten patients underwent MRI examination. The MRI appearance of PMTs varied with tissue origin, lesion size, and internal composition. Two cases of soft-tissue PMTs demonstrated heterogeneous signal intensities on T1-weighted images, with increased signal intensity on T1 and decreased signal intensity on T2, ill-defined margins, cystic changes, and heterogeneous enhancement. Larger lesions were associated with compression of adjacent structures, as seen in one plantar lesion measuring approximately 42 × 36 mm. One case of an occipital bone lesion (13 × 10 mm) showed bone destruction with isointensity on T1, hyperintensity on T2, and marked enhancement after contrast administration (Fig. 2D-F). Two cases of femoral and pubic lesions exhibited long T1 and long T2 signals, with associated pathological fractures. Both demonstrated pronounced enhancement following contrast administration. Four cases involving the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses presented with mixed signals, adjacent bone absorption, and heterogeneous enhancement (Fig. 3D-F) (Table 3).

[99mTc]Tc-MDP planar BS

Three patients underwent [99mTc]Tc-MDP planar BS. In all cases, tracer uptake was confined to sites corresponding to fractures, with no abnormal uptake observed elsewhere. Areas of increased radiotracer accumulation were consistent with active bone remodeling at fracture sites. The most prominent findings involved the ribs, where multifocal fractures demonstrated intense tracer uptake (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The primary clinical manifestation of PMT is TIO, characterized by progressive bone pain, muscle weakness, and difficulty walking. Pathological fractures are common, and severe cases may present with height loss. Previous studies report that the average time from symptom onset to clinical diagnosis of TIO is approximately 3 years, and the interval from clinical suspicion to identification of the causative tumor can extend to 5 years [7]. In our cohort, all 10 patients were diagnosed with hypophosphatemic osteomalacia with long-term bone pain and fatigue. The median time from symptom onset to diagnosis was 2 years, with one patient experiencing a 4-year delay. Delayed diagnosis of PMT is influenced by several factors. First, the clinical symptoms are nonspecific; chronic fatigue and bone pain are easily misattributed to conditions such as spondyloarthritis or intervertebral disc herniation. Second, PMTs are typically slow-growing and often small, which makes detection on conventional imaging challenging. Finally, limited awareness of PMT among clinicians contributes to diagnostic delays. This study highlights the clinical importance of a systematic diagnostic approach for PMTs, particularly in addressing the well-recognized delay in diagnosis.

Laboratory findings in PMT patients are often characteristic. They typically show hypophosphatemia, increased urinary phosphate excretion, and elevated alkaline phosphatase, while serum calcium is usually within normal ranges. Complete surgical resection of the tumor can rapidly correct hypophosphatemia, with blood phosphate levels typically normalizing within 1 to 2 weeks after surgery [8]. Our results are consistent with those of previous studies. In our study, PTH levels were elevated in 8 patients, with a mean of 119.35 ± 69.83 pg/mL (normal reference range 15.0–65.0 pg/mL). Previous reports suggest that most PMT patients have PTH levels within the normal range [8]. This discrepancy may be explained by the pathophysiology of TIO. Tumor-derived FGF23 inhibits renal 1-α-hydroxylase activity, reducing the conversion of 25-hydroxyvitamin D to its active form, 1,25(OH)₂D. The resulting vitamin D deficiency can reduce blood calcium, which in turn stimulates the parathyroid glands to secrete more PTH to maintain calcium homeostasis [9]. In the current cohort, serum 1,25(OH)₂D levels were below the normal range in 3 patients, while the remaining 7 patients had values at the lower end of the normal range. Overall, the mean 1,25(OH)₂D concentration was 22.2 ± 4.28 µg/L (reference range, 20.0–50.0 µg/L).

The nine PMT patients in this study demonstrated highly consistent pathological and immunohistochemical features. Histologically, PMT typically exhibits abundant spindle cell infiltration, rich vascularization, and small vessel proliferation. These features align with the “vascular stromal tumor” pattern first described by Weidner, reflecting its strong vascular dependence and low-grade malignant potential [7]. In some cases, reactive proliferation of osteoblasts and osteoclasts was observed, highlighting the tumor’s frequent involvement of bone. Immunohistochemically, SATB2 was positive in all tested cases (9/9), supporting its role as a sensitive marker for osteoblastic tumors and its diagnostic value in bone-derived PMT, consistent with prior reports [10]. SSTR2 was also positive in all tested cases (6/6). Given its role as a target in [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE PET imaging, SSTR2 provides dual utility for both diagnosis and localization of PMT [11, 12]. Partial positivity for CD34 and CD56 indicates the presence of endothelial and neuroendocrine components but lacks specificity. Markers including S-100, SOX10, Desmin, and SMA were consistently negative, excluding neurogenic, myogenic, or other soft tissue origins. The Ki-67 proliferation index varied from 1% to 15%, suggesting that while PMT is typically low-proliferative, its biological behavior may be heterogeneous. Occasional positivity for beta-catenin and ERG was observed but lacks definitive diagnostic significance. Overall, the pathological and immunohistochemical profile of PMT is critical for accurate diagnosis. SATB2 and SSTR2, in particular, are highly sensitive markers that, when interpreted alongside clinical and imaging findings, aid in distinguishing PMT from other spindle cell tumors such as schwannomas and chondrosarcomas.

Most PMTs are benign, solitary lesions. Accurate localization using a combination of functional and anatomical imaging is essential, and surgical resection remains the preferred treatment [13]. The underlying mechanism of PMT is tumor-induced hypophosphatemia, leading to osteomalacia. Plain radiographs are useful for assessing systemic and local bone density loss, trabecular thinning, and pathological fractures, but their sensitivity for detecting the primary tumor is limited [10]. CT imaging of PMT lesions typically demonstrates soft tissue nodules or masses. When located in bone, lesions appear as nodular or rounded areas with slightly increased density. Tumors may exhibit cystic changes or hemorrhage. Small lesions often have well-defined margins, whereas lesions involving surrounding bone may show osteolytic destruction. Contrast-enhanced CT usually reveals moderate to marked enhancement, reflecting the rich vascularity of the tumor stroma [13, 14]. CT also allows detailed assessment of local bone changes and fractures. The PMT cases in this study demonstrated imaging features consistent with these findings. MRI provides superior soft tissue contrast compared with X-ray and CT, allowing better evaluation of lesion extent and relationships with adjacent structures. PMTs generally show isointense or slightly hypointense signal on T1-weighted images and hyperintense or mixed hyperintense signal on T2-weighted images. Contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrates heterogeneous enhancement, which aligns with previous reports [15].

Current evidence indicates that high SSTR2 expression is a characteristic histological feature of PMTs [16]. [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE with high affinity for SSTR2, recently approved by the FDA for evaluating neuroendocrine tumors, is used off-label for PMT imaging [5]. However, its clinical use is limited by the high cost and the short half-life of [68Ga]. [18F]F-OC also shows high affinity for SSTR2. High lesion uptake of [18F]F-OC provides excellent sensitivity and specificity, comparable to [68Ga]Ga-labeled SSTR analogs [17]. The present study demonstrates that whole-body [18F]F-OC PET/CT is a reliable functional imaging modality for tumor localization in patients with TIO, successfully identifying the causative lesions in all seven examined cases, with higher sensitivity than conventional anatomical imaging. [18F]F-FDG PET/CT also remains a valuable tool for detecting PMT lesions and evaluating systemic conditions, as reported in multiple studies. Its main limitation is the lack of specificity, which may result in false-positive uptake, particularly in areas of nonspecific inflammation [18]. Similarly, [99mTc]Tc-MDP planar BS lacks specificity for PMT but is useful for assessing skeletal involvement. [99mTc]Tc-MDP accumulates at fracture sites and can help identify pain associated with occult fractures, which may represent an early clinical manifestation of PMT.

However, this study has several limitations that should be considered. First, due to the rarity of PMT, the sample size is small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the study was conducted at a single center, which may not fully represent the diversity of PMT cases encountered in different clinical settings or geographic regions. Future research should focus on larger, multi-center studies to increase the sample size and improve generalizability. Furthermore, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as it only included patients with pathologically confirmed PMT. Prospective studies would help reduce selection bias and provide more reliable data. In addition, in one patient who underwent [18F]F-FDG PET/CT, the scanning range was limited to the base of the skull to the mid-thigh, which may have led to missed lesions outside the field of view, particularly in the distal extremities. This underscores the importance of whole-body imaging (vertex to toes) in patients with suspected TIO or PMT. The study also lacks a control group or comparison with other similar tumors, making it difficult to fully assess the specificity and sensitivity of the imaging and histopathological findings.

In summary, PMT is a rare neoplasm in which TIO is the predominant clinical manifestation. PMT should be suspected in patients with persistent, multisite bone pain and osteomalacia, often with fractures or pseudofracture lines on imaging, accompanied by hypophosphatemia and elevated alkaline phosphatase, after exclusion of common causes such as primary or secondary hyperparathyroidism. Based on our findings, a stepwise multimodal diagnostic strategy is proposed. Following clinical and biochemical suspicion of TIO, whole-body [18F]F-OC PET/CT should be performed as the first-line modality for tumor localization owing to its high specificity for SSTR-expressing PMT. Once a lesion is localized, dedicated high-resolution CT and or MRI serve as second-line tools to define lesion morphology, margins, internal structure, and relationships to adjacent critical structures for surgical planning. Histopathological confirmation with immunohistochemistry, in which SATB2 and SSTR2 are highly sensitive markers, establishes the definitive diagnosis. [18F]F-FDG PET/CT, while also enabling whole-body imaging, represents a non–tumor-specific modality for PMT and therefore serves as an important complementary technique to [18F]F-OC PET/CT, particularly for assessing metabolic heterogeneity and excluding alternative diagnoses. In parallel, [99mTc]Tc-MDP planar BS plays a complementary role in systemic skeletal assessment, documenting the extent of osteomalacia-related fractures or pseudofractures rather than primary tumor localization. This integrated, stepwise approach enhances diagnostic accuracy and supports timely and effective surgical management.
