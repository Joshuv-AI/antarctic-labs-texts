Integration of InSAR and numerical modelling to assess tailings pond slope deformation affected by reservoir water
Authors: Liwei Lu, Menghua Li, Liang Chao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSlope stability in tailings ponds is crucial for both environmental safety and operational efficiency, as instability can lead to significant hazards. This study introduces a novel integrated approach combining SBAS-InSAR and fluid–structure interaction numerical modeling to enhance the monitoring and stability analysis of tailing pond slopes. A fluid–structure interaction model is presented, incorporating the effects of pore pressure generated by reservoir water level on slope stability, thereby improving the accuracy of internal stress and deformation analysis. Sentinel-1 SAR data were used to monitor surface displacements over a 2-year period (2021–2023), while a three-dimensional numerical model was employed to simulate the mechanical behavior of the tailings dam, considering different material properties and reservoir water level conditions. The results reveal notable spatial patterns of slope deformation, with the presence of reservoir water level and stratigraphic boundaries leading to significant changes in stress and displacement profiles. Comparison between the InSAR results and numerical simulations shows a high level of agreement, with error and statistical analyses, respectively, validating the robustness of the integrated approach. This study provides a comprehensive multi-source method for tailing pond slope monitoring, offering a more accurate and reliable technique for predicting slope instability and improving risk assessment.Keywords: Tailing ponds, Fluid–structure interaction, SBAS-InSAR, Reservoir water, Slope stabilitySubject terms: Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

The primary function of tailings ponds is to store tailings generated during mining operations and to prevent their direct discharge into the environment, thereby avoiding ecological issues such as water pollution and soil contamination1. The destabilization of tailings ponds, including incidents such as dam failure, landslides, and collapses, can have severe consequences2. When a tailings pond destabilizes, the tailings slurry can rapidly flow as a mudflow, destroying downstream villages, factories, and roads, resulting in casualties and property damage. A large volume of tailings slurry may also flow into rivers, lakes, or groundwater systems, causing water pollution. Additionally, fine particulate matter in the tailings can settle on the soil surface, impacting soil structure and fertility, leading to vegetation death or reduced crop yields3,4.

Slope stability failure, such as tailings dam landslides and the collapse of engineering structures, pose a significant threat to regional public safety and the long-term sustainability of engineering infrastructure. On 10 August 2008, an iron ore tailings failure in Shanxi Province, China, resulted in 277 fatalities5. On 4 October 2010, a tailings pond at an aluminum plant in Devecser, Hungary breached, releasing 1 million cubic metres of pollutants, causing 10 deaths, 150 injuries, and contaminating 400,000 km2 of farmland and wetlands6. On 5 November 2015, a dam failure in Brazil, the collapse of the Fundão iron ore tailings dam, released tailings in unprecedented volumes and spread, setting a new record for the scale of tailings pond failures and causing immeasurable impacts on the Atlantic Rainforest ecosystem7. A catastrophic structural breach occurred at an iron ore tailings storage facility in Brumadinho, Minas Gerais, Brazil, on 25 January 2019, resulting in 259 confirmed deaths and 11 missing persons. The leaked heavy metal waste entered the Paraopeba River, causing a sharp increase in total suspended solids and excessive concentrations of toxic elements, severely impacting the livelihoods of communities in the basin8.

Slope instability primarily refers to the phenomenon where natural factors, human activities, or other environmental changes, such as the material properties of tailings9,10, dam structure11, groundwater seepage12, rainfall erosion13 and earthquakes14, reduce the shear strength of the soil or rock on a slope, leading to a disruption in the internal mechanical equilibrium. This results in sliding, slumping, or deformation, ultimately causing the collapse or failure of the slope structure. Groundwater activity is a key factor contributing to slope instability15. Tailings slurry, a mixture of solid particles and water, is produced during the process of discharging process water into the tailings storage facility. The accumulation of precipitation and the particle characteristics of the tailings contribute to the presence of reservoir water level in the tailings stockpile under the influence of gravity16. Dynamic changes in slope hydrological conditions, such as infiltration of atmospheric precipitation and rising water tables, alter the distribution of pore water pressure fields, triggering a reduction in soil matrix suction and a redistribution of effective stress, which systematically deteriorates shear strength parameters and weakens stability17,18.

The study of the principles and mechanisms behind the slope instability of tailings ponds helps identify the key factors influencing instability. Building on this understanding, mathematical models or numerical simulations can be developed to assess the risks associated with tailings pond instability. Slope instability in tailings ponds is typically progressive, with early signs of deformation or seepage. Early detection of these signs through effective monitoring allows for timely intervention with preventive measures. Furthermore, data from monitoring can be used to refine and validate the models, enhancing the accuracy and reliability of predictive outcomes. The application of remote sensing technologies overcomes the limitations of traditional field methods in data acquisition, especially in areas with inaccessible or unstable slopes. A combination of remote sensing techniques, such as digital photogrammetry, laser scanning, infrared thermography, and hyperspectral imaging, offers a new perspective for the comprehensive characterisation of rocky slopes19. Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR) measurements introduce a novel approach to slope monitoring20,21. By utilizing Sentinel-1 InSAR data, accelerated creep behaviour was observed in open-pit slope instability analysis22. Slope monitoring of tailings ponds using second-order statistics of InSAR data provides sufficient warning time before disaster strikes23. The process of slope instability is often nonlinear and complex, potentially involving sliding, deformation, and swelling. Numerical simulations can analyze the strain distribution within slope mechanisms, predicting potential failure modes and locations, thus enabling early detection and intervention. Refined finite element models that reflect the internal structure of slopes can be used for numerical simulations and fluid–structure interaction analysis of high slopes24. Dynamic simulations of the slope failure process, including occurrence, development, and damage yield, offer effective insights into the failure modes and damage evolution mechanisms of complex slopes25. Numerical simulations have shown spatial and temporal variations in the hydraulic response of hillslopes and identified areas where flow landslides might be triggered26.

This study employs remote sensing technology in combination with numerical simulation to analyze the stability of tailing pond slopes from multiple perspectives. Optical remote sensing and SBAS-InSAR provide surface information with varying scales and accuracies, and their integration with numerical simulation enables the comprehensive use of multi-source data, enhancing both the completeness and precision of monitoring. Optical remote sensing offers detailed surface data and facilitates visualisation of the study area. SBAS-InSAR, with its large-scale and long-term deformation data, is particularly useful for monitoring tailing pond slope stability. The deformation characteristics and patterns of the slopes can be derived from this data, and its small baseline algorithm enhances the reliability of displacement measurements. Consequently, the monitoring data can be compared with and validated against numerical simulation results, thereby improving both the reliability of the numerical model and the accuracy of predictions. In this study, a numerical model of the tailings dam, considering the significant influence of pore water pressure, was developed. A fluid–solid coupling constitutive model of porous media was established to interpret the stress development path and spatial displacement characteristics of the tailing slope under varying reservoir water level. By quantitatively examining the relationship between stress and depth, the effect of reservoir water on stress transfer was explored, revealing its influence on slope surface displacement.

SBAS-InSAR

InSAR technology has rapidly advanced since the 1990s and has become a crucial tool for monitoring surface deformation30. By processing the phase difference between two or more radar images, InSAR can provide high-precision deformation information of the surface. To address its limitations, the Multi-Temporal InSAR (MT-InSAR) technique has been introduced. Among the many methods of MT-InSAR31, Persistent Scatterer InSAR (PS-InSAR) and Small Baseline Subset InSAR (SBAS-InSAR) are the two most widely used techniques. However, the main drawback of PS-InSAR is its reliance on permanent scatterers, which limits its applicability in areas covered by vegetation or relatively flat surfaces. The SBAS-InSAR technique, another temporal InSAR method, was developed based on PS-InSAR. This method involves selecting interferogram pairs with shorter temporal and spatial baselines to form multiple small baseline sets. Not only does this reduce the effects of spatial decorrelation, but it also allows for the extraction of slow surface deformation through time series analysis.

The core principle of SBAS-InSAR is based on the decomposition of the interferometric phase and the fitting of time series. Numerical simulation

Since its introduction, FLAC3D (Fast Lagrangian Analysis of Continua in 3 Dimensions) has gradually become a widely used numerical simulation tool in geological and geotechnical engineering due to its computational advantages based on the Lagrangian method32,33. Compared to the traditional limit equilibrium method (LEM), FLAC3D provides more detailed information about the stress state, accounts for material nonlinear behaviour, and its seepage-stress coupling function allows for the analysis of the effects of rainfall and changes in groundwater levels on slope instability. This gives it a significant advantage when combined with real-world conditions. In contemporary research, FLAC3D has become one of the mainstream tools for slope instability analysis, particularly in large-scale 3D simulations. Compared to other numerical simulation methods, such as the finite element method (FEM) and the DEM, FLAC3D performs significantly better when dealing with large deformation problems, especially in simulating slope sliding and failing processes, as it is better at capturing dramatic material deformation and instability process.

FLAC3D constructs its numerical solution system based on an explicit time-stepping algorithm and the finite volume method. The step-by-step iterative calculation process can be broken down as follows: solving the strain rate tensor based on the current node kinematic parameters, coupling the updated strain rate with the previous stress state through the material constitutive model, and obtaining the stress field solution for the current step. Then, a dynamic equilibrium equation is established according to Newton’s second law, and the node displacement and velocity fields are iteratively corrected in combination with the stress distribution and external forces. This sequence of calculations is executed in a continuous cycle of time steps, with convergence analysis performed by dynamically tracking the maximum unbalanced force. When the maximum unbalanced force gradually approaches zero, it indicates that the system is tending towards mechanical equilibrium; if it stabilizes at a non-zero constant, it suggests that the system has entered a local or global steady-state plastic flow. Projection of surface 3D coordinates to the LOS direction

The deformation information inverted by InSAR is measured along the radar line of sight (LOS). Its mathematical interpretation corresponds to the radial projection of the displacement vector of the surface target between the ground point and the satellite platform. This is due to the tilt of the SAR satellite’s viewing angle towards the surface, where the component of the satellite’s three-dimensional displacement projected along the satellite’s LOS direction is measured, rather than the actual three-dimensional displacement of the surface.

The view of the SAR satellite is tilted to the surface, and the LOS displacement is a projection of the three dimensional motion component of the surface along the direction of the SAR line of sight, rather than a complete description of the three dimensional motion. The three-dimensional displacement can be expressed as three components: eastward displacement , northward displacement , and vertical displacement . Results and discussion

SBAS-InSAR analysis of tailings pond

The baseline data (Fig. 3) were derived from calculations. The images from 8 May 2022 and 22 May 2022 (indicated by black dots in Fig. 3) were identified as the master images for the ascending and descending datasets, respectively. Subsequently, the remaining images were interfered with these master images to generate 156 pairs of ascending interferometric images and 166 pairs of descending interferometric images (Fig. 3a, b). Based on the SBAS principle34 and the coherence quality of the datasets, the perpendicular baseline threshold was set to 300 meters and the temporal baseline threshold to 80 days, with a minimum connection number of 3. The maximum absolute normal baseline for the ascending dataset is 276.8 m, the minimum absolute baseline is 0.6 m, and the temporal baseline ranges from 12 days to 72 days. For the descending dataset, the maximum absolute normal baseline is 292.2 m, the minimum absolute baseline is 0.4 m, and the temporal baseline ranges from 12 to 48 days. The processing results indicated that the data were suitable for the SBAS-InSAR process, with minimal uncorrelations.

The Sentinel-1 ascending SAR data from 25 May 2021 to 27 May 2023 and the descending SAR data from 8 June 2021 to 29 May 2023 were preprocessed using Gamma software. The preprocessing steps included image alignment, interferograms generation, geocoding, and data preparation for subsequent SBAS analysis. The SBAS-InSAR analysis was carried out using StaMPS, which processes multiple interferograms, identifies small baseline sets, and performs phase time-series analyses to extract surface deformation signals over extended periods. In the SBAS-InSAR processing chain, the coherence threshold was set to 0.6, and the 30-m resolution ALOS World 3D Digital Elevation Model (AW3D DEM) was used for topographic phase removal and geocoding35. Goldstein filtering was applied to the differential interferograms to reduce noise, improve phase continuity, and mitigate random phase jumps and disturbances. StaMPS36 employs spatial high-pass filtering and temporal low-pass filtering37 to isolate and subsequently remove the Atmospheric Phase Screen (APS) from the mixed phase components. For phase unwrapping, the StaMPS 3D method was employed. Unlike traditional two-dimensional unwrapping, which operates within a single interferogram, the 3D method unravels phase in both spatial and temporal dimensions, effectively addressing phase jumps and noise to enhance unwrapping accuracy.

The spatiotemporal surface deformation derived from phase unwrapping results formed the basis for constructing annual average deformation rate field (Fig. 4). The sign of the LOS deformation rate carries clear physical meaning: positive and negative values correspond to the relative approach or divergence of the surface target and the satellite platform, respectively. The monitoring data revealed that the maximum annual average deformation rates for the LOS direction in the ascending and descending datasets were − 40.1 mm/a and − 36.7 mm/a, respectively. Notably, the spatial distribution of deformation from both datasets showed significant coupling. In residential areas, agricultural land, and transportation facilities, the LOS deformation rate indicates overall stability. In contrast, the key monitoring area-the tailings pond slope-exhibited different deformation responses due to the SAR satellite’s geometric imaging characteristics. The ascending dataset mainly captured deformation at the lower section of the slope, while the descending dataset not only detected deformation in the lower region but also revealed significant deformation at the top of the slope. Further analysis of the dam deformation pattern showed a systematic trend: the deformation gradient increased from the base to the top of the slope, and the area extending from the edge of the dam to the middle of the slope also shows an increasing deformation trend. This spatial evolution was consistently observed in both the ascending and descending datasets.

Numerical modelling of tailings pond

According to geological data, the stratigraphy at the tailing pond site, from youngest to oldest, is as follows: Quaternary Holocene Artificial Accumulation Layer , Quaternary Holocene Floodplain consisting of peaty clay, sand, and gravel, Quaternary Upper Pleistocene Floodplain , and Upper Pleistocene Floodplain . The Quaternary Holocene Artificial Accumulation Layer is a key component of the tailing pond, primarily composed of accumulated tailings sand and gravel fill, forming the initial dam. The tailings sand is categorized based on grain size into medium tailing sand, fine tailing sand, tailing silt, and tailing silty clay. Tailing sand refers to the waste material left after the beneficiation process, which includes crushing, grinding, and sorting ore to extract valuable mineral components. It should be noted that the inherent physicochemical properties of tailings sand endow it with a distinct cohesion that is absent in conventional sand. When the slurry-like mixture of tailings sand and water enters the tailing storage facility, gravity causes the tailings sand to gradually settle, forming a solid pile. As the tailing slurry flows from the top of the dam to the end of the reservoir, according to hydrodynamic principles, the hydraulic gradient gradually decreases with the flow of the slurry, reducing the slurry’s ability to carry tailings. As a result, the tailings are gradually precipitated, from coarse to fine particles. Macroscopically, the accumulated tailings are thick at the front of the dam and thin at the rear of the reservoir, with a vertical thickness that is greater at the top and thinner at the bottom. Microscopically, the tailings exhibit interlayer and cross-bedding structures.

A three-dimensional model of the slope of the tailing pond can be created using terrain data collected from the field for terrain modelling. During the point cloud data processing, the least squares method combined with the B-spline interpolation algorithm is applied to generate a smooth surface, which defines the boundary surface of the tailing pond slope. The sub-floor of the tailing sand is determined based on the deposition pattern of the tailings, and the characteristic surface of the tailing pond is established. This process results in the construction of a solid body between the surface and the boundary. Furthermore, grids of different sizes are created according to the particle size distribution of the tailing sand in the pond, with parameters detailed in Table 4. In the numerical simulation, boundary conditions are defined to describe the system behavior at the boundaries. This ensures the existence of a unique solution for the system of equations, thereby guaranteeing the solvability of the problem. Properly defined boundary conditions also ensure that the mechanical response between the numerical simulation and the actual working conditions remains consistent, thus enhancing the physical fidelity of the results. Balancing model simplification with physical reality, the boundaries at the bottom and sides of the tailings sand, as well as the interface between the tailings sand and the starter dam, were fully constrained in all directions. During the monitoring period, no external factors damaged the base or initial dam of the tailing pond, so these components are assumed to be stationary in the simulation, while the equations of motion are applied to the stacked dams, composed of four different particle sizes of tailings. The presence of reservoir water in a tailings storage facility can alter the effective stresses, which in turn may influence the stability and safety of the dam. Tailings ponds are subjected to fluid–structure interaction during operation, and this coupled system involves time-varying interaction effects between the unsaturated seepage field and the stress field, leading to effective stress redistribution, shear modulus degradation, and other mechanical behaviours under control. The tailings pond seepage discharge facilities are functioning normally, with no observed infiltration damage or seepage escape point, and the entire dam slope remains dry. Slope surface deformation is an external manifestation of the overall stability of the slope, while the internal stress state represents an intrinsic factor. The stress distribution within the dam body slope can be derived by profiling the 3D model obtained from numerical simulation (Fig. 7). Since the slope surface deformation of the dam is primarily governed by stable settlements, the maximum principal stresses are considered for stress analysis. As the basic unit for numerical simulations is the grid point, the volume-weighted average of all areas connected to the grid point is used. The dam profile analysis (Fig. 7b) indicates that the maximum principal stress field exhibits significant terrain coupling characteristics, with its spatial distribution closely matching the slope geometry. The stress follows a vertical gradient attenuation pattern along the slope normal, with the stress peak concentrated in the deepest part directly below the slope top. Comparative analysis reveals that the displacement field obtained through numerical simulation (Fig. 7c) shows an inverse distribution: the displacement value increases with the distance from the slope surface, the maximum deformation occurring at the slope top and forming a radial diffusion pattern toward the deeper and lower parts of the slope.

Effective stress refers to the stress in a rock or soil mass that is directly carried by solid particles, which in turn significantly influences the shear strength of the soil or rock. To investigate the variations in internal stress, we analyzed the stress values at 9 sampling points (Fig. 8), recording both total and effective stress changes with depth. It was observed that, apart from sampling points 1 and 7, abrupt stress changes were noted at multiple locations across the remaining seven sampling points. This phenomenon is attributed to the fact that the boundary layers within the slope represent the interfaces between different geotechnical materials. These materials exhibit variations in mechanical properties, such as elasticity modulus, shear strength, and density. The resulting changes in mechanical properties disrupt the continuity of stress transfer across the layered boundaries, leading to sudden stress fluctuations. Monitoring data revealed a distinct gradient in both the maximum principal stress and effective stress near the submerged surface, highlighting the impact of reservoir water on reducing effective stress. Below the submerged level, pore water pressure follows a hydrostatic distribution, with an increasing trend with depth, which directly contributes to the attenuation of effective stress. According to Terzaghi effective stress principle, the effective stress in the difference between total stress and pore water pressure. Sampling points 2 and 7, located at shallower water depths, exhibited minimal displacements (Table 5). In contrast, sampling points 5, 6, and 8, with deeper water levels, showed larger displacement values. Although sampling points 2, 4, and 7 are characterized by similar water depth levels, the saturated zone heights at points 2 and 4 are greater, resulting in higher surface displacements compared to point 7. Validate

To validate the reliability of the fluid–structure interaction model considering reservoir water, 9 InSAR monitoring points were selected (Fig. 9) for comparison with the numerical simulation results. The InSAR results depicted in Figure. 9 have been compared with numerical simulation outcomes at identical sampling locations, demonstrating strict spatial correspondence between the two. Given that the slopes of the tailing pond have a stepped step, the selected points are located at different elevations, with multiple points at the same height, and the locations of the points align with the concentration areas of the InSAR measurements. The results from both datasets are shown together in a single plot, which was done purely for visualization purposes to increase the apparent density of measurement points and to provide a clearer representation of the spatial deformation pattern. Spatially, the numerical simulation results exhibit an arc-shaped pattern originating from the center of the slope crest, with varying radii (Fig. 9b). Displacements are larger closer to the center of the slope crest, while smaller displacements are observed further from the center, near the edge of the facility. The InSAR monitoring results generally correlate with the numerical simulation outcomes, showing smaller displacements in the lower and edge portions of the slope, and larger displacements as the measurement points approach the higher regions and the center (Fig. 9a).

In enable comparison of the numerical simulation results at the sampling points with the InSAR measurements, projection transformations and coordinate unification of the numerical results are required. The 3D displacement fields from the numerical simulation were exported as data tables at specified sampling points that correspond to the InSAR observation points. The 3D vector was projected onto the satellite’s LOS direction using a geometric formula (Eq. 12). The InSAR results in the WGS84 coordinate system were converted to the same projected coordinate system (CGCS2000) as the numerical model using the coordinate transformation tools in ArcGIS. A comparative analysis of the InSAR monitoring data and the numerical simulation assimilation results (Fig. 10) reveals that the maximum relative deviation is 15.56% (− 8.8 mm) and the minimum relative deviation is 0.08% (0.02 mm). The full-scale absolute deviation ranges from 0.02mm to 10mm. To further verify the consistency between the InSAR-derived LOS displacements and the numerical simulation results, a statistical comparison was carried out. The analysis shows the RMSE of 3.7 mm. These values quantitatively demonstrate the strong agreement between the two datasets, confirming that the numerical simulations reliably reproduce both the displacement magnitudes and spatial trends observed by InSAR.

Conclusion

In this study, a multi-level geotechnical structure numerical model, coupled with the pore water pressure field, was developed to explore the control mechanism of the water table on the progressive instability deformation of slopes. Surface deformation data of the tailing pond slopes were obtained using SBAS-InSAR, with the displacement data serving as a constraint to the numerical model within a small error range. This study combines optical remote sensing, SBAS-InSAR, and a coupled fluid–solid numerical model to fully leverage the advantages of remote sensing technology in long-term and large-scale monitoring, as well as the ability of numerical simulations in detailed internal stress analysis. This approach compensates for the limitations of using a single technology. This cross-validation method, which integrates remote sensing data and numerical models, enhances the credibility of the prediction results and provides a new technical approach for similar studies. It significantly improves the comprehensiveness and accuracy of slope monitoring predictions.

Limitations and future work

Although the integrated InSAR–numerical modelling framework presented in this study demonstrates good consistency and provides valuable insights into the deformation of tailings pond slopes, several assumptions and limitations should be acknowledged. First, the numerical model assumes isotropic material properties within each stratigraphic unit, which may oversimplify the inherent heterogeneity of tailings deposits. Second, hydrological conditions were simplified by considering stable reservoir water levels, without accounting for transient processes such as rainfall infiltration or rapid drawdown. Third, InSAR measurements are constrained to the radar line-of-sight direction, potentially underestimating horizontal displacement components.

In addition, the comparison between InSAR-derived displacements and numerical results should be understood as a macroscopic validation of deformation patterns under representative conditions, rather than a one-to-one correspondence in time and space. Local in-situ monitoring methods (e.g., inclinometers, GPS surveys, total stations) can capture micro-scale displacements that are not fully resolved by satellite imagery. Due to data limitations, such in-situ observations were not incorporated in this study. Future research should therefore aim to integrate multi-scale monitoring-including satellite remote sensing, ground-based instruments, and refined numerical simulations-to enhance the robustness and comprehensiveness of slope stability assessments.
