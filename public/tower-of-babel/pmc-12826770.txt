Combined Endovascular and Microsurgical Treatment for a Giant Thrombosed Posterior Cerebral Artery Aneurysm: A Case Report
Authors: Airi MIYAZAKI, Yoshihiko MOTOHARA, Yuji DEKITA, Yoshihiro TAKESUE, Tatemi TODAKA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Giant thrombosed aneurysms of the posterior cerebral artery are rare and pose significant challenges in both diagnosis and management. We present a case of a 61-year-old woman who had been under observation for 5 years because of a partially thrombosed posterior cerebral artery aneurysm. The aneurysm gradually enlarged to 50 mm, resulting in left hemiparesis and homonymous hemianopsia. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed cerebral edema and contrast enhancement of the aneurysmal wall and intraluminal space. Digital subtraction angiography demonstrated a slowly perfused aneurysm located at the P1-P2 segment, with a medial posterior choroidal artery branching from the short P1 segment. Given the limited microsurgical exposure and the presence of critical perforators, a hybrid approach was selected. Endovascular parent artery occlusion was first performed using coils and n-butyl cyanoacrylate, targeting both the intraluminal space and the vasa vasorum. Seven days later, microsurgical proximal clipping and partial thrombectomy were carried out via a subtemporal approach. Although transient neurologic deterioration occurred postoperatively, significant improvement was observed with rehabilitation. Follow-up imaging showed marked reduction in aneurysmal size and localized infarction in the posterior cerebral artery territory. This case illustrates the effectiveness of a combined endovascular and microsurgical strategy for treating giant thrombosed posterior cerebral artery aneurysms. Individualized treatment planning based on anatomic complexity, collateral flow, and perforator preservation is essential for achieving successful outcomes in such challenging cases.

Introduction

Posterior cerebral artery (PCA) aneurysms are rare, accounting for approximately 1% of all intracranial aneurysms.1) However, a relatively high proportion of PCA aneurysms (ranging from 24% to 42%) are classified as large or giant, making their management particularly challenging.1-3) Giant thrombosed aneurysms, especially those involving the P1-P2 segment, are associated with complex anatomy, mass effect, and a high risk of complications, yet optimal treatment strategies remain controversial due to their rarity and morphological variability. Treatment options include both endovascular and microsurgical approaches, each with specific risks. Key considerations include preservation of perforating arteries, control of inflow and outflow, and, in some cases, management of the vasa vasorum, which may contribute to aneurysmal growth even after embolization.4,5) While some reports describe successful outcomes with bypass and parent artery occlusion (PAO),6,7) others have favored trapping or direct clipping.8)

Here, we report a rare case of a giant, partially thrombosed PCA aneurysm at the P1-P2 segment that was successfully treated using a hybrid strategy combining embolization with n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) and coils, followed by direct surgical thrombectomy. This case highlights the importance of individualized planning and combined-modality treatment in managing complex PCA aneurysms.

Case Report

A 61-year-old woman with a history of systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis had been under observation for 5 years because of a partially thrombosed PCA aneurysm. Gradual enlargement of the aneurysm was noted, and she developed left hemiparesis and homonymous hemianopsia, which were severe enough to prevent her from standing or walking independently. Her consciousness was clear, though she complained of a headache. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a 50-mm aneurysm with surrounding cerebral edema on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging, and contrast enhancement of the aneurysmal wall and intraluminal space (Fig. 1). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) showed a slowly perfused aneurysm at the P1-P2 segment of the right PCA (Fig. 1). Three-dimensional rotational angiography revealed a medial posterior choroidal artery branching from a short remaining P1 segment, precluding a balloon occlusion test. We planned a combined approach: endovascular embolization of the intraluminal space followed by surgical clipping of the P1 origin and evacuation of the intraluminal hematoma. Bypass surgery was not performed because collateral flow and delayed peripheral perfusion were anticipated. Our goal was to block both the blood flow into the aneurysmal cavity and the vasa vasorum to prevent further aneurysmal growth.

Endovascular treatment

Under general anesthesia, a 4-French (4 Fr), 90 cm guiding catheter (ASAHI FUBUKI Dilator Kit; Asahi Intec, Aichi, Japan) was introduced into the right vertebral artery via a transfemoral approach. An Echelon-10 microcatheter (ev3, Irvine, CA, USA) was delivered using a CHIKAI 14 (Asahi Intec); however, because of the tortuosity of the P1 segment, the microcatheter deviated into the thrombus. A Marathon microcatheter (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) with a J-shaped CHIKAI X10 (Asahi Intec) was subsequently able to traverse the intraluminal space. Coils and 15% NBCA were deployed to embolize the vasa vasorum and intraluminal space, extending from the normal P2 segment to its proximal entry point. Although slow residual flow was observed in the intraluminal space immediately after embolization, follow-up DSA at 5 days demonstrated complete obliteration and maintained patency of the choroidal artery. Despite the presence of leptomeningeal collateral flow from the middle cerebral artery to the PCA territory, MRI revealed an infarction in the PCA territory, and the patient's symptoms remained unchanged (Fig. 2).

Proximal clipping and thrombectomy

Seven days after embolization, the patient underwent proximal clipping and thrombectomy. Using a subtemporal approach, the oculomotor nerve was dissected free from the aneurysmal wall, and the origin of the P1 segment dissection of the PCA was clipped. Subsequently, a portion of the temporal lobe was incised to evacuate the intraluminal hematoma. Within the aneurysmal cavity, NBCA and coils used to embolize the intraluminal space were identified. During the procedure, motor-evoked potentials in the left upper limb disappeared; therefore, only partial thrombectomy was performed. The aneurysmal wall was left open at the end of the surgery (Fig. 3).

Postoperative course

Postoperatively, the patient developed clip-induced right oculomotor nerve palsy and experienced transient worsening of left hemiparesis. MRI revealed no new infarctions and demonstrated a reduction in aneurysmal size; however, the medial posterior choroidal artery could not be visualized because of clip artifacts. After rehabilitation, marked improvement was observed at the 3-month postoperative outpatient follow-up in the patient's oculomotor nerve palsy, left hemianopsia, and left hemiparesis. Although sensory disturbance persisted in the left hemibody, she was able to walk with a cane. MRI showed further reduction in aneurysmal size, and infarction in the occipital lobe was confined to a localized area. At the subsequent outpatient visit 6 months after surgery, the aneurysm had further decreased in size (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Giant thrombosed aneurysms are rare, and optimal treatment strategies have yet to be established due to variations in management depending on their location. In the limited number of reports on giant aneurysms located at the P1-P2 segment of the PCA, various treatment strategies have been described. Sato et al. (2021)8) reported a case treated with trapping and conducted a literature review on microsurgical approaches. Furthermore, Shin et al. (2013)6) and Ponce et al. (2004)7) reported cases treated with bypass in combination with coil embolization of the aneurysm and PAO. As illustrated in these reports, treatment strategies revolve around whether to perform trapping or aneurysm embolization with PAO, and whether to combine these with bypass surgery. Regardless of the treatment strategy chosen, the primary goals are the preservation of perforating arteries and complete obliteration of the aneurysm. The PCA territory benefits from abundant collateral circulation, and PAO has been reported to result in favorable occlusion rates with minimal functional deficits.9,10) Accordingly, a deconstructive technique such as PAO is regarded as the primary treatment option for achieving complete obliteration of PCA aneurysms. In the present case, PAO was successfully performed without bypass, and the resulting infarction was minimal.

Consideration of the vasa vasorum is important in achieving complete and durable aneurysm obliteration. Iihara et al.4) reported a case in which the aneurysm showed continued growth after coil embolization, likely due to persistent perfusion via the vasa vasorum. Conversely, Otawa et al.5) achieved favorable and stable aneurysm occlusion by performing PAO along with NBCA embolization targeting the vasa vasorum. Visualization of the vasa vasorum by microangiography allowed for successful coil embolization in the present case.

The decision between endovascular and microsurgical approaches should be guided by factors such as the ability to achieve secure PAO, visualization of the vasa vasorum, and preservation of perforating arteries. In the present case, the giant size of the aneurysm precluded adequate visualization for direct microsurgical treatment. Accordingly, initial endovascular PAO was performed, followed by a direct surgical approach to reinforce the occlusion and evacuate the hematoma. Embolization with NBCA, in addition to coils, was effective in treating the broad intraluminal space. The potential risks and complications inherent to each therapeutic approach must also be considered during treatment planning. In the report by Trevisi et al.11) on treatment strategies for fusiform-type aneurysms, deconstructive techniques were associated with a significantly higher risk of complications compared with reconstructive techniques. However, surgical PAO showed a lower complication rate than bypass procedures (22.6% vs. 36.6%). Among all treatment modalities, endovascular PAO demonstrated the most favorable outcomes compared with other endovascular and surgical techniques.

Regarding the craniotomy, a combination of the transsylvian and subtemporal approaches was initially planned for this case. However, zygomatic arch removal was ultimately unnecessary, and the P1 segment of the PCA could be adequately exposed using only a subtemporal approach with a craniotomy measuring approximately 7 × 6 cm. Although a combined transsylvian approach with wide temporal lobe retraction to expose the entire aneurysm, or trapping through an extended craniotomy without adjunctive endovascular treatment, could have been alternative options, the combined endovascular and microsurgical strategy employed in this case represented the most minimally invasive approach. This case highlights the potential of a hybrid strategy combining endovascular PAO and direct surgical thrombectomy in managing giant thrombosed PCA aneurysms with complex anatomy.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the successful management of a giant thrombosed aneurysm through a combined endovascular and microsurgical approach.
