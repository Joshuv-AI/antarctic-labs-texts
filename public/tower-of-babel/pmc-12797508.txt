Chinese healthcare professionals’ perceptions of interventions in elder abuse: a qualitative study
Authors: Qinqiuzi Yi, Caie Chen, Yuan Zhang, Danchen Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElder abuse is a growing public health concern in aging Chinese populations, yet timely intervention by healthcare professionals is often hindered by cultural factors. This study aimed to explore hospital-based healthcare professionals’ perceptions of elder abuse interventions across personal, behavioral, and environmental dimensions, to inform future training program design.MethodsThis qualitative study explored perceptions of elder abuse interventions through semi-structured interviews with 24 participants (12 medical doctors and 12 nurses) from two tertiary hospitals in Yichang, China. Data were analyzed using directed content analysis based on Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) to examine cultural influences and professional differences.ResultsPerceptions regarding elder abuse interventions were dynamically influenced by the interaction of personal factors (knowledge and skills, outcome expectancies, self-efficacy, and role cognition), behavioral factors (direct and indirect intervention), and environmental factors (family, hospital, and government levels). A distinct cultural theme—family boundary concerns—significantly influenced intervention willingness. Nurses highlighted empathy, life care, and public support, whereas medical doctors emphasized professional responsibility and institutional coordination.ConclusionsFindings support the SCT triadic reciprocal determinism: personal factors influence and are influenced by behavioral and environmental elements. Consequently, a multilevel strategy aligned with SCT, such as enhancing personal capacity, strengthening institutional support, and optimizing systemic safeguards, is essential to empower hospital-based healthcare professionals to intervene in elder abuse.

Background

Elder abuse is a pervasive and serious public health and human rights concern. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines elder abuse as “a single or repeated act or lack of appropriate action, occurring within any relationship where there is an expectation of trust that causes harm or distress to an older person.” It commonly manifests in various forms, including physical, psychological, sexual, and financial abuse, as well as neglect [1]. According to the WHO, one in six older adults has experienced some form of abuse, with neglect being the most common type [2]. Elder abuse negatively affects the physical and mental health and quality of life of older adults. Furthermore, it poses a significant public health burden and is closely associated with higher mortality and hospitalization rates and increased social isolation [3–5].

According to the 2024 National Bulletin on the Development of Undertakings for the Aged released by the Office of the National Working Committee on Aging under the Ministry of Civil Affairs of the People’s Republic of China, China has 310.31 million people aged ≥ 60 years, making up 22% of the total population [6]. This represents a rapid increase from the 21.1% recorded in 2023 [7], highlighting the accelerated pace of population aging. This rapid aging, combined with non-aging factors such as intergenerational conflict [8] and rising caregiving stress [9], has amplified public health concerns about elder abuse. National and regional studies have reported prevalence rates of elder abuse in China ranging from 13% to 20% [10–12], substantially higher than the 7.83% prevalence of elder abuse reported by the 2021 meta-analysis for East Asia [13]. However, cultural norms such as filial piety and the stigma of exposing “family shame” often cause elder abuse to be perceived as a private matter, resulting in widespread underreporting and limited public awareness [14, 15].

Healthcare professionals play a vital role in identifying and intervening in elder abuse, particularly in hospital settings where they frequently encounter older adults presenting with abuse-related injuries or psychological distress. Their willingness and ability to intervene, however, are strongly shaped by cultural expectations and professional roles. In China’s collectivist context, concerns about maintaining family harmony may discourage intervention. Existing research on elder abuse interventions in China remains limited and has largely focused on nurses and nursing assistants [16–18], leaving a significant gap in understanding the perceptions and roles of medical doctors, and how their views may differ from those of nurses.

According to Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), behavior results from reciprocal interactions among personal, behavioral, and environmental factors. Personal factors influence behavior, with behavior affecting the cognitive system, promoting adjustment, and deepening cognition. Environmental factors determine the basic direction and intensity of behavior; conversely, the sustained influence of individual behavior can also have a transformative impact on the environment. Environmental factors stimulate and shape human cognition, whereas personal cognition and beliefs influence how individuals perceive the environment [19].

Healthcare professionals’ personal perceptions and attitudes are key drivers of their interventions in elder abuse, both reflecting and shaping their views. Additionally, the hospital environment, government, and societal norms can constrain or encourage interventions, thereby molding perspectives. Therefore, the intervention measures adopted by healthcare professionals result from combined influences of their perceptions of the problem, the internal working environment of the hospital, and a broader sociocultural context. Based on these insights, we propose that SCT provides a foundation for exploring healthcare professionals’ perceptions of interventions in elder abuse, capturing the complex factors that influence their decisions.

Therefore, this study aimed to employ SCT as a theoretical foundation to explore hospital-based medical doctors’ and nurses’ perceptions of elder abuse interventions in China. The findings aim to inform culturally sensitive training and multilevel strategies to enhance healthcare professionals’ capacity to prevent and respond to elder abuse.

Methods

Design and participants

We employed directed content analysis, guided by SCT, to examine hospital-based healthcare professionals’ comprehensive perceptions of elder abuse interventions through semi-structured interviews. The participants were medical doctors and nurses from two tertiary A-grade hospitals in Yichang City, Hubei Province, China. Within China’s three-tier healthcare system, tertiary A-grade hospitals denote the pinnacle of medical care, functioning as regional referral centers that integrate clinical services, education, and research. The selection of participants was meticulous, ensuring that the study captured perspectives from professionals working in representative, advanced hospital settings. We focused on tertiary hospital settings for two reasons. First, tertiary A-grade hospitals treat older adults with more complex health conditions, including those caused by abuse, increasing the likelihood of professionals encountering abuse cases. Second, unlike community health centers or residential aged care facilities, tertiary hospitals have integrated medical, administrative, and security support systems that shape unique intervention behaviors. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) professionals working in departments with primarily older adult patients (e.g., geriatrics, neurology, and infectious diseases); (2) at least 3 years of clinical work experience (prior experience in handling elder abuse cases was not required, as the study aimed to capture a broad range of perceptions and experiences, including those with limited exposure); (3) a professional qualification certificate; and (4) voluntary participation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) professionals working in departments that typically do not serve older adults (e.g., obstetrics and pediatrics); (2) medical interns or trainees who did not yet hold full professional qualification. Based on the study by Guest et al. showing that the required sample size for qualitative interviews could be achieved within 12 interviews [20], 24 interviews (12 medical doctors and 12 nurses) were conducted. The interviews were conducted from April to June 2025. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, as evidenced by no new codes or themes emerging during the final two interviews.

This study focused on healthcare professionals’ perceptions of interventions in elder abuse perpetrated by family members. This focus was chosen given that elder abuse perpetrated by family members is the much more prevalent form and is most closely linked to the cultural factors, such as“filial piety” and “family shame.”

Recruitment and data collection

This study employed a combination of convenience and purposive sampling for participant selection. First, we contacted hospital administrative staff to explain the study’s objectives and criteria, both verbally and in writing. We also sent an instruction manual for the interview survey. The manual included a form for potential participants to complete and return to the contact address provided, indicating their consent to participate in the interviews. After receiving the applications, the researcher scheduled an interview, during which the study objectives were verbally explained to the participants.

The interview guide was developed based on SCT as the primary framework, informed by a literature review. It included eight open-ended questions addressing personal factors (e.g., role perceptions, knowledge, self-efficacy, outcome expectations), environmental factors (e.g., perceived facilitators and barriers within organizational and social environments), and behavioral factors (e.g., past and potential intervention behaviors). To further elaborate on the environmental factors, we incorporated the micro, mezzo, and macro levels of Social Ecosystem Theory (SET) [21] to explore facilitators and barriers across different systemic tiers. The interview guide was reviewed and revised for validity by three researchers. Three interviewers (Yi, Chen, and Huang) had prior experience in qualitative research and received specific training for this study, which covered interview techniques, ethical considerations, and avoiding leading questions. Their responsibilities included conducting interviews, ensuring a comfortable and confidential environment, and validating transcripts for accuracy. They conducted all 30–45 min face-to-face interviews to ensure consistency and neutrality throughout the data collection process, which were audio-recorded with their consent and transcribed verbatim for analysis and validation.

We used qualitative directed content analysis to analyze the healthcare professionals’ perceptions of interventions in elder abuse. The directed content analysis methodology explores a phenomenon of interest using theory as a guide [22, 23]. Following established procedures of directed content analysis [22, 23], the process entailed five key steps. First, a preliminary coding framework was deductively developed based on the three core dimensions of SCT. For a clearer analytical structure, we classified the environmental factors by applying Social Ecosystem Theory (SET). This framework distinguishes three nested levels: the Micro system (encompassing patient families and direct care interactions), the Mezzo system (hospital setting), and the Macro system (governmental policy context). Second, four researchers independently coded the transcripts to identify and extract relevant data segments. Third, through an iterative process of constant comparison, these codes were grouped into subcategories and broader categories. Fourth, the final categorical structure was mapped back to the SCT framework to ensure theoretical consistency. Finally, any coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the researchers to reach a consensus. A cross-comparative analysis was conducted between the medical doctors and nurses. The purpose of this comparison was to explore how distinct professional roles and responsibilities shape differing perceptions and strategies in elder abuse interventions, thereby uncovering profession-specific challenges and providing a foundation for developing tailored support and training. To ensure the rigor and trustworthiness of the qualitative analysis, we adhered to the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba [24, 25]. Specific strategies were implemented as follows: (1) Credibility was achieved through prolonged engagement with the data via repeated reading of transcripts and peer debriefing among research team members to resolve coding discrepancies; (2) Dependability was enhanced by maintaining a complete audit trail that documented the entire research process, including sampling decisions, iterations of the coding framework, and analytical memos, allowing the research process to be auditable; (3) Confirmability was addressed through the practice of reflexivity, where researchers continually reflected on how their own backgrounds and assumptions might influence data interpretation, and through the detailed documentation of the decision-making process during analysis; (4) Transferability was supported by providing contextual descriptions of the participants, and recruitment procedures, enabling readers to assess applicability to similar settings.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Ethics Committee of China Three Gorges University (approval number: 2024M010), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All the participants provided informed consent. During the formal interviews, the participants received verbal and written explanations of the purpose of the study, and their agreement to participate was provided in writing.

Results

Participants

Medical doctors consisted of four males (33.3%) and eight females (66.7%), representing six departments: Geriatrics; Burn, Plastic, and Wound Repair Surgery, which manages a significant number of older adults with conditions such as diabetic foot ulcers; Infectious Diseases; Neurology; Gynecology; and Dermatology. Nurses were predominantly female (91.7%) and worked in three departments identified as high-risk for encountering potential elder abuse cases. This designation refers to departments where the patient demographics (e.g., a high proportion of older adults) and the nature of their conditions (e.g., chronic wounds indicative of possible neglect, cognitive impairments that increase vulnerability) create a heightened likelihood for healthcare professionals to identify signs of abuse or neglect.

Personal factors

Both medical doctors’ and nurses’ perceptions of interventions for elder abuse were divided into four major categories: “knowledge and skills,” “outcome expectancies,” “self-efficacy,” and “role cognition.”

Knowledge and skills

Both medical doctors and nurses highlighted the importance of ‘service capability,’ ‘professional knowledge,’ ‘self-defense skills,’ and ‘Career development quality’ in terms of unknown “knowledge and skills.” However, medical doctors prioritized [treatment skills], [Experience accumulation], and [Responsibility development]—“(treatment) professional skills need to be stronger,” and “enhancing one’s experience by working in different positions and places (D5)”—whereas nurses highlighted [Disease-related knowledge], [Clinical observation ability], [Personal quality], and [policy study]: “I think it is more about communication. Or perhaps daily observation abilities” (N2).

Outcome expectancies

Both groups expressed positive and negative outcome expectancies. Among the common negative outcome expectancies, [family boundary concerns] were primarily rooted in traditional Chinese values that prioritize family privacy. As N4 noted, “Even a wise judge cannot resolve family matters; we cannot delve deeply into them and resolve conflicts for others.” N9 said “I feel like it’s still their family’s business. Sometimes you just don’t feel comfortable talking about it too much with others.” D5 stated, “Family disputes are not something to get involved in,” while D7 said, “It’s probably not wise to interfere too much in family matters.” This reflects their sensitivity toward internal familial issues. Nurses’ positive outcome beliefs were often rooted in [empathy-driven] and [supportive condition guarantee]—“Because (we will also grow old), I can empathize,”(N3)—while medical doctors emphasized [Duty-driven] and [Smooth communication expectation], as seen in the response by D1, who noted that: “it is the duty of my work,” and D7, who said that “the patient’s family is easy to approach and willing to communicate.”

Self-efficacy

Regarding self-efficacy, health professionals demonstrated confidence, albeit in different areas. Nurses expressed confidence in their ‘perception of capability boundary confidence,’ ‘intrinsic motivation confidence,’ and ‘self-regulation ability confidence’. As N3 noted, “We are willing to do so within the scope of our capabilities” and N2 said, “Individuals possess agency, and so do I.” Conversely, medical doctors primarily focus on confidence in ‘time and energy control. Notably, time and energy control appeared in medical doctors’ self-efficacy and negative outcome expectations, indicating that while medical doctors believe they can manage these aspects, they might avoid situations that lack sufficient external support. As D2 mentioned, “Because he has been hospitalized for a long time and has limited patience, you may see something that can be prevented, but when you don’t see it, you simply cannot prevent it from happening.”

Role cognition

In terms of role cognition, both groups identified as ‘conflict mediators’ and ‘proactive interveners,’ but with differences. Nurses further emphasized their nursing role, e.g., “It is my primary responsibility, the essential role of a nurse,” [N10] while medical doctors viewed themselves as ‘emotional comforters’ and ‘resource coordinators,’ e.g., “I may serve as an intermediary for the old adult to communicate with the outside world and seek social assistance” [D8]; “I believe it is a role of care and encouragement” [D12].

Behavioral factors

Participants’ intervention behaviors reflected both direct and indirect strategies.

Direct interventions

Nurses’ direct intervention focused on ‘life care.’ For example, N8 mentioned, “Providing him with some small assistance in his daily life,” and N5 stated, “During his hospitalization, we must ensure his safety.” Although both professionals performed ‘protective clinical interventions’, the nurses emphasized [regular clinical observations]. In contrast, medical doctors primarily achieve this through [providing medical advice] and [entrusting nurses to observe].

Indirect interventions

Medical doctors and nurses consistently used three approaches: ‘administrative reporting,’ ‘security intervention,’ and ‘external agency assistance.’ For example, D8 stated, “(In severe cases) we would contact the police for intervention.” N3 also stated, “If it involves physical violence, such as that situation, we would generally call the hospital’s security department, and the police would intervene.”

Environmental factors

Participants described multilevel environmental influences—family, hospital, and government—that shaped intervention willingness and feasibility.

Micro-system (family)

At the micro-system level (patient families), ‘Family Cooperation barriers’ are common healthcare professional barriers. Nurses faced difficulty in obtaining abuse information, such as, “mainly not knowing what exactly happened between older adults and their family members. Is it the family’s issue or the older adult’s issue?” (N10); and “others might not be willing to speak up” (N8). Medical doctors, meanwhile, encountered the challenge of “not being understood by the patient” (D5) and “family members or the patient not understanding” (D1), making them face the predicament of “difficulty in gaining recognition.”

Mezzo-system (hospital)

Both groups emphasized the importance of ‘Organizational operation support’ and ‘Personnel and capacity building.’ However, regarding ‘Organizational operation support,’ nurses stress the need for [institutional contingency plans support]. While medical doctors call for increased investment in [responsible for external resource coordination], [intervention response mechanism guarantee] and [geriatrics department development]. For example, D5 stated, “The relevant departments of the hospital may be able to respond more promptly. For instance, if such an incident occurs within the hospital—where an older adult patient is abused, and security personnel are slow to respond despite repeated prompts—the patient may be subjected to further abuse by their family members.” D10 added, “I believe the hospital can contribute by developing geriatric departments.” Additionally, nurses emphasized higher expectations and demands for rewards, commendations, or supportive policies provided by the hospital, with the need for incentives and recognition. N2 mentioned, “Just like reporting adverse events, the hospital should offer rewards.”

Macro-system (government)

Healthcare professionals emphasized the need to ‘Improving legal norms,’ ‘Strengthen enforcement mechanisms,’ ‘Provide behavioral support and guarantee,’ and ‘Capacity building.’ However, beyond the aforementioned points, nurses placed particular emphasis on ‘Positive public opinion guidance,’ reflects the nurses’ desire that the government should create a favorable environment and social atmosphere through positive publicity and education, especially through positive reports on healthcare professionals’ patient relationships. For example, “The government should report more positive news. Positive news is rarely reported, while negative news spreads quickly online. If there is a report of abuse in a hospital, it can escalate to a large scale online, and the government may not investigate the actual circumstances of the abuse” (N9).

Personal factors influence healthcare professionals’ intervention behaviors. Medical doctors’ pathways highlight time and energy control confidence, the role of emotional comforter and resource coordinator. However nurses’ pathways emphasize perception of capability boundary confidence, intrinsic motivation confidence, self-regulation ability confidence, and the nursing role. Behavioral factors represent concrete intervention practices, including direct interventions (verbal, behavioral, and protective clinical actions) and indirect interventions (seeking institutional or external support). These behaviors both reflect and reshape personal perceptions through continuous feedback and self-regulation. Environmental factors at micro (patient family), mezzo (hospital), and macro (government) levels interact bidirectionally with personal and behavioral domains. Medical doctors’ pathways highlight improving legal norms, strengthening enforcement mechanisms, providing behavior support and guarantee, and capacity building that belong to government-affiliated elements, while nurses’ pathways further emphasizes the importance of proactive public opinion guidance by the government. Overall, this reciprocal process reflects the SCT principle of triadic reciprocal determinism.

Discussion

Dynamic interactions and cultural adaptation of SCT dimensions

Previous studies have applied SCT to the behavioral intentions of healthcare professionals in domains such as patient safety and chronic disease management [26–28]. However, the application of SCT to understanding healthcare professionals’ intentions regarding elder abuse remains insufficient. This study extends the application of SCT by integrating cultural and contextual factors into the unique setting of elder abuse interventions, revealing the interactive role of social cognition and cultural norms in shaping intervention behaviors. Using SCT as an analytical framework, we identified three core dimensions—personal factors (knowledge, skills, outcome expectancies, self-efficacy, and role cognition), behavioral factors (direct and indirect intervention), and multilevel environments (family, hospital, and government)—that jointly influence healthcare professionals’ perceptions and actions. Unlike traditional SCT applications focusing on individual cognition, our model emphasizes the cultural modulation of these mechanisms, revealing how family-centered values influence healthcare professionals’ decisions to intervene.

The pervasive influence of “family boundary concerns”—rooted in the Chinese cultural adage that “even an honest official finds it hard to settle a family quarrel”—emerged as contextual moderators. This concern consistently dampened positive outcome expectancies and heightened perceived risks across both doctor and nurse groups, thereby inhibiting intervention initiation. In this sense, our study not only validates the reciprocal mechanism proposed by SCT [19], but also extends it by incorporating cultural determinants, contributing to a more contextually grounded and cross-culturally sensitive theoretical framework.

Personal factors trigger and regulate behavioral factors. “Knowledge and skills,” “outcome expectations,” “self-efficacy,” and “role perception,” motivate professionals to implement various direct and indirect intervention behaviors. These factors constitute the internal driving force behind intervention behaviors in elder abuse. This aligns with Fang et al. [29], who found that nurses’ knowledge and attitudes strongly predicted willingness, reflecting that healthcare professionals’ self-efficacy and perceived outcome expectations serve as strong predictors of intervention willingness. Therefore, it is essential to strengthen these intrinsic motivations through targeted training programs emphasizing self-efficacy, contextualized learning, and role awareness.

Behavioral factors, in turn, provide feedback that reshapes personal cognition. For example, when healthcare professionals encounter setbacks during an intervention, such as failing to successfully stop abusive behavior or witnessing a recurrence of abuse after the intervention, they often re-evaluate whether their knowledge and skills are sufficient. Moreover, they may cause negative expectations regarding intervention outcomes and the fear of similar efforts may fail to resolve the issue, leading to further complications. Similar feedback loops have been reported in studies of clinical error management and error-reporting behavior [30, 31]. Drawing from these insights, it may be effective to integrate reflective practice sessions and peer-support into professional development programs to help healthcare professionals reframe negative intervention experiences and maintain their confidence in addressing elder abuse.

Environmental factors also empower and influence healthcare professionals’ individual factors through multilevel systems, while stimulating and supporting their intervention behavior strategies. Multilevel environmental systems—including legal regulations, institutional support, and interdepartmental collaboration—play a crucial role in enhancing intervention behaviors [32]. At the hospital level, staffing, capacity building, and organizational operational support provide institutional safeguards and resource backing for practical interventions. Existing research also indicates that supportive work environments and training opportunities can enhance healthcare professionals’ readiness for interventions and reduce bystander hesitation [33]. Moreover, in Chinese contexts, cultural and familial values emphasizing family harmony and privacy—often summarized in the saying that “even an honest official cannot judge family matters”—create strong family boundary concerns that inhibit abuse reporting and external intervention [34, 35]. Similar collectivist values have been observed in other East Asian societies such as Japan and Korea, where filial obligation and respect for family also discourage disclosure of intra-family conflict and reduce reporting or help-seeking by professionals [36, 37]. However, compared with these contexts, Chinese cultural norms place stronger emphasis on maintaining “face” (mianzi) [38, 39], which further reinforces healthcare professionals’ reluctance to intervene in family-related issues. Moreover, unlike Japan and South Korea, which share a Confucian cultural heritage, China currently lacks specific legislation targeting elder abuse prevention and does not mandate healthcare professionals to report suspected cases. Consequently, the absence of clear legal obligations and institutional protocols reduces healthcare professionals’ perceived responsibility and confidence in intervening, reinforcing a tendency to avoid involvement in family conflicts. Healthcare workers often proceed cautiously when confronting potential family conflicts, fearing that intervention may be perceived as overstepping boundaries and thereby exacerbating familial tensions. Moreover, family cooperation barriers at the micro-family level also contribute to personal negative outcome expectations regarding “family boundary concerns” and influence the implementation of “seeking support” intervention strategies.

Healthcare professionals also actively adapt to and reshape their environment. Nurses learn policies to fit institutional systems, whereas doctors leverage their coordinating roles to build resource networks. Their behaviors feed back into the environment, prompting hospitals and governments to strengthen enforcement mechanisms, interdepartmental coordination, and resource support. These three dimensions constitute interactive and reinforcing dynamic systems. Based on this, when empowering healthcare professionals to intervene in elder abuse, in addition to existing research indicating the need for more frequent and practical education at the personal level [40], it is also necessary to focus on multiple environmental perspectives, such as organizational and government policies. Strengthening organizational support and establishing government policies can empower primary care nurses to address elder abuse in primary care institutions and communities [41].

Professional differences

Although medical doctors can identify cases of elder abuse, their reporting intentions are generally low, especially in cases of physical abuse, emotional abuse, and neglect [42]. Thus, medical doctors prioritize advancing their “treatment skills” and “experience accumulation” to support clinical decision-making and intervention judgments. This reflects their uncertainty in distinguishing accidental harm from abuse and aligns with their emphasis on technical competence. In contrast, nurses rely more on their clinical experience than on knowledge gained through professional training and education when identifying and addressing elder abuse [43]. Therefore, nurses focused on disease-related knowledge, clinical observation skills, and policy learning. Additionally, nurses’ “empathy-driven” expectations align with the nursing role’s emphasis on daily care, while doctor’ “smooth communication” expectations correspond to their roles as coordinators who integrate resources for proactive intervention. These findings suggest that differences in self-construction between medical doctors and nurses may influence their perceptions of independence and interdependence. Medical doctors typically exhibit high levels of independent self-construction [44]. Thus medical doctors are more inclined to actively intervene in cases of elder abuse by integrating resources, reflecting their role perceptions as emotional comforters and resource coordinators.

Regarding behavior factors, nurses indicated that they directly intervene through “life care” approaches, reflecting the caring and nurturing characteristics of nursing work [45]. In contrast, medical doctors demonstrate their dominant role in the medical process by promoting intervention implementation through medical orders or delegating observations to others during direct interventions.

In terms of environmental factors, nurses value institutional incentives and public recognition. Given the relatively low social status of nursing in China [46, 47]. Therefore, organizational rewards and positive public messaging can enhance respect for nurses and encourage interventional behavior. In contrast, medical doctors emphasize an intervention response mechanism that guarantees and builds an elder abuse prevention cooperation system to ensure an efficient and systematic response to intervention actions.

Practical and policy implications

Our findings have several implications. First, continuous training and education should be provided. However, considering the different needs of doctors and nurses, targeted training is essential. Doctors should strengthen their clinical judgment, diagnostic differentiation, and cross-departmental resource integration capabilities. Nurses should improve their clinical observation skills, disease-related knowledge, policies, and legal knowledge. Second, “family boundary concerns,” rooted in Chinese cultural norms, often hinder professionals’ willingness to intervene. To address this issue, legislation and policies should clarify the harm caused by elder abuse and establish standard procedures for multi-departmental cooperation. This will provide healthcare professionals with a basis for action and help shift the perception of such issues from solely “family matters.” Moreover, education and outreach can further reshape societal attitudes. Thus, It is essential to prioritize the education and outreach to further create a supportive intervention climate. Third, research indicates that the absence of established procedures, psychological support, and incentive mechanisms in hospital can make health care professionals feel uneasy when intervening in elder abuse cases, leading to a decline in their performance [48, 49]. Therefore, institutional mechanisms recognizing professionals’ contributions can enhance their confidence and engagement. Finally, government should enhance legal regulations, establish cross-departmental collaboration mechanisms to ensure effective coordination after abuse reports, and create an environment that supports healthcare professionals through liability clauses, incentives, and psychological support.

Limitations and future directions

Our study has some limitations. First, using convenience sampling, the study participants were healthcare professionals from two tertiary A-grade hospitals in Yichang, Hubei Province, China. The sample concentrated in high-level medical institutions and excluded those from grassroots hospitals, private hospitals, communities, or elderly care institutions, limiting the generalizability of the findings to a broader population of Chinese healthcare professionals. Second, the study relied on self-reported data from participants, who may have been influenced by professional norms such as “healthcare professionals should actively intervene,” potentially leading to discrepancies between their views and reported responses. Third, we used directed content analysis, which involves subjective judgments during coding and categorization. While discussions among the three researchers helped reduce bias, some subjectivity may persist. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into Chinese healthcare professionals’ perceptions of interventions in elder abuse, which can inform future policy and intervention designs. Future research could expand the scope to include community health centers and long-term care institutions in order to examine variations in intervention perceptions and practices at different levels. Additionally, adopting mixed methods or longitudinal designs could quantitatively validate the relationships among self-efficacy, environmental support and intervention behaviors identified in this study, thereby strengthening the theoretical framework derived from SCT.

Conclusions

Healthcare professionals’ accurate perceptions of interventions in elder abuse are dynamically influenced by the dynamic interaction of personal, behavioral, and environmental factors. In particular, China’s unique cultural context—family boundary concerns—significantly influenced healthcare professionals’ willingness to intervene. Additionally, there were differences between medical doctors and nurses. These findings support the “triadic reciprocal determinism” mechanism of SCT. Personal factors activate and modulate behavioral factors, whereas behavioral factors provide feedback and reshape personal factors. Environmental factors empower and influence the personal characteristics of healthcare professionals through multilevel systems, stimulating and supporting interventional behavioral strategies. Conversely, individuals actively respond to and shape their environment, while behavior also exerts feedback effects on the environment, prompting corresponding adaptations. Therefore, to enhance healthcare professionals’ willingness to intervene in elder abuse, a multilevel strategy involving personal capacity building, organizational support, and institutional safeguards is required to form a positive feedback loop.
