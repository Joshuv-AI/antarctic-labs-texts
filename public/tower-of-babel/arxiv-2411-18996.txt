Intersection of subspaces in A 2 A^{2} for a three-dimensional division algebra A A over a finite field
Daisuke Tambara

Abstract
Let A A be a three-dimensional nonassociative division algebra over a finite field F F . Let A A act on the space A 2 = A ⊕ A A^{2}=A\oplus A by left multiplication. For a nonzero vector v ∈ A 2 v\in A^{2} we have a three-dimensional subspace A ​ v Av in A 2 A^{2} . This paper concerns about possible dimension of intersections A ​ v ∩ A ​ v ′ Av\cap Av^{\prime} for v , v ′ ∈ A 2 v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2} . One of our results is that there exists a two-dimensional intersection if and only if A A is isotopic to a commutative algebra. We use a classical theorem that A A is a twisted field of Albert.



Introduction

Finite nonassociative division algebras have long been studied since Dickson’s work
[
6
]
,
as seen from a survey by Cordero and Wene
[
5
]
.
Albert, in his study of the relationship between finite division algebras and finite projective planes
[
4
]
, considered the left vector spaces
A^{n}
over a division algebra
A
. He noticed that a basic property of ordinary vector spaces does not hold for nonassociative algebras

[
4
, Section 5]
.
Compared with abundant works on projective planes over finite division algebras, little attention seems to have been attracted to vector spaces over them.
In this paper we shall make a closer look at the nature of subspaces of
A^{n}
in the special case where
n=2
and
A
is three-dimensional over a finite field.

Let
A
be a division algebra over a field
F
.
Let
A
act on the space
A^{2}
by left multiplication:
a(x,y)=(ax,ay)
.
For an element
v\in A^{2}
we have a subspace
Av=\{av\mid a\in A\}
, which has the same dimension as
A
unless
v=0
.
The paper is concerned with intersection of the subspaces
Av
for
v\in A^{2}
.
When
A
is a field,
A^{2}
being an ordinary vector space over
A
, two different spaces
Av
and
Av^{\prime}
intersect trivially.
But when
A
is nonassociative, nontrivial intersection may happen.
We are interested in what dimension
Av\cap Av^{\prime}
can take.
We answer the question in a special case below.

Assume that
F
is a finite field and
A
is a three-dimensional nonassociative division algebra over
F
.
We first decide when
Av=Av^{\prime}
for
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
.
We call
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
a
nondegenerate vector
if
x,y
are linearly independent over
F
.

Theorem A
.

For any nondegenerate vectors
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
we have
Av=Av^{\prime}
if and only if
Fv=Fv^{\prime}
.

Our second result characterizes algebras
A
admitting a two-dimensional intersection
Av\cap Av^{\prime}
.
Recall that two algebras
A
and
A^{\prime}
are said to be
isotopic
if there exist linear isomorphisms
f,g,h\colon A\to A^{\prime}
such that
f(a)g(b)=h(ab)
for all
a,b\in A
.

Theorem B
.

There exist
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
such that
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
if and only if
A
is isotopic to a commutative algebra.

In proving these we use a theorem that any three-dimensional nonassociative division algebras over a finite field is an Albert twisted field, due to Kaplansky and Menichetti (
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
10
]
). Also we follow Kaplansky’s ‘algebraically closed style’, in which the base field is extended to its algebraic closure.

Based on these results we compute the number of subspaces
Av^{\prime}
complementary to a given subspace
Av
(Propositions 9.12 and 9.16).

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section 1 we review basic facts about Albert’s twisted fields.
In Section 2 we consider an algebra obtained from a twisted field by base extension to an algebraic closure, which we here call a
split Albert algebra
.
In Section 3 we prove a split version of
Theorem A, from which we deduce the theorem in Section 4.
In Section 5 we prove the ‘if’ part of Theorem B.
In Section 6 we prepare some propositions on intersections
Av\cap Av^{\prime}
.
We analyse the situation in which two-dimensional intersections occur
for a split Albert algebra in Section 7, thereby
deduce the ‘only if’ part of Theorem B in Section 8.
Finally we compute the number of complementary subspaces in Section 9.

By a division algebra we mean a vector space
A
over a field equipped with a bilinear map
A\times A\to A\colon(x,y)\mapsto xy
such that for every nonzero
a\in A
the left multiplication
L_{a}\colon x\mapsto ax
and the right multiplication
R_{a}\colon x\mapsto xa
are bijections. We do not put the axiom of an identity element, so this is a pre-semifield in Knuth’s terminology (
[
9
]
). Sometimes we refer to
A
as

(A,m)
, denoting the bilinear map
A\times A\to A
by
m
.

1
Twisted fields

We review here the construction of a twisted field associated with a cubic extension, and then
describe its multiplication after the base extension to a splitting field.

Let
K/F
be a cyclic cubic extension and
\sigma
a generator of its Galois group.
Let
N
denote the norm map
K\to F
for the extension.
Let
c\in K
be an element not equal to
x^{\sigma}x^{-1}
for any
x\in K^{\times}
.
This amounts to requiring that
N(c)\neq 1
.

Define a map
\mu\colon K\times K\to K
by

\mu(x,y)=xy^{\sigma}-cx^{\sigma}y
.
The pair
(K,\mu)
is a division algebra over
F
.
The map
\mu
can be modified by linear automorphisms into a multiplication admitting an identity element. The resulting unital division algebra is called a twisted field (
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
).
As an identity element is irrelevant to our work, we call less strictly the algebra
(K,\mu)
the twisted field associated with the triple
(K/F,\sigma,c)
.

If
c=-1
,
(K,\mu)
is commutative.

Two algebras
A
and
A^{\prime}
are said to be
isotopic
if there exist linear isomorphisms
f,g,h\colon A\to A^{\prime}
such that
f(a)g(b)=h(ab)
for all
a,b\in A
(
[
1
]
).

Let
c^{\prime}\in K
with
N(c^{\prime})\neq 1
and let
(K,\mu^{\prime})
be the corresponding twisted field.
If
N(c)=N(c^{\prime})
, then
(K,\mu)
and
(K,\mu^{\prime})
are isotopic.
Indeed, write
c^{\prime}/c=a^{\sigma}a^{-1}
with
a\in K^{\times}
;
Then
a\mu^{\prime}(x,y)=\mu(ax,y)
for all
x,y\in K
.

In particular, if
N(c)=-1
, then
(K,\mu)
is isotopic to a commutative algebra.

It is known that any three-dimensional nonassociative unital division algebra over a finite field is isomorphic to a twisted field in the strict sense. This theorem was conjectured and partly proved by Kaplansky, and completed by Menichetti
(
[
8
]
,
[
10
]
).
As we are ignoring unitality, we should phrase this theorem in terms of isotopy rather than isomorphism:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
F
be a finite field and
A
a three-dimensional nonassociative division algebra over
F
.
Then
A
is isotopic to a twisted field
(K,\mu)
for a cubic extension
K/F
and an element
c\in K
.

Following Kaplansky’s ‘algebraically closed style’(
[
7
]
), we discuss splitting of twisted fields.

Let
(K,\mu)
be the twisted field associated with
(K/F,\sigma,c)
.
Since
K/F
is a Galois extension,
we have an algebra isomorphism

\omega\colon K\otimes K\to K^{3}

taking

x\otimes y
to
(xy,xy^{\sigma},xy^{\sigma^{2}})
,
where the algebra structures of
K\otimes K
and
K^{3}
are the standard ones.
This transforms the automorphism
1\otimes\sigma
of
K\otimes K
into
an automorphism
\rho
of
K^{3}
given by

\rho\colon(x_{i})\mapsto(x_{i+1})
,
where the index is taken modulo 3.
Put
c_{i}=c^{\sigma^{i}}
and
\gamma=(c_{i})_{i}\in K^{3}
.
Then
\omega
transforms the multiplication
1\otimes\mu
on
K\otimes K
into a multiplication
\nu
on
K^{3}
given by

\nu(\xi,\eta)=\xi\eta^{\rho}-\gamma\xi^{\rho}\eta
,
or in coordinates

\nu((x_{i}),(y_{i}))=(x_{i}y_{i+1}-c_{i}x_{i+1}y_{i})
.
Let
(e_{0},e_{1},e_{2})
be the standard basis of
K^{3}
.
Then

\nu(e_{i},e_{j})=\begin{cases}e_{i}\quad&\text{if $j=i+1$,}\\
-c_{j}e_{j}&\text{if $i=j+1$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In summary

Proposition 1.2
.

After the scalar extension
K/F
, the twisted field
(K,\mu)
becomes isomorphic to the
K
-algebra
(K^{3},\nu)
with multiplication
\nu
given by the above formula.

The isomorphism
\omega
transforms the automorphism
\sigma\otimes 1
of
K\otimes K
into
an automorphism
\lambda
of
K^{3}
given by

\lambda\colon(x_{i})\mapsto(x_{i-1}^{\sigma})
.
This is not
K
-linear but semi-linear relative to
\sigma
.
It permutes the basis as

\lambda\colon e_{i}\mapsto e_{i+1}
.

2
Split Albert algebras

We consider here an algebra of the form
(K^{3},\nu)
of Section 1 a little more generally, the base field set down to
F
.

Let
U
,
V
,
W
be three-dimensional vector spaces over
F
respectively having bases

(\alpha_{i})
,
(\beta_{i})
,
(\gamma_{i})
, where the index
i
runs through
0,1,2
.
Let
d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}\in F^{\times}
. Define a bilinear map

\phi\colon U\times V\to W
by

\phi(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})=0,\;\phi(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i+1})=\gamma_{i+2},\;\phi(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i+2})=d_{i+1}\gamma_{i+1},

where the index is taken modulo 3.
Put
d=d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}
.
We assume
d\neq-1
throughout.
Let us call
\phi
a
split Albert algebra
.

The multiplication
\nu\colon K^{3}\times K^{3}\to K^{3}
of Section 1 is a special case of
\phi
where
F
is set as
K
and

U=V=W=K^{3}
,

\alpha_{i}=e_{i},\;\beta_{i}=e_{i},\;\gamma_{i}=e_{i-1}
,
and

d_{i}=-c^{\sigma^{i}}
.
Note that
d=-N(c)
.

When only a single
\phi
is concerned, we write
\phi(x,y)=xy
.

For
x\in U
let
L_{x}\colon V\to W
be the map
y\mapsto xy
.
For
y\in V
let
R_{y}\colon U\to W
be the map
x\mapsto xy
.

Let
x=x_{0}\alpha_{0}+x_{1}\alpha_{1}+x_{2}\alpha_{2}
.
Then

x\beta_{i}=x_{i-1}\gamma_{i+1}+x_{i+1}d_{i-1}\gamma_{i-1}
.
Relative to the present bases the linear map
L_{x}\colon V\to W
is represented by a matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&d_{0}x_{2}&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&0&d_{1}x_{0}\\
d_{2}x_{1}&x_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Its determinant is
(1+d)x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}
.
Since
d\neq-1
,
L_{x}
is invertible if and only if none of
x_{i}
is zero, in which case call
x
a
regular element
.
On the other hand, if
x_{0}=0
and
(x_{1},x_{2})\neq(0,0)
, then
\operatorname{Ker}L_{x}
is a one-dimensional space spanned by
x_{1}\beta_{1}-d_{0}x_{2}\beta_{2}
. Similarly, for any nonzero nonregular element
x\in U
one sees that
\operatorname{Ker}L_{x}
is one-dimensional. It follows that if
y,y^{\prime}\in V
are linearly independent over
F
, then the map
U\to W^{2}\colon x\mapsto(xy,xy^{\prime})
is injective.

Let

y=y_{0}\beta_{0}+y_{1}\beta_{1}+y_{2}\beta_{2}
.
Then

\alpha_{i}y=y_{i+1}\gamma_{i-1}+y_{i-1}d_{i+1}\gamma_{i+1}
.
Relative to the present bases the linear map
R_{y}\colon U\to W
is represented by a matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&y_{2}&d_{0}y_{1}\\
d_{1}y_{2}&0&y_{0}\\
y_{1}&d_{2}y_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}

with determinant

(1+d)y_{0}y_{1}y_{2}
.
So
R_{y}
is invertible if and only if none of
y_{i}
is zero. In this case we call
y
a regular element.
Then
R_{y}^{-1}
is represented by a matrix

\frac{1}{1+d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-d_{2}\frac{y_{0}}{y_{1}y_{2}}&d_{0}d_{2}\frac{1}{y_{2}}&\frac{1}{y_{1}}\\
\frac{1}{y_{2}}&-d_{0}\frac{y_{1}}{y_{0}y_{2}}&d_{0}d_{1}\frac{1}{y_{0}}\\
d_{1}d_{2}\frac{1}{y_{1}}&\frac{1}{y_{0}}&-d_{1}\frac{y_{2}}{y_{0}y_{1}}\end{pmatrix}.

Kaplansky called an algebra
A
a left Dickson algebra if the determinant of the left multiplication
L_{x}
is a product of linearly independent linear forms on
x

[
7
]
.
The right counterpart is called a right Dickson algebra.
Thus a split Albert algebra is a left and right Dickson algebra as long as
U=V=W
.

We discuss some isomorphisms between split Albert algebras.
For any bilinear maps
l\colon X\times Y\to Z
and
l^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}\to Z^{\prime}
, an isomorphism
l\to l^{\prime}
means a triple
(f,g,h)
of linear isomorphisms
f\colon X\to X^{\prime}
,
g\colon Y\to Y^{\prime}
,
h\colon Z\to Z^{\prime}
such that
hl=l^{\prime}(f\times g)
.
An isotopy between algebras is a special case where
X=Y=Z
,
X^{\prime}=Y^{\prime}=Z^{\prime}
.

We use notation
\phi=\phi_{d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}}
to make clear the dependence on
d_{i}
.
Given
r_{i},s_{i}\in F^{\times}
let
f\colon U\to U
,
g\colon V\to V
,
h\colon W\to W
be
respectively the linear maps

\alpha_{i}\mapsto r_{i}\alpha_{i},\quad\beta_{i}\mapsto s_{i}\beta_{i},\quad\gamma_{i}\mapsto r_{i+1}s_{i+2}\gamma_{i}.

Then
(f,g,h)
gives an isomorphism
\phi_{d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}}\to\phi_{d_{0}^{\prime},d_{1}^{\prime},d_{2}^{\prime}}
,
where

d_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{r_{i+1}}{r_{i-1}}\frac{s_{i-1}}{s_{i+1}}d_{i}.

Note that

d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}=d_{0}^{\prime}d_{1}^{\prime}d_{2}^{\prime}
.

The linear isomorphisms

\alpha_{i}\mapsto\alpha_{i+1},\quad\beta_{i}\mapsto\beta_{i+1},\quad\gamma_{i}\mapsto\gamma_{i+1}

give an isomorphism
\phi_{d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}}\to\phi_{d_{2},d_{0},d_{1}}
.

The linear isomorphisms

\alpha_{i}\mapsto\alpha_{1-i},\quad\beta_{i}\mapsto\beta_{1-i},\quad\gamma_{i}\mapsto d_{i}^{-1}\gamma_{1-i}

give an isomorphism
\phi_{d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}}\to\phi_{d_{1}^{-1},d_{0}^{-1},d_{2}^{-1}}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
y,y^{\prime}\in V
be regular elements.
The characteristic polynomial of
R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}
is given by

\det(XI-R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y})=(X-\frac{y_{0}}{y^{\prime}_{0}})(X-\frac{y_{1}}{y^{\prime}_{1}})(X-\frac{y_{2}}{y^{\prime}_{2}}).

Proof.

Relative to the basis
(\alpha_{i})
,
R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}
is represented by a matrix

\frac{1}{1+d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}t_{2}+t_{1}&d_{2}(t_{0}-t_{2})\frac{y^{\prime}_{0}}{y^{\prime}_{1}}&d_{2}d_{0}(t_{0}-t_{1})\frac{y^{\prime}_{0}}{y^{\prime}_{2}}\\
d_{0}d_{1}(t_{1}-t_{2})\frac{y^{\prime}_{1}}{y^{\prime}_{0}}&d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}t_{0}+t_{2}&d_{0}(t_{1}-t_{0})\frac{y^{\prime}_{1}}{y^{\prime}_{2}}\\
d_{1}(t_{2}-t_{1})\frac{y^{\prime}_{2}}{y^{\prime}_{0}}&d_{1}d_{2}(t_{2}-t_{0})\frac{y^{\prime}_{2}}{y^{\prime}_{1}}&d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}t_{1}+t_{0}\end{pmatrix}

with

t_{i}=\frac{y_{i}}{y^{\prime}_{i}}
.
We know

\det(R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y})=\frac{y_{0}y_{1}y_{2}}{y_{0}^{\prime}y_{1}^{\prime}y_{2}^{\prime}}=t_{0}t_{1}t_{2}.

The trace of the above matrix is readily found to be

t_{0}+t_{1}+t_{2}
.
One of the principal 2-minor of the matrix is

\begin{vmatrix}d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}t_{2}+t_{1}&d_{2}(t_{0}-t_{2})\frac{y^{\prime}_{0}}{y^{\prime}_{1}}\\
d_{0}d_{1}(t_{1}-t_{2})\frac{y^{\prime}_{1}}{y^{\prime}_{0}}&d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}t_{0}+t_{2}\end{vmatrix}=(1+d)(dt_{0}t_{2}+t_{1}t_{2}).

It follows that the sum of the principal 2-minors of the matrix is equal to

t_{0}t_{1}+t_{1}t_{2}+t_{2}t_{0}
.
This proves the proposition.

∎

3
The equation
U(x,y)=U(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
for a split Albert algebra

Let
\phi=\phi_{d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}}\colon U\times V\to W
be a split Albert algebra. We regard
\phi
as multiplication:
\phi(u,v)=uv
.
This induces a bilinear map
U\times V^{2}\to W^{2}
:
(u,(x,y))\mapsto(ux,uy)=u(x,y)
.
We write
U(x,y)=\{u(x,y)\mid u\in U\}
for
(x,y)\in V^{2}
. This is a subspace of
W^{2}
.

Recall that
x=\sum_{i}x_{i}\beta_{i}\in V
is called a regular element if none of
x_{i}
is zero.

The main result of this section is

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in V
be regular elements. Then

U(x,y)=U(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
if and only if

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=k(x,y)
or
(y,y^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})
for some
k\in F^{\times}
.

The theorem will follow from the two propositions below.
Recall from Section 2 that for regular elements
x,x^{\prime}\in V
we have a linear map

R_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{x}\colon U\to U
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in V
be regular elements. Then

U(x,y)=U(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
if and only if
R_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{x}=R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}
.

Proof.

Suppose
U(x,y)=U(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. For any
a\in U
there exists a unique
a^{\prime}\in U
such that

a(x,y)=a^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
,
namely

ax=a^{\prime}x^{\prime},\;ay=a^{\prime}y^{\prime}
.
Then

a^{\prime}=R_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{x}(a),\;a^{\prime}=R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}(a)
,
hence

R_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{x}(a)=R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}(a)
. Thus

R_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{x}=R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}
.
The argument can be reversed.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in V
be regular elements.
We have
R_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{x}=R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}
if and only if

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=k(x,y)
or
(y,y^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})
for some
k\in F^{\times}
.

Proof.

The sufficiency is clear.
Let us prove the necessity.
Suppose
R_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{x}=R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}
.
Put
s_{i}=x_{i}/x^{\prime}_{i}
,
t_{i}=y_{i}/y^{\prime}_{i}
.
By Proposition 2.1 the triples
(s_{0},s_{1},s_{2})
and
(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
are equal up to permutation.
We divide cases according as the type of the permutation.
By the cyclic symmetry
i\mapsto i+1
of the indices and the symmetry
(x,x^{\prime})\leftrightarrow(y,y^{\prime})
, it is enough to consider three cases.
Case I:
(s_{0},s_{1},s_{2})=(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
;
Case II:
(s_{2},s_{0},s_{1})=(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
;
Case III:
(s_{0},s_{2},s_{1})=(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
.

Case I:
(s_{0},s_{1},s_{2})=(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
.
Then
x_{i}/x_{i}^{\prime}=y_{i}/y_{i}^{\prime}
and
y_{i}/x_{i}=y_{i}^{\prime}/x_{i}^{\prime}
.

(i) Case where
t_{0},t_{1},t_{2}
are not all equal.
By the cyclic symmetry we may assume
t_{0}\neq t_{1},t_{0}\neq t_{2}
.
The matrix of
R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}
is exhibited in Section 2.
Its rows and columns will be indexed by
0,1,2
.
Comparing the
(0,1)
-entry and
(0,2)
-entry of the matrices of
R_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{x}
and
R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}
, we have

(s_{0}-s_{2})\frac{x_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{1}^{\prime}}=(t_{0}-t_{2})\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{y_{1}^{\prime}},\quad(s_{0}-s_{1})\frac{x_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{2}^{\prime}}=(t_{0}-t_{1})\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{y_{2}^{\prime}}.

Since
s_{0}-s_{2}=t_{0}-t_{2}\neq 0
,
s_{0}-s_{1}=t_{0}-t_{1}\neq 0
, it follows that

\frac{x_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{1}^{\prime}}=\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{y_{1}^{\prime}},\quad\frac{x_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{2}^{\prime}}=\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{y_{2}^{\prime}},

hence

\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{0}^{\prime}}=\frac{y_{1}^{\prime}}{x_{1}^{\prime}}=\frac{y_{2}^{\prime}}{x_{2}^{\prime}}.

Call this element
k
.
Then

y^{\prime}=kx^{\prime}
.
We have also
y_{i}/x_{i}=k
,

y=kx
.

(ii) Case where
t_{0}=t_{1}=t_{2}
.
Put
t_{i}=k
. Then
y=ky^{\prime}
and
x=kx^{\prime}
.

Case II:
(s_{2},s_{0},s_{1})=(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
.
Comparing the diagonal entries of the matrices of
R_{x^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{x}
and
R_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}R_{y}
, we have

ds_{2}+s_{1}=dt_{2}+t_{1},\quad ds_{0}+s_{2}=dt_{0}+t_{2},\quad ds_{1}+s_{0}=dt_{1}+t_{0}.

Substituting
s_{i}=t_{i+1}
, we have

dt_{0}+t_{2}=dt_{2}+t_{1},\quad dt_{1}+t_{0}=dt_{0}+t_{2},\quad dt_{2}+t_{1}=dt_{1}+t_{0}.

Thus

\displaystyle dt_{0}-t_{1}+(1-d)t_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

(1)

\displaystyle dt_{1}-t_{2}+(1-d)t_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(2)

\displaystyle dt_{2}-t_{0}+(1-d)t_{1}

\displaystyle=0.

(3)

Comparing the off-diagonal entries of the two matrices and substituting
s_{i}=t_{i+1}
, we have

\displaystyle(t_{1}-t_{0})\frac{x_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(t_{0}-t_{2})\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{y_{1}^{\prime}},

(4)

\displaystyle(t_{1}-t_{2})\frac{x_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{2}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(t_{0}-t_{1})\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{y_{2}^{\prime}},

(5)

\displaystyle(t_{2}-t_{0})\frac{x_{1}^{\prime}}{x_{0}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(t_{1}-t_{2})\frac{y_{1}^{\prime}}{y_{0}^{\prime}},

(6)

\displaystyle(t_{2}-t_{1})\frac{x_{1}^{\prime}}{x_{2}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(t_{1}-t_{0})\frac{y_{1}^{\prime}}{y_{2}^{\prime}},

(7)

\displaystyle(t_{0}-t_{2})\frac{x_{2}^{\prime}}{x_{0}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(t_{2}-t_{1})\frac{y_{2}^{\prime}}{y_{0}^{\prime}},

(8)

\displaystyle(t_{0}-t_{1})\frac{x_{2}^{\prime}}{x_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(t_{2}-t_{0})\frac{y_{2}^{\prime}}{y_{1}^{\prime}}.

(9)

By elementary operations one sees that (1)–(3) are equivalent to equations

\displaystyle(1-d+d^{2})(t_{1}-t_{2})

\displaystyle=0,

(10)

\displaystyle t_{0}-t_{1}+d(t_{1}-t_{2})

\displaystyle=0.

(11)

(i) Case where
1-d+d^{2}\neq 0
.
Then
t_{0}=t_{1}=t_{2}
,
which falls into (ii) of Case I.

(ii) Case where
1-d+d^{2}=0
.
By (11), if
t_{1}=t_{2}
, then
t_{0}=t_{1}=t_{2}
again.
Assume
t_{1}\neq t_{2}
.
Putting (11) into (5) and (7), we have

(t_{1}-t_{2})\frac{x_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{2}^{\prime}}=-d(t_{1}-t_{2})\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{y_{2}^{\prime}},\quad(t_{2}-t_{1})\frac{x_{1}^{\prime}}{x_{2}^{\prime}}=-d(t_{2}-t_{1})\frac{y_{1}^{\prime}}{y_{2}^{\prime}},

hence

\frac{x_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{2}^{\prime}}=-d\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{y_{2}^{\prime}},\quad\frac{x_{1}^{\prime}}{x_{2}^{\prime}}=-d\frac{y_{1}^{\prime}}{y_{2}^{\prime}}.

These two equations yield

\frac{x_{0}^{\prime}}{x_{1}^{\prime}}=\frac{y_{0}^{\prime}}{y_{1}^{\prime}}.

Putting this into (4) and (6), we have

t_{1}-t_{0}=t_{0}-t_{2},\quad t_{2}-t_{0}=t_{1}-t_{2},

hence

2t_{0}=t_{1}+t_{2},\quad 2t_{2}=t_{0}+t_{1}.

It follows that

2(t_{0}-t_{2})=t_{2}-t_{0}.

If
\text{char}(F)\neq 3
, then

t_{0}-t_{2}=0
,
so

t_{0}=t_{1}=t_{2}
,
contrary to the present assumption.
If
\text{char}(F)=3
, the equation
d^{2}-d+1=0
gives
d=-1
, which is to be excluded.

Case III:
(s_{0},s_{2},s_{1})=(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
.
Comparing the
(2,2)
-entry of the two matrices, we have

ds_{1}+s_{0}=dt_{1}+t_{0}
.
In our case this becomes

dt_{2}+t_{0}=dt_{1}+t_{0}
.
This gives
t_{1}=t_{2}
. So
s_{1}=s_{2}
.
Then
(s_{0},s_{1},s_{2})=(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
.
So we are back in Case I.

We conclude that

y=kx,\;y^{\prime}=kx^{\prime}

or
x^{\prime}=kx,\;y^{\prime}=ky

for some

.
This proves the proposition.

4
The equation
Av=Av^{\prime}
for a division algebra

Let
F
be a finite field. Let
A
be a three-dimensional nonassociative division algebra over
F
.
We have the left action of
A
on
A^{2}
:
a(x,y)=(ax,ay)
.
For
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
we say
v
is
nondegenerate
if
x,y
are linearly independent over
F
, and
degenerate
otherwise.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate vectors. Then

Av=Av^{\prime}
if and only if
Fv=Fv^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
K/F
be a cubic extension with generating automorphism
\sigma
.
By Theorem 1.1
A
is isotopic to the twisted field
(K,\mu)
associated with an element
c\in K^{\times}
.
By Proposition 1.2 we have an isomorphism
(f,g,h)
of bilinear maps from
the multiplication map
(K\otimes A)\times(K\otimes A)\to K\otimes A
to
a split Albert algebra
\phi_{d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}}\colon U\times V\to W
over
K
.
Write
v=(x,y)
,
v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
Suppose
Av=Av^{\prime}
. Then
U(g(x),g(y))=U(g(x^{\prime}),g(y^{\prime}))
.
Since
x\neq 0
and
A
is a division algebra, the right multiplication
a\mapsto ax
on
A
is invertible. Hence
R_{g(x)}\colon U\to W
is invertible, namely
g(x)
is a regular element.
Similarly
g(y),g(x^{\prime}),g(y^{\prime})
are regular elements.
Applying Theorem 3.1 to these elements, we have

(g(x^{\prime}),g(y^{\prime}))=k(g(x),g(y))
or

(g(y),g(y^{\prime}))=k(g(x),g(x^{\prime}))

for some
k\in K^{\times}
.
Since
g
is an isomorphism, we have

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=k(x,y)
or
(y,y^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})
.
In either case
k\in F^{\times}
.
But the second case would imply that
v,v^{\prime}
are degenerate.
Therefore we must have
v^{\prime}=kv
.
∎

As for degenerate vectors
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
it is easy to decide when
Av=Av^{\prime}
. See Proposition 6.3.

5
Commutative algebras

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
F
be a finite field. Let
A
be a three-dimensional nonassociative division algebra over
F
.
Suppose that
A
is isotopic to a commutative algebra.
Then there exist
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
such that
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
.

Proof.

We may assume that
A
itself is commutative.
Let
v=(x,y)
be a nondegenerate vector.
Take
x^{\prime}\in A-Fx
.
Take
y^{\prime}\in A
such that
x^{\prime}y=xy^{\prime}
.
By the commutativity
we have

x^{\prime}(x,y)=x(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
Also

y^{\prime}x=yx^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}y=yy^{\prime}
, hence

y^{\prime}(x,y)=y(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
Put
v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
We have

x^{\prime}v=xv^{\prime},\;y^{\prime}v=yv^{\prime}
.
So

\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle v=\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}
.
This is two-dimensional and contained in
Av\cap Av^{\prime}
.
Since
x,x^{\prime}
are independent, so are
v,v^{\prime}
.
Therefore
Av\neq Av^{\prime}
by Theorem 4.1.
It follows that
Av\cap Av^{\prime}
is two-dimensional and coincides with

\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle v=\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}
.
∎

6
Nontrivial intersection of
Av

We make here some preparations for the remaining part of Theorem B of Introduction.
Propositions 6.1–6 state elementary facts.
Propositions 6.7–9 are facts peculiar to three-dimensional nonassociative division algebras over a finite field.

Let
A
be an algebra over
F
.
We have an operation
A\times A^{2}\to A^{2}
:
a(x,y)=(ax,ay)
.
For any
v\in A^{2}
we have a subspace
Av=\{av\mid a\in A\}\subset A^{2}
.
We say
v
is
regular
if
av=0
implies
a=0
.
In this case the map
a\mapsto av
gives a linear isomorphism
A\to Av
.

Proposition 6.1
.

(i) Suppose that
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
are both regular.
Then we have an isomorphism
Av\cap Av^{\prime}\cong\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A^{2}\mid av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}\}

given by the correspondence
av\leftrightarrow(a,a^{\prime})
.

(ii)
Let
P\in GL_{2}(F)
.
We have a linear isomorphism
A^{2}\to A^{2}
given by
v\mapsto vP
, where
v
is regarded as a row vector.
Let
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
and put
w=vP
,
w^{\prime}=v^{\prime}P
.
Then the above isomorphism induces an isomorphism
Av\cap Av^{\prime}\cong Aw\cap Aw^{\prime}
.
And we have

\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A^{2}\mid av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}\}=\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A^{2}\mid aw=a^{\prime}w^{\prime}\}.

(iii)
Let
Q\in GL_{2}(F)
.
Let
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
and put

\begin{pmatrix}u\\
u^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=Q\begin{pmatrix}v\\
v^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

Then we have a linear isomorphism

\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A^{2}\mid av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}\}\cong\{(b,b^{\prime})\in A^{2}\mid bu=b^{\prime}u^{\prime}\}

under the correspondence

(a,-a^{\prime})=(b,-b^{\prime})Q
.

Proof will be obvious.

Proposition 6.2
.

Suppose that
A
is a division algebra.
Let
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
,
v=(x,y)
,
v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. Suppose
y=0
,
x\neq 0
.
Then

Av\cap Av^{\prime}\neq 0
if and only if
y^{\prime}=0,x^{\prime}\neq 0
.
And in this case

Av=Av^{\prime}=A\oplus 0
.

Proof.

We have

Av=\{(ax,0)\mid a\in A\}=\{(a,0)\mid a\in A\}=A\oplus 0.

Suppose
Av\cap Av^{\prime}\neq 0
. There exists
a\in A
such that

ax^{\prime}\neq 0,\;ay^{\prime}=0
.
It then follows that
x^{\prime}\neq 0
and
y^{\prime}=0
.

Conversely if
y^{\prime}=0
and
x^{\prime}\neq 0
, then

Av^{\prime}=A\oplus 0=Av
.

∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Suppose that
A
is a division algebra.
Let

,
v=(x,y)
,
v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. Suppose

for some

Av\cap Av^{\prime}\neq 0

Let

Put

,

,

Then

\displaystyle Av\cap Av^{\prime}\neq 0\iff

\displaystyle Aw\cap Aw^{\prime}\neq 0\quad(\text{by Proposition 6.1(ii)})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle y^{\prime}-\lambda x^{\prime}=0,x^{\prime}\neq 0\quad(\text{by Proposition 6.2}).

In this case

Aw=Aw^{\prime}=A\oplus 0
.
Then

Av=Av^{\prime}=\{(a,\lambda a)\mid a\in A\}
.
∎

Recall that
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
is said to be degenerate if

are linearly dependent over
F
. In this term the proposition is restated:

Corollary 6.4
.

Suppose that
A
is a division algebra. Let

is degenerate.
Then
Av\cap Av^{\prime}\neq 0
if and only if

, in which case

is also degenerate.

Proposition 6.5
.

Suppose that
A
is a division algebra.
Let
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
,
v=(x,y)
,
v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

x\neq 0
,
x^{\prime}\neq 0

.

By Proposition 6.1(i) we have

We have

When

.

.
When

∎

Proposition 6.6
.

Suppose that

Proposition 6.7
.

Assume
F

Let

,

,

Suppose that the

\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle
,
\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle

\langle y,y^{\prime}\rangle

\langle x,y\rangle=\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle

Since the
F

Assume
\langle x,y\rangle=\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle

\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle=\langle y,y^{\prime}\rangle

with

.
We have the isomorphism of Proposition 6.1(i):

Av\cap Av^{\prime}\cong\{(a,a^{\prime})\mid ax=a^{\prime}x^{\prime},ay=a^{\prime}y^{\prime}\}.

The right multiplication by
x
on

is denoted by

.
The equation

K/F
be a cubic extension with generating automorphism

(1)

\phi=\phi_{d_{0},d_{1},d_{2}}\colon U\times V\to W
with

d_{i}=-c^{\sigma^{i}}
(Section 2).
Let
\tilde{\phi}\colon\tilde{U}\times\tilde{V}\to\tilde{W}
be the map obtained from
\phi
by the scalar extension
\tilde{F}/K
.

Combining these isomorphisms,
we have an isomorphism
(f,g,h)
from the bilinear map
\tilde{m}\colon\tilde{A}\times\tilde{A}\to\tilde{A}
to the bilinear map
\tilde{\phi}\colon\tilde{U}\times\tilde{V}\to\tilde{W}
.
Now we take
\tilde{F}
as a base field and apply Theorem 7.2 to the elements
g(x),g(y),g(x^{\prime}),g(y^{\prime})\in\tilde{V}
.
The consequence is that
d=d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}=1
, namely
N(c)=-1
.
Then
(K,\mu)
is isotopic to the twisted field associated with
-1\in K^{\times}
, which is commutative.
Hence
A
is isotopic to a commutative algebra.

∎

9
Intersection of a given dimension

Throughout this section
F
is a finite field and
A
is a three-dimensional nonassociative division algebra over
F
.
We showed in Section 4 that two-dimensional intersections of
Av
occur when
A
is isotopic to a commutative algebra. In this section we look at intersections of
Av
more closely. Especially, given
v\in A^{2}
we compute the number of
v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
such that
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=0,1,2
, respectively.

9.1
the commutative case

In this subsection we treat the case where
A
is commutative.
First we supplement Theorem 4.1 with uniqueness assertion.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate.
Let
x^{\prime}\in A
and suppose that
x,x^{\prime}
are independent over
F
.
Then there exists a unique
y^{\prime}\in A
such that
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
for
v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
And for such
y^{\prime}
we have

Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle v=\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The existence was shown in the proof of Theorem 4.1: if
y^{\prime}\in A
is taken so that
x^{\prime}y=xy^{\prime}
, then

Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle v=\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}
for
v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
.

Let us prove the uniqueness.
Suppose that we have
y_{1}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime}\in A
such that

\dim(Av\cap Av_{1}^{\prime})=2
,
\dim(Av\cap Av_{2}^{\prime})=2

for
v_{1}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime})
,
v_{2}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime})
.
Since
\dim Av=3
, we must have

(Av\cap Av_{1}^{\prime})\cap(Av\cap Av_{2}^{\prime})\neq 0
,
so

Av_{1}^{\prime}\cap Av_{2}^{\prime}\neq 0
.
But
v_{1}^{\prime}
and
v_{2}^{\prime}
have the same
x
-coordinate. Therefore, by Proposition 6.5
they have the same
y
-coordinate, that is,
y_{1}^{\prime}=y_{2}^{\prime}
.

∎

Proposition 9.2
.

Let
v=(x,y),v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in A^{2}
. Suppose
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
. Then

Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle v=\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By Proposition 6.8
x,y
are independent and
x,x^{\prime}
are independent.
By Proposition 9.1 we have

Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle v=\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}
.
∎

Proposition 9.3
.

Let
v,v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime}\in A^{2}
. Suppose
\dim(Av\cap Av_{1}^{\prime})=\dim(Av\cap Av_{2}^{\prime})=2
. If
Av\cap Av_{1}^{\prime}=Av\cap Av_{2}^{\prime}
, then
Av_{1}^{\prime}=Av_{2}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Write
v_{1}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime})
,
v_{2}^{\prime}=(x_{2}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime})
.
Since
\dim(Av\cap Av_{1}^{\prime})=2
and
\dim(Av\cap Av_{2}^{\prime})=2
, we have by Proposition 9.2
that
Av\cap Av_{1}^{\prime}=\langle x_{1}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime}\rangle v
and

Av\cap Av_{2}^{\prime}=\langle x_{2}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime}\rangle v
.
Suppose
Av\cap Av_{1}^{\prime}=Av\cap Av_{2}^{\prime}
. Then

\langle x_{1}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle x_{2}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime}\rangle
.
Also
Av_{1}^{\prime}\cap Av_{2}^{\prime}\neq 0
.
If
Av_{1}^{\prime}\neq Av_{2}^{\prime}
, Proposition 6.8 would imply that
\langle x_{1}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime}\rangle\neq\langle x_{2}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime}\rangle
. So we must have
Av_{1}^{\prime}=Av_{2}^{\prime}
.

∎

Proposition 9.4
.

Let
v=(x,y),v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate. Suppose that
v,v^{\prime}
are independent.
Let
a,a^{\prime}\in A
with
a\neq 0
,
a^{\prime}\neq 0
. Suppose
av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}
.
If
a^{\prime}\in\langle x,y\rangle
, then
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
.

Proof.

Suppose
a^{\prime}=\lambda x+\mu y
with
\lambda,\mu\in F
.
Take a matrix

P=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&*\\
\mu&*\end{pmatrix}\in GL_{2}(F).

Then

(x,y)P=(a^{\prime},*)
.
Put

\tilde{v}=vP
,

\tilde{v}^{\prime}=v^{\prime}P
.
Then
\tilde{v}
and
\tilde{v}^{\prime}
are nondegenerate;

\tilde{v},\tilde{v}^{\prime}
are independent;

a\tilde{v}=a^{\prime}\tilde{v}^{\prime}
;

Av\cap Av^{\prime}\cong A\tilde{v}\cap A\tilde{v}^{\prime}
.

Replacing
v,v^{\prime}
by
\tilde{v},\tilde{v}^{\prime}
, we may assume
a^{\prime}=x
from the beginning.
Then the equation
av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}
reads

ax=xx^{\prime}
,
ay=xy^{\prime}
,
hence

a=x^{\prime}
,
x^{\prime}y=xy^{\prime}
.
Then, as in the proof of Theorem 5.1 we have

y^{\prime}v=yv^{\prime}
and

\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle v=\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}\subset Av\cap Av^{\prime}
.
Since
v,v^{\prime}
are independent, we have
Av\neq Av^{\prime}
.
So

Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle v=\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}
.
This is two-dimensional.
∎

Proposition 9.5
.

Let
v=(x,y),v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in A^{2}
. Suppose
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
. Let
a,a^{\prime}\in A

with
a\neq 0
,
a^{\prime}\neq 0
. Suppose
av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}
.
Then
a^{\prime}\in\langle x,y\rangle
.

Proof.

By Proposition 9.2 we have

Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}
.
Then
a^{\prime}v^{\prime}\in\langle x,y\rangle v^{\prime}
, hence
a^{\prime}\in\langle x,y\rangle
.

∎

Proposition 9.6
.

Let
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate and
v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
. Let
a,a^{\prime}\in A
with
a\neq 0
,
a^{\prime}\neq 0
.
Suppose
av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}
.

(i)
\langle a\rangle=\langle a^{\prime}\rangle\iff\langle v\rangle=\langle v^{\prime}\rangle\iff Av=Av^{\prime}
.

(ii) Suppose
\langle a\rangle\neq\langle a^{\prime}\rangle
.
If
a^{\prime}\in\langle x,y\rangle
then
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
, and
if
a^{\prime}\notin\langle x,y\rangle
then
Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle av\rangle
.

Proof.

Since
0\neq av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}\in Av\cap Av^{\prime}
, we have
Av\cap Av^{\prime}\neq 0
, so

v^{\prime}
is nondegenerate by Corollary 6.4.

(i) The fist equivalence is clear, the second due to Theorem 4.1.

(ii) Let
\langle a\rangle\neq\langle a^{\prime}\rangle
. Then
\langle v\rangle\neq\langle v^{\prime}\rangle
,
Av\neq Av^{\prime}
.
Proposition 9.4 says if
a^{\prime}\in\langle x,y\rangle
then
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
,
while Proposition 9.5 says
if
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
then
a^{\prime}\in\langle x,y\rangle
.
So

a^{\prime}\in\langle x,y\rangle
if and only if
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
.
Hence

a^{\prime}\notin\langle x,y\rangle
if and only if
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=1
, in which case
Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle av\rangle
.
This proves (ii).
∎

Let
q
denote the number of elements of the finite field
F
.
The following proposition is valid irrespective of the commutativity of
A
.

Proposition 9.7
.

We have

\displaystyle\#\{v\in A^{2}\mid\text{$v$ is nondegenerate}\}=(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q),

\displaystyle\#\{v\in A^{2}\mid\text{$v$ is degenerate and nonzero}\}=(q^{3}-1)(q+1),

and

\displaystyle\#\{Av\mid\text{$v\in A^{2}$ is nondegenerate}\}=(q^{3}-1)(q+1)q,

\displaystyle\#\{Av\mid\text{$v\in A^{2}$ is degenerate and nonzero}\}=q+1.

Proof.

The number of
(x,y)\in A^{2}
such that
x,y
are independent is

(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q)
.
The number of
(x,y)\neq 0
such that
x,y
are dependent is

(q^{6}-1)-(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q)=(q^{3}-1)(q+1)
.
This proves the first two equalities.

Owing to Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 6.4 the last two follow by division by

\#F-1=q-1
and
\#A-1=q^{3}-1
.

∎

Proposition 9.8
.

Let
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate. Then

\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2\}=q^{3}-q.

Proof.

By Proposition 6.8 and Proposition 9.1 we have a bijection

\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2\}\to A-\langle x\rangle

taking
v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
to
x^{\prime}
.
It follows that

\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2\}=\#(A-\langle x\rangle)=q^{3}-q.

∎

Proposition 9.9
.

Let
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate.
We have a bijection

\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\in A^{2},\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2\}\to\{M\subset Av\mid\dim M=2,M\neq\langle x,y\rangle v\}

taking
Av^{\prime}
to
Av\cap Av^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By Proposition 9.2 and Proposition 6.8
if
v^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in A^{2}
and

\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
then
Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle v\neq\langle x,y\rangle v
.
Therefore we have a map

\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\in A^{2},\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2\}\to\{M\subset Av\mid\dim M=2,M\neq\langle x,y\rangle v\}

taking
Av^{\prime}
to
Av\cap Av^{\prime}
.
By Proposition 9.3 this map is injective.
Its target has cardinality

(q^{3}-1)/(q-1)-1=q^{2}+q
,
and the domain has cardinality

(q^{3}-q)/(q-1)=q^{2}+q
by the preceding proposition.
Hence the map is bijective.

∎

Proposition 9.10
.

Let
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate.

(i) For any one-dimensional subspace
L\subset Av
we have

\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid Av\cap Av^{\prime}=L\}=\begin{cases}q^{3}-q^{2}\quad&\text{if $L\subset\langle x,y\rangle v$, }\\
(q^{2}-1)(q-1)&\text{if $L\not\subset\langle x,y\rangle v$.}\end{cases}

(ii)

\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=1\}=q^{3}(q^{2}-1).\\

Proof.

(i) Let
L=\langle av\rangle
with
a\in A
,
a\neq 0
.
By Proposition 9.6 we have a bijection

\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle av\rangle\}\cong\{a^{\prime}\in A\mid a^{\prime}\notin\langle a\rangle,\;a^{\prime}\notin\langle x,y\rangle\},

in which
v^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}
are related by the equation
av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}
.
If
a\in\langle x,y\rangle
, the righthand set equals

A-\langle x,y\rangle
,
which has cardinality
q^{3}-q^{2}
.
If
a\notin\langle x,y\rangle
, the righthand set equals

A-(\langle x,y\rangle\cup\langle a\rangle)
,
which has cardinality

q^{3}-(q^{2}+q-1)=(q-1)(q^{2}-1)
.
It follows that

\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle av\rangle\}=\begin{cases}q^{3}-q^{2}\quad&\text{if $a\in\langle x,y\rangle$, }\\
(q^{2}-1)(q-1)&\text{if $a\notin\langle x,y\rangle$.}\end{cases}

This proves (i).

(ii)
The number of one-dimensional subspaces
L\subset Av
contained in
\langle x,y\rangle v
is
q+1
, and that of
L
not contained in
\langle x,y\rangle v
is
q^{2}
.
Using (i), we compute

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=1\}

\displaystyle=(q^{3}-q^{2})(q+1)+(q^{2}-1)(q-1)q^{2}

\displaystyle=q^{3}(q^{2}-1).

∎

Proposition 9.11
.

Let
v\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate.
We have

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\text{$v^{\prime}$ is nondegenerate and $Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0$}\}

\displaystyle=(q-1)(q^{5}-q^{3}-2q^{2}-2q-1).

Proof.

We know

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\text{$v^{\prime}$ is nondegenerate}\}=(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q),

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=3\}=q-1,

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2\}=q^{3}-q,

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=1\}=q^{3}(q^{2}-1).

Also

Av\cap Av^{\prime}\neq 0
only if
v^{\prime}
is nondegenerate by Corollary 6.4

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\text{$v^{\prime}$ is nondegenerate and $Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0$}\}

\displaystyle=(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q)-\{(q-1)+(q^{3}-q)+q^{3}(q^{2}-1)\}

\displaystyle=(q-1)(q^{5}-q^{3}-2q^{2}-2q-1).

∎

Proposition 9.12
.

Let
0\neq v\in A^{2}
.
If
v
is nondegenerate,

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0\}=q^{5}-q^{3}-2q^{2}-q.

If
v
is degenerate,

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0\}=q^{2}(q^{3}+q^{2}-1).

Proof.

Suppose first that
v
is nondegenerate.
Then
Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0
for all degenerate
v^{\prime}
by Corollary 6.4.
We have by Proposition 9.7

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,\text{$v^{\prime}$ is degenerate}\}=q+1.

And by Proposition 9.11

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid\text{$v^{\prime}$ is nondegenerate and $Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0$}\}=q^{5}-q^{3}-2q^{2}-2q-1.

Therefore

\displaystyle\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0\}

\displaystyle=(q+1)+(q^{5}-q^{3}-2q^{2}-2q-1)

\displaystyle=q^{5}-q^{3}-2q^{2}-q.

Suppose next that
v
is degenerate.
Then
Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0
for all nondegenerate
v^{\prime}
.
By Proposition 9.7

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid\text{$v^{\prime}$ is nondegenerate}\}=(q^{3}-1)(q+1)q.

For a degenerate
v^{\prime}
we have
Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0
if
Av\neq Av^{\prime}
.
By Proposition 9.7

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,\text{$v^{\prime}$ is degenerate},Av\neq Av^{\prime}\}=q.

Therefore

\displaystyle\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0\}

\displaystyle=(q^{3}-1)(q+1)q+q

\displaystyle=q^{2}(q^{3}+q^{2}-1).

∎

9.2
the noncommutative case

We next treat the case where
A
is not isotopic to a commutative algebra.
By theorem 8.1 it never occurs that
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2
for
v,v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
.

Proposition 9.13
.

Let
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate and
v^{\prime}\in A^{2}
.
Let
a,a^{\prime}\in A
with
a\neq 0
,
a^{\prime}\neq 0
. Suppose
av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}
.

(i)
\langle a\rangle=\langle a^{\prime}\rangle\iff\langle v\rangle=\langle v^{\prime}\rangle\iff Av=Av^{\prime}
.

(ii) When
\langle a\rangle\neq\langle a^{\prime}\rangle
, we have

Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle av\rangle
.

Proof.

(i) is proved as in the commutative case.

(ii)
Let
\langle a\rangle\neq\langle a^{\prime}\rangle
. Then
\langle v\rangle\neq\langle v^{\prime}\rangle
,
Av\neq Av^{\prime}
by (i).
And
0\neq av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}\in Av\cap Av^{\prime}
. So
\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=1
, hence

Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle av\rangle
.
∎

Proposition 9.14
.

Let
v=(x,y)\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate.

(i) For any one-dimensional subspace
L\subset Av
we have

\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid Av\cap Av^{\prime}=L\}=q^{3}-q.

(ii)

\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=1\}=q(q+1)(q^{3}-1).

Proof.

(i)
Let
L=\langle av\rangle
with
a\in A
. By the preceding proposition we have a bijection

\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid Av\cap Av^{\prime}=\langle av\rangle\}\cong\{a^{\prime}\in A\mid a^{\prime}\notin\langle a\rangle\},

in which
v^{\prime}
corresponds to
a^{\prime}
if
av=a^{\prime}v^{\prime}
.
This set has cardinality
q^{3}-q
.

(ii)
The number of one-dimensional subspaces
L\subset Av
is
q^{2}+q+1
.
It follows from (i) that

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=1\}

\displaystyle=(q^{3}-q)(q^{2}+q+1)=q(q+1)(q^{3}-1).

∎

Proposition 9.15
.

Let
v\in A^{2}
be nondegenerate. We have

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\text{$v^{\prime}$ is nondegenerate and $Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0$}\}

\displaystyle=(q-1)(q^{5}-2q^{3}-3q^{2}-2q-1).

Proof.

We know

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\text{$v^{\prime}$ is nondegenerate}\}=(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q),

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=3\}=q-1,

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=2\}=0,

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\dim(Av\cap Av^{\prime})=1\}=q(q+1)(q^{3}-1).

And

Av\cap Av^{\prime}\neq 0
only if
v^{\prime}
is nondegenerate.
It follows that

\displaystyle\#\{v^{\prime}\in A^{2}\mid\text{$v^{\prime}$ is nondegenerate and $Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0$}\}

\displaystyle=(q^{3}-1)(q^{3}-q)-\{(q-1)+q(q+1)(q^{3}-1)\}

\displaystyle=(q-1)(q^{5}-2q^{3}-3q^{2}-2q-1).

∎

Proposition 9.16
.

Let
0\neq v\in A^{2}
.
If
v
is nondegenerate,

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0\}=q^{5}-2q^{3}-3q^{2}-q.

If
v
is degenerate,

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0\}=q^{2}(q^{3}+q^{2}-1).

Proof.

Suppose that
v
is nondegenerate.
Then
Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0
for all degenerate
v^{\prime}
.
We have by Proposition 9.7

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,\text{$v^{\prime}$ is degenerate}\}=q+1.

And by Proposition 9.15

\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid\text{$v^{\prime}$ is nondegenerate and $Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0$}\}=q^{5}-2q^{3}-3q^{2}-2q-1.

Therefore

\displaystyle\#\{Av^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\neq 0,Av\cap Av^{\prime}=0\}

\displaystyle=(q+1)+(q^{5}-2q^{3}-3q^{2}-2q-1)

\displaystyle=q^{5}-2q^{3}-3q^{2}-q.

The proof for the degenerate case is the same as that of Proposition 9.12.

∎
