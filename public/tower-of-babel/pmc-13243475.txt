A unified decision oriented framework for sustainability assessment of electronic waste and recycled rubble aggregate concretes
Authors: Muhammad Tariq Bashir, Ratan Lal, Hasnain Saifullah, Ahmed Almutairi, Muhammad Ali Sikandar, Md Munir Hayet Khan, Wassef Ounaies
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA unified, decision-oriented framework was developed and applied to evaluate the sustainability of waste-derived concretes by integrating mechanical performance, economic feasibility, environmental impact, microstructural characteristics, and stakeholder perceptions into a single analytical platform. Two waste-based concretes, electronic waste concrete (EWC) with 5% e-waste replacement and rubble waste concrete (RWC) with 30% recycled demolition aggregates, were comparatively assessed against conventional concrete. The framework incorporated compressive strength testing, Sustainable Earned Value Management (SEVM), life cycle assessment (LCA), SEM–EDX and XRD analyses, and a structured survey of 249 construction professionals. Results indicated that both EWC (39.42 MPa) and RWC (40.00 MPa) achieved performance comparable to conventional concrete (41.17 MPa). Economic evaluation demonstrated cost-neutrality for EWC and a 9.7% cost reduction for RWC, while environmental analysis confirmed reductions in resource consumption and the carbon footprint. Microstructural analysis revealed that performance differences were primarily governed by interfacial characteristics rather than changes in hydration products. The proposed framework enables multi-criteria decision-making by linking laboratory performance with economic viability and re-al-world adoption constraints. Overall, RWC demonstrated superior performance, while EWC provided a viable circular solution for electronic waste management. This study establishes a scalable and adaptable decision-support tool for sustainable material selection in construction.Keywords: Construction and demolition waste, Sustainable construction, Sustainable resource management, Life cycle assessment, Circular economySubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Concrete is the most widely used construction material worldwide due to its high compressive strength, versatility, and economic viability. However, traditional concrete production is resource-intensive and environmentally burdensome. The cement industry alone contributes nearly 8% of global CO₂ emissions1–3, while extraction of natural aggregates representing almost 70% of concrete volume accelerates depletion of finite natural resources, increases embodied energy, and disrupts ecological systems4. With global aggregate demand projected to reach 50–55 billion tons by 2030, the urgency for sustainable and circular material alternatives is evident5–7.

Construction and demolition waste (CDW) has simultaneously emerged as one of the largest global waste streams8. CDW includes concrete, masonry, brick, ceramics, metals, and glass, much of which remains non-hazardous yet underutilized. Recycling CDW into recycled aggregates (RA) presents a viable pathway to reduce landfill pressures, conserve natural aggregate resources, and align with circular economy principles. Studies indicate that recycled concrete aggregate (RCA) can replace natural aggregates by 20–30% in structural applications when appropriate processing, grading, and contamination control are implemented3,9. Additional studies demonstrate successful use of RA in non‐structural applications such as paving blocks, masonry units, and concrete bricks, with acceptable strength, durability, and reduced embodied carbon emissions10,11.

Supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), including fly ash (FA), ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), and silica fume (SF), have been shown to mitigate the higher porosity and water absorption of RA, thereby enhancing mechanical strength, reducing permeability, and improving long-term durability12–17. These findings collectively position RAC as an environmentally preferable alternative when designed with complementary SCMs.

Parallel to CDW, electronic waste (E-waste) is one of the fastest‐growing global waste streams, reaching 62 billion kg in 202218. Only a small fraction is recycled due to complex composite structures and low recovery value, with e‐plastics constituting approximately 20% of total E‐waste19. E‐plastics are non‐biodegradable, chemically complex, and prone to generating microplastics, posing long‐term risks to soil and water ecosystems.

Recent research shows that incorporating E-waste as a partial aggregate replacement in concrete typically 5–20% can effectively reduce natural aggregate consumption and landfill dependency20. Experimental studies report acceptable compressive strength, flexural performance, and workability at controlled substitution levels. Moreover, life cycle assessment (LCA) studies show 1.26–23.8% reductions in global warming potential (GWP) when E‐waste aggregates are combined with SCMs such as GGBS and fly ash19, aligning with environmental performance objectives for low‐carbon concretes.

Despite the increasing body of literature, adoption barriers persist. Challenges include inconsistent quality of recycled aggregates, variable material properties, contamination, and uncertainties related to long-term durability9,21–23. For E‐waste concretes, complexity in collection, segregation, and pre‐processing presents further limitations. Industry practitioners also report concerns regarding specification compliance, lack of standardized guidelines, and insufficient field‐scale validation5,6,20.

Economic considerations present a mixed outlook. While the use of RA and E-waste aggregates may reduce procurement and landfill disposal costs, additional processing and transportation requirements can offset these benefits19. Financial viability therefore depends on local resource availability, quality control measures, and project‐specific performance requirements.

Beyond technical and economic dimensions, sustainability perceptions influence adoption. Many stakeholders acknowledge the environmental benefits of waste-based concretes but remain uncertain about durability, standardization, and long‐term reliability. Holistic evaluation combining laboratory testing, LCA, economic modeling, and stakeholder perceptions is therefore essential to bridge the gap between researches and practice24–26.

Although substantial research has been conducted on recycled aggregate concrete and e-waste utilization, most studies assess these materials independently and emphasize only one performance dimension, such as mechanical strength or environmental impact. This fragmented methodology restricts their relevance for real-world decision-making, which requires the simultaneous consideration of multiple criteria. Therefore, a significant gap persists in establishing an integrated evaluation approach that systematically incorporates technical performance, economic feasibility, environmental sustainability, and stakeholder acceptance within a unified framework.

To address this gap, this study introduces a unified, decision-oriented framework that integrates mechanical, economic, environmental, microstructural, and stakeholder-based assessments within a single analytical platform. The primary innovation of this research is the systematic linkage of these dimensions, which enables comprehensive benchmarking and informed multi-criteria decision-making. By connecting laboratory-scale evaluation with practical implementation requirements, the proposed framework offers a scalable and adaptable decision-support tool for sustainable material selection in construction.

Materials and methods

Research design and framework

A mixed-methods research approach was adopted to evaluate the feasibility of waste-derived concretes relative to conventional concrete. The methodology integrates laboratory-based mechanical testing, sustainability-oriented economic evaluation, life cycle environmental assessment, microstructural and mineralogical characterization, and a structured stakeholder perception survey. This comprehensive framework was selected to ensure triangulation and robustness of findings, explicitly positioning the study as a screening-level comparative investigation rather than a structural qualification or design-oriented assessment.

Concrete mix design and mechanical testing

The experimental phase included the preparation and testing of three concrete mixes: normal concrete (NC) as the control, electronic waste concrete (EWC) with 5% of the natural coarse aggregate replaced by processed electronic waste, and rubble waste concrete (RWC) with 30% of the natural coarse aggregate replaced by recycled demolition rubble (Table 1). The selected replacement levels align with substitution ranges commonly reported in the literature for screening-level evaluation27. All mixes were prepared using a conventional 1:2:4 proportion for cement, fine aggregate, and coarse aggregate, respectively, with a constant water–cement ratio to isolate the effect of aggregate replacement.

For each mix, three replicate specimens were tested, and the average compressive strength was reported. While this sample size is consistent with preliminary experimental studies in concrete research, statistical reliability was further assessed using standard deviation analysis. The observed variability remained within acceptable limits (less than 5%), indicating good consistency in the experimental results. It is acknowledged that larger sample sizes would enhance statistical robustness, and this will be addressed in future studies focusing on detailed structural validation.

Cylindrical specimens were cast according to standard procedures and cured under controlled conditions for 28 days. Compressive strength testing was performed in accordance with ASTM C39 to characterize short-term mechanical performance.

Sustainability-oriented economic assessment using SEVM

The economic feasibility of waste-derived concretes was assessed using the Sustainable Earned Value Management (SEVM) framework, which enables simultaneous evaluation of cost efficiency and schedule performance in construction processes28. A comprehensive cost analysis accounted for material procurement, processing, transportation, and disposal costs for both conventional and waste-based concrete mixes.

Within the SEVM framework, Planned Value (PV), Earned Value (EV), and Actual Cost (AC) were determined for each mix. Based on these parameters, Cost Performance Index (CPI), Schedule Performance Index (SPI), Cost Variance (CV), and Schedule Variance (SV) were calculated. These indicators offered insights into the relative cost-effectiveness and potential scheduling implications of incorporating electronic and rubble waste aggregates under controlled laboratory conditions.

Life cycle environmental assessment

A Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) framework was employed to evaluate the environmental impacts of conventional and waste-derived concretes, following the principles and guidelines outlined in ISO 14040 and ISO 14044. To enhance methodological transparency and reproducibility, the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) in this study is defined using a functional unit of 1 m³ of concrete, which enables consistent comparison between conventional and waste-derived mixes. The life cycle inventory data were compiled from a combination of primary experimental inputs (material quantities and mix proportions) and secondary data obtained from published literature and established databases relevant to construction materials. The analysis follows a screening-level approach; therefore, widely reported emission factors and energy coefficients were adopted to estimate environmental impacts associated with raw material extraction, processing, and transportation. Although no specialized LCA software was employed, the calculations were performed using standardized LCA principles in accordance with ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 guidelines. This approach ensures a transparent and consistent basis for comparative environmental evaluation, while maintaining flexibility for future studies to incorporate more advanced databases and software tools for detailed impact assessment. The system boundary was defined as cradle-to-gate, encompassing raw material extraction and collection, waste processing and preparation, transportation, and concrete production. The use and service-life phases were excluded in line with the screening-level scope of the study.

The environmental assessment quantified differences in carbon emissions, energy consumption, and natural resource conservation resulting from the use of recycled aggregates and waste materials. This approach is consistent with previous LCA-based evaluations of recycled aggregate concrete and waste-modified cementitious materials19,24.

Microstructural and mineralogical characterization

Microstructural and mineralogical analyses were conducted to examine the influence of waste-derived aggregates on cement hydration, matrix densification, and interfacial bonding. Concrete samples from NC, RWC, and EWC were collected after 28 days of curing and subsequently oven-dried to halt further hydration.

Scanning Electron Microscopy coupled with Energy-Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDX) was employed to investigate surface morphology, pore structure, and the quality of the interfacial transition zone (ITZ), as well as to determine elemental composition and distribution within the cement matrix. In parallel, X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to identify crystalline phases and assess whether the incorporation of recycled aggregates altered the mineralogical composition or hydration products of cement. The combined SEM–EDX and XRD analyses provided complementary insights into microstructural integrity and phase stability, supporting the interpretation of observed mechanical performance trends.

Stakeholder survey design and statistical analysis

A structured questionnaire survey was administered to 249 construction professionals from both academia and industry, including engineers, project managers, contractors, consultants, and researchers. The questionnaire survey was employed as a triangulation tool to complement the experimental, economic, and environmental analyses. Specifically, it enabled the validation of laboratory and analytical findings against industry perceptions, while also identifying practical implementation barriers, stakeholder acceptance levels, and gaps between perceived and measured performance. This integration strengthens the decision-oriented relevance of the study by linking technical feasibility with real-world applicability.

The questionnaire assessed five constructs including awareness of waste materials, cost considerations, environmental impact, durability and performance, and adoption and implementation challenges. Responses were recorded using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). The selection of constructs was informed by an extensive review of relevant literature and refined through consultation with three domain experts.

The reliability of the survey instrument was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, with values above 0.7 indicating acceptable internal consistency29. A purposive sampling strategy was employed to ensure participation from professionals actively engaged in construction practices, including engineers, contractors, consultants, and academics. Participants were selected through professional networks and institutional affiliations to ensure domain expertise. While this approach ensured informed responses, it may introduce sampling bias toward technically knowledgeable individuals. To mitigate this, efforts were made to include participants from diverse roles and experience levels across both academia and industry. Nevertheless, the results should be interpreted as indicative of informed professional perspectives rather than fully representative of the entire construction sector. Data analysis was conducted using descriptive and inferential statistical techniques in SPSS. The Relative Importance Index (RII) was used to rank perception factors and identify key trends in stakeholder responses.

Data sources and methodological scope

Both primary and secondary data sources were utilized in this study. Primary data included laboratory test results and survey responses, while secondary data comprised peer-reviewed journal articles, industry reports, and policy documents. The overall methodology was designed to support a comparative, screening-level evaluation of waste-derived concretes, providing early-stage evidence to inform sustainability benchmarking and adoption pathways.

The experimental program involved laboratory-based testing conducted in accordance with ASTM standards. The environmental assessment followed ISO 14040 and ISO 14044, which ensure methodological transparency and do not require ethical approval. The stakeholder survey was based on voluntary participation, with no collection of personal or sensitive data; all responses were anonymized and obtained with informed consent. Therefore, institutional ethical approval was not required.

Respondent demographics and data reliability

The questionnaire survey involved 249 respondents representing academia and industry. Engineers comprised the majority (82%), followed by project managers, contractors, clients, and consultants. Educational backgrounds were diverse, with 46% holding master’s degrees, 44% holding bachelor’s degrees, and 10% held doctoral degrees, indicating a technically informed sample. Almost 86% had between 5 and 10 years of professional experience, reflecting a cohort engaged in current construction practices and potentially more receptive to innovative materials.

The internal consistency of the survey data was validated using Cronbach’s Alpha, with most constructs exceeding the threshold of 0.7 as shown in the Fig. 2. This confirmed the instrument’s reliability and the robustness of the findings derived from respondents’ perceptions.

Cronbach’s alpha values for different constructs.

Awareness and perceptions of waste concrete

The study revealed a strong overall awareness of waste materials in concrete production. Descriptive analysis showed high scores for awareness of waste material usage (mean = 4.38) and familiarity with such materials (mean = 4.12). RII results supported this trend, with awareness of use ranking highest (RII = 0.711), indicating a solid technical understanding among professionals of recycled materials in construction.

However, awareness of environmental benefits was comparatively lower. The lowest RII value (0.616) was associated with recognition of waste concrete’s sustainability impacts, suggesting that while respondents were technically knowledgeable, their appreciation of environmental advantages remained limited. This underscores the need for targeted outreach, educational initiatives, and demonstration projects to improve understanding of the ecological value of waste concrete.

A comparative analysis across stakeholder groups further highlighted important differences in perceptions. Engineers showed the highest confidence in the mechanical performance of waste concrete (mean = 4.21), whereas consultants expressed greater concern about long-term durability (mean = 3.74). Academics placed the most significant emphasis on environmental benefits (RII = 0.712), while contractors prioritized cost savings (RII = 0.701). However, this high awareness does not directly translate into implementation confidence, as comparatively lower RII values for environmental understanding and durability concerns suggest the presence of a knowledge–practice gap. This indicates that while technical awareness is well established, further efforts are required in the form of training, standardization, and field validation to enhance practical adoption. Project managers demonstrated moderate awareness but remained cautious about implementation challenges.

Integrated economic, SEVM, and sustainability analysis

Economic considerations continue to play a central role in material selection, particularly when introducing innovative materials such as recycled waste aggregate5,6. Survey responses indicated a moderate level of confidence in the economic benefits of waste concrete, with the mean score of 3.90 reflecting cautious optimism among stakeholders. While the majority acknowledged the possibility of cost advantages, nearly 30% expressed disagreement, indicating a need for clearer cost-based evidence. This sentiment was reflected in RII values, where “increasing cost analysis research” ranked highest (0.671), whereas “highlighting cost savings” received the lowest score (0.593). These values underscore the importance of rigorous and transparent economic evaluation frameworks such as SEVM.

SEVM analysis for E-waste concrete

The SEVM model was applied to assess the economic feasibility of incorporating 5% E-Waste as a partial replacement for natural coarse aggregate. Material consumption for the three cylinders followed the standard 1:2:4 mix proportion. For traditional concrete, the total material cost amounted to PKR 39.63 (≈ USD 0.1415; 14.15¢). By comparison, the E-Waste concrete, which replaced 5% of coarse aggregate with shredded electronic waste, yielded a slightly reduced cost of PKR 39.11 (≈ USD 0.1396; 13.96¢). The cost difference of PKR 0.52 (≈ USD 0.0019; 0.19¢) per three cylinders demonstrates a marginal saving, indicating that at low substitution levels, the economic advantage of E-Waste is modest but positive.

In terms of SEVM performance, the planned value (PV) for the traditional mix was PKR 39.63 (≈ USD 0.1415), while the earned value (EV) and actual cost (AC) for the E-Waste concrete were equal at PKR 39.11 (≈ USD 0.1396). This produced a CPI of 1.0, confirming that the project remained fully on budget. The schedule performance index (SPI), calculated as EV/PV = 39.11/39.63, equaled 0.987, indicating a minor schedule delay but still broadly consistent with planned progress. Cost variance (CV = EV – AC) was zero, whereas schedule variance (SV = EV – PV) was − PKR 0.52 (≈ − USD 0.0019; −0.19¢), reflecting the small difference between planned and earned values.

Despite the minimal economic savings, the E-Waste mix provides clear sustainability advantages. By reducing landfill-bound electronic waste and conserving natural aggregate resources, the mix supports circular-economy objectives. Although the financial benefits are limited at low substitution levels, the SEVM findings confirm that E-Waste concrete is a cost-neutral and environmentally advantageous alternative. Similar cost advantages of recycled aggregates over natural aggregates have been reported in comparative economic studies4,30.

SEVM analysis for rubble waste concrete

A more substantial economic impact was observed in the case of rubble waste concrete, where 30% of natural coarse aggregates were replaced with recycled demolition rubble. Traditional concrete for three cylinders again cost PKR 39.63 (≈ USD 0.1415; 14.15¢). In contrast, rubble concrete, due to the significantly lower cost of recycled rubble, reduced total material expenditure to PKR 35.79 (≈ USD 0.1278; 12.78¢). This corresponds to a cost saving of PKR 3.84 (≈ USD 0.0137; 1.37¢), equivalent to a 9.7% reduction in cost, marking a far stronger economic benefit than the E-waste substitution.

SEVM results further supported this conclusion. With a planned value (PV) identical to the traditional mix (PKR 39.63; ≈ USD 0.1415; 14.15¢), the rubble mix achieved earned value (EV) and actual cost (AC) values of PKR 35.79 (≈ USD 0.1278; 12.78¢). As with E-waste concrete, the cost performance index (CPI) remained at 1.0, indicating efficient cost control. However, the schedule performance index (SPI) dropped to 0.90, suggesting a slightly greater delay relative to the planned schedule, possibly due to the additional processing, sorting, or handling requirements associated with demolition rubble. The schedule variance (SV = EV − PV) was − PKR 3.84 (≈ − USD 0.0137; −1.37¢), directly mirroring the cost savings achieved.

The sustainability advantages of rubble waste concrete are even more pronounced. The reduction in natural aggregate extraction, combined with the diversion of demolition waste from landfills, strengthens its environmental profile31. Moreover, the notable cost savings enhance their appeal to both industry and policymakers, aligning with green building principles and sustainable procurement strategies.

Integrated interpretation in context of sustainability

Across both SEVM evaluations, the economic trends clearly demonstrate that waste-derived concretes offer viable financial and environmental benefits, although the magnitude varies with waste type and substitution level. E-Waste concrete provides small but positive cost savings (≈ 0.19¢ USD per three cylinders) alongside strong environmental justification, particularly in regions facing escalating electronic waste challenges. In contrast, rubble waste concrete delivers substantially greater financial benefits (≈ 1.37¢ USD savings per three cylinders) while simultaneously reducing the ecological footprint associated with quarrying activities and construction and demolition waste disposal.

Complementing the SEVM findings, survey respondents demonstrated strong awareness of cost-related considerations yet expressed uncertainty regarding the actual scale of savings an uncertainty now addressed by the detailed SEVM outcomes. Environmental perceptions were also favorable, with 72% recognizing the environmental burden of traditional concrete and supporting greater LCA-based research. This aligns with the empirical evidence from LCA studies showing that recycled aggregate concrete reduces embodied carbon by approximately 13.47 kg CO₂ per ton, consistent with previous literature cited in the manuscript.

Overall, integrating SEVM, cost modelling, mechanical testing, and stakeholder perception data provides a holistic understanding of the viability of waste concrete. While both E-Waste and rubble aggregates support sustainability goals, the economic advantage is notably greater for rubble waste concrete, strengthening the case for its large-scale adoption. E-Waste concrete, though economically modest, remains a strategically important material for addressing global electronic waste challenges and promoting circular material flows. Mechanical performance and durability

A comparative assessment of the mechanical behaviour of E-Waste concrete and RCA concrete provides deeper insight into how different waste aggregates influence compressive strength performance. Figure 5 shows that the 5% E-Waste concrete achieved an average compressive strength of 39.42 MPa, whereas the traditional concrete exhibited an average of 41.17 MPa. In contrast, Fig. 6 illustrates that the 30% RCA concrete reached an average strength of 40.00 MPa, which is also close to the traditional mix. The comparison of the two figures illustrates that the RCA concrete demonstrates slightly better strength retention relative to the E-Waste concrete, despite incorporating a substantially higher replacement level.

Comparison of compressive strengths of traditional concrete and 5% E-Waste concrete.

Comparison of compressive strengths of traditional concrete and 30% RCA (rubble) concrete.

These differences stem from the inherent characteristics of the two waste materials. RCA retains fragments of old mortar and exhibits mechanical properties more closely aligned with natural aggregates, contributing to improved interlock and adhesion within the cement matrix. This is reflected in the compressive strength trends depicted in Fig. 6, where the RCA cylinders show minimal deviation from the traditional concrete. In contrast, E-waste particles introduce lower stiffness into the mixture. These factors reduce bonding efficiency at the interfacial transition zone and result in slightly lower strength values observed, even at a modest 5% replacement level. Figure 6 also shows the compressive strength comparison, where E-waste and rubble waste concretes exhibited only slight reductions of 4.3% and 2.8% compared to the control, remaining within acceptable structural tolerance limits.

E-Waste concrete, although demonstrating acceptable compressive strength at low replacement levels (with an approximate 4.3% reduction compared to the control mix), is more suitable for applications where minor strength reductions are permissible. This recommendation is based on both mechanical results and microstructural observations, which indicate a comparatively weaker interfacial transition zone due to the polymeric nature of e-waste particles. Accordingly, E-Waste concrete is better suited for non-structural or low-load applications such as paving units, blocks, and secondary structural elements, whereas stricter structural applications may require materials with higher bonding efficiency and strength retention.

The observed compressive strength retention for rubble waste concrete is consistent with previous studies reporting strength retention in the range of 90–100% depending on aggregate quality and replacement ratio. This agreement reinforces the reliability of recycled concrete aggregates in structural applications. In contrast, the slightly lower performance of e-waste concrete aligns with findings reported for polymer-based aggregates, where reduced stiffness and weaker interfacial bonding limit strength development. These comparisons indicate that material composition and interfacial behavior are more influential than replacement ratio alone, emphasizing the need for material-specific optimization strategies.

Furthermore, the comparative behavior indicates that RCA can replace natural aggregates at significantly higher ratios without substantial reductions in mechanical performance. This makes RCA more suitable for both structural and non-structural concretes. E-Waste concrete, although demonstrating acceptable compressive strength at low replacement levels, remains more appropriate for applications where small decreases in strength are permissible. Nevertheless, its incorporation still adds meaningful environmental value by safely immobilizing electronic waste within a stable cementitious matrix.

Survey results also verified the experimental data, with respondents assigning a mean score of 4.07 to the statement “waste concrete performs comparably to traditional concrete.” However, concerns regarding long-term durability persisted. The highest RII score in this category (0.700) related to the need for further research into durability, reflecting industry caution about potential chemical incompatibilities, aggregate variability, and performance under aggressive environmental conditions. While the short-term equivalence of compressive strength is well established, stakeholders require long-term reliability assurance before endorsing widespread structural use.

Adoption and implementation challenges

Despite optimism about the potential of concrete waste, large-scale implementation remains uncertain.

The lowest RII across all categories (0.593) was associated with the statement that “challenges of waste concrete are well understood by stakeholders.” This indicates limited awareness of practical barriers, such as inconsistent material quality, the absence of standardized guidelines, and a lack of processing infrastructure. These findings emphasize the need for standardized recycling protocols, quality assurance systems, and training programs to enhance industry confidence. Furthermore, supportive policies, including financial incentives and regulatory frameworks, could accelerate adoption and build credibility for waste concrete as a mainstream alternative.

Microstructural and mineralogical characterization

The microstructural features and phase composition of normal concrete (NC), rubble concrete (RC), and e-waste concrete (EWC) were examined using scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDX) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) to elucidate the influence of recycled materials on matrix densification, interfacial bonding, and crystalline phases.

The combined SEM–EDX and XRD analyses indicate that rubble waste concrete exhibits superior microstructural compatibility, characterized by improved mechanical interlocking and a relatively stable interfacial transition zone, which contributes to its higher strength retention. In contrast, e-waste concrete shows localized interfacial weaknesses due to the smooth and polymeric nature of e-waste particles, which reduces bonding efficiency within the cement matrix. However, XRD results confirm that the fundamental cement hydration products remain unchanged in all mixes, indicating that performance differences arise primarily from microstructural characteristics rather than mineralogical alterations. Therefore, while rubble waste concrete is suitable for broader structural applications, e-waste concrete remains a viable sustainable alternative for controlled applications with acceptable performance trade-offs.

SEM morphology and EDX analysis

The SEM micrograph of normal concrete reveals a relatively dense and compact cementitious matrix, characterized by gel-like hydration products and limited visible porosity at the 10 μm scale (Fig. 7). Such morphology is indicative of effective cement hydration and strong paste–aggregate bonding. The corresponding EDX spectrum is dominated by C, O, and Si, confirming the presence of calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and silica-rich phases originating from cement and natural aggregates.

SEM micrographs and EDX spectra (a) normal concrete, (b) rubble waste concrete, and (c) electronic waste concrete, illustrating differences in matrix density, interfacial transition zones, and elemental composition.

In contrast, rubble concrete, incorporating 30% demolition waste as aggregate replacement, exhibits a more heterogeneous and rougher microstructure, with angular particles and localized clusters associated with old mortar remnants attached to recycled aggregates. Despite this heterogeneity, the matrix remains relatively well-packed, suggesting adequate mechanical interlocking between the recycled aggregate and new cement paste. The EDX spectrum shows prominent C, O, and Si peaks, along with a noticeable Mg signal, which may be attributed to Mg-bearing phases from recycled masonry or aged cement paste. This microstructural integrity explains why RC achieved compressive strength comparable to that of normal concrete, despite the use of recycled constituents23,26.

The e-waste concrete, containing 5% electronic waste, displays a comparatively less homogeneous microstructure, with evidence of a weaker interfacial transition zone (ITZ) between the cement paste and e-waste particles. This weaker bonding can be associated with the smooth surface texture and polymeric nature of specific e-waste components, which hinder strong paste adhesion. EDX analysis confirms the incorporation of e-waste through the detection of Sn and Br, elements commonly associated with electronic components and brominated flame retardants, in addition to the dominant C and O peaks. The observed weak ITZ and presence of non-mineral phases provide a microstructural explanation for the slightly reduced compressive strength of EWC compared with NC and RC, while remaining within acceptable limits at low replacement levels.

XRD analysis

The XRD patterns of all concrete mixes exhibit similar dominant crystalline phases, indicating that the introduction of recycled materials did not fundamentally alter the cement hydration mechanism. The principal peaks correspond to quartz (Q, SiO₂), calcite (C, CaCO₃), and portlandite (P, Ca(OH)₂) (Fig. 8).

XRD patterns of normal cement, rubble waste concrete, and electronic waste concrete.

For normal concrete, strong quartz peaks emerging from siliceous aggregates, while calcite peaks are attributed to limestone filler and partial carbonation of calcium-bearing hydration products. Portlandite peaks confirm typical cement hydration behavior.

The concrete rubble shows comparable quartz, calcite, and portlandite peaks, though with slight variations in peak intensities. These variations reflect the inherited mineralogy of recycled demolition waste, including previously carbonated phases from old concrete. The persistence of these hydration-related phases supports the mechanical performance observed for RC and indicates that recycled aggregates remain chemically compatible with the cementitious matrix23,26.

Similarly, the e-waste concrete retains the characteristic quartz, calcite, and portlandite peaks, confirming that cement hydration products dominate the crystalline structure. No new major crystalline phases associated with e-waste are detected, suggesting that e-waste primarily influences concrete performance through microstructural and interfacial effects rather than crystalline phase formation. This finding aligns with SEM observations, in which performance differences are mainly attributed to ITZ quality rather than mineralogical changes.

Overall, the combined SEM–EDX and XRD results demonstrate that rubble waste concrete offers superior microstructural compatibility, while e-waste concrete remains structurally viable at low substitution levels, supporting its potential use as a sustainable construction material with controlled performance trade-offs.

Practical implication

The integration of mechanical, economic, environmental, and stakeholder perception analyses provides a comprehensive understanding of the feasibility of waste-derived concretes. The integrated results confirm structural, economic, and environmental viability; however, adoption remains constrained by non-technical factors such as standardization, durability concerns, and limited field validation. Policymakers can address these through guidelines, incentives, and LCA-informed specifications. From a practical perspective, rubble waste concrete can be directly implemented in structural and non-structural applications due to its strong performance across all indicators. In contrast, e-waste concrete is more suitable for controlled applications where environmental benefits are prioritized. This integrated framework thus provides a decision-support tool for sustainable material selection in real-world construction projects.

Limitations and scope of the study

This study provides a screening-level evaluation of waste-derived concretes under controlled laboratory conditions. The experimental program utilized a limited sample size and focused primarily on short-term mechanical performance, specifically 28-day compressive strength. Long-term durability aspects, including creep, shrinkage, chemical resistance, and fatigue behavior, were not addressed. The environmental assessment was restricted to a cradle-to-gate life cycle boundary, thereby excluding use-phase and end-of-life impacts. The economic evaluation, conducted using SEVM under controlled conditions, may yield different results in real-world construction scenarios due to variations in local material availability, processing requirements, and logistics.

Additionally, the stakeholder survey, although involving experienced professionals, reflects informed perceptions that may not comprehensively represent all industry segments.

Future research should prioritize long-term durability assessment, comprehensive life cycle analysis, and large-scale field validation to improve the robustness and practical applicability of the proposed unified framework.

Conclusion

This study introduces a comprehensive, decision-oriented framework for assessing the sustainability and practical feasibility of waste-derived concretes. The framework integrates mechanical performance, economic assessment, environmental analysis, microstructural characterization, and stakeholder perception within a single analytical structure.

The results demonstrate that both RWC and EWC are viable alternatives to conventional concrete. RWC retained 97.2% of control strength at 30% replacement, while EWC retained 95.7% at 5% replacement. Economic analysis indicates that EWC yields a marginal saving of PKR 0.52, whereas RWC achieves a substantially higher saving of PKR 3.84 (9.7%) with a CPI of 1.0, despite a slight reduction in SPI to 0.90. Environmental evaluation reveals embodied carbon reductions of approximately 13.47 kg CO₂ per ton. Microstructural analysis confirms that performance differences are primarily governed by interfacial characteristics rather than changes in hydration products.

The principal contribution of this work is the integration of multi-dimensional outcomes into a coherent framework that enables informed, multi-criteria decision-making. By linking technical performance with economic, environmental, and stakeholder considerations, the framework offers a practical basis for evaluating material feasibility beyond isolated performance indicators.

The findings further indicate that adoption barriers are primarily non-technical, as stakeholder responses highlight concerns regarding durability, standardization, and implementation (RII = 0.593). In this context, RWC is identified as the most suitable option for near-term large-scale application, while EWC serves as a complementary solution for targeted and controlled use.

Overall, the proposed framework functions as a scalable decision-support tool for sustainable construction. Future research should prioritize long-term durability assessment, comprehensive life-cycle evaluation, and field-scale validation to enhance applicability and facilitate broader adoption of waste-derived materials.
