Factors affecting tuberculosis (TB) prevention behaviors among household contacts in Phitsanulok Province, northern Thailand: implications for TB prevention strategy plan
Authors: Thanyarat Wongchana, Archin Songthap
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary tuberculosis can spread by airborne transmission when an infected person coughs, sneezes and speaks via expectorates. Therefore, household contacts who are close to a tuberculosis patient are at the highest risk of contracting the disease. This analytical cross-sectional study aimed to determine the factors affecting prevention behaviors among household contacts in Phitsanulok Province, northern Thailand.MethodsThe study sample included 193 household contacts with pulmonary tuberculosis in Phitsanulok Province. They were randomly selected using a multistage sampling technique. Data were collected by a self-administered questionnaire consisting of 8 parts: (1) sociodemographic characteristics, (2) tuberculosis knowledge, (3) perceived susceptibility of tuberculosis infection, (4) perceived severity of tuberculosis, (5) perceived self-efficacy of tuberculosis prevention, (6) perceived outcome of tuberculosis prevention, (7) social support of tuberculosis prevention, and (8) tuberculosis prevention behaviors. Frequencies, percentages, means, standard deviations, and multiple regression analysis were explored for data analysis.ResultsMost participants (91.2%) had tuberculosis prevention behaviors at a high level (scores of 4.35). Factors that significantly affected tuberculosis prevention behaviors included perceived self-efficacy in tuberculosis prevention (β = 0.474), social support (β = 0.220), current smoker (β = -0.171), driving to the hospital (β = 0.150), unknown history of tuberculosis vaccination (BCG) (β = -0.122), and those who were older siblings, aunts, uncles, younger siblings, or nephews of participants (β = -0.116). These 6 factors explained 38.5% of tuberculosis prevention behaviors.ConclusionHousehold contacts to pulmonary tuberculosis should be educated about the perceived self-efficacy of tuberculosis prevention, and receive more social support to prevent the disease. The focus should be on current smokers, those who drive to the hospital, those who do not know the history of tuberculosis vaccination, and relatives of patients (older sibling, aunt, uncle, younger sibling, nephew) to reduce the risk of pulmonary tuberculosis in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10327-x.Keywords: Pulmonary tuberculosis, Household contacts, TB prevention behaviors

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by the mycobacterium tuberculosis. Tuberculosis can occur in any organ of the body; however, 80% commonly occur in the lungs. Pulmonary tuberculosis can spread by airborne transmission when an infected person coughs, sneezes and speaks via expectorates. The transmission occurs when contaminated droplets are inhaled into the respiratory tract [1]. A person with pulmonary tuberculosis in the infectious stage can transmit the bacteria to people around them. Approximately, one infected person can spread the disease to 10–15 people annually [2]. Therefore, household contacts who are close to a tuberculosis patient are at the highest risk of contracting the disease, primarily through unprotected exposure to coughing or sneezing. The risk of infection and subsequent illness among these household contacts is between 8% and 60% [3].

In 2022, the World Health Organization reported that 75% of household contacts in Thailand were latent TB infections [4]. Additionally, tuberculosis detection rose from 1.96% in 2018 to 2.07% in 2020 among household contacts [5]. In 2022, Health Region 2, Thailand detected tuberculosis among household contacts by 2.88%. The infection rate in Phitsanulok, Uttaradit, Sukhothai, Phetchabun, and Tak were 3.71%, 3.03%, 2.57%, 2.49%, and 2.24%, respectively [6].

The impacts that might result from tuberculosis infection among family members include physical, mental, and emotional health. Further, tuberculosis infection can affect social problems and budgeting problems for healthcare services. The physical health problems involve pathology of the respiratory system at the lung function and the social problem is the stigmatization and discrimination from people around the infected patients [7]. Tuberculosis patients sometimes feel stressed to themselves [8]. Some of them have to stop working and seek healthcare services. As a result, they can lack income to spend time taking care of themselves [9].

The previous studies on TB prevention behaviors among household contacts revealed that their perception and preventive behaviors were inadequate. Approximately two-thirds (67.8%) of household contacts had high perceived barriers to performing TB prevention measures [10]. Further, 49.1% had a moderate level of knowledge about tuberculosis infection, and 55.4% had a moderate level of perceived severity of tuberculosis, such as they believed that untreated tuberculosis would not lead to death. Additionally, 62.4% had a moderate level of perceived susceptibility to tuberculosis infection, with incorrect perceptions such as tuberculosis cannot be easily transmitted to others. Slightly more than half of the participants (52.3%) reported perceived benefits of TB prevention practices at a moderate level, and 51.8% of household contacts had a low level of preventive practices. Only 10% of household contacts disposed of patients’ sputum or saliva containers by burning or burying. Furthermore, only 13.6% actively sought information on TB prevention [11]. Regarding TB screening among household contacts, it was found that the prevalence of household contacts who did not undergo tuberculosis screening was as high as 73.0% [12]. The relevant literature reviews and research on prevention behaviors showed that motivation is a critical internal factor that influences an individual’s behavioral modification [13]. Although the Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) and Social Support Theory have been widely applied, their use among household contacts of TB patients remains limited. There is little knowledge related to household contact with tuberculosis in Phitsanulok, northern Thailand. In addition, the incidence of tuberculosis from household contacts in Phitsanulok is the highest in this region.

Therefore, we applied protection motivation theory (PMT) [14, 15] and social support theory [16] to determine factors that influence tuberculosis prevention behaviors among household contacts. Protective motivation theory helps describe how influencing individuals’ perceptions of health problems can result in changing behaviors to prevent tuberculosis from infected persons. Social support theory helps describe how family members, health care providers, or government agencies support knowledge, and motivation to household contacts for tuberculosis prevention. Our study focused on household contacts aged 20–59 years since the infectious rate of household contacts in this age group is the highest. If they are infected by tuberculosis, they have to stop working and lose income. Moreover, the disease can affect their daily lives and quality of life. The findings from this study can be utilized to form a strategic plan to reduce the transmission of tuberculosis among household contacts in northern Thailand. The findings can also be used as a guideline to develop a program to prevent the incidence of tuberculosis infection among household contacts for healthcare providers, and village health volunteers in this region.

Methods

Study design

This was an analytical cross-sectional study conducted between May and July 2024 to determine factors that influence tuberculosis prevention behaviors among household contacts in Phisanulok Province, northern Thailand.

Settings

A multistage random sampling technique was utilized to recruit participants. Five districts out of 9 from Phitsanulok Province were randomly selected as a representative using a simple random sampling technique. Then, participants from each district were recruited via a systematic random sampling based on the population size.

Participants

The study population was 268 household contacts with pulmonary TB patients in Phitanulok Province. The sample included 193 household contacts. The sample size was calculated using the formula to estimate the population mean [17] using the n4stuies application. A total of 268 study populations (N) were used with a variance (σ2) of tuberculosis prevention behaviors was 1.94 [18]. We set the error of estimation (e) at 0.194, and the alpha (α) was 0.05. The inclusion criteria included; (1) being aged 20–59 years, (2) being able to write and read Thai, and (3) willingness to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria were; (1) having a sudden illness on the date of data collection, and (2) being unable to complete the questionnaire.

Research tool

We developed the questionnaire for data collection based on the theoretical concepts and related studies which composed of 8 parts: (1) sociodemographic characteristics, (2) tuberculosis knowledge, (3) perceived susceptibility of tuberculosis infection, (4) perceived severity of tuberculosis, (5) perceived self-efficacy of tuberculosis prevention, (6) perceived outcome of tuberculosis prevention, (7) social support of tuberculosis prevention, and (8) tuberculosis prevention behaviors. Sociodemographic characteristics included 11 items regarding data on household contacts. Knowledge was collected through 10 yes-no questions and classified into 3 categories using a sum score [19]: good (scores of 8–10), average (scores of 6–7), and low (scores of 0–5). The perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, and perceived outcome involved 10 5-level rating scale questions beginning from strongly agree to strongly disagree and classified into 3 levels [20] using mean scores for high (3.68–5.00), average (2.34–3.67) and low (1.00–2.33). Perceived self-efficacy included 10 5-rating scale questions ranging from strongly believe to strongly disbelieve and divided into 3 categories using mean scores for high (3.68–5.00), average (2.34–3.67), and low (1.00–2.33). Social support involved 10 5-rating scale questions starting from always to never and was divided into 3 groups [20] using mean scores for high (3.68–5.00), average (2.34–3.67), and low (1.00–2.33). Tuberculosis prevention behaviors were evaluated among 30 5-rating scale questions starting from always to never and divided into 3 groups [20] using sum scores for high (3.68–5.00), average (2.34–3.67), and low (1.00–2.33). The validity of the questionnaire was assessed by 5 experts in public health, infectious diseases, and epidemiology. All questions with an item objective congruence (IOC) index higher than 0.5 were considered to meet the standard criteria of the validity test. The reliability of the questionnaire was evaluated among 30 respondents who were not included in this study. We tested the reliability by Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, except for knowledge, which was assessed by the Kuder-Richardson 20 (KR-20). The reliability scores of knowledge, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived self-efficacy, perceived outcome, social support, and tuberculosis prevention behavior were 0.958, 0.880, 0.789, 0.795, 0.900, 0.899, and 0.951, respectively.

In our study, we applied protection motivation theory (PMT) and social support theory to identify factors affecting tuberculosis prevention behaviors among household contacts. Protective Motivation Theory is an internal factor that seeks to understand people’s motivation to undertake protective behaviors. PMT believes that people’s motivation can engage in protective behaviors which are influenced by four main perception factors including perceived severity, perceived susceptibility, perceived outcome, and perceived self-efficacy [15].

The social support believes in external factors that will help individuals to engage in disease-prevention behaviors. In this case, the social support may come from family members, neighbors, healthcare providers, community leaders, or village health volunteers [16]. The factors that involve social support such as emotional support, information support, instrumental support, and appraisal support.

Therefore, when these two theories are combined, it will enhance people to have internal factors (4 perception factors of PMT) supported by external factors under social support. We believe that these two theories can cause people to have complete disease prevention behaviors. Therefore, we would like to find out how much the combined internal and external factors can explain TB preventive behaviors among household contacts. Data collection

Data were separately collected at the 5 selected districts using a self-administered questionnaire. We collected data in the community where the participants lived via home visits. We used the list of household contacts and tuberculosis patients from the district hospitals in each area where the patients were treated and followed up. Participants who met the inclusion criteria and were willing to participate in this study were required to complete the questionnaire. It took approximately 40 min to complete all parts of the questionnaire. Then, the researchers rechecked the questionnaires for completion. All completed questionnaires were subsequently utilized for data analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using a statistical software program (Jamovi). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means and standard deviations, were used to describe sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge about tuberculosis, perceived susceptibility to tuberculosis infection, perceived severity of tuberculosis, perceived self-efficacy of tuberculosis prevention, perceived outcome of tuberculosis prevention, social support of tuberculosis prevention, and tuberculosis prevention behaviors. Stepwise multiple regression analysis was explored to determine the factors that affect tuberculosis prevention behaviors among household contacts. All significance levels were considered a p-value less than 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

Of 193 participants, 59.1%were female, and 31.6% were aged 40–49 and 50–59 years (mean = 42.46, S.D.= 11.48). The majority of participants (59.10%) were married, 29.0% finished primary school, 75.1% had no health conditions, and 51.8% did not know the history of tuberculosis vaccination (BCG). Most of them (80.3%) were nonsmokers, 46.6% were children of TB patients, 70.5% spent time with the patient less than 8 h per day, 23.3% had three household members, and 54.9% drove to the hospital (Table 1).

Factors affecting tuberculosis prevention behaviors of household contacts

The results revealed that participants who had higher mean scores for perceived self-efficacy tended to have better tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those with lower mean scores (β = 0.474). Similarly, participants who had greater mean scores of social supports were more likely to have better tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those with lower mean scores (β = 0.220). However, participants who were current smokers had lower mean scores for tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those who never smoked (β = -0.171), Moreover, participants who drove to the hospital were more likely to have tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those who rode motorcycle (β = 0.150), and participants who did not know the history of tuberculosis vaccination(BCG) had lower mean scores for tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those who received it (β = -0.122). Lastly, participants who were older siblings, aunts, uncles, younger siblings, or nephews seemed to have lower mean scores for tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those who were husbands (β = -0.116). All of these significant factors explained 38.5% of tuberculosis prevention behaviors among household contacts (adjusted R square = 0.385) (Table 3).

Discussion

Our study aimed to determine the factors that affect tuberculosis prevention behaviors among household contacts. The factors that significantly affected tuberculosis prevention behaviors (p-value < 0.05) included perceived self-efficacy, social support, smoking, driving to the hospital, unknown history of tuberculosis vaccination (BCG), and relationship with the participants as an older sibling, aunt, uncle, younger sibling, or nephew.

Participants who had higher mean scores for perceived self-efficacy tended to have better tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those with lower mean scores. This finding was in line with that of the study by Natthachitra et al. [21], which found that perceived self-efficacy was positively associated with tuberculosis prevention behaviors. It might be due to persons perceive and expect that they have abilities to behave on tuberculosis prevention. After taking action on prevention, they receive positive results from the prevention behaviors based on their expectation [12].

Participants who had higher mean scores of social support on tuberculosis prevention behaviors were more likely to have better prevention behaviors than those who had lower mean scores. This finding was similar to that in the study by Nataly [22], which reported that social support network was the strongest evidence associated with TB prevention among household contacts. It can be explained that social support from family, and community can help prevent TB transmission within households. Furthermore, strong social support can encourage participants to seek healthcare settings for treatment adherence and provide emotional and practical support. Thus., social support can contribute to better treatment outcomes and reduced TB transmission [23].

The present study found that participants who never smoked tended to have better tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those who currently smoke. This finding was similar to that in the study by Kyaw Ko Ko Htet et al. [24], which reported that household contacts who actively smoke seemed to have better TB prevention. It might be because participants who smoke are not concerned about tuberculosis transmission. Further, they might have less perceived self-efficacy to prevent tuberculosis prevention. As a result, participants who do not smoke possibly have better TB prevention behaviors [25].

Our study found that participants who drove (e.g. car, van, or truck) to the hospital seemed to have better tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those who took a motorcycle to the hospital. This finding was similar to the results in the study by Natsima et al. [12], which reported that traveling to the hospital by public transportation has a 4.56 times higher risk of not going for tuberculosis screening than household contacts who travel by private vehicle. It might be participants who drive to the hospital by their own vehicles and do need not to wait for taxis or public transportation. Therefore, they might not miss the follow-up for treatment. In addition, our finding was consistent with the previous study [26], which indicated that participants who traveled to the hospital by motorcycle were associated with tuberculosis patient delay. This might be because they face some transportation problems such as long distances or being unable to ride motorcycle by themselves to access healthcare services from the healthcare settings.

The present study found that participants who received tuberculosis vaccination (BCG) tended to have better tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those who did not remember the history of TB vaccination, Our finding was consistent with the previous study [27], which indicated that participants who did not receive TB vaccination had a strong association with pulmonary tuberculosis infection. This might because the TB vaccine can increase the immune in the body to prevent tuberculosis infection [28]. In our study, participants who did not remember whether they received TB vaccination had low TB prevention behaviors. They might not be informed about BCG vaccination since they were not vaccinated, or they might be the persons who did not receive knowledge about TB, and TB prevention behaviors.

Lastly, participants who were husbands tended to have better tuberculosis prevention behaviors than those who were older siblings, aunts, uncles, younger siblings, or nephews of household contacts. This finding is the same as the study by Mullika et al. [29], which found that the husband, wife, and children of participants were related to tuberculosis prevention behaviors. It might be because they must take care of the tuberculosis patients in the family, they need to be healthy to take care of the infected patients. If they get TB infected, they might lose their jobs to take care of themselves.

Strengths and limitations.

Our study collected data directly from household contacts with tuberculosis patients in the highest incidence area in northern Thailand. We also applied two theories to determine factors that may influence tuberculosis prevention behaviors. These are the strengths of our study.

The limitations that might have affected the results were the other factors we did not explore, such as perceived stigma regarding tuberculosis. This factor might be associated with tuberculosis prevention behaviors in the Thai social context. Our study applied protection motivation theory and social support theory. The findings indicated that the significant factors can explain TB prevention behaviors among household contacts by 38.5%. We found only perceived self-efficacy under the protection motivation theory affected TB prevention behaviors. However, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, and perceived outcome were not related to the outcome.

Conclusion and suggestions

Our study revealed that factors affecting TB prevention behaviors among household contacts involved perceived self-efficacy of tuberculosis prevention, social support, current smoking, driving to the hospital, unknown history of tuberculosis vaccination, and relationship with the patient as older sibling, aunt, uncle, younger sibling, or nephew. In protection motivation theory, healthcare providers should educate individuals to increase perceived self-efficacy for TB prevention. In social support theory, healthcare providers should give them the information regarding TB prevention behaviors. Further, healthcare providers should also provide information about the risk of cigarette smoking to TB infection for those who are current smokers. In addition, participants who ride motorcycle to the hospital should be supported with the appropriate transportation or home visits to help them access to healthcare services. This includes giving education to participants who do not remember the history of BCG vaccination, and those who are older siblings, aunts, uncles, younger siblings, or nephews of household contacts to gain more knowledge about TB and TB prevention behaviors.

Further studies should include perceived stigma regarding tuberculosis to determine whether it influences tuberculosis prevention behaviors among household contacts in Phitsanulok Province.
