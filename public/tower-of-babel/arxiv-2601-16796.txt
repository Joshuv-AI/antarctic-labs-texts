Adaptive integration of 5 5 -convex and 5 5 -concave functions
Szymon Wąsowicz

Abstract
. This paper introduces an adaptive numerical integration method specifically designed for 5 5 -convex and 5 5 -concave functions of class C 6 ​ [ a , b ] C^{6}[a,b] . The approach is based on a refined inequality involving the three-point Gauss quadrature ( 𝒢 \mathcal{G} ) and the four-point Lobatto quadrature ( ℒ \mathcal{L} ). We utilize a specific linear combination, Q = 3 4 ​ 𝒢 + 1 4 ​ ℒ Q=\frac{3}{4}\mathcal{G}+\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{L} , and demonstrate that for functions with a sixth derivative of constant sign, the approximation error is effectively controlled by the difference between these two quadratures. We provide a rigorous justification for the stopping criterion of the adaptive algorithm. Numerical experiments, including the approximation of the integrals of a reciprocal function as well as of the exponential function, show that the proposed method significantly outperforms existing adaptive techniques designed for lower-order convexity, requiring substantially fewer subintervals to achieve high precision (up to 10 − 16 10^{-16} ).

1.
Remarks on adaptive methods of approximate integration

Numerical integration of a function
f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
is typically performed using a
quadrature rule
:

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\approx Q[f;a,b]:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}f\bigl(\lambda_{k}a+(1-\lambda_{k})b\bigr),

where the coefficients
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in[0,1]
and the weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
(usually positive) are fixed parameters. A well-known example is the
simple Simpson’s rule
:

\mathcal{S}[f;a,b]=\frac{b-a}{6}\biggl[f(a)+4f\biggl(\frac{a+b}{2}\biggr)+f(b)\biggr].

Every quadrature rule induces a
composite rule
, created by subdividing the interval
[a,b]
into
n
subintervals with equally spaced end-points
a=x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}=b
:

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\approx\sum_{k=1}^{n}Q[f;x_{k-1},x_{k}].

Let
\varepsilon>0
be a fixed tolerance. In most cases, a simple quadrature rule is insufficient, as
|Q[f;a,b]-\mathcal{I}[f;a,b]|>\varepsilon
. To achieve the desired precision, we apply the composite rule
Q_{n}
with
n
large enough so that:

|Q_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{I}[f;a,b]|<\varepsilon.

Such a method is called
adaptive
. To determine
n
for functions that are sufficiently regular, error bounds can be utilized. For instance, for the composite Simpson’s rule over
f\in C^{4}[a,b]
, the following estimation is widely used:

|\mathcal{S}_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{I}[f;a,b]|\leqslant\frac{(b-a)^{5}}{2880n^{4}}\sup\bigl\{|f^{(4)}(x)|:x\in[a,b]\bigr\}.

If the right-hand side is less than
\varepsilon
, a suitable
n
can be easily determined. However, this approach can be complicated due to difficulties in estimating the maximum of
|f^{(4)}|
, or even impossible if
f
lacks sufficient regularity.

Another approach to adaptive integration is based on the successive incrementation of
n
. Various
stopping criteria
exist in the literature. Lyness
[
7
]
proposed using local errors:

\varepsilon_{k}=\frac{1}{15}\biggl|\mathcal{S}\Bigl[f;x_{k},\frac{x_{k}+x_{k+1}}{2}\Bigr]+\mathcal{S}\Bigl[f;\frac{x_{k}+x_{k+1}}{2},x_{k+1}\Bigr]-\mathcal{S}[f;x_{k},x_{k+1}]\biggr|.

Another criterion, proposed by Clenshaw and Curtis
[
3
]
and investigated by Rowland and Varol
[
10
]
, uses the stopping inequality:

|\mathcal{S}_{2n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{I}[f;a,b]|\leqslant|\mathcal{S}_{2n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{S}_{n}[f;a,b]|,

valid for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and functions
f\in C^{4}[a,b]
with
f^{(4)}
of a constant sign. An interesting survey of such criteria was provided by Gonnet
[
4
]
(also available on the arXiv repository,
arXiv:1003.4629
).

Following these ideas, an adaptive method based on Simpson and Chebyshev quadratures was introduced in
[
12
]
:

C[f;a,b]=\frac{b-a}{3}\biggl[f\biggl(\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}a+\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{4}b\biggr)+f\biggl(\frac{a+b}{2}\biggr)+f\biggl(\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{4}a+\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}b\biggr)\biggr]

within the class of 3-convex functions. In this paper, we present a similar method for 5-convex functions. Numerical experiments show that it requires significantly fewer computations than the method described in
[
12
]
.

2.
Higher-order convexity

Given that our study focuses on
5
-convex functions belonging to the
C^{6}
class, it is appropriate to provide a concise overview of the general theory of higher-order convexity. The idea of examining functions characterized by non-negative divided differences was originally introduced by Hopf
[
5
]
. This line of research was further expanded by Popoviciu
[
8
,
9
]
, who explored the characteristics of this family of functions. According to his definition, a function
f
is classified as
n
-
convex
provided that

[x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1};f]\geqslant 0,

where the divided differences are determined through the recursive formula
[x_{0};f]=f(x_{0})
and

[x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1};f]=\frac{[x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1};f]-[x_{0},\dots,x_{n};f]}{x_{n+1}-x_{0}}.

Popoviciu established several fundamental properties of these mappings. Notably, he demonstrated that
n
-convexity on an interval
[a,b]
implies the existence of the
(n-1)
-th derivative on
(a,b)
. It should be noted, however, that while
x\mapsto x^{n}_{+}
(where
x_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
) is
n
-convex on
\mathbb{R}
, it lacks an
n
-th derivative at the origin. Furthermore, he proved (
[
8
, p. 18]
) that for odd
n
, the
n
-convexity is preserved under composition with affine transformations.

Another significant finding by Popoviciu is that the non-negativity of the
(n+1)
-th derivative on
[a,b]
is a sufficient condition for a function to be
n
-convex. Within this framework,
1
-convexity coincides with the standard definition of a convex function. Analogously,
f
is termed
n
-
concave
if the function
-f
satisfies the condition for
n
-convexity. Consequently, functions whose sixth derivative maintains a constant sign (as discussed earlier) are either
5
-convex or
5
-concave.

3.
Introduction

In this paper, we focus on the adaptive approximation of integrals of
5
-convex or
5
-concave functions of class
C^{6}[a,b]
, i.e., functions whose sixth derivative is continuous and has a constant sign on the interval of integration
[a,b]
. We will employ the
three-point Gauss quadrature

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]

\displaystyle=\frac{b-a}{18}\Biggl[5f\biggl(\frac{5+\sqrt{15}}{10}a+\frac{5-\sqrt{15}}{10}b\biggr)+8f\biggl(\frac{a+b}{2}\biggr)+5f\biggl(\frac{5-\sqrt{15}}{10}a+\frac{5+\sqrt{15}}{10}b\biggr)\Biggr]

and the
four-point Lobatto quadrature

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[f;a,b]

\displaystyle=\frac{b-a}{12}\Biggl[f(a)+5f\biggl(\frac{5+\sqrt{5}}{10}a+\frac{5-\sqrt{5}}{10}b\biggr)+5f\biggl(\frac{5-\sqrt{5}}{10}a+\frac{5+\sqrt{5}}{10}b\biggr)+f(b)\Biggr].

Bessenyei and Páles
[
2
]
proved that if a function
f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
is
5
-convex, then

(1)

\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]\leqslant\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\leqslant\mathcal{L}[f;a,b].

Together with Komisarski
[
6
, Theorem 5]
, we refined this inequality for
5
-convex functions of class
C^{6}[-1,1]
, proving that

(2)

0\leqslant\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)dx-\mathcal{G}[f;-1,1]\leqslant\mathcal{L}[f;-1,1]-\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)dx.

Note that this inequality can be immediately extended to any interval
[a,b]
. Let
f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a
5
-convex function of class
C^{6}[a,b]
. The affine transformation

(3)

t=\frac{2}{b-a}(x-a)-1,\quad x\in[a,b].

maps the interval
[a,b]
onto
[-1,1]
, and the function
g\colon[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

g(t)=\frac{b-a}{2}f\biggl(\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{b-a}{2}t\biggr)

is
5
-convex of class
C^{6}[-1,1]
, as
g^{(6)}
is continuous and non-negative on
[-1,1]
. Applying (
2
) to the function
g
, simple calculations lead to the inequality

0\leqslant\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx-\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]\leqslant\mathcal{L}[f;a,b]-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx.

From this, we immediately obtain:

Proposition 1
.

If the function
f\in C^{6}[a,b]
is
5
-convex, then

\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]\leqslant\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\leqslant\frac{\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]+\mathcal{L}[f;a,b]}{2}.

This serves as the starting point for our considerations.

4.
The Adaptive Method

Let

Q[f;a,b]=\frac{\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]+\frac{\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]+\mathcal{L}[f;a,b]}{2}}{2}=\frac{3}{4}\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]+\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{L}[f;a,b].

A direct consequence of this definition and Proposition
1
is the following corollary, which is central to the construction of our adaptive method.

Corollary 2
.

If the function
f\in C^{6}[a,b]
is
5
-convex, then

\biggl|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx-Q[f;a,b]\biggr|\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\mathcal{L}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]\bigr).

Proof.

If
\alpha\leqslant t\leqslant\beta
, then
\Bigl|t-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\Bigr|\leqslant\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}
(an easy geometric observation). It suffices to set
\alpha=\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]
and
\beta=\frac{\mathcal{G}[f;a,b]+\mathcal{L}[f;a,b]}{2}
.
∎

Inequality (
1
) was refined in Proposition
1
by taking the arithmetic mean of the quadratures
G[f;a,b]
and
\mathcal{L}[f;a,b]
. We will now show that taking a further arithmetic mean in the form of
Q[f;a,b]
does not inherently improve our results, as there is no fixed relation between the integral of a
5
-convex function and the quadrature
Q[f;a,b]
. To this end, let us consider two functions
f(x)=(x-0.6)^{7}_{+}
and
g(x)=(x-0.7)^{7}_{+}
. Since
(x^{n}_{+})^{\prime}=nx^{n-1}_{+}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geqslant 2
, it follows that
f,g\in C^{6}[-1,1]
. Their sixth derivatives are clearly non-negative, so
f,g
are
5
-convex. It can be computed that

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)dx

\displaystyle\approx 8.2\cdot 10^{-5},\quad Q[f;-1,1]\approx 7.0\cdot 10^{-5},

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}g(x)dx

\displaystyle\approx 8.2\cdot 10^{-6},\quad Q[g;-1,1]\approx 9.1\cdot 10^{-6}.

Thus,

\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)dx>Q[f;-1,1]\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{-1}^{1}g(x)dx<Q[f;-1,1].

If the interval
[a,b]
is relatively long, estimating the precision of the integral approximation
\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx
using quadrature
Q[f;a,b]
based on Corollary
2
may be imprecise. In such cases, as is standard in numerical analysis, we resort to
composite quadratures
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we divide the interval
[a,b]
into
n
subintervals of equal length with points
a=x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}=b
and define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}[f;x_{k-1},x_{k}],

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}[f;x_{k-1},x_{k}],

\displaystyle Q_{n}[f;a,b]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}Q[f;x_{k-1},x_{k}].

Error forms for the quadratures
\mathcal{G}[f;-1,1]
and
\mathcal{L}[f;-1,1]
for
f\in C^{6}[-1,1]
can be found in almost any numerical analysis textbook (for a quick reference, see e.g., Abramowitz, Stegun
[
1
, pp. 887, 888]
). By applying transformation (
3
), these can be rewritten for the interval
[a,b]
, and the errors of the composite quadratures can then be estimated. Thus, for
f\in C^{6}[a,b]
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx-\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]\biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(b-a)^{7}}{2016000\,n^{6}}\|f^{(6)}\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx-\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]\biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(b-a)^{7}}{1512000\,n^{6}}\|f^{(6)}\|_{\infty}

which implies that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx,

which in turn gives us

(4)

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\bigl|\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]\bigr|=0.

The following result is the heart of our method.

Theorem 3
.

If the function
f\in C^{6}[a,b]
is
5
-convex or
5
-concave, then

\biggl|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx-Q_{n}[f;a,b]\biggr|\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\bigl|\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]\bigr|.

Proof.

First, assume that
f\in C^{6}[a,b]
is
5
-convex. Given the construction of the quadratures
\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]
and
\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]
, by Corollary
2
we have:

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx-Q_{n}[f;a,b]\biggr|

\displaystyle=\biggl|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\biggl(\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k}}f(x)dx-Q[f;x_{k-1},x_{k}]\biggr)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\biggl|\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k}}f(x)dx-Q[f;x_{k-1},x_{k}]\biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(\mathcal{L}[f;x_{k-1},x_{k}]-\mathcal{G}[f;x_{k-1},x_{k}]\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]\bigr)=\frac{1}{4}\bigl|\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]\bigr|.

If the function
f
is
5
-concave, we apply the above inequality to the
5
-convex function
-f
. Taking into account the linearity of the integral and the operators
Q_{n},\mathcal{L}_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we arrive at:

\biggl|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx-Q_{n}[f;a,b]\biggr|\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]\bigr)=\frac{1}{4}\bigl|\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]\bigr|.

∎

From Theorem
3
, it immediately follows:

Corollary 4
.

If the function
f\in C^{6}[a,b]
is
5
-convex or
5
-concave and

\bigl|\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]\bigr|\leqslant 4\varepsilon,

then

\biggl|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx-Q_{n}[f;a,b]\biggr|\leqslant\varepsilon.

The above inequality will serve as the
stopping criterion
for our adaptive integration method for
5
-convex or
5
-concave functions of class
C^{6}[a,b]
. The algorithm proceeds in three steps:

1.

Take a function
f\in C^{6}[a,b]
that is either
5
-convex or
5
-concave.

2.

Set the desired approximation accuracy
\varepsilon>0
and look for
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\biggl|\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx-Q_{n}[f;a,b]\biggr|\leqslant\varepsilon.

Calculations begin with
n=1
.

3.

Increase
n
until the inequality
\bigl|\mathcal{L}_{n}[f;a,b]-\mathcal{G}_{n}[f;a,b]\bigr|\leqslant 4\varepsilon
is satisfied. Note that such an
n
exists due to (
4
), so the algorithm is guaranteed to terminate. By Corollary
4
, we obtain an approximation of the integral of
f
with the specified accuracy.
