Crystalloid fluids choices during cardiopulmonary bypass, outcomes of acid–base and lactate levels in cardio surgery
Authors: Mehdi Pabarjay, Rasoul Azarfarin, Alireza Alizadeh-Ghavidel, Seyed Hossein Hamidi, Hooman Bakhshandeh, Fazel Tarkhan, Shayan Alijanpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUltrafiltration is integral to cardiopulmonary bypass, yet the optimal replacement fluid for zero-balance ultrafiltration requires further investigation to guide clinical practice. This study aimed to compare the efficacy of Normal saline, Ringer’s solution, and Ringer’s lactate on lactate management, acid–base balance, and clinical outcomes.MethodsThis semi-experimental study enrolled 90 patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) into three groups of 30. In Group-R, Ringer’s solution was used as replacement fluid in Z-BUF. In Group-RL, Ringer’s lactate and in Group-NS, Normal saline was used. Lactate and pH levels were measured at five points: before CPB and after anesthesia induction, after cardioplegia, before starting Z-BUF, after Z-BUF and after CPB.ResultsWhile there was no significant difference in baseline lactate (T1), an increasing trend was observed up to T3. At T4, immediately after Z-BUF, lactate levels were significantly higher in Group-RL (3.5 ± 1.0 mEq/L) compared to Group-R (2.3 ± 1.0 mEq/L) and Group-NS (1.9 ± 0.6 mEq/L) (p < 0.05). By T5, lactate in Group-RL decreased, showing no significant difference between groups. There were no statistically significant differences in pH between groups at any time point. However, the magnitude of pH decrease from T3 to T4 was smaller in Group-RL (7.38 ± 0.05 to 7.37 ± 0.03) compared to Group-R (7.39 ± 0.05 to 7.35 ± 0.04) and Group-NS (7.41 ± 0.07 to 7.35 ± 0.05).ConclusionWhile Ringer's lactate demonstrated favorable pH stability, its clinical superiority in preventing significant acidosis or reducing bicarbonate requirements, and the implications for postoperative nursing monitoring and fluid management protocols, require validation in larger trials.

Introduction

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is a critical but non-physiological support system in cardiac surgery, associated with complications such as hemodilution, metabolic acidosis, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome [1, 2]. Ultrafiltration (UF) techniques, including zero-balance ultrafiltration (Z-BUF), are used near the end of CPB. In our institution, Z-BUF is a standard component of the CPB weaning protocol, employed to mitigate the systemic inflammatory response and correct fluid overload by removing inflammatory mediators and excess volume [3, 4]. A primary rationale for Z-BUF is volume and electrolyte management, including the reduction of elevated serum potassium. During Z-BUF, filtered volume is replaced with an equal volume of crystalloid solution [5]. The choice of replacement fluid presents a direct opportunity for the perfusionist and the surgical team, in collaboration with nursing staff responsible for postoperative hemodynamic and metabolic monitoring, to influence the patient's immediate metabolic state [6].

The choice of crystalloid—balanced (e.g., Ringer's, Ringer's lactate) versus unbalanced (0.9% Normal saline)—may influence acid–base homeostasis due to differences in chloride content and buffer precursors [7]. Normal saline, with a supraphysiologic chloride concentration (154 mmol/L), can induce hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis [8]. In contrast, Ringer's lactate contains lactate anions, which are metabolized in the liver to bicarbonate, offering a potential buffering capacity [9]. This hepatic conversion mechanism is a key physiological rationale for using buffer-containing solutions during periods of metabolic stress like CPB [10].

Given the routine use of Z-BUF, we conducted this study to compare the effects of three common crystalloids—Normal saline, Ringer's solution, and Ringer's lactate—as replacement fluids on perioperative lactate levels and acid–base balance. We hypothesized that Ringer's lactate would better stabilize pH during Z-BUF compared to the other solutions due to its lactate-bicarbonate conversion pathway. Furthermore, this investigation holds relevance for nursing science by examining a modifiable intraoperative factor (fluid selection) that may affect postoperative metabolic stability, thereby influencing the complexity and focus of nursing assessments and interventions in the critical care unit.

Methods

Study design and setting

This semi-experimental, single-center, parallel-group study was conducted at Shahid Rajaie Cardiovascular Medical and Research Institute. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. The protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (IR.RHC.REC.1398.008). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Patients aged 18–70 years with an ejection fraction > 30% scheduled for elective cardiac surgery requiring CPB were eligible. Exclusion criteria included: refusal of consent, previous cardiac surgery, pre-existing kidney disease (creatinine > 1.5 mg/dL), diabetes requiring intraoperative insulin, intraoperative diuretic use, and planned use of mechanical circulatory support (e.g., intra-aortic balloon pump, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation).

Sample size and randomization

The sample size was estimated to detect a clinically meaningful difference in serum lactate levels during zero-balance ultrafiltration. Based on pilot data, a difference (d) of 1.0 mEq/L with a standard deviation (σ) of 1.0 mEq/L was assumed. Using a standard formula for comparing means and adjusting for the non-randomized, repeated-measures design (k = 1.2), a minimum of approximately 19 participants per group was calculated for 80% power at a 95% confidence level. To enhance robustness and align with clinical feasibility, the sample size was rounded to 30 per group, resulting in a total of 90 participants.

In this non-randomized design, participant allocation to one of the three study groups was performed sequentially, guided by a pre-defined assignment protocol rather than randomization. To maintain consistency in administration, the prepared study fluids were provided to the perfusion team in standard, sequentially numbered packs.

Intervention and procedure

The decision to employ UF was made preoperatively by the surgical team. Standard anesthesia induction and CPB protocols were followed uniformly. Non-pulsatile flow was maintained throughout CPB. Mild to moderate hypothermia (32–34°C) was used. The CPB circuit was primed with a standardized solution of 1300 mL of Ringer's solution. The decision to employ UF was made preoperatively by the surgical team. Z-BUF was initiated after the release of the aortic cross-clamp during the rewarming phase, prior to separation from CPB, and continued for a standardized period targeting the removal of 20–25 mL/kg of ultrafiltrate. Heparin (300–400 U/kg) was administered to achieve an activated clotting time (ACT) ≥ 480 s. CPB was established using standard cannulation techniques. A hemofilter was connected to the CPB circuit via a passive shunt. Additional intraoperative data collected included cardiopulmonary bypass flow index, aortic cross-clamp time, and total operation time.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 24.0. Categorical variables are presented as counts [11] and were compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For repeated measures of lactate and pH over the five time points, a two-way mixed-model ANOVA (with time as the within-subjects factor and group as the between-subjects factor) was used, followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons where a significant interaction was found. For between-group comparisons at specific time points, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc test was employed. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committees of Shahid Rajaie Cardiovascular, Medical and Research Institute (IR.RHC.REC.1398.008). The study was conducted in full conformity with the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant during the preoperative visit, after a thorough explanation of the study's purpose and procedures. Confidentiality of participant data was maintained throughout the research process.

From phase T1 to T3, which is considered the initiation phase of the research, all groups were maintained under similar conditions. However, differences became apparent from phase T3 to T4, primarily due to variations in the compositions of the administered solutions. Notably, in the Ringer's Lactate group, an increase in serum lactate levels was observed during T4, which is expected and physiologically plausible given the presence of lactate in the solution. Based on the hepatic metabolism mechanism of lactate and its conversion to bicarbonate, a relative decrease in lactate levels was observed in T4. In T3, an arterial blood gas (ABG) sample was collected, followed by the initiation of Z-BUF using different solutions. After completing Z-BUF, another ABG sample was obtained at T4. Additionally, in T5, a final ABG sample was collected 20 min after the completion of Z-BUF to reassess the patient's condition.

Discussion

This study provides a detailed comparative analysis of three crystalloid solutions within a specific and standardized Z-BUF protocol, a technique routinely applied at our center during CPB weaning. While the use of Z-BUF varies across institutions, its targeted application for inflammatory mediator and fluid removal offers a controlled context to evaluate the isolated metabolic effects of different replacement fluids. The novelty of our work lies in its direct, prospective comparison of lactate and pH dynamics during this discrete procedural window, offering granular data that can inform precise fluid selection by the cardiac team. This study prospectively compared the effects of three crystalloid solutions used as Z-BUF replacement fluid on lactate and pH dynamics in adults undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB. The principal findings are: 1) Ringer's lactate caused a predictable, transient rise in serum lactate during Z-BUF (T4) that attenuated after CPB (T5); 2) No statistically significant differences in arterial pH were found between groups at any time point; and 3) Ringer's lactate was associated with a smaller numerical decrease in pH during the Z-BUF period compared to Ringer's solution and Normal saline.

The significant increase in lactate observed in the RL group at T4 is an expected pharmacological effect, directly attributable to the lactate content of the infused solution (approximately 28 mEq/L). This finding aligns with the basic pharmacology of the fluid and is not indicative of tissue hypoperfusion in this context [11]. This highlights the importance of monitoring lactate as a potential predictor of adverse outcomes, suggesting that even minor differences in lactate levels could have clinical implications.

All three groups demonstrated a rise in lactate levels up to the point of cardioplegia (Lat2), with Group-R reaching 2 mEq/L, Group-RL at 1.9 mEq/L, and Group-NS at 1.5 mEq/L. The lactate levels continued to rise to the initiation point of T3. By this stage, levels had risen to 2.4 mEq/L in Group-R, 2.1 mEq/L in Group-RL, and 1.8 mEq/L in Group-NS. The increase trend in Lactate levels from Lat1 to Lat3 showed a significant difference between Group-NS and the two Group-R and RL. Due to the conversion of lactate in Ringer's Lactate solution to bicarbonate in the liver, the patient is less prone to developing acidosis; this phenomenon is observable during phases T4 and T5.

During the Z-BUF procedure (from Lat3 to Lat4), a notable divergence emerged between Group-RL and the other two groups (R and NS). Lactate levels in Group-RL rose to 3.5 mEq/L, while Group-R and Group-NS showed smaller increases to 2.3 mEq/L and 1.9 mEq/L, respectively. Following the administration of Lat4, lactate levels increased in both the Ringer's (Group-R) and Normal Saline (NS) groups, while the lactate level in the Ringer's Lactate (Group-RL) group decreased. As a result, no significant differences were found among the three groups. In the study by Surabhi et al., it was noted that the mean bicarbonate levels in the Ringer's Lactate group decreased during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), dropping from 24.28 mEq/L at time point T1 to 20.98 mEq/L at time point T2. Additionally, the greatest increase in mean blood lactate levels occurred, rising from 0.85 mmol/L at T1 to 4.29 mmol/L at T2 and 4.17 mmol/L at T3. The mean strong ion difference also decreased during CPB in the Ringer's Lactate group, falling from 41.102 mEq/L at T1 to 35.66 mEq/L at T2, indicating the presence of metabolic acidosis [12]. In the Group-NS, considering the surgical course and the weaning from the pump, it is expected that the body's physiological response would involve an increase in serum lactate levels. In contrast, in the Ringer's Lactate group, due to the hepatic conversion of lactate to bicarbonate, a reduction in lactate levels postoperatively was observed.

Lactate levels in the three groups revealed a significant difference between Group-RL and the other two groups, likely due to the presence of lactate in the Ringer’s lactate solution. However, lactate is converted to bicarbonate ions over time, which explains the observed decrease from Lat4 to Lat5 in Group-RL. It is important to select crystalloid fluids during CPB based on their composition, potential effects on acid–base balance, and overall fluid management strategy. Although there were no significant differences in pH levels among the three groups, Group-RL exhibited less variation in pH levels, transitioning from pH 3 to pH 4 compared to Group-R and Group-NS. In the study by Surabhi et al., the Ringer's Lactate group experienced a statistically significant reduction in pH during CPB, decreasing from 7.428 at time point T1 to 7.335 and 7.358 at time points T2 and T3, respectively [12]. Alston and colleagues conducted a comparison between Ringer's solution and Ringer's lactate during the priming phase, concluding that there was a significant difference between the two groups, with the Ringer's group exhibiting a more pronounced decrease in pH [13]. In our study, while a decrease in pH was noted, this difference was not statistically significant. The use of Ringer's lactate as the replacement fluid resulted in smaller fluctuations in pH levels, likely due to the conversion of lactate to bicarbonate ions in the solution, as well as the concentration of chloride ions it contains. These two factors may contribute to a less acidic environment compared to Ringer’s solution and Normal saline. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the source of intraoperative lactate elevations. The transient rise with Ringer's lactate, a known pharmacological effect, must be distinguished from lactate accumulation due to hypoperfusion. This distinction is crucial for accurate clinical interpretation by intensive care nurses, preventing unnecessary alarm and guiding appropriate monitoring priorities. Furthermore, the trend toward greater pH stability with Ringer's lactate suggests a potential for a more predictable postoperative metabolic course, which could simplify nursing management and reduce the need for corrective interventions like bicarbonate administration.

This study has several important limitations. First, we did not measure strong ion difference, chloride levels, or bicarbonate administration, which are critical for a complete understanding of acid–base balance. Second, the study population excluded patients with significant hepatic or renal dysfunction; therefore, the results may not be generalizable to patients where lactate metabolism or fluid handling is impaired. Third, the single-center design and relatively small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings. However, the rigorous within-study protocol and detailed physiological measurements provide a solid foundation for hypothesis generation. Future multi-center studies with larger cohorts, including high-risk patients, and incorporating nursing-sensitive outcomes (e.g., time to metabolic stabilization, frequency of arterial blood gas analyses, incidence of arrhythmias) are warranted to confirm these findings and establish evidence-based guidelines for perioperative fluid management.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing CPB, using Ringer's lactate as a Z-BUF replacement fluid results in a transient, predictable increase in serum lactate that resolves after CPB. While no statistically significant differences in arterial pH were detected, Ringer's lactate was associated with less numerical pH fluctuation during the filtration period compared to Ringer's solution and Normal saline. These findings indicate that Ringer's lactate is a physiologically plausible option for Z-BUF. However, its definitive clinical superiority in preventing significant acidosis or reducing bicarbonate requirements remains unproven. Future larger trials should incorporate comprehensive electrolyte panels, strong ion difference analysis, and nursing-relevant clinical outcomes such as bicarbonate use, vasopressor requirements, and duration of mechanical ventilation to fully elucidate the impact of crystalloid choice on patient trajectory and resource utilization in the postoperative phase.
