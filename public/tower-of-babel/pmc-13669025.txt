Outcomes and adverse events of postoperative stereotactic interventional radiotherapy/brachytherapy for brain metastases: a systematic review
Authors: Mateusz Bilski, Federico Mastroleo, Izabela Baranowska, Katarzyna Konat-Bąska, Rupesh Kotecha, Ranjini Tolakanahalli, Paul Rogowski, Luca Tagliaferi, Bruno Fionda, Giulia Marvaso, Stefano Durante, Andrea Vavassori, Barbara Alicja Jereczek-Fossa, Mark R. Waddle, Umberto Ricardi, Jacek Fijuth, Łukasz Kuncman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postoperative radiotherapy is commonly used after resection of brain metastases, but durable local control with a low rate of adverse events (AEs) remains challenging. This systematic review summarizes outcomes and AEs of postoperative stereotactic interventional radiotherapy (S-IRT; brachytherapy) for resected brain metastases. PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched through June 30, 2024. No review protocol was registered. Eligible studies reported postoperative S-IRT after metastasis resection. Data were extracted independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Risk of bias and methodological quality were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale and MINORS. Because of clinical and methodological heterogeneity, results were synthesized narratively, and no meta-analysis was performed. Eighteen studies (9 prospective, 8 retrospective, 1 case series) including 542 patients were eligible. Eleven studies used Cs-131, six used I-125, and one used both. Prescribed doses ranged from 60 to 80 Gy, usually at a depth of 5 mm. One-year local control ranged from 83 to 100%. Symptomatic radionecrosis occurred in 4.6% of Cs-131 and 9% of I-125 implants; leptomeningeal disease rates were 1.6% and 12.5%, respectively. Postoperative S-IRT appears promising, but most studies were non-comparative and heterogeneous, so findings should be considered hypothesis-generating.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11547-026-02273-2.

Introduction

Annually, more than 350,000 cases of brain metastases (BM) are diagnosed worldwide [1, 2]. The incidence of synchronous brain metastases is as high as 10% in small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) and 16% in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), followed by melanoma and kidney cancer, each accounting for approximately 1.5% of cases [2]. Furthermore, metachronous BM develops in up to 50% and 20–30% of patients diagnosed with lung and breast cancer, respectively [3]. Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), fractionated radiotherapy (SFRT), and surgical resection are well-established treatment options for limited BM treatment [4, 5]. According to international guidelines, surgery is often chosen for patients with a single brain metastasis and well-controlled extracranial disease, or when there is no primary tumor diagnosis. This approach is also considered for patients with large metastases greater than 3–4 cm that are symptomatic, cystic, necrotic, or located in eloquent brain regions or the posterior fossa [6–9]. Surgery may benefit cases with CNS-specific molecular profiles or eligibility for immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy to reduce corticosteroid use [6, 7]. Although some studies suggest that en bloc resection may lead to lower rates of leptomeningeal disease (LMD) and fewer recurrences than piecemeal resection, there is no clear recommendation regarding which technique to choose [6, 7, 10]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that 48.1% (95% CI 41.2–58.9%) of patients experienced local recurrence within 1 year after resection [11]. This, along with data from randomized phase III trials, has established the standard for adjuvant stereotactic radiotherapy for resected brain metastases, which has been incorporated into many international guidelines [6–9]. The adjuvant use of SRS or SFRT improves local control (LC) rates to a median of 80.5% (range 60.5–91%) and results in less neurocognitive deterioration compared to whole brain radiotherapy (WBRT) [7–9]. However, several concerns remain with this approach.

One significant issue with SRS and SFRT is achieving a high LC rate in patients with large cavity volumes. A randomized phase III trial demonstrated that the one-year local recurrence rate can be as high as 40–46% when low doses of 12–14 Gy are administered[12], and larger planning target volumes (PTVs) exceeding 12 cm3 are associated with even poorer LC rates [13]. Additionally, irradiating larger target volumes may result in higher rates of radionecrosis (RN). The estimated one-year risk of RN exceeds 20%, with symptomatic cases in 10% (range 2–29%) of patients, even when a fractionated approach is used [14]. Another concern is the potential spread of LMD. Studies have shown that nodular leptomeningeal recurrence following postoperative stereotactic radiotherapy occurs in 14–28% of cases, with about 75% of affected patients ultimately succumbing to the disease [8, 15]. Another challenge lies in delineating the target volume, as surgical cavity sizes can change over time. The most pronounced changes occur within the first 4 weeks after surgery, and cavity size may decrease or increase by up to 40% [16]. This variability can result in misinterpretations, target misses, and higher irradiation volumes in normal brain tissue, contributing to lower LC rates and increased occurrences of RN [14].

These challenges have prompted the search for innovative solutions to increase the dose in the target area while stabilizing the irradiated volume and potentially reducing the risk of radionecrosis. One promising advancement in radiotherapy for patients with resected brain metastases is stereotactic interventional radiotherapy (S-IRT)/brachytherapy. The adoption of this technique is increasing due to its potential to meet the aforementioned needs, although it has not yet been incorporated into international guidelines. A recent systematic review summarized the outcomes and AEs of interventional stereotactic radiotherapy/brachytherapy for unresected brain metastases [17]. However, postoperative S-IRT delivered to the resection cavity presents a distinct clinical scenario, with different considerations regarding target definition, dose distribution, AE profile, and recurrence patterns.

This systematic review aimed to evaluate the outcomes and potential AEs of different postoperative S-IRT techniques for brain metastases.

Materials and methods

Study design

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design (PICOS) framework was employed to formulate the research question and guide the search strategy. The primary aim was to identify studies assessing brachytherapy/interventional radiotherapy as an adjuvant therapy following resection of brain metastases. No review protocol was registered in PROSPERO or in any other prospective registry; the eligibility criteria, the outcomes of interest, and the approach to data synthesis were nevertheless defined a priori, before screening commenced. Because substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity was anticipated across the eligible literature—in particular a predominance of single-arm designs and marked variability in radioisotope, dose prescription, and outcome definitions—a narrative synthesis was planned from the outset, and no meta-analysis was attempted. A comprehensive search was conducted in four major databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science). In addition, the reference lists of selected articles were reviewed to identify any further relevant studies. The search included articles published through June 30, 2024.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was employed using relevant keywords, acronyms, and synonyms. Terms including “brachytherapy” and “interventional radiotherapy” were combined with “brain,” “central nervous system,” and “intracranial.” The term “metastasis” and its variants were also included to ensure broad coverage of metastatic conditions. Controlled vocabulary was used where available (MeSH terms in PubMed and exploded Emtree terms in Embase). Studies that included only primary brain tumors (e.g., gliomas or meningiomas) were excluded to focus exclusively on secondary malignancies. The full Boolean search string executed in each database, together with the platform searched, the date of the search, and the number of records retrieved, is reported in Online Resource 1.

Study selection

All retrieved articles were imported into Rayyan’s reference management tool [18], where duplicates were removed before screening. Three independent reviewers conducted the selection process in two stages. First, titles and abstracts were screened to exclude studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Next, the full texts of potentially eligible articles were examined using the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The screening and selection processes were carried out blind to reduce the risk of bias. Following the PICOS framework, retrospective or prospective clinical trials with published results in English were included (Fig. 1). Studies were excluded at full-text review if they addressed primary brain tumors, non-postoperative settings, did not provide extractable clinical outcome data for resected brain metastases, were non-original articles, or lacked accessible full text.Fig. 1Summary flow chart of steps taken during systematic review preparation

Risk of bias assessment

Two complementary instruments were applied because the included studies were heterogeneous in design and no single instrument is appropriate to all of them. Both instruments were applied independently by two reviewers, and any disagreement was resolved by a third reviewer. First, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies was applied [19]. Fifteen of the 18 included studies were single-arm, and two NOS items cannot be earned in the absence of a comparator group: selection of the non-exposed cohort (1 point) and comparability of cohorts on the basis of the design or analysis (2 points). For these studies, both items were recorded as not applicable rather than scored as 0, and the maximum attainable score was 6 rather than 9; NOS totals for single-arm and comparative studies are therefore not directly comparable. To make the two follow-up domains reproducible, we specified in advance that “was follow-up long enough for outcomes to occur” would be awarded where the reported median follow-up was at least 12 months, or where a protocol-specified follow-up schedule covered the horizon of the stated primary endpoint, and that “adequacy of follow-up” would be awarded where follow-up was complete, where fewer than 5% of participants were lost, or where those lost were individually described.

The case series by Schulder et al. was not appraised with the NOS, which is not designed for that study design. Second, because the NOS cannot discriminate between non-comparative studies on the dimensions that most constrain this literature, all 18 studies were additionally appraised using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) [20]. The first eight MINORS items were applied to the 16 non-comparative studies (maximum 16 points), and the four additional items were applied to the two comparative studies (maximum 24 points), each item being scored 0 (not reported), 1 (reported but inadequate), or 2 (reported and adequate). MINORS has no validated categorical thresholds for classifying studies as high or low quality; scores are therefore reported descriptively, as absolute values and as a proportion of the maximum attainable, and are deliberately not dichotomized into quality bands. Both appraisals are presented in full, with the supporting passage underlying every item-level judgment, in Online Resources 2 and 3.

Results

A total of 1,183 studies were identified through database searches, and after duplicate removal and the first screening phase, 75 reports were sought for full-text screening. Citation searching led to placing an additional record, bringing the total number of reports to 76. Of these, 75 were assessed for eligibility, and 18 studies were included in the final review after excluding ineligible studies. Risk of bias analysis

Both appraisals are reported in full in Online Resources 2 and 3. Across the applicable NOS domains, the 15 single-arm cohorts scored 4 to 6 out of 6 (median 5), and the two comparative cohorts scored 8 of 9 [24] and 7 of 9 [33]. The representativeness of the cohort, the ascertainment of exposure, and the assessment of outcome were adequately addressed in every study. The two follow-up domains were not: follow-up was judged long enough for the reported outcomes to occur in 9 of the 17 cohort studies, and adequacy of follow-up could be confirmed in only 6, because most reports did not state how many participants were lost to follow-up or describe those who were. These high scores on the applicable domains reflect adequate reporting of cohort definition, exposure ascertainment, and outcome assessment; they do not reflect the presence of a comparator group and should not be interpreted as indicating high-certainty evidence.

MINORS scores in the 16 non-comparative studies ranged from 4 to 14 of a maximum of 16 (median 9, corresponding to 56% of the maximum attainable), and were 18 of 24 [24] and 14 of 24 [33] in the two comparative studies. Three domains were deficient across almost the entire evidence base: a prospective calculation of the study size was reported in only 1 of 18 studies [36], loss to follow-up below 5% was documented in only 2 of 18, and assessment of the primary endpoint independent of, or blinded to, the treating team was reported in only 4 of 18. Data were collected prospectively in 6 of 18 studies. Taken together with the absence of a comparator group in 16 of the 18 studies, these findings indicate a body of evidence of moderate methodological quality that remains susceptible to selection bias, outcome-ascertainment bias, and attrition bias, and that does not support causal inference regarding the comparative effectiveness of S-IRT.

Study and patient characteristics

Among the 18 studies included in the analysis, eight were retrospective [21–28] primarily conducted at a single institution [21–24, 26–28]. The only exception was the analysis by Dagnew et al. [25], which involved data from two centers. Schulder et al. conducted a case series study [29]. Most retrospective studies lacked a control group [21–23, 25–28], except for Julie et al. [24], who compared patients with BM treated with S-IRT with those treated with SRS. Nine studies were prospective [30–38], with seven being single-institution, single-arm studies [27, 30, 32–35, 37]. Rogers et al. [36] was a multi-institutional Phase II study, while Imber et al. [31] conducted a prospective registry. Notably, 11 of these studies were conducted before 2015 and included data more than 10 years old [21, 22, 24, 25, 27–30, 36–38]. In contrast, only three studies recruited patients within the last five years [31, 32, 34]. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria were extracted for each study. The total number of patients included across the studies was 542 (280 women and 262 men), with individual study populations ranging from 9 [26] to 95 [28], yielding an average of 30 patients. The median age of participants ranged from 47.5 [29] to 65 years [28, 30, 38]. Adjuvant tumor bed S-IRT was performed in 583 cases. In nine studies, resection with subsequent S-IRT of more than one tumor bed area was permitted [22, 23, 28, 30–33, 35, 37]. Only three studies reported postoperative cavity volumes. Kutuk et al. [32] reported a median cavity volume of 3.23 cm3, and in Dharnipragada et al. [34] it was 12.28 cm3. Wernicke et al. reported a median resected tumor volume of 10.31 cm3 [38]. Other studies provided only preoperative tumor volumes [21, 28, 31, 36] or diameters [21–27, 30, 33, 35, 37]. The Schulder et al. study did not report data regarding the size of the metastatic lesions or the postoperative cavity. All studies involved multi-histology BMs, except Chen et al., which additionally included primary tumors [23]. non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) was the most common, accounting for 254 cases (44%). The second most common were breast cancer and melanoma, each with 76 cases (13%), followed by renal cell carcinoma (RCC) with 21 cases (3.6%). In five studies, only radiation-naive BMs were included [21, 24, 25, 36, 38], while four studies focused solely on recurrent BMs [29, 31–33]. Wu et al. analyzed multiple recurrent BMs [33]. The remaining studies included a mixed population. Baseline KPS was reported in nine studies [21, 22, 24, 25, 29, 32, 35, 36, 38]. Only four studies reported using active systemic treatment perioperatively [23, 27, 32, 33]. The reported median baseline KPS values were as follows: 70 [35], 80 [22], and 90 [21, 25, 29, 36]. A KPS of less than 70 was reported in only four studies [24, 28, 29, 31] and accounted for a small percentage of the total population [24, 28]. The median follow-up time ranged from 5 months [27] to 6.1 years [22]. Treatment technique and dosimetry

The detailed characteristics of the radioisotope type, implantation technique, source activity, and S-IRT dose—including dose prescription and specifications—are presented in Table 2. Among the selected studies, cesium-131 (Cs-131) seeds were used in 11 studies [23, 24, 26, 27, 30–32, 34, 35, 37, 38], while iodine−125 (I-125) isotopes were utilized in 6 studies [21, 22, 25, 28, 29, 36]. Wu et al. initially performed S-IRT seed implantation with I-125 before transitioning to Cs-13133. Most studies reported using seed implantation techniques; specifically, four studies [31, 32, 34, 35] employed GammaTile®, a brachytherapy carrier embedded with Cs-131 seeds, while one study used GliaSite RTS, an inflatable variable-volume balloon catheter designed for afterloading a liquid I-125 solution [36]. In total, 17 studies administered permanent radioactive seed applications. In contrast, Rogers et al. employed a temporary GliaSite balloon catheter filled with an aqueous I-125 solution, which was removed after the prescribed radiation dose was delivered [36]. Almost every study included a comprehensive section on reference doses, dose prescriptions, and dose specifications. The reference doses varied widely, ranging from 43 [29] to 400 Gy [30]. However, most studies prescribed doses of either 60 Gy [31, 32, 34–36] or 80 Gy [23, 24, 27, 30, 37, 38]. In most cases, the prescribed dose was aimed at a depth of 5 mm from the resection cavity [21–27, 30–35, 37, 38]. Two studies prescribed doses to a depth of 1 cm [29, 36]. Clinical outcomes and salvage therapy

Across studies reporting outcomes in newly diagnosed and/or recurrent brain metastases, 1-year LC ranged from 83 to 100% [27, 32, 36, 38]. Two studies precisely described the difference in 1-year LC between ndBMs and rBMs, with 1-year LC for rBMs and ndBMs ranging from 75 to 86% and 92–100%, respectively [22, 35]. For I-125-based S-IRT, 1-year LC ranged from 38.5 to 96% [21, 22, 28, 29, 33, 36]. In the Cs-131-based S-IRT, 1-year LC ranged from 83.3 to 100% [23, 24, 26, 27, 30–32, 34, 35, 37, 38], with four reports reporting 1.5-year LC of 88.8% to 100% [23, 24, 32, 38]. Wernicke et al. also analyzed the pattern of LCRC, defined as the range of 5–20 mm from the resection cavity [27, 37]. A better 1-year LCRC could be achieved after S-IRT of ndBMs vs. rBMs, accounting for 55.6% and 89%, respectively [27, 37]. In the total cohort from the included studies, local failures were more frequent after I-125 vs. Cs-131-based S-IRT and were diagnosed in 30 (12.5%) and 20 (7%) patients, respectively. One study evaluating both I-125 and Cs-131 reported a lower LC rate of 61.5% at a median follow-up of 11.3 months. Notably, local recurrences occurred within the 50% isodose line, highlighting the importance of treatment planning [33]. In the Chen et al. study, all local recurrences (2 cases) and locoregional recurrence within 1 cm of the cavity (1 case) appeared after subtotal resection [23]. The distant intracranial control (DIC) rates varied, ranging from 30% at 14.5 months [32] to 95.83% at 1 year [21]. DIC rates were slightly lower in the rBMs cohort, with a difference of 3% reported [22, 28]. Five studies provided insights into the time to distant intracranial failure (DIF) [22, 27, 30, 32, 37]. The median DIF time ranged from 2.5 [32] to 12.5 months [37]. Only one study analyzed the difference in DIF between rBMs and ndBMs; 6.9 months and 9.5 months were reported, respectively [30]. The median OS ranged from 7.3 [22] to 23 months, with the highest value observed in patients with Recursive Partitioning Analysis (RPA) class 1 [21]. One study analyzed the difference between ndBMs and rBMs, reporting a better OS of 12 months vs. 7.3 months [22]. Of the reviewed papers, nine reported the use of local salvage therapies in 4% to 37.5% of patients following S-IRT failure [21, 23–25, 28, 30, 31, 35, 36].

Procedure-related complications, AEs, and quality of life

Sixteen selected manuscripts provided valuable insights into procedure-related complications [21, 24–29, 31–38], with one manuscript noting a lack of detail on the timing of symptom onset [23]. Encouragingly, three reports documented the absence of any procedure-related complications [26, 35, 36]. Notably, no fatal procedure-related complications were reported in most manuscripts [21–26, 28, 30–38]. However, Schulder et al. did identify two fatal complications linked to the surgical aspect of the procedure, associated explicitly with Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) and embolism [29]. Wernicke et al. reported a case where a patient with a history of seizures passed away from aspiration pneumonia two weeks after surgery [27]. Meanwhile, 15 reports indicated procedure-related complications affecting 3.8% to 20% of patients in smaller cohorts. Wound infections were the most common issue, with incidence rates ranging from 1.38 to 8.3% [24, 28, 30, 31, 34, 37, 38]. Wernicke et al. specifically highlighted three infections observed at a rate of 23.1% among heavily pretreated patients with histories of multiple craniotomies and brain radiation; one of these patients had HIV with a CD4+ count below 500 before surgery [27]. Two reports documented the resolution of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism in six patients, with incidence rates ranging from 3.8 to 7% [25, 30]. Additionally, early-onset seizures were identified in three studies, affecting three patients with rates from 3.3 to 5% [24, 31, 38]. In two additional manuscripts, late-onset seizures were reported among seven patients, with incidence rates between 7.7 and 14% [27, 37]. Pseudomeningocele was documented in only four patients, with rates ranging from 7.1 to 20%, typically accompanied by mild headaches that resolved within 1 to 2 weeks [27, 32, 33]. One of these cases required administration of bevacizumab [34]. In total, 36 patients (6.64%) experienced symptomatic radiation necrosis (sRN) [21–25, 29, 31–33, 35, 36]. Notably, six studies reported no cases of sRN (0%), with five using Cs-131 [26, 27, 34, 37, 38] and one using I-125 [28]. The incidence of sRN varied, with rates between 2.38 and 7.7% [21, 23–25, 33], 10% to 12.5% for Cs-131 [32, 35], 15% to 16.7% for I-125 [29, 36], and 20% to 23% [22, 31]. Specifically, the incidence of sRN was 9% for I-125 applications and 4.6% for Cs-131 applications. Six studies provided valuable data on potential LMD, noting that 6 patients were affected, with incidence rates ranging from 0 [26, 32, 34, 35] to 4.2% for Cs-131 [38], and reaching 12.5% for the I-125 application [22]. The observed rates for LMD with Cs-131 and I-125 were 1.6% and 12.5%, respectively. Moreover, seven studies assessed patients’ neurologic conditions at baseline and during follow-up [21, 25, 30, 32, 34, 36, 37]. Three studies utilized Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scores [21, 25, 32], while one report employed an unspecified scale for neurologic cognition evaluation [34]. Furthermore, three reports used more detailed assessments, including the Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE) and the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Brain (FACT-BR) scales [30, 36, 37]. Most studies reported improved neurologic conditions [30, 37] or noted that conditions remained improved or stable [21, 25, 34]. One study documented a slight median decline of 10 points in KPS at the last follow-up [32], while another noted an 18-point drop in the FACT-BR score after one year [36].

Discussion

This systematic review provides an updated overview of the efficacy and AEs of S-IRT following resection of brain metastases. A prior systematic review conducted in 2020 aggregated data from studies on both intact and surgical cavity irradiation, encompassing publications up to January 2018. Since then, eight additional studies have emerged [23, 24, 26, 31–35]. It is noteworthy that the majority of contemporary research on this topic employs prospective designs, thereby enhancing the quality and robustness of the evidence available [23, 31–34]. Various treatment techniques have been refined based on prior clinical experiences. These techniques include the direct placement of I-125 seeds into the resection cavity [21, 22, 25, 28, 29], the implementation of balloon-based systems such as the GliaSite RTS [36], and barrel-like applications of Cs-131-connected strands [23, 24, 26, 27, 30, 33, 37, 38]. The most recent innovation involves using a collagen carrier containing Cs-131 seeds [31, 32, 34, 35], which are positioned within the cavity to accommodate potential shrinkage of the resection site over time. Earlier investigations involving permanent I-125-based S-IRT indicated an actuarial risk of developing sRN of 12% at 1 year post-treatment, 33% at 2 years, 60% at 3 years, and 87% at 4 years, illustrating an escalating risk that correlates with patient longevity [21, 22]. Regarding the incidence of sRN, rates as high as 23% have been reported following direct implantation of I-125 seeds, with a prescribed median dose of 400 Gy at 5 mm [22]. Challenges arose in maintaining seed positioning as the cavity volume decreased, leading to increased radiation exposure [22]. In light of these challenges, Raleigh et al. modified their technique to involve the end-on insertion of I-125 sources into brain tissue, employing Tisseel (Baxter) to secure the sources and reduce migration, with the aim of improving local control rates [28]. In our review, local failures following I-125-based S-IRT were documented in 30 patients, representing an occurrence rate of 12.5%. Furthermore, we observed that the overall incidence of sRN from I-125 applications was 9%, a notable improvement over previous reports. This enhanced understanding of sRN incidence underscores the necessity for ongoing research and the refinement of treatment modalities within this specialty.

Strategies to reduce the incidence of RN include lowering the total brachytherapy dose to less than 60 Gy and/or prescribing treatment to a depth of less than 1 cm[36]. Our analysis has demonstrated that improved implantation techniques and enhanced experience with dose prescription effectively reduce the risk of sRN, especially when utilizing I-125 sources. It is also critical to consider that the risk of RN, particularly associated with I-125 sources, is significantly elevated in patients with rBMs, reaching 25% compared to only 7% for ndBMs (p < 0.005) [28]. The most plausible explanation for this discrepancy is the compounded risk of AEs resulting from prior irradiation. It is important to note that many reports included in our analysis lacked information on the necessity of subsequent surgeries or on histopathological confirmation of RN. Furthermore, one must consider that older diagnostic modalities, such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), may result in false-positive diagnoses of RNs. The first prospective report detailing cesium-131 (Cs-131) permanent stranded seed implantation, using an activity of 3–5 mCi, was published in 2014 [38]. The 10-cm suture-stranded Cs-131 seeds, spaced at intervals of 0.5 cm, were deployed in strings containing ten seeds each. These strings were arranged within the cavity similarly to barrel staves, exerting outward pressure to prevent tangential collapse and maintain a spacing of 7 to 10 mm between seeds. The use of Tisseel (Baxter) was then implemented to line the cavity, thereby limiting cavity shrinkage and further preventing seed dislodgement [27, 37, 38].

The innovative approach of positioning Cesium-131 (Cs-131) seeds within a collagen carrier known as the GammaTile, developed by GT Medical Technologies (Tempe, AZ), was then presented [35]. This device facilitates maintenance of intersource spacing while preventing direct contact between the seeds and brain tissue [31, 32, 34, 35]. Cs-131 sources are symmetrically arranged at 1-cm intervals from both superior and inferior viewpoints; however, they exhibit asymmetric spacing concerning source depth, with a 0.3 cm offset from the brain and a 0.1 cm offset from the inner cavity side. Furthermore, the 3 mm offset between the seeds and the tile surface mitigates the potential for elevated radiation doses that would result if the seeds were placed directly in contact with the brain. The GammaTile minimizes postclosure source shifting, thereby reducing the risk of localized overdose areas, referred to as “hot spots” due to source clumping and areas of underdose, designated as “cold spots" [31, 32, 34, 35]. Some researchers have employed a ratio of 1 seed per cm[2] of surface area to estimate the requisite number of seeds, calculating the cm[2] area based on preoperative MRI results [35]. This methodology was later challenged by Imber et al., who reported a weak correlation between preoperative tumor geometry and the implanted tiles, underscoring the need to optimize planning criteria [31]. Notably, the median time for GammaTile implantation is reported to be between 2 and 5 min, in contrast to the implantation of permanent I-125 sources, which extends the procedure duration by approximately 30 min [30–32, 34]. Overall, the implantation procedure is classified as “very complex” according to the Complexity Index of Interventional Radiotherapy (brachytherapy) Implants (COMIRI) scale [1].

In our analysis, the 1-year LC rates for I-125 ranged from 38.5 to 96% [21, 22, 25, 28, 29, 33, 36], while for Cs-131, the LC ranged from 83.3 to 100% [23, 26, 27, 30–32, 34, 35, 37, 38]. Furthermore, four reports documented a 1.5-year LC of 88.8% to 100% for Cs-131-based S-IRT [23, 24, 32, 38]. It is noteworthy that high local control rates can also be attained in larger brain metastases, with volumes reaching up to 76 cm3, exhibiting a 14.4-month LC of 90% [28]. The incidence of local failures was higher following I-125 than Cs-131-based S-IRT, with diagnoses in 30 patients (12.5%) and 20 patients (7%), respectively.

According to the findings from the RTOG 90–05 trial, the application of SRS to tumors with diameters ranging from 2.1 to 3.0 cm is correlated with a 7.3-fold increase in the risk of severe, life-threatening, or fatal central nervous system (CNS) AEs when compared to the treatment of tumors measuring 2.0 cm or smaller. For tumors measuring 3.1–4.0 cm, the risk increases up to 16-fold. Additionally, RN rates may reach up to 50% after repeat SRS for rBMs [26]. The use of stereotactic fractionated radiotherapy (SFRT) could mitigate these two issues by safely delivering radiation in smaller fractional doses [8, 39]. Our analysis revealed that sRN occurred in only 36 patients (6.64%) [21–25, 29, 31–33, 35, 36]. Notably, six studies reported no instances of sRN (0%), particularly when Cs-131 was utilized. The incidence of sRN was 9% for I-125 and 4.6% for Cs-131 applications. Contrary to previously reported data on SRS, our analysis found no correlation between RN risk and treatment volume.

Dagnew et al. identified two cases (3.8%) of histopathologically confirmed RN that manifested 6 and 9 months post-surgery, presenting initially with headache and focal neurological symptoms [25]. Moreover, it was suggested that patients with brain metastases exceeding 3 cm and receiving more than 40 mCi of total radiation activity may possess an elevated potential for RN occurrence when permanent I-125 seeds are implanted [25]. In the initial report using stranded Cs-131 sources, including treatment of radiation-naive metastases, 0% of RN cases were documented [38]. This report reported a lower seed activity of 3.82 mCi per seed and prescribed a dose of 80 Gy [38]. For radiorecurrent tumors, only one case of aRN was reported, with a slightly higher seed activity of 3.01 U, which subsequently decreased to 2.25 U, after which no RN was observed [27]. In a subsequent prospective cohort study involving tumors with a median size of 3 cm (2–6.8 cm), a RN rate of 0% was also noted [37]. It was proposed that a combination of lower seed activity and a reduced prescription dose might further decrease the incidence of RN while having minimal impact on LC [27]. In another prospective study utilizing the Gliasite delivery system with Cs-131 in a cohort of large tumors averaging 3.2 cm, only two RNs occurred, both of which resolved after 2 and 4 weeks of dexamethasone administration [35]. In the context of SRS radiorecurrent BMs, Gammatile-based S-IRT reported 8 (40%) instances of RN, with 4 (20%) classified as symptomatic [31]. Furthermore, Kutuk et al. detailed one case (8%) of sRN occurring 7 months post Gammatile-based treatment in a patient who had received prior EBRT and S-IRT, with BED exceeding 90%, 95%, and a maximum dose of 176.6 Gy, 155.8 Gy, and 1227.7 Gy, respectively [32]. Nakaji et al., in their cohort comprising large and rBMs, documented two cases (12.5%) of RN classified as Grade 2 and Grade 3 according to the CTCAE v. 5.0, both of which resolved after 2 and 4 weeks of dexamethasone administration [35]. In another prospective study involving large brain metastases measuring up to 6.8 cm, a RN rate of 0% was reported [37]. Moreover, Xia et al. reported a 0% RN rate in their reirradiation cohort when using Cs-131 in S-IRT [26].

In terms of dosimetry, Cs-131 exemplifies superior dose distributions and clinical outcomes compared to I-125. The principal distinction between these two radioisotopes lies in their markedly different half-lives: Cs-131 has a half-life that is six times shorter than that of I-125. This characteristic facilitates more rapid and precise delivery of the therapeutic dose [31–33, 35]. One of the challenges encountered in the treatment of brain metastases following surgical resection pertains to the remodeling of the surgical cavity. The shrinkage of the postoperative resection bed can significantly affect the spatial distribution of the dose, which in turn impacts treatment efficacy. As the resection cavity contracts over time, seeds may be positioned closer together, potentially leading to suboptimal dosimetric coverage, characterized by “hot spots” and increased exposure of healthy brain tissue to radiation, thereby increasing the risk of RN. Notably, due to the shorter half-life of Cs-131, 90% of the intended dose is delivered to the cavity within one month, whereas I-125 delivers merely one-third of the dose in the same timeframe. This provides a distinct advantage for patients, particularly those with larger cavities, compared with I-125 sources [24, 28, 32].

In the reviewed literature, there was no direct association between S-IRT and wound complication rates, nor could such complications be definitively linked to S-IRT. The rapid dose fall-off from brachytherapy sources, in accordance with the inverse-square law, is generally adequate to mitigate the majority of wound complications in patients with brain metastases. In cases where local dura or skin conditions suggest compromised healing, the use of pedicle-based pericranial flaps may be considered [28]. Moreover, it has been suggested that the likelihood of wound infection may be increased in patients with a history of prior infections at the same site, necessitating a more cautious approach [34]. The STaRT multi-histology registry (NCT04427384) has enrolled over 250 patients across 33 centers, revealing that rates of wound breakdown, cerebral edema, readmission, and infection are comparable to those observed after craniotomy without GammaTile-based S-IRT [31, 40, 41]. Infection rates across all cases were approximately 3%, which aligns with data reported specifically for craniotomy [42]. In our analysis, the median postoperative hospital stay among most studies averaged 3 days, with a range of 2–10 days.

Our analysis identified six reports that presented potential LMD rates, with incidence ranging from 0 [26, 32, 34, 35] to 4.2% for Cs-131 [38] and as high as 12.5% for the I-125-based S-IRT [22]. The observed rates for LMD using Cs-131 and I-125 were 1.6% and 12.5%, respectively. Huang et al. reported that among the five patients (13%) who developed LMD, the interval from implantation, as detected through imaging or positive cytology, ranged from 2.0 to 8.2 months, with a median of 3.4 months. All patients underwent resection of supratentorial brain metastases [22]. Wernicke et al. documented a single case diagnosed seven months postimplantation [38]. It is important to note that, similar to the trial conducted by Julie et al., LMD was categorized as regional failure; this approach may have been used in earlier reports included in this analysis [24]. Although our systematic review demonstrated a low risk of LMD, S-IRT remains limited compared with SRS due to its invasive nature in both the radical and adjuvant settings. Additionally, comprehensive coverage of regions at risk of recurrence, such as meningeal margins and venous sinuses—areas specifically recommended for inclusion by recent SRS guidelines—is technically challenging with brachytherapy, potentially increasing the risk of local recurrence and LMD [8].

From a radiobiological perspective, low-dose-rate brachytherapy aims to deliver continuous radiation at rates of 0.3 to 3.5 Gy/h, in contrast to SRS, which administers higher dose rates of 1.5 to 10 Gy/min, typically within one hour. The continuous nature of low-dose-rate irradiation facilitates tissue reoxygenation during treatment, thereby increasing radiosensitivity [37]. We identified only one matched-pair study comparing outcomes and AEs between Cs-131 S-IRT and SRS in patients with resected brain metastases.[24]. The local recurrence (LR) rate was significantly lower with S-IRT than with SRS, at 10% versus 28.3% (OR 0.281, 95% CI 0.082–0.949; p = 0.049). Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank testing revealed a significantly higher likelihood of freedom from LR (p = 0.027) and distant recurrence (p = 0.018) following S-IRT compared with SRS (p = 0.027), although no significant difference in OS was observed (p = 0.093). Additionally, S-IRT was associated with a threefold lower risk of radiation necrosis development, at 3.3% versus 10% (p = 0.417) [24]. Although that study suggested favorable local control and AE outcomes with S-IRT, these findings should be interpreted with caution because most included studies were heterogeneous single-arm cohorts and no randomized comparative data are currently available.

Additional concerns arise in the treatment of rBMs, where salvage SRS may be less effective for larger cavities and carry a higher risk of radionecrosis. It has been noted that in the context of rBMs following salvage SRS, a volume of the brain receiving a dose greater than 12 Gy (V12 Gy>9 cc) serves as a predictive factor for sRN[43]. A recent study presented a comprehensive dosimetric comparison between S-IRT and SRS plans utilizing a regimen of 30 Gy delivered in 5 fractions. The comparisons included GammaKnife (GK), CyberKnife (CK), and Intensity Modulated Proton Therapy (IMPT) versus S-IRT. Recognized risk factors for the development of RN include the biologically effective dose at 10 Gy (BED10Gy) for 95% and 90%, as well as volumetric doses (V8 3 Gy, V10 3 Gy, V12 3 Gy, and V24 3 Gy) to normal brain tissue. In the analysis, both 0 mm and 5 mm expansion margins were applied to the clinical target volume. For the 0 mm margin, the median BED10Gy 90% for S-IRT was considerably higher than for CK (p = 0.04), GK (p = 0.005), and IMPT (p = 0.003). Conversely, when using a 5 mm margin, the median BED10Gy 90% for S-IRT was significantly lower than both CK and IMPT, with no significant difference compared with GK. Furthermore, the median V24 3 Gy was significantly reduced with S-IRT compared with all other modalities (p < 0.01). These dosimetric findings are encouraging, but they should be interpreted cautiously and not be considered a substitute for prospective comparative clinical evidence [44]. This caution is further supported by recent literature showing substantial heterogeneity in the definitions and technical requirements of stereotactic radiotherapy, despite its widespread use, as well as marked international variability in SBRT practice and implementation [4, 5].

Previous reports indicated a local recurrence rate of 40% at 1 year following resection of rBMs, with a trend toward improved yet still suboptimal LC when comparing adjuvant SRS reirradiation with observation (29% vs. 44%, p = 0.07) [45]. Moreover, the timing of salvage reirradiation after resection has been shown to be critical for achieving local control. Local failure rates of 2.3%, 14.5%, and 48.5% were observed when salvage SRS was initiated before 4 weeks, between 4 and 8 weeks, and after 8 weeks, respectively, following surgical intervention [46]. This underscores the additional benefit of the S-IRT approach, in which irradiation commences immediately after implantation.

The neurocognitive function of patients, as well as their quality of life, are critical factors to consider in the selection of a radiation therapy modality for surgical cavity irradiation. Our analysis indicates that the majority of studies have reported either an improvement in neurologic conditions [30, 37] or that these conditions remained stable or improved following S-IRT [21, 25, 34]. Pham et al. have documented that Cs-131-based S-IRT does not result in a decline in MMSE scores or an acute decline in cognitive function. Furthermore, they described improvements in physical, functional, emotional, and brain tumor-specific well-being post-implantation, suggesting that the preservation or enhancement of QoL is achievable[30]. In a prospective cohort receiving Cs-131, MMSE scores at 4 and 12 months exhibited improvement compared to baseline (30 vs. 29, p < 0.017; 30 vs. 29, p < 0.001, respectively). Likewise, scores from the FACT-BR improved at both 4 and 6 months relative to baseline (162 vs. 143, p < 0.004; 164 vs. 143, p < 0.005, respectively) [37]. Additionally, Kutuk et al. reported that the KPS was stable in 60% of patients, although a slight decline of 10–20 points was noted at the last follow-up, with a median score of 80[44].

Ongoing prospective randomized trials are expected to provide higher-level evidence on S-IRT. Currently, three studies are investigating this approach in patients with BMs [47–49]. Among them, the ROADS trial is particularly important because it is the only randomized study comparing postoperative S-IRT with SRS for SB-RFS, OS, and neurocognitive outcomes [48]. Notably, during the preparation of this manuscript, ASTRO invited feedback on a draft guideline on radiation therapy for WHO grade 4 diffuse glioma, marking a broader re-emergence of brachytherapy in contemporary neuro-oncology discussions [50].

Limitations. Several limitations constrain the interpretation of this review. First, and most importantly, the evidence base is almost entirely non-comparative: 16 of the 18 included studies were single-arm, and only one [24] compared S-IRT with the current standard of postoperative stereotactic radiotherapy. Comparisons between S-IRT and SRS, and between Cs-131 and I-125, therefore rest on across-study contrasts between cohorts that differ in treatment era, patient selection, prior irradiation, cavity size, and outcome definitions, and are subject to confounding that cannot be quantified. Second, the crude proportions reported here—for example, the aggregate rates of symptomatic radionecrosis and leptomeningeal disease by isotope—are unweighted descriptive summaries of heterogeneous cohorts rather than meta-analytic estimates, and no confidence intervals can legitimately be attached to them. Third, methodological quality was moderate rather than high: a prospective calculation of the study size was reported in only one study [36], loss to follow-up was documented at below 5% in only two studies, and endpoint assessment independent of the treating team was reported in only four studies. Fourth, follow-up was short relative to the endpoints reported in a substantial minority of studies, with a median follow-up below 12 months in eight cohorts, which will tend to underestimate late local failure and late radionecrosis alike. Fifth, the included literature spans nearly three decades and 11 studies contain data more than 10 years old; changes over that period in imaging, surgical technique, systemic therapy, and the radiological diagnosis of radionecrosis limit the comparability of early and recent series. Sixth, most cohorts were small and single-institutional, and publication and reporting bias in a literature dominated by favorable single-arm experiences cannot be excluded; with 18 heterogeneous studies and no pooled comparative estimates, formal assessment of small-study effects was not feasible. Finally, no review protocol was registered, which reduces protection against post hoc changes in approach, although the eligibility criteria and outcomes of interest were specified before screening began. For these reasons, the findings of this review should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as establishing the comparative effectiveness of postoperative S-IRT.

Conclusions

The S-IRT following BMs resection demonstrates a high LC rate in both newly diagnosed and radiation recurrent cases. Even in instances of large resection cavities, the rates of local failure remain low, particularly with Cs-131-based S-IRT. Additionally, the occurrence of sRN is notably low, nearly twice as low when Cs-131 is employed. The perioperative complication rates are comparable to those reported with surgical intervention alone. Overall, postoperative S-IRT appears to be a promising option for selected patients, particularly for large or previously irradiated cavities. However, the current evidence base remains heterogeneous and predominantly non-comparative; therefore, S-IRT should currently be considered a promising alternative rather than a replacement for postoperative SRS. Until the ongoing randomized trials report, postoperative S-IRT should be offered within clinical trials or, outside them, in carefully selected patients discussed in a multidisciplinary setting.
