Idiopathic Granulomatous Mastitis in a Nulliparous Woman With Autism Spectrum Disorder Receiving Long‐Term Psychotropic Treatment and Normal Serum Prolactin Level: A Case Report
Authors: Esmat Davoudi‐Monfared, Fatemeh Shahabi, Asieh Sadat Fattahi, Marzieh Rahimi, Mahsa Akbari Oryani, Azadeh Jabbari Nooghabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIdiopathic granulomatous mastitis (IGM) is a rare inflammatory breast disorder, primarily attributed to immunological dysregulation, hormonal imbalances, or fertility‐related conditions. We report a 26‐year‐old female with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) under long‐term multidrug psychotropic treatment who developed IGM. She had been treated with methylphenidate for at least 23 years and aripiprazole, clozapine, citalopram, valproate sodium, and propranolol for at least 10 years. The patient was sexually inactive, had no fertility‐related risk factors, and no prior use of contraceptive pills. She had no history of breast‐related disorders. She presented with induration and erythema of the right breast. Ultrasound examination showed multiple organized hypoechoic collections and recommended core needle biopsy (BIRADS 4a). Pathologic evaluation revealed granulomatous mastitis, and no neoplastic lesion was identified. Her prolactin level was 17.8 ng/mL (reference range: 4.7–23.3 ng/mL). She was prescribed oral antibiotics, corticosteroids, and anti‐inflammatory drugs. Due to the patient's improvement during follow‐up visits, the oral corticosteroid was tapered and antibiotic therapy was discontinued. On the last visit, 5 months and 24 days after the first visit, the breast presentation exhibited complete improvement, and corticosteroid therapy was discontinued. Although a clear relationship cannot be determined from a single case, prolonged exposure to psychotropic medications may contribute to immune dysregulation and oxidative stress, potentially associated with the development of IGM.Keywords: autism spectrum disorder, case report, granulomatous mastitis, psychotropic treatment

Introduction

Idiopathic granulomatous mastitis (IGM) is a benign inflammatory disease in the breast, with an annual prevalence of 2.4 cases per 100,000 individuals [1]. The global distribution of IGM is not uniform. Turkey had the highest report rate (39.1%), followed by Iran (18.1%), China (13.4%), the United States (11%), and India (2.2%) [2]. Previous studies have reported that IGM constitutes 1.5%–3.1% of benign inflammatory breast disorders, mainly affecting women aged 30 to 40 years with a history of childbirth [3, 4].

The exact etiology of IGM remains unknown, but various factors, including infection, pregnancy, hyperprolactinemia, lactation, α‐1 antitrypsin deficiency, smoking, oral contraceptive use, diabetes, trauma, and autoimmune disease have been proposed as potential factors [5]. Although hyperprolactinemia has been suggested as a potential mechanism that correlates psychotropic treatment to IGM [6], cases without increased prolactin levels are rarely reported in the literature [7]. Accurate IGM diagnosis is crucial, due to its clinical and radiological mimicry of breast cancer, potentially leading to misdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions [8]. IGM management is influenced by both disease severity and the individual patient's preferences. Even with appropriate therapy, the lesions may remain, reappear, or result in fistula formation, highlighting the need for continuous follow‐up of affected patients [9]. In this case report, a sexually inactive, nulliparous woman with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is presented who developed IGM.

Case Presentation

Case History/Examination

A 26‐year‐old single woman presented to our university‐affiliated clinic on January 19, 2025, with chief complaints of redness and pain in the right breast. She was a known case of ASD under long‐term treatment. There were no other systemic or autoimmune comorbidities. She was receiving aripiprazole 15 mg once daily, clozapine 25 mg once daily, citalopram 40 mg once daily, valproate sodium 200 mg once daily, propranolol 20 mg twice daily, and methylphenidate 10 mg twice daily. The patient had been receiving methylphenidate for at least 23 years and other medications for at least 10 years. She had no consumption of cigarettes, hookah, or alcohol. The patient's menarche occurred at the age of 18, and her menstrual cycles were irregular. The patient was sexually inactive, had no previous history of pregnancy, breastfeeding, or abortion, and no prior use of OCPs was documented. She had no history of chest or breast trauma, breast surgery, breast cancer, or prior breast infection or inflammation. Additionally, there was no family history of breast cancer, autoimmune disorders, or mastitis among her first‐ and second‐degree relatives. There was a history of prostate cancer in her father and lung cancer in one of her second‐degree relatives. The patient's body mass index was 19.9 (weight = 53.5 kg, height = 1.64 m). The waist circumference was 80 cm.

Upon breast examination, erythema of approximately 2 × 1 cm was observed at the 3 o'clock position of the right breast on inspection. In addition, there was a fistula orifice with serous discharge located at the 3 o'clock area, close to the areolar border. Scaling and superficial skin changes were also present in the same region. On palpation, an induration area was noted extending from approximately 3 o'clock to 7 o'clock, measuring approximately 7–8 cm in length. The indurated area was firm, ill‐defined, and mildly tender on deep palpation. No fluctuance was noted. No palpable axillary lymphadenopathy was detected. Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Ultrasound examination was performed on both breasts and the bilateral axillary regions. In the right breast, there were multiple organized hypoechoic collections at the 3 o'clock (50 × 27 mm), 6 o'clock (29 × 13 mm), and 7 o'clock (21 × 11 mm) positions. No pathological lesions or adenopathy were detected in the left breast and bilateral axillary regions. The radiologist indicated BIRADS 4a (low suspicious malignancy) for the right breast and recommended an ultrasound‐guided core needle biopsy for the definite diagnosis. Subsequently, an ultrasound‐guided core needle biopsy was performed, and pathologic evaluation revealed breast parenchyma with granulomatous inflammation composed of epithelioid histocytes, multinucleated giant cells, and other inflammatory cells within and around lobules, compatible with granulomatous mastitis as shown in Figure 2A,B. No neoplastic lesion was identified. Cytologic evaluation of the breast fluid revealed hemorrhagic fluid with a prominent mixed inflammatory infiltrate and foamy macrophages, without atypical cells (Figure 2C). The interval between symptom onset and pathological diagnosis was 20 days.

(C) Cytologic evaluation of the breast fluid revealed hemorrhagic fluid containing numerous mixed inflammatory cells, including neutrophils, lymphocytes, and histiocytes, without atypical cells (stain, hematoxylin and eosin; magnification, ×400).

Laboratory investigations showed a 1‐h ESR of 26 mm and a C‐reactive protein level of 0.7 mg/L. The white blood cell count was 8.4 × 103/mcL, and the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio was 1.91. The thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH) level was 1.64 mIU/L, and the serum prolactin level was 17.8 ng/mL; both were within normal ranges. The vitamin D level was 21.9 ng/mL. The differential diagnoses of tuberculosis and sarcoidosis were excluded by negative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for Mycobacterium tuberculosis and a normal serum angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) level, respectively.

She was prescribed to receive trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole (cotrimoxazole) 400/80 mg twice daily (two each time), prednisolone 5 mg three times daily, and naproxen 250 mg twice daily for pain and inflammation management. Medications to control ASD also continued under the supervision of a psychiatrist.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient returned for follow‐up assessment 26 days after the initial visit. On physical examination, no palpable induration was detected. A 1 × 4 cm erythematous area was observed on the right breast, extending from the 3 to 6 o'clock position. The previously mentioned fistula had been closed, and there was no discharge. At this visit, the patient's weight was 53 kg, and her waist circumference was measured at 79 cm. The prednisolone dosage was tapered to 10 mg daily. Naproxen 250 mg twice daily was continued. No antibiotics were prescribed during this visit (duration of antibiotic therapy was 10 days). Pantoprazole 20 mg once daily, and a calcium‐vitamin D supplement were initiated. The patient's breast presentation at the second visit is shown in Figure 1B.

At the third visit, 1 month after the second, no significant changes or new findings were observed. The prednisolone was tapered to 5 mg daily, and it was recommended that the other medications be continued. The patient was instructed to reduce the prednisolone dosage to 2.5 mg daily after 2 weeks. During the last visit on July 13, 2025, the breast presentation exhibited complete improvement. Corticosteroid therapy was discontinued, and Naproxen 500 mg daily was prescribed. Calcium‐vitamin D supplement was also recommended. No significant weight change or complications were detected compared to prior visits. Discussion

IGM is a rare inflammatory breast disorder, primarily attributed to immunological dysregulation, hormonal imbalances, or fertility‐related conditions. This study presents IGM in a nulliparous patient with no fertility‐related risk factors. Our case had an established history of long‐term psychotropic drug usage for ASD. We discuss the potential role of drug‐induced immunological or hormonal alterations as pathophysiological mechanisms of IGM.

Methylphenidate induces oxidative stress and affects both enzymatic and nonenzymatic antioxidant defenses, consequently causing damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA. Experimental research has demonstrated that methylphenidate triggers inflammatory responses, disrupts apoptotic signaling [10], and modifies oxidative stress adaptations [11], primarily via dopaminergic pathways and TNF‐α modulation [12]. Although current evidence remains insufficient to establish methylphenidate as a causative agent for classical autoimmune disease, rare instances of autoimmune‐like reactions (e.g., vasculitis) have been documented [13]. While evidence from human studies is limited, it is suggested that methylphenidate‐induced immune dysregulation may constitute the underlying pathogenic mechanism of mastitis development. In our case, the patient had been maintained on methylphenidate for 23 years, and it remains unclear if this medication was implicated in the pathogenesis of IGM.

Although clozapine [14] rarely elevates prolactin levels compared to typical antipsychotics, its dopamine D2 receptor antagonism can induce hyperprolactinemia in susceptible individuals, a well‐established risk factor for mastitis [15]. Aripiprazole is a partial dopamine D2 agonist and has shown beneficial effects as an adjunctive drug in the treatment of psychotropic‐induced hyperprolactinemia [16]. In addition, previous studies indicate that oral administration of citalopram has shown contrasting effects on the elevation of prolactin levels [17, 18]. Our patient had been maintained on clozapine, citalopram, and aripiprazole for 10 years without any evidence of hyperprolactinemia on laboratory testing. Thus, it remains unclear if they were implicated in the pathogenesis of IGM in our case.

Currently, only a limited number of granulomatous mastitis cases caused by psychotropic‐induced hyperprolactinemia have been documented [19, 20, 21]. A retrospective study by Tian et al. [6] reported 19 granulomatous mastitis patients with psychiatric disorders, who were treated with antipsychotic medications for 0.5–10.2 years. The majority of patients received multiple antipsychotics. All patients had elevated prolactin levels, ranging from 35.15 to 200 ng/mL. None of the patients had a long‐term history of OCP use, whereas 21.1% had used OCPs temporarily. 26.3% of patients had no obstetrical history. There was no personal or family history of breast cancer among the patients. Systemic therapy, including oral corticosteroids, antimycobacterial drugs, and surgical excision, was administered to the patients. In contrast, our patient was maintained on multiple psychotropic medications for more than a decade without an elevation in prolactin levels. In the present case, IGM was successfully treated with a combination of prednisone and naproxen, along with a short course of trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole.

Few cases of biopsy‐proven IGM have been reported in nulliparous women, and increased prolactin levels have been attributed to some of these cases [22, 23]. Boldrini et al. [7] reported a 35‐year‐old female, with no history of breast trauma, pregnancy, or lactation, who exhibited a left breast lump, pain, and swelling, subsequently diagnosed as IGM. The patient had a prolactin‐secreting pituitary adenoma surgically excised over 10 years ago, with no subsequent recurrence on neuroimaging follow‐up. Her prolactin level was within normal range. The unique aspect of our case report compared with the published literature is the diagnosis of symptomatic IGM in a single nulliparous woman without evidence of hyperprolactinemia despite receiving long‐term multidrug psychiatric therapy.

A critical factor in this case is the prolonged duration of pharmaceutical usage. Prolonged methylphenidate exposure combined with extended use of other psychotropics may elevate the risk of atypical side effects [24] or immunological alterations [25] that are rarely observed in short‐term treatment. Chronic citalopram administration exerts complex immunomodulatory effects, including suppression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (notably IL‐6 and TNF‐α) and upregulation of anti‐inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL‐10)—modifications that may contribute to its therapeutic effectiveness in mood disorders. However, some studies highlight that sustained use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) can dysregulate redox homeostasis, potentially triggering rare but clinically significant immune‐mediated adverse events in genetically predisposed individuals [26]. Based on pharmacodynamic interaction, the clozapine‐citalopram combination may synergistically impair endocrine function (via hyperprolactinemia) and promote immune dysregulation with pro‐inflammatory consequences [27]. Although conclusive evidence from human studies has not yet established a definitive association between long‐term coadministration of clozapine and citalopram and IGM, the pathophysiological plausibility is supported by their established pharmacological mechanisms [28].

There are several potential confounding factors that warrant further discussion. Emerging evidence suggests that ASD may be associated with alterations in inflammatory responses and immune regulation [29]. Although no direct association between ASD and IGM has been determined, the ASD‐related immune dysfunction may contribute to susceptibility to inflammatory disorders like IGM in affected women. Irregular menstrual cycles, as observed in our patient, may reflect an underlying hormonal imbalance, which has been suggested as a contributing factor in the pathogenesis of IGM [30]. Moreover, the patient had a low vitamin D level, which has been shown to have a significant role in the immune system modulation by regulating the production of inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1, IL‐6, and TNF‐α. In the context of inflammatory conditions such as IGM, vitamin D deficiency can push the immune system into an aggressive, hyperactive, and deregulated state, which may lead to flare‐ups or worsening of inflammatory diseases [31]. Although autoimmune dysfunction has been proposed in the pathogenesis of IGM [30], our patient had no relevant clinical or family history or signs suggestive of an autoimmune disorder. Therefore, autoimmune serologic tests were not performed in our patient, which could be considered a limitation in this case report. In addition, the α1‐antitrypsin deficiency and other relevant genetic predispositions were not evaluated in our patient. Other unmeasured confounding variables may also be implicated.

For quantitative measurement of the probability of IGM as an adverse psychotropic drug reaction, the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale [32] was calculated for our case report, with a score of 3, indicating a Possible adverse drug reaction. According to the Bradford–Hill criteria of temporality [33], prolonged exposure to psychotropic medications preceded the onset of granulomatous mastitis, suggesting a potential temporal association in this case report. However, temporality alone is not adequate to establish a clear relationship. No medication withdrawal or rechallenge was performed to further assess causality, and the patient's serum prolactin level was within the normal range despite prolonged psychotropic treatment. Thus, even though the temporal sequence may suggest that psychotropic medications could have contributed to endocrine or immune alterations in IGM pathogenesis, this relationship should be interpreted with caution, and a causal relationship cannot be established from a single case report. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the potential relationship between chronic psychotropic treatment and rare inflammatory breast disorders, as well as the underlying immunological and endocrine pathways involved.

Conclusion

In this study, we presented a case of IGM in a nulliparous woman receiving long‐term multidrug psychiatric therapy without evidence of hyperprolactinemia. This case report highlights a potential association between prolonged psychotropic medication use and the development of IGM in a single patient without conventional fertility‐related risk factors. Chronic exposure to psychotropic medications may contribute to immune dysregulation, oxidative stress, and hyperprolactinemia (absent in this case), creating a plausible pathophysiological basis for IGM. While a definitive association cannot be concluded from a single case, clinicians should remain vigilant for atypical inflammatory or immune‐mediated complications in patients receiving prolonged multidrug psychiatric therapy.
