What we know, don’t know, and need to know about patient authorship: a scoping review, evidence gap analysis, and research roadmap
Authors: Karen L Woolley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient authorship is an innovation, challenging a centuries-old medical publishing model. To guide best practices for patient authorship, a strong evidence base is required. The objectives of this research were to: (1) Identify ‘what we know’ about patient authorship via a scoping review. (2) Describe ‘what we don’t know’ about patient authorship via an evidence gap analysis. (3) Propose ‘what we need to know’ about patient authorship via a research roadmap.MethodsThis patient-led, voluntary, research project was conducted according to best practice guidelines. Using a registered protocol, the Population, Concept, and Context framework was applied and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses – Scoping Reviews checklist completed. The literature search strategy, prepared with a university information specialist, focused on peer-reviewed publications reporting empirical evidence on patient authorship. Database searches (MEDLINE, EMBASE; all dates up to 03/04/2025) were complemented by hand-searching and expert recommendations. References were downloaded into EndNote 21, screened using Covidence, and data (40 variables) extracted. Published evidence gaps were coded using eight deductive topic-summary themes and categorised (ethical/effective practices; lifecycle stage -planning, generating, sharing, evaluating). Frequency analysis of evidence gaps was used to generate proposed research questions.ResultsOf 1,036 retrieved publications, 29 were eligible, yielding 1,160 datapoints. Most publications on patient authorship are recent (86% published between 2020-2025), accessible (90% open access), include patient authors (83%), but are limited in terms of reporting (34% used GRIPP2) and representativeness (97% from high-income countries; 79% from English-language countries). Published evidence on patient authorship exists to support ethical and effective practices for each publication lifecycle stage. However, 90 evidence gaps were identified. The resulting research roadmap includes research questions to address high-frequency gaps.ConclusionsThis study provides the first structured and integrated synthesis of peer-reviewed, published, empirical evidence on what is known, not known, and what we need to know about patient authorship. Citable and structured evidence is now readily available to help support patient authorship practices and resources. The novel roadmap could help reduce research waste and accelerate evidence-based best practice guidelines for patient authorship. Background

For centuries, publications in medical journals about patients have not been authored by patients.

However, the principle of ‘nothing about us, without us’ is starting to penetrate medical publishing. Emerging evidence is showing a rapid increase in the number of patient-authored publications [1, 2]. This acceleration in patient authorship is consistent with and reflects the increasing involvement of patients in medical research [3–5]. Indeed, international recognition of the importance and value of collaborating with patients as research partners has led to fundamental changes in international ethical and reporting guidelines for medical research. For example, for the first time, the global and highly influential Declaration of Helsinki [6] now requires researchers to engage meaningfully with patients before, during, and after research, including the dissemination of results. Similarly, the updated SPIRIT Statement for protocols [7] and the CONSORT Statement for publications [8] have introduced explicit items for transparent documentation of patient involvement in planning, conducting, and reporting randomised controlled trials. With a specific focus on medical publications, the widely used Good Publication Practice guideline [9] now includes specific recommendations on how to involve patients in planning, writing, and sharing publications. Leading medical journals and publishers are also changing their policies and practices to proactively encourage patient involvement in publications [10–12]. Guidance is also being provided to help patient authors and their co-authors understand how patients can meet authorship criteria, such as those established by the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors [1, 9]. Collectively, these changes are shifting the paradigm of medical research and publishing, one where patients can and should be involved – as authors, peer-reviewers, and editors [1, 10–14]. This shift from exclusion to inclusion may be considered as an innovation in a 350-year-old medical publishing model and the focus on patient authorship is now progressing from ‘can it be done?’ to ‘how can it be done well?’ [1, 15].

As with any innovation, empirical evidence is required to identify and encourage evidence-based best practices for patient authorship. The science of patient authorship, however, is a nascent field of study. Consequently, and understandably, the emerging evidence base can be limited, fragile, and fragmented, and published guidance may draw heavily on expert opinion [9, 13, 15, 16]. Expert-driven recommendations should help patients and their research partners navigate the publication process and limit the professional and personal costs of learning by trial and error. However, experts in the field, including patient and non-patient authors, have progressively recognised the need for a larger and stronger evidence base to guide best practices for patient authorship [10, 15, 17–19]. Ideally, throughout each stage of the publication lifecycle, patient authors and their partners should be able to follow ethical and effective patient authorship practices that are based on evidence (Fig. 1).

A research roadmap could help build a robust evidence base for patient authorship in a strategic, timely, and cost-effective manner. To date, there is no research roadmap for patient authorship. Without a research roadmap, evidence may still accumulate, but in an ad hoc and fragmented manner, and with greater risks of unnecessary duplication, delays, costs, and research waste [20, 21]. However, key to the development of a world-first research roadmap for patient authorship is understanding what is currently known and unknown about patient authorship. Methods

Scoping review (what we know)

Scoping reviews provide a systematic and transparent method to describe the evidence on a topic, particularly for emerging fields of study [22, 23]. As patient authorship research is a rapidly evolving area of study and practice [1], a scoping review was considered the most appropriate method to identify and summarise the current evidence base. This scoping review followed best practice recommendations for conducting [22] and reporting [23] scoping reviews. A checklist has been completed for the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses – extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA – ScR; Additional File 2).

Protocol and registration

To enhance transparency and reduce the risk of research waste, an a priori protocol for this scoping review was completed and registered on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/BGK24). Ethics approval was not required as the research was based on published peer-reviewed evidence.

Eligibility criteria and key definitions

This review included publications that reported empirical evidence on the practice of patient authorship of peer-reviewed publications in medical journals. Practices related to patient authorship may occur in one or more of the four main stages of the publication lifecycle (Fig. 1) – publication planning, publication generation, publication sharing, and publication evaluation [1]. For this project, publication planning includes identifying topics, potential authors, target journals, timelines, resources; publication generation includes actions taken during the publication process such as reading the literature, analysing and interpreting data, drafting and reviewing the manuscript; publication sharing includes drafting and reviewing publication extenders such as a plain language summary (PLS), podcast, video etc, as well as raising awareness of publications; publication evaluation includes actions taken after the publication, including assessing the impact of a publication, comparing patient-authored publications to other publications etc …

The Population, Concept, and Context framework for scoping reviews [22] and the following eligibility criteria and definitions were applied:

The Population was patients. Patient authorship research is defined as a field of study that examines patient authors and patient-authored publications. Published definitions were used for patient author and patient-authored publications [2]. In brief, a patient author is a person who lives with or is affected by disease, provides a valuable patient perspective to the publication, and meets authorship criteria from the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE). A patient-authored publication is an article authored or co-authored by a patient and is published in a peer-reviewed journal.

Information sources and search

A three-step search strategy was used to source potential publications. First, an initial limited search of MEDLINE (via PubMed) was undertaken to identify relevant articles on patient authorship. Second, the text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles and associated key words and MeSH terms were used to develop a full search strategy. The search strategy was prepared with a university-based, experienced, information specialist librarian. The strategy was also reviewed using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) Checklist [24]. The MEDLINE and EMBASE electronic databases were searched with the search strategy adapted for each database (see Additional File 3). The search was conducted on April 3rd, 2025 and no limits were set (eg, on time of publication up until April 3rd, 2025, language, therapeutic area). References were downloaded into EndNote 21 (Clarivate, Philadelphia, USA). Duplicates were removed and eligibility screening conducted using Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). Third, the electronic search strategy was complemented by hand-searching the references lists from retrieved publications and contacting researchers in the field of patient authorship for potential references that reported empirical evidence on the practice of patient authorship.

Selection of sources of information

A pilot test on screening references using Covidence was conducted. An experienced patient authorship researcher reviewed the title and abstract information against the eligibility criteria for the first 25 references. Following the successful pilot test, the titles and abstracts for the remaining references were screened. The full texts of selected citations were then assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by the same reviewer. Reasons for exclusion of retrieved citations were recorded. Data charting and items

To chart the data needed to meet the purpose of the review, a customised data extraction tool was developed (Microsoft Excel, Redmond, USA). A pilot test of the data chart tool was conducted by an experienced patient authorship researcher on the full text of the first 10 eligible publications. Based on this test, additional drop-down menus were created and the order of several columns in the data chart changed to make extraction more consistent and efficient. Across all included publications, 40 data items were extracted, including key publication, authorship, and research characteristics, as well as the extent of empirical evidence related to the ethics and effectiveness of patient authorship as reported for each of the four stages of the publication lifecycle. Classification of country income for first authors and patient authors was based on the World Bank Group categories. Both quantitative (eg, numbers, percentages) and qualitative (eg, quotes) information was extracted (Additional File 4). Illustrative quotes are presented for each stage of the publication lifecycle and descriptive statistics are used to summarise the results.

Evidence gap analysis (what we don’t know)

Qualitative mapping was used to analyse evidence gaps identified during the scoping review [25]. The sampling frame comprised all publications that reported evidence gaps. Each unit of analysis was an evidence gap, not a publication. A customised data extraction tool was developed (Microsoft Excel, Redmond, USA; Additional File 5) and pilot tested by an experienced patient authorship researcher for the first 25 evidence gaps. No changes were made following the pilot test. Data familiarisation was completed by reviewing the verbatim text for each evidence gap reported in the publications. Each gap was analysed in three ways. First, for the thematic analysis, initial descriptive codes were developed based on the verbatim text of the evidence gaps. The code for each evidence gap was then classified into one of eight primarily deductive topic-summary themes (rather than inductive interpretive themes). These a priori themes were based on issues raised in the peer-reviewed literature and confirmed during the data familiarisation process. Second, each evidence gap was categorised according to whether a gap related primarily to ethical or effectiveness patient authorship practices. Third, each gap was categorised in relation to the stages of the publication lifecycle. If a gap could not be categorised to a particular stage, it was categorised as ‘other’. Illustrative quotes are provided to provide context to the evidence gap themes and descriptive statistics are used to summarise categorical data.

Research roadmap (what we need to know)

To provide an evidence-based and transparent guide for the research roadmap, the three most frequent evidence gap themes for each stage of the publication lifecycle were used as the basis for generating potential research questions. The research questions within each lifecycle stage were then categorised as those relating to ethical or to effectiveness practices for patient authorship. The research questions are suggestive, not prescriptive. Given the different interest-holders affecting and affected by patient authorship, each group will need to consider which questions are most important and feasible to address in their future research programs and whether collaboration with other groups may be warranted.

Patient and public involvement

This was a patient-led, voluntary, research project. Patient involvement occurred throughout the planning, conduct, and reporting of the research. The Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public, Version 2 (GRIPP2) checklist is available (Additional File 6). Although this research was informed by discussions about patient authorship with leading patient advocates, medical publication professionals, research funders, editors, and publishers, the author (KLW) was solely and fully responsible for the conduct and reporting of the research. Based on the published literature, the author (KLW) is highly representative of most patient authors in terms of education, sex, age, and country income level and language [1]. The author’s patient perspective is drawn from lived experience as a bereaved parent, a caregiver to multiple immediate family members with cancer and other chronic conditions, a clinical trial participant, and from having had multiple surgeries and hospitalisations.

Use of artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (ChatGPT 4 and 5) was used to help prepare the evidence gap data chart and to extract and tabulate data. All entries were human-verified and an independent cross-check on tabulated sub-totals and totals was used as an additional human-in-the-loop review.

Key characteristics of publications in the evidence dataset

Publications on patient authorship have reported evidence obtained from quantitative (38%; 11/29), qualitative (28%; 8/29), and mixed methods (34%; 10/29) research. Most publications (69%; 20/29) did not focus on a specific disease area. The evidence base is relatively new with the majority of publications (86%; 25/29) occurring in the past five years (2020–2025). Across all publications, the mean journal impact factor was 10.23 (min 1.3; max 93.7; median 3.00), the most frequent journal was Research Involvement and Engagement (11/29; 38%), and the most frequent publisher was BMC Springer Nature (13/29; 45%). The most frequent funder was government (9/29; 31%).

Research roadmap – what we need to know

To guide potential future research on patient authorship, interest-holders could consider the questions proposed below for each stage of the publication lifecycle, both in terms of ethical and effectiveness practices (Tables 7, 8, 9, 10). The transparent and evidence-based presentation of these research questions should help guide different interest groups as they prioritise their key questions (eg, based on importance and feasibility for their group or for a collaboration with other interest-holders) and should also help reduce research waste across different interest groups. Given the frequency that ‘methods’ was raised as an evidence gap and as methods can influence research throughout the publication lifecycle, questions are also proposed for how to study patient authorship (Table 11).

Discussion

Patient authorship is a new and important topic in medical publishing, but the science of patient authorship is in its early stages [1, 14, 15]. To help advance ethical and effective patient authorship practices, throughout the publication lifecycle, a better understanding of the science of patient authorship is required. This is the first study to examine: 1) what we know about patient authorship via a scoping review; 2) what we don’t know about patient authorship via an evidence gap analysis; 3) what we need to know about patient authorship via a research roadmap.

Based on an analysis of more than 1000 datapoints from 29 peer-reviewed publications, the scoping review identified evidence supporting both ethical and effective practices for patient authorship for each of the 4 stages of the publication lifecycle. However, the review also showed that evidence is still lacking. Collectively, authors of these 29 publications reported 90 evidence gaps. The gap analysis highlighted that there were unmet needs in each of the publication lifecycle stages, for both ethical and effective practices. The most frequent evidence gap related to operational processes/best practices. To help build a stronger evidence base to guide ethical and effective patient authorship, a research roadmap was prepared. The roadmap includes suggested research questions for the most frequent unmet needs for each stage of the publication lifecycle. In terms of practical implications, those interested in patient authorship can now: 1) readily access a free, organised, peer-reviewed, and citable evidence base to raise awareness of the value of patient authorship and help justify the resources required to support it; 2) clearly identify where evidence is still limited and recognise where practices may rely on expert opinion or trial and error; 3) potentially contribute to the evidence base for patient authorship, while minimising the risk of research waste, by reviewing a roadmap of well-justified, evidence-based research questions and addressing those that best align with their priorities.

What we know about patient authorship

Although patient authorship is a relatively new topic in medical publishing, peer-reviewed published empirical evidence is emerging (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5). However, this evidence can be challenging to find [1, 2, 14, 17–19]. Moreover, as encountered in this scoping review, evidence specifically related to patient authorship can be difficult to distinguish from evidence related to the broader topic of ‘patient and public involvement’. In light of these challenges, this scoping review should prove particularly valuable as it provides a novel, timely, appropriate, and practical means of collating, organising, and presenting that evidence. By categorising the evidence across the publication lifecycle, interest-holders can find, examine, and leverage peer-reviewed evidence that is most relevant to their area of focus (eg, planning, generating, sharing, or evaluating patient-authored publications).

In terms of practical implications, evidence from this scoping review could be used to help address real-world patient authorship issues. These issues include those recently highlighted by different interest-holders [15–18], such as:

Value: Notably, the scoping review has shown that evidence on the value of patient authorship exists for each stage of the publication lifecycle, with both patient and non-patient authors recognising the value of involving patients in publications. Interest-holders should be able to cite and leverage this published evidence to help convince more research teams and patient communities to collaborate on patient authorship projects. This evidence could also be evaluated and used by journals, publishers, funders, and investors to help justify why they encourage or require patient authorship.

Resources: The evidence reinforces that resources (eg, time, training, plain language documentation, funds for open access fees) are required for patient authorship projects. Further research is needed to clarify compensation for patient authors [16, 51], but funds to pay patient authors, transparently and equitably, for their time and expertise should also be considered. Without adequate resourcing, patient authorship projects may not start or may not be as successful as they could be. However, in practical terms, the costs of resourcing patient authorship projects should be considered against the costs of not supporting patient authorship projects. These costs may be intangible (eg, damage to an organisation’s credibility and reputation for not partnering with patients) and tangible (eg, the cost of reduced publication relevance and reach).

Compliance: If compliance extends to complying with reporting guidelines, then this review has revealed clear and consistent evidence of noncompliance with use of the GRIPP2 reporting guideline for patient involvement. Even in this dataset of publications, where 83% of publications had substantial patient involvement (ie, patient authors), only 34% had a GRIPP2 form available. This current practice is unacceptable. From a practical perspective, evidence from this review could be used in patient authorship training to reinforce to authorship teams that they need to ‘do better’. There are calls for improvements in GRIPP2 [31, 34], but for now, authors should use this reporting guideline for patient involvement research. In terms of compliance with the rules of authorship, this scoping review suggests that there are some causes for concern. Although the published evidence did not include explicit examples of unethical patient authorship practices and no patient-authored publications have been retracted, potential discrepancies have been noted between involvement and authorship [4, 40, 48, 52]. While such potential discrepancies need further investigation, it is possible that unethical ghost- and guest-authorship practices may be occurring, even inadvertently. Researchers who use patients for authorship-level contributions, but don’t consider or actively deny these patients authorship are exposing themselves to the risk of retraction for ghost authorship. Similarly, researchers who ‘give’ patients unearned/tokenistic authorship are also risking retraction for guest authorship. Patient authorship projects may be particularly susceptible to these unethical authorship practices as patients may have limited knowledge of good publication practices and may encounter power imbalances [3]. Efforts are being made to raise awareness of these and other ‘patient authorship’ issues [1, 53]. Across interest-holder groups, those responsible for compliance, formally or informally, should review the evidence from this scoping review to help ensure that their ‘rules’ (eg, policies, standard operating procedures, work practices) minimise the risk of unethical patient authorship practices.

Planning: Existing evidence indicates that patients may be less involved in planning publications than they are in planning clinical trials and that many of the success factors for patient authorship rely on early involvement. Publication planning teams should be able to use this evidence to consider how they might support patients, who are interested and have or can acquire relevant competencies, to serve on both trial and publication planning teams. Failure to involve patients in planning may limit the identification of evidence gaps and proposal of relevant research and publications [16, 54]. Existing evidence also indicates that patient authorship projects should not be restricted to one type of topic, publication type, or target journal. This evidence should encourage interest-holders to check for any unnecessary restrictions or bias they might hold as to what is ‘appropriate’ for patient authorship.

Planning: Existing evidence indicates that patients may be less involved in planning publications than they are in planning clinical trials and that many of the success factors for patient authorship rely on early involvement. Publication planning teams should be able to use this evidence to consider how they might support patients, who are interested and have or can acquire relevant competencies, to serve on both trial and publication planning teams. Failure to involve patients in planning may limit the identification of evidence gaps and proposal of relevant research and publications [16, 54]. Existing evidence also indicates that patient authorship projects should not be restricted to one type of topic, publication type, or target journal. This evidence should encourage interest-holders to check for any unnecessary restrictions or bias they might hold as to what is ‘appropriate’ for patient authorship.

Patient authors: The evidence from this scoping review has shown that patient authors are increasing, can have lead authorship roles, and can enhance the relevance and reach of publications. However, the evidence also indicates that patient authors may not fully represent the broader patient communities affected by their published research. Patient authors and those who work with them come from predominantly high-income and English-language countries. Previous research also suggests that many patient authors are middle-aged/older women [1]. Notably, the earliest publication retrieved using the PubMed search on ‘Patient Author’ exemplifies these demographic characteristics, being authored by a middle-aged/older woman, from a high-income, English-speaking country [55]. In practical terms, this situation may be challenging to correct, particularly as a similar over-representation can occur in patient advocacy organisations, where the path to patient authorship may begin [1]. Interest-holders should be aware of this situation and develop identification and training initiatives that will enhance the representativeness of patient authors.

What we don’t know about patient authorship

The evidence base emerging to guide patient authorship is encouraging, but insufficient. This quantitative and qualitative gap analysis provided a way to summarise, for the first time, the extent and type of evidence gaps reported by researchers in this field. With 90 evidence gaps (ie, the ‘known unknowns’) identified across 8 major themes, the science of patient authorship has much to address. Moreover, as patient authorship progresses, further evidence gaps (ie, the ‘unknown unknowns’) will likely be discovered.

Among the themes used in the gap analysis, the most frequent evidence gap was noted for operational processes/best practices. Across the publication lifecycle, the most frequent evidence gaps occurred during the generation and evaluation stages. These findings support not only the premise that patient authorship is shifting from ‘can it be done?’ to ‘how can it be done well?’ [1, 15], but also the interest in being able to assess how well patient authorship was actually done. With the rapidly increasing number of patient-authored publications [1, 2], there is a pressing need to address gaps in both ethical (ie, doing the right things) and effective (ie, doing things right) practices. Doing so could help inform patient authorship updates to publication guidelines and reporting checklists. For example, in the next update to the widely used Good Publication Practice guideline for peer-reviewed publications [9] and the associated guideline for conference abstracts and presentations [56], expert-based guidance on patient authorship could be complemented by stronger evidence-based guidance. Robust evidence addressing key gaps might even help prompt explicit evidence-based guidance on patient authorship from the influential ICMJE and encourage reporting guideline developers to include patient authorship as an example of ‘Patient and Public Involvement’ in their Explanation and Elaboration documents. In the 2025 SPIRIT [57] and CONSORT [58] Explanation and Elaboration documents, Patient and Public Involvement reporting items have been introduced, but patient authorship examples are not provided.

Another frequent evidence gap that is critical to address relates to the methods to study patient authorship. Methods research may be seen as ‘dry’ compared to other areas of research, but robust methods are required to advance patient authorship practices. One critical methods gap, namely, how to identify patient-authored publications, was recognised in the early stages of studying patient authorship [2, 14]. This methods gap has become increasingly evident with further research efforts. Currently, there is no gold standard for identifying patient-authored publications, which limits the datasets that researchers can generate to address other evidence gaps. As an interim measure, the advanced affiliation search function on PubMed is being used for research on patient-authored publications and patient authors [1, 2, 14]. This method, which relies on the use of affiliation tags (eg, the use of ‘Patient Author’), provides a free, practical, transparent, global, and reproducible way to study patient authorship. Relevant to the evidence gap noted for ‘identifying patient author candidates’, potential patient authors can be found via this method within minutes, with the added advantage of being able to add other search terms to customise the output (eg, identifying patient authors by therapeutic area, geography, timeframe, publication type etc …). Patient authors, with a proven publication record, can now be found more readily (eg, for future research and publication projects) if they use the ‘Patient Author’ affiliation. Testament to the value of the ‘Patient Author’ affiliation, it is now being encouraged by patient advocates, journals, publishers, and publication professionals [12, 16, 59]. Importantly, this method still enables patient authors to use other affiliations (eg, their patient advocacy organisation), can still provide patient authors with anonymity if desired, and results in fewer errors than the use of other affiliation terms [1]. However, this method has limitations (eg, is likely to underestimate the number of patient-authored publications and patient authors). Critical evidence gaps remain for enhanced ways to study patient authorship.

What we need to know about patient authorship

The need for a larger and stronger evidence base to guide patient authorship has been increasingly recognised by diverse interest-holders, including patients, academics, publication professionals, funders, journal editors and publishers. While patient authorship may affect and be affected by each interest-holder in different ways, the need for evidence-based guidance to enhance ethical and effective practices is a common need. This research roadmap provides these diverse interest-holders with suggested patient authorship research questions across the publication lifecycle (Tables 7, 8, 9, 10), as well as for research methods (Table 11).

This research roadmap for patient authorship is the first of its kind and has the potential to accelerate evidence-based best practices, while minimising the risk of research waste. Reflecting the adage, ‘A problem well stated is a problem half solved’, the roadmap provides potential researchers with an organised list of evidence-based research questions, thereby helping to lower the cognitive and practical barriers to initiate a research project. Rather than expending effort on what to study and how to justify it, researchers can focus on how a roadmap-listed question aligns with their needs and how they will choose to answer it. Researchers retain agency.

The success of this research roadmap could be influenced by a number of factors, including:

Oversight: The research roadmap can be used independently by any researcher or research group. A research group might be formed within a specific interest-holder community (eg, patient advocates) or could become a collaboration amongst different interest-holder communities (eg, patient advocates, publication professionals, funders). However, the success of this research roadmap could be enhanced if an oversight committee was formed. This committee could raise awareness of the roadmap, help prioritise questions, encourage researcher interest, monitor progress against the roadmap, and update the roadmap as deemed necessary. The committee should include representatives from different interest-holder groups, especially patients. Given the focus, expertise, success, and representativeness of the ISMPP Patient Engagement Task Force [15], a dedicated research committee could help the Task Force meet its strategic objectives and accelerate the evidence base for patient authorship.

Incentives, including funding: Given the nascent stage of research on patient authorship, incentives will likely be needed to attract, support, and fund researchers. While there are incentives for greater patient involvement in research coming from different interest-holders (eg, patients, regulators, market access authorities, investors), there are no specific incentives dedicated to research on patient authorship. The oversight committee, as suggested above, or others committed to patient authorship (eg, research funders, publishers) could aim to: 1) introduce a ‘Patient Author of the Year’ award; 2) obtain a waiver for open access fees for each finalist in the Patient Author of the Year award; 3) secure funding for at least one roadmap research project per year. With this scoping review showing that most patient authorship research is funded by government sources, additional funding from other interest-holders should be encouraged. Without support for a more broad and robust evidence base, the science of patient authorship will stagnate. Valuable lessons about how to advance patient authorship research could be learned from how advances have been made in peer review research, particularly in terms of raising awareness of the need for robust research [60]. For example, explicit and ongoing support for research on peer review has helped identify poor practices (eg, inefficient, biased, untested, unethical) and promote evidence-based best practices (eg, transparent, reliable, equitable) [60]. Why should patient authorship be exempt from rigorous and timely research on ethical and effective practices?

Relationships: Notwithstanding the importance of governance and incentives, the success of the roadmap will also depend on human relationships. The relationships among those supporting research and the relationships among those conducting the research will both be key. Ideally, collaborative relationships will be nurtured or extended among different interest-holders who, collectively, have a vested interest in seeing advances in patient authorship practices [13, 16]. As recognised in the science of peer review, valuable evidence can be generated by those outside of medical publishing [60]. The science of patient authorship may also benefit from research built on collaborative relationships with those outside of medical publishing, such as those with expertise in citizen science and customer involvement. Group think can lead to missing gaps that can be obvious to those new to patient authorship; once seen, these gaps cannot be unseen [54]. Within patient authorship research teams, respectful relationships must also be nurtured and sustained. Power sharing should be a key consideration when forming and sustaining teams [3, 16, 17] and evidence from this scoping review indicates that a well-trained patient authorship guide can help both patient and non-patient authors. Relevant to this point, the ISMPP Certified Medical Publication Professional exam now includes questions on patient authorship (Danita Sutton, ISMPP; personal communication); updates on this exam and other testing and training initiatives for patient authorship guides include a focus on respectful relationships. Another key consideration is sustaining relationships. For example, with the new patient and public involvement items in the SPIRIT [7] and CONSORT [8] reporting guidelines and the call for transparency about patient involvement in clinical trial design [54], proactive efforts should be made to sustain relationships with patients from their involvement in trial design through to their involvement to trial publications.

Strengths and limitations

The results of this research should be considered in terms of both its strengths and limitations.

The strengths of this project include: 1) addressing a topic that is important now and will continue to gain attention, particularly with the emergence of more patient-led research; 2) a priori registration of the protocol to reduce the risk of research waste, facilitate early feedback, and enable reproducibility; 3) use of previously published and proven methods and involvement of an information specialist for the search strategy; 4) access to a large dataset with data extracted directly from publications that had passed peer review; 5) use of both quantitative and qualitative analyses; 6) enhancing the practical application of results by identifying and organising citable evidence across the publication lifecycle, presenting research questions according to high unmet needs, and ensuring researchers retain agency in terms of selecting which questions to study and how to study them; 7) researcher plurality enabling multiple perspectives to inform the research, including that of a patient, academic, publication professional, peer reviewer and editor. The limitations of this project include: 1) use of the PubMed Advanced Search function on affiliations for patient-authored publications. This was only one part of the overall search strategy (see Additional File 3), but was deemed reasonable as some, but not all, patient-authored publications might have focused on patient authorship. Even with expert assistance from an information retrieval specialist and the use of both electronic and manual literature searching methods, potentially relevant publications could have been missed; 2) while search limits were minimal, the need for a cut-off date excluded potentially relevant publications, particularly given the recent increases in patient authorship publications; 3) while this research was informed by discussions with experts in patient authorship from different interest-holder groups, the author was solely responsible for the conduct and reporting of this research; 4) the use of frequency analysis for prioritizing evidence gaps provided a transparent, reproducible, and quantitative method, but lower frequency evidence gaps could still be a priority for some interest-holders; 4) while some research teams may have investigated evidence gaps, if they have not published their research in full in a peer-reviewed journal, it is difficult to know if a gap has been sufficiently addressed. Unfortunately and ironically, the publication rate of evidence on publication practices that may be presented at conferences targeting publication professionals is very low [61].

Conclusions

Patient authorship represents a profound shift in a centuries-old medical publishing model. Consistent with the principle of ‘nothing about us without us’, publications about patients are now being authored with patients. To help accelerate evidence-based best practices for ethical and effective patient authorship, a better understanding of patient authorship evidence is required - what is known, unknown, and needs to be known about patient authors and patient-authored publications? This is the first study to systematically address these key topics through a scoping review, gap analysis, and research roadmap. The findings, based on 1000 datapoints from 29 peer-reviewed publications on patient authorship, highlight that peer-reviewed evidence is emerging to help support patient authorship practices throughout the publication lifecycle. However, critical evidence gaps remain, particularly for proven best practices and methods to study patient authorship. The resulting research roadmap provides a practical, organised, and flexible guide to help researchers identify and address important evidence gaps that best align with their priorities. Collectively, the findings from this study lay the foundation to help ensure that patient authorship practices can be ethical, effective, and evidence based.
