Effect of Holder pasteurization on nutrient and immune component retention in human milk: a comparison between water and dry pasteurizers
Authors: Miori Tanaka, Midori Date, Kasumi Takayama, Nagisa Matsuura, Katsumi Mizuno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHolder pasteurization (HoP; 62.5 °C, 30 min) is the method used most frequently in human milk banks to reduce the risk of infection transmission through donor human milk (DHM), but it has the disadvantage of potentially affecting the contents of some bioactive milk components. Dry pasteurizers avoid water handling and are easier to operate than water pasteurizers; however, the effect of dry pasteurization on human milk quality has not been fully elucidated. The aim of this study was to determine the effect of HoP by water or dry pasteurization on the nutrient content of human milk.MethodsMilk samples were assigned to 4 groups: No-HoP (untreated milk), Sterifeed (water pasteurizer), Barkey (dry pasteurizer), and Racoon (dry pasteurizer). The processing temperature of each machine was measured over time. Levels of macronutrients, minerals, secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), lactoferrin, and human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) in DHM were analyzed before and after HoP.ResultsThe mean plateau temperature and the highest temperature of the water pasteurizer was more than 0.5 °C lower and more than 1 °C lower, respectively, than for the dry pasteurizers. Macronutrients, minerals, and HMOs were unaffected or minimally affected by the HoP process in experiments conducted using each type of pasteurizer, whereas sIgA and lactoferrin levels were decreased significantly. The losses of sIgA and lactoferrin through dry pasteurization were more than 10% greater than the losses incurred by water pasteurization. The respective reductions in sIgA, calculated from median values, were 23% for Sterifeed (P < 0.05), 42% for Barkey (P < 0.001), and 44% for Racoon (P < 0.001). The reductions in lactoferrin were 75%, 86%, and 86%, respectively (all P < 0.001). After the cooling process in the two dry pasteurizers, energy decreased by 6% and calcium decreased by 2% in Barkey (all P < 0.05), while lactoferrin increased by 3% in Racoon (P < 0.05). No other components showed significant changes.ConclusionsThese results show that dry pasteurization causes a higher reduction in immune components than water pasteurization, suggesting that dry method is not suitable for processing DHM.

Background

Human milk is the best nutrition for feeding all newborn infants due to its various benefits, including improved growth and cognitive development [1, 2]. Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months is recommended by the World Health Organization. Especially in preterm infants, breast-fed infants have a lower incidence of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), chronic lung disease, retinopathy of prematurity, and late-onset sepsis than formula-fed infants [3, 4]. Human milk contains not only macronutrients and minerals but also immune components (e.g., secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), lactoferrin), growth factors (e.g., epidermal growth factor, insulin-like growth factor), metabolic hormones (e.g., leptin, adiponectin), and human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) [5]. These bioactive substances have antimicrobial, antiviral, and immunomodulatory activities and contribute to the prevention of NEC and preterm birth complications [6–9].

Donor human milk (DHM) is the first alternative for preterm infants when their mother’s own milk is unavailable [10, 11]. DHM should be pasteurized to prevent the potential risk of pathogen transmission from donors to preterm infants. Holder pasteurization (HoP), a low-temperature and long-time method to heat milk at 62.5 °C for 30 min, is the current gold standard recommended by most international human milk bank guidelines [12, 13]. However, heat treatment including HoP greatly reduces immune components in human milk [14, 15], which may compromise protective effects against NEC and complications in preterm infants [7, 8]. Czank et al. [16]. demonstrated that the concentrations of sIgA, lactoferrin, and lysozyme dramatically decreased above 58 °C and with duration. Specifically, raising the pasteurization temperature from 57 °C to 58 °C reduced the retention of sIgA, lactoferrin, and lysozyme by 8.5%, 34%, and 31%, respectively, whereas 90% or more of all three components were retained at 57 °C. Therefore, the accurate control of the plateau duration (62.5 °C, 30 min), keeping the time as short as possible and the temperature as low as possible within the defined range, is important to reduce the loss of immune components.

Recently, in addition to water pasteurizers that heat bottles in a water bath, dry pasteurizers that consist of electrically heated aluminum alloy blocks with holes for bottles have been introduced into several human milk banks worldwide. The dry method is hygienic and simple to operate because no water is used, although it has been reported that the heating temperature is higher than that of the water method, resulting in lower retention of sIgA and lactoferrin [17]. Whereas water pasteurizers transfer heat mainly through convection in the surrounding water bath, dry pasteurizers rely on conduction from the heated metal blocks, leading to different heating profiles. In Japanese human milk banks, DHM is pasteurized by one water pasteurizer and two dry pasteurizers. However, no studies have evaluated the compositional changes of DHM before and after HoP for each machine in Japan. We hypothesized that differences in the retention of nutrients and immune components might occur among the three pasteurizers currently used in Japanese human milk banks. Furthermore, there is no information comparing water pasteurizers and dry pasteurizers in terms of the effects on nutrient contents other than immune components. The aim of this study was to investigate the comparative effects of water pasteurization and dry pasteurization on the concentrations of macronutrients, minerals, immune components, and HMOs in human milk. We also used two different dry pasteurizers to examine whether the difference in cooling time after HoP between machines affects the retention of milk components.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective observational study to determine the macronutrient, mineral, immune component, and HMO contents of human milk before and after HoP with three different pasteurizers, which are used in Japanese human milk banks. This study used three pasteurizers: one water pasteurizer [Sterifeed S90 (Sterifeed); S90/USB, MediCare Colgate, Kentisbeare, UK; temperature accuracy ± 0.5 °C], and two dry pasteurizers [Barkey clinitherm pasteur (Barkey); 10/80, Barkey, Leopoldshöhe, Germany; temperature accuracy ± 0.5 °C] and [Racoon dry pasteurizer (Racoon); HMP-4, Mita Rika Kogyo, Osaka, Japan; operated with a temperature control designed to maintain milk within 62.5–65 °C]. This study was conducted at The Nippon Foundation Human Milk Bank (TNFHMB) between January 2023 and August 2024. All donors provided written consent for using their human milk for research purposes. Ethical approval was obtained from the Showa Medical University Research Ethics Review Board (approval number: 2024-204-B). All experimental protocols were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Milk samples

Human milk samples were collected using a convenience sampling approach from 17 donors registered with TNFHMB who met the eligibility criteria. The donors included mothers of 13 term (gestational age ≥ 37 weeks) and 4 preterm (gestational age < 37 weeks) infants, with a gestational age range from 23 weeks to 41 weeks. Milk samples were included regardless of gestational age, the age of the child, or other donor characteristics. The demographic characteristics of the mothers and infants are shown in Table 1. The donor registration requirements included the ability to produce more human milk than what her child needs. Exclusion criteria included a history of blood transfusions or organ transplants and a history of treatment for malignancies such as leukemia or lymphoma in the past 3 years. All donors had completed a screening process consisting of a health questionnaire and a medical interview with doctors or midwives who reviewed the health questions and assessed the donor’s suitability. Blood screening test for the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV I/II), the human T-cell leukemia virus (HTLV I/II), hepatitis B and C, and syphilis was then undertaken. Milk was obtained by hand or pump expression. Each donor was taught the collecting techniques by our midwives. After expression, milk in clean bags was frozen and stored in a freezer at the donor’s home. Refrigerated transport was used to transfer milk from the donor’s home to TNFHMB under conditions of -15 °C (or lower), with a transport duration of 1–2 days. On arrival at TNFHMB, milk was stored at -30 °C until HoP.

Frozen milk was thawed in a refrigerator at 4 °C overnight and then pasteurized within 24 h. In preparation for HoP, thawed milk was pooled from multiple expression sessions of the same donor to reduce nutrient variability that may occur under varied conditions, including time of day and whether expression occurs before or after breastfeeding. If milk expressions spanned multiple postpartum weeks, the sample was assigned to the week corresponding to the first expression date. We collected samples before HoP from each pool, and then routine HoP treatment was performed with Sterifeed, Barkey, and Racoon.

For Sterifeed treatment, milk (230–250 mL) was divided into a single-use baby polypropylene bottle (250 mL; MediCare Colgate) and heated at 62.5 ± 0.5 °C for 30 min in hot water. After heating, Sterifeed can automatically cool milk to 4 °C by immersing it in cold water (typically 4 °C), and then milk samples were collected.

Barkey and Racoon pasteurizers consist of aluminum blocks that are heated by electrical heating elements. For Barkey treatment, milk (120–150 mL) was divided into a sterile single-use polypropylene bottle (150 mL; Medela, Baar, Switzerland) and heated at 62.5 ± 0.5 °C for 30 min. In Sterifeed and Barkey treatments, the pasteurization parameters were set at 62.5 ± 0.5 °C for 30 min with automatic temperature regulation of the pasteurization cycle achieved by use of a sensor-equipped reference bottle. By contrast, the Racoon pasteurizer does not use a sensor-equipped reference bottle for direct regulation of milk temperature. Instead, it regulates the temperature of the aluminum block via an independently controlled heating unit, which indirectly maintains the milk at 62.5–65 °C for 30 min. According to the manufacturer, the temperature of the aluminum block is set at 66 °C with an accuracy of ± 0.283 °C, and remains between 64.8 °C and 67.2 °C during the pasteurization process. For Racoon treatment, milk (80–100 mL) was divided into a disposable baby polypropylene bottle (100 mL; Mita Rika Kogyo). After HoP with Barkey and Racoon pasteurizers, milk samples were collected before cooling, and the milk was chilled to 10 °C in a freezer (-30 °C). After cooling, milk samples were collected.

Therefore, our investigated conditions were as follows: No-HoP, Sterifeed (post-cooling), Barkey (pre-cooling), Barkey (post-cooling), Racoon (pre-cooling), and Racoon (post-cooling) (Fig. 1). To assess the nutrient content of human milk before and after HoP using the three different pasteurizers, we compared No-HoP, Sterifeed (post-cooling), Barkey (post-cooling), and Racoon (post-cooling). To determine the effect of the cooling duration of dry pasteurization on human milk composition, we compared Barkey (pre-cooling) with Barkey (post-cooling) or Racoon (pre-cooling) with Racoon (post-cooling). A 7 mL sample was taken from each pool and stored at -30 °C to measure macronutrient levels, and a 2 mL sample was taken and stored at -80 °C to measure mineral, immune component, and HMO levels. We have confirmed that no bacteria were detected in bacteria culture tests after HoP.

Measurement of time and temperature

During each Sterifeed treatment, the temperature was recorded every 15 s for each cycle of pasteurization in a reference bottle and the data were stored on external hard drives. During Barkey and Racoon treatments, we continuously monitored the temperature in additional bottles every 10 s and captured the data using a digital data logger (Elitech RC-4HC; Elitech, London, UK; accuracy ± 0.5 °C). The temperature probe was placed in the middle of the bottles. As we experimentally confirmed that the time-temperature curve for water was similar to that of human milk under the same heating conditions, reference bottles were filled with water in all pasteurizers to reduce the amount of wasted milk. We used 4 quality control parameters for HoP: duration of plateau (≥ 62.5 °C), mean temperature during plateau, highest temperature, and time from 62.5 to 10 °C. Four sets of pasteurization cycles were recorded in all pasteurizers for this study, and mean time and temperature in each parameter were calculated.

Measurement of macronutrients in human milk

Macronutrient content was measured via mid-infrared transmission spectroscopy using a Miris Human Milk Analyzer (Miris AB, Uppsala, Sweden) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Milk samples were heated to 40 °C in a thermal block and homogenized at 1.5 s/mL using a Miris Ultrasonic Processor (Miris AB), and then 2.5 mL of sample was injected into the cuvette via syringe. Heating at 40 °C is part of the standard sample preparation protocol recommended by the manufacturer; it has not been reported to affect fat emulsification or measurement accuracy. The components measured by the analyzer (all in g/dL) were crude protein (reference method: Kjeldahl), fat (reference method: Röse-Gottlieb), and carbohydrate. Energy was calculated by the formula: 4.40 kcal × crude protein (g) + 9.25 kcal × fat (g) + 4.00 kcal × carbohydrate (g). The analyzer has demonstrated accuracy of ± 13% for carbohydrate and ± 10% for crude protein, fat, and energy, and has been previously shown to be adequate for macronutrient measurement of human milk [18]. Analyses were performed within approximately 3 weeks after HoP.

Measurement of minerals in human milk

The concentrations of calcium (Ca), inorganic phosphorus (IP), and zinc (Zn) were measured by a colorimetric assay using the Accuras Auto Ca Ⅱ (Shino-Test, Tokyo, Japan), Accuras Auto IP (Shino-Test), and Accuras Auto Zn (Shino-Test), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Milk samples were diluted 5-fold with a diluted solution (Shino-Test). These biochemistry tests were performed by a Clinical Chemistry Analyzer CA-270 (Furuno Electric, Hyogo, Japan). Before measuring milk samples, we checked that the concentrations of Aalto Control LEVEL Ⅰ α (Shino-Test), Aalto Control LEVEL Ⅱ α (Shino-Test), and Zn control (Shino-Test) were within expected ranges for each run. Analyses were performed within approximately 5 weeks after HoP.

The reliability of this colorimetric method for human milk has been confirmed. Intra-assay coefficients of variation were consistently low (≤ 7.4%), recovery rates were within 94.5–104.9%, and significant correlations were observed between Ca, IP, and Zn concentrations determined by this colorimetric method and those measured by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy.

Measurement of immune components in human milk

sIgA and lactoferrin levels were determined by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Milk samples were centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove the fat layer. The concentrations of sIgA were measured using a Secretory Immunoglobulin A ELISA Kit (Immundiagnostik, Bensheim, Germany). Lactoferrin levels were assayed using a Human Lactoferrin ELISA Kit (Immunology Consultants Laboratory, Portland, OR, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Analyses were performed within approximately 5 weeks after HoP. All samples were measured in duplicate to ensure reliability.

Measurement of HMOs in human milk

6 neutral HMOs were measured by high-performance anion-exchange chromatography-pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC-PAD). Milk samples were centrifuged at 14000 g for 30 min at 4°C to remove the fat layer, and the lower aqueous layer was collected. This operation was repeated twice. Collected samples were applied to 10 kDa Amicon Ultra 0.5-mL filters (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and centrifuged at 14000 g for 30 min at 4°C to remove protein. Samples were diluted with ultrapure water, reaching a final dilution factor of 500, and 2.5 µL of each sample was injected to an ICS 6000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a CarboPac PA-200 column (1 × 250 mm, 302861, Thermo Fisher Scientific), which was preceded by a CarboPac PA guard column (1 × 50 mm, 302862, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Eluents were generated electrolytically using ultrapure water and eluent generator cartridges that produced potassium hydroxide (KOH) within the instrument. The gradient program was as follows: 0–12 min, 20 mM KOH; 12–20 min, from 20 mM KOH to 50 mM KOH; 20–30 min, from 50 mM KOH to 100 mM KOH; 30–41 min, from 100 mM KOH to 200 mM KOH; and 41–48 min, 200 mM KOH. Before starting the gradient, the column was equilibrated with 20 mM KOH for 20 min. The flow rate was 50 µL/min. A pulsed amperometric detector with a disposable gold electrode utilizing the Dionex Gold-Carbo-Quad waveform (detection potential + 0.1 V maintained for 400 ms, followed by 10 ms at -2.0 V, a rapid increase to + 0.6 V, and 60 ms at -0.1 V [19]) monitored the HMO signals. All samples were measured within 2 months, which minimized the deterioration of the column over time. Chromeleon version 7.3.1 was used for instrument control and analysis of chromatograms (Fig. 2). The peaks were integrated using valley-to-valley baseline or vertical division, depending on the condition. HMOs were quantified by comparison with a multipoint calibration curve obtained from the corresponding standard: 2’-fucosyllactose (2’-FL) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 3’-fucosyllactose (3’-FL), lactodifucotetraose (LDFT), lacto-N-tetraose (LNT) (Biosynth, Staad, Switzerland), lacto-N-fucopentaose I (LNFP-I), and lacto-N-difucohexaose I (LNDFH-I) (Dextra Laboratories, Reading, UK). Analyses were performed more than 1 year after HoP.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 10 software package (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). All continuous variables were tested for normality by a D’Agostino–Pearson test. Nonparametric data for maternal age, body mass index, gestational age, postpartum week, infant birth weight, and nutrient content of human milk were shown as the median and interquartile range (IQR). For nonparametric variables, comparisons between two groups were performed by a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, whereas a Friedman test followed by a Bonferroni-adjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used for multiple comparisons. The parametric variables of time and temperature were expressed as the mean and standard deviation (SD). For parametric variables, differences among groups were assessed using a repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test. All percentage changes reported in the Results were calculated from median values. The sample size (n = 17) was sufficient for detecting differences with ≥ 80% power and P < 0.05 based on a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software [20]. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.

Results

Time and temperature parameters for pasteurization cycles

The time and temperature measurements during the pasteurization cycles using three different pasteurizers are summarized in Table 2, and the time-temperature curves are shown in Fig. 3. There were significant differences between the three pasteurizers for all quality control parameters, and these differences were mostly between the Sterifeed and Racoon treatments. The duration of plateau in the Racoon treatment was 1.3 and 1.5 times longer than those in Sterifeed and Barkey treatments, respectively (all P < 0.05). The Sterifeed mean temperature during plateau was 0.8 °C lower than Barkey (P = 0.133) and 1.3 °C lower than Racoon (P < 0.01).The highest temperature for Sterifeed was 1.3 °C lower than Barkey (P = 0.106) and 1.4 °C lower than Racoon (P < 0.01). Furthermore, the cooling time from 62.5 °C to 10 °C in Sterifeed was 2.9 times shorter than Barkey (P < 0.01) and 2.0 times shorter than Racoon (P < 0.05).

Effect of HoP with different pasteurizers on nutrient and immune component retention in human milk

The levels of macronutrients, minerals, immune components, and HMOs before and after HoP are shown in Table 3. Compared with No-HoP, none of the pasteurization treatments caused significant changes in the concentrations of protein, IP, Zn, 2’-FL, 3’-FL, LDFT, LNFP-I, LNDFH-I, and LNT. Fat levels in Racoon were 6% lower than in No-HoP (P < 0.05). The concentrations of carbohydrate in Sterifeed were 1% lower than in No-HoP (P < 0.05). Compared with No-HoP, energy levels were 15% and 7% lower in Barkey and Racoon, respectively (all P < 0.05). In all pasteurizers, Ca, sIgA, and lactoferrin levels were significantly decreased after HoP. Ca levels were 0.5%, 4%, and 3% lower in Sterifeed, Barkey, and Racoon, respectively (P < 0.01, P < 0.001, and P < 0.001). The reductions in immune components were much higher after HoP with the dry pasteurizers than with Sterifeed. The respective percentage reductions in sIgA for Sterifeed, Barkey, and Racoon treatments were 23%, 42%, and 44% (P < 0.05, P < 0.001, and P < 0.001). The respective reductions in lactoferrin were 75%, 86%, and 86% (all P < 0.001).

Effect of cooling duration on human milk composition

To determine whether the cooling duration after HoP affected the nutritional composition of human milk, we measured the levels of macronutrients, minerals, immune components, and HMOs before cooling and after cooling for Barkey and Racoon treatments. As shown in Table 4, energy and Ca levels were decreased by 6% and 2%, respectively, after cooling in Barkey (all P < 0.05). In Racoon treatment, the concentrations of lactoferrin increased by 3% after cooling (P < 0.05). There were no significant changes in protein, fat, carbohydrate, IP, Zn, sIgA, and HMO levels between before and after cooling in both Barkey and Racoon treatments (Wilcoxon signed-rank test).

Discussion

Preterm infants have higher nutritional needs than term infants; however, changes in DHM components, particularly immune components such as sIgA and lactoferrin, after HoP represent a major challenge in preventing complications and ensuring adequate postnatal growth. For better preservation of immune components, methods other than HoP, such as high-temperature and short-time pasteurization, high-pressure processing, ultraviolet irradiation, and ultrasonic processing, have been developed [21, 22]. However, data on the microbiological safety of these novel technologies remain scarce, and the viability of their routine use in human milk banks is yet to be assessed. This study compared water and dry pasteurization to identify HoP methods that better preserve DHM components.

The analysis of macronutrients and minerals revealed a slight decrease in fat, carbohydrate, and Ca in DHM after HoP with some pasteurizers, with no significant changes in protein, IP, and Zn. Several studies have also shown a slight decrease [23, 24] or no significant change [25, 26] of macronutrients in DHM following HoP. The differences between No-HoP and HoP (fat: 6% in Racoon; carbohydrate: 1% in Sterifeed) were similar to those observed in other studies (fat: 4.8%–5.5%; lactose: 0.9%–1.3%) [23, 24]. Additionally, most minerals were reported to be largely preserved after HoP [25, 27], except for iron (Fe), which decreased by 36% [28]. Braga et al. [25]. reported a difference of 4% between Ca levels before and after HoP, which is consistent with our data (0.5%, 4%, and 3% for Sterifeed, Barkey, and Racoon, respectively). Indeed, macronutrient and mineral levels of DHM in this study were within the range of previously reported values for non-pasteurized human milk (e.g., protein 1.2 ± 0.3 g/dL, fat 3.5 ± 1.5 g/dL, Ca 25.0 ± 7.1 mg/dL) [29]. In view of previous findings and our data, the influence of HoP on macronutrient and mineral contents is not a significant clinical problem, regardless of the type of pasteurizer. Furthermore, the concentrations of 6 HMOs were unaffected by HoP regardless of the pasteurizer type, which is in accordance with a previous study using a water pasteurizer [30]. As 2’-FL is known to protect against NEC via anti-inflammatory pathways [9], well-preserved HMOs may contribute greatly to NEC prevention in preterm infants using DHM.

Immune components in human milk, such as sIgA and lactoferrin, support neonatal immune defense and have been shown to reduce the incidence of NEC and late-onset sepsis in preterm infants [31, 32]. Protein generally denatures rapidly as the temperature increases. Heat treatment at 57 °C had minimal impact on sIgA and lactoferrin with retention rates above 90%, whereas the loss rates of immune components dramatically increased above 58 °C and with duration (0.5%/min for sIgA and 1.4%/min for lactoferrin at 58 °C) [16]. In fact, increased retention rate of immune components was observed when optimized HoP was achieved by reducing the water pasteurizer mean temperature and plateau duration by 1.5 °C and 11 min, respectively [33]. The reduction rates in previous reports using water pasteurizers were approximately 25%–30% for sIgA and 65%–80% for lactoferrin [33, 34], and reductions measured in our study were within this range when using Sterifeed. However, use of the dry pasteurizers Barkey and Racoon resulted in a 10% greater reduction in immune components and a higher heating temperature when compared with the water pasteurizer Sterifeed, which corresponds to a previous study [17]. We also observed that the duration of plateau in the Racoon treatment was significantly longer than in Sterifeed and Barkey treatments. Unlike Sterifeed and Barkey, which regulate the pasteurization cycle using a sensor-equipped reference bottle, Racoon controls the temperature of the aluminum block. This indirect regulation may explain the increase in milk temperature and the observed extension of the plateau duration. A previous study showed that the retention of immune components is more sensitive to heating temperature than to time. A 1.7% increase in temperature (from 57 °C to 58 °C) decreased the retention of sIgA and lactoferrin by 8.5% and 34%, respectively, whereas a similar proportional change in holding time resulted in only a 0.68% decrease in sIgA and a 0.95% decrease in lactoferrin [16]. In this study, although Barkey had a shorter plateau duration than Sterifeed, it exhibited comparable immune component loss to Raccoon. These findings support the interpretation that the higher temperatures in the dry pasteurizers are the major factor driving greater losses, although we cannot completely exclude the possibility that plateau duration or cooling time may also contribute.

The heating temperature and time in HoP process depend on various factors, including milk volume, milk composition, bottle material, and bottle thickness. In the present study, we used polypropylene bottles in all pasteurizers, but we could not account for bottle thickness because the bottles were provided with each pasteurizer. With regard to milk volume, the volume of milk processed was larger in the order of Sterifeed, Barkey, and Racoon (230–250 mL, 120–150 mL, and 80–100 mL, respectively). Previous studies have revealed a significant association between milk volume and heating time, with a longer duration for larger volume in both water pasteurizers and dry pasteurizers [35, 36]. However, Müller et al. [17]. demonstrated that dry pasteurization resulted in a higher heating temperature and greater reductions in sIgA and lactoferrin than water pasteurization, even when the milk volume was standardized. Therefore, it is likely that the difference in heating method between water pasteurization and dry pasteurization, rather than milk volume, accounts for the difference in heating temperature and time. Temperature fluctuation during HoP is different according to the type of pasteurizer; for example, temperature was controlled more precisely and homogeneously between different bottles in a water pasteurizer than in an air pasteurizer [35]. We also found that pasteurization temperature and time were very similar among different HoP cycles in Sterifeed treatment, but they were extremely heterogenous with differences in mean temperature of up to 1.2 °C and plateau duration of up to 6.3 min from cycle to cycle in Barkey and Racoon treatments. These results indicate that the good homogeneity of temperature between cycles and bottles in water pasteurization might contribute in part to the higher retention rate of immune components and highlight the importance of pasteurization cycle verification in dry systems. HoP criteria are not standardized in human milk bank guidelines worldwide, many of which do not yet define maximum heating temperature or time. Based on previous findings and our own, it will be necessary to provide more detailed HoP guidelines on heating temperature and time in the near future.

The European Milk Bank Association recently recommended some criteria for HoP, including the plateau temperature, plateau duration, and cooling duration [37]. In contrast, human milk bank guidelines in other regions only define the holding temperature and time for HoP (62.5 °C, 30 min) and do not state the cooling period. Hence, we tested the effect of cooling time on DHM composition using two dry pasteurizers in this study. The time from 62.5 °C to 10 °C in Barkey treatment was 17.4 min longer than in Racoon treatment, confirming a previous result showing a longer cooling duration for larger bottles in a dry pasteurizer [36]. In addition, the levels of macronutrients, minerals, immune components, and HMOs were comparable before and after cooling in both Barkey and Racoon treatments, with energy and Ca in Barkey decreasing by 6% and 2%, respectively, and lactoferrin in Racoon increasing by 3%. The slight increase in lactoferrin might reflect analytical variance or partial resolubilization of proteins during cooling. We also demonstrated that cooling DHM at 4 °C increased the cooling time by approximately 1 h longer than cooling at -30 °C, but there was no difference in the retention of immune components at either temperature. When cooling at 4 °C, the respective reductions in sIgA were 43% for Barkey and 43% for Racoon, and reductions in lactoferrin were 86% and 89%, respectively. Thus, we speculated that the difference in cooling duration was not responsible for the significant loss of immune components in dry pasteurization relative to water pasteurization, as observed in this study. This is consistent with previous evidence showing that immune proteins such as sIgA and lactoferrin are largely preserved at temperatures below 58 °C [16]; therefore, once DHM cooled below this threshold, prolongation of cooling was unlikely to cause further protein degradation.

Limitations

A limitation of our study is that several factors, such as milk volumes and bottle thickness, were not considered, and the bottle volumes were not identical in all pasteurizers. However, dry pasteurization has been reported to have a higher heating temperature and higher loss of immune components than water pasteurization, irrespective of milk volume [17], which agrees with our results. Using this work as a pilot study, further improvements of dry pasteurizers are needed to optimize HoP cycles and preserve the immune components in DHM.

Conclusions

In conclusion, dry pasteurization resulted in a higher reduction in immune components than water pasteurization, suggesting that dry method is not suitable for processing DHM. These results indicate the importance of accurately monitoring the temperature during HoP cycles to reduce the loss of immune components and improve the quality of DHM.
