Myocardial Infarction With Normal Coronary Arteries in 2 Patients With Patent Foramen Ovale

MINOCA in 2 PFO Patients

We report 2 cases of AMI due to suspected paradoxical coronary artery embolism through a PFO. In case 1, emergency coronary angiography showed a thrombus occluding the distal diagonal branch of the left anterior descending coronary artery in an otherwise normal coronary circulation. No angiographic evidence of coronary embolism or coronary artery disease was present in case 2, who presented a cryptogenic cerebral ischemic event shortly after AMI. Both patients underwent transcatheter PFO closure for the prevention of recurrent thromboembolic events. Paradoxical coronary artery embolism (PCAE) is a rare and underdiagnosed cause of about 3% of myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) cases.1 We describe 2 patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) attributable to patent foramen ovale (PFO)–mediated PCAE. In both patients, 2-dimensional (2D) transthoracic/transesophageal echocardiography (TTE/TEE) with color Doppler and contrast-enhanced transcranial Doppler ultrasound (c-TCD) demonstrated a PFO with a significant right-to-left shunt (RLS).Take-Home Messages•Paradoxical coronary artery embolism causing myocardial infarction is rare and requires a high degree of clinical suspicion for diagnosis.•Percutaneous patent foramen ovale closure should be considered to prevent future embolic events.

Case 1

A 42-year-old female ex-smoker with a history of refractory aura migraine and combined oral contraceptive pill for heavy menstrual bleeding presented to the emergency department with acute chest pain. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm and ST-segment depression in the lateral leads. Cardiac biomarkers were elevated (peak high-sensitive troponin I: 16.70 ng/mL). A diagnosis of AMI was made.

Coronary angiography revealed distal occlusion of the first diagonal branch of the left anterior descending coronary artery, with an otherwise normal coronary tree. Prolonged intravenous glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor infusion was performed after percutaneous coronary intervention and aspiration thrombectomy failed to restore vessel perfusion. Diagnostic work-up with 2D TTE/TEE with color Doppler showed normal findings but revealed a PFO with a long tunnel (14 mm) without atrial septal aneurysm (Figure 1A). RLS was observed, with c-TCD showing 13 microbubbles under basal conditions (Figure 1B) and >50 microbubbles (“shower” pattern) after Valsalva maneuver (Figure 1C). A probable paradoxical embolism in a hypercoagulable state was suspected. However, a hypercoagulability work-up was negative, and lower limb Doppler assessment showed no evidence of deep venous thrombosis. Carotid artery Doppler ultrasound was also normal. The RoPE (Risk of Paradoxical Embolism) score was 8, confirming high probability that PFO was pathogenetically related to the index event (PFO-attributable fraction: 84%). Similarly, according to the PASCAL (PFO-Associated Stroke Causal Likelihood) classification system, this patient with ≥7 RoPE score was stratified as having a probable causal relatedness. After heart team discussion, transcatheter PFO closure was recommended.Figure 1Long-Tunnel PFO With Significant Right-to-Left(A) Two-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography color Doppler at 70° showing a 14-mm long tunnel patent foramen ovale (red arrows). Case 2

A 47-year-old active male smoker with a history of dyslipidemia treated with bempedoic acid/ezetimibe therapy was admitted to the coronary care unit for sudden onset of oppressive chest pain radiating to the left arm. High-sensitive troponin I was elevated (peak value: 1,562 ng/L) without ischemic changes on a 12-lead electrocardiogram.

Given the characteristics of the chest pain, prompt coronary angiography was performed and showed normal coronary arteries. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) revealed hypokinesia of the posteroinferior wall of the left ventricle with myocardial edema and transmural late gadolinium enhancement, consistent with a recent ischemic injury (Figures 3A and 3B). Additionally, a tunnel-like space was shown, raising the suspicion of a PFO (Figure 3C). This was confirmed by 2D TTE/TEE with color Doppler, while 2D contrast TTE (bubble test) showed significant RLS (40 microbubbles after Valsalva maneuver). Major arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation, were ruled out by 1-week Holter monitoring. Thus, MINOCA caused by PCAE was considered a plausible diagnostic hypothesis, and the patient was placed on a waiting list for a PFO closure procedure and was discharged in good clinical condition on dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg/d and clopidogrel 75 mg/d). A week later, he was admitted to a stroke unit for sudden deviation of the labial commissure to the left side, slurred speech, and left arm hyposthenia. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed a cortico-subcortical ischemic lesion in the angular gyrus. Neurological symptoms progressively improved. Repeat contrast TEE/TEE and c-TCD confirmed a large RLS after Valsalva maneuver. Discussion

The occurrence of AMI in the absence of demonstrable coronary artery disease, first described in autopsy studies by Gross and Sternberg in 1939,2 has been reported in a proportion ranging from 2% to 10% of patients presenting with AMI.3 Beltrame4 first proposed the concept of MINOCA in 2013. Coronary artery embolism is recognized as an important nonatherosclerotic cause of MINOCA, with a prevalence ranging between 4% and 13%. The occurrence of PCAE through a PFO with RLS is another cause of AMI1 that has been documented in case reports and cohort studies.5, 6, 7 Its prevalence is difficult to estimate because this type of embolic-mediated AMI is often underdiagnosed, and ante mortem detection is difficult. The probability for AMI to be PFO mediated is dependent on many patient characteristics, including age, low-risk profile for atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, conditions predisposing to a hypercoagulable state, and angiographic features consistent with a visible thrombus in an otherwise normal coronary tree.

Although there is no absolute certainty that the AMI of our 2 patients was caused by coronary embolism due to a paradoxical thrombotic embolus passing through the PFO, there are many factors pointing to this pathogenic mechanism. They include the relatively young age of both, the hormone therapy, a potential predisposing prothrombogenic factor, and angiographic evidence of coronary artery thrombus without atherosclerotic lesions in case 1 and the cryptogenic cerebral ischemic event shortly after AMI in case 2 that similarly showed a normal coronary tree. The absence of deep venous thrombosis on Doppler examination should come as no surprise. Indeed, this is rarely detected in cases of presumed paradoxical embolism because the sensitivity of this test is suboptimal for the detection of small thrombi, particularly when located below the knee. Thus, the absence of lower limb venous thrombosis does not have strong negative predictive value.

There are 2 possible explanations for the absence of coronary artery obstruction or thromboembolism at the time of AMI in case 2. The first could be that the embolic thrombus underwent spontaneous lysis before coronary angiography. This hypothesis may be supported by the study of Sporns et al8 who, using stent retrievers for extraction and histological analysis of thrombi in ischemic stroke patients, demonstrated that cardioembolic thrombi have higher amounts of fibrin compared to noncardioembolic thrombi. An alternative explanation could be that the ischemic event was the result of intense coronary vasospasm and was not a paradoxical embolus.9 Indeed, we can hypothesize that a vasoactive substance, such as serotonin, may have bypassed degradation in the lungs and entered the arterial system through the PFO in a higher concentration than normal, triggering intense coronary spasm that resulted in AMI. The transmural late gadolinium enhancement detected on CMR in this patient is the typical finding of myocardial ischemic events. Indeed, CMR with late gadolinium enhancement is the most sensitive noninvasive imaging technique to detect even small myocardial infarction. In this regard, it is interesting to note that subclinical myocardial infarctions diagnosed with CMR were observed in 10.8% of patients with PFO after a first cryptogenic cerebral ischemic event, strengthening its pathophysiologic role.10

The management of PCAE is similar to that of acute coronary syndrome due to atherosclerosis and consists in emergent coronary angiography. The failure of manual thrombus aspiration in case 1 may be explained by the fact that crossing into the distal lumen of the diagonal branch with the aspiration device was challenging and moved more distally the thrombus. In PCAE cases, an embolic source should be sought, including venous thrombosis, hypercoagulable state and PFO. Thus, screening with agitated saline bubble study by 2D TTE/TEE or c-TCD is key. If a PFO is found, its closure should be considered.

Conclusions

Our cases support the pathophysiologic role of PFO in acute coronary syndrome patients with normal coronary arteries and highlight the need for an individualized patient approach for diagnosis and management by an expert cardiology and neurology team. In addition to management of acute coronary syndrome, PFO closure should be considered for the prevention of recurrent thromboembolic events.
