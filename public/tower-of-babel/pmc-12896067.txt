Evaluation of IR BIOTYPER® as a new tool for Enterobacter cloacae complex typing and its potential in management of nosocomial infections
Authors: Lucía Pérez Rodríguez, Jorge García García, Violeta García Casas, Alberto Vázquez Blanquiño, Ana Alberola Romano, Esther Recacha, Inés Portillo Calderón, Natalia Chueca, Federico García
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional typing methods, such as pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) and Whole genome sequencing (WGS) have limitations in terms of labor intensity, cost, and delayed results. Background

Multidrug-resistant bacteria are a serious threat to public health. In recent years, the World Health Organization (WHO) and United Nations (UN) have collaborated in concerted efforts to combat and mitigate this challenge that demands prompt action [1–3]. Species within the Enterobacter cloacae complex exhibit high levels of resistance to current antibiotics and can readily acquire carbapenemase activity, significantly limiting available treatment options ( [3]– [4]). Controlling the spread of these highly resistant bacteria is particularly challenging due to the scarcity of effective therapies and their efficient transmission within hospital settings, often leading to healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) [5]. According to the WHO, approximately 40,000 deaths occur annually due to HCAIs. The prevalence of nosocomial infections is as high as 25% in middle-income countries and 5–15% in high-income countries [6–8]. A European study revealed that for every 20 hospitalized patients, at least one acquires a preventable HCAI [9].

In order to ensure hospital hygiene and prevent the occurrence of such infections, it is imperative to implement stringent control measures and robust bacterial typing methodologies for comprehensive epidemiological analyses. Thus, a pressing need has emerged within clinical microbiology laboratories for a rapid and efficient methodology for detection and typing of pathogens that cause outbreaks at hospital level. Traditionally, clinical microbiology laboratories have relied on typing methods based on both phenotypic and genotypic techniques. These include molecular biology techniques with high discriminatory power, such as multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), and more recently, whole-genome sequencing (WGS). Nevertheless, these techniques are labor-intensive and require highly trained and skilled personnel. Additionally, their high cost together with delayed time to results has limited their implementation in most clinical microbiology laboratories [10].

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) can be used to differentiate bacterial strains by analyzing the absorption of infrared light by different molecules present in the bacterial cell wall. Thus, it is possible to analyze the composition of lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, especially carbohydrates and lipopolysaccharides [11]. FTIR-based systems, such as the IR Biotyper®, have already been successfully applied to the typing of other clinically relevant microorganisms, including Klebsiella pneumoniae [12], Staphylococcus aureus, and Non-Fermenting Gram-Negative Bacteria like Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa or Stenotrophomonas maltophilia [13] demonstrating good concordance with established molecular typing methods. In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the IR Biotyper® system in typing carbapenemase-producing E. cloacae complex, to assess its potential as an alternative for the early detection of multidrug-resistant bacteria transmission within the hospital environment.

Methods

Bacterial isolates and sample preparation

A total of 72 strains of carbapenem-resistant E. cloacae complex recovered from routine microbiological samples were collected between 2018 and 2022. Isolates were identified by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, and their susceptibility testing was performed using the broth microdilution technique with MicroScan Walkaway-96 system®.

Regarding the preparation of the isolates for their analysis in IR Biotyper®, to ensure consistency, all isolates were seeded on Mueller-Hinton agar and incubated for 24 h at 37 ± 2◦C following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, a 1-µl overloaded loop of bacteria was resuspended in 100 µL of deionized water in an IR Biotyper® suspension vial, containing metal cylinders for homogenization, incubated at 95 °C for 10 min, and after centrifugation and vortexing, 15 µL of each isolate suspension was pipetted into four wells of a 96-spot silicon sample plate. The sample plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 10–20 min and subsequently processed using IR Biotyper®.

FTIR spectroscopy analysis

For the Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis, the IR Biotyper® system was used (Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany; Part No. 1845471). Four consistent spectra for each isolate were acquired, corresponding to four technical replicates. Acquisition process was initiated using IRBT Client software (version 3.1) with default analysis settings. Spectra (4000–500 cm–1) of the isolates and background were acquired. Each spectrum was collected in single-beam mode with 32 scans, at a resolution of 4 cm1 at a rate of 3 scans/s. The complete spectra range from 4000 to 500 cm− 1 was automatically processed, normalized, and the second derivative was calculated using OPUS 8.2 software (Bruker Optics GmbH). Data that did not meet the default quality criteria (0.4 < absorption < 2, signal/noise > 40, signal/water > 20, fringes [910-6] < 100) were excluded from further analysis. Data were analyzed using offline IRBT Client by constructing dendrograms with the Euclidian distance and the UPGMA [14] clustering method.

Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis analysis

PFGE analysis of XbaI (Enterobacterales)-digested DNA (http://www.cdc.gov/pulsenet) was used to determine the degree of genetic relatedness between isolates [15]. A dendrogram was created with Bionumerics 8.1 software (bioMérieux), using the Dice coefficient with position tolerance settings of 1% optimization and 1% band position tolerance. Isolates differing by two or more bands in PFGE assays were assigned to a different pulsotype.

Whole-genome sequencing analysis

WGS of Enterobacter cloacae complex isolates was performed. All isolates were seeded on MacConkey Agar and subsequently, bacterial DNA extraction was performed using QIAamp DNA Kits for DNA Extraction (QIAGEN®), based on a spin-column procedure. Eluted DNA was quantified and sequenced using the Illumina DNA Prep kit (formerly Nextera Flex; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and run on the Illumina NextSeq 1000 platform. The raw sequencing reads (FASTQ files) have been submitted to the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject ID PRJNA1251642, with accession numbers ranging from SAMN48012119 to SAMN48012186. The requested BioProject and accession numbers have been included in Additional File 1.

Quality of raw reads was assessed with FASTQC v0.12.1 and low-quality regions and adapter sequences were trimmed using FASTP v0.23.4 (minimum Phred score of 15, maximum 50% low-quality bases, maximum 5 ambiguous bases per read, minimum read length 40 bp). Resulting high-quality reads were assembled using MEGAHIT v1.2.9 to generate contigs for each isolate. Annotation of contigs was carried out with PROKKA v1.14.15. To evaluate genome completeness, cleaned reads were mapped back to their corresponding assemblies using Bowtie2, and samtools depth was used to calculate the mean sequencing depth and percentage of the genome covered by at least one read. Genomes were included in downstream analyses only if they had a low number of contigs, mean sequencing depth ≥ 10, and genome coverage ≥ 75%.

ROARY v3.11.2 was used to explore the pangenome and determine the set of core genes, and to produce the multiple sequence alignment of these core genes. Then, a phylogenetic tree was generated from the core gene alignment using FASTTREE v2.1.11 with 100 bootstrap replicates to assess the robustness of the tree topology. The resulting tree was visualized using the R package ggtree, with bootstrap values and branch lengths representing evolutionary distances. Additionally, to assess the single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) distances between isolates, Snippy v4.6.0 was employed. The reference genome of E. cloacae chosen for the alignment was GCF_905331265.2. SNPs-based alignments were represented as a phylogenetic tree as previously described.

Finally, the sequence type (ST) of each isolate was determined using the assembled genomes as input to the PubMLST database [16]. This combination of pangenome, SNP-based, and MLST analyses provided a comprehensive characterization of the isolates’ genetic relationships. Additionally, genomic resistance profiling was conducted by generating the resistance profile of each sequenced isolate using the Staramr tool.

Estimation of the cut-off value (COV)

In order to assess the appropriate cut-off value for E. cloacae complex, following the recommendations by Hu et al. [17], three separate tests were carried out (A, B & C). Each evaluation consisted of a random selection of ten E. cloacae complex isolates, representing five distinct sequence types identified by multilocus sequence typing. The MLST results were used as a reference to establish the optimal cut-off value in IR Biotyper®. The COVs were calculated as the average distance between the nodes constituting a cluster and the maximum distance between the two current nodes/clusters. The actual cut-off value was established as the average of the COVs of each analysis.

Ability of IR Biotyper® to annotate bacterial isolates

PFGE was used to initially characterize our 66 E. cloacae complex isolates. In order to evaluate FTIR as a typing method, we have used the cut-off value obtained as described in the previous paragraph for analyzing the same 66 strains as above using IR Biotyper®. We have then compared these results with those obtained through PFGE and WGS.

Calculation of clustering concordance

We used an online tool, specifically www.comparingpartitions.info, to assess quantitative data belonging to the discriminatory power and concordance of various typing methods. In order to measure the discriminatory power of each method, we employed Simpson’s index of diversity (SID). To analyze the concordance of IR Biotyper® typing results with PFGE and WGS results, the adjusted Rand index (ARI) with 95% confidence interval was used [14]. The ARI value can range from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating agreement by random chance and 1 representing a perfect correlation between the two methods.

Real world use for the methodology described

Six E. cloacae complex isolates were prospectively analyzed with IR Biotyper® during a suspected hospital outbreak and compared using whole-genome sequencing (Illumina Nextera protocol on the NextSeq 1000 platform, as described in a previous paragraph). SNP analysis was performed using the highest-quality isolate (sample 864340) as reference, selected based on completeness, contamination, contig number, and N50. The analysis included comparison with publicly available genome of the same sequence type (ST666) GCF_034008275.1.

Compared to PFGE, IRBT was able to distinguish all spectra of the 66 E. cloacae complex isolates from eight sequence types, categorizing them into nine distinct IR types. IR Biotyper® clustered the 34 ST78 isolates into two different IR types, with one of these types comprising only a single sample. This sole inconsistency observed involved sample 382,664, in which IRBT was categorized as the sole member of cluster B, whereas the PFGE method assigned it to a the larger ST78 cluster. WGS for this discordant sample confirmed that it belonged to sequence type 78, rather than a different cluster as predicted by IR Biotyper®. To quantify the genetic relatedness and address this discrepancy, we performed a SNP-based distance analysis among isolates. The resulting SNP distance matrix and hierarchical clustering are presented in Additional Fig. 1, demonstrating that isolate 382,664 clusters closely with other ST78 isolates, supporting its inclusion in this sequence type despite its distinct IRBT profile.

Comparison between PFGE, IR Biotyper® and WGS is shown in Fig. 4. Simpson’s Index of Diversity (SID) was used to evaluate the discriminatory power of the three methods. IR Biotyper® showed a SID of 0.709 [95% CI (0.610–0.808)], slightly higher than PFGE, which had a SID of 0.694 [95% CI (0.592–0.796)]. The SID for WGS was 0.704 [95% CI (0.604–0.804)], calculated based on 63 isolates due to sequencing failure in three samples. These results indicate that all three methods demonstrated a comparable level of discriminatory power in this study. The concordance among the three methods was assessed using Adjusted Rand Index (ARI). It was 0.963 when comparing IR Biotyper with PFGE and 0.89 in comparison with WGS, which indicates an exceptionally close proximity to the maximum achievable value, nearly perfect match between the methods.

Real life outbreak investigation

Six E. cloacae complex isolates from blood culture samples associated with a potential outbreak in a dialysis unit were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 5, IR Biotyper® grouped all six isolates into the same cluster, clearly separated from the two tested control strains, which belonged to the same species but to different sequence types. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) confirmed that the outbreak isolates were genetically similar and grouped into a single cluster, supporting the existence of a clonal outbreak event. This was assessed using a reference-based SNP calling approach using Snippy, which showed that five of the six isolates clustered tightly with very low pairwise SNP distances when using the isolate with highest quality (sample 864340) as reference. One isolate did not meet the sequencing quality and coverage criteria described in the Methods section and was therefore excluded from the SNP analysis, preventing determination of its precise phylogenetic position within the cluster. Comparison with publicly available genome of the same sequence type (ST666) confirmed that the SNP distances within the outbreak isolates remain much lower than those to unrelated ST666 genome, supporting the conclusion of a clonal outbreak. Figure 6 includes the SNP distance matrix, allowing for a clearer visualization of the genetic distances among the analysed isolates.

Discussion

Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) remains a historically validated method for microbial typing, though whole genome sequencing (WGS) is increasingly adopted as the new gold standard in high-resolution epidemiological surveillance. However, as it requires highly skilled professionals and is laborious, expensive and time consuming, alternatives are being explored to enable clinical microbiology laboratories to provide results more quickly and efficiently [15, 18]. In this study, we evaluate FTIR, in particular, IR Biotyper® system as typing method. We show that this new tool can group and type isolates of E. cloacae complex species with a high degree of concordance to PFGE and WGS. Furthermore, in the case of hospital outbreaks allows a real time detection, which is especially relevant for preventing and managing its dissemination.

Previous studies have shown that there is a strong correlation between the results obtained through FTIR analysis and other common typing methods. This demonstrates the ability of FTIR methodology to link and group microbiological isolates with an epidemiological relationship. However, the cutoff value automatically assigned by the IR Biotyper® system software is not always capable of achieving good discrimination because this cutoff value varies depending on the microorganism being analyzed. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate an internal reference COV for each microorganism before conducting sample analysis.

In our study, we first evaluated the COV for E. cloacae complex using the results obtained through MLST as a reference. We have observed that the determination of the COV is of paramount importance for the use of IR Biotyper®. We obtained a result of 0.280 [0.169, 0.390], which is in line with the findings of other studies, such as Martak et al. [19]. However, other authors such as Hu Y et al. [17] present significantly different COVs. We believe these differences are due to several important factors to consider. Firstly, the microorganism analyzed, as in our case and Martak’s research, the COVs are referenced to E. cloacae complex, whereas in Hu Y’s study, this COV is referenced to Klebsiella pneumoniae. Secondly, the reference method we are using to establish the COV. In ours and in Martak’s study, the reference method was MLST, whereas in Hu Y’s research, whole genome sequencing served as the reference method. Here it is important to highlight that, so far, both the microorganism under study and the need to select a common standard reference method are of extreme importance for the establishment of the COV. It should be desirable that this technology could overcome these important challenges for routine clinical use.

Next, the concordance of IR Biotyper® clustering in comparison to PFGE and WGS was evaluated by calculation of the adjusted Rand index. In this case the obtained values were 0.963 and 0.89 respectively., indicating an almost perfect concordance between the typing methods evaluated. Concordance levels are in line, but slightly higher, than those obtained by Hu Y et al. [17]. Furthermore, both IR Biotyper®, PFGE and WGS consistently distinguished between eight different sequence types with a remarkable consistency of 98.48% and 98.41% respectively. Inconsistencies were observed in only one sample.

Finally, a prospective investigation of outbreaks involving E. cloacae complex was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of IR Biotyper® in the context of standard infection control procedures and the management of HCAIs. Our results support the ability of IR Biotyper® to cluster isolates from the same outbreak into a single cluster. These results are also supported by our comparison with WGS of the isolates, finding a 100% agreement.

The fast time-to-results of the IR Biotyper® may be considered most important feature to control the spread of outbreaks and the emergence of HCAIs. Providing results in less than 3 h once the microorganism has been cultured represents a significant difference compared to other typing methods mentioned, such as PFGE or WGS. These conventional methods exhibit a long-time horizon, making them impractical for timely deployment of effective therapeutic interventions and substantial enhancement of patient outcomes. In addition, both PFGE and WGS are considerably more labor-intensive processes when compared to the procedure required to conduct IR Biotyper® analysis. Additionally, the costs associated with sequencing processes through WGS are much higher, and while it is expected that these costs will decrease over time, it still seems difficult to see these sequencing methods being suitable for routine analyses in most microbiology laboratories in the near future. Also, for analysis we used the software provided in IR Biotyper®, as previous strain typing studies based on FTIR typically relied on hardware and third-party statistical analysis software [20]; we believe that the combination of FTIR hardware and software in IR Biotyper® provides a convenient solution. All of these findings demonstrate that IR Biotyper® has the potential to be integrated into the routine practices of clinical microbiology laboratories for investigating outbreaks and for rapid and efficient management of HCAIs.

While the IR Biotyper® offers a rapid and cost-effective approach for assessing clonal relatedness, it has several limitations. It is not intended for diagnostic purposes, but rather as a complementary tool in epidemiological investigations. Its performance is highly sensitive to culture conditions, as variations in media, temperature, or incubation time can significantly affect the spectral profiles derived from cell wall components. Additionally, although the system provides automatic cut-offs, these often require manual adjustment depending on the species, which may introduce variability. Lastly, we acknowledge that IR-based typing reflects phenotypic differences—particularly in polysaccharide composition—that do not always correlate with genomic variability [21, 22].

Conclusions

Our study proposes FTIR as a powerful tool for the typing of E. cloacae complex, offering high discriminatory power. Specifically, IR Biotyper® emerges as a novel typing method with advantages that include straightforward sample processing and affordable operating costs. Furthermore, in contrast to other conventional typing methods it presents results in a shorter time. For all these reasons, IR Biotyper® is ideal as an excellent choice for routine, real-time management of hospital outbreaks in clinical microbiology laboratories. However, we must not lose sight of the urgent need for the standardization of COVs, which is a fundamental challenge to address in order to facilitate the use of IR Biotyper® and the extrapolation of results between laboratories.
