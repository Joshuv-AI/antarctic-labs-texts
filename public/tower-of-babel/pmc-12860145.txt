Kinetic modeling of terpenoid production in E. coli: insights into subpopulation emergence and process optimization
Authors: Prashant Mainali, Naulchan Khongsay, Benjamin Shang Yong Au-Yeung, Congqiang Zhang, Kumar Selvarajoo, Yvonne Chow, Chueh Loo Poh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Escherichia coli is a promising host for terpenoid production, yet kinetic models tailored for such strains are limited—hindering effective bioprocess optimization and control. To address this, we developed a kinetic model of E. coli engineered to produce viridiflorol, aiming to capture the dynamics of key bioprocess variables under both uninduced and induced conditions. To account for growth and metabolism of cells, a simplified central metabolic pathway of E. coli was considered by including acetyl-CoA as the intracellular metabolite due to its role as a common precursor for viridiflorol, acetate, and the TCA cycle. Initial modeling efforts based solely on metabolism failed to reproduce experimental trends, prompting the inclusion of cellular stress caused by IPTG-induced heterologous pathway activation. However, this adjustment alone could not fully explain the observed trends, leading us to hypothesize the emergence of two distinct subpopulations post-induction: (1) stressed, slow-growing producers and (2) dormant non-producers that eventually outgrow producers. After iterative refinements, the model successfully replicated experimental trends with R2 value for glucose, cells, acetate, dissolved oxygen, and viridiflorol being 0.98, 0.88, 0.86, 0.70, and 0.94 respectively. Finally, model simulations were performed for insights which suggested that lowering IPTG concentration can improve viridiflorol production by delaying the emergence of the non-producer population. Guided by the simulations, an optimal IPTG concentration (9.375 µM) for a batch condition was identified, resulting in a titre of 0.14 g/L of viridiflorol. In contrast, a non-optimal IPTG concentration (150 µM) yielded a titre of only 0.03 g/L. Furthermore, experimental validation of the subpopulation hypothesis showed the coexistence of producer and non-producer populations. Taken together, the model captured the bioprocess variable trends, predicted an optimal IPTG concentration for a batch process, and provided insights into the existence of two subpopulations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02895-7.Keywords: Cellular stress, Kinetic model, Simulation, IPTG, Subpopulations, Terpenoids

Introduction

The advances in synthetic and metabolic engineering have extended the scope of traditional biotechnology that was largely constrained to the production of citric acid, penicillin, and ethanol to the production of high-value chemicals and therapeutics. Escherichia coli is the most common host in industrial biotechnology owing to the well-established genetic manipulation technology [1], high protein production rate [2], and its ability to grow in high cell density [3]. As a result, E. coli has been an industrial workhorse producing pharmaceuticals [4, 5], nutraceuticals [6], flavours [7] & fragrances [8], biofuel [9], and recombinant proteins [10]. This has spurred interest to scale up the bioproduction of these compounds from lab scale to industrial scale. However, bioprocess development and optimization are laborious, resource intensive and time-consuming. Conventionally, the optimal bioprocess conditions are determined by statistical-based design of experiments (DoE) method [11]. While the optimal may be determined, this approach does not provide insights to the process and cannot be used for bioprocess control. On the other hand, kinetic models of the bioprocess can be developed to gain mechanistic insights of the bioprocess [12], to control the bioprocess, and to optimize dynamic variables such as feeding profiles [13, 14].

Kinetic models are a parametric, mechanistic approach to simulating biological systems, using ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to describe time-dependent changes in metabolic concentrations and fluxes. By integrating known biochemical reactions, enzyme kinetics, and regulatory mechanisms, these models provide a quantitative framework to predict cellular behavior [15]. In metabolic engineering, kinetic models are particularly valuable for identifying rate-limiting steps in heterologous pathways, optimizing enzyme expression, and predicting how genetic modifications (e.g., knockout/overexpression) affect product yields [16]. For example, Jasmeet et al. [16] used kinetic model prediction to determine rate limiting enzymatic steps and improve limonene yield by 8–10 folds. Beyond cellular-scale modeling, kinetic approaches are also widely used in bioprocess engineering, where they describe macroscale variables (e.g., biomass growth, substrate consumption, byproduct formation) without requiring detailed metabolic networks. For instance, kinetic models for wild-type E. coli cultures under aerobic, well-mixed conditions have been developed and can predict dynamics of biomass, glucose, acetic acid, and dissolved oxygen in bioreactors [12–14, 17]. These models have demonstrated predictability in batch and fed-batch conditions [17–19] and have also been used to control bioprocesses [20]. However, most of the earlier reported models are specifically tailored for wild-type E. coli strains. Consequently, there is a need to extend these models to encompass engineered E. coli where additional variables should be considered including heterologous expression of enzymes for biosynthesis, production of target molecule, effect of induction based on concentration of the inducer, presence of population heterogeneity, and change in growth behaviour of the cells that could influence the bioprocess [21–23].

Kinetic models of E. coli engineered for recombinant protein production have been reported. In one of the earliest attempts to model the post-induction behaviour of recombinant E. coli, beta-galactosidase-producing E. coli was modeled using shake flask experiments where the growth rate and the recombinant protein production were found to be dependent upon the inducer concentration. There was a decrease in the specific growth rate after the induction and the study attributed the observation to the metabolic burden caused by overexpression of the recombinant protein [24]. The model was further extended to optimize the inducer concentration and final protein production in a fed-batch condition [25]. Similarly, a separate work explored the impact of induction on a recombinant E. coli strain, revealing inhibition of cellular capacities for glucose uptake and respiration. A model for E. coli was then developed in the study, incorporating a non-constant glucose and oxygen uptake rate [26]. There are other relevant models published for E. coli after induction but most of the models are developed for recombinant protein expression [14, 24]. However, despite the development of induction-specific models [27], their utilization for bioprocess optimization using engineered E. coli expressing heterologous biosynthesis pathways is under explored.

Given a recent surge in interest in using E. coli as a cell factory to produce terpenoids, it becomes important to study the bioprocess of terpenoid producing strains [28]. Terpenoids are highly valued for their diverse applications, including flavor and fragrance compounds, antimicrobial agents, anticancer therapeutics, and nutraceuticals, and are currently extracted from plants. Terpenoid production using E. coli potentially obviates the conventional extraction of these compounds from large quantities of plants which is neither economically lucrative nor environmentally sustainable [29]. A wide range of high value natural products can be produced through the terpenoid pathways [30]. To our knowledge, published kinetic model in the bioprocess context for engineered E. coli that produce terpenoids is scarce. This warrants the development of a cellular kinetic model for terpenoids producing strain to guide the optimization of bioprocess including determining the optimal inducer concentration.

In this study, we sought to develop the kinetic model of E. coli that was previously engineered to produce viridiflorol, a high-value terpenoid compound [8] as a case study. Firstly, a model was developed to describe the growth and metabolism of cells under no induction at 37 °C. Thereafter, the model was extended to describe bioprocess variables under different inducer (IPTG) concentrations in batch culture conditions wherein the model hypothesized the presence of two populations (producer and non-producer). The model was subsequently used to determine the optimal inducer concentration for a batch condition when glucose concentration and cell concentration was 30 g/L & 1.2 g/L (OD600 = 3) respectively. Finally, the hypothesis of the model related to the presence of two populations (producer and non-producer) was experimentally validated.

Materials and methods

Strain

In this study, E. coli MG1655 ΔrecAΔendAΔaroAΔaroBΔaroC(DE3) strain was used. The three essential genes (aroA, aroB, and aroC), which are required for cell survival, were deleted from the chromosome and added on three separate plasmids respectively. Three plasmids also carried genes encoding enzymes involved in viridiflorol biosynthesis, as described in a previous study [8]. For the study involving sub-population, enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) was integrated in the chromosome.

Bioreactor experiments

The experiments without induction were performed using a 2 L bioreactor (Sartorius Stedim, Germany) with an initial working volume of 1 L. The experiments with induction were performed with an initial working volume of 600 ml (500 ml media + 100 ml dodecane) in a 1 L sartorius bioreactor (Sartorius Stedim, Germany). The media composition was as follows: 2 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 4.2 g/L KH2PO4, 11.24 g/L K2HPO4, 1.7 g/L citric acid, 0.5 g/L MgSO4 and 10 ml/l trace element solution, pH 7.0. The trace element solution (100X) had the following composition: 0.25 g/L CoCl2· 6H2O, 1.5 g/L MnSO4·4H2O, 0.15 g/L CuSO4· 2H2O, 0.3 g/L H3BO3, 0.25 g/L Na2MoO4·2H2O, 0.8 g/L Zn(CH3COO)2, 5 g/L Fe(III) citrate and 0.84 g/L EDTA, pH 8.0 [8]. A PID controller maintained the pH at 7 using 28% NH4OH and 1 M H2SO4. The air flow rate and stirrer speed in the experiments of Figs. 2a-d and 4a-d were maintained at different volumetric oxygen transfer coefficient kLa to test the predictability of the oxygen consumption profile of the model. In other experiments, the PID controller maintained a saturation level of 30% by adjusting the air flow rate and stirrer speed, effectively controlling the level of dissolved oxygen. Dodecane (100 ml) was added to the bioreactor upon IPTG induction in all experiments, with IPTG being added at 0 h.

Analysis

200 µl of the cell suspension was used to measure the optical density (OD600) of the cells using microplate reader (BioTek, Vermont, USA) at 600 nm wavelength. OD600 of the cells was converted to g/L of the cells using a calibration curve. In the induced experiment, In the induced experiments where dodecane was added to the fermentation broth, dodecane was first removed by centrifugation. The supernatant, containing R medium and dodecane, was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of deionized water and OD600 nm measurement was performed.

Glucose and acetic acid were measured via HPLC chromatography (Agilent 1260 Infinity, USA) using an Aminex column (BioRad, California, USA). The sample injection volume was 5.00 µL. A mobile phase of 0.01 N H2SO4 with a flow rate of 0.6 ml/minute was employed for the analysis. The column temperature was maintained at 35 °C. The RID sensor, operated at a temperature of 30 °C, detected glucose and acetic acids. The retention time for glucose and acetic acids was 9 and 16 min respectively.

The top layer of the fermentation broth was separated by centrifuging it at 13,000 g for 4 min. The viridiflorol samples for analysis were prepared by diluting 4 µl of the top layer into 1000 µl of hexane. These samples were then analysed using gas chromatography (Agilent 7890, USA) with an Agilent 5977B MSD. The samples were injected into an Agilent VF-WAXms column with a split ratio of 40:1 at 240 °C. The oven temperature started at 100 °C for 1 min, then increased to 150 °C at 50 °C/min, followed by 240 °C at 15 °C/min, and maintained at 240 °C for an additional 2 min. The compound concentrations were determined by interpolating with a standard prepared using authentic viridiflorol (Sigma Aldrich). The mass spectrometer was operated in EI mode with full scan analysis (m/z 30–300, 2 spectra/s).

Flow cytometry

Flow-cytometry measurements were made using a flow cytometer (BD Accuri plus, UK). The cells to be analysed were pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in a PBS buffer. The cells were diluted 250 times in PBS buffer before analysis. 200 µl of the diluted cells were dispensed into an individual well in a 96-well flat bottom plate. The microfluidic channel used for the analysis was FITC-A. The analysis proceeded until 40,000 events were recorded. The events with FSC-H of less than 1000 were discarded. The graphs were plotted using the online flow cytometry data analysis tool [Floreada.io]. ImageJ was used to calculate the percentage of two populations when two distinct peak was present in flow cytometry data (Supplementary Information, Fig. S3–S20). The percentage of cells at two population state i.e. non-fluorescent and fluorescent at different IPTG concentration on different time points that was calculated from flowcytometry data (Supplementary Information, Fig. S3–S20) are included in Supplementary Information, Table S4–S11.

kLa measurement

To calculate the kLa at a particular stirrer speed and airflow rate, the dissolved oxygen (DOT) value in the bioreactor was reduced to a 0% saturation level by sparging the bioreactor with nitrogen gas. After the oxygen level reached zero, the respective regime of airflow rate and stirrer speed at which kLa needs to be calculated was maintained. As the DOT value started to increase, kLa was calculated from the slope of a semi-logarithm plot of the dissolved oxygen response against time. The maximum kLa for the bioreactor was calculated to be 160 when the stirrer and airflow rate operated at the maximum capacity. In an experiment where the DOT was maintained at 30% using bioreactor control, the kLa input in the model was kept to 160. In other cases, when the kLa was known, that value of kLa was used in the model.

Model simulation

The ordinary differential equations (ODEs) were solved using ODE23 solver in MATLAB (R2021b). The ODE23 uses explicit Runge-Kutta pair of Bogacki and Shampine to solve non-stiff differential equations [31].

Parameter fitting

The unknown parameters for the model were estimated using an optimization/search algorithm wherein the algorithm finds the sets of parameters value that reduces the discrepancy between the prediction from the resulting model and the experimental observations [32]. One set of batch experiments performed with no IPTG provided the experimental data to parameterize 19 parameters (SI, Table S1) while three sets of batch experiments performed with different IPTG concentrations provided experimental data to parameterize another 16 parameters for induced condition (SI, Table S2). Results and discussion

Model development

The model can predict five extracellular variables of interest (i.e., glucose, cells, acetic acid, Dissolved Oxygen (DOT), and viridiflorol concentration) for the engineered E. coli growing in a standard bench-top bioreactor at 37 °C in a batch condition at different IPTG concentration. The model is non-structured and non-segregated when no inducer is added while non-structured but segregated when inducer is added. For induced conditions, the model assumes there are producer and non-producer populations (x1, x2). The metabolic pathways for the producer and non-producer populations are shown in Fig. 1a and b, respectively. In the producer population (Fig. 1a), IPTG induction activates the heterologous pathway, channelling nine molecules of acetyl-CoA toward the synthesis of one molecule of viridiflorol. One molecule of viridiflorol (C₁₅H₂₆O) is synthesized from three C₅ isoprene units, each derived from three acetyl-CoA molecules via the mevalonate pathway; therefore, a total of 9 molecules of acetyl-CoA are required to make one molecule of viridiflorol. The model hypothesizes that this process also leads to the accumulation of cellular stress, which inhibits pathway fluxes i.e. glucose uptake (), TCA cycle flux (). In contrast, the non-producer population (Fig. 1b) does not activate the heterologous pathway, resulting in no viridiflorol production and no associated stress accumulation.

Experiments for parameterization of the model

The development of a model for induced conditions necessitates the prior establishment of a well-developed model of growth and metabolism for uninduced state, where IPTG is not added to the bioreactor and the heterologous pathway is not triggered. A batch experiment (Fig. 2, a - d) was performed with 27 g/L of glucose and 0.14 g/L (OD600 = 0.34) of cells to obtain data to calibrate the uninduced model.

From the batch experiment, the parameters associated with growth and metabolism including all the uptake rates and saturation constants were determined. The results show cell growth associated with glucose consumption and acetate production. The glucose taken up by the cells is metabolized to acetyl-CoA, which can be further directed toward the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle or converted to acetate, depending on the extracellular glucose concentration. In a glucose-rich environment such as in this experiment, acetyl-CoA was diverted to acetate as well through what is known as overflow metabolism [37] because of the limited capacity of the TCA cycle. In the presence of glucose, the preferred carbon source, carbon catabolite repression leads to diauxic behaviour [38], whereby cells will assimilate acetate only when all the glucose is consumed [39], and produce a less substantial increase in cell biomass. The rate of consumption of oxygen can be observed in Fig. 2d. The demand for dissolved oxygen (DOT) increased with increasing cell biomass over time. The volumetric mass transfer coefficient (kLa) of the bioreactor was progressively increased from 21 h− 1 at 0 h to 120 h− 1 at the end of the fermentation run. kLa was manually adjusted by modifying the agitation speed (rpm) and airflow rate whenever the dissolved oxygen (DO) level approached 30%. Upon increasing the kLa, DOT level increased sharply. This run was performed to determine parameters related to oxygen consumption and was operated under predefined agitation and aeration conditions with measured kLa values at each configuration. In other runs, a PID controller was used to automatically maintain the DOT level at 30%. A reliable kLa model (as a function of agitation speed, airflow rate), together with a kinetic model that includes the oxygen consumption rate, can be used to predict when dissolved oxygen becomes limiting. In Fig. 2d, after glucose was depleted, the oxygen demand decreased and the DOT level remained high, implying that the cells had a higher oxygen demand when growing on glucose compared to acetate due to decreased growth rate on acetate. Through the batch experiment, the uptake rates and associated saturation constants in the model were able to be estimated. The R2 value and MAE (Mean Absolute Error) (Table 2) values for cells, glucose, acetate, and oxygen were satisfactory, showing that the model could predict the trends well, and the average errors between prediction and experimental data were minimal.

To extend the model for the induced condition, we then performed three batch experiments (Fig. 2e–h) induced with IPTG of 150 µM, 20 µM, and 12.5 µM respectively. To characterize the spectrum of induction responses, IPTG concentrations of 12.5 µM, 20 µM, and 150 µM were chosen. These represent low, moderate, and high induction levels, respectively. At 12.5 µM, induction is minimal and thus, reduce metabolic burden. 20 µM provides a moderate induction level to observe transitional changes in cell behaviour with increasing IPTG concentration. At 150 µM, T7 promoter nears saturation, allowing to understand the effects of strong expression and high metabolic burden on the cells. The initial glucose for all three experiments was approximately 30 g/L and the initial cell concentration at the time of induction was approximately 1.2 g/L (OD600 = 3). The initial cell density was set higher than in the calibration experiment to ensure cells had passed the lag phase and were in an optimal state at the time of IPTG induction, thereby avoiding confounding effects from lag-phase dynamics.

The model structure was developed as described in 3.1 based on observations of the kinetic data for glucose, cell, acetate, and viridiflorol concentrations. Cell growth was seen to slow down in proportion to the concentration of IPTG after a few hours of induction (Fig. 2f). An IPTG concentration of 150 µM (Fig. 2f) resulted in a rapid growth reduction, whereas a lower IPTG concentration of 12.5 µM led to a slower deceleration of growth and reached a higher final cell density overall. A similar pattern was observed in glucose consumption, with the rate of decline in glucose consumption being proportional to the IPTG concentration. Glucose consumption was fastest when the IPTG concentration was the lowest (Fig. 2e, IPTG–12.5 µM). These findings led to the hypothesis that the observed reduction in metabolic activity, reflected in both glucose consumption and cell growth, was primarily caused by the IPTG induction, which we refer to as “cellular stress”. The level of stress experienced by the cells appeared to be directly proportional to the concentration of IPTG used for induction. However, acetate production was highest when IPTG concentration was lowest. Similarly, viridiflorol production was also highest when IPTG concentration was lowest (12.5 µM).

A peculiar observation was made in the experimental data of glucose, cells, acetate, and viridiflorol when IPTG of 150 µM was used. High concentration of IPTG is often used in induction. After a few hours of induction, there was a decrease in glucose consumption rate which eventually led to a hiatus in glucose consumption and cell growth. However, after this hiatus, rapid growth of cells and consumption of glucose began whilst there was no further viridiflorol production. On the other hand, when induced at a low IPTG concentration of 12.5 µM, this pattern was not observed. This resembles the observations reported by a previously published independent study [40] which observed a similar phenomenon where the addition of high concentrations (>100 µM) of IPTG to an E. coli producing recombinant protein resulted in a temporary growth cessation for a few hours and resumption of growth after a few hours [40]. However, the study did not further clarify the reason for the resumption of growth. In our study, glucose consumption was the fastest when the IPTG concentration was the lowest (Fig. 2e, IPTG–12.5 µM) and viridiflorol production continued as long as glucose was available. However, under an IPTG concentration of 150 µM, viridiflorol production was not observed even when glucose was being consumed. Based on the collective findings, we hypothesized the presence of two distinct subpopulations of cells in the culture at the beginning: the producer population, which constitutes the majority of the cells, and a smaller subset of the non-producer population. Our hypothesis, that a subset of cells are non-producers from the outset, closely parallels the phenomenon of persister cells in antibiotic resistance. Dormancy is the widely accepted mechanism underlying persistence, and while IPTG is not an antibiotic, it can induce cellular stress, potentially triggering a dormant state [41]. We postulate that, initially, only a small fraction of cells belongs to the non-producer population. These cells are metabolically inactive, meaning they are not growing. In contrast, the producer population is actively growing and synthesizing engineered pathway enzymes and producing viridiflorol. As the cells are producing engineered pathway enzymes, they accumulate cellular stress, which gradually slows their metabolic activity. Once stress reaches a critical level, growth in the producer population stalls. Concurrently, non-producer cells begin to grow. However, they do not produce viridiflorol and remain free from stress throughout. This behavior may be explained by bistability, in which a small subpopulation of cells adopts a slow-growing or dormant state, contributing to cellular persistence [40, 41]. The model built with this hypothesis was able to describe the bioprocess variables at different IPTG concentrations for all the experiments that were used for calibrating the model (Fig. 2e - h). Overall, the R2 and MAE (Mean Absolute Error) values (Table 2) obtained for the experiments used to calibrate the model for cells, glucose, acetate, and viridiflorol were satisfactory. The model’s predicted trend aligns well with the experimental data for all the experiments. The corelation plots of the experimental values and model prediction for the experiment used in validation of the model all variables are included in Supplementary Information, Figure S1. This model can therefore be used to gain deeper insights into the process by studying final value of stress variables, evader values, and the proportions of producer and non-producer populations across different IPTG concentrations in a batch fermentation. Furthermore, the model can also be used to identify the optimal IPTG concentration for maximizing viridiflorol production, while providing a basis to explore the underlying mechanisms that make this concentration optimal.

Model insights and validation

Once the model was formulated and parameterized, insights from the model were obtained via in-silico simulation. The simulation was initiated with 30 g/L glucose and 1.2 g/L (OD600 = 3) of cells. For each starting condition, only the IPTG concentration was varied from 0 µM to 200 µM, resulting in a total of 200 simulation runs. The model predicted values of stressa and stressb accrued in cells at the end of fermentation at IPTG concentrations of 0 µM to 200 µM are plotted in Fig. 3a. The stress accrued in cells is positively correlated to the IPTG concentration, rising sharply between 12 and 25 µM and plateauing at higher concentrations. Likewise, in Fig. 3b, the predicted evader value at the end of fermentation at IPTG concentration of 0 µM to 200 µM is plotted. The plot reveals that at low IPTG concentrations (< 12.5 µM), the final stressa and stressb values are lower than 1, which results in lower evader values. At high IPTG concentrations (>12.5 µM), stressa and stressb values reach maximum (i.e., 1), which results in higher evader values, implying the rise of a non-producer population at high IPTG concentration. In Fig. 3c, the predicted producer and non-producer populations at the end of fermentation for each IPTG concentration (0–200 µM) is plotted. The model predicts that the final non-producer population has a positive correlation to the IPTG concentration, rising sharply between 12 and 25 µM and plateauing at higher concentrations. The simulation results reveal that the final non-producer population increases while the producer population decreases with increasing IPTG concentration. When the IPTG concentration is 0, all the cells at the end of the fermentation remain as the producer population. We considered the term producer population to refer to the cells’ capacity for viridiflorol synthesis. At 0 µM IPTG, no induction occurs and, consequently, no viridiflorol is produced. Although these cells are technically non-producers in the absence of IPTG, they experience no IPTG-induced metabolic burden and therefore retain the potential to produce viridiflorol once IPTG is added. Similarly, the final viridiflorol titre at the end of each batch fermentation at IPTG concentration 0 µM to 200 µM is plotted in Fig. 3e. Based on the predicted viridiflorol titre concentrations, the optimal IPTG concentration was determined to be 9.375 µM. The steep decrease in viridiflorol production at high IPTG concentrations is attributed to the increased cellular stress, leading to a rapid rise in the non-producer population. The population heterogeneity in microbial bioprocesses is a well-accepted observation arising from the metabolic state of the cells, the stochastic nature of gene expression, and various extrinsic environmental factors such as substrate gradients [43]. In the engineered E. coli cells, there are other observed cases where non-producing populations overtake producing populations leading to poor product yield [44, 45].

Similarly, the final acetate concentration in batch fermentation (Fig. 3, d) reached its highest value of 8.8 g/L at 12.5 µM of IPTG. This suggests that the expression of heterologous enzymes could increase the flux towards acetate production. In a separate study, it was reported that acetate accumulated following induction in a glucose-limited fed-batch experiment involving recombinant E. coli [46]. The authors speculated that the metabolism was reorganized after the induction that triggered acetate accumulation. Similarly, the authors in a separate work also observed increased acetate production after induction in a recombinant protein-producing E. coli and they speculated that it may be a result of a limited capacity of the TCA cycle and active TCA cycle regulation/reorganization [47]. Higher acetate accumulation has been correlated to recombinant protein production. Acetate could be an indicator of the metabolic reorganization in the cells due to recombinant protein production [48]. Therefore, it can be speculated that the diversion of additional flux to acetate could indicate the effect of induction. The current observation aligns with the experimental verification by Basan et al. [49] wherein the expression of the gratuitous protein in E. coli resulted in a decrease in respiratory enzymes. In our model, it was hypothesized that the IPTG-induced cellular stress decreased the TCA enzyme activity (Eq. 1.16) more rapidly than glucose uptake (Eq. 1.08) which aligns with Basan’s experimental observation.

Likewise, in Fig. 3f, the simulation started with the same conditions as before for four IPTG concentrations (6.25 µM, 12.5 µM, 20 µM, 100 µM), and the rate of viridiflorol production versus fermentation time was plotted. It revealed that the initial rate of production was highest when the IPTG concentration was highest (100 µM) and lower when the IPTG concentration was lower (6.25 µM, 12.5 µM). However, the initial rate of viridiflorol production at 100 µM did not last long. It decreased rapidly, likely because of the high cellular stress caused by IPTG-induced heterologous pathway activation (IPTG-induced stress) in the population. In a separate published work, the initial production rate of chloramphenicol acetyl-transferase (CAT) in E. coli was observed to be highest at the highest IPTG concentration but the initial rate could not persist throughout the fermentation time, similar to the result of our current work [50]. Finally, our model suggests that the optimal inducer concentration should be in the range where the IPTG-induced stress for the producer population following induction is minimal while still having enough cellular levels of the heterologous enzymes to catalyze the bioconversion. Additionally, the rise of non-producer population should be avoided. Using our model, IPTG concentration of 9.375 µM was found to be optimal (Fig. 3, e) for batch conditions when the initial glucose concentration is 30 g/L and cell biomass is 1.2 g/L (OD600 = 3). In engineered E. coli bioprocesses, the amount of inducer concentration (IPTG) is a crucial parameter to optimize to induce the heterologous enzyme expression for optimal production. However, high concentration of inducer might not always lead to high production of protein/compound of interest [51] as observed in our experimental results. It is likely due to growth inhibition of the cells by the production of heterologous proteins [52]. It has also been observed that at 37 °C, the optimization of IPTG has more scope of optimization than the lower temperature [40]. We also observed that the production of viridiflorol steeply increased in the IPTG concentration range (6.25–12.5 µM). Taken together, the concentration of IPTG is an important variable to optimize to improve production, and the development of a model can guide to this process.

Finally, we further performed one new batch experiment with no induction and 3 new batch experiments at 3 IPTG concentrations (i.e., 6.25 µM, 9.375 µM, and 50 µM) to test the predictability of the model. These concentrations were selected to represent both the low (6.25 µM and 9.375 µM) and high (50 µM) ranges of IPTG induction, with 9.375 µM representing the model-predicted optimal concentration. Importantly, these specific IPTG concentrations had not been used in previous model calibrating experiments. Firstly, a batch experiment was performed at different initial condition (Initial glucose: 5.18 g/L, Initial cells: 0.16 g/L (OD600 = 0.4) than that was used for calibrating the model to test whether the model could predict well the trends of the measurable parameters under a different initial condition. In the batch experiment (Fig. 4, a-d), the initial glucose was less while initial cells were higher than in the batch experiment that was used to calibrate the model. The results show that the model successfully captured the trends of glucose, cells, acetate, and dissolved oxygen throughout the entire batch run, demonstrating its ability to predict these variables accurately under a different initial condition. Notably, the model effectively accounted for overflow metabolism and carbon catabolite repression.

Similarly, the experimental data and model predictions for glucose, cells, acetate, and viridiflorol, when induced with 6.25 µM, 9.375 µM, and 50 µM of IPTG respectively, are presented in Fig. 4e–h. A notable pattern observed in the experimental data is a brief hiatus in glucose consumption a few hours after induction, followed by a rapid surge in consumption. This phenomenon is particularly evident when cells were induced using 50 µM of IPTG concentration. Furthermore, the data revealed that glucose consumption was fastest at lowest IPTG concentrations, and the highest cell biomass was observed at the lowest IPTG concentration (6.25 µM). On the other hand, the highest acetate concentration was observed at 9.375 µM IPTG, coinciding with the highest viridiflorol concentration (0.14 g/L). Interestingly, the model predictions also support these experimental findings. The experimental data and model predictions collectively suggest that the optimal IPTG concentration for this batch condition is approximately 9.37 µM, indicating the effectiveness of the model in optimizing the final viridiflorol concentration. The R2 and MAE (Mean Absolute Error) values for all the validation experiments are provided in the Table 3. The correlation plots of the experimental values and model prediction for the experiment used in validation of the model all variables are included in Supplementary Information, Figure S2. R² values for all variables indicate a satisfactory model fit, with glucose, cells, acetate, dissolved oxygen, and viridiflorol showing values of 0.98, 0.88, 0.86, 0.70, and 0.94, respectively. Similarly, the MAE (Mean Absolute Error) values, reflecting the average difference between the predicted and experimental results, were low, providing additional evidence of the model’s predictive performance.

Experimental validation of subpopulations

An important hypothesis considered in this model is that there are producer population and a fraction of non-producer population. The producer cells are initially abundant and actively growing, while a small fraction of non-producer cells remains dormant. Upon IPTG induction, the producer population experiences metabolic stress, leading to a gradual reduction in growth rate. In contrast, the dormant non-producer cells becomes metabolically active and resumes growth. Over time, the non-producer population outgrows the producer cells as the latter’s growth continues to slow under stress. A schematic of the hypothesis is presented in Fig. 5a. To test the hypothesis experimentally, an eGFP gene encoded under the control of the T7 lac promoter was inserted into the genome of E. coli that already had the three plasmids with the necessary enzymes to produce viridiflorol. All the heterologous genes and the eGFP gene were under the control of the T7 lac promoter, ensuring that eGFP expression served as a reliable proxy for producer activity. This approach also ruled out plasmid loss as a confounding factor for the emergence of non-producer cells. A separate study by Gelain et al. supports this approach, where a GFP gene placed downstream of a key enzyme in the limonene biosynthesis pathway positively correlated with limonene titer and was used to study subpopulation dynamics [23]. As per the hypothesis, the producer population would produce eGFP upon induction, and over time, the producer population would decrease while the non-producer population will increase, resulting in the drop in the overall eGFP production of the culture with time. A high concentration of IPTG (150 µM) was chosen for this bioreactor experiment. The bioreactor operating condition was identical to the other induced batch experiments. In Fig. 5b, the breakdown of dynamics of the producer and non-producer population is shown as per model prediction. Flow cytometry measurements were performed to analyse the subpopulations to check if experiments corroborate to model prediction. Similar to what was suggested by the model, as shown in Fig. 5d, the flow cytometry measurement of cells at various time points shows the presence of 2 subpopulations (one population produced green fluorescence while one population did not). At 0 h before induction, there was a single major population of non-fluorescent cells while at two hours after induction, population of fluorescent cells was higher. After two hours, the number of fluorescent cells started to decrease while the number of non-fluorescent cells increased. The flow cytometry data used to generate the Fig. 5d is included in Supplementary Information, Fig. S3. The percentage of two population i.e. non-fluorescent and fluorescent population at different timepoint calculated from Fig S3 data is included in Supplementary Information, Table S3. The viridiflorol production saturated after five hours (Fig. 5c). Thus, after two hours, the rise of non-producers could have halted the production of viridiflorol. Though the model prediction for the viridiflorol concentration is slightly lower from the actual experimental concentration, the model predicted the experimental trend well. One possible reason for discrepancy between the predictions and experimental results for viridiflorol concentration is that the strain used in this experiment was also engineered to express eGFP, whereas the model was developed on cells without eGFP.

To further validate the observed trends of subpopulations at different IPTG concentrations, we performed an additional flask-scale experiment using the same eGFP encoded E. coli strain across various IPTG concentrations. The cell growth trends across all IPTG concentrations are shown in Fig. 6a. In the flask-scale experiments, we observed growth patterns similar to that in the bioreactor experiments. Specifically, cells induced with low IPTG concentrations exhibited growth patterns like those without IPTG induction. Conversely, cells exposed to high IPTG concentrations showed a temporary cessation of growth, followed by a delayed resumption and ultimately reaching a final cell density comparable to that of non-induced cells. However, the timing of the growth resurgence was slower in the flask-scale experiments compared to the bioreactor experiments, which is expected from the difference in scale [53]. Figure 6b and c depict the distribution of producer and non-producer populations at 24 h and 30 h respectively, as predicted by our model. Correspondingly, Fig. 6d and e show the experimentally determined distributions of fluorescent (producer) and non-fluorescent (non-producer) populations at 24 h and 30 h. The flow cytometry data used to generate Fig. 6d, e is included in Supplementary Information, Fig. S5-S17. The percentage of cells at two population state at different IPTG concentration on different time points are included in Supplementary Information, Table S4–S11. The non-fluorescent cells at 24 and 30 h can be considered proxies for non-producer population as they continue to grow without producing the fluorescent protein. The experimental results show a trend similar to the model predictions regarding the proportions of producer and non-producer populations at the corresponding time points. Although there are discrepancies in the exact percentages and IPTG concentrations, the overall trends align well, validating our model. The final non-producer population appears to be positively correlated to the IPTG concentration used, indicating that higher stress from IPTG induction leads to a higher proportion of non-producer population. These findings support our hypothesis and validate the accuracy of the model. Importantly, they suggest that higher IPTG concentrations do not necessarily correlate with improved production. Overloading cells with the high heterologous pathway expression can lead to high stress and an increase in non-producer cells population, undermining productivity. Therefore, the findings suggest that it is advisable to optimize IPTG concentrations to balance induction efficiency and cellular growth. While this study highlights that subpopulation formation is likely a common feature in engineered E. coli, especially under high IPTG induced heterologous pathway activation, future research on the underlying biological mechanisms could open new avenues for metabolic engineering strategies. Studies investigating the origins of population heterogeneity in bioprocesses are limited. Nevertheless, several general mechanisms have been proposed to explain the emergence of subpopulations [43]. One mechanism is gene expression noise, which can generate multimodal distributions of cellular traits. Such noise may arise from extrinsic factors, such as fluctuations in the environment, or from intrinsic stochasticity in gene expression, even under stable conditions. This noise in gene expression can give rise to bistability, in which cells segregate into distinct subpopulations representing the ON and OFF states of a regulatory process [54, 55]. Bistability has been linked to cellular persistence, a phenomenon where a small subpopulation enters a slow-growing or dormant state before the onset of stress, such as antibiotic exposure. These persister cells tolerate stress but exhibit reduced growth in its absence, whereas the remaining population grows rapidly under normal conditions yet becomes inhibited under stress [42]. In the E. coli strain used in this study, essential genes were deleted from the chromosome and added on plasmids, making plasmid retention indispensable for survival. Under such conditions, the non-producer cells observed are unlikely to have lost their plasmids. Instead, a fraction of the population may have downregulated or switched off T7 promoter–driven gene expression. Over time, these cells could outgrow the stressed population that are actively expressing the T7-driven genes. This behavior resembles bet-hedging, a survival strategy in which cells diversify phenotypically to prepare for potential environmental fluctuations. This is plausible given that expression of the engineered pathway is resource-intensive and tends to inhibit growth at elevated IPTG concentrations. In the context of bioprocesses, exploring how key process parameters, such as temperature and carbon source, influence subpopulation dynamics would deepen our understanding and enable robust fermentation process optimization.

The model developed in this study effectively predicted the trends of bioprocess variables across a range of IPTG concentrations for a batch process. This was achieved by incorporating the effects of IPTG-induced stress on growth and metabolism, as well as including the intracellular metabolite acetyl-CoA, which provided the flexibility to accurately predict acetate and viridiflorol production. This underscores the importance of considering cellular stress caused by IPTG-induced heterologous pathway activation in modeling growth and metabolism for bioprocess. We also identified the optimal IPTG concentration for batch fermentation to enhance viridiflorol production through simulations, highlighting the significance of modeling in optimizing the process. Furthermore, this model can be adapted for fed-batch processes. Model incorporates relevant equations that describe the core metabolism of E. coli, that includes carbon catabolite repression and overflow metabolism, making it suitable for both batch and fed-batch processes. However, some fine tuning might be required. Moreover, because the model includes acetyl-CoA, it can be adapted for other terpenoid-producing strains as well. Importantly, in this work, the model’s prediction of two subpopulations was experimentally validated using flow cytometry, demonstrating its effectiveness in studying population heterogeneity in bioprocesses. The current study suggests that the emergence of non-producer populations may significantly impact productivity in the E. coli bioprocess. This finding emphasizes the necessity of investigating subpopulations within bioprocesses and incorporating them into traditional bioprocess models. Moreover, recent work in E. coli bioprocessing has shown that cascade fermentation—utilizing two bioreactors where induction occurs only in the second reactor, while fresh, metabolically unburdened cells are transferred from the first to the second—can enhance protein production compared to conventional single bioreactor setups [56]. This approach highlights the challenges posed by the emergence of non-producer cells, and a viable strategy to mitigate this issue is to maintain a continuous supply of metabolically unburdened cells. The model developed in this study can be useful in optimizing such setups. However, stress responses in engineered cells and the emergence of sub-populations are strain-dependent and should be parameterized accordingly for accurate predictions. Integrating models that account for population heterogeneity will further enhance the efficacy of current bioprocess models.

Conclusion

This study modeled the bioprocess dynamics of a viridiflorol-producing E. coli strain under batch conditions, with a focus on the impact of varying levels of heterologous pathway expression. Experimental data obtained across a range of IPTG concentrations revealed that cellular stress alone was insufficient to fully explain the observed growth arrest at high IPTG levels and subsequent recovery of growth. For the model to better predict the observations, it was required the incorporation of subpopulation dynamics, as an effect of cellular stress, where we hypothesized the emergence of non-producer subpopulations due to cellular stress. This hypothesis was experimentally validated, confirming the existence of producer and non-producer subpopulations.

The resulting coarse-grained kinetic model integrates core metabolic processes, IPTG dose-dependent effects, the coupling between metabolic state and viridiflorol production, and the dynamic shifts between subpopulations. It revealed that high IPTG concentrations intensify cellular stress, suppressing viridiflorol production and accelerating the dominance of non-producer cells. Stress-induced growth inhibition of producers allows non-producers—which evade metabolic burden—to outcompete them over time. Optimal IPTG dosing must balance sufficient enzyme expression for production with minimal stress to delay non-producer proliferation. Overall, the experimental validation of subpopulation dynamics underscores the importance of mechanistic modeling and how model can add value in the bioprocess. By providing a framework to dissect stress-mediated population heterogeneity, this study lays a foundation for optimizing other terpenoid-producing strains, potentially accelerating bioprocess development.
