Can LLMs Hit Moving Targets? Tracking Evolving Signals in Corporate Disclosures
Chanyeol Choi, Jihoon Kwon, Minjae Kim

Abstract
Moving targets ( Cohen & Nguyen 2024 ) – managers’ strategic shifting of key performance metrics when the original targets become difficult to achieve – have been shown to predict subsequent stock underperformance. However, our work reveals that the method employed in that study exhibits two key limitations that hinder the accuracy – noise in the extracted targets and loss of contextual information – both of which stem primarily from the use of a named entity recognition (NER). To address these two limitations, we propose an LLM-based target extraction method with a newly defined metric that better captures semantic context. This approach preserves semantic context beyond simple entity recognition and yields consistently higher predictive power than the original approach. Overall, our approach enhances the granularity and accuracy of financial text-based performance prediction.

Introduction

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have spurred efforts to leverage these tools for uncovering economically meaningful signals from unstructured financial texts such as earnings calls and corporate disclosures.
In line with prior research, our work applies LLMs to enhance the target and metric extraction methodology proposed in
Cohen & Nguyen 2024
.
Their study documents that managers strategically shift emphasis to new performance metrics when existing ones deteriorate – a “
moving targets
” behavior that predicts subsequent stock price declines.

Unlike the original paper, which relied on a Named Entity Recognition (NER)-based approach to extract and quantify moving targets, this study introduces an LLM-enhanced methodology.
We argue that leveraging the contextual understanding of LLMs enables more interpretable and accurate measurement of strategic communication patterns in financial disclosures.

Through the rest of the paper, we introduce and validate our methodology and its effectiveness.
Our contributions can be summarized as follow:

•

In Sec.
2.1
, we identify two key limitations of the original NER-based methodology –
noise in the extracted targets
and
loss of contextual information
– that hinder accurate measurement of moving targets.

•

In Sec.
2.2
, we propose LLM-driven solution to overcome these limitations: an
LLM-based target extraction
method with a
newly defined metric
based on semantic-similarity.

•

Our experiment in Sec.
3
demonstrates that these LLM-based enhancements deliver more precise, interpretable, and predictive measures of firms’ moving target behavior.

2
Methodology

In this section, we present LLM-driven methods that enhance the measurement of firms’
moving targets
from earnings call transcripts by overcoming key limitations of the original NER-based approach.
We first motivate our method by pinpointing two potential sources of inaccuracy—noise in extracted targets and loss of contextual information.
We then introduce an LLM-based target extraction method that computes a
moving targets
score leveraging semantic-similarity.
The overall filtering and metric-computation processes are illustrated in Fig.
1
.

2.1
Motivation

Prior work
(
Cohen & Nguyen 2024
)
has relied heavily on NER to extract performance targets from earnings call transcripts.
However, we found that this approach suffers from limitations in both accuracy and explainability.

First, Fig.
3
shows that the NER-based method extracts generic words, indicating the potential problem of
noise
.
Second, Fig.
4
shows that contextual qualifiers are dropped, highlighting the potential
loss of contextual information
.

Such noise and context loss may introduce distortions in measuring the strategic shifting of key performance metrics.
As a result, NER-based extraction alone has limitations in precisely capturing firms’ strategic shifts in performance metrics.
To address these issues, we propose an LLM-based target extraction and scoring method.

Notation.

Let
c
denote a firm and let
t
denote a year–quarter index (e.g., 2025 Q3).
The text of firm
c
’s earnings call transcript at year–quarter
t
is denoted by
\mathbf{T}_{c,t}
.
We define two finite sets of targets extracted from these transcripts as

\mathcal{T}_{c,t}=\{T^{1}_{c,t},\dots,T^{n}_{c,t}\}
and

\mathcal{T}_{c,t-4}=\{T^{1}_{c,t-4},\dots,T^{m}_{c,t-4}\}
,
where
T^{i}_{c,t}
and
T^{j}_{c,t-4}
denote the
i
-th and
j
-th extracted targets at times
t
and
t-4
, respectively.
For simplicity, we denote that
n=|\mathcal{T}_{c,t-4}|
and
m=|\mathcal{T}_{c,t}|
are the corresponding set sizes.
We denote the LLM employed for post-filtering and extracting of targets as
h_{\text{LLM}}
.

2.2
LLM-Based Target Extraction

In this section, we describe our proposed
LLM-based target extraction
and formulation of the
moving targets
score that leverages
semantic similarity
between targets mentioned in a firm’s earnings call transcripts.
Fig.
1
provides an overview of the full computation pipeline.
In the remainder of this subsection we formalize the notation and describe each component to compute
MT_{c,t}
, which represents the
moving targets
score of firm
c
at year-quarter
t
.

Target Extraction.

For each firm
c
and year–quarter
t
, we process the entire transcript
\mathbf{T}_{c,t}
in a single LLM call, with a prompt
P_{\text{ext}}
that instruct the model
h_{\text{LLM}}
to separately extract all performance targets from the management presentation and from the analyst Q&A sections.
This procedure
h_{\text{LLM}}(T^{i}_{c,t}|P_{\text{ext}})
also yields a finite set of targets

\mathcal{T}_{c,t}
for firm
c
in year-quarter
t
.
This LLM-based extraction replaces original NER-based extraction and is designed to capture semantically rich targets that may be expressed through paraphrase or complex sentence structures.

Semantic Similarity.

In the original formulation of the moving targets
(
Cohen & Nguyen 2024
)
,
the degree of movement is quantified as

\textit{MT}_{c,t}=\frac{\text{\# of targets in }\mathcal{T}_{c,t-4}\text{ not mentioned in }\mathcal{T}_{c,t}}{\text{\# of targets in }\mathcal{T}_{c,t-4}}.

In particular, the numerator represents the number of
missing targets
,
quantifying those previously emphasized performance metrics that no longer appear in the current transcript
\mathbf{T}_{c,t}
.
Unlike the simple keyword matching used in NER, which allows straightforward counting, the LLM-based extraction often yields semantically equivalent targets expressed with different wording, making direct application of the original counting formula difficult.

To overcome this limitation, we introduce an semantic similarity-based metric.
First, the extracted targets
T^{i}_{c,t}
and
T^{j}_{c,t-4}
is encoded into a dense vector embedding
\mathbf{e}(T^{i}_{c,t})=f_{T}(T^{i}_{c,t})
using a off-the-shelve pre-trained text encoder
f_{T}(\cdot)
that captures contextual meaning.
We then compute all pairwise cosine similarities

S_{ij}=\cos\bigl(\mathbf{e}(T^{i}_{c,t}),\;\mathbf{e}(T^{j}_{c,t-4})\bigr)

(1)

between current and previous targets.
For each previous target
T^{j}_{c,t-4}
, we apply max-pooling

\tilde{S}_{j}=\max_{i}S_{ij}

to identify its best semantic match in the current quarter.

Pooling.

Suppose there exist two targets that convey the same meaning but differ slightly in wording across the calls at
t
and
t\!-\!4
.
If we rely on max-pooling alone, such pairs may receive lower similarity scores, causing them to be penalized as if they were different targets.
To address this issue, we apply a thresholding function that treats semantically equivalent targets as fully retained:

g(\tilde{S}_{j})=\begin{cases}1,&\tilde{S}_{j}\geq\tau,\\
\tilde{S}_{j},&\tilde{S}_{j}<\tau,\end{cases}

(2)

with cutoff
\tau
so that semantically equivalent targets are counted as fully retained, thereby correcting for paraphrases and making the metric robust to minor wording or formatting changes.

Scoring.

Finally, we define the
Moving Targets
score as

MT_{c,t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{b}g(\tilde{S}_{j}).

This semantic similarity–based metric extends the original discrete measure by preserving semantic equivalence even when targets are phrased differently.
This metric quantifies the extent to which performance targets previously emphasized by a firm are dropped or semantically altered after four quarters, similar with
Cohen & Nguyen 2024
.

3
Experiments

In this section, we empirically evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed LLM-based method.
We begin by describing the dataset and summary statistics, then outline the implementation details and validation experiments.
Finally, we present results that assess whether the LLM-based method reduces noise, preserves contextual information, and improves the predictive accuracy over NER-based method.

3.1
Data and Summary Statistics

We conduct our analysis using earnings call transcripts as the primary source of firm-level disclosures, following
Cohen & Nguyen 2024
.
While we also experimented with 10-K and 10-Q MD&A sections, we found that earnings calls provided the most informative setting for detecting shifting in targets.
Our study focuses on a restricted sample of S&P 100 firms due to computational cost.
The sample covers between January 2010 and December 2024, producing 5,615 firm-quarter observations across 64 quarters.

On average, the
LLM-based method
extracted 150.21 targets per call, with 77.95 originating from the presentation and 76.89 from the Q&A.
Of the 5,176 observations for which a moving target score could be computed, the mean score was 0.8713, with a standard deviation of 0.0848.
On the other hand, the
NER-based method
extracted 40.36 targets per call, with 31.77 originating from the presentation and 9.61 from the Q&A.
The mean score was 0.7147, with a standard deviation of 0.1656.

3.2
Implementation Details

To extract performance targets, we employ Google’s
Gemini-2.5-Pro
model and design prompts that separately request targets from the management presentation and the analyst Q&A portions of each transcript, as shown in Tab.
5
.
This design enables the model
h_{\text{LLM}}
to identify semantically rich and context-aware targets that may be expressed through paraphrase, in contrast to the keyword-oriented NER extraction.
Additionally, we employ OpenAI’s
text-embedding-3-large
model as an off-the-shelf pre-trained text encoder
f_{T}(\cdot)
in Eq.
1
.
We set
\tau=0.65
for the threshold in Eq.
2
.

For validation, we adopt the empirical design of
Cohen & Nguyen 2024
. We construct equal-weighted quintile portfolios sorted on the moving targets score in a calendar-time framework, and we estimate Fama–MacBeth cross-sectional regressions with standard controls. This design allows us to test whether the LLM-based scores retain predictive power for subsequent stock returns, thereby providing a direct comparison to the original methodology.

Figure 2:

Comparison of excess returns, Fama–French 3-factor alphas, and 5-factor alphas under the NER-based vs. LLM-based scoring approaches.
Estimates are based on a calendar-time portfolio strategy that goes long in firms in the lowest quintile (Q1) of the Moving Targets distribution and short in those in the highest quintile (Q5).
This design captures the return spread between firms with relatively fewer abandoned targets and those with more pronounced target shifting.
The LLM-based approach consistently delivers stronger predictive power than the NER-based one.

3.3
Results

The LLM-based method extracts targets with reduced noise.

Recall that our analysis shows the NER-based method frequently extracts generic fragments that lack clear economic meaning, as presented in Tab.
1
.
In contrast, Tab.
2
show that the LLM-based method identifies substantively meaningful items such as
market share
,
revenue growth
,
cash flow
, and
dividends
.
This indicates that LLM-based extraction substantially mitigates the noise problem and more accurately captures firms’ moving target metrics.

Targets extracted by the LLM-based method retain richer contextual information.

Another limitation of NER-based extraction is the systematic loss of contextual qualifiers. For example, as shown in Fig.
4
, NER extracts only broad terms like
revenue
, dropping distinctions across segments such as
Data Center revenue
or
Compute revenue
.
In contrast, Fig.
5
illustrates that the LLM-based method preserves these qualifiers, producing more granular targets such as
Quarterly Data Center revenue growth
or
Quarterly Compute revenue growth
. This richer contextual information enhances explainability and allows a more precise mapping between managerial communication and firm fundamentals.

The LLM-based method enhances predictive accuracy for subsequent stock performance declines.

We next evaluate the economic value of the extracted metrics using portfolio sorts and Fama–MacBeth regressions. Tab.
3
shows that quintile portfolios formed on the LLM-based Moving Targets score exhibit significantly negative Q5–Q1 spreads, with alphas remaining robust after controlling for standard risk factors. By contrast, the NER-based scores yield weaker and mostly insignificant spreads.
Consistent evidence from Fig.
2
and Tab.
4
indicates that the LLM-based score loads with stronger negative coefficients in cross-sectional regressions of returns, offering predictive power for subsequent stock underperformance.
Together, these results confirm that LLM-based extraction not only reduces noise and preserves context, but also delivers a more economically meaningful predictor of firms’ future stock performance.

4
Conclusion & Future Work

This paper proposed an LLM-based method for measuring firms’
moving targets
from earnings call transcripts.
By reducing noise, preserving contextual detail, and improving predictive accuracy, our approach extends the original NER-based method and delivers more economically meaningful estimates of firms’ target shifting.

Future work may further enhance this method by analyzing sector-specific variations in moving targets.
In particular, industry- and sector-level classification, combined with contextual prompt engineering, could yield even more precise and explainable measures of managerial emphasis across heterogeneous business environments.
