Discrepancy Between Clinical Suspicion and Legal Outcomes in Suspected Child Abuse: A 10-Year Single-Center Study
Authors: Hye-Ri Jang, Chloe Jooan Lee, Ju Hyun Back, Eun Young Kim, Jaeso Cho, Hunmin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite mandatory reporting, many suspected child abuse cases never proceed to formal investigation in Korea. We retrospectively reviewed 83 pediatric patients evaluated for suspected child abuse at a tertiary hospital between 2015 and 2025, screened using the Finding Instrument for Non-accidental Deeds score, and analyzed clinical characteristics, injury severity, and investigative outcomes. The mean age was 4.3 years, and mechanical injury was the most common abuse type (n = 68). Twenty-four patients (28.9%) were severe cases requiring surgery or intensive care or resulting in death. Overall, 33 patients (39.8%) were determined as ‘no suspicion,’ including 11 of 24 severe cases (45.8%); this proportion decreased from 45.7% to 37.8% after the 2021 policy reform. A critical gap persists between medical recognition of child abuse and the legal response, warranting closer integration of clinical assessment with child protection and investigative systems.Keywords: Child Abuse, Mandatory Reporting, Child Protective Services, Mass Screening

Child maltreatment is a major global public health concern. A meta-analysis of 111 studies estimated a self-reported prevalence of physical abuse of 226 per 1,000 children, compared with 3 per 1,000 in informant-based studies, indicating that most abuse remains hidden from official systems.1 Childhood maltreatment is associated with depression, suicidality, substance use, and long-term cognitive and physical impairment.2,3 The stakes are particularly high in young children: inflicted traumatic brain injury is the leading cause of serious head injury in infancy, with an incidence of 29.7 per 100,000 person-years during the first year of life.4 Recognition in medical settings is imperfect; approximately one-third of abusive head trauma cases are missed at the initial visit,5 and many severely abused infants have previously presented with ‘sentinel’ injuries that were not acted upon.6 Even when abuse is suspected, clinicians fail to report a substantial proportion of injuries that they consider likely to be abusive.7

In Korea, child abuse reporting has increased steeply over the past decade, yet physician participation remains low relative to other mandated reporters,8 and international comparisons suggest that officially reported maltreatment captures only a fraction of the true burden.9 Public outrage following the death of a 16-month-old girl in 2020 prompted legislative reform, including an immediate-separation system enforced from March 30, 2021, which allows authorities to separate a child from caregivers when abuse is repeatedly or strongly suspected.10 Whether these strengthened policies have translated into more consistent investigative and legal responses to medically identified abuse remains unknown. We therefore reviewed 10 years of suspected child abuse evaluations at a tertiary referral hospital to quantify the discrepancy between clinical suspicion and subsequent legal disposition.

We retrospectively reviewed pediatric patients (< 18 years of age) evaluated for suspected child abuse at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital between 2015 and 2025, identified from institutional child abuse response records and electronic medical records. Because structured screening instruments improve the identification of abuse by healthcare providers,11 we retrospectively applied the Finding Instrument for Non-accidental Deeds (FIND) score, a screening tool developed for Korean emergency departments through a modified Delphi process,12 to all candidate cases. The instrument comprises items addressing physical findings suspicious for inflicted injury, severe injury such as intracranial hemorrhage or fracture, inconsistency in the caregiver’s account, a reported mechanism incompatible with the injury, inappropriate caregiver–child interaction, inadequate hygiene or clothing, and unusually delayed presentation. Severe cases were defined as those requiring surgery or pediatric intensive care unit admission, or resulting in death. Investigative outcomes were categorized from medical and social work records as ‘no suspicion’ (closed by investigative authorities without further action), ‘managed by police or related agencies,’ or ‘not reported,’ and were stratified by the enforcement date of the immediate-separation system (March 30, 2021). Under the Korean prosecutorial framework, a disposition of ‘no suspicion’ exists in two forms—‘crime not established’ and ‘insufficient evidence’ (Regulation on Prosecutorial Case Affairs, Article 115); all ‘no suspicion’ dispositions in the present cohort corresponded to the ‘insufficient evidence’ category, in which a case was closed because the available evidence was insufficient to establish a criminal offense despite documented medical suspicion. In practice, after a hospital report the investigating police forwarded a case for prosecution only when non-medical corroborating evidence was available, such as a confession by the alleged perpetrator, statements from people around the child describing the abusive circumstances, or incriminating text messages or video recordings, rather than on the basis of the medical assessment alone. Descriptive statistics were used.

In Korea, child abuse reporting is governed by the Act on Special Cases concerning the Punishment of Child Abuse Crimes. Under Article 10, any person may report suspected abuse, whereas heads of medical institutions and medical personnel are designated mandatory reporters who must report immediately upon knowledge or reasonable suspicion; failure to do so without justifiable cause is subject to an administrative fine of up to KRW 10 million. Because reporting is required on suspicion alone, clinicians assess at-risk children using the mandatory-reporter checklist of the National Center for the Rights of the Child, comprising nine items, with reporting recommended when one or more items are met, and file the report to the police through the 112 emergency line. Local police then responds on site to interview the child, the caregiver, and the reporting clinician, and the case is transferred through the police station with jurisdiction over the child’s residence to the metropolitan police agency for investigation.

A total of 83 patients were included (mean age at presentation, 4.3 years; mean follow-up, 8.5 months) (Table 1). Seventeen patients (20.5%) had one positive FIND item, 25 (30.1%) had two, and 40 (48.2%) had three or more; one patient (1.2%) with no positive item was included on the basis of overall clinical suspicion documented by the treating physicians. Mechanical injury was the most common abuse type (n = 68, 81.9%), followed by neglect (n = 7, 8.4%), emotional abuse (n = 5, 6.0%), and intoxication (n = 3, 3.6%). Twenty patients (24.1%) required intensive care unit admission, nine (10.8%) underwent surgery (three neurosurgical procedures for intracranial hemorrhage, comprising craniectomy with evacuation of subdural hemorrhage or subdural drainage; three general or abdominal procedures, two orthopedic procedures for long-bone injury; and one oral and maxillofacial procedure for a mandibular fracture), 19 (22.9%) required ongoing treatment after discharge, and six (7.2%) died. In total, 24 patients (28.9%) met the definition of severe cases.

Overall, 49 patients (59.0%) were managed by police or related agencies, whereas 33 (39.8%) were determined as ‘no suspicion’ and one (1.2%) was not reported (Table 1). Among the 24 severe cases, 11 (45.8%) were nevertheless closed as ‘no suspicion,’ including patients who required intensive care, underwent surgery, or died. Before March 30, 2021, 21 of 46 patients (45.7%) were closed as ‘no suspicion,’ compared with 14 of 37 (37.8%) thereafter, while the proportion managed by police or related agencies increased from 52.2% to 62.2%. Among the ‘no suspicion’ cases, the proportion of severe cases was 33.3% (7/21) before and 28.6% (4/14) after the reform.

The principal finding of this study is a substantial and persistent gap between medical recognition of suspected child abuse and the formal investigative or legal response. Nearly 40% of children who screened positive on a structured instrument, and almost half of those with life-threatening outcomes, did not proceed beyond a determination of ‘no suspicion.’ This discrepancy reflects the differing evidentiary standards applied in clinical assessment and criminal investigation, rather than necessarily indicating that any individual legal determination was incorrect. This is consistent with international evidence that child abuse cases attrite at every step of the criminal justice process; a meta-analysis found that child abuse was less likely than most other felonies to result in charges or incarceration.13 Contributing factors may include the difficulty of establishing perpetration in preverbal children, reliance on caregiver testimony, evidentiary thresholds that clinical findings alone cannot satisfy, and incomplete communication between hospitals and investigative agencies. Systematic screening and structured hospital responses improve detection,14,15 and current guidance emphasizes meticulous documentation and multidisciplinary evaluation to support downstream investigation.16 Our data suggest, however, that detection alone is insufficient when medical evidence is not effectively translated into the child protection and justice systems.

The determination pathway in Korea also differs structurally from those of other high-income countries (Table 2). In the United States, reports are received and screened by state child protective services agencies, which investigate and render substantiation decisions as child-welfare determinations, with law enforcement involved in parallel only when criminal conduct is suspected.17 In Japan, regional child guidance centers hold primary statutory responsibility: a universal reporting duty and the nationwide 189 hotline channel reports to these centers, which must confirm the child’s safety within 48 hours and may place the child in temporary protective custody.18,19 In Korea, by contrast, reports are filed to the police, and although dedicated child abuse officials have conducted joint investigations in municipalities since 2020, the initial determination of ‘no suspicion’ is effectively made within the criminal-justice framework rather than by a child-welfare or multidisciplinary body.10 When the evidentiary standards of criminal investigation are applied at the entry point, medically suspected cases lacking prosecutable evidence may exit the protection system entirely rather than being diverted to welfare-based assessment.9

Beyond these quantitative findings, our institutional experience anecdotally suggests additional friction at the hospital–agency interface, although these observations were not systematically measured. After mandatory reporting, the transfer of cases from the local police substation to the responsible police station, and the subsequent assignment of a dedicated child abuse official, could take several days to more than a week, with limited communication to the hospital. During this interval, acutely admitted children were sometimes discharged before the process was completed. Medical staff often had to re-explain the clinical circumstances repeatedly across successive officials and agencies, and investigative outcomes were seldom relayed back to the hospital unless actively requested. In contrast, we noted at least one case in which continuous direct communication between a single investigating agency and the medical team appeared to coincide with a more expeditious legal process, although no causal inference can be drawn from a single observation. While these experiences are anecdotal and require formal evaluation, they are consistent with prior calls for hospital-based multidisciplinary child protection teams with a designated coordinator and a single communication channel across agencies.16

The decrease in ‘no suspicion’ determinations after the 2021 reform (45.7% to 37.8%), with a corresponding increase in agency-managed cases, suggests that the strengthened policy environment may have improved investigative uptake, although the difference was modest and our sample size precludes formal inference. Notably, even after the reform, more than one-third of clinically screened cases, including severe cases, were closed without further protective action. Given the risk of repeated abuse after sentinel presentations6 and the lifelong sequelae of maltreatment,2,3,20 such closures warrant mechanisms to ensure that children with residual clinical concern remain linked to child-protective follow-up even when a criminal investigation is not pursued.

This study has limitations. It was a retrospective, single-center study at a tertiary referral hospital, which may overrepresent severe cases. The FIND score was applied retrospectively rather than at the point of care. Investigative outcomes were ascertained from medical and social work records, which may not capture final judicial dispositions or actions taken outside our institution. The periods before and after the 2021 reform differ in duration and case mix, and secular trends in awareness may confound the comparison.

In conclusion, over a 10-year period at a tertiary center, a considerable proportion of clinically suspected child abuse cases, including nearly half of severe cases, did not proceed through formal investigative pathways. These findings indicate a critical gap between medical recognition and legal response, and highlight the need for structured interfaces between hospitals, child protection agencies, and law enforcement, including standardized forensic documentation, hospital-based multidisciplinary child protection teams, and feedback mechanisms informing clinicians of final case outcomes.

Ethics statement

The present study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. B-2512-1014-105). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design.
