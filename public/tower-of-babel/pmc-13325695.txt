A Generalized Automated Framework for Thermal Characterization: From Bulk Materials to Complex Heterostructures
Authors: Miguel Ángel Tenaguillo, Elena Pérez‐Picazo, Olga Caballero‐Calero, Cristina V Manzano, Marisol Martin‐González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrecise determination of thermal conductivity in multilayer structures remains a critical challenge for advancing thermoelectric technologies. Conventional techniques often struggle with surface roughness or require complex preparation, hindering the characterization of electrodeposited materials. To address this gap, a fully automated, MATLAB‐based computational framework operationalizing the generalized photoacoustic theory for a robust inverse analysis is presented. The methodology is rigorously benchmarked against Bi2Te3/3D anodic aluminum oxide (3D‐AAO) reference nanocomposites, reproducing established literature values with high fidelity. Subsequently, the capability of this framework is demonstrated through the characterization of electrodeposited copper selenide (CuxSe) films. Results reveal a systematic dependence of thermal conductivity on copper stoichiometry, offering insights into anharmonicity‐driven phonon scattering mechanisms. Comprehensive sensitivity analyses confirm that the photoacoustic signal is predominantly dominated by the film's thermal conductivity. Thus, this work delivers an accessible, validated tool for in situ characterization of structurally non‐ideal films, accelerating next‐generation energy material development.Keywords: copper selenides, electrodeposition, inverse analysis, photoacoustic method, thermal conductivity, thermoelectric materials

Introduction

Thermal management has emerged as a critical factor dictating the performance and reliability of next‐generation electronic and energy conversion devices [1, 2]. This challenge intensifies as dimensions shrink to the nanoscale, where the efficient dissipation of waste heat transitions from a secondary design consideration to a fundamental bottleneck that governs component lifespan and efficiency [3]. The issue is particularly acute in complex, multilayered architectures—such as those in 3D‐integrated circuits, high‐power light‐emitting diodes, and thermoelectric generators—whose overall performance is intrinsically limited by heat transport across these structures [4]. In such heterogeneous systems, the primary impediment to heat flow is often not the intrinsic properties of the constituent materials, but rather the thermal boundary conductance (TBC) at their interfaces [5, 6]. Discontinuities in phonon spectra across these junctions lead to significant scattering and give rise to high interfacial thermal resistance.

Consequently, designing novel thermal interface materials (TIMs), from graphene‐based composites to liquid metal alloys, demands metrology techniques capable of precisely resolving the properties of individual layers and their boundaries within a complete material stack [7, 8, 9]. However, characterizing these properties in‐situ presents a significant experimental challenge for films synthesized via techniques like electrodeposition. This fabrication method often yields microstructures with inherent surface roughness, porosity, or inhomogeneities that may confound conventional measurement methods and complicate data interpretation [10, 11].

While non‐contact optical pump‐probe techniques, such as Time Domain (TDTR) and Frequency Domain Thermoreflectance (FDTR), are mainstays for thermal analysis due to their exceptional temporal resolution [12, 13], their reliance on detecting subtle changes in surface reflectivity makes them highly sensitive to surface quality. Significant roughness or non‐uniformity can introduce artifacts that necessitate complex sample preparation or transfer to idealized substrates [14, 15], posing a substantial barrier for electrodeposited‐grown, structurally non‐ideal films.

The photoacoustic method (PA) offers a robust alternative for these challenging systems. In this technique, a modulated light source induces periodic heating, and the resulting thermal waves diffusing into an adjacent gas generate an acoustic signal detected by a microphone [16]. Because this mechanism averages the thermal response over the illuminated area, it is inherently less sensitive to surface topography than methods based on specular reflection [17, 18]. Additionally, the thermal penetration depth is controlled by the modulation frequency, enabling non‐destructive depth‐profiling of multilayer structures [19]. The theoretical framework for analyzing such N‐layer systems, established by Hu et al., robustly models optical absorption in any layer and accounts for interfacial thermal resistances [20].

Despite the capability of the PA technique and the rigor of the Hu et al. model, a critical methodological gap has hindered its widespread adoption: the lack of an accessible, flexible software tool for solving the complex non‐linear inverse problem required to extract the thermal parameters of the multilayer stack. This work addresses this gap by introducing a fully automated, MATLAB‐based framework that operationalizes the Hu et al. theory for a practical application.

We first validate the framework's accuracy against well‐characterized Bi2Te3/“3D‐AAO” nanocomposite reference samples. Although these structures are not planar multilayers, our generalized N‐layer theoretical framework mathematically treats these 3D architectures as effective homogeneous layers, successfully reproducing literature values with high fidelity. We then demonstrate its capability by determining the previously uncharacterized thermal conductivity of electrodeposited CuxSe films, a material system of high interest for thermoelectric applications [21], revealing a clear dependence on stoichiometry that is critical for their thermoelectric applications. Finally, we compare the advantages of our approach to other common thermal characterization techniques highlighting its particularity for electrodeposited‐grown material systems.

Although inverse photoacoustic analysis for multilayer characterization is established in principle, this work addresses a three‐fold gap (i) the absence of accessible, user‐friendly software implementation, creating a barrier to adoption across materials science laboratories; (ii) the lack of rigorous validation specifically for electrodeposited structures, which are increasingly important for scalable thermoelectric and energy harvesting device fabrication; and (iii) the opportunity to demonstrate that this non‐contact, non‐destructive technique uniquely captures stoichiometry‐dependent phase transitions in complex ionic materials (Cu+ dynamics in CuxSe), information often obscured by conventional contact‐based thermal measurement methods.

This convergence of accessibility, validation, and application to a technologically pertinent material system makes this work particularly timely for materials discovery in next‐generation energy conversion.

Experimental Validation and Results

To rigorously establish the robustness and accuracy of our MATLAB‐based inverse analysis methodology, a validation study was conducted using well‐characterized reference samples. The objective was to reproduce previously published thermal conductivity values, thereby confirming the reliability of our experimental setup and computational framework before proceeding to the analysis of novel materials. The chosen reference systems were the 3D Bi2Te3 interconnected nanowire network composites presented by Ruiz‐Clavijo et al., [22], as they represent a complex, multi‐material system whose effective thermal properties are non‐trivial and have been previously determined using the photoacoustic technique.

Validation with Reference Bi2Te3 Nanocomposites

For this validation, we analyzed two specific samples from the original work study, fabricated by electrodeposition within 3D nanoporous anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) templates [23] and a recent review [24]. These samples consist of arrays of Bi2Te3 nanowires of 50 nm in diameter, which are connected via transverse nanowires to their first neighbors each certain length. These samples differ in their internal nanostructure, specifically in their average distance between transverse nanochannels (P), which is controlled by the periodic pulse timing during anodization of the 3D AAO (540 s and 360 s) and then corresponds to the distance between the connecting nanowires in the electrodeposited 3D Bi2Te3 interconnected nanowire network. All samples were prepared for measurement following the procedure detailed in the previous section.

The determination of thermal properties from photoacoustic data constitutes a non‐linear inverse problem, which we solve by minimizing the objective function, χ2(Equation 8), using the “fmincon” solver from MATLAB's Optimization Toolbox. The error surface of such a problem is often complex, potentially featuring multiple shallow, local minimum rather than a single, well‐defined global minimum. Consequently, the solver's convergence can be sensitive to the specified parameter bounds.

To address this, we implement a systematic iterative fitting strategy. The process begins with a broad, physically‐informed search range, which is then progressively narrowed based on the initial results of the fitting. The observation of slightly different convergence values from distinct, constrained fitting ranges indicates a broad and shallow minimum in the error landscape. In such cases, claiming the result of a single fit would confer a false sense of accuracy. We therefore adopted a robust approach of averaging the results from several stable, constrained fits. This procedure yields a final value that better represents the center of the most probable solution region, pragmatically accounting for the inherent sensitivity of the inverse problem in these complex systems.

The first reference sample analyzed was the composite with a wider interconnection spacing (P ≈ 720 nm), corresponding to the 540 s pulse fabrication 3D AAO. This sample has a reported out‐of‐plane thermal conductivity value of κref = 0.63 W/m·K. [22] Following our iterative strategy, our analysis yielded an averaged thermal conductivity of κfit = (0.62 ± 0.12) W/m·K. This result shows an excellent agreement with the reference, with a deviation of 2.4%, strongly validating the accuracy of our methodology. The fit between experimental data and the theoretical curve is shown in Figure 1a.

Next, the second sample, featuring a denser and more complex nanostructure (P = 346 nm, from the 360 s pulse 3D AAO fabrication), was analyzed. The published thermal conductivity for this sample is κref = 0.55 W/m·K. [22] The application of our fitting procedure, using appropriately constrained search ranges informed by a priori physical knowledge (i.e., the composite's thermal conductivity must be lower than that of a bulk Bi2Te3), produced a final fitted value of κfit = (0.51 ± 0.10) W/m·K. The corresponding data and fit are shown in Figure 1b.

The fitted value corresponds to a deviation of 8.5% from the reference value. While still a good agreement, the larger deviation compared to the first sample is insightful. We attribute this to the physical limitations of the 1D heat transfer model when applied to a system with significant 3D transport features. In the sample with denser interconnections (P = 346 nm), 3D effects such as thermal flux line curvature and enhanced phonon scattering at the numerous junctions—described as “heat viscosity” by Ruiz‐Clavijo et al. —become more pronounced. These phenomena introduce transport characteristics that are not perfectly captured by the 1D model, leading to a larger (yet still reasonable) discrepancy. Characterization of CuxSe Films

Having rigorously established the validation of our inverse analysis framework, we proceeded to a more challenging objective: the characterization of a multilayered system whose thermal properties for one specific layer were previously unknown. For this purpose, we selected a series of copper selenide (CuxSe) films. This system serves as an ideal test case, not only due to its contemporary relevance in thermoelectric applications, but also because its fabrication via electrodeposition presents material characteristics—such as inherent surface roughness and complex interfacial properties—that often pose significant challenges for conventional thermal conductivity measurement techniques.

The CuxSe films were synthesized via a three‐electrode electrochemical deposition process onto a gold‐coated silicon wafer (Au/Cr/Si). Distinct stoichiometries were obtained by applying specific constant potentials: 0.155 V for Cu1.41Se, 0.125 V for Cu1.55Se, 0.063 V for Cu1.80Se, and 0.065 V for Cu2.16Se. All electrodeposition procedures were carried out for a duration of 2 hours at room temperature. The electrolyte solution consisted of 15 mM CuSO4·5H2O (≥99.0%, Merck) + 5 mM H2SeO3 (99.999%, Merck) + 0.05 M H2SO4 (95%–97%, L.T. Baker), and a Pt mesh was used as counter electrode while the reference electrode consisted of Ag/AgCl (0.3 M KCl). Additionally, for the photoacoustic measurements, an 80 nm titanium layer was subsequently deposited on the CuxSe surface. The resulting multilayer stack analyzed in our model, depicted schematically in Figure 3a, was therefore Ti/CuxSe/Au/Cr/Si. This multilayer configuration is representative of many functional devices fabricated through sequential deposition processes and underscores the need for a characterization technique capable of handling such complexity.

The central scientific finding of this investigation is the clear and pronounced dependence of thermal conductivity on the copper‐to‐selenium ratio, as plotted in Figure 3b. The thermal conductivity is the highest for the copper‐deficient phase (k = 1.66 W/m·K for Cu1.41Se) and decreases systematically and significantly as the material becomes more copper‐rich [25], reaching a minimum of k = 0.83 W/m·K for the Cu2.16Se sample. This trend, which does not correlate with the measured grain or crystallite sizes shown in Table 2, points toward stoichiometry‐driven phonon scattering mechanisms that will be described in depth in the subsequent sections.

To rigorously validate the fidelity of the inverse solver and ensure the exclusion of parasitic boundary effects, we performed a systematic Interfacial Thermal Resistance (ITR) analysis on reference Ni films and the Ti‐CuxSe boundary (Figure S1). The analysis of Ni control samples yielded consistent ITR values with the literature, independent of thickness, confirming the model's ability to strictly decouple interfacial contributions from bulk transport. Furthermore, this sensitivity allowed us to resolve distinct transport regimes in the CuxSe films: while copper‐deficient compositions (x ≤ 1.80) maintain a low thermal resistance analogous to the control samples, the structural transition in the copper‐rich phase (x = 2.16) induces an order of magnitude surge in ITR. Accounting for these boundary dynamics ensures that the reported effective thermal conductivities represent the intrinsic bulk properties of the material.

Analysis of Model Sensitivity

To rigorously validate the fitting results, a sensitivity analysis was performed to ascertain the robustness of the inverse model and to identify the parameters that most significantly influence the photoacoustic signal. This analysis is crucial for confirming that the experimental data contains enough information to reliably determine the parameter of interest —in this case, the thermal conductivity of the CuxSe layer. The analysis is based on the normalized sensitivity coefficient, defined as the partial derivative of the model output (the photoacoustic phase shift, ∆ϕ) with respect to a given parameter (pj ), normalized by the parameter itself:

This dimensionless coefficient quantifies the relative change in the output signal for a relative change in a specific input parameter, thereby isolating its influence. We evaluate its absolute value, |S p,j |, to compare the magnitude of the influence of each parameter—density (ρ), specific heat (Cp ), thickness (L), and thermal conductivity (κ)— irrespective of whether they increase or decrease the phase shift.

The results of this analysis, presented in Figure 4 for all four sample compositions, are unequivocal. Across the entire experimental frequency range, the thermal conductivity (κ) of the CuxSe layer consistently emerges as the parameter to which the model output is most sensitive. Its sensitivity coefficient is approximately an order of magnitude higher than those of density and specific heat across the experimental frequency range, and only becomes comparable to the thickness sensitivity within a limited frequency window. This pronounced sensitivity demonstrates that the photoacoustic phase shift is predominantly governed by the thermal transport characteristics of the CuxSe layer. This finding imparts a high degree of confidence in the values extracted through the inverse fitting procedure, as the algorithm is primarily driven by the parameter we seek to determine, minimizing the risk of correlations or convergence to non‐physical solutions influenced by less sensitive parameters. Importantly, the CuxSe layer thickness enters the model as an independently measured input parameter, obtained by profilometry, rather than as a fitted quantity, so that the strong sensitivity to κ is not entangled with an ill‐defined thickness.

Uncertainty Analysis and Statistical Validation

A comprehensive assessment of measurement uncertainty in a complex, multilayer model through analytical error propagation is notoriously challenging. Such an approach would require a precise knowledge of the uncertainties associated with every fixed parameter in the model, from the thicknesses of all five layers to the thermal and optical properties of the Ti, Au, Cr, and Si layers, many of which are not known with high precision.

To address around this complexity, we adopted a pragmatic and experimentally grounded statistical approach. Each of the four CuxSe samples was measured multiple times under identical conditions, providing a direct assessment of the experimental repeatability and overall uncertainty. The mean and standard deviation of the thermal conductivity for each composition were calculated from these repeated measurements and are presented in Table 3. The calculated standard deviations, which range from 0.04 to 0.15 W/m·K, are consistent with the conservative 20% error estimation, considering the numerous contributions that raise uncertainty, depicted by the error bars in Figure 3b, thereby justifying its use as a reasonable representation of the total experimental uncertainty. This 20% conservative uncertainty implicitly includes the combined effect of uncertainties in the fixed thermal and optical parameters of the transducer and substrate layers, which are difficult to propagate analytically in such a multilayer model.

To further validate that the observed trend in thermal conductivity is a genuine physical effect dependent on stoichiometry and not a product of random experimental variation, we performed a one‐way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). This statistical test is ideally suited to determine whether there are any statistically significant differences between the means of two or more independent groups. The ANOVA returned a p‐value well below 0.05, indicating a statistically significant difference among the four sample groups. Following this analysis, it is a standard practice to identify which specific pair of groups, or in this case samples, are different by applying a Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post‐hoc test. The results, summarized in Table 3, confirm that the thermal conductivities of the Cu1.41Se (tetragonal structure), Cu1.55Se (cubic structure), and the Cu‐rich (Cu1.80Se and Cu2.16Se) with a combination of cubic and monoclinic structures are statistically distinct from one another.

Stoichiometry, Structure, Morphology, and Thermal Transport

The central experimental result—the systematic decrease in thermal conductivity with increasing copper content (Figure 3b)—presents an intriguing departure from the predictions of classical defect physics. From a conventional standpoint, the Cu‐deficient sample, Cu1.41Se, contains the highest concentration of point defects (copper vacancies), which should act as effective phonon scattering centers and lead to the lowest lattice thermal conductivity. Our observation of the opposite trend indicates a more complex and dominant scattering mechanism at play, intrinsically linked to the stoichiometry and superionic nature of the material.

These stoichiometric variations also induce changes in nanostructure, crystallinity, and morphology. High‐resolution XRD (Figure 2) reveals progression from tetragonal (Cu1.41Se) to cubic (Cu1.55Se) and mixed cubic‐monoclinic phases (Cu‐rich), while Table 2 shows modest variations in crystallite size (30–40 nm), and thickness (2.5–6.2 µm), plus electrodeposition‐induced surface features. In general, such phase transitions, size refinement, and morphology can lower κ via phonon scattering or dispersion changes; however, none correlate with the measured κ(x) trend (highest in tetragonal Cu1.41Se, lowest in mixed Cu2.16Se). This weak structural correlation is consistent with superionic Cu2−xSe reports, where intrinsic anharmonicity and liquid‐like cations dominate over boundaries. Thus, the data strongly suggest that stoichiometry‑tuned anharmonicity is the primary κ controller here, surpassing grain‑size and interface scattering effects.

The solution to this apparent paradox lies in the concept of lattice anharmonicity [26]. Recent studies on the similar Cu2‐xS system by Song et al. [27] provide the crucial physical insight. Their work, based on detailed structural analysis and one‐particle potential (OPP) calculations, demonstrates that copper vacancies do not merely act as static scattering centers. Instead, they fundamentally alter the potential energy landscape in which the remaining copper ions vibrate. In stoichiometric or copper‐rich compositions (e.g., Cu2.16Se), the material exists in a state of extreme anharmonicity. The mobile Cu ions are dynamically disordered, moving within broad, shallow, and highly anharmonic potential wells. This dynamic disorder is the primary source of intense phonon‐phonon scattering, which is ultimately responsible for the material's intrinsically low, “glass‐like” thermal conductivity.

Counter‐intuitively, as copper vacancies are introduced (moving toward Cu1.41Se), the potential wells surrounding the remaining copper ions become deeper, more localized, and more harmonic [27]. This reduction in anharmonicity significantly weakens the phonon‐phonon scattering channel. The net effect is that, despite the introduction of vacancies, the overall lattice thermal conductivity increases because the dominant anharmonic scattering mechanism has been suppressed. Our results are consistent with, and provide indirect experimental support for, this “anharmonicity‐tuning” mechanism in the CuxSe system.

This interpretation refines the original “Phonon‐Liquid Electron‐Crystal” (PLEC) concept. While the idea of a “liquid‐like” sublattice of ions is a useful picture, inelastic neutron scattering studies by Voneshen et al. on Cu2Se have shown that the ion hopping timescales are too slow to directly disrupt the propagation of most heat‐carrying acoustic phonons [28]. This conclusion is rooted in a critical mismatch of fundamental timescales. The majority of heat‐carrying acoustic phonons possess vibrational periods on the femtosecond‐to‐picosecond scale (corresponding to THz frequencies). In contrast, inelastic neutron scattering data reveals that the diffusive hopping of the Cu ions, while fast, occurs over characteristic timescales of several picoseconds. Consequently, a heat‐carrying phonon completes many vibrational cycles before the local ionic environment has time to dynamically rearrange. This means that phonons don't interact with the diffusing ions, rather, they propagate through a statically disordered lattice. This static disorder, however, is precisely what creates the broad and anharmonic potential wells that are the true source of the higher‐order phonon scattering. Therefore, the ultralow thermal conductivity arises not from the kinematic disruption of phonons by a diffusive liquid, but rather from the intrinsic anharmonicity inherent to this dynamically disordered, superionic state [28]. Further evidence for the correlation between anharmonicity and suppressed thermal conductivity comes from studies on related superionic systems. For instance, work by Izumi et al. on AgCrSe2 demonstrates that anisotropic atomic displacement parameters (ADPs), which are direct measure of the amplitude and anisotropy of atomic vibrations, increase with compositional changes that enhance anharmonicity, correlating directly with a reduction in thermal conductivity [29]. Our XRD results (Figure 2), showing a transition toward more disordered cubic and monoclinic phases in Cu‐rich samples, are the structural manifestation of this increased dynamic disorder and associated anharmonicity.

Furthermore, in systems with such an extreme anharmonicity, higher‐order scattering processes become critically important. As shown by Feng et al., [26] the conventional picture limited to three‐phonon processes is often insufficient. In such strongly anharmonic systems, higher‐order scattering processes, including four‐phonon interactions, have been shown to become significant, or even dominant, channels for thermal resistance in related materials, and are likely to contribute here as well. Our composition‐dependent κ data in CuxSe are consistent with this picture of enhanced higher‐order phonon scattering in the most anharmonic, Cu‐rich regimes.

To summarize, the systematic evolution of thermal conductivity with stoichiometry delineates three distinct transport regimes (Figure 5). In the substoichiometric limit (1.41 < x < 1.55), heat propagation is primarily hindered by defect scattering arising from the high concentration of copper vacancies. As the copper content increases into the intermediate range (x = 1.80), the material enters a superionic transition regime, where increasing Cu+ mobility introduces substantial dynamic disorder within the rigid selenium sublattice. Finally, at the highest stoichiometry level (x = 2.16), the system approaches a regime often described as phonon‑liquid‑like, in which extreme lattice anharmonicity and the dynamically disordered Cu sublattice suppress the phonon mean free path to near‑interatomic distances, yielding κ values comparable to highly disordered solids. The sensitivity of our technique allows these subtle but critical structure–property relationships to be resolved clearly in thin‑film CuxSe.

Therefore, while the CuxSe system already operates near the amorphous or “phonon‐liquid” limit where further size reduction yields diminishing returns, our results demonstrate that nanostructured superionic thermoelectrics can still be effectively engineered by targeting stoichiometry‐driven anharmonicity rather than relying exclusively on grain‐size reduction.

Comparative Analysis

To contextualize our methodology, it is useful to compare it with thermoreflectance techniques, which are used for thermal conductivity measurement of thin layers, such as Time‐Domain Thermoreflectance (TDTR) and Frequency‐Domain Thermoreflectance (FDTR), particularly when analyzing structurally complex electrodeposited films. These optical pump‐probe methods, which rely on detecting changes in a transducer's surface reflectivity, are highly sensitive to the sample's surface quality and structural uniformity.

This sensitivity presents practical challenges. For instance, analyzing our Cu1.80Se film, with a thickness of 5.1 µm and analogous to those analyzed previously, using a commercial PicoTR system, required an elaborate ex‐situ preparation. The electrodeposited film had to be physically transferred from its native Au/Cr/Si substrate onto a non‐conductive glass substrate using an SU‐8 polymer resin as an adhesive. The original growth substrate was subsequently removed by immersion in liquid nitrogen, a process that leverages thermal expansion mismatch and carries a non‐trivial risk of inducing micro‐cracks or delamination of the film. Furthermore, the measurement required the deposition of a 100 nm platinum (Pt) layer acting as a transducer, which is considerably more costly than the Ti layer used for our PA measurements.

The thermal conductivity extracted from the TDTR analysis was (1.02 ± 0.20) W/m·K. To ensure a rigorous cross‐validation, these measurements were conducted on a sample from the same synthesis batch (Cu1.80Se) using a 20 MHz modulation frequency (45 µm pump spot at 25 mW; 25 µm probe spot at 2 mW). Although the physical specimens differed, since TDTR used a 5.1 µm film with a 100 nm Pt transducer, whereas PA evaluated a 3.3 µm film with an 80 nm Ti transducer, the generalized N‐layer model explicitly accounts for these distinct thicknesses and interfacial structures, ensuring the extraction of consistent intrinsic thermal properties. While this value is consistent with the stoichiometric trend established by our PA measurements (Figure 3b), its acquisition required analyzing a fundamentally altered material stack: Pt/CuxSe/SU‐8/Glass. In this transferred multilayer, the extra Pt/CuxSe, CuxSe/SU‑8, and SU‑8/glass interfaces add non‐negligible interfacial thermal resistances; their values have to be modelled or fitted in TDTR, which naturally increases the uncertainty and complexity of the analysis.

This necessity for in situ characterization of electrodeposited‐fabricated devices is not merely a matter of convenience; it is a critical enabler for technologies designed to mitigate energy inefficiency on a global scale. Improving the thermal management of industrial processes and electronic systems is widely recognized as a major route to reducing global energy losses and associated emissions, underscoring the need for reliable thermal metrology at the thin‑film and interface level. The ability to rapidly and accurately assess the thermal properties of novel material systems without altering them, as our photoacoustic methodology allows, directly accelerates the design‐fabrication‐characterization cycle. This, in turn, is fundamental for developing the next generation of thermoelectric generators and thermal interface materials needed to reclaim a significant fraction of this otherwise squandered energy, thereby offering a direct pathway to enhanced efficiency and reduced carbon footprint across multiple industrial sectors.

In contrast to these challenges, our PA methodology offers a key advantage, which lies in its signal generation and averaging. The PA signal originates from heat diffusion into a gas layer over a much larger area than the focused laser spot in thermoreflectance techniques. This process inherently averages over the microscopic variations in roughness and other properties, enabling a more robust characterization of the film's effective thermal properties in its electrodeposited‐grown state, making it less susceptible to local topological features.

Conclusions

We have developed and implemented a MATLAB‐based software tool that automates the inverse analysis of photoacoustic data from multilayer nanostructures, making the capable theoretical model of Hu et al. practically accessible. The methodology's accuracy and robustness were confirmed in two ways. First of all, our analysis of Bi2Te3/“3D‐AAO” reference nanocomposites reproduced literature values with excellent agreement. In second place, we successfully characterized a series of CuxSe films, determining their previously unknown thermal conductivity and revealing its clear dependence on stoichiometry, identifying a clear evolution from defect‐dominated phonon scattering in Cu‐deficient phases toward a strongly anharmonic regime in the Cu‐rich compositions, in line with the phonon‐liquid‐like picture reported for Cu2Se.

Beyond its scientific insights, this work delivers an accessible, open‐source tool that provides an accessible, open‐source implementation of high‐precision thermal metrology for structurally non‐ideal films, which are often challenging for conventional thermoreflectance techniques. By enabling high‐throughput screening of compositional libraries, in our laboratory implementation, the automated workflow enables the characterization of on the order of 10–12 samples per day under routine operating conditions, this framework significantly accelerates the design‐fabrication‐characterization cycle for next‐generation energy conversion materials. Ultimately, this validated methodology provides a reliable pathway for extracting critical design parameters, positioning it as a useful enabler for innovation in thermoelectric energy harvesting and advanced electronic cooling materials.

Theoretical Framework

This section presents an extended version of the generalized theoretical model for the photoacoustic (PA) effect in a multilayered structure, originally developed by Hu et al. [20] This framework forms the physical and mathematical basis for the inverse analysis method presented in this work. The model provides a comprehensive description of the thermal wave propagation through an N‐layer system, accounting for optical absorption within any layer and the thermal contact resistance at the interfaces.

Geometry and Model Assumptions

The physical system under consideration, depicted schematically in Figure 6, consists of N layers of finite thickness, indexed from i = 1 to N. This stack is bounded by a thermally thick backing material (substrate, indexed as i = 0) and a thermally thick, optically non‐absorbing gas medium (indexed as i = N + 1). The analysis is based on a 1D coordinate system, where the x‐axis is oriented perpendicular to the layer interfaces. The interface between layer i and layer i+1 is located at position li , and the thickness of layer i is given by Li = li – li‐1 .

Photoacoustic Signal Derivation

The photoacoustic signal originates from the periodic heating of the gas layer adjacent to the sample surface. According to the composite piston model, this pressure variation is primarily driven by the “thermal piston” effect for solid samples, where a thin layer of gas expands and contracts, acting as a piston on the rest of the gas volume. The mechanical piston effect, due to the physical thermal expansion of the sample itself, is generally negligible for solids at typical modulation frequencies and can be disregarded.

The amplitude and phase of the PA signal are therefore directly determined by the periodic temperature fluctuation at the sample‐gas interface (x = lN(0) in the gas coordinates). Unlike wave‐like propagation, thermal transport is a diffusive process, which inherently introduces a phase shift between the periodic heat source (the modulated laser beam) and the resulting temperature oscillation. This shift is mathematically captured within the complex thermal wave vector σi=(1+j)ω/(2αi), where the (1 + j) pre‐factor introduces an intrinsic phase shift of π/4 into the phasor representation of the propagating thermal wave. The subsequent derivation of the pressure signal in the gas via the thermal piston mode, which relates the gas expansion to the temperature at the sample surface, introduces an additional phase shift that results in the final characteristic −π/4 term in the measured signal.

This equation forms the theoretical basis for the inverse fitting procedure. By calculating the theoretical phase shift ∆ϕ as a function of the modulation frequency (f = ω/(2π)) and comparing it to the experimental data, the unknown thermal properties embedded within BN+1 expression can be determined.

Experimental and Computational Methodology

This section details the experimental apparatus used for acquiring photoacoustic (PA) data and the architecture of the custom MATLAB‐based software developed for performing the inverse analysis. The algorithms were implemented within the MATLAB environment (MathWorks, Natick, MA). Additionally, to ensure broad reproducibility, the code is compatible with version R2020b or later and operates without dependencies on property add‐on toolboxes.

Experimental Methodology

The experimental setup employed in our laboratory for photoacoustic (PA) measurements, illustrated schematically in Figure 7, is meticulously designed to optimize the signal‐to‐noise ratio and ensure the validity of the 1D heat transfer model. The core components are detailed below.

Schematic design of the photoacoustic (PA) experimental setup. An intensity‐modulated laser beam is guided via an optical fiber to the sample surface, which is enclosed within the PA cell. Periodic heating of the sample generates acoustic waves in the adjacent gas layer. These waves are detected by a high‐sensitivity microphone. The resulting electrical signal is then processed by a signal conditioner and analyzed by a lock‐in amplifier, which measures the phase shift relative to the laser modulation reference signal.

The excitation source consists of two distinct fiber‐coupled diode lasers, used alternatively depending on the modulation frequency of interest, allowing the coverage of a broad modulation frequency range. For low‐frequency regime (up to 4 kHz), a 980 nm laser (LDF‐10 Series) is utilized, while for the high‐frequency regime (up to 50 kHz), a 1070 nm Ytterbium fiber laser (IPG Photonics) is employed. The laser output is modulated by a built‐in function generator controlled by the lock‐in amplifier, allowing for precise frequency sweeps, typically conducted within an effective range of 10 Hz to 20 kHz.

It is critical to note that the experimental design rigorously upholds the 1D heat transfer assumption central to the theoretical model. The laser output produces a large spot diameter (∼5 mm), which remains significantly larger than the thermal diffusion length (μ=α/(πf)) across the entire operational frequency range. Even at the lowest modulation frequency (f = 10 Hz), where µ is maximal, this condition (µ << spot radius) is robustly met. This spatial constraint ensures that the lateral heat diffusion is negligible, forcing the AC thermal wave to propagate almost exclusively in the 1D, out‐of‐plane (x‐axis) direction.

Furthermore, the assumption of negligible convective and radiative heat losses is well‐founded within the operational parameters of this technique. The photoacoustic signal itself originates from the rapid, periodic heat conduction from the sample surface to the adjacent gas layer (ambient air), which generates the thermal piston effect [16, 17]. In contrast, natural convection and radiative heat transfer are comparatively slow processes, primarily functioning as DC or sub‐Hz phenomena [16]. Given our high modulation frequency range (10 Hz–20 kHz), these parasitic loss mechanisms are effectively screened; they cannot respond to the rapid thermal oscillations, rendering their AC components insignificant relative to the dominant conductive heat flux that constitutes the PA signal.

The photoacoustic cell is custom‐machined from poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), a material chosen for its low reflectivity at the operational wavelengths, which minimizes parasitic signals arising from scattered light absorption within the cell body. The cylindrical acoustic cavity has a diameter of 4 mm and a length of 8.4 mm. These dimensions are specifically chosen to push the fundamental axial and radial acoustic resonances frequencies beyond the typical experimental frequency range, thus ensuring a non‐resonant response and validating the theoretical model's assumptions.

Signal detection is performed using a high‐sensitivity condenser pressure‐field microphone (G.R.A.S. 46 BL ¼’ CCP), which is ideal for measuring sound pressure in a small, closed cavity. The microphone signal is fed into a preamplifier and then to a DSP lock‐in amplifier (AMETEK, model 7270), which performs synchronous detection of the PA signal's amplitude and phase relative to the modulation frequency.

For the thermal characterization of multilayer samples, a standardized procedure is followed. An 80 nm thick titanium (Ti) layer is deposited onto the sample surface via sputtering deposition. This metallic transducer layer serves two crucial functions: it ensures strong, uniform optical absorption at the surface, validating the heat source term in the model (Equation 3), and it provides a consistent surface for all samples, simplifying the comparison between different measurements. Lastly, normalization of the experimental phase shift is performed using a thermally thick (2 mm in thickness) quartz wafer with 80 nm of Ti evaporated in its surface, which is measured in the same conditions as the problem sample and serves to take into account the cell's singularities using this sample as a reference.

Software Architecture and Workflow

To facilitate the robust application of the theoretical model described above, a flexible and automated software tool was developed entirely within the MATLAB environment. The software is designed to handle the complete workflow from data input to final parameter extraction and visualization.

The architecture is modular, with distinct scripts handling specific tasks. The workflow initiates with data ingestion modules (leer_archivos_SAM.m, leer_archivos_DAT.m), which parse standardized input files. “.SAM” files define the multilayer structure, including the initial guess values for each property of each layer, physical bounds (lb, ub), and fitting flags for each thermal property (e.g., thermal conductivity κ, thickness L, thermal boundary resistance Rth ) of every layer. “.DAT” files contain the raw experimental phase shift versus frequency data for both the sample and reference.

The core of the program is the “ajuste_nonlinear_lsq.m” module, which elaborates the inverse fitting process. This module calls upon a forward calculation engine, “calcular_curva_teorica.m”, to generate the theoretical phase shift based on the current set of parameters. An objective function, “calcular_error.m”, quantifies the deviation between the theoretical curve and the normalized experimental data. The optimization algorithm systematically adjusts the designated parameters to minimize this error. Finally, a “plot_phase_shift.m” module provides a graphical representation of the experimental data, the best‐fit theoretical curve, and the extracted parameters. This modular design allows for high flexibility; users can easily define complex N‐layer systems and select any combination of thermal parameters for fitting simply by editing the input “.SAM” file (Figure 8).

Inverse Analysis and Fitting Strategy

The determination of unknown thermal properties is framed as an inverse problem, where the goal is to find a set of parameters that minimizes the discrepancy between the experimental and theoretical phase shifts.

To minimize this objective function, we employ the “fmincon” algorithm from MATLAB's Optimization Toolbox, which is a powerful solver for constrained nonlinear multivariable functions. The use of constraints is critical for the stability and physical meaning of the solution. The lower and superior bounds (lb, ub) are established to restrict the search space to physically realistic values (e.g., κ > 0) and prevent the algorithm from exploring non‐physical solutions, thereby improving convergence. The initial guess vector (x0 ) is selected based on literature estimates or previous measurements to guide the algorithm toward a globally optimal solution and away from potential local minimums.

To enhance the robustness of the fit and ensure convergence to the correct value, an iterative fitting strategy is employed. Initially, a broad search range is defined for the parameter of interest (e.g., the thermal conductivity, κ). Once an initial adjusted value is obtained, the process is repeated in successively narrower ranges centered around that value. The consistency of the results across these iterations, combined with an analysis of the error surface, confirms the uniqueness and reliability of the solution found.

Prior to the fitting process, the raw experimental data undergoes a crucial normalization procedure. The phase shift from the thermally thick quartz reference is subtracted from the sample's phase shift to cancel out frequency‐dependent instrumental artifacts. Additionally, an instrumental phase offset of −π/2, characteristic of the lock‐in detection of a diffusive thermal process in bulk materials, is corrected for. This yields the final experimental phase shift ∆ϕexp used in the objective function.

This numerical correction is a fundamental signature of the measurement physics, arising from two distinct, sequential diffusive processes. The total theoretical phase shift of −π/2 for thermally thick, surface‐absorbing reference sample can be decomposed into two essential components of −π/4 each. The first component originates from the intrinsic nature of thermal diffusion from the sample surface into the gas. Unlike propagating waves, a diffusive heat flux inherently establishes a temperature profile that shifts in phase behind the periodic heat source. This shift is mathematically captured within the (1 + j) pre‐factor in the complex thermal wave vector (σ). The second component is introduced by the signal generation mechanism itself. The thermal piston model dictates that the acoustic pressure is proportional to the spatially averaged temperature within the gas boundary layer. The fact of introducing the exponentially decaying thermal wave across this layer to find the average introduces a further, mathematically necessary phase shift of −π/4 [16]. The normalization procedure thus serves to nullify these fundamental physical constants of the technique, isolating the phase contribution that arises solely from the thermal transit time and interfacial impedances within the multilayer structure itself.
