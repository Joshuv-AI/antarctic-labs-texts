Compact Snapshot Hyperspectral Imaging With Neural Dispersion‐Engineered Metalens
Authors: Peng Liu, Jiaru Chu, Yuhang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere is a growing demand for ultra‐compact, low‐cost, and high‐fidelity snapshot hyperspectral imaging devices across various fields. However, conventional systems struggle to fulfill all these evolving requirements. Although a single diffractive optical element offers a compact solution, its degrees of freedom in light phase modulation are limited. Here, we exploit wavelength‐dependent characteristics of the point spread function (PSF) to design a dispersion‐engineered metalens that serves as a key element for hyperspectral imaging. The PSF of the metalens is tailored to undergo an extra lateral shift as a function of wavelength, thereby encoding richer spectral information. An efficient differentiable computational model is developed to simulate the hyperspectral imaging process, together with a downstream spectral reconstruction network. The metalens structure and the reconstruction network are subsequently optimized in an end‐to‐end deep learning joint optimization framework. We fabricated the optimal metalens using two‐photon grayscale lithography and built a prototype hyperspectral imaging camera. Both indoor and outdoor experimental results validated its outstanding spatial‐spectral reconstruction performance, highlighting the effectiveness in practical applications.Keywords: computational spectral imaging, dispersion engineering, end‐to‐end joint optimization, metalens

Introduction

Hyperspectral imaging is an advanced optical imaging technology, which captures not only spatial details but also continuous spectral data at each pixel, forming a three‐dimensional (3D) data cube that weaves together spatial and spectral information [1, 2, 3]. Driven by its superior information acquisition capability over visible color (RGB) imaging, hyperspectral imaging has been widely applied in fields such as remote sensing, agricultural monitoring, biomedical imaging, food safety, environmental surveillance, defense, and security [4, 5, 6, 7]. However, conventional hyperspectral imaging generally relies on a set of dispersion optics, relay lenses, spectral filters, and scanning devices with rather complex optical setups, resulting in bulky equipment and high cost. Furthermore, the imaging speed is often constrained due to the scanning mechanism, which is inefficient and thus prohibitive for real‐time imaging of dynamic scenes [8].

To meet the growing demand for real‐time spectral data acquisition, snapshot hyperspectral imaging has emerged as a promising solution. This approach overcomes the inherent speed limitation of traditional scanning hyperspectral imaging systems by capturing both spectral and spatial information of a scene in a single shot. Representative implementations include the coded aperture snapshot spectral imager, the prism‐mask imaging spectrometer, and the Fourier transform snapshot spectral imager, among others [9, 10, 11]. It is worth noting that standard refractive and reflective optical elements continue to form the foundational components of these systems, which often give rise to bulky configurations and large form factors. Consequently, they are challenged by the stringent size and integration constraints of consumer‐grade applications.

Achieving compact snapshot spectral imaging systems entails employing a single optical element to accomplish the modulation and encoding of spectral information. Such approaches fall primarily into two categories: filter‐based encoding and dispersion‐based encoding. One common filter‐based implementation directly integrates a spectral filter array onto the image sensor to reconstruct the spectra. This array can be regarded as an extended form of the Bayer filter used in RGB imaging, containing multiple narrowband or broadband spectral filters [12, 13]. However, as the number of filters increases for accurate spectral reconstruction, the spatial resolution of reconstructed hyperspectral images decreases significantly. As for dispersion‐based encoding, it captures the spectral information by manipulating the wavelength‐dependent dispersion of the point spread function (PSF). Recent advancements have utilized diffractive optical elements (DOEs) with wavelength‐dependent PSFs to encode spectral information [14, 15, 16]. However, constrained by their micron‐scale feature sizes, DOEs exhibit limited flexibility in phase modulation and therefore offer only restricted capability for encoding spectral information. Specifically, current DOE‐based PSF dispersion modulation is confined to the radial direction because imparting additional phase profiles may cause overly steep phase gradients, which in turn induce higher‐order diffraction and reduce optical efficiency. In contrast, the emerging planar optical metasurfaces enable more flexible optical field manipulation, offering a highly compelling alternative for ultra‐compact hyperspectral imaging [17].

Metasurfaces, comprised of subwavelength meta‐unit arrays, enable tailored control over light's amplitude, phase, and polarization at the nanoscale. Compared to other refractive and diffractive optical elements, metasurfaces offer greater precision and flexibility in light modulation while being more compact and lightweight [18, 19]. All these distinctive advantages make them highly attractive for a wide range of applications, spanning diverse fields from 3D/polarization imaging to holographic display and from achromatic imaging to spectroscopy [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. In spectral imaging, however, they have yet to mature for consumer‐level implementations, where the achieved image quality also remains underdeveloped. Furthermore, many existing works treat the metasurface designs and the spectral reconstruction algorithms as separate processes. This disconnect renders it challenging to fully leverage the metasurface dispersion information at the design stage for accurate spectral image reconstruction [25, 26, 27, 28]. Currently, some cutting‐edge studies aim to be task‐driven by jointly modeling optical design and algorithmic processing as a unified framework, enabling end‐to‐end optimization to build a system with optimal performance [29, 30]. In terms of metasurface fabrication, the mainstream approach is based on electron beam lithography and etching, although a few studies have explored the use of femtosecond laser direct‐write lithography to enable low‐cost and rapid prototyping [31, 32].

In this work, we propose a dispersion‐engineered metalens, a functional metasurface subset, for hyperspectral imaging. In contrast to most existing approaches that rely on manually designed metalens structures and hyperspectral reconstruction algorithms, we develop a differentiable spectral imaging model and employ an end‐to‐end joint learning framework to co‐optimize both the metalens structure and the reconstruction algorithm. In addition, rather than using metasurfaces as an array of narrowband or uncorrelated broadband spectral filters, which may degrade spatial resolution, we use the phase‐encoding approach [33, 34, 35]. In this design, the metalens serves as both an imaging component and a spectral encoder by leveraging the wavelength‐dependent lateral shift of its PSF. Therefore, the optical system is simplified, whereas the spatial resolution of the image sensor is maximally preserved.

Comparative simulations verified that the proposed method outperforms traditional separated designs and existing end‐to‐end joint optimization methods without the special metalens dispersion engineering. We further fabricated the learned metalens using two‐photon grayscale lithography and developed a prototype hyperspectral camera to validate the feasibility. Hyperspectral imaging and reconstruction experiments performed in both indoor and outdoor environments highlight its robustness and potential for practical usages. We believe that the proposed method provides an innovative and efficient solution for compact snapshot hyperspectral imaging, significantly advancing its deployments across a broader range of fields.

Specifically, we make the following contributions: (i) We propose a snapshot hyperspectral imaging system based on a dispersion‐engineered metalens, which precisely modulates the PSF dispersion along both the radial and transverse directions, thereby encoding richer spectral information. (ii) We construct a fully differentiable hyperspectral imaging framework that incorporates an efficient imaging model together with a downstream reconstruction network. Within this framework, the metalens structure and the spectral reconstruction network are jointly optimized in an end‐to‐end manner to achieve optimal spectral encoding and reconstruction performance. (iii) We evaluate the proposed method and compare it with existing diffractive‐lens‐based encoding implementations. The results show that, for the reconstruction task involving 25 spectral channels, the peak signal‐to‐noise ratio improves by approximately 4 dB. (iv) We build a prototype system and conduct comparative experiments against a commercial spectrometer. Both indoor and outdoor experiments validate the effectiveness and accuracy of practical hyperspectral imaging.

Results

Dispersion‐Engineered Hyperspectral Imaging Framework

The proposed snapshot hyperspectral imaging system consists of two main parts including the optical hardware and the reconstruction network, with the overall process illustrated in Figure 1. In the front‐end optical part, a dispersion‐engineered metalens is designed with distinct PSFs across different wavelength bands, accompanied by wavelength‐dependent lateral PSF displacements, enabling effective encoding. The spectral information of the scene is first encoded by the metalens and then projected onto the CMOS sensor of the RGB camera, yielding a raw measurement. This raw image is routed to a downstream hyperspectral reconstruction network, which processes and decodes it to recover the full spectral content of the scene as well as the clean color image.

Hyperspectral imaging framework based on the dispersion‐engineered metalens.

In simulating the imaging process, the forward spectral encoding is implemented by convolving the PSFs at different wavelengths with their corresponding ground‐truth hyperspectral images. Subsequently, the convolution results across all spectral bands are weighted and integrated according to the spectral response curves of the CMOS sensor to generate a noise‐free image. Random noise is then added to simulate realistic imaging conditions. In the downstream stage, a multi‐resolution convolutional neural network is designed to decode the sensor image for spectral reconstruction, followed by the synthesis of the sharp RGB image. By introducing a hybrid loss function, the entire framework enables end‐to‐end joint optimization of the metalens structure and the downstream neural network for accurate snapshot hyperspectral imaging.

Hyperspectral Image Reconstruction Network

The spectral information of the scene is encoded into an RGB image by the dispersion‐engineered metalens, and, subsequently, a reconstruction network is established to recover the hyperspectral images. Inspired by the multi‐scale deblurring network [36], we designed a multi‐scale U‐Net to perform the hyperspectral reconstruction task, as illustrated in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1. During the encoding–decoding process, upsampling and downsampling operations are employed to facilitate information exchange across multiple resolution levels. The encoder processes multi‐scale inputs in parallel and produces matching outputs at each scale. The first branch receives the original input, whereas the second and third branches take inputs that are downsampled by factors of two and four, respectively. In the final stage, the three output heads yield hyperspectral images at different scales, which are then fused to reconstruct the full‐resolution hyperspectral images and the RGB image. More detailed information regarding the neural network can be found in Supporting Information S1: Note S2.

Synthetic Assessment

Before conducting practical experiments, we first compared the proposed method with some related approaches on the same hyperspectral image datasets to validate its effectiveness and superiority. In addition, we evaluated the impact of the wavelength‐dependent PSF lateral shift on the final spectral reconstruction performance. To this end, we combined four publicly released datasets for simulation experiments, namely the ICVL, HSDB, CAVE, and KAUST datasets [38, 39, 40, 41]. The merged dataset was randomly split into three subsets, with 576 samples allocated for training, 72 for validation, and 72 for testing. Furthermore, the images were randomly cropped into 512 × 512 patches and augmented using random flips.

To rigorously assess the performance of our approach, we compared it with three representative baselines, including (i) a snapshot hyperspectral imaging method employing a nonoptimized Fresnel lens (NOFL) that does not incorporate special dispersion engineering, (ii) a diffraction‐based snapshot hyperspectral imaging method using end‐to‐end learned quantization‐aware diffractive optics (QDO) [14], and (iii) a snapshot hyperspectral imaging method based on diffractive rotation (DR) [15]. Specifically, the QDO method incorporates height quantization constraints within an end‐to‐end optimization framework to ensure compatibility with fabrication. The DR method employs a pre‐designed DOE whose PSF rotates with wavelength. This wavelength‐dependent PSF rotation provides a key cue for spectral reconstruction [28]. However, the DOE structure is not jointly optimized with the downstream reconstruction network.

For fairness, all methods were trained on the same merged dataset with an identical number of epochs and under the same simulated sensor noise conditions. The QDO method employed the network from its original work, whereas the other two baseline methods and the proposed method utilized the multi‐resolution hyperspectral reconstruction network designed in this work. The inputs for all four methods consisted of spectral data with dimensions of 25 × 512 × 512, which were simulated into the encoded RGB images according to their respective spectral coding schemes, and then decoded by the neural network. Each method reconstructed 25 spectral bands spanning from 420 to 660 nm for demonstration, and the reconstruction quality was quantitatively evaluated using metrics structural similarity index measure (SSIM) and peak signal‐to‐noise ratio (PSNR).

Figure 2 illustrates two representative scenes selected from the CAVE dataset. It is apparent that the RGB images encoded by the three baseline methods are overall blurry, whereas the encoded images from the proposed method exhibit pronounced spectral dispersion at image texture edges, containing richer dispersed spectral information. As observed from the comparison of spectral profiles at the randomly selected pixels, the reconstructed spectra obtained by the proposed method show the closest agreement with the ground‐truth spectra, demonstrating a higher degree of spectral fidelity. In addition, we quantitatively evaluated the spectral reconstruction results of the four methods using SSIM and PSNR metrics, as summarized in Table 1. The proposed approach achieves superior hyperspectral reconstruction quality, with an SSIM of 0.96 and a PSNR of 32.54 dB, confirming a substantial improvement over all the baseline methods. These results underscore its robustness in the joint reconstruction of optical spectra and color images from complex scenes.

Subsequently, to further validate the improvement in spectral reconstruction enabled by the dispersion‐engineered metalens, we assessed the performance under different levels of PSF dispersion. Specifically, we varied the lateral displacement Δshift of the PSF at the assigned center wavelength of 546 nm from 0 to 4 mm with an increment of 1 mm while keeping the training dataset and simulated sensor noise identical. The final structural parameters of the metalens were obtained through the established end‐to‐end optimization framework. One typical learned metalens height map with Δshift of 1 mm is presented in Supporting Information S1: Figure S2.

Figure 3 and Table 2 present the qualitative and quantitative comparisons of the hyperspectral reconstruction results under different lateral PSF shifts, respectively. The results demonstrate that applying moderate lateral shifts to the PSF improves hyperspectral reconstruction. However, as the lateral shift further increases, the dispersion becomes more pronounced, resulting in a decline in the reconstruction performance. This indicates that moderate PSF dispersion shift is beneficial for accurate hyperspectral reconstruction, whereas excessive dispersion poses greater challenges to the spectral reconstruction network, causing a degradation in its performance. Given the above observation, we set the optimal lateral PSF displacement at the center wavelength to 1 mm in our final metalens design for practical fabrication.

Experimental Implementation

Through the end‐to‐end optimization, we obtained the structural design of the metalens. The detailed optimization procedure is provided in Supporting Information S1: Note S3. Each meta‐unit measured 400 nm × 400 nm, with an overall metalens diameter of 5 mm. We used two‐photon grayscale lithography to fabricate our metalens, controlling the phase precisely by tuning the height of each meta‐unit and discretizing the phase into 10 levels. The lithography process employed the IP‐DIP2 photoresist, which has a refractive index of 1.556 at the wavelength of 546.1 nm. Further details and analyses regarding the effect of height quantization and the dominant phase modulation mechanism of the meta‐units are provided in Supporting Information S1: Notes S4–S6.

Electron microscopy imaging of the fabricated metalens was performed and the results indicate a reasonable agreement between the designed and fabricated metalens structures, as shown in Figure 4. The metalens and an appropriately sized aperture were then assembled into a unit and installed on a Sony camera via a custom lens barrel, with the metalens positioned 36 mm in front of the sensor. The metalens orientation was manually adjusted to align the lateral shift direction of the PSFs with the width direction of the camera sensor, which has an image resolution of 24 megapixels and a pixel size of 5.94 μm. To ascertain the engineered dispersion characteristic of the metalens, a black‐and‐white checkerboard pattern was first captured using the hyperspectral camera prototype, and the output raw image is shown in Figure 4c. Noticeable lateral chromatic dispersion can be observed around the vertical edges of the checkerboard. A horizontal cross‐section is taken at the center of the image, and the corresponding RGB intensity profiles are plotted in Figure 4d. The results show that the RGB channels exhibit a clear chromatic dispersion shift along the lateral direction, which is consistent with our design expectations.

Discussion

In summary, we proposed a snapshot hyperspectral imaging method based on a single dispersion‐engineered metalens. The designed metalens exhibits wavelength‐dependent PSFs that undergo extra lateral shifts, thereby enabling more effective spectral encoding. To support this design, we devised an efficient strategy for computing the PSFs. Subsequently, we established a differentiable spectral forward‐encoding model and connected it with a downstream reconstruction network, forming an end‐to‐end differentiable hyperspectral imaging framework. By designing a hybrid loss function and training on available hyperspectral image datasets, we achieved a joint optimization of both the metalens structure and the spectral reconstruction network. Simulation results indicate that the introduction of moderate lateral dispersion can effectively enhance hyperspectral reconstruction performance while also demonstrating clear superiority of the proposed method over representative existing methods. We fabricated the learned metalens using two‐photon grayscale lithography and constructed a hyperspectral camera prototype. Experimental results show that the imaging system achieves accurate and robust spectral reconstruction in both indoor and outdoor scenarios, validating the feasibility and effectiveness in practical applications.

Although the proposed method demonstrates promising functional performance, there remains considerable room for improvement. For instance, the spectral dispersion information encoded by the dispersion‐engineered metalens is primarily concentrated at texture edges, which may lead to reduced reconstruction performance in sparsely textured scenes, even though the scenes in Figures 3 and 5 show that good performance can still be achieved in such cases. In the future, segmenting the imaging aperture for localized dispersion encoding could be explored to achieve superior spectral imaging performance across a wider range of scenes. However, the realization of this approach is contingent on the development of more advanced optical forward‐modeling. Nevertheless, this work provides valuable insights for the development of compact, lightweight, and high‐fidelity snapshot hyperspectral imaging systems with potential applications in remote sensing, biomedical imaging, industrial inspection, and consumer electronics. We also believe that the synergy between optical designs and computational frameworks will enable metasurfaces to extend their impact on hyperspectral imaging and beyond.

Experimental Section

The metalens was fabricated using a two‐photon grayscale lithography system (Nanoscribe Quantum X). After a set of process optimizations, the laser scanning linewidths were set at 200 nm in the horizontal direction and 100 nm in the vertical direction. Prior to fabrication, a fused silica substrate was prepared and cleaned with isopropanol. IP‐Dip2 photoresist was then dropped onto the center of the substrate, which was placed on the sample stage for focus alignment. The 3D nano‐lithography process was initiated using a 63 × objective lens, taking approximately 7.5 hours to complete. After nano‐lithography, the sample was developed in propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) solution for 20 minutes to fully remove unpolymerized photoresist from the substrate surface and finally cleaned in isopropyl alcohol (IPA) solution for 5 minutes.
