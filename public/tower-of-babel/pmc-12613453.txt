Changes in life expectancy across selected countries in the Western Pacific Region and their underlying risk factors, 1990–2021
Authors: Yan Zheng, Stefanos Tyrovolas, Paul SF Yip, Isaac Sasson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn accordance with the global trend, over the past decades, countries in the Western Pacific Region (WPR) have also experienced substantial improvement in life expectancy. However, this accomplishment was coupled with the differences between countries.MethodsUsing the latest data from the Global Burden of Disease study, we explored the underlying reasons for changes in life expectancy among observed WPR countries.ResultsCompared with observed high-income countries, lower-middle-income countries remain disadvantaged with respect to life expectancy, despite their substantial growth over time. The patterns of contribution varied considerably across countries. Background

Health inequalities refer to the systematic, avoidable, and unfair differences in health outcomes between populations and between social groups within the same population [1]. Despite the consistent initiatives and efforts to reduce health inequalities [2], it remains a crucial challenge worldwide [3]. Life expectancy at birth (we refer to it as life expectancy in the following text) is a commonly used measure of population health that summarizes the mortality patterns across all age groups. Differences in life expectancy between populations are therefore thought to reflect inequalities in underlying health conditions. Although there has been a steady improvement in global life expectancy in recent decades, low- and lower-middle-income countries still lag behind and face poorer health outcomes [4].

As one of the six World Health Organization (WHO) regions, the Western Pacific Region (WPR) is home to almost 2.2 billion people across 38 countries and areas [5]. Over the past decades, this Region has experienced noteworthy gains in life expectancy, but not all populations benefit from this progress equally, thus exhibiting substantial variation in longevity [6]. Increases in the average regional life expectancy may mask considerable heterogeneity across countries in patterns of disease burden, and consequently in life expectancy, despite their geographic proximity. Japan, for instance, is both a regional and world leader in life expectancy [7]. In contrast, lower-middle-income countries such as Laos, Papua New Guinea, and the Philippines have considerably lower life expectancy than the regional as well as the global average [8].

The trends in life expectancy are driven by changes in age-, cause- specific mortality rates [9–12], which are associated with multiple risk factors [12, 13]. Related studies have been conducted among both high-income and upper-middle-income countries in the Region [9–12, 14]. However, for low- and lower-middle-income countries, little has been known about their changes in life expectancy and underlying reasons, which may differ considerably from upper-middle-income and high-income countries, particularly given the different paces and stages of population ageing [15, 16]. In recent years, due to the varying impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on mortality rates in different countries [17], the regional disparity in life expectancy could further change.

To address these issues, this study investigates the trends in life expectancy between 1990 and 2021 across countries in the WPR. Using demographic methods, we also evaluate the contribution of changes in age- and cause-specific mortality in each observed country in the Region, highlighting similarities and differences across countries’ income levels. Lastly, to shed light on the underlying reasons, this study evaluates the impact associated with various risk factors. Such analysis is critical in identifying key strategies for enhancing health conditions and health equity across the Region, and towards reaching Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

Methods

Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study provides a broad picture of health estimates for 204 countries and territories. In this study, we included WPR countries having a total population over one million, and then classified them into different income groups based on their Gross National Income per capita (US$) calculated using the World Bank Atlas method [18]: Lower-middle-income countries (Cambodia, Laos, Papua New Guinea, Philippines, and Viet Nam); Upper-middle-income countries (China, Malaysia); High-income countries (Australia, Japan, New Zealand, Singapore, South Korea).

All-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality across age groups and sexes between 1990 and 2021 were mainly used for the analyses. These mortality inputs were downloaded from the GBD Results tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/) [19]. In the GBD, mortality information was collected via multiple sources such as vital registration and verbal autopsy [20]. GBD 2021 includes 288 causes of death. These diseases and injuries were grouped into a hierarchy with four levels. Level 1 includes three broad categories, namely, communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional diseases (CMNN); non-communicable diseases (NCDs); and injuries. Level 2 presents 22 clusters of causes, and Level 3 and Level 4 include even more specific causes [21]. In this project, we selected Level 2 GBD causes, including cardiovascular diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes and kidney diseases, digestive diseases, enteric infections, HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections, maternal and neonatal disorders, mental disorders, neoplasms, respiratory infections and tuberculosis, self-harm and interpersonal violence, substance use disorders, and transport injuries. We separated respiratory infections and tuberculosis into two categories, namely, respiratory infections and tuberculosis without COVID-19 and COVID-19. Before 2019, respiratory infections and tuberculosis (without COVID-19) is equal to respiratory infections and tuberculosis, as the number of deaths from COVID-19 was 0.

Data on multiple risk factors were also collected from the GBD 2021 to evaluate the potential association between age-standardized mortality rates and risk factor exposures among selected WPR countries between 1990 and 2021. In GBD, age-standardized rates were calculated with the GBD world population age standard [20]. GBD classifies all GBD risk factors in a hierarchy with four levels. Level 1 risk factors are environmental and occupational, behavioural, and metabolic risks. Level 2 includes 20 risks or clusters of risk factors such as air pollution, child and maternal malnutrition, dietary risks, drug use, tobacco, unsafe sex, unsafe water, sanitation, and handwashing (WaSH). Level 3 and Level 4 include more specific categories of risks, which were mentioned in detail in the GBD publication [13]. In this study, the age-standardized mortality rates from CMNN, NCDs, and injuries were also reported to evaluate the effect of various risk factors.

All-cause and cause-specific mortality rates by age group and sex for each observed population were used to compute abridge life tables and derive the corresponding life expectancy between 1990 and 2021. Given the dramatic impact of COVID-19 on global health, we divided the entire study period into two segments: 1990–2019 and 2019–2021. Arriaga’s decomposition method [22] was used to determine which of these age groups and causes of death are primarily responsible for changes in life expectancy for each country in both periods. The methods used to calculate 95% uncertainty intervals for the estimates have been reported in detail previously [21], and additional information about the method used in the study is provided in the supplementary file.

Results

Country-specific trends in life expectancy

Between 1990 and 2019, life expectancy improved for all observed countries in the WPR (Table 1). Among lower-middle-income countries, Laos underwent the most substantial gains in life expectancy for both sexes (16.3 years among females and 15.8 years among males), followed by Cambodia (12.1 years and 11.2 years, respectively), while the Philippines experienced the smallest increases (2.9 years among females and 3 years among males). For upper-middle-income countries, China presented much greater increases in both females (10.6 years) and males (8.9 years) when compared with Malaysia (2.6 years and 2.9 years, respectively). Among high-income countries, the largest gains in life expectancy were observed for females (10.3 years) and males (12.4 years) in South Korea, followed by Singapore (9.4 years among females and 10.6 years among males).

In 2019–2021, middle-income countries experienced different magnitude of declines in life expectancy, except China. The Philippines experienced the most substantial reductions for both sexes (−2.5 years for females and − 3.6 years for males), followed by Papua New Guinea (−1.8 years and − 3.2 years, respectively) and Malaysia (−1.4 years and − 2.4 years, respectively). Among high-income countries, life expectancy continued increasing in Australia, Japan, and New Zealand. However, life expectancy almost stagnated in Singapore and even declined slightly in South Korea among both sexes.

Overall, the variation in life expectancy between countries in the Region declined almost steadily since the 1990s for both females and males (Figure S1). However, after 2019, the variation in life expectancy between WPR countries increased markedly, surpassing even its 1990 level for males.

Age decomposition of changes in life expectancy

Figure 1 presents the age-specific contributions to changes in life expectancy for the observed countries over the study period. In 1990–2019, the increases in both female and male life expectancy among lower-middle-income countries were explained by reductions in mortality in most age groups, particularly among children under 5 years. In China, apart from under-5 age group, the declines in mortality among middle-aged adults and the elderly also played an important role in improving life expectancy for both sexes. In high-income countries, the reductions in mortality among middle-aged and older adults, and particularly the latter, were the primary drivers of gains in life expectancy. However, it is important to note that the old-age mortality increased in the Philippines (among males aged 65–84 years) and Malaysia (among females aged 85 + years), which negatively affected their life expectancy.

Contributions of different age groups to changes in life expectancy for observed countries in the Western Pacific Region, 1990–2021. Note. Countries are ordered by the magnitude of gains in life expectancy between 1990 and 2019, separately for females and males

Between 2019 and 2021, despite the continued decline in infant mortality rate, adult and old-age mortality increased substantially, resulting in different magnitude of declines in both female and male life expectancy in lower-middle-income countries (e.g., Papua New Guinea and the Philippines) as well as upper-middle-income countries (e.g., Malaysia). In China and some high-income countries, for instance, New Zealand, the growth in life expectancy continued, which was largely attributable to consistent reductions in mortality among the elderly. In contrast, a reverse trend in old-age mortality was observed in South Korea, which was the major reason for its decline in life expectancy. In Singapore, as the negative impact of the rising mortality among people aged 85 + was offset by the declining mortality in other old age groups, its life expectancy did not have detectable change.

Cause-of-death decomposition of changes in life expectancy

Between 1990 and 2019, in lower-middle-income countries, the observed increases in both female and male life expectancy were driven by the reductions in mortality from CMNN and cardiovascular diseases (Fig. 2). In China, in addition to aforementioned causes, the declines in mortality from chronic respiratory diseases and neoplasms also made important contributions to the gains in life expectancy among both sexes. In Malaysia, the increases in both female and male life expectancy were mainly due to the reductions in mortality from cardiovascular diseases. In observed high-income countries, the increases in life expectancy were largely attributable to reductions in mortality from cardiovascular diseases and neoplasms. Reductions in mortality from other causes, such as chronic respiratory diseases, respiratory infections and tuberculosis, and digestive diseases were also important for the increases in life expectancy in some high-income countries (e.g., South Korea). In addition, transport and unintentional injuries contributed positively to gains in life expectancy in the region, which could be more obviously seen among males.

Contributions of major causes of death to changes in life expectancy for observed countries in the Western Pacific Region, 1990–2021. Note. Countries are ordered by the magnitude of gains in life expectancy between 1990 and 2019, separately for females and males

In 2019–2021, the substantial increases in mortality from COVID-19 and other related outcomes of the pandemic led to reductions in life expectancy among all observed lower-middle-income countries and Malaysia. This negative impact was more substantially seen in Papua New Guinea and the Philippines, particularly among males. In China and some high-income countries such as Japan and New Zealand, the negative impact of the pandemic was minor and was offset by the positive impact associated with the declines in mortality from other causes, for example, cardiovascular diseases. However, in Singapore and South Korea, the negative contributions of the rising mortality from the COVID-19 and the pandemic related diseases were much greater, which was the main reason for the stagnation and even decline in life expectancy.

Age-cause-specific decomposition of changes in life expectancy

Figure 3a-d illustrates the contributions of different age groups and major causes of death to changes in life expectancy, by sex over the observation period. From 1990 to 2019, in lower-middle-income countries such as Cambodia and Laos, the gains in life expectancy could be mainly explained by reductions in under-5, 5–24, 25–44 mortality from CMNN , as well as reductions in mortality from communicable diseases and cardiovascular diseases at 45–84 years. In China, in addition to the reductions in under-5 mortality from communicable diseases and maternal-neonatal disorders, the reductions in mortality from NCDs, including cardiovascular diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, and neoplasms in the 45–84 age groups also made substantial contributions to the gains in life expectancy. In Malaysia, the gain in life expectancy was mainly attributed to the decline in mortality from cardiovascular diseases in the 45–84 age groups, although this positive contribution was partly offset by the rising mortality from cardiovascular diseases and communicable diseases at advanced ages among females. In high-income countries, declines in middle and old-age mortality from cardiovascular diseases and neoplasms were mainly responsible for the improvement in life expectancy. Meanwhile, the reductions in old-age mortality from other causes, including chronic respiratory diseases, respiratory infections and tuberculosis, and digestive diseases also made positive contributions to life expectancy in Japan, Singapore, and South Korea to varying degrees. The declines in mortality from external causes among young people and adults were also important drivers of the increase in male life expectancy among several countries, including China and South Korea.

Between 2019 and 2021, life expectancy declined notably in observed lower-middle-income countries (e.g., Papua New Guinea and the Philippines) and Malaysia. This was largely attributed to the increases in mortality from COVID-19 and other COVID-19 associated outcomes among adults and the elderly. In China and some high-income countries such as Japan and New Zealand, the impact of the COVID-19 was limited, and continuous declines in old-age mortality from respiratory infections and tuberculosis and NCDs (e.g., cardiovascular diseases) were mainly responsible for their continuous gains in life expectancy. In South Korea, the increases in adult- and old-age mortality from the COVID-19 and the pandemic related diseases were the main reasons for the decline in life expectancy over this period.

Cross-national differences in risk-attributable age-standardized mortality

Figure 4 shows the all-cause age-standardized mortality attributable to Level 1 risk factors among the observed WPR countries over the study period. Between 1990 and 2019, all-cause age-standardized mortality attributable to environmental and occupational risks declined substantially in lower-middle-income countries (e.g., Cambodia and Laos) and China, whereas those attributable to metabolic risks declined more conspicuously in high-income countries. The contribution of risk factors to age-standardized mortality rates varied substantially across different causes of death, as shown in Figures S2. For example, between 1990 and 2019, age-standardized mortality from CMNN attributable to unsafe WaSH and child and maternal malnutrition declined obviously in lower-middle-income countries. NCDs-associated age-standardized mortality attributable to risk factors, including air pollution and dietary risk declined in both middle-income (e.g., China and Laos) and high-income countries (e.g., South Korea and Singapore), whereas those related to other risks such as high systolic blood pressure, declined more conspicuously in high-income countries. During the subsequent period, however, no detectable changes have been found in risk-attributable age-standardized mortality.

Discussion

Between 1990 and 2019, there were marked increases in life expectancy across all observed countries in the WPR, and the cross-national difference generally shrank among both females and males. During the period 2019–2021, due to the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, declines in life expectancy were seen in all observed lower-middle-income countries, as well as Malaysia and South Korea. Meanwhile, it further enlarged the differences in life expectancy across countries.

Our study found that the age-, cause-specific contributions to changes in life expectancy varied considerably across countries. Among lower-middle-income countries, gains in life expectancy from 1990 to 2019 were driven by reductions in mortality in most age groups, and particularly the under-5 age group, as well as the progress made with respect to CMNN and cardiovascular diseases [6, 15]. In China, in addition to declines in mortality among children under 5 years and from CMNN and cardiovascular diseases, declines in mortality from other NCDs such as chronic respiratory diseases and neoplasms among adults and the elderly also contributed to the increased life expectancy [14, 23]. In high-income countries, the gains in life expectancy were driven by reductions in middle and old-age mortality from NCDs, including cardiovascular diseases and neoplasms [9–12]. Meanwhile, the reductions in mortality from other causes such as respiratory diseases and digestive diseases partly explained the increases in life expectancy in Japan, Singapore, and South Korea [9, 10]. Additionally, the decline in mortality from external causes, e.g., transport injuries, also increased life expectancy in many countries in the Region, particularly among males [10, 14, 15].

These achievements were accompanied by progress in reducing multiple risk factors, which were closely associated with intensified public health interventions, the efforts of member states, and the support from the WHO in the Region. Among lower-middle-income countries, the efficacy of vaccination programs, the enhancement of maternal and child health services, and the improvement in sanitation and nutrition have collectively resulted in the mitigation of numerous risks (e.g., child and maternal malnutrition) and the decrease in mortality from CMNN diseases [15]. In upper-middle-income and high-income countries, the increases in life expectancy, which could be attributed to the positive impact of controlling NCDs-associated risk factors, were closely related to the implementation of preventive health measures (e.g., tobacco control and the promotion of healthy lifestyles) and the effective performance of health systems [10–12, 23]. In South Korea and Singapore, the notable improvement in the universal health coverage service coverage index may partly account for their substantial progress in life expectancy [15]. Furthermore, government regulations may have played an important role in the decline of mortality from external causes in some countries, e.g., traffic injuries in China and South Korea [10, 14].

Nonetheless, the adverse impact of multiple risk factors in the Region also warrants concern. In high-income countries, risk factors for NCDs such as tobacco use and obesity have been identified as key factors in the slowdown of life expectancy improvement [11, 12]. Lower-middle-income countries are also confronted with burden from numerous factors, including accessing safe drinking water or sanitation services, air pollution, and malnutrition [6, 15]. These findings suggest that modifying the profile of risk factors remains essential for ensuring long-term reductions in mortality and gains in life expectancy for countries in the region. Furthermore, the WPR is undergoing rapid ageing and the number of people aged 65 years and over is expected to increase substantially in the coming years [6, 15]. This trend may pose challenges to healthcare systems in the WPR, underscoring the urgent need to address healthcare demands and promote healthy ageing. Lastly, the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic and its adverse effect on life expectancy highlight the need to enhance the resilience of health systems.

This study, however, is not without limitations. An important limitation is the accuracy of GBD data for some low- and middle-income countries. Due to the unavailability of complete and reliable data from civil registration or vital statistics systems, specific data processing steps were used by GBD to produce demographic estimates, which may cause our results for these countries to be less certain [20, 21]. To validate the results, we replicated the age-specific decomposition analysis using data from the United Nations World Population Prospects. Generally, the contribution patterns based on two data sources are similar across the Region as a whole. However, for some countries, the gains in life expectancy and age-specific contributions could differ. Furthermore, the results related to the COVID-19 pandemic should be interpreted with concern. This is because for countries and territories lacking all-cause mortality data, the modelling of deaths and mortality related to COVID-19 was conducted only through associations with covariates [20, 21]. Additionally, the analyses were conducted at the country level, without considering the disparities in life expectancy within each country. Significant geographic and socioeconomic heterogeneity could result in disparate health challenges across various subpopulations within a country, e.g., between urban and rural areas, between high- and low- socioeconomic positions [24]. It is therefore recommended that more detailed analyses could be conducted in the future, so as to facilitate a deeper understanding of the trends reported in this study.

Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated long-term trends in life expectancy across countries in the WPR and identified the contributions of different age groups and major causes of death to those trends. Importantly, our analysis was not restricted to high-income countries, but also included a systematic evaluation of mortality patterns in lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income countries. Our analysis underscores the importance of public health measures that seek to mitigate the adverse impact of specific risk factors such as tobacco use, obesity, and air pollution. Paying greater attention to those risk factors and their associated causes of death and enhancing their capacity to provide treatment for these diseases, particularly in lower-middle-income countries, can aid towards meeting the SDGs agenda and reduce their gaps in life expectancy with high-income countries in the Region.
