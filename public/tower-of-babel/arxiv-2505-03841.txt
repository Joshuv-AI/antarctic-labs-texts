Contact-Aware Safety in Soft Robots Using High-Order Control Barrier and Lyapunov Functions
Kiwan Wong, Maximilian Stölzle, Wei Xiao, Cosimo Della Santina, Daniela Rus, Gioele Zardini

Abstract
Robots operating alongside people, particularly in sensitive scenarios such as aiding the elderly with daily tasks or collaborating with workers in manufacturing, must guarantee safety and cultivate user trust. Continuum soft manipulators promise safety through material compliance, but as designs evolve for greater precision, payload capacity, and speed, and increasingly incorporate rigid elements, their injury risk resurfaces. In this letter, we introduce a comprehensive High-Order Control Barrier Function (HOCBF) + High-Order Control Lyapunov Function (HOCLF) framework that enforces strict contact force limits across the entire soft-robot body during environmental interactions. Our approach combines a differentiable Piecewise Cosserat-Segment (PCS) dynamics model with a convex-polygon distance approximation metric, named Differentiable Conservative Separating Axis Theorem (DCSAT), based on the soft robot geometry to enable real-time, whole-body collision detection, resolution, and enforcement of the safety constraints. By embedding HOCBFs into our optimization routine, we guarantee safety, allowing, for instance, safe navigation in operational space under HOCLF-driven motion objectives. Extensive planar simulations demonstrate that our method maintains safety-bounded contacts while achieving precise shape and task-space regulation. This work thus lays a foundation for the deployment of soft robots in human-centric environments with provable safety and performance.

II-C

High Order Control Barrier Functions and High Order Control Lyapunov Functions

The soft robot dynamics can be expressed in control-affine form as an ODE:

\scriptsize\dot{\bm{x}}=\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{\bm{q}}\\
\bm{M}^{-1}(\bm{\tauit}_{\mathrm{c}}-\bm{C}\dot{\bm{q}}-\bm{G}+\bm{K}\bm{q}-\bm{D}\dot{\bm{q}})\end{array}\right]}_{f(\bm{x})}+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{0}_{3N\times m}\\

,

(3)

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\bigl[L_{f}^{r}V+L_{g}L_{f}^{r-1}V\,\bm{u}+O(V)+\betait_{r}(\phiit_{r-1})\bigr]\leq 0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\,\bm{x}\neq\bm{0}_{n}.

The High-Order CBF (

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{u},\deltait}

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}+p\,\deltait^{2},

(4)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle L^{r}_{f}b(\bm{x})+L_{g}L_{f}^{r-1}b(\bm{x})\,\bm{u}+O\bigl(b(\bm{x})\bigr)+\alphait_{r}(\psiit_{r-1}(\bm{x}))\;\geq\;0,

III
High-Order Control Barrier and Lyapunov Function for Environment-Aware Control

This section describes how we can design High-Order CBFs and High-Order CLFs for environment-aware soft robot control.
Most importantly, we can ensure contact force limits, and with this, safety, by deploying High-Order CBFs while relying on High-Order CLFs for defining motion behavior and objectives.
To enable this, we require access to differentiable algorithms that perform collision detection and resolution between the soft robot body and convex polygonal environment obstacles.
We consider a planar soft robotic arm operating within a two‐dimensional workspace
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

n_{\mathrm{obs}}

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{obs}}=\{\mathcal{O}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{O}_{n_{\mathrm{obs}}}\}

We also assume the robot is fully actuated, with
m=3N=\frac{n}{2}
.

Collision Detection

III-B

Collision Resolution

The distance metric
h(\bm{q})
now allows us to resolve the collision, which means that we project the collision forces onto the soft robot dynamics and vice versa.
In this work, we specifically, without loss of generality, use a linear spring-damper model to capture the collision characteristics. Future work might explore more advanced contact models here.
The collision force
F_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is given as

\footnotesize F_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}(h_{i,j},\dot{h}_{i,j})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }h_{i,j}>0\\[6.0pt]
-k_{\mathrm{c}}\,h_{i,j}-c_{\mathrm{c}}\,\dot{h}_{i,j},&\text{if }h_{i,j}\leq 0\end{cases}

where
k_{\mathrm{c}}
the contact stiffness, and
c_{\mathrm{c}}
the damping coefficient.
To recover differentiability, we approximate the collision dynamics using softplus-based smoothing with
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\small F_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}(h_{i,j},\dot{h}_{i,j})=k_{\mathrm{c}}\ln\left(1+e^{-h_{i,j}/\varepsilon}\right)+c_{\mathrm{c}}\ln\left(1+e^{-\dot{h}_{i,j}/\varepsilon}\right).

(6)

This contact force can now easily be applied to both the environment and the soft robot by projecting it along the contact surface vector. Specifically, for a given surface normal
\bm{n}_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\lVert\bm{n}_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}\rVert_{2}=1

\bm{p}_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\small\begin{split}\bm{\tauit}_{\mathrm{c}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{srpoly}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}\bm{J}_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}^{\top}(\bm{q})\,F_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}(h_{i,j},\dot{h}_{i,j})\,\bm{n}_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N},\end{split}

(7)

where
\bm{J}_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 3N}
is the positional Jacobian of the contact point on the surface of the soft robot that is:

\footnotesize\dot{\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{c}}=\underbrace{\left(\bm{J}_{x,y}+\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)\left(\bm{p}_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}-\operatorname{FK}_{x,y}(\bm{q},s_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}})\right)^{\top}\bm{J}_{\thetait}\right)}_{\bm{J}_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}(\bm{q})}\dot{\bm{q}}

(8)

\bm{J}_{\thetait}(\bm{q},s_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}})\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 3N}

III-C

Ensuring Safety via High-Order CBFs

Below, we introduce a set of relative-degree-two High-Order CBFs dedicated to maintaining safety. We start with an High-Order CBFs that is standard in rigid-robotics applications and ensures complete avoidance of contact between the robot and its environment. While effective, these constraints can sharply curb performance, encourage overly cautious behavior, and block truly collaborative human–robot interactions.

To overcome such drawbacks, we highlight a High-Order CBF limiting the contact force to
F_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{max}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. This approach allows controlled contact between the soft robot and its surroundings while guaranteeing that such contact remains safe
[
10
]
. Our formulation is inspired by extensive studies on injury-severity criteria for rigid collaborative robots
[
2
]
and by ISO/TS 15066:2016
[
15
]
, which specifies body-part–dependent force thresholds as proxies for injury risk.

1.

Contact Avoidance High-Order CBF.

To ensure safety with respect to a forbidden region
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{W}
, we define a High-Order CBF based on the signed distance between the robot segment
P_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}
. Let
r_{\mathrm{safe}}\geq 0
be a prescribed safety margin. Using the previously defined smooth distance metric
h_{i,\mathcal{A}}(\bm{q})=d(P_{i}(\bm{q}),\mathcal{A})
, we define

b_{i,\mathcal{A}}(\bm{x})=h_{i,\mathcal{A}}(\bm{q})^{2}-r_{\mathrm{safe}}^{2}.

(9)

Then
b_{i,\mathcal{A}}(\bm{x})\geq 0
guarantees that
P_{i}
maintains a distance of at least
r_{\mathrm{safe}}\geq 0
from
\mathcal{A}
.

2.

Contact Force Limit High-Order CBF.

For each obstacle
\mathcal{O}_{j}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, define

b_{i,j}(\bm{x})=F_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{max},j}-F_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}(h_{i,j},\dot{h}_{i,j}),

(10)

where
F_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}(h_{i,j},\dot{h}_{i,j})\geq 0
is the contact force, for example, stemming from a linear spring-damper contact model as defined in (
6
), and
F_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{max},j}
is maximum allowable contact force in static settings as, e.g., defined in ISO/TS 15066:2016
[
15
]
. This barrier function ensures that the contact force between the
i
th soft robot part and the
j
th obstacle remains below the threshold.

III-D

Achieving Effective Motion Behavior via Higher-Order Control Lyapunov Functions

With the barrier conditions established, the next step is to define motion objectives that encourage task completion.

For High-Order CBF constructions requiring an

[
25
]
, despite its efficiency for convex polygons and widespread use over support mapping methods such as the Gilbert-Johnson-Keerthi algorithm
[
26
]
, is fundamentally unsuitable due to its reliance on non-smooth

\max

This lack of differentiability prevents its direct application in force- or distance-based high-order control formulations.
To address this limitation, prior work has introduced a differentiable variant of Separating Axis Theorem, named Smooth SAT, that approximates these non-differentiable operations using multi-level smoothing techniques to achieve
C^{\infty}
continuity
[
21
]
. However, the Smooth SAT overestimates the polygon separation distance, which can lead to a violation of the safety constraint in downstream applications.
Alternatively, methods like DCOL
[
27
]
offer differentiable collision detection for convex primitives, but they cannot quantify penetration depth and are therefore unsuitable for collision resolution (e.g., computing the contact force).
Similarly, the randomized smoothing approach
[
28
]
and the accelerated optimization-based method
[
29
]
focus on differentiable collision checking and computational speed-up, respectively, but do not return differentiable signed distance or penetration depth, which limits their use in differentiable contact dynamics.
In this letter, we propose a new differentiable variant of Separating Axis Theorem, coined Differentiable Conservative SAT, which replaces the layered smoothing pipeline of Smooth SAT
[
21
]
with a single LogSumExp approximation while providing a conservation metric for calculating distance between convex polygons.
Differentiable Conservative SAT preserves the
C^{\infty}
differentiability required for high-order control while significantly simplifying implementation and reducing computational overhead.

IV-A

Definition of Separating Axis Theorem

Before defining our distance metrics, we briefly revisit the Separating Axis Theorem framework, which forms the foundation of convex polygon–distance computations based on orthogonal projections.

Definition 3 (Separating axis):

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be convex sets.
A unit vector
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called a
separating axis
for
A
and
B
if the projections of the sets onto
\bm{a}
are disjoint; that is, if either

\max_{\bm{x}\in A}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{x}<\min_{\bm{y}\in B}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{y}\quad\text{or}\quad\max_{\bm{y}\in B}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{y}<\min_{\bm{x}\in A}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{x}.

Theorem 1 (Separating Axis Theorem
[
25
]
):

Two convex sets
A
and
B
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are disjoint if and only if there exists a separating axis between them.

Lemma 1 (Sufficiency of Edge Normals in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
):

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be convex polygons. Then it suffices to test for separation along the set of directions orthogonal to the edges of
A
and
B
. Specifically, let
S(A)
and
S(B)
denote the sets of edge directions of
A
and
B
, respectively. Define
\mathcal{A}=\left\{\bm{\ell}^{\perp}:\bm{\ell}\in S(A)\cup S(B)\right\}
,
where
\bm{\ell}^{\perp}
denotes the unit vector orthogonal to edge
\bm{\ell}
. Then
\mathcal{A}
is a complete set of candidate separating axes.

Proof.

By the Separating Axis Theorem (
Theorem

1
), if
A
and
B
are disjoint, there exists a direction
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that their projections onto
\mathbf{n}
do not overlap:

\small\max_{x\in A}\mathbf{n}^{\top}x<\min_{y\in B}\mathbf{n}^{\top}y\hskip 9.24994pt\text{or}\hskip 9.24994pt\max_{y\in B}\mathbf{n}^{\top}y<\min_{x\in A}\mathbf{n}^{\top}x.

Since the support function of a convex polygon is piecewise linear and attains its extrema at vertices, any separating direction must be orthogonal to some edge of
A
or
B
. Therefore, it suffices to test separation along directions in
\mathcal{A}
. If no such direction yields separation,
A
and
B
must intersect.
∎

By combining
Theorem

1
and
Lemma

1
, separation testing—and more specifically, the computation of separating distances—can be reduced to a finite set of one-dimensional projections. These observations underlie the projection-based metrics (Separating Axis Theorem, Smooth SAT, Differentiable Conservative SAT) used throughout this letter.

Following by the above statements, we could derive the distance between two polygons A and B.

Projections.

Let
\mathbf{A}_{i},i\in\mathcal{I}_{A},\mathbf{B}_{j},i\in\mathcal{I}_{B}
denote the vertices of convex sets
A,B
, respectively, where
\mathcal{I}_{A}
and
\mathcal{I}_{B}
denote the index sets of the vertices of
A,B
respectively.
For each axis
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}
, the scalar projections of polygon vertices
\mathbf{A}_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{A}
, and
\mathbf{B}_{j}
for
j\in\mathcal{I}_{B}
, onto
\mathbf{a}
are defined as
A_{i,a}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{i}
and
B_{j,a}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}
.

Per-axis distance.

The signed separation (positive if separated, negative if overlapping) between the projected intervals along axis
\mathbf{a}
is given by

\small g_{\mathrm{SAT}}(\mathbf{a})=\max\bigl\{d_{1}(\mathbf{a}),\,d_{2}(\mathbf{a})\bigr\}.

(12)

where

\footnotesize\begin{split}d_{1}(\mathbf{a})=\min_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{B}}B_{j,a}-\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{A}}A_{i,a},\>d_{2}(\mathbf{a})=\min_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{A}}A_{i,a}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{B}}B_{j,a},\end{split}

(13)

Global metric.

The overall Separating Axis Theorem-based signed distance between polygons
A
and
B
is then defined as

\small h_{\mathrm{SAT}}(A,B)\;=\;\max_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\,g_{\mathrm{SAT}}(\mathbf{a}).

(14)

This value is positive when the polygons are separated, zero if they touch, and negative when they overlap.

IV-B

Differentiable Conservative SAT (Differentiable Conservative SAT)

To enable safe and differentiable distance evaluation for use in High-Order CBF+High-Order CLF controllers, and resolve the underestimation of the polygon extents present in Smooth SAT
[
21
]
, we introduce the Differentiable Conservative SAT metric, which provides a conservative estimate of the polygon separation/penetration distance at an increased computational efficiency compared to Smooth SAT. Unlike Smooth SAT, which smooths each intermediate geometric operation and is primarily designed for quadrilateral shapes, Differentiable Conservative SAT operates directly on global signed separation distances. It applies a single LogSumExp operation to obtain a
C^{\infty}
approximation of the Separating Axis Theorem metric that naturally extends to arbitrary convex polygons. Furthermore, Differentiable Conservative SAT consistently underestimates the true separation distance, ensuring that safety constraints enforced via Control Barrier Functions remain valid even under model uncertainty or near-contact conditions. This makes it particularly suitable for collision-aware control of systems with complex geometries.

Definition 4 (Differentiable Conservative SAT (Differentiable Conservative SAT)):

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be convex polygons, and let
\mathcal{A}
denote the set of separating axes. For each axis
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}
, define the separation terms
d_{1}(\mathbf{a})
and
d_{2}(\mathbf{a})
as in (
12
). Let

\mathcal{D}=\left\{d_{m}(\mathbf{a})\;\middle|\;\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A},\;m\in\{1,2\}\right\}.

Then, the Differentiable Conservative SAT distance is defined as

h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}(A,B):=\frac{1}{\alphait_{\max}}\log\left(\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}e^{\alphait_{\max}d}\right)-\frac{\log(2|\mathcal{A}|)}{\alphait_{\max}}.

Lemma 2 (Bounds for Differentiable Conservative SAT):

-\frac{\log(2|\mathcal{A}|)}{\alphait_{\max}}\leq h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}(A,B)-h_{\mathrm{SAT}}(A,B)\leq 0.

Proof.

Let
M=\max\mathcal{D}=h_{\mathrm{SAT}}(A,B)
, and define
e:=\frac{1}{\alphait_{\max}}\log\left(\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}e^{\alphait_{\max}d}\right)
.

Lower bound:
One term in the sum is
e^{\alphait_{\max}M}
, so

\displaystyle\sum e^{\alphait_{\max}d}\geq e^{\alphait_{\max}M}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow e\geq M

\displaystyle\Rightarrow h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}(A,B)

\displaystyle-M\geq-\frac{\log(2|\mathcal{A}|)}{\alphait_{\max}}.

Upper bound:
Since
|\mathcal{D}|=2|\mathcal{A}|
, we have

\displaystyle\sum e^{\alphait_{\max}d}\leq 2|\mathcal{A}|\cdot e^{\alphait_{\max}M}\Rightarrow e\leq M+\frac{\log(2|\mathcal{A}|)}{\alphait_{\max}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}(A,B)-M\leq 0.

∎

Theorem 2 (Conservative Approximation via Differentiable Conservative SAT):

Let

\displaystyle e:=\frac{1}{\alphait_{\max}}\log\left(\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}e^{\alphait_{\max}d}\right),h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}(A,B):=e-\frac{\log(2|\mathcal{A}|)}{\alphait_{\max}}.

Then:

(a)

h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}(A,B)\in C^{\infty}
,

(b)

h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}(A,B)\leq h_{\mathrm{SAT}}(A,B)
for all
A,B
,

(c)

If
h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}(A,B)=0
, then the true SAT distance satisfies

0\leq h_{\mathrm{SAT}}(A,B)<\frac{\log(2|\mathcal{A}|)}{\alphait_{\max}}.

(15)

Proof.

(a)
Since
e
is composed of exponential and logarithmic operations over a finite sum of smooth terms, it follows that
e\in C^{\infty}
. Subtracting a constant preserves smoothness, hence
h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}\in C^{\infty}
.

(b)
Follows directly from
Lemma

2
.

(c)
If
h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}(A,B)=0
, then by definition,
e=\frac{\log(2|\mathcal{A}|)}{\alphait_{\max}}
.

From
Lemma

2
, it follows that

\small 0\leq h_{\mathrm{SAT}}(A,B)\leq\frac{\log(2|\mathcal{A}|)}{\alphait_{\max}},

since
h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}\leq h_{\mathrm{SAT}}
, with
h_{\mathrm{DCSAT}}=0
.

which confirms the conservative approximation of the real distance: if the smoothed metric Differentiable Conservative SAT hits zero, the true Separating Axis Theorem distance remains non-negative.
∎

Algorithm

1
.

IV-C

Benchmarking DCSAT

While the original Smooth SAT formulation
[
21
]
was designed specifically for rectangles or axis-aligned quadrilaterals, its underlying principle—approximating projection half-extents using smooth absolute value functions—can be generalized to arbitrary convex shapes.
To enable a fair comparison across general geometries, we implemented this natural extension and evaluated it against our proposed Differentiable Conservative SAT and the classical Separating Axis Theorem
[
25
]
.

Fig.

3
visualizes the zero-level contours of both smooth metrics relative to the classical SAT boundary.
Notably, Differentiable Conservative SAT consistently generates a conservative underestimation of the true separation distance, as verified by the consistently negative values in the Differentiable Conservative SAT error column of
Table

I
, ensuring that the smoothed constraint remains valid under model error or near-contact conditions.
In contrast, Smooth SAT
[
21
]
tends to overestimate the true separation at low sharpness levels, as can be seen in the positive values in the SSAT error column of
Table

I
, which can compromise safety-critical guarantees in barrier-based control.

Although both smoothed variants are implemented efficiently in JAX, Differentiable Conservative SAT achieves comparable or better computational performance in large batch collision detections compared to Smooth SAT
[
21
]
despite its global formulation.
As shown in
Table

I
, it maintains fast execution across polygon sizes while preserving full
C^{\infty}
smoothness. To evaluate scalability, we benchmark the runtime of our method (DCSAT) against SSAT with increasing batch sizes (Table II). Each batch contains multiple robot segment–obstacle polygon pairs, where each obstacle polygon has
N=8
.
Results show that DCSAT consistently outperforms SSAT in runtime, and the relative speedup grows with the batch size, from 1.53× at batch size 32 to 2.39× at batch size 256.
Taken together, these results suggest that Differentiable Conservative SAT offers a robust and practical surrogate to classical Separating Axis Theorem for use in applications that require differentiability and a conservative approximation of the polygon separation distance.

V
Experiments

V-A

Baseline Methods

Safety Unaware HOCLF.

This baseline captures the typical approach found in current soft-robotic control research
[
22
]
: controlling the system without explicitly enforcing safety constraints. Here, we drop the HOCBF terms from the QP and retain only the HOCLF component—specifically, the Operational-Space Regulation HOCLF—within the QP constraints.

Contact Avoidance Artificial Potential Field.

The purpose of this baseline is the represent the scenario that is widespread in the rigid robotics literature (e.g., collision avoidance
[
2
]
) - the aim to fully avoid contact - in this case via application of a repulsive Artificial Potential Field approach
[
30
]
.
We adopt an integral-free (i.e.,
K_{\mathrm{i}}=0
) variant of operational-space impedance controller from (
2
) to operate on positions of the soft robot segment tips with
\bm{\omicronit}=\big[\bm{p}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\big]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
. Combined with the repulsive artificial potential force
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{rep}}
, the resulting control law is:

\footnotesize\begin{split}\bm{u}=&\>\bm{u}_{\omicronit}+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{srpoly}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}\bm{J}_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}}^{\top}\bm{f}_{\mathrm{rep}_{i,j}},\\
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{rep}_{i,j}}=&\>\begin{cases}k_{\mathrm{rep}}\frac{h_{i,j}^{-1}-r_{\mathrm{safe}}^{-1}}{h_{i,j}^{2}}\,\bm{n}_{\mathrm{c}_{i,j}},&\text{if $h_{i,j}\leq r_{\mathrm{safe}}$}\\
\bm{0}_{2},&\text{if $h_{i,j}>r_{\mathrm{safe}}$}\end{cases}.\end{split}

(16)

Please note that the proportional term of
\bm{u}_{\omicronit}
in (
2
) corresponds to force stemming from an attractive Artificial Potential Field.

Contact Avoidance HOCBF+HOCLF.

The purpose of this baseline is similar to the last one, but instead of relying on an artificial potential field to avoid contact and a classic operational space controller for incorporating the task objective, we rely here on the HOCBF+HOCLF framework by combining a
Contact Avoidance HOCBF
with a
Operational Space Regulation HOCLF
.

Contact Force-Limit HOCBF Filter.

In this baseline, we replace the High-Order CLF objectives with a nominal operational-space controller whose commands are filtered for safety by solving the Quadratic Program with
Contact Force Limit HOCBF
constraints. Specifically, we employ
\mathbf{u}_{\omicronit}(\mathbf{x})
from (
2
), where the operational-space is defined as
\bm{\omicronit}=\big[\bm{p}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\bm{p}_{N}^{\top}\big]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
, representing the positions of the segment tips.

Implementation & Simulation Details

We build on the
CBFpy

[
31
]
package that offers an easy-to-use and high-performance implementation of (high-order) Control Barrier Function+Control Lyapunov Function in JAX while leveraging analytical gradients obtained via autodifferentiation.
Our simulations consider a planar, two-segment PCS soft robot (
N=2
) implemented in the
JSRM
package
[
20
]
. Each segment is
0.13\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
long with a backbone radius of
0.02\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
; the elastic modulus is
2\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}
and the shear modulus is
1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}
. The actuation matrix is set to the identity,
\bm{A}(\bm{q})=\mathbb{I}_{3N}
. No state or input bounds are enforced. The closed-loop ODE is integrated with a numerical solver implementing Tsitouras’ 5/4 method.

For the contact model, we choose
k_{\mathrm{c}}=$3000\text{\,}\mathrm{N}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}$,c_{\mathrm{c}}=0
and set the contact force limit to
F_{\mathrm{c},\max,j}=$15\text{\,}\mathrm{N}$
and, in contact-avoidance scenarios, impose a minimum safety clearance of
r_{\mathrm{safe}}=$0.005\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
, and a repulsive artificial potential field stiffness of
k_{\mathrm{rep}}=$3000\text{\,}\mathrm{N}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{M}$
. The
\varepsilon
value for softplus-based smoothing force is
2\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
.

Navigation Results

We implemented multiple scenarios to validate our framework. Among them, the search & rescue task is the most representative for comparing different baselines and is therefore highlighted in this letter (see supplementary materials for additional scenarios).
Specifically, we compare the behavior of the
Contact Force-Limit HOCBF+HOCLF
control strategy against the baselines.
We present the results in
Fig.

4
and
Fig.

5

Indeed, the results demonstrate that the
safety-unaware

contact-avoidance

contact-force limit

VI
Conclusion

This letter introduces a High-Order CBFs+High-Order CLFs framework
[
18
]
for the control of soft robots, grounded in a differentiable Piecewise Constant Strain model
[
19
,
20
]
and a novel differentiable and conservative polygon distance metric Differentiable Conservative SAT.
Our method allows (1) soft robots to embrace contact with the environment but ensure safety via contact force-limit High-Order CBFs evaluated along the entire soft robot body, (2) the flexible and expressive specification of control objectives, such as shape and end-effector regulation or object manipulation, via High-Order CLFs.
The Differentiable Conservative SAT metric offers a
C^{\infty}
approximation of the classical Separating Axis Theorem, yielding conservative signed distances with reduced computational overhead compared to existing baseline methods that overestimate the distance, leading to potentially unsafe behavior.
Simulated experiments in navigation scenarios validate the framework’s ability to maintain geometric safety and guide soft robots toward task objectives.
Current limitations of the work include the focus on planar settings, the simplistic contact model, and the reliance on accurate proprioception and exteroception.
