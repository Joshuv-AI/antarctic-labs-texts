Momentum space AC Josephson effect and intervalley coherence in multilayer graphene
Authors: Mainak Das, Chunli Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectron transport driven by the phase coherence and interference of quantum many-body wavefunctions is a fascinating phenomenon with potential technological significance. Superconductivity, for example, enables dissipationless transport through macroscopic phase twisting, while charge-density waves show broadband noise and AC-DC interference once their phase is depinned. We identify an analogous phase-coherent dynamics in the intervalley-coherent (IVC) state frequently observed in rhombohedral multilayer graphene. We show that a static magnetic field drives an oscillating intervalley current, which produces an oscillating orbital magnetization and thereby a detectable AC Hall response. This mechanism is similar to the Josephson effect observed in superconductors but now happening in momentum space. In this analogy, the static magnetic field acts as the DC voltage, while the oscillating intervalley current is the AC Josephson current. We provide microscopic calculations of all the phase-number free-energy parameters in rhombohedral trilayer graphene, establishing this effect as an experimental signature of IVC order.Subject terms: Superconducting properties and materials, Electronic properties and materials

Introduction

Magnetism arising from spin and valley ordering in lightly-doped multilayer graphene systems, both with1–12 and without moiré potential13–28, has become a well-established phenomenon following the discovery of the integer quantum anomalous Hall effect in twisted bilayer graphene29–32. There are three primary types of spin-valley ordered states: spin-polarized ferromagnets, valley-polarized orbital ferromagnets, and the intervalley-coherent (IVC) state. Spin-polarized ferromagnets typically expand their phase boundaries under in-plane magnetic fields, whereas valley-polarized orbital ferromagnets are known for exhibiting a strong anomalous Hall effect. These states, characterized by Ising-like order parameters at temperature below the magnetic anisotropic energy, also generate magnetic stray fields detectable via nano-SQUID33–36. In contrast, the intervalley-coherent (IVC) state, characterized by its XY-like order parameter, remains elusive and is typically identified by eliminating other symmetry-broken states12–14 and through comparison with mean-field theory37–41. Theories suggest that IVC fluctuations could be the microscopic origins of superconductivity28,40,42 in multilayer graphene and homobilayer moiré transition metal dichalcogenides43. The IVC state was previously thought to exhibit no prominent transport signatures and can mainly be detected through STM experiments44–49. We note that the difference in the dielectric screening environment can influence the observable magnetic grounstates in STM versus transport experiments50,51.

In this study, we report a unique transport phenomenon associated with the IVC state: an out-of-plane static magnetic field induces an oscillating orbital magnetization, leading to an AC Hall effect, as shown in Fig. 1. This effect, analogous to AC Josephson effect in a superconductor, can be detected using probes sensitive to finite-frequency Hall conductivity σxy(ω), such as microwave impedance microscopy52, and may serve as a definitive diagnostic tool for identifying the IVC state.

Momentum-space AC Josephson effect

In multilayer graphene with a low electron density of about 10−4 electrons per carbon atom (typical in most experiments), electrons occupy a tiny fraction of the Brillouin zone, specifically around the opposite corners, which are denoted as valleys τ:{K,K′=−K}. The operator cK+k†c−K+k acting on Ψvac creates an exciton with wave-vector 2K. Thus, Ψϕ describes a condensate of such 2K excitons with ϕ representing the global phase of the condensate. The spin degree of freedom is not important to our discussion, and it suffices to treat the electrons and holes as having the same spin projection. Analogous to the BCS state in superconductors, the state Ψϕ possesses a well-defined global phase but an indefinite exciton number. This exciton picture of electron-hole pairing at distinct wave-vectors has been widely used to describe one-dimensional charge-density-wave (CDW) metals like NbSe3 (e.g., ref. 55), where each exciton carries wavevector 2kF, with kF being the Fermi wavevector. However, there is an important difference between a 1D CDW and our 2K exciton condensate in higher dimensions. In 1D, the number eigenstate with N excitons carries a net velocity of 2NℏkF/m* (m* is the effective mass) and thus describes a current-carrying state. In our case, however, a state with Nτ excitons ΨNτ does not carry a net velocity or current if electrons in each valley remain in independent chemical potential equilibrium. The reason is that, in higher dimensions, the total velocity in each valley vanishes upon integrating over the Fermi surface, ∫d2knF(ϵk,τ−μ)∇kϵk,τ=0. Therefore, although the exciton number eigenstate possesses finite crystal momentum (ΨNτkΨNτ≠0) it generally does not produce a measurable net current. However, two-dimensional materials with low-energy states at the honeycomb Brillouin zone corners, such as rhombohedral multilayer graphene, are special. In this system, Bloch states at the ±K points with the same energy acquire opposite orbital angular momenta when the Dirac points are gapped by a displacement field, interactions, or both. In this case, an exciton formed from an electron and a hole in opposite valleys has the same chirality—unlike the textbook situation where electrons and holes bend in opposite directions, here they bend in the same direction. As a result, the exciton number eigenstate ΨNτ carries a finite orbital moment with measurable consequences. In particular, an external magnetic field B shifts the energies of opposite valleys in opposite directions, effectively acting as a valley-contrasting chemical potential56:

This process is analogous to the Josephson coupling in Josephson junctions, which maintains phase coherence across a junction by tunneling Cooper pairs. Similarly, three-body Umklapp scattering enforces phase coherence in the IVC exciton condensate by tunneling three electrons from one valley to another. In both regimes, the dynamics inevitably lead to an oscillating orbital magnetization.

3-body Umklapp scattering

At dilute doping where charge carriers are concentrated in the two opposite valleys, the Umklapp process that transfers electrons between these valleys necessitates at least a three-body scattering process. This is because a two-body scattering process does not provide sufficient momentum to match a reciprocal lattice vector. Such large-momentum transfer processes means a delta-function-like potential in the K⋅p continuum approximation. At second order in Coulomb potential and electron-phonon coupling, only one diagram contributes to this three-body scattering, as shown in Fig. 5. Under a lattice translation a, cK→cKeiK⋅a, this diagram picks up a phase ei6K⋅a=1 since 6K is a reciprocal lattice vector. This scattering process begins with three electrons all initially in the same valley (K). These electrons undergo two distinct scattering events to transition to the opposite valley K′. First, two of the electrons scatter, moving one to the final valley K′ (modulo G) and the other to an intermediate state at Γ point with a matrix element V(K). Subsequently, the electron at the Γ point scatters with the remaining electron at K, transferring both to valley K′ (modulo G) with matrix elements V(K). This type of lattice-scale interaction receives higher-order contributions from both electron-electron and electron-optical phonon interactions. We50,51,63 and others79–82 have estimated the strength of two-body lattice-scale interactions, finding them significantly enhanced by the large density of states at graphene’s π band. In fact, without at least an order of magnitude enhancement in two-body lattice-scale interactions, their magnitudes would be insufficient compared to the Zeeman energy to explain the experimental findings83. Figure 8 in ref. 51 attempts to quantify this renormalization. Therefore, by treating γ as a phenomenological constant and leveraging our better theoretical control of other constants in FIVC, we propose detecting the oscillating magnetization induced by a static magnetic field as a practical experimental method to determine this parameter.

Damping

The orbital magnetization oscillation cannot persist indefinitely; it eventually dissipates energy and settles into a partially valley-imbalanced state. Below, we discuss three distinct channels through which the order-parameter dynamics can lose energy: finite-temperature effects, atomic-scale disorder, and intrinsic damping.

(1) Finite temperature effect: the universality class of FIVC is XY-like, and its topological defects are phase singularities known as merons84. Below the Kosterlitz-Thouless (KT) transition temperature TKT, or when γ is sufficiently strong, merons and antimerons are bound, preventing phase slippage. However, for T > TKT, unbound merons cause valley imbalance through phase slippage. This binding is crucial for the observability of our proposed phase-coherent transport phenomena. We have estimated TKT based on a microscopic Hartree-Fock calculation of IVC domain wall (see Supplemental Material for details on the band structure model, intervalley coherent states without nematicity, a discussion of three-body Umklapp scattering, and estimation of valley stiffness and damping parameters) and found TKT = ρ(π/2kB) ~ 2.5 K. This temperature is two orders of magnitude higher than the lowest temperatures accessible in many 2D transport experiments13–28, making our proposed phenomena experimentally feasible.

(2) Atomic-scale disorder: atomistic defect in graphene such as missing carbon atoms or sp2 → sp3 hybridization distort the nearby electronic charge and bond densities. Depending on the defect symmetry, the resulting pattern can range from a uniform Kekulé bond order to a Kekulé vortex with a winding phase profile85. The latter is also directly visualized by STM in monolayer graphene’s zeroth Landau level45. For concreteness, let us consider a uniform Kekulé pattern generated by a divacancy. Ab initio calculations estimate the structural energy cost at 7–8 eV per atom86. Unlike conventional charge-density-wave systems, which occur near commensurate fillings (e.g., 1/2, 1/3, 1/4), gated graphene has carrier densities that are orders of magnitude smaller. For ne = 1012cm−2, with unit-cell area Auc=3a2/2≃5.24,Å2, the filling fraction is only ν = 1.3 × 10−4 π-band electrons per carbon atom. Thus, the probability of a carrier occupying a defect site is vanishingly small. The corresponding defect-formation energy per electron is Vim ~ 8 × 1.3 × 10−4eV ≈ 1meV. (3) Intrinsic damping: when extrinsic defects and finite-temperature effects are negligible, oscillations are ultimately limited by intrinsic damping. A useful perspective is to view the dynamics as an analog of ferromagnetic resonance in magnetism. On the valley Bloch sphere, the poles correspond to time-reversal-broken states with majority occupation in valley K or K′, while the equator (the XY-plane) represents IVC states. If the ground state is polarized at the north or south pole, there is no natural external field that couples directly to the IVC order parameter since valley is fundamentally just crystal momentum. Coherent rotations about the poles—valley-wave excitations—are very difficult to excite, since an external probe would need to supply the large momentum transfer between K and K′ (cK†cK′). By contrast, if the ground state is an XY-valley ferromagnet (an IVC state), a uniform out-of-plane magnetic field B acts as a valley-contrasting chemical potential that couples directly to valley-imbalance. In this analogy, the term Nτ2/2χ provides the uniaxial valley anisotropy energy favoring Nτ = 0, while the three-body Umklapp term Fump provides the in-plane valley anisotropy energy to pin the IVC phase when B = 0. At finite B, the dynamics of the orbital magnetization closely resemble ferromagnetic resonance: an out-of-plane B field drives oscillations of the orbital magnetization and produces strong absorption of an ac field Bac at three times the orbital Larmor frequency. The intrinsic damping can then be interpreted as a valley analog of the Kamberský mechanism89–92, where time-dependent valley-order parameter dynamics generates particle-hole excitations near the Fermi surface, and quasiparticle decay channels transfer energy from the collective mode to the lattice. Discussions

We uncovered an interesting phase-coherent dynamics in IVC and demonstrated their experimental observability by computing all parameters of the IVC phase-number free energy FIVC using a detailed microscopic theory. A static magnetic field leading to oscillating orbital magnetization is a direct consequence of the macroscopic phase coherence of the IVC. In contrast, a half-metal without IVC (e.g., with K↑ and K′↓ occupied) exhibits no such oscillatory dynamics of the orbital magnetization. This distinction provides a clear experimental signature, which could be probed using microwave techniques52,93.

IVC has also been theoretically identified in a wide range of two-dimensional materials. Notably, valley-projected moiré minibands with finite Chern numbers are common in graphene moiré superlattices10,94 and twisted homobilayer transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)57. Metallic IVC states have recently been proposed as the symmetry-broken phases that can mediate pairing glue for superconductivity observed in 5∘ twisted WSe243,95. The phase-coherent transport discussed for the IVC metal in RTG can be directly generalized to this system, with modified parameters (χ−1, gv, γ) readily computed using the methods we outlined above.

Recent work by Wang and Vafek96 carried out a comprehensive Hartree-Fock study of correlated phases in MATBG under finite magnetic field. With realistic heterostrain, they found that the ground state at small B is incompressible and exhibits intervalley coherence, analogous to the finite B version of Intervalley Kekule spiral97. If the valley polarization develops as the applied B increases, our Ginzburg-Landau theory should apply and predict oscillating magnetization. We note that ref. 98 has shown that weak localization is enhanced in time-reversal-invariant IVC states. If this enhancement can be reliably distinguished from intervalley scattering effects—also present in the normal metallic states—it could provide a useful diagnostic for detecting IVC order. In contrast, the phase-coherent response we propose arises from interaction-driven three-body Umklapp scattering and should be more readily distinguishable from disorder-related effects.

Methods

We use constrained Hartree-Fock method with Lagrange multipliers to compute the valley stiffness ρ99,100 and the anisotropic susceptibility χ−1 associated with collective valley rotations. The orbital magnetization is obtained from the mean-field wavefunctions and eigenenergies of the constrained Hartree-Fock calculation, following the justification in refs. 101–103. The strength of the three-body Umklapp scattering term is estimated using second-order perturbation theory. The damping parameter is evaluated using Kambersky’s theory in the intraband limit. The nonlinear equations of motion are solved using standard adaptive Runge-Kutta methods.
