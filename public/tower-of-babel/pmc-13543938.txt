Stretching a doublet into a triplet for FLT3 AML
Authors: Keith W. Pratz
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Short et al1 describe the long-term clinical outcomes of newly diagnosed FLT-3–mutated acute myeloid leukemia (AML) treated with the combination of the FLT-3 inhibitor gilteritinib (GILT) with azacitidine and venetoclax (AZA/VEN). This article documents a remarkably high response rate in a challenging-to-treat subtype of AML with FLT3 mutations. A prospective randomized study is currently enrolling to examine the clinical utility of the triplet approach combining AZA, VEN, and GILT vs the standard doublet approach of AZA/VEN for the treatment of patients with FLT3-ITD AML (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06317649). Before the routine use of the triplet regimen can be advocated, the results of this randomized study are needed to justify the potentially serious myelosuppression observed with the triplet combination. In the interim, however, the results documented by Short et al in this issue of Blood Advances highlight the encouraging potential of augmenting the clinical activity of VEN-based therapies with the addition of a third targeted agent.

The treatment landscape of AML has changed dramatically over the past 7 years with the development of VEN-based low-intensity therapies for AML that target the BCL-2 apoptosis pathway. The most widely used regimen is the doublet combination of AZA/VEN, supported by the favorable results of the VIALE-A trial, which evaluated patients with newly diagnosed AML who were unfit for conventional chemotherapy.2 AZA/VEN therapy leads to remissions in a wide spectrum of historically difficult-to-treat myeloid leukemias, and this clinical activity is associated with shorter lengths of initial hospital stays compared with conventional induction chemotherapy.3 In patients treated with AZA/VEN, the likelihood of achieving remission and the duration of those remissions can be predicted by leukemia-associated molecular changes in a pattern distinct from those used to predict outcomes after conventional induction chemotherapy.4 Leukemia-associated mutations in 4 genes predict clinical outcomes of AZA/VEN-based therapy. Mutations in TP53 are associated with inferior response rates and survivals, whereas mutations in FLT3-ITD, KRAS, or NRAS are associated with intermediate outcomes. The absence of any of those 4 mutations confers a more favorable outcome.4 With oral agents approved for both frontline (midostaurin and quizartinib) and relapsed FLT3-mutant AML (GILT), Short et al undertook a study aimed at improving the intermediate outcomes of FLT3-ITD–mutated AML treated with AZA/VEN alone through the addition of an FLT3 inhibitor, GILT, to VEN-based therapy. Preclinically, FLT3 mutation–mediated resistance to VEN-based BCL-2–targeted therapy can be mitigated by the inhibition of FLT3 with small-molecule inhibitors.5 Daver et al subsequently reported enhanced responses in patients with relapsed and refractory FLT3-ITD AML treated with a combination of VEN and GILT vs GILT alone.6 Initial evaluations of the triplet combination of AZA/VEN/GILT were reported in 2024, with remarkable response rates in patients with newly diagnosed FLT3-mutated AML but a limited duration of follow-up.7

In this updated study report, Short et al describe in greater detail the long-term outcomes of FLT3-mutated AML treated with the triplet therapy AZA/VEN/GILT. The composite response rate was 96%, and median overall survival was 29.7 months. Among the FLT3-ITD cohort, the median overall survival was 21.8 months, which compares favorably to the 9.9 months reported with AZA/VEN-based therapy.8 As with high-intensity therapy, in AZA/VEN-treated patients, the achievement of measurable residual disease (MRD)–negative remission assessed by flow cytometry has been associated with a favorable long-term survival.9 Short et al also report that the triplet AZA/VEN/GILT MRD-negative composite remission rate was 93% as measured by flow cytometry and 65% as measured by next-generation sequencing–based MRD to levels <5 × 10−5. Encouragingly, those patients who achieved MRD-negative responses with AZA/VEN/GILT were associated with longer durations of response and overall survival.

However, the addition of GILT to AZA/VEN did come at a cost of myelosuppression and challenges with repetitive cycles of administration. The median number of cycles administered was only 3 in this cohort, and the median time to achieving an absolute neutrophil count >1.0 × 109/L in cycle 2 was 49 days. This myelosuppression occurred despite the routine use of peg-filgrastim after cycle 1, reinforcing the challenges of long-term myelosuppression with this regimen. Myelosuppression was mitigated by dosing delays and adjustments of the doses of VEN (48% of the patients) and/or AZA (44%) along with the routine use of growth factors for patients in remission. Twelve patients received allogeneic transplantation, but the transplant did not confer favorable long-term survival benefits in this analysis. The transplant outcomes in this small data set reinforce the challenges of showing enhanced overall survival in elderly patients with AML who receive allogeneic transplants. Ultimately, the routine use of the triplet combination of AZA/VEN/GILT will be justified by data gleaned from randomized trials, including the actively enrolling National Cancer Institute–sponsored Myelomatch study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06317649).10

The response rates and MRD-negative remission rates reported by Short et al compare favorably with a prospective study incorporating GILT into conventional cytarabine- and anthracycline-based induction for FLT3-mutated AML (85% and 40%, respectively)11 despite the appreciably older patient population. The recent report of the PARADIGM study that compares AZA/VEN-based induction to conventional induction therapy did not study FLT3-mutated AML, but in non-FLT3 AML, AZA/VEN was reported to have a superior response rate and event-free survival.12 Future frontline FLT3 studies should aim to test if lower-intensity backbone therapy such as AZA/VEN could achieve these higher response rates with lower degrees of toxicity in younger adults. It is anticipated that with this approach, more patients will maintain fitness for allogeneic transplant. The challenges with these studies include the identification of dosing strategies that achieve adequate disease control without the costs of severe myelosuppression. As with any novel therapy, familiarity with toxicity profiles is essential for the safe administration of triplet therapies such as AZA/VEN/GILT. However, the promising response rates reported by Short et al support the ongoing optimization of this regimen with the hopes of curing a larger number of patients with FLT3-mutant AML.
