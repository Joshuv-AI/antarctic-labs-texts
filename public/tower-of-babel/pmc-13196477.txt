Identifying pre-treatment risk factors for cancer-related cognitive decline in patients with breast cancer
Authors: Rob Colaes, Gwen Schroyen, Ahmed Radwan, Rebeca Alejandra Gavrila Laic, Charlotte Sleurs, Shannon Helsper, Uwe Himmelreich, Sigrid Hatse, Ann Smeets, Sabine Deprez, Stefan Sunaert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo explore pre-treatment risk factors for cognitive decline in patients with breast cancer using a machine learning approach applied to a comprehensive multimodal clinical, biological, and neuroimaging dataset.MethodsSixty-seven women with early breast cancer were assessed at diagnosis (T0), 8 months (T1), and 17 months after diagnosis (T2). Cognitive decline was defined using the Reliable Change Index. Patients were classified as showing no decline or decline at either follow-up. Five feature sets were evaluated: (1) patient characteristics, treatment, and psychosocial measures; (2) inflammatory and neural health markers, (3) structural brain volumes, (4) resting-state functional MRI connectivity, and (5) diffusion MRI measures. Random forest models were first trained on feature set 1, then sequentially combined with sets 2-5 to explore their additive predictive value. Each model underwent standardized preprocessing, recursive feature selection of the top 6 predictors, and tuning before random forest classification. A final composite model was constructed by pooling the six top predictors from each feature set to assess potential complementary multimodal information. Feature contributions were examined using SHAP values.ResultsOf 67 patients, 33 (49%) experienced cognitive decline following treatment. Models achieved prediction accuracies of 76%, improving up to 81% when MRI measures and/or serum markers were included. Key baseline predictors of cognitive decline included more aggressive subtypes, planned systemic therapy, perceived stress, and limited cognitive and brain reserve.ConclusionMachine learning explored potential pre-treatment risk factors for cancer-related cognitive decline in patients with breast cancer. These findings highlight potential risk factors that could support risk-stratification.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the leading cancer in women, with approximately 2.3 million new diagnoses worldwide annually. Due to earlier diagnosis and better treatment options, prognosis has significantly improved [1]. However, approximately 24% of patients with breast cancer experience objective cognitive decline following treatment, measured using neuropsychological tests [2]. The most commonly affected cognitive domains are memory, attention, processing speed, and executive functioning [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]. The prevalence of self-reported cognitive complaints is even higher, with around 44% of patients reporting problems [8]. Cancer-related cognitive decline severely impacts patients’ daily functioning and quality of life [9] [10] and has been associated with anxiety, depression, fatigue, and stress [11] [12] [13]. Although the pathophysiology of cognitive decline following cancer treatment remains unclear, several contributing mechanisms have been suggested, including hormonal changes, treatment-related direct neurotoxicity, DNA damage, genetic predisposition, and indirect immune-mediated inflammatory processes [10] [14] [15]. Neuroimaging studies, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have shown structural and functional brain alterations associated with these cognitive changes [16] [17] [18] [19].

Cancer-related cognitive decline is characterized by substantial inter-individual variability in the severity and persistence of symptoms [20]. While some patients show no cognitive decline, others suffer from impairment for up to 10 to 20 years following treatment [21] [22]. Given this variability among patients, prediction models may serve as valuable tools in identifying key risk factors and patients likely to experience cognitive decline. Early identification of these patients would enable timely interventions and support informed decision-making, ultimately leading to more personalized clinical care [23]. As a result, the use of predictive models for cancer-related cognitive decline has increased in recent years [24]. In patients with breast cancer, tumor- and treatment-related risk factors have been identified, such as tumor human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (Her2) positivity and receiving chemotherapy [25] [26]. Individual patient characteristics shown to be predictive of cognitive decline include higher age, lower educational level, apolipoprotein E4 positivity, and obesity [26] [27] [25] [28]. Lower educational levels may reflect lower cognitive reserve, which refers to an individual's ability to adapt or compensate for neurological changes [29]. Emotional well-being prior to treatment may also play a role, with higher levels of stress, depression, anxiety, fatigue, and poorer sleep quality being associated with an increased risk for cognitive decline [30] [31] [28] [27]. Additionally, higher baseline levels of the inflammatory markers C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin 6 (IL-6) have been identified as risk factors in patients with breast cancer, suggesting that peripheral inflammatory responses may contribute [32]. Alongside cognitive reserve, brain reserve has emerged as a complementary concept, which refers to the brain's physical capacity to tolerate damage. Individuals with greater brain reserve may be more resilient to cognitive decline [33]. Structural and functional brain alterations have been found to be present pre-treatment, suggesting that certain patients may begin therapy with pre-existing neural vulnerabilities [34] [35] [36]. Consistent with this concept, altered white matter microstructure at baseline, measured by diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), has been shown to predict cognitive decline in patients with breast cancer, potentially reflecting lower white matter reserve [37]. Similarly, disruptions in functional connectivity prior to treatment, as measured by resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI), have been associated with later cognitive impairment [38].

However, studies using prediction models to investigate cancer-related cognitive decline in patients with breast cancer are still scarce. Cancer-related cognitive decline is a multifactorial issue influenced by various treatment- and patient-related factors [20], yet most prediction models use single data modality. Incorporating multiple data modalities may not only provide a more comprehensive understanding and improve predictive performance, but also enable direct comparison of predictive value across modalities [24].

The current study aims to explore potential risk factors for cognitive decline in patients with breast cancer using a machine learning approach based on a comprehensive pre-treatment assessment, which included patient characteristics, questionnaires on emotional well-being, blood-based inflammatory and neural health markers, and multimodal MRI. We hypothesize that specific patient and treatment characteristics, including older age, greater disease severity, lower cognitive reserve, poorer emotional well-being, higher inflammation, and lower brain reserve at diagnosis, may increase the risk of cognitive decline.

Methods

Participants

Women (≤65 years) newly diagnosed with primary non-metastatic early breast cancer were recruited through the Multidisciplinary Breast Center at the University Hospitals Leuven. The current dataset is derived from an earlier longitudinal study comparing chemotherapy exposed patients with non-chemotherapy exposed patients and healthy controls (HC) [39] [40]. This cohort included 67 patients of whom 32 women were scheduled to undergo (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy and 35 patients were not exposed to chemotherapy. The chemotherapy regimen included four cycles of epirubicin (90 mg/m2) and cyclophosphamide (600 mg/m2), followed by four to twelve cycles of paclitaxel (80 mg/m2). 46 HC were recruited online and matched for age, sex and education. Participants were assessed at diagnosis (T0), 8 months after diagnosis (T1), and 17 months after diagnosis (T2), or at matched intervals. Chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy was administered between T0 and T1, with T1 assessed 3 months post-chemotherapy. T0 assessments were conducted post-surgery for all patients, or prior to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in those receiving it. Patients and HC were included only if they attended all three assessments. Exclusion criteria were MRI contraindications, a history of cancer treatment, drug or alcohol abuse, psychiatric/neurological condition/injury, intellectual disability, or systemic steroid use. Neuropsychological testing, self-reported questionnaires, MRI, and blood markers were all assessed on the same day. MRI acquisition

MRI acquisition included 3D T1-weighted images, 3D FLAIR, proton MR spectroscopy (1H-MRS) within the bilateral posterior cingulate cortex, rs-fMRI, and multi-shell diffusion MRI (dMRI). Details are provided in the supplementary materials.

Prediction model of cognitive decline

Assessment of reliable cognitive decline

Neuropsychological assessment

At each time point, patients underwent a 1-h neuropsychological test battery [42], following the International Cognition and Cancer Task Force guidelines [43]. All neuropsychological assessments were conducted in a quiet testing room at the University Hospitals Leuven and were administered by trained neuropsychologists. The test battery included 18 outcomes from the Grooved Pegboard [44], the Auditory Verbal Learning Test [45], the Bourdon-Wiersma Dot [46], the Stroop color word [47], the trail making test [48], the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale IV [49], and the Controlled Oral Word Association [50]. An overview is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Reliable Change Index

Following ICCTF guidelines [43], cognitive decline was defined using the Reliable Change Index (RCI). The RCI assesses whether changes in test scores exceed measurement error and practice effects, based on comparisons with our healthy controls [51]. We applied the Iverson et al. (2001) formula, which accounts for practice effects [52], and quantified test-retest reliability with the intraclass correlation coefficient to capture consistency and agreement across time [53]. For each patient and follow-up (T1, T2), RCIs were calculated for each test relative to baseline (T0). Patients scoring below −1.64 (90% CI) on ≥2 tests were classified as showing cognitive decline from baseline to both timepoints of follow-up. For prediction, the outcome was binary: patients were classified as either showing no decline vs. cognitive decline at least one follow-up.

Predictors of cognitive decline

To predict cognitive decline, we considered five sets of baseline predictor variables (“features”): (1) clinical and psychosocial, (2) inflammatory and neural health markers, (3) regional brain volumes, (4) functional connectivity (intra- and inter-network connectivity), and (5) white matter microstructure features. Each set's features are provided in Supplementary Tables 2–6.

Clinical and psychosocial feature set

Patient, tumor, and treatment-related characteristics, together with psychosocial characteristics, were combined into a single feature set, as these factors have previously been shown to predict cognitive decline [26] [28] [31].

Tumor and treatment characteristics, as well as body mass index (BMI), were obtained from the electronic patient record system of the University Hospital Leuven. Education and menopausal status were self-reported. Proxy verbal intelligence quotient (IQ) was estimated as a measure for cognitive reserve, using a Dutch version of the National Adult Reading Test [54]. Self-reported cognitive and psychosocial functioning were evaluated using self-reported questionnaires, which included the Cognitive Failure Questionnaire (CFQ) [55], the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [56], the Fatigue Assessment Scale (FAS) [57], the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) [58], and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) [59].

Inflammatory and neural health markers feature set

The second feature set included both peripheral (serum) inflammatory and neural health markers, as well as 1H-MRS brain metabolites, to test whether pre-treatment inflammatory response and neural health may predict cognitive decline. Previously, CRP, a marker of inflammation, was identified as a potential risk factor in breast cancer [32]. Serum neurofilament light-chain (NfL), a marker of axonal degeneration, has been shown to predict cognitive decline in patients with multiple sclerosis and Parkinson's disease [60] [61]. 1H-MRS metabolites may provide complementary risk factors, as they reflect both inflammatory and neural health markers.

Concentrations of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells (sTREM-1, sTREM-2), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), interleukins (IL-6, IL-18), tumor necrosis factor-ɑ (TNF-α), soluble receptor for advanced glycation-end products (sRAGE), and fractalkine (CX3CL1) were measured in the collected serum samples using the Human Neuroinflammation panel 1 cytometric bead array (LEGENDplexTM, BioLegend®, San Diego) on a FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Details on the preprocessing of both the serum inflammatory markers and NfL have been described previously [39]. CRP levels were extracted from the electronic patient record system of University Hospital Leuven. The 1H-MRS metabolites were preprocessed in Osprey [62] and quantified in LCModel [63] while correcting for partial volume effects. These included choline (Cho), glutamate-glutamine complex (Glx), myo-inositol (m-Ins), N-acetylaspartate (NAA), creatine (Cre), and the ratio of NAA/Cre. Details on the acquisition and preprocessing of the 1H-MRS metabolites have been described previously [40].

Brain volumes feature set

Regional brain volumes and white-matter lesion volumes were combined into a structural feature set, as both atrophy in gray matter regions and higher lesion burden have been shown to predict cognitive decline in patients with multiple sclerosis [64] [65] [66].

The T1-weighted images were segmented using FastSurfer (v2.1.1) [67], based on the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville atlas [68] [69]. This resulted in 82 regional volumes, which were normalized for intracranial volume. Cortical regions overlapping at least 25% with the rs-fMRI networks were retained. White-matter lesion volumes were quantified (icometrix icobrain MS), providing total lesion volume and regional volumes in deep, juxtacortical, and periventricular white matter [70] [71] [72].

Functional connectivity feature set

Functional connectivity features were derived from cognition-related resting-state networks, as disruptions in functional connectivity have been reported to predict cognitive decline in patients with breast cancer [38].

Regions belonging to the default mode (DMN), dorsal attention (DA), salience (SAL), fronto-parietal (FP), and language (LANG) networks were selected using the CONN toolbox (v22a) [73], and both hippocampi were additionally included from the Harvard–Oxford atlas [69]. Average connectivity strengths were calculated within and between regions of each network, yielding 21 within- and between-network functional connectivity features. Detailed rs-fMRI preprocessing steps are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

White matter microstructure feature set

A fifth feature set combined diffusion-based measures, including white matter fixel metrics and the diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index. Baseline DTI, reflecting white matter microstructure, has been shown to predict cognitive decline in patients with breast cancer [37]. Additionally, a higher DTI-ALPS index has predicted slower cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease [74].

Cognition-related white matter bundles were selected using the fun with tracts pipeline [75], which included the arcuate fasciculus, cingulum, prefrontal commissural of the corpus callosum, frontal aslant tract, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, inferior longitudinal fasciculus, and the superior longitudinal fasciculus. For each bundle, metrics of microstructural integrity (i.e. fiber density (FD), fiber cross-section (FC), and fiber density–cross-section (FDC)) were calculated (MRtrix3) [76]. Additionally, the average bilateral DTI-ALPS index was calculated [77] [78]. Details on the preprocessing of the dMRI and DTI-ALPS index have been described previously [40].

Random forest classifier

Random forest models were used to explore risk factors of cognitive decline due to their robustness with small datasets, capacity to capture non-linear relationships, and proven accuracy in patients with breast cancer [38]. To examine added predictive value, the clinical and psychosocial feature set was combined with each of the four remaining sets as input to the prediction model. The prediction pipeline (Fig. 1) consisted of five steps: (1) missing data imputation, (2) selection of the six most important predictors using recursive feature elimination, (3) hyperparameter tuning, (4) random forest classification, and (5) model interpretation with Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP).Fig. 1Workflow of the random forest prediction pipeline. The prediction pipeline was run in R (v4.3.2) using packages caret, ranger, fastshap, and h2o. Abbreviations: Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP).

Workflow of the random forest prediction pipeline. The prediction pipeline was run in R (v4.3.2) using packages caret, ranger, fastshap, and h2o. Abbreviations: Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP).

To limit model complexity and reduce the risk of overfitting, recursive feature selection was used to iteratively remove the least important features, retaining only the six predictors with the highest importance within each feature set. Only these six features were then used as input to the model. A final composite set was constructed by combining the top six predictors from all five sets, to characterize the most informative cross-modal features. Out-of-bag (OOB) error was used as an internal measure of prediction accuracy to compare performance between feature sets [79]. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC), as well as sensitivity and specificity values, providing measures of the model's ability to accurately classify patients who developed cognitive decline versus those who did not. SHAP values were used to interpret how much each feature contributed to the model's prediction for an individual patient. Mean absolute SHAP values across patients indicated feature importance [80]. All analyses were performed in R (v4.3.2), with methodological details provided in the supplementary materials.

Cognitive decline

Patients were classified into two cognitive groups: no decline (n = 34, 51%) or cognitive decline at one or both follow-up timepoints (n = 33, 49%). Cognitive decline was observed in 22 patients (33%) from baseline to T1 and in 23 patients (34%) from baseline to T2. Inflammatory and neural health markers feature set

The inflammatory and neural health markers feature set showed the highest prediction accuracy of 81%, with Cho and VEGF contributing most strongly to the model. SHAP values indicated that patients with higher levels of Cho, higher levels of VEGF, lower verbal IQ, higher PSS, higher CRP, and higher NAA/Cre were more likely to experience cognitive decline (Fig. 2B).

Brain volumes feature set

The brain volumes feature set showed a prediction accuracy of 79%, with the left superior parietal volume and verbal IQ contributing most strongly to the model. SHAP values indicated that patients with smaller left superior parietal and right accumbens volumes, lower verbal IQ, higher PSS scores and larger pars opercularis volumes bilaterally were more likely to experience cognitive decline (Fig. 2C).

Functional connectivity feature set

The functional connectivity feature set showed a prediction accuracy of 77%, with verbal IQ and PSS contributing most strongly to the model. SHAP values indicated that patients with a lower verbal IQ, higher PSS, weaker DMN-LANG and DA-HIP between-network connectivity, Her2-positive tumors, and those scheduled for targeted therapy were more likely to experience cognitive decline (Fig. 2D).

White matter microstructure feature set

The white matter diffusion feature set showed a prediction accuracy of 78%, with verbal IQ and PSS contributing most strongly to the model. SHAP values indicated that patients with a lower verbal IQ, higher PSS, lower FD in the left FAT and left IFOF, Her2-positive tumors, and those scheduled for targeted therapy were more likely to experience cognitive decline (Fig. 2E).

Final feature set

The final feature set showed a prediction accuracy of 80%, with verbal IQ and VEGF contributing most strongly to the model. SHAP values indicated that patients with a lower verbal IQ, higher levels of VEGF and Cho, smaller left superior parietal volume, higher PSS, and lower FD in the left IFOF were more likely to experience cognitive decline (Fig. 2F).

Discussion

To our knowledge, our study is the first to use multimodal data to predict cancer-related cognitive decline in patients with early breast cancer, providing novel insights into potential pre-treatment risk factors. Among the 67 patients included in our study, 33 (49%) experienced cognitive decline following treatment. Our prediction models showed accuracies ranging from 76% to 81%, with slightly higher estimates when MRI features and/or serum markers were included. Patients with aggressive subtypes, scheduled therapies, stress, and pre-existing cognitive or structural vulnerabilities were more likely to experience cognitive decline. Importantly, these features varied in their influence across individuals.

Tumor-related factors and inflammation

Patients with Her2-positive tumors were more likely to experience cognitive decline, consistent with prior evidence in older patients [25]. As Her2-positivity represents a more aggressive breast cancer subtype [81], this finding may reflect an association between tumor biology and increased vulnerability to cognitive decline. Higher levels of VEGF, a key mediator of tumor angiogenesis and progression, was likewise identified as a potential risk factor [82]. Together, these observations could indicate that more aggressive subtypes may be associated with an increased vulnerability for cognitive decline, which is potentially mediated by systemic inflammatory pathways. This is supported by our finding that higher levels of inflammatory CRP were associated with an increased risk, consistent with prior studies associating CRP at diagnosis with both objective and subjective cognitive decline [32] [83]. The predictive value of higher Cho levels, a MRS marker of increased membrane turnover and glial activation, may also align with a potential neuroinflammatory mechanism [84]. Collectively, these findings may align with the hypothesis that peripheral and neuroinflammatory responses at diagnosis may be associated with the risk for cancer-related cognitive decline.

Treatment-related factors

Importantly, tumor aggressiveness is inherently linked to treatment scheduling and intensity. In this study, most Her2-positive patients received trastuzumab, and targeted therapy itself emerged as a potential risk factor. Similarly, patients who received radiotherapy were more likely to cognitive decline, which is in line with prior work [85] [86] [87] [88]. Chemotherapy, however, was not retained in our models, despite being identified as an important risk factor in previous studies [26] [28]. As all patients receiving targeted therapy also underwent chemotherapy, this may reflect collinearity. Hence, a combined regimen, rather than chemotherapy alone, may be a stronger predictor of cognitive decline. Together, these findings highlight the multifactorial nature of cancer-related cognitive decline, potentially driven by both cancer subtype and treatment exposure.

Psychological stress and brain resilience

Psychological stress, which could be pre-existing or triggered by the diagnosis, consistently emerged as a predictor. Stress, when becoming chronic, may dysregulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, increase neuroendocrine load, and activate proinflammatory signaling, thereby adversely affecting brain vulnerability [89] [90]. Beyond stress, variability in cognitive and brain reserve may also contribute to differences in susceptibility. Patients with lower proxy verbal IQ, as indication of cognitive reserve, were more likely to experience cognitive decline, consistent with prior evidence linking lower education to increased susceptibility [26] [28]. Incorporating MRI modalities provided complementary insights into brain structure and function, of which some emerged as potential risk factors. Smaller regional brain volumes of the left superior parietal cortex and right accumbens area, regions linked to working memory and behavior [91] [92], were retained as predictors. Microstructural vulnerabilities were also evident: lower FD in the FAT and IFOF, suggesting lower axonal density [93], also showed associations with higher predicted risk. As these bundles support language and working memory [94] [95], their involvement in cognitive decline is plausible. This is consistent with a previous DTI study, suggesting that white-matter microstructure at baseline may be linked with later cognitive decline [37]. In addition, weaker functional connectivity between cognitive networks, including the DMN, LANG, DA, and HIP, were associated with an increased risk, consistent with prior evidence linking baseline DMN alterations to cognitive decline post-treatment [38]. These networks are linked to memory, attention, and higher cognitive functioning [96]. Together, these findings may reflect that patients with lower brain reserves lack required resilience, leaving them with fewer cognitive adaptation strategies and limited structural capacity to compensate for tumor- and treatment-related effects [29] [33].

Clinical implications and future directions

Beyond exploring specific risk factors for cognitive decline, our results suggest that the impact of the different predictors varied across patients, highlighting the potential for personalized interventions. For instance, the risk profile in some patients appeared to be primarily associated with psychological stress, suggesting mindfulness or physical exercise interventions may be beneficial [97] [98]. In others, lower cognitive reserve appeared to be more strongly associated, for whom targeted cognitive training may be more effective [99]. This heterogeneity emphasizes the potential value of multimodal data in clarifying how cognitive decline may develop. Although MRI modalities offered additional insights into the potential role of brain reserve, they only slightly improved predictive performance and discrimination metrics, suggesting limited diagnostic utility. In contrast, serum markers obtained through a simple blood draw provided a practical and clinically accessible means of identifying patients at risk [100]. We therefore encourage future studies to examine whether intervention effects vary depending on specific risk profiles, and to consider integrating multiple data modalities to enhance prediction, identify potential new risk factors, and evaluate the usefulness of measurements already collected in routine clinical care.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the limited sample size restricted generalizability and prevented us from distinguishing short- and long-term cognitive decline, as decline across follow-up timepoints was combined. Nevertheless, the comprehensive multimodal data collected in this focused breast cancer cohort remains an important strength of the study. Second, model accuracy was estimated using the OOB error without nested cross-validation, potentially inflating performance [101]. We therefore interpret OOB accuracy as an internal metric for comparing feature sets, not as a measure of absolute prediction error. Third, although recursive feature elimination reduces dimensionality by only retaining the top six predictors, the small sample and large feature sets still increase the risk of selecting cohort-specific rather than generalizable predictors. Fourth, the inclusion of patients aged ≤65 years with early-stage breast cancer may explain why age and disease stage did not emerge as predictors despite previous reports [25] [26] [27]. Importantly, risk profiles may differ in older patients or in those with more advanced disease. In addition, comorbidities and lifestyle-related factors, including sleep disturbances and physical activity, were not acquired and may represent additional relevant risk factors. Fifth, cognitive decline was defined as an RCI lower than −1.64 on at least two of the 18 tests. Although we adhered to the ICCTF guidelines by using the RCI [43], the choice of this threshold and the requirement of two tests remain relative. Finally, as the analysis used an existing longitudinal cohort, the sample size was predetermined and may not be optimal for the requirements of the current machine learning approach.

Conclusion

In summary, these findings may suggest that aggressive tumor subtypes, planned systemic therapies, stress-related responses, and pre-existing cognitive or structural vulnerability may predispose patients to experience cognitive decline after treatment for early breast cancer. Given the exploratory nature of this work, the identified risk factors require further validation in larger, multicentre cohorts. Recognizing these risk factors at diagnosis may enable early identification of high-risk patients and inform personalized prevention strategies within routine oncological care.
