Preparatory use of neurodynamics to enhance upper limb function in patients with acquired brain injury: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Federico Salniccia, Marina Castel-Sánchez, Alberto Bermejo-Franco, Almudena Buesa-Estéllez, Javier López-Ruíz, Vanesa Abuín-Porras
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent studies on neurodynamics have shown beneficial effects on the musculoskeletal system and peripheral nerve physiology. Hence, the application of neurodynamics in people who have suffered an acquired brain injury has the potential to improve their functionality to perform daily activities. This study aimed to assess the immediate effects of a single session of median nerve neurodynamics versus a placebo intervention on the affected upper limb (UL) in patients with acquired brain injury (ABI). A single-blind randomised clinical trial was conducted with 24 participants, divided into an experimental group (n = 12) and a control group (n = 12. Outcomes measures included grip strength (dynamometer), spasticity (Modified Ashworth Scale), pain (algometer and Visual Analogue Scale), range of motion (goniometer), and surface electromyography. Statistically significant improvements were observed in the experimental group in terms of reduced spasticity in the triceps and carpal flexors, and increased wrist flexion and extension range of motion. Additionally, modulation of muscle activity was detected in the triceps, biceps, and wrist flexors and extensors. In contrast, changes in grip strength and pain perception were not statistically significant. Although a downward trend in grip strength was noted in both groups, this observation should be interpreted cautiously and does not imply a therapeutic benefit without further functional corroboration. These findings suggest that neurodynamics may produce short-term neuromuscular effects in ABI patients, particularly in muscle tone and joint mobility. However, further research is needed to determine the clinical relevance and sustainability of these effects.Clinical trial registration number: Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04652934). Introduction

Acquired Brain Injury (ABI) is an injury to the brain that occurs after birth and is unrelated to congenital or degenerative disease, producing a range of consequences that may be temporary or permanent and cause partial disability, functional or psychosocial impairment, as described by the World Health Organisation (WHO)1. The most common cause is cerebrovascular accident (CVA), followed by traumatic brain injury (TBI), anoxia, brain infections and tumors2.ABI often results in long-term or permanent impairment that affects motor, cognitive and psychosocial functioning, significantly reducing quality of life and independence in activities of daily living.

Among the most disabling consequences of ABI are motor impairments, particularly in the upper limb (UL), which hinder functional use and limit participation in daily tasks3,4.Rehabilitation strategies for ABI aim to restore function a reduce disability through multidisciplinary approaches. Manual therapy techniques have proven effective in treating musculoskeletal and neurological, improving mobility, reducing pain and enhancing functionality5–7. Recent reviews have also highlighted the potential of neurodynamic techniques to influence neuromuscular function in both musculoskeletal and neurological populations, including post-stroke rehabilitation and lateral epicondylalgia, further supporting their clinical relevance and mechanistic plausibility8–11.

One such technique is neurodynamics (ND), which involves the mobilization of neural tissues to restore their mechanical and physiological properties12.Although the evidence on this technique is still scarce due to the small number of studies, their low methodological quality, and often small sample sizes, most studies demonstrate effects and benefits at different levels, from the central nervous system to the musculoskeletal system, including improvements in functionality and pain modulation without adverse effects for patients6,7,13.

This study introduces a novel approach by applying median nerve neurodynamics as a preparatory technique—rather than a therapeutic intervention per se—in patients with chronic ABI. The rationale is based on the hypothesis that ND may prime the neuromuscular system, facilitating subsequent motor performance by modulating muscle tone, improving joint mobility, and optimizing neural input. This preparatory use reflects real-world clinical practice, where therapists often employ manual techniques to enhance the effectiveness of active rehabilitation. This rationale is further supported by recent evidence demonstrating the utility of neurodynamic techniques in modulating neuromuscular function in post-stroke populations, including electromyographic outcomes, as well as their effectiveness in managing peripheral nerve-related conditions such as lateral epicondylitis10,11.

Furthermore, our study is among the first to assess the immediate neuromuscular effects of a single ND session in ABI, using surface electromyography (sEMG) during a functional reaching task. This provides insight into acute changes in muscle activation patterns, spasticity, and range of motion, contributing to the understanding of ND’s role in neurorehabilitation.

The aim of this randomized controlled trial was to compare the effects of median nerve neurodynamics versus a placebo intervention on upper limb function in patients with ABI. The primary outcomes included surface electromyography, grip strength, spasticity, range of motion and pain. We hypothesized that ND would produce measurable neuromuscular changes, supporting its use as a preparatory tool in clinical practice.

Methods

The research was approved by the ethics committee from Hospital Universitario Fundación Alcorcón, Madrid, Spain (number 20/154) and it was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. The study was registered in Clinicaltrials.govNCT04652934 (03/12/2020).

Trial design

A randomized controlled trial was conducted; according to CONSORT Guidelines14. A simple randomisation was carried out using GraphPad software. In this study the subjects were blinded, guaranteed by applying a placebo neurodynamic technique without the subject being aware of the real technique’s parameters. Blinding was also applied to the professional in charge of carrying out the measurements and to the statistician.

Selection and description of participants

Participants for this study were recruited from different clinics and patient associations in the Community of Madrid, Spain.

The inclusion criteria were mild or moderate ABI (due to traumatic or non-traumatic causes) in the chronic phase (evolution of more than 18 months), upper limb impairment with free active movement and cognitive level that allows for understanding orders using the Mini-Mental Test. The exclusion criteria were absence of voluntary movement in the upper limb, rigid contracture that does not allow passive joint movements, medication for spasticity and botulinum toxin infiltration in the last six months.

All enrolled participants voluntarily participated and provided written informed consent.

Interventions

Experimental group

Participants in the experimental group underwent the Upper Limb Neurodynamic Test (ULNT1) technique described by Butler on the affected limb15. This technique consisted of the professional performing a shoulder depression, 90º shoulder abduction (ABD), hand and wrist in a neutral position, external shoulder rotation, and elbow extension. After positioning the limb in this position, wrist flexion-extension movements were performed gently and rhythmically, with 20 movements every minute marked by a metronome, for 3 min three times, with 1 min of rest between each series.

Placebo group

The protocol used by Beneciuk et al.16 was applied to the placebo group. This intervention was specifically designed to mimic the therapist–patient interaction and movement parameters of the experimental intervention without applying mechanical stress to the neural tissues. While it involves passive joint movement and proprioceptive input, it does not induce the neural tension required to elicit neurodynamic effects. This approach aimed to control for non-specific effects such as therapist contact, attention, and sensory stimulation, while minimizing the likelihood of true neuromodulatory responses. It was performed as follows: a cervical neutral position was maintained (0° cervical tilt), 45° shoulder ABD without shoulder depression, 45° shoulder external rotation combined with 45° elbow flexion with the forearm in pronation. Then, ten wrist flexion-extension movements were performed at a rate of 6 s per cycle (3 s of flexion and 3 s of extension) marked by a metronome. The resistance noted marked when to change movements. Once the ten cycles of movements were completed, wrist flexion was maintained for 10 s.

Outcomes

Surface electromyography

Surface electromyography activity was recorded using the DelsysTrigno™ Research + Wireless System with Avanti sensors, operatingat a sampling rate of 1000 Hz and a band-pass filter between 3–460 Hz.For signal processing, we used The EMGworks Analysis software from Delsy. First, the signal was analysed using power spectral density. Then, a bandpass filter was applied based on the data analyzed in the previous step, and the absolute values were obtained. Electromyographic data were collected during a standardized functional reaching task, as described in previous studies by Cuesta-Gomez et al.17,18, to enhance ecological validity and assess muscle activation in a task-relevant context.

To ensure intra-subject comparability and reduce inter-session variability, EMG signals were normalized using the mean absolute value of each muscle’s activation during the task, recorded within the same session. Root Mean Square (RMS) values were automatically computed by the Delsys software and expressed as a percentage of normalized activation. This approach was selected based on recommendations for within-session comparisons in populations with neurological impairments19.

Electrode placementfollowed the indications of Surface Electromyography for the Non-Invasive Assessment of Muscles (SENIAM)20, the study by De Luca et al.21and manufacturer recommendations. Prior to electrode application, the skin was cleaned with alcohol and shaved if necessary to minimize impedance and signal artifacts. Electrodes were positioned over the midpoint of the muscle belly, aligned with the direction of the muscle fibers, and secured using double-sided adhesive interfaces.

The following muscles were assessed on the affected upper limb: trapezius, pectoralis major, triceps brachii, biceps brachii, flexor carpi, extensor carpi, and thenar eminence. The same anatomical landmarks were used for both EMG and algometry measurements to ensure consistency across outcome domains (Fig. 2).

Setup of the surface electromyography sensors.

Grip strength

A portable hydraulic dynamometer (Jamar Plus; Sammons Preston, Rolyon, Bolingbrook, IL) was used to measure hand grip strength. This type of instrument was chosen because it is portable and there are no significant differences between fixed and portable instruments22.

The measurement protocol was adapted from the instructions recommended in the study by Roberts et al.23 to suit the objectives of our study and the profile of our subjects.

Spasticity

The Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) was used to measure spasticity. It is a tool often used in the clinic to monitor changes in muscle tone in patients who have suffered nervous system damage and present with upper motor neuron syndrome and in clinical trials24.

The scale is administered manually and does not require instruments. To perform the test, the patient was lying supine and relaxed. The joint was brought to maximum counter range, and a full range passive movement was performed in one second. The resistance felt during the movement was scored from normal (0) to extreme hypertonia (4), with increasing levels of muscle stiffness and resistance to passive movement24,25.

The following muscles of the affected limb were measured: pectoralis major, biceps brachii, triceps brachii, wrist extensor, wrist flexor, extensor digitorum, flexor digitorum, and thenar eminence.

Range of motion

A universal goniometer (AnthroFlex) was used to measure changes at the joint level. Three measurements were taken for each movement, and the average of the three was taken as the ROM result26.

The joints measured were shoulder flexion, shoulder external rotation, extension elbow, flexion wrist, extension wrist.

Pain

An analogue algometer [Wagner Pain Test™ Model FPK Algometeris] was used to assess pressure pain to better objectify these measurements by having greater inter- and intra-rater reliability27. The patient was positioned in the supine position. The measurement point was the muscle belly of the muscles to be assessed, which were marked with a surgical marker. These muscles were: trapezius, pectoralis major, triceps brachii, biceps brachii, wrist extensor, wrist flexor, thenar eminence.

At this stage, the algometer was meticulously placed and continuous, constant pressure was maintained until the patient felt pain. Three measurements were taken, and the average of the three was used28. In addition to measuring pain on pressure, the pain face and the visual analogue scale were used to assess the subjects’ pain perception29.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated for the interaction of a two-group repeated measures design with the G*Power program (81). To achieve a power of 0.80 (β = 0.20) with a medium effect size (ηp2 = 0.06) and an α of 0.05, 24 subjects were required.

Statistical methods

IBM SPSS V.29 and Jamovi V.2.3 were used for data analysis. The choice between parametric and non-parametric tests was based on the distributional properties of the data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. When assumptions for parametric testing were met, mixed repeated-measures ANOVA (MR ANOVA) was applied. For variables that violated these assumptions or were ordinal in nature (e.g., spasticity scores from the Modified Ashworth Scale), non-parametric tests were used: the Mann–Whitney U test for between-group comparisons and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for within-group comparisons. This approach ensured appropriate statistical treatment of the data while maintaining analytical rigor. Each dependent variable was sent to a MR ANOVA. In the post hoc analyses, each group’s evolution was analyzed separately, and the significance level was adjusted using Bonferroni. The contrast of means between groups was performed with the Mann–Whitney U test, and the Wilcoxon W test for related measures was performed on each group separately.

The effect size of all contrasts was expressed as partial eta squared (ηp2), with reference values of 0.01, 0.06 and 0.14 for small, medium and large, respectively. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 for all analyses, and power (1-β) = 0.80.

Results

No statistically significant differences were observed between the control and experimental groups at baseline regarding demographic and clinical variables. Specifically, both groups were comparable in terms of age, gender, diagnosis, affected side, time since injury, spasticity, and range of motion (all p > 0.05) (Table 1). Although most participants had suffered a stroke, individuals with traumatic brain injury (TBI) were also included, as both etiologies fall under the ABI definition and share similar motor impairments and rehabilitation needs.

No common pattern is observed between muscles or between measurements (minimum, mean and maximum) in terms of electromyographic activations. It appears that the mean electromyographic values are slightly lower after the intervention, with a main effect observed in triceps and biceps. It was found that the experimental group significantly reduced the mean activation in the biceps and its minimum activation in the carpal extensor. Finally, two different evolutions were found depending on the group, in the minimums of the carpal extensor muscle and in the mean values of the carpal flexor (Fig. 3). After a comparison of all muscles simultaneously, by computing post–pre differences for each participant and entering these into a MANOVA, no multiple group effect was found, neither for minimum (p = 0.153), mean (p = 0.687) or maximum (p = 0.690).

Grip strength

Strengths between 14 and 16 kg were recorded. Grip strength scores were reduced after the intervention, as shown by the main effect of time, F (1,22) = 38.52, p = 0.012, ηp2 = 0.25. Post hoc analysis was close to significance in reduction in both groups (p = 0.068 for placebo and p = 0.051 for experimental). No effect was found for the interaction between group and time, F (1,22) = 0.04, p = 0.581, ηp2 < 0.01; nor for the main effect of group, F (1,22) = 0.02, p = 0.878, ηp2 < 0.01.

Algometry and pain intensity

All the algometry variables showed that both groups evolved in a similar way, remaining at the same values they had. The only remarkable thing was that the experimental group showed higher values than the placebo throughout the study.

VAS values for the muscles analyzed were between 4 and 6 points. Both groups behaved in a similar and constant manner throughout the intervention, and no main effect or interaction effect was found. It should be noted only that the placebo group did significantly reduce pain in the trapezius (p = 0.032) and pectoralis (p = 0.026) and pectoralis muscles after the intervention.

Discussion

The aim of the study was to compare the effects of median nerve neurodynamics versus placebo treatment on the affected UL patients with ABI in a single application.

Surface electromyography

Although the electromyographic changes observed were generally modest and variable across participants, some statistically significant effects were detected—specifically, a reduction in mean activation of the biceps brachii and minimum activation of the carpal extensor in the experimental group. These findings suggest a potential neuromuscular modulation effect following the neurodynamic intervention. The term “modulation” is used here to describe directional changes in muscle activity—either increases or decreases—rather than uniform or robust effects across all muscles. These findings are consistent with recent literature emphasizing the role of neurodynamic techniques in modulating neuromuscular activity, including studies that combine EMG analysis with neural mobilization in post-stroke populations10, as well as systematic reviews supporting their effectiveness in peripheral nerve-related conditions such as lateral epicondylitis11.

This variability is not unexpected given the heterogeneity of the ABI population and the exploratory nature of the study. Moreover, the EMG data were collected during a functional reaching task, which adds ecological relevance but also introduces variability due to task complexity and compensatory strategies. Previous studies have reported similar inconsistencies in EMG responses following neurodynamic techniques in neurological populations8,9,30. For patients with ABI it may be more difficult because of altered muscle synergies that vary according to the severity of the lesion31. Also, co-contraction is a strategy of the nervous system to give stability to body parts when they are performing a certain movement thanks to the simultaneous contraction of opposing or antagonistic muscles. But in patients with ABI, activation is increased32,33 as a compensation to increase the stability of the limbs, increasing the stiffness of their joints34. In patients it involves greater energy expenditure by the structures implicated and the tissues and cells that compose them35–38.

The relevance of EMG modulation following a single neurodynamic session must be interpreted within the broader context of neurorehabilitation. While EMG changes alone do not equate to functional recovery, they may reflect short-term neuromuscular responsiveness to manual stimuli, which could be leveraged to enhance subsequent motor training. This concept aligns with the emerging use of manual techniques as priming strategies, aimed at preparing the neuromuscular system for active rehabilitation. In this study, the use of a functional reaching task during EMG acquisition adds ecological validity, allowing us to observe muscle activation in a task-relevant context. However, given the modest and variable nature of the changes observed, these findings should be considered exploratory. Future research should investigate whether repeated neurodynamic sessions can produce cumulative EMG adaptations and whether such changes correlate with improvements in functional performance and patient-reported outcomes.

These preliminary results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future research should consider larger sample sizes, repeated sessions, and refined EMG normalization protocols (e.g., maximal voluntary contraction or task-specific baselines) to better characterize the neuromuscular effects of neurodynamics in individuals with ABI.

Grip strength

Related to grip strength, patients with hemiparesis seem to show lower grip strength than healthy people39. In the present study, a tendency to a decrease in maximal grip strength was observed in both the experimental and placebo groups, although not statistically significantly. In other research, a relationship between the application of this technique and an improvement in strength is also not found13,40,41. In the context of brain injury, however, the results of the present study have a positive interpretation, if one considers the grip reflex that these patients usually have42,43. It has been observed that this reflex, considered pathological, is associated with giving greater stability when a certain precision is required when performing a manual task44. Having an altered strength that depends on a reflex instead of a command sent by the cerebral cortex influences the dexterity for the performance of some activities of daily living(ADLs)45 such as grasping or releasing an object like a glass; a movement that was used to study muscle activity in the present study. Therefore, a decrease in grip strength could translate to a regulation of grip strength, decreasing the energy expenditure to perform the task and adapting the strength to the weight of the object; in this case picking up an empty plastic cup. This interpretation remains speculative and should be viewed with caution, as the observed decrease in grip strength did not reach statistical significance and lacks functional corroboration. Future studies are needed to explore whether such changes reflect improved motor regulation or reduced pathological grasp reflexes.

Although a reduction in grip strength was observed in both the experimental and placebo groups, this change did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, it should not be interpreted as a confirmed therapeutic benefit. However, we propose a hypothesis-generating interpretation: in individuals with acquired brain injury, excessive grip force is often associated with pathological grasp reflexes or compensatory co-contraction strategies. A slight decrease in grip strength may reflect a neuromuscular modulation effect, potentially indicating a reduction in abnormal muscle tone or improved motor regulation. This interpretation is speculative and must be viewed with caution, as it is not supported by statistically significant evidence. Future studies with larger samples and functional assessments are needed to explore this possibility further and determine whether such changes translate into meaningful improvements in hand function.

Spasticity

In terms of spasticity, a reduction in spasticity was observed in all the muscles measured, being statistically significant for the triceps brachii in the experimental group and for the carpal flexor in both groups. These findings have been studied in other studies where the subjects had similar characteristics. In the study by Saxena et al. (2021) statistically significant differences were found to reduce spasticity in wrist and finger flexor muscles using neurodynamics for 4 weeks46, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. Regarding the reduction of spasticity in carpal flexors, it can be attributed to two reasons. The first, due to its innervations; the median nerve47. When mobilized, the physiology changes and for this reason the connection with the spastic muscle could affect its tone. The second is the movement received by the structures of the UL, in this case the wrist joint. Here mobilization is not limited to the nerve, but also to the soft tissue structures related to the whole UL. Therefore, a mobilization of muscle tissue would help regulate tone, thus reducing spasticity. This would explain the changes in the placebo group, which also receives a mobilization that, by modifying some of its parameters such as reducing the positioning angles of the UL joints, simulates the neurodynamic technique described by Beneciuk et al. (2009)16. However, none of the studies found to date have measured triceps brachii spasticity, the present study being the first to analyze this variable, to the best of our knowledge. Considering that the triceps is not innervated by the median nerve, the reduction in spasticity could be attributed to the phenomenon of reciprocal inhibition. When a limb is subjected to active or passive movement, a decrease in the spinal reflex is recorded, indicating inhibition of the antagonist musculature48–50.

In contrast, the reduction in finger flexor spasticity—present in both groups—may reflect the influence of passive mobilization and proprioceptive input, as the placebo intervention also involved joint movement. These results highlight the localized and potentially mechanism-specific effects of neurodynamic techniques. However, the absence of significant changes in other muscle groups underscores the need for cautious interpretation and supports the exploratory nature of this study. Future research should include larger samples and stratified analyses to better delineate the neuromuscular pathways involved and to confirm the reproducibility of these preliminary findings.

Range of motion

Relating to ROM, no significant changes were observed in the shoulder and elbow. These results coincide with those of the systematic review carried out by González-Matilla et al. (2022) where it is concluded that ND does not produce significant changes in the lower limbs (LL) in patients with brain damage6. However, changes have been observed in subjects belonging to another type of population51, as well as in patients with cervical radiculopathy52 and postmastectomy53. However, in our study we did find significant improvements in the ROM of wrist flexion and extension movements in the experimental group. In addition to changes in UL, an increase in cervical rotation has been documented after applying neurodynamic techniques in patients with cervical problems7. The benefits of ND are not limited only to the upper extremities or upper body. Improvements in hip and knee ROM have been reported in both healthy individuals54,55. The effects of this technique have also been studied in people suffering from low back pain related to peripheral neural sensitization, finding positive results in the mobility of the same joints mentioned above56 and through mobilization of the cervical spine, improving neck mobility57. The results obtained suggest that ND can have a positive impact on range of motion in several joints of the body and can be used in clinical practice so that patients can perform wider movements that allow them to better manage in their environment and consequently improve their performance in activities of daily living as well as their quality of life.

Although some of the changes observed in this study were statistically significant, it is essential to consider their clinical relevance. In particular, the improvements in wrist range of motion (ROM) in the experimental group—approximately 9° in extension and 10° in flexion—approach or meet the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) reported in similar populations. While specific MCID thresholds for wrist ROM in patients with acquired brain injury (ABI) are not well established, studies in populations with cervical radiculopathy and post-surgical upper limb dysfunction suggest that changes of 10° to 15° may be considered clinically meaningful (Rafiq et al., 2022; Joshi et al., 2023). These improvements, though modest, may contribute to enhanced performance in functional tasks such as reaching, grasping, or manipulating objects—activities that are often compromised in individuals with chronic motor impairments. Future studies should incorporate validated functional outcome measures and patient-reported outcomes to better assess the real-world impact of neurodynamic interventions.

Pain

The absence of statistically significant pain reduction in the experimental group suggests that a single session of median nerve neurodynamics may not be sufficient to elicit an immediate analgesic effect in individuals with chronic ABI. This contrasts with previous studies reporting pain relief following neurodynamic interventions, which typically involved multiple sessions over several weeks and often included additional therapeutic modalities. Therefore, the lack of effect in our study may be attributed to the limited dosage and the preparatory nature of the intervention.Studies that have used ND as an analgesic technique have consistently reported significant improvements in the reduction of pain in patients with diverse pathologies such as cervical pain, epicondylalgia, lumbar pain and even in those who did not present symptoms, the pain threshold increased6,13.

Interestingly, the placebo group demonstrated significant reductions in perceived pain in the trapezius and pectoralis major muscles. This may reflect a contextual or placebo response, which is well-documented in manual therapy research and can be influenced by therapist interaction, patient expectations, and the sensory experience of passive movement. In fact, in the study by Beneciuk et al. (2009) using the ND placebo technique, they also found beneficial effects for pain reduction in the placebo group16. These findings underscore the importance of considering non-specific effects when interpreting outcomes in manual therapy trials.

Notably, our study utilized a single session of neurodynamics, whereas other studies that reported pain improvement administered treatment three days a week for two weeks, including additional interventions58. This suggests that the frequency and combination of treatments may play a crucial role in achieving significant pain reduction. In line with this, other authors suggest that the number of sessions administered may explain the differing findings59.

Future research should explore the cumulative effects of repeated neurodynamic sessions and assess their potential role in pain modulation when integrated into broader rehabilitation programs. Additionally, including patient-reported outcomes related to pain interference and quality of life may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the clinical relevance of these interventions.

In conclusion, this exploratory study suggests that a single session of median nerve neurodynamics may produce short-term neuromuscular effects in patients with chronic ABI, particularly in terms of reducing spasticity in the triceps and carpal flexors and improving wrist range of motion. These findings were statistically significant and may support the use of neurodynamics as a preparatory technique in neurorehabilitation. Other observed changes, such as trends in grip strength and electromyographic activity, did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution. Although a downward trend in grip strength was noted, it did not reach statistical significance and is therefore considered a hypothesis-generating observation rather than a confirmed benefit. Overall, the findings provide preliminary evidence that neurodynamic techniques may influence neuromuscular parameters in ABI populations. However, future studies with larger sample sizes, repeated intervention sessions, functional outcome measures, and patient-reported outcomes are necessary to determine the clinical relevance, sustainability, and real-world applicability of these effects.

Limitations

This study acknowledges certain limitations. One important limitation of this study is the small sample size (n = 24), which reduces statistical power and limits the generalizability of the findings. However, the sample size was determined a priori using G*Power software based on a medium effect size and a power of 0.80, consistent with recommendations for exploratory studies. This study was designed as a preliminary investigation to assess the feasibility and immediate neuromuscular effects of a single session neurodynamic intervention in patients with ABI. This approach aimed to assess the technique’s effectiveness as a routine clinical tool for tissue preparation before subsequent physiotherapy treatments. Consequently, the study did not explore potential cumulative effects from repeated applications and did not include patient follow-up. Future investigations may address this by incorporating follow-up periods to evaluate sustained effects. Lastly, beyond the median nerve, including the ulnar and radial nerve mobilization, could provide insights into varied outcomes based on the parameters measured in this study in future research. Additionally, although the placebo intervention was carefully designed to avoid neural tissue stress, it involved passive joint movement and proprioceptive input, which may have elicited some therapeutic effects. This represents a common challenge in manual therapy research, where creating a truly inert placebo is difficult. Future studies may consider alternative control conditions or include additional physiological markers to better isolate the specific effects of neurodynamic mobilization. Furthermore, this study did not include functional outcome measures such as task-specific performance or activities of daily living (ADL) scales. This decision was based on the nature of the intervention—a single session of neurodynamics applied as a preparatory technique rather than a therapeutic intervention per se. Given the short timeframe and the exploratory scope of the study, we did not expect to observe meaningful changes in functional capacity. Future studies involving repeated sessions or integration with active rehabilitation protocols should incorporate validated functional and patient-reported outcomes to better assess the clinical relevance and ecological validity of neurodynamic techniques in patients with acquired brain injury.

Additionally, the inclusion criteria limited the sample to individuals with chronic ABI who retained sufficient cognitive and motor function to participate in the intervention and assessments. While this approach enhanced internal validity and ensured the feasibility of the protocol, it also narrows the generalizability of the findings. The results may not be directly applicable to patients with more severe impairments, aphasia, or those in the acute or subacute phases of recovery. Future studies should explore the effects of neurodynamic techniques in broader ABI populations to better understand their applicability across different clinical profiles.
