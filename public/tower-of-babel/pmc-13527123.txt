Nebivolol as a potential adjunct therapy for sepsis-induced cardiac dysfunction: evidence from a rat model
Authors: Ruveyda Ummugulsum Unal, Onural Ozhan, Elif Sude Balkaya, Azibe Yildiz, Feyzi Dogru, Mehmet Ertugrul Balkar, Ayse Maden, Ayse Yucekaya, Zeynep Kucukakcali, Hakan Parlakpinar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis frequently leads to cardiac dysfunction driven by oxidative stress (OS), inflammation, and conduction abnormalities. Nebivolol (NEB), a selective β1-blocker with vasodilatory and antioxidant properties, has shown organ-protective effects in sepsis, but its cardiovascular role remains unclear. This study investigated the effects of NEB in cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced sepsis in rats. Thirty-two rats were assigned to four groups: Sham, NEB, CLP, and NEB + CLP. Hemodynamic parameters, ECG findings, cardiac and vascular OS markers, serum biochemistry, and histopathology were evaluated. CLP caused tachycardia, conduction disturbances, increased OS, and marked myocardial and aortic injury. NEB reduced heart rate, improved PR interval, and partially ameliorated oxidative and histological alterations. However, NEB did not improve blood pressure, myocardial edema, or aortic injury. NEB offered localized protection against sepsis-induced myocardial injury by modulating oxidative and inflammatory pathways. While these biochemical and histopathological improvements were significant, they were not accompanied by changes in systemic blood pressure or a complete reversal of all structural alterations during the acute phase. These findings support further investigation of NEB as a potential adjunctive therapy in sepsis.

Introduction

Sepsis is a severe inflammatory condition characterized by an excessive innate immune response to infection, leading to multi-organ involvement and associated with high mortality1. It is estimated that 48.9 million cases and 11 million deaths worldwide are associated with sepsis, accounting for almost 20% of all global deaths2. Early recognition of sepsis symptoms, prompt initiation and empirical antimicrobial therapy, timely reporting of culture results, effective eradication of the infection source, and adequate organ support are modifiable factors that can significantly influence patient outcomes3. Severe sepsis can lead to cardiac dysfunction, which manifests as decreased cardiac index and ejection fraction (EF), diastolic dysfunction, and impaired contractility4,5. The pathophysiology of sepsis is still unknown, although it likely causes reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are released by neutrophils and endothelial cells and can harm tumors or healthy cells indiscriminately. Overproduction of ROS depletes the body’s natural antioxidant system and triggers oxidative stress (OS), immunosuppression, inflammation, and apoptotic pathways6.

Septic shock is characterized not only by systemic inflammation but also by profound vasodilation and decreased vascular tone, leading to a relative insufficiency of cardiac output rather than an absolute reduction7. Despite normal or even increased cardiac output in some cases, the mismatch between oxygen delivery and tissue demand contributes to organ dysfunction8. This hemodynamic pattern is often accompanied by excessive sympathetic activation and persistent tachycardia, which may further impair cardiac efficiency and increase myocardial oxygen consumption9. In this context, β-blockers have been proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy to control excessive adrenergic stimulation, improve cardiac efficiency, and modulate the dysregulated cardiovascular response observed in sepsis.

β-adrenergic blockade has been shown to improve cardiac function, maintain cardiac output, and reduce inflammatory cytokines in sepsis models and some clinical studies. These effects include improved cardiac efficiency and reduced heart rate (HR) without compromising organ perfusion, suggesting a cardioprotective role for β-blockers in sepsis10. β1-adrenoceptor blockade has demonstrated cardioprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in animal models of sepsis. Pre-treatment with β1-blockers before septic insult improved survival, reduced proinflammatory cytokines, and preserved cardiac function in rats. However, starting blockade after sepsis onset did not improve survival, though it did stabilize hemodynamics and restore cardiac contractility11.

Despite these potential benefits, the use of β-blockers in sepsis remains controversial. Excessive β-adrenergic blockade may interfere with compensatory sympathetic responses that are essential for maintaining cardiac output and tissue perfusion, particularly in the early phase of sepsis12. In this context, inappropriate timing or dosing of β-blockers may lead to hemodynamic instability and impaired organ perfusion10. Moreover, some clinical studies have reported unfavorable outcomes associated with β-blocker administration under certain conditions, including reduced survival rates13. These findings highlight that the effects of β-blockers in sepsis are highly context-dependent and may vary according to the timing of administration, disease severity, and underlying cardiovascular status.

Recent animal studies indicate that nebivolol (NEB), a β1-blocker with vasodilatory and antioxidant properties, markedly improves sepsis-related kidney injury by attenuating OS, inflammatory responses, and apoptotic processes. While these studies focus on renal outcomes, the mechanisms—antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and endothelial-protective—are relevant to cardiac protection as well14. NEB has shown superior effects compared to other β-blockers in improving endothelial function, vascular remodeling, and right heart function in pulmonary hypertension models, indicating its potential to positively influence hemodynamics and cardiac performance15,16. NEB administration in rats with cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced sepsis improved kidney function and reduced renal OS markers. Additionally, it modulated key molecular pathways involved in fibrosis and apoptosis and improved histopathological kidney damage, indicating strong nephroprotective effects in this sepsis model17. In CLP-induced sepsis, NEB significantly reversed liver injury. It lowered hepatic OS, reduced inflammatory mediators, and modulated apoptosis and regeneration pathways. Histological analysis confirmed that NEB mitigated liver tissue damage and inflammation, supporting its hepatoprotective role18. There is currently no direct experimental or clinical evidence specifically demonstrating NEB’s effect on cardiac hemodynamics in sepsis17. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the potential cardioprotective effects of NEB in a rat model of sepsis.

Materials and methods

Study design

All experimental procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Inonu University Faculty of Medicine (Protocol no.: 2023/5-7). All methods were carried out in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and in compliance with relevant institutional and national regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals19. A total of 32 male Wistar albino rats, aged 19 weeks and weighing 313–423 g, were obtained from the Inonu University Laboratory Animal Research Center (n = 8 per group). Rats were 19 weeks old at the beginning of the experiment, representing young adult animals with fully developed cardiovascular and immune systems, which provides stable baseline physiology and reduces age-related variability in sepsis responses. The rats were housed at 21 ± 2 °C, 60% ± 5% humidity, 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and fed ad libitum with available tap water and a standard pellet diet. The NEB (Vasoxen® 10 mg, Menarini, Italy) was diluted with distilled water (weight/volume) before being given orally by gavage to ensure precise and consistent dosing. The sample size in this study was planned a priori in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the 3R principles (replacement, reduction, and refinement)19. The sample size calculation was based on previously reported differences in OS markers and histopathological injury scores in CLP-induced sepsis models, as these parameters constituted the primary endpoints of the present study18. Given that direct cardiac functional measurements were not included in the study design, OS biomarkers and tissue injury scores were considered the most appropriate variables for estimating effect size and determining the required sample size. An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) for a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, fixed effects, omnibus F test) with 4 groups. Based on previously published CLP-induced sepsis studies reporting large between-group differences in OS biomarkers and histopathological injury scores, we assumed a large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.70), with α = 0.05 and power (1–β) = 0.80. Under these assumptions, the minimum required total sample size was N ≈ 28 rats (≈ 7 per group). To account for potential attrition due to perioperative mortality or technically unusable samples, the planned sample size was increased to 32 rats (8 per group), which exceeds this minimum. For the same ANOVA model (4 groups, α = 0.05), a total sample size of N = 32 yields an achieved power of approximately 0.89 (df_between = 3, df_within = 28) for detecting an effect size of f = 0.70.

Animals were assigned to the four experimental groups using simple randomization based on a computer-generated random number list to ensure unbiased group allocation.

In the sham group, a midline abdominal incision of approximately 1 cm was made to expose the cecum and adjacent intestinal segments. After replacing the cecum into the peritoneal cavity, the abdominal wall was closed with 4 − 0 silk sutures. Each rat in this group received 1 mL of distilled water orally once daily for 10 consecutive days.

The NEB group was administered NEB orally by gavage at a dose of 10 mg/kg once daily for 10 consecutive days. On the 10th day, the animals underwent the same surgical procedure as described for the sham group.

In the CLP group, a midline abdominal incision of about 1 cm was made to expose the cecum and adjacent intestinal loops (Fig. 1a and b). The cecum was ligated underneath the ileocecal valve (Fig. 1c). The cecal base was punctured twice on the same side using a 22-gauge needle (Fig. 1d). Gentle pressure was then applied to extrude a small amount of fecal content from the perforation site (Fig. 1e). The cecum was repositioned into the abdominal cavity, and the peritoneum was closed with 4 − 0 silk sutures (Fig. 1f). The CLP model was established following the procedure described by Kose et al. and Ozhan et al., with minor modifications20,21. For fluid resuscitation, 1 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride (physiological saline) was administered subcutaneously. Each rat in this group received 1 mL of distilled water orally once daily for 10 consecutive days.

The NEB + CLP group received NEB orally at a dose of 10 mg/kg once daily for 10 consecutive days. On the 10th day, the animals were subjected to the same surgical procedure as described for the CLP group.

Although the overall study timeline spanned 11 days due to the 10-day NEB pre-treatment period, all outcome assessments were performed 24 h after CLP induction, corresponding to the acute phase typically evaluated in CLP-based sepsis models (Fig. 1). All outcome assessments were performed 24 h after CLP induction to capture the acute phase of sepsis, during which inflammatory responses, OS, and early organ injury are most prominent. The 24-hour time point is commonly used in CLP-induced sepsis models and allows for standardized comparison with previous studies22. In addition, this time frame enables the evaluation of early hemodynamic, biochemical, and histopathological changes before the onset of severe late-stage complications and increased mortality.

NEB was administered at a dose of 10 mg/kg based on previous experimental study demonstrating its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and organ-protective effects in rat models23. This dose has been reported to effectively modulate OS and endothelial function without causing significant hemodynamic instability24. Furthermore, the selected dose was considered sufficient to achieve systemic pharmacological activity of NEB, including β1-adrenergic blockade and nitric oxide–mediated vasodilatory effects25. Pre-treatment protocols are widely used in sepsis models to investigate the prophylactic or organ-protective potential of experimental agents, particularly β1-blockers, which have demonstrated greater efficacy when administered prior to the septic insult12. Humane endpoints were defined in line with internationally accepted ethical standards. Following CLP, animals were inspected at 4–6 h intervals for indicators of severe distress, such as hypothermia, reduced activity, respiratory difficulty, or lack of response to external stimuli. The development of sepsis was confirmed through daily clinical assessment, with typical manifestations—including lethargy, piloerection, decreased locomotion, and reduced food intake—appearing consistently within 6–12 h after surgery. On day 11, anesthesia was induced with 75 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride (Ketasol 10%, Richter Pharma AG, Wels, Austria) and 8 mg/kg xylazine (Xylazinbio 2%, Bioveta, Ivanovice na Hané, Czech Republic) intraperitoneal injection. Hemodynamic assessments were conducted in anesthetized rats using invasive carotid artery cannulation, while a three-lead electrocardiography (ECG) setup was employed to monitor the electrical activity of the heart. Body temperature was maintained throughout anesthesia with a rectal heating probe. HR, blood pressure (BP), and ECG parameters were continuously recorded for three minutes using the BIOPAC MP-100 system26. PR, QRS, and QT intervals were subsequently analyzed in milliseconds (ms) in accordance with the Lambeth Convention guidelines27. Animals displaying these criteria were euthanized prior to reaching the experimental endpoint. Euthanasia was carried out under deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal administration of ketamine combined with xylazine followed by exsanguination through cardiac puncture. Intracardiac blood samples were obtained, and the heart together with the descending aorta were excised during autopsy. The excised tissues were sectioned symmetrically for subsequent biochemical and histopathological evaluations. All blood and tissue samples were appropriately packaged and stored at -80 °C in the Cardiovascular Pharmacology Laboratory until biochemical analyses were carried out. All investigators responsible for ECG analysis, biochemical measurements, and histopathological evaluations were blinded to the group allocations to prevent observer bias.

Biochemical analyses

Tissue biochemistry

When the analyses began, the tissues were rinsed by immersing them in a beaker containing Tris-HCl. Their weights were then measured and recorded. Tissues placed in glass tubes were homogenized for one minute (min) using an IKA-WERKE T 25 B device after adding pH 7.4 Tris-HCl buffer. Homogenization was completed by adding a small amount of buffer solution and homogenizing for one minute. A portion of the resulting homogenate was set aside for analysis. The remaining homogenate was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 45 min at + 4 °C in a Hettich D 78,532 refrigerated centrifuge. The clear supernatant was separated for analysis. The relevant spectrophotometric readings were performed using SHIMADZU UV-160 A and BIOTEK SYNERGY LX multi-mode reader devices.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) measurement

MDA was measured according to the method of Uchiyama and Mihara28. The method is based on the combination of MDA, a lipid peroxidation product, with thiobarbituric acid at 95 °C. After applying the procedures to the standards and homogenate, the n-butanol phase separated in the tubes belonging to the samples was read with a spectrophotometer at 535 and 520 nm wavelengths, and the difference was recorded. Results were expressed as nmol/g tissue.

Glutathione (GSH) measurement

The GSH concentration in the homogenate was measured spectrophotometrically using the Ellman method29. Each homogenate sample was mixed with 10 mM 5,5-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) and 17.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). The reaction was initiated by adding 0.5 units of glutathione reductase and 0.4 mM nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). After 5 min, the absorbance of the samples was measured at 410 nm, and the GSH concentration was calculated according to a standard curve. The results were expressed as µmol/g tissue.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity measurement

SOD activity was determined using the method of Sun et al.30. The SOD activity test is based on the principle of inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium chloride (NBT) reduction. SOD activity is inversely proportional to the absorbance value of formazan at 560 nm. Results were calculated in U/g protein.

Protein quantification

Protein quantity analysis was performed using the modified Lowry method to calculate the data for the other markers studied31. Folin reagent was added to the alkaline copper-protein solution containing the samples and vortexed. This application ensured that the reduction reaction occurred before the folin reagent decomposed. The standards and samples were read at a wavelength of 750 nm in a spectrophotometer. The results were calculated according to the standard graph obtained and expressed in µg/mL.

Measurement of catalase (CAT) activity

CAT activity was determined according to Aebi’s method32. The test principle is based on determining the rate constant (k, 1/s) or the hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) decomposition rate at a wavelength of 240 nm. Decomposition begins with the addition of supernatant to the H₂O₂ solution, and the change is monitored in a spectrophotometer. This change was monitored for a specific period, and the activity and rate constant were calculated based on the absorbance change. Results were reported as K/g protein.

Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity measurement

GPx activity was measured using the Paglia and Valentine method33. An enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding H₂O₂ to tubes incubated with supernatant added to a tube containing NADPH, reduced glutathione, sodium azide, and glutathione reductase. This reaction was monitored at 340 nm wavelength using a spectrophotometer for a specific period of time. The activity calculated from the observed absorbance change during this process was given as U/mg protein.

Calculation of the oxidative stress index (OSI) using total antioxidant status (TAS) and total oxidant status (TOS) measurements

TAS and TOS measurements were performed using specially developed Rel Assay Diagnostics (MEGA TIP San. Tic. Ltd. Sti.) brand test kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions, and calculated as specified in the manual.

The TAS measurement was determined in the supernatant using the Erel method34. The method is based on the oxidation of the 2,2’-azinobis-3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS) molecule to the ABTS⁺ molecule in the presence of H2O2. In this measurement method, the ABTS radical is used. The ABTS radical loses its blue and green color depending on the antioxidant amount and antioxidant capacity. This color change is measured at a wavelength of 660 nm for evaluation. There is an inverse relationship between the color change and the antioxidant amount in the sample. The reaction rate is adjusted using Trolox, a well-known method. The unit is mmol Trolox equivalent/L. TOS was measured colorimetrically according to the Erel method using the supernatant obtained35. The method is based on the oxidation of the ferrous ion-o-dianisidine complex of oxidants to ferric ions. The presence of glycerol molecule in the reaction medium is necessary for oxidation to occur. In acidic medium, ferric ions form a colored complex with xylenol orange chromogen. At a wavelength of 530 nm, the intensity of this complex is measured with a spectrophotometer. The unit is µmol H2O2 equivalent /L.

The OSI was calculated by dividing the TOS by the TAS. The unit is the arbitrary unit (AU)36.

Serum biochemistry

Whole blood was collected via cardiac puncture under deep anesthesia. For hematology, samples were placed into K2-EDTA tubes and analyzed within 2 h at room temperature. For serum biochemistry, blood in plain tubes was allowed to clot (20–30 min), centrifuged (≈ 3000 g, 10 min), and analyzed the same day; if analysis was delayed, aliquots were stored at − 80 °C and thawed once.

Serum concentrations of albumin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and glucose were determined at Inonu University Turgut Özal Medical Center. Analyses were performed using an automated chemistry analyzer (e.g., Roche Cobas c501, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) employing enzymatic colorimetric and spectrophotometric methods according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Lymphocyte count, lymphocyte percentage, monocyte count, and monocyte percentage were obtained from an automated hematology analyzer using impedance and/or flow-cytometric scatter for leukocyte differentials. Absolute counts were derived from the instrument’s total white blood cells multiplied by differential percentages. Daily calibration verification and internal controls (low/normal/high) were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histopathological analyses

At the end of the experiment, the heart and vascular tissues were fixed in 10% formaldehyde. Sections 4, 5 μm thick were taken from the paraffin blocks prepared after tissue tracking. The sections were stained using the hematoxylin-eosin (H-E) staining method to determine the general morphological structure. Evaluations were performed using a Leica DFC-280 research microscope and the Leica Q Win Image Analysis System (Leica Micros Imaging Solutions Ltd., Cambridge, UK). Tissue sections were evaluated for edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, and cardiac/vascular muscle cell degeneration (dense eosinophilic cytoplasm, pyknotic nucleus). Ten randomly selected areas were examined in each section and scored according to the degree of histological changes: 0: no change, 1: mild, 2: moderate, 3: severe change17,37.

Statistical analyses

IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. The distributional characteristics of quantitative variables were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and supported by visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. For variables that satisfied the normality assumption, intergroup comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Tukey HSD test when homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) was met, or the Tamhane T2 test when this assumption was violated.

For variables that did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used to assess overall group differences, followed by Mann–Whitney U tests for pairwise comparisons. Given that multiple biomarkers were tested simultaneously, the risk of Type I error inflation was addressed by applying a multiple-comparison adjustment. Accordingly, post-hoc p-values derived from pairwise tests were corrected using the Bonferroni method, and adjusted p-values were used.

Quantitative data were presented as median (minimum–maximum; interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables and as arithmetic mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables. A p-value < 0.05 (two-tailed) was accepted as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

The study was designed to evaluate acute outcomes at 24 h after CLP; therefore, survival analysis was not included as a predefined endpoint. Two deaths occurred in the NEB + CLP group, most likely attributable to sepsis-related systemic deterioration, as no findings suggestive of NEB toxicity were observed.

Hemodynamics

The HR value of the sham group was found to be statistically significantly higher in comparison to the NEB group (p < 0.05). The HR value of the NEB group was statistically significantly lower than that of the CLP group (p < 0.05). The HR value of the CLP group was found to be significantly higher than that of the NEB + CLP group (p < 0.05). There was no statistically significant difference between the groups when the systolic, diastolic, and mean BP values of the rats were compared (p ≥ 0.05). The PR value of the sham group was significantly lower than that of the NEB + CLP group (p < 0.05). There was a significant reduction in the PR value of the CLP group in comparison to the NEB + CLP group (p < 0.05). The PR value of the NEB group was significantly higher than that of the NEB + CLP group (p < 0.05). The QT value of the sham group was significantly lower than that of the CLP and NEB + CLP groups (p < 0.05). When the QRS intervals of the rats were compared, there was no statistically significant difference between the groups (p ≥ 0.05).

Biochemistry

Biochemical analyses of heart tissue

Biochemical parameters related to OS in cardiac tissue are presented in Table 3; Fig. 5. There was no statistically significant difference in MDA and GSH values among the groups (p ≥ 0.05). SOD activity was significantly lower in the NEB group compared with the sham group (p < 0.05)and significantly lower in the CLP group compared with both the sham and NEB groups (p < 0.05). CAT and GPx activities were also significantly reduced in the NEB group compared with the sham group (p < 0.05), while the CLP group showed further reductions compared with both sham and NEB groups (p < 0.05). TOS and OSI levels were significantly elevated in the NEB group compared with the sham group (p < 0.05), and further increased in the CLP group compared with both sham and NEB groups (p < 0.05). TAS levels did not differ significantly among the groups (p ≥ 0.05).

Discussion

A key feature of severe sepsis is cardiac dysfunction, characterized by impaired contractility, diastolic dysfunction, and reduced EF4,5. This condition is driven partly by excessive ROS production, which depletes antioxidant defenses and induces OS, subsequently triggering inflammation, immunosuppression, and apoptosis6. Histopathological analyses reveal significant myocardial damage, including cell swelling, fiber fragmentation, inflammatory infiltration, and apoptosis in cardiac tissue following CLP. These changes are accompanied by impaired cardiac function38,39. Concurrent vascular dysfunction, marked by altered NO and VEGF levels, indicates endothelial injury and impaired reactivity40,41. CLP-induced sepsis leads to a substantial increase in OS within cardiac and vascular tissues. This is evidenced by elevated levels of MDA, myeloperoxidase, and 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine, which are established markers of lipid peroxidation and DNA oxidative damage. Concurrently, antioxidant defenses such as SOD, CAT, and GSH are significantly reduced, further exacerbating oxidative injury38,39,41. Our data align with certain aspects of this model, as the CLP group exhibited significant cardiac and vascular tissue damage alongside elevated OS markers (e.g., increased MDA and OSI in the aorta, decreased SOD, CAT, and GPx in heart tissue). Notably, some OS parameters exhibited inconsistencies. While SOD, CAT, GPx, TOS, and OSI were significantly altered, cardiac MDA and GSH levels showed no statistical differences between groups. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in the sensitivity and temporal responsiveness of individual OS markers, as well as the complex and dynamic nature of oxidative processes in sepsis42.

Clinical and animal studies demonstrate that β-blockers (e.g., esmolol and landiolol) reduce sepsis-induced tachycardia, improve cardiac function, and lower mortality after initial resuscitation. These agents may also reduce inflammatory cytokine production and protect against further cardiomyocyte damage. Most clinical trials and meta-analyses focus on ultrashort-acting β-blockers like esmolol and landiolol10,43. Previous studies suggest that NEB may exert its protective effects through multiple pathways, including reduction of NADPH oxidase–derived ROS, improvement of nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) coupling, and modulation of inflammatory and apoptotic signaling24,44,45. The present study demonstrates significant protective outcomes; nonetheless, further mechanism research is required to definitively link these improvements to the specific signaling pathways described in previous literature. In non-sepsis models, NEB has demonstrated protective effects against doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity in rats, reducing myocyte distortion, inflammatory cell infiltration, and markers of apoptosis and inflammation46. In isolated rat aortic rings, NEB protected against ROS-induced endothelial damage, likely through direct ROS scavenging47. In endothelial cell studies, NEB reduced ROS and superoxide production, preserving NO levels even under OS48. NEB also inhibited NADPH oxidase activity and prevented NO synthase uncoupling in hyperlipidemic animal models, further reducing vascular OS44. Although the exact mechanism was not directly investigated in this study, previous research suggests that NEB may enhance antioxidant defenses through multiple pathways. These include reducing NADPH oxidase–derived ROS, improving eNOS coupling, and increasing endogenous antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, CAT, and GPx44,49,50. NEB’s ability to reduce the oxidative burden may contribute to the secondary activation and preservation of key antioxidant enzymes. Interestingly, the NEB-only group demonstrated reductions in certain antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, and GPx) compared to the sham group. This finding may reflect a context-dependent effect of NEB under physiological conditions, where a lower oxidative burden could lead to reduced activation of endogenous antioxidant defense systems. In other words, decreased enzyme activity in the absence of significant OS does not necessarily indicate a detrimental effect, but may instead represent a reduced need for antioxidant response24,42. Nevertheless, this observation should be interpreted cautiously and warrants further investigation. Taken together, these findings suggest that the antioxidant effects of NEB are not uniform across all parameters and should be interpreted within the context of specific biomarkers rather than as a generalized reduction in OS.

The present study demonstrates that NEB administration demonstrates potential cardioprotective effects in a rat model of sepsis induced by CLP. Accordingly, the term “cardioprotection” in the present study should be interpreted in the context of structural and biochemical preservation rather than direct functional improvement. Our findings show that NEB partially mitigated sepsis-induced tachycardia, attenuated histopathological damage to cardiac tissue (specifically reducing inflammatory cell infiltration and cardiomyocyte degeneration), and ameliorated OS markers in the aorta. These results provide experimental evidence suggesting that NEB may influence cardiac and vascular responses in CLP-induced sepsis.

The rationale for investigating β-blockers in sepsis stems from evidence that β-adrenergic blockade can improve cardiac efficiency, stabilize hemodynamics, and reduce inflammatory cytokines10. Specifically, β1-adrenoceptor blockade has been shown in animal models to improve survival when administered prophylactically, preserve cardiac function, and even attenuate sepsis-induced immunosuppression by protecting CD4 T-cell function11,51. Our results align with these findings. The NEB + CLP group showed a significantly lower HR compared to the untreated CLP group, indicating a restoration of autonomic balance and reduced cardiac workload. This hemodynamic stabilization occurred without compromising BP, suggesting that NEB’s effects were partially cardioprotective rather than detrimental to perfusion.

Most of the clinical evidence regarding β-blocker use in sepsis has been obtained with ultrashort-acting β1-selective agents such as esmolol and landiolol43. These agents enhance cardiac efficiency by mitigating excessive adrenergic stimulation without impairing tissue perfusion, particularly in patients with post-resuscitation tachycardia52. In contrast, NEB has a different pharmacological profile. In addition to β1-selective blockade, NEB exhibits vasodilatory, endothelial-protective, and antioxidant properties, which may provide additional benefits in the setting of sepsis-induced oxidative and vascular injury53. However, this pharmacological distinction should be interpreted cautiously. Unlike esmolol and landiolol, which have been evaluated in clinical sepsis settings primarily as rate-controlling agents, NEB has not been sufficiently studied in septic patients. Therefore, the present findings do not support direct clinical extrapolation or superiority over other β-blockers. Rather, they suggest that NEB may have partial and parameter-specific protective effects in a preclinical CLP model, warranting further investigation in therapeutic and clinically relevant study designs.

The unique advantage of NEB over conventional β-blockers lies in its ancillary properties. In addition to being a highly selective β1-blocker, NEB is known to stimulate endothelial NO synthase, promoting vasodilation and improving endothelial function15,16. This is particularly relevant in sepsis, where endothelial dysfunction is a hallmark. Our study extends this understanding by showing that NEB’s benefits are not limited to the vasculature. The significant reduction in aortic MDA levels and OSI, coupled with the restoration of TAS in the NEB + CLP group, indicates a potent systemic antioxidant effect. This is consistent with prior studies in the same CLP model, where NEB was shown to ameliorate kidney and liver dysfunction by reducing OS, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and modulating apoptotic pathways like transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)/Smad/p53 and protein kinase B (AKT)/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)14,18,45. The mechanisms identified in those studies—antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic—are directly applicable to the cardioprotective effects observed here. Although biochemical and histopathological findings indicate vascular injury, functional assessments were not performed. These include in vivo dose–response testing and ex vivo aortic ring experiments. Therefore, conclusions regarding vascular reactivity should be interpreted with caution.

In the early phase after ischemia-reperfusion, myocardial edema is primarily due to increased vascular permeability and cardiomyocyte swelling, even before significant inflammatory cell infiltration occurs. This edema is detectable by imaging and is associated with disintegration of myofilaments and mitochondrial damage in cardiomyocytes54. Infiltration of leukocytes, especially neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages, typically increases after the initial edema phase, peaking between 1 and 7 days post-injury. These cells contribute to further tissue damage, cytokine release, and remodeling54–56. Degeneration is marked by pyknotic nuclei, myofibril disintegration, and cell death. This is observed in both acute injury (e.g., sepsis, ischemia-reperfusion) and chronic inflammatory cardiomyopathies, where ongoing inflammation and fibrosis lead to progressive cardiomyocyte loss57–59. NEB administration led to a marked reduction in myocardial apoptosis and degeneration, as evidenced by decreased TUNEL-positive cardiomyocytes and improved histological appearance after myocardial infarction60. Histopathological findings support the effects of NEB. The CLP group showed severe myocardial edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, and cardiomyocyte degeneration. In contrast, the NEB + CLP group demonstrated reduced infiltration and degeneration. These findings suggest that NEB may protect cardiomyocytes from sepsis-induced damage. This effect may be related to modulation of OS, inflammation, and apoptosis. However, persistent edema in the NEB + CLP group suggests that while NEB effectively targets cellular inflammation and death, its impact on vascular permeability or fluid dynamics remains limited during acute sepsis.

Despite the observed beneficial effects of NEB on certain parameters, the overall findings indicate only partial protection. Notably, NEB did not improve blood pressure, myocardial edema, or aortic histopathological injury. These results suggest that the effects of NEB are selective and do not extend uniformly across all aspects of sepsis-induced cardiovascular dysfunction. Therefore, the term “cardioprotection” should be interpreted cautiously and within the context of limited and parameter-specific improvements. Although NEB reduced HR and improved several OS parameters and selected cardiac histological findings, it did not ameliorate arterial BP, myocardial edema, or aortic tissue injury. Therefore, the protective effects observed in this study should be interpreted as partial cardioprotection, likely mediated through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways rather than widespread hemodynamic or structural improvement. These effects are likely associated with modulation of OS and related cellular processes; however, the precise molecular mechanisms were not directly investigated and remain to be elucidated in future studies.

The serum biochemistry results further corroborate the systemic protective role of NEB. The CLP-induced drop in serum albumin was reversed in the NEB + CLP group. Similarly, the dramatic elevations in liver enzymes (AST, ALT) and the depletion of lymphocytes in the CLP group, which are indicators of multi-organ stress and immunosuppression, were not fully normalized by NEB but were consistent with its known organ-protective profile from previous studies18,45. Interestingly, while serum AST and ALT levels remained elevated in both CLP and NEB + CLP groups, indicating persistent hepatocellular injury, serum albumin levels were significantly higher in the NEB + CLP group compared to the untreated CLP group. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by the complex regulation of albumin in sepsis. In addition to reflecting hepatic synthetic function, albumin levels are influenced by systemic inflammation, capillary leakage, and fluid shifts.

The partial restoration of albumin levels in the NEB-treated group may suggest a modulatory effect on vascular permeability or systemic inflammatory responses rather than a direct protective effect on hepatocellular injury. Therefore, these findings indicate that the effects of NEB on systemic biochemical parameters are selective and do not uniformly reflect organ-specific protection.

The present study has several important limitations. First, direct assessment of microcirculatory function and comprehensive cardiac performance was not performed. Sepsis is characterized by profound microcirculatory dysfunction contributing to tissue hypoxia and organ failure61. However, parameters such as echocardiography, ejection fraction, fractional shortening, or cardiac output were not evaluated. Although HR and ECG parameters provide information on cardiac electrical activity, they do not fully reflect cardiac performance. Therefore, the observed effects of NEB should be interpreted cautiously, as they are based on indirect indicators, including OS markers and histopathological findings.

Second, the study was designed as a prophylactic model, with NEB administered for 10 consecutive days prior to CLP induction. This approach does not fully reflect clinical practice, where treatment is initiated after the onset of sepsis, thereby limiting translational applicability.

Third, mechanistic insight at the molecular level remains limited. Although OS markers (TOS, TAS, OSI, SOD, CAT, and GPx) and histological findings provide valuable information, no direct assessment of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), apoptosis-related markers (e.g., caspase-3 or TUNEL), or signaling pathways (e.g., eNOS-related mechanisms) was performed. Accordingly, the mechanistic interpretations of NEB’s effects should be considered indirect and exploratory.

Fourth, another limitation of the present study is the absence of survival analysis. Although mortality is a critical outcome in sepsis research, the current study was designed to assess acute biochemical, hemodynamic, and histopathological changes at a single time point (24 h after CLP). Therefore, the effects of NEB on survival could not be evaluated.

Finally, despite the vasodilatory properties of NEB and the vasoplegic nature of sepsis, no significant differences in systolic, diastolic, or mean arterial pressure were observed at 24 h after CLP. The use of a moderate sepsis model and the associated fluid resuscitation protocol must be taken into account when interpreting these hemodynamic results.

Conclusion

This study provides experimental evidence suggesting that NEB may exert partial protective effects against sepsis-induced cardiac injury. These effects appear to be mediated through modulation of HR, reduction of OS, and attenuation of histopathological damage. However, since direct functional cardiac parameters were not assessed, these findings should be interpreted as indicative of indirect cardioprotective potential rather than definitive improvement in cardiac function. Its benefits are mediated through a combination of hemodynamic stabilization (reducing tachycardia), potent antioxidant activity (ameliorating OS in vascular tissue), and direct cytoprotection (reducing histopathological damage to the heart). These findings, coupled with its established safety profile in treating hypertension and heart failure62, position NEB as a promising candidate for adjunctive therapy in sepsis. However, it is crucial to note that all current evidence, including this study, comes from animal models14,18,45. However, NEB did not improve blood pressure, myocardial edema, or aortic histopathological changes. Therefore, the overall findings should be interpreted as indicative of partial rather than definitive cardioprotection. It should also be noted that NEB was administered prior to CLP induction, reflecting a prophylactic experimental design. Therefore, the potential clinical applicability of these findings remains limited, and the effects of NEB in a therapeutic setting after the onset of sepsis require further investigation. The safety and efficacy of NEB in human septic patients remain untested, and clinical trials are needed to translate these promising preclinical results into clinical practice.
