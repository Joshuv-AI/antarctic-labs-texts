Abstract
In the spanning-tree congestion problem ( STC ), given a graph G G , the objective is to compute a spanning tree of G G for which the maximum edge congestion is minimized. While STC is known to be ℕ ​ ℙ \mathbb{NP} -hard, even for some restricted graph classes, several key questions regarding its computational complexity remain open, and we address some of these in our paper. (i) For graphs of maximum degree Δ \Delta , it is known that STC is ℕ ​ ℙ \mathbb{NP} -hard when Δ ≥ 8 \Delta\geq 8 . We provide a complete resolution of this variant, by showing that STC remains ℕ ​ ℙ \mathbb{NP} -hard for each degree bound Δ ≥ 3 \Delta\geq 3 . (ii) In the decision version of STC , given an integer K K , the goal is to determine whether the congestion of G G is at most K K . We prove that this variant is polynomial-time solvable for K K -edge-connected graphs.

Introduction

Constructing spanning trees for graphs under specific constraints is a well-studied problem in graph theory and algorithmics.
In this paper, we focus on
the spanning-tree congestion problem (
STC
)
, that arises naturally in some network design and routing problems.
The problem can be viewed as a special case of the graph sparsification problem where a graph
G
is
embedded into its spanning tree
T
by mapping each edge
(x,y)
of
G
to the unique
x
-to-
y
path in
T
.
The congestion of an edge
e\in T
is defined as the number of edges of
G

whose corresponding path in
T
traverses
e
, and the congestion of
T
is the maximum congestion of its edges.
In the
STC
problem, we are given a graph
G
and the objective is to compute
a spanning tree with minimum congestion. This minimum congestion value is referred to as the
spanning-tree congestion of

G

and denoted by
\mathrm{stc}(G)
.

The concept of spanning-tree congestion was introduced under different names in the late
1990s
[
1
,
30
,
28
,
13
]
,
and in 2003 formalized by Ostrovskii
[
25
]
, who established some key properties.
The problem has been extensively studied since then, and
numerous results regarding its graph-theoretic properties and computational complexity
have been reported in the literature. Below we review briefly those that are most relevant to our work.

STC
is
\mathbb{NP}
-hard, with the first
\mathbb{NP}
-hardness proof given by Löwenstein
[
22
]
in 2010.
It remains
\mathbb{NP}
-hard for planar graphs
[
26
]
, chain graphs and split graphs
[
24
]
.
On the other hand,
STC
is polynomial-time solvable for a wide variety of special graph classes, including
complete
k
-partite graphs, two-dimensional tori
[
16
]
, outerplanar graphs
[
3
]
,
two-dimensional Hamming graphs
[
17
]
, co-chain graphs
[
11
]
, and interval graphs
[
21
]
.

In the decision version of
STC
, in addition to a graph
G
we are also given an integer
K
, and the goal is to determine if
\mathrm{stc}(G)\leq K
.
A natural variant of
STC
, when the congestion parameter
K
is a fixed constant (rather than given as input) is denoted
{K\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}
.
The
{K\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}
problem was shown to be
\mathbb{NP}
-complete for
K\geq 5
by
Luu and Chrobak
[
23
]
, building on earlier results
for larger constants
[
26
,
2
]
.
On the other hand,
{K\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}
is solvable in linear time for
K\leq 3

[
26
]
.
The complexity status of
{4\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}
remains an intriguing open problem.
For graphs of radius
2
,
{K\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}
is
\mathbb{NP}
-complete for
K\geq 6

[
23
]
,
and its complexity is open for
K=4,5
.
For any constant
K
,
{K\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}
is linear-time solvable for bounded-degree graphs, bounded-treewidth graphs, apex-minor-free graphs
[
2
]
,
and chordal graphs
[
27
]
.

Kolman
[
14
]
observed that the existing
\mathbb{NP}
-hardness proofs used graphs
of unbounded degree, and raised the question about the complexity of
STC

for graphs of constant degree. For constant-degree graphs, it has only been known
that
{K\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}
is linear-time solvable if the congestion bound
K
is also constant
[
2
]
.
Recently, Lampis et al.
[
18
]

reported progress on this problem, by proving that,
for any constant
\Delta\geq 8
,
STC
is
\mathbb{NP}
-hard for graphs with maximum degree
\Delta
,
leaving open the complexity of
STC
for degree bounds between
3
and
7
.

Our contributions.

Addressing the problem left open by Lampis et al.
[
18
]
,
we prove (see Section
3
) the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Problem
STC
is
\mathbb{NP}
-hard for graphs of maximum degree at most
3
.

Naturally, this theorem is true for all degree bounds
\Delta\geq 3
, and it remains true for
3
-regular graphs.
(Vertices of degree
1
or
2
can be removed from the graph, without affecting its maximum congestion value.)
This result fully resolves the status of
STC
for bounded-degree graphs.

We also study the
{K\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}
problem for
K
-edge-connected graphs (see Section
5
),
proving the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2
.

There is an
{\tilde{O}}(m)
-time algorithm that,
given a
K
-edge-connected graph
G
, determines whether

\mathrm{stc}(G)=K
.

Above,
m
is the number of edges, and
the
{\tilde{O}}(m)
time bound is actually independent of
K
.
Our solution is based on the so-called cactus representation of
K
-cuts in
K
-edge-connected graphs
that was developed by Dinic et al.
[
6
]
(see also
[
9
]
).
We further refine this characterization for graphs with congestion
K
, to obtain additional properties that lead to
a fast algorithm.
Besides its own interest, this result sheds new light on the complexity of
{4\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}
,
showing that its difficulty is related to the presence of cuts of size less than
4
in the graph.

Other related work.

General bounds for the spanning-tree congestion value have been well studied.
For graphs with
n
vertices and
m
edges it is known that the congestion is at most
O(\sqrt{mn})

and that there are graphs where this value is
\Omega(\sqrt{mn})

[
5
]
.
Since the congestion of an
n
-clique is
n-1
, this implies that, somewhat counter-intuitively, the congestion value is
not monotone: adding edges can actually decrease the congestion, and quite substantially so.

This non-monotonicity is particularly challenging in the context of approximations. Indeed, very little is known
about the approximability of
STC
. While the upper bound of
n/2
on the approximation ratio
is trivial (achieved by
any
spanning tree
[
27
]
), the best known lower bound is only
1.2
,
implied directly by the
\mathbb{NP}
-completess of
{5\textrm{-}\textsf{STC}}

[
23
]
.
In a recent work, Kolman
[
15
]
developed
an algorithm with approximation ratio
{\tilde{O}}(\Delta)
, where
\Delta
is the maximum vertex degree.
Yet the general problem remains open; in particular it is not known if it is possible to achieve ratio
O(n^{\delta})
, for some
\delta<1
.

The spanning-tree congestion is related to the tree spanner problem which seeks a spanning tree with minimum stretch factor.
The two problems are in fact equivalent in the case of planar graphs: the spanning-tree congestion of a planar graph
is equal to the minimum stretch factor of its dual plus one
[
8
,
26
]

(cf.
[
4
,
8
,
7
]
and the references therein for
further discussion). It is worth mentioning here that
the complexity status of the tree 3-spanner problem has remained open since its introduction in 2014
[
4
]
.

The
STC
problem can be relaxed by dropping the restriction that the tree to be computed
is a spanning tree of the graph. In this version, the tree
must include all vertices, but its edges do not need to be present in the underlying graph. This variant
arises in the context of multi-commodity tree-based routing
[
29
,
13
]
,
and appears to be computationally easier that
STC
, as it admits an
O(\log n)
approximation.

Readers interested in learning more about the
STC
problem are referred to
the survey by Otachi
[
27
]
that covers the state-of-the-art as of 2020, and
to the recent paper by Lampis et al.
[
18
]
that has
additional information about some recent work, in particular about the parametrized complexity of
STC
.

Throughout the paper, by

we denote an undirected graph with

For a vertex

For a set of edges

.

Recall that a connected graph

For any subset

of vertices, by

(or
{\partial{\bar{X}}}
) we denote the set of

and
{\bar{X}}=V\setminus X
, and we call it a

.
We refer to

and
{\bar{X}}
as the

of cut
{\partial X}
.
If

, we say that
{\partial X}
is a
K
-cut
,
and if
|X|=1
or

then we call cut

trivial
.

If

is a spanning tree of

and

is an edge of

from

disconnects

into two

of

, we denote the

and the component not

(we interpret these as sets of vertices of

The cut

(So

are its two shores.)
Its cardinality

is called the

and is
denoted by

\mathrm{cng}_{T}(e)

is understood from context.
The
congestion of tree

The minimum value of

.
It is easy to see that this definition, expressed in terms of induced cuts, is

\mathbb{NP}
-Hardness for Degree-
3
Graphs

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
.
Our proof is via a polynomial-time reduction from an
\mathbb{NP}
-complete
version of SAT (defined below), mapping a boolean expression
\phi

into a graph
G
and integer
K
, such that
\mathrm{stc}(G)\leq K
if and only if
\phi
is satisfiable.

G
consists of multiple
gadget

as well as one additional
root gadget
, with appropriate connections in and between these gadgets.
Some gadgets are constructed from smaller
sub-gadgets
.
The most basic gadget is called a
double-weight gadget
, and it allows us to use
edges that are assigned two weight values, with appropriate interpretation.
Using double-weighted edges, we construct a more complex
flower gadget

that will be used as the root gadget and as the gadgets for some clauses.
The restriction to degree
3
makes these constructions quite intricate.
A considerably simpler proof for graphs of degree at most
4
can be found in Appendix
A
.

Problem
(M2P1N)-SAT
.

This is an
\mathbb{NP}
-complete restriction of SAT
[
23
]
,
whose instance is a boolean expression in conjunctive normal form with the following properties:

(i) each clause contains either three positive literals (3P-clause), or two positive literals (2P-clause), or two negative literals (2N-clause), and

(ii) each variable appears exactly three times, exactly once in each type of clause,
and any two clauses share at most one variable.

We use the following conventions: letter
\phi
is an instance of
(M2P1N)-SAT
,
boolean variables are denoted with Latin letters
x,y,z
, while for clauses we use Greek letters

\kappa
,
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
and
\pi
. Typically,

\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
denote a 2N-clause, 3P-clause and 2P-clause, respectively,

\kappa
is a clause of any type, and
\pi
is a positive clause.
For a variable
x
, by the
2N-clause of
x
we mean the unique
2N-clause that contains the negative literal of
x
. Similarly, the
2P-
and

are the unique 2P- and 3P-clauses, respectively, that contain the positive literal of
x

Double weights.

In the graph we construct from an instance of
(M2P1N)-SAT

we will need the notion of double-weighted edges, introduced by Luu and Chrobak
[
23
]
.
Consider a pair of weight functions
\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{1}},\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{2}}:E\to{\left\{{1,...,M}\right\}}
,
where
M
is a positive integer whose value is polynomial in

Given a spanning tree
T
and an edge
e={\left({u,v}\right)}\in T

weighted congestion
of

\mathrm{cng}_{G,T}(e)\;=\;\textstyle\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{2}}(e)+\sum_{e^{\prime}\in{\partial T_{e}}\setminus{\left\{{e}\right\}}}\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{1}}(e^{\prime})\ .

The definitions of
\mathrm{cng}(G,T)
and
\mathrm{stc}(G)
extend naturally to double-weighted graphs.
We also define the
weighted degree
of

to be
\text{deg}_{G}(v)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{1}}(u,v)
.
(Note that it depends only on weight function
\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{1}}

As long as the meaning is clear from context, we will often drop the word

and
write simply

When

(a,b)=(\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{1}}(e),\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{2}}(e))

We will only use weight functions such that

so one can think of

An important intuition is that the edges in

contribute their heavy weight to their induced cuts, while other edges
contribute their light weight to the cuts they belong to. This is why using double-weight edges gives us more control over the
structure of trees with optimal congestion, thus greatly simplifying the construction of our target graph.

An edge

with
\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{1}}(e)=\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{2}}(e)
can be replaced by

As shown by Luu and Chrobak
[

]

an edge with weight
a\!:\!b
(under some mild assumptions) can be replaced by an appropriate

so that the resulting graph

has the property that
\mathrm{stc}(G)\leq K
if and only if
\mathrm{stc}(G^{\prime})\leq K
.
In their construction,
the maximum degree of

becomes large if

Lampis et al.
[
18
]
provide a low-degree double-weight
gadget, but it is not sufficient for our purpose (because it contains vertices of degree

).

The construction of our degree-

double-weight gadget

, where

represent a double weight

,
is given in Appendix
B
.
There, we also prove (see Lemma
B.1
) that for

and

,

has the following property:
if the maximum degree in

•

For each 2P-clause

By inspection, all vertices in this construction have degree 3, and

The number of edges

Any edge belonging to a variable, clause, or root gadget

.
We identify flowers by the corresponding clause (or root); for example by

.
(Similar terminology applies to flower terminals and centers.) For the sake of uniformity,

.
In the proof it will be convenient to occasionally work in the auxiliary multi-graph

given by contracting all internal edges of

,
with each flower and variable gadget contracted to a single vertex, for which we use the same notation as the gadget itself.
We extend this convention to the edges in

: for example a root-clause edge
(t_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{R},1},t_{\mathcal{R}}^{\alpha,1})
in

in

between

and

Correctness.

We now need to prove that our construction is correct, namely that it satisfies the condition
(\ast)
. We prove the two
implications in
(\ast)
separately.

(
\Rightarrow
)
Given a satisfying assignment for
\phi
, we convert it into a spanning tree
T
for
G
as follows:
(1) for each gadget, add a spanning-tree of unweighted edges for this gadget to
T
(one always exists for flowers and 4-cycles),
(2) add all root-variable edges,
(3) for each clause
\kappa
, pick any (exactly one) variable
x
whose assignment satisfies
\kappa

and add the clause-variable edge from
\kappa
to
x
to
T
.

It is easy to see that
T
is a spanning tree for
G
. Furthermore, we can obtain a spanning tree
T^{*}
for
G^{*}
by contracting the internal edges in
G
, and every clause is a leaf in
T^{*}
. We now show that
\mathrm{cng}_{G,T}(e)\leq K
for all
e\in T
.

When
e\in T
is unweighted, we trivially have
\mathrm{cng}_{G,T}(e)\leq|E|\leq K
. Otherwise
e={\left({x,t_{\mathcal{R}}^{x}}\right)}\in T
is an external root-variable edge,
in which case the vertices for any given gadget will be together on one shore of the cut induced by
e
, implying
\mathrm{cng}_{G,T}(e)=\mathrm{cng}_{G^{*},T^{*}}(x,\mathcal{R})
.
Thus, it suffices to deal with congestion in
G^{*}
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
be the 2N-clause, 3P-clause, and 2P-clause containing
x
respectively.
One shore of the cut induced by
(x,\mathcal{R})
is given either by
(i)
{\left\{{x}\right\}}
,
(ii)
{\left\{{\kappa,x}\right\}}
for
\kappa\in{\left\{{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\right\}}
, or
(iii)
{\left\{{x,\beta,\gamma}\right\}}
.
In case (i),
\mathrm{cng}_{G^{*},T^{*}}(x,\mathcal{R})=K-5+\text{deg}_{G^{*}}(x)-1\leq K-2
.
In case (ii),
\mathrm{cng}_{G^{*},T^{*}}(x,\mathcal{R})=K-5+3+2\leq K
, since
\kappa
is incident to at most 3 edges in the cut and
x
is incident to 2 edges besides
(\mathcal{R},x)
.
In case (iii),

\mathrm{cng}_{G^{*},T^{*}}(x,\mathcal{R})=K-5+2+2+1=K
, since both positive clauses are incident to 2 cut edges while
x
is incident to 1 besides
(\mathcal{R},x)
.

(
\Leftarrow
)
Given a spanning tree
T
for
G
with
\mathrm{cng}(G,T)\leq K
, our goal is to construct a satisfying assignment for
\phi
. Ideally,

would have a form similar to the spanning tree described in the
\Rightarrow
direction, but this may not be the case.
Nevertheless,

has enough structure to define a variable assignment.

First, we give a suitable definition of

between clause-gadgets and variable-gadgets that will be used to define our boolean assignment.
For a clause
\kappa
containing variable

, a
traversal from
\kappa
to

is a path in
T
that starts from the center of
\kappa
and
contains exactly two external edges: the first being the clause-variable edge from
\kappa
to
x
(the
entering edge
),
and the second being any other external edge adjacent to the
x
-gadget (the
exiting edge
).
We say that

traverses
x
from
\kappa
when such a path exists. Intuitively, a traversal from
\kappa
to

is a natural way to
lift the edge
(\kappa,x)
from
G^{*}
to a path in
G
. We emphasize that a traversal begins at the
centers
of flowers,
as opposed to terminals or dummy nodes; this becomes relevant later in Claim
3.3
.

The following lemma is sufficient to construct an assignment for
\phi
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Tree
T
has the following properties:
(a)
T
does not contain any root-clause edges.
(b) If
T
traverses
x
from a negative clause, then
T
does not traverse
x
from any positive clause.

Assuming the lemma above holds, we define our satisfying assignment as follows: for each variable
x
, make it false if
T
traverses it from a
negative clause, otherwise make it true. Lemma
3.2
(a) implies that
T
includes for each clause
\kappa
a
traversal from
\kappa
to some variable
x
appearing in it. (In particular, a traversal appears as a prefix of the path in
T
from

\kappa
’s center to the center of the root.)
x
is false when
\kappa
is negative by definition of the assignment,
and
x
is true when
\kappa
is positive by Lemma
3.2
(b). Therefore all clauses are satisfied.

Proof.

First we prove Lemma
3.2
(a). Suppose for contradiction that
T
contains some root-clause edge
e
from a two-literal clause
\kappa
.
If
\kappa=\alpha
is a 2N-clause, then
e=(t_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{R},j},t_{\mathcal{R}}^{\alpha,j})
, for
j\in{\left\{{1,2}\right\}}
,
and there are two edge-disjoint paths (neither containing
e
) from
t_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{R},j}
to
t_{\mathcal{R}}^{\alpha,j}
:
for each variable
x
of
\alpha
, walk from
\alpha
to the
x
-gadget, and then to the root.
(By the structure of flowers, discussed earlier, we can ensure that these two paths are indeed edge-disjoint.)
This implies that
\mathrm{cng}_{G,T}(e)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{w}_{2}}(e)+2=K-1+2=K+1
, contradicting
\mathrm{cng}_{G}(T)\leq K
. A similar reasoning applies when
\kappa
is a 2P-clause.

Next we prove Lemma
3.2
(b). Let

be a variable and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma

be the 2N-clause, 3P-clause, and 2P-clause of
x
, respectively. Assume for contradiction that

traverses
x
from
\alpha
and at
least one positive clause
\beta
or
\gamma
. We show that this assumption implies that
\mathrm{cng}_{G}(T)>K
.
First we prove the following claim.

Claim 3.3
.

For some clause
\pi\in{\left\{{\beta,\gamma}\right\}}
, the path in
T
from the center of
\alpha
to
\pi

contains exactly two external edges, the first being the clause-variable edge
{\left({t_{\alpha}^{x},x^{\mathrm{2N}}}\right)}
from
\alpha
to
x
,
and the second being the clause-variable edge
{\left({\gamma,x^{\mathrm{2P}}}\right)}
or
{\left({\beta,x^{\mathrm{3P}}}\right)}
from

to
\pi
.

To justify Claim
3.3
, let

be a traversal from
\alpha
to
x
. If the exiting edge of

is
{\left({x^{\mathrm{2P}},\gamma}\right)}

(resp.
{\left({x^{\mathrm{3P}},\beta}\right)}
), then

is simply the path in

from the center of
\alpha
to
\gamma
(resp.

), and the

(resp.
\beta
).
Otherwise, the exiting edge of

is
{\left({x^{\mathcal{R}},t^{x}_{\mathcal{R}}}\right)}
, which of course implies that

and

are both in

.
Now let
\pi\in{\left\{{\beta,\gamma}\right\}}
be a clause where

from

, and let

be a corresponding traversal.
Then in

, the entering edge must be succeeded by an edge containing

, due to the way variable gadgets are labeled.
This implies that

and

overlap. The path from
\alpha
’s center to

is then given by joining the sub-path in

from

\alpha
to

from

Continuing the proof of Lemma

(b), let
\pi\in{\left\{{\beta,\gamma}\right\}}
be a clause satisfying Claim
3.3
.
By Lemma
3.2
(a), the path in
T
from the center of
\alpha
to the center of the root must contain some root-variable edge;
suppose
e={\left({y^{\mathcal{R}},t_{\mathcal{R}}^{y}}\right)}
is the first such edge on this path. Clearly, the center of the root lies on the shore

T^{-y^{\mathcal{R}}}_{e}
of the cut
{\partial T_{e}}
, while the center of
\alpha
lies on the opposite shore

.
On the other hand, the path described in

does not contain any root-variable edges, implying that

also lie on shore

.
In conjunction with Observation

, we obtain:

Corollary 3.4
.

The core of the root is on the shore

, while

,
\pi
, and the core of

are on the shore

.

We now show that
\mathrm{cng}_{T}(e)>K
. The definition of

,

,

, and

,

, or a core vertex of

,
then ends at a core vertex of the root. As explained in the discussion of flower gadgets, it is sufficient to specify terminals as path endpoints.
Three of these paths are

\displaystyle t_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{R},1}-t_{\mathcal{R}}^{\alpha,1},

\displaystyle t_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{R},2}-t_{\mathcal{R}}^{\alpha,2},

\displaystyle t_{\alpha}^{z_{1}}-z^{\mathrm{2N}}_{1}-z^{\mathrm{2P}}_{1}-z^{\mathcal{R}}_{1}-t_{\mathcal{R}}^{z_{1}}.

\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{2N}}-x^{\mathrm{3P}}-x^{\mathcal{R}}-t_{\mathcal{R}}^{x},

\displaystyle\beta-z^{\mathrm{3P}}_{2}-z^{\mathcal{R}}_{2}-t_{\mathcal{R}}^{z_{2}},

\displaystyle\beta-z^{\mathrm{3P}}_{3}-z^{\mathcal{R}}_{3}-t_{\mathcal{R}}^{z_{3}},

\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{2N}}-x^{\mathrm{2P}}-\gamma-t_{\mathcal{R}}^{\gamma}.

If

\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{2N}}-x^{\mathrm{2P}}-x^{\mathcal{R}}-t_{\mathcal{R}}^{x},

\displaystyle\gamma-t_{\mathcal{R}}^{\gamma},

contradicting

4
Cactus Representation and Spanning-Tree Congestion

In this section, laying the groundwork for our algorithm in Section
5
,
we analyze the structure of
K
-edge-connected graphs whose spanning-tree congestion is
K
.
We start, in Section
4.1
,
by reviewing the properties of
K
-edge-connected graphs, captured by so-called
cactus representation
.
Then, in Section
4.2
, we focus on the special
case of graphs with congestion
K
, and we prove that this congestion assumption implies
additional structural properties of such graphs, that will lead to an efficient algorithm.

Throughout this section we assume that
G=(V,E)
is a given
K
-edge-connected graph
with
n=|V|
vertices and
m=|E|
edges.

4.1
Cactus Representation

Two cuts
{\partial X}
and
{\partial Y}
are called
nested
if one
of the four pair-wise shore intersections
X\cap Y
,
{\bar{X}}\cap Y
,

X\cap\bar{Y}
and
{\bar{X}}\cap{\bar{Y}}
is empty. If all four
intersections are non-empty then we say that cuts
{\partial X}
and

{\partial Y}

cross
. A family of cuts is called
laminar

if any two cuts in it are nested. By
{\partial_{Y}X}
we denote the subset of cut
{\partial X}
consisting of edges whose both endpoints are in
Y
.

Theorem 4.1
.

[
6
]
.
Let
G
be a
K
-edge-connected (multi-)graph.

(a)

If
K
is odd, then
G
has no crossing
K
-cuts.
That is, the family of
K
-cuts is laminar.

(b)

If
K
is even, then any two crossing
K
-cuts

{\partial X}
,
{\partial Y}
in
G
satisfy

|{\partial_{Y}X}|=|{\partial_{{\bar{Y}}}X}|=|{\partial_{Y}{\bar{X}}}|=|{\partial_{{\bar{Y}}}{\bar{X}}}|=K/2
.
There are no edges between
X\cap Y
and
{\bar{X}}\cap{\bar{Y}}
,
and between
X\cap{\bar{Y}}
and
{\bar{X}}\cap Y
.

Figure 3
:
A
4
-edge-connected multigraph and all its

4
-cuts. Parallel lines represent double parallel edges.
Each vertex
v_{i}
is identified by its index
i
.
For
X={\left\{{v_{4},v_{5},v_{6},v_{7},v_{8}}\right\}}

and
Y={\left\{{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5},v_{6},v_{7}}\right\}}
,
cuts
{\partial X}
,
{\partial Y}
cross.

This theorem can be refined to produce an even more informative
representation of
K
-cuts, in terms of so-called
cactus graphs
.
For cactus graphs, we will use
terminology of
nodes
and
links
(instead of vertices and
edges). A connected multigraph is called a
cactus graph
if every link
belongs to exactly one cycle. (Equivalently, it is a
2
-edge-connected
graph whose biconnected components are cycles.)
Cactus cycles of length
2
are called
trivial
.
A degree-
2
node of a cactus is called an

external node
, and any other node is called an

internal node
. From the definition, it follows that each
cactus has at least one external node. If all its cycles are trivial, the cactus forms a tree whose
adjacent nodes are connected by two parallel links and the external nodes are its leaves.

Theorem 4.2
.

[
6
]
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a
K
-edge-connected graph. Then
there is a cactus graph
{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}=(U,F)
and an associated mapping
\phi:V\to U
with the following properties:

(a)

For each set
X\subseteq V
,

{\partial X}
is a
K
-cut if and only if
X=\phi^{-1}(Q)
for some
Q\subseteq U
such that

{\partial Q}
is a
2
-cut of
{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}
.

(b)

If
K
is odd, then all cycles in
{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}
are trivial;
that is,
{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}
is a tree with adjacent nodes connected by two parallel links.

The pair
{\mathfrak{C}}_{G},\phi
is called a
cactus representation

of
G
(see Figures
3
and
4
).
A cactus representation of
G
of size
O(n)
can be computed in
near-linear time
{\tilde{O}}(m)

[
12
]
.

:

. The numbers represent indices of vertices in
G

that are mapped by
\phi
to the corresponding node in
{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}
.
Note that the pre-image of a node in
{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}
could be empty.

Basic
K
-cuts.

Per Theorem

, each
K
-cut of

is represented by a

{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}

Each

consists of two links that belong to the

\ell\choose 2

K

. Two

-cuts cross in

if and only
if they are represented by two crossing

{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}
; that
is, the four links in these two

A shore of any

This motivates the following definition.

A

-cuts represented by two

From now on, as a rule, when talking about a cut

{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}

For each node

{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}

Observation 4.3

(a)

A basic

(b)

5
A Linear-Time Algorithm

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
, by developing
an
{\tilde{O}}(m)
-time algorithm that, given a
K
-edge-connected graph
G
, determines whether
\mathrm{stc}(G)=K
.
We start by sketching the basic ideas leading to our algorithm.

A natural attempt to design an algorithm would be to exploit the tree-like structure of cactus graphs,
applying the dynamic programming strategy to recursively compute some congestion-related information for the
K
-cuts of
G
.
The first challenge one encouters is that in general (for even values of
K
), the
K
-cuts do not form a laminar structure.

This is where the concept of
basic

K
-cuts, introduced in Section
4.1
, is
helpful. The family of basic
K
-cuts
is
laminar.
More specifically, we will choose an arbitrary degree-
K
vertex
r
in
G
, called the
root
of
G

(see Section
5.1
.)
We can then identify each basic
K
-cut
{\partial Z}
by the shore
Z
that does not contain
r
.
The subset relation between these shores defines a tree structure on the basic
K
-cuts.
The algorithm can then process the basic
K
-cuts bottom-up in this order.

What information such an algorithm would need to maintain for each basic
K
-cut
{\partial Z}
?
A naïve approach would be to somehow keep track of all “candidate crossing-edge sets”
A\subseteq{\partial Z}
,
namely the sets
A
for which there is a spanning tree
\widetilde{T}
of
G
with congestion
K
such that
\widetilde{T}\cap{\partial Z}=A
.
This raises two issues. One, the resulting algorithm’s running time would be exponential in
K
.
Two, obviously, the algorithm does not yet know
\widetilde{T}
when
{\partial Z}
is considered.
What’s worse, even the congestion of tree edges that are wholly inside shore
Z
cannot be uniquely determined
based only on the subgraph induced by
Z
. (It may be possible to address the latter issue by storing appropriate
information about the tree topology inside
Z
, but at the price of further increasing the time complexity.)

The characterization of
K
-edge-connected graphs with congestion
K
,
developed in Section
4
, suggests an alternative approach.
The key property is Corollary
4.8
,
which says that if
\widetilde{T}
is a spanning tree with congestion
K
then
for each basic
K
-cut
{\partial Z}
, all edges in
\widetilde{T}\cap{\partial Z}
have a common endpoint.
Instead of focussing on tree edges crossing
{\partial Z}
, we can instead
attempt to recursively compute these common endpoints.
This is not quite possible, for the same reason as above: we cannot determine if a vertex
is such a common endpoint, for some congestion-
K
spanning tree, without knowing the whole graph
G
.
Generally, the analysis in Section
4
, while it
provides crucial ideas, is not sufficient in itself to derive a dynamic-programming algorithm
because it is expressed in terms of global properties of
G
.
For this, we need a definition of “candidate common endpoints” that is based only on the
information from the already processed subgraph.

To address this, in Section
5.1
we establish analogs
of the properties in Section
4
expressed in terms of
the rooted versions of
{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}
and
G
.
One key observation is this: for any vertex
w
and for
any
spanning tree
T

such that
T\cap{\partial Z}\subseteq E_{w}
, the congestion of
T
’s edges within the subgraph induced
by
Z\cup{\left\{{w}\right\}}
depends only on this subgraph. If all these congestion values are
K
,
we call
T

safe
, and we call
w
a
hub
for
{\partial Z}
. (We also require, for technical reasons,
that
w
has at least one edge crossing
{\partial Z}
.)
If
w
is a hub for
{\partial Z}
, it only means that the information from the subgraph
Z\cup{\left\{{w}\right\}}

is not sufficient to eliminate
w
as a possible common endpoint of the edges from
{\partial Z}
that
belong to some congestion-
K
spanning tree of
G
. (Later, when processing some ancestor
K
-cut of
Z
,
it may turn out that such a tree does not exist for
w
.)
We prove that the set of hubs of
{\partial Z}
, denoted
\widetilde{H}(Z)
, can be
determined from the hub sets of
{\partial Z}
’s children, thus establishing a recurrence relation that
drives the dynamic programming algorithm (see Section
5.2
).

While it may not be obvious, computing all hub sets
\widetilde{H}(Z)
is sufficient to determine
whether the congestion of
G
is
K
. (That is, for any
w\in\widetilde{H}(Z)
we do not need to keep track of which tree
edges from
w
cross
{\partial Z}
, circumventing the issue we mentioned earlier.)
The reason is this: once (and if) we reach the root
r
,
the condition on
r
being a hub for its cut
{\partial Z}
(with
Z=V\setminus{\left\{{r}\right\}}
)
is equivalent to
G
having a spanning tree with congestion
K
.

5.1.1
Safe Trees and Hubs

Through the rest of this section, by a

To simplify notation we will sometimes treat trees in
G
as sets of vertices
and for such a tree

to mean

.

We now define the concepts of

and

, mentioned earlier in
the beginning of Section

.
Roughly, a tree in
G
is considered

if it cannot be eliminated as a possible

\widetilde{T}
(a spanning tree with congestion

) based only on the subgraph of

These trees are used as partial solutions constructed (implicitly) in the algorithm.
As the algorithm proceeds, it may expand a safe tree, but only by
connecting its root to a vertex in the new, larger safe tree.
A safe tree can get discarded, if we discover that it cannot be expanded to a larger safe tree.

To formally define safe trees, we need to extend some concepts from Section
2
,
defined for spanning trees, to arbitrary trees in
G
. If
T
is any tree in
G

and

is an edge of

from

disconnects

into two connected components. As before, given a vertex

of

, we denote the component not containing

by

.
As usual, by
{\partial T^{-w}_{e}}
we denote the set of edges between
this component and the rest of the graph.

For any tree
T
in
G
and any vertex
w
in
T
,

(T,w)
denotes the rooted version of
T
, with
w
designated as its
root. Such a rooted tree
(T,w)
is called

if for each edge

of

we have

|{\partial T^{-w}_{e}}|=K
.
The concept of a safe tree is a natural generalization of the
notion of a spanning tree with congestion

, in the following sense:

Observation 5.2
.

Let
T
be a spanning tree in
G
and
w
any vertex. Then
(T,w)
is
safe if and only if
\mathrm{cng}(G,T)\leq K
.

The lemma below generalizes Corollary

to an arbitrary safe tree.

Lemma 5.3

Let
{\partial Z}
be a basic

-cut and

a safe tree in

Then all edges of

Proof.

For a contradiction, suppose that

and

are two edges in

such that all four of their endpoints are
distinct. Consider a path connecting these edges in

and any edge

on this path in-between

and

. Then the cut

is a

However,

contains exactly one of the

with its both endpoints, while the endpoints of the other edge are

and

are crossing, as each

Since

From the above paragraph, any pair of edges in

an endpoint. We still need to argue that

must share a common endpoint. Indeed, this is trivial when

When

Any other edge in

, to share an endpoint with each of these three edges, must have

We are now ready to define hubs.
Let

be a basic cut in

By

is a
safe tree.

Let

be a basic

We now describe how the set of hubs

We break it into three cases: when

The case when

Recall that each external node is a Type-

Lemma 5.7
.

Let

be an external node of

(necessarily of Type-
1
)
with

. Then
\widetilde{H}(W^{b})=\{v\}\cup N(v)
.

Proof.

The witness tree for

If

As also explained in Observation

This, together with Observation

Observation 5.8
.

Let

be an internal node of
{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}
, and denote by

the children of
its corresponding

-cut

.
If

then

for some

Now we examine the case when

is an internal node of Type-

We show that the only possible in-hub for

is the vertex

. The only possible out-hubs
are neighbors of

outside

, providing that

is an in-hub itself.
Formally, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 5.9

Let

be a Type-

{\mathfrak{C}}_{G}
, and let

be the (unique)

\widetilde{H}(W^{b})\;=\;\begin{cases}{\left\{{v}\right\}}\cup(N(v)\setminus W^{b})&if $v\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{H}(Z_{i})$\\

(1)

Proof.

The first condition, namely that

In the rest of the proof we show that Equation (

,
vertex

.
The theorem will follow from the three claims established below.

Claim 5.10
.

\widetilde{H}(W^{b})\cap(\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}Z_{i})=\emptyset

To prove this claim, suppose that

, and let

Consider an edge

to

in

By the definition of a safe tree,

But

Claim 5.11
.

v\in\widetilde{H}(W^{b})

We start with the
(\Leftarrow)

For all

, consider the witness tree for

and

. By Observation

their union is a

v
and

.
Together with

this implies that
v
is an in-hub for
W^{b}
.

To prove the
(\Rightarrow)
implication, suppose that
v\in\widetilde{H}(W^{b})
and let
T
be its safe tree.
We will show that
v
is an out-hub for each

. Indeed, for
any edge

from

in

,

for some

and

, as

{\partial T^{-v}_{e}}
is a

.

Now consider an arbitrary but fixed

, and the tree

consisting of all edges

from

to
Z_{i}
in

plus a union of all corresponding trees

. This

spans

. For each

5.5

implies that

is a witness tree for

and
{\partial Z_{i}}
and that
w=v
is an out-hub for
Z_{i}
.

Claim 5.12
.

Let

. Then

w\in\widetilde{H}(W^{b})
iff
v\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{H}(Z_{i})
and
w\in N(v)\setminus W^{b}
.

The
(\Leftarrow)

then,
by Claim

,
v
is an in-hub for
W^{b}
.
Then the assumption that
w\in N(v)\setminus W^{b}
implies that
w
is an out-hub for
W^{b}
,
directly by definition.

It remains to prove the
(\Rightarrow)
implication. Suppose that

w\in\widetilde{H}(W^{b})
and let
T
be its witness tree. By the definition of
\widetilde{H}(W^{b})

we have
w\in V({\partial W^{b}})
.

We argue first that the degree of
w
in
T
is
1
.
Otherwise, if the degree of
w
in
T
is at least
2
, let
e
be an edge from

such that

(such an edge necessarily exists.)
Then

does not span whole

as there
is another edge from
w
to

in

. Thus

,
and
{\partial T^{-w}_{e}}
is a

.

Therefore the degree of
w
in
T
is
1
. Let this single edge from
w
be
(w,x)
. Then
Observation
5.6
implies that
(T,w)
is a safe tree if and
only if
(T^{-w}_{e},x)
is a safe tree. Then

witnesses that

is an in-hub for
W^{b}
.
But Claims

and
5.11
imply
that only

can be an in-hub for

, providing that

,
completing the proof of the
(\Rightarrow)
implication in Claim

.
∎

If

is an internal node of Type
0
,

may have multiple in-hubs,
but, as we show in the lemma below, they still must be common hubs for all

’s.

may either be neighbors of such in-hubs, or common out-hubs for all

’s.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let

be a Type-

internal node of the cactus. Let

be the children of

. Then

\widetilde{H}(W^{b})\;=\;\bar{H}\cup(N(\bar{H}\cap W^{b})\setminus W^{b}),\quad\textrm{where}\;\textstyle\bar{H}=V({\partial W^{b}})\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{H}(Z_{i}).

(2)

Proof.

We first show the
(\supseteq)
inclusion. Consider some
w\in\bar{H}
. Then
w
is a hub for each set

Z_{i}
. Consider the corresponding witness trees

for
w
and
Z_{i}
for all

.
By Observation
5.6
their union is a safe tree rooted at

and thus it is a witness tree for

and
{\partial W^{a}}

is an in-hub for

.

Next, consider some

.
That is,

, and

By the previous paragraph,

.

To show the

, and let

be its witness tree.
Recall that

spans

, so any neighbor of

.
We need to show that

Case 1
:

. We will show that in this case

.
Note that

We start with the following simple observation.
For any edge
(w,u)
in

{\partial T^{-w}_{e}}
is a

-cut
and

.

Now, fix some arbitrary index

. It remains to show that

.
Let

be all the edges in

from

to

.
We claim that the subtrees

possibly with the exception of

Indeed, consider any vertex

on the path from

, as

Using Observations

,
5.6
, and

Case 2
:
w\not\in W^{b}
and the degree of

in

is at least
2
.
We will show that in this case

.
The argument is essentially the same as in Case 1.
We have that

, by the definition of a hub, and
it remains to show that
w\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{H}(Z_{i})
.

For any edge
(w,u)
in
T
,
{\partial T^{-w}_{e}}
is a

-cut
and

This implies that

—
the same property that we had in Case 1.

Following the same argument as in Case 1, for any index

we can obtain the witness

by combining the branches of

, showing that

. Since

we conclude that

.

Case 3
:

w\not\in W^{b}
and the degree of

in

is

.
In this case, we show that

.
Let this single edge from

be

. Then

implies that

is a safe tree if and

∎

5.1.3

In the previous sub-section we characterized hubs associated with

, for

5.2
The Algorithm

As explained at the beginning of this section, to determine whether

\mathrm{stc}(G)=K

The algorithm follows a dynamic programming paradigm, processing all basic

As presented (see the pseudo-code in Algorithm

We start with external nodes. If

In both cases, this computation can be implemented efficiently using standard data structures.

is the number of vertices
of

The Double-Weight Gadget

In this section we describe the construction of our double-weight gadget

The double-weight gadget is constructed from a sub-gadget that we call a

The bottleneck gadget.

For an integer

(b2) for any spanning tree

and

2

are at most

.

We now show a construction of
\mathcal{B}(w)
. We remark that congestion properties of some grid-like graphs of degree

[
19

However, these results are not sufficient for our purpose because they don’t explicitly address condition (b2) of bottleneck graphs.

Let

be the specified parameter.
Our gadget is a hexagonal grid, depicted as a slanted wall-of-bricks grid of dimensions

illustrated in Figure

More precisely, geometrically the grid has

The gate

is the bottom left corner while

The gates have degree 2 and all other vertices have degree 2 or 3, so property (b3) is satisfied.
