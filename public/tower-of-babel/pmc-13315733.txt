Contralateral sensate free anterior auricular flap with a crus helix cartilage for burn auricular deformity: A Case report
Authors: Kirito Kojima, Mitsunaga Narushima, Chizuki Kondo, Kosuke Yamagata, Kohei Mitsui, Kohei Hashimoto, Kento Hosomi, Ryohei Ishiura, Chihena Banda, Yukio Seki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe characteristic intricate 3- dimensional shaped thin cartilage, covered by thin skin, make the external ear prone to cartilage exposure and significant deformation after injury. Auricular reconstruction after such injuries often involves the use of local flaps with or without cartilage grafts. However, this is challenging when surrounding skin scarring limits the use of local flaps.We report a case of auricular reconstruction using a contralateral free anterior auricular flap with vascularized helical cartilage for reconstruction of a post-burn defect of the ear helix. Post operative outcomes were favorable, with no complications and successful flap survival. The patient was able to wear glasses, and sensory recovery was achieved with a Semmes Weinstein monofilament value of 2.44 with 2 years follow up.This method provides both aesthetic and functional reconstruction, offering a valuable option for cases when scarring limits other reconstructive methods.Keywords: Ear, Reconstruction, Burn, Free anterior auricular flap, Auricle, Microsurgery

Introduction

The unique anatomical characteristics of the external ear including thin intricate 3-dimentionally shaped cartilage covered by thin skin, make it prone to cartilage exposure and significant deformation or loss after facial injuries or burns. Auricular reconstruction after such injuries can often involve the use of local flaps with or without cartilage grafts to recreate the external ear and restore function.1, 2, 3, 4 However, in post-burn cases, the surrounding skin of the affected auricle is often damaged making ear reconstruction particularly challenging due to the lack of adequate soft tissue cover. Herein, we report a case of auricular reconstruction of the superior helix defect following burn injuries using an composite chondrocutaneous anterior auricular free flap including crus helix cartilage.

The case

A 27-years old man presented to our department with an ear deformity 1 year after a 35% total body surface area (TBSA) accidental flame burn of the head, neck, both upper limbs, anterior body trunk and lumbar region. Debridement and skin grafting were performed for the neck, right auricle, and both upper arms. However, the injury resulted in loss of the upper one-third-of his superior helix and loss of the crus of his right ear with scarring of the surrounding skin leaving him unable to wear glasses. (Fig. 1a)

Preoperative views 1 year after the burn injury. (A) Post ear burn deformity. (B)Design of the flap from the contralateral ear.

Due to the scarring and skin grafts around the ear, we considered that reconstruction with a local flap would have a higher risk of skin necrosis and a Ysuboptimal aesthetic outcome (Supplementary Figure 1A-B).Therefore, we decided to reconstruct the ear defect using a free anterior auricular flap from the contralateral side including part of the crus helix cartilage.

A 7 × 2 cm pre-auricular flap including 1 × 3 cm crus helix was designed on the contralateral left anterior ear (Fig. 1b). The flap was elevated with a 5 cm vascular pedicle of the superficial temporal artery (STA) and the posteriorly located superficial temporal vein (STV). In addition to the main trunk of the STA and the STV, a branch extending toward the superior crus of the helix and the auriculotemporal nerve (ATN), which courses toward the auriculotemporal region were included in the flap. The flap was designed to receive its blood supply through retrograde arterial and venous flow.

The retro-auricular and superior-auricular skin of the injured right ear was elevated anteriorly to recreate the scaphoid fossa of the helix. The STA, STV and ATN were identified in the anterior region of the right ear and prepared as recipient vessels. The contralateral left pre-auricular flap was then harvested and transferred to reconstruct the right ear defect (Fig. 2).

Illustration showing the position of the flap and vessel and nerve anastomosis. STA (A:2.0 mm) was anastomosed to contralateral STA (a1:1.5 mm), the peripheral arteries (a2:2.0 mm, a3:0.7 mm) were anastomosed because of poor blood circulation at the distal end of the flap. STV (V: 2.0 mm) was anastomosed to contralateral STV (v1:2.0 mm), vein branches (v2, v3) were ligated. ATN (N) was anastomosed to contralateral ATN branch (n1).

The flap’s artery (a1) and vein (v1) were anastomosed to STA (A) and STV (V) each. The diameter of the flap’s artery (a1) and vein(v1), STA(A), and STV (V) were 1.5, 2.0, 2.0, and 2.0 mm respectively (Fig. 2).

Based on the color change in the distal flap, weak peripheral blood flow was suspected, additional anastomoses were performed in the flap. The distal side of the STA (a2) and the STA branch (a3) were anastomosed. The diameter of the distal side of the STA (a2) and the STA branch (a3) were 2.0 and 0.7 mm each (Fig. 2). The flap’s STV branches (v2 and v3) were ligated.

The flap was inserted on the upper right ear with the vascularized cartilage from the left ear fixed to the superior margin of the cartilage defect to reconstruct the right ear helix and the thin pliable flap skin was used to cover the inner and outer surfaces of the cartilage recreating the auriculo-cephalic sulcus.

Postoperatively, the flap survived without any complications. Two years after surgery, the morphology of the upper area of the helix was preserved (Fig. 3). Although the right upper helix had a slight thickening of the flap, the patient was satisfied and did not desire additional treatment. Functional recovery, including the ability to wear glasses, was successfully achieved with sensory recovery of a Semmes-Weinstein monofilament (SW) value of 2.44. A minor deformity of the crus of the helix was observed, however, no functional impairments was noted, and the patient did not express concern regarding the deformity (Fig. 3). The total follow-up duration was 2 years.

Postoperative photograph 2 years after, showing the shape is maintained allowing the patient to wear glasses.

Discussion

Reconstruction of the auricle after burns is often challenging because scar contracture in the surrounding tissues limits the availability of suitable skin, necessitating a case-by-case approach to selecting the optimal reconstructive method.

For cases in which the cartilaginous framework is preserved and composite reconstruction is unnecessary, reconstruction using tubed flaps is an effective option.3, 4, 5 Di Mascio et al. described harvesting a tubed flap from the mastoid region,6 and Lawrence M.Field also reported a preauricular tubed pedicle flap.7 These methods provide excellent color and texture match. In the present case, post-burn damage and the scarring that developed 1 year the injury after limited local skin pliability and posed a potential risk of poor vascularity in the auricular region.

We selected a free flap from the contralateral side to provide stable cover and vascularized cartilage in restoring definitive shape and cosmetic appearance as our first option for single stage reconstruction, reserving a tubed local flap as an alternative treatment option or salvage procedure. Staged tube which may include an additional auricular cartilage graft buried subcutaneously, followed by later elevation of a tubed flap are a useful alternative option and in settings where access to microsurgery is limited. However, care must be taken in selecting local donor flap skin in postburn setting as reduced pliability and excess scarring may limit optimal ear shape reconstruction.

The use of a temporoparietal fascia flap combined with costal cartilage grafting.8 and skin grafting may also present an alternative option. This may be considered suitable when the temporoparietal region remains intact, as in cases of total ear loss. However, the disadvantages include poor color and texture match due to the necessity of skin grafting and the rigid, bulky shape of costal cartilage, which is hyaline cartilage, compared to the natural elastic cartilage of the auricle.

A free radial forearm flap combined with costal cartilage grafting is also an option. Its long vascular pedicle allows anastomosis to the facial vessels in the neck in cases where the temporal region is scarred.9 Nevertheless, this approach results in poor color and texture match and creates a thick, stiff auricle, and it requires multiple staged operations when used as a prefabricated flap.

In contrast, free auricular flaps offer excellent color and texture match and include auricular cartilage, providing sufficient structural strength to maintain shape for wearing glasses or masks. The flaps harvested from the auricle and nourished by the superficial temporal artery have been reported for nasal alar10 and eyelid reconstruction11 because of their compatibility with facial skin. Furthermore, a previous report described reconstruction of a central helical rim defect caused by shotgun injury using a free flap from the contralateral central helix.12 Based on these considerations, we selected a free flap including auricular cartilage from the contralateral helix as the ideal option for the upper helical rim defect after burn injury in our case.

In this case, no flap necrosis or significant postoperative atrophy occurred, demonstrating that this technique can achieve both good aesthetic and functional results. The main drawback of this approach is technical complexity. It requires microvascular anastomoses and is therefore more technically demanding than local flap procedures such as tubed flaps. In the present case, intraoperative color change indicative of compromised distal flap perfusion was observed, necessitating additional microvascular anastomoses between a2 and a3 in the flap. In addition, the size of the flap is limited because the donor site is the contralateral auricle. A previous report described the use of free auricular flaps measuring 4.0 × 3.6 cm for nasal tip defect reconstruction.10 In contrast, our technique allowed harvesting a flap measuring 7.0 × 2.0 cm (with 3.0 cm of cartilage) by including preauricular skin in the flap further demonstrating the robustness of this innovative free flap.

In conclusion, though auricular reconstruction after severe burns often receives low priority compared with lifesaving procedures, aesthetic and functional restoration plays an essential role in improving the patient’s quality of life. Because burn injuries frequently limit the availability of reconstructive tissue, this technique represents an innovative alternative for reconstruction after severe burn injuries, particularly in cases where compromised vascularity is anticipated, making local flaps less favorable as a first-line option, and the contralateral auricle remains intact.
