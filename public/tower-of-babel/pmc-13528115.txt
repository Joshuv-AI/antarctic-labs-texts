Laryngeal mask airway use during liver transplantation: perioperative outcomes in a propensity score-matched cohort
Authors: Xiang’an Wu, Tianxing Dai, Jianliang Yang, Feng Yuan, Zhitao Chen, Kai Li, Dan Gui, Jianfeng Chen, Shulan Xie, Hanyu Yang, Lan Lan, Jianxing He, Guoying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhether a laryngeal mask airway (LMA) can be safely used during liver transplantation remains unclear. However, in clinical practice, the choice of an airway device may also reflect a broader recovery-oriented perioperative strategy rather than an isolated technical substitution.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed adult patients who underwent primary elective liver transplantation at our center between January 2024 and November 2025. The patients were grouped according to their primary intraoperative airway device (LMA or endotracheal tube [ETT]). Propensity score matching was used to reduce baseline imbalance. Intraoperative variables, postoperative airway-related events, postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), intensive care unit (ICU) stays, and postoperative hospital stays were compared.ResultsAfter matching, 25 and 44 patients in the LMA and ETT groups, respectively, were analyzed. The LMA group showed a higher rate of immediate airway device removal, lower rocuronium use during anesthetic maintenance, less postoperative noninvasive ventilation, less postoperative sore throat, and a shorter postoperative hospital stay. PPCs were numerically less frequent in the LMA group, but the between-group difference was not statistically significant after matching. No increase in major airway-related adverse events was observed in the LMA group.ConclusionsIn carefully selected liver transplant recipients, use of LMA within a recovery-oriented perioperative context appeared feasible and was associated with several favorable early postoperative outcomes.Keywords: Liver transplantation, laryngeal mask airway, postoperative pulmonary complications, enhanced recovery, propensity score matching

Introduction

Liver transplantation is an established therapeutic option for end-stage liver disease and selected hepatic malignancies [1]. Despite continuous advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care, postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), particularly pulmonary infection and atelectasis, remain common after liver transplantation because of prolonged operative duration, major surgical stress, transfusion and fluid shifts, and complexity of postoperative respiratory management. PPCs are associated with delayed postoperative recovery, prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stays, and increased healthcare burden [2–4].

Endotracheal intubation (ETT) is the conventional airway management strategy for liver transplantation because it provides a secure airway and facilitates controlled ventilation. However, the laryngeal mask airway (LMA) has increasingly been used in selected major abdominal procedures as part of low-stimulation anesthetic and fast-track recovery pathways [5,6]. Potential advantages of LMA include reduced airway irritation, less postoperative sore throat, lower requirements for neuromuscular blockade, and facilitation of earlier airway device removal [7,8]. Nevertheless, evidence supporting the use of LMAs during liver transplantation is limited.

In real-world perioperative practice, the choice of airway device may also correlate with broader perioperative management decisions, including extubation planning, analgesic strategies, and enhanced-recovery measures. Therefore, the present study compared the perioperative outcomes associated with LMA and ETT during liver transplantation using propensity score matching to reduce baseline imbalance. We particularly focused on postoperative respiratory outcomes, sore throat, noninvasive ventilation, ICU stay, and postoperative hospital stay, while recognizing that any observed differences may reflect both the airway strategy and the surrounding perioperative recovery pathway.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study. Clinical data were retrospectively collected from adult recipients who underwent liver transplantation at our institution between January 2024 and November 2025. All donor livers were allocated using the China Organ Transplant Response System (COTRS). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institute (approval number ES-2026-K214-01). The requirement for written informed consent for this retrospective analysis was waived because of the retrospective design and use of de-identified clinical data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 18-75 years; (2) primary elective liver transplantation; and (3) completion of routine preoperative cardiac, pulmonary, and renal assessments, with no absolute contraindication to surgery or general anesthesia. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) retransplantation or combined organ transplantation, (2) emergency liver transplantation, (3) preoperative endotracheal intubation or mechanical ventilation, and (4) incomplete clinical data.

A total of 164 recipients were included and classified into the LMA and the ETT groups according to the primary intraoperative airway management strategy. Twenty-nine recipients were managed with LMA and 135 with ETT before matching.

Airway management and perioperative decision-making

In both groups, standard intraoperative monitoring was performed after arrival in the operating room, and the airway was established after the induction of general anesthesia. The primary airway strategy and postoperative airway removal plan were determined by the attending anesthesiologist in discussion with the surgical team according to the preoperative aspiration risk, respiratory status, graft and surgical complexity, hemodynamic condition, expected postoperative ventilation requirements, and clinician judgment.

In the LMA group, an appropriately sized second-generation of LMA (size 3-5), equipped with a gastric drainage channel, was selected according to the patient’s body weight. After lubrication, the LMA was inserted along the posterior pharyngeal wall, and the cuff was inflated with 20–30 mL of air. Correct placement was confirmed by adequate chest wall movement, bilateral breath sounds, and the presence of a continuous end-tidal carbon dioxide waveform. Then a nasogastric tube was inserted. Intraoperative mechanical ventilation was predominantly delivered using volume-controlled ventilation (VCV), while intermittent positive-pressure ventilation (IPPV) or synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV) was used in selected cases.

In the ETT group, oral endotracheal intubation was performed under video laryngoscopic guidance following rapid-sequence induction, using an endotracheal tube with an internal diameter of 7.0-8.0 mm. After cuff inflation, correct tube placement was confirmed by bilateral chest auscultation and the presence of a continuous end-tidal carbon dioxide waveform. A nasogastric tube was also inserted in this group. Mechanical ventilation was predominantly delivered using VCV, with IPPV or SIMV used in selected cases. No aspiration events occurred in either group.

Anesthetic regimens were generally similar between the groups. Induction mainly included midazolam, sufentanil, etomidate, and neuromuscular blocking agents (rocuronium or cisatracurium). Maintenance was primarily intravenous and consisted of continuous infusion of propofol or ciprofol, remifentanil, and dexmedetomidine, with neuromuscular blocking agents adjusted as needed to maintain an appropriate degree of relaxation. Regional anesthesia was performed by the surgeons under direct vision and included a rectus sheath block (RSB) and/or transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block. The blocking solution consisted of a mixture of 0.5% ropivacaine and 2% lidocaine, with 2.5 mL injected at each block site.

Data collection and outcome definitions

Preoperative baseline characteristics, intraoperative variables, and postoperative outcomes were collected and compared between groups.

Preoperative baseline variables included age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, alcohol use, and comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, preoperative arrhythmia, chronic bronchitis, emphysema, malignancy, viral hepatitis, cirrhosis, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, liver failure, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, pulmonary ventilation function grade (SPVD), Child-Pugh class, Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score, Mallampati grade, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class.

Intraoperative variables included liver transplantation technique, use of laryngoscopy, ventilator mode, immediate removal of the airway device, airway placement time, changes in blood pressure and heart rate during airway placement, anesthesia duration, operative duration, duration of the anhepatic phase, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion volume of red blood cells and fresh frozen plasma, use of TAP or RSB blocks, and administration of major anesthetic agents and neuromuscular blocking agents during induction and maintenance. Extubation performed in the operating room or in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) was defined as immediate extubation, and the time from the end of surgery to extubation was recorded as the immediate extubation time.

Postoperative outcomes included postoperative analgesia, use of patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA), postoperative noninvasive ventilation, PPCs, pleural effusion, laryngeal edema, hoarseness, sore throat, ICU reintubation, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and delirium. The ICU length of stay and postoperative hospital stay were also recorded. In this study, PPCs were defined as pulmonary infection, atelectasis, pulmonary edema, bronchospasm, respiratory failure and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Diagnostic criteria were based on established international standards. Postoperative pneumonia was defined as a new pulmonary infiltrate with clinical evidence of infection, according to ATS/IDSA guidelines [9]. According to the EPCO definitions, atelectasis was defined as radiographic lung opacification with volume loss; bronchospasm as new-onset wheezing requiring bronchodilator therapy; and respiratory failure as PaO2 <60 mmHg on room air, PaO2/FiO2 <300 mmHg, or SpO2 <90% requiring supplemental oxygen [10]. ARDS was defined according to the Berlin criteria [11].

The primary outcomes of interest were the incidence of PPCs and postoperative sore throat. Secondary outcomes included postoperative noninvasive ventilation, ICU length of stay, postoperative hospital stay, and other postoperative complications.

Statistical analysis

Propensity score matching was performed to reduce baseline imbalance and selection bias. Covariates for propensity score matching were selected according to their clinical relevance, degree of baseline imbalance, and availability in the database, while considering the limited sample size and the risk of model overfitting. Eventually, BMI, sex, malignancy, diabetes mellitus, emphysema, MELD grade, ASA class, and NYHA class were included as covariates. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression. Variable-ratio nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was then performed, with up to two ETT patients matched to each LMA patient within a prespecified caliper width of 0.2. When two suitable ETT matches were not available within the caliper, one matched ETT patient was retained. The final matched cohort therefore comprised 25 LMA patients and 44 ETT patients.

Continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range (M [IQR]) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages (n [%]) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. As comparability improved after matching, post-PSM analyses were considered the primary analyses, whereas pre-PSM findings were treated as supplementary. Logistic regression analyses were performed to explore the factors associated with postoperative outcomes. Given the limited number of events in the matched cohort, regression findings were interpreted cautiously. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

After propensity score matching, 25 and 44 recipients in the LMA and ETT groups, respectively, were included (Figure 1). There were no statistically significant between-group differences in age, sex, BMI, smoking and drinking status, comorbidities, Child-Pugh class, MELD grade, Mallampati grade, or ASA class. The difference in NYHA class was no longer significant (p = 0.577), indicating improved comparability of the baseline characteristics after matching. However, the study population was predominantly male and included nearly all patients in the LMA group. This sex distribution may reflect the case mix at our center during the study period but may limit generalizability to broader transplant populations.

Intraoperative management and surgical parameters

A comparison of the intraoperative management and surgical parameters is shown in Table 2. After matching, there were no statistically significant between-group differences in anesthesia duration, operative duration, duration of the anhepatic phase, intraoperative blood loss, red blood cell transfusion volume, or fresh frozen plasma transfusion volume, suggesting comparable overall surgical complexities. Regarding the transplantation technique, the proportion of classic liver transplantation was 96.0% in the LMA group and 75.0% in the ETT group, whereas the piggyback technique was used in 4.0% and 25.0% of recipients, respectively. This difference approached statistical significance (p = 0.060), suggesting possible residual imbalance in the surgical technique.

With respect to airway management, the use of a laryngoscope was 100.0% in the ETT group and 12.0% in the LMA group (p < 0.001), which is consistent with the technical characteristics of the two airway strategies. The ventilator mode differed significantly between groups (p = 0.023). SIMV was used more frequently in the LMA group than in the ETT group (16.0% vs 0%, respectively), suggesting that some degree of spontaneous breathing may have been retained more often in the LMA group. Immediate removal of the airway device was substantially more frequent in the LMA group than in the ETT group (88.0% vs 15.9%, p < 0.001). The markedly higher rate of immediate airway device removal in the LMA group should be interpreted cautiously because the choice of LMA itself may have been linked to a preplanned fast-track extubation strategy rather than representing an independent postoperative effect of the device. In contrast, airway placement time did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.165).

Regarding anesthetic management (Table 3), the doses of the major induction agents were comparable between the two groups. During the maintenance phase, rocuronium consumption was significantly higher in the ETT group than in the LMA group [0.72 (0.45, 0.82) mg/kg/h vs. 0.47 (0.07, 0.60) mg/kg/h, p = 0.021], suggesting that a deeper neuromuscular blockade may have been necessary to ensure tolerance of the tracheal tube and facilitate mechanical ventilation. Conversely, the lower rocuronium requirement observed in the LMA group during maintenance therapy may reflect differing anesthetic demands associated with the airway strategy. Nevertheless, this finding should not be interpreted in isolation but rather within the context of the overall perioperative management approach. The TAP block rate was significantly higher in the LMA group than in the ETT group (92.0% vs. 52.3%, p = 0.002), whereas the RSB rate did not differ significantly between the groups (24.0% vs. 9.1%, p = 0.182) (Table 2). In addition, the LMA group exhibited higher rates of patient-controlled intravenous analgesia. These findings suggest that the observed differences between the groups extend beyond the choice of airway device and may instead reflect a broader, recovery-oriented perioperative management pattern.

Hemodynamic Changes during induction and perioperative blood gas changes

During anesthetic induction, baseline systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, and heart rate before induction were generally comparable between the groups after matching (Figure 2(A, B)). During airway placement, hemodynamic variables fluctuated in both groups. Overall, systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure increased at the time of airway device insertion and declined 60 s later in both groups, with descriptively greater increases in the ETT group. Diastolic blood pressure showed a mild downward trend in both groups, with less pronounced fluctuations in the LMA group. Heart rate in the LMA group decreased slightly at insertion compared with the pre-induction level and then rebounded mildly, whereas the ETT group showed an increase at insertion and, although it decreased 60 s later, it remained higher than that in the LMA group. Taken together, these descriptive findings suggest less pronounced hemodynamic fluctuations with LMA during induction.

The results of the postoperative dynamic blood gas analysis are shown in Figure 2(C, D). In the matched cohort, pH trajectories were generally similar between the groups, with both showing a slight decrease in the early postoperative period followed by gradual recovery. PaCO2 increased mildly from the day of surgery to postoperative day 1 and then stabilized, with similar trends in the two groups and no evidence of persistent CO2 retention in the LMA group. Regarding PaO2, a transient increase was observed in the ETT group on the day of surgery, but this difference was largely confined to the early postoperative period. By postoperative days 2-3, the two groups converged, possibly reflecting continued mechanical ventilation or noninvasive ventilation during the immediate postoperative period. The oxygenation index decreased markedly on the day of surgery in both groups compared with the preoperative level and then gradually recovered. Overall, these dynamic trends did not suggest adverse postoperative ventilatory or oxygenation effects associated with LMA.

Postoperative outcomes

Postoperative Outcomes are showed in Table 4. Regarding postoperative analgesia, after matching, the rate of postoperative analgesia was significantly higher in the LMA group than in the ETT group (100.0% vs. 77.3%, p = 0.026), and the rate of PCIA use was also significantly higher (100.0% vs. 65.9%, p = 0.003). Together with the higher rates of TAP block and immediate airway device removal in the LMA group, these findings suggest that the LMA group may have been more likely to undergo a multimodal enhanced recovery pathway.

For respiratory and airway-related outcomes, postoperative noninvasive ventilation was used significantly less often in the LMA group than in the ETT group (20.0% vs 59.1%, p = 0.004). PPCs were numerically less frequent in the LMA group than in the ETT group (8.0% vs 25.0%), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.157). The incidence of pleural effusion was similar between groups (68.0% vs 63.6%, p = 0.341). After matching, no cases of laryngeal edema or ICU reintubation after extubation were observed in either group after matching. The incidence of hoarseness was lower in the LMA group than in the ETT group (4.0% vs 9.1%); however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.763). In contrast, postoperative sore throat was significantly less frequent in the LMA group than in the ETT group (4.0% vs 27.3%, p = 0.040).

With respect to recovery outcomes, ICU length of stay showed a decreasing trend in the LMA group compared to the ETT group [2.0 (2.0, 4.0) days vs 3.0 (2.0, 5.0) days], although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.178). Postoperative hospital stay was significantly shorter in the LMA group than in the ETT group [17.0 (15.0, 21.0) days vs 20.0 (16.75, 29.0) days, p = 0.036].

Postoperative complications and PPC risk associated with LMA use

After matching, compared with ETT, LMA was associated with lower odds of postoperative noninvasive ventilation (OR = 0.18, 95% CI 0.04-0.61, p = 0.002) and postoperative sore throat (OR = 0.11, 95% CI 0.00-0.87, p = 0.023). No statistically significant between-group differences were identified in pleural effusion or postoperative hoarseness. The OR for postoperative delirium was 1.78 (95% CI 0.02-143.69, p = 1.000), indicating limited stability of this estimate because of the low event rate (Figure 3(A, B)).

Before matching, LMA was associated with lower odds of developing PPCs (OR = 0.20, 95% CI 0.002-0.85, p = 0.017) (Figure 3(A)). After matching, the OR remained below 1 (OR = 0.26, 95% CI 0.03-1.39), suggesting a possible trend toward lower PPC risk in the LMA group, although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.113) (Figure 3(B)). In stepwise forward logistic regression analyses, LMA remained an independent factor associated with lower PPC risk before matching (adjusted OR = 0.25, 95% CI 0.05-0.81, p = 0.019) (Figure 3(C)). After matching, a similar directional association was observed, but statistical significance was not reached (adjusted OR = 0.26, 95% CI 0.04-1.10, p = 0.101) (Figure 3(D)). These findings suggest a possible protective trend, but not definitive evidence, for reduced PPCs with LMA in the matched cohort.

Factors associated with postoperative hospital stay and NIV use

After matching, univariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with postoperative hospital length of stay. In the Cox model, discharge was defined as the event; therefore, an HR greater than 1 indicated a higher rate of discharge and a shorter hospital stay. In the univariate analysis, LMA use was associated with earlier discharge (HR, 2.073; 95% CI, 1.222–3.517; p = 0.007). However, this association was not significant after multivariable adjustment (adjusted HR, 1.429; 95% CI, 0.341–5.993; p = 0.626). TAP block (adjusted HR,1.500; 95% CI, 0.754-2.984; p = 0.248) and PCIA (adjusted HR, 1.299; 95% CI, 0.592-2.852; p = 0.515) were also not independently associated with hospital length of stay (Supplementary Table 1). Thus, the LMA use and shorter hospital stay observed in the LMA group cannot be attributed solely to the airway device or to the more frequent use of regional analgesia and PCIA. The observed difference may reflect the combined effects of patient selection, baseline clinical status, postoperative complications, and other components of perioperative care.

In the univariate logistic regression analysis, LMA use was associated with a lower likelihood of postoperative NIV use (OR, 0.173; 95% CI, 0.055–0.546; p = 0.003). However, LMA use was not an independent predictor in the multivariable model. NYHA class was independently associated with NIV use (adjusted OR, 5.04; 95% CI, 1.55–16.42; p = 0.007), suggesting that preoperative cardiopulmonary functional status may be more important than the airway device in determining the need for postoperative NIV (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective propensity score-matched cohort study, the use of an LMA during liver transplantation was associated with less postoperative noninvasive ventilation, lower incidence of postoperative sore throat, and shorter postoperative hospital stay than management in the ETT group. However, the overall pattern of findings suggests that these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of an isolated airway-device effect. Even after matching, patients in the LMA group were more likely to receive a TAP block and patient-controlled intravenous analgesia and required less rocuronium during maintenance, indicating that LMA use occurred within a broader low-stimulation, recovery-oriented perioperative strategy. The marked between-group difference in immediate airway device removal was also more consistent with a preplanned extubation pathway and clinician selection than with the purely technical advantage of the device itself. Therefore, our results are better understood as showing that LMA can be incorporated into a recovery-oriented pathway in carefully selected liver transplant recipients, rather than proving that LMA independently improves postoperative recovery. This interpretation aligns with the current liver transplantation Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) literature, which emphasizes multimodal perioperative pathways, including early extubation, multimodal analgesia, early mobilization, and early nutritional recovery, rather than any single intervention in isolation [4,12,13].

One of the most consistent findings of the present study was the low incidence of postoperative sore throat in the LMA group. This observation is biologically plausible and consistent with previous airway literature [8,14]. Postoperative sore throat is largely related to airway instrumentation, mucosal trauma, cuff pressure, and the subsequent inflammatory response, and it is an important component of postoperative discomfort from the patient’s perspective [15,16]. Compared with ETT, an LMA avoids passage through the vocal cords and reduces direct tracheal mucosal compression, which may lessen laryngopharyngeal irritation [7]. In our cohort, the reduction in sore throat remained apparent both before and after matching, suggesting that this outcome is one of the few findings that may be reasonably be linked, at least in part, to intrinsic differences between the devices. This interpretation is supported by systematic reviews showing that postoperative sore throat is common after airway instrumentation and tends to occur less frequently with supraglottic airway devices than with tracheal intubation [8,14,16].

In addition to upper-airway comfort, another clinically relevant finding was the lower postoperative use of noninvasive ventilation in the LMA group. Although postoperative noninvasive ventilation is not equivalent to PPCs or reintubation, it may reflect greater respiratory instability after airway device removal and a greater need for transitional respiratory support. Several factors could explain this association. First, LMA use may be associated with less airway stimulation during insertion and removal, potentially contributing to smoother emergence and reduced post-extubation airway irritation. Second, the much higher rate of immediate airway device removal in the LMA group suggests a perioperative strategy favoring the earlier restoration of spontaneous breathing. Third, the more frequent use of the TAP block and postoperative PCIA in the LMA group may have facilitated more effective breathing mechanics and early postoperative mobilization. Accordingly, the apparent advantage of the LMA group may be most evident in the early recovery phase, rather than in the harder pulmonary endpoints. In this regard, a large retrospective cohort study by Hammer et al. found that, in adult noncardiac surgical cases where both airway approaches were considered feasible, supraglottic airway use was associated with a lower risk of emergent postoperative intubation than tracheal intubation, providing indirect support for a possible early recovery benefit in appropriately selected patients [17].

The incidence of PPCs, the incidence was numerically lower in the LMA group both before and after matching, but the difference was no longer statistically significant in the matched analysis. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Pulmonary complications after liver transplantation are multifactorial and are influenced by pre-existing pulmonary status, severity of liver disease, intraoperative hemodynamic instability, transfusion burden, fluid balance, reperfusion-related injury, postoperative respiratory management, and infection-related factors [18–20]. Therefore, it is unlikely that the airway devices alone determines the occurrence of PPCs in this setting. Our data support, at most, a possible protective trend associated with LMA use, but they do not establish a definitive effect on PPCs.

The higher rate of immediate airway device removal, the less use of postoperative NIV and the shorter postoperative hospital stay in the LMA group further supports the idea that LMA was used as part of a fast-track or recovery-oriented perioperative pathway. Contemporary ERAS recommendations for liver transplantation consistently recognize early extubation, multimodal analgesia, mobilization, and early oral or enteral intake as the core elements of recovery promotion and resource optimization [4,13,21]. In this context, our findings are clinically meaningful because they suggest that LMA is compatible with such a pathway in selected recipients. At the same time, these pathway-level differences also limit causal interpretation: the observed benefit cannot be attributed to the airway device alone, because the LMA group also differed in analgesic strategy and neuromuscular blocker use. Univariate regression analysis of NIV use and postoperative hospital stay further confirmed that LMA was indeed a factor contributing to shorter hospital stay and reduced NIV use. However, multivariable regression analysis suggested that postoperative recovery could not be attributed to LMA as a single factor, but might instead be determined by a combination of factors, including patients’ baseline physical status, postoperative complications, and perioperative management strategies. Therefore, the present data therefore support feasibility and compatibility with recovery-oriented care more strongly than they support the superiority of LMA as an isolated intervention.

Another consideration is the availability of transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) in the event of intraoperative haemodynamic instability. A conventional second-generation LMA may make probe insertion technically more difficult because the two devices share the oropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal space, and manipulation of the TOE probe may displace the LMA, impair its seal, or compromise ventilation, occasionally necessitating conversion to endotracheal intubation [22]. Nevertheless, accumulating evidence indicates that LMA use does not absolutely preclude TOE examination [22–24]. When adequate acoustic windows are obtainable, focused transthoracic echocardiography may serve as an initial alternative. If TOE is deemed necessary, careful probe insertion under direct visualization is recommended, with ongoing reassessment of device position, ventilatory adequacy, and air leakage [25]. In cases where intraoperative TOE is anticipated, an endotracheal tube or a dual-channel supraglottic airway specifically designed for endoscopic procedures may be a more suitable option [23].

This study had several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, it was subject to selection bias and information bias. Some important variables were not routinely recorded and could not be compared, including preoperative oxygen requirement and oxygen saturation, gastric residual volume, peak airway pressure, and oropharyngeal leak pressure during surgery. Second, although propensity score ­matching reduced measured baseline imbalance, unmeasured confounding may have remained, such as anesthesiologist preference, extubation plans, analgesic management, ICU practice, and postoperative rehabilitation. Third, the matched sample size was small, especially in the LMA group, which limited the power to detect rare outcomes, such as PPCs and major airway events. Fourth, most recipients were male, and the small number of female recipients prevented sex-specific analysis. Thus, the findings may not be fully generalizable to female recipients. Finally, the groups remained imbalanced with respect to TAP block, postoperative analgesia, PCIA, and neuromuscular blocker exposure, reinforcing the possibility that the observed differences may reflect overall perioperative management strategy rather than the airway device alone.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in carefully selected liver transplant recipients, LMA use within a recovery-oriented perioperative strategy may be safe and feasible and is associated with favorable early recovery outcomes, including less postoperative noninvasive ventilation, less postoperative sore throat, and shorter postoperative hospital stay. These findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating an isolated causal effect of the airway devices. Larger multicenter prospective studies with more standardized perioperative pathways and more comprehensive respiratory outcome definitions are needed to determine whether LMA have an independent effect on pulmonary complications, ventilation-related outcomes, or other clinically important transplant endpoints.
