Concordance of HER2 status between primary tumor and circulating tumor cells in breast cancer
Authors: Peipei Xie, Xiaoli Zhang, Tianyi Liu, Yuchun Song, Qi Zhang, Duo Wan, Shijia Wang, Shulian Wang, Wen Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring tumor progression, HER2 expression undergoes dynamic changes. Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) can be used to monitor HER2 expression in real-time and hold potential for clinical application. This study aimed to evaluate the consistency of HER2 expression between primary tumors and CTCs in patients with breast cancer (BC).MethodsWe used a previously established telomerase reverse transcriptase-based CTC detection method (TBCD) combined with anti-HER2 antibody to detect CTC and HER2-positive CTC (HER2 + CTC) in 4 ml of peripheral blood from patients with breast cancer prior to radiotherapy. The results indicated that the status of HER2 in CTC was inconsistent with the histological results.ResultsDiscordance in HER2 status between primary tumor and CTC was observed in 32.6% of patients (kappa value = 0.325, p = 0.03). And among patients with histologically HER2-negative breast cancer, the detection rate of HER2 + CTC was 32.1% (9/28).ConclusionsWe found that the HER2 status of CTC in peripheral blood was inconsistent with the histological findings. Further research should explore the clinical significance of detecting HER2-positive CTCs, and it is desired that real-time HER2 status testing of CTCs could hold potential value for patients with breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01663-0.Keywords: Circulating tumor cells, HER2, Breast cancer, Consistency

Introduction

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), also known as Erb-B2, is a trans-membrane tyrosine kinase receptor belonging to the ERBB/HER family. It initiates signal transduction through intracellular pathways, such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK, which play a crucial role in the survival and proliferation of cancer cells. HER2 is associated with poor prognosis in various types of cancer [1], and has become an important therapeutic target in various cancers. Targeted drugs against HER2, including monoclonal antibodies (e.g., trastuzumab, pertuzumab), tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., lapatinib, tucatinib), and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) such as ado-trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) and trastuzumab deruxtecan, have significantly improved outcomes of primary and metastatic gastric cancer, breast cancer (BC), and other malignancies [2–4].

HER2 expression is typically histologically assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) of tumor tissue samples obtained from surgical or needle biopsies. According to the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guideline, only HER2-positive patients are recommended to receive HER2-targeted therapy [5]. However, many studies have shown that HER2-negative breast cancer patients can also benefit from HER2-targeted therapy, especially HER2-targeted ADCs [6–11], which means that a large proportion of breast cancer patients who can finally benefit from HER2-targeted treatment strategies are neglected. Additionally, several key issues still have to be resolved. The use of small volume biopsy specimens can lead to false-negative HER2 results. Moreover, HER2 status between primary and recurrent tumors is usually inconsistent, Shi et al. found that among 247 patients with recurrent breast cancer, the overall inconsistency rate of HER2 status between primary tumors and recurrent tumors was 38.1% [12]. Furthermore, HER2 expression changes dynamically during tumor progression. Research by Tarantino et al. indicates that histological expression of HER2 changed after neoadjuvant treatment in 29.5% (319/1080) of breast cancer patients, and compared to HER2-positive patients, HER2-0 and HER2-low patients were more likely to have changes in HER2 expression after neoadjuvant treatment (32.3% vs 21.3%, p < 0.001) [13].

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) are viable tumor cells that shed from the primary tumor and enter the circulatory system, carrying genetic and phenotypic information specific to the tumor [14–16]. CTCs may offer a promising alternative for real-time HER2 testing, with the advantage of being less invasive. Numerous studies have evaluated differences in HER2 expression between tumor tissues and CTCs using various capture technologies [7, 17, 18]. However, the methods used in different studies vary significantly, and there is currently a lack of efficient, rapid, and cost-effective capture technologies for CTC detection. The achievement of highly specific capture, isolation, and downstream analysis of extremely rare CTCs from billions of background cells remains a challenge. These limitations greatly hinder the clinical application of the evaluation of CTC HER2 status.

In this study, we used a previously established telomerase reverse transcriptase-based detection technique (TBCD) [19–21], combined with anti-HER2 antibody to evaluate its performance in detecting HER2-positive CTC (HER2 + CTC). Furthermore, we assessed the concordance of HER2 expression between peripheral blood and tumor tissue samples from patients with unresectable or recurrent advanced breast cancer and analyzed the correlation between the characteristics of patients and CTC.

Methods

Clinical study design

From 2022 to 2024, 60 BC patients were recruited from the Cancer Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. The inclusion criteria included written informed consent, histologically or cytologically confirmed BC, availability of primary lesion HER2 status via IHC/FISH (HER2-positive criteria: IHC 3 + and IHC2 + , FISH + ; HER2-negative criteria: IHC2 + and FISH-, IHC1 + , IHC0), and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 0 or 1. The exclusion criteria included severe cardiac dysfunction, such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50% or congestive heart failure (CHF) ≥ grade 2 (CTCAE v5.0 or New York Heart Association classification ≥ grade2).

Before radiotherapy, 4 ml of peripheral blood (PB) was collected for CTC detection. Peripheral blood CTC and HER2 status on CTC were detected using telomerase reverse transcriptase-based CTC detection method (TBCD) combined with anti-HER2 antibody. In patients with CTC ≥ 1, the concordance ofHER2 status between primary tumors and CTCs was analyzed.

Blood sampling and CTC detection

The process of detecting HER2 + CTC is depicted in Fig. 1. PB samples of 4 ml were collected in EDTA tubes, stored at 4 °C, and processed within one hour of collection. The blood samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min to discard the plasma layer. Red blood cells were lysed with a lysis buffer (0.15 M NH4Cl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 10 mM KHCO3; pH = 7.2) at a ratio of 1:10 (blood cell to lysis buffer). The samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in 5 ml of sterile 1 × PBS and gently washed. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 2 ml of sterile serum-free medium for plating. Cells were transduced with oHSV1-hTERTp-eGFP (MOI = 1) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h. Subsequently, 2 ml of serum-free medium was added and cells were further incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Transduced cells were collected and stained with anti-CD45 and anti-HER2 antibodies. After washing with PBS, flow cytometry was performed to identify CTCs and HER2 + CTCs. “CD45-/GFP+” cells were recorded as CTCs, and “CD45-/GFP+/HER2+” cells were recorded as HER2 + CTCs.

Procedure for detecting HER2 + CTC in peripheral blood using TBCD combined with anti-HER2 antibody. Four milliliters of PBwere collected in 4 ml of EDTA tubes. After lysing red blood cells, cells transduced with oHSV1-hTERTp-eGFP (MOI = 1) were incubated in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 16-24 h. The transduced cells were collected and stained with an anti-CD45 antibody, anti-HER2 antibody. Flow cytometry was used to identify different subtypes of CTC

Identification of CTCs using amnis ImageStream MK II

Standard CTC detection protocols were applied to 4 ml of PB. Processed blood samples were incubated with APC-Cy7 labeled anti-CD45 antibody (Biosciences) and APC-labeled anti-HER2 antibody (BioLegend). Detection was performed using Amnis ImageStream MK II (Luminex), and imaging data analysis was performed using IDEAS 6.2 software.

Study approval

This study was carried out according to the recommendations of the Ethics Committee of the Cancer Institute and Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, with written informed consent obtained from all participants (19/317-2101).

Statistics

Chi-square and kappa tests were used to compare HER2 status differences between tumor tissues and CTC. The nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis test were used to compare patients’ CTC, HER2 + CTC, and Ki67 levels (asymmetric and continuous data). Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to characterize the correlation between CTC and Ki67 levels. All statistical analyses were two-sided, conducted using R 4.3.2, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

TBCD combined with anti-HER2 antibody is a feasible method to detect HER2 + CTC

To detect HER2 + CTC in the peripheral blood of these patients, we employed TBCD combined with anti-HER2 antibody. We analyzed the expression of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and HER2 in MCF7 and T47D using the CCLE cell line database and validated the findings through flow cytometry antibody staining (Fig. 2a, b). In TERT-positive cancer cell lines (MCF7 and T47D), oHSV1-hTERT-GFP induced GFP expression within 24 h after transduction (Fig. 2c).Using TERT and HER2 expressing MCF7 and T47D cell lines, we conducted spike-in and recovery rate experiments by adding 5, 20, 100, and 200 tumor cells into 4 ml of healthy human peripheral blood. The results showed a recovery rate with correlation coefficients of r2 = 0.9986 and r2 = 0.9963 (Fig. 2d). Additionally, flow imaging technology was used to visualize CTC, HER2 + CTC, TERT + white blood cells (WBCs), and normal WBCs captured from peripheral blood from BC patients, where TBCD combined with anti-HER2 antibody was also used. The results indicated that, compared to WBCs, CTCs were larger in size and had relatively irregular shapes. At the molecular phenotype level, CTCs exhibited high GFP expression (TERT +) and deficient expression of leukocyte common antigen (CD45), while HER2 + CTC showed high GFP expression (TERT +) and HER2 expression, without CD45 expression. Conversely, all WBCs expressed high levels of CD45 and were identified as CD45+/TERT-. However, a small subset of WBCs expressed high levels of both CD45 and GFP, indicating a CD45+/TERT+ phenotype. This finding suggests that our technique effectively captured HER2 + CTC (Fig. 2e).

Patient characteristics

From 2022 to 2024, we enrolled 43 patients with breast cancer finally. The study design is illustrated in Fig. 3. The median age of the patients was 48 years, with a range from 27 to 69 years. Among patients with detectable CTC, 34.9% (15/43) of the patients were confirmed histologically HER2 positive in the primary tumor, and 65.1% (28/43) of the patients were HER2 negative. The molecular types of breast cancer patients included Luminal A (2.3%), Luminal B (72.1%), HER2 + (16.3%,) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) (9.3%). And 86.1% (37/43) of tumor tissues had high levels of Ki67 index (over or equal to 14% [22]). The clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1.

HER2 expression in CTC and primary tumor

We evaluated the concordance between histological and CTC HER2 status in patients with BC using TBCD combined with anti-HER2 antibody staining. The detection of CTC was carried out within one year of the histological biopsy. The results showed that the total detection rate of CTC in all patients is 71.7% (43/60), and in histologically HER2 positive (hHER2 +) patients, the detection rate of HER2 + CTC (cHER2 +) was only 66.7% (10/15) (the rate in IHC3 + and IHC2 + , FISH + patients is 69.2% (9/13) and 50.0% (1/2) respectively), whereas in histologically HER2 negative (hHER2 -) patients, the detection rate of HER2 + CTC was 32.1% (9/28) (the rate in IHC2 + , FISH-; IHC1 + and IHC0 patients is 16.7% (2/12), 54.5%(6/11) and 20.0% (1/5) respectively) as showed in Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Table 1. And HER2 discordance between primary tumor and CTC was observed in 32.6% (14/43) of the patients. These findings indicated a significant discordance between CTC and histological HER2 status (kappa value = 0.325, p = 0.030) as showed in Table 2. Furthermore, we also found that although the total number of CTC was not correlated with the Ki67 index (r2 = 0.026, p = 0.87), the number of HER2 + CTC was correlated with the Ki67 index (r2 = 0.38, p = 0.011), as shown in Fig. 4c and d. Compared to cHER2 - patients, cHER2 + patients had significantly higher levels of Ki67 index (p = 0.0106) and more CTCs (p = 0.0387), suggesting that HER2 + CTC may be associated with a more malignant tumor proliferation phenotype (Fig. 4e, f). Furthermore, HER2 positive CTC in estrogen receptor (ER)/progesterone receptor (PR) negative patients was significantly higher than those in ER/PR positive patients (p = 0.0132) (Fig. 4g).

Discussion

HER2-targeted therapy has significantly improved the treatment efficacy and prognosis of breast cancer patients [23–25]. Typically, only patients with HER2-positive tumors receive this therapy, based on initial histological assessments [5, 7]. However, HER2 expression in tumor samples can vary with disease progression and treatment. Tarantino et al. indicated that histological expression of HER2 changed after neoadjuvant treatment in 29.5% (319/1080) of breast cancer patients, and compared to HER2-positive patients, HER2-negative patients were more likely to have changes in HER2 expression after neoadjuvant treatment (32.3% vs. 21.3%, p < 0.001) [13]. These findings suggest that HER2-positive CTCs have potential guiding value for HER2-targeted therapy in breast cancer patients.

CTC-based liquid biopsy captures heterogeneous tumor cells and represents a promising approach for noninvasive detection, screening, and prognosis monitoring in cancer. This characteristic of CTC addresses some of the limitations of biopsy, thereby enhancing the comprehensive monitoring of HER2 status for HER2-targeted therapy [16]. Currently, the detection of HER2 + CTC involves various enrichment techniques to isolate CTC, followed by immunofluorescence and FISH to detect HER2 expression in CTC. Enrichment techniques primarily rely on surface antigen differences in CTC, utilizing antibodies, peptides, aptamers, etc., to bind to the surface antigens of CTC. Magnetic fields or other forces are then used to separate CTC, as demonstrated in Food and Drug Administration(FDA)-approved systems, such as CellSearch, which employs immunomagnetic selection based on EpCAM expression combined with negative selection for CD45, pan-CK, and other markers to enhance the sensitivity and specificity of CTC enrichment. Furthermore, differences in the intrinsic characteristics of CTC compared to those of blood cells, such as size, density, deformability, and electrical properties, can aid in their separation. Following enrichment, HER2 expression on CTC can be further characterized using fluorescently labeled anti-HER2 antibodies or FISH techniques [7, 26, 27].

Alternatively, amplification of the HER2 gene copy number in CTC can be effectively identified by FISH [9, 10]. Our study enriches CTC with infinite proliferative potential and high telomerase activity. In tumors, telomerase activity is reactivated, with approximately 90% of the tumor cells exhibiting abnormal telomerase activity. Using specific tools, such as virus infection targeting cells with high telomerase activity and labeling them with green fluorescence, this method effectively detects epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and EpCAM-negative CTC [20, 21, 28]. To overcome the limitations of tumor heterogeneity, active tumor cells are captured using fluorescently labeled anti-HER2 antibody for immunofluorescence staining, enabling the successful detection of HER2 + CTC with high precision and reliability.

In our study, we found that the HER2 status of CTC does not always correlate with hHER2 expression. Among BC patients, the positive detection rate of cHER2 + was 32.1% in hHER2- patients. Our study showed that the incidence of discordance in HER2 status between cHER2 status and hHER2 expression among all patients is 32.6% (kappa value = 0.325, p = 0.03). Consistent with previous research, there were discrepancies in HER2 expression between primary tumor cells and CTC in cancer patients. For instance, Wang et al. found a statistically significant difference in HER2 status between CTC and primary tumor in breast cancer (kappa value = 0.191, p = 0.012) [7]. Pestrin et al. found non-concordant results in 32% of advanced breast cancer cases, and 29% of HER2-negative primary tumors had HER2-positive CTCs (kappa value = 0.278) [17]. De Gregorio et al. found that the discordance in HER2 phenotype between primary tumor and CTCs was observed in 134 (18.8%) HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer patients [18]. The discordance between cHER2 status and hHER2 expression may be attributed to tumor heterogeneity, biological changes in tumors over time, and the impact of prior treatments on clonal subpopulations. Furthermore, our research indicates that HER2 + CTC is closely related to malignant proliferation phenotypes such as the Ki67 index, suggesting that we need to pay more attention to cHER2 + patients.

It is worth noting that there is no unified standard method for HER2-positive CTC detection, and there are still multiple technical challenges and difficulties, which may also contribute to the HER2 downgrading on CTCs. In breast cancer, the concordance rate of HER2 expression in tumors and CTCs obtained varies due to the different sensitivity and specificity of different assays. Chen et al. found HER2-positive CTCs in 41.9% (13/31) of patients with histological HER2-positive patients by using the LiquidBiopsy system [29]. Wang et al. found 75.0% (12/16) of histological HER2-positive patients with HER2-positive CTCs by a filtration-based microfluidic device for HER2-positive CTCs detection [30]. In addition, problems during peripheral blood sample collection, storage, and processing operations, as well as isolation issues associated with the rarity of CTCs, may also be responsible for the inconsistency in the expression of HER2 between primary tumors and CTCs.

Multiple studies have shown that in patients with primary tumors and/or metastatic tumors that are HER2-negative, the HER2-positive status of CTC has potential implications for targeted therapy. Patients with hHER2- but cHER2 + may benefit from HER2-targeted treatments. Fehm et al. conducted a phase III clinical trial in patients with metastatic breast cancer, focusing on 101 cHER2 + patients. These patients were randomized to receive treatment with the HER2-targeted drug lapatinib. The results showed that, compared to the control group, patients with HER2 + CTC who received lapatinib treatment experienced a significant improvement in OS (20.5 vs 9.1 months, p = 0.009) [27]. Wang et al. used peptide-functionalized magnetic nanoparticles (Pep@MNPs) to detect CTC in 52 patients with advanced breast cancer. Among 24 patients who were hHER2 + and received HER2-targeted treatment, only cHER2 + patients achieved objective responses (60.0%), while cHER2- patients did not (p = 0.034) [7]. The above results indicate that it is essential to assess HER2 expression on CTC in patients with advanced breast cancer, as this assessment holds significant importance for guiding adjustments in targeted therapy regimens for patients.

In conclusion, our research showed that in patients with breast cancer, the HER2 expression of primary tumor and CTC is not completely consistent. Our HER2 + CTC detection technology can complement the histological detection of HER2 biopsy and may become a potential tool to guide HER2-targeted treatment of breast cancer patients. However, our small sample size may introduce biases and affect outcomes to some degree. Our future research should involve a larger sample size and more rigorously designed clinical trials to validate and strengthen our current findings.
