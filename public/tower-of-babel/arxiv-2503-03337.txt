A unified approach for degree bound estimates of linear differential operators
Louis Gaillard

Abstract
. We identify a common scheme in several existing algorithms addressing computational problems on linear differential equations with polynomial coefficients. These algorithms reduce to computing a linear relation between vectors obtained as iterates of a simple differential operator known as pseudo-linear map . We focus on establishing precise degree bounds on the output of this class of algorithms. It turns out that in all known instances (least common left multiple, symmetric product,…), the bounds that are derived from the linear algebra step using Cramer’s rule are pessimistic. The gap with the behaviour observed in practice is often of one order of magnitude, and better bounds are sometimes known and derived from ad hoc methods and independent arguments. We propose a unified approach for proving output degree bounds for all instances of the class at once. The main technical tools come from the theory of realisations of matrices of rational functions and their determinantal denominators.

Introduction

In this work, we study four central algorithmic

We specifically focus on the Hermite

(

)
, the computation
of a differential equation for an algebraic function
(

)

and two closure operations on D-finite functions: least common

(

,

)

and symmetric product
(

,

)
.
These four problems can all be seen as particular instances
of a large class of algorithmic problems that share the

Let

be a field of

be the usual derivation on the

\displaystyle a_{0}\in k[x]^{n},\quad a_{i+1}=\left(\partial_{x}+T\right)\cdot a_{i},~i\geq 0,

is a

In (

is an example of a

(

a_{i}=\theta^{i}a_{0}
.
In other words, we consider the following problem.

Problem 1.

Given

and

\rho=\dim\spn_{k(x)}(\theta^{i}a,i\geq 0)\leq n

\eta=(\eta_{0},\dots,\eta_{\rho})\in k[x]^{\rho+1}\setminus\{0\}
such that

(2)

\eta_{\rho}\cdot\theta^{\rho}a+\cdots+\eta_{1}\cdot\theta a+\eta_{0}\cdot a=0.

In the case
\rho=n
,
i.e
.

is a cyclic vector
for

T

(
11
,
7
,
2
,
5
)
,
the above relation

(

)

Problem

of the method of Krylov iterates
for computing the characteristic

(

in the differential context.
As further developed in Section

problems mentioned above reduces to solving

with a specific choice of

Hermite reduction-based creative telescoping

Creative telescoping refers to a family of methods that have proven successful
for symbolic integration and summation. The general
concept was first introduced by
Zeilberger
(
28
)
and it applies to a
large class of functions. Here we focus on the simple
integration of a bivariate rational function

f(x,y)=p/q\in k(x,y)\setminus\{0\}
with
p
and
q
coprime.
For any integration domain
\omega
, the definite integral

F(x)=\int_{\omega}f(x,y)\,dy

is known to be D-finite and the problem consists in computing a linear differential equation
satisfied by
F
. The heart of the method relies on finding a

telescoper
for
f
, namely a linear differential operator

L\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>\setminus\{0\}
such that

L(x,\partial_{x})\cdot f(x,y)=\partial_{y}\cdot h(x,y)
for some rational function

h
. Under reasonable assumptions on
h
and
\omega
, the operator
L
is then proved
to annihilate
F
.
Performing Hermite
reduction
(
14
)
on
\partial_{x}^{i}(f)

iteratively enables to compute the coefficients of
L
as a
solution to
Problem
1

(
6
)
.

Differential equation for an algebraic function

A power series
\alpha(x)
over the field
k
is said to be
algebraic

if it is a root of a nonzero bivariate polynomial
P(x,y)\in k[x,y]
.
It is known that such an algebraic function is also
D-finite
(
1
, p. 287)
,
and we call
differential resolvent

an operator annihilating all the roots of
P
.
Cockle’s algorithm
(
12
,
8
)

computes a differential resolvent
as a linear relation between the images of
\alpha^{(i)}

in the quotient ring
k(x)[y]/(P)
that is a finite dimensional
vector space over
k(x)
.

Least common left multiple and symmetric product

D-finite functions are closed under sums and
products, and the algorithms
derived from the proof are expressible as instances
of Problem
1

(
26
, Thm. 2.3)
.
Efficient algorithms for these two operations are
needed for manipulating D-finite functions represented by
annihilating operators
(
24
,
25
)
.
For
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
, a minimal
order linear differential operator
that annihilates all the sums (resp. products)
of solutions of the
L_{i}
’s is called a
least common left
multiple (LCLM) (resp. symmetric product).

Our thesis is that studying Problem
1

can contribute to a deeper understanding
of the four specific instances mentioned above, since
this common formulation underlines a shared structure between these problems.
Following this idea, we specifically propose a unified approach to prove
better degree bounds on the polynomials
\eta_{i}
in (
2
)
(or to retrieve the best known
bounds)
for these four instances at once.
Such bounds are useful as they provide estimates on the output size of algorithms
addressing these problems, and thus determine the notion of optimal algorithms in an
algebraic model of complexity. In addition, there are examples
for algorithms whose running time
is dependent on the output
size
(
27
,
8
)
.
In this setting, proving tight degree bounds is of key importance for
complexity analysis.

A straightforward approach to obtain degree bounds is to consider
\eta

as the solution of the linear system defined
in (
2
) and to derive degree
bounds using classical techniques such as Cramer’s
rule
(
6
,
18
)
.
By doing so, if

is a degree bound on both the numerator and the

stated and proved in Section
3

Experiments suggest that this bound is reached for
generic

instances of Problem

our four instances, in which the matrix
T
possesses a structure,
the bound above is an overestimation of the actual degrees.
For instance, let us consider the case of the algorithm based on Hermite’s reduction
applied to a rational function

with

(resp.

) a degree bound
in

(resp.

By applying Cramer’s rule in the linear algebra step

the degree of the telescoper is bounded by

O(d_{y}^{3}d_{x})

(
6

.
However, an independent analysis based on the Almkvist-Zeilberger
algorithm for rational functions
(
3
)
yields a better
bound in
O(d_{y}^{2}d_{x})

(
6
, Thm. 25)
.
The aim of this
work is to explain the gap for each instance we study.

2.1.3.
Strict properness

It remains to study when the matrix

is strictly proper in order to

1

Let
\Delta=\det M
. Since

Lemma 0.

Up to a sign,

in

Let

)

the matrix of the multiplication by

,

, and

the matrix of

Therefore, one can write

q
do not belong to a certain nontrivial
hypersurface. In other words, this is a genericity assumption.
Thus, in the generic situation, we retrieve the best known bound
on the degree of a minimal telescoper for a bivariate rational
function
(
6
, Thm. 25)
, namely
a degree governed by
2d_{y}^{2}d_{x}
.
This bound was derived from the analysis of the Almkvist-Zeilberger
algorithm for rational functions
(
3
)
.
This is the first time a degree
bound in
O(d_{y}^{2}d_{x})
is derived directly from the Hermite reduction-based
algorithm.

2.2.
Differential resolvent

Let
P(x,y)\in k[x,y]
be square-free with respect to
y
and let

d_{x}
and
d_{y}
be the degrees in
x
and
y
of
P
. We write

P_{x}=\partial_{x}(P)
and
P_{y}=\partial_{y}(P)
.
In this section,
\mathcal{A}
again denotes the vector space
k(x)[y]_{<d_{y}}
.

2.2.1.
Reduction to Problem
1

Following the lines
of
(
8
, Sec. 2.1)
, we first show that a
differential resolvent of
P
can be computed as a solution of
Problem
1
. Let

\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d_{y}}
be the roots of
P
in the algebraic
closure of
k(x)
. For any root
\alpha
of
P
, by differentiating

P(x,\alpha)=0
and since
P
is square-free, we have

\partial_{x}(\alpha)=-P_{x}(x,\alpha)/P_{y}(x,\alpha)
.
Hence the derivation

\partial_{x}
uniquely extends to
k(x)(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d_{y}})
. By
induction, for all
i\geq 1
, there exists a polynomial

C_{i}\in k[x,y]
such that

\alpha^{(i)}=C_{i}(x,\alpha)/P_{y}(x,\alpha)^{2i-1}
,
for any root
\alpha
of
P
. Since
P
and

P_{y}
are coprime, one can define
D_{i}(x,y)
to be the polynomial
in
\mathcal{A}

such that
D_{i}(x,\alpha)=\alpha^{(i)}

for all roots
\alpha
of
P
. Note that
D_{0}=y
.
And thus it suffices to find the minimal
linear dependence between the
D_{i}
’s to compute the differential
resolvent of
P
.

Moreover, by differentiating
\alpha^{(i)}=D_{i}(x,\alpha)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha^{(i+1)}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}(D_{i}(x,\alpha))+\partial_{x}(\alpha)\cdot\partial_{y}(D_{i}(x,\alpha))

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}(D_{i}(x,\alpha))-\frac{P_{x}(x,\alpha)}{P_{y}(x,\alpha)}\partial_{y}(D_{i}(x,\alpha)).

Therefore
D_{i+1}=(\partial_{x}+T)\cdot D_{i}
, where
T
is the
k(x)
-linear
map
T\colon a\mapsto-\partial_{y}(a)P_{x}/P_{y}~\mathrm{mod}~P
on

\mathcal{A}
. Finally, the
differential resolvent of
P
is the solution of
Problem
1
for
(T,y)
.

2.2.2.
Realisation of
T

Fix the monomial basis
(1,y,\dots,y^{d_{y}-1})
of

\mathcal{A}
and view
T
as a matrix in
k(x)^{d_{y}\times d_{y}}
.

Lemma 0.

There exist polynomial matrices
X,M
and
Y
, with
M
invertible
and
\deg\det M\leq(2d_{y}-1)d_{x}
such that
T=XM^{-1}Y
.

Proof.

Recall that
Ta
is the solution
V
of the following
Bézout equation

\displaystyle-\partial_{y}(a)P_{x}=U\cdot P+V\cdot P_{y}.

So if

Y\colon a\in\mathcal{A}\mapsto-\partial_{y}(a)P_{x}\in k(x)[y]_{<2d_{y}-1}
and
X
is the projection
(U,V)\mapsto V
onto the
last
d_{y}
coordinates, we have
T=XM^{-1}Y
where
M
is the
Sylvester map associated to
P
and
P_{y}
and
X,M,Y
are three
polynomial matrices. So
\det M
is the resultant of
P
and
P_{y}

(with respect to
y
) and it has degree at most
(2d_{y}-1)d_{x}
.
∎

2.2.3.
Strict properness

An analysis similar to that
of Section
2.1
shows that it
suffices that
M^{-1}=O(1/x^{d_{x}})
for
T
to be strictly
proper. This is in particular the case when
\det M
has maximal
degree
(2d_{y}-1)d_{x}
,
i.e
.
, when
\res_{y}(P,P_{y})
has maximal degree.
By Lemma
3
, this property
holds when the coefficient of degree
d_{x}
of
P
in
x
has degree

d_{y}
and is square-free.

2.2.4.
Degree bound

Theorem 6.

Let
P\in k[x,y]
be square-free with respect to
y
,

(d_{x},d_{y})=(\deg_{x}P,\deg_{y}P)
. If the coefficient
of degree
d_{x}
of
P
in
x
has degree

d_{y}
and is square-free, then there exists a differential resolvent of

P
of order
r\leq d_{y}
and degree at most

r(2d_{y}-1)d_{x}-1/2\cdot r(r-1)
.

Proof.

Under this condition, the matrix
T
is strictly proper. Moreover, it has
realisation
T=XM^{-1}Y
with
\deg\det M\leq(2d_{y}-1)d_{x}
by
Lemma
5
. The claimed
bound follows from Theorem
1
.
∎

Again, the condition above is a genericity assumption on
P
.
Our bound is in
O(d_{y}^{2}d_{x})
and governed by
2d_{y}^{2}d_{x}
.
The previous best known bound behaves like

4d_{y}^{2}d_{x}

(
8
, Thm. 1)
and its
proof is independent from Cockle’s algorithm.
So this is an improvement of the constant from
4
to
2
in
the generic situation.

Experimentally if we assume that
d_{x}=d_{y}=d
, we observe that the
output degree is bounded by
d(2d^{2}-3d+3)
. Our bound gives

d(2d^{2}-3d/2+1/2)
and the previous bound
is
d(4d^{2}-11d/2+7/2)

(
8
)
. This is
the first time that a bound with the first term
2d^{3}
is obtained.

2.3.
Least common left multiple

Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}
be linear
differential operators in
k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>

and let
L=\lclm(L_{1},\dots,L_{s})
be a least common left multiple.
Again, we apply Theorem
1
to
bound the degree of
L
and compare with the existing
bound
(
9
)
.
For simplicity, we first assume
s=2
and
L=\lclm(L_{1},L_{2})
as the
approach will be easily generalised for arbitrary
s
.

2.3.1.
Reduction to Problem
1

and realisation

We follow the standard approach recalled in
(
9
, Sec. 4.2.2)

or
(
19
, Algo. 4.27)
.
Let
r_{1}
,

r_{2}
be the respective orders of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, and

\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
be the sum of generic solutions of
L_{1}
and

L_{2}
respectively. The coefficients of
L
are read from a linear relation
between the successive derivatives of
\alpha
. These derivatives all
lie in the finite dimensional vector space spanned by

A=(\alpha_{1},\dots\alpha_{1}^{(r_{1}-1)},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{2}^{(r_{2}-1)})
. Let

L_{1}=p_{1,r_{1}}\partial_{x}^{r_{1}}+\cdots+p_{1,0}
and

L_{2}=p_{2,r_{2}}\partial_{x}^{r_{2}}+\cdots+p_{2,0}
with
p_{1,r_{1}}
and

p_{2,r_{2}}
nonzero. By induction suppose that for
\ell\geq 0
, we have
written

\alpha^{(\ell)}=\sum_{j=0}^{r_{1}-1}a_{j}\alpha_{1}^{(j)}+\sum_{j=0}^{r_{2}-1}b_{j}\alpha_{2}^{(j)}
with
a_{j},b_{j}\in k(x)
.
Then differentiation yields

\displaystyle\alpha^{(\ell+1)}=\sum_{j=0}^{r_{1}-1}\left(a_{j}^{\prime}\alpha_{1}^{(j)}+a_{j}\alpha_{1}^{(j+1)}\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{r_{2}-1}\left(b_{j}^{\prime}\alpha_{2}^{(j)}+b_{j}\alpha_{2}^{(j+1)}\right).

Finally, we use
L_{1}(\alpha_{1})=L_{2}(\alpha_{2})=0
to rewrite

\alpha_{1}^{(r_{1})}
and
\alpha_{2}^{(r_{2})}
on the generating set
A
.
So if
V_{\ell}
denotes
the vector of coefficients of
\alpha^{(\ell)}
in
A
for all
\ell
, we
obtain
V_{\ell+1}=(\partial_{x}+T)\cdot V_{\ell}
, where

T\in k(x)^{(r_{1}+r_{2})\times(r_{1}+r_{2})}
is the block diagonal
matrix
\diag(C_{1},C_{2})
with
C_{i}
the companion matrix associated to

L_{i}
,
i.e
.

C_{i}\in k(x)^{r_{i}\times r_{i}}
is companion with last
column
-1/p_{i,r_{i}}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}p_{i,0}&\cdots&p_{i,r_{i}-1}\end{bmatrix}^{t}
.
But such a block companion matrix
T
is clearly not strictly proper
since some of its entries are
1
.

In order to be able to apply Theorem
1

we propose
to express the problem in a different generating set. Let
\mathfrak{d}=x\partial_{x}
,
also known as the Euler operator. We choose to write the successive
derivatives of
\alpha
as linear combinations of elements of
the new generating set

B=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{d}^{r_{1}-1}\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\mathfrak{d}^{r_{2}-1}\alpha_{2})
. Now if
W_{\ell}
denotes the vector of
coefficients of
\alpha^{(\ell)}
in
B
for all
\ell
, by differentiating
we get that
W_{\ell+1}=(\partial_{x}+T_{\mathfrak{d}})\cdot W_{\ell}
with

T_{\mathfrak{d}}\in k(x)^{(r_{1}+r_{2})\times(r_{1}+r_{2})}
the matrix whose
columns are the derivatives of the elements of
B
expressed on
B
.
Notice that for
i=1,2
and
\ell\geq 0
, we have

(\mathfrak{d}^{\ell}\alpha_{i})^{\prime}=1/x\cdot\mathfrak{d}^{\ell+1}\alpha_{i}
. Also, any
linear differential operator in
x
and
\partial_{x}
can be rewritten as a
linear differential operator of the same order in
x
and
\mathfrak{d}
with
the following degree bounds on the coefficients.

Lemma 0.

Let
L=\sum_{j=0}^{r}p_{j}(x)\partial_{x}^{j}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
of
order
r
with
d_{j}=\deg p_{j}
. There exists an operator

\tilde{L}=\sum_{j=0}^{r}q_{j}(x)\mathfrak{d}^{j}\in k[x]\left<\mathfrak{d}\right>
of order
r
whose solution set is the same
as
L
. Moreover, we have
\deg q_{r}=d_{r}
and

\deg q_{j}\leq r+\max_{\ell\geq j}(d_{\ell}-\ell)
for all
j
.

Proof.

The result is a direct consequence of the well-known identity

x^{j}\partial_{x}^{j}=\mathfrak{d}\cdots(\mathfrak{d}-j+1)
for all

j\geq 0

(
23
, p. 26)
.
∎

By Lemma
7
,

L_{i}=q_{i,r_{i}}(x)\mathfrak{d}^{r_{i}}+\cdots+q_{i,0}(x)
with

q_{i,r_{i}}
nonzero for
i=1,2
. Let
D_{i}
be the companion matrix of
size
r_{i}
whose last column is
-1/q_{i,r_{i}}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}q_{i,0}&\cdots&q_{i,r_{i}-1}\end{bmatrix}^{t}
.
One can write

T_{\mathfrak{d}}=1/x\cdot\diag(D_{1},D_{2})
.
Therefore
T_{\mathfrak{d}}=XM^{-1}
with

\displaystyle M=x\cdot\diag(1,\dots,1,-q_{1,r_{1}},1,\dots,1,-q_{2,r_{2}}),

and
X
the block diagonal matrix whose blocks are companion matrices
associated to
(q_{1,0},\dots,q_{1,r_{1}-1})
and

(q_{2,0},\dots,q_{2,r_{2}-1})
.

2.3.2.
Strict properness

The matrix
~T_{\mathfrak{d}}~
is strictly proper
when
\diag(D_{1},D_{2})=O(1)
and thus when

\deg(q_{i,j})\leq\deg(q_{i,r_{i}})
for all
j
and
i=1,2
.
By Lemma
7
, we deduce
that
\deg(q_{i,j})\leq\deg(q_{i,r_{i}})
when

\deg(p_{i,j})+r_{i}-j\leq\deg(p_{i,r_{i}})
for all
j
.
This is equivalent to
x=\infty
not being an irregular
singularity of
the equation defined by
L_{i}

(
23
, §20)
.
Hence, we conclude that if
x=\infty
is not an irregular
singularity of
L_{1}
nor of
L_{2}
, then
T_{\mathfrak{d}}
is strictly proper.

2.3.3.
Degree bound

Theorem 8.

Let
L_{1},L_{2}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
be of
respective orders
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, and degrees at most
d
in
x
.
Suppose
x=\infty
is not an irregular singularity of
L_{1}
nor of
L_{2}
.
Then there exists an LCLM
L
of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
of order
r\leq r_{1}+r_{2}
and
degree at most
r(r_{1}+r_{2}+2d)-1/2\cdot r(r-1)
.

Proof.

The coefficients of
L
can be directly derived from the solution to
Problem
1
with input
(T_{\mathfrak{d}},a)

where

\displaystyle a=(\overbrace{1,0,\dots,0}^{r_{1}},\overbrace{1,0,\dots,0}^{r_{2}})^{t}.

Besides,
T_{\mathfrak{d}}
is strictly
proper by assumption. Also,
T_{\mathfrak{d}}=XM^{-1}
with

\deg\det M\leq 2d+r_{1}+r_{2}
. Thus the result follows from
Theorem
1
.
∎

This result generalizes with
s\geq 2
differential equations
with no irregular singularity at infinity
and a matrix
T_{\mathfrak{d}}
with
s
different blocks. Let
r_{i}
denote
the order of
L_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq s
,
R=\sum_{i=1}^{s}r_{i}
and
d

be a bound on the degrees in
x
of all the
L_{i}
’s. A least common
left multiple
L=\lclm(L_{1},\dots,L_{s})
can be computed as the
solution of Problem
1
for

T_{\mathfrak{d}}=XM^{-1}\in k(x)^{R\times R}
and

\displaystyle a=(\overbrace{1,0,\dots,0}^{r_{1}},\dots,\overbrace{1,0,\dots,0}^{r_{s}})^{t},

where
X,M
are polynomial matrices. This time
\Delta=\det M
is
the product of
x^{R}
and the leading coefficients of the
L_{i}
’s (up
to a sign), so
\deg\Delta\leq sd+R
. Hence by
Theorem
1
, the order of
L
is less than
R

and its degree in
x
is bounded by
R(sd+R)
. This is slightly
larger than the bound
d(s(R+1)-R)

(
9
, Thm. 6)
, but
as
R\leq sd
since
x=\infty
is not an irregular point,
the leading term of both bounds
behaves like
dsR\leq ds^{2}\max_{i}(r_{i})
.

2.4.
Symmetric product

Let
L_{1},L_{2}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
and
L=L_{1}\otimes L_{2}

be a symmetric product. Let
r_{i}
be the order of
L_{i}

and
d_{i}
be its degree for
i=1,2
. For
i=1,2
, we write

L_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{r_{i}}p_{i,j}\partial_{x}^{j}
with
p_{i,j}\in k[x]
. It
is known that the order of
L
is at most
r_{1}r_{2}
and
the degree of
L

in

can be bounded by
r_{1}^{2}r_{2}^{2}(d_{1}+d_{2})

(
18
, Thm. 8)
.
However, experiments
suggest that this bound is not tight since one expects the degree of

to be bounded by
(r_{1}r_{2}-r_{1}-r_{2}+2)(d_{1}r_{2}+d_{2}r_{1})
.

2.4.1.
Reduction to Problem
1

By Leibniz’s rule, we express the successive derivatives of

\alpha=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
according to the elements

(\mathfrak{d}^{h}\alpha_{1}\mathfrak{d}^{p}\alpha_{2})
for
0\leq h<r_{1}
and

0\leq p<r_{2}
. We again use the Euler operator to satisfy the

and
suppose that we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{(\ell)}=\sum_{h=0}^{r_{1}-1}\sum_{p=0}^{r_{2}-1}e_{h,p}(x)b_{h,p},

. We denote by

the

in this decomposition.
Differentiating gives

\displaystyle\alpha^{(\ell+1)}=\sum_{h,p}\left(e_{h,p}^{\prime}b_{h,p}+1/x\cdot e_{h,p}(b_{h+1,p}+b_{h,p+1})\right).

Next the relations
L_{1}(\alpha_{1})=L_{2}(\alpha_{2})=0

rewrite
b_{r_{1},p}
and

in the expression above. Thus,

where

and

and

.
For

, let

For

In the basis

(b_{0,0},\dots,b_{0,r_{2}-1},b_{1,0},\dots,b_{r_{1}-1,r_{2}-1})

be seen as the matrix

(6)

\displaystyle T=1/x\cdot(D_{1}\otimes I_{r_{2}}+I_{r_{1}}\otimes D_{2})\in k(x)^{r_{1}r_{2}\times r_{1}r_{2}},

Also, the coefficients of

2.4.2.
Realisation of

We have

T=(1/x\cdot D_{1})\otimes I_{r_{2}}+I_{r_{1}}\otimes(1/x\cdot D_{2})

By Section

,

Next,

2.4.3.
Strict properness

From (

),

for

:

and

as an irregular singularity.

2.4.4.
Degree bound

Theorem 9.

Let
L_{1},L_{2}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
be of
respective orders
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
and degrees
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
.
Suppose

is not an irregular singularity of

nor of

.
Then

of

and degree at

Proof.

Under this assumption,
T
is strictly proper. The result is
implied by
Theorem
1

with
\deg\Delta=2r_{1}r_{2}+d_{1}r_{2}+d_{2}r_{1}
.
∎

Our new bound, although restricted to the assumption that the point at infinity is
not irregular, leads to an
improvement of the bound in

18

and

(

)

Our approach also generalizes to the computation of a symmetric
product of
s\geq 2
operators satisfying the same condition at infinity. Let

L=L_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{s}

of order

and

for all

.
Let

and

.

.
Let

\alpha=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{s}
where
L_{i}(\alpha_{i})=0
for all

successive derivatives of

for

,

.
Therefore

of Problem

with

. And one can write

.
Then,
\deg\det M\leq s(R+dr^{s-1})
so
L
is of order
at most
R
and degree bounded by
sR(R+dr^{s-1})=O(dr^{2s-1})
.
One should compare with the previous best known bound

sR^{2}d=O(dr^{2s})

(
18
, Thm. 8)
.

3.
Direct bound by Cramer’s rule

For the purpose of explaining our approach to show Theorem
1
,
we start by proving a bound that is directly obtained from the
formulation of Problem
1
:
\eta
is a
solution of a linear system. This is the bound one should expect by
adopting the most natural viewpoint on the input rational matrix

T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
,
i.e
.
viewing
T
as its numerator
N
which
is a polynomial matrix divided by its denominator
\mathrm{den}\in k[x]
.

Theorem 1.

Let
T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
with denominator
\mathrm{den}\in k[x]
, and

a\in k[x]^{n}
. Let
d=\deg(\mathrm{den})
,
D=\deg(\mathrm{den}\cdot T)
,

d_{a}=\deg a
and
\tilde{D}=\max(d-1,D)
. Then, there exists a
solution
\eta=(\eta_{0},\dots,\eta_{\rho})\in k[x]^{\rho+1}\setminus\{0\}

of
Problem
1
for
(T,a)
of the
form
\eta_{i}=\mathrm{den}^{i}p_{i}
with
p_{i}\in k[x]
of degree at most

\rho d_{a}+(1/2\cdot\rho(\rho+1)-i)\tilde{D}
.

Proof.

By induction on
i\geq 0
, we show that
\theta^{i}a=b_{i}/\mathrm{den}^{i}

with
b_{i}\in k[x]^{n}
of degree at most
d_{a}+i\tilde{D}
. This is
true for
i=0
and

\theta^{i+1}a=\partial_{x}(b_{i}/\mathrm{den}^{i})+T\cdot b_{i}/\mathrm{den}^{i}=1/\mathrm{den}^{i+1}(\mathrm{den}\cdot b_{i}^{\prime}-i\mathrm{den}^{\prime}\cdot b_{i}+(\mathrm{den}\cdot T)b_{i})
. So we
have
b_{i+1}=\mathrm{den}\cdot b_{i}^{\prime}-i\mathrm{den}^{\prime}\cdot b_{i}+(\mathrm{den}\cdot T)b_{i}
, and

\deg(b_{i+1})\leq\deg(b_{i})+\tilde{D}
, thus the claim holds.

Next consider the solution
\nu\in k(x)^{\rho}
of the linear system

K\nu=-\theta^{\rho}a
where
K=\begin{bmatrix}a&\theta a&\cdots&\theta^{\rho-1}a\end{bmatrix}\in k(x)^{n\times\rho}
.
The solution
\eta
of Problem
1
can
be read from
\nu
just by multiplying by the denominator of
\nu
.
By definition of
\rho
,
\rk(K)=\rho
so after a deletion of

(n-\rho)
appropriate rows in the previous system, we obtain an
equivalent square system
\tilde{K}\nu=c
, where
\tilde{K}
and

c
are obtained from
K
and
-\theta^{\rho}a
after the deletion of
rows.

By Cramer’s rule, we have
\nu_{i}=\det K_{i}/\det\tilde{K}
for all

0\leq i<\rho
where
K_{i}
is obtained from
\tilde{K}
after
replacing column
i+1
by
c
. Moreover by a direct expansion we
have
\det\tilde{K}=p/\mathrm{den}^{\rho(\rho-1)/2}
with
p\in k[x]
of
degree at most
\rho d_{a}+1/2\cdot\rho(\rho-1)\tilde{D}
and

\det K_{i}=p_{i}/\mathrm{den}^{\rho(\rho+1)/2-i}
with
p_{i}\in k[x]
of
degree at most
\rho d_{a}+(1/2\cdot\rho(\rho+1)-i)\tilde{D}
. So
finally for
0\leq i<\rho
,
\nu_{i}=p_{i}/(\mathrm{den}^{\rho-i}p)
.

And thus
\eta_{i}=\mathrm{den}^{\rho}p\cdot\nu_{i}=\mathrm{den}^{i}p_{i}
for

0\leq i<\rho
and
\eta_{\rho}=\mathrm{den}^{\rho}p
, whence the result.
∎

In summary, the above proof (which is similar to the approach
in
(
7
, §2)
)
boils down to the study of some of the
minors (up to sign) of a matrix
K^{*}
whose columns are iterates of

\theta
,

\displaystyle K^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}a&\cdots&\theta^{\rho-1}a&\theta^{\rho}a\end{bmatrix}.

And we just bound the size of each minor by simply expanding the
determinant and bounding the degrees in every column. For proving
Theorem
1
, we first show that if the input
matrix
T
is strictly proper and
has a realisation
T=W+XM^{-1}Y
then the minors of the
matrix
K^{*}
can always be written with denominator dividing

(\det M)^{\rho}
(see
Proposition
1
). Now let
D
be
a degree bound on the denominator of
T
. If we have a
small

realisation of
T
meaning that
\deg\det M=O(D)
then all these
minors are actually smaller than naive expansions suggest.

4.
Matrices of rational functions

We follow the lines
of
(
13
,
17
)

for the main definitions and results we need here.

4.1.
Linearisation of matrix fraction description

Let
T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
with a
realisation (
3
). Such a
realisation for
T
is not unique. For example, for any non-singular

m\times m
polynomial matrices
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, one has the
realisation

(7)

\displaystyle T=W+XD_{2}(D_{1}MD_{2})^{-1}D_{1}Y.

Recall that a matrix
U\in k[x]^{n\times n}
is unimodular if its
determinant is a nonzero element in
k
. When
D_{1}
or
D_{2}
are not
unimodular the
realisation (
7
) can be
reduced
.

Let
A,B
be
m\times p
and
m\times h
polynomial matrices. A
matrix
D\in k[x]^{m\times m}
is a
common left divisor
of
A
and
B

if there exist polynomial matrices
A_{1},B_{1}
such that
A=DA_{1}

and
B=DB_{1}
. The matrices
A
and
B
are said to be
left
relatively prime
if their only left common divisors are
unimodular. Similarly, one can define
common right divisors
and

relatively right prime
matrices simply by taking transposes in the above
definitions. Now a realisation (
3
) of
T
is said
to be
irreducible
if
M,Y
are left relatively prime and
M,X
are
right relatively prime. One can always reduce a given realisation to
an irreducible one by the following.

Proposition 0.

(
13
, Thm. 7-9)

If
T
has the realisation (
3
) then there exist
polynomial matrices
X_{0},M_{0},Y_{0}
of size

n\times m,m\times m,m\times n
respectively such that
T
has
the following irreducible realisation

(8)

\displaystyle T=W+X_{0}M_{0}^{-1}Y_{0}.

Moreover there exist
N,D\in k[x]^{n\times n}
such that

(9)

\displaystyle T=ND^{-1}

is an irreducible realisation (
N
and
D
are right relatively
prime).

A realisation of the form (
9
) is
also called an irreducible (right)
matrix fraction description
(MFD)
for
T
. The proof of
Proposition
1
is effective, in the sense
that irreducible
realisations (
8
)
or (
9
) can be computed from an arbitrary realisation for

T
.

Now given an MFD (
9
) for
T
, one can

linearise
it into a simple form called a
state-space
realisation

(
17
)
.

Proposition 0.

(
17
, Sec. 6.4)

Given a right MFD:
T=ND^{-1}\in k(x)^{n\times n}
,
let
m=\deg\det D
, one can
always construct a so-called
state-space
realisation

(10)

\displaystyle T(x)=W(x)+B(xI_{m}-A)^{-1}C,

with
W\in k[x]^{n\times n}
and
B,A,C
matrices with entries
in
k
of size
n\times m,m\times m,m\times n

and
I_{m}
the identity matrix of size
m
.

A state-space realisation (
10
)
is a particular example of a general
realisation (
3
). When
T
is strictly proper, one
has
W=0
in (
10
) and the idea of
linearisation comes from the fact that we only have a polynomial
matrix of degree 1 involved. We use a state-space realisation of

T
in our proof since this form is convenient when dealing with
differentiation. In particular, if
T
is strictly proper, it has a simple
derivative
T^{\prime}=-B(xI_{m}-A)^{-2}C
.

4.2.
Determinantal denominators

We now recall results about the denominator of minors of
rational matrices.
For a rational matrix
R\in k(x)^{m\times p}
and a positive integer
\ell
,
we denote by
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
the monic least common
denominator of all minors of
R
of size at most
\ell
. We also set

\varphi_{0}(R)=1
. The polynomials
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
are called the

determinantal denominators
of
R

(
13
)
.
Note that
\varphi_{1}(R)
is the monic least common denominator of the
entries of
R
. So one can write
R=1/\varphi_{1}(R)\cdot N
with
N

a polynomial matrix.
By definition, for
\ell\geq 0
,
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
divides

\varphi_{\ell+1}(R)
and
\varphi_{\ell}(R)=\varphi_{\ell+1}(R)
for

\ell\geq\rk(R)
. Moreover by a direct expansion of the determinant,

\varphi_{\ell}(R)
divides

\varphi_{1}(R)^{\ell}
. We recall how determinantal
denominators behave with respect to sums, products and inverse of matrices.

Proposition 0.

(
13
, Thm. 1-2)

If a rational matrix
R
is the sum
R_{1}+R_{2}
of two rational matrices

R_{1},R_{2}
, then for all
\ell
,
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
divides

\varphi_{\ell}(R_{1})\varphi_{\ell}(R_{2})
. Moreover, if
\varphi_{1}(R_{1})

and
\varphi_{1}(R_{2})
are coprime then equality holds. Similarly
if
R
is the product
R_{1}R_{2}
of two rational matrices
R_{1},R_{2}
then for all

\ell
,
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
divides

\varphi_{\ell}(R_{1})\varphi_{\ell}(R_{2})
.

Proposition 0.

(
13
, Thm. 4)

Let
R
be a non-singular
m\times m
rational matrix.
If
\det R=c\cdot\alpha/\beta
with
c\in k^{*}
,

\alpha,\beta\in k[x]
monic,
then
\beta\varphi_{m}(R^{-1})=\alpha\varphi_{m}(R)
.

Proposition 0.

(
13
, Thm. 10)

Let
T=W+XM^{-1}Y
be a realisation of the
form (
3
). For all
\ell\geq 0
,

\varphi_{\ell}(T)
divides
\varphi_{\ell}(M^{-1})
.
If the realisation is irreducible then equality holds.

Corollary 0.

Let
T=W+XM^{-1}Y
be a realisation of the
form (
3
). For all
\ell\geq 0
,

\varphi_{\ell}(T)
divides
\det M
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5
,
\varphi_{\ell}(T)

divides
\varphi_{\ell}(M^{-1})
, which
divides
\varphi_{m}(M^{-1})
as
M
is an
m\times m
matrix.
Next, by Proposition
4
,

\varphi_{m}(M^{-1})
is equal to
\det M\cdot\varphi_{m}(M)
up to
a nonzero constant and

\varphi_{m}(M)=1
since
M
is a polynomial matrix.
∎

Proposition
5

and Corollary
6
also
apply for MFD (
9
) or state-space
realisation (
10
)
as they are also examples of realisation (
3
).

5.
Degree bound

In this section, we prove our main result
(Theorem
1
).
Let
T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
be a strictly
proper matrix with realisation (
3
).
We define the operator
\theta=\partial_{x}+T

and take
a\in k[x]^{n}
.
We aim at
bounding the degrees in the coefficients of the solution of
Problem
1
for
(T,a)
.
Let
\rho=\dim(\spn_{k(x)}(\theta^{i}a,i\geq 0))
and

\Delta=\det M
.

By Propositions
1
and
2

and the strict properness assumption,
one can write an irreducible
state-space realisation (
10
)
for
T
in the form

(
10
′
)

\displaystyle T=B(xI_{m}-A)^{-1}C\eqcolon BL^{-1}C,

with
A,B,C
scalar matrices.
Since the above state-space realisation is irreducible, we have

\varphi_{\ell}(T)=\varphi_{\ell}(L^{-1})
for all
\ell\geq 0
by
Proposition
5
and this divides
\Delta

by Corollary
6
.
The degree bound is a consequence of the following result.

Proposition 0.

Let
s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{r}
be non-negative integers and consider the
following matrix of iterates of
\theta
:

(11)

\displaystyle K=\begin{bmatrix}\theta^{s_{1}}a&\cdots&\theta^{s_{r}}a\end{bmatrix}\in k(x)^{n\times r}.

Then,
\varphi_{\ell}(K)
divides
\Delta^{s_{r}}
for all
\ell\geq 0
.

Note that Proposition
1
is somehow
unexpected. Indeed, if one expands an arbitrary minor of
K
of order
r

by taking
\Delta
as denominator of
T
, one expects this minor to have
denominator
\Delta^{s_{1}+\cdots+s_{r}}
. It is not a priori clear why there should be
such simplifications in the denominator of the minors. Besides,
as for Theorem
1
, experiments suggest that
Proposition
1
still holds
if the matrix
T
is no longer strictly proper.

5.1.
Proof of
Proposition
1

Let
\mathcal{O}=k(x)\left<\partial_{x}\right>
be the ring of linear
differential polynomials with rational function
coefficients. Then,
\theta=I_{n}*\partial_{x}+T

can be seen as a
n\times n
matrix with
coefficients in

\mathcal{O}

(
16
)
.
Multiplication of
matrices of operators, denoted by
*
, is defined in the usual way by

\displaystyle\partial_{x}*R=R*\partial_{x}+R^{\prime},

for any rational matrix
R\in k(x)^{n\times n}
. Next for any matrix

U\in k(x)^{n\times p}
with columns
u_{1},\dots,u_{p}
,
let
\theta(U)
be the result of applying

\theta
to each column of
U
, namely

\displaystyle\theta(U)=\begin{bmatrix}\theta u_{1}&\cdots&\theta u_{p}\end{bmatrix}.

A simple reasoning by induction proves the following.

Lemma 0.

For all
s\geq 0
,

\theta^{s}=\sum_{j=0}^{s}\binom{s}{j}\theta^{j}(I_{n})*\partial_{x}^{s-j}\in\mathcal{O}^{n\times n}
.

We now compute a convenient expression for the matrices
\theta^{j}(I_{n})
.
For
1\leq i\leq j
, we
denote by
\Lambda_{i}^{j}
the product

\displaystyle\Lambda^{j}_{i}=\prod_{\ell=i}^{j-1}\left[(CB-\ell I_{m})L^{-1}\right],

with
L=xI_{m}-A
and
A,B,C
the scalar matrices
from (
′
‣
5
).
We adopt the convention that
\Lambda_{j}^{j}=I_{m}
.

Lemma 0.

For
1\leq i\leq j
,

\Lambda^{j+1}_{i}-\left(\Lambda^{j}_{i}\right)^{\prime}=(CB-iI_{m})L^{-1}\Lambda^{j}_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
j\geq 1
, the result holds for
i=j
since

\Lambda^{j+1}_{j}=(CB-jI_{m})L^{-1}
and
\Lambda_{j}^{j}=I_{m}
. Suppose it
holds for
1<i+1\leq j
and recall that
(L^{-1})^{\prime}=-L^{-2}
.
Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\Lambda^{j+1}_{i}-\left(\Lambda^{j}_{i}\right)^{\prime}=(CB-iI_{m})\left[L^{-1}\Lambda^{j+1}_{i+1}-(L^{-1}\Lambda^{j}_{i+1})^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=(CB-iI_{m})L^{-1}\left[\Lambda^{j+1}_{i+1}-(\Lambda^{j}_{i+1})^{\prime}+L^{-1}\Lambda^{j}_{i+1}\right].

And by the induction hypothesis, the following concludes the proof,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{j+1}_{i+1}-(\Lambda^{j}_{i+1})^{\prime}+L^{-1}\Lambda^{j}_{i+1}=(CB-(i+1)I_{m})L^{-1}\Lambda^{j}_{i+1}+L^{-1}\Lambda^{j}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=(CB-iI_{m})L^{-1}\Lambda^{j}_{i+1}=\Lambda^{j}_{i}.\qed

Lemma 0.

For
j\geq 1
,
\theta^{j}(I_{n})=BL^{-1}\Lambda^{j}_{1}C
.

Proof.

For
j=1
,
\theta(I_{n})=T=BL^{-1}C
, so the result
holds.
Suppose the result holds for
j\geq 1
. Then,

\theta^{j+1}(I_{n})=(T+\partial_{x})(\theta^{j}(I_{n}))=BL^{-1}CBL^{-1}\Lambda_{1}^{j}C-BL^{-2}\Lambda_{1}^{j}C+BL^{-1}(\Lambda^{j}_{1})^{\prime}C
. By Lemma
3
,

\displaystyle\theta^{j+1}(I_{n})=BL^{-1}\left((CB-I_{m})L^{-1}\Lambda_{1}^{j}+(\Lambda^{j}_{1})^{\prime}\right)C=BL^{-1}\Lambda^{j+1}_{1}C.\qed

Now by Lemmas
2

and
4
, for
s\geq 0
, one can write

\displaystyle\theta^{s}a=\sum_{j=0}^{s}\theta^{j}(I_{n})\binom{s}{j}a^{(s-j)}=a^{(s)}+BL^{-1}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{s}\Lambda^{j}_{1}C\binom{s}{j}a^{(s-j)}\right].

For
i\geq 1
and
s\geq 0
, let
u_{i}^{s}=C\binom{s}{i}a^{(s-i)}
and

\lambda_{i}^{s}=\sum_{j=i}^{s}\Lambda_{i}^{j}u_{j}^{s}
. Note that
u_{i}^{s}=0

and
\lambda_{i}^{s}=0
when
i>s
. Also
u_{i}^{s}
is a vector of
polynomials. So we have
\theta^{s}a=a^{(s)}+BL^{-1}\lambda_{1}^{s}
.
Hence the matrix
K
in (
11
)
can be written as

\displaystyle K=\begin{bmatrix}a^{(s_{1})}&\cdots&a^{(s_{r})}\end{bmatrix}+BL^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{1},

with

\mathcal{L}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}^{s_{1}}&\cdots&\lambda_{i}^{s_{r}}\end{bmatrix}
for
i\geq 1
.
In the above sum, the left term is a polynomial matrix.
So by Proposition
3
,

\varphi_{\ell}(K)
divides
\varphi_{\ell}(L^{-1})\varphi_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{1})
.
Therefore
\varphi_{\ell}(K)
divides
\Delta\varphi_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{1})
.
It only remains to prove that
\varphi_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{1})
divides
\Delta^{s_{r}-1}
.
This is done in the following lemma, which
completes the proof of Proposition
1
.

Lemma 0.

For
1\leq i\leq s_{r}
and
\ell\geq 0
,
\varphi_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{i})

divides
\Delta^{s_{r}-i}
.

Proof.

\mathcal{L}_{s_{r}}
is a polynomial matrix, so

\varphi_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{s_{r}})=1
for all
\ell\geq 0
. Suppose
the result holds for
1<i+1\leq s_{r}
. For all
s\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{s}=\sum_{j=i}^{s}\Lambda_{i}^{j}u_{j}^{s}=u_{i}^{s}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{s}\Lambda_{i}^{j}u_{j}^{s}=u_{i}^{s}+(CB-iI_{m})L^{-1}\lambda_{i+1}^{s}.

Thus, one can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{i}^{s_{1}}&\cdots&u_{i}^{s_{r}}\end{bmatrix}+(CB-iI_{m})L^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{i+1}.

Since the left term is a polynomial matrix, by
Proposition
3
,

\varphi_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{i})
divides

\varphi_{\ell}(L^{-1})\varphi_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{i+1})
. Hence by
induction it divides
\Delta^{s_{r}-i}
.
∎

5.2.
Proof of the degree bound

We are now ready to establish the degree bound stated in Theorem
1
.
The beginning of the proof does not differ from the proof
of Theorem
1
,
except that we take
\Delta
as a denominator of
T=XM^{-1}Y
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We have
T=XM^{-1}Y
so one can write
T
with denominator

\Delta=\det M
. And because
T
is strictly proper,
\Delta T

is a polynomial matrix of degree
<\delta=\deg\det M
. Thus
similarly as in the proof of Theorem
1

in Section
3
, one can write
\theta^{i}a
for
all
i\geq 0
as
b_{i}/\Delta^{i}
with
b_{i}\in k[x]^{n}
of degree at
most
d_{a}+i(\delta-1)
.

Let us again study the solution
\nu\in k(x)^{\rho}
of the linear
system
K\nu=-\theta^{\rho}a
with
K=\begin{bmatrix}a&\theta a&\cdots&\theta^{\rho-1}a\end{bmatrix}\in k(x)^{n\times\rho}
.
We also denote

K^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}K&\theta^{\rho}a\end{bmatrix}\in k(x)^{n\times\rho+1}
.
As in the proof of Theorem
1
, applying Cramer’s
rule to this linear system shows that for all
0\leq i<\rho
,

\nu_{i}=\pm\mathfrak{m}_{i}/\mathfrak{m}
with
\mathfrak{m}_{i}

(resp.
\mathfrak{m}
) a
\rho\times\rho

minor of
K^{*}
(resp.
K
).

By a direct expansion, we get
\mathfrak{m}=p/\Delta^{\rho(\rho-1)/2}

with
p\in k[x]
of degree at most
\rho d_{a}+1/2\cdot\rho(\rho-1)(\delta-1)
.
However, by Proposition
1
,
it can be written with
denominator
\Delta^{\rho-1}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}=\frac{p}{\Delta^{\rho(\rho-1)/2}}=\frac{q}{\Delta^{\rho-1}},

with
q=p/\Delta^{(\rho-1)(\rho-2)/2}

a polynomial
of degree at most

\displaystyle\deg q\leq\rho d_{a}+(\rho-1)\delta-1/2\cdot\rho(\rho-1).

Similarly,
\mathfrak{m}_{i}
can be written

p_{i}/\Delta^{\rho(\rho+1)/2-i}
with
p_{i}\in k[x]
of degree
at most
\rho d_{a}+(1/2\cdot\rho(\rho+1)-i)(\delta-1)
.
Morever, by Proposition
1
,
it can be written with denominator
\Delta^{\rho}
.
So we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{\Delta^{\rho(\rho+1)/2-i}}=\frac{q_{i}}{\Delta^{\rho}},

with
q_{i}=p_{i}/\Delta^{\rho(\rho-1)/2-i}

a polynomial
of degree at most

\displaystyle\deg q_{i}\leq\rho d_{a}+\rho\delta-(1/2\cdot\rho(\rho+1)-i).

Finally for
0\leq i<\rho
,
\nu_{i}=\pm q_{i}/(\Delta q)
.
And thus
\eta_{i}=\Delta q\cdot\nu_{i}=\pm q_{i}
for
0\leq i<\rho

and
\eta_{\rho}=\Delta q
, whence the result.
∎

6.
Perspectives

Theorem
1
allows to establish precise degree
bounds in several concrete problems under some conditions (genericity,
point at infinity not irregular). For future work, we aim at proving
a similar result without the
strict properness assumption. This would yield bounds that hold
unconditionally in our four instances. Also, there are other
problems that are instances of Problem
1

for which we could also derive bounds. This is for example the case
for the composition of algebraic functions and D-finite
functions
(
20
)
. Finally, the long-term goal is to
exploit the unified viewpoint we enlighted for the design of new
efficient algorithms. We notably intend to have algorithms
solving Problem
1
whose
runtime is sensitive to the
size
of the realisation of
T

we have in input.
