E-learning module on tobacco counselling for students of medicine and dentistry in India: a needs analysis using mixed-methods research
Authors: Chandrashekar B R, Nishath Khanum, Praveen Kulkarni, Madhu Basavegowda, Kishor M, Suma S
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn the background of lack of integrated tobacco counselling training (TCT) in the existing medical and dental curriculum, the present study was undertaken to determine the need for developing an e-learning module on tobacco counselling for students of medicine and dentistry in India.MethodsA mixed-method research with a modified exploratory sequential design was undertaken between January and August 2022 in a private medical and a dental college in Southern India, where the qualitative research was done by conducting in-depth interviews with six subject experts and two focus group discussions (FGDs) separately among six medical and six dental interns to identify the gaps in current TCT. Based on the themes from qualitative research, a questionnaire was developed and validated to elicit the knowledge and competence in tobacco counselling. This questionnaire was subsequently used to explore quantitatively the current knowledge and competence in tobacco counselling among the final-year MBBS (bachelor of medicine and bachelor of surgery) and BDS (bachelor of dental surgery) students. The results of the questionnaire study were triangulated with the themes of the qualitative research.ResultsThe qualitative research proffered the lack of structured TCT in the present curriculum and emphasised the need for an integrated, self-paced e-learning tobacco counselling training module (TCTM) in the background of curricular time constraints to accommodate a conventional classroom-based TCTM. During the in-depth interviews and FGDs, the participants suggested including the global burden and pattern of tobacco consumption, tobacco control laws and tobacco counselling methods, as some of the key contents of the module. The quantitative data analysis revealed the lack of knowledge and competence of students in tobacco counselling. The results of the quantitative phase substantiated the themes that emerged from the qualitative phase.ConclusionThere is a need for introducing a customised, self-paced, integrated e-learning module on tobacco counselling for the students of medicine and dentistry in India. The e-learning module can be included as a choice-based value addition to the regular curriculum.

Introduction

Tobacco use is an emerging public health problem globally and is acquiring the dimension of an epidemic.1 Smoking is a leading cause of preventable deaths across nations. It is estimated that five million preventable deaths occur every year globally. Approximately, one person dies every 6 s.2 India is the second largest producer and consumer of tobacco after China. More than one-third of adults in India use tobacco. This accounts for 275 million.3 Although reports of the Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) 2 in India show an improvement in tobacco control efforts compared with reports of GATS 1 (a decrease of 6-point percentage in tobacco use was found between GATS 1 and GATS 2),4 tobacco addiction is still a public health challenge in India considering the number of tobacco users.3 4

Medical and dental graduates as future healthcare leaders are tasked with assisting to promote the health and welfare of the population including their patients.5 6 Healthcare professionals should have the competency to provide tobacco counselling to motivate their patients to quit tobacco.7 Tobacco users are more likely to quit tobacco if they are counselled and encouraged by their healthcare professionals to quit the habit by connecting them with pharmacotherapy with counselling.5 8 Literature indicates that most graduates in medical and dental schools in India and throughout the world receive only limited tobacco counselling training (TCT).9 They are ill equipped to treat tobacco dependence.9 A study among undergraduate students in a dental college in India has demonstrated that the scores for knowledge and behaviour towards tobacco cessation were low among these students.10 However, the participating students expressed willingness to undergo TCT. The study highlighted that there is a need for administrative, curriculum and policy changes to systematically introduce TCT in a dental institution.10

Medical schools often offer only didactic lectures on tobacco use, and its ill effects with very little emphasis on providing integrated training using experiential learning in performing tobacco counselling.11 12 The lack of confidence and skills in tobacco counselling among healthcare practitioners can adversely impact their professional practice. Healthcare practitioners with inadequate TCT will not be effective in motivating their tobacco-using patients to quit the habit.13 This will further contribute to the increasing health, economic and social burden of tobacco while adversely impacting tobacco control efforts.13 Literature suggests that TCT of healthcare professionals will lead to improved patient outcomes in terms of motivating tobacco users to quit the habit.1417 A systematic TCT programme for medical and dental undergraduate students at the institutional level is the need of the hour.10

The Medical Council of India in its updated guidelines for introducing competency-based medical education to medical graduates in India has directed that the medical students should be able to ‘describe, discuss, and counsel patients appropriately on smoking cessation’.18 Some of the potential barriers to incorporating TCT in the curriculum of medicine, dentistry and other healthcare professions are competing priorities, lack of trained staff and limited resources in the training schools.9 19 These challenges are highlighted in the 2017 report of the Cochrane Tobacco Addiction Group Priority Setting group. The report raised questions on TCT of healthcare professionals.20

In this background, we undertook a study earlier to elicit the need for introducing a customised tobacco counselling training module (TCTM) for students of dentistry.21 During the needs analysis of this study, which included in-depth interviews among dental subject experts and focus group discussions (FGDs) among students of the bachelor of dental surgery (BDS) programme, we found that the tobacco-related topics were taught in a compartmentalised manner by different specialties in dentistry at different time frames in the existing BDS curriculum in India.21 As a result, the learners were not able to integrate what was taught, failed to perceive the importance of tobacco counselling and also lacked skills in tobacco counselling. The study highlighted the necessity to develop and introduce a customised TCTM for students of dentistry.21 Further, we developed, validated and introduced a customised classroom-based TCTM for dental undergraduate students using the Analyse, Design, Develop, Implement and Evaluate framework on a pilot basis.21 Subsequently, TCTM was expanded to cover full batches of students.22 During the programme evaluation of our previous studies, TCTM was found effective in enhancing the knowledge, attitude and ability of undergraduate dental students to correctly identify oral manifestations and their skills in tobacco counselling following its implementation.21 22 The stakeholders, which included students who participated in the programme and the faculty who delivered the classroom-based TCTM, expressed satisfaction with the contents and relevance. However, they expressed that the implementation of this conventional classroom-based TCTM required more than 20 hours of classroom training and 10 hours of small group sessions each to complete the module. It was challenging to accommodate this in the regular teaching curriculum.21 22 They suggested exploring some alternate strategies so that TCTM can be accommodated in the curriculum of dentistry overcoming the curricular time constraints.21 22

With this background, in this research, we included the students and staff of a medical college and a dental college in Southern India. We explored the need for an integrated TCTM and also identified the components to be included, the mode of delivery of such a module without affecting the regular curriculum. This was elicited by presenting the lessons learnt from our previous studies about implementation and sustainability challenges in the light of an already time-constrained curriculum as a secondary objective. This research paper summarises the results of the needs analysis undertaken among the staff and students of medical and dental colleges to develop a customised e-based TCTM for students of medicine and dentistry. The e-learning module is explored as an alternative to conventional classroom-based TCTM to overcome the challenges of curricular time constraints in accommodating such training modules.

Material and methods

In this study, we adopted a modified exploratory sequential mixed-methods design using a grounded theory qualitative approach to elicit the perceptions of subject experts, interns and undergraduate students in medical and dental colleges. The subject experts were teachers working in private medical and dental colleges in Southern India, who were involved in training the medical and dental students in the present curriculum. The research was carried out from January to August 2022. The present study was conducted in three phases (figure 1). The first phase was qualitative research that included in-depth interviews among subject experts and two FGD among six medical and dental interns each. This phase elicited perceptions from the potential stakeholders (subject experts and interns) about gaps in the TCT in the existing curriculum, the need for customised TCT for medical and dental students, perceived challenges for implementing a classroom-based TCTM and suggestions about alternative strategies to overcome the challenge of curriculum time constraints to introduce classroom-based training. We also explored the perceived topics to be included in such a customised module. In the second phase, based on the themes that emerged from qualitative research, a questionnaire was developed and validated to elicit the knowledge, attitude, self-confidence, ability to identify a tobacco user through oral manifestations and competence in undertaking tobacco counselling. The questionnaire was intended to be used among the final year students studying MBBS (bachelor of medicine and bachelor of surgery) and BDS programmes in a private medical and dental college respectively in Southern India to assess their current knowledge and competence in tobacco counselling. In the third phase, we used the questionnaire on 45 final-year MBBS and 64 final-year BDS students as intended to verify, confirm or generalise the findings of the modified exploratory qualitative phase. The modified exploratory sequential design was chosen as we needed to first explore the perceptions of interns and subject experts about current TCT in the existing curriculum (phase 1—qualitative study), then, develop a questionnaire based on themes of the qualitative study (phase 2), which was subsequently used among the final year medical and dental students in phase 3 (quantitative study). Here, the questionnaire was used on students rather than on a sample of subject experts and interns because we felt that the application of this questionnaire on students would provide an opportunity to elicit whether the lack of customised training as perceived by interns (who already completed training under existing curriculum) and subject experts (who were involved in delivering existing curriculum) had an adverse impact on the knowledge, attitude, self-confidence and competence in undertaking tobacco counselling on final year students (who were currently in the final stage of completing their training under the existing curriculum). We felt this modification to the conventional exploratory sequential design would allow us to integrate and evaluate the results better than what we could have done by using the questionnaire on other interns and subject experts.23 Phase 1 (qualitative study): In this phase, we conducted in-depth interviews among six subject experts and one FGD each among six medical and six dental interns. A purposive sampling was used to recruit participants and experts to the FGDs and in-depth interviews.

Steps in the exploratory sequential design mixed-method study.

The selection of subject experts for in-depth interviews was based on the criteria that they should be actively involved in the training of students in the existing curriculum, possess rich knowledge of how TCT was offered in the existing curriculum and be willing to participate in the study. A structured in-depth interview guide was prepared (online supplemental file 1), validated by two subject experts and used. The selection of subject experts for in-depth interviews was planned from the departments of Community Medicine (CM), Public Health Dentistry (PHD) and the Indian Association of Public Health Dentistry (IAPHD) to get rich data till data saturation. The in-depth interviews were conducted among one expert from CM, one expert from the Medical Education Unit of a Medical College, who was also a teacher from the CM department, two experts from the PHD department, and two office bearers of the IAPHD, who were also subject experts and teachers in PHD department in a dental institution in Southern India. These experts were actively involved in teaching the students in the existing curriculum. Informed consent was obtained from these selected subject experts and the date and time of the in-depth interview was scheduled according to their convenience.

The selection of interns for the FGD was based on the following criteria:

The selection of medical and dental interns was made by a consensus among the staff of CM and PHD departments, respectively, because these teachers were involved in training these interns as mentors and supervisors. These staff knew the strengths of the selected interns in contributing to the needs analysis in the present study and selected them purposively. One FGD each among medical and dental interns provided rich data that we felt reached data saturation in line with what we gathered from in-depth interviews among subject experts. The FGD was conducted using a validated FGD guide (online supplemental file 2). The in-depth interviews were completed first among the subject experts, which was followed by two FGDs.

The in-depth interviews and FGDs were conducted in English in a quiet room by a trained investigator (CBR) with help from a recording assistant (NK). CBR was experienced in conducting such interviews, was involved in the development and introduction of the classroom-based TCTM in the previous studies and was well versed with the context, objectives and study participants. He trained the recording assistant (NK) in data collection for qualitative research (note-taking, writing memos, reflections and coding), in two sessions of 3 hours each. NK was also involved in developing the in-depth interview and FGD guides. The in-depth interviews and FGDs were audio recorded using a digital audio recorder. The average time of in-depth interviews and FGDs were 20 and 45 min, respectively. Besides, the investigator as well as the recording assistant maintained notes for each in-depth interview and FGD along with their reflections on the session. During note-taking, the keywords and phrases were marked with quotation marks to capture the interviewee’s language. At the end of each session, both CBR and NK prepared memos where structured information about participants, interview proceedings, key findings and emerging themes were noted. CBR and NK discussed the notes, reflections and memos. CBR and NK listened to the audio recordings multiple times. NK transcribed the audio recordings as abridged transcripts. Each such transcript was first verified by CBR, who undertook the in-depth interviews or FGDs. Then, the transcript was shared with the participants for comments and correction. The member checking ensured that the interviews and discussions were correctly interpreted. The classical content analysis method was used for analysis of the transcripts. The units for analysis were constructed, followed by the development of codes. First two transcripts were coded independently by CBR and NK. These coded transcripts were exchanged. In case of disagreement, both CBR and NK discussed and reviewed the coding before the final coding. Inductive analysis was done by NK through an iterative process by reading and rereading the transcripts, to generate codes and subcodes. The subsequent transcripts were coded by NK. Categories were developed by grouping codes and subthemes, which highlighted a similar issue. These categories were then merged and grouped depending on the similarities between emerging themes and subthemes. Under the themes, the verbatim quotes were included. Phase 2 (development of a questionnaire): Based on the themes that emerged from the in-depth interviews and FGDs, a 20-item questionnaire was initially designed. The questionnaire was designed to elicit the knowledge and level of competence of the final year MBBS and BDS students in undertaking tobacco counselling. The questionnaire was intended to be self-administered, where each participating student marked an appropriate response from the options provided. The questionnaire was subjected to face and content validation by six subject experts. The relevance of each item was rated by experts on a five-point Likert scale. Based on suggestions from subject experts, the questionnaire was modified. The final questionnaire had 18 items. The linking of themes, subthemes and codes of qualitative analysis to various items in the questionnaire is diagrammatically depicted in figure 2. A cognitive interview using a retrospective verbal probing technique was done among five final-year students of medicine and dentistry to elicit response process validity.

Linking of the themes, subthemes and codes to the questionnaire items.

Phase 3 (quantitative study): A questionnaire study was conducted in this phase among the final-year MBBS and BDS students. All the final-year students in a medical and dental college in the academic year 2021–2022 were invited to participate. However, the students who offered informed consent were recruited for the study. The questionnaire was self-administered. All the students who offered informed consent completed the questionnaire. The response rate was 100%.

The data analysis of the quantitative research was done using SPSS version 24. Pearson’s χ2 test was used for comparison of frequency distribution between different groups for each item. The statistical significance was fixed at 0.05. Data triangulation was done to verify the themes of qualitative phase with the results of quantitative phase.

Results

Six subject experts participated in the in-depth interviews. Six medical and six dental interns participated in the FGDs. A total of 109 final-year (45 MBBS and 64 BDS) students participated in the questionnaire survey.

Qualitative phase

Six themes emerged from the in-depth interviews and FGDs, which are described below.

Gaps in current TCT in existing curriculum

One of the themes that emerged from the in-depth interviews and FGDs was the lack of structured TCT in the existing curriculum for BDS and MBBS programmes. It was found that the tobacco-related topics are covered in a compartmentalised manner because of which the students were not able to apply the knowledge and skills in tobacco counselling.

The curriculum in MBBS program do not have a structured module for training students in tobacco counselling. Although the students are taught about the health hazards of smoking and smokeless tobacco, we do not train them in counseling said an expert from CM (SH).

Need for an integrated tobacco counselling training module

Another theme that emerged was the need for a comprehensive TCT in an integrated manner by a multispecialty team in the curriculum of MBBS and BDS programmes. It was expressed by subject experts and medical and dental interns that a customised TCTM can improve the knowledge, attitude and skills in tobacco counselling among students of MBBS and BDS programmes.

A subject expert from IAPHD (VK) said ‘There is a need for training BDS students to offer tobacco counseling. Since every BDS student should identify tobacco users based on oral manifestations and offer counseling, they should also be trained by experts from different fields in separate sessions. This will make the students apply tobacco counseling in daily practice’.

A female dental intern (DFI 1) expressed ‘Though the students can identify a tobacco user, they are not confident to provide tobacco counseling. There is a lack of knowledge about the role of dentists in tobacco cessation. There is a need for an integrated tobacco counseling training module to be incorporated into the existing curriculum’.

The challenges in accommodating such training in the current curriculum

The subject experts expressed concerns that it would be difficult to accommodate TCT as a separate training programme during the regular academic hours because of time constraints in an already overburdened curriculum. This will be a challenge as students prioritise what is important for exams rather than focusing on TCT.

An expert from CM (PK) said ‘The time constraints to introduce a separate training program during the regular college hours is a challenge considering the vast existing curriculum. The students will concentrate on what is important for exams first. It will be challenging to motivate them to understand the importance of undergoing this training’.

A male dental intern (DMI 2) expressed ‘Time will be challenging. But, if it is important from a practice point of view, we should find a way by accommodating this training in the practical hours of some specialties’.

Alternate strategies suggested to overcome challenges in implementing the training module in the curriculum

Here, the interviewer explained the curricular time constraints faced during the implementation of TCTM previously and sought suggestions on how to overcome the curricular time constraints. The subject experts and interns expressed that a customised TCTM could be incorporated as a value-added course to the regular curriculum. They suggested that an E-learning module can be tried to check whether it is as effective as classroom-based training.

One subject expert from PHD (SW) said ‘After COVID-19, even the students are trained in the application of online training. The recorded sessions of TCTM can be introduced as an e-learning module. This will be convenient, self-paced, and accessible from anywhere, anytime’.

A female medical intern (MFI 1) said, ‘It would be better to put the tobacco counseling training under the e-learning platform because it can be accessed from mobile phones whenever the students are free, like, even when someone is traveling’.

One subject expert from CM (PK) said ‘The training program should have some sessions to highlight how tobacco is consumed in different forms, why people get addicted, how difficult it is for someone to come out of such habits, as a doctor what one should do when encountered with a tobacco user, where these tobacco addicts should be referred to for management’.

A male dental intern (DMI 1) said, ‘We get a lot of patients, who use tobacco. We should be trained to identify tobacco users based on clinical manifestations and oral lesions. The videos on how to do counseling will also help’.

Evaluation of training

In continuation, when the interviewer asked the subject experts and interns about how to assess the effectiveness of such training modules on the online platform, the subject experts and interns suggested to evaluate the change in knowledge, attitude, self-confidence and ability to identify oral manifestations using the Multiple Choice Questionnaire (MCQ)-based questionnaire. They have suggested that the skills in tobacco counselling should be assessed by asking the participants to upload a video of counselling undertaken by them in a simulated setting in the form of role-play or tobacco counselling offered in real-life settings or by taking feedback from the patients and their attendants.

One subject expert from CM (SH) said ‘The components related to the change in knowledge, attitude, self-confidence, and ability to identify oral manifestations can be assessed using MCQ-based questionnaire and skills using role play or counseling in real-life settings. A checklist should be used while evaluating the skills in tobacco counseling’.

A male medical intern (MMI 1) said ‘A questionnaire before and after the program will be good to assess the knowledge improvement. But, someone should assess the skills of tobacco counseling either at the time of counseling or by asking the patient and their attendants to give feedback’.

Quantitative findings

A total of 109 students, 45 from MBBS and 64 from BDS programmes participated in the questionnaire study. The gender distribution of participants in study is detailed in online supplemental file 5). 94.5% of students informed that tobacco-related topics are taught separately by different departments (table 2). 9.4% of the students from the BDS programme answered that tobacco-related topics are taught in an integrated manner while all students of the MBBS programme said that these topics are taught separately by different departments. 66.1% of the students responded that the tobacco-related educational component was not assessed with no statistically significant difference between the students of MBBS and BDS programmes (p=0.766, table 2). All the students expressed that they prefer integrated TCT (table 2). The majority of the students responded that their level of competence was either novice or advanced beginners with very few opting for competent, proficient and expert choices for their knowledge and competence in tobacco counselling with no significant difference between MBBS and BDS students (table 3).

The results of the quantitative questionnaire study indicated that tobacco-related topics are taught separately by different departments and not in an integrated manner. As a result, the students lacked knowledge about the patterns of tobacco consumption, the role of health professionals in tobacco control, tobacco control laws, the application of behavioural theories for tobacco counselling, tools for assessing tobacco dependence, the ability to identify and differentiate oral manifestations in a tobacco user and skills in tobacco counselling substantiating the themes generated in the qualitative phase of the research and the topics suggested to be included in the training module by the interns and subject experts.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the need for customised TCT for medical and dental students using a modified exploratory sequential design. It was found during the qualitative phase that the TCT in the existing curriculum was done in a compartmentalised manner rather than an integrated approach, leading to a lack of knowledge among the students about patterns of tobacco use, assessing tobacco dependence, tobacco control laws, tobacco control programmes, ability to identify and differentiate tobacco-related oral lesions and skills in tobacco counselling. The results of the subsequent questionnaire study among final-year MBBS and BDS students confirmed the perceptions of subject experts and interns. 94.5% of the students expressed that tobacco-related topics are taught separately by different departments. All participating students in the present study preferred integrated training by a team of experts. The study highlighted that there is a need to introduce multidisciplinary, integrated TCT for the students of medicine and dentistry in India. The majority of the participating students in the present study expressed themselves as either novice or advanced beginners for all questions that assessed their level of knowledge, ability to identify a tobacco user through general and oral manifestations, self-confidence in differentiating various oral manifestations in a tobacco user, competence in assessing the level of addiction and to undertake tobacco counselling. A study by Binnal et al to evaluate the factors influencing multidisciplinary approach towards tobacco control among medical, dental and nursing students in India found that only 14.18% of students received formal training in tobacco cessation, and a majority of the participating students perceived that they should be trained in tobacco cessation.24 The study highlighted the lack of training and inadequate knowledge among the participating students to undertake tobacco counselling while emphasising the need for curriculum revision in these programmes similar to the results of our study.24

Karadoğan et al stated that ‘Medical school undergraduate training on tobacco cessation is crucial to shape attitudes and engage new generations of physicians. However, both students and educators state that the medical curriculum is insufficient regarding tobacco cessation’.25 In the same study, the final-year medical students reported attending only one lecture on tobacco cessation in an academic year and 41% of these students considered the contents on tobacco cessation as insufficient in number and content with poor knowledge on tobacco cessation similar to the perceptions of final year MBBS and BDS students in our study.25 A scoping review by Elfandi and Poudyal concluded that a customised lecture-based curriculum combined with peer role-play or standardised/real patient interactions may help in the effective development of necessary knowledge and skills among undergraduate medical students to provide tobacco cessation counselling to patients.12 These observations are similar to the perceptions expressed by interns and subject experts in our study.

The subject experts and interns perceived that the introduction of an integrated e-learning module on tobacco counselling by a multispecialty team will facilitate overcoming curricular time constraints in accommodating customised TCT into the curriculum of MBBS and BDS programmes in India.

Milella et al introduced an e-learning module to medical school students in Italy. The e-learning module was effective in enhancing the knowledge about tobacco dependence and treatment among participating students.26 It was found that the introduction of an e-learning module compared with conventional classroom-based modules had several advantages such as flexibility (can be accessed by the students from anywhere at any time), self-paced meaning that students can complete the modules at their own pace devoting more time on topics that they feel as important, and change in knowledge can be quickly evaluated using automated tests.26 These advantages are the justifications expressed by subject experts and interns in our study for suggesting an e-learning module on tobacco counselling in place of conventional classroom-based training. Studies have shown that the introduction of different teaching methods such as role-play, supervised interaction with real patients, online courses with video training and traditional classroom-based courses on tobacco counselling have been found to augment the knowledge and skills related to tobacco counselling among the medical and other healthcare professions students.2730

India is facing a major tobacco epidemic, with the country being the second-largest producer and consumer of tobacco. To combat this issue, the introduction of TCTM in healthcare professional courses can be one of the measures.14 Healthcare professionals play a crucial role in helping people quit tobacco use. They can provide counselling, support and medications to help people overcome their addiction to tobacco.2730 The present study identified gaps in knowledge and skills related to tobacco counselling among medical and dental students owing to a lack of structured training. This underscores the importance of introducing a TCTM in the current teaching curriculum. By providing healthcare professionals with the necessary knowledge and skills to counsel patients on tobacco cessation, we can make significant progress in controlling the tobacco epidemic in India. The feedback as part of the evaluation of our previous studies21 22 indicated curricular time constraints as one of the challenges to accommodate classroom-based training into the existing curriculum. The perceptions of subject experts and interns in this study regarding the introduction of classroom-based modules were similar to the concerns expressed by subject experts in our previous studies. The subject experts and interns in the present study have suggested an e-learning module as an alternative to conventional classroom-based TCTM.

Therefore, we must take immediate action to introduce tobacco cessation e-training modules in healthcare professional courses in India.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of the present study was building on the feedback of subject experts from a dental college in the previous study, who expressed concerns that accommodation of classroom-based TCTM was a challenge due to curricular time constraints. In this study, we elicited the perceptions of subject experts from medical and dental colleges along with interns in a qualitative study about the need for a customised TCTM and how to overcome the curricular time constraints associated with introduction of conventional classroom-based TCTM. The gaps in training under the existing curriculum expressed by experts and interns were subsequently confirmed by the final-year MBBS and BDS students in a quantitative questionnaire study. The limitation was that the questionnaire developed from the themes of qualitative phase should have been first used among the sample of subject experts and medical and dental interns before using it on final-year MBBS and BDS students to strictly align with exploratory sequential design. However, we used the questionnaire directly on the students with the thinking that it would be better to confirm the perceptions expressed by experts and interns with those of students, who are in the final stages of completion of the programme under the existing curriculum.

Conclusion

The subject experts from CM, PHD and IAPHD expressed that there is no integrated and customised TCT by a multispecialty team of experts for medical and dental students in the existing curriculum. The lack of knowledge on tobacco-related topics and lack of competence among final-year MBBS and BDS students confirmed the perceptions expressed by the subject experts and interns. There is a need to introduce an integrated tobacco counselling e-learning module rather than a classroom-based module, as a choice-based value addition to the existing medical and dental curriculum in India to overcome the curricular time constraints.
