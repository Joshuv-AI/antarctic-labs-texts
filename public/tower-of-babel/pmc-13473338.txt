ARF6 promotes the nuclear translocation of β-catenin to facilitate AFP deficiency-mediated metastasis in CTNNB1S33Y-mutant hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Ruitian Zhang, Longxuan Dong, Junyi Yang, Qinglong Guo, Di Pan, Chen Xun, Libin Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The mortality rate associated with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains alarmingly high, positioning it as a primary lethal cancer worldwide, primarily driven by its high degree of malignancy, recurrence, and metastasis. The high heterogeneity of HCC necessitates the urgent identification of new therapeutic targets and molecular pathways. Our previous research observed that alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) deficiency led to pro-metastatic roles in HCC with specific genotypes. Here, we further investigated the underlying molecular mechanism. Through proteome microarray screening, we identified that ADP-ribosylation factor 6 (ARF6) was a key AFP-interacting protein and AFP deficiency upregulated ARF6 activity, which subsequently promoted the metastatic capacity of HepG2 cells. Mechanistically, the abnormal activation of ARF6 induced the dissociation of β-catenin from its membrane-bound anchors, specifically E-cadherin. The consequent increase in free cytoplasmic β-catenin facilitates its nuclear translocation, which in turn activates Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Crucially, we discovered that the pro-metastatic function of activated ARF6 and the sensitivity to the ARF6 inhibitor were strictly dependent on the CTNNB1 genotype. In highly invasive MHCC-97H cells, which harbor a CTNNB1 nonsense mutation, ARF6 inhibitor SecinH3 failed to inhibit migration and invasion. However, the overexpression of CTNNB1S33Y mutation in MHCC-97H cells further amplified the increasing transcriptional activity of β-catenin triggered by the ARF6 abnormal activation. Importantly, the reintroducion of CTNNB1S33Y mutation restored the inhibitory effect of SecinH3 in vitro and significantly suppressed the hepatic and pulmonary metastasis in a tail vein injection HCC metastasis mouse model. These findings establish a CTNNB1S33Y-dependent mechanism for ARF6-mediated metastasis, indicating that CTNNB1 activating mutations serve as a "permissive signal" for ARF6's pro-metastatic program. Our study underscores the importance of CTNNB1 genotype screening for precisely stratifying patients for ARF6-targeted therapies, offering new medication guidance for highly heterogeneous HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10565-026-10211-1.

Introduction

According to the most recent global cancer statistics (2022) provided by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), liver cancer occupies the third in mortality and the sixth position in incidence worldwide (Bray et al. 2024). Among primary liver cancer cases, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant histological subtype, comprising 75%–85% of the total, and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma at 10%–15% (Bray et al. 2018). HCC has a high degree of malignancy, is prone to recurrence and metastasis, and its high heterogeneity is likely to lead to the failure of advanced diagnosis and treatment (Safri et al. 2024). Moreover, the prevalence of late-stage presentation in HCC patients frequently renders them ineligible for optimal curative strategies. The average survival time of patients is less than one year (Yang et al. 2019). Therefore, the drug treatment of HCC urgently requires new targets and the identification of molecular pathways.

Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is a glycoprotein associated with mammalian tumors. Elevated serum AFP levels usually indicate a high risk of liver cancer development and a poor prognosis (Bai et al. 2017). Serum AFP can not only be used for diagnosis, but also serve as a predictive indicator for liver cancer invasion and recurrence (Qin and Tang 2004). Circulating levels of AFP are significantly augmented in more than half of the HCC patient population, often exceeding 0.5–1 mg/mL (Butterfield et al. 2014). Therefore, since the 1970 s, serum AFP has served as a cornerstone clinical biomarker for HCC diagnosis (Kumada et al. 1999). Recently, researchers’ focus has shifted toward elucidating the intracellular regulatory functions of AFP in tumor initiation and progression (Li et al. 2011, 2020; Yang et al. 2008; Zhu et al. 2015). High doses (> 100 mg/L) of AFP inhibited the growth of various malignancies such as HepG2 and Jurkat cells (Toder et al. 1983). However, low doses of AFP even have the opposite effect. The addition of 20 mg/L AFP significantly potentiated the expansion of the Bel-7402 cell population, which might because AFP inducted expression of recognized oncogenes, such as mutative p53 and p21 protein (Li et al. 2004). Researchers have confirmed that intracellular AFP inhibits the PTEN gene and promotes cell growth by directly interacting with phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) and competing for binding with retinoic acid receptor β (RAR-β) (Li et al. 2011; Zhu et al. 2015). In Huh7 cells, depletion of AFP was found to destabilize mutant p53 levels, ultimately precipitating programmed cell death (Yang et al. 2008). Furthermore, research has demonstrated that both extracellular and membrane-associated AFP exert a potent cytoprotective effect, effectively shielding HepG2 cells from TNF-mediated cytotoxicity and programmed cell death (Semenkova et al. 1997). AFP shows different influences in the tumorigenesis and progression of HCC. Therefore, further in-depth research on AFP is still needed.

Beyond its impact on HCC cell survival, it has been reported that AFP facilitates the invasive and metastatic potential of Bel-7402 and HLE cells by up-regulating markers such as keratin 19 (K19), epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM), CXC chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) and matrix metalloproteinase 2/9 (MMP2/9) (Lu et al. 2016). Interestingly, in our earlier research, we found that AFP deletion inhibited the growth of HepG2 cells but promote its invasion and metastasis (Xu et al. 2023). Therefore, we wander a deeper exploration of the molecular mechanism of this phenomenon. As a small GTPase, adenosine diphosphate (ADP) -ribosylation factor 6 (ARF6) interconverts between two primary conformational states: the active GTP-coupled form and the inactive GDP-coupled form. The functional activation of ARF6 requires the enzymatic conversion of GDP-ARF6 into the GTP-bound counterpart (Sun et al. 2023). It can regulate endocytic traffic and peripheral actin remodeling, so that to regulate migratory and invasive of cells (D'Souza-Schorey and Chavrier 2006; Schweitzer et al. 2011). ARF6 affects the motility of cells through facilitating β-catenin release from the membrane-bound cadherin pool (Grossmann et al. 2013; Pellon-Cardenas et al. 2013). Currently, our results indicate that in different human hepatoma cell lines, the regulatory effect of ARF6 on β-catenin is inconsistent. Mutations in CTNNB1 represent a molecular hallmark of a distinct subclass of HCC (Calderaro et al. 2017; Kim and Jeong 2019), this might be the key to making the difference. Thus, in this study, we mainly found that ARF6 was the key factor that responsible for the pro-metastatic effect of AFP deficiency in HCC cells with CTNNB1S33Ymutation by regulating β-catenin pool, and this will provide new medication guidance for the treatment of highly heterogeneous HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2 were incubated in MEM (Minimum Essential Medium; Gibco, 11090081) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Wisent Bio Products, 080–910), added 1% Sodium Pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Gibco™, 11360070), 1% non-Essential Amino Acids solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Gibco™, 11140050) and 1% GlutaMAX™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Gibco™, 35050061) and in a humidified environment with 5% CO2 at 37℃.

The engineered human hepatoma HepG2 cells by CRISPR/Cas9 technique to generate human hepatoma HepG2 cells deleted for AFP (KO including KO #1 and KO #2) were provided by Genloci Biotechnologies Inc.. The culture conditions for KO #1 and KO #2 cells are the same as for HepG2 cells.

Human hepatocellular carcinoma cells MHCC-97H cells were purchased from Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). MHCC-97H cells were cultured in DMEM, supplemented with 10% FBS (Wisent, Inc., CAN, Quebec, QC, Canada)﻿ and 100 U/mL of streptomycin and penicillin (GIBCO, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Chino, CA, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Reagents

Inhibitors used in the study included SecinH3 (ARF6 inhibitor, CSNpharm, # CSN19207), MK2206 (AKT inhibitor, # HY-108232), and SC79 (AKT activator, # HY-18749) from MedChemExpress. All reagents were prepared in DMSO as concentrated stocks (30 mM for SecinH3, 1 mM for others) and preserved at −80 °C until further use.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates were isolated in RIPA lysis buffer solution containing protease inhibitor cocktail (GLPBIO, GK10014) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (GLPBIO, GK10011). Quantification of them adjusted according to protein concentration measurements (Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay, Thermo Scientific), separated using SDS–polyacrylamide gels, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK) and ECL chemiluminescence detection kits (Vazyme Biotech, E412-02) with appropriate species-specific secondary antibodies were used to detect specific protein bands. Antibodies uesed are: anti-AFP (1:1000, Abclonal A11865), anti-AFP (1:1000, Huabio # ER80601), anti-pAKT (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology #4060), anti-AKT (1:1000, Proteintech 60203–2-lg), anti-E-Cadherin (1:1000, Proteintech 20874–1-AP), anti-Vimentin (1:1000 Cell Signaling Technology #5741), anti-N-Cadherin (1:1000, Proteintech 22018–1-AP), anti-β-Catenin (1:1000 Abclonal A19657), anti β-Actin (1:200000, Abclonal AC026), anti-GAPDH (1:5000 Abclonal AC002), anti-LaminA/C (1:200000 Abclonal A19524), anti-Tubulin (1:5000 Abclonal AC008). Each blot shows one representative out of at least three. Perform quantitative analysis of WB bands using ImageJ software.

Human proteome microarray assays

The identification of proteins interacting with AFP was performed on HuProt™ 20K microarrays (CDI Laboratories, Baltimore, MD, USA) by Wayen Biotechnologies Inc. (Shanghai, China). The arrays were incubated in a 4 °C pre-cooled blocking buffer (PBS-T with 5% BSA) for 1.5 h at room temperature. Throughout the blocking process, the samples were kept in the dark and maintained under constant shaking at 50 rpm. After the chips were cleaned by 1 × PBS-T, the Biotin/DMF labeled protein diluted with blocking buffer was added into the incubator box, and the chips were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After cleaning chips with 1 × PBS-T solution and deionized water, 0.1% Cy5-Streptavidn Solution (Cat#SA1011, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and the chips were added into the petri dish, and the reaction was carried out for 20 min at 50 rpm. After the chips cleaned again, they were placed in the small chip centrifuge (ChipMate PMC-082, Tomy, Japan) at 1000 rpm and centrifuged for 2 min for drying. Fluorescence signals on the microarrays were acquired using a GenePix 4000B scanner (Axon Instruments, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) at a wavelength of 635 nm with the laser power set to 100%. Subsequent data extraction and processing were performed utilizing GenePix Pro 6.0 software. HuProt™ human proteome microarray covers 19,851 human proteins, comprehensively screening potential interacting proteins of AFP at the whole proteome level. The binding of antibodies to AFP is evaluated using the Z-score scoring method, with each protein tested in duplicate.

Immunoprecipitation

To prepare antibody-bead complexes, specific primary antibodies—including those targeting β-catenin or E-cadherin— were immobilized onto protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, HY-K0202) through a 4 h incubation at 4 ◦C with gentle rotation. Following a wash step with 1 × PBS-T to remove unbound proteins, the pre-cleared cell lysates were introduced to these complexes and incubated overnight at 4 ◦C under constant agitation. The resulting precipitates were subsequently washed three times with 1 × PBS-T buffer to ensure purity for downstream analysis.

Transwell migration/invasion assays

Transwell chambers (Corning® BioCoat™ Control Insert-No ECM, 8 μm pore size) were employed to assess the metastatic potential of the cells. In the invasion assay, the membranes were pre-treated with a 1:4 dilution of matrigel (Corning® Matrigel® Matrix High Concentration, Phenol-Red Free, LDEV-free) for 30 min, this step was omitted in the migration assay. Cells, previously starved for 24 h in serum-free medium, were introduced to the upper chamber, while the lower compartment contained medium with 20% serum as a chemoattractant. To facilitate accurate quantification, the non-migrating cells on the interior of the inserts were mechanically cleared with a cotton swab. Subsequently, the cells that had successfully traversed the membrane to the lower surface were subjected to methanol fixation prior to staining with 0.01% crystal violet. Place the chamber under a 200 × microscope and randomly select 3 fields of view for image acquisition. Use ImageJ software to count the number of migrated/invaded cells, and represented as cell numbers of total area.

Glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assays

Activation of ARF6 was assessed by using GST pull-down assays. The BL21 (DE3) strain carrying the pGEX-2TK-GST-GGA3-PBD plasmid (Miaoling Plasmid Platform, G71004) was used to express the GST-GGA3-PBD recombinant protein, which was then captured and purified using GST purification beads (Sangon Biotech, C650031). The experiment followed a reported protocol (Giubilaro et al. 2021). Cells or tissue fragments were lysed for 30 min at 4 °C in 300 µL of lysis buffer (pH 7.5, 50 mM Tris–HCl, 140 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10%glycerol (v/v), 1% Nonidet P-40 (v/v), 1 mM dithiothreitol) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The cell lysates were transferred to fresh tubes with 20 µg of either GST-GGA3-PBD coupled to GST purification beads and rotated for 2 h at 4 °C. The beads were collected, and the amount of captured active ARF6-GTP was analyzed by Western blotting.

Animal studies

All animal procedures were sanctioned by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China Pharmaceutical University. The experimental protocols adhered strictly to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by the National Academy of Sciences and the National Institutes of Health, ensuring humane treatment throughout the study.

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) female BALB/c nude mice (age: 6 weeks; weight: 18–22 g) were obtained from Jiangsu Huachuang Sino Pharmaceutical Technology Co., LTD (Certificate No. SYXK(SU)2023–0019). We constructed three stable MHCC-97H cell lines, which expressed the constitutively active ARF6 (Q67L) mutation (MHCC-97HARF6−Q67L), CTNNB1 (S33Y) mutation (MHCC-97HCTNNB1−S33Y), or both (MHCC-97HARF6+CTNNB1). Animals were randomly divided into four experimental groups for constructing HCC metastasis mouse model. 8 × 106 cells of each type cells were separately inoculated into the designated groups of mice via tail vein injection. On the 20th day, the mice in MHCC-97HWT and MHCC-97HCTNNB1−S33Y groups were divided into 2 groups respectively based on weight (n = 5) and they were administrated as following methods: Control group (normal saline, i.p., once daily) and SecinH3 group (2 mg/kg, i.p., once daily). After continuous administration for 21 days, all the mice were sacrificed and dissected. The lungs were fixed in Bouin's solution (G-CLONE, Cat# RS4141) for 1 h, washed with 70% ethanol to detect metastasis on lungs, and formalin was used to fix livers.

The TOP/FOP flash luciferase reporter assay

We transfected MHCC-97H cells using the jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus, 101000027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each well, a total of 1.2 μg of DNA—consisting of either 1 μg of 8×TOPFlash and 0.2 μg of pRL-TK, or 1 μg of 8×FOPFlash and 0.2 μg of pRL-TK—was diluted in 200 μL of jetPRIME buffer. The mixture was vortexed for 10 s. Subsequently, 2.4 μL of jetPRIME reagent (maintaining a 1:2 DNA to reagent ratio, w/v) was added, followed by immediate vortexing and a 10 min incubation at room temperature. The transfection complexes were then added dropwise to the culture medium. Cells were harvested 36 h post-transfection. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were determined using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, DL101). The relative luciferase activity was calculated by normalizing Firefly activity to Renilla activity. The transcriptional activity of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway was expressed as the TOP/FOP ratio. And the relative TOP/FOP ratio was determined by normalizing the values of all experimental groups to the MHCC-97HNC group, which was set to 1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 6 and 8 software (GraphPad Software). Data represent mean values ± SD from at least three independent experiments (n ≥ 3). Two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used for two-group comparisons, while one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc test was applied for multi-group comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ARF6 is the key signal for the increased invasion caused by AFP deletion

According to our previous experiments of our research group, HepG2 AFP KO cells with have stronger motor ability and an increase in nuclear translocation of β-catenin than the wild-type HepG2 cells (HepG2WT) (Xu et al. 2023). To explore the deep mechanism behind this phenomenon, we used a protein microarray assay which containing 19,851 proteins to globally screen the AFP protein interactors in vitro. The result was listed by Z-Score in Supplementary Table 1. Among the top 25 proteins, there are three transporter-related proteins (GGA3, SEC13 and ARF6). After literature investigation, it was found that SEC13 has little relationship with tumor occurrence and development. GGA3 can affect cell proliferation and metastasis through its interaction with ARF6, which is associated with migration and invasion in the occurrence and development of various tumors (Crupi et al. 2020; Parachoniak et al. 2011). ARF6 was among the proteins that was found to participate in stronger interactions with AFP (Fig. 1A). Based on the TCGA database, ARF6 is highly expressed in a variety of tumors and affects the survival of patients with liver cancer (Fig. 1B, C). Therefore, we focused on ARF6 for further research. Then, we validated the physical interaction between AFP proteins and ARF6 by performing coimmunoprecipitation (CoIP) experiments in HepG2 cells. We employed the GST-tagged GGA3 protein binding domain (GST-GGA3-PBD) to isolate the active GTP-bound form of ARF6 (ARF6-GTP) from cellular extracts or tissue extracts to visualize the distribution of ARF6-GTP. Comparing the expression of ARF6 protein and mRNA in HepG2 AFP KO cells with HepG2WT cells, no significant changes were found in the total ARF6 protein expression level, but the active GTP-bound form of ARF6 and ARF6 mRNA was shown higher level in HepG2 AFP KO cells (Fig. 1E, F). Consistent with the results of in vitro study, though there was no marked variation in the expression of ARF6 in liver nodule tissues from DEN/CCl4-induced primary HCC mouse model, which established before (Xu et al. 2023), the active GTP-bound form of ARF6 was more distributed in the in situ liver cancer nodules of AFP KO mice compared with AFP wild-type mice (Fig. 1G). Then we found that the GTP analog GTPγS tetralithium could interfere with the binding of AFP to the small GTPase ARF6 (Fig. 1H). Therefore, AFP competes for the GTP-binding site of ARF6, regulating ARF6 activation; when AFP is knocked out, ARF6 becomes overactivated. The specific downstream genes of ARF6, including RAC1, AMAP1, GPLD, P38 (Li et al. 2017) was also activated upon AFP deficiency (Fig. 1I). HepG2 AFP KO cells showed stronger invasion and migration (Fig. 1J). After ARF6 activity was inhibited by specific inhibitor SecinH3 or ARF6 expression was knockdown, the migration and invasion ability of HepG2 AFP KO cells were both significantly weakened (Fig. 1K-L, S1A). In summary, ARF6 plays a key role in the AFP deficiency-mediated promotion of invasion and migration, and AFP deficiency upregulates ARF6 activity and mRNA expression.

ARF6-regulated invasion and migration in HepG2 cells does not necessarily rely on AKT pathway

We have found that AFP deficiency upregulates ARF6 activity. It is reported that AKT is essential for the invasion of ARF6-dependent melanoma (Yoo et al. 2019). Whereas our previous study demonstrated that AFP deficiency led to the inactivation of AKT pathway (Xu et al. 2023). Therefore, we explored the influence of AKT pathway in ARF6-regulated invasion and migration in HepG2 cells. The specific inhibitor of AKT, MK2206 was administered to HepG2WT and HepG2 AFP KO cells respectively. It was found that MK2206 could reduce the invasion and metastasis in HepG2WT cells, but had no significant effect in HepG2 AFP KO cells (Fig. 2A). Similarly, AKT agonist SC79 increase invasion and metastasis in HepG2WT cells, but did not influence HepG2 AFP KO cells. Both MK2206 and SC79 had no significant influence on ARF6 and ARF6-GTP (Fig. 2B, C). Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) confers the ability for malignant cells to metastasize and invade. As shown in Fig. 2D, AKT inhibitor MK2206 suppressed EMT in HepG2WT cells by restoring the expression of the epithelial junction protein E-cadherin and concomitantly downregulating the protein levels of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and Vimentin. Instead, MK2206 could not play a similar role in HepG2 AFP KO cells, in which ARF6 was abnormally activated. Correspondingly, in Fig. 2E, AKT agonist SC79 promoted EMT in HepG2WT cells, but not in HepG2 ARF6 KD cells. Abnormal activation of ARF6 promotes the nuclear translocation of β-catenin by assisting its dissociation from E-cadherin on the intracellular membrane

It is well known that the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway can enhance the migration and invasion abilities of cells by promoting EMT in HCC (Chen et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2017). Previous studies have confirmed that knockout of the AFP gene can affect the WNT5A/β-catenin signaling pathway and upregulate the expression level of the EMT transcription factor Slug, thereby promoting EMT in HCC cells (Xu et al. 2023). In HepG2 AFP KO cells, the protein expression of β-catenin as well as its nuclear translocation were all upregulated (Fig. 3A-C). It is reported that ARF6 can control the dissociation of β-catenin from cadherin, affect β-catenin-mediated transcription in melanoma cells (Grossmann et al. 2013). The binding of β-catenin with E-cadherin in HepG2 AFP KO cells was significantly reduced (Fig. 3D), as well as the co-location of β-catenin and E-cadherin at cellular membrane (Fig. 3E). We have found that AFP deficiency induced abnormal activation of ARF6 in HepG2 cell. After ARF6 was pharmacologically inhibited by SecinH3, the location of β-catenin in cellular membrane in HepG2WT cells was also significantly increased, and the location of β-catenin in cytoplasm and nucleus was deceased (Fig. 3F). In HepG2 AFP KO cells, SecinH3 promoted the binding of β-catenin bound to E-cadherin on the cell membrane (Fig. 3G, H). Moreover, activated ARF6 directly bond E-cadherin and ARF6 inhibitor SecinH3 blocked this binding, leading to the destabilization of the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex in HepG2AFP KO cells (Fig. 3G). To verify whether this mechanism is universally applicable in HCC cells, experiments were conducted on the highly invasive HCC cell line MHCC-97H. Surprisingly, the pharmacological inhibition of ARF6 could not inhibited migration and invasion abilities of MHCC-97H cells anymore (Fig. 4A), and the subcellular localization of β-catenin in MHCC-97H cells deviated from the patterns observed in HepG2 cells, no significant alterations in β-catenin levels were detected in the nuclear or cytosolic fractions, although a reduction in its membrane fractions was noted following SecinH3 treatment. (Fig. 4B). In previous studies, it was found that AFP deficiency could not promote the EMT phenotype of HCC cell line Huh7 cells, which was related to the DNA sequence of β-catenin it contained (Xu et al. 2023). Therefore, DNA sequencing was performed on MHCC-97H cells, and it was found that they also contained CTNNB1 mutations (Fig. 4C). However, the expression of β-catenin protein of MHCC-97H cells was lower compared with that of HepG2 cells and Huh7 cells (Fig. 4D), which is consistent with the literature reports that MHCC-97H cells have nonsense mutations, failing to show a significant induction of β-catenin target genes (Qiu et al. 2019). Therefore, we speculate that both CTNNB1 mutation and overexpression need to be met simultaneously to be consistent with the mechanism that the activation of ARF6 promoted the migration and invasion of HepG2 cells. CTNNB1S33Y lies within the ubiquitination recognition motif of the β-catenin protein and confers a gain of function to β-catenin by increasing its nuclear accumulation of and β-catenin-driven transcription (Groen et al. 2008). MHCC-97H cells were transfected with the CTNNB1(S33Y) plasmid and could stably express β-catenin protein with S33Y mutation. It was found that ARF6 inhibitor SecinH3 restored its inhibition on the migration and invasion abilities of MHCC-97HCTNNB1(S33Y) (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, the changes in the subcellular localization of β-catenin caused by SecinH3 in MHCC-97HCTNNB1(S33Y) cells were consistent with its effect on HepG2 cells, showing that the location of β-catenin in cellular membrane was increased, and the location of β-catenin in cytoplasm and nucleus was deceased (Fig. 4F). To manipulate ARF6 signaling, researchers often employ mutants that mimic specific nucleotide-bound states. A prime example is the ARF6Q67L mutation, which abolishes GTPase activity and sustains a constant GTP-bound conformation, representing a perpetually active state of the protein (D'Souza-Schorey et al. 1995). β-catenin transcriptional activity was examined using a dual-luciferase reporter gene assay, and the results showed that the activation of ARF6 by ARF6Q67L mutation significantly enhanced the transcriptional activity of β-catenin in MHCC-97H cells, and the overexpression of CTNNB1S33Y mutation further amplified the effect (Fig. 4G). Therefore, the conjecture we mentioned earlier is proved. The promotion by activated ARF6 on migration and invasion of HCC cells is related to the genotype of CTNNB1. Discussion

Despite therapeutic advancements, HCC persists as a major cause of oncology-related deaths, where malignant metastasis serves as the critical determinant of adverse prognosis and the principal obstacle to successful therapy (Bray et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2019). Despite advances in systemic therapies, the high heterogeneity of HCC and its propensity to metastasize pose significant challenges to effective management. Understanding the molecular mechanisms that govern HCC metastasis is critical for developing targeted therapies (Safri et al. 2024). In this study, we investigated the role of ADP-ribosylation factor 6 (ARF6) in HCC metastasis and identified a critical dependency on the genotype of catenin beta 1 (CTNNB1), encoding β-catenin. Notably, only when CTNNB1 harbors an activating mutation (eg. CTNNB1S33Y) does the sustained activation of ARF6 significantly enhance the migration, invasion, and hematogenous metastasis of tumor cells. Concurrently, pharmacologic inhibition of ARF6 exhibits superior therapeutic efficacy; in contrast, such effects are negligible in the context of CTNNB1 nonsense mutations. HCC is recognized as one of the most heterogeneous malignancies. CTNNB1 ranks among the most significantly mutated driver genes in HCC, serving as the central node for activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. It is altered in approximately 37% of human HCC specimens (Schulze et al. 2015). Supporting its pivotal role, studies have identified activating CTNNB1 mutations in over 50% of hepatic tumors from transgenic mouse models, and analogous mutations are present in 26% of a panel of 31 human HCC cell lines (de La Coste et al. 1998). However, the clinical advancement of small molecules designed to directly target the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is substantially hampered by their frequent association with significant gastrointestinal toxicity, which greatly limits their clinical application (Xu et al. 2022). Our studies showed that, in HepG2 cells expressing the CTNNB1S33Y mutation, ARF6 directly destabilizes the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex by preferentially binding to E-cadherin, thereby displacing β-catenin from the cell membrane. In wild-type cells, the ability of cytosolic β-catenin is limited through its sequestration with a a multi-protein destruction complex, primarily composed of adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) and AXIN1. This interaction facilitates the ubiquitination of β-catenin, thereby priming it for subsequent proteasomal-mediated degradation (Pez et al. 2013). However, in CTNNB1S33Y mutant cells, its degradation position is likely affected by the mutation, resulting in a large increase in β-catenin that successfully enters the nucleus to promote invasion and metastasis. Critically, this phenomenon was effectively abrogated by SecinH3, a pharmacologic inhibitor of ARF6. However, in MHCC-97H cells with CTNNB1 nonsense mutations, SecinH3 failed to exert a significant inhibitory effect on cell migration and invasion. Reintroduction of the CTNNB1S33Y mutant plasmid into these MHCC-97H cells restored the phenotypic changes observed in HepG2 cells. Interestingly, the effect of ARF6 inhibition in destabilizing the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex may have a certain dependence on the expression of β-catenin. In Figure S1B-C, in MHCC-97H cells, the activation of ARF6 by ARF6Q67L mutation reduces the binding of ARF6 and E-cadherin, instead SecinH3 only has a significant effect when CTNNB1S33Y mutation is overexpressed. The related mechanisms need to be further explored in future studies. Nevertheless, these results suggest that mutant β-catenin capable of driving β-catenin overexpression serves as a "permissive signal" for ARF6 to execute its pro-metastatic program.

In the established tail vein metastasis model, overexpression of ARF6Q67L in CTNNB1S33Y expressing cells increased the number of lung metastatic foci by 2.3-fold (p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed in parental MHCC-97H cells with CTNNB1 nonsense mutations. Furthermore, CTNNB1S33Y-expressing cells showed a more robust response to SecinH3 treatment. These in vivo data confirm the critical role of the CTNNB1S33Y mutation in mediating the therapeutic efficacy of SecinH3. The identification of ARF6’s dependency on CTNNB1 genotype has important clinical implications. ARF6 inhibitors, such as SecinH3 (a small-molecule inhibitor of ARF6 guanine nucleotide exchange factors), have shown promise in preclinical models of breast cancer (Zhao et al. 2016), and NAV-2729 a small-molecule to hinder of ARF6 and GEF joining), which have shown promise in preclinical models of melanoma and prostate cancer (Wang et al. 2021; Yoo et al. 2016). Our data suggest that such inhibitors may be effective in HCC patients with CTNNB1 mutations, holding promise for reducing metastatic burden. In contrast, in patients with wild-type CTNNB1 expression, targeting ARF6 is unlikely to aid in metastasis control, necessitating alternative therapeutic strategies.

Most CTNNB1 mutations occur at these critical sites- D32, G34, T41, S33, S37 and S45, which is encoded by Exon 3 and crucial for the degradation of β-catenin (Idrissi et al. 2024). While CTNNB1-mutant HCCs generally exhibit well-differentiated morphology and metabolic alterations (Dantzer et al. 2024), their progression rates are dependent on the specific mutation site (Rebouissou et al. 2016). For instance, the CTNNB1S45Y mutation has been shown to possess a more potent oncogenic capacity compared to the CTNNB1S33Y variant, as evidenced by more rapid tumor formation in MET-driven HCC models in mice (Tao et al. 2016). Clinically, mutations at codon 36 of CTNNB1 (e.g., H36Y/L) have also been identified as high-confidence driver events in both liver and prostate cancers (Krishna et al. 2026). We extended our validation to the clinically prevalent CTNNB1H36Y mutation, which resides in the same N-terminal regulatory domain as S33Y. The overexpression of CTNNB1H36Y mutation in MHCC-97H cells also amplified the enhanced transcriptional activity of β-catenin triggered by the abnormal activation of ARF6 (Figure S1D). And the CTNNB1H36Y mutation-induced metastatic phenotype was also effectively reversed by ARF6 inhibition (Figure S1E). Thus, the ARF6-dependency might be a general feature across the N-terminal activating mutants of CTNNB1.

Limitations and conclusion

Our in vitro and xenograft models robustly demonstrate the CTNNB1 genotype-dependency of ARF6 inhibition. Despite these significant findings, our study has several limitations that suggest important avenues for future research. Firstly, it is unclear how ARF6 selectively promotes the dissociation and nuclear accumulation of β-catenin in CTNNB1S33Y mutant cells but fails to do so in cells harboring nonsense mutations. Previous research suggests that ARF6-GTP functions as a 'coincidence-detection code' by directly binding to effector proteins and enhancing their affinity for membrane lipids, ensuring precise spatial recruitment (Ren et al. 2020). We proved that ARF6 may act similarly in HCC, specifically, ARF6-GTP bind to E-cadherin, thereby weakening the E-cadherin and β-catenin binding ability to facilitate β-catenin dissociation. Future studies should thus focus on identifying the direct targets of ARF6-GTP at the E-cadherin complex and validating this "coincidence-detection" model in HCC, as well as investigating the precise molecular mechanism of degradation mediated by CTNNB1 mutation. Secondly, the high degree of heterogeneity in CTNNB1 mutation patterns in HCC include various point mutations, in-frame deletions, and larger exon 3 duplications. CTNNB1-mutated HCCs are characterized by unique biological profiles and divergent responses to current standard therapies. Recently, a novel ‘immune-like’ subclass of HCC has been identified, which exhibits both a high frequency of CTNNB1 mutations and robust T-cell infiltration (Lehrich and Monga 2026). ARF6 is known to be involved in cell-to-cell communication. Future studies should thus investigate the complex role of the TME, where ARF6 activity might modulate crosstalk with stromal cells (e.g., cancer-associated fibroblasts) in a CTNNB1-dependent manner, potentially influencing the entire metastatic niche. Finally, we chose an experimental metastasis model by tail vein injection. In contrast to spontaneous metastasis models, experimental metastasis models provide a more rapid and consistent research approach. However, this convenience comes with inherent limitations: (i) the omission of early metastatic events and (ii) a departure from clinical reality, as the systemic injection of massive tumor cell populations does not accurately represent the natural history of cancer dissemination (Ottewell et al. 2006). Organoid models faithfully recapitulate the 3D structure and genomic heterogeneity of the primary tumor, and thus provide a more clinically translatable and precise model for mechanistic studies and drug screening (Broutier et al. 2017). Therefore, our future work necessitates validation using patient-derived organoids (PDOs) to better simulate the tumor microenvironment of patients and make our study more clinically relevant and facilitating the successful translation of research findings.

In conclusion, our study identifies ARF6 as a pro-metastatic driver in HCC, and the exertion of its function dependent on the CTNNB1 genotype. ARF6 promotes metastasis in CTNNB1S33Y expressing cells by activating β-catenin signaling and downstream metastasis-related genes; however, this effect is negligible in cells harboring CTNNB1 nonsense mutations. These findings underscore the importance of CTNNB1 genotype in guiding ARF6-targeted therapies and provide a framework for personalized strategies to prevent HCC metastasis.
