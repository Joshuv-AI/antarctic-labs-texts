Prevalence, Patterns, and Factors Associated With Adverse Drug Reactions Among People Living With HIV Receiving Antiretroviral Therapy at a Secondary Healthcare Facility in Ghana: A Retrospective Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Agatha Ampaabeng‐Kyeremeh, Emmanuel Kumah, Samuel Egyakwa Ankomah, Dorothy Serwaa Boakye, Collins Kokuro, Margretta Odame Antwi, Samuel Kofi Agyei, Juvina Antwi, Adam Fusheini, Peter Agyei‐Baffour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDespite major progress in antiretroviral therapy (ART) scale‐up, adverse drug reactions (ADRs) remain a common challenge in HIV care and may compromise adherence, regimen continuity, and treatment outcomes. In Ghana, facility‐level evidence on the burden and characteristics of ART‐related ADRs remains limited. This study assessed the prevalence, patterns, and factors associated with ADRs among people living with HIV receiving ART at a secondary healthcare facility.MethodsA retrospective cross‐sectional study was conducted using 355 patient records from January to December 2024 at St. Martin de Porres Hospital, Eikwe. Data on sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, ART regimen type, treatment duration, adherence documentation, and recorded ADRs were extracted using a structured data collection form. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics and ADR patterns. Associations between ADR occurrence and selected variables were assessed using chi‐square tests and Welch's t‐tests. Variables with p ≤ 0.20 were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to examine factors independently associated with ADRs. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsOverall, 43.9% of patients experienced at least one documented ADR. Gastrointestinal reactions were most frequent (32.1%), followed by neurological (21.2%) and skin reactions (16.7%). Most ADRs were mild (52.6%), while 16.0% were classified as severe. The majority of ADRs had resolved (62.2%); however, 12.2% resulted in treatment discontinuation. ART regimen type and treatment duration were significantly associated with ADR occurrence at the bivariate level. In multivariable analysis, use of second‐line (AOR: 1.84; 95% CI: 1.12–3.05) and third‐line regimens (AOR: 3.57; 95% CI: 1.33–10.70), undocumented adherence (AOR: 2.08; 95% CI: 1.10–4.00), and treatment duration under 6 months (AOR: 4.12; 95% CI: 1.35–12.54) were independently associated with ADRs.ConclusionADRs remain a substantial concern in routine ART delivery at the facility level. The observed associations with regimen type, early treatment duration, and adherence documentation highlight key programmatic areas for strengthening pharmacovigilance and patient monitoring. Improving ADR documentation and adherence monitoring may support safer and more sustainable ART delivery in similar resource‐constrained settings.Keywords: adherence, adverse drug reactions, antiretroviral therapy, Ghana, HIV, pharmacovigilance, treatment outcomes

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a major global public health challenge, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), where the burden of infection is highest [1, 2, 3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 39 million people worldwide are currently living with HIV, with about 30.7 million receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) [4, 5]. The scale‐up of ART has transformed HIV from a fatal illness into a manageable chronic condition by suppressing viral replication, improving immune function, and reducing HIV‐related morbidity and mortality [6, 7].

Despite these advances, adverse drug reactions (ADRs) continue to pose significant challenges to the long‐term effectiveness of ART. ADRs—defined as harmful or unintended effects resulting from medication use—may undermine adherence, necessitate regimen modification, or lead to treatment discontinuation. Among people receiving ART, commonly reported ADRs include gastrointestinal disturbances, neurological symptoms, skin reactions, and hematological abnormalities, with more severe events such as hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and hypersensitivity reactions reported less frequently but carrying substantial clinical consequences [8].

Evidence from high‐income settings demonstrates that ADRs persist even with newer ART formulations. Studies from Sweden and Switzerland have documented long‐term treatment‐related toxicities and patient‐specific vulnerabilities that negatively affect adherence and treatment continuity [9, 10]. Although well‐established pharmacovigilance systems in these settings support early detection and management of ADRs [11], such systems remain less developed in many LMICs, where the majority of people living with HIV reside.

In sub‐Saharan Africa, ADRs are a recognized contributor to ART interruption and suboptimal treatment outcomes. The WHO identifies ADRs as a key reason for treatment modification and discontinuation in African ART programs [12]. Facility‐based studies from Ethiopia, Cameroon, and Uganda have reported ADR prevalence rates ranging from 25% to over 30%, with gastrointestinal, dermatological, neurological, and hematological reactions most frequently observed [13, 14, 15, 16]. These findings highlight the persistent burden of ADRs in routine HIV care across the region and underscore the need for context‐specific evidence to inform clinical monitoring and pharmacovigilance practices [17].

Several contextual factors may exacerbate the occurrence and impact of ADRs in resource‐constrained settings. Limited diagnostic capacity can complicate differentiation between ADRs and opportunistic infections, while gaps in healthcare worker training may hinder timely recognition and reporting of drug‐related harms [17]. In addition, comorbid conditions such as tuberculosis, hepatitis, hypertension, and diabetes, along with polypharmacy, malnutrition, and the use of herbal medicines, may increase susceptibility to drug toxicity [18]. Although large‐scale initiatives such as the U.S. President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) and the Global Fund have substantially expanded ART access and improved survival [19, 20], the sustainability of HIV treatment programs depends not only on coverage but also on the safety and tolerability of ART regimens in real‐world settings.

In Ghana, ART coverage has expanded to over 70% of eligible individuals [21], yet ADRs remain a notable cause of treatment modification and attrition within HIV care programs. Existing local studies are limited in number and scope. For example, research from Tamale West Hospital identified gastrointestinal and neurological ADRs as being associated with reduced adherence [22], while another study highlighted gaps in pharmacovigilance knowledge and practice among nurses, potentially affecting early detection and reporting of ADRs [23]. However, facility‐level data from secondary healthcare settings in many parts of the country remain scarce.

Against this backdrop, the present study examined the prevalence, patterns, and factors associated with ADRs among people living with HIV receiving ART at St. Martin de Porres Hospital, Eikwe, a secondary healthcare facility in Ghana's Western Region. By characterizing the types, severity, and programmatic correlates of documented ADRs in routine care, this study aims to contribute context‐specific evidence to support improved pharmacovigilance, patient monitoring, and ART safety within similar resource‐limited healthcare settings.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

A quantitative, retrospective cross‐sectional study was conducted to assess the prevalence, patterns, and factors associated with ADRs among people living with HIV receiving ART at St. Martin de Porres Hospital, Eikwe, in Ghana's Western Region. St. Martin de Porres Hospital is a 200‐bed faith‐based general hospital established in 1959 under the Archdiocese of Cape Coast and currently managed by the Catholic Diocese of Sekondi–Takoradi as part of the National Catholic Health Service. The hospital operates in collaboration with the Ministry of Health and the Ghana Health Service and serves as a referral center for HIV care and ART services within the district and surrounding communities.

Study Population, Eligibility Criteria, and Sampling

The study population comprised adults living with HIV who received ART at St. Martin de Porres Hospital between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024. Eligible patient records belonged to individuals aged 18 years and above, contained complete demographic information, documented clinical and ART‐related details, and recorded documentation of ADRs or clear evidence indicating the absence of ADRs. Records were excluded if they belonged to individuals younger than 18 years, were transferred‐out cases with incomplete follow‐up information, contained substantial missing, incomplete, or inconsistent demographic, clinical, ART, or ADR documentation, or had illegible or irretrievable data that could compromise the accuracy and reliability of data abstraction.

Data Collection

Patient records within the defined study period were retrieved from both electronic and paper‐based ART clinic sources. Using a consecutive sampling approach, eligible records meeting the inclusion criteria were reviewed sequentially in chronological order of clinic attendance until the required sample size (n = 355) was reached.

Data were collected retrospectively using a structured data extraction form (see Supporting Information S1: File 1) developed based on routine clinical documentation standards and previous studies on ART‐related ADRs. The form captured information on sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, residence, and occupation), clinical variables (comorbidities, ART regimen type, duration on ART, and adherence documentation), and ADR‐related characteristics (type, recorded severity, and outcome). ADRs were identified based on clinician‐documented entries in patient medical records. No formal causality assessment tools (e.g., WHO–UMC or Naranjo algorithm) were applied, and severity classification was based on routine clinical judgment as recorded in patient files.

The data extraction tool was pretested on a subset of patient records to assess clarity, completeness, and consistency, and minor revisions were made prior to full‐scale data collection. Four trained research assistants systematically reviewed eligible patient files and extracted relevant information under supervision to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Data Processing and Analysis

Extracted data were entered into Microsoft Excel and subsequently imported into the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 for analysis. Data cleaning involved verification for completeness, identification of missing values, and internal consistency checks. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and to estimate the prevalence and patterns of ADRs. Measures of central tendency were used to describe continuous variables such as CD4 count and duration on ART. Bivariate analyses were conducted to examine associations between ADR occurrence and selected demographic and clinical variables using Pearson's chi‐square test for categorical variables. The Welch two‐sample t‐test was used to compare mean CD4 counts and treatment duration between patients with and without documented ADRs. Variables with a p‐value ≤ 0.20 in bivariate analyses were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to examine factors independently associated with ADR occurrence. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% CIs. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was defined a priori as p < 0.05. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, and the completed checklist is provided as Supporting Material (Supporting Information S2: File 2).

Ethical Considerations

The study adhered to the principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national research ethics guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the Faculty of Health and Medical Sciences of the Presbyterian University, Ghana (Ref No. PUG/ST/AS/24070027). Permission to access patient records was granted by the management of St. Martin de Porres Hospital. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of routinely collected clinical data, informed consent from individual patients was not required. All extracted data were anonymized at the point of collection, and no personally identifiable information was retained in the analytical dataset.

Clinical Characteristics

As shown in Table 2, most participants were receiving first‐line ART regimens (67.6%) and demonstrated good adherence (67.9%). Slightly over half of the patients (50.9%) had no documented comorbid conditions. Among those with comorbidities, tuberculosis (21.4%) and hypertension (15.5%) were the most frequently reported. Regarding immunological status, 38.9% of patients had a baseline CD4 count below 200 cells/µL. Prevalence and Patterns of ADRs

Overall, 43.9% of individuals receiving ART experienced at least one documented ADR, while 56.1% had no recorded ADRs (Table 3). Among patients who developed ADRs, gastrointestinal reactions were the most frequently reported (32.1%), followed by neurological reactions (21.2%) and skin reactions (16.7%). Hepatotoxic reactions accounted for 15.4% of ADRs, whereas hematological (7.7%) and metabolic (7.1%) reactions were less common. In terms of severity, more than half of the ADRs were classified as mild (52.6%), while 31.4% were moderate and 16.0% were severe. Regarding outcomes, 62.2% of ADRs had resolved at the time of record review, either spontaneously or following clinical intervention. However, 25.6% remained ongoing, and 12.2% resulted in treatment discontinuation.

Factors Associated With ADRs

The bivariate analysis (Table 4) showed that only two treatment‐related factors were significantly associated with the occurrence of ADRs: ART regimen type (χ² (2) = 12.25, p = 0.002) and duration on ART (χ²(4) = 13.37, p = 0.004). No significant associations were observed for sociodemographic variables, including sex (p = 0.60), marital status (p = 0.30), place of residence (p = 0.40), occupation (p = 0.2), and age group (p = 0.14). Similarly, adherence level (p = 0.08), comorbidities (p = 0.07), and immunological markers – baseline CD4 count (p = 0.40) and current CD4 count (p = 0.40) – were not significantly associated with ADR occurrence.

Further analysis of immunological parameters showed no significant mean differences between groups. The mean baseline CD4 count was 252.0 cells/µL among patients with ADRs and 253.6 cells/µL among those without (mean difference = −1.6 cells/µL; 95% CI: −26.4 to 23.2; Welch two‐sample t‐test; p = 0.90). The latest CD4 count averaged 452.3 cells/µL for patients with ADRs and 434.6 cells/µL for those without (mean difference = 17.7 cells/µL; 95% CI: −23.5 to 58.9; Welch two‐sample t‐test; p = 0.40). Similarly, CD4 improvement (difference between baseline and latest counts) did not differ significantly, with mean increases of 200.3 and 180.9 cells/µL, respectively (mean difference = 19.4 cells/µL; 95% CI: −25.0 to 63.8; Welch two‐sample t‐test; p = 0.40).

In the multivariable logistic regression (Table 5), which included all variables with p ≤ 0.20 in the bivariate analysis, ART regimen type, undocumented adherence, absence of comorbidities, and shorter treatment duration were independently associated with ADR occurrence. Compared with first‐line therapy, second‐line regimens were associated with higher odds of ADRs (AOR: 1.84, 95% CI: 1.12–3.05; p = 0.02), while third‐line regimens showed markedly higher odds (AOR: 3.57, 95% CI: 1.33–10.70; p = 0.02). Patients with undocumented adherence had higher odds of ADRs than those with documented good adherence (AOR: 2.08, 95% CI: 1.10–4.00; p = 0.03). Having no comorbidity was associated with lower odds of ADRs compared with diabetes (AOR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.19–0.98; p = 0.05). Regarding treatment duration, patients on ART for less than 6 months had more than fourfold increased odds of ADRs compared with those on therapy for over 5 years (AOR: 4.12, 95% CI: 1.35–12.54; p = 0.01).

Discussion

This study found that 43.9% of individuals living with HIV receiving ART at St. Martin de Porres Hospital experienced at least one ADR. This prevalence aligns with findings from Ethiopia, where approximately 37.6% of patients on dolutegravir‐based regimens reported ADRs [24], and Cameroon, with reported ART‐related ADR rates around 40% [15]. In Uganda, a recent study documented ADR prevalence near 45% among HIV patients on ART [25]. High rates have also been observed in India, where about 90% of patients initiating first‐line ART experienced at least one ADR, underscoring the pervasive challenge of ART toxicity in diverse global settings [26]. Although ART has transformed HIV into a manageable chronic condition, its long‐term toxicity continues to challenge patient safety, adherence, and program sustainability.

Demographic variables such as sex, marital status, residence, occupation, and age showed no significant associations with ADRs. Although females accounted for slightly more ADRs (57%), the difference was not statistically significant, consistent with studies from Ethiopia and Cameroon [14, 15]. This contrasts with findings from Europe, where Klein and Morgan [27] reported higher ADR susceptibility among females due to hormonal and pharmacokinetic factors. The absence of demographic associations in this study may reflect Ghana's standardized ART regimen use and uniform follow‐up protocols.

In contrast, clinical characteristics were strongly associated with ADR occurrence. Duration on ART emerged as a significant predictor: individuals treated for less than 6 months were over four times more likely to experience ADRs than those on therapy for over 5 years. This finding concurs with Sherfa et al. [13] and Nasasira et al. [28], who reported that ADRs are more common during the early phases of therapy, likely due to physiological adjustment and clinicians' progressive experience in optimizing regimens over time. Early‐onset ADRs may also prompt regimen switches that reduce subsequent risk, partially explaining the inverse relationship between duration and ADR frequency.

The type of ART regimen was another significant determinant. Patients on second‐ or third‐line therapy had markedly higher odds of experiencing ADRs compared with those on first‐line regimens. This pattern is consistent with Asirifi et al. [21] in Ghana and Back and Marzolini [29] internationally, both highlighting that regimen complexity and higher pill burdens increase toxicity risk. The finding supports the global trend toward dolutegravir‐based regimens, which are associated with improved tolerability and lower discontinuation rates. However, as Ghana's ART program expands its use of these newer regimens, ongoing monitoring is essential to detect emerging side effects such as weight gain or neuropsychiatric symptoms associated with dolutegravir use.

Adherence was found to be significantly associated with the occurrence of ADRs. Individuals whose adherence status was undocumented were about twice as likely to experience ADRs compared with those who demonstrated good adherence. This finding suggests that incomplete adherence monitoring and record‐keeping may reflect broader gaps in pharmacovigilance practices. Desta et al. [30] similarly reported that poor adherence and inadequate documentation often conceal early manifestations of ADRs, delaying detection and appropriate management. Inadequate tracking of patient adherence not only undermines clinical follow‐up but may also indicate limited patient engagement or weak health system support structures. Strengthening adherence monitoring systems, ensuring complete documentation, and providing regular staff training on pharmacovigilance are therefore essential to improving ART safety and sustaining treatment outcomes.

Interestingly, individuals without comorbidities had a lower likelihood of experiencing ADRs, highlighting the potential role of additional health conditions in increasing susceptibility to treatment‐related complications. This aligns with the understanding that comorbidities may complicate ART management through polypharmacy, drug–drug interactions, or overlapping toxicities [31, 32]. While tuberculosis and other conditions did not reach statistical significance in this study, their presence may still necessitate closer clinical monitoring to prevent ADRs. As ART programs continue to integrate chronic disease management, attention to cumulative drug burden and vigilant follow‐up remains essential to minimize adverse outcomes and ensure safe, effective therapy.

Taken together, these findings confirm that ADRs remain a substantial clinical and public health concern among individuals living with HIV in Ghana. The significant influence of ART regimen type, treatment duration, and adherence underscores the interplay between pharmacological and programmatic factors in determining ADR risk. Strengthening pharmacovigilance, patient counseling, and adherence support systems will be vital to mitigating ADRs and sustaining ART effectiveness. Additionally, transitioning fully to better‐tolerated regimens such as tenofovir–lamivudine–dolutegravir, alongside continuous monitoring for new toxicity patterns, represents a crucial step toward safer long‐term HIV care.

Implications for Clinical Practice

These findings carry several implications for ART service delivery at St. Martin de Porres Hospital and similar facilities. First, the elevated ADR risk during early treatment phases underscores the need for intensified monitoring in the first 6 months following ART initiation or regimen change. Implementing structured symptom checklists and routine laboratory assessments during this period could facilitate early detection and timely intervention. Second, patients on second‐ and third‐line regimens require enhanced surveillance protocols, with particular attention to cumulative drug exposures and potential drug‐drug interactions. Third, addressing gaps in adherence documentation must be prioritized as a quality improvement initiative. Developing standardized assessment tools, providing staff training, and integrating adherence monitoring into routine clinical workflows would strengthen both patient management and pharmacovigilance capacity. Fourth, establishing clear ADR management protocols—including severity classification criteria, reporting procedures, and regimen modification guidelines—would promote consistency in clinical decision‐making and facilitate data collection for future research.

Limitations of the Study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective cross‐sectional design inherently limits the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships between ART and ADRs. The analysis was based on routinely collected medical records, which may be subject to incomplete documentation and reporting bias, particularly regarding adherence levels and the detailed characterization of ADRs. Indeed, the significant association observed between undocumented adherence and ADR occurrence may partly reflect gaps in record‐keeping rather than true clinical differences. Second, as the data were derived from a single secondary‐level hospital, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other healthcare settings in Ghana, such as tertiary referral centers or primary care facilities, where ART monitoring practices and patient populations may differ. Additionally, selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out, as the study included only patients with complete clinical and ART‐related data, potentially excluding individuals with the most severe outcomes or those lost to follow‐up.

Third, the identification and classification of ADRs relied primarily on clinical documentation rather than standardized pharmacovigilance reporting systems, which may have led to underreporting or misclassification of mild or transient reactions. Laboratory‐confirmed ADRs such as hepatotoxicity and hematologic abnormalities may have been underestimated due to the absence of consistent biochemical data in patient records. Finally, important potential confounders—such as nutritional status, concurrent use of herbal or over‐the‐counter medications, and genetic variability influencing drug metabolism—were not captured in the existing hospital database. Their omission could have influenced the observed associations between treatment regimens and ADR occurrence.

Conclusion

This study found that ADRs are relatively common among people living with HIV receiving ART at a secondary healthcare facility, with specific patterns related to ART regimen type and treatment duration. While sociodemographic factors and immunological markers showed no significant association, treatment‐related factors emerged as key correlates of ADR occurrence. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring high‐risk regimens and patients with shorter ART durations. Strengthening adherence documentation, pharmacovigilance, and timely clinical interventions can further support safe and continuous ART. Future research should focus on improving adherence tracking, expanding pharmacovigilance capacity, and exploring individual susceptibility factors to better inform evidence‐based management of ADRs.
