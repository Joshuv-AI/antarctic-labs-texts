Hybrid oscillator-qudit quantum processors: stabilizer states and symplectic operations
Sayan Chakraborty, Victor V. Albert

Abstract
We construct stabilizer states and error-correcting codes on combinations of discrete- and continuous-variable systems, generalizing the Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) quantum lattice formalism. Our framework absorbs the discrete phase space of a qudit into a hybrid phase space parameterizable entirely by the continuous variables of a harmonic oscillator. The unit cell of a hybrid quantum lattice grows with the qudit dimension, yielding a way to simultaneously measure an arbitrarily large range of non-commuting position and momentum displacements. Simple hybrid states can be obtained by applying a conditional displacement to a Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) state and a Pauli eigenstate, or by encoding some of the physical qudits of a stabilizer state into a GKP code. The states’ oscillator-qudit entanglement cannot be generated using symplectic (i.e., Gaussian-Clifford) operations, distinguishing them as a resource from tensor products of oscillator and qudit stabilizer states. We construct general hybrid error-correcting codes by relating stabilizer codes to non-commutative tori and obtaining logical operators via Morita equivalence. We provide examples using commutation matrices, integer symplectic matrices, and binary codes.

II
Simple LCA states

The key features of LCA states and codes are already evident for the case of a single oscillator and qubit.
We overview this case before concluding this section with the natural qudit extension.

A qubit stabilizer state is a
+1
-eigenstate of a group of commuting Pauli operators.
All single-qubit Pauli strings anticommute, so a state whose stabilizer group contains at least two non-identity elements must be defined on two or more qubits.
The smallest example of such a state is the Bell state
|00\rangle+|11\rangle
, stabilized by
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{x}}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{x}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{z}}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{z}}
(tensor product will be implied from now on).
Here,
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{x}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{z}}
are the standard unitary qubit Paulis.

An oscillator stabilizer state is a
+1
-eigenstate of a group of commuting displacement operators.
Let
\exp(-i a \hat{p})
and
\exp(i b \hat{x})
be displacement operators for a single mode, defined using momentum and position operators,
\hat{p}
and
\hat{x}
, respectively, and real displacements
a,b
.
The simplest example, often called the
qunaught
GKP state
Walshe et al. 2020
, is the unique
+1
-eigenstate of the two commuting single-mode displacements for which
a=b=\sqrt{2\pi}
.
Moving along a square using these two displacements traces out an area of
2\pi

\exp(i 2\pi)=1

The corresponding qunaught GKP state is a superposition, or comb, of oscillator positions

|\varnothing\rangle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}|\ell\sqrt{2\pi}\rangle_{\hat{x}}\quad\quad\text{(qunaught state)},

(1)

where
|x\rangle_{\hat{x}}
are (non-normalizable) oscillator position vectors
Albert 2025

A hybrid oscillator-qubit stabilizer state is a
+1
-eigenstate of a group of commuting tensor products of displacement and Pauli operators.
A trivial example is a tensor product of a Bell state with the qunaught GKP state, whose hybrid stabilizer group is the tensor product of the corresponding oscillator and qubit groups.
This state is separable since the qubit and oscillator factors of its group elements are independent.
In other words, commutation is ensured since
the displacements and Pauli elements commute individually and independently of each other.

An LCA stabilizer state admits a stabilizer group whose oscillator and qubit factors
do not
commute individually.
The simplest such group is generated by the pair

\hat{S}_{X}=e^{-i\sqrt{\pi}\hat{p}}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{x}}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\hat{S}_{Z}=e^{i\sqrt{\pi}\hat{x}}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{z}}~.

(2)

The oscillator parts of the above stabilizers anticommute since the phase-space area of the square traced out by them is
\pi

This phase is exactly compensated by the anticommutation of the two Paulis.

The unique state stabilized by the above group is

II.2
Measuring the displacement

We show that the Dirac-delta orthogonal set of displaced states,

In this way, the qudit is “absorbed” into the

To show this, we pretend the physical qubit of the LCA state is in its own GKP mode and infer the required volume by freezing out any displacements that take the state out of the encoded subspace.

II.3
Non-symplectically generated entanglement

The LCA state (
3
) couples each GKP comb with its own qubit basis state, meaning it is entangled across the oscillator-qubit partition.
This entanglement is maximal w.r.t. the qubit, so tracing out the oscillator yields identity on the qubit.
All LCA states are entangled in this sense, and the set of such states forms a completely entangled subspace
Wallach 2000
;
Parthasarathy 2004
;
Parthasarathy 2005
;
Walgate and Scott 2008
of the oscillator-qubit space.
Displaced versions of each state, as in Eq. (
7
), form a completely entangled basis for the space.

The LCA state is also a stabilizer state, namely, it is an eigenstate of a commuting subgroup of the group of oscillator displacements and qubit Paulis.
However, it cannot be obtained from a separable oscillator-qubit state under symplectic transformations, i.e., under any unitaries that preserve this group.
This is in contrast to qubit stabilizer states and qunaught GKP states, which are all interconvertible via Clifford and Gaussian operations, respectively.
In this sense, LCA states form a new class of resource states whose entanglement cannot be created or destroyed by Gaussian-Clifford operations.

The simple reason for this feature is that oscillator-qubit symplectic transformations are always tensor products of a Gaussian operation on the oscillator and a Clifford gate on the qubit.
To see this, we can express the qubit Pauli matrices as exponentials of qubit position and momentum variables,
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{x}}=(-1)^{\hat{m}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{z}}=(-1)^{\hat{\jmath}}
, where
\hat{\jmath}=(1-\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{z}})/2
and
\hat{m}=(1-\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{x}})/2
, respectively
Albert et al. 2017
.
Any entangling symplectic gate must shift the oscillator position or momentum by some multiple of
\hat{m}
or
\hat{\jmath}
.
This multiple is real-valued,
while the qubit position is binary.
Therefore, there can never be an entangling gate of such a form since it would not preserve the binary phase space of the qubit.

Consider, for example, the oscillator-qubit conditional displacement by a real
b
,

\displaystyle\textsc{cdisp}_{b}

\displaystyle=1\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|+e^{ib\hat{p}}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|

(10a)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(ib\hat{p}\otimes\hat{\jmath}\right)~.

(10b)

This gate does not preserve the displacement-Pauli group, i.e., it is not Gaussian-Clifford, because it shifts the oscillator position,

\hat{x}\to\hat{x}+b\hat{\jmath}
,
by a non-binary shift of the qubit.
When conjugating the corresponding displacement, this translates to

e^{ia\hat{x}}\to e^{ia(\hat{x}+b\hat{\jmath})}=e^{ia\hat{x}}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{x}}^{ab/\pi}

(11)

for any real
a
.
The power of
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{x}}
needs to be an integer, but that is not the case for generic
a,b
.

One can show there are no entangling symplectic operations more formally by noticing that such operations correspond to homomorphisms between the configuration spaces of the oscillator and qubit,
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, respectively, and recalling that there are no such homomorphisms.
Roughly speaking, one can never map a finite group into the reals because there is no way to break the periodicity of the group’s elements without violating its multiplication rules (see Appx.
G.2
and Refs.
Prasad and Vemuri 2008
;
Bermejo-Vega 2016
).

Initialization

The LCA state is obtained by applying an oscillator-qubit conditional displacement (

to a tensor product of the GKP code’s zero codeword and the qubit

The comb of the codeword consists of teeth separated by

This yields the two combs making up the LCA state (

(12)

Another way to see that non-Gaussian resources are required to make the LCA state is to observe that the state can be obtained by initializing two qubits in a Bell state and encoding one of them into the GKP code.
The qubit’s Paulis are mapped to the anti-commuting logical operators of the code,
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathsf{x}}\to\exp(-i\sqrt{\pi}\hat{p})
and

.
The stabilizer generators extend to

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{X}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{c}}\hat{p}}X^{\dagger}

(13a)

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{Z}

\displaystyle=e^{i\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{c}}\hat{x}}Z~,

(13b)

where
X,Z
are the usual modular-qudit Pauli matrices satisfying
XZX^{\dagger}Z^{\dagger}=\exp(\ensuremath{-i2\pi/c})
.
The commutation of the qudit factors of each stabilizer compensates the phase arising from the non-commuting displacements.

The two stabilizers admit a unique joint eigenstate with eigenvalue
+1
,

\displaystyle|\text{LCA}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\ell\sqrt{{\textstyle\frac{2\pi}{c}}}\right\rangle_{\hat{x}}\left|-\ell\right\rangle

(14a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{c}}\left(\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left(sc+j\right)\sqrt{{\textstyle\frac{2\pi}{c}}}\right\rangle_{\hat{x}}\right)\left|-j\right\rangle~,

(14b)

where unmarked kets,
\left|-\ell\right\rangle
and
\left|-j\right\rangle
, are the
c
distinct qudit
Z
-eigenstates, defined for argument modulo
c
.

The second form of the state shows that each qudit eigenstate,
\left|-j\right\rangle
, is paired with its own GKP state, and that the
c
different GKP states are spaced apart by
\sqrt{2\pi/c}
.
This means that a displacement of
c\sqrt{2\pi/c}=\sqrt{2\pi c}
is required to map the state back to itself.
This is a factor of
\sqrt{c}
longer than the stabilizing displacement of the qunaught GKP state, obtained from the above at
c=1
.

The momentum-space form of the state can be obtained by inserting a resolution of the identity in the modal momentum and qudit-
X
bases, yielding a similar comb form,

|\text{LCA}\rangle=\sqrt{2\pi c}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{c}}\left(\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left(sc+j\right)\sqrt{{\textstyle\frac{2\pi}{c}}}\right\rangle_{\hat{p}}\right)|j\rangle_{X}~,

(15)

where
|j\rangle_{X}
is the
j
th
X
-eigenstate.
The same argument holds for momentum shifts, and the shortest pure-momentum displacement stabilizer is a momentum shift of
\sqrt{2\pi c}
.

The square unit cell enclosed by
\sqrt{2\pi c}
-displacements in each direction has area
A_{\text{LCA}}=2\pi c
, notably proportional to the qudit dimension.
The set of LCA states displaced by displacements whose values lie in the unit cell is a complete set because of a qudit version of the volume argument we made for the oscillator-qubit case.

To state the argument, we embed the qudit into its own qudit GKP code, after which the oscillator qudit LCA state becomes a GKP-qudit Bell state,

|\text{LCA}\rangle\to\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{c}}|\overline{j}\rangle_{\text{GKP}}|\overline{-j}\rangle_{\text{GKP}}\propto\,e^{i\hat{p}_{1}\hat{x}_{2}}|\overline{0}\rangle_{\text{GKP}}|\overline{+}\rangle_{\text{GKP}}~.

(16)

The proportionality shows that, up to normalization, this Bell state can be created from a separable logical state using the above conditional oscillator-oscillator displacement.
This conditional displacement
is
a Gaussian-Clifford operation, unlike its oscillator-qudit cousin, meaning that it preserves phase-space volume.

Both the qudit GKP-zero and qudit GKP-plus states are Gaussian-deformed versions of the qunaught GKP state, so the joint volume of their unit cell is the same as the product of two qunaught states.
The volume of detectable displacements of the GKP qudit is
2\pi/c
, yielding the correct LCA-state volume,

A_{\text{LCA}}=\frac{A_{\varnothing}^{2}}{A_{\text{GKP}}}=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{2\pi/c}=2\pi c\,.

(17)

Using similar arguments as for the oscillator-qubit case, we conclude that oscillator-qudit symplectic transformations are always tensor products of Gaussian operations and modular-qudit Clifford gates (see Appx.
G.2
).
As such, oscillator-qudit LCA states cannot be obtained by acting with such transformations on a separable state.
The two ways to initialize an LCA state — encoding one half of a qudit Bell state into a GKP qudit, or applying a conditional oscillator-qudit displacement — also carry over to the qudit case.
The oscillator-qudit conditional displacement required for the latter is

\textsc{cdisp}_{\sqrt{2\pi/c}}=e^{i\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{c}}\hat{p}\otimes\hat{\jmath}}=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{c}}e^{ij\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{c}}\hat{p}}\otimes|j\rangle\langle j|~.

(18)

II.6
Hybrid Zak bases

A type of dual basis that exists for GKP codes — the discrete Zak basis
Gel’Fand 1950
;
Zak 1967
— can also be extended to the LCA case
Kaniuth and Kutyniok 1998
;
Enstad 2020
.

The set of distinct displaced LCA states,

|\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\rangle=e^{-i\xi_{1}\hat{p}}e^{i\xi_{2}\hat{x}}|\text{LCA}\rangle
,
is sometimes called the continuous or periodic Zak basis
Englert et al. 2006
;
Ketterer et al. 2016
;
Fabre et al. 2020
;
Pantaleoni et al. 2023
.
It is parameterized by two angles,
0\leq\xi_{1,2}<\sqrt{2\pi c}
, and the fact that it is complete implies that one can parameterize functions on the real line with those on a two-dimensional torus.

Taking the two-dimensional Fourier transform on this torus yields an integer-valued hybrid analogue of the Zak basis,

|n_{1},n_{2}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2\pi c}}d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}e^{i(n_{1}\xi_{2}-n_{2}\xi_{2})}|\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\rangle\,,

(19)

for integers
n_{1,2}
.
Since the unit cell area of the displacements behind displaced LCA states is
2\pi c
, the integers parameterizing the discrete Zak basis can be thought to form a grid of spacing
\sqrt{2\pi c}
— a factor of
\sqrt{c}
larger than the grid of the original discrete Zak basis.

III
Simple LCA codes

We define LCA codes for a single oscillator and a single mode.
These span the joint
+1
-eigenvalue eigenspace of a commuting set of displacement-Pauli operators.

III.1
Stabilizers

LCA codes are defined by a non-negative integer
c
and integer
d
that satisfy

\gcd(c,d)=1\quad\leftrightarrow\quad d\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{c}^{\times}\quad\quad\text{(GCD condition).}

(20)

In other words,
d
is an element of the multiplicative group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{c}^{\times}
of integers modulo
c
— the subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{c}
for which multiplication modulo
c
is well-defined.
The value of
c
is the qudit dimension, and the possible logical dimensions
K
(not all are possible for a given
c
!) are determined by
d
.

The stabilizer group is generated by the operators

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{X}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{2\pi K}{c}}\hat{p}}X^{\dagger d}

(21a)

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{Z}

\displaystyle=e^{i\sqrt{\frac{2\pi K}{c}}\hat{x}}Z~.

(21b)

Since
X^{c}=Z^{c}=1
, stabilizers with the same
K
and different values
d,d^{\prime}
are equivalent whenever
d\equiv d^{\prime}
modulo
c
.
There are thus at most
c-1
different “classes” of qudit factors, corresponding to the
c-1
distinct nontrivial powers of
X^{\dagger}
.
This is saturated for prime
c
, in which case there are exactly
c-1
code classes.

For a given
d\mod c
, the possible values of
K
differ from each other by multiples of
c
and can be modulated by a free integer parameter
\theta
,

K=c\theta+d\quad\quad\text{(logical dimension).}

(22)

All integer values of
d,\theta
are allowed in principle, and an absolute value should be taken on the right-hand side to accommodate this (see Appx.
F.2
for such a treatment).
Instead, we fix
\theta\geq 0
and
0\leq d\leq c-1
, which guarantees a non-negative
K
.
This is done without loss of generality since a code with any other
d
is equivalent to one with a
d
in our defined range via a compensating change in
\theta
, and since a code with
c\theta+d<0
is equivalent to a code with the signs of
\theta,d
flipped.

The commutation relation between the two stabilizers is satisfied due to the qudit and oscillator phases cancelling each other, and due to
\theta
being an integer,

\hat{S}_{X}\hat{S}_{Z}\hat{S}_{X}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{Z}^{\dagger}=e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{c}(c\theta+d-d)}=e^{-i2\pi\theta}=1~.

(23)

The
K=d=1
case for general
c
reduces to the simple LCA states from Sec.
II.5
.
The other classes have
d>1
and thus host logical codespaces.
We call these cases
(c,d)
-
LCA codes
and refer to the
\theta=0
special cases as
simple
(c,d)
-LCA codes
— a family that includes the qunaught GKP state (
c=d=1
), simple LCA states (
c>d=1
), as well as all LCA codes with
K=d>1
.
Any single-mode single-qudit LCA code can be obtained from a simple LCA code by tuning
d
and
\theta
.

III.2
Codewords

Given
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{K}
, a basis of codewords is defined by

\displaystyle\!\!|\overline{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left(\ell K+\mu\right){\textstyle\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{Kc}}}\right\rangle_{\hat{x}}\left|-(\ell d+\mu)\right\rangle

(24)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{c}}\!\!\left(\sum_{s\in\sqrt{2\pi Kc}\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left(jK+\mu\right){\textstyle\sqrt{{\textstyle\frac{2\pi}{Kc}}}}+s\right\rangle_{\hat{x}}\right)\!\!\left|-(jd+\mu)\right\rangle,

where

is a position vector, and where the unmarked ket is a canonical qudit
Z

c

The second equation splits the hybrid comb into a superposition of
c

The qudit state is unique because
-(jd+\mu)

Since each codeword consists of

III.4
Error detection & correction

We present two decoding strategies that use the syndromes of the code in different ways.
The first protects against pure displacements, with the largest uncorrectable displacement being a factor of
\sqrt{c}
larger than that of the GKP code.
The second protects against arbitrary qudit errors at the expense of a smaller set of correctable displacements relative to those of the GKP code.

Pure displacements

The set of displaced states,

|\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\mu\rangle=e^{-i\xi_{1}\hat{p}}e^{i\xi_{2}\hat{x}}|\overline{\mu}\rangle~,

(31)

for logical index
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{K}
and displacements satisfying

0\leq\xi_{1},\xi_{2}<\sqrt{2\pi c/K}~,

(32)

forms a complete set of error states for the oscillator-qudit system.
The corresponding phase-space volume of the unit cell of the hybrid lattice is
2\pi c/K
, and any displacement that keeps the code in this unit cell is detectable.

The shortest codespace-preserving displacement in each direction is the
c
th power of a logical operator.
For example,

\overline{X}^{c}=e^{-ic\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{Kc}}\hat{p}}X^{\dagger c}=e^{-id_{\text{LCA}}\hat{p}}~.

(33)

This is a factor of
\sqrt{c}
larger than the same displacement for a
K
-dimensional square-lattice GKP code.
By analogy with
\sqrt{2\pi/K}=d_{\text{GKP}}
being the distance of the GKP code,
we can define

d_{\text{LCA}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi c}{K}}=\sqrt{c}~d_{\text{GKP}}\quad\quad\text{(code distance)}

(34)

to be the pure-displacement distance of the LCA code.

Displacements in either direction,
\epsilon_{1}
in position and
\epsilon_{2}
in momentum, can be corrected as long as the absolute value of either displacement is less than
d_{\text{LCA}}/2
,

0\leq|\epsilon_{1}|,|\epsilon_{2}|<\frac{d_{\text{LCA}}}{2}\quad\quad\text{(correctable pure disp's)}.

(35)

This is a larger range of correctable displacements than that of a GKP code, but correction need not hold when qudit errors are considered.

Qudit errors

An alternative decoding strategy sacrifices some protection against displacements but protects against
arbitrary
qudit errors.

Each error space, labeled by a syndrome pair
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
, can be related to the codespace via either a pure displacement or a combination of qudit error and smaller displacement.
Here, we assume the latter, yielding a way to protect against all qudit errors and the following set of shifts,

0\leq|\epsilon_{1}|,|\epsilon_{2}|<\frac{d_{\text{LCA}}}{2c}=\delta\quad\text{(disp's + qudit errors)}.

(36)

This is a smaller range of correctable displacements than that of a GKP code.

We describe this decoding procedure first for position shifts and Pauli-
X
errors.
The syndrome range
[0,d_{\text{LCA}})\ni\xi_{1}
is partitioned into
c
regions,

\displaystyle=\left[0,\delta\right)\cup\left[(2c-1)\delta,2c\delta\right)

(37a)

IV
Standard form of

Simple

Such codes can be studied in a lattice formalism by collecting powers of each stabilizer generator into a lattice generator matrix

IV.1
Single-mode case

We first build a lattice generator matrix out of the simple
(c,d)
-LCA code from the previous section, with
\theta=0
and nonzero

Each column of the matrix

T=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{d/c}&0\\
0&\sqrt{d/c}\\
-d&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}T_{\textsc{cv}}\\
T_{\textsc{dv}}\end{pmatrix}

describes one of the two stabilizer generators of the single-mode single-qudit LCA code. The first two entries in each column, from top to bottom, are the amounts the generator displaces the mode’s position and momentum, divided by
\sqrt{2\pi}
.
The second two entries are the powers of
X
and
Z
, in that order.

The second equality above expresses the matrix as a block matrix of two blocks, the
2\times 2
continuous-variable (

) block defining the mode’s operators making up each generator, and the similarly sized discrete-variable (
dv
) block defining the qudit’s operators.

Encoding a set of commuting operators, the matrix

satisfies a symplectic condition

T^{\intercal}JT=0~,\quad\quad\text{where}\quad\quad J=J_{\textsc{cv}}\oplus J_{\textsc{dv}},

(42)

where
T^{\intercal}
is the transpose of
T
, and where the hybrid symplectic form

is a block-diagonal matrix consisting of

and
dv

J_{\textsc{dv}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/c\\
-1/c&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{\textsc{cv}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(43)

Since the oscillator and qudit pieces do not commute independently, the
cv
and
dv

Instead, the two pieces contribute the same matrix

, but with opposite signs,

\displaystyle T_{\textsc{dv}}^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{dv}}T_{\textsc{dv}}

\displaystyle=+Z=\frac{d}{c}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

(44a)

\displaystyle T_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{cv}}T_{\textsc{cv}}

\displaystyle=-Z~.

(44b)

The contributions of these two pieces cancel, thereby satisfying Eq. (
42

IV.2
Multi-mode normal form

The single-mode code yields a natural generalization to

We define diagonal matrices,

\displaystyle\bm{d}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{p})

\displaystyle\bm{c}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{p})\,,

(45b)

From now on,

The multi-mode standard form is

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}T_{\textsc{cv}}\\
T_{\textsc{dv}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle T_{\textsc{cv}}

\displaystyle=\bm{1}_{2}\otimes\sqrt{\bm{d/c}}

(46b)

\displaystyle T_{\textsc{dv}}

\displaystyle=(\bm{-d})\oplus\bm{1}_{p}~,

The

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

\displaystyle J_{\textsc{cv}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

The degree of non-commutation for the

The logical dimension

\displaystyle K=|\text{Pf}\,\bm{d/c}|\det\bm{c}=\det\bm{c}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\frac{d_{j}}{c_{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{p}d_{j}~.

(50)

Above, we use the formula for the Pfaffian of an anti-symmetric matrix.

The matrix

encodes the degree of non-commutation of the

and

On the

;

IV.3
Relation to commutation matrices

We can obtain the LCA standard form (
46
) by starting with a general commutation matrix of a Pauli algebra, determining the minimal qudit dimensions needed to realize that algebra, and appending an oscillator displacement operator to each Pauli to compensate their non-commutativity.

We consider a Pauli matrix set without specifying the dimension of the qudits that this set acts on.
Such a set is defined by its group commutators, which, for any two elements
P_{j},P_{k}
of the set, are of the form
P_{i}P_{j}P_{i}^{\dagger}P_{j}^{\dagger}=\exp(-i 2\pi v/u)
for some integers

We convert this information into a

anti-symmetric rational commutation matrix

;
Gunderman 2023
;
Sarkar and Yoder 2024

th entry is

th entry is
-v/u

Any rational matrix

can be made into an integer matrix

by multiplying by

To determine the minimal dimensions

of the
p
qudits required to realize the Pauli algebra defined by

into

(a.k.a. skew-normal form, normal form with respect to congruence, or symplectic normal form)

Kuperberg 2001
, Thm. 18)

;
Sarkar and Yoder 2024
;
Conrad 2024

(
Lang 2002
, p. 598, Exercise XV.17)
.
This can be done via an invertible integer matrix

\pm 1
(i.e., is unimodular).
Such matrices form the group
\mathrm{GL}(2p,\mathbb{Z})
, and one of them brings

into normal form that satisfies

R^{\intercal}AR=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\frac{\bm{h}}{m}\quad\quad\text{(alt.{} Smith form)},

(51)

where

is a diagonal matrix of non-negative integers

that are ordered so that they divide each other, i.e.,

In this section, we assume none of the

We can equate each diagonal element

to a ratio

with
\gcd(K_{j},c_{j})=1
.
Moreover, we can split up

into some integer multiple

and a nonzero remainder

These are defined to match the notation of the LCA codes.
Altogether, we have

\frac{\bm{h}}{m}=\bm{\theta}+\bm{d/c}~,

(52)

where we have used the notation
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots)
for any bold symbol.

We can absorb the
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)

(53)

where the matrices

\displaystyle\varTheta

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\bm{\theta}

(54a)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\bm{d/c}~.

(54b)

Setting

yields the same

matrix as that arising from a tensor product of simple LCA codes in standard form (

).

In summary, the above procedure splits a general anti-symmetric matrix

into a piece

that can be used to define a simple multi-mode LCA code, and another piece

that can toggle the code’s logical dimension.
A slight modification of the oscillator encoding map accommodates this toggling,

\displaystyle T_{\textsc{cv}}

\displaystyle=\bm{1}_{2}\otimes\sqrt{\bm{\theta+d/c}}

(55a)

\displaystyle T_{\textsc{dv}}

\displaystyle=(\bm{-d})\oplus\bm{1}_{p}~,

(55b)

yielding a more general tensor-product LCA code.
This code’s encoder satisfies

T^{\intercal}JT=\varTheta-Z+Z=\varTheta~,

(56)

the defining condition for an integral symplectic lattice.
The GKP case is recovered by setting all

.

The partitioning

into a multiple of
c_{j}
and a remainder is not necessary, and an alternative formulation can relax both

and

to be arbitrary integers (see Appx.
F.2
for such a treatment).
That way, a

can be absorbed into

can be used to construct the same LCA code.

These constructions extend further to those that consider

and

In other words, the two matrices cannot put into alternating Smith normal form using the same unimodular transformation.

V
General LCA codes

We borrow

;
Schwarz 1998
;
Connes et al. 1998
;
Rieffel and Schwarz 1999
;
Seiberg and Witten 1999
;

arbitrary

We continue to keep

Each

V.1
From lattices to non-commutative tori

The defining equation (
56
) for an LCA code,
T^{\intercal}JT=\varTheta
, can be interpreted as an embedding of a lattice into a system of oscillators and qudits defined by the hybrid symplectic form
J=J_{\textsc{cv}}\oplus J_{\textsc{dv}}
(
47
).
Columns of the generator matrix
T
define stabilizers that leave the lattice invariant, while the anti-symmetric integer Gram matrix
\varTheta

defines how these stabilizers commute.

More precisely, two stabilizers,
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
, commute as

U_{i}U_{j}=e^{-2\pi i\varTheta_{ij}}U_{j}U_{i}\,.

(57)

This stabilizer algebra is an example of a
non-commutative torus
.
In this case, the torus is commutative because
\varTheta
is integer, but rational matrices, such as commutation matrices (see Sec.
IV.3
), or irrational matrices define non-commutative variants.

The stabilizer algebra is called a torus because it is in one-to-one correspondence with the unit cell of the lattice defined by the stabilizer generators.
For example, the unit cell of the square-lattice GKP code, whose phase-space lattice
\mathscr{D}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, is the two-dimensional torus
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathscr{D}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
, where
\mathbb{T}\cong U(1)
is the circle group.
Since “tori” and “lattices” are dual to each other in this way, we use the two words interchangeably to describe
\varTheta
.

The coefficients of LCA logical displacements,
\overline{X}
and
\overline{Z}
(
28
), can also be collected into columns of their own generator matrix
S
.
This matrix defines an embedding of the code’s dual lattice and its associated dual torus
\varTheta^{\perp}
, satisfying

S^{\intercal}JS=-\varTheta^{\perp}~.

(58)

Here,
\varTheta^{\perp}
is rational since the logicals do not commute, making this dual torus non-commutative.

In the case of GKP codes
Gottesman et al. 2001
, the dual torus is related to the original as
\varTheta^{\perp}=\varTheta^{-1}
.
The purpose of the non-commutative torus formalism is to determine the dual torus and the corresponding logical operators, defined by
S
, for general LCA codes.

V.2
Morita equivalence and
\textrm{SO}(2p,2p~|~{\mathbb{Z}})

The torus
\varTheta
and its dual
\varTheta^{\perp}
are related by a general duality transformation — Morita equivalence — that is valued in a particular group.

Consider first the single-mode
(c,d)
-LCA code from Sec.
III
.
The code’s two-dimensional torus
\varTheta=\theta\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, is defined solely by the stabilizer commutator phase
\theta
(
23
).
The code’s dual torus is determined by the commutator phase of the logical operators (
29
).
This phase is obtained from the stabilizer commutator phase via the mapping

\theta\to\frac{a\theta+b}{c\theta+d}~,

(59)

where
(c,d)
are the defining parameters of the code, and where
(a,b)
are obtained from these parameters via the Bezout identity (
27
),
bc-ad=1
.
The four integers can be arranged into the matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-a&-b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
whose determinant is one, revealing that the mapping between the tori is a special case of a Möbius transformation.

The mapping between phases can be upgraded to a matrix-valued mapping directly between
\varTheta
.
For an LCA code on
p
modes, define the
4p
-dimensional block matrix

g=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}~,

(60)

which acts on the matrix defining the torus as

g\cdot\varTheta=(A\varTheta+B)(C\varTheta+D)^{-1},

(61)

and whose
2p
-dimensional blocks
A,B,C,D
satisfy

\displaystyle A^{\intercal}C+C^{\intercal}A=B^{\intercal}D+D^{\intercal}B

\displaystyle=0

(62a)

\displaystyle A^{\intercal}D+C^{\intercal}B

\displaystyle=\bm{1}_{2p}\,.

(62b)

The conditions on the blocks imply that
g
lies in the group
\textrm{SO}(2p,2p~|~{\mathbb{Z}})
, the group of orientation-preserving (i.e., determinant-one) integer-valued rotations in
4p
dimensions that preserve the quadratic form
x_{1}x_{2p+1}+x_{2}x_{2p+2}+\cdots+x_{2p}x_{4p}
.
Any two tori related by such a

via Eq. (
61
) can be shown to be
Morita equivalent

Rieffel and Schwarz 1999
;
Li 2004
;
Li 2004
;
Elliott and Li 2008
.
This yields a classification of different tori into Morita-equivalent classes.
Reference
Dereli and Popov 2021
discusses Morita equivalence in the case of GKP codes.

This classification is exceedingly coarse-grained.
Not only is a commutative torus
\varTheta
Morita equivalent to its non-commutative dual,
all
rational tori are equivalent to each other
(
Elliott and Li 2008
, Corr. 4.2)
because

transformations allow one to add, multiply, and divide by nearly arbitrary integers.
Nonetheless, one can work out the precise

necessary to obtain the dual torus of any given torus.
This yields logical operators for general LCA codes.

General construction

We state the LCA construction in the language of quantum coding theory and provide a parallel mathematical discussion in Appxs.

Logical dimension

Calculating the logical dimension

Namely,

is the ratio of the size of the torus
\varTheta
to its dual
\varTheta^{\perp}

to its dual the logical lattice

K^{2}=\frac{|({\mathbb{R}}^{2p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}}^{2})/\mathscr{D}|}{|({\mathbb{R}}^{2p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}}^{2})/\mathscr{D}^{\perp}|}~.

(68)

Here, we use the shorthand

An interesting simplification (see Appx.

K=\left|\text{Pf}(\varTheta-Z)\right|\det\bm{c}=\frac{\det\bm{ct}}{m^{p}}\,.

(69)

It is not obvious this is an integer, but it is so because the product of the qudit dimensions is designed to cancel out any denominators in the Pfaffian.

VI
Logical Clifford gates

GKP codes admit logical Clifford gates via Gaussian transformations.
We anticipate that LCA codes similarly admit logical Clifford gates via Gaussian-Clifford transformations.
We define such transformations and prove this for several families of LCA codes.

VI.1
Gaussian-Clifford transformations

Continuous and modular degrees of freedom cannot be mixed in a way that preserves the hybrid displacement-Pauli group (see Sec.
II.3
and Appx.
G.2
).
The hybrid symplectic group on
p

k

\text{Sp}(2p,{\mathbb{R}})\times\text{Sp}(2k,{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}})\,.

(70)

The latter factor is shorthand for the group of Pauli-group preserving operations on
k

c_{1}
through
c_{k}
.
This group includes the Clifford group of each qudit

A Gaussian-Clifford operation can be represented by the block matrix

(71)

Logical gates from lattice symmetries

Each torus

The symmetry group, or

These depend on the structure of

\operatorname{Aut}(\varTheta)=\operatorname{Sp}(2,{\mathbb{Z}})\quad\quad\quad\text{($p=1$ case)}

GKP case

Embedding a two-dimensional lattice into an oscillator yields a GKP code.
It is known that each automorphism

\displaystyle V_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{cv}}V_{\textsc{cv}}

\displaystyle=T_{\textsc{cv}}^{-\intercal}W^{\intercal}\varTheta WT_{\textsc{cv}}^{-1}

VII
Examples

We discuss ways to concatenate LCA codes, mention logical-mode encodings, and provide further multi-mode multi-qudit examples using integer symplectic matrices and linear binary codes.

Hybrid concatenation

Any qudit stabilizer code can be converted into an LCA code by encoding one or more of its physical qudits into a GKP qudit.
The non-Gaussian nature of the GKP encoder makes it impossible to encode information into this code using only Gaussian-Clifford operations.

One can also take any qudit stabilizer code and encode each of its physical
d
-dimensional qudits into a
(c,d)
-LCA qudit.
This reduces to a qudit stabilizer code concatenated with a GKP code for
c=1

Including an additional discrete degree of freedom for each physical system may lower code thresholds further than
concatenating with a GKP code

Fukui et al. 2017
;
Fukui et al. 2018
;
Vuillot et al. 2019
;
Noh and Chamberland 2020
.

Logical-mode encodings

Minimial analogues of oscillator-into-oscillator GKP codes
Noh et al. 2020
can be constructed on a two-mode and single-qudit system as follows.
Initialize mode 1 in an arbitrary state, initialize mode 2 and the qudit in an LCA state, and then apply a two-mode Gaussian transformation on the two modes.
This encoding extends the two-mode-squeezing code, which underlies the standard form of oscillator-into-oscillator GKP codes
Wu et al. 2023
, to the LCA case.
The use of the qudit may reduce the logical noise floor of the resulting logical-mode code against displacement noise.

VIII
Conclusion & homework

We develop the theory of oscillator-qudit stabilizer states and error-correcting codes.
By “stabilizer”, we mean that the states or codes are joint
+1
-eigenvalue eigenspaces of a commuting group of oscillator displacements and qudit Pauli strings.

Our framework is based on mathematical embeddings of integral symplectic lattices into locally compact Abelian (LCA) groups, which define the hybrid configuration space of oscillator-qudit systems.
As such, we refer to our constructions as
LCA states
and
LCA codes
.

LCA states are entangled with respect to the oscillator-qudit partition.
They are superpositions of tensor-product states, each of which consisting of a Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) quantum lattice state
Gel’Fand 1950
;
Zak 1967
;
Aharonov et al. 1969
;
Gottesman et al. 2001
and a qudit basis state.
They cannot be obtained from a tensor product of oscillator and qudit stabilizer states via Gaussian-Clifford transformations because such transformations are non-entangling.
This is in contrast to GKP and qudit stabilizer states, which can all be obtained from each other via Gaussian and Clifford operations, respectively.
Since an LCA state can be obtained from a qudit stabilizer state by encoding a part of the state into a GKP code, the distinction between state classes highlights the usefulness of the GKP encoding map as a distinct resource (cf.
Baragiola et al. 2019
).

LCA states

LCA states are characterized by a rational anti-symmetric matrix
Z
that is called, in other contexts, a commutation matrix
Englbrecht et al. 2022
;
Gunderman 2023
;
Sarkar and Yoder 2024
(cf. the frustration graph matrix
Makuta et al. 2025
;
Mann et al. 2025
).
The matrices
\mp Z
quantifies the non-commutativity among the parts of each LCA stabilizer generator that are supported on the oscillator and qudit factors, respectively.
Their opposite signs guarantee that the non-commutativity between the two parts cancels, ensuring that all stabilizers commute.

The shortest undetectable displacement of an oscillator-qudit LCA state is larger than that of the GKP state by a square-root of the physical qudit dimension.
As such, LCA states can be used to measure a larger set of non-commuting displacements than GKP states, making them useful for various metrological protocols
Valahu et al. 2024
;
Duivenvoorden et al. 2017
;
Zhuang et al. 2020
;
Labarca et al. 2025
.

LCA states correspond to symplectic lattices in phase space, but another class of oscillator stabilizer states — analog/Gaussian stabilizer states
Lloyd and Slotine 1998
;
Braunstein 1998
;
Barnes 2004
;
Kwon et al. 2025
— correspond to hyperplanes.
Embedding hyperplanes into hybrid oscillator-qudit systems
Jakobsen and Lemvig 2016
is an interesting topic for future investigation, especially given their highly entangled nature
Zhang et al. 2009
;
Kwon et al. 2025
.

The LCA framework combines real-space and binary/
q
-ary lattices into one hybrid structure.
Since both types of lattices yield computationally hard problems,
the LCA framework provides an intriguing playground for the development of lattice-based quantum algorithms and post-quantum encryption schemes
Regev 2009
;
Conrad et al. 2024a
;
Kuperberg 2025
.
It will also be interesting to investigate connections of LCA states to Landau-Level physics
Mumford 2007
;
Haldane and Rezayi 1985
;
Gottesman et al. 2001
;
Dereli and Popov 2021
;
Rymarz et al. 2021
;
Fan et al. 2023
.

LCA codes

We construct LCA codes using the theory of non-commutative tori
Rieffel 1988
;
Schwarz 1998
;
Connes et al. 1998
;
Rieffel and Schwarz 1999
;
Seiberg and Witten 1999
;
Yoneya 2000
;
Li 2004
;
Elliott and Li 2008
, an important example in non-commutative geometry.
Integral symplectic lattices correspond to commutative tori, and our construction extends their embeddings into real space — GKP codes — to those into products of real space and discrete qudit factors.
The main reason for deploying the intricate mathematical theory is to determine the non-commutative torus of logical operators that is dual to the commutative torus defining an LCA code’s stabilizers.

We develop two separate error-correcting strategies, one against pure displacement noise, and another against a smaller set of displacements and all qudit errors.
It would be interesting to develop an “intermediate” strategy protecting against small displacements and small, but not all, qudit errors.

GKP codes can increase the error-correcting power of qubit encodings by concatenating each physical qubit into a GKP code
Fukui et al. 2017
;
Fukui et al. 2018
;
Vuillot et al. 2019
;
Noh and Chamberland 2020
.
GKP mode-into-mode encodings can even help with storing an infinite amount of quantum information
Noh et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2023
.
We anticipate that our various outlined LCA concatenation strategies should further boost code performance and error-correcting thresholds.

The non-commutative torus framework is mathematically mature, but more work needs to be done to draw it closer to applications.
While it yields an exceedingly general class of codes, it is not clear when two given general LCA codes are equivalent via symplectic operations.
Logical gates on LCA codes correspond to automorphisms of non-commutative tori
Echterhoff et al. 2010
;
Jeong and Lee 2015
;
Chakraborty and Luef 2019
;
Chakraborty 2023
;
Chakraborty 2025
, and studying their structure and degree of fault tolerance is of primary interest (cf.
Gottesman et al. 2001
;
Conrad et al. 2024b
;
Burchards et al. 2024
for the GKP case).
The generalized Möbius transformation for obtaining the logical operators may be related to similar transformations in other contexts
(
Van den Nest et al. 2004
, Eq. (3))
(
Menicucci et al. 2011
, Eq. (2.24))
.

General embeddings of non-commutative tori can be done into LCA groups that contain integer factors
{\mathbb{Z}}
, which define linear rotors
Albert et al. 2017
.
But when the torus is commutative, the rotor factors decouple from the oscillator and qudit factors.
While the LCA construction does not extend to rotor-oscillator and rotor-qudit codes, one can instead use rotor-oscillator entangling symplectic operations (generated by
\hat{x}\otimes\hat{L}
and
\hat{p}\otimes\hat{L}
for rotor momentum
\hat{L}
) and qudit-oscillator entangling symplectic operations (generated by
\hat{m}\otimes\hat{L}
and
\hat{\jmath}\otimes\hat{L}
in the notation of our paper) to make entangled states and codes.
We leave development of such stabilizer encodings as a topic for future investigation.

T_{m_{\textsc{cv}}}=e^{-\sqrt{2\pi}im_{\textsc{cv}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}},\quad M_{s_{\textsc{cv}}}=e^{\sqrt{2\pi}is_{\textsc{cv}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}}.

(97)

Here,
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2},\cdots,\hat{x}_{p})
is a vector of position operators, and the same for the vector momentum operator
\hat{\mathbf{p}}
.
The displacement operator on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
is then defined as

D(x_{\textsc{cv}})=D(m_{\textsc{cv}},s_{\textsc{cv}}):=M_{s_{\textsc{cv}}}T_{m_{\textsc{cv}}},

(98)

x_{\textsc{cv}}=(m_{\textsc{cv}},s_{\textsc{cv}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\times\widehat{{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}.

These operators satisfy the following commutation relation:

D(m_{\textsc{cv}},s_{\textsc{cv}})\,D(l_{\textsc{cv}},t_{\textsc{cv}})=e^{-2\pi i\langle x_{\textsc{cv}},J_{\textsc{cv}}^{\prime}y_{\textsc{cv}}\rangle}\,D(m_{\textsc{cv}}+l_{\textsc{cv}},s_{\textsc{cv}}+t_{\textsc{cv}}),

(99)

where

x_{\textsc{cv}}=(m_{\textsc{cv}},s_{\textsc{cv}}),~y_{\textsc{cv}}=(l_{\textsc{cv}},t_{\textsc{cv}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\times\widehat{{\mathbb{R}}^{p}},~J_{\textsc{cv}}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{1}_{p}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(100)

Moreover, the standard Heisenberg commutation relation holds:

D(x_{\textsc{cv}})D(y_{\textsc{cv}})=e^{-2\pi i\langle x_{\textsc{cv}},J_{\textsc{cv}}y_{\textsc{cv}}\rangle}D(y_{\textsc{cv}})D(x_{\textsc{cv}}),\quad\text{for all }x_{\textsc{cv}},y_{\textsc{cv}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{p}}.

(101)

Now let us define
D(m_{\textsc{dv}},s_{\textsc{dv}})
, which are the usual qudit Weyl displacement operators. Define the operators
T_{m_{\textsc{dv}}}
and
M_{s_{\textsc{dv}}}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}})
by

T_{m_{\textsc{dv}}}\,\xi(t)=\xi\left(m^{-1}t\right),\quad M_{s_{\textsc{dv}}}\,\xi(t)=\langle t,s_{\textsc{dv}}\rangle\,\xi(t),

(102)

for
\xi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}})
and
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}}
. The pairing is given by

\langle t,s_{\textsc{dv}}\rangle=e^{2\pi i{\sum_{i=1}^{k}}\frac{s_{i}t_{i}}{c_{i}}},\quad\text{where }s=(s_{i})_{i=1}^{k},\;t=(t_{i})_{i=1}^{k}.

(103)

Then, for
x_{\textsc{dv}}=(m_{\textsc{dv}},s_{\textsc{dv}})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}}\times\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}}}
, the displacement operator is defined as

D(m_{\textsc{dv}},s_{\textsc{dv}}):=M_{s_{\textsc{dv}}}T_{m_{\textsc{dv}}}.

(104)

These operators satisfy the discrete Heisenberg commutation relation:

D(x_{\textsc{dv}})\,D(y_{\textsc{dv}})=e^{-2\pi i\langle x_{\textsc{dv}},J_{\textsc{dv}}y_{\textsc{dv}}\rangle}\,D(y_{\textsc{dv}})\,D(x_{\textsc{dv}}).

(105)

Finally, if we define the general displacement operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}})
as

D(x):=D(m_{\textsc{cv}},s_{\textsc{cv}})\otimes D(m_{\textsc{dv}},s_{\textsc{dv}}),\quad\text{for }x=(m,s)\in M\times\widehat{M},

(106)

then the full Heisenberg commutation relation reads

D(x)\,D(y)=e^{-2\pi i\langle x,Jy\rangle}\,D(y)\,D(x),\quad\text{for all }x,y\in M\times\widehat{M}.

(107)

Remark 1
.

Note that in the case
M=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, Eq. (
99
) does not exactly match the convention commonly used in the physics or mathematics literature (e.g.,
(
Conrad et al. 2022
, Equation 6)
). To align with the standard convention, one needs to modify the definition of the displacement operator
D(m_{\textsc{cv}},s_{\textsc{cv}})
by including a phase factor
e^{-\pi i\langle s_{\textsc{cv}},m_{\textsc{cv}}\rangle}
. This modification does not affect Eq. (
101
) or Eq. (
107
). In fact, we incorporate this modification by introducing phases to the displacement operators later in Definition
1
.

We call a subgroup

of

Define the

From now on, we only consider lattices inside

We have already defined a natural bicharacter

Let

Appendix D
The group
\mathrm{SO}(n,n\mid\mathbb{Z})
and the corresponding lattices

Let
\mathrm{O}(n,n\mid\mathbb{R})
be the group of linear transformations of the space
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
preserving the quadratic form
x_{1}x_{n+1}+x_{2}x_{n+2}+\cdots+x_{n}x_{2n}
, and
\mathrm{SO}(n,n\mid\mathbb{Z})
be the subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n,n\mid\mathbb{R})
consisting of matrices with integer entries and determinant 1. We can write an element
g
of
\mathrm{O}(n,n\mid\mathbb{R})
as

g=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}\right),

(116)

where
A,B,C,D
are
n\times n
matrices satisfying

A^{\intercal}C+C^{\intercal}A=0=B^{\intercal}D+D^{\intercal}B,\quad A^{\intercal}D+C^{\intercal}B=\bm{1}_{n}.

(117)

We now define a (partial) action of
\mathrm{SO}(n,n\mid\mathbb{Z})
on the space of symplectic Gram matrices
\mathscr{T}_{n}
as

g\cdot\varTheta:=(A\varTheta+B)(C\varTheta+D)^{-1}

(118)

whenever
C\varTheta+D
is invertible. It is known that for each
g\in\mathrm{SO}(n,n\mid\mathbb{Z}),
this action is defined on a dense open subset of
\mathscr{T}_{n}

Rieffel and Schwarz 1999
;
Li 2004
.

A particular
g
can be used to obtained the dual lattice
-g\cdot\varTheta
from a given LCA code defined by
\varTheta
.
The columns of the Gram matrix
S
of that lattice can then be used to define logical Pauli operators.

Theorem 2

(Theorem
1
, detailed restatement)
.

Let
Z
be matrix in
\mathscr{T}_{n}
such that its entries are all rational numbers. Then there exists a
g\in\mathrm{SO}(n,n\mid\mathbb{Z})
and a finite abelian group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}},
such that for any
\varTheta\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
with
\varTheta-Z
invertible there are embedding maps
T,S:L^{*}\rightarrow H^{*},

H^{*}=\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{*p}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{*k}
with
M=\mathbb{R}^{p}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\bm{c}},
for the symplectic Gram matrices
\varTheta
and
-g\cdot\varTheta
respectively satisfying
S\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right)=\left(T\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right)\right)^{\perp}.

Proof.

We refer to the proof of Proposition 4.1 in
Li 2004
. However, we write down the explicit description of
T,S,g,
and
g\cdot\varTheta.

Since
Z
is rational, there is some
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
mZ
is integral.
Thinking of
mZ
as a bilinear alternating form on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
we can bring it to alternating Smith normal form.
Namely, we can find an
R\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
, some integer
1\leq k\leq p
and unique positive integers
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\geq 0
satisfying
h_{1}|h_{2}|\cdots|h_{k}
such that

mZ=R^{\intercal}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\bm{h}&0\\
-\bm{h}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)R~,

(119)

where
\bm{h}=\operatorname{diag}\left(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\right)
. Let
d_{j}/c_{j}=h_{j}/m
with
\textnormal{gcd}\left(d_{j},c_{j}\right)=1
and
c_{j}>0
for each
1\leq j\leq k
. Set
W=\mathbb{Z}_{c_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{c_{k}}
.

Let
\varTheta\in\mathscr{T}_{n}
with
\varTheta-Z
is invertible.
So we can find a
T_{\textsc{cv}}\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})
such that
T_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{cv}}T_{\textsc{cv}}=\varTheta-Z
using the alternating Smith normal form of
\varTheta-Z
.
Let
\bm{d}=\operatorname{diag}\left(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\right),

\bm{c}=\operatorname{diag}\left(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\right),
and let

T_{\textsc{dv}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\bm{d}&0&0\\

(120)

T_{\textsc{cv}},T_{\textsc{dv}}
and
T
have sizes
n\times n,2k\times n
and
(n+2k)\times n
, respectively.

, where

is defined in Eq. (
90
). Notice that as a linear map from

to

carries the lattice

into
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{*p}\times\mathbb{Z}^{k}\times\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. Thus the conditions of an embedding map are satisfied and hence

is an embedding map for
\varTheta
.

For each
1\leq j\leq k
since
\textnormal{gcd}\left(d_{j},c_{j}\right)=1

a_{j},b_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
a_{j}d_{j}+b_{j}c_{j}=1
. Let

\bm{b}=\operatorname{diag}\left(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\right),\quad\bm{a}=\operatorname{diag}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\right).

(121)

\bm{a}&0&0\end{array}\right)\end{array}\right),

(122)

where

has size
n\times n

S_{\textsc{cv}}=J_{\textsc{cv}}\left(T_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}R^{\intercal}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\bm{c}^{-1}&0&0\\
0&\bm{c}^{-1}&0\\
0&0&-\bm{1}_{2p-2k}\end{array}\right).

(123)

Then
S
is an embedding map for

-\varTheta^{\perp}=S^{\intercal}JS

(124)

where

\displaystyle\varTheta^{\perp}=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\bm{c}^{-1}&0&0\\
0&\bm{c}^{-1}&0\\
0&0&-\bm{1}_{2p-2k}\end{array}\right)R(\varTheta-Z)^{-1}R^{\intercal}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\bm{c}^{-1}&0&0\\
0&\bm{c}^{-1}&0\\
0&0&-\bm{1}_{2p-2k}\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-\bm{a}\bm{c}^{-1}&0\\
\bm{a}\bm{c}^{-1}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right).

(125)

Now we have to find
g=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}A^{\prime}&B^{\prime}\\
C^{\prime}&D^{\prime}\end{array}\right)\in\mathrm{SO}(n,n\mid\mathbb{Z})
such that
\varTheta^{\perp}=g\cdot\varTheta
. We have

\displaystyle A^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-\bm{a}&0\\
\bm{a}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)R^{-\intercal},\quad B^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\bm{b}&0&0\\
0&\bm{b}&0\\
0&0&-\bm{1}_{2p-2k}\end{array}\right)R

(126)

(127)

D.1
Qudit symplectic transformations

The construction of codes in Theorem
2
can be generalized straightforwardly.
Let
L\in\mathrm{GL}(2k,\mathbb{Z})
be an integer symplectic matrix, meaning that it satisfies
L^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{dv}}L=J_{\textsc{dv}}
.
Such a matrix can arise from symplectic transformations on the qudit side (see
(
Newman and Smart 1964
, Theorem 1)
), and any such matrix is automatically a modular symplectic matrix since satisfaction of the integer symplectic condition guarantees satisfaction of the modular one.
We then modify the definition of
T
in Theorem
2
as follows:

T^{L}_{\textsc{dv}}=L\begin{pmatrix}\bm{d}&0&0\\
0&\bm{1}_{k}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad R=LT_{\textsc{dv}},\qquad T^{L}=\begin{pmatrix}T_{\textsc{cv}}\\
T^{L}_{\textsc{dv}}\end{pmatrix}.

(128)

A direct calculation shows that
(T^{L})^{\intercal}JT^{L}=\varTheta
; hence
T^{L}
is an embedding of
\varTheta
.

If we further assume

L\begin{pmatrix}\bm{d}&0&0\\
0&\bm{1}_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{d}&0&0\\
0&\bm{1}_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}\tilde{L},

(129)

for some
\tilde{L}\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
, the quantities
\varTheta^{\perp},g
, and
S
can be obtained exactly as in Theorem
2
by absorbing
\tilde{L}
into
R
; that is, replace
R
with
\tilde{L}R
in the arguments of Theorem
2
. Note that this assumption is not required to define the code; rather, it ensures a convenient description of the dual lattice as stated in Theorem
2
. The condition holds in many cases, for example, when
\bm{d}=\bm{1}_{k}
.

Logical dimension calculation

If

|\mathscr{D}^{\perp}/\mathscr{D}|=\frac{|G/\mathscr{D}|}{|G/\mathscr{D}^{\perp}|}

Rieffel 1988
,
|G/\mathscr{D}|
is
|\det(T_{\cv})|
and
|G/\mathscr{D}^{\perp}|
is
|\det(S_{\cv})|.
Hence
K^{2}=|\det(T_{\cv})|/|\det(S_{\cv})|.

We now aim to justify why the numbers

K^{2}=\frac{|\det(T_{\cv})|}{|\det(S_{\cv})|},

(130)

and
K
are integers, where
\varTheta
is an integer matrix. Recall that

S_{\textsc{cv}}=J_{\textsc{cv}}(T_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal})^{-1}R^{\intercal}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{c}^{-1}&0&0\\
0&\bm{c}^{-1}&0\\
0&0&-\bm{1}_{2p-2k}\end{pmatrix},\quad T_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{cv}}T_{\textsc{cv}}=\varTheta-Z,\quad\bm{c}^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{c_{k}}\right).

(131)

Then,

\det(S_{\cv})=\det\left(J_{\textsc{cv}}(T_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal})^{-1}R^{\intercal}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{c}^{-1}&0&0\\
0&\bm{c}^{-1}&0\\
0&0&-\bm{1}_{2p-2k}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\det(T_{\cv})^{-1}\det(R)\det(\dia{c}^{-1})^{2}.

(132)

Hence,

\frac{\det(T_{\cv})}{\det(S_{\cv})}=\frac{\det(T_{\cv})^{2}}{\det(R)\det(\dia{c}^{-1})^{2}}=\frac{\det(\varTheta- Z)}{\det(R)\det(\dia{c}^{-1})^{2}}.

(133)

Now consider the matrix

Z=R^{\intercal}\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{d/c}&0\\
-\bm{d/c}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}R,\quad\bm{d/c}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{d_{1}}{c_{1}},\ldots,\frac{d_{k}}{c_{k}}\right).

(134)

We compute

\det(\varTheta- Z)=\det\left(\varTheta-R^{\intercal}\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{d/c}&0\\
-\bm{d/c}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}R\right)=\det\left(R^{-\intercal}\varTheta R^{-1}-\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{d/c}&0\\
-\bm{d/c}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\right)\cdot\det(R)^{2}.

(135)

Let us denote

\widetilde{\varTheta}=R^{-\intercal}\varTheta R^{-1},\quad\tilde{Z}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{d/c}&0\\
-\bm{d/c}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(136)

Since
\det(R)=\pm 1
, and we are ultimately interested in the absolute value of
\det(T_{\cv})/\det(S_{\cv})
, we may disregard
\det(R)
and focus on the quantity

\frac{\det(\widetilde{\varTheta} - \tilde{Z})}{\det(\dia{c}^{-1})^{2}}.

(137)

Noting that
\det(\dia{c}^{-1})^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{k}}\right)^{2}
, we have

\frac{\det(\widetilde{\varTheta} - \tilde{Z})}{\det(\dia{c}^{-1})^{2}}=\left(c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{k}\cdot\mathrm{Pf}(\widetilde{\varTheta}-\tilde{Z})\right)^{2},

(138)

where
\mathrm{Pf}(A)
denotes the Pfaffian of the antisymmetric matrix
A
. Recall that the Pfaffian of a
2p\times 2p
antisymmetric matrix
A=(a_{ij})
is given by

\operatorname{Pf}(A)=\sum_{\pi\in S_{2p}}(-1)^{|\pi|}\prod_{s=1}^{p}a_{\pi(2s-1)\,\pi(2s)},

(139)

where the sum runs over all permutations
\pi\in S_{2p}
satisfying
\pi(2s-1)<\pi(2s)
for all
1\leq s\leq p
and
\pi(1)<\pi(3)<\cdots<\pi(2p-1)
; as usual,
|\pi|
denotes the signature of
\pi
.

Let the entries of
\widetilde{\varTheta}-\tilde{Z}
be
a_{ij}
. All
a_{ij}
are integers except when
j=i+p
, in which case
a_{ij}
is a rational number with denominator
c_{i}
. Consequently, each monomial appearing in
\operatorname{Pf}(\widetilde{\varTheta}-\tilde{Z})
may have at most one factor with denominator
n_{i}
for each
i
. Hence, every term in the Pfaffian has denominator of the form
c_{1}^{a_{1}}c_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots c_{k}^{a_{k}}
, where each exponent
a_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Therefore, multiplying
\operatorname{Pf}(\widetilde{\varTheta}-\tilde{Z})
by
c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{k}
clears all denominators and yields an integer.

Appendix F
Examples

The construction above generalizes the usual construction of GKP codes, which is constructed as a lattice inside
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.
Here in this section, we want to sketch some special cases of the above construction that are themselves interesting.

F.1
Lattices inside
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\times\widehat{{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}

This is the case of the usual GKP codes. In this case,
n=2p
, and
Z=0.

Let
\varTheta\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
be such that
\det(\varTheta)\neq 0
. This means that
\varTheta-Z
is invertible. It follows from the proof of Theorem
2
that
g=\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{1}_{2p}\\
\bm{1}_{2p}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(n,n\mid\mathbb{Z}).
Hence

\varTheta^{\perp}=g\cdot\varTheta=\varTheta^{-1},

(140)

and one obtains a GKP lattice within the group
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\times\widehat{{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}
, assuming
\varTheta
is an integer matrix. The logical dimension is given by the Pfaffian of the matrix
\varTheta
. We now describe how to obtain the logical Pauli
X
and
Z
operators.

For any such integer Gram matrix
\varTheta
, there exists an invertible matrix
R\in\mathrm{GL}(2p,\mathbb{Z})
such that

R^{\intercal}\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\bm{d}\right)R=\varTheta,

(141)

where
\bm{d}
is a diagonal matrix
\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{p})
in alternating Smith normal form, satisfying
d_{1}\mid d_{2}\mid\cdots\mid d_{p}
.
Furthermore, the lattice is uniquely determined by
\bm{d}
; that is, if two diagonal matrices
\bm{d}_{1}
and
\bm{d}_{2}
both satisfy this condition, then
\bm{d}_{1}=\bm{d}_{2}
. The matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\bm{d}
is called the
standard form
of
\varTheta
in the context of GKP codes.

Start with a Gram symplectic matrix in standard form:

\varTheta=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\bm{d}.
Then the Gram matrix of the dual lattice is given by

\varTheta^{\perp}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\bm{d}^{-1}.

The corresponding lattice generator is given by
S=J(T^{\intercal})^{-1}
, where
T^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{cv}}T=\varTheta
. Let us choose
T
to be the standard lattice matrix
T_{\bm{d}}
associated with
\bm{d}
, given by

T_{\bm{d}}=\bm{1}_{2}\otimes\bm{\sqrt{d}}.

(142)

Any other
T
of lattice type
\bm{d}
differs from
T_{\bm{d}}
by a symplectic transformation; that is,
T=WT_{\bm{d}}
where
W
is symplectic. Define

S_{\bm{d}}:=J(T_{\bm{d}}^{\intercal})^{-1},\quad S:=J(T^{\intercal})^{-1}.

Then we have:

S=J(T^{\intercal})^{-1}=J((WT_{\bm{d}})^{\intercal})^{-1}=JW^{-\intercal}T_{\bm{d}}^{-\intercal}=WS_{\bm{d}}.

Denote by
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\perp}
the element
S_{\bm{d}}(e_{i})
for
i=1,2,\ldots,2p
, and define

\overline{Z}_{i}=D\left(\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\perp}\right),\quad\overline{X}_{i}=D\left(\mathbf{e}_{i+p}^{\perp}\right),

(143)

for
i=1,2,\ldots,p
. Then, using Eq. (
101
), we obtain

\overline{X}_{i}\overline{Z}_{i}=\exp\left(2\pi i/d_{i}\right)\overline{Z}_{i}\overline{X}_{i}.

(144)

A simple calculation shows that
\overline{X}_{i}^{d_{i}}
and
\overline{Z}_{i}^{d_{i}}
belong to the stabilizer group. Hence, the operators
\overline{X}_{i}
and
\overline{Z}_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,p
define qudit systems.

Let us now consider a simple example: the square lattice GKP code. Take

\varTheta=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
-2&0\end{pmatrix}.

We may choose

T=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}&0\\
0&\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad S=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(145)

This yields logicals
\overline{Z}=D(S(e_{1}))
and
\overline{X}=D(S(e_{2}))
.

F.2
Lattices inside
(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c})\times\widehat{(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c})}

In this section, we classify all the LCA codes for
n=2
.
We know that the entries of the antisymmetric matrix
Z
are rational. Hence, for
n=2
, the matrix
Z
is determined by a single rational number, which we denote by
-d/c=:z
, where
c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
,
c>0
, and
\textnormal{gcd}(c,d)=1
.
Therefore,

Z=\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
-z&0\end{pmatrix}.

(146)

Let
\varTheta=\begin{pmatrix}0&\theta\\
-\theta&0\end{pmatrix}
be an integer matrix with
\theta\neq z
so that
\varTheta-Z
is invertible.
We are free to pick the oscillator part of the encoding,
T_{\textsc{cv}}
, as long as

T_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{cv}}T_{\textsc{cv}}=\varTheta-Z=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\theta+\frac{d}{c}\\
-\left(\theta+\frac{d}{c}\right)&0\end{array}\right)~.

(147)

Letting
R=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)
in the proof of Theorem
2
, we have
T_{\textsc{dv}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-d&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)
which yields the full encoding map
T=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}T_{\textsc{cv}}\\
T_{\textsc{dv}}\end{array}\right)
. Then the code is generated by the stabilizers
D(T(e_{1}))
and
D(T(e_{2}))
(with phases) which act on the Hilbert space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{c}).
A direct computation shows that the dimension of the code is given by
|c\theta+d|
. For a general
R,
which is necessary in
\mathrm{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z}),
the map
T_{\textsc{dv}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-d&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)R.
In this part, we will assume
R=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)
. The general case is similar.

In the case
\theta+\frac{d}{c}>0,
we call
T_{\textsc{cv}}=\sqrt{\theta+\frac{d}{c}}\bm{1}_{2}
,
R=\bm{1}_{2}
the standard lattice matrices. If
\theta+\frac{d}{c}<0,
then by conjugating
\varTheta-Z
by
R=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)
as in Eq. (
141
), we can assume that
\theta+\frac{d}{c}>0.

We will first show that for a fixed
\theta
, different values of
z
lead to distinct codes.

When
c=1
, a quick computation shows that the code corresponds to the usual 2-dimensional GKP code (see previous subsection) with logical dimension
|\theta+d|
. Hence, all such codes are distinct. When
c\neq 1
, Theorem
2
yields codes inside the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c})
. If two rational numbers
-d_{1}/c_{1}
and
-d_{2}/c_{2}
yield the same code, then
c_{1}=c_{2}
. Comparing the logical dimensions, we get
d_{1}=d_{2}
. Hence, we conclude that each rational number
-d/c
gives a different code for a fixed
\theta
.

We now determine the logical operators of the code corresponding to a rational number
z=-d/c
. To do this, we compute the map
S
and the operators
V_{1}=D(S(e_{1}))
and
V_{2}=D(S(e_{2}))
. Since
-d
and
c
are coprime, there exist integers
a
and
b
such that
-ad+bc=1
. From Theorem
2
, we compute
g
and
S
for
z=-d/c
(
d\neq 0
) as:

g:=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(2,2\mid\mathbb{Z}),~\text{for}~A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-a\\
a&0\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}b&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}c&0\\
0&c\end{pmatrix},\quad D=\begin{pmatrix}0&d\\
-d&0\end{pmatrix}~,

(148)

and with

S=\begin{pmatrix}S_{\textsc{cv}}\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
a&0\end{pmatrix}\end{pmatrix},

(149)

where
S_{\textsc{cv}}
is a
2\times 2
matrix given by

S_{\textsc{cv}}=J_{\textsc{cv}}(T_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1/c&0\\
0&1/c\end{pmatrix}.

(150)

Then
S
is an embedding map such that

-\varTheta^{\perp}=S^{\intercal}JS=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{a\theta+b}{c\theta+d}\\
-\frac{a\theta+b}{c\theta+d}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(151)

It follows that the operators
V_{1}=D(S(e_{1}))
and
V_{2}=D(S(e_{2}))
satisfy the commutation relation:

V_{1}V_{2}=e^{-2\pi i\frac{a\theta+b}{c\theta+d}}V_{2}V_{1}.

(152)

Now, since the logical dimension is
|c\theta+d|
, if
a\theta+b=1
, then we can take

\overline{Z}=V_{1}=D(S(e_{1})),\quad\overline{X}=V_{2}=D(S(e_{2})).

(153)

If
a\theta+b\neq 1
, we first show that
a\theta+b
and
c\theta+d
are coprime. Define:

x:=a\theta+b,\quad y:=c\theta+d.

(154)

Suppose a prime
p
divides both
x
and
y
:

p\mid x,\quad p\mid y.

(155)

Then:

a\theta\equiv-b\mod p,\quad c\theta\equiv-d\mod p.

(156)

Multiply the first congruence by
d
and the second by
b
:

ad\theta\equiv-bd\mod p,\quad bc\theta\equiv-bd\mod p.

(157)

Subtracting yields:

(ad-bc)\theta\equiv 0\mod p.

(158)

Since
ad-bc=-1
, we obtain
\theta\equiv 0\mod p
. Substituting back,

x\equiv b\mod p,\quad y\equiv d\mod p.

(159)

Thus,
p\mid b
and
p\mid d
, and so
p\mid ad-bc=-1
, which implies
p=1
— a contradiction. Hence
\gcd(x,y)=1
.

Therefore, there exists an integer
k
such that
k(a\theta+b)\equiv 1\mod(c\theta+d)
. In this case,
(V_{1})^{k}
and
V_{2}
satisfy the correct commutation relation. Hence, we can take

\overline{Z}=(V_{1})^{k}=(D(S(e_{1})))^{k},\quad\overline{X}=V_{2}=D(S(e_{2})).

(160)

Remark 2
.

Note that in the above construction
g
is determined by the matrix
\mathfrak{g}\in\begin{pmatrix}-a&-b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\rm{Sp}(2,{\mathbb{Z}}).

Appendix G
Clifford Gates of LCA codes

In this section, we will discuss the Clifford gates for the LCA codes in the cases
M=\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
M=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c}
. These Clifford gates arise from certain automorphisms of noncommutative tori. The reader is referred to
Echterhoff et al. 2010
;
Jeong and Lee 2015
;
Chakraborty and Luef 2019
;
Chakraborty 2023
;
Chakraborty 2025
for a comprehensive study of these automorphisms in the setting of noncommutative tori.

G.1
The Case
M=\mathbb{R}^{p}

Recall from Subsection
F.1
that, in the case when
M=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have
g=\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{1}_{2p}\\
\bm{1}_{2p}&0\end{pmatrix}
, and the embedding map
T=T_{\textsc{cv}}
is invertible. For an integer symplectic Gram matrix
\varTheta
with
\text{det}(\varTheta)\neq 0
, we define the following group:

\operatorname{Aut}(\varTheta)=\left\{W_{\varTheta}\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\mid W_{\varTheta}^{\intercal}\varTheta W_{\varTheta}=\varTheta\right\}

(161)

Lemma 3
.

If
W_{\varTheta}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\varTheta)
, then
W=TW_{\varTheta}T^{-1}
is a symplectic matrix inside
\operatorname{Sp}(2p,\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

This is straightforwardly done by verifying the symplectic condition
W^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{cv}}W=J_{\textsc{cv}}
.
∎

For any element
W\in\mathrm{Sp}(2p,\mathbb{R})
, we can choose an operator
\widehat{W}
from the complex metaplectic group
\mathrm{Mp}(2p,\mathbb{R})
up to a phase. It is well known (see
(
de Gosson 2011
, Thm. 128)
) that

\widehat{W}A(l)D(T(l))\widehat{W}^{\dagger}=A(WT(l))D(WT(l)),\quad l\in\mathbb{Z}^{2p}.

(162)

Using
WT=TW_{\varTheta}
we get

\widehat{W}U_{l}\widehat{W}^{\dagger}=U_{W_{\varTheta}l},\quad l\in\mathbb{Z}^{2p}.

(163)

Given an element in
\mathrm{Aut}(\varTheta)
, we now define an action on the logical operators. First, we define the subgroup

\mathcal{R}:=\left\langle\rho(R)=\begin{pmatrix}R&0\\
0&(R^{-1})^{\intercal}\end{pmatrix}~\bigg|~R\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})\right\rangle\subseteq\mathrm{SO}(n,n\mid\mathbb{Z}).

(164)

Let
W_{\varTheta}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\varTheta)
and assume that

g\rho\left(W_{\varTheta}^{\intercal}\right)g^{-1}\subset\mathcal{R}

(165)

inside
\mathrm{SO}(n,n\mid\mathbb{Z})
. Then
W_{\varTheta}
defines an element in
\mathrm{Aut}(-g\cdot\varTheta)
. Indeed, since
g\rho\left(W_{\varTheta}^{\intercal}\right)g^{-1}\subset\mathcal{R}
, we have

g\rho\left(W_{\varTheta}^{\intercal}\right)g^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}S&0\\
0&(S^{-1})^{\intercal}\end{pmatrix}

(166)

for some
S\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
. Then
S^{\intercal}
is an element of
\mathrm{Aut}(-g\cdot\varTheta)
:

(S^{\intercal})^{\intercal}(g\cdot\varTheta)S^{\intercal}=S(g\cdot\varTheta)S^{\intercal}=(g\rho\left(W_{\varTheta}^{\intercal}\right)g^{-1})\cdot(g\cdot\varTheta)=g\rho\left(W_{\varTheta}^{\intercal}\right)\cdot\varTheta=g\cdot\varTheta.

(167)

Now, if
W_{\varTheta}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\varTheta)
and

g=\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{1}_{2p}\\
\bm{1}_{2p}&0\end{pmatrix}
,
one can easily verify that
S^{\intercal}=(W_{\varTheta}^{-1})^{\intercal}
. Hence,
(W_{\varTheta}^{-1})^{\intercal}\in\operatorname{Aut}(-\varTheta^{\perp}).
Therefore,
(W_{\varTheta}^{-1})^{\intercal}
implements the desired action on the logical side. More explicitly, if
\bm{\sigma}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{d_{p}})^{\times 2}
is the symplectic representation of the GKP logical Pauli operator
P(\bm{\sigma})=\prod_{i}\bar{Z}_{i}^{\hat{\sigma}_{i}}\bar{X}_{i}^{\hat{\sigma}_{i+p}}
up to a phase (as described in Subsection
F.1
), then
\bm{\sigma}\to(W_{\varTheta}^{-1})^{\intercal}\bm{\sigma}\mod\bm{d}
, where “mod
\bm{d}
” means that the
i
-th and
p+i
-th rows of a vector are taken modulo
d_{i}
.

The implementing unitary for the above logical action on the code space is given by the unitary operator
\widehat{W}
.
One may need to apply a displacement operator in addition to achieve the correct action (see the issue mentioned on Page 9 of Ref.
Burchards et al. 2024
).

We now outline how to prepare the codewords for the code with respect to the symplectic Gram matrix
\varTheta
and a given
T
such that
T^{\intercal}JT=\varTheta
. Suppose we can prepare a codeword
\ket{\psi}
for
\varTheta_{\bm{d}}
with the embedding map
T_{\bm{d}}
, where
\varTheta_{\bm{d}}
is the standard form of
\varTheta
, and
T_{\bm{d}}
is the standard lattice matrix. Then we know that
(T_{\bm{d}})^{\intercal}JT_{\bm{d}}=\varTheta_{\bm{d}}
, and
V^{\intercal}\varTheta V=\varTheta_{\bm{d}}
, for some matrix
V\in\mathrm{GL}(2p,\mathbb{Z})
. The matrix
S=TVT_{\bm{d}}^{-1}
is a symplectic matrix, and the corresponding metaplectic operator
\widehat{S}
transforms
\ket{\psi}
into the desired codeword.

To prove the above, first note that
D(T_{\bm{d}}(l))\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi},

l\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.
We have to show that
\widehat{S}\ket{\psi}
is a stabilized by
D(T(l))
for all
l\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.
Now

\widehat{S}^{\dagger}D(T(l))\widehat{S}\ket{\psi}\propto D(S^{-1}T(l))\ket{\psi}\propto D(T_{\bm{d}}V^{-1}(l))\ket{\psi}\propto\ket{\psi},

(168)

since
V^{-1}(l)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.
This proves our claim.

G.2
The Case
M=\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{c}

Recall that in this case, the code is determined by a rational number
z=-d/c
. Now, start with a parameter
\theta
such that
\theta\neq z
, so that the code is well-defined.

Assume that
W_{\theta}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\varTheta)
, where

\varTheta=\begin{pmatrix}0&\theta\\
-\theta&0\end{pmatrix},\quad W_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\
r&s\end{pmatrix}.

(169)

By definition,
W_{\theta}
is in the integer symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. Using Eq. (
148
), one checks that

g^{\prime}\rho\left(W_{\theta}^{\intercal}\right)g^{\prime-1}\subset\mathcal{R},

(170)

where the group
\cal R
is defined in Eq. (
164
), and where the corresponding action on the logical side is given by
(W_{\theta}^{-1})^{\intercal}
, as in the previous subsection.

We now aim to determine the unitary operator
W
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c})
that implements the above action. To this end, we seek an analogue of Eq. (
162
) for this setting, and want to find the corresponding matrix
W
. Recall that in this case,
T=\begin{pmatrix}T_{\textsc{cv}}\\
T_{\textsc{dv}}\end{pmatrix}
, where
T_{\textsc{cv}}
satisfies
T_{\textsc{cv}}^{\intercal}J_{\textsc{cv}}T_{\textsc{cv}}=\varTheta-Z
, and
T_{\textsc{dv}}=\begin{pmatrix}-d&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}R
.

Now we claim that such a
W
exists. Indeed, motivated by
(
Chakraborty and Kundu 2025
, Section 3)
,
W
can be taken as

W=\begin{pmatrix}T_{\textsc{cv}}W_{\theta}T_{\textsc{cv}}^{-1}&0\\
0&T_{\textsc{dv}}W_{\theta}T_{\textsc{dv}}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

(171)

where
we have assumed that
T_{\textsc{dv}}W_{\theta}T_{\textsc{dv}}^{-1}=:W_{c}
is an integer matrix. This condition assures that the entries of
W_{c}
can be taken modulo
c
, so that
W_{c}\in\mathrm{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{c}).
For
R=\bm{1}_{2},
the integrality of
W_{c}
boils down to the condition
d\mid r.

Then
W
preserves the symplectic form
J
, i.e.,

W^{\intercal}JW=J,\quad\text{where}\quad J=\begin{pmatrix}J_{\textsc{cv}}&0\\
0&J_{\textsc{dv}}\end{pmatrix}.

(172)

It is also straightforward to verify that

WT(l)=T(W_{\theta}(l)),\quad\text{for all }l\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

(173)

To obtain an analogue of Eq. (
162
), note that our
W
does not entangle the qudit and the oscillator, so one can apply Eq. (
162
) separately to both parts.
For the qudits, the analogous equation is given in
Feichtinger et al. 2007
or in
(
Gross et al. 2021
, Lemma 2.1, (1))
. It should be noted that due to the phase factors appearing in
(
Gross et al. 2021
, Lemma 2.1, (1))

Oscillator-qudit symplectic transformations

In the following, we prove that

\operatorname{Sp}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c})\cong\left\{\begin{pmatrix}W&0\\
0&V\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;W\in\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{R}),\;V\in\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{c})\right\}\cong\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{R})\times\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{c}).

(177)

First, we note that

\operatorname{Sp}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c})\subseteq\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2}).

(178)

Since the groups involved are abelian, any
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2})
can be written in block matrix form as

f=\begin{pmatrix}f_{11}&f_{12}\\
f_{21}&f_{22}\end{pmatrix},

(179)

where each element is a group homomorphism,

f_{11}\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad f_{12}\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2}),\quad f_{21}\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad f_{22}\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2},\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2}).

(180)

Using the standard fact that a finite group cannot be mapped into the real line and vice versa,

\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2})=0=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2}),

(181)

we conclude that
f_{12}=0
and
f_{21}=0
.
Thus,
f
is of the form

f=\begin{pmatrix}f_{11}&0\\
0&f_{22}\end{pmatrix},

(182)

with
f_{11}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
f_{22}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2})
.
Moreover, if
f\in\operatorname{Sp}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}_{c})
, then
f
must preserve the symplectic form, which implies

f_{11}\in\operatorname{Sp}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{R}),\quad f_{22}\in\operatorname{Sp}(\mathbb{Z}_{c}^{2})=\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{Z}_{c}).

(183)

This completes the proof.
