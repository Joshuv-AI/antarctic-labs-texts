Lifestyle-dependent evolution and CtrA-mediated lifestyle transitions shape phage resistance in marine Roseobacter
Authors: Chengcheng Li, Shailesh Nair, Zenghu Zhang, Jiulong Zhao, Zengmeng Wang, Chao Zhou, Rui Zhang, Nianzhi Jiao, Yongyu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Marine bacteria alternate between planktonic and surface-attached lifestyles, facing continuous phage predation. However, how these lifestyles shape resistance evolution remains poorly understood. Using a Roseobacter model strain, we demonstrate that surface-attached populations exhibit 26-fold higher survivability than planktonic counterparts during lytic phage infection. This advantage emerges through the evolution of heterogeneous subpopulations exhibiting diverse resistance phenotypes, a pattern absent in planktonic populations. Whole-genome sequencing of 139 heritable phage-resistant mutants revealed fundamentally divergent mutational patterns, with planktonic populations predominantly harboring tandem repeat mutations, whereas surface-attached populations favor non-coding mutations. Despite this, both lifestyles independently converged on mutations in the CtrA phosphorelay system, identifying CtrA as a previously unrecognized evolutionary target of phage-driven selection and triggering planktonic-to-surface-attached switch. Further analyses revealed systematic downregulation of motility genes and enhancement of biofilm formation, mechanistically linking phage resistance to lifestyle transitions. The identified CtrA mutations occur in regions highly conserved across ecologically important marine Alphaproteobacteria (Rhodobacterales) that are known to switch between planktonic and surface-attached states, suggesting lifestyle-dependent evolutionary trajectories may broadly shape phage resistance in marine ecosystems.

Introduction

The ocean is a global hotspot for bacterial diversity and evolution that fundamentally drives global biogeochemical cycles. In this dynamic environment, bacterial communities inherently thrive in two distinct lifestyles: planktonic and surface-attached1. Planktonic bacteria drift freely in the water column, while the surface-attached bacteria aggregate into complex, spatially structured communities known as biofilms, adhered to sediments, particles, or the surfaces of other organisms1. These contrasting modes of existence present distinct challenges. Planktonic bacteria face heightened risks of predation by protists and viruses, alongside fluctuating environmental stressors, while surface attachment can lead to overcrowding, resource competition, and nutrient limitation2–4. These lifestyle differences profoundly influence bacterial physiology, ecology, and evolutionary trajectories, resulting in variations in metabolic activity, nutrient acquisition, stress tolerance, and interactions with other microorganisms5–8.

Interactions between bacterial communities and their viral predators, bacteriophages (phages), represent a central force structuring populations in both surface-associated and planktonic contexts9,10. As the most abundant biological entities in the oceans, phages exert immense predatory pressure on their bacterial hosts, continuously selecting for innovative infection mechanisms11. In response, bacteria evolved diverse anti-phage defense mechanisms, such as surface modifications (Sm), CRISPR-Cas (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats-CRISPR associated) immunity, etc.11. This perpetual coevolutionary cycling between bacteria and their phages thus drives continuous evolution and adaptation of bacterial communities in natural environments12,13. Notably, this phage-bacteria co-evolutionary dynamics and outcomes differ substantially between free-living and surface-attached states. Recent advances demonstrate that phage–bacteria interactions in well-mixed planktonic systems typically exhibit rapid but transient arms-race dynamics that stabilize through resistance-infectivity tradeoffs and evolutionary constraint14,15, whereas spatially structured environments, including biofilms, colonies, and surface-attached communities, enable sustained coevolutionary diversification through localized selection pressures, reduced phage diffusion, and the emergence of heterogeneous resistance strategies16,17. Within structured communities, bacteria typically exhibit greater tolerance to phage predation than their planktonic counterparts16,18, leading to population-level immunity, analogous to “herd immunity”19, whereby community-level resistance arises from the coexistence of subpopulations with distinct resistance strategies13,20. However, systematic experimental comparisons of resistance evolution across planktonic versus surface-attached lifestyles and its influence on bacterial population dynamics and ecosystem functioning remain rare, particularly in marine systems where bacteria frequently switch between free-living and surface-associated states.

The marine Roseobacter clade represents a globally abundant and ecologically significant group of prokaryotes, comprising up to 25% of bacterial cells in pelagic marine environments and contributing substantially to ocean biogeochemical cycles21,22. Members of this clade exist in both planktonic and surface-attached states, frequently switching between these states by associating with algae and other organic particles, which critically regulate their role in modulating nutrient fluxes in marine ecosystems22. Owing to their cosmopolitan distribution and abundance, phages infecting Roseobacter (roseophages) are also widespread, likely exerting significant selective pressure on these populations23–25. Despite this, the extent to which phage predation influences Roseobacter lifestyle transition and how these dynamics affect the evolution of phage resistance across the two states remains poorly understood.

In this study, we investigated the interactions between a representative Roseobacterium (Roseobacter denitrificans OCh114) and three lytic phages. We demonstrate that the surface-attached state confers a 26-fold higher survivability compared with the planktonic state. This heightened survival stems from the physiological adaptation that allowed the evolution of a phenotypically heterogeneous population, exhibiting diverse resistance phenotypes. The planktonic OCh114 population was phenotypically homogeneous, with most isolates displaying heritable phage resistance. Here, we define phenotypic heterogeneity/homogeneity as the distribution of resistance phenotypes (resistant, transiently resistant, and susceptible cells coexisting) within populations. We further uncover distinct mutational landscapes underlying heritable phage resistance in these two states, with planktonic populations exhibiting a higher prevalence of tandem repeats, while surface-attached populations favor non-coding mutations. Despite this, heritable resistance in both states arises from spontaneous mutations in the CtrA phosphorelay system, an evolutionary target under phage selection that induces a switch from planktonic to surface-attached growth and confers robust survival advantage. Importantly, these CtrA-related mutations occur in regions conserved across diverse bacterial members, suggesting that such lifestyle transitions and the acquisition of population-level resistance under phage pressure may represent a widespread adaptive strategy in natural marine environments.

Results

Experimental framework for lifestyle-dependent phage resistance in R. denitrificans OCh114

To investigate how bacterial lifestyle shapes phage resistance evolution, we challenged R. denitrificans strain OCh114 with three genotypically distinct lytic phages, RDJL125, RDJL3, and RDJL6 (Supplementary Fig. S1) under planktonic (liquid culture) and surface-attached states (agar plates) conditions across a multiplicity of infection (MOI) range of 0.01-103, encompassing environmentally relevant virus-to-prokaryote ratio (VPR)26 (Fig. 1). Here, we refer to surface-attached lifestyle as bacteria grown on agar plates (see methods). These phages were genotypically distinct and exhibited different lifecycle parameters (Supplementary text, Supplementary Fig. S1), thereby enabling assessment of whether lifestyle-dependent resistance mechanisms are generalizable across genetically and biologically distinct phages. We quantified survival, screened for heritable resistance, and performed whole-genome sequencing of resistant isolates and controls from both lifestyles (Fig. 1). This comparative design enabled the identification of lifestyle-specific evolutionary trajectories and genetic targets of phage-driven selection.

Surface-attached lifestyle confers substantially higher survivability in R. denitrificans OCh114 compared to planktonic counterparts

In planktonic (liquid) culture, all three lytic bacteriophages rapidly lysed OCh114 cells within 8–16 h (MOI = 0.01-103), as evidenced by a substantial reduction in OD600 from 0.4 to 0.2 (Fig. 2A). At lower MOIs (≤102), adsorption efficiency was generally low and varied substantially across the three phages. For planktonic populations at MOI of 103, survival after one infection cycle only (see Methods) was 3.3% ± 0.4% (Fig. 2B), and declined to 0.001% across all MOIs after 24 h (Supplementary Fig. S2B). For the surface-attached state, bacteria were first exposed to phages in liquid culture for 20 min to allow adsorption before transfer to agar surfaces (see Methods), thereby minimizing adsorption limitations associated with solid media15. At an MOI of 103, surface-attached survival reached 86.1% ± 14.4%, representing a 26-fold higher survival than planktonic populations (Two-way ANOVA: F = 102.4, p < 0.001; Fig. 2B). This difference reflects genuine lifestyle-dependent protection, as both planktonic and surface-attached populations began with equivalent adsorption efficiency (>90% ECOI). At lower MOIs (0.01-102), surface-attached populations maintained 97–100% survival, while planktonic survival increased progressively with decreasing MOI (Fig. 2B). However, these lower MOI comparisons cannot distinguish lifestyle-dependent protection from differential infection efficiency, as ECOI at MOI ≤ 102 was both low and highly variable across phages (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

Surface-attached populations exhibit heterogeneous resistance, while planktonic populations exhibit homogeneous resistance under phage pressure

We next characterized resistance phenotypes among survivors from both lifestyles. For surviving surface-attached populations, we randomly selected 60 colonies per phage treatment at MOI 103 and assessed phage susceptibility over five successive passages (see “Methods”). Of 180 tested isolates, 38.9% (70 isolates) displayed stable, heritable resistance, 15.6% (28 isolates) exhibited transient resistance that reverted to susceptibility within 2-3 passages, and 45.5% (82 isolates) remained susceptible from the first passage (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S3). We also examined resistance phenotypes at lower MOIs (0.01-102) for surface-attached populations to verify that phenotypic heterogeneity was specific to high infection efficiency conditions. All 180 isolates from lower MOIs remained fully susceptible (Supplementary Fig. S3), confirming that the observed resistance at MOI 103 reflects a genuine evolutionary response to phage pressure.

Examination of colony morphology revealed that at lower MOIs (0.01-102), surface-attached colonies exhibited circular morphology resembling that of the untreated controls (Fig. 2D). In contrast, at MOI 103, colonies displayed distinct characteristics, including smaller size, non-uniformity, lighter pigmentation, and irregular edges (Fig. 2D). These irregular morphologies could arise from phage-mediated lysis of susceptible cells at colony peripheries30 or, alternatively, from the coexistence of multiple subpopulations with different resistance phenotypes and growth rates within individual colonies.

To directly assess whether morphological heterogeneity reflects within-colony phenotypic diversity, we isolated 10 single clones from different sectors (outer, inner, and intermediate) of a single colony each from each phage treatment (RDJL 1, RDJL 3, and RDJL 6) at MOI 103. Consequently, 1, 4, and 9 of the respective monoclonal isolates exhibited stable, heritable phage resistance upon repeated screening for 5 generations (Fig. 2E). The remaining isolates showed phage susceptibility after 1-3 passages, confirming non-heritable resistance and potential phage-susceptible members. This pattern of presence of heritable and transient resistance phenotypes within individual colonies was consistently observed across five independent experiments and reflects sustained phage infection and selection pressure during colony growth, which drove the emergence of within-colony heterogeneity in surface-attached populations. In contrast, planktonic populations exhibited limited phenotypic diversity. Following 24 h of phage exposure at an MOI of 103, isolates recovered from near-transparent lysates were overwhelmingly resistant, with an average of 92.6% (125 of 135 isolates) displaying stable, heritable resistance across five generations (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S3).

Distinct mutational pattern underlie heritable phage resistance in surface-attached versus planktonic OCh114

Baseline sequencing confirmed that the ancestral population was genetically homogeneous, ensuring that observed mutational differences between planktonic and surface-attached populations reflect lifestyle-specific evolution (Supplementary text, Supplementary Data 1, and Supplementary Fig. S4).

To uncover the genotypic basis of the evolved heritable resistance in OCh114 under phage predation, we performed whole-genome sequencing on both control isolates (without phage exposure) and phage-resistant isolates in parallel. Specifically, we sequenced 15 planktonic and 15 surface-attached control isolates, alongside 139 heritable phage-resistant isolates, comprising 74 and 65 isolates randomly selected from 125 planktonic and 70 surface-attached phage-resistant populations (Fig. 1), respectively, that evolved under exposure to phages RDJL1, RDJL3, or RDJL6 at an MOI of 103.

Eight planktonic and 11 surface-attached control isolates harbored mutations in 13 and 9 unique sites, respectively, relative to the ancestral genome (Supplementary Data 2). These mutations likely represent intrinsic genetic variation arising during culturing conditions. Correspondingly, intrinsic mutation rates were calculated as 4.8 × 10⁻⁹ and 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ mutations per site per generation for planktonic and surface-attached states (Supplementary Text, Supplementary Data 3), respectively, indicating comparable baseline mutation rates. On the contrary, phage-resistant isolates exhibited significantly higher number of mutation sites compared to controls (p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. S5). After excluding all mutations shared with controls, we identified 340 total mutations across 139 phage-resistant isolates (Supplementary Data 4 and 6). In planktonic populations, RDJL1-, RDJL3-, and RDJL6-resistant isolates carried 64, 58, and 50 mutations at 102 unique genomic sites (on average 2.56, 2.42, and 2.00 mutations/strain) (Supplementary Fig. S6A). In surface-attached populations, the three phages selected 54, 51, and 63 mutations at 85 unique sites (on average 2.73, 2.10, and 2.86 mutations/strain), respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6A). These similar mutation loads across phages within each lifestyle indicate comparable selective pressure regardless of phage genotype.

Among planktonic isolates, seven genomic sites were mutated by all three phage treatments, while eight shared sites emerged in surface-attached populations (Supplementary Fig. S6B and C). Notably, 10 mutation sites were shared between planktonic and surface-attached lifestyles (Supplementary Fig. S6D). Despite this overlap in mutated genomic sites, planktonic and surface-attached populations exhibited distinct mutational profiles (Supplementary Fig. S6E). The relative frequencies of coding mutation types (synonymous, missense, loss-of-function) differed significantly between lifestyles (χ² = 8.86, p < 0.05) with planktonic isolates exhibiting a trend toward higher loss-of-function (LOF) mutations (41.4% vs. 29.4%, Fisher’s exact test, Odds ratio = 1.69, 95% CI: 0.98-2.96, p = 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. S6E), indicating stronger disruptive selection during the evolution of phage resistance under free-living conditions. Mutations were not randomly distributed but frequently clustered in tandem repeat (TR) regions. Overall, 32.3% (33/102) of unique sites in planktonic isolates and 24.7% (21/85) in surface-attached isolates occurred within TRs (Supplementary Fig. S6F). However, planktonic isolates carried significantly more TR mutations per strain than surface-attached isolates (mean = 1.17 vs 0.7 in surface-attached, p = 0.009, Fig. 3A). Strikingly, over two-thirds (69.6%) of these non-coding mutations in surface-attached mutants were concentrated in two intergenic regions, hereafter referred to as non-coding position 1 (NC-1; 3,162,846 – 3,162,854) and non-coding position 2 (NC-2; 1,787,070–1,787,075, Supplementary Data 6). Mutations-induced surface modification confers phage resistance in both surface-attached and planktonic OCh114

Further analysis of phage-resistant isolates exhibiting heritable resistance in both planktonic and surface-attached states revealed a highly nonrandom distribution of mutations. Rather than being dispersed across the genome, mutations were strongly concentrated in five loci: RD1_1962, RD1_1258, RD1_2601, RD1_2657, and RD1_2711 (Fig. 3C). Collectively, mutations in these genes accounted for 97.2% of planktonic mutants (72 of 74) and 96.9% of surface-attached mutants (63 of 65), consistent with intense positive selection acting on a limited genetic target space. Mutations occurring at identical genomic positions were treated as a single event to avoid counting potential clonal expansion of the same mutant. Based on this criterion, 57 unique mutation sites were identified in planktonic populations and 42 in surface-attached populations (Supplementary Fig. S7A, Supplementary Data), indicating that these mutations largely represented independent mutational events rather than the expansion of a single clone. Only four nucleotide positions were shared between lifestyles, RD1_1962 positions 1873748 (C to T) and 1875210 (AGCGC to A), RD1_2601 position 2484323 (C to T), and RD1_2711 position 2582186 (G to A), indicating that convergence occurred predominantly at the gene level through largely distinct mutational routes.

Gene-level convergence was also evident across phage genotypes despite extensive nucleotide level diversity. Although the three genetically distinct phages selected largely non overlapping mutation sites, with only 6 of 102 sites shared in planktonic populations and 8 of 85 in surface-attached populations (Supplementary Fig. S6B and C), they repeatedly targeted the same functional genes. Among the 44 genes mutated in planktonic isolates, seven were shared across all three phage treatments, including four of the five major loci RD1_1258, RD1_1962, RD1_2657, and RD1_2711 (Supplementary Fig. S7B and Supplementary Data 4), which we subsequently demonstrate to confer phage resistance. Similarly, six of the 27 mutated genes in surface-attached populations were shared across treatments, including RD1_1962, RD1_2601, and RD1_2711 (Supplementary Fig. S7C and Supplementary Data 6). Together, these findings indicate that phage-specific selection pressures generate distinct mutational spectra while exhibiting limited convergence on common functional pathways.

Functional annotation of the five recurrently mutated genes revealed two major targets of selection. RD1_1962 encodes a predicted glycosyltransferase implicated in cell surface modification31, while the remaining 4 genes encode components of the CtrA phosphorelay system, a master regulatory cascade controlling cell cycle progression and lifestyle transitions32,33, including the 2’,3’-cyclic-nucleotide 2’-phosphodiesterase (RD1_1258), the response regulator CtrA (RD1_2601), the histidine phosphotransferase ChpT (RD1_2657), the histidine kinase CckA (RD1_2711). Mutations in RD1_1962 were present in 40.5% of planktonic mutants (30 of 74) and 41.5% of surface-attached mutants (27 of 65) (Supplementary Fig. S7D, Supplementary Data), with 43.9 % of these mutations (25 of 57) introducing frameshifts within predicted functional domains (Fig. 4A). In parallel, mutations in RD1_1258, RD1_2601, RD1_2657, and RD1_2711 were detected in 63.5% (47/74) of planktonic mutants and 55.4% (36/65) of surface-attached mutants (Supplementary Fig. S7D, Supplementary Data), resulting in amino acid substitutions or frameshifts within their key functional domains (Fig. 4A). Phosphorylated CtrA acts as a transcription factor regulating flagellar biogenesis genes32. Red dots represent glycosylated proteins that may serve as phage receptors. Mutations disrupting this pathway led to loss of flagella and phage resistance.

To validate whether these genes causally determine resistance, we isolated five single-mutation strains from planktonic phage-resistant populations, M1258 (mutation only in RD1_1258), M1962 (only in RD1_1962), M2601 (only in RD1_2601), M2657 (only in RD1_2657), and M2711 (only in RD1_2711). Genetic complementation with wild-type gene copies fully restored phage susceptibility in all five strains (Fig. 4B), confirming that mutations in these five genes (RD1_1962, RD1_1258, RD1_2601, RD1_2657, and RD1_2711) conferred phage resistance.

Further analysis revealed that phages RDJL1, RDJL3, and RDJL6 efficiently adsorbed to wild-type OCh114 over time but failed to adsorb to gene-mutated resistant strains M1258, M1962, M2601, M2657, and M2711 (Supplementary Fig. S8), indicating these mutants lacked or possessed altered adsorption receptor (s) for the three phages. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of the wild-type OCh114 showed that the three phages predominantly gathered at the flagella (Supplementary Fig. S9), consistent with flagellotropic adsorption, where phages exploit flagellar structures for initial attachment. Aligning with this, transcriptomic analysis revealed that all 24 genes related to flagellar synthesis and assembly were significantly downregulated in the gene-mutated resistant strains M1258, M2601, M2657, and M2711 (p.adj ≤ 0.05; Fig. 5B), resulting in non-motile phenotypes (Fig. 4C). Therefore, the phage-resistant mechanism of these mutant genes was proposed to involve a deficiency in the phage receptor-flagella complex, inhibiting phage adsorption and conferring resistance (Fig. 4D). However, no changes in flagellar synthesis genes or motility were observed in M1962 mutant (Fig. 4C and Fig. 5B). Mutations in CtrA phosphorelay pathway underpins planktonic to surface-attached lifestyle transition

Mutants M1258, M2601, M2657, and M2711 carried mutations concentrated in genes related to the functioning of the CtrA phosphorelay system (RD1_1258 encoding a 2’, 3’-cyclic-nucleotide 2’-phosphodiesterase, RD1_2601 encoding CtrA, RD1_2657 encoding ChpT, and RD1_2711 encoding CckA). This pathway plays a crucial role in regulating cell cycle progression, cellular differentiation, flagellar biogenesis, and lifestyle transitions across diverse members of alphaproteobacterial species32,33. Comparative transcriptomics analysis performed in liquid culture in the absence of phage pressure revealed significant differential expression (p.adj ≤ 0.05) of 18.9, 3.9, 24.5, and 7.4% of KEGG-annotated genes in mutants M1258, M2601, M2657, and M2711, respectively, compared to the wild-type strain (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Data 9–12). Notably, key CtrA regulators including genes cckA, chpT, ctrA, pleD, ccrM, dnaA, and ftsA were significantly downregulated (Fig. 5B). This was coupled with suppression flagella synthesis (e.g., fliC, flgE), assembly (e.g., fliQ, flhA), and chemotaxis gene (motA, cheY, cheW, cheB, cheA, mcp) expression, resulting in impaired chemotactic activity37 (Fig. 5B). In addition, genes linked to exopolysaccharide, lipopolysaccharide, and biofilm formation (wcaL, wbbL, wlbA, xagB, and cpdA) were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5B). In aligning with this, these mutants exhibited enhanced biofilm formation up to 3-fold higher (p < 0.001) than the wild-type strain, which decreased after gene complementation except for M1258, whose phage sensitivity was not totally restored by gene complementation (Fig. 5C). However, this acquisition of phage resistance incurred fitness trade-off with these 4 mutants exhibiting significantly extended lag phases (23.3-27.6 h vs 15.3 h for wild-type; p < 10−6) and reduced cumulative growth (AUC: 16.5-19.5 vs 25.9 for wild-type; p < 10−7; Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. S10), while maximum growth rates remained comparable (p > 0.05). These costs likely reflect the pleiotropic costs of disrupting the CtrA regulatory hub.

Subsequently, screening of 139 resistant strains revealed that all harboring mutations in the four CtrA-related genes (RD1_1258, RD1_2601, RD1_2657, and RD1_2711) demonstrated enhanced biofilm formation (Supplementary Fig. S11), albeit with variable magnitude, possibly influenced by other mutant genes or mutations in the non-coding regions. In addition, genes encoding two-component systems (TCSs) from the OmpR family, specifically the sensor histidine kinases PhoR, TorS, and TctE were 1-2-fold upregulated in these mutants (Supplementary Data 9-12). This upregulation may indicate adaptive responses to phosphate starvation (PhoR)38, anaerobic or low-oxygen respiration (TorS)39, and redox sensing (TctE)40, potentially associated with the biofilm lifestyle and trade-offs incurred in motility and chemotaxis, which remains to be investigated further. However, they exhibited higher intracellular ATP (Fig. 5E), likely resulting from reduced energy expenditure on growth, motility, and chemotaxis41,42. The accumulated energy reserves may support energetically demanding biofilm formation, thereby enhancing the survival of planktonic-to-surface-attached evolved mutant communities under phage pressure43–45.

Such intricate transcriptional regulation and enhanced biofilm formation among the mutants suggested that phage attack may induce a planktonic to surface-attached (biofilm-like) lifestyle transition in OCh114, which typically exists in a planktonic state and does not spontaneously form biofilm under standard laboratory conditions46. To validate this, we infected OCh114 cells with phages RDJL 1, RDJL 3, and RDJL 6, respectively, at MOI = 0.1. While the three phages rapidly lysed planktonic OCh114 cells, visible biofilm formation was observed on the tube walls after 48 hours of phage exposure, despite clear liquid culture (Fig. 5F). In contrast, cells in the controls without phage addition predominantly persisted in a planktonic state (OD600 = 0.8) with minimal biofilm formation. Furthermore, we employed confocal laser scanning microscopy to visualize the temporal changes of biofilm development in wild-type OCh114 exposed to the above three phages (at MOI = 0.1). At 24 and 48 h, the phage-treated OCh114 population showed substantial aggregation of viable cells embedded in biofilm matrices compared to the controls (Fig. 5G). Moreover, at 72 h, the phage-treated OCh114 population exhibited robust biofilm formation, contrasting with the dispersal observed in the control group’s biofilm (Fig. 5G). This indicated that phage predation drives a planktonic to surface-attached lifestyle switch in R. denitrificans OCh114 by forming robust biofilms. Moreover, OCh114-biofilm induction occurred across a broad range of phage MOIs (0.01- 103) encompassing environmentally relevant virus-to-prokaryote ratios (VPR)26 (Supplementary Fig. S12), suggesting it is a general response not restricted to certain virus-host ratios and potentially occurs in natural environments. However, these observations of OCh114 forming robust biofilm upon phage exposure cannot distinguish whether biofilm formation represents enrichment of resistant mutations or infection-induced phenotypic responses in susceptible cells before lysis.

Discussion

The evolutionary dynamics of marine phage-bacteria interactions have traditionally been conceptualized through the lens of planktonic communities, where arms race models predict cyclical patterns of resistance evolution and viral counter-adaptation9,47. However, the pervasive spatial heterogeneity of marine environments, where bacteria routinely transition between free-living and surface-associated states5–8, introduces fundamental complexities that challenge these simplified frameworks. Our investigation of R. denitrificans OCh114 reveals that lifestyle-dependent selective regimes generate qualitatively distinct evolutionary responses to viral predation, with surface attachment associated with markedly higher survival than in planktonic populations.

This remarkable resilience observed in surface-attached OCh114 cannot be attributed solely to physical protection due to surface attachment, as our experiment allowed efficient phage adsorption prior to surface attachment. Instead, it likely reflects fundamental differences in population structure and evolutionary dynamics between surface-attached and planktonic states. The surface-attached environment likely imposes distinct biophysical conditions that differ substantially from planktonic culture, including spatial structuring and potentially increased local viscosity due to extracellular polymeric substance release48. Such biophysical constraints could limit phage diffusion to host cell surfaces49,50, providing a temporal window for bacterial growth and resistance evolution. Critically, the surface-attached population exhibited marked phenotypic heterogeneity, comprising heritable resistant, transiently resistant, and potentially susceptible subpopulations coexisting within single colonies, a pattern absent in planktonic populations. Such coexistence could allow resistant cells to reduce local phage availability, indirectly shielding nearby susceptible cells, analogous to community-level immunity documented in spatially structured bacterial systems13,20,51,52. Alternatively, or additionally, physical barriers from biofilm matrix may constrain phage movement independently of cellular resistance2,18, compensatory evolution in resistant strains may restore susceptibility while maintaining fitness53, or phage decay during incubation may reduce selective pressure54, thereby contributing to the observed survival pattern. These mechanisms may not be mutually exclusive and likely operate concurrently. Distinguishing their relative contribution to the observed markedly higher resistance in surface-attached requires further investigations. Nevertheless, surface-attached OCh114 maintains phenotypic diversity under sustained phage predation, which correlates with the observed higher apparent survival, whereas the planktonic population experiences rapid, widespread phage-induced mortality, yielding homogeneous, heritably resistant survivors. This lifestyle-dependent divergence in population structure has profound evolutionary implications, as planktonic populations face immediate, strong selective pressure a trajectory often leading to coevolutionary stagnation17, while surface-attached populations, buffered by spatial structure and heterogeneity, may sustain more complex coevolutionary dynamics.

This stark difference in selective pressures, i.e., gradual, community-buffered pressure in surface-attached versus rapid, direct selection in planktonic systems, resulted in lifestyle-specific mutational patterns among phage-resistant mutants. Planktonic mutants exhibited elevated prevalence of mutations within tandem repeat (TR) regions, which are hypermutable hotspots for slipped-strand mispairing during DNA replication55. This pattern likely reflects an adaptive response to strong directional selection in well-mixed environments. Free-living bacteria experience immediate phage exposure without the spatial refugia available in surface-attached communities, creating intense pressure for rapid resistance evolution. TR mutations allow rapid and reversible modulation of gene expression55, potentially allowing swift adjustment of phage receptor abundance or related resistance traits56, while maintaining the capacity to revert upon relief of phage pressure, providing immediate fitness benefits without long-term evolutionary constraints. Aligning with this, a higher number of mutants exhibited TR mutations within the phage-resistance conferring genes (RD1_1962, RD1_2657, RD1_1258, and RD1_2711). Conversely, surface-attached mutants displayed a significantly higher proportion of non-coding mutations, predominantly concentrated within two distinct intergenic regions designated NC-1 and NC-2. The NC-1 region lies downstream of gene RD1_3302, which encodes a class II/III pyridoxal phosphate-dependent aminotransferase that catalyzes essential transamination reactions in amino sugar metabolism. These amino sugars represent critical building blocks for peptidoglycan synthesis in bacterial cell walls and lipopolysaccharide assembly in outer membranes, both recognized as common phage attachment sites57. Mutations within NC-1 may affect RD1_3302 expression through altered transcription termination58, potentially leading to altered cell wall architecture or modified LPS structures that reduce phage receptor density or accessibility without compromising essential cellular functions. Moreover, the NC-2 region is positioned within the tripartite tricarboxylate transporter (TTT) operon (TCTABC), which governs the import of organic substrates, including citrate, into the tricarboxylic acid cycle, a central metabolic hub governing cellular energetics59. Regulatory mutations within NC-2 likely result in fine-tuned downregulation of the TTT operon and reduced citrate import capacity, consequently diminishing TCA cycle activity. While such metabolic restrictions may impose growth costs, they could increase polysaccharide intercellular adhesin, a major mediator of biofilm formation, while simultaneously altering cell surface charge distributions, potentially reducing phage adsorption60–62. However, the functional role of these non-coding mutations in phage resistance remains to be determined. Importantly, both of these non-coding mutations occurred exclusively in surface-attached mutants that simultaneously carried phage-resistance mutations in the core resistance genes (RD1_1258, RD1_1962, RD1_2601, RD1_2657, and RD1_2711, Supplementary Data 6), indicating that these non-coding changes may not confer resistance independently. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that they may provide weak resistance requiring synergy with core mutations, serve compensatory functions offsetting fitness costs, or represent regulatory adaptations reinforcing the surface-attached lifestyle, which warrants future experimental investigation. Collectively, these findings suggest that phage resistance evolves through distinct, lifestyle-specific strategies, with planktonic populations favoring rapid, reversible genetic switches, whereas surface-attached populations rely on multilayered regulatory optimization integrating metabolism, cell surface remodeling, and stable resistance mutations to balance growth and defense.

Despite these evolutionary differences, mutations driving stable resistance in both states localized within genes related to the CtrA phosphorelay regulatory system, a vital network governing critical cellular processes like cell cycle, motility, and biofilm formation in Alphaproteobacteria33,63, and nfrB, encoding a glycosyltransferase, all providing surface modification phage resistance to both populations. While CtrA activity is known to play a central role in regulating bacterial physiological transitions across diverse environmental conditions64,65, CtrA mutations have not been reported to arise under phage-driven selection. Our results identify the CtrA regulatory axis as a recurrent target of phage-driven selection across contrasting lifestyles. Mutations in the CtrA system led to the downregulation of the majority of the crucial regulator genes, including ctrA, cckA, chpT, pleD, ccrM, and dnaA. Such downregulation of the CtrA pathway or deactivation of the genes encoding ctrA has been demonstrated to induce a switch from planktonic to a surface-attached lifestyle in Caulobacter crescentus66. Although the CtrA pathway may function differently among different Roseobacter members65,67, it plays a crucial role in regulating flagellar synthesis, assembly, and biofilm formation across diverse Alphaproteobacterial members, including Roseobacter37,65,68,69. Corroborating this, CtrA- mutant strains exhibited systematic downregulation of motility and chemotaxis genes, and upregulation of exopolysaccharide production genes. Critically, these mutants constitutively display enhanced biofilm formation even in the absence of phage (Fig. 5C), establishing that resistance evolution is genetically coupled to stable lifestyle shifts rather than transient infection responses. Wild-type OCh114, which exhibits minimal biofilm formation under standard laboratory conditions46, develops robust biofilms during phage exposure across environmentally relevant virus-to-prokaryote ratios3,26 (Fig. 5F, G and Supplementary Fig. S12). Whether this population-level biofilm induction reflects infection-triggered stress responses in susceptible cells or rapid enrichment of resistant mutants remains unresolved. Phage-induced biofilm formation is widely documented across bacterial taxa and can arise from resistance-associated phenotypic changes as well as non-evolutionary mechanisms70,71, including phage-stimulated host aggregation72, accumulation of extracellular DNA within the biofilm matrix73, and cell-lysis–mediated signaling2,74, functioning as a generalized adaptive strategy that promotes bacterial persistence under phage-mediated environmental stress15,18,75. However, the fitness costs of CtrA-mediated resistance raise questions about its long-term persistence under fluctuating phage pressure in dynamic marine environments. Spatial and temporal heterogeneity in phage abundance may favor resistance locally while selecting against it elsewhere. The prevalence of tandem repeat mutations in planktonic-derived resistant isolates is notable, as such mutations may permit reversible phenotypic switching under changing selection55. In addition, biofilm formation can confer fitness benefits beyond phage resistance76. Whether these effects offset the growth costs observed here, and how resistant and susceptible lineages are structured across heterogeneous marine habitats, remain open questions.

It should be emphasized that our investigation focused specifically on heritable mutations conferring surface modification-mediated resistance mechanisms, which emerged as the primary drivers of both phage resistance and lifestyle transitions. However, the increased survivability observed in surface-attached populations likely resulted from the integration of population-level diverse resistance strategies, including heritable genetic changes, non-heritable adaptations, and biofilm formation. Non-heritable resistance mechanisms potentially arose from physiological adaptations, quorum-sensing behavior, or reversible TR and non-coding mutations conferring transient resistance that may allow bacterial populations to persist without permanent genetic modifications and retain the capacity to revert to ancestral physiological state upon relief of selective pressure77. However, the exact mechanistic basis for these warrants detailed future investigation.

In conclusion, our study provides novel insights into the distinct evolutionary trajectories undertaken by marine bacteria in response to phage predation in surface-attached and planktonic states, highlighting the crucial role of surface-attached lifestyles in driving bacterial diversification, resistance strategies, and adaptive transitions (Fig. 6). The cosmopolitan and globally abundant Roseobacter clade play important roles in marine biogeochemical cycling, carbon fluxes, and symbiotic interactions with algae21,22. Importantly, mutations in CtrA phosphorelay genes (RD1_2601, RD1_2657, RD1_2711) occur in regions highly conserved across multiple marine Alphaproteobacterial lineages, specifically within the Rhodobacterales order encompassing Roseobacter, Sulfitobacter, Phaeobacter, and 30 related genera (Supplementary Data 13). 60% of these bacteria (83/138) were isolated from surface-associated environments, including coral, seaweed, marine sediments, and phytoplankton (Supplementary Data 13). Rhodobacterales are ubiquitous, ecologically dominant, and the primary surface-colonizing bacterial group in marine waters78, comprising 30–70% of marine biofilm communities in the initial colonization stage79. The conservation of CtrA regulatory capacity within this clade, whose members routinely transition between planktonic and surface-attached states, suggests that CtrA-mediated lifestyle transitions in response to phage pressure may be widespread in marine ecosystems. While it remains experimentally unresolved whether phage predation triggers similar CtrA-mediated lifestyle transitions in other bacterial taxa, phylogenetically distant bacterial lineages have been documented to increase biofilm formation and cell aggregation under diverse phage predation2,70,72,73. This phylogenetically widespread capacity for lifestyle-mediated survival responses suggests that in marine systems, the phage-bacteria evolutionary arms race may be characterized not by a simple linear progression of resistance and counter-resistance, but by a dynamic equilibrium maintained through distinct evolutionary trajectories across lifestyle states and community-level emergent properties.

However, it is important to acknowledge that natural marine systems are considerably more complex with varying physicochemical conditions than those employed in our experiments. While we utilized rich organic (RO) media for its ability to support robust growth of R. denitrificans OCh114, we acknowledge that this may not fully represent natural marine conditions and could potentially influence the relative fitness of planktonic versus surface-associated bacteria. To address this, we further validated our findings using 10-fold diluted RO media containing carbon compounds equivalent to the low-nutrient heterotrophic media (0.08 g/L)80, which revealed a negligible impact of media composition on observed survivability rates (Supplementary Text, Supplementary Fig. S13). In addition, the choice of using solid agar media as a surface-attached substrate may not fully capture the complexities of the natural surface-attached lifestyle. Natural surface-attached lifestyles, such as biofilms and particle-associated communities, exhibit complex features including mature extracellular matrix architecture, metabolic gradients, and dynamic flow conditions, which may confer different degrees of protection and remain to be determined. Nevertheless, future studies should prioritize the development of more environmentally realistic experimental systems to better capture the complexity of natural marine conditions. Moreover, the relative contributions of heritable versus non-heritable resistance mechanisms to overall population survival represent a gap in our mechanistic understanding and may explain how community-level defense systems maintain stability under fluctuating selective pressures. The reversibility of lifestyle transitions and the conditions under which surface-attached populations revert to planktonic growth remain unexplored and is essential for understanding long-term evolutionary trajectories in natural marine systems.

Methods

Bacterial strains, phages, and culture conditions

The marine bacterium R. denitrificans OCh114 was routinely grown in rich organic (RO) medium (1 g/L tryptone, 1 g/L yeast extract, 1 g/L sodium acetate, 1 × 10−5 M FeCl3· 6H2O, 1 × 10−5 M Na2EDTA· 2H2O, 4 × 10−8 M CuSO4· 2H2O, 3 × 10−8 M NaMoO4· 2H2O, 8 × 10−8 M ZnSO4· 7H2O, 5 × 10−8 M CoCl2· 6H2O, 9 × 10−7 M MnCl2· 4H2O, pH 7.5) or on RO agar plates containing 1.5% (w/v) agar for all experiments unless otherwise stated. Liquid cultures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 140 rpm, while solid plate cultures were incubated at 28 °C under static conditions. Escherichia coli WM306481 was employed as a conjugation donor strain. Three lytic bacteriophages RDJL1, RDJL3, and RDJL6 were utilized in this study (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Data 14). While RDJL1 was previously described25, phage RDJL3 and RDJL6 were newly isolated in this study from seawater samples collected from the East China Sea and the South Yellow Sea, respectively. Details on the isolation and characterization of phages RDJL3 and RDJL6 are provided in the supplementary materials.

Experimental procedures for planktonic and surface-attached phage assays

The overall experimental approach is illustrated in Fig. 1. Ancestral OCh114 was revived from -80 °C storage and cultured in phage-free liquid medium until logarithmic phase, and then spread on an agar plate. Three independent colonies were isolated by streak plating and subjected to whole-genome sequencing, then pooled for reference genome assembly (see below). Ten additional colonies were sequenced individually to confirm genomic homogeneity (Supplementary Text).

For phage challenge experiments, a genetically homogeneous ancestral OCh114 was revived following the same above protocol, grown to logarithmic phase, thoroughly mixed, and aliquoted into two cultures for independent phage exposure under planktonic and surface-attached conditions. Growing bacteria in liquid medium to the logarithmic phase ensured all cells began with uniform metabolic activity, cell density, and physiological state, providing a well-controlled starting point for comparing lifestyle-specific evolutionary dynamics. From each aliquot, bacterial cultures were serially diluted to varying cell densities (approximately 103 to 108 CFU/mL) to create different MOI treatments. A constant phage inoculum (106 PFU/mL) was prepared separately for the three phages and then added to each bacterial dilution in liquid RO medium, yielding final MOIs ranging from 0.01 to 103. Bacterial dilution may alter phage–host interactions through quorum sensing, attachment, or aggregation82, but any density-dependent effects would therefore affect both lifestyles equally within each MOI treatment. Each phage × MOI combination was performed in three evolutionary replicates, which we define as cultures derived from the same clonal ancestral population but evolving independently under selected experimental conditions.

After 20 minutes of incubation to allow effective phage adsorption to bacterial cells, as confirmed by the center of infection assay (Supplementary Fig. S2), and no phage replication, as indicated by one-step growth curves (Supplementary Fig. S1), the phage-bacteria mixtures were processed according to lifestyle conditions. For surface-attached assays, 100 μL was immediately spread onto RO agar plates. For the planktonic assays, cultures were maintained in liquid medium for an additional 24 h (total incubation time determined from OD600 growth curve to ensure stable phage–bacterium interactions, Fig. 2A). Controls were processed similarly for the two conditions but without addition of phages.

Determination of survival frequency under surface-attached conditions

After spreading on agar, plates were incubated at 28 °C until visible colonies emerged (7 days, approximately 21 cell divisions; Supplementary Text, Supplementary Data 3). Determination of survival frequency under planktonic conditions

To enable a fair comparison with surface-attached bacteria, survival under planktonic conditions was quantified after a single phage infection cycle. To prevent progeny phage release and secondary infections, incubation times for phage-bacterium mixtures in liquid culture were defined based on phage-specific one-step growth curves (Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Text) and set to the late latent period (50 min, 3.5 h, and 1.5 h for phages RDJL1, RDJL3, and RDJL6, respectively) based on their latent periods (80 min, 5 h, and 2.5 h; Supplementary Text). Following phage-specific incubation, liquid cultures were harvested and subjected to centrifugation (6000 × g, 5 min). Cell pellets were resuspended in fresh medium, and the centrifugation-resuspension procedure was repeated three times to eliminate remaining free phages. Subsequently, the resuspended bacteria were serially diluted and plated on RO agar to quantify the number of surviving bacteria. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for approximately 7 days until visible colonies emerged. Where, x₁ represents the number of surviving bacteria in the phage-treated group and x₀ represents the initial bacterial count prior to phage addition. Given that wild-type R. denitrificans OCh114 has a doubling time of approximately 5 h (Supplementary Text, Supplementary Data 3), bacterial replication during the short single-cycle assay was considered negligible and was therefore not accounted for.

Identification of phage-resistant bacteria in surface-attached and planktonic states

From each planktonic and surface-attached interaction, 15 and 20 colonies were randomly selected across three independent biological replicates, yielding a total of 135 and 180 colonies, respectively. The smaller number of planktonic isolates was chosen based on preliminary experiments indicating a higher frequency of heritable resistance in planktonic populations. In parallel, 15 wild-type OCh114 strains from each condition (30 strains in total) were isolated under the same conditions but without phage exposure. Phage resistance of these bacteria was then assessed using the spot assay83. Briefly, isolates were grown in liquid media until they reached the logarithmic phase. One milliliter of an aliquot of bacteria in the log phase was mixed with 4 mL of molten RO (containing 0.5% agar) and spread on a solid RO agar plate (containing 1.5% agar). Then, 5 μL of each phage lysate (108 PFU/ml) was spotted onto the surface of the agar and allowed to incubate for 12 h at 28 °C. Bacterial sensitivity to phages was determined by the presence of a lysis-clear zone at the spot, and isolates were considered phage-resistant only when the lysis zone was completely absent. Wild-type OCh114 subjected to the same treatments served as controls. Any visible clearing, including partial lysis, was scored as susceptible.

To assess resistance stability, each colony was purified by streak plating to obtain single colonies, and one purified colony was tested for resistance (passage 1). Resistant isolates were subjected to four additional passages (5 passages in total), with each passage involving streak plating and approximately five days of growth on agar plates to allow formation of single colonies, followed by resistance testing. Isolates maintaining resistance across all five passages were designated as heritably resistant, while those reverting to susceptibility within 1-3 passages were classified as transiently resistant or susceptible. Wild-type OCh114 subjected to the same treatments served as controls. All experiments were performed in three independent replicates. Heritably resistant isolates were then verified for taxonomy via 16S rRNA gene sequencing84 and cryopreserved in 15% glycerol at − 80 °C until further use.

For within-colony heterogeneity analysis in the surface-attached state, cells were picked from different spatial sectors (outer, inner, intermediate) of single colonies that emerged following phage exposure at MOI 10³, streak-plated to obtain purified colonies, and tested for resistance as described above.

Whole-genome sequencing and analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China)81 and DNA integrity was assessed via agarose gel electrophoresis and quantified by Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA), following the manufacturer’s recommendations, and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Novogene Bio. Tech., China) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were filtered using Trimmomatic v.0.39 to remove adapters and low-quality sequences. Contaminating human (GRCh38, NCBI accession GCF_000001405.26) was removed by Bowtie2 v.2.5.185 alignment with default settings. For reference genome generation, cleaned reads from three ancestral isolates were pooled and co-assembled using SPAdes v3.15.413 with default settings to maximize sequencing depth and assembly contiguity. Genome quality was assessed using CheckM v1.2.286, yielding a high-quality draft genome (completeness = 98.9%, contamination = 0%).

Cleaned reads of the phage-treated isolates were then aligned to the assembled wild-type genome using Bowtie2. Alignment files were processed with Samtools v.1.685, and variants were called using BCFtools mpileup v.1.1687 with quality score > 20 and read depth > 10 as cutoffs. Baseline mutation rates were inferred from individual control isolates, which may miss some low-frequency genetic variation but enable direct comparison with evolved phage-resistant clones. Variants occurring at the genomic termini, which are most likely due to sequencing noise, were removed from further analysis. To remove growth-state-associated and other confounding mutations, all variants shared between control and phage-resistant isolates were excluded. The remaining mutations were therefore considered to have arisen from phage-induced selection in each state, and identical mutations were treated as a single mutational event for further analysis. Variants were functionally annotated using SnpEff v5.2 against R. denitrificans OCh114 genome database (CP000362.1), retaining only the highest-impact annotation per variant based on Sequence Ontology terms88. Annotated VCFs were parsed to classify variants as coding or non-coding and to extract predicted functional impact. Variants were also screened for repeat context (homopolymers and simple tandem repeats) using sequence-based evidence and caller annotations.

For homogeneity verification, the 10 ancestral OCh114 isolates were also sequenced and assembled into contigs separately as described above. Assembled contigs were used for homogeneity assessment through average nucleotide identity (ANI) clustering using FastANI v.1.3389, while cleaned reads were aligned to the reference genome for variants calling as described previously.

Construction and complementation of gene-mutated mutants

For the RD1_1962 gene encoding a glycosyltransferase, an insertion mutation was generated by homologous recombination using the pCVD442 suicide vector system90. Briefly, two homology arms flanking RD1_1962 were amplified from the OCh114 genome using Phanta Super-Fidelity DNA polymerase (Vazyme, China) and primers glyT-5F2 and glyT-5R2. Subsequently, the kanamycin resistance cassette was PCR-amplified from pET28a using primers glyT-28Kan-F and glyT-28Kan-R. These three fragments were fused and cloned into the pCVD442 suicide vector to generate pCVD442-GlyT-Kan. This construct was then mobilized into the wild-type OCh114 strain via conjugation with E. coli WM3064 as the donor strain81 on half-concentrated marine broth (hMB) (0.5 g/L yeast extract, 2.5 g/L tryptone, 0.05 g/L FePO4, pH 7.6) agar plates. Transconjugants were selected on glycerol medium (20 g/L NaCl, 5 g/L MgCl2· 6H2O, 2 g/L Na2SO4, 0.5 g/L KCl, 0.5 g/L CaCl2· 2H2O, 0.2 g/L NaHCO3, 0.1 g/L ferric citrate, 2 g/L yeast extract, 1 g/L casamino acids, 1 mL glycerin, pH 7.5) agar plates supplemented with 60 μg/mL kanamycin. Correct insertion mutants were verified by colony PCR using primers GlyT-outF3/28Kan-seqF and 442R2/GlyT-outR3 (Supplementary Data 15). In addition, PCR amplification and sequencing (Sangon, China) were performed to verify strains harboring frameshift mutations in the genes RD1_1258, RD1_2601, RD1_2657, and RD1_2711. Strains carrying these frameshift mutations were utilized as representative gene knockout strains (Supplementary Data 14).

To complement these mutations, the respective wild-type genes along with their native promoter regions were amplified from OCh114 using primers listed in Supplementary Supplementary Data 15. The amplified products were cloned into the broad-host-range vectors pRK415 or pBBR1MCS-291. The resulting complementation plasmids were transferred into the corresponding gene-mutated resistant strains via conjugation with E. coli WM3064 donor cells as described above. Transconjugants were selected on glycerol medium agar plates containing 60 μg/mL kanamycin or 20 μg/mL tetracycline, depending on the antibiotic resistance marker. The phage susceptibility of these gene-mutated and gene-complemented strains was confirmed by spot assay92.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Log phase culture of the wild-type and mutants with heritable resistance was used for total RNA extraction using the mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Ambion, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity was assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7 as a cutoff. Libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA with Ribo-Zero Gold kit (Illumina, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and sequencing was done on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (OE Biotech Co., Ltd, China) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Quality control and alignment to the reference OCh114 genome were performed as described above. Functional annotation was carried out using Kofamscan. Gene read counts were quantified using featureCounts v.2.0.193 based on the gene annotation file from the OCh114 reference database (NCBI accession GCF_000014045.1). Counts were normalized using the transcripts per million (TPM) method93. The non-normalized raw count matrix was used for differential gene expression analysis with DESeq2 v.1.28.193 using a false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value (p.adj) cutoff of ≤ 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using clusterProfiler v.4.6.293 with the fgsea algorithm and p.adj ≤ 0.05.

Phage adsorption assays

The ability of phages to adsorb to wild-type OCh114 and the gene-mutated mutant strains was examined using the adsorption assay94 with slight modifications. In brief, phage suspensions of RDJL1, RDJL3, or RDJL6 were added separately to each log-phase culture of wild-type and gene-mutated resistant strains (M1258, M2601, M2657, M2711, and M1962) suspension at an MOI of 0.01 (to avoid the high background of free phages present at higher MOIs95). The phage-bacterium mixtures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking (140 rpm) to facilitate phage adsorption. At 20, 40, and 60 min post-infection (which is within the latent period of the three phages according to the one-step growth curve, Supplementary Fig. S1), 500 μL samples were withdrawn and centrifuged at 13,000 x g for 5 min to pellet cells and adsorbed phages. Supernatants containing unabsorbed free phages were filtered through 0.22 μm filters (Millipore, USA) to remove any residual bacterial cells. The filtered supernatants were serially diluted in SM buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, and 10 mM MgSO4, pH 7.5) and titrated via plaque assays to enumerate unabsorbed phage particles remaining at each time point94.

To visualize phage adsorption, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed. Phage-bacterium mixtures were incubated as described above for 30 min to allow adsorption. Samples were centrifuged at 2000 x g for 2 min, and the cell pellets were washed twice with SM buffer by resuspension and centrifugation. The final pellets were resuspended in SM buffer and applied onto carbon-coated copper grids. Samples were negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 3 min, air-dried, and examined using a JEM-1010 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) operated at 80 kV.

Phage infection and biofilm assays

The biofilm formation capability of the wild-type and the resistant mutants was determined using 96-well polypropylene microtiter plates46. Briefly, 2 μL log-phase OCh114 cultures were inoculated into 200 μL fresh RO medium in 96-well plates and incubated statically at 28 °C for 24 h. After incubation, planktonic cells were discarded, and the plates were gently washed three times with PBS, and 200 μL methanol was used for bacteria fixation for 15 min. After staining with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 10 min at room temperature, wells were washed three times with PBS to remove the unbound dye, and destained by adding 200 μL of 100% ethanol and incubating for 15 min with gentle shaking. The absorbance of the solubilized crystal violet was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite M200 PRO, Tecan, Switzerland) to quantify biofilm biomass. It has to be noted that the crystal violet assay does not distinguish between changes in bacterial biomass and extracellular matrix formation and stains both.

The impact of phage predation on biofilm formation by OCh114 was examined using glass tubes96, cover slips92, and 96-well polypropylene microtiter plates46. For the glass tube assays, 5 mL of log-phase OCh114 cultures (OD600 = 0.6) were mixed with phage RDJL1, RDJL3, and RDJL6 separately at MOI = 0.01-103. Control tubes without phages were included. After incubation for 48 h at 28 °C with shaking, tubes were emptied and gently rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove planktonic cells. Biofilm formation was determined by staining with the 0.1% crystal violet stain.

For accurate visualization of cells within biofilms, a modified approach was adopted. Briefly, 2 mL log-phase OCh114 cultures were inoculated into 6-well flat-bottom culture plates containing sterile coverslips. Treatment wells contained phages at MOI = 0.1, while control wells were not inoculated with any phages. Plates were incubated statically at 28 °C, and coverslips were retrieved after 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, for analysis. Coverslips were gently rinsed with PBS and stained with the LIVE/DEAD Bac-light viability Kit (Molecular Probes Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained biofilms were then examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy at excitation/emission wavelengths of 480/500 nm and 490/635 nm.

Phenotypic characterization to assess the impact of phage resistance mutations on bacterial fitness

Growth dynamics were determined by inoculating 8 µL of overnight cultures (OD600 = 0.9) of wild-type OCh114 and gene-mutated strains (M1258, M2601, M2657, M2711, and M1962) into 800 μL fresh RO medium in 48-well plates. Cultures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking, and OD600 was measured hourly for 48 hours using an automated Infinite M200 PRO spectrophotometer (Tecan, Switzerland) with three biological replicates per strain. From the resulting OD600 values growth parameters were calculated as follows: (1) lag time was determined as the x-intercept when the exponential phase line is extrapolated back to the initial OD; (2) maximum growth rate (μmax) was determined as the slope of ln(OD600) versus time during exponential phase, identified by a 4-point sliding window regression with maximum R2; and (3) cumulative growth was defined as area under the growth curve (AUC) by trapezoidal integration of OD600 over the 48 hours.

In addition, intracellular ATP levels were measured using the ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 mL cultures grown to the mid-log phase were centrifuged, and cell pellets were resuspended in 200 μL of lysis buffer provided with the kit. After lysis by vortexing, the cell lysates were centrifuged at 4 °C at 12,000 x g for 5 min, and the supernatants were collected. Supernatants were mixed with ATP detection reagent in opaque 96-well plates, and luminescence was measured using a microplate reader (Infinite M200 PRO, Tecan, Switzerland) equipped with a chemiluminescence detector.

Homolog distribution and evolutionary conservation analysis of phage resistance genes

Taxonomic distribution and evolutionary conservation of key phage resistance genes across marine bacterial taxa were analyzed using the ConSurf web server97 using default settings. In brief, homolog searches were performed against the NCBI nucleotide database using HMMER with an E-value cutoff of 0.001. Query sequences included genes encoding CtrA phosphorelay components (RD1_1258, RD1_2601, RD1_2657, and RD1_2711) and glycosyltransferase NfrB (RD1_1962). Homologs were retained if they met the following criteria: ≥ 80% query coverage, ≥ 60% sequence identity, and E-value ≤ 0.001. Homologs for gene RD1_1258 did not meet these criteria and were excluded from further analysis. Redundant sequences were removed using CD-HIT with a 95% sequence identity threshold, retaining a maximum of 150 non-redundant homologs per gene. Taxonomic classification and isolation source information were extracted from GenBank metadata to determine the ecological niches of bacteria harboring homologous genes.

For each gene, multiple sequence alignments of retrieved homologs were constructed using MAFFT. Phylogenetic trees were built using the Neighbor-Joining method with maximum likelihood distances. Evolutionary conservation scores for each amino acid position were calculated using the Bayesian method implemented in ConSurf.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in biological triplicate. Data were analyzed using R. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, Student’s t test was used to compare two groups. For non-normally distributed data, non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests (Wilcoxon rank-sum tests) were conducted. Statistical comparisons between mutants and wild-type were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test. For mutation analysis, differences in mutation type distributions between lifestyles were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test with Cramér’s V as the effect size measure. Fisher’s exact test was used for 2 × 2 contingency tables comparing proportions of loss-of-function mutations and non-coding mutations between lifestyles, with odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to compare tandem repeat mutations per sample between lifestyles. Enrichment of mutations in key genes was assessed using binomial tests. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
