Mechanistic Insights into PET-MPs-Aggravated Ischemic Stroke via Integrated Computational–Experimental Approaches
Authors: Yuanjia Zhang, Yiqian Zhu, Jie Wu, Jierui Zhang, Jinghong Xu, Yu Yu, Jiajing Xiao, Wenqi Huang, Zhongxing Wang, Tingting Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics, especially the environmentally pervasive polyethylene terephthalate microplastics (PET-MPs), are important environmental pollutants, and their potential risks to the health of the nervous system are increasingly a concern. Evidence indicates the presence of PET-MPs in human tissues, including the brain; however, their specific role in cerebrovascular diseases such as ischemic stroke remains poorly understood. This study applied an integrated multidisciplinary framework combining network toxicology, machine learning, molecular docking, as well as experimental validation to investigate how PET-MP exposure exacerbates ischemic stroke and delineates the underlying mechanisms. Computational analyses identified 15 core target genes linking PET-MP exposure to key pathological pathways involved in neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. Experimental validation using established models of cerebral ischemia, specifically, middle cerebral artery occlusion in mice and oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) in BV2 microglial cells, demonstrated that PET-MP exposure significantly exacerbates cerebral infarction, neurological deficits, microglia-mediated neuroinflammation, and oxidative damage. These findings establish PET-MPs as an environmental risk factor capable of worsening ischemic stroke pathology and provide crucial mechanistic evidence for the environmental health risk assessment related to microplastic pollution.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke constitutes a major contributor to global mortality and long-term disability, with its pathological progression being modulated by a range of environmental factors. In recent years, microplastics (MPs) have attracted the attention of the scientific community as a common environmental pollutant because of their durability in ecosystems, biological accumulation potential, and proven multiorgan toxicity. − Among the diverse forms of MPs identified in both environmental samples and human exposure studies, polyethylene terephthalate microplastics (PET-MPs) are consistently identified as one of the most prevalent categories, reflecting their extensive use in packaging, textiles, and related industries. − This pervasive presence raises particular concern for high-risk stroke populations, such as individuals with underlying atherosclerosis, hypertension, or diabetes, who may experience chronic low-dose PET-MPs exposure through sources including the prolonged consumption of plastic-packaged pharmaceuticals.

To contextualize this specific risk, it is crucial to recognize that MPs, notably PET-MPs, ubiquitously infiltrate the human body through a variety of pathways, including ingesting contaminated food, inhaling particles in the air or skin contact. Research suggests that humans may consume up to 250 g of MPs annually, and their presence has been documented in various human tissues. − Long-term accumulation of PET-MPs in human organs leads to and significantly exacerbates diseases of various organs spanning the lungs, kidneys, liver, cardiovascular system, and reproductive system. − Beyond these peripheral effects, MPs have demonstrated the capability to traverse the blood–brain barrier (BBB), gaining access to the central nervous system (CNS). − This is corroborated by a recent study in human subjects, which identified notably high concentrations of MPs in brain tissue samples. These findings indicate that MPs can accumulate in the brain, a revelation that prompts serious concern about their potential neurotoxicity in humans.

Emerging evidence suggests that MPs may also contribute to cerebrovascular diseases. MPs, including PET-MPs as one of the most prevalent types, have been detected in human vascular tissues and are associated with increased cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk. In addition, experimental studies indicate that circulating MPs can impair cerebral blood flow and vascular function. Meanwhile, MPs can cross the BBB and induce neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, which are key processes in ischemic stroke. However, their specific role in ischemic stroke remains unclear. Beyond cerebrovascular effects, a growing body of research has linked MPs to various neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, anxiety and depressive disorders, as well as epilepsy. − Importantly, following accumulation in brain tissue, PET-MPs can prompt a cascade of pathological alterations including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal apoptosis. − As the principal immune effector cells in the brain, microglia play a pivotal role in postischemic neuroinflammation and possess a robust phagocytic capacity, making them the primary cell type responsible for the uptake and response to PET-MPs. These mechanisms are not merely incidental; they closely parallel the core drivers of secondary brain injury after ischemic stroke. The latter is characterized by a robust inflammatory response and oxidative burst triggered by ischemia-reperfusion. The striking overlap between MP-induced neurotoxicity and poststroke pathogenic pathways strongly suggests that PET-MPs may act as a potential risk modifier capable of exacerbating ischemic brain injury.

Given the widespread environmental distribution, bioaccumulation potential, and the plausible mechanistic overlap between PET-MP neurotoxicity and ischemic stroke pathology, we hypothesized that PET-MP exposure could exacerbate ischemic brain injury. To test this hypothesis and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, this study employed a systematic integration of multidisciplinary methodologies, aiming to elucidate how PET-MPs exacerbate ischemic stroke. Experimental validation in a mouse model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) confirmed that PET-MP pre-exposure significantly exacerbates ischemic brain damage. In vitro studies further revealed that PET-MPs directly trigger proinflammatory and oxidative stress responses in microglia. Crucially, key molecular targets predicted were validated in this cellular model, establishing a coherent mechanistic link from target identification to microglia-mediated neurotoxicity. These results reveal fundamental mechanisms behind PET-MP neurotoxicity. These findings provide a basis for understanding how PET-MPs may influence ischemic stroke progression and offer insights into the biological effects of environmental PET-MP exposure.

Materials and Methods

Prediction of Potential Ischemic Stroke Targets of PET-MPs and Identification of Intersection Genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with ischemic stroke were identified from the GSE16561 data set using the limma R package with standard thresholds. Relevant chemical information for PET and PET-related monomers was obtained from PubChem. Potential targets were predicted using the ChEMBL, SwissTargetPrediction, and BindingDB platforms. To ensure sufficient coverage at this exploratory stage, all predicted human targets with probability scores of >0 were retained for subsequent analysis. Predicted targets were intersected with ischemic stroke-related gene sets via Venn analysis, and the overlapping genes were identified as potential key targets for the PET-MP intervention in ischemic stroke.

Protein–Protein Interaction Network Construction and Core Target Gene Identification

A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of the intersecting genes was constructed using the STRING database with a confidence threshold of >0.4 to retain sufficient network connectivity for subsequent topological analysis, restricted to Homo sapiens, and medium false discovery rate stringency. Cytoscape (v3.10.1) was employed for network visualization and subsequent analysis. The CytoNCA plugin was used to calculate node centrality parameters (e.g., degree and betweenness). Core target genes were selected by using a cutoff of the median centrality index across all nodes.

Construction and Validation of the Microglial Machine Learning Model

Single-cell RNA-seq data (GSE174574) were processed and analyzed by using the Seurat R package following standard workflows. In this study, 12 widely utilized machine learning algorithms were employed to identify key genes central to PET-MP-mediated aggravation of ischemic stroke. By creating pairwise combinations of different algorithms, a total of 106 binary algorithmic models were developed. The GSE174574 data set, comprising 57,852 high-quality single-cell profiles, was randomly split into a training set and a testing set at a ratio of 7:3. All preprocessing steps, including feature scaling, class imbalance handling, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning, were performed on the training set.

To minimize overfitting in the high-dimensional single-cell setting, 5-fold cross-validation was conducted within the training set during model development. Performance across the cross-validation folds was summarized and used for model comparison, while the independent testing set was reserved solely for the final assessment of model generalizability. The discriminative capability of each model was assessed by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Additionally, heat maps were employed to visualize the performance disparities among the different models and genes.

Molecular Docking

In the molecular docking analysis, PET and PET-related monomers (BHET, MHET, and TPA) were used as structural surrogates to represent key chemical units of PET-MPs, as intact microplastic particles are not suitable for conventional docking simulations. The Protein Data Bank and PubChem were used to obtain the 3D structures of target proteins and the stereochemical information for PET (CID: 18721140) and PET-related monomers (CID: 13739, 174073, and 7489), respectively. The optimization process in PyMOL (version 3.1) included the removal of ligands and solvent molecules. Hydrogen atoms were incorporated using the refinement module (version 1.5.6) of AutoDock Tools. Molecular docking simulations were executed through the command–line interface of AutoDock Vina (version 1.1.x), with the resultant complexes subsequently subjected to visualization and analysis in PyMOL (version 3.1) and Discovery Studio 2019.

Acquisition and Analytical Profiling of PET-MPs

The PET-MPs were procured from Tesulang Chemical Material Co. Ltd. (Dongguan, China). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan) and field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM; ZEISS Sigma 300, Germany) were employed to characterize the microstructural features of the PET-MPs. Further characterization was achieved through Fourier transform infrared (FTIR; Nicolet iS5, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) spectroscopy. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano instrument (Malvern, UK) with a fixed scattering angle of 13° (forward scattering), and particle size distribution was analyzed based on intensity-weighted measurements with at least three independent replicates.

Establishment of Animal Models

Wild-type male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from Guangdong Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) and housed under specific-pathogen-free grade conditions. Following acclimatization, mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 18 per group): Sham, MCAO, and MCAO + PET-MPs group. All procedures were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (approval no. SYSU-IACUC-2025-002515).

To estimate a human-relevant exposure level, a body surface area (BSA)-based interspecies dose conversion was applied according to the standard formula: Mouse equivalent dose (mg/kg) = Human dose (mg/kg) × [Km (human)/Km (mouse)], where Km is defined as the ratio of body weight (kg) to body surface area (m2). Based on established parameters, Km values for humans and mice are approximately 37 and 3, respectively. The estimated human intake of MPs ranges from 0.1 to 5 g per week, corresponding to approximately 0.23–11.9 mg/kg/day for a 60 kg adult. Using the BSA conversion, this translates to an equivalent mouse exposure of approximately 0.05–2.9 mg/day. The workflow for dose estimation and experimental dose selection is illustrated in Figure S1. In the present study, mice (body weight 20–25 g) were administered PET-MPs at a dose of 12.5 mg/kg/day via oral gavage for 28 days, corresponding to approximately 0.25–0.31 mg/day per mouse. This exposure level falls within the range of previously reported environmentally relevant doses and is consistent with prior toxicological studies investigating microplastic-induced biological effects. , Sham and MCAO groups received an equivalent volume of distilled water. PET-MPs were ultrasonically dispersed in distilled water before administration.

After 28 day exposure, transient MCAO was performed to generate a model of focal cerebral ischemia. The methodology for establishing the MCAO model has been previously documented, with detailed procedures provided in the Supporting Information.

Establishment of the OGD/R Cell Model and PET-MPs Intervention

The murine microglial cell line BV2 was cultured in high-glucose DMEM (4.5 g/L d-glucose) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To simulate ischemic conditions in vitro, an oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) protocol was used. Cells were randomly assigned to the following groups: control and OGD/R + PET-MPs (0, 25, 50, or 100 μg/mL) groups. For OGD induction, except for the control group, the culture medium was replaced with a glucose-free, FBS-free DMEM, and cells were placed in a hypoxic chamber (1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2) at 37 °C for 4 h. After OGD, cells were returned to normal culture medium and normoxic conditions (21% O2 and 5% CO2) for 24 h of reperfusion.

Statistical Analysis

All data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was applied. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, and significance was set at a p-value < 0.05.

Functional enrichment analysis of ischemic stroke-related DEGs was performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID). GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in immune- and inflammation-related processes, with consistent patterns observed across biological process, cellular component, and molecular function categories (Figure D). KEGG pathway analysis further supported these findings, highlighting pathways associated with immune regulation and defense responses (Figure E).

To avoid bias from an arbitrary DEG threshold, GSEA was additionally performed using the entire expression matrix of GSE16561 (Figure S2A). GSEA further demonstrated the significant upregulation of several critical immune and inflammatory pathways in ischemic stroke samples. To identify additional stroke-related targets, this study performed an analysis using the GeneCards and OMIM databases. As depicted in Figure S2B, a total of 7827 ischemic stroke-related targets were identified, with 53 overlapping with the GEO data set analysis. Following the removal of redundant targets, a total of 7914 stroke-related targets were obtained, as illustrated through a Venn diagram.

PPI Network Analysis Reveals Key Targets for PET-MPs-Induced Exacerbation of Ischemic Stroke

To examine the association of PET-MPs with ischemic stroke, we utilized network-based analytical approaches. A total of 74 PET-MP target genes were curated from the ChEMBL, BindingDB, and SwissTargetPrediction databases after removing duplicates. The intersection of these with 7914 stroke-associated target genes yielded 51 core genes implicated in PET-MP-mediated exacerbation of ischemic stroke (Figure F). Considering the broad nature of the disease-related gene pool, the relevance score distribution from the GeneCards database was examined (Figure S3), supporting the use of a moderate threshold for initial gene set construction. Interactions among the overlapping targets were modeled as a PPI network via the STRING database, applying a medium confidence interaction score cutoff. This network comprised 48 nodes and 155 edges, highlighting the intricate interactions between potential targets (Figure G). The network topology was visualized with node colors representing the connectivity degree (Figure H). Subsequently, a comprehensive analysis was performed using the cytoNCA plugin in Cytoscape to assess six centrality metrics, leading to the identification of ten core genes (Figure I).

Characterizing the Immune Microenvironment of Key Targets in PET-MPs-Aggravated Ischemic Stroke

The immune cell infiltration associated with candidate PET-MP-related hub genes in ischemic stroke was analyzed using the Cibersort algorithm. This approach allowed us to quantify the infiltration profiles of 22 immune cell subtypes in the GSE16561 stroke cohort (Figure A). The most salient finding from comparing immune profiles was a significant increase in the number of monocytes within the stroke cohort. This increase was the most substantial change detected across all immune cell populations. Furthermore, activated NK cells, macrophages M0 and M2, and various T-cell subtypes also demonstrated proportional increases. The results in Figure B demonstrate a significant remodeling of the immune architecture in ischemic stroke patients. Figure C further elucidates that the ten core target genes are associated with the infiltration levels of immune cells, such as monocytes and macrophages.

The aggravation of ischemic stroke by PET-MPs and alterations in immune cell function appears to be intricately linked. However, it is important to note that the GSE16561 data set, derived from peripheral blood samples, does not directly reflect the true immune infiltration environment within the ischemic stroke-affected brain tissue, particularly regarding critical pathological processes such as glial cell responses and myeloid cell migration. Consequently, an additional analysis was conducted using the mouse stroke single-cell sequencing data set GSE174574 to gain insights into the immune microenvironment at the focal stroke site. Single-cell clustering analysis of the GSE174574 data set revealed highly heterogeneous cell populations within the brain tissue (Figure S4A), including microglia (MG), dendritic cells (MdC), neutrophils (NEUT), lymphocytes (LYM), choroid plexus macrophages (CAM), endothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells, all of which exhibit characteristics typical of the ischemic stroke inflammatory microenvironment. To further explore peripheral and central immune responses, a comparative KEGG pathway analysis was performed, revealing convergent enrichment in multiple immune- and inflammation-related pathways (Figure S4B), suggesting a shared inflammatory signature across systemic and local immune responses in ischemic stroke. To elucidate the key molecular alterations associated with ischemic stroke at immune and cellular functional levels, a differential expression analysis was initially conducted utilizing homologous genes from humans and mice (Figure S4C). The resulting volcano plot indicated a predominance of upregulated genes within the inflammatory and immune response pathways. In contrast, several downregulated genes were predominantly associated with metabolic and cytoskeletal regulation.

Hub Gene Discovery for PET-MPs-Aggravated Ischemic Stroke Using Machine Learning

Utilizing the microglia DEGs as candidate features, 106 binary prediction models were constructed based on pairwise combinations of 12 machine learning algorithms. Using 57,852 high-quality single-cell profiles from the GSE174574 data set, the models were trained and tested at a 7:3 split, with 5-fold cross-validation performed within the training set to reduce overfitting. Most models achieved an AUC greater than 0.80 on both training and validation data sets, indicating strong discriminative capability between stroke and control samples. Notably, slight variations in performance between training and validation sets were observed across different model combinations, reflecting differences in generalization ability. In this context, the RF + GBM model exhibited superior performance, attaining the highest AUC across all data sets (Figure D). By integration of variable importance scores from the RF + GBM and other models, six key genes were identified. The ROC curves for these six genes, presented in Figure E, confirm their high diagnostic accuracy. To validate the biological significance of these six key genes, their expression was profiled and compared between ischemic stroke patients and controls (Figure F). In addition, to further evaluate the robustness and generalizability of the identified hub genes, external validation was performed using an independent data set (GSE22255). ROC analysis based on these genes yielded an AUC of 0.808 (Figure S5), indicating acceptable predictive performance across data sets.

Functional Characterization of Hub Genes Underlying PET-MPs-Induced Ischemic Stroke Aggravation

Figure G shows the 15 hub genes identified by integrating the PPI analysis with machine learning. GO enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement in pathways related to inflammatory responses, cellular responses to external stimuli, protein phosphorylation, and cell migration and apoptosis. These genes were mainly localized to the plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and extracellular space and were enriched in functions such as protein kinase activity and receptor binding, highlighting their roles in signal transduction and inflammation (Figure S6A,B). Additionally, KEGG analysis identified key pathways, including the RTK/PI3K/MAPK signaling pathway, pathways related to inflammation and immune response, coagulation- and angiogenesis-related pathways, metabolic and oxidative regulation pathways, and endocrine tolerance pathways (Figure H).

Key Target Protein Analysis via Molecular Docking and Molecular Dynamics Simulations

To delineate the interaction efficiency, molecular docking studies were performed using AutoDock Vina, with PET and PET-related monomers (BHET, MHET, and TPA) included to broadly capture the potential interaction profiles of polymeric structures and their degradation products. Each of the 15 identified proteins suggested potentially favorable interactions to PET and PET-related monomers, with computed energies under the −5.0 kcal/mol threshold, suggesting potential interactions between PET-derived chemical structures and these targets (Figure S7A). Because oligomeric structures better reflect the interfacial and steric features of polymeric materials, PET was used as a representative model to approximate MP–protein interactions. PyMOL was employed to visualize the conformations with the lowest-energy binding conformations between these 15 core targets and PET, including overall complex structures and residue-level interactions (Figure S7B–P).

In addition, detailed binding conformations, residue-level interaction analyses, and molecular dynamics simulation results, including root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) profiles and free energy landscape analyses, are presented in Figure S8 to further support the stability of these interactions. Structural visualization and dynamic simulations were focused on PET as a representative polymeric structure, allowing an evaluation of protein interactions under a more structurally complex and particle-relevant context.

Analytical Profiling of PET-MPs

Imaging via SEM demonstrated that the PET-MPs were irregular, fragment-like particles with rough surfaces and heterogeneous edges, and most particles were in the submicrometer to a few-micrometer range (Figure A). TEM images further confirmed the irregular morphology and showed that the PET-MPs displayed angular, shard-like structures rather than smooth spheres, consistent with mechanically fragmented PET debris (Figure B). The chemical identity of the particles was verified by FTIR spectroscopy (Figure S9A). Characteristic absorption peaks of PET were observed, including the ester carbonyl (CO) stretching at ∼1710 cm–1, C–O–C stretching at ∼1240 cm–1, and C–O stretching in the ∼1100–1000 cm–1 region, along with fingerprint bands at ∼870–720 cm–1, confirming the PET composition of the particles.

To evaluate the physicochemical behavior of PET-MPs, DLS and ζ-potential measurements were performed in culture media and normal saline. PET-MPs showed an increased hydrodynamic diameter and a slight reduction in ζ-potential in complete medium compared with basal medium, suggesting potential surface interactions, while remaining relatively stable (Figure S9B,C). No marked aggregation or sedimentation was observed over 24 h, as supported by stable size distribution and a persistent Tyndall effect (Figure S9D). In normal saline, PET-MPs exhibited comparable size and ζ-potential, indicating similar dispersion properties under in vivo conditions.

PET-MPs Exacerbate Ischemic Brain Injury by Aggravating Neuroinflammation, Pro-inflammatory Microglial Activation, and Neuronal Damage

To examine the presence and distribution of PET-MPs in the brain, fluorescent imaging was performed by using a separate cohort treated with fluorescently labeled PET-MPs. Sparse punctate fluorescent signals were observed in the ischemic hemisphere. These signals were primarily localized in regions exhibiting characteristic morphological features of ischemic injury, corresponding to the infarct core and periinfarct areas (Figure C). Although this approach does not provide absolute quantification, it enables the spatial visualization of PET-MPs within brain tissue and supports their presence in regions affected by ischemic damage.

To determine whether PET-MPs aggravate ischemic brain injury, the infarct volume, cerebral blood flow, neurological function, and behavior were evaluated in the MCAO model. TTC staining showed that the MCAO group exhibited a large infarct in the ipsilateral hemisphere. PET-MP exposure further increased infarct size and volume following MCAO (Figure D,E). Laser speckle contrast imaging showed reduced cerebral blood flow after MCAO, which was further aggravated and poorly restored after reperfusion in the MCAO + PET-MPs group (Figure F). Consistently, the mNSS scores (Table S1) were significantly increased in the MCAO group and further elevated in the MCAO + PET-MPs group (Figure G). In the open field test, PET-MP exposure further reduced locomotor activity in MCAO mice, as indicated by decreased total distance and average speed together with prolonged immobility time (Figure H,I).

Histological staining supported the functional findings. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed that PET-MP exposure further aggravated neuronal damage in the hippocampal CA1 region and cortex after MCAO, with increased neuronal loss and more pronounced structural disruption (Figure J). LFB staining showed that PET-MP exposure further aggravated myelin damage in the hippocampus following MCAO, as indicated by reduced staining intensity and expanded pale regions (Figure K). Together, these data show that PET-MP exposure exacerbates cerebral infarction, perfusion deficits, neurological dysfunction, and histopathological injury after ischemic stroke.

Given the exacerbation of infarct size, cerebral perfusion, and neurological deficits, PET-MPs exposure was further evaluated for its effects on neuroinflammation. PET-MPs markedly enhanced pro-inflammatory responses after MCAO, with increased tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6 levels and a further decrease in IL-10 compared with MCAO alone (Figure A). To assess microglial polarization, Western blot analysis of ipsilateral brain tissue was performed. PET-MPs exposure further promoted M1 polarization and suppressed M2 polarization after MCAO, as indicated by increased M1 marker-inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression, decreased M2 marker Arginase-1 (Arg1) levels (Figure B,C), and consistent changes in microglial phenotype by immunofluorescence analysis (Figure D–F). These data indicate that PET-MPs shift microglial polarization toward a pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotype and suppress the M2-like reparative phenotype in the ischemic brain.

Neuronal damage was evaluated by TUNEL staining and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) immunofluorescence. MCAO caused a marked increase in the percentage of TUNEL+ neurons, which was further augmented in the MCAO + PET-MPs group (Figure G). Likewise, 8-OHdG fluorescence, reflecting oxidative DNA damage, was increased after MCAO and reached the highest level in MCAO + PET-MPs mice (Figure H). Together, these findings demonstrate that PET-MP exposure enhances neuroinflammation, promotes detrimental microglial polarization, and exacerbates neuronal apoptosis and oxidative injury following ischemic stroke.

PET-MPs Exacerbate OGD/R-Induced Oxidative Stress and Neuroinflammatory Activation in BV2 Microglia

To distinguish baseline effects from those associated with ischemic conditions, BV2 cells were treated with PET-MPs under normoxic conditions alongside the OGD/R model. PET-MPs alone induced modest but measurable increases in nitric oxide (NO) production and pro-inflammatory cytokines, indicating baseline microglial activation. Notably, these responses were markedly enhanced under OGD/R conditions, with significantly higher levels observed across corresponding concentrations, suggesting an exacerbating effect of PET-MPs in the context of ischemic injury (Figure S10A–C).

To investigate the potential exacerbation of OGD/R-induced injury in microglia by PET-MPs, oxidative stress and inflammatory responses were evaluated in BV2 cells subjected to increasing concentrations of PET-MPs (0, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL) following OGD/R. These concentrations were selected to establish a dose–response relationship and were consistent with ranges commonly used in in vitro studies. , Given the limited data on tissue-level PET-MPs exposure, these doses are intended for mechanistic investigation and may not directly reflect physiological conditions. Live/dead staining indicated that OGD/R alone significantly decreased BV2 cell viability compared to the control group, and exposure to PET-MPs induced a further, dose-dependent reduction in cell viability (Figure A,B). Correspondingly, intracellular ROS levels, assessed via DCFH-DA fluorescence, were elevated post-OGD/R and exhibited further increases with higher concentrations of PET-MPs (Figure C,D). Mito-SOX staining demonstrated a similar trend for mitochondrial ROS, with PET-MPs significantly amplifying OGD/R-induced mitochondrial oxidative stress (Figure E,F). JC-1 staining revealed a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential following OGD/R; this mitochondrial depolarization was further exacerbated by PET-MP exposure (Figure G,H). PET-MP exposure further aggravated oxidative stress after OGD/R, as evidenced by increased 8-OHdG staining, elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation, and reduced antioxidant capacity as reflected by decreased 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) scavenging activity (Figures I,J and S11A,B).

At the transcriptional level, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) indicated that PET-MP exposure further enhanced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, while further suppressing the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 after OGD/R (Figure S11C–F). Additionally, double immunofluorescence staining showed that PET-MP exposure further promoted M1 polarization and suppressed M2 polarization after OGD/R, as indicated by increased CD86 and decreased CD206 fluorescence (Figure K,L). Taken together, the data indicate that PET-MPs aggravate both oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in BV2 cells during OGD/R.

Validation of Core Genes Underlying PET-MPs-Mediated Exacerbation of Ischemic Injury in BV2 Cells

To validate the core genes through which PET-MPs may aggravate ischemic injury, the mRNA levels of 15 target genes were examined, and BV2 microglia were subjected to OGD/R and exposed to PET-MPs at 0, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL (Figure S12). Several key genes involved in inflammatory signaling and apoptosis, including CASP3, C5AR1, MAPK14, PTGS2, and MTOR, exhibited consistent upward trends, with more pronounced changes observed at higher PET-MPs concentrations. In contrast, a subset of genes showed relatively stable expression across conditions, indicating a degree of selectivity in the transcriptional response.

To provide additional support at the protein level, Western blot analysis was performed on a subset of representative targets at 100 μg/mL PET-MPs (Figure M,N). These targets, including AKT1 (p-AKT/AKT), MAPK14 (p-p38/p38), PTGS2 (COX-2), CASP3 (cleaved caspase-3), MTOR, and C5AR1, were selected based on their central roles in the identified signaling pathways and their relevance to inflammation, apoptosis, and stress-related responses. The highest concentration (100 μg/mL) was chosen to represent the condition with the most pronounced transcriptional changes, enabling clearer assessment of corresponding protein-level alterations. Increased levels of COX-2, cleaved caspase-3, and C5AR1 were observed, along with moderate changes in AKT and p38 signaling pathways.

Discussion

MPs are now recognized as a major environmental concern, attracting considerable attention given their persistent nature, potential for bioaccumulation, and possible toxicity to various organs, including the CNS. However, their specific impact on the progression of acute cerebrovascular events, such as ischemic stroke, remains poorly defined. To address this, we employed an integrated strategy to systematically investigate the role of PET-MPs in exacerbating the ischemic brain injury.

Ischemic stroke represents a major global contributor to long-term functional disability, with neuroinflammation and oxidative stress being central to its pathogenesis. An expanding body of research indicates that MPs, including PET-MPs, possess neurotoxic potential and may exacerbate poststroke injury. , In the present study, the integrated analysis of GSE16561 and GSE174574 provided complementary insights into immune dysregulation associated with the ischemic stroke. Specifically, GSE16561 reflects systemic immune alterations in peripheral blood, whereas GSE174574 captures local immune responses within the ischemic brain microenvironment at the single-cell resolution. Comparative analysis revealed convergence in several inflammation- and immune-related pathways, including cytokine-related signaling, antigen processing and presentation, and leukocyte transendothelial migration, supporting coordinated peripheral and central immune activation in the context of PET-MP-aggravated ischemic injury. Nevertheless, the peripheral blood data set cannot directly substitute for brain-resident immune mechanisms, and its interpretation should therefore be considered complementary rather than equivalent. Moreover, while peripheral blood-derived signatures may not fully reflect the unique characteristics of brain-resident microglia, these findings provide important complementary insights into immune dysregulation and can guide further investigation of CNS mechanisms.

To experimentally validate the aggravating role of PET-MPs, an MCAO mouse model combined with 28 day oral exposure to PET-MPs (12.5 mg/kg/day) was employed. Importantly, the selected dose falls within the range estimated from the human exposure. This level was chosen to ensure detectable biological effects within a controlled experimental time frame and should be interpreted as an experimentally optimized rather than average exposure level. This is broadly consistent with exposure paradigms reported in previous PET-MP toxicology studies.

PET-MP exposure aggravated ischemic brain injury and neurological deficits. Given the pivotal role of microglia in postischemic neuroinflammation and their high phagocytic capacity, this study focused on their response to PET-MPs. Mechanistically, PET-MPs promoted a pro-inflammatory shift in microglia and increased the release of neurotoxic mediators. The classical M1/M2 framework represents a simplified model of microglial activation, as microglia exhibit diverse and dynamic states in ischemic stroke. Thus, these markers are interpreted as indicators of pro- or anti-inflammatory tendencies rather than strict polarization states. Nevertheless, the results consistently indicate a shift toward a pro-inflammatory response under PET-MP exposure. Consistent with the in vivo findings, PET-MP exposure in microglia was associated with enhanced oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory activation, suggesting a synergistic role of these processes in mediating PET-MP-aggravated injury under ischemic conditions. In addition, the relatively small sample size (n = 3) in some in vitro experiments may limit the statistical power; however, the observed trends were consistent across independent experiments, supporting the robustness of the findings.

Importantly, the integrated approach used here provides insights beyond generalized inflammation and oxidative stress by prioritizing candidate hub genes and pathway-associated molecular alterations potentially involved in PET-MPs-aggravated ischemic injury. To connect these phenotypic changes with the initial computational predictions, the predicted core target gene expression was examined. PET-MPs exposure significantly upregulated a cluster of core pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic targets (Figure S12), including CASP3, PTGS2, MAPK14, C5AR1, and MTOR, several of which exhibited dose-dependent increases. These changes suggest the involvement of key pathological pathways, such as caspase-mediated apoptosis, the COX-2/PGE2 pathway, p38 MAPK signaling, and complement C5a receptor activation. − Several additional genes, including FSCN1, KDR, EPB41L2, and PAPSS1, were also identified through bioinformatics analysis. However, these genes are not well characterized in the context of ischemic stroke or microglial biology and are therefore considered as candidate targets that may contribute to PET-MPs-induced responses but require further investigation.

Notably, several of the identified hub genes, including AKT1 and MTOR, are key components of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which is widely recognized as a critical neuroprotective pathway in ischemic stroke. Activation of this pathway has been shown to attenuate oxidative stress, suppress neuroinflammation, and inhibit apoptosis. In the present study, protein analysis revealed decreased p-AKT/AKT levels under PET-MP exposure (Figure M,N), suggesting suppression of this neuroprotective signaling axis. Together, these findings suggest that PET-MPs may impair PI3K/AKT signaling, potentially weakening endogenous neuroprotective mechanisms and contributing to an aggravated neuroinflammation and oxidative injury. Although pathway alterations are supported by protein-level evidence, further studies using pathway-specific inhibition or genetic approaches are required to clarify the causal contributions of this pathway.

It should be noted that PET-MPs are particulate materials rather than conventional small-molecule compounds. Therefore, the use of databases such as ChEMBL, SwissTargetPrediction, and BindingDB does not imply direct ligand–target interactions but serves to infer potentially affected molecular pathways based on structurally related compounds or degradation products. In this context, PET and PET-related monomers (BHET, MHET, and TPA) were used to represent complementary aspects of PET-MPs, where monomers reflect potential degradation products and PET provides a simplified structural approximation of polymeric materials. The computational analysis thus reflects potential molecular-level and structural interaction features, whereas the biological experiments involve intact PET-MPs, which exert effects through additional mechanisms, such as cellular uptake and physical interactions. These approaches address complementary aspects of PET-MP toxicity and should be interpreted at the pathway level, acknowledging that particle-specific effects cannot be fully captured by small-molecule-based predictions.

In addition, the aggravating effects of PET-MPs may share mechanistic similarities with other environmental risk factors implicated in ischemic stroke, such as fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and heavy metals. Exposure to PM2.5 has been shown to exacerbate ischemic injury through endothelial dysfunction and impaired cerebral blood flow, thereby affecting cerebrovascular integrity. Likewise, heavy metal exposure (e.g., lead) is more closely associated with intracellular oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory responses in the ischemic brain. Compared with these pollutants, PET-MPs may exhibit distinct biological behavior due to their particulate nature and strong cellular uptake by microglia, potentially leading to sustained intracellular stress and prolonged inflammatory activation. These findings suggest that PET-MPs may act both through shared pathways and through unique particle-specific mechanisms to aggravate ischemic injuries.

In conclusion, this multimodal study demonstrates that environmental PET-MPs act as potential modifiers of ischemic stroke pathology. The proposed mechanism involves PET-MP uptake by microglia, accompanied by enhanced oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory state. Beyond confirming these pathological changes, the integrated computational–experimental approach enabled the prioritization of candidate hub genes and pathway-associated molecular alterations that are potentially involved in this process. These findings provide a more structured mechanistic framework for understanding how PET-MPs may exacerbate ischemic brain injuries.
