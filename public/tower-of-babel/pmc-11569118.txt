EYA3 promotes the tumorigenesis of gastric cancer through activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway and inhibition of autophagy
Authors: Zhen Xu, Jianhua Liu, Mingjun Yang, Kaibin Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer (GC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with a high rate of postoperative recurrence and poor long-term survival. The Eyes Absent (EYA) protein family plays a significant role in cancer progression, with EYA3 being implicated in promoting GC cell proliferation and tumor growth. Utilizing the DepMap database, we identified EYA3 as a gene of interest in GC. We analyzed EYA3 expression in GC tissues and cell lines, performed in vitro assays to assess its role in cell proliferation, and conducted gene set enrichment analysis to explore its relationship with autophagy and the mTORC1 signaling pathway. In vivo, we used a xenograft tumor model to examine the effects of EYA3 expression on tumor progression. EYA3 was consistently upregulated in GC tissues, and its high expression correlated with a decrease in patient survival rates. Silencing EYA3 in GC cell lines resulted in reduced cell proliferation. Inhibition of autophagy and activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway were observed as mechanisms by which EYA3 may promote GC cell growth. In vivo experiments supported the in vitro findings, showing slower tumor growth with reduced EYA3 expression. Our study confirms the upregulation of EYA3 in GC and its association with poor prognosis. EYA3 promotes GC cell proliferation and tumor growth by activating the mTORC1 signaling pathway and inhibiting autophagy. These findings highlight the potential of EYA3 as a therapeutic target for GC, providing a foundation for future research and treatment strategies. Despite the promising data, the limitations of sample size and the need for further mechanistic studies are acknowledged.Keywords: EYA3, Gastric Cancer, Proliferation, mTORC1, AutophagySubject terms: Cancer, Molecular biology, Gastroenterology

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer incidence and mortality globally, with a persistently low five-year survival rate despite advances in early diagnosis and treatment strategies1. Diagnosis often occurs at advanced stages when the disease has deteriorated, leading to poor prognoses for patients2. Postoperative recurrence is a common issue, with approximately 60% of patients experiencing varying degrees of recurrence after surgery, significantly impacting long-term survival rates3. However, the pathogenesis of GC is complex, necessitating the exploration of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve patient outcomes.

The Eyes Absent (EYA) protein family, characterized by phosphatase activity and transcriptional co-activator functions, has emerged as a crucial participant in the regulation of cellular proliferation, organ development, and DNA damage repair—key processes in cancer development and progression4,5. The EYA protein family consists of four members (EYA1-4), which are evolutionarily conserved across species and share a unique EYA domain responsible for their phosphatase activity6. The family has been shown to be closely associated with cancer progression; for instance, overexpression of EYA1 in breast and lung cancers correlates with increased tumor aggressiveness and metastatic potential7,8. EYA1 modulates the cell cycle and apoptosis by interacting with other molecules such as the SIX family proteins and DACH1, thereby influencing tumor cell behavior9. EYA3, as a member of the EYA family, has been demonstrated to be associated with the progression of various cancers, but there are no reports yet regarding its role in GC10,11.

Autophagy, a conserved cellular process, plays a dual role in cancer12. While autophagy initially acts as a tumor suppressor by eliminating damaged organelles and proteins, under certain conditions, such as nutrient abundance or specific cellular signaling pathways, it can paradoxically inhibit tumor proliferation, highlighting its complex role in cancer biology13,14. It is regulated through various mechanisms, among which the mTORC1 (mechanistic Target of Rapamycin Complex 1) pathway is the most classic15. When mTORC1 is activated, it inhibits the initiation of autophagy by phosphorylating and suppressing key autophagy-initiating proteins, such as the Unc-51 Like Autophagy Activating Kinase 1 (ULK1) complex16. A thorough investigation into the interaction between the mTORC1 signaling pathway and autophagy in cancer growth could lead to a better understanding of how these processes drive tumor progression and the identification of novel therapeutic targets to curb cancer dissemination and deterioration.

In this study, we identified EYA3 from the EYA family as closely associated with GC progression using online databases, a finding supported by clinical data. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments showed that EYA3 promotes GC cell proliferation. The research revealed a mechanism where EYA3 inhibits autophagy to enhance GC proliferation. These results underscore the potential of EYA3 as a therapeutic target for GC, providing new insights into the roles of autophagy and mTORC1 in cancer biology.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

A total of 20 fresh pairs of gastric carcinoma specimens were procured from Nantong Haimen People’s Hospital. The histopathological confirmation of GC was conducted by dual independent pathologists. Prior to surgical excision, none of the patients had received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy.

Cell culture and siRNA-mediated gene silencing

GES-1 normal gastric mucosal cells and GC cell lines, including BGC-823 and SGC-7901 were acquired from Zishi Biotech (Shanghai, China). The AGS and HGC27 cell lines were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China). MKN45 cell line was sourced from the BeNa Culture Collection (Henan, China). Cell cultivation was carried out in RPMI-1640 medium (Yocon, Shanghai, China), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics consisting of penicillin (100 units/mL) and streptomycin, sourced from Clark (Shanghai, China) and Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA), respectively, under conditions of 5% CO2 and at a temperature of 37 ℃. EYA3-targeting siRNAs (5′-CCGGCCCTTCTAGAAGTCCATCTTT-3′) and si-NC (negative control, 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′) were provided by Genepharma (Shanghai, China). Transfection procedures were adhered to as per jetPRIME’s (101000046, Strasbourg, France) instructions.

CCK-8, colony formation, and EDU (5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine) assays

The proliferation of GC cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay, colony formation assay, and EDU incorporation assay17. In the CCK-8 assay, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1000 cells/well, with a final volume of 200 μl per well, and incubated for 5 days. For the colony formation assay, cells were counted and plated at a density of 1000 cells per well (or 1 × 103 cells/well) in 6-well plates, followed by a 14-day incubation period. In the EdU experiment, proliferating GC cells were detected using the BeyoClick™ EdU-555 Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, fluorescence images were acquired with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica).

Extraction of RNA and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

To obtain total RNA, the TRIzol reagent from Invitrogen (USA) was employed, adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s guidelines outlined in reference (32). Subsequently, the isolated RNA served as the template for the synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA), utilizing the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (R312-01, Vazyme). For quantitative analysis of gene expression, qPCR was carried out with the Taq Pro Universal SYBR Mix (Q712-02, Vazyme), ensuring accurate and reproducible results. For quantitative analysis of gene expression, qPCR was carried out with the Taq Pro Universal SYBR Mix (Q712-02, Vazyme), ensuring accurate and reproducible results. For specific amplification of EYA3, the primers used were: Forward, 5′-AACATGCTGCTGGATAAATCTGG-3′; Reverse, 5′-TGTATCACATCGTACCATGCCT-3′. Additionally, GAPDH primers were utilized as an internal control: Forward, 5′-TGGCAAGACAACGTGAAAGA-3′; Reverse, 5′-CTGGGAAAATGCATCTGG-3′.

Western blot

The experimental procedure is the same as before18. Proteins were extracted from cell lines and subjected to Western blot analysis using antibodies specific to EYA3(21196-1-AP), GAPDH (60004-1-Ig), ULK1 (29005-1-AP), LC3(14600-1-AP), p-ULK1 (80218-1-RR), mTOR 66888-1-Ig) and p-mTOR (67778-1-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

Autophagic flux determination

To evaluate autophagic flux, the mRFP-GFP-LC3 lentivirus, purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China), was utilized for labeling and tracking LC3 proteins in SGC-7901 cells. The cells were transfected with mRFP-GFP-LC3 and then further transfected with either si-EYA3 or si-NC using Polyplus jetPRIME (101000046, Strasbourg, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This was followed by an optional 24 h treatment with 10 μM MHY1485 (S7811, Selleck) or 5 mM 3-Methyladenine, HY-19312, MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA). Fluorescence images were acquired using a Thunder microscope (Leica). Yellow puncta, representing the overlap of both GFP and RFP signals, indicated the presence of early autophagosomes, whereas red puncta, showing only the RFP signal, signified late autolysosomes19.

In vivo tumorigenesis assays

Male BALB/c nude mice were acquired and housed in the Experimental Animal Center at the Nantong University Medical School. A total of 20 mice underwent subcutaneous injection of 1 × 10^7 SGC-7901 cells into their flanks. Once the tumors reached a diameter of 5–6 mm, the mice were randomly assigned to two groups of five each. One group received intratumoral injections of si-EYA3, while the other received si-NC, administered alternately every two days for a total of three injections. Starting from Day 6, the body weight and tumor dimensions of the mice were measured every three days using precise calipers. The tumor volume was then calculated using the established formula: volume = (length × width^2 × 0.5). This regular measurement schedule provided a more accurate tracking of tumor growth patterns and the overall health condition of the mice. Upon completion of the study, the mice were sacrifced under deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital, and their tumors were excised, weighed, and prepared for further analysis, either through fresh-frozen preservation or formalin-fixed paraffin embedding. The conduct of these animal experiments was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the Nantong University Medical School (Approval Number: P20230307-004).

Immunohistochemistry analysis (IHC)

Using the Autostainer Universal Staining System (LabVision, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), 4 μm paraffin-free sections from array blocks were stained with polyclonal rabbit antibodies targeting EYA3, Ki-67, LC3B, and p-mTOR. A horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody specific to rabbit was applied. Phosphate-buffered saline was used as a negative control in place of the primary antibody. Blinded evaluations of EYA3 (Proteintech, 21196-1-AP, 1:500), Ki-67 (Proteintech, 27309-1-AP, 1:2000), LC3B (Proteintech, 14600-1-AP, 1:500), and p-mTOR (Proteintech, 67778-1-Ig, 1:500) were conducted. Staining intensity was categorized into four levels: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong). The percentage of positive cells was also scored: 1 (0–10%), 2 (11–50%), 3 (51–80%), and 4 (81–100%)20.

Microarray data acquisition and bioinformatics

The dependency of GC on EYA family genes was interrogated using the DepMap portal (https://depmap.org/portal/), with expression analysis conducted on datasets from TCGA and GEO (GSE66229, GSE65801, GSE63089, GSE51575, GSE33335, GSE29998, GSE27342, GSE179252, and GSE13195). Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter (www.kmplot.com), and GSEA (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) generated angenerate an ordered list of all related genes based on their correlation with SCYL1 expression. The “c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” and “h.all.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt” were selected as reference gene sets.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 22. The significance of differences between experimental conditions was determined using the t-test, with a p-value threshold of < 0.05 for significance. All experiments were repeated for three times at least. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Upregulation of EYA3 in GC tissues correlates with poor prognosis

In an effort to identify key EYA families involved in GC, we utilized the Deepmap database for initial screening. EYA3 stood out as a gene whose suppression significantly impaired the functionality of GC cells, as shown in Fig. 1A. Subsequent analysis of 10 paired GC tissue microarrays from various datasets confirmed the upregulation of EYA3 in GC tissues compared to non-cancerous counterparts (Fig. 1B,C). This pattern was consistent in the analysis of unpaired samples from TCGA-STAD (Fig. 1D). To validate these findings, we conducted PCR on 20 fresh GC tissue pairs, which reinforced the upregulation of EYA3 in cancerous tissues (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, prognostic analysis using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter linked high EYA3 expression to a decrease in survival rates, underscoring the potential clinical significance of these findings (Fig. 1F).

EYA3’s contribution to GC cell proliferation in vitro

According to previous reports, EYA3 can promote the growth of Ewing’s sarcoma21. Additionally, based on the aforementioned results, we found that EYA3 is highly expressed in GC. Therefore, we decided to explore its role in promoting the growth of GC cells. A comparison of EYA3 expression in various GC cell lines revealed a higher expression relative to the GES-1 line, as confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 2A). After silencing EYA3 in SGC7901 cells, we observed a significant reduction in proliferation, as evidenced by colony formation, CCK-8, and EDU assays (Fig. 2C–G), suggesting that EYA3 may play a critical role in the proliferation of GC cells.

EYA3 inhibits autophagy in GC cells, thereby promoting GC proliferation

To further understand the mechanisms by which EYA3 influences GC cell growth, we performed GSEA on TCGA-STAD samples, which showed an inverse relationship between EYA3 expression and autophagy (Fig. 3A,B). Experimental manipulation of autophagy in SGC7901 cells, through the use of a GFP-mRFP-LC3 lentivirus, provided visual confirmation of increased autophagic activity following EYA3 knockdown (Fig. 3C,D). Western blot analysis supported these findings, with increased LC3 and decreased P62 levels indicating autophagy induction (Fig. 3E). Moreover, the restoration of GC cell migration and invasion upon autophagy inhibition with 3-MA suggests a complex regulatory role of EYA3 in these processes (Fig. 3F–J).

EYA3 inhibits autophagy in GC cells by activating the mTORC1 signaling pathway

The connection between EYA3 and mTORC1 signaling was further elucidated through GSEA, which indicated a positive correlation with mTORC1 pathway activation in GC (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis of mTORC1-related proteins post-EYA3 knockdown revealed decreased phosphorylation, suggesting that EYA3 may act to suppress autophagy by activating mTORC1 signaling (Fig. 4B). The use of MHY1485, an mTORC1 activator, demonstrated that mTORC1 signaling could counteract the autophagy induced by EYA3 knockdown (Fig. 4C,D). Previous studies have shown that activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway significantly promotes GC proliferation22–24. Therefore, these findings indicate that EYA3 inhibits autophagy in GC cells by promoting mTORC1 pathway activation, thereby contributing to GC proliferation.

EYA3 promotes the progression of GC in vivo

To validate the in vitro findings, we employed a xenograft tumor model, which showed that tumors with reduced EYA3 expression grew at a slower rate, resulting in smaller tumor volume and weight (Fig. 5A–C). Immunohistochemical analysis of these tumors further supported the role of EYA3 in promoting GC, with a positive correlation observed between EYA3 and p-mTOR, and a negative correlation with LC3B (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these results underscore the potential of EYA3 as a therapeutic target, given its role in promoting GC proliferation through the modulation of mTORC1 signaling and autophagy (Fig. 5E).

Discussions

Our study confirmed the correlation between the upregulation of EYA3 in gastric cancer (GC) tissues and poor prognosis. Through in vivo and in vitro experiments, we revealed that EYA3 promotes GC cell proliferation and tumor growth by activating the mTORC1 signaling pathway and inhibiting autophagy, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target.

The role of EYA3 in tumor progression has been recognized, as in breast cancer, where EYA3 is associated with increased metastatic potential and worsened prognosis, suggesting a general role for EYA3 in tumor biology25. Meanwhile, EYA3 also promotes tumor growth and angiogenesis in Ewing sarcoma by regulating VEGFA levels and enhancing DNA damage repair and cell survival, representing a potential therapeutic target for inhibiting these processes21. However, research on EYA3 in GC is insufficient. Our study fills this gap, highlighting the significance of EYA3 in the impact on GC and providing new directions for clinical research and treatment.

Autophagy and mTORC1 are key pathways for maintaining cellular homeostasis and responding to stress, and their dysregulation contributes to tumor formation26. For instance, the activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway is associated with the suppression of autophagy in various cancers, a relationship that is particularly important in GC as it may affect tumor cells’ response to treatment and survival capabilities27. Our study offers deeper insights into the biology of GC and potential treatments by exploring how EYA3 regulates these pathways. The limitations of our study include a small sample size and potential unknown regulatory mechanisms involving EYA3. Further research with more samples and detailed mechanisms is needed to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusions

EYA3’s promotion of GC proliferation and tumor growth through the inhibition of autophagy and activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway positions it as a promising therapeutic target. Despite its limitations, our study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms of GC and lays the foundation for new treatment strategies, contributing valuable knowledge and direction for future research and therapy in GC.
