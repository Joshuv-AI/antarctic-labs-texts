1.
Introduction

Hypergeometric functions are a cornerstone of special functions in mathematics and physics, providing solutions to a wide array of differential equations and unifying many classical functions within a generalized framework.
Their study connects pure mathematics, applied sciences, and engineering, offering tools to model phenomena ranging from quantum mechanics to statistical and combinatorial problems.

1.1.
The univariate theory

The study of hypergeometric functions originated with Euler in 1769.
He investigated the following second-order linear ordinary differential equation (ODE),

(1.1)

\displaystyle z(1-z)\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{{}^{2}}F(z)}{\dif{z^{2}}}}+(c-(a+b+1)z)\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}F(z)}{\dif{z}}}-abF(z)=0,

gave a power series solution, and established an integral representation of this series.

The solution of
Equation

1.1
was later studied extensively by Gauss in 1812 and is now known as the
Gauss hypergeometric function

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
.
It is the unique solution of
Equation

1.1
that is analytic at
z=0
and satisfies
F(0)=1
.

The Gauss hypergeometric function is

(1.2)

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(a\right)_{{k}}\left(b\right)_{{k}}}{\left(c\right)_{{k}}}\frac{z^{k}}{k!},

where
\left(a\right)_{{k}}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1)
is the
Pochhammer symbol
.
The series converges for
|z|<1
provided that
c\neq 0,-1,-2,\dots
.
It includes, as special or limiting cases, the power, logarithm, and exponential functions, as well as orthogonal polynomials such as the Jacobi polynomials.

Equation

1.1
was subsequently studied by Kummer (1836), most notably, by Riemann (1857), and by Schwarz (1873) and Klein (1894).
Let
p(z)
and
q(z)
be meromorphic functions.
We say that the second-order linear ODE

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{{}^{2}}F(z)}{\dif{z^{2}}}}+p(z)\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}F(z)}{\dif{z}}}+q(z)F(z)=0,

has a regular singularity at
z=z_{0}
if
z_{0}
is a pole of order 1 for
p(z)
and a pole of order at most 2 for
q(z)
.
Riemann showed that any equation of the form
Equation

1.3
with three regular singularities can be transformed into Euler’s hypergeometric differential equation
Equation

1.1
, with singularities at
0
,
1
, and
\infty
.

More generally, a series
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}
is said to be
hypergeometric
if the ratio
c_{k+1}/c_{k}
is a rational function of
k
.
Many elementary and non-elementary functions in mathematics and physics are hypergeometric functions.
It is customary to let
c_{0}=1
and write

\displaystyle\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_{k}}=\frac{(k+a_{1})\cdots(k+a_{p})}{(k+b_{1})\cdots(k+b_{q})}\frac{z}{k+1},

which leads to

(1.4)

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{a};\underline{b};z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(a_{1}\right)_{{k}}\cdots\left(a_{p}\right)_{{k}}}{\left(b_{1}\right)_{{k}}\cdots\left(b_{q}\right)_{{k}}}\frac{z^{k}}{k!},

where
\underline{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
and
\underline{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{q})
.
Similar to
{}_{2}F_{1}
, the hypergeometric function
{}_{p}F_{q}
is the unique solution of the differential equation

(1.5)

\displaystyle\left(z\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}}{\dif{z}}}\prod_{k=1}^{q}\left(z\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}}{\dif{z}}}+b_{k}-1\right)-z\prod_{k=1}^{p}\left(z\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}}{\dif{z}}}+a_{k}\right)\right)(F(z))=0.

For details, see
[
Erd+81
]
, the Bateman Manuscript Project.

Hypergeometric differential equations are canonical examples in the Fuchsian theory, which studies linear ODEs with singularities using the Frobenius series method.
A central problem in Fuchsian theory is to understand the monodromy of solutions, i.e., how solutions transform under analytic continuation along loops encircling singularities.
The monodromy of the hypergeometric differential equation for
{}_{n}F_{n-1}
has been studied in detail in
[
BH89
]
.

Besides differential equations, central topics in the theory of hypergeometric functions include integral representations (Euler, Barnes, etc.), summation formulas (Gauss, Pfaff–Saalschütz, Dougall, etc.), asymptotics, and transformations (Euler, Pfaff, Kummer, etc.).
We refer to
[
AAR99
]
for a comprehensive survey of special functions, especially hypergeometric functions.

Basic hypergeometric series (i.e.,
q
-analog) are discussed briefly in
Section

5.2
.

1.2.1.
Zonal polynomials and hypergeometric functions

Herz
[
Her55
]
used recursive applications of the Laplace transform and its inverse to define hypergeometric functions
{}_{p}F_{q}
with matrix arguments.
Later, Constantine
[
Con63
]
gave a series expansion for them in terms of
zonal polynomials
, which were introduced by James and Constantine in the 1960s.

Zonal polynomials take as input an
n\times n
symmetric positive-definite matrix
X
. Equivalently, they can be viewed as symmetric polynomials in the eigenvalues
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of
X
.
Like other families of symmetric polynomials,
n
-variate zonal polynomials are indexed by
partitions
of length at most
n
.
Such a partition is a tuple
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\lambda_{n}\geqslant 0
.

Constantine
[
Con63
]
introduced the following hypergeometric functions associated with zonal polynomials, or simply
zonal hypergeometric functions
, with one and two matrix arguments
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})

(1.6)

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{\left(\underline{a}\right)_{{\lambda}}}{\left(\underline{b}\right)_{{\lambda}}}\frac{C_{\lambda}(x)}{|\lambda|!},

(1.7)

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{\left(\underline{a}\right)_{{\lambda}}}{\left(\underline{b}\right)_{{\lambda}}}\frac{C_{\lambda}(x)C_{\lambda}(y)}{|\lambda|!C_{\lambda}(\bm{1}_{n})},

where
\left(\cdot\right)_{{\lambda}}
is a generalization of the usual Pochhammer symbol
\left(\cdot\right)_{{k}}
,
C_{\lambda}
denotes the zonal polynomial (in the
C

|\lambda|=\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{n}
is the size of
\lambda
, and
\bm{1}_{n}=(1,\dots,1)
(
n

The full definitions are given in
Section

2
.

The properties of zonal polynomials and zonal hypergeometric functions, and their applications in statistics, together with historical accounts, are collected in
[
Mui82
]
; see also the discussion below.
Beyond statistics, zonal polynomials also appear in representation theory, where they serve as spherical functions of the Gelfand pair
(GL_{n}(\mathbb{R}),O_{n})

[
Mac15
, Ch. VII]
.

1.2.2.
Jack polynomials and hypergeometric series

Around 1970, Jack
[
Jac71
]
defined a family of symmetric polynomials, now known as
Jack polynomials

J_{\lambda}(x;\alpha)
.
These depend on a parameter
\alpha
and reduce to Schur polynomials and zonal polynomials when
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=2
, respectively. They are eigenfunctions for a second-order operator
\square
known as the
Laplace–Beltrami operator
.
More generally, there are simultaneous eigenfunctions of a commuting family of differential operators
D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{n}
known as the
Debiard–Sekiguchi operators
.
See
[
KS06
]
for the historical background on Jack polynomials and related symmetric functions such as the Hall–Littlewood and Macdonald polynomials.

In a widely circulated manuscript from the late 1980s, now available as
[
Mac13
]
, Macdonald introduced further generalizations of hypergeometric series in the alphabets
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and

(1.8)

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x;\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{\left(\underline{a};\alpha\right)_{{\lambda}}}{\left(\underline{b};\alpha\right)_{{\lambda}}}\alpha^{|\lambda|}\frac{J_{\lambda}(x;\alpha)}{j_{\lambda}},

(1.9)

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x,y;\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{\left(\underline{a};\alpha\right)_{{\lambda}}}{\left(\underline{b};\alpha\right)_{{\lambda}}}\alpha^{|\lambda|}\frac{J_{\lambda}(x;\alpha)J_{\lambda}(y;\alpha)}{j_{\lambda}J_{\lambda}(\bm{1}_{n};\alpha)},

where
\left(\cdot;\alpha\right)_{{\lambda}}
and
j_{\lambda}
are defined in
Section

2
.
Macdonald implicitly treated
Equations

1.8
and
1.9
as formal power series, and we will adopt this convention.
We call these the hypergeometric series associated with Jack polynomials, or simply
Jack hypergeometric series
, in one or two alphabets.

It should be noted that Korányi
[
Kor91
]
independently introduced the one-alphabet case
Equation

1.8
, and Yan
[
Yan92
]
the two-alphabet case
Equation

1.9
. As generalizations of the zonal case, when
\alpha=2
,
Equations

1.8
and
1.9
specialize to
Equations

1.6
and
1.7
, respectively.

Many classical properties of univariate hypergeometric functions have been extended to the multivariate setting—for example, Laplace transforms
[
Rös20
,
BR23
]

[
Yan92
,
FW08
]

In addition, multivariate Hermite, Laguerre, and Jacobi polynomials (which correspond to the special cases
{}_{2}F_{0}
,
{}_{1}F_{1}

1.2.3.
Hypergeometric functions associated with root systems

Hypergeometric functions associated with root systems generalize the Gauss hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}(z)
to multivariate settings in a different direction, governed by finite reflection groups.
The foundational theory was developed by Heckman–Opdam in the late 1980s
[

In 1993, Beerends–Opdam
[

1.2.4.
Applications of multivariate hypergeometric series

With the notation in place, we briefly discuss applications in statistics.
The classical real Wishart ensembles are probability distributions over symmetric positive-definite matrices.
They play a central role in likelihood-ratio tests
[

]

[

They are closely related to zonal polynomials, which are Jack polynomials with parameter

The complex and quaternion Wishart ensembles correspond to

In

The parameter

The eigenvalue distributions of

Jack polynomials and Jack hypergeometric series are key tools in such generalizations.

In

Main results

In this paper we construct differential equations that characterize
{}_{p}F_{q}(x)
and
{}_{p}F_{q}(x,y)

\displaystyle\L=\L_{q}^{(x)}(\underline{b}),\quad\M={}_{p}\M^{(x)}(\underline{a}),\quad\N=\N_{q}^{(x)}(\underline{b}),\quad\R={}_{p}\R^{(x)}(\underline{a}).

The operator

Our lowering and raising operators,

Our eigen-operators,

The ingredients are recalled in

We now define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(x,y)}=\L^{(x)}-\R^{(y)},\quad\mathcal{B}^{(x)}=\L^{(x)}-\M^{(x)},\quad\mathcal{C}^{(x)}=\N^{(x)}-\R^{(x)}.

We also write

1.5.
Techniques

We now briefly discuss the techniques used in our work.

1.5.1.
A

The existing proofs of

The key insight of this paper was twofold. The first was the realization that all existing differential operators could be written as the difference of two one-alphabet operators,

\L

It turns out that this approach generalizes to all

and some results of Lassalle

In the Jack case, Macdonald

For

Rather than constructing each eigen-operator with prescribed eigenvalue

A

The usual

\displaystyle\left(a\right)_{{m}}\coloneqq\frac{\Gamma(a+m)}{\Gamma(a)}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+m-1),\quad m\geqslant 0.

Define the

2.2.
Symmetric polynomials

Let
\Lambda_{n,\mathbb{Q}}
be the algebra of symmetric polynomials in
n
variables over
\mathbb{Q}
.
For
\eta=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{n}
, let
x^{\eta}\coloneqq x_{1}^{\eta_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\eta_{n}}
.
The
monomial symmetric polynomial
is

(2.9)

\displaystyle m_{\lambda}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{\eta}x^{\eta}

where
\eta
runs over all distinct permutations of
\lambda
.
Then
(m_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
forms a natural basis of
\Lambda_{n,\mathbb{Q}}
.

For
1\leqslant r\leqslant n
, the
r
-th
elementary symmetric polynomial

e_{r}
and
power sum

p_{r}
are

(2.10)

\displaystyle e_{r}(x)\coloneqq m_{(1^{r})}(x)=\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leqslant n}x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{r}},\text{\quad and \quad}p_{r}(x)\coloneqq m_{(r)}(x)=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}x_{i}^{r}.

For a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
, define
e_{\lambda}\coloneqq e_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots e_{\lambda_{l}}
and
p_{\lambda}\coloneqq p_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots p_{\lambda_{l}}
, where
l=\ell(\lambda)
.
Then
(e_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}}
and
(p_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\prime}}
also form bases for
\Lambda_{n,\mathbb{Q}}
.

The
Schur polynomial

s_{\lambda}
is

(2.11)

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\det(x_{i}^{n-j+\lambda_{j}})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}}{\det(x_{i}^{n-j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}},

where the denominator
\det(x_{i}^{n-j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}
is the Vandermonde determinant

(2.12)

\displaystyle V(x)\coloneqq\prod_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j}).

Then
(s_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
also forms a basis for
\Lambda_{n,\mathbb{Q}}
.

2.3.
Jack polynomials

Let
\Lambda_{n,\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}=\Lambda_{n,\mathbb{Q}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
, where
\alpha
is an indeterminate over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Define the
\alpha
-Hall inner product
on
\Lambda_{n,\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)}
by

(2.13)

\displaystyle\langle p_{\lambda},p_{\mu}\rangle_{\alpha}\coloneqq\delta_{\lambda\mu}z_{\lambda}\alpha^{\ell(\lambda)},

where
z_{\lambda}\coloneqq\prod_{r}(r^{m_{r}}m_{r}!)
and
m_{r}\coloneqq\#\{\,i\mathclose{}\nonscript\;|\penalty\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\lambda_{i}=r\,\}
is the multiplicity of
r
in
\lambda
.

It is well known (
[
Sta89
]
and
[
Mac15
, Ch. VI]
) that the
integral form Jack polynomial

J_{\lambda}=J_{\lambda}(\cdot;\alpha)
is uniquely determined by orthogonality, triangularity and normalization:

(2.14)

\displaystyle\langle J_{\lambda},J_{\mu}\rangle_{\alpha}=0,\quad\lambda\neq\mu,

(2.15)

\displaystyle J_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu\leqslant\lambda}\tilde{K}_{\lambda\mu}m_{\mu},

(2.16)

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{\lambda,(1^{n})}=1.

The coefficients
\tilde{K}_{\lambda\mu}=\tilde{K}_{\lambda\mu}(\alpha)
are generalizations of the Kostka numbers
K_{\lambda\mu}
for Schur polynomials.
Knop–Sahi
[
KS97
]
gave a combinatorial interpretation for
\tilde{K}_{\lambda\mu}(\alpha)
and showed that they are polynomials with non-negative integer coefficients in
\alpha
.

Jack polynomials specialize to several familiar families.
At
\alpha=0,\,1
and
\infty
,
J_{\lambda}(\cdot;\alpha)
becomes
e_{\lambda^{\prime}}
,
s_{\lambda}
and
m_{\lambda}
(up to normalization), respectively.
In addition, when
\alpha=2
,
J_{\lambda}(\cdot;\alpha)
is the zonal polynomial, see
[
Mac15
, Ch. VII]
and
[
Mui82
, Ch. 7]
.

Jack polynomials are
stable
in the sense that
m
-variate (
m\leqslant n
) Jack polynomials are specialization of
n
-variate ones:

(2.17)

\displaystyle J_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m};\alpha)=J_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},0,\dots,0;\alpha),\quad\ell(\lambda)\leqslant m.

Define the
\alpha
-hook-length
functions:

(2.18)

\displaystyle c_{\lambda}(i,j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq a_{\lambda}(i,j)\alpha+l_{\lambda}(i,j)+1=(\lambda_{i}-j)\alpha+\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-i+1,

(2.19)

\displaystyle c_{\lambda}^{\prime}(i,j)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(a_{\lambda}(i,j)+1)\alpha+l_{\lambda}(i,j)=(\lambda_{i}-j+1)\alpha+\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-i,

and set

(2.20)

\displaystyle c_{\lambda}\coloneqq\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}c_{\lambda}(i,j),\quad c_{\lambda}^{\prime}\coloneqq\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}c_{\lambda}^{\prime}(i,j),\quad j_{\lambda}\coloneqq c_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}^{\prime}.

These satisfy
\langle J_{\lambda},J_{\lambda}\rangle_{\alpha}=j_{\lambda}
.
The
dual form
Jack polynomials is

(2.21)

\displaystyle J_{\lambda}^{*}\coloneqq\frac{J_{\lambda}}{j_{\lambda}}.

In statistics (e.g.,
[
Yan92
,
Dub+13
]
), one often uses the
C
-form
, normalized by

(2.22)

\displaystyle(x_{1}+\dots+x_{n})^{d}=\sum_{|\lambda|=d}C_{\lambda}^{(\beta)}(x).

This form relates to the dual form via

(2.23)

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}^{(\beta)}(x)=(2/\beta)^{|\lambda|}\cdot|\lambda|!\cdot J_{\lambda}^{*}(x;\alpha=2/\beta).

The zonal polynomial in
Equations

1.6
and
1.7
and in
[
CM72
,
Fuj75
,
Mui82
]
is
C_{\lambda}^{(\beta=1)}
.
From now on, we do not use the
C
-form; instead, the symbol
C_{\lambda}
will denote coefficients.

By
[
Mac15
, VI.(10.25)]
, the evaluation of
J_{\lambda}
at
\bm{1}_{n}=(1,\dots,1)
is

(2.24)

\displaystyle J_{\lambda}(\bm{1}_{n};\alpha)=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}((j-1)\alpha+n-i+1)=\alpha^{|\lambda|}\left(n/\alpha\right)_{{\lambda}}.

The
unital form
is

(2.25)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\lambda}(x;\alpha)\coloneqq\frac{J_{\lambda}(x;\alpha)}{J_{\lambda}(\bm{1}_{n};\alpha)},

so that
\Omega_{\lambda}(\bm{1}_{n};\alpha)=1
.

2.4.
Binomial formula

The following
binomial formula for Jack polynomials
is fundamental:

(2.26)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\lambda}(x+t\bm{1}_{n};\alpha)=\sum_{\mu\subseteq\lambda}t^{|\lambda|-|\mu|}\binom{\lambda}{\mu}\Omega_{\mu}(x;\alpha),

where
t\bm{1}_{n}=(t,\dots,t)
and
\binom{\lambda}{\mu}
is the
generalized binomial coefficient
.

These coefficients were first studied in the zonal case in statistics
[
Bin74
,
Mui82
]
, and in the Schur case, Lascoux
[
Las78
]
used them to compute the Chern classes of the exterior and symmetric squares of a vector bundle (see also
[
Mac15
, p.47 Example 10]
).
In the 1990s, they were studied by Lassalle
[
Las90
,
Las98
]
, Kaneko
[
Kan93
]
, Okounkov–Olshanski
[
OO97a
,
OO97
]
.
The generalized binomial coefficients can be given by evaluation of
interpolation Jack polynomials
, first studied in
[
Sah94
,
KS96
,
OO97
]
.
Recently, the
BC
-symmetry analog of the binomial coefficients and interpolation polynomials were studied in
[
CS24
]
.

Although
Equation

2.26
depends on the number of variables
n
, the generalized binomial coefficients
\binom{\lambda}{\mu}

do not
depend on
n
.
They admit explicit formulas in the adjacent case
\lambda\cover\mu
, and the general
\binom{\lambda}{\mu}
can be obtained recursively via a weighted-sum formula. See
[
Kan93
,
Las98
,
Sah11
,
CS24
]
.

Fact 1
.

Let
\lambda\cover\mu
with
\lambda/\mu=(i_{0},\lambda_{i_{0}})
, and let
R
and
C
be the set of other boxes in the row and column of
\lambda/\mu
, respectively, that is,

(2.27)

\displaystyle R=\{\,(i_{0},j)\mathclose{}\nonscript\;|\penalty\nonscript\;\mathopen{}1\leqslant j\leqslant\lambda_{i_{0}}-1\,\},\quad C=\{\,(i,\lambda_{i_{0}})\mathclose{}\nonscript\;|\penalty\nonscript\;\mathopen{}1\leqslant i\leqslant i_{0}-1\,\}.

Then

(2.28)

\displaystyle\binom{\lambda}{\mu}=\prod_{s\in C}\frac{c_{\lambda}(s)}{c_{\mu}(s)}\prod_{s\in R}\frac{c_{\lambda}^{\prime}(s)}{c_{\mu}^{\prime}(s)}.

2.5.
Jack hypergeometric series

We now define the
n
-variate
hypergeometric series associated with Jack polynomials
, or
Jack hypergeometric series
:

(2.29)

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}(x)={}_{p}F_{q}^{(n)}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x;\alpha)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{\left(\underline{a}\right)_{{\lambda}}}{\left(\underline{b}\right)_{{\lambda}}}\alpha^{|\lambda|}J_{\lambda}^{*}(x;\alpha),

(2.30)

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}(x,y)={}_{p}F_{q}^{(n)}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x,y;\alpha)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{\left(\underline{a}\right)_{{\lambda}}}{\left(\underline{b}\right)_{{\lambda}}}\alpha^{|\lambda|}\Omega_{\lambda}(x;\alpha)J_{\lambda}^{*}(y;\alpha).

Here
p,q\geqslant 0
are arbitrary,
\underline{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
and
\underline{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{q})
are indeterminates.
We view these as
formal power series
; analytic convergence has been studied in
[
Kan93
, Proposition 1]
and
[
BR23
, Theorem 6.5]
.
We often omit some parameters when there is no confusion.

By stability of Jack polynomials
Equation

2.17
, Jack hypergeometric series in one alphabet are also stable:

(2.31)

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}^{(m)}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x_{1},\dots,x_{m};\alpha)={}_{p}F_{q}^{(n)}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x_{1},\dots,x_{m},0\dots,0;\alpha).

One may then define Jack hypergeometric series in infinite many variables (in one alphabet), in the sense of
[
Mac15
, Section I.2]
.
In this paper, we only work with the
n
-variate case and superscript
n
will be omitted.

It follows directly that

(2.32)

\displaystyle{}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x,y;\alpha)={}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{a};\underline{b};y,x;\alpha),\quad{}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x,\bm{1}_{n};\alpha)={}_{p}F_{q}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x;\alpha).

Macdonald proved the following special cases in
[
Mac13
, §6]
:

(2.33)

\displaystyle{}_{0}F_{0}(x;\alpha)

\displaystyle=\exp(p_{1}(x)),

\displaystyle{}_{1}F_{0}(a;x;\alpha)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-x_{i})^{-a}.

See also
[
Yan92
, Proposition 3.1]
.
Macdonald noted that the Cauchy identity for Jack polynomials leads to the following summation formula:

\displaystyle{}_{1}F_{0}(n/\alpha;x,y;\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{J_{\lambda}(x)J_{\lambda}(y)}{j_{\lambda}}=\prod_{i,j=1}^{n}(1-x_{i}y_{j})^{-1/\alpha}.

2.6.
Differential operators

We recall some differential operators, all acting term-wise on formal power series.
Our notation is slightly different from Macdonald’s
[
Mac13
, p. 47]
.

For
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, let
\partial_{i}=\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}}{\partial{x_{i}}}}
.
Define

(2.34)

\displaystyle E_{r}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{r-1}\partial_{i},\quad r\geqslant 1,

(2.35)

\displaystyle\square

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\partial_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n}\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\partial_{i}.

Macdonald our
\square
by
\square_{2}
.
We shall often write

as

and

Note that

is the

, and

[

The Laplace–Beltrami operator is closely related to the Calogero–Moser–Sutherland model.
See
[
Rös03
]
for an overview.

For operators

and

, and recursively

, for

, with
\ad_{A}^{0}(B)=B
.
We also recall

3.2.

Now we consider the general case:

\displaystyle\L^{(x)}=\L_{q}^{(x)}(\underline{b})

be the space over

\displaystyle F(x,y)=\sum_{\lambda}C_{\lambda}(\alpha)\alpha^{|\lambda|}\frac{J_{\lambda}(x)J_{\lambda}(y)}{j_{\lambda}J_{\lambda}(\bm{1}_{n})},

Proposition 1
.

The following are equivalent for

(1)

F(x,y)\in\mathscr{F}^{(x,y)}_{D}
.

(2)

.

(3)

and

are
\tau
-invariant, where
\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathscr{F}^{(x,y)}\to\mathscr{F}^{(x,y)}
is the involution of interchanging
x
and

.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2) and (3) is obvious.
We now prove that (2)
\implies
(1).
Since
(J_{\lambda}(y))
forms a basis, we may write

for some symmetric formal power series

.
Applying

, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}\left(D^{(x)}(t)(A_{\lambda}(x))-\prod_{i}(\lambda_{i}-(i-1)/\alpha+t)\cdot A_{\lambda}(x)\right)J_{\lambda}(y)=0,

which implies

\displaystyle D^{(x)}(t)(A_{\lambda}(x))=\prod_{i}(\lambda_{i}-(i-1)/\alpha+t)\cdot A_{\lambda}(x)

By the well-known fact that the Debiard–Sekiguchi operator characterizes Jack polynomials, we conclude that

is a scalar multiple of

.

As for (3)

\displaystyle D^{(x)}F-D^{(y)}F=D^{(x)}F-D^{(y)}\tau F=D^{(x)}F-\tau D^{(x)}F=0.

Proof.

We first prove that the uniqueness.
The proof is based on
[

]

Let

3.3

5.2.2.

In another manuscript in 1980s,

\displaystyle{}_{r}\Phi_{s}(\underline{a};\underline{b};x;q,t)

The general case

[
