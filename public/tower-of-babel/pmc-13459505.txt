Effect of selenium application methods on growth, water productivity and essential oil of peppermint (Mentha piperata L.) under drought stress
Authors: Vahid Shamsabadi, Hamid Reza Roosta, Mahdi Moradi, Mohammadnaser Modoodi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelenium (Se) is one of the beneficial elements for plants and can enhance the antioxidant capacity and tolerance of plants against the adverse effects of drought stress. This research was conducted to investigate the effects of applying different forms of selenium in mitigating drought stress on the growth characteristics, water use efficiency, and essential oil percentage of peppermint (Mentha × piperita). Plants were subjected to two irrigation regimes (100% and 50% of crop evapotranspiration [ETc⦌) and selenium was applied at four levels: no application (control), mixing with irrigation water at a concentration of 5 mg L⁻¹ sodium selenate, foliar spraying of 5 mg L⁻¹ sodium selenate before imposing drought stress, and foliar spraying of 5 mg L⁻¹ sodium selenate after imposing drought stress on peppermint plants.ResultsThe results showed that increasing drought severity significantly reduced biomass, root weight, photosynthetic pigments, essential oil percentage and relative water content, but increased water productivity, total phenol, electrolyte leakage, and antioxidant activity. Application of selenium, particularly as a foliar spray before drought, effectively mitigated the negative impacts of drought stress, leading to notable increases in shoot fresh and dry weight, essential oil yield, water productivity, pigment concentrations, relative water content, total phenol, electrolyte leakage, and antioxidant activity.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that low-concentration selenium application is a practical strategy to improve drought tolerance and productivity in peppermint, and suggest foliar spraying of selenium prior to stress as the most effective treatment for optimizing growth and essential oil yield under water-limited conditions.Keywords: Selenium, Essential oil, Yield, Water scarcity, Foliar spray

Introduction

Soil water content is a key factor in determining the crop area under cultivation and consequently agricultural production, which is limited in dry and semi-arid regions such as Iran [1]. This limitation and water scarcity have a significant impact on plant performance, especially medicinal plants [2]. Therefore, due to water resource constraints, adopting strategies to increase water productivity and improve the effective substances of agricultural plants is essential [3]. Given the recent droughts in the world and the increasing demand for water and food, implementing methods to maximize water productivity is crucial [4]. The use of water-saving techniques and promoting the concept of water efficiency in agricultural production is a practical and common method in various parts of the world for sustainable water resource management [5]. Efficiency, in fact, refers to the amount of yield or income obtained from a unit of water consumption [6]. Examining the possibility of cultivating medicinal plants in regions facing water scarcity is of particular importance. Plants may face various environmental stresses during their growth, which can have different effects on their growth and performance depending on their sensitivity and growth stage. Drought stress is considered one of the most limiting factors for plant growth, leading to a decrease in biomass and yield [7].

Mint species have a fibrous root system distributed in the surface soil layers; therefore, access to adequate soil moisture in this region is critical, and its limitation leads to reduced growth and yield parameters [8]. Nezami et al. [9] investigated the effects of drought stress on the growth characteristics of three mint species (M. piperita, M. longifolia, and M. spicata) and reported that drought stress reduced growth parameters in all three species.

Akbarzadeh and Shahnazari [10] conducted a study to investigate the effect of partial root irrigation on peppermint plants and reported that applying partial root irrigation at 55, 40, and 25% of ET compared to full irrigation resulted in a decrease in dry yield. Jahani et al. [11] demonstrated that applying drought stress to peppermint plants led to a decrease in leaf number, leaf area, shoot dry weight, and chlorophyll content, while mild stress improved the essential oil percentage. Shamsabadi et al. [12] showed that applying 75 and 50% of ET as drought stress reduced the fresh and dry weight, height, leaf number, and stem nodes in peppermint plants, while water productivity increased. Additionally, the application of mild stress (75% of ET) increased essential oil percentage, while severe stress (50% of ET) decreased it. Understanding the compounds that alleviate the effects of drought stress in plants is essential. Although selenium toxicity at high concentrations is detrimental to plants and considered a stress factor, the beneficial effects of low concentrations of selenium in protecting plants against non-beneficial stresses through complex mechanisms have been identified [13]. Selenium is not an essential nutrient for plants but can serve as a useful element for them [14]. The selenium concentration in soil is relatively low in most parts of the world, and in most plants, the selenium concentration is lower than 0.1 mg/kg of plant dry weight [15]. Selenium is primarily absorbed by plants in the form of selenate and selenite or organic Se, as selenate salts are highly soluble and easily absorbed by plants [16]. Selenium is present in the structure of some enzymes and compounds in plants and can inhibit the formation of free radicals through enzymatic activity [17]. Selenium in combination with proteins produces compounds called selenoproteins, which include antioxidant enzymes [18]. Selenium has antioxidant properties and increases the antioxidant capacity and plant tolerance in drought stress conditions [19]. Most growth and developmental characteristics of plants increase with selenium concentration [20]. Khademi et al. [21] showed that the application of selenium at a concentration of 8 mg/L in brussels sprouts increased the fresh and dry weight of leaves and stems, but at higher concentrations, the leaf area, fresh and dry weight of leaves, stems, and roots decreased due to the toxic effects created by high selenium concentrations in this plant.

Research by Zhu et al. [22] demonstrated that foliar application of nano-selenium in tea plants (Camellia sinensis L.) under drought stress increased biomass, water use efficiency, soluble sugars, amino acids, and phenols. Nawaz et al. [23] reported that selenium foliar application coinciding with drought stress in wheat plants enhanced antioxidant activity, soluble sugars, and amino acids.

Shamsai et al. [24] demonstrated that selenium foliar application, by increasing antioxidant compounds (phenols and antioxidant enzymes) and photosynthetic pigments, alleviated the adverse effects of stress and enhanced drought tolerance in rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis L.). Selenium application as a foliar spray significantly reduced the negative effects of drought stress in dragonhead (Dracocephalum moldavica L.) [25]. The study by Naeemi et al. [26] showed that selenium foliar application under drought stress conditions in the medicinal plant pumpkin (Cucurbita pepo L.) increased relative leaf water content and reduced the adverse effects of drought stress compared to the control treatment. Shamsabadi et al. [12] demonstrated that foliar spray of selenium at 5 mg/L of sodium selenate led to an increase in fresh and dry weight, water productivity, essential oil percentage, plant height, leaf number, and leaf nodes of peppermint plants. Jafari and Moghaddam [27] demonstrated in a study that sodium selenate and sodium selenite at a concentration of 4 mg L⁻¹ improved the physiological characteristics of peppermint (Mentha × piperita).

Today, medicinal and aromatic plants have received significant attention in various fields, including agriculture and pharmaceutical industries. Peppermint is a herbaceous perennial plant that is considered a medicinal and aromatic plant and is widely used in the pharmaceutical industry. The effect of applying selenium in different ways (irrigation or foliar spray) and at different times on drought stress has not been studied so far. Therefore, the main objective of this study was to compare and investigate the effects of selenium in different application forms (along with irrigation water and foliar spray before and after drought stress application) on water productivity, growth characteristics, photosynthetic pigments, and essential oil percentage of peppermint (Mentha piperita L.) under drought stress conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The study was conducted in a greenhouse with an ambient temperature of 25/18°C (day/night), relative humidity of 50 ± 5%, a light period of 13 h/day, and geographical coordinates of 60.64 degrees east and 35.23 degrees north, at an altitude of 982 m above sea level in Khorasan Razavi province, Torbat Jam, Iran. The pots used in this study had a diameter of 18.5 and a height of 17 cm, filled with soil. The soil weight per pot was 6 kg.

The chemical and physical composition of the soil used is presented in Table 1. The total number of treatments was eight, each with three replications, and four pots were planted per replication. A total of 96 pots of peppermint (Mentha piperita L.) were cultivated. Additionally, three peppermint rhizomes of equal size were planted in each pot. The rhizomes of peppermint, obtained from the Iranian Institute of Medicinal Plants, Karaj, Iran. The Institute of Medicinal Plants declared that peppermint seedlings were obtained under national and international guidelines, and the seedlings were prepared under the supervision and permission of the Agricultural Engineering Research Institute and all authors comply with all the local and national guidelines. The voucher specimens of the plants were deposited at the herbarium of the Iranian Institute of Medicinal Plants, Karaj, Iran.

Experimental design and treatments

The experiment was conducted in a factorial design in a completely randomized design with factors of drought stress, including two levels (100%ETc and 50%ETc [crop evapotranspiration⦌), and selenium application with four levels (no application (zero), mixing with irrigation water at a rate of 5 mg/L of sodium selenate dissolved in water (I), foliar spray of selenium at a rate of 5 mg/L of sodium selenate dissolved in water before drought stress application (BDS), and foliar spray of selenium at a rate of 5 mg/L of sodium selenate dissolved in water after drought stress application (ADS)), each with three repetitions. To ensure proper establishment of seedlings, plant watering was done one day apart and thoroughly for up to two weeks after rhizome transplantation. Subsequently, the process of applying treatments began. In the selenium treatment mixing with irrigation water, sodium selenate at a rate of 5 mg/L was mixed with irrigation water and applied to the desired pots during each irrigation, and drought stress treatments were simultaneously applied. Finally, after six weeks of selenium and drought stress treatments, the plants were harvested. In the BDS treatment, the desired pots were exposed to sodium selenate treatment (Na2SeO4) with a concentration of 5 mg/L as a foliar spray. The foliar spray procedure was carried out three times, one day apart, and 24 h after the last foliar spray, the pots were subjected to drought stress for 5 weeks and then the plants were harvested. In the ADS treatment, after proper establishment of seedlings, pots were first exposed to drought stress, and after 4 weeks of drought stress, foliar spray of selenium at a rate of 5 mg/L of sodium selenate was performed three times, one day apart.

Two weeks after the foliar spray, the plants were harvested. In this study, regular irrigation water was used. The irrigation water quality characteristics are presented in Table 2.

The amount of water used in the treatments corresponding to 100% of water requirement was 10 L, and in the 50% water requirement treatments, it was 5 L.

In this study, the nutritional needs of plants (Hoagland solution) were estimated at the beginning of the experiment based on valid scientific sources [31] and provided as a solution in irrigation water at a consistent amount for the plants to receive their required nutrients by the end of the growth period. Plant biomass

To measure the shoot fresh and dry weight of the samples, the shoots were first separated, weighed for fresh weight, then dried in an oven at 70 °C for 48 h for dry weight determination.

Essential oil

For the extraction and measurement of essential oil, the harvested plants were dried at room temperature (25 °C) in the shade for two weeks, followed by steam distillation using an essential oil extractor. For this purpose, 15 g of the dried shoot of peppermint (including leaves, stems, and flowers) was placed in a special flask with distilled water (in the ratio of 1:15), and the extraction process began by heating the flask containing water and plants using a heater. After boiling and once the extraction of essential oil began, the process continued for 2 h before turning off the device. After cooling (about half an hour), the volume of the extracted essential oil was directly read from the graduated collection tube, and the resulting oil was collected for weighing and storage [32]. Since the amount of essential oil is calculated in ml per dry mass of the plant, the moisture content of each sample was measured before extraction by placing it in an oven (at 105 °C) for three hours. Percentage of electrolyte leakage

The percentage of electrolyte leakage, denoted as EL, was determined using the method of Lutts et al. [35]. EC1 represents the electrical conductivity of young leaf segments placed in test tubes containing distilled water and incubated on a shaker at 100 rpm for 24 h at room temperature. EC2 represents the electrical conductivity of the samples after being placed in an autoclave at 120 °C for 20 min.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SAS Version 9.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA, http://www.sas.com). For statistical analysis, a two-way ANOVA model was employed to determine significant treatment effects. Subsequently, significant mean differences (P < 0.05) were calculated using the Duncan test as a post hoc analysis to identify distinct treatment groups.

Results and discussion

Based on the results from the analysis of variance (Table 3), besides the simple effects of selenium and drought stress for all measured traits, except for the simple effect of selenium on electrolyte leakage, their interactive dual effect on shoot dry weight, essential oil percentage, relative water content, photosynthetic pigments and antioxidant activity of peppermint plants showed a significant interaction. The comparison of means indicated that as the levels of drought stress increased from 100 to 50% of ET, the shoot fresh weight, shoot dry weight, essential oil percentage, root weight, chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, carotenoids and relative water content, of peppermint plant decreased by 34.94, 33.64, 33.52, 61.33, 21.11, 26.66, 21.23, 17.24, 13.04% respectively, while water productivity, antioxidant activity, total phenol and electrolyte leakage increased by 30.69, 28.92, 27.29 and 49.89%. Furthermore, in the selenium treatments including BDS (foliar spray of selenium at the rate of 5 mg/L before applying drought stress treatment), ADS (foliar spray of selenium at the rate of 5 mg/L after applying drought stress treatment), and I (mixing selenium at the rate of 5 mg/L with irrigation water), water productivity increased by 66.87, 41.79, and 29.25% respectively compared to no selenium application, the essential oil percentage increased by 66.04, 38.68, and 30.19% respectively, total chlorophyll increased by 30.58, 22.66, and 17.89%, respectively, antioxidant activity increased by 30.54, 20.05, and 12.43%, respectively, total phenol increased by 38.60, 28.48, and 14.87%, respectively and relative water content increased by 18.87, 13.72, and 8.03%, respectively (Table 4).

The results of the interactive effects of drought stress and selenium on shoot dry weight of peppermint plants showed increases of 60.25%, 42.26%, and 27.20% with the application of selenium in BDS, ADS, and I treatments compared to no selenium application under 50% ET (Fig. 1). These results indicate that the application of selenium in peppermint plants, especially in the BDS treatment, resulted in the highest increases in shoot dry weight under drought stress conditions. The stimulation of plant growth due to selenium application is attributed to increased antioxidant enzyme activity, which can have a positive impact on cell division, carbon fixation, starch synthesis, and ultimately lead to an enhancement in plant growth. A study by Turakainen [36] reported that selenium acts as an antioxidant at low concentrations and promotes plant growth by stabilizing carbon fixation and promoting cell division. The results of this study align with previous research. Shamsabadi et al. [12] showed that drought stress resulted in decreased dry weight of peppermint plants, while foliar spray of selenium at 5 mg/L before applying drought stress treatment enhanced the dry weight of the plants. Ebrahimi- Sborezi et al. [37] demonstrated that severe water deficit stress (periodic deprivation of 55% of available water in the root zone) led to a 44% reduction in dry weight of peppermint plants.

The results of the interactive effects of drought stress and selenium on the essential oil percentage showed increases of 61.18%, 43.53%, and 26.20% with the application of selenium in BDS, ADS, and I treatments compared to no selenium application under 50% ET (Fig. 2). Ebrahimi- Sborezi et al. [37] showed that severe water deficit stress resulted in a reduction in the essential oil percentage of peppermint plants. García et al. [38] demonstrated that 70% water deficit stress led to a decrease in the essential oil yield of peppermint plants. Shamsabadi et al. [12] showed that foliar spray of selenium at a rate of 5 mg/L before applying drought stress treatment resulted in an increased essential oil percentage in peppermint plants. It appears that under severe drought stress treatments, due to the reduction in shoot fresh and dry weight, the essential oil percentage decreases. However, through selenium foliar application and the subsequent alleviation of drought stress effects, it is possible to increase the essential oil percentage.

While the interactive effects of drought stress and selenium did not have a significant effect on root weight, the simple effects analysis showed that the highest root weight in the I treatment of selenium, indicating increased root growth in peppermint plants when selenium was applied with irrigation water. Research indicates that adding selenium to the plant growth environment leads to increased cell division in the meristematic part, ultimately contributing to increased root length, volume, and weight. The results of this study are consistent with the study of Jafari and Moghadam [39] demonstrated that applying two forms of selenium (sodium selenate and sodium selenite at a concentration of four mg/L) in combination with a nutrient solution increased the growth characteristics of peppermint plants, including root weight and length. Han-Wens et al. [40] showed that adding selenium to the growth environment of garlic plants increased the root volume. The results of the interactive effects of drought stress and selenium on photosynthetic pigments (Fig. 3) indicated that the BDS treatment had the highest increase in photosynthetic pigments (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids) of peppermint plants compared to other selenium treatments. Therefore, the application of selenium as a foliar spray before applying drought stress treatment had the most significant impact on reducing the effects of drought stress.

The reduction in photosynthetic pigment concentration under drought stress conditions is attributed to their degradation due to increased activity of chlorophyllase, peroxidase, and phenolic compounds. On the other hand, drought stress leads to an increase in the concentration of growth regulators, under the influence of which photosynthetic pigments are degraded.

Selenium foliar application prevents the degradation of photosynthetic pigments under environmental stress conditions, particularly drought stress, and increases the antioxidant capacity of stressed plants [41]. Filck and Hartikainen [42] reported that the reduction of photosynthetic pigments due to drought stress is mainly attributed to the destruction of chloroplast structure and the photosynthetic apparatus, degradation of chlorophyll synthesis precursors, or inhibition of chlorophyll biosynthesis. Selenium foliar application can largely prevent chloroplast degradation and enhance photosynthetic pigments.

The results of the interactive effects of drought stress and selenium on relative water content showed increases of 14.08%, 9.53%, and 4.96% with the application of selenium in BDS, ADS, and I treatments compared to no selenium application under 50% ET (Fig. 4). The results indicated that the BDS treatment had the highest increase in relative water content of peppermint plants compared to other selenium treatments. Therefore, the application of selenium as a foliar spray before applying drought stress treatment had the most significant impact on reducing the effects of drought stress.

Drought stress reduces the relative water content of leaves by decreasing turgor pressure and water potential. However, selenium foliar application, particularly before the onset of stress (BDS), can mitigate the negative effects of drought stress and increase leaf relative water content. Mahdavi Ardakani et al. [43] demonstrated in a study that the relative water content of leaves in marjoram plants under drought stress increased by 40% with selenium foliar application at a concentration of 8 mg L⁻¹ compared to no selenium application. Studies indicate that selenium enhances plant leaf relative water content under drought stress by improving root development, osmotic regulation, and maintaining membrane structure [44].

The results of the interactive effects of drought stress and selenium on antioxidant activity showed increases of 34.82%, 21.70%, and 12.96% with the application of selenium in BDS, ADS, and I treatments compared to no selenium application under 50% ET (Fig. 5). The results indicated that the BDS treatment had the highest increase in antioxidant activity of peppermint plants compared to other selenium treatments. Therefore, the application of selenium as a foliar spray before applying drought stress treatment had the most significant impact on reducing the effects of drought stress. Amerian et al. [45] demonstrated in a study that selenium application at low concentrations under drought stress increased the activity of antioxidant enzymes in the aerial parts of onion (Allium cepa) plants.

In conclusion, the application of selenium, especially in the BDS treatment, had a positive effect on the growth, essential oil percentage, water productivity, and photosynthetic pigments of peppermint plants under drought stress conditions. The findings suggest that selenium plays a crucial role in mitigating the adverse effects of drought stress and enhancing the growth and productivity of peppermint plants. Further research on the interaction between selenium and drought stress in medicinal plants can provide valuable insights for sustainable agriculture practices.

Based on the correlation matrix (Fig. 6), the relationships among the measured traits, essential oils, chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, carotenoids, relative water content, and DPPH scavenging activity, were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Strong, positive correlations were observed among photosynthetic pigment traits. Specifically, Chl a showed very high correlations with Chl b (r = 0.94), Chl T (r = 0.99), and Car (r = 0.97). Similarly, Chl b was strongly correlated with Chl T (r = 0.98) and Car (r = 0.94), while Chl T and Car exhibited an almost perfect correlation (r = 0.97). RWC also displayed strong positive relationships with Chl a (r = 0.99), Chl T (r = 0.99), and Car (r = 0.97), and a moderately high correlation with Chl b (r = 0.95). EO showed moderate positive correlations with all pigment traits and RWC, ranging from r = 0.52 (RWC) to r = 0.70 (Car), with the highest EO correlation observed for Car (r = 0.70). In contrast, DPPH-SA exhibited consistently negative but weak to moderate correlations with all other traits. The strongest negative relationships were with Car (r = -0.25) and Chl a (r = -0.24), while correlations with RWC (r = -0.16), Chl b (r = -0.11), and Chl T (r = -0.20) were weaker. Overall, the results indicate strong interdependency among chlorophylls, carotenoids, and RWC, with EO also positively linked to these traits. Conversely, DPPH-SA showed an inverse relationship with the measured parameters, suggesting that higher pigment content and water status may be associated with reduced DPPH radical scavenging activity under the tested conditions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that selenium application, particularly via foliar spray before drought stress, significantly improves growth, water productivity, relative water content, antioxidant activity, total phenol, electrolyte leakage, photosynthetic pigments and essential oil percentage in peppermint under water-limited conditions. Increasing drought severity reduced biomass, pigment concentrations, root weight, relative water content and essential oil percentage, yet water productivity, antioxidant activity, total phenol and electrolyte leakage was enhanced. Selenium treatments mitigated the adverse effects of drought by boosting shoot fresh and dry weight, root development, photosynthetic pigments, antioxidant activity, total phenol and essential oil yield. Foliar spraying of selenium before drought was the most effective method. These findings support the use of selenium as a practical agronomic strategy to enhance drought tolerance and productivity in peppermint, offering a valuable approach for sustainable production in arid and semi-arid environments.
