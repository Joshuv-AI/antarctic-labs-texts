The Fontan circulation: Arrhythmia is just one price we have to pay
Authors: Jack Rychik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Fontan circulation has transformed the outlook for patients with single-ventricle congenital heart disease, allowing survival into adolescence and adulthood for thousands who would otherwise not have survived. However, this success comes at the cost of a unique and complex physiology associated with multiple long-term complications, among which arrhythmia remains a major concern. In this invited commentary, the findings of a recent systematic review and meta-analysis comparing arrhythmia outcomes after extracardiac and intracardiac Fontan completion are discussed in the broader context of Fontan physiology. While extracardiac Fontan appears to be associated with a lower arrhythmia risk, the clinical significance of different rhythm disturbances and their impact on overall outcomes requires careful interpretation. Exercise limitation, hepatic issues, and quality of life are among the other important effects of Fontan circulation that should be taken into account in addition to arrhythmia. Ultimately, the choice of Fontan pathway should be guided by overall long-term benefits rather than arrhythmia risk alone, emphasizing the need for robust longitudinal data and collaborative registries to help future decision-making.

COMMENTARY

The Fontan operation permits survival of individuals afflicted with the life-threatening birth defect of single-ventricle type congenital heart disease. Tens of thousands with this condition are now alive worldwide who would otherwise not be alive had it not been for this strategy of care. Unfortunately, the resultant circulation comes with significant limitations and a price to pay. We are now familiar the myriad challenges associated which include not just elements of cardiovascular dysfunction, but also complications of the liver, kidney, lymphatics, bone, growth, neurodevelopment, and mental health.[1]

One complication of cardiovascular nature is the burden of arrhythmia. Abnormalities of slow rhythm, such as junctional rhythm, sinus node dysfunction, as well as fast rhythms like atrial flutter, make up the signature of disturbances noted. The fragile Fontan circulatory physiology is exquisitely sensitive to the loss of coordinated synchronous atrial–ventricular contraction. Thus, it is no surprise that such arrhythmias lead to the potential for inefficient cardiac output delivery by impairing ventricular filling, elevating filling pressures, and increasing systemic venous congestion. This leads to a breakdown in optimal cardiovascular performance, essential for keeping Fontan circulatory-related challenges in check.

Despite the commonality of the basic physiology amongst individuals, complications associated with the Fontan circulation are highly variable in their clinical presentation from individual to individual. Identifying what factors strongly contribute to the presence and magnitude of a particular complication in any one patient is important. Fakhri, Bunyamin, and Gunawan, in the last issue of Annals of Pediatric Cardiology, tried to gain insight into which type of Fontan surgery provides the least arrhythmia risk.[2] They performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 studies with over 6000 patients and grouped outcome findings by extracardiac Fontan and intracardiac Fontan subtypes. The intent was to determine if a less “atrially stressed” approach, such as the extracardiac conduit, in which atrial incision is minimized and chronic atrial hypertension avoided, might offer benefit over an intracardiac Fontan. The authors did indeed find better outcomes with less arrhythmia burden in the extra-cardiac group. However, they also included atriopulmonary Fontan together with the lateral tunnel in their intracardiac group. The atriopulmonary Fontan has been abandoned in the current era for precisely this reason of inducing severe arrhythmia; including this historical relic into the comparison is somewhat unfair. Nevertheless, this comprehensive review portrays the complexity of the question and offers insight into the arguments for and against any one strategy, with conclusions favoring the extra-cardiac approach.

These findings, though, prompt consideration of several points. First, there may be a lack of full consensus on what comprises true “arrhythmia” in this population and to what degree specific rhythm abnormalities contribute to important aberrations in physiology or clinically apparent symptoms. For example, should sinus bradycardia be considered an arrhythmia in the Fontan circulation? Many papers consider abnormally low resting heart rates that are sinus rhythm as an “arrhythmia.” We might accept such findings as reflecting beneficial efficiency in an athlete, yet we do not seem to do so in a Fontan circulation. Let us consider, for argument’s sake, that a low resting sinus rhythm in an individual with Fontan circulation may, in fact, reflect an adaptive physiological response in order to prolong diastolic filling times and thus improve ventricular filling and optimize stroke volume. If variability in heart rate is evident, resting sinus bradycardia should therefore not be considered an “arrhythmia.” While this phenomenon is likely present in both extra-cardiac and lateral tunnel type Fontan types, it is difficult to tease out the prevalence of sinus bradycardia from the greater literature data set. A confident answer to the question of whether sinus bradycardia is a precursor for other complications, such as lymphatic insufficiency, remains doubtful. Asynchrony, such as in a junctional rhythm, may be another matter,[3] as this can lead to elevated venous congestion, yet interestingly, many with this finding remain totally asymptomatic. Atrial flutter in a Fontan circulation is likely the most deleterious arrhythmia, due to its features of tachycardia and absence of atrial-ventricular synchrony. The point here to make is that not all differences in heart rhythm are of the same impact and magnitude.

Next is the matter of perspective for the presence of arrhythmia risk overall. Our goals are always to provide the best opportunity for a normal quality and duration of life to those with single-ventricle congenital heart disease. Arrhythmia should be considered within the context of this objective. We should not be looking at arrhythmia as an outcome solely on its own, but rather how it interplays with other systems.

Since Marcelleti’s introduction of the use of an extra-cardiac conduit over 40 years ago,[4] the technique has gained tremendous popularity and is now perhaps the most commonly performed Fontan procedure worldwide. Proponents have always made the case of arrhythmia reduction in support of this approach. However, recently, a new lens and perspective have been offered. Conduits do not grow, and in fact, pathway cross-sectional areas diminish over time with neo-endothelization and luminal thrombus formation. Through the test of time, we now realize that conduits sized appropriately for a 3- or 4 years old are insufficient when it comes to an adult. A 16 mm or 18 mm diameter conduit may be satisfactory for a child, yet it may be wholly insufficient for a young adult. In particular, as we are strongly recommending activity and exercise as a therapeutic strategy for these individuals, a relatively narrowed conduit may be barely passable at rest but critically limiting when it comes to exercise, where doubling of the cardiac output may be necessary. Conduits may limit volumetric passage, leading to impaired ventricular filling and diminished stroke volume at rest or exercise. The organ most immediately affected by conduit Fontan pathway restriction is the liver.[5] Small diameters in addition to the non-compliant passages of a conduit may aggravate the potential for promoting what we now know is progressive liver fibrosis, ubiquitous in all,[6] and the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma. A lateral tunnel that partially incorporates atrial tissue but develops an adaptive passageway geometry may provide a more favorable architecture for hepatic venous effluent egress, thus reducing hepatic stress.[7] Such a pathway may also expand to allow for increased flow during periods of increased demand, such as exercise.

Hence, this question is key-to what extent and magnitude should arrhythmia prevention and suppression indeed be the dictating factor that influences the type of Fontan operation undertaken? Each technique, the extra-cardiac conduit or lateral tunnel Fontan, has its own benefits and disadvantages. In an era where malignant deleterious arrhythmia may be well treated by medications or ablative procedures, should a statistically significant (but questionably clinically significant) increased risk of arrhythmia be so influential as to be the major determinant in driving choice between an extracardiac conduit and lateral tunnel? On theoretical grounds, there may be several benefits to the lateral tunnel that outweigh increased arrhythmia risk. Changes in practice style in our field can be slow, but many centers are now taking another look at the lateral tunnel as an attractive option for Fontan surgery.

Ultimately, it is not arrhythmia per se, but its impact that is of utmost importance. Exploring associations between specific rhythm patterns and other vulnerable systems in a Fontan circulation is worthwhile. Our recently discovered relationship between heart rate response during exercise and mental health challenges is one such example worthy of further investigation.[8]

Realizing that there is no one definitively superior strategy over another, one should thoughtfully consider not just the price of a particular strategy when making decisions, but more importantly, its’ value-with clinical “value” defined as looking at all possible measures of product quality per unit price. Yes, arrhythmia is one price we must pay, but consideration of overall value is key in deciding which pathway to choose. At the moment, we may not have all the information necessary to make the proper decision as to which strategy is superior long-term: extracardiac conduit or lateral tunnel. The only way to figure this out and improve our selection process is to collect as much data from as many individuals as we can and identify factors that influence trajectory and outcome variability. Registries such as the Single Ventricle Outcomes Network, are growing in importance and are essential to providing answers to these critical questions.[9] It is only through more data collection that we can hope to answer these and other essential questions.
