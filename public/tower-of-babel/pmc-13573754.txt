Central role of civil society in advancing sexual and reproductive health and rights
Authors: Jaime M Gher, Dana Zhang, Nana Abuelsoud, Naureen Shameem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protecting meaningful participation, feminist leadership, and rights based governance amid growing political and financial pressures is essential to sustain gains in sexual and reproductive health, write Jaime M Gher and colleagues

Many of the advances made in sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) and justice were not simply granted by states or multilateral institutions. They were built through decades of feminist organising, advocacy, knowledge production, and movement building. Feminist civil society (box 1) helped secure the recognition of sexual and reproductive health as human rights, brought the experiences of affected communities into policymaking spaces, and created mechanisms through which states and institutions could be held accountable for their commitments.

These gains are now under growing pressure. Rising authoritarianism, declining confidence in multilateralism, and weakening political commitments to human rights and international cooperation are reshaping the global environment in which SRHR and justice are advanced and protected. While the United Nations (UN) has long provided a critical space for civil society and marginalised communities to promote human rights norms and support accountability, the institution is facing a crisis of confidence and credibility.1 Combined with a $2.4bn funding shortfall,2 this has led to calls for drastic reform to ensure that the UN is “agile, integrated, and better equipped to respond to contemporary global challenges.”3

As the UN system reconfigures, largely through closed door discussions, concerns are mounting that core programming around human rights, gender equality, and SRHR and justice may be deprioritised.4 The UN80 initiative, a system-wide reform process reviewing the UN’s structures, mandates, and use of resources, has included an assessment of a possible merger between UN Women and UN Population Fund (UNFPA).5 Women’s rights and SRHR advocates have warned that such a merger could weaken the visibility and institutional protection of both mandates.6 These developments coincide with the rise of anti-rights movements that manipulate concepts of gender and SRHR to build support for anti-democratic practices, frame feminist campaigners and LGBTQ+ people as threats to national identity and “traditional values,” impose rigid hierarchies, and justify state violence.5 7 8

The exclusion of feminist civil society from decisions about this changing system is therefore not only a question of representation. It removes an important source of accountability. Feminist movements, grassroots organisations, and affected communities help identify gaps between institutional commitments and lived realities, expose rights violations, and press states and multilateral institutions to respond. When their participation is reduced or delayed until proposals have already been developed, reform processes lose knowledge, legitimacy, and mechanisms for identifying harm.

We are at an inflection point. As geopolitical conditions are shifting and progress on gender equality and SRHR and justice comes under threat, investment in and meaningful participation by feminist civil society must be strengthened. While principles of participation and accountability may appear to be losing ground, that can only happen if that ground is ceded. This article, part of a collection of articles on the impacts of shifts in the multilateral system on SRHR and justice, highlights feminist civil society’s pivotal role in advancing SRHR gains, challenging negative and exclusionary shifts within multilateral institutions, and amplifying the voices of those most affected. It argues that meaningful participation is part of the accountability infrastructure needed to protect these gains and calls for multilateral institutions to be reformed with feminist civil society, not around it.

Role of feminist civil society in SRHR

Feminist civil society has played a leading role in constructing and reinforcing the multilateral human rights system, particularly as it affects gender equality. It has complemented government efforts, amplified the voices of individuals and communities, and provided accounts of lived realities—all key components of effective governance, constructive human rights negotiation, and sustainable development.

Sustained feminist mobilisation around the 1994 International Conference on Population and Development and the 1995 Beijing Platform for Action helped secure recognition of SRHR as human rights and strengthen accountability for rights violations.5 Years of organising around these processes also consolidated lasting relationships, cross regional coalitions, and links between local and global advocacy that continued beyond the conferences themselves. Feminist civil society has also influenced some donor priorities, encouraging more equitable and care centred approaches to supporting rights work.

As normative and structural advancements were achieved, however, anti-rights groups have made sustained efforts to delegitimise civil society organisations while simultaneously co-opting their strategies.29 There has also been an increase in efforts and practices that restrict progressive civil society’s participation in key decision making and standard setting.9 This is visible in the proposed merger of UNFPA and UN Women. Civil society representatives reported that, although some update meetings had taken place, there had been no formal consultation that allowed them to influence the assessment before its findings were drafted.10

During the covid-19 pandemic, the shift to virtual and hybrid processes intensified existing barriers to civil society participation, including unequal digital access and fewer informal opportunities to influence negotiations.11 Since then, civil society participation has remained constrained in several UN spaces, including the General Assembly’s high level week, the High Level Political Forum, Commission on the Status of Women, UN Climate Change Conference, and Internet Governance Forum.9

As civil society is increasingly excluded from or muted within the UN reform process, feminist coalitions are standing up once again. They are now raising concerns about proposals to restructure, merge, or weaken UN entities working to advance SRHR, including UNAIDS and UNFPA, and about the lack of clear commitments to safeguard these critical issues.12 Feminist activists are calling for this process to protect gender equality, human rights, and SRHR mandates and embed meaningful civil society participation throughout the reforms.13

Political and financial pressures are narrowing participation

Feminist civil society efforts to advance SRHR and justice are increasingly shaped by converging political, institutional, and financial pressures. Multilateral systems are facing a profound institutional and financial crisis, raising concerns about the stability of global governance structures that support human rights and public health.5 These pressures are not separate trends: funding cuts, UN reform debates, shrinking civic space, civil society exclusion, and anti-rights mobilisation interact in ways that narrow feminist movements’ ability to influence decisions, monitor commitments, provide community based services, and hold institutions accountable.

In many contexts, shifting geopolitical priorities and shrinking civic space are creating a more constrained environment for rights based advocacy, service provision, and accountability. Far right and authoritarian groups have gained political influence in many regions, placing further pressure on multilateral cooperation and narrowing the space for dissent. In this context, the deprioritisation of gender equality and SRHR agendas is not accidental but closely tied to broader governance shifts. Within the UN system itself, only 36% of entities reporting on system-wide implementation met their 2024 targets for women’s empowerment and gender equality.14

Changes in official development assistance have further intensified these pressures, reducing not only resources for services but also the conditions that enable civil society participation and accountability. Reduction in bilateral aid, the reallocation of earmarked funding for gender equality, and SRHR and justice, and declining contributions to multilateral institutions are reshaping the global development landscape. Overall development assistance is projected to decrease by around $62bn annually by 2026, with aid focused on human rights declining by nearly $2bn.15 At least 12 countries have announced aid reduction in 2024, reflecting shrinking political support for rights based programming alongside increased military expenditures.16

As public funding contracts, private foundations have stepped in to fill some gaps. However, philanthropic support cannot replace predictable public investment in SRHR or the civic infrastructure needed to sustain accountability.17 Reliance on discretionary funding can leave programmes and advocacy vulnerable to shifting donor priorities and political climates.18 When public obligations are replaced by voluntary support, the financing of public health and human rights risks becoming less stable, less accountable, and more uneven.

The consequences are already visible. Recent cuts to US development assistance, including the 2025 dismantling of major USAID funded programmes and reductions affecting the President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), have disrupted HIV prevention and treatment services in countries such as Zambia.19 The cuts closed 32 centres providing HIV testing, treatment, and support to more than 20 000 people from key populations, alongside programmes serving adolescent girls and young women. They also disrupted access to pre-exposure prophylaxis and community led monitoring.20

More broadly, the loss of US global family planning funding is projected to deny 47.6 million women and couples in low and middle income countries access to modern contraceptives, resulting in an estimated 17.1 million unintended pregnancies and 34 000 preventable pregnancy related deaths.21 These cuts do more than reduce services. By disrupting community based programmes and community led monitoring, they also weaken the systems through which gaps in access are identified and failures in implementation are brought to public attention.

Alongside these disruptions to services and monitoring, feminist civil society organisations working on SRHR and LGBTQ+ rights face distinct financial, regulatory, and political pressures. Many operate with limited core funding, leaving them vulnerable to sudden donor exits or shifts in political priorities.22 In several contexts, crossborder funding has come under increased scrutiny through “foreign agent” laws and related measures that restrict international solidarity and narrow the space for rights based advocacy.23 These pressures are intensified by major resource imbalances: in 2023, the combined income of three global anti-rights organisations exceeded $500m, more than twice the total funding received by 1174 feminist and women’s rights organisations surveyed across 128 countries.24

These same political and financial pressures shape debates about the future of multilateral governance. In a context of resource scarcity, reform proposals framed around efficiency or consolidation may have uneven consequences for politically contested areas such as SRHR and justice. Proposals to restructure or merge agencies such as UNFPA into broader institutional frameworks risk diluting the visibility of SRHR mandates and weakening states’ commitments to these agendas. 25 26 For feminist civil society, less visible mandates can mean fewer opportunities to shape programme priorities, identify implementation gaps, monitor state commitments, and seek accountability when services and protections are not delivered.

Yet feminist civil society continues to adapt and mobilise across multiple levels, reinforcing its critical role in resisting regression. As access to global governance negotiations becomes more constrained, some feminist groups have shifted their strategies towards national and regional arenas while continuing to draw on international human rights frameworks. Recent high court decisions in Nigeria and Malawi, for example, affirmed access to safe and legal abortion for women who become pregnant after sexual violence. 27 28 Civil society advocacy helped translate international human rights standards into domestic legal protections that can be used to challenge denials of care and press health systems to provide services permitted under national law.

Regional networks also provide spaces for coordination and mutual support. In Latin America, transnational mobilisation around abortion rights has enabled activists to share legal strategies and coordinate responses to renewed political pressure, including recent challenges before Brazil’s Supreme Court.29 Such coordination can strengthen legal interventions, defend existing protections, and help prevent political or judicial challenges from restricting access to abortion care.

These developments show the resilience and adaptive capacity of feminist civil society. At the same time, many efforts rely on limited resources, informal coordination, and a small number of overstretched organisations. Without sustained multilateral commitments and reliable financing for SRHR and justice, national and regional gains remain uneven and vulnerable to reversal. Meaningful civil society participation in multilateral reform is therefore not only procedural; it is part of the accountability infrastructure needed to keep SRHR visible in programme design, identify failures in implementation, and ensure that global commitments result in accessible services and enforceable protections.

Reforming multilateralism with feminist civil society

Multilateralism is facing both profound risk and a critical inflection point. Amid rising anti-rights mobilisation, states’ political commitments to human rights norms and multilateral cooperation are under significant strain, while feminist civil society participation in global governance is increasingly restricted. For feminist movements and women and LGBTQ+ communities worldwide, these pressures intersect with broader economic, political, and climate crises that deepen existing inequalities. At the same time, institutional disruption can create opportunities to reconsider how global governance functions and whose knowledge and experience shape it.

The international human rights system has never been confined to the UN. Many important advances in gender equality and SRHR and justice have been driven by feminist civil society working across local, national, and transnational spaces. Through sustained advocacy, litigation, knowledge production, and movement building, feminist groups have helped shape global norms, hold institutions accountable, and translate international commitments into concrete change.

As UN reform discussions continue, several priorities are essential to safeguarding progress on gender equality and SRHR and justice. Reform efforts must reaffirm contemporary human rights standards capable of responding to gender injustice and violations of bodily autonomy, particularly where anti-rights groups are seeking to reverse existing protections. Strengthening accountability mechanisms remains equally critical. Multilateral institutions, working with feminist civil society, must retain the capacity to monitor violations and ensure that states and non-state organisations, including corporations, can be held accountable for harms related to gender discrimination, sexual violence, and restrictions on reproductive and bodily autonomy.

Meaningful participation of feminist civil society must also remain a central principle of global governance, particularly during the current UN reform process. Feminist movements, grassroots organisations, and affected communities bring indispensable knowledge and lived experience to norm building and policymaking processes. Participation should begin before reform proposals are finalised and include timely access to information, opportunities to influence decisions, and clear feedback on how civil society recommendations have been considered. Adequate resources for interpretation, accessibility, travel, and sustained engagement are also necessary to ensure that participation is substantive rather than symbolic. Excluding these groups from decision making spaces not only undermines democratic legitimacy but also weakens the effectiveness of responses to complex global challenges.

Finally, safeguarding SRHR and justice requires stable and sustainable financing. Although philanthropic and feminist funds remain important, long term protection of SRHR depends on sustained public investment and political commitment. Funding should support not only short term participation in global forums, but also the national mobilisation, movement building, community based monitoring, and accountability work that make meaningful influence possible.

The UN was founded in the aftermath of an era marked by widespread violations of human dignity and global conflict. To ensure that reform responds to today’s human rights concerns, we recommend a movement informed process of reimagining global governance grounded in protection, accountability, anti-discrimination, and bodily autonomy. As the examples discussed here show, feminist civil society has already begun building elements of this future through regional and cross regional collectives, feminist funds, donor collaboratives, and mechanisms for monitoring commitments, documenting violations, and demanding accountability. This critical effort sustains human rights work when formal systems stagnate. Reform should build on these emerging practices and recognise feminist civil society as central to sustaining and advancing human rights.
