Freezeout at constant energy density and spin polarization in heavy-ion collisions
Andrea Palermo, Masoud Shokri

Abstract
Using the local equilibrium density operator, we develop a geometry-informed linear response theory that takes into account the parameterization of the freezeout hypersurface before the gradient expansion is carried out. Assuming local equilibrium on an iso-energy density hypersurface, we study the Λ \Lambda hyperon spin polarization, and compute corrections to the isothermal case due to finite density. We argue that corrections to isothermal freezeout should be small, and that even in heavy ion collisions with energy as low as s N ​ N = 11.5 \sqrt{s_{NN}}=11.5 GeV they constitute at most a 10 % 10\% effect. They may, however, become relevant for local polarization observables.



I
Introduction

The quark-gluon plasma (QGP) is an almost perfect relativistic fluid created in high-energy heavy-ion collisions.
In non-central collisions, part of the angular momentum of the QGP—inherited from the initial colliding system—is transferred to the spin of final-state hadrons
Liang and Wang 2005
;
Liang 2007
;
Gao et al. 2008
;
Becattini et al. 2008
.
This phenomenon can be probed by various experimental observables, such as the polarization of
\Lambda
particles and the spin alignment of vector mesons
Becattini et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
.

\Lambda
polarization was first observed by the STAR experiment
Adamczyk et al. 2017a
, and since then, numerous other measurements have followed
Adam et al. 2019
;
Abdulhamid et al. 2023a
;
Acharya et al. 2022
;
Abou Yassine et al. 2022
;
Abdallah et al. 2021
;
Adam et al. 2018
;
Adamczyk et al. 2017a
;
Abdulhamid et al. 2023b
;
Acharya et al. 2020
.
These data span a wide range of collision energies, even showing evidence of spin polarization in pPb collisions
Hayrapetyan et al. 2025
.

A viable theoretical description of
\Lambda
polarization is provided by quantum relativistic statistical mechanics through the so-called local equilibrium density operator
Zubarev et al. 1979
;
van Weert 1982
;
Becattini et al. 2019
, which we adopt in this work.
Alternative approaches to
\Lambda
polarization include quantum field theory, kinetic theory, and spin hydrodynamics
Liu and Yin 2021
;
Fu et al. 2021
;
Weickgenannt et al. 2021
;
Wagner 2025
;
Sapna et al. 2025
;
Singh et al. 2025
.
Notably, recent developments in semi-classical spin hydrodynamics suggest a rapid equilibration of spin degrees of freedom in moderate to high-energy collisions, supporting the applicability of the local equilibrium framework
Wagner et al. 2024
;
Chiarini et al. 2024
.

In Refs.
Becattini et al. 2021a
;
Becattini et al. 2021b
, the inclusion of shear-induced polarization was shown to restore agreement between local polarization data and theoretical expectations, relying on the additional assumption that the freezeout hypersurface, where the fluid-dynamic description ceases to apply, is isothermal.
While this assumption is well justified in very high-energy heavy-ion collisions, where chemical potentials are negligible and temperature is the only relevant intensive thermodynamic variable, it becomes questionable at lower energies.
In such cases, one or more chemical potentials (e.g., the baryon chemical potential) are non-negligible at freezeout, and must be taken into account.

In this work, we develop a linear response framework that incorporates the geometry of the freezeout hypersurface. We focus on the case where freezeout occurs at constant energy density, which is common practice. Nonetheless, our method is readily generalizable to other freezeout conditions. The constant-energy-density assumption introduces thermodynamic constraints that relate variations of temperature and chemical potentials along the freezeout hypersurface. These relations can be exploited to compute gradient corrections to the mean spin vector of particles. We derive modified expressions for the
\Lambda
polarization that reflect these geometric-thermodynamic constraints, and show that they reduce to the isothermal freezeout formulae of Ref.
Becattini et al. 2021b
when the equation of state is independent of chemical potential. We assess the magnitude of these geometric corrections using two different equations of state and find that they are modest—at most
\sim 10\%
—in low-energy collisions.
A full hydrodynamic implementation will be pursued in future work.

This paper is organized as follows.
After a brief review of the spin polarization calculation using the local equilibrium density operation in Sec.
II
, we introduce our geometric approach in Sec.
III
and apply it to compute the spin vector of
\Lambda
particles within linear response theory.
In Sec.
IV
, we evaluate the geometric corrections coefficient for two different equations of state, as a function of temperature and chemical potential.
Finally, Sec.
V
summarizes our findings.
Supporting material is provided in the Appendix
A
.

II
Brief review of polarization at local equilibrium in linear response theory

In this section, we summarize the main steps in deriving the mean spin vector as carried out in Refs.
Becattini et al. 2021a
;
Becattini et al. 2021b
.
The mean spin vector for a spin-
1/2
field is given by the following formula (c.f. Eq. (71) of Ref.
Becattini 2021
):

S^{\mu}(p)=\frac{\int_{\Sigma_{D}}\differential{\Sigma}\cdot p\tr\left[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}W_{+}(x,p)\right]}{\int_{\Sigma_{D}}\differential{\Sigma}\cdot p\tr\left[W_{+}(x,p)\right]}\,,

(1)

where
\differential{\Sigma}_{\mu}
is the surface element on the freezeout hypersurface
\Sigma_{D}
.
The function
W_{+}
represents the particle component of the Wigner function, defined as
De Groot 1980

W_{+}(x,k)=-\frac{\theta(k^{2})\theta(k^{0})}{(2\pi)^{4}}\int{\rm d}^{4}ye^{-ik\cdot y}\langle:\Psi(x-y/2)\bar{\Psi}(x+y/2):\rangle\,.

(2)

It corresponds to the expectation value of the Wigner operator
\widehat{W}_{+}(x,k)
,

W_{+}(x,k)=\langle\widehat{W}_{+}(x,k)\rangle=\Tr\left(\widehat{\rho}\,\widehat{W}_{+}(x,k)\right)\,,

(3)

where
\widehat{\rho}
is the density operator.
If the density operator is known, the Wigner function is then unambiguously determined. However, obtaining it is challenging in practice.

In
global equilibrium
, the exact calculation of the Wigner function is possible using the analytic distillation technique
Becattini et al. 2021c
;
Palermo et al. 2021
;
Palermo and Becattini 2023
; global equilibrium in a relativistic quantum system is achieved when the density operator takes the form

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}=\frac{1}{Z}\exp(-b\cdot\wP+\frac{1}{2}\varpi:\wJ+\zeta\wQ),

(4)

where
\zeta
,
b_{\mu}
and
\varpi_{\mu\nu}
are constant scalar, four-vector and antisymmetric tensor Lagrange multipliers, respectively, and
\widehat{P}_{\mu}
,
\widehat{J}_{\mu\nu}
, and
\widehat{Q}
are the four-momentum, boost-angular momentum and scalar charge (e.g., electric charge) operators.
In Eq. (
4
),
Z
is the partition function given by
Z=\Tr[\exp(-b\cdot\wP+\frac{1}{2}\varpi:\wJ+\zeta\wQ)]
, such that
\Tr(\wrho)=1
.
A nonvanishing thermal vorticity tensor
\varpi_{\mu\nu}
causes the four-temperature vector
\beta_{\mu}
to be space-time dependent, given by

\displaystyle\beta_{\mu}=b_{\mu}+\varpi_{\mu\nu}x^{\nu}\;,

(5)

which satisfies the Killing condition
\partial_{(\mu}\beta_{\nu)}=0
.
The scalar temperature is defined as
T=1/\sqrt{\beta^{2}}
, whence the four-velocity of the fluid is
u^{\mu}=T\beta^{\mu}
.
The spacetime dependence of
\beta_{\mu}
implies that
u^{\mu}
corresponds to a rigidly rotating and/or accelerating flow
Shokri and Rischke 2023
.
The scalar
\zeta
is the ratio between the chemical potential
\mu
and the scalar temperature, i.e.,
\zeta=\mu/T
.

However, in heavy-ion collisions, the assumption of global equilibrium is unrealistic.
A better description of heavy-ion collisions can be achieved by assuming that the final hadrons, such as
\Lambda
hyperons, are produced in a state of
local thermodynamic equilibrium
,
where each fluid cell can be approximated as an infinitesimally small thermodynamic system.
Consequently, applying the maximum entropy principle locally to each fluid cell yields the following expression for the density operator
Zubarev et al. 1979
;
van Weert 1982
;
Becattini et al. 2019
:

\widehat{\rho}=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left\{-\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}(y)\left[\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}(y)\beta_{\nu}(y)-\widehat{j}^{\mu}(y)\zeta(y)\right]\right\}\,,

(6)

where
\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}
and
\widehat{j}^{\mu}
are the energy-momentum and charge density current operators, respectively.
Regarding the Lagrange multipliers,
\zeta
is not a constant, but a locally defined scalar field, and
\beta_{\mu}
is no longer a Killing vector.
The density operator (
6
) involves an integration over the freezeout hypersurface
\Sigma_{D}
.
Consequently, when computing the mean spin with equation (
1
), the integral on this surface appears twice, a point that will become important later.
Note that we adopt the Belinfante pseudogauge, where the energy-momentum tensor is symmetric and the spin tensor vanishes.

To calculate the mean spin vector using the local equilibrium density operator, we first insert Eq. (
6
) into Eq. (
3
) to obtain,

W^{(LE)}_{+}(x,k)=\frac{1}{Z}\Tr\left\{\widehat{W}_{+}(x,k)\,\exp\left[-\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}(y)\left(\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}(y)\beta_{\nu}(y)-\widehat{j}^{\mu}(y)\zeta(y)\right)\right]\right\}\,.

(7)

This expression can be recast as

W^{(LE)}_{+}(x,k)=\frac{1}{Z}\Tr\left\{\widehat{W}_{+}(x,k)\,\exp\left[-\beta(x)\cdot\widehat{P}+\zeta(x)\widehat{Q}-\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}(y)\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}(y)\delta\beta_{\nu}(x,y)-\widehat{j}^{\mu}(y)\delta\zeta(x,y)\right]\right\}\,,

(8)

where
\delta\beta
and
\delta\zeta
represent the
exact
variations of the thermodynamic field between the points
x
and
y
,

\delta\beta_{\nu}(x,y)=\beta(y)-\beta(x)\,,\qquad\qquad\delta\zeta(x,y)=\zeta(y)-\zeta(x)\,.

(9)

In Eq. (
8
), we used the definitions of the total four-momentum and charge operators

\widehat{P}^{\mu}=\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}\,,\qquad\widehat{Q}=\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}(y)\widehat{j}^{\mu}\,.

We now assume that the terms involving the variations of the fields in Eq. (
9
) contribute to the Wigner function as small corrections compared to the leading terms involving the fields themselves.
Namely, we expand the exponential in Eq. (
8
) within the framework of linear response theory; the details of this procedure can be found, for example, in Refs.
Becattini et al. 2019
;
Becattini et al. 2021a
.
With this approach, the local equilibrium Wigner function (
8
) can be approximated as:

\displaystyle W_{LE}(x,k)

\displaystyle\simeq\langle\widehat{W}(x,k)\rangle_{\beta(x),\zeta(x)}-\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\mu}(y)\left[\delta\beta_{\nu}(x,y)\langle\widehat{W}(x,k)\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}(y+iz\beta(x))\rangle^{c}_{\beta(x),\zeta(x)}\right.

(10)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.\delta\zeta(x,y)\langle\widehat{W}(x,k)\widehat{j}^{\mu}(y+iz\beta(x))\rangle^{c}_{\beta(x),\zeta(x)}\right]\,.

Here,

\expectationvalue{\widehat{W}(x,k)\widehat{X}}_{\beta(x),\zeta(x)}\equiv\frac{1}{Z}\Tr\left[\widehat{W}(x,k)\widehat{X}\,\exp(-\beta(x)\cdot\wP+\zeta(x)\wQ)\right]\,,

(11)

where
\widehat{X}\in\{\widehat{1},\widehat{T}^{\mu\nu}(y),\widehat{j}^{\mu}(y)\}\,
, and the superscript
c
denotes the connected two-point function:

\langle\widehat{O}\widehat{X}\rangle^{c}=\langle\widehat{O}\widehat{X}\rangle-\langle\widehat{O}\rangle\langle\widehat{X}\rangle\;.

This procedure can be used to calculate the leading-order corrections (in the field variations eq. (
9
)) to the Wigner function, and expectation values can be computed therefrom. These steps are general and hold for the Wigner function of any field.
From now on, we will focus on the calculation of the mean spin of Dirac fermions.

Noting that the leading term in Eq. (
10
) does not contribute to the numerator of Eq. (
1
), we insert Eq. (
10
) into Eq. (
1
) to obtain the mean spin vector at local equilibrium:

\displaystyle S^{\mu}(p)=

\displaystyle-\frac{\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma(x)\cdot p\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\rho}(y)\delta\beta_{\nu}(x,y)\tr\left(\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\langle\widehat{W}(x,k)\widehat{T}^{\rho\nu}(y+iz\beta(x))\rangle^{c}_{\beta(x),\zeta(x)}\right)}{\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma\cdot p\tr\left(\langle\widehat{W}(x)\rangle_{\beta(x),\zeta(x)}\right)}\,

\displaystyle+\frac{\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma(x)\cdot p\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma_{\rho}(y)\delta\zeta(x,y)\tr\left(\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\langle\widehat{W}(x,k)\widehat{j}^{\rho}(y+iz\beta(x))\rangle_{\beta(x),\zeta(x)}^{c}\right)}{\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma\cdot p\tr\left(\langle\widehat{W}(x)\rangle_{\beta(x),\zeta(x)}\right)}\,.

(12)

Up to now, the variations
\delta\beta_{\nu}
and
\delta\zeta
are exact variations, but it is not possible to proceed further without approximating them in some way. For instance, as it is usually done, we can Taylor-expand the components of
\beta_{\mu}(y)
and
\zeta(y)
around
x
, which is the point where the Wigner function is evaluated:

\displaystyle\delta\beta_{\nu}\approx(y-x)^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\beta_{\nu}\,,\qquad\qquad\delta\zeta\approx(y-x)^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\zeta\,.

This is justified, because the two point functions
\langle\widehat{W}(x,k)\widehat{X}(y+i\beta(x))\rangle^{c}_{\beta(x),\zeta(x)}
vanish for large space separations
(x-y)
, and in the hydrodynamic regime
\delta\beta
and
\delta\zeta
are small on the length scale where the two point function is non-zero.
Upon using this approximation, one finds
Becattini et al. 2021a
;
Buzzegoli 2023

S^{\mu}(p)=S_{A}^{\mu}(p)+S_{S}^{\mu}(p)+S_{\zeta}^{\mu}(p)\,,

(13)

where the antisymmetric, symmetric, and spin-Hall contributions, are labeled as

\displaystyle S_{A}^{\mu}(p)=-\frac{1}{8m\,\mathcal{N}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\sigma}{\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma\cdot p\;\varpi_{\nu\rho}n_{F}(p)(1-n_{F}(p))}\,,

(14a)

(14c)

Here,
n_{F}=\left[e^{(p\cdot u-\mu)/T}+1\right]^{-1}
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution,
\hat{t}_{\nu}=\left(1,\mathbf{0}\right)
in Cartesian coordinates,
p^{0}=p\cdot\hat{t}
, and we have defined

\mathcal{N}\equiv\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma\cdot p\,n_{F}(p)\,.

(15)

While general, the derivation of Eq. (
14
) may not be the most accurate in specific cases.
Indeed, in Ref.
Becattini et al. 2021b
it was observed that a more precise calculation of the mean spin vector in high-energy collisions can be achieved by noting that the freezeout hypersurface is approximately isothermal, i.e., the temperature at all points on
\Sigma_{D}
satisfies
T(x)=T_{D}\,
, where
T_{D}
is constant.

Consequently, using the definitions of the Lagrange multipliers
\beta_{\mu}
and
\zeta
, we find that the exact variations are

\displaystyle\delta\beta_{\nu}=\beta_{\nu}(y)-\beta_{\nu}(x)=\frac{1}{T_{D}}(u_{\nu}(y)-u_{\nu}(x))=\frac{\delta u_{\nu}}{T_{D}}\,,

\displaystyle\delta\zeta=\zeta(y)-\zeta(x)=\frac{1}{T_{D}}(\mu(y)-\mu(x))=\frac{\delta\mu}{T_{D}}\,,

where
\delta u^{\nu}
and
\delta\mu
are the exact variations of four-velocity and chemical potential.
Therefore, to proceed further, we approximate only the latter variations using a first-order Taylor expansion, but keep the temperature constant:

\displaystyle\delta\beta_{\nu}\approx(y-x)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial_{\alpha}u_{\nu}}{T_{D}}\,,\qquad\qquad\delta\zeta\approx(y-x)^{\alpha}\frac{\partial_{\alpha}\mu}{T_{D}}\,.

(16)

Substituting these expressions into Eq. (
II
) yields the results of Ref.
Becattini et al. 2021b
, expressed in terms of kinematic vorticity
\omega
and kinematic shear
\Xi
.
For the chemical potential sector, the result agrees with those of Refs.

\partial\zeta

\displaystyle S_{A}^{\mu}(p)=-\frac{1}{8m\,\mathcal{N}T_{D}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\sigma}\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma\cdot p\;\omega_{\nu\rho}n_{F}(p)(1-n_{F}(p))\,,

(17a)

\displaystyle S_{S}^{\mu}(p)=-\frac{1}{4m\,\mathcal{N}T_{D}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\sigma}\frac{p^{\lambda}}{p^{0}}\hat{t}_{\nu}\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma\cdot p\;\Xi_{\rho\lambda}n_{F}(p)(1-n_{F}(p))\,,

(17b)

\displaystyle S^{\mu}_{\zeta}(p)=+\frac{1}{4m\,\mathcal{N}T_{D}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}p_{\sigma}\frac{\hat{t}_{\nu}}{p^{0}}\int_{\Sigma_{D}}{\rm d}\Sigma\cdot p\;\partial_{\rho}\mu\;n_{F}(p)(1-n_{F}(p))\,.

(17c)

We emphasize that temperature gradients have not been neglected during the calculations; substitutions (
16

While the components of the temperature gradient tangent to the isothermal surface vanish, the normal component does not; it defines the vector normal to the surface via
n_{\mu}\propto\partial_{\mu}T

Equation (
17

However, the assumption of an isothermal freezeout hypersurface applies only to high-energy heavy-ion collisions, and it is not a reasonable approximation at more moderate collision energies.
In practice, the freezeout hypersurface is often defined as the set of fluid cells with a constant energy density.
In high-energy collisions, the chemical potential at freezeout is negligible, making the iso-energy density and isothermal hypersurfaces effectively equivalent to a very good precision.
This approximation breaks down when the chemical potential becomes comparable to the temperature, which occurs at collision energies below
\sqrt{s_{NN}}\lesssim 39
GeV (see, e.g., ref
Adamczyk et al. 2017b
).
Consequently, Eq. (
17
) is no longer a reliable approximation for the spin vector of
\Lambda
particles at those energies and must be reconsidered.
In the next section, we introduce an approach to systematically address this problem.

III
Geometric Approximations on the Freezeout Hypersurface

In this section, we develop a scheme to more accurately account for the variations of thermodynamic quantities on the freezeout hypersurface
\Sigma_{D}
, which is defined as the iso-energy density level surface:

e(x)=e_{0}\,,

(18)

where
e_{0}
is a constant representing the critical energy density.
The gradient of the function
e(x)
determines the normal vector
n_{\mu}
to
\Sigma_{D}
.
One may introduce a set of coordinates
\lambda^{a}
on
\Sigma_{D}
, with
a=1,2,3
, such that the tangent vectors to
\Sigma_{D}
are given by

\mathrm{e}^{\mu}_{a}=\partialderivative{x^\mu}{\lambda^a}\;,

(19)

satisfying

\mathrm{e}^{\mu}_{a}\partial_{\mu}e=0\;.

(20)

The energy density is given, via the equation of state, as a function of
T
and
\mu
(assuming a single conserved charge).
Thus, the variation of the energy density can be expressed as

\delta e=\left.\frac{\partial e}{\partial T}\right|_{\mu}\delta T+\left.\frac{\partial e}{\partial\mu}\right|_{T}\delta\mu\,,

(21)

where the subscript indicates the thermodynamic variable held constant in each partial derivative.
On the hypersurface
\Sigma_{D}
, Eq. (
20
) implies
\delta e=0
, and therefore

\left.\frac{\partial e}{\partial T}\right|_{\mu}\delta T=-\left.\frac{\partial e}{\partial\mu}\right|_{T}\delta\mu\,.

(22)

This equation, which relates tangent variations of temperature and chemical potential, holds strictly on the hypersurface.
In principle, if the hypersurface coordinates
\lambda^{a}
are known, one can Taylor-expand the quantities in the integrand in Eq. (
6
) in
\lambda^{a}
, thereby treating only the
hypersurface variations
.
However, this is not feasible in hydrodynamic simulations, where
\lambda^{a}
and thus the tangent derivatives of
T
and
\mu
are not explicitly available.
Therefore, in practice, one resorts to using
spacetime variations
, i.e., variations computing Taylor expanding in with respect to the Minkowski coordinates
x^{\mu}
rather than the hypersurface coordinates
\lambda^{a}
.
This necessarily introduces an approximation, as the spacetime and hypersurface variations can differ substantially.

To see how using Eq. (
22
) mitigates this problem, we return to the case of high-energy heavy-ion collisions, where the chemical potential is negligible.
Suppose the simulation employs an equation of state depending on both temperature and chemical potential, and the freezeout hypersurface is identified with equation (
18
).
Since we are considering a very high-energy heavy-ion collision, the dependence of the energy density on chemical potential along the freezeout hypersurface is negligible.
As a result, Eq. (
22
) yields
\delta T\simeq 0
.
This shows that an error would have been introduced had we approximated the hypersurface variation with the spacetime variation without employing Eq. (
22
).
The error is of order
\order{\partial T}
, and since the polarization vector is itself of order
\order{\partial}
, neglecting Eq. (
22
) introduces a significant relative error.
On the other hand, the hypersurface variation of the chemical potential
\delta\mu
remains unconstrained, so the best approximation at our disposal, and the most natural one, is to replace it with its spacetime variation:
\delta\mu\simeq(y-x)^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\mu
.

This example illustrates that, although hypersurface and spacetime variations are not generally equivalent, the hypersurface variation of either temperature or chemical potential can still be approximated by its spacetime variation.
The choice of which hypersurface variation to approximate with the corresponding spacetime variation should be guided by the geometry of the hypersurface and the underlying physical conditions.

III.1
Spacetime versus hypersurface variations

We now return to the general case, where the chemical potential is not negligible.
At an arbitrary point on the hypersurface, where neither
\left.\partial e/\partial T\right|_{\mu}
nor
\left.\partial e/\partial\mu\right|_{T}
vanish, Eq. (
22
) can be rewritten as

\delta T=-\mathcal{C}\delta\mu\,.

(23)

Here, we have defined the following thermodynamic coefficient:

\mathcal{C}=\frac{\left.\partial e/\partial\mu\right|_{T}}{\left.\partial e/\partial T\right|_{\mu}}\,,

(24)

which can also be expressed in terms of other thermodynamic coefficients, as shown in Appendix
A
.

We point out that, in our previous illustrative example of high-energy collisions,
\absolutevalue{\mathcal{C}}\ll 1
, and consequently
\absolutevalue{\delta T}\ll\absolutevalue{\delta\mu}
on
\Sigma_{D}
.
This suggests that the magnitude of
\mathcal{C}
determines which variation is larger on the hypersurface.
It can therefore serve as a guiding criterion: the larger of the two variations is approximated by its spacetime counterpart via a Taylor expansion, while the smaller is determined using Eq. (
23
).

To clarify this criterion, let us consider the case
|\mathcal{C}|<1
, which implies that on
\Sigma_{D}

|\delta T|<|\delta\mu|\,.

(25)

Following our guiding principle, we approximate
\delta\mu
by its spacetime variation and write

\delta T=-\mathcal{C}(y-x)^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\mu\,.

(26)

In the hydrodynamic regime, where
\partial T
and
\partial\mu
are of comparable magnitude, this expression ensures that the approximation error for
\delta T
is suppressed by
\mathcal{C}

III.3
Generalization to multiple chemical potentials

We conclude this section by briefly addressing the case of multiple conserved charges, each associated with a distinct chemical potential
\mu_{i}
.
In this case, the variations on an iso-energy density hypersurface are related by

\left.\frac{\partial e}{\partial T}\right|_{\mu_{i}}\delta T+\sum_{i}\left.\frac{\partial e}{\partial\mu_{i}}\right|_{T,\mu_{j\neq i}}\delta\mu_{i}=0\,.

(35)

To generalize our prescription, we identify the intensive variable
X\in\{T,\mu_{i}\}
for which the magnitude
\absolutevalue{\partial e/\partial X}
is largest among all elements in the set, and correspondingly
\delta X
is the smallest variation. Accordingly, the hypersurface variations
\delta Y_{i}
of the remaining intensive variables
Y_{i}\neq X
are approximated using their spacetime counterparts, and the variation
\delta X
is then determined in terms of
\delta Y_{i}
from Eq. (
35

For example, if

\left|\left(\frac{\partial e}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu_{i}}\right|<\left|\left(\frac{\partial e}{\partial\mu_{i}}\right)_{T,\mu_{j\neq i}}\right|\,,

(36)

then temperature is treated as the dependent variable, and its variation is expressed as

\delta T=-\sum_{i}\mathcal{C}_{i}(y-x)^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\mu_{i}\,,\qquad\mathcal{C}_{i}=\frac{\left.\partial e/\partial\mu_{i}\right|_{T,\mu_{j\neq i}}}{\left.\partial e/\partial T\right|_{\mu_{i}}}\,,

(37)

with
\absolutevalue{\mathcal{C}_i}<1
for all

.

If, on the other hand, the largest derivative of the energy is the one with respect to some particular

\delta\mu_{j}

\delta\mu_{j}=-\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\left.\partial e/\partial\mu_{i}\right|_{T,\mu\neq\mu_{i}}}{\left.\partial e/\partial\mu_{j}\right|_{T,\mu\neq\mu_{j}}}(y-x)^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\mu_{i}-\frac{\left.\partial e/\partial T\right|_{\mu}}{\left.\partial e/\partial\mu_{j}\right|_{T,\mu\neq\mu_{j}}}(y-x)^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}T\,.

Our results are sensitive to both the choice of equation of state and the modeling of the freezeout hypersurface. In this section, we evaluate

Owing to the smallness of

V
Conclusions

In this work, we developed a geometric-thermodynamic framework that generalizes the isothermal freezeout prescription used in Ref.
Becattini et al. 2021b
to describe local polarization in heavy-ion collisions.
Our approach is based on the assumption that freezeout occurs on an iso-energy density hypersurface, and it naturally reduces to the isothermal case when the energy density depends only on temperature.
We derived a set of polarization formulae that account for geometric corrections to the standard prescription, characterized by the thermodynamic coefficient
\mathcal{C}
.

By evaluating
\mathcal{C}
for two different equations of state, we found that it remains small—typically below 10%—even at low collision energies.
This suggests that the isothermal freezeout approximation is robust across a broad range of energies.
Nonetheless, the corrections introduced by our generalized prescription may still play a non-negligible role in refining predictions of local polarization observables, particularly in low-energy collisions or in scenarios involving a more complex equation of state, such as one featuring a critical point.

Appendix A
Value of
\mathcal{C}
in terms of other thermodynamic coefficients and functions

The coefficient
\mathcal{C}
introduced in the text has been defined as:

\mathcal{C}=\frac{\left.\partial e/\partial\mu\right|_{T}}{\left.\partial e/\partial T\right|_{\mu}}.

(40)

The derivatives involved in this ratio can be expressed in terms of other thermodynamic coefficients.
In particular, we express
\mathcal{C}
in terms of the isothermal compressibility
\kappa_{T}
, the expansion coefficient
\alpha
, and heat capacities at constant volume and pressure,
c_{V}
and
c_{P}
.
The definitions of these coefficients are (see, for instance,
Floerchinger and Martinez 2015
):

\displaystyle\kappa_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial P}\right)_{T},\qquad\qquad\alpha=-\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{P},

(41a)

\displaystyle c_{V}

\displaystyle=T\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}\right)_{n},\qquad\qquad c_{P}=\frac{Tn}{s}\left(\frac{\partial s/n}{\partial T}\right)_{P}=\frac{T}{s}\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}\right)_{P}+T\alpha.

(41b)

We now recall several thermodynamic identities: the Euler relation, which follows from extensivity; the first law of thermodynamics; and the Gibbs-Duhem relation.
These read:

\displaystyle e+P

\displaystyle=Ts+\mu n\,,

(42a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}e

\displaystyle=T{\rm d}s+\mu{\rm d}n\,,

(42b)

\displaystyle{\rm d}P

\displaystyle=s{\rm d}T+n{\rm d}\mu\,.

(42c)

Assuming that
s
and
n
are functions of
T
and
\mu
, we can express Eq. (
42b
) as

{\rm d}e=\left[\left(T\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}+\mu\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)\right]_{\mu}{\rm d}T+\left[\left(T\frac{\partial s}{\partial\mu}+\mu\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\right)\right]_{T}{\rm d}\mu\,,

(43)

which allows the identification of
(\partial e/\partial T)_{\mu}
and

(44)

Landau and Lifshitz 1980

\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu}=\frac{\partial(n,\mu)}{\partial(T,\mu)}=\frac{\partial(n,\mu)}{\partial(T,P)}\frac{\partial(T,P)}{\partial(T,\mu)}\,,\qquad\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T}=\frac{\partial(n,T)}{\partial(\mu,T)}=\frac{\partial(n,T)}{\partial(P,T)}\frac{\partial(P,T)}{\partial(\mu,T)}\,,

Using this identity at constant

\left(\partialderivative{s}{T}\right)_{n}=\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu}+\left(\partialderivative{s}{\mu}\right)_{T}\left(\partialderivative{\mu}{T}\right)_{n}\,,\qquad\qquad\left(\partialderivative{s}{T}\right)_{P}=\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu}+\left(\partialderivative{s}{\mu}\right)_{T}\left(\partialderivative{\mu}{T}\right)_{P}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\partialderivative{s}{\mu}\right)_{T}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\partialderivative{\mu}{T}\right)_{n}\left(\partialderivative{s}{T}\right)_{P}+\frac{s}{n}\left(\partialderivative{s}{T}\right)_{n}\right]\left[\left(\partialderivative{\mu}{T}\right)_{n}+\frac{s}{n}\right]^{-1}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T}
