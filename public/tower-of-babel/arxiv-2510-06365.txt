The classification of quasi-elliptic fibrations and unexpected plane cubics in characteristics 2 and 3
Jake Kettinger

Abstract
In this paper, we categorize all isomorphism classes of quasi-elliptic surfaces over a field k k of characteristic 2 or 3. For every quasi-elliptic surface X X , we classify all possible sequences of blow-downs from X X to the projective plane ℙ k 2 \mathbb{P}^{2}_{k} . We then use these categorizations to identify all unexpected plane cubic curves in characteristic 2 and present a proof of the lack of unexpected cubics in characteristic 3. Before the work in this paper– based partly on the author’s thesis– the complete classification of unexpected plane cubic curves in characteristic 2 was unknown, as well as the question of the existence of unexpected plane cubic curves in characteristic 3.

Introduction to quasi-elliptic fibrations

This paper concerns the notion of a quasi-elliptic surface
X
. The main result of this paper is found in Theorem
1

3

\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
for
\text{char}\,k=3

to a plane, up to automorphism of

(-2)
-curves of the Picard group, up to automorphism of

In section 4, we describe the Dynkin diagrams associated to quasi-elliptic surfaces in characteristic 3, and perform the same analysis as in section 2 to classify all possible ways to blow up a plane to get a quasi-elliptic surface, up to automorphism of
X
. Finally, we investigate each possible manner of blowing up
\mathbb{P}^{2}

We will show that every quasi-elliptic fibration

Each section of

Proposition 1
.

Let

be a set of seven (not necessarily distinct) points in

Theorem 1
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic 3. There are no unexpected cubic curves in
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
coming from the quasi-elliptic fibrations listed above.

Proof.

Recall that an unexpected cubic curve comes from choosing seven points
P_{1},P_{2},\dots,P_{7}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that there is a cubic with a double point at the general point
P
. By Proposition
1
, an unexpected cubic yields a quasi-elliptic fibration that is isomorphic to the blowup of
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
at the following nine points: the seven points
P_{1},\dots,P_{7}
, a general point
P
and a point infinitely-near
P
. Since
P
is general, it will not be equal to any of the
P_{i}
. Therefore we need only consider the quasi-elliptic fibrations that are attained by blowing up
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
at some point exactly twice, with the other seven blowup points elsewhere. Out of all of the quasi-elliptic fibrations we’ve seen in this chapter, there are only two that come from blowing up
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
at some point exactly twice. We need only check that quasi-elliptic fibrations with the Dynkin diagrams
e_{1}-e_{2}
2\ell-156789
\ell-134
2\ell-123789
e_{3}-e_{4}
\ell-356
\ell-125
e_{5}-e_{6}
2\ell-345789
-K-e_{7}+e_{9}
e_{8}-e_{9}
e_{7}-e_{8}

and
2\ell-123456
\ell-689
e_{6}-e_{7}
e_{4}-e_{5}
e_{3}-e_{4}
e_{2}-e_{3}
\ell-128
e_{8}-e_{9}
e_{1}-e_{2}
\ell-167

will not yield an unexpected cubic.

In the
\widetilde{A}_{2}^{\oplus 4}
diagram, we have an example of a pencil spanned by
2x^{2}z+yz^{2}
and
xy^{2}+2yz^{2}
. This pencil has a base points of multiplicity 2 at
(0,0,1)
,
(0,1,0)
, and
(1,0,0)
and has a base point of multiplicity 3 at
(1,1,1)
. We can create a two-dimensional linear system of cubics by introducing the third polynomial
(xz+2y^{2})(x+2y)
. This linear system has two base points of multiplicity 2: one at
(0,0,1)
and one at
(1,0,0)
. It also has a base point of multiplicity 3 at
(1,1,1)
. These seven points are all the base points of the linear system. Since unexpected cubics have a general double point, we must now determine whether this linear system has a general double point.

Let us examine the linear combination

F(xz+2y^{2})(x+2y)+G(2x^{2}z+yz^{2})+H(xy^{2}+2yz^{2})

and the generic point
P=(a,b,c)
. It is sufficient to reduce to the affine case
z=1
, and we want to determine whether there are values for
F
,
G
, and
H
such that

F(x+2y^{2})(x+2y)+G(2x^{2}+y)+H(xy^{2}+2y)

has a double point at
(A,B)=(a/c,b/c)
. By shifting
x\mapsto x+A
and
y\mapsto y+B
, we get that
(F,G,H)
annihilates the constant and linear components of the resulting polynomial if and only if

(F,G,H)\in\ker\begin{pmatrix}-A^{2}B+B^{3}&-A^{2}+B&AB^{2}-B\\
-B^{2}-A-B&A&B^{2}\\

A^{4}B^{2}-A^{3}B^{3}-A^{2}B^{4}+A^{4}B+A^{2}B^{3}-AB^{4}-B^{5}+A^{3}B-AB^{3}+A^{3}\neq 0

and so this two-dimensional linear system of cubics does not has a generic double point. Therefore these seven base points do not admit an unexpected cubic. We can repeat this process by instead creating a two-dimensional linear system that omits the double point at
(0,0,1)

(1,0,0)

\gamma^{1}

\gamma^{2}

\beta^{1}
are collinear, and
\beta^{1}

\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2},\gamma^{1},\dots,\gamma^{5}
or
\beta^{1},\beta^{2},\gamma^{1},\dots,\gamma^{5}

In the first scenario, we want a cubic with a generic double point

\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2},\gamma^{1},\dots,\gamma^{5}

No other diagram of a blow-down of a quasi-elliptic fibration could yield an unexpected cubic because no other diagram involves a point being blown up exactly twice, which is necessary for the generic double point. Therefore there are no unexpected cubics in characteristic 3 coming from the quasi-elliptic fibrations enumerated above.

Corollary 3
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic 3. There are no unexpected cubic curves in
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists an unexpected cubic in
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
. Then there are seven (possibly infinitely-near) points
P_{1},\dots,P_{7}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
such that there exists a cubic curve
C
containing the seven points and is singular at a general point
P_{8}
. Then consider a cubic curve
C^{\prime}
containing
P_{1},\dots,P_{7},P_{8}
and is singular at some point
Q^{\prime}
, so then
C.C^{\prime}=P_{1}+\cdots+P_{7}+2P_{8}
. Then we may form a pencil
\mathcal{P}
of cubic curves spanned by
C
and
C^{\prime}
whose base locus is
P_{1}+\cdots+P_{7}+P_{8}^{(1)}+P_{8}^{(2)}
, where
P_{8}^{(2)}
is an infinitely-near point to
P_{8}^{(1)}
corresponding to the tangent line of
C^{\prime}
at
P_{8}\in\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
.

The pencil
\mathcal{P}
thus admits a quasi-elliptic surface
X
by blowing up the nine points of the base locus. Then
X
must admit a Dynkin diagram of
\widetilde{A}_{2}^{\oplus 4}
,
\widetilde{A}_{2}\oplus\widetilde{E}_{6}
, or
\widetilde{E}_{8}
. By Corollary
2
,
X
must be extremal and so has finitely many
(-1)
-curves. Furthermore, up to reflections across
(-2)
-classes (i.e. conjugation), each of the diagrams embed in the
(-2)
-curves of
X
in a unique way by Theorem 5.5 of
[
7
]
. Therefore
X
must be isomorphic to one of the seven surfaces explored in this chapter. As we have already seen in Theorem
1
, there are no unexpected cubics coming from the seven surfaces of this chapter, giving us a contradiction.
∎
