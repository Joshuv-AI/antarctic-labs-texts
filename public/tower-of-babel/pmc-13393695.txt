Effect comparison between a new hypofractionated radiotherapy regimen and conventional moderately hypofractionated regimen for localized prostate cancer
Authors: Yuchen Han, Sijin Zhong, Xuesi Dong, Mengqi Zhang, Yexiong Li, Jianzhong Shou, Xingang Bi, Shulian Wang, Wenwen Zhang, Ningning Lu, Hui Fang, Yuan Tang, Bo Chen, Shunan Qi, Lijuan Niu, Yueping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of Prostate cancer (PC) has risen steadily in China, and hypofractionated radiotherapy offers a biologically advantageous and time-efficient treatment strategy. However, long-term data in Chinese populations are limited. This study evaluated the 10-year outcomes of hypofractionated radiotherapy regimen (45 Gy in 9 fractions) for localized PC. A propensity score–matched comparison was conducted to contextualize its effectiveness and toxicity relative to a moderately hypofractionated regimen.MethodsFrom October 2011 to April 2017, thirty patients with localized PC who received hypofractionated radiotherapy (45 Gy in 9 fractions) were enrolled in this prospective trial. Ninety-five patients treated with a moderately hypofractionated regimen at the same center between February 2009 and October 2019 were selected. The propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance the potential confounding bias. Survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. Genitourinary (GU) and gastrointestinal (GI) toxicities (grade ≥ 2) were assessed using the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 3.0) and Radiation Therapy Oncology Group late morbidity criteria. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS); secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), biochemical recurrence-free survival (bRFS), PC-specific survival (PCSS), and GU and GI toxicities.ResultsAfter a median follow-up of 10.3 years, patients treated with the 45 Gy in 9 fractions regimen achieved favorable long-term outcomes, with the 10-year PFS, bRFS, and OS rates of 78.1%, 93.3%, and 84.3%, respectively, with no prostate cancer–related deaths. PSM analysis yielded 60 comparable patients: (36 in the 67.5 Gy/25 fractions group and 24 in the 45 Gy//9 fractions group). No significant differences were observed between the 45 Gy/9 fractions and 67.5 Gy/25 fractions groups in 10-year OS (72.1% vs. 73.5%, P = 0.583), PFS (68.8% vs. 75%,P = 0.205), or freedom from failure(95.8% vs.94.0%, P = 0.757) Regarding toxicity, the 45 Gy /9 fractions was associated with significantly lower GI toxicity compared with the moderately hypofractionated regimen (P = 0.018), whereas GU toxicity did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.757).ConclusionsAfter long-term follow-up, the hypofractionated radiotherapy regimen of 45 Gy /9 fractions showed encouraging survival outcomes and acceptable toxicity in patients with localized PC. In the propensity score-matched analysis, no statistically significant differences in oncologic outcomes were observed between the 45 Gy/9 fractions regimen and the moderately hypofractionated regimen, while lower gastrointestinal toxicity was observed in the 45 Gy/9 fractions group. however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously in light of the limited sample size, non-randomized design, and technical differences between the cohorts.Trial registrationChiCTR-ONC-12001895. Background

Due to the aging of the Chinese population and the increasing prevalence of high-fat diets, the incidence of prostate cancer (PC) has risen significantly in recent years in China [1–4]. The age-standardized incidence rate of PC in China was 9.7 per 100,000 individuals in 2022, representing a dramatic 64.4% increase from 5.9 per 100,000 in 2012 [5, 6].

Radiotherapy is one of the primary treatments for PC. As PC cells are believed to have a low α/β ratio (approximately 1–3 Gy) [7], they are more sensitive to hypofractionated radiotherapy than to conventional fractionation [8, 9]. Phase III clinical trials such as HYPO-RT-PC and PACE-B have demonstrated that hypofractionated radiotherapy is non-inferior to conventional fractionation in terms of both safety and efficacy. In the HYPO-RT-PC trial, the failure-free survival (FFS) at 5 years was 84% in both treatment groups. In the PACE-B trial, the 5-year biochemical or clinical FFS was 94.6% in the stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) group and 95.8% in the conventionally fractionated or moderately hypofractionated radiotherapy group, with no significant difference.

Furthermore, Kishan et al. conducted a meta-analysis of 12 studies with long-term follow-up, reporting that the 7-year cumulative incidence of grade ≥ 3 GU and GI toxicities was 2.4% and 0.4%, respectively [10]. These findings suggest that hypofractionated radiotherapy is a suitable definitive treatment for localized PC. However, direct comparisons between ultra-hypofractionated and moderately hypofractionated regimens remain limited, particularly with long-term follow-up data. Furthermore, real-world evidence from Asian populations is scarce. Propensity score matching offers a method to balance confounding factors in observational studies, enabling meaningful comparisons between treatment cohorts when randomized trials are not feasible.

In 2011, we launched the first prospective study in China evaluating hypofractionated radiotherapy (45 Gy in 9 fractions) for localized PC. This manuscript presents a propensity score-matched comparison with a moderately hypofractionated regimen (67.5 Gy in 25 fractions) treated at the same institution, focusing on 10-year survival and toxicity outcomes.

Materials and methods

Patients

From October 2011 to April 2017, thirty patients with localized PC were enrolled in this prospective, phase II trial. The inclusion criteria were: (1) histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma of the prostate, (2) Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score ≥ 70,(3) American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging T1–T2N0M0, (4) Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) < 20 ng/mL, and (5) Gleason score ≤ 7. (The original inclusion criteria primarily targeted patients with low- and intermediate-risk PC patients. However, because radical prostatectomy was commonly selected for these patients at our institution during the study period, a small number of patients with relatively favorable high-risk features were also enrolled in our study, including T3 disease without nodal or distant metastasis, Gleason score 8, or pretreatment PSA levels of 20–30 ng/mL)Patients who had undergone prior PC surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or neoadjuvant hormone therapy lasting more than 90 days were excluded. Patients were categorized into risk groups according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recurrence risk criteria (version 2, 2024).

Outcomes of 45 Gy/ 9 fractions regimen were compared with those of a moderately hypofractionated regimen using PSM analysis. The comparator included 95 patients with localized PC treated with 67.5 Gy /25 fractions regimen between February 2009 and October 2019. This dose was equivalent to 79.3 Gy (based on EQD2; α/β = 2). Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) use in both groups was aligned with NCCN guidelines.

The study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The phase II study titled “Hypofractionated Precision Radiotherapy for Prostate Cancer Patients” was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital (NCC2013S-004), Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and Peking Union Medical College. All patients provided written informed consent.

Treatment

For image guidance, three gold fiducial markers were transrectally implanted into the prostate under ultrasound guidance at least two weeks prior to simulation. To ensure consistent bladder and rectal volumes for each fraction, patients were instructed to empty their rectum using a glycerin enema and achieve a comfortably full bladder by drinking water 1 h before simulation and treatment. Immediately before simulation and each treatment session, an endorectal balloon (ERB) was inserted into the rectum and inflated with air (median: 60 mL) to minimize rectal irradiation. Computed tomography (CT) images were co-registered with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for delineation of clinical target volumes (CTV) and surrounding critical structures. The target volume included: the prostate alone for low-risk patients; the prostate plus 1 cm proximal seminal vesicles for intermediate-risk patients; and the prostate with 1.5 cm proximal seminal vesicles for high-risk patients. Target delineation refers to the recommendations for prostate cancer radiotherapy target delineation based on the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) guidelines PTV was generated by expanding the CTV by 5 mm in all directions except posteriorly, where a 3-mm margin was applied. The organs at risk were delineated according to the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) prostate cancer radiotherapy protocols.

The prescribed dose was 45 Gy in 9 fractions (equivalent to 78.8 Gy in equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions [EQD2], α/β = 2). Dose constraints for organs at risk were predefined during treatment planning, including the rectum, bladder, femoral heads, penile bulb, small bowel, and colon. Detailed planning constraints are summarized in Table 1. To verify treatment setup, cone-beam CT was performed before each fraction and matched with the planning CT based on fiducial positions. Radiotherapy was delivered on consecutive days. Androgen deprivation therapy, including a luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone agonist with or without bicalutamide, was permitted for patients classified as intermediate-, high-, and very high-risk.

Patients in the moderately hypofractionated group were treated according to a previously published institutional protocol [11]. To ensure a comparable treatment period, localized PC patients treated with moderately hypofractionated regimen between 2009 and 2019 were included as the comparator group, approximately corresponding to the enrollment period of the 45 Gy/9 fractions group (2011–2017).All patients underwent CT simulation in the supine position with pelvic immobilization and a comfortably full bladder. CT images were co-registered with MRI for target delineation. CTV included the prostate alone for low-risk patients and the prostate with proximal seminal vesicles for intermediate- and high-risk patients. The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by a 7-mm isotropic expansion, except posteriorly where a 5-mm margin was applied.

Radiotherapy was delivered using intensity modulated radiotherapy or volumetric modulated arc therapy to a total dose of 67.5 Gy in 25 fractions (2.7 Gy per fraction), once daily, five fractions per week. Image guidance was performed using cone-beam CT or electronic portal imaging during treatment. Androgen deprivation therapy was administered according to NCCN guidelines based on risk stratification.

Covariates

The baseline covariates analyzed in this study included patient demographic characteristics and clinical-pathological features. Age at diagnosis was treated as a categorical variable and dichotomized into ≤ 70 years and > 70 years. Risk group stratification was defined according to the NCCN guidelines, categorizing patients into low, favorable intermediate, unfavorable intermediate, and high/very high-risk groups. Regarding treatment characteristics, Androgen Deprivation Therapy usage was classified based on duration: no ADT, short-term (< 6 months), and long-term (> 6 months). These variables served as the baseline for the subsequent propensity score matching analysis.

Follow-up assessments

Acute GU and GI toxicities were defined as those occurring during or within 90 days after radiotherapy. They were evaluated weekly according to the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 3.0. During radiotherapy, the toxicities were evaluated weekly. Post-treatment follow-up assessments were conducted at 1 and 3 months after radiotherapy, followed by evaluations every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months until year 5, and annually thereafter. At 3 months after radiotherapy, the assessments included digital rectal examinations, PSA and testosterone levels, late GI and GU toxicities (occurring beyond 90 days post-treatment) based on the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) late morbidity criteria, as well as MRI and CT scans every 6 months for the first 5 years and then annually thereafter. Bone scans were performed as clinically indicated. The highest cumulative grade of toxicity was recorded for each patient.

Study endpoints and definitions

Biochemical relapse was defined according to the RTOG Phoenix definition (PSA nadir + 2 ng/mL), regardless of ADT status. bRFS was calculated from the start of radiotherapy to the date of biochemical failure. PFS was defined as the time from the start of radiotherapy to biochemical relapse, local progression, distant metastasis, or death from any cause. OS was calculated from the start of radiotherapy to death from any cause. PCSS was defined as the time from the start of radiotherapy to death caused by prostate cancer or disease-related complications. Freedom from failure (FFF) was defined as the time from the start of radiotherapy to the first occurrence of biochemical failure, local progression, distant metastasis, or the initiation of salvage ADT. Deaths from non-prostate cancer causes were censored.

Propensity score matching and statistical analysis

Survival outcomes for the 45 Gy/9 fractions group were analyzed independently using the Kaplan–Meier method in SPSS (version 26.0) to estimate OS, PFS, bRFS, and PCSS.

In order to minimize selection bias and balance baseline characteristics between the two treatment cohorts, we performed a PSM analysis using R software (version 4.4.1). Propensity scores were calculated via logistic regression based on the following covariates: age at diagnosis (categorical: ≤ 70 years vs. > 70 years), risk group (categorical: low, favorable intermediate, unfavorable intermediate, or high/very high), and ADT duration (categorical: no ADT, ≤ 6 months, or > 6 months). The matching was performed using a 2:1 nearest neighbor algorithm without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.2. The matched cohort was then analyzed in SPSS for survival outcomes using Kaplan–Meier curves, with group differences assessed via the log-rank test. Acute and late GI and GU toxicities were evaluated cumulatively in the matched cohort. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Between October 2011 and April 2017, thirty patients were enrolled and completed the planned treatments in the 45 Gy group. The baseline characteristics are shown in Table 2. One patient each presented with small pulmonary nodules and abnormal pubic signals at enrollment, which were later confirmed as metastatic disease during follow-up. ADT was administered to intermediate-, high-, and very high-risk patients, with a median ADT duration of 6 months (see Table 3). With a median follow-up of 10.3 years (range: 7.25–12.7 years), seven patients died of non–PC causes: Alzheimer’s disease, myocardial infarction, postoperative complications of liver cancer, pneumonia, renal failure and other comorbidities. No local recurrence was observed. Three patients experienced tumor recurrence: one with biochemical relapse and two with distant metastases. Thus, the 10-year bRFS rate was 93.3% (95% confidence interval [CI], 83.9%–100%), OS was 84.3% (95% CI, 59.8%–96.6%), PFS was 78.1% (95% CI, 53.7%–91.3%), and PCSS was 100% (Fig. 1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, no previous studies on Chinese patients have reported fraction doses ≥ 5 Gy for the treatment of localized PC. This prospective trial primarily enrolled low- and intermediate-risk Chinese patients treated with hypofractionated radiotherapy (45 Gy in 9 consecutive fractions at 5 Gy/fraction) with or without ADT. The results demonstrated 10-year bRFS, OS, PFS, and PCSS rates of 93.3%, 84.3%, 78.1%, and 100%, respectively. These outcomes appear broadly consistent with those reported in major prospective trials of ultra-hypofractionation, such as HYPO-RT-PC (5-year FFS of 84%) and PACE-B (5-year bRFS of 95.7%), although cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in patient populations, treatment techniques, and follow-up duration [12, 13].Importantly, no local recurrences were observed in our 45 Gy/9 fractions cohort, likely reflecting careful patient selection and advances in technology, particularly daily image guidance using fiducial markers that enhanced target accuracy. To further validate the effectiveness of this regimen, we performed a propensity score-matched analysis comparing the 45 Gy/9 fractions regimen with the moderately hypofractionated regimen administered during the same period at our institution. The moderately hypofractionated comparator used in the present matched analysis was based on our previously reported institutional experience with 67.5 Gy in 25 fractions, which showed encouraging biochemical control and acceptable late toxicity in Chinese patients with pelvic-confined prostate cancer [11]. Similar favorable outcomes and acceptable toxicity with modern moderately hypofractionated radiotherapy have also been reported in other institutional series using image-guided techniques [14–16]. After adjusting for baseline characteristics and matching EQD2 exposures, there were no statistically significant differences in survival outcomes, including OS, PFS, or FFF. Our results align with previous studies utilizing PSM to compare radiotherapy fractionation schemes. For example, in a study comparing 35 Gy/5 fractions regimen and 71–74 Gy/28 fractions regimen, used PSM to reduce selection bias. Their analysis demonstrated no significant differences in 3-year biochemical disease-free survival (bDFS) or OS between the two treatment groups(bDFS: 71–74 Gy/28 fraction 93.7% vs. 35 Gy/5 fraction regimen 96.2%), mirroring our own results where no differences were found in oncologic outcomes between 45 Gy/9 fractions and 67.5 Gy/25 fractions after applying PSM [17]. Further supporting our approach, another study by Marvaso et al. compared extreme hypofractionation (32.5–35 Gy/5 fraction) with moderate hypofractionation (70.2 Gy/26 fraction) and also used PSM to balance treatment groups, showed no significant variation in PFS or OS across the overall cohort or propensity-matched subgroups, supporting extreme hypofractionation as an oncologically comparable alternative to moderate hypofractionation [18]. Although no phase III trials have directly compared ultra-hypofractionated and moderately hypofractionated radiotherapy, our real-world matched data support the view that both approaches offer comparable efficacy for managing localized PC. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as our study was not designed as an equivalence or non-inferiority trial.

Our study also provides supporting evidence for the effectiveness of hypofractionated radiotherapy in Chinese patients. Other studies in Asian populations have reported similar outcomes. In a retrospective Malaysian study involving 81 patients (10.2% low-risk, 26.5% intermediate-risk, 63.3% high-risk), SBRT at 24 to 34 Gy in 3 to 5 fractions (with 93.9% receiving 5 fractions) resulted in a 5-year bRFS of 95.2% [19]. Similarly, a Japanese study of 55 patients treated with 36 Gy in 4 fractions reported a 3-year biochemical control rate of 89.8% [20]. Taken together, these results, including ours, suggest that SBRT is an effective treatment modality for Asian patients with localized PC.

Currently, the optimal ultra-hypofractionated radiotherapy regimen for localized PC remains undetermined. Given the low ɑ/β ratio of PC (about 1–3 Gy), the EQD2 of 45 Gy delivered in 9 fractions corresponds to approximately 72–90 Gy. We consider this dosage adequate for intermediate-risk disease control. The results of our 10-year follow-up suggest that this regimen may provide durable tumor control in selected patients. Therefore, it may warrant further prospective investigation. The 45 Gy/9 fractions regimen demonstrated a favorable safety and tolerability profile, with rare acute grade 2 GU and GI toxicities and no acute ≥ grade 3 toxicities were observed. Over a 10-year follow-up, only a small number of patients developed late grade 2 or 3 GU or GI toxicities, and no grade 4 toxicities were reported. These outcomes compare favorably with those from published studies. For example, the PACE-B study reported acute ≥ grade 2 GI and GU toxicity rates of 10% and 23%, respectively, and late ≥ grade 2 GU and GI rates of 7.5% and 0.8% for the 36.25 Gy/5 fraction group after 5-years [13, 21]. Similarly, RTOG 0938 reported a 1.6% late grade 3 toxicity rate in both 36.25 Gy/5 fraction group and 51.6 Gy/12 fraction group [22].

Our propensity score-matched comparison between 45 Gy in 9 fractions and 67.5 Gy in 25 fractions revealed a higher cumulative incidence of late ≥ grade 2 GI toxicity in the moderately hypofractionated (30.6% vs. 4.2%), whereas GU toxicity rates were comparable (16.6% vs. 16.7%). In another matched analysis comparing extreme and moderately hypofractionated regimens (5 fractions of 6.5–7 Gy to a total dose of 32.5–35 Gy and 26 fractions of 2.7 Gy to a total dose of 70.2 Gy), higher cumulative GU toxicity was observed in the extreme arm, although no significant difference remained at final follow-up [18]. For cumulative GI toxicity, no significant difference was observed between the two groups. These findings regarding toxicities are inconsistent with our findings. We speculate that the remaining discrepancies between studies may be attributed to differences in margin expansion and technical implementation. For the 45 Gy/9 fractions group, the CTV-to-PTV margin expansion of 5 mm (except 3 mm posteriorly) was applied in all directions. For the 67.5 Gy/25 fractions group, 7 mm (except 5 mm posteriorly) was applied in all directions due to lack of image guidance. Larger margins expansion inevitably increases the risk of injuries to the normal tissues, including the rectum and bladder. A prospective phase II study of moderately hypofractionated helical tomotherapy used a 5-mm isotropic margin and reported acceptable GI and GU toxicity, suggesting that tighter margins may reduce organ-at-risk exposure [15]. Furthermore, our gold fiducials for image guidance and ERB for rectal protection likely contributed to improved treatment accuracy and rectal dose reduction, which may explain the favorable toxicity profile observed in 45 Gy/9 fractions group. Therefore, the lower gastrointestinal toxicity observed in the 45 Gy/9 fractions group cannot be attributed solely to the fractionation schedule itself. Taken together, these findings suggest that the 45 Gy in 9 fractions regimen may be associated with an acceptable safety profile when delivered with modern image guidance and supportive techniques.

ERB has been proven to improve prostate stabilization, minimize intrafraction motion, and reduce rectal radiation exposure. One early study demonstrated that the ERB reduced mean prostate displacement from 1.8 mm to 1.3 mm and the maximum displacement from 4 mm to ≤1 mm within 2 min of treatment [23]. Another study from the Netherlands, which involved 48 patients with localized PC who were treated with three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (dose of 67.5 Gy), showed that ERBs significantly reduced the inner rectal volume exposed to doses > 40 Gy. The percentage of rectal wall mucosal area exposed to 40–60 Gy was reduced from about 48% in patients treated without an ERB to 40% with an ERB (P = 0.048). The incidence of late rectal toxicity ≥ grade 1 was significantly lower for the group with ERB (P = 0.003) [24]. These findings of the use of rectal balloons have demonstrated that this technique effectively reduces radiation-induced rectal damage, thereby enhancing treatment safety and improving patients’ quality of life [25–27]. lower incidence of rectum toxicities observed in our trial was, in part, attributable to the ERB for rectum protection.

To our knowledge, this trial represents the longest follow-up study of ultra-hypofractionated radiotherapy in China. The hypofractionated regimen has not been previously reported, and this is the first study in China to integrate gold fiducial guidance and ERB use in hypofractionated radiotherapy. The outcomes demonstrate satisfactory survival, effective disease control, and manageable toxicity. When compared to moderately hypofractionated regimen using PSM, no significant difference in efficacy was observed, and GI toxicity was significantly lower with this regimen. However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of the ultra-hypofractionated cohort was relatively limited, which may reduce the statistical power to detect small differences between treatment groups. Second, although we used PSM to adjust baseline differences between the hypofractionated and moderate arms, this retrospective comparison is not equivalent to a randomized controlled trial. In addition, technical differences between the two cohorts, including image guidance, the usage of endorectal balloon, and planning target volume margins, may also have influenced the observed toxicity outcomes. Third, although the rectal volume was controlled via the ERB, the bladder volume was regulated by the patient’s compliance under physician guidance, introducing potential variability. Lastly, wider adoption of this regimen in China may be limited due to technical and infrastructure constraints.

Conclusion

In this single-center study, the hypofractionated radiotherapy regimen of 45 Gy in 9 fractions showed encouraging long-term survival outcomes and an acceptable toxicity profile in predominantly low- and intermediate-risk Chinese patients with localized prostate cancer. Using a propensity score–matched design, no statistically significant differences in survival outcomes were observed between this hypofractionated regimen (45 Gy in 9 fractions) and the moderately hypofractionated regimen. Lower gastrointestinal toxicity was observed in the 45 Gy/9 fractions group; however, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size, the non-randomized design, and the technical differences between the two cohorts. This shortened treatment offers more convenience for patients and may improve adherence under modern radiotherapy techniques and image-guidance support. Further prospective studies with larger patient populations are warranted to confirm these results.
