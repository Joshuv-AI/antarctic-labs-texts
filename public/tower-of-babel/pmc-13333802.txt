Texture conditioned GAN based self supervised framework for fabric defect detection
Authors: R. Sujitha, K. Venkatasalam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Automated fabric inspection systems are essential for maintaining consistent textile quality in modern manufacturing, where high-speed production and complex surface textures 1c`challenge traditional visual inspection. Intelligent, data-efficient anomaly detection methods are therefore increasingly important. Most existing fabric defect detection approaches depend on supervised or weakly supervised learning, requiring extensive labeled defect samples that are costly to acquire and insufficiently representative of real industrial variability. Additionally, many methods fail to explicitly preserve fine-grained texture semantics, leading to false alarms or missed defects on complex woven and patterned fabrics. This paper introduces a Self-Supervised Texture-Decomposition and Defect Amplification Network (STDAN). This novel framework uniquely decomposes fabric images into structure-consistent texture bases and residual anomaly cues without requiring defect annotations. STDAN integrates a texture-consistency encoder with a generative defect amplification module that exaggerates latent irregularities through controlled perturbation in the feature space rather than pixel-level synthesis. A contrastive self-supervised objective aligns normal texture representations while separating amplified defect responses, enabling the detector to learn discriminative anomaly boundaries from normal data alone. Experimental evaluations on multiple public fabric inspection benchmarks show that STDAN achieves superior detection accuracy and localization precision compared with state-of-the-art unsupervised and GAN-based methods, particularly for small, low-contrast, and previously unseen defects. The technique demonstrates strong robustness to texture variation and illumination changes, confirming its practical applicability. By uniquely combining texture decomposition and feature-level defect amplification, STDAN offers a scalable, annotation-free solution for reliable fabric quality inspection in industrial environments.

Introduction

Background and motivation

The textile manufacturing sector requires high-quality, steady inspection of fabrics to achieve product uniformity, reduce material wastage, and ensure customer satisfaction1. The modern production lines are high-throughput, and the fabrics have more intricate textures, such as woven, knitted, jacquard, and patterned surfaces2. These textures exhibit high periodicity, minor structural variation, and sensitivity to changes in illumination, making defect detection in text a difficult visual inspection task3. Manual inspection is a tedious, subjective, and error-prone process, especially when dealing with smaller, low-contrast, or sporadic defects. As a result, automated visual inspection systems are now considered an important part of smart textile production4.

The initial automated techniques for inspecting fabric used manual features, including statistical texture descriptors and frequency-domain analysis, as well as rule-based thresholding. Although they work in controlled environments, the methods are sensitive to both texture variation and variation in real-world production5. With the development of deep learning, the role of detecting fabric defects has advanced to a new stage, as it enables feature understanding from data6. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and encoder-decoder networks are successful in supervised defect classification and segmentation problems. Their success however requires the presence of large scale/ pixel level annotated defect datasets which are expensive and impractical to acquire in an industrial setting7.

In the manufacturing environment, defects are infrequent, highly differentiated, and, in many cases, unpredictable. This information asymmetry makes supervised forms of learning inefficient and constrained in the generalization of knowledge to previously unknown defect types8. Therefore, unsupervised and self-supervised anomaly detection systems are increasingly becoming popular. These processes acquire representations of normal fabric structures and detect a defect among the learned normal representations9. Recent methods use autoencoders, reconstruction-based models, and generative adversarial networks (GANs) to learn normal texture models. Although they promise better results, most available unsupervised approaches do not distinguish fine defects or inherent texture changes as expected, particularly in patterned fabrics.

The task is challenging: maintaining fine-grained texture semantics while being sensitive to localized anomalies. Reconstruction/synthesis at the pixel level can result in blurred representations or unrealistic defect modeling, thereby decreasing detection reliability. Moreover, it remains an open research problem to learn discriminative anomaly boundaries from normal samples alone10. The challenges have spurred the development of texture-conscious, annotation-free inspection schemes capable of strong detection and localization of a wide range of fabric defects in real-world scenarios.

Limitations

Despite many achievements, available fabric defect detectors have several key shortcomings. First, procedures that are monitored or poorly monitored demand large amounts of labeled defect information, which is limited, costly to label, and not well-representative of realistic industrial variability. This dependency reduces scalability and the usefulness of deployment. Second, many unsupervised methods treat fabric textures as homogeneous patterns, which do not explicitly capture the structured semantics of texture. Consequently, normal variations in texture are not distinguished from defects, resulting in high false-alarm rates.

Third, reconstruction-based anomaly detectors also require the hypothesis that defects are not replicable by normal training models. Nevertheless, high-quality neural networks are more likely to reconstruct flawed regions, particularly for small or low-contrast defects, thereby diminishing detection sensitivity. Fourth, the current state of GAN-based defect synthesis schemes generally synthesizes defects at the pixel scale, yielding aesthetically unnatural or unrealistic defects with fabric-wide patterns that are not consistent with actual fabric texture distributions. This undermines their ability to conduct effective training to develop strong anomaly detectors.

Lastly, the vast majority of existing techniques do not provide a way to enhance small anomaly signals during training. Learning discriminative decision boundaries from only normal data is difficult to achieve without directly reinforcing weak defects. These constraints demonstrate the necessity of a self-supervised framework which integrates texture-aware representation learning, feature-level defect amplification and discriminative anomaly modeling.

Related work

Texture-aware defect detection and reconstruction in industrial inspection

The Texture-Aware Multiscale GAN for Veneer Defect Reconstruction (TAM-GAN) presents a texture consistent veneer defect reconstruction method. The model uses cascaded generators and discriminators to recover defect areas without deforming natural veneer textures. A texture-loss-based on LBP improves reconstruction of fine-grained details, and normalization of the region improves spatial accuracy. The efficiency and visual quality are enhanced through end-to-end training. Experimental findings show that it achieves good restoration performance, with a PSNR of 35.32 and an SSIM of 0.97111.

The Text-Driven Garment Editing Mapper for Fashion Image Editing (TD-GEM) allows language-based image editing of fashion. The model fuses GAN inversion with CLIP-directed latent manipulation to allow editable garment attributes on full-body human photographs. Other inversion approaches, such as e4e and Pivotal Tuning Inversion, are evaluated for accuracy. The optimized mapper is fast and high-quality, making it possible to make effective edits such as changing the color of garments and increasing or decreasing the sleeve length12.

Weakly Supervised Nested U-Net (WS-NU-Net) is used to solve the problem of industrial defect detection with limited supervision. A nested U-Net backbone with integrated attention modules captures the feature relationships. Training uses both artificially created defects and a limited number of actual defect samples. Experiments with MVTec AD, DAGM, MT, and CT data reveal high image-level and pixel-level detection performance compared with current state-of-the-art solutions13.

The Cross Interaction Transformer (CIFormer) is used to detect surface defects of ceramic tiles. The architecture uses Coarse-Fine Granularity Interaction and Adaptive Cross-Scale Attention modules to learn subtle defects patterns and differentiate them with visually similar backgrounds respectively. Multi-scale feature aggregation enhances sensitivity to minute defects. Existing experimental findings indicate that it is possible to achieve significant improvements in precision, recall, and mAP over current ceramic defect detection methods14.

The Data-Enhanced Virtual Texture Generation Network (DVTG-Net) is used to reduce the data imbalance in industrial inspection. The system produces natural surface textures via OpenGL rendering and parameter exploration via reinforcement learning. A discriminator-critic model that judges generated samples and gives a feedback to optimize. The generation process automatically labels, reducing manual labor. It was experimentally demonstrated that DVTG-Net can have a profound positive effect on recall in datasets by making the data more diverse and exhausting the defect properties15.

Generative and diffusion-based fashion image synthesis and virtual try-on

The recent studies on the problem of industrial inspection use more and more unsupervised and self-supervised anomaly detection techniques to address the lack of labeled defect data. Autoencoders that are based on reconstruction and GAN learn normal samples representations and detect anomalies by reconstruction error or feature deviation but tend to reconstruct defective regions well, similar to normal samples making them less sensitive to minor defects. Recent embedding based and contrastive learning models enhance anomaly discrimination through the learning of compact representations of normal textures and the separation of abnormal patterns in the latent space. Although such improvements have been made, current methods typically do not have explicit methods of modeling structured texture semantics or strong enhancement of weak anomaly cues, thus restricting their applicability to complex patterned fabrics. These constraints encourage the texture-conscious and feature-discriminative framework embraced by the proposed STDAN framework.

The Multi-Modal Content-Texture Fusion Network (MCF-Net) is used to strengthen the ability of AI-generated images to be detected. The strategy combines RGB images, Local Binary Pattern maps and Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix features using parallel branches with common weights. Semantic and micro-textural inconsistencies are shared at the feature-level. Tests on benchmark datasets show that the experimental performance in terms of robustness and generalization is better than that of their single-modality counterparts, providing an effective digital image forensics tool16.

The MSA-YOLO-LIB, a multi-scale attention-enhanced framework of detecting lithium-ion battery welding defects is used to detect defects. EfficientNet-B0 offers lightweight feature extraction, PANet offers cross-scale fusion, and a YOLOv8 head with multi-head attention enhances localization accuracy. Parallel dilated convolutions detect small-scale texture anomalies and big structural flaws. Experiments on several datasets show improved recall and F1 Score, and real-time inference can be performed17.

KnitDiff is a constraint-sensitive diffusion model that is used to create intelligent knit patterns. The KnitPrompt Engine is a Knit Diffusion backbone that converts natural language inputs into technically valid knit designs. Adaptive preference learning, a knit vocabulary builder, and multidimensional evaluation are used to ensure manufacturability and aesthetic quality. Reinforcement learning maximizes generation. Findings reveal better textile design performance in terms of texture realism, structural integrity, and user satisfaction18.

The Digital Twin Packaging Virtual Image Generation platform (DT-PVIG) supports the virtual packaging design. The system is based on GAN-based image synthesis and the digital twin principles to create lifelike packaging images without 3D modeling. The stored image data allow representation of the scene and enhancement of fidelity through efficient use. The usefulness of the platform is validated by simulation experiments that demonstrate high-quality virtual packaging images, which are useful in advertising, fashion, and product design19.

The LSCGB Texture-Aware Network (LSCGB-TAN) and a newly built large-scale benchmark are used to develop CG image forensics. The dataset contains more than 140,000 CG and photographic images that are diverse and least biased. LSCGB-TAN enhances multi-level texture characteristics and characterizes channel-wise texture correlations using Gram matrices. The experimental findings indicate an obvious performance improvement compared to current CG-PG classification techniques20.

Texture-centric image generation and forensic analysis

The generation models created in the texture synthesis and fashion image models propose valuable ideas used in an anomaly detection context and especially in manipulation of the latent space and texture-consistency levels. Instead of concentrating on the image generation tasks, the more recent research proves that the controlled feature-space shifts may alter local texture properties, but the overall structure coherence is preserved. The design of STDAN was inspired by these principles, where defect amplification is done in latent feature space rather than pixel space to eliminate unnatural artifacts. The concept of stable texture descriptors and controllable variations are directly applied to the suggested texture-decomposition module, which allows better identifying subtle anomalies without adversely affecting realistic distribution of fabrics texture.

Sustainable fashion design is trained on SD-FashionDiff, an image generation pipeline that uses Stable Diffusion models with the LoRA enhancement. The framework also focuses on accurately representing fabric texture and structural accuracy to minimize the need for physical samples. The visual quality and semantic correspondence of quantitative assessments based on FID, SSIM, LPIPS, and CLIP-S are high. The method allows for faster prototyping, less waste, and greater creative freedom in digital fashion processes21.

GenWear is a single conditional diffusion system that synthesizes controllable garment images. The system is based on a frozen Paint-by-Example UNet and incorporates SketchCtrl, ColorFiLM, text adapters and texture adapters to control the system in a disentangled multi-modal manner. Structural fidelity is maintained, and semantic and material realism are allowed. The experiments on the VITON-HD dataset show state-of-the-art results in garment controllability and visual quality22.

MF-vtON is an implicitly masked latent diffusion-based model that allows virtual try-on generation. A pseudo-input strategy that involves text boosts diversity in clothing whereas a gated manipulation process eliminates catastrophic forgetting during training. The use of texture-aware injection preserves subtle garment information, and a sophisticated inference strategy balances the outputs. Massive experimental results demonstrate a better realism, identity, and generalization, over current virtual try-on methods23.

It is an implementation of high-fidelity virtual try-on based on a two-stage latent diffusion architecture called DiffFit. The former stage completes geometry-conscious garment warping to achieve precise body placement and the latter stage is the refinement step that entails cross-modal conditional diffusion to refine the texture. Separating geometric and appearance modeling enhances the stability and realism in generation. The experimental tests conducted on large-scale benchmarks show better maintenance of garment features, illumination and general appearance24.

The Attribute-Aware Open-Vocabulary Crack Detection Framework (AOVCD) is used to detect open-vocabulary road crack. The crack categories are expressed as visual attribute combinations and enable one to identify invisible defect types using vision-language correspondence. Multi-label learning of geometric, spatial, and textural features is supported by attribute-level annotations. Experimental findings indicate that, on average, precision and equal error are significantly improved over CLIP-based zero-shot baselines25. Research gap

Although this has been achieved in a very short time, there are still a number of research gaps. Current approaches typically struggle to maintain fine-grained texture consistency under complex real-world factors, especially when training data is sparse or skewed. Various methods rely on synthetic data or unrealistic assumptions, so it is not possible to generalize to unobserved defects, materials, or poses. In addition, the existing vision-language and diffusion-based systems lack unified interpretable control mechanisms, sound evaluation, and efficient implementation at industrial scale, in real-time, and for sustainability-oriented applications.

Reconstruction-based models, feature-distribution learning models, and more recent self-supervised contrastive models have evolved into unsupervised anomaly detection to perform industrial inspection. Reconstruction-based approaches identify defects based on reconstruction error but tend to fail when abnormality regions are reconstructed too well and feature-distribution modeling techniques enhance the localization of defects by checking deviations in deep feature space. More closely related, contrastive learning methods have facilitated representation learning without annotation; though normally at the expense of pixel-level augmentations, which can form unrealistic anomalies. By contrast, the suggested STDAN framework combines texture-consistency modeling and feature-level defect amplification, which makes it a part of modern work in self-supervised anomaly detection research but is also capable of overcoming the drawbacks of both reconstruction and augmentation-based approaches. Such a positioning explains why STDAN is an extension of current anomaly detection paradigms with the physical plausibility of physically plausible latent-space perturbations that are specific to the analysis of industrial fabric texture.

Methodology

Problem definition

The fabric defect detection problem we solve is a self-supervised anomaly detection problem. Given a training set of only defect-free fabric images, the task is to detect and localize defects during inference without defect annotations. Framework overview

The originality of the suggested STDAN framework is that it is the first attempt to develop a unified presentation of the texture-consistency learning and physically constrained feature-level defect amplification to the self-supervised anomaly detection framework. Compared to traditional residual modeling methods, which use reconstruction error as a byproduct of encoderdecoder learning, STDAN directly breaks down fabric representations into a learned texture basis and residual anomaly component, with texture bases optimized by attention-guided feature alignment and contrastive consistency instead of reconstruction minimization itself. Moreover, the suggested defect amplification process is radically different to the previous synthetic anomaly generating processes which are based on pixel level augmentation or uncontrolled latent perturbation. Rather, perturbations are introduced based on adaptable structured feature subspace controlled by statistics of texture, so that artificial anomalies are physically okay and in agreement with the periodicity of fabrics. Through its combination of texture decomposition, constrained feature perturbation and contrastive representation learning in a single framework, STDAN goes beyond incremental augmentation policies and presents a principled way of modeling normal texture space and deviation of anomalies in latent space.

The suggested STDAN has four fundamental parts: texture-consistency coding, texture decomposition, feature-level defect amplification, and self-supervised contrastive learning. Structure-preserving texture features are extracted by the encoder and decomposed into dominant normal texture bases and irregularities. The latent space amplifies residual features to enhance weak anomaly signals. Normal and amplified representations of defects are then divided by a contrastive learning goal. Lastly, the computation of anomaly scores and localization maps is based on feature deviations. This combined system enables powerful non-annotation-based recognition of defects in complex textured materials.

Overall STDAN Architecture (End-to-End Flow).

The entire end-to-end process of the proposed STDAN is shown in Fig. 1. Input to the system is unlabeled images of fabric, and it is initially processed with a texture-consistency encoder that extracts structure-conserving texture features and removes regions of illumination and sensor noise. The features extracted are further subjected to a texture decomposition module, which decomposes the dominant normal texture bases and the residual component, which could hold information about anomalies. This breakdown enables the model to represent the normal texture manifold without defect supervision explicitly. Lastly, an anomaly detection and localization head takes a comparison of normal and residual features to estimate an anomaly score and produce pixel-level defect heatmaps. The structure allows strong sensitivity to the weak and invisible flaws in complicated fabric textures.

The suggested Texture-Consistency Encoder (TCE) is made up of a hybrid CNN-Transformer network that is intended to acquire both local texture features and global contextual relationships together. To be more precise, a convolutional backbone using a ResNet architecture is initially utilized to derive low-level spatial features of the input fabric image so that it can be used to model fine-grained texture structures. The resulting feature map is transformed into a sequence of patch tokens and fed through Transformer encoder blocks to be trained on learning long-range texture correlations. Thus, the encoder is not a completely Transformer model, but a CNN-Transformer hybrid which integrates convolutional inductive bias with global representation learning based on self-attention.

Texture representation and decomposition

Texture-consistency encoding

The texture representation plays an important role in the inspection of fabrics since they are repetitive and structured in nature. The texture-consistency encoder is created in such a way that it is possible to detect fine-grained spatial patterns with being invariance to changes in illumination and small manufacturing variations. It tries to learn a small latent representation that only captures the underlying fabric structure as opposed to pixel-wise appearance.

Residual feature modeling

The feature map is then put through a texture decomposition module, which decomposes normal texture bases out of the feature map and the rest as residual features. Normal texture basis is used to denote dominant repetitive patterns, which are usually common to non-defective fabric. Conversely, residual features are local variations, deformities, or anomalous response which can reflect possible defects. This binary analysis inhibits regular texture variations from being confused as abnormalities and isolates defect-relevant information. The framework promotes residual amplification of defects of interest and sensitivity to low-contrast defects by modelling residual features separately.

The process of texture decomposition can be outlined in the form of an algorithm (Algorithm 1) to have a concise description of the procedure of the proposed transformer-based representation learning.

Multi-Head Self-Attention Layer.

The attention score is computed over several heads to obtain attention score of a sequence according to various contextual dependencies, as shown in Algorithm 1. The inputs are projected as features into query and key and value spaces by each head, and a scaled dot-product attention is computed and produces weighted outputs. The head outputs are added together and linearly projected to form the final representation. Normalization and residual connectivity improve gradient flow and stability. This mechanism allows the model to pay attention to various features of the input at the same time and enhances the understanding of sequences and feature extraction.

Defect feature amplification

Latent Perturbation Strategy.

STDAN proposes a feature-level defect amplification mechanism to support the problem of poor or obscure defect indications. Rather than synthesizing pixel-level defects, the residual anomaly features are disturbed in the latent space with controlled stochastic variations. It is a latent perturbation approach that maintains a consistency of texture globally but selectively enhances local irregularities.

Feature-Level Generation.

The disturbed features are trained on a generative defect amplification network, trained in a GAN based architecture, which is trained to learn to amplify feature defects without forming unrealistic artifacts. Working in the feature space enables the model to produce a variety of meaningful representations of the anomalies in accordance with the learned texture space. A feature consistency constraint is used to make sure amplified defects are realistic and coherent. These increase features can be used as effective negative samples in self-supervised learning, which can help the model to learn more powerful discriminative decisions between normal features and anomalies.

Fig. 2Feature-level defect amplification and generation flow.

Feature-level defect amplification and generation flow.

The inner workings of the feature-level defect amplification process in STDAN are explained in detail in Fig. 2. The obtained cues of residual anomalies in the texture decomposition are initially sent to a controlled latent perturbation model, which injects stochastic changes subject to texture-consistency constraints. This step expands weak or unclear irregularities and maintains the global fabric structure. The perturbed features undergo a GAN-based defect amplification network, which is trained to amplify defect features in the latent space, as opposed to the pixel-level amplification of defect features. The methodology does not produce unrealistic images and improves the variety of defects. A consistency regularizer on features is a regularizer that makes sure that the amplified features are in a plausible texture distribution rather than collapse into mode or greatly distort the texture. The amplified features obtained are now good negative samples in self-supervised learning.

Specifically, the encoded feature tensor (F) is reshaped and decomposed into a set of learnable normal texture bases (B), activation coefficients (A), and a residual anomaly component (R). The basis matrix (B) is implemented as a trainable parameter within a basis-projection layer, while the activation coefficients (A) are computed via a similarity projection followed by Softmax normalization. The reconstructed normal texture representation is used to isolate residual anomaly features (R = F- ĥat{F}).

To enforce this decomposition during training, we introduced an explicit decomposition loss function consisting of (i) a reconstruction loss (|F-BA|_2^2), (ii) a sparsity constraint on the activation coefficients (|A|_1), and (iii) a Frobenius regularization term on the basis matrix (|B|_F^2). This loss is jointly optimized with the contrastive learning objective and adversarial feature amplification module.

Self-supervised learning

Contrastive objective

STDAN is an approach that uses a self-monitored contrastive learning to learn discriminative anomaly-conscious feature representations. Normal texture characteristics and exaggerated defect characteristics are mapped to a common embedding space. The contrastive objective reduces the distance between embeddings of normal texture samples whilst increasing the distance between them and amplified defect embeddings.

Embedding optimization

This learning approach allows the model to build a small normal texture space and force anomaly representations out of this space. Optimization embedding is done with marginal based contrastive loss, which guaranteed stable convergence and strong separation. This technique is more efficient than reconstruction-based methods because it directly maximises feature discriminability rather than reconstruction fidelity. Therefore, the learned embeddings have a better response to the fine-scale anomalies and are less responsive to the complexity of the texture. The contrastive framework enables the model to make generalization on unknown defect types and only needs normal samples to be trained.

Self-supervised contrastive learning and decision process.

The self-supervised contrastive learning strategy and final decision-making process of STDAN is described in Fig. 3. Regular texture bases and enhanced defect characteristics are individually encoded into a common feature encoding. Optimization in the contrastive learning module is such that it minimizes intra-class variance of normal texture embeddings maximizing inter-class separation between normal and amplified defect texture embeddings. By this, the model is trained on a discriminative anomaly-conscious feature representation, but it does not depend on defect labels. An anomaly scoring and decision module, where feature-distance-based anomaly scores are computed and adaptive thresholding to identify defect presence is then applied to the learned embeddings. The learning strategy will allow to select well between normal or subtle anomalies in the textures, which will help detect and localize defects correctly in the inspection of real-world fabrics.

Defect detection and localization

Anomaly scoring

Within the process of inference, the fabric images are fed on a trained STDAN model and anomaly scores and defect localization maps are computed. Anomaly scoring is also done by evaluating the difference between extracted feature embeddings and the learned normal texture distribution. Shores that are higher are a sign of more deviation from normality and are interpreted as the likelihood of defects.

Algorithm 3Attention-Based Prediction.

Attention-Based Prediction.

In algorithm 3, an encoder is applied to produce contextual representations, then attention-based pooling is applied to compress sequence information into a fixed-size vector. The pooled vector is run through a feed-forward network to give class logits, which are normalized using softmax to obtain prediction probabilities. The normalization of layers and residual connections makes it more stable. The algorithm is capable of predicting output labels accurately and at the same time highlighting key aspects within the input sequence making it a robust predictor of classification tasks by combining sequence modeling, attention pooling, and classification layers.

The attention-based feature aggregation procedure characterized by algorithm 3 is employed to acquire compact latent representations to contrastive learning. As an alternative to generating class predictions, the aggregated representation is mapped to a normalized embedding space, and the contrastive objective is maximized between similarity relationships between normal texture features and amplified defect features. This is an algorithm involved only when using self-supervised representation learning stage that does not carry out explicit classification. In the process of inference, the anomaly detection is done by calculating the deviation of features of the learned normal texture distribution.

Localization map generation

Spatial localization requires the projection of feature-level responses to anomalies in the image space to heatmaps of defects at the pixel level. These maps are used to point out areas that have an abnormal texture behavior, hence localizing areas of defects in a very accurate way (holes, stains, or misweaves). This method of scoring global anomalies and generating local heat maps enables STDAN to accommodate both image and pixel-level tasks. This multi-ability is especially valuable with the industry applications that need to detect defects and also localize them accurately to make a quality control decision.

The suggested STDAN model adheres to a purely self-supervised training model where the samples of normal fabric are only used when optimizing, and no defect labels are needed. Training The texture-consistency encoder is trained to learn normal texture representations with contrastive learning, and the feature-level defect amplification module produces synthetic anomalies by using controlled perturbations in latent feature space. Through thresholding of the normalized anomaly score, final anomaly decisions are obtained, which in turn, ensures a sharp distinction between training and inference processes as well as enhancing reproducibility.

Experiments

To explicitly analyze the reliability of the suggested Self-Coated Texture Layout and Defect Amplification Network (STDAN), the FabricSpotDefect, FD_Dataset and Lusitano datasets were subjected to standard measures of anomaly detection, such as Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), Precision, Recall and F1-Score. In contrast to the preceding sections which only outlined the experimental configurations, this section presents the real performance that was attained by STDAN. The findings show that the combination of a suggested texture-decomposition approach and a feature-level defect amplification can allow correctly localizing anomalies without the need of having annotated defect samples. In all datasets, STDAN has a high detection accuracy, which implies that it can be generalized to a wide variety of fabric textures and defect patterns.

The proposed STDAN framework was carefully compared to the state of the art state-of-the-art anomaly detection methods in both image level and pixel level evaluation metrics in a consistent theoretical experimental conditions. Comparative outcomes prove that STDAN is more effective because of its texture-decomposition strategy and feature-level defect amplification mechanism that make it more accurate in the representation and localization of anomalies. Moreover, a quantitative ablation experiment with the gradual removal of the important parts, such as texture decomposition, defect amplification, and contrastive learning was provided and confirmed that every module plays a significant role in enhancing the overall performance and justified the proposed framework of amplification.

Discussion

Ablation study

The analysis of the contribution of each component in STDAN is done through ablation studies. The elimination of texture decomposition is a major cause of the false alarms, and the elimination of defect amplification lowers the sensitivity to tiny defects. The lack of contrastive learning results in weaker anomaly separation, confirming its importance. These analyses show that the modules work together to increase performance.

The ablation experiment aimed at the comparison between the standard pixel-level defect synthesis and the suggested feature-level amplification mechanism. Artificial defects in the pixel-level environment were introduced directly into image space by randomization of masks and perturbation of textures, and the presented method used designed stochastic perturbation of the representations in latent features. Empirical evidence indicates that pixel-level synthesis frequently produces unnatural artifacts that result in heatmaps of anomalies that are noisy, and higher false detections. Conversely, feature-level amplification maintains underlying fabric statistics and amplifies fine-scale defect information, leading to more consistent localization and better detection. These results verify that the learning abnormalities in the feature space give a physically realistic and more discriminative training signal, which justifies the usefulness of the suggested amplification module.

Table 7 contains the quantitative results of ablation and demonstrates the contribution made by each of the components of the proposed STDAN framework. The model with the defect amplification module removed (Variant A) attains an AUC of 95.83% and F1-score of 92.76, which means that texture decomposition is not sufficient to extract small signs of anomalies. An addition of pixel-level defect augmentation (Variant B) yields a slight increase in performance, with a final AUC of 96.41% and F1-score of 93.65%, however, because of artificial artifacts posed by image-space perturbation, results are worse than those of the full model. In comparison, the full STDAN architecture, using feature-level defect amplification, has the best performance at 98.21 AUC and 96.28 F1-score and shows controlled latent-space perturbation generates more discriminative representations of anomalies and texture consistency. These findings have been found to confirm quantitatively that the implementation of texture decomposition and feature-space defect amplification are a much more effective method of object detection and localization than simple versions.

The outcomes indicate that the elimination of the defect amplification module (Variant A) causes the performance deterioration to be observed, which proves that texture decomposition is not enough to learn the discriminative anomaly representations. Even though pixel-level augmentation (Variant B) is more effective than Variant A, it emerges with unrealistic artifacts that leave some consistency in detection. Contrastingly, the suggested feature-level amplification causes physically realistic perturbations in latent representations, which allows the model to acquire subtle defect features without altering normal texture statistics. This will result in excellent localization of anomalies and overall detection.

Robustness Analysis was conducted to test the performance of STDAN in different environmental conditions, such as the change in brightness, Gaussian noise, contrast difference, and perspective distortions. Based on the findings, the suggested texture-decomposition model is capable of stable localization of anomalies in the face of moderately serious perturbations, and its generalization to dynamically changing datasets is high. However, at times, extreme changes in illumination and extreme geometric distortions can result in a false reconstruction of texture, resulting in a false positive or defects missed. To clearly identify these limitations, a short analysis of a failure case is thus included to give a realistic evaluation of the model’s robustness in real industrial settings.

Limitations

STDAN has its limitations, even though it has strengths. The method assumes that the training data is highly free of defects and that high levels of contamination can reduce performance. Also, the amplification at the feature level adds additional computational costs during the training. The future will introduce work to enhance resilience to polluted training data, simplify the computation, and expand the framework to multi-modal inspection conditions such asinfrared or hyperspectral analysis of fabrics.

Conclusion

This paper proposed a self-supervised system called STDAN, an automated method for detecting defects in fabrics that integrates both texture decomposition and feature-level defect amplification. STDAN can be used to learn to model residual features, using a texture-consistency encoder, latent-space perturbation, and without defect labels by learning discriminative representations of normal fabric textures. The combination of a contrastive self-supervised objective makes it possible to perform precise separation between normal and anomalous features and allows the model to identify subtle and low-contrast and previously unseen defects with high reliability.

Experimental outcomes of the various public fabric inspection databases have shown that STDAN is a 95% detection, 91% localization, 97% ROC AUC, 1.99 inter-class feature separation, 92% texture robust and 97% defect sensitive approach in comparison to state-of-the-art GAN-based and unsupervised methods. The technique has a high level of resistance to texture, illumination, and change in size defects, and it can be validated that the technique is highly applicable in reality as it is used in the quality control of industries.

Generally, STDAN offers an effective and scalable, annotation-free, and efficient way of automated fabric inspection. The localization and detection of its wide range of textiles are guaranteed by its combination of both texture-conscious learning of features and defect amplification. Future directions will look into reducing the computational complexity, multi-modal fabric analysis, and resistance to highly polluted training data, which will further expand its ability to be used in industry.
