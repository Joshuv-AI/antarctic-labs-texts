Association between BDNF methylation and expression of the NLRP3 inflammasome in the brain is disrupted in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Hao Tang, Helene A Fachim, Michael K Harte, Gavin P Reynolds
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractActivation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and dysfunction of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) are both implicated in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). While in vitro and in vivo models support these mechanisms, there is little research investigating their relationship in human brain tissue, and whether this relationship may be abnormal in the AD brain. Temporal cortex tissue taken post-mortem from AD patients and control subjects was analysed for expression of genes for the NLRP3 pathway (NLRP3, PYCARD, CASP1), the potassium channel THIK1 (KCNK13), and related pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18). BDNF mRNA expression and DNA methylation in the BDNF exon IV promoter were determined. Correlations between these BDNF measures and NLRP3-related genes were investigated. A significant relationship between BDNF mRNA expression and mean methylation was observed in control subjects but not in AD. In controls, significant correlations with both BDNF expression and mean BDNF methylation were observed for NLRP3, CASP1 and IL1B. These correlations were lost in the AD group, which also showed a significant correlation of KCNK13 expression with both BDNF measures, not seen in controls. This study indicates that the relationship between BDNF and NLRP3 activation, as measured by NLRP3 inflammasome components and proinflammatory cytokine products, is disrupted in AD. The contrary correlation emerging in AD between BDNF measures and KCNK13 expression, shown to be elevated in AD, indicates the possible relationship of this finding with BDNF dysfunction. These findings enhance our understanding of AD pathophysiology, demonstrating the importance of understanding the neuroinflammatory-neurotrophic balance in neurodegenerative processes.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, DNA methylation, BDNF, Neuroinflammation, NLRP3 inflammasome

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder and the major cause of dementia in the aging population (Chowdhary et al. 2022). Amyloid beta (Aβ) aggregates and intracellular deposition of neurofibrillary tangles containing phosphorylated tau protein are the characteristic pathological hallmarks of the disease. However, the relationship of this proteinopathy to, and the mechanisms involved in, the neurodegenerative process which results in profound neuronal damage and death remains unclear. Several factors contributing to this neurodegeneration, in addition to amyloid cascade and tau hyperphosphorylation, include neuroinflammation, oxidative stress and failure of neurotransmitter systems (Dash et al. 2025; Nimgampalle et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2022). These known pathologies, as well as currently undetected ones, likely interact with each other, complicating the understanding of the mechanisms underlying neurodegeneration and challenging the development of treatment.

One inflammatory mechanism potentially important in AD is an activation of the NOD-like receptor pyrin domain containing 3 inflammasome (NLRP3). This comprises a complex of three proteins: the cytosolic sensor molecule NLRP3, an apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase-activating recruitment domain (ASC, coded by the PYCARD gene) and an effector molecule, cysteine protease pro-caspase-1 (CASP1) (Rathinam and Fitzgerald 2016). The assembly of ASC with NLRP3 provides the platform for activating pro-caspase-1, leading to caspase-1 recruitment, oligomerization and subsequent pro-enzyme activation (Kelley et al. 2019). Cleavage of pro-interleukins by caspase-1 can produce the mature, biologically activated forms of IL-1β and IL-18 (Lamkanfi and Dixit 2014). While in vitro and in vivo model systems provide strong support for NLRP3 involvement (Heneka et al. 2013; Ising et al. 2019), we previously reported no significant differences between AD and control brain tissue in the gene expression of components of the NLRP3 pathway (Tang and Harte 2021). Nevertheless, a dysfunction leading to increased inflammation, that may be mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome, remains a potential mechanism contributing to the neurodegenerative process in AD.

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is also implicated in AD. BDNF is involved in the survival, development, and function of neurons as well as being a critical factor in synaptic plasticity; it is therefore important in learning and memory processes and their dysfunction (Azman and Zakaria 2022). As such it has been studied in several neurological and psychiatric diseases, including AD. BDNF depletion is associated with several of the pathological hallmarks of AD, notably tau phosphorylation and Aβ accumulation, as well as neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis (Numakawa and Odaka 2021). Aberrations in BDNF expression and its mechanisms of regulation have therefore attracted significant attention due to its potential role in the pathophysiology of the disease.

DNA methylation is an epigenetic mechanism that involves the addition of methyl groups to the DNA molecule, often resulting in gene silencing. In the context of AD, changes in methylation of the BDNF gene have been observed; increased methylation of BDNF promoter regions is associated with reduced gene expression in AD, both in the brain (Rao et al. 2012) and peripheral blood (Nagata et al. 2015; Xie et al. 2017). Notwithstanding some negative reports (Fransquet et al. 2020; Aarons et al. 2019), this hypermethylation has been proposed as a mechanism contributing to a downregulation of BDNF, exacerbating neuronal dysfunction and cognitive deficits in AD.

The mechanisms underlying an altered DNA methylation of BDNF in AD are not entirely understood but may involve oxidative stress, inflammation, and dysregulation of enzymes involved in DNA methylation, such as the DNA methyltransferases (Cheng et al. 2024). Chronic inflammation, a hallmark of AD, can influence epigenetic modifications, leading to changes in gene expression that affect neuronal health and function. Understanding the relationship between BDNF expression, DNA methylation, neuroinflammation and AD is therefore valuable in opening avenues for potential therapeutic interventions.

It is established that inflammation can have effects on BDNF function such that an inflammatory challenge can result in reduced BDNF with consequences that may include reductions in the functional integrity of neurons (Golia et al. 2019). This interaction between inflammation and BDNF extends to mechanisms involving the NLRP3 inflammasome (Fu et al. 2020) (Fig. 1A). In the current study we have investigated the expression and DNA methylation of a regulatory sequence in BDNF in post mortem brain tissue in AD and control subjects and its relationship to the expression of components of the NLRP3 pathway (Fig. 1B). These include the genes NLRP3, PYCARD and CASP1, KCNK13 – coding for the upstream regulatory potassium channel THIK1 which we have previously shown to be increased in expression in AD (Tang et al. 2023) – and IL1B and IL18, genes for the downstream proinflammatory cytokines activated by caspase-1.

Methods

Post mortem AD brain tissue from the temporal cortex was obtained from the Manchester Brain Bank at Salford Royal NHS Foundation Trust. Matched control samples were also supplied by the brain bank. The Manchester Brain Bank has been approved by the Newcastle & North Tyneside 1 Research Ethics Committee (REC reference 09/H0906/52 + 5). Under conditions agreed with the Research Ethics Committee, tissue or data can be supplied for research without the requirement for individual researchers to apply for further ethical approval. All tissue collection, storage and use followed the requirements of the UK Human Tissue Act. All samples were anonymised and taken from brains that had undergone neuropathological assessment. AD cases had a clinical diagnosis of dementia and were all defined as having moderate or advanced Alzheimer’s disease on the basis of Braak staging for the extent of neurofibrillary tangles (stages III-IV and V-VI respectively). The majority of cases also had assessment of amyloid plaques; these results were fully consistent with other clinical and neuropathological diagnostic criteria.

Standard procedures were employed with tissue storage, handling and examination according to UK research ethics. Unfixed samples were stored at − 80◦C immediately after dissection until tissue homogenization. Research was conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Demographic parameters and clinical diagnosis were provided by the brain bank.

Quantitative real-time PCR

RNA expression was quantified using reverse transcriptase semi-quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) as previously described (Tang and Harte 2021). All the procedures were performed according to the MIQE guidelines (Bustin et al. 2009). Briefly, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ Reagent (Thermo Fisher, UK) followed by RNA clean up using Monarch® RNA Cleanup Kit (New England Biolabs, UK). RNA purity and concentrations were assessed using a NanoDrop 2100 (Thermo Fisher, UK). Single-stranded cDNA was synthesized from 1 ng of total RNA using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (ThermoFisher, UK). cDNA concentrations were measured on a NanoDrop 2100 and diluted to 500 ng/µL in RNase-free water.

Reference genes (RGs) were selected as previously described (Tang and Harte 2021). Primer pairs were designed, synthesized and specificity analysed by dissociation curve. RT-qPCR reactions were carried out using Power SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher, UK) on a 7900HT Real-Time PCR System with 384-well format (Applied Biosystems, UK). The final volume for each reaction was 10 µL with 1 µL (0.25 µM) of corresponding primers, 1 µL (500 ng/µL) of total cDNA, 3 µL RNase-free water, and 5 µL SYBR green. All samples were run in triplicate. The thermal cycler parameters were as follows: UDG activation at 50◦C for 2 min, and DNA polymerase activation at 95◦C for 10 min, followed by amplification of cDNA for 40 cycles with denaturation at 95◦C for 15 s and annealing/extension at respective annealing temperature for each gene for 1 min. Dissociation curve analyses were carried out at the end of each run for PCR product verification. The intra-assay variation was controlled as standard deviation of triplicates < 0.2 and was analysed by using raw Ct value of RGs and there were no significant variations between plates.

DNA extraction, bisulphite conversion, and pyrosequencing

Genomic DNA from temporal cortex of human postmortem tissue of AD and matched controls was purified using QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. EpiTec Fast DNA Bisulfite Kit (Qiagen) was used to convert unmethylated cytosine to uracil in the CpG sites with a calculated mean conversion of 99%. For analysis, a previously-studied sequence in the BDNF exon IV promoter containing transcription factor binding sites was amplified by PCR, the percentage methylation at each of four CpG sites determined with a PyroMark Q48 Autoprep (Qiagen UK). PCR reactions, amplification conditions and preparation of forward, reverse and sequencing primers were carried out according to our previous studies (Fachim et al. 2021).

Statistical analysis was undertaken using SPSS v30. Spearman rank tests were used to identify significant correlations, and t-tests or Z-tests were used to identify differences between groups or correlations respectively.

Discussion

These findings demonstrate a relationship between correlates of BDNF transcription – promoter DNA methylation and gene expression – and the expression of NLRP3-related genes in the human brain. These relationships, observed in post-mortem human temporal cortical brain tissue from individuals with age-consistent normal neuropathology, appear to be lost in tissue samples from AD subjects. They are seen in the absence of any significant changes in either these BDNF measures or NLRP3 gene expression in AD. One exception to this is apparent; expression of the THIK1 gene KCNK13, which has a tendency to an inverse relationship with BDNF expression in control subjects, shows a strong and positive correlation in AD tissue, the latter also reflected in the association with BDNF methylation. While we primarily employed rank correlation analyses as a conservative approach avoiding the assumptions in parametric analyses, undertaking a linear regression analysis confirmed our findings in which a highly significant relationship between expression of the six NLRP3 genes and BDNF methylation was observed in control samples but lost in AD.

Some studies have identified a marked reduction in BDNF protein (Hock et al. 2000) and mRNA expression (Holsinger et al. 2000) in the brains and peripheral blood (Gezen-Ak et al. 2013) of individuals with AD, in which the reported reduction correlates with cognitive decline and the neuronal loss characteristic of AD. However while reductions in BDNF are frequently reported, reviews report inconsistencies (Du et al. 2018; Cade et al. 2022) with conflicting findings including increases in the brain (Durany et al. 2000) and increased blood BDNF concentrations in AD patients (Faria et al. 2014). While one interpretation is that such increases may reflect a compensatory activation and/or release of BDNF (Faria et al. 2014), measurement of BDNF in plasma and serum is liable to many artefactual influences that may produce misleading findings (Gejl et al. 2019). Furthermore, there are multiple peripheral sources of BDNF which indicate that blood concentrations and their changes are not solely associated with CNS function. Previous studies also suggest the possible role of neurotrophic factors in addition to BDNF in the pathology of AD (Du et al. 2018; Durany et al. 2000).

We have previously reported that expression of NLRP3 components in the AD temporal cortex is not significantly different from control subjects (Tang and Harte 2021). However, we did observe an elevation in glial fibrillary acidic protein, a marker of astrocytic activation. We have also identified an elevation of KCNK13 expression and a corresponding reduction in KCNK13 promoter methylation in the AD brain, again using temporal cortex tissue samples that have been studied here (Tang et al. 2023).

There is a significant positive correlation between BDNF gene expression and BDNF methylation, an unexpected finding since increased methylation is considered to suppress transcription. However, it is notable that we find lower BDNF methylation, but increased BDNF expression, are both associated with increased expression of NLRP3 and CASP1, and a reduced expression of IL1B. While life events may influence BDNF methylation (Fachim et al. 2021), it does appear that changes in inflammatory status can occur in parallel with BDNF methylation (e.g. Al-Zharani et al. 2025) suggesting a possibe influence of inflammation on BDNF methylation.

However, whatever underlies them, these “normal” relationships between BDNF and inflammatory factors, seen in age-matched control brain tissue which is relatively unaffected by neurodegenerative processes, are lost in AD brain. The effect is apparent for correlations with both expression and BDNF methylation, indicating a disruption of the normal, presumably homeostatic, relationships between these measures of inflammatory activity and the protective neurotrophic factor of BDNF.

It is notable how the relationships with KCNK13 expression are the opposite of those found for NLRP3 and its related genes. We previously showed KCNK13 expression to be activated in the AD brain tissue as well as in an animal model of AD. That this ion-channel gene, coding for THIK1 which contributes to the control of NLRP3 inflammasome function, is upregulated in the AD brain is interesting and our findings suggest an involvement, absent in the control subjects, of BDNF in this process – although whether causal or consequential is unclear.

There are many limitations to consider in this preliminary study. Measurement of gene expression in the post-mortem brain is compromised by the degeneration of RNA; this may not always be accurately compensated for by concurrent measurement of housekeeping gene expression. This is one reason we chose to investigate DNA methylation for BDNF, which is a relatively stable measure and potential correlate of gene expression. However none of the reported measures showed a significant correlation with the post-mortem interval. Further studies investigating protein products would be a valuable approach to corroborating these findings. We were limited to the study of one brain region strongly affected by changes associated with AD; future work including the study of other regions, particularly the hippocampus, would be more informative. Our sample size is substantial for a post mortem study, and brains had undergone neuropathological assessment, although factors such as cause of death and agonal state have not been accounted for and may influence measurements, particularly for RNA.

There remains much to understand about these findings. As yet it is unclear what the direction of causality is: whether, in the normal brain, the control of BDNF expression is changing in response to inflammasome activation, or vice versa. Certainly there is evidence for both processes and it is likely there is a dynamic interrelationship between BDNF expression and inflammatory changes in the brain (Al-Zharani et al., 2025; Dadkhah et al. 2024).

Notwithstanding the preliminary nature of this study, and the various unanswered questions that it raises, the relationships seen in control brains provide evidence for an interrelationship between BDNF function and one aspect of neuroinflammatory activity associated with the innate immune response in the human brain. The loss of this relationship in AD identifies a new feature of the disruption of neural function in the disease; understanding what specific factors underlie this could provide novel opportunities for intervention in ameliorating the consequences of the chronic inflammatory processes occurring in AD.
