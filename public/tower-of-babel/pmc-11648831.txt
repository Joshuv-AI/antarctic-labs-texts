Study of Sterically Crowded Alkanes: Assessment of Non‐Empirical Density Functionals Including Double‐Hybrid (Cost‐Effective) Methods
Authors: P. Maiz‐Pastor, E. Brémond, A. J. Pérez‐Jiménez, C. Adamo, J. C. Sancho‐García
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe theoretically study here the homolytic dissociation reactions of sterically crowded alkanes of increasing size, carrying three different (bulky) substituents such as tert‐butyl, adamantyl, and [1.1.1]propellanyl, employing a family of parameter‐free functionals ranging from semi‐local, to hybrid and double‐hybrid models. The study is complemented with the interaction between a pair of HC(CH3)3 molecules at repulsive and attractive regions, as an example of a system composed by a pair of weakly bound sterically crowded alkanes. We also assessed the effect of incorporating reliable dispersion corrections (i. e., D4 or NL) for all the functionals assessed, as well as the use of a tailored basis set (DH‐SVPD) for non‐covalent interactions, which provides the best trade‐off between accuracy and computational cost for a seemingly extended applications to branched or crowded systems. Overall, the PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD and r2SCAN‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD methods represent an excellent compromise providing relatively low, and thus very competitive, errors at a fraction of the cost of other quantum‐chemical methods in use.

Introduction

The benchmarking of Density Functional Theory (DFT) models for covalently and non‐covalently bound systems still demands a large worldwide effort,[ 1 , 2 ] in the search of the best possible trade‐off between accuracy and computational cost for the corresponding extension to larger and challenging systems. Generally speaking, since the most time‐effective DFT calculations performed with semi‐local functionals are known to lack (e. g., inter‐molecular) long‐range interactions driving the binding of weakly bound systems, some ad hoc and successful corrections have been introduced along the years to cope with this pitfall.[ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] Those corrections, which are added to a standard DFT calculation at a marginal computational cost, are often used as well to deal with intra‐molecular long‐range interactions, have inspired other low‐cost (e. g. DFT‐3c) methods[ 7 , 8 ] or are, generally speaking, also recommended for organic chemistry reactions or for any routine electronic structure calculation. [9]

On the other hand, the assessment of DFT methods is normally pursued upon gathering systems and/or reactions of interest in close and compact datasets, some of them composed of challenging cases from the electronic structure point of view, [10] while providing the corresponding reference values calculated by e. g. a high‐level wavefunction‐based method. Then, those reference values are used to assess other approximations formally scaling lower than the cost of the high‐level method, in the hope of spanning the size of the systems tackled without a significant loss in accuracy, and/or or to learn about the underlying built‐in physics of the DFT approximation chosen. This working principle has motivated the compilation along the years of large datasets of reaction energies[ 11 , 12 ] or weakly bound complexes,[ 13 , 14 , 15 ] extensively used so far.

In this direction, an interesting dataset of homolytic alkane dissociation (into doublet radicals) reactions has recently become available [16] (see Figure 1) gathering reactions named as R1H−R9H and involving three different (bulky) substituents (tert‐butyl, adamantyl, and [1.1.1]propellanyl) affecting the steric crowding of the compounds (see Figure 2). For those R1H−R9H reactions, according to the original publication, [16] the performance of DFT methods largely varied according to: (i) the nature (semi‐local, hybrid or double‐hybrid) of the exchange‐correlation functional used, according to their formulation in terms of density functional approximations and wavefunction‐based ingredients; and (ii) the use of a correction for dispersion interactions, explicitly added following modern schemes (e. g. B3LYP‐D4) or introduced through the underlying development and parameterization of the functional (e. g. M06‐2X [17] ).

Actually, albeit the use of a correction for dispersion interactions generally improves the results given by any density functional, we would like to systematically study its effect for a family of non‐empirical (i. e., free of parameterization) exchange‐correlation models based on the Perdew‐Burke‐Ernzerhof (PBE [18] ) and restored‐regularized Strongly Constrained and Appropriately Normed (r2SCAN [19] ) functionals, encompassing hybrid and double‐hybrid models too. Furthermore, we will also assess a cost‐efficient protocol for those double‐hybrid density functionals, yet allowing for accurate results, based on a tailored basis set (DH‐SVPD, vide infra) for these omnipresent non‐covalent interactions. Note that the conclusions of this study are expected to be transferable to the longstanding issue of protobranching effects[ 10 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] or for the accurate calculation of reaction energies and barrier heights,[ 24 , 25 ] thus constituting a benchmarking effort.

Computational Details

The set of non‐empirical density functionals [26] used here obeys a generalized expression such as: (1) Excρ=axExEXX+1-axExρ+acEcPT2+1-acEcρ,

with ax (ac ) the weight given to the EXact‐eXchange (EXX) and to the 2nd. order Perturbation Theory (PT2) terms, respectively, allowing one to define: (i) a Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) model if ax=ac=0 (e. g. PBE or r2SCAN); (ii) a hybrid density functional expression if ac=0 but keeping ax≠0 (e. g. PBE0 [27] or r2SCAN0 for a value of ax=1/4 ); or (iii) a Quadratic Integrand Double‐Hybrid (QIDH) density functional method if both ax≠0 and ac≠0 taking specifically the values of ax=3-1/3 and ac=1/3 (e. g. PBE‐QIDH [28] or r2SCAN‐QIDH [29] ). For all these functionals, a correction for non‐covalent dispersion effects is added through the D4 [30] or the Non‐Local (NL) formalism, [31] employing for the latter the Vydrov and van Voorhis (VV10) exchange‐correlation functional. [32] For the sake of completeness, seen that the formal computational cost of these methods scales with the system size N from ON3 (GGA) and ON4 (hybrid) to ON5 (double‐hybrid), we will also compare the results with 2nd. order Møller‐Plesset theory (MP2) and its Spin‐Component‐Scaled (SCS‐)MP2 variant, [33] both methods scaling as ON5 too. Note also the existence of other SCS‐based variants[ 34 , 35 ] or MP2‐based extrapolation techniques.[ 36 , 37 ]

All the calculations are done with the very large def2‐QZVP basis set [38] to avoid as much as possible basis set incompleteness issues and basis set superposition errors, employing the ORCA 5.0 package [39] with the RIJCOSX approximation [40] and the corresponding auxiliary basis sets, together with the dense DEFGRID3 numerical integration grid for the DFT calculations. Additionally, for the MP2 and SCS‐MP2 methods we will also assess the effect of the Domain‐based Local Pair Natural Orbital (DLPNO) technique to reduce the associated computational cost [41] (with the TightPNO threshold). The geometry of all the compounds was taken from Ref. [16], as well as the reference (i. e., electronic energies) values for the homolytic dissociation reaction energies and the weakly bound HC(CH3)3 dimer at different intermolecular distances, all calculated at the PNO‐LCCSD(T)‐F12b/AVQZ’/tight high‐level which is believed to be affected by an uncertainty below 0.5 kcal/mol. [42]

Finally, we will also employ the PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD extension of the PBE‐QIDH double‐hybrid method, after coupling the latter functional with a tailored basis set for non‐covalent interactions. That DH‐SVPD basis set consists on a optimization of the small def2‐SVPD basis set, [43] thus not resting on external reference data and considerably reducing the basis set size (and the associated cost consequently) with respect to the def2‐QZVP one (basically a double‐ξ vs. a quadruple‐ξ basis set). This PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD protocol has been successfully applied so far to: (i) a set of isodesmic reactions for increasingly longer alkanes; [44] (ii) the structural parameters of non‐covalently bound systems; [45] (iii) the weakly bound C60 dimers and relative stabilities of C60 isomers; [46] (iv) the non‐covalent interaction energies of the L7 and CiM13 datasets, [47] the latter composed of systems up to more than 1000 atoms and 14000 basis functions; and (v) the calculation of reaction energies and barrier heights of diverse types. [48] We will therefore extend here the validation of this protocol to the set of homolytic dissociation reactions and the weakly bound HC(CH3)3 dimer considered, as well as to the corresponding r2SCAN‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD form.

Results and Discussion

Study of Homolytic (Alkane) Dissociation Reactions

We will use in the following the ‘Mean‐Signed Deviation’ (MSD), the ‘Mean Absolute Deviation’ (MAD), and the ‘Root Mean‐Squared Deviation’ (RMSD) for assessing the performance of all the methods, respectively calculated as MSD=1n∑inxi , MAD=1n∑inxi , and RMSD=1n∑inxi2 , with n the number of RnH reactions for each substituent (xi=ΔrEcalculated-ΔrEreference ) with the reaction energies given as ΔrE=Efragments-Ealkanes (see Figure 1). The maximum (unsigned) error, MAX=maxxi , ∀i∈1,...,n , provided by any of the methods for the whole set of RnH reactions will also be considered. Note that the number n of RnH reactions slightly differs according to the size of the substituents added (n=8 for tert‐butyl, n=7 for adamantyl, and n=9 for [1.1.1]propellanyl).

Since both double‐hybrid density functionals and MP2 methods share the same ON5 formal scaling, we have first calculated the homolytic dissociation reaction energies by canonical MP2 and modified DLPNO‐MP2, SCS‐MP2, and DLPNO‐SCS‐MP2 variants (Table 1). All these methods systematically stabilize the alkanes with respect to the radicals, resulting in a positive MSD (equal to the MAD vaues for all cases) ranging from 11.1 (DLPNO‐SCS‐MP2) to 13.1 (MP2) kcal/mol, and thus considerably large. The use of the DLPNO technique slightly improves the results with respect to the MP2 or SCS‐MP2 method; however, all these methods fall far behind the double‐hybrid models (vide infra) and will thus not be further discussed. Note also that there are extensions to modify MP2(−based) methods for the accurate treatment of non‐covalent interactions;[ 49 , 50 , 51 ] however, that is beyond the main scope of this study which is the assessment of non‐empirical density functionals of varying cost. We will thus analyze next the PBE‐based functionals, with the performance of the corresponding r2SCAN‐based forms shown in another section.

The addition of the D4 correction leads to a generalized improvement of the results, approximately halving the MAD values for PBE, PBE0, and PBE‐QIDH. We have also used for PBE, the functional most affected by errors, a previous but still widely used version for a dispersion correction, D3(BJ), [52] and tested the effect of 3‐body (dubbed as ABC) effects, [53] with slightly poorer results than the D4 expression; we will thus fix the D4 expression for the rest of the methods. Note that the PBE‐D4 and PBE0‐D4 results behave almost equivalently (MAD ≈8.4 kcal/mol for both cases) once that D4 correction is used, which is largely improved by the PBE‐QIDH‐D4 method with a MAD of 2.4 kcal/mol. The effect of the D4 correction affects not very differently the functionals, in the sense that it improves the PBE, PBE0, and PBE‐QIDH result by 54 %, 53 %, and 58 %, respectively, depending if a wavefunction‐based term is progressively introduced or not.

A further improvement is obtained by the NL correction in all cases, still decreasing the MAD value for PBE‐NL and PBE0‐NL to 5.9 and 6.3 kcal/mol, respectively, with PBE‐QIDH‐NL reaching a MAD value as low as 1.8 kcal/mol, which can be considered as an excellent threshold for practical applications to other systems. [54] Interestingly, the PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD method is able to provide also a very competitive MAD value of 2.3 kcal/mol, indeed lower than the PBE‐QIDH itself and very close to the PBE‐QIDH‐D4 estimate, at a fraction (roughly 40 %) of the computational cost of the latter methods used with the very large def2‐QZVP basis set. Therefore, the PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD method constitutes a very promising way to effectively incorporate the covalent and non‐covalent effects.

The good performance of the QIDH‐based methods (PBE‐QIDH, PBE‐QIDH‐D4, PBE‐QIDH‐NL, and PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD) does not arise from a spurious compensation of errors, as can be seen from Figure 3 comparing the values obtained for all the RnH reactions for each method and for each of the substituents. The deviation between the reference and QIDH‐based values increases as a function of the value of n for all substituents, clearly indicating a stronger steric demand which is alleviated incorporating the dispersion corrections. The largest deviation is found for PBE‐QIDH and the R9H reaction with the [1.1.1]propellanyl (Prop) substituent, as it happens for most of the methods according to the MAX values reported in Table 2. The excellent performance of the PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD computational protocol is particularly interesting, with all the points homogeneously affected of small errors.

Comparison With Literature Results and Lessons Gained

The comparison with some of the previously published error values of Ref. [16] is done next, with the due caution seen that the previous study also included some fluorinated reactions substituting H by F atoms. However, as the own authors stated, both hydrogenated (RnH) and fluorinated (RnF) reactions led to close errors if the same functional was employed, thus indicating the transferability of the results between both (hydrogenated and fluorinated) reaction sets. We will qualitatively confirm this finding by taking the PBE0 functional for cross‐comparison (the only functional overlapping in both the original study and here) and recovering the previously published MAD values (in kcal/mol) of 18.3 (PBE0), 9.5 (PBE0‐D4), and 6.1 (PBE0‐NL) for both RnH and RnF reactions. These values need to be compared with our own results of 17.7, 8.4, and 6.3 kcal/mol, respectively, and thus in qualitative agreement (up to 1 kcal/mol) with the statement made before about the close performance of a method for both reaction types.

This allows us to approximately compare next the MAD values obtained before for other (parameterized) double‐hybrid functionals such as PWPB95, [55] ωPr2SCAN50, [56] and revDSD‐PBEP86 [57] for which MAD values of 9.7, 8.4, and 7.9 kcal/mol, respectively, were before obtained; thus serving to situate the PBE‐QIDH functional as the top‐performer (MAD=5.8 kcal/mol) among this set of double‐hybrid functionals, in absence of any correction for dispersion. The addition of the D4 correction to PWPB95, ωPr2SCAN50, and revDSD‐PBEP86 led to still reduced their MAD values to 2.2, 4.7, and 3.6 kcal/mol, to be compared with a PBE‐QIDH‐D4 value of 2.4 kcal/mol. Finally, the NL correction was also added before to PWPB95 and ωPr2SCAN50, giving MAD values of 1.6 and 4.4 kcal/mol, with PBE‐QIDH‐NL approaching the best possible position among the double‐hybrid functionals with a MAD value of 1.8 kcal/mol. Note also the good ranking achieved by the PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD computational protocol, with a MAD value of 2.3 kcal/mol, at a fraction of the cost of all the other methods listed here.

Study of the Weakly Bound HC(CH3)3 Dimer

We have also studied the same prototype system chosen in Ref. [16] as a model to evaluate the performance of the methods for stereoelectronic effects at varying distances, consisting on a HC(CH3)3 dimer (see Figure 4) at fixed monomer‐to‐monomer distances (R) covering both the repulsive and attractive regions (3.0≤R≤5.4 Å) thanks to the PNO‐LCCSD(T)‐F12b/AVQZ’/tight reference results also available for that set of distances. For each of the methods we first calculate the binding energy ΔE=Edimer-2Emonomer to next represent in Figure 5 the deviations δ ΔE of each method with respect to the reference results: δ ΔE=ΔEcalculated-ΔEreference .

Weakly bound HC(CH3)3 dimer at a C−C distance of 0.5 nm (taken as example) between the central C atoms.

Deviations (kcal/mol) for the weakly bound HC(CH3)3 dimer at different C−C distances for PBE and PBE0 (top) as well as for PBE‐QIDH (middle) and r2SCAN‐QIDH (bottom) methods, with and without dispersion corrections. The dotted‐dashed lines indicates a deviation of ±1 kcal/mol with respect to the reference results.

The non‐corrected PBE and PBE0 methods display a large (positive) error at all distances, arising from the too repulsive nature of the functional, although the deviations are progressively reduced for larger distance. The use of either the D4 or the NL correction is an effective way to alleviate the overrepulsion observed for the PBE and PBE0 methods, behaving similarly except for very short distances with both corrections providing deviations below ±1 kcal/mol (roughly speaking) for R>4.0 Å. The PBE‐QIDH method, in absence of any dispersion correction, is known to partially introduce the attractive nature of the weak interactions thanks to the the PT2 term entering into its formulation, see Eq. (1), and thus reducing the overrepulsion of the PBE and PBE0 methods for all distances. However, it is also seen from Figure 5 how it overestimates the non‐covalent interactions (too attractive) for very short intermolecular distances. Hence, the addition of a dispersion correction (PBE‐QIDH‐D4 or PBE‐QIDH‐NL) does not help to reduce the deviation for that region, and actually the error is magnified up to a point around R>4.0 Å, where it is able to provide a low (below ±1 kcal/mol) deviation.

Interestingly, the cost‐effective PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD method displays an almost systematic (and small) deviation for all the range of R distances examined, with a close agreement with the reference values for all repulsive and attractive regions, and thus within the “chemical accuracy” threshold (a deviation approximately at ±1 kcal/mol) for most of the points. Indeed, if we average the (absolute) values of the δ ΔE deviations (δΔE=1ni∑R1|δΔE| , with ni the number of distances scanned) we obtain numbers as high as 3.73 and 3.45 kcal/mol for PBE and PBE0, respectively, which are considerably reduced upon including the D4 (1.05 and 0.88 kcal/mol for PBE and PBE0, respectively) and NL (1.78 and 1.47 kcal/mol for PBE and PBE0, respectively) corrections. However, the PBE‐QIDH functional deserves a more detailed inspection from its lower average deviation of 1.28 kcal/mol, and thus largely decreased with respect to PBE or PBE0. The use of the D4 or NL correction gives an average deviation of 1.34 and 1.47 kcal/mol, respectively, and thus slightly larger than that of the uncorrected method. The evolution of the curves shown in Figure 5 helps to understand again that performance. Finally, for the cost‐effective PBE‐QIDH/DH‐SVPD method we find the lowest average deviation among all the evaluated methods and their variants, which amounts to only 0.85 kcal/mol.

r2SCAN vs. PBE‐Based Results

Conclusions

The accurate description of stereoelectronic effects in sterically hindranced hydrocarbons remains challenging, needing the reliable description of mid‐ and long‐range electronic interactions. A set of homolytic dissociation reactions of crowded (singlet) alkanes into (doublet) fragments was recently made available in the literature, with the aim of assessing the accuracy of different DFT methods for describing this issue. These systems are complemented with the study of a weakly bound HC(CH3)3 dimer at several intermolecular distances covering different electronic ranges. This contribution compares the results from several non‐empirical density functionals against the high‐level reference results available for these systems, searching the best trade‐off between accuracy and computational cost.

We have assessed standard and dispersion‐corrected semi‐local, hybrid, and double‐hybrid methods, all of them based on the parameter‐free PBE or r2SCAN exchange‐correlation forms, together with a very large (def2‐QZVP) basis set to avoid any basis set numerical error. Additionally, we have also non‐empirically combined these forms with defined weights of exact‐like exchange and 2nd. order perturbation theory, resulting in a systematic improvement of the results. The advantage of this procedure, compared with previous results, relies on the absence of any parameterization throughout all rungs of Jacob's Ladder studied, thus disentangling the effect of: (i) the use of one (PBE) or another (r2SCAN) exchange‐correlation expression through the hierarchy of approximations; (ii) the role played by wavefunction‐based exchange and correlation effects incorporated into the hybrid or double‐hybrid expressions; and (iii) the incorporation of a modern dispersion correction (i. e., the D4 pairwise expression or the VV10 non‐local correlation functional) at all levels. Note that the use of a double‐hybrid density functionals is an effective way to incorporate all the concurring electronic effects at all interacting ranges.

Overall, the double‐hybrid functionals tested (PBE‐QIDH and r2SCAN‐QIDH) are the most accurate methods for any of the respective families (PBE‐based or r2SCAN‐based) even if the lower rungs of the hierarchy (e. g. PBE0 or r2SCAN0) are combined with those accurate D4 or non‐local dispersion corrections. However, the price to be paid is the higher computational cost of the former vs. the latter, formally scaling as ON5 vs. ON4 , respectively, with N related to the size of the system. Interestingly, in the search of the best trade‐off between accuracy and computational cost, the combination of those PBE‐QIDH and r2SCAN‐QIDH expressions with a small and tailored basis set (DH‐SVPD) for non‐covalent interactions, thus considerably reducing the corresponding computational cost with respect to the original calculations, preserves the accuracy of the dispersion‐corrected PBE‐QIDH or r2SCAN‐QIDH methods at a fraction of their cost. We thus believe these results are interesting and timely for applications to other real‐world sytems involving crowded and complex sytems.
