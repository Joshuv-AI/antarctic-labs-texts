Spheres and balls as independence complexes
Susan M. Cooper, Sara Faridi, Thiago Holleben, Lisa Nicklasson, Adam Van Tuyl

Abstract
. The terms “whiskering”, and more generally “grafting”, refer to adding generators to any monomial ideal to make the resulting ideal Cohen-Macaulay. We investigate the independence complexes of simplicial complexes that are constructed through a whiskering or grafting process, and we show that these independence complexes are (generalized) Bier balls. More specifically, the independence complexes are either homeomorphic to a ball or sphere. In a related direction, we classify when the independence complexes of very well-covered graphs are homeomorphic to balls or spheres.

In 1990, Villarreal

From an algebraic perspective, Villarreal’s construction is intrinsically related to polarizations of monomial ideals. In view of this, Faridi in 2005

Our first contribution in this paper is to show that Murai’s construction coincides with Faridi’s construction, in the same sense that Bier’s construction coincides with Villarreal’s. We begin with a review of necessary background

2.1.
Simplicial Complexes

A (abstract)
simplicial complex

\Delta
on a

is a set of subsets of

with the condition
that if

and

, then

.
An element of
\Delta
is a called a
face
of
\Delta
, and
a
facet
of
\Delta
is a maximal face under inclusion. The

of a face

\dim(F)=|F|-1
,

of

is the maximum of the dimensions of its faces,
and
\Delta
is

if all its facets have the same dimension.
A

is a simplicial complex with exactly one facet.
We often denote
\Delta
as
\langle F_{1},\ldots,F_{q}\rangle
meaning
F_{1},\ldots,F_{q}
are the facets of
\Delta
(in particular, a simplicial complex is uniquely determined by its facets).
The

and

of a face

of
\Delta
are, respectively, the subcomplexes

\mathrm{link}_{\Delta}(F)=\{G\in\Delta\colon G\cup F\in\Delta,\ G\cap F=\emptyset\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathrm{del}_{\Delta}(F)=\{G\in\Delta\colon G\cap F=\emptyset\}.

A subset
W\subseteq V
is an
independent set
of

\Delta
if it contains no facet of
\Delta
, and

\Ind(\Delta)
, called the
independence complex
of
\Delta

is the simplicial complex whose faces are the independent sets of
\Delta
; in other words,

\Ind(\Delta)=\{W\subseteq V\colon W\text{ is an independent set of }\Delta\}.

For every simplicial complex
\Delta
, we may associate a topological space
|\Delta|
contained in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some
k
such that for every face
\sigma\in\Delta
,
|\Delta|
contains the convex hull of the corresponding vertices of
\sigma
(see
[
25

for more details on this construction).
We call
|\Delta|
the

of
\Delta
.
When the geometric realization of a

simplicial complex
\Delta
is homeomorphic to a sphere

, we say
\Delta
is a
simplicial sphere
, and when it is homeomorphic to a ball
B^{d}
, we say
\Delta
is a
simplicial ball
.

In this paper we are interested in
identifying simplicial spheres and
simplicial balls. One way to determine this property is to check
if the complex is a shellable (or even more strongly, vertex decomposable) pseudomanifold.

Our main context is the “Cohen-Macaulay” property in algebra (
Definition

2.5
), which forces simplicial complexes to be pure. Therefore, we will focus on pure simplicial complexes for the next definitions.

Definition 2.1

(
Pseudomanifolds, shellable, vertex decomposable).

Let
\Delta
be a pure
d
-dimensional simplicial complex.

•

\Delta
is

if its facets can be

such that for all

\langle F_{i}\rangle\cap\langle F_{1},\ldots,F_{i-1}\rangle

.

•

\Delta
is

if

(1)

\Delta=\emptyset
, or

\Delta
is a simplex, or

•

•

•

•

The series of implications in (

) shows how these classes of simplicial complexes relate to one another. In the sections that follow, we will rely on the following statement to detect if a simplicial complex is a simplicial sphere or ball.

Theorem 2.2

(
[

.

Let
\Delta
be a shellable pseudomanifold. Then

•

if
\Delta

•

if
\Delta

2.2.
Square-free monomial ideals

There are two one-to-one correspondences between simplicial complexes and square-free

\Delta
with vertex set

2.3.
The Cohen-Macaulay property

The Cohen-Macaulay property – defined algebraically for a local ring
R
via the property that
\depth(R)=\dim(R)
– is the dominating algebraic property of the square-free monomial ideals that we study in this paper. For quotients by square-free monomial ideals, the Cohen-Macaulay property was characterized by Reisner
[
29
]
in terms of the vanishing of reduced homology groups of the links of faces of Stanley-Reisner complexes. Reisner’s criterion has been used to great effect in combinatorics ever since its appearance, and remains the most concrete description of Cohen-Macaulayness for the class of square-free monomial ideals. In
Definition

2.5
we will use Reisner’s criterion as the definition of Cohen-Macaulay square-free monomial ideals.

Definition 2.5

(
Cohen-Macaulay ideals (Reisner’s Criterion)).

Let
I
be a square-free monomial ideal
of
R=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
with non-face
complex
\Delta=\mathcal{N}(I)
. Then

R/I
is a
Cohen-Macaulay ring
if and only if
for any
F\in\Delta
,

\tilde{H}_{i}(\mathrm{link}_{\Delta}(F),k)=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad i<\dim(\mathrm{link}_{\Delta}(F)).

We call
\Delta
a
Cohen-Macaulay
simplicial complex
if
R/\mathcal{N}(\Delta)

is a Cohen-Macaulay ring.

The simplest example of a simplicial complex that satisfies the conditions in
Definition

2.5
is a simplex, where all homologies of links are zero.
A more interesting basic example is a hollow simplex, where all the link homologies are one-dimensional.
The hollow simplex is
an example of what is known as a “homology sphere”. For a field
k
, a simplicial
\Delta
is a
k
-homology sphere
if for all
F\in\Delta

\tilde{H}_{i}(\mathrm{link}_{\Delta}(F),k)=\begin{cases}k&\mbox{if $i=\dim(\mathrm{link}_{\Delta}(F))$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Simplicial spheres, introduced earlier, are examples of homology spheres and are therefore Cohen-Macaulay (
[
30
, Theorem 5.1, Corollary 5.2]
). It is known that every Cohen-Macaulay
simplicial complex is strongly-connected (
[
6
, Proposition 11.7]
,
[
2
, Proposition 1.2.12]
).

The various classes of simplicial complexes
introduced in this section satisfy
the implications

(2.2)

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\mbox{simplicial sphere}&\Rightarrow&\mbox{homology sphere}&\Rightarrow&\mbox{pseudomanifold}&\Rightarrow&\mbox{pure}\\
&&&\Searrow&&\Searrow&\Uparrow\\
\mbox{vertex decomposable}&\Rightarrow&\mbox{shellable}&\Rightarrow&\mbox{Cohen--Macaulay}&\Rightarrow&\mbox{strongly-connected}\\
\end{array}.

All of the reverse implications in (
2.2
)
fail to hold in general. However, as we
will show in the sequel,
implications can be reversed for special families of independence complexes.

We conclude this section with a description of the “polarization” of a monomial ideal, which is an operation that transforms a general monomial ideal into a square-free one, while maintaining its Cohen-Macaulay properties. If
m=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}\in k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is a monomial, then its
polarization

{\mathcal{P}}(m)
is the square-free monomial

{\mathcal{P}}(m)=(x_{1,1}x_{1,2}\cdots x_{1,a_{1}})\cdots(x_{n,1}\cdots x_{n,a_{n}})\in k[x_{i,j}\colon 1\leq i\leq n,\ 1\leq j\leq a_{i}].

If
I
is minimally generated by monomials
m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}
, then the
polarization of

I
is the square-free monomial ideal

{\mathcal{P}}(I)=\big({\mathcal{P}}(m_{1}),\ldots,{\mathcal{P}}(m_{q})\big)

in the polynomial ring containing all variables appearing in the monomials
{\mathcal{P}}(m_{1}),\ldots,{\mathcal{P}}(m_{q})

3.
Grafting, whiskering, spheres, and generalized Bier balls

When the Cohen-Macaulay property is present in a monomial ideal, both its facet and Stanley-Reisner complexes demonstrate strong combinatorial characteristics. However, despite Reisner’s criterion, it is quite difficult to determine if a ring is Cohen-Macaulay, because the process involves computing homology groups, which itself could depend on the characteristic of the base field. A fruitful line of research, therefore, has been to identify classes of monomial ideals
I

\mathcal{N}(I)
or

is Cohen-Macaulay no matter what field we use.
For example, when

is a simplicial sphere,
then

[

This is a generalization of

[

]

Remark 3.3

(
Whiskering a graph

[

)
.

In 1990, Villarreal introduced the whiskering construction for the edge ideal of a graph, citing Vasconcelos and Herzog’s observations that this process served as a way to obtain a Cohen-Macaulay square-free monomial ideal
J=w(I)
from any square-free monomial ideal
I

Then in 2005, Faridi introduced the notion of grafted simplicial complexes to generalize Villarreal’s construction, in the sense that higher degree monomials could be added to any monomial ideal to produce a Cohen-Macaulay ideal. Both of these constructions were introduced in a different language by Bier in an unpublished manuscript from 1992, and Murai in
[
27
]
. The notion of a whiskered graph specifically has also appeared in the graph theory literature under several names, for example the “leafy-extension” of a graph
[
8
]
and the “corona product” of a graph with a single vertex
[
19
]
.

As we will see below in
Theorem

, the independence complex of a grafted complex is exactly what is known as a
generalized Bier ball
, which was defined by Murai
[
27
]
as the Stanley-Reisner complex of the polarization of an artinian monomial ideal.
Bier spheres first appeared in unpublished work of Thomas Bier in 1992
according to
[
27
]
.
This construction was then generalized by Murai in
[
27
]
, where it is shown that the independence complex of the polarization of any artinian monomial ideal is a simplicial ball or sphere. The original case due to Bier is the case of an artinian monomial ideal that contains the square of every variable, or, by
Theorem

3.4
, the independence complex of a whiskered simplicial complex. Generalized Bier balls are always balls, except for one special case where they turn out to be spheres (as shown below).

4.
Simplical balls and spheres via general whiskering

The previous section showed that
grafting a simplicial complex, which was defined in 2005 as a generalization of Villarreal’s graph whiskering from 1990, results in a simplicial complex whose independence complex is a simplicial
ball or sphere.
In 2012 Cook and Nagel
[
10
]
introduced another generalization of Villarreal’s graph-whiskering called the
“clique-whiskering” of graphs. To clique-whisker a graph,
the vertex set
V
is partitioned into

V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{s}

such that the induced graph on each

is a clique, that is, a subset of the vertices
that are all adjacent to each other.
For each clique, a new vertex is added to the graph,
and this new vertex is joined to all vertices in the clique. Whiskered graphs are then the case that the graph
is partitioned into cliques of single vertices.
The independence complexes of clique-whiskered graphs
enjoy many similar properties to
independence complexes of whiskered graphs, e.g.,
they are pure and vertex decomposable, and in particular, Cohen-Macaulay.

Biermann and Van Tuyl

in 2013 generalized
Cook and Nagel’s construction to all simplicial complexes.
In vew of the earlier discussions, it is natural to ask if any of these Cohen-Macaulay “generalized whiskered complexes” of Biermann and Van Tuyl are
simplicial balls or spheres.
It turns out that this is rarely the case, and when it is, the generalized whiskered complex is in fact the independence complex of a grafted (or more specifically, whiskered) complex.

5.
Simplicial balls and spheres as flag complexes of very well-covered graphs

The implications in (
2.2
) summarized
how one property of a simplicial complex leads to another one.
While the reverse implications fail to hold in general, in this
section we show that all the implications (except
the pure property) can be reversed for independence
complexes of very well-covered graphs. Some of these reverse implications were discovered earlier in
[
12
,
24
]
. Our main contribution is to show that
the independence complex of a Cohen-Macaulay very well-covered graph with no isolated vertices is always a simplicial ball or sphere.
As a by-product of our work, we conclude that assuming the size of a facet of a pure flag complex is half the number of vertices – a restriction often considered in graph theory – is enough to make several interesting properties, such as being Cohen-Macaulay and being a simplicial ball be equivalent.

We begin by recalling some relevant graph
theory.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with vertex

and edge set

\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\}
is not an independent
set of
G
, then there is at least one edge
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}

among these vertices. Suppose that
i<j
. Note
that
\{y_{i},x_{j}\}
is not an edge in
G
by
Theorem

5.3
(5).
Consequently,
\{y_{i},x_{j}\}
belongs to some
facet

H
of
G
as every independent set belongs
to a maximal independent set.
Then
H\setminus\{y_{i}\}
is a face of
dimension
d-2
that is contained in
H
. However,
it is not contained in
(H\setminus\{y_{i}\})\cup\{x_{i}\}
since

x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are adjacent. So
H\setminus\{y_{i}\}

is only contained in the facet
H
.

These two cases now
show that
\Ind(G)
has a boundary.
∎

We are now ready to summarize
our new contributions to the
properties of
\Ind(G)
when
G
is a
very well-covered graph. In particular,
all the reverse implications in (
2.2
) that
do not involve the pure property hold
for this class of simplicial complexes.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
G
be a very well-covered graph on
2d
vertices with no
isolated vertices.
Then the following
conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\Ind(G)
is strongly connected;

(2)

I(G)
is Cohen-Macaulay;

(3)

\Ind(G)
is a pseudomanifold;

(4)

\Ind(G)
is a simplicial ball or sphere.

In particular, the following more refined statements are equivalent:

•

G=dK_{2}
for some
d\geq 1
;

•

\Ind(G)
is a pseudomanifold with no boundary;

•

\Ind(G)
is a homology sphere;

•

\Ind(G)
is a simplicial sphere.

Proof.

Theorems

5.2
and
5.5
show the
equivalence of
(1),(2)
and
(3)
.
We always have
(4)
implies
(3)
.
It remains to show
(3)
implies
(4)
. If
{\rm Ind}(G)
is a
pseudomanifold with boundary, we have
I(G)
is Cohen-Macaulay.
Since
I(G)
is Cohen-Macaulay,
we know that
\Ind(G)
is shellable
by
Theorem

5.2
.
So
\Ind(G)
is a shellable pseudomanifold. The rest now follows
from
Theorem

5.5
, and the the fact that a shellable pseudomanifold is a simplicial sphere if it has no boundary, and is a simplicial ball otherwise
by
Theorem

2.2
.
∎

It follows immediately that if
G
is a whiskered graph, then
{\rm Ind}(G)
is a pseudomanifold, this was one of the main tools the authors used in
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
.
We can apply
Corollary

5.6
to deduce a further corollary.
Recall that a graph
G=(V,E)
is a
bipartite
graph
if the vertices can be partitioned as
V=V_{1}\cup V_{2}
such that
every edge
e\in E
satisfies
e\cap V_{1}\neq\emptyset
and

e\cap V_{2}\neq\emptyset
.

Corollary 5.7

(
Cohen-Macaulay bipartite graphs).

Let
G
be a bipartite graph with no
isolated vertices.
If
I(G)
is Cohen-Macaulay,
then
\Ind(G)
is a pseudomanifold. If
G\neq dK_{2}
then

\Ind(G)
is a simplicial ball, and otherwise it is a simplicial sphere.

Proof.

Let
V=V_{1}\cup V_{2}
be the bipartition of
V
. Since
I(G)
is
Cohen-Macaulay,
\Ind(G)
is
pure, and since
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are maximal independent sets, we must have
|V_{1}|=|V_{2}|=|V|/2
. So,
G
must be very well-covered,
since every maximal independent set must also have cardinality
|V|/2
.
Now apply
Corollary

5.6
.
∎

6.
Applications to Gorenstein rings

In
[
30
, Theorem II.5.1]
Stanley shows that if

R/\mathcal{N}(\Delta)
is a Gorenstein ring, where
R=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
and
\mathcal{N}(\Delta)
is the Stanley-Reisner ideal of
\Delta
, then the simplicial complex
\Delta
is either a
k
-homology sphere, or a cone over some
k
-homology sphere. In particular, if every variable of
R
divides a generator of
\mathcal{N}(\Delta)
, then
R/\mathcal{N}(\Delta)
is Gorenstein if and only if
\Delta
is a
k
-homology sphere, and in particular,
\Delta
has non-trivial homology.

Throughout this paper, we have shown that two classes of complexes, namely the independence complexes of grafted complexes and very well-covered graphs are always balls, except in the cases where their respective Stanley-Reisner ideals are complete intersections. In a different language, our result implies the following:

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
I
be a square-free monomial ideal such that either

(1)

the facet complex of
I
is grafted, or

(2)

the facet complex of
I
is a very-well covered graph.

Then
I
is Gorenstein if and only if it is a complete intersection.

Proof.

Let
\Delta
be the Stanley-Reisner complex of
I
and assume
\Delta
is either the independence complex of a grafted complex or the independence complex of a very well-covered graph. By
Theorems

3.4
and
5.3
we know
\Delta
is not a cone over a face, and in particular by
[
30
, Theorem 5.1 Part 2]
, if
I
is Gorenstein,
\Delta
must have non-trivial homology. The result then follows since by
Theorems

3.4
and
5.5

\Delta
has non-trivial homology exactly when the corresponding Stanley-Reisner ideal is a complete intersection.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Since polarization preserves the Gorenstein property, and the polarization of every artinian monomial ideal is grafted (
Theorem

3.4
),
Proposition

6.1
(1) can be seen as a different proof for the fact that the only Gorenstein artinian monomial ideals are the monomial artinian complete intersections.

In the case of edge ideals, known results from graph theory on well-covered graphs
[
28
,
17
]
can be used to directly obtain more information on the classification of Gorenstein edge ideals. For the sake of completeness, below we include a proof of a slightly weaker version of
[
20
, Remark 3.5]
to showcase how the techniques from this paper can be applied to the problem of classifying Gorenstein square-free monomial ideals.

Proposition 6.3

(
see
[
20
, Remark 3.5]
).

Suppose that
G
is a graph such that
I(G)
is
Gorenstein. Then the connected components
of
G
are either
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
, or graphs
with girth
\leq 5
.

Proof.

By
[
31
, Lemma 2.2]
,
G
is a
Gorenstein graph if and only if each connected
component of
G
is Gorenstein. A direct
computation shows that
K_{1}
and
K_{2}

are Gorenstein. So, it is suffices
to show that if
G
is a connected Gorenstein graph
with
G\neq K_{1}
and
G\neq K_{2}
, its girth
is at most five.

Since
I(G)
is Gorenstein,
I(G)

is Cohen-Macaulay, and
thus
G
is well-covered.
Suppose
G
has girth six or greater.
By
[
17
, Corollary 5]
,
either
G=C_{7}
, or
G
is whiskered. Since
I(C_{7})
is not Cohen-Macaulay,
C_{7}
is
also not Gorenstein. Hence,
we only have to show
G
is not whiskered.

To see that
G
cannot be a whiskered graph, note that by
[
30
, Theorem II.5.1]
the independence complexes of Gorenstein graphs without isolated vertices correspond to flag homology spheres. In particular, the independence complex of
G
has non-trivial homology. However, the independence complex of a whiskered graph that is not
dK_{2}
is a simplicial ball by
Corollary

5.6
and hence has trivial homology. So we conclude
G
cannot be a whiskered graph. So the girth of
G
is
at most five.
∎
