Association between systemic inflammation response index and advanced stages in U.S. cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome patients: a cross-sectional study using NHANES 2015–2020 data
Authors: Jing Zhang, Dingyuan Tu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe American Heart Association introduced cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, a highly prevalent multistage comorbidity in the U.S. We investigated the association between the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), a new inflammatory marker, and advanced CKM syndrome.MethodsUsing National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data (2015–2020), we classified U.S. adults into five CKM syndrome stages (0–4). CKM syndrome stages 3 or 4 were considered advanced. The SIRI is determined by the formula: (monocyte count×neutrophil count)/lymphocyte count. Multivariable weighted logistic regression was used to assess advanced CKM syndrome risk by SIRI quartiles. Mediation analysis evaluated contributions of other inflammatory factors.ResultsAmong 10,405 CKM syndrome patients, 1,730 had advanced stages. Patients in the highest SIRI quartile had an odds ratio of 1.61 (95% confidence interval: 1.25–2.08) for being in advanced stages compared to those in the lowest quartile, with a 14% risk increase per unit rise in SIRI. Receiver operating characteristic analysis indicated SIRI had superior discriminative ability over other inflammatory indices for predicting advanced CKM syndrome. Mediation analysis revealed that high sensitivity C-reactive protein accounted for a minor proportion (4.94%) of the association between SIRI and advanced CKM syndrome, suggesting SIRI captures distinct inflammatory pathways.ConclusionsElevated SIRI independently predicted advanced CKM syndrome in U.S. adults and outperformed established inflammatory biomarkers, highlighting its clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05109-5.Keywords: Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, Cardiovascular disease, Systemic inflammation response index, National health and nutrition examination survey

Introduction

Acknowledging new evidence linking cardiovascular disease (CVD) with significant comorbidities, the American Heart Association (AHA) established the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome framework in October 2023 [1]. The disorder is described as systemic, arising from the pathophysiological interactions between metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and CVD. The AHA developed a framework to demonstrate the progression of CKM syndrome, categorizing stages from 0 (no risk factors) to 4 (clinical CVD) according to risk factors and established disease. A recent study found that CKM syndrome is widespread among U.S. adults, with 90% having stage 1 or higher and 15% diagnosed with stages 3 or 4 [2]. The significant economic and social burden associated with CKM syndrome underscores the urgent need for measures to delay its progression.

In addition to the risk factors used to establish CKM syndrome stage construct, several other factors (e.g. adverse social determinants of health, mental health disorders, sleep disorders and chronic inflammatory conditions) increase the likelihood of progression along CKM syndrome stages [1]. Among these factors, systemic inflammation plays a crucial role in numerous chronic diseases, impacting the cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic systems. The system inflammation response index (SIRI) is integrated and innovative complete blood cell count (CBC)-derived inflammatory biomarker has recently been derived from counts of neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes. This index is extensively utilized in research to evaluate the link between chronic inflammatory status and various human diseases, including CVD [3], CKD [4], metabolic syndrome [5], cancers [6–8], demonstrating superior predictive capability compared to the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet to lymphocyte ratio, and other inflammatory markers. However, no prior studies have explored the relationship between this newly established, readily available index and CKM syndrome.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the link between SIRI and advanced stages of CKM syndrome. Furthermore, we also compared its predictive value against other CBC-derived and high sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) derived inflammatory biomarkers, including systemic immune inflammation index (SII), NLR, inflammatory burden index (IBI) and hsCRP.

Methods

Study design and population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) employs a complex, stratified, multistage probability design to acquire representative samples of the noninstitutionalized civilian U.S. population. It is approved by the National Center for Health Statistics research ethics review board. Participants were interviewed at home to collect demographic, socioeconomic, dietary, and health-related information. They also received a detailed medical examination in the mobile examination center. All NHANES protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants provided informed consent.

This study analyzed NHANES data from 2015 to 2020, as the survey began categorizing Asian race separately starting with the 2011–2012 cycle, and information on hs-CRP was only available in NHANES 2015–2020. Participants will be excluded if they meet any of the following criteria: (1) under 20 years of age (n = 14,265); (2) being pregnant (n = 214); (3) lacking demographics data (marital status, household income, and education attainment) (n = 2,815) or questionnaire data (smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, and diet quality) (n = 1,689); (4) missing data on complete blood count (n = 1,611) or hs-CRP (n = 396); (5) lacking information on CKM syndrome indicators (n = 1,907). Finally, a total of 11,888 adults were included in this study (Fig. 1).

Assessment of CBC and HsCRP derived inflammatory indicators

In NHANES 2015–2020, complete blood count parameters were derived using the Beckman Coulter DxH 800 instrument. Lymphocyte, neutrophil, and monocyte counts were presented as ×103 cells/µL. Besides, hs-CRP laboratory analysis was done using the immunoturbidometry method and a Roche Cobas 6000 Analyzer. In our study, SIRI was identified as the primary exposure variable. The SIRI level was determined by the formula: monocyte count×neutrophil count/lymphocyte count [9]. Besides, we also explored the association of other CBC and hsCRP derived inflammatory biomarkers with advanced CKM syndrome. These inflammatory biomarkers included SII (platelet count×neutrophil count/lymphocyte count), NLR (neutrophil count/lymphocyte count), IBI (neutrophil count×hsCRP/lymphocyte count) and hsCRP [10, 11].

Definition of CKM syndrome stage 0 to 4

This study categorizes CKM syndrome stages 0 to 4 following the AHA Presidential Advisory Statement on CKM syndrome [1], incorporating modifications for NHANES data based on the criteria from Aggarwal et al. [2]. Stage 0 comprised participants with a body mass index (BMI) under 25 kg/m2 (or < 23 kg/m2 if Asian ethnicity), with normal waist circumference (< 88 and < 102 cm for women and men, or < 80 and < 90 cm for women and men if Asian ethnicity), without metabolic risk factors (hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, prediabetes), and no signs of CKD or subclinical or clinical CVD. Stage 1 was defined by a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (or < 23 kg/m2 if Asian ethnicity), waist circumference ≥ 88/102 cm in women/men (or 80/90 cm in women/men if Asian ethnicity), or prediabetes. Stage 2 included participants with metabolic risk factors (hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, diabetes), or moderate-to-high-risk CKD. Based on the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes clinical practice guideline recommended by the AHA [1], the CKD risk stage was defined according to estimated glomerular filtration rate and urinary albumin-creatinine ratio categories as: low risk, moderate risk, high risk, and very high risk. Stage 3 was identified based on the presence of a high predicted 10-year CVD risk (based on the Framingham risk score) or very-high-risk CKD. Stage 4 comprised participants with established clinical CVD (coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, heart failure, or stroke). According to Aggarwal et al. [2], CKM syndrome included stage 1 or higher, with advanced stages being stage 3 or 4, as these stages indicate participants with or at high risk for CVD.

Covariates

Covariates were chosen based on previous literature of SIRI and CKM syndrome [12, 13], including age, sex, ethnicity, educational level, family income, marital status, smoking status, alcohol status, physical activity, and overall diet quality. Ethnicity was classified into Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic Asian, Hispanic and other race or ethnicity. Education levels were categorized into three categories: less than high school, high school or equivalent, and college or above. Family income was represented by the income to poverty ratio, which is the annual family income divided by the poverty guideline. This ratio was categorized into three groups: less than 1, 1 to 3, and more than 3 [14]. Marital status was dichotomized into coupled, and single or separated. Smoking status was categorized as never smokers (< 100 cigarettes lifetime), former smokers (≥ 100 cigarettes lifetime and no smoking now), and current smokers (≥ 100 cigarettes lifetime and currently smoking). Alcohol status was classified as non-drinker, current heavy drinkers (≥ 3 drinks per day for females, ≥ 4 drinks per day for males, or binge drinking [≥ 4 drinks on same occasion for females, ≥ 5 drinks on same occasion for males] on 5 or more days per month), and current low to moderate drinkers (not meet the criterion above). Physical activity level was defined according to the frequency and intensity of participant’s self-reported leisure-time physical activity in the past month: inactive, insufficient, and active, based on metabolic equivalent intensity levels [15]. Diet was assessed with the participant’s 24-hour dietary recall, and diet quality was measured using the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015). It was developed by the National Cancer Institute and the U.S. Department of Agriculture to measure the overall diet quality of participants as well as the quality of various dietary components [16]. HEI-2015 is made up of 13 parts, which include 9 adequacy elements (total fruits, whole fruits, total vegetables, greens and beans, whole grains, dairy, total protein foods, seafood and plant proteins, and fatty acids) and 4 moderation components (refined grains, sodium, added sugars, and saturated fat). Adequacy components are dietary elements that need to be consumed in larger quantities, with a higher score reflecting a more sufficient intake. Conversely, moderation components pertain to dietary elements that should be restricted, with a higher score reflecting a reduced consumption. The HEI-2015 total score combines adequacy and moderation components, ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting better diet quality [16].

Statistical analysis

All analyses used NHANES sampling weights to obtain U.S. nationally representative estimates. Sample characteristics were presented as frequencies and weighted proportions for the categorical variables and weighted mean with standard errors (SE) for continuous variables. The CKM syndrome patients were stratified into four groups according to SIRI quartiles. Chi-squared test and generalized linear models were utilized to assess differences among Q1-Q4 groups for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Three survey-weighted logistic regression models were applied to estimate odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) for the association between SIRI level and advanced CKM syndrome. The crude model included no covariate adjustments. Model 1 adjusted for sociodemographic variables including age, sex, ethnicity, educational level, family income, and marital status. Model 2 further adjusted for smoking status, alcohol status, physical activity, and HEI-2015. Furthermore, we adopted restricted cubic spline (RCS) regressions to model the association between continuous SIRI and advanced CKM syndrome. The Akaike information criterion (AIC) was employed to choose the RCS with 3 to 8 knots, selecting the model that exhibited the lowest AIC value. Due to its nonnormal distribution, the SIRI was log-transformed before being used in this analysis. Additionally, the predictive efficacy of SIRI and other CBC and hsCRP derived inflammatory biomarkers was assessed through the examination of area under the curve (AUC) values and the use of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.

We performed a mediation analysis to estimate the proportion of the total effect of SIRI on advanced CKM syndrome which was explained by the other inflammatory indicator components, including hs-CRP and platelet count. The inflammatory markers were chosen based on previous literature [17]. The R “mediation” package was adopted to estimate the average causal mediation effect, average direct effect, and average total effect. The 95% CI was estimated by non-parametric bootstrapping with 1000 replications.

We also conducted three sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our findings. First, individuals with a history of cancer were excluded. Second, excluding eligible participants with missing covariate data could cause selection bias. To address this problem, multivariate single imputation was applied using a machine learning algorithm with the missRanger R package. Third, according to the CKM health presidential advisory [1], there are several therapies with multiple beneficial effects on advanced CKM syndrome. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are known to prevent kidney failure and to have cardioprotective effects, and Glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) can not only improve insulin resistance and glycemia, but also reduce weight and cause significant reductions in CVD mortality. Besides, statins and nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDS) can significantly influence systemic inflammation. We conducted a sensitivity analysis that excluded participants who were treated with SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1RAs, statins or NSAIDS. According to the previous literature, statins included 8 different drug types: atorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin, fluvastatin, pitavastatin, lovastatin, pravastatin, and cerivastatin [18]. NSAIDs included 17 different drug types: acetaminophen, aspirin, celecoxib, diclofenac, diflunisal, etodolac, flurbiprofen, ibuprofen, indomethacin, ketorolac, meloxicam, naproxen, nimesulide, oxaprozin, piroxicam, salsalate, and sulindac [19]. Fourth, we constructed ordinal logistic regression models to evaluate the relationship between SIRI and CKM syndrome stages.

In addition, stratified analyses were performed based on age (< 60 or ≥ 60 years), sex, ethnicity (white or non-white), educational level, family income, marital status, smoking status (never or ever/current), alcohol status (drinkers or non-drinkers), and physical activity. Data analysis utilized R software (version 4.4.2), with complex survey analyses performed via the survey R package. A two-sided p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Participants characteristics

Of 11,888 U.S. adults included in this study, the percentages of CKM syndrome stage 0–4 were 12.47%, 27.42%, 45.56%, 3.88% and 10.67%, respectively. 87.53% of U.S. adults met the diagnostic criteria for CKM syndrome (stage 1 or higher), while 14.55% were diagnosed with advanced CKM syndrome. We then removed 1,483 participants in CKM syndrome stage 0, and finally this study included 10,405 CKM syndrome patients (Fig. 1). The average age of the 10,405 CKM syndrome patients was 49.47 (0.29) years, of which 49.77% were male and 67.28% were non-Hispanic White.

Table 1 presented the baseline characteristics of CKM syndrome patients by quantiles of the SIRI. Compared with patients in the lower SIRI group, participants in the higher SIRI group were more likely to be older, male, non-Hispanic White, current and former smoker, heavy drinker, have lower level of education, less high-income households, lower levels of physical activity, lower HEI-2015 score, higher level of SII, NLR, IBI, hs-CRP, and were more likely to be in advanced stages (all p < 0.05).

Association between SIRI and advanced CKM syndrome

Table 2 shows the association of SIRI and other inflammatory biomarkers with advanced CKM syndrome. For SIRI, after adjusting for potential confounders such as age, sex, ethnicity, educational level, family income, marital status, smoking status, alcohol status, physical activity, and HEI-2015, the OR for patients in the highest quantile were 1.61 (95% CI: 1.25–2.08, p < 0.001) for advanced CKM syndrome compared to those in the lowest quantile. A 1-unit increase in the SIRI level was associated with a 14% higher risk of progressing to advanced CKM syndrome stages (95% CI: 1.06–1.22, p < 0.0001). Besides, compared to the lowest quartile, the OR of advanced CKM syndrome was increased in the highest quantile by 97% of IBI (OR: 1.97, 95% CI: 1.45–2.67, p < 0.0001), and 102% of hs-CRP (OR: 2.02, 95% CI: 1.52–2.70, p < 0.0001), respectively. A 1-unit increase in the IBI and hs-CRP level was associated with a 1% higher risk (95% CI: 1.00-1.01, p = 0.02) and 3% higher risk (95% CI: 1.02–1.04, p < 0.0001) of advanced CKM syndrome.

ROC analysis

To determine the effectiveness of SIRI in predicting advanced CKM syndrome relative to other inflammatory markers, we measured the AUC values (Fig. 3). Our results showed that SIRI demonstrated superior predictive value (AUC = 0.633) for advanced CKM syndrome than the other four inflammatory biomarkers (NLR: 0.615, IBI: 0.578, HsCRP: 0.545, SII: 0.543). Using Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1), we derived an optimal SIRI cutoff of 1.121 from the ROC curve. This cutoff corresponded with a sensitivity of 0.612 and a specificity of 0.585. The optimal cutoff value, sensitivity and specificity of the other four inflammatory biomarkers were presented in Additional file 1: Table S6. In addition, we explored whether combining CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers with hsCRP could bring better predictive efficacy (Fig. 3). There was almost no change when combining SIRI with hsCRP (SIRI with hsCRP: 0.634, SIRI: 0.633). These results indicated that SIRI showed to be the most parsimonious and accurate inflammatory biomarker for predicting advanced CKM syndrome.

Mediation effects

After adjusting for all covariates, Fig. 4 showed the mediation proportions of hs-CRP and platelet count in SIRI attributed to advanced CKM syndrome. Discussion

To our knowledge, this cross-sectional study is the first to investigate the association between SIRI level and advanced CKM syndrome in a large, nationally representative sample. CKM syndrome patients in the highest quantile of SIRI level had a 61% higher risk of being in advanced stages, compared with those in the lowest quantile, and the estimated risk increase was 14% for per 1 unit of SIRI elevation. Besides, the RCS curve indicated the risk of advanced CKM syndrome increased with the increase of SIRI level and showed a non-linear relationship. ROC analysis suggested that SIRI could be a more precise marker for advanced CKM syndrome when compared to SII, NLR, IBI, and hs-CRP.

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) take a considerable and increasing toll on individuals, societies and health systems. The Global Burden of Disease Study reports that NCDs accounted for 56.8% of total deaths in 1990, rising to 74.4% by 2019. The disability-adjusted life years attributed to NCDs rose from 37.8 to 66.0% over the same timeframe [20]. The majority of NCDs include CVD, CKD and diabetes. CVD is the primary cause of death worldwide, with deaths rising from 12.1 million in 1990 to 20.5 million in 2025, and with an expected 35.6 million deaths in 2050 [21]. Diabetes is another leading cause of NCDs burden worldwide. In 2022, an estimated 828 million adults had diabetes, contributing to 1.4 million deaths [22, 23]. Moreover, the prevalence and mortality of CKD is also alarming, with 697.3 million cases and 1.4 million deaths globally in 2019 [24]. By 2040, CKD is anticipated to be the fifth leading cause of death globally, according to projections by global health burden of disease epidemiologists [25]. CVD is a prevalent and critical adverse outcome in individuals with diabetes or CKD, as it is the primary cause of mortality in these populations [26, 27]. These epidemiological data and comorbid conditions highlight the need for a better understanding and management of these diseases.

The AHA recently defined CKM syndrome as the interaction between obesity, diabetes, CKD, and CVD [1]. Based on risk factors and established disease, the AHA introduced a staging framework ranging from stages 0 to 4. Recent studies indicated that CKM syndrome is highly prevalent among U.S. adults, with about 90% meeting the criteria for CKM stage 1 or higher, and 15% of U.S. adults are identified as having advanced CKM stages (stage 3 or 4) [2]. The high prevalence of CKM syndrome underscores the pressing need for strategies to slow down the advancement of this comorbidity. According to the CKM health presidential advisory [1], CKM syndrome primarily arises from excess or dysfunctional visceral adipose tissue, which releases proinflammatory products that damage arterial, cardiac, and kidney tissues. These inflammatory processes also decrease insulin sensitivity, leading to impaired glucose tolerance [28]. Effectively managing the inflammatory response can halt the progression of CKM syndrome.

SIRI, derived from peripheral blood neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte counts, serves as a novel indicator for evaluating the balance between systemic inflammation and immune response [9]. It has superior predictive value than conventional inflammatory indicators given that it had a better effect on reflecting the inflammatory state. Previous studies have verified that it provided a more thorough and effective assessment of inflammation levels and was linked to various chronic diseases. Based on the 85,154 individuals from the Kailuan Chinese community cohort, Jin et al. found that elevated SIRI increased the risk of coronary heart disease, stroke, and other subtypes of CVD [3]. Another study on U.S. adults reported that SIRI exhibited superior discriminative ability and accuracy in predicting CKD, albuminuria, and low estimated glomerular filtration rate when compared to other inflammatory biomarkers [4]. Meanwhile, a cross-sectional study based on the Northeast China Rural Cardiovascular Health Study revealed that a higher level of SIRI tended to accompany a higher risk of metabolic diseases (metabolic syndrome, dyslipidemia, and central obesity) [5]. Utilizing the NHANES database in the U.S., our study expanded on prior research by demonstrating that elevated SIRI levels are linked to advanced stages in CKM syndrome patients. The estimated risk increase was 14% for per 1 unit of SIRI elevation. Moreover, the RCS model showed that the risk of advanced CKM syndrome increased with the increase of SIRI level and showed a non-linear relationship. As the SIRI level is derived from the lymphocyte, neutrophil, and monocyte counts, which are readily available from a complete blood count, our research findings hold significant clinical and public health implications for the prevention and management of CKM syndrome.

NLR, as the ratio of neutrophils over lymphocytes, reflects the balance between innate immunity and adaptive immunity [29]. NLR has been demonstrated to be an independent risk factor for CVD [30], CKD [4], diabetes [31], all of which are components of CKM syndrome. Notably, NLR has prognostic value for subclinical atherosclerosis even in older adults [32], who at high risk for advanced CKM syndrome. In our study, the ROC analysis suggested that SIRI could be a more precise marker for advanced CKM syndrome when compared to NLR. SIRI integrates neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes, potentially capturing a broader spectrum of myeloid inflammation. Our analyses underscored the centrality of chronic inflammation involving multiple leukocyte lineages in CKM pathogenesis.

Although SIRI exhibited the strongest discriminative capacity among the evaluated inflammatory markers, its modest predictive accuracy precludes its use as a diagnostic tool or standalone screening test for advanced CKM syndrome. This limitation aligns with prior studies of novel inflammatory indices, which often achieve AUC < 0.7 in complex multi-organ syndromes [4, 33]. The moderate performance underscores the multifactorial pathogenesis of advanced CKM syndrome, wherein inflammation is one component intertwined with metabolic, hemodynamic, and fibrotic pathways. Consequently, SIRI is likely better suited for initial epidemiological risk stratification than clinical diagnosis. While its discriminative capacity (AUC = 0.633) is insufficient for individual-level decision-making, it provides a graded, cost-effective marker for identifying populations at heightened risk of advanced CKM syndrome, particularly in resource-constrained settings where CBC is routinely available. In such contexts, elevated SIRI could prompt further organ-specific assessments (e.g., albuminuria quantification, cardiac imaging), thereby optimizing early detection efforts. This mirrors the utility of other moderate-accuracy indices (e.g., Fibrosis 4 Score in liver fibrosis) that guide targeted testing rather than replace it. Future studies must validate optimal SIRI thresholds and evaluate its incremental value when combined with clinical risk scores. Furthermore, we identified an optimal SIRI cutoff of 1.121 using Youden’s index, which maximized sensitivity (0.612) and specificity (0.585) in our cohort. Nevertheless, this value remains exploratory and must be validated prospectively in diverse populations. We advocate for large-scale studies to standardize SIRI thresholds across ethnicities and comorbidities, and assess utility in guiding early interventions. Until then, SIRI should serve as a research tool, complementing instead of replacing established guidelines. Besides, as SIRI alone outperformed hs-CRP and its combination with hs-CRP did not improve AUC (0.634), future studies should investigate SIRI’s interplay with non-overlapping pathways (e.g., oxidative stress, fibrosis markers) to construct clinically actionable algorithms.

Although hs-CRP mediated a statistically significant but quantitatively limited proportion (4.94%) of the association between SIRI and advanced CKM syndrome, this should not be overinterpreted clinically. Rather, SIRI’s stronger association with advanced CKM primarily stems from its ability to integrate cellular immune responses (neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes) reflective of myeloid activation and tissue inflammation [9]. It is the intrinsic qualities of SIRI, rather than the minimal mediation by hs-CRP, that account for its clinical utility in capturing inflammation beyond acute-phase proteins [34]. We acknowledge that the small mediation effect (4.94%) indicates hs-CRP contributes minimally to the pathway between SIRI and advanced CKM syndrome. This reinforces that SIRI’s predictive value is driven predominantly by non-hs-CRP-mediated mechanisms, including neutrophil-driven tissue damage and monocyte-macrophage activation. The mediation result should be viewed as generating hypotheses rather than providing clinical guidance.

The association between SIRI level and advanced CKM syndrome may be attributed to the fact that SIRI is a novel systemic inflammatory biomarker based on the counts of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and monocytes in peripheral blood. Previous studies have suggested that neutrophils were crucial in the inflammatory response of atherosclerosis, which could release inflammatory mediators, chemotactic substances, and oxygen-containing free radicals that cause endothelial cell damage and subsequent tissue ischemia [35, 36]. Besides, the differentiation of monocytes to macrophages and subsequent transformation to lipid-laden foam cells constitute major events in the development of atherosclerotic lesions [37]. Renal fibrosis is also associated with the misregulation of transforming growth factor-beta from monocytes. Renal fibrosis has also been linked to dysregulation of the monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 [38]. Conversely, lymphocytes have a regulatory function in inflammatory responses, and oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor in lymphocytes could contribute to the stabilization of atherosclerotic plaque and prevention of atherosclerosis [39]. Therefore, SIRI is biologically plausible to associate with advanced CKM syndrome stages, which is characterized by or subclinical or clinical CVD.

A key strength of this study is its large, nationally representative sample. Furthermore, SIRI was evaluated through common methodology, ensuring it remains an accessible and cost-effective biomarker with potential clinical applications. We also note some limitations of this study. First, our cross-sectional design cannot determine the directionality of the association between SIRI and advanced CKM syndrome. Chronically elevated SIRI may drive progression towards advanced CKM stages, while the inflammatory milieu and organ damage present in advanced CKM stages may cause SIRI elevation. Future prospective cohort studies with serial measurements of SIRI before the onset or progression of CKM stages, are essential to establish the temporal relationship, truly assess SIRI’s predictive value for future risk of developing advanced CKM stages, and investigate whether changes in SIRI over time correlate with disease progression or regression. Second, the SIRI, hs-CRP, and other inflammatory indices were measured at a single time point in NHANES. This approach cannot account for intra-individual fluctuations in inflammatory activity or capture long-term exposure patterns, limiting the ability to differentiate chronic inflammation from transient elevations. Consequently, this single measurement may lead to misclassification of participants’ true inflammatory status. Critically, and as highlighted by the potential for exposure misclassification, this limitation may also result in misclassification of the advanced CKM syndrome outcome, particularly among individuals in transitional disease stages or those experiencing fluctuations in inflammation levels. For instance, participants with temporarily low SIRI might be incorrectly classified as having low-grade CKM syndrome despite underlying progression, or vice versa. Such stage misclassification could potentially bias the observed associations, likely attenuating the effect estimates towards the null. Given the dynamic nature of cardiometabolic conditions, this measurement limitation necessitates caution in interpreting causal inferences about chronic inflammation derived from our findings. Future longitudinal studies with serial SIRI measurements are warranted to investigate how temporal changes in systemic inflammation drive transitions to advanced CKM stages and to minimize the risk of misclassification inherent in single-point assessments. Third, the absence of definitive imaging data (echocardiography, coronary angiography) can lead to an underestimation of advanced CKM syndrome (stages 3 or 4), particularly for individuals with subclinical cardiovascular disease. Critically, there is no evidence or plausible mechanism suggesting that this misclassification probability depended on a participant’s SIRI value, so it is likely non-differential. Such non-differential misclassification of the outcome typically biases effect estimates towards the null hypothesis. Consequently, the observed OR associating higher SIRI with advanced CKM syndrome in our study likely represent a conservative underestimate of the true strength of this association. Despite this limitation biasing our results conservatively, a significant positive trend was still observed. Finally, our findings are derived exclusively from NHANES data, which represents noninstitutionalized U.S. civilians. Consequently, the generalizability of our results to non-U.S. populations, institutionalized individuals, or populations with substantially different geographic, ethnic, and socioeconomic profiles may be limited. These differences could significantly impact lifestyle behaviors, healthcare access, and the distributions of CKM-related risk factors. Therefore, external validation of SIRI’s association with advanced CKM syndrome in diverse multi-national cohorts, including non-U.S. populations and institutionalized settings, is essential to confirm the universality and robustness of our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, elevated SIRI is significantly associated with advanced CKM syndrome stages in U.S. adults. However, due to the cross-sectional design, we cannot determine whether SIRI elevation precedes or results from advanced disease. While SIRI shows promise as a potentially useful inflammatory biomarker for risk stratification, its predictive utility and causal role require rigorous validation in longitudinal cohorts. Future studies must track serial SIRI measurements before CKM syndrome progression to establish temporal relationships and clarify directionality. Until more evidence is presented, our results should be viewed as indicating an association, not a causal relationship, and should be used cautiously in clinical applications.
