Precisely designed keystone metabolites boost shrimp disease resistance by recruiting symbionts via the lipoxin A4–AP-1 pathway
Authors: Haonan Sha, Jiong Chen, Jianguo He, Bin He, Jie Huang, Jizhong Zhou, Xuan Dong, Jinbo Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGut metabolites and symbionts are indispensable for host health, yet the precise identification of keystone metabolites and construction of synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) to enhance disease resistance remains limited.ResultsUsing Litopenaeus vannamei as a model, we identified pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine (1:2) as keystone metabolites by borrowing the microbial ecology principles of bio-indicators and driver taxa. Dietary supplementation with these metabolites sufficiently protected shrimp from white feces syndrome (WFS). Multi-omics analyses demonstrated that keystone metabolites exerted positive effects by enriching beneficial Ruegeria lacuscaerulensis, Bacillus subtilis and Nioella nitratireducens, strengthening the gut network stability, and enhancing shrimp immunity, which collectively potentiated WFS resistance. The recruited three strains were consumers and producers of the two keystone metabolites, and discriminative strains between healthy and diseased shrimp across global datasets. A SynCom constructed from the three strains (4:3:2) replicated the efficacy of keystone metabolites. Both keystone metabolites and SynCom elevated shrimp gut and hepatopancreas lipoxin A4 (LXA4) levels, which suppressed the pro-inflammatory transcription factor AP-1, as validated by in vivo inhibition assay.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that precisely designed keystone metabolites enhance shrimp disease resistance through the recruitment of key symbionts–LXA4–AP-1 axis. The rationally designed keystone metabolites and SynCom are compelling biocontrol solutions in improving host disease resistance. Introduction

Gut metabolites are central regulators of host health and fitness [1]. Certain metabolites play disproportionate roles, and when they are depleted or absent, both the host and its gut microbiota exhibit marked differences in phenotype and fitness [1]. On this basis, supplementation with keystone metabolites enables targeted reprogramming of the gut metabolome and microbiota, thereby precisely modulating host health. Case studies have identified keystone metabolites that strengthen host disease resistance, such as supplementation with L-proline restraining Streptococcus agalactiae [2], and taurine suppressing Vibrio parahaemolyticus infection [3]. These benefits are attributed to direct or indirect recruitment of beneficial strains and stimulation of host immunity. Keystone metabolites therefore represent rational targets for treating disease and restoring individualized metabolic imbalance. However, the astonishing diversity and context dependence of gut metabolites pose significant challenges for precision nutrition through dietary interventions tailored to host–microbiome interactions.

Traditionally, beneficial metabolites are identified based on the most strikingly altered or differential metabolites between healthy and diseased cohorts. Such approaches risk inadvertently excluding keystone metabolites that may occur at low levels [4]. Furthermore, treating metabolites as equal in ratio disregards their interactions and the unequal importance of each component. Combination of a few gut microbial indicators maximizes accuracy in distinguishing host health status. Keystone microbes contribute disproportionately to community function and stability through intensive interactions, rather than dominance [5]. The field of microbial ecology has provided tools to identify such species. For example, the Random Forest algorithm harnesses the diversity and collective intelligence of multiple decision trees, improving biomarker screening between groups and reducing overfitting [6]. Network analysis can identify taxa that trigger transitions from one equilibrium to another [7], for instance, from health to disease. Inspired by these principles, we hypothesized that integrating metabolic biomarker and driver metabolite–guided strategies could precisely identify keystone metabolites. Practically, the ratio of keystone metabolites could be properly formulated according to their proportions in the gut microbiota of healthy hosts, whereas empirical evidence is lacking.

Evidence from diverse hosts indicates that the gut microbiota progressively degrades ingested food and safeguards against infection [3, 8]. By metabolizing exogenous compounds, the gut microbiota modifies metabolic phenotypes, which in turn restructure the microbial community, demonstrating traits of coevolution [9]. A classic example is the conversion of indigestible carbohydrates into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) by the gut symbionts, such as butyrate and pyruvate that are available to the host [10]. Butyrate promotes an antimicrobial state via HDAC3 inhibition, thereby enhancing microbicidal activity against Salmonella infection [11]. Conversely, butyrate deficiency causes gut inflammation and alters the microbiome, thereby increasing disease susceptibility [12]. Similarly, exogenous pyruvate alleviates colitis by targeting cytosolic phospholipase A2 [11]. Thus, a key and intuitive recognition is that the tripartite association among gut metabolites, microbiota, and host immune collectively determines host health. However, most of these interactions still lack causal evidence. Complexity further arises because the astonishing diversity of gut metabolites, symbionts, and immune pathways is often context dependent, complicating the identification of keystone metabolites and symbionts for precisely targeted health interventions. Systematic exploration of microbial, metabolic, and immune responses, coupled with rigorous validation, is essential for revealing the regulatory mechanisms, yet substantial knowledge gaps remain.

We selected Litopenaeus vannamei as a model animal because it is the most widely cultured shrimp species globally and is threatened by multiple diseases [13]. White feces syndrome (WFS), for example, a devastating disease, reduces shrimp survival by 20–30%, causing billions of economic loss yearly [14]. The small size of shrimp facilitates capture of the entire gut microbiota and metabolome without biases introduced by gut spatial structure. Several lines of evidence have linked WFS occurrence to gut microbial and metabolic abnormalities [15, 16]. In addition, V. fluvialis (Vf), V. coralliilyticus (Vc) and V. tubiashii (Vt) were the causing pathogens of WFS, which have been validated in our recent work [17]. However, whether keystone metabolites can directly (through immune stimulation) or indirectly (through recruitment of keystone symbionts) enhance shrimp disease resistance has not been established. The crosstalk between gut and hepatopancreas therefore provides an excellent model for studying metabolite-mediated regulation of microbiota–host interactions, which in turn guides the precise designation of biocontrol strategies—defined by rationality and mechanism.

We hypothesized that keystone metabolites directly stimulate shrimp immunity or recruit beneficial symbionts into the gut, thereby enhancing colonization resistance to infection. Our objectives were to (i) identify and validate gut keystone metabolites that are dispensable to shrimp health, (ii) characterize the functions of gut symbionts preferentially recruited under keystone metabolite supplementation and assess their contributions to WFS resistance, and importantly, (iii) explore and verify the crosstalk among shrimp immunity, gut metabolites and microbiota to develop mechanistic insights into systems-level shifts underlying WFS resistance (Fig. 1). Specifically, keystone metabolites were identified by adapting the concepts of bio-indicators and driver taxa from microbial ecology. Their benefits were validated by exposure infection after dietary intervention. We then integrated metagenomic and metabolomic analyses to decode alterations in the gut microbiome following dietary intervention after supplementation. Keystone symbionts were isolated and used to construct a SynCom for verification of disease resistance, followed by whole-genome sequencing and global distribution analyses across shrimp health status using meta-data globally. Most importantly, moving beyond the conventional equal ratio of a few beneficial strains, we determined the ratio of SynCom inoculation by recapitulating the in situ relative abundances of keystone strains in the healthy shrimp gut. Finally, using bidirectional mediation analysis [18] and in vivo inhibition tests, we demonstrated that keystone metabolites acted as signaling molecules through the gut microbiota–host immunity axis by recruiting gut keystone symbionts, which stimulated the production of lipoxin A4 (LXA4), in turn inhibiting the shrimp transcription factor AP-1. Our study reveals regulatory mechanisms by which rationally designed keystone metabolites and their recruited symbionts enhance host disease resistance. Overall, this study establishes a keystone metabolites–guided framework that integrates metabolomic features with SynCom construction, thereby providing a novel methodology for enhancing host disease resistance.Fig. 1Schematic overview of the experimental design for enhancing shrimp resistance to white feces syndrome (WFS) via the gut microbiota–lipoxin A4 (LXA4)–AP-1 axis mediated by keystone metabolites. Results

Identification and validation of keystone metabolites enhanced shrimp WFS resistance

Untargeted metabolomics detected 472 and 470 gut metabolites (combined with the positive and negative models) in healthy and WFS shrimp under field conditions, respectively (Fig. S1). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) showed that the gut metabolome of WFS shrimp was distinct from that of healthy controls (ANOSIM r = 0.53, P = 0.003; Fig. 2A). Using |log2 fold change| > 1, P < 0.05, and variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 as criteria, we identified 86 differential metabolites in WFS shrimp relative to healthy controls, of which 33 were upregulated and 53 were downregulated (Fig. S2). Metabolic biomarkers characterizing shrimp health status were then identified using the Random Forest algorithm. Three metabolites were enriched (e.g., adenine and D-carnitine) and nine were depressed (e.g., pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine) in WFS shrimp compared with healthy counterparts (Fig. 2B and Fig. S3). To further identify keystone metabolites essential to shrimp health, we compared the co-occurrence networks of differential metabolites. Depressed pyruvic acid (0.73 vs. 0.33) and DL-glutamine (0.71 vs. 0.25) were the keystone metabolites driving the network shift from healthy to WFS shrimp, based on eigenvector centrality (EC) (Fig. 2C and Table S1). Importantly, pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine levels were strongly and negatively correlated with the relative abundances of WFS-causing Vf, Vc and Vt that were also significantly enriched in WFS shrimp compared with healthy controls (P < 0.05 for each pathogen; Fig. 2D, E). In accordance, the significant negative correlations between the keystone metabolites and the three pathogenic Vibrio species further confirmed the reliability of our screening results. To validate the hypothesis that pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine contribute protective effects (combined in a 1:2 ratio according to their proportions in healthy shrimp gut; Table S1), shrimp were fed diets supplemented with these metabolites for 14 days and then challenged with a cocktail of Vf, Vc and Vt (Experiment 1; Fig. 2F). The first mortality in the MV shrimp (keystone metabolite supplementation plus Vibrio infection) occurred at 4 days post-infection (dpi), 2 days later than in the VI group (infection without supplementation) (Fig. 2G, H). At 9 dpi, cumulative mortality in MV shrimp was 12.7%, close to that of the CK group (control without supplementation or infection, 9.6%), and significantly lower than that of the VI cohort (45.2%) (Fig. 2H). Accordingly, the relative abundances of Vf, Vc, and Vt were significantly suppressed in MV shrimp relative to VI shrimp at 9 dpi (Fig. 2I).

Histological analysis revealed that VI shrimp at 9 dpi exhibited severe hepatopancreatic damage, including tubular degeneration and reduced B and E cells (Fig. S4A–D). By contrast, the hepatic tubules of MV shrimp were well preserved without signs of necrosis or severe cellular damage (Fig. S4A). Similarly, VI shrimp displayed significantly shorter villi (P < 0.05) and marked exfoliation from the basement membrane compared with CK shrimp, whereas these abnormalities were alleviated by metabolite supplementation (Fig. S4B, E). In MV shrimp at 9 dpi, supplementation significantly increased levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-10 (IL-10) and interferon-α (IFN-α), as well as activities of catalase, alkaline phosphatase, and lysozyme. In contrast, pro-inflammatory cytokines of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and IL-6 levels were reduced compared with VI shrimp (Fig. S5A–G). Growth performance was also improved: body weight and length of MS shrimp (metabolite supplementation without infection) increased by 14.1% and 7.0%, respectively, compared with CK shrimp at 0 dpi (Fig. 2J, K). Even under Vibrio infection, MV shrimp exhibited significantly higher body weight (8.3%, P = 0.005) and body length (3.4%, P = 0.018) than CK shrimp at 9 dpi. These improvements were correlated with enhanced pepsin and lipase activities stimulated by metabolite supplementation (Fig. S5H, I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that dietary supplementation with pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine efficiently protects shrimp against WFS by enhancing immune and digestive activities, preserving gut and hepatopancreas integrity, and suppressing pathogens and pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Keystone metabolite supplementation ameliorated gut metabolism in WFS-infected shrimp

To determine whether supplementation mitigated WFS-induced metabolic changes, untargeted metabolomics was performed on gut samples collected from Experiment 1 at 9 dpi. In total, 519 metabolites with varied abundances were identified (Fig. S6). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separations among the four experimental groups (PC1 = 32.4%, PC2 = 14.2%; Fig. S7A). Using |log2 fold change| > 1, P < 0.05, and VIP > 1 as thresholds, we screened differential metabolites between groups. Vibrio infection (treatment VI) enriched 40 metabolites (e.g., leukotriene C4, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), phosphocholine, and myristic acid) and suppressed 27 metabolites (e.g., L-dopa, cystine and taurochenodeoxycholic acid [TAA]) relative to CK shrimp (Fig. S7B–D). Supplementation restored 28 of the 40 enriched metabolites and 19 of the 27 suppressed metabolites (treatment MV, Fig. S7C, D). Notably, compared with CK shrimp at 9 dpi, indole-3-acetic acid was most enriched in MS shrimp (2.81-fold), followed by proline (2.77-fold) and LXA4 (2.69-fold). By contrast, pentadecanoic acid (2.76-fold), taurocholic acid (2.67-fold) and xanthine (2.62-fold) were most enriched in VI shrimp compared with the control group. In MV shrimp, LXA4 (3.21-fold), indole-3-acetic acid (2.97-fold) and 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid (2.78-fold) were significantly elevated compared with the VI shrimp (Fig. S7B).

The gut metabolic network of MS shrimp exhibited greater stability than that of CK shrimp at 9 dpi, characterized by higher modularity, greater proportions of negative associations, stronger robustness and compositional stability, as well as lower vulnerability (Fig. S8A–E). Infection alone markedly reduced network stability, whereas supplementation ameliorated this destabilizing effect in MV shrimp, restoring stability through increased modularity and reduced vulnerability compared with VI shrimp (Fig. S8E). Importantly, pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine again emerged as the driver metabolites distinguishing healthy and WFS shrimp in the laboratory challenge (Fig. S8A, D and Table S2), mirroring what was observed in field shrimp (Fig. 2C). Overall, dietary supplementation with keystone metabolites effectively ameliorates infection-induced gut metabolic disorders.

Keystone metabolite supplementation rescued WFS-induced dysbiosis in the gut microbiome

Given the critical roles of the gut microbiota in modulating metabolic activities [19, 20], we performed microbial metagenomic analysis on gut samples collected from Experiment 1. After quality control, 233.04 GB of high-quality reads were obtained from the 24 shrimp gut samples at 9 dpi, of which 95.0% passed the Q30 threshold (Table S3). Supplementation with keystone metabolites significantly enhanced gut microbial diversity and richness, whereas infection significantly reduced these indices compared with control shrimp (Fig. S9A). Notably, the MV and CK groups exhibited comparable α-diversity, both significantly higher than the VI group, suggesting that supplementation counteracted the diversity loss observed after Vibrio infection on its own. PCoA revealed significant differences in the gut microbiota among treatments (Global R2 = 0.48, P < 0.001; Fig. S9B). Distances between MV and CK shrimp were closer, and distinct from VI and MS cohorts (Fig. S9C), indicating that supplementation neutralized infection effects. PERMANOVA further showed that keystone metabolite supplementation and Vibrio infection explained 20.6% and 15.1% of the variance in gut microbiota composition, respectively (P < 0.001 in each case), with their interaction accounting for 12.3% (P = 0.005) (Table S4).

Proteobacteria (57.25%; mainly composed of 33.48% Alphaproteobacteria and 21.84% Gammaproteobacteria), Bacteroidota (25.39%) and Firmicutes (15.16%) were the dominant bacterial phyla in shrimp gut. Keystone metabolites supplementation increased the relative abundance of Alphaproteobacteria and Actinobacteriota, while depressed Gammaproteobacteria, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota compared with CK shrimp (Fig. S10A). At the genus level, the relative abundances of Ruegeria, Halomonas and Streptococcus in the MS shrimp were higher than in the CK shrimp, whereas Xanthomarina Vibrio and Algoriphagus exhibited a downward trend (Fig. S10B). At 9 dpi, Vibrio-infected shrimp showed 55 significantly enriched (P < 0.05) species, including Muricauda alvinocaridis, Halomonas litopenaei and Maribacter algicola, and 61 significantly reduced species, such as Spirillospora rifamycini, Paracoccus homiensis and Demequina flava. These alterations were partially counteracted by supplementation (Fig. S11). The co-occurrence network of differential species revealed that infection compromised network stability (modularity 0.579; negative associations 29.33%), whereas supplementation enhanced it (modularity 0.820; negative associations 36.31%) relative to controls. Moreover, infection-induced destabilization was neutralized by supplementation, with network stability comparable between CK and MV shrimp (modularity 0.704 vs. 0.662; negative associations 33.61% vs. 32.68%) (Fig. S9D and Table S5). Similarity percentage analysis identified Vf, Vt and Vc as the top three species contributing to community differences between VI and CK cohorts, accounting for 14.6%, 13.5% and 8.2% of variance, respectively. By contrast, Ruegeria lacuscaerulensis, Bacillus subtilis and Nioella nitratireducens were the most influential species between MV and VI shrimp, contributing 21.2%, 16.0% and 8.0% of variance, respectively, and together explaining 45.2% of variance between MS and CK cohorts at 9 dpi (Table S6). Compared with CK shrimp, the three strains were significantly suppressed in VI shrimp but enriched in MS and MV shrimp (Fig. S9E). Furthermore, the Random Forest model identified 14 biomarkers, which encompassed these three specific strains, and resulted in an overall diagnostic accuracy of 95.8% for the 24 samples distributed across four treatment cohorts (Fig. S12). Crucially, the three strains were the top differential taxa and biomarkers between groups. Collectively, Vibrio infection reduces the diversity and stability of the gut microbiota and suppresses specific strains, whereas prophylactic supplementation with keystone metabolites alleviates these effects. Based on these results, we hypothesized that these three strains were beneficial, and this was subsequently tested in Experiment 4.

Keystone metabolite supplementation altered microbial functions to protect against WFS

Given the functional importance of the gut microbiota for host health [19], we assessed its functional potentials using metagenomic analysis of gut samples collected from Experiment 1. Functional diversity was significantly stimulated by supplementation but suppressed by infection at 9 dpi (Fig. 3A). PCoA biplots revealed significant functional differences among treatments (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.45, P = 0.003; Fig. 3B). Compared with CK shrimp, 14 pathways (e.g., map00220 Arginine biosynthesis, map00380 Tryptophan metabolism, map00640 Propanoate metabolism) were significantly enriched and 11 pathways (e.g., map02024 Quorum sensing, map03070 Bacterial secretion system, map00730 Thiamine metabolism) were significantly reduced in MS shrimp at 9 dpi. In contrast, VI shrimp exhibited reductions in 17 pathways (e.g., map00650 Butanoate metabolism, map00480 Glutathione metabolism, map00906 Carotenoid biosynthesis) and increases in 18 pathways (e.g., map02020 Two-component system, map02010 ABC transporters, map02040 Flagellar assembly) compared with CK shrimp (Fig. 3C and Table S7). To focus on the effects of keystone metabolites, the co-occurrence network was used to depict the interrelationships among differential metabolites, differential species, and DFPs between MV and VI shrimp at 9 dpi, resulting in 71 nodes and 272 edges with strong and significant correlations (|r| > 0.80, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3E). In summary, supplementation indirectly modifies gut metabolism by remodeling the gut microbiome, which synergically alleviates shrimp WFS.

Keystone metabolite supplementation altered host transcriptome in improving WFS resistance

To investigate shrimp responses to keystone metabolite supplementation in enhancing WFS resistance, we performed transcriptome analysis of hepatopancreas samples collected from Experiment 1 using RNA sequencing. Although it is debatable whether mRNA abundance directly reflects enzymatic activity, we observed that the activities of lipase, lysozyme, and alkaline phosphatase were significantly and positively correlated with their corresponding gene expression (Fig. S13), indicating that transcriptional alterations, at least in part, reflected functional activity. Gene expression was quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) [21]. PCoA biplots revealed significant differences in expression profiles among treatments (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.35, P < 0.001; Fig. S14A). Based on false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1, we detected 117 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between MS and CK shrimp, and 76 DEGs between VI and CK cohorts at 9 dpi (Fig. S14B). Pathway enrichment analysis showed that supplementation significantly upregulated genes associated with glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, and glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (Fig. S14C). In contrast, Vibrio infection predominantly activated immune pathways, including NF-κB, mTOR, PI3K–Akt, and MAPK signaling, while suppressing metabolic pathways such as streptomycin biosynthesis, propanoate metabolism, and thiamine metabolism (Fig. S14D). Supplementation partially counteracted these effects, as reflected by fewer DEGs between MV and CK shrimp at 9 dpi, although significant induction of glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism remained debatable in MV shrimp (Fig. S14D–F).

Co-expression network analysis identified 10 modules with strong intra-module connectivity (Fig. 4A, B). The module most relevant to shrimp immunity, characterized by frequent inflammatory cytokine nodes, was selected as the key module. The lightcyan module was strongly correlated with IL-6 (r = − 0.55, P < 0.01), TNF-α (r = − 0.48, P < 0.05), IL-10 (r = 0.53, P < 0.01) and IFN-α (r = 0.48, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). This module included 11 hub genes, such as K04448 AP-1, K04441 p38 MAP kinase (p38) and K04374 cyclic AMP-dependent transcription factor ATF-4 (ATF-4) (Fig. 4C), 10 of the 11 hub genes were affiliated with the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 4D). By aligning these hub genes to the KEGG database, we reconstructed the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 4E). In VI vs. CK shrimp, all of these genes, except K20216 dual specificity protein phosphatase 1, were significantly upregulated, whereas no such changes were observed between MV vs. CK shrimp. Moreover, the lowest expression levels of these genes were detected in MS shrimp (Fig. 4E). Therefore, it is promising that keystone metabolites supplementation efficiently suppresses pro-inflammatory responses, regardless of infection. LXA4 is a key mediator regulated the gut–hepatopancreas axis under keystone metabolite supplementation

To evaluate the role of LXA4 in keystone metabolite–mediated protection, Experiment 2 was designed (Fig. 5G). LXA4 is a by-product of arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism, in which 15-lipoxygenase catalyzes a rate-limiting step [22]. Therefore, the 15-lipoxygenase inhibitor PD146176 was employed to interfere with LXA4 production. Specifically, shrimp were fed pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine whereas they received intramuscular injections of PD146176 (1 μg/g) every 2 days for 14 days, followed by a Vibrio challenge (MSP group). Treatments for CK1 (control shrimp), MV1 (supplementation plus Vibrio infection), and VI1 (infection without supplementation) mirrored those for CK, MV and VI in Experiment 1 (Fig. 5G). LXA4 levels in the gut and hepatopancreas were significantly decreased in MSP shrimp compared with MV1 shrimp and CK1 controls (Fig. 5H), confirming effective inhibition of AA–LXA4 conversion. At 9 dpi, cumulative mortality in the MSP group was lower than that in VI1 shrimp (infection without supplementation; 34.2% vs. 45.0%) but substantially higher than that of MV1 shrimp (34.2% vs. 4.2%) (Fig. 5I), reinforcing that inhibition of LXA4 biosynthesis suppressed the protective effects of supplementation. Consistently, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) revealed that hepatopancreas anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and IFN-α in MSP shrimp were higher than in VI1 shrimp, whereas the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6 and AP-1 were lower in MSP shrimp at 9 dpi, although these differences were insignificant (Fig. S16A–E). Additionally, the levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and IFN-α were significantly positively correlated with gut LXA4 level (Fig. S17). Furthermore, inhibition of endogenous LXA4 production caused a marked rebound in AP-1 expression within the MSP shrimp (Fig. S16E). Nevertheless, the relative abundances of B. subtilis, R. lacuscaerulensis and N. nitratireducens were significantly greater in MSP than in VI1 shrimp (Fig. S16F–H and Table S9). Histological analysis suggested that MSP shrimp exhibited more compactly organized epithelial cells and better-preserved connections to the basement membrane than those in the VI1 shrimp at 9 dpi (Fig. S16I), but hepatic corpuscle atrophy, loose tissue arrangement, and the numbers of B and E cells were comparable between the two groups (Fig. S16J–L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the LXA4–AP-1 pathway is indispensable for keystone metabolite–mediated disease resistance.

Global distribution of B. subtilis, R. lacuscaerulensis, and N. nitratireducens between shrimp health states

To assess the global distribution of the three beneficial taxa, we compared their relative abundances between healthy and diseased shrimp using a meta-analysis including eight diseases across 15 studies (Table S10). In total, 200,672 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were identified across 627 gut microbiotas in shrimp. The three taxa were screened by matching 16S rRNA genes with 100% nucleotide identity to B. subtilis, N. nitratireducens, and R. lacuscaerulensis. They were detected in 11, 9 and 8 studies, respectively, and co-occurred in 7 studies (Table S11). Importantly, their relative abundances were significantly reduced in diseased shrimp in six independent studies, including those on acute hepatopancreatic necrosis disease (APHND), “cotton shrimp-like” disease (CSL), translucent disease (TD) and WFS (Table S11). Although the pathogens causing CSL and TD remain unidentified, both diseases are associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis [23, 24].

Validation of B. subtilis, R. lacuscaerulensis, and N. nitratireducens effects on shrimp disease resistance

Scanning electron microscopy revealed that the three strains were rod-shaped with varying diameters and lengths (Fig. 6A). R. lacuscaerulensis carried genes for de novo synthesis of B vitamins, including riboflavin (ribA, ribBA and ribD), pantothenate (panB and panC), and cobalamin (cobS, cobU and cobD). Key genes for propionate (mcm, fadA and fadB) and butyrate (thlA and phaA) synthesis were detected in N. nitratireducens. Discussion

Gut metabolites are highly diverse, making it a major challenge to identify the keystone metabolites responsible for host disease resistance. By integrating metabolic biomarkers with driver metabolites, we exemplify a rational approach to design keystone metabolites using microbial ecology principles, with their ratio determined according to their proportions in the gut of healthy hosts. Keystone metabolites efficiently potentiated shrimp disease resistance by recruiting beneficial symbionts and inhibiting pro-inflammatory responses (Fig. 7). Accordingly, a SynCom was constructed and validated in microcosm trials, reproducing the efficacy of keystone metabolites. Moreover, we identified a molecular mechanism in which LXA4 suppressed transcription factor AP-1 expression, a pathway indispensable for strengthening shrimp disease resistance. A rational approach for precise identification of keystone metabolites

Gut metabolites bidirectionally influence host physiology and the gut microbiota, which in turn synergistically determine disease outcomes [25]. Thus, depletion of specific metabolites compromises host immunity and exacerbates disease progression, whereas supplementation with depleted metabolites may restore resistance. For instance, exogenous L-proline enhances amino acid metabolism and improves tilapia survival against S. agalactiae infection [2]. Current identification strategies rely on striking differences in metabolite abundance between groups, but this overlooks the dynamic, non-linear interactions among metabolites. In addition, combinations of “top” differential metabolites are often selected artificially (e.g., a few top differential metabolites) and applied in equal ratios, which may not reflect biological relevance. Here, we adopted microbial ecology principles for identifying keystone metabolites. The Random Forest algorithm provides a combination of biomarkers that discriminates healthy from diseased cohorts, whereas network analysis identifies taxa driving transitions from health to disease. The primary advantage of the keystone metabolites-guided strategy is that it provides a rational, mechanism-oriented framework linking endogenous metabolic signatures to community assembly and host phenotypes. In contrast, top-down approaches are effective for identifying core functional groups but are often limited by low predictability and reproducibility [26]. Random screening could screen effective combinations without prior assumptions, whereas it is resource-intensive and often difficult to interpret biologically [27]. Applying these logics, we identified pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine as depleted keystone metabolites in shrimp with WFS (Fig. 2). Pyruvic acid acts as a cytoprotective agent by enhancing antioxidant defenses and scavenging H2O2 [28], whereas DL-glutamine deficiency impairs intestinal cell replication, tight-junction proteins, and epithelial permeability [29]. Gut commensals, such as Bacteroides members, are capable of producing substantial quantities of organic acids and amino acids within the gut [30]. Consistently, genomic analysis of our SynCom strains revealed metabolic pathways conferring synthesis or utilization of pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine (Fig. 6B), suggesting their potential to actively modulate the intraluminal metabolic profiles. Nevertheless, in a complex host–gut microbe system, metabolic shifts often represent a “co-metabolic” outcome, and the involvement of the hepatopancreas—a central metabolic hub in shrimp—cannot be overlooked during WFS infection. However, germ-free or gnotobiotic model for shrimp is lacking, which impedes disentangling the microbial vs. host contributions. For example, gnotobiotic shrimp could be generated by exposure to an antibiotic cocktail that is known to strongly affect shrimp physiology [31]. Importantly, we rationally determined their supplementation ratio based on proportions in healthy shrimp. The resulting formula produced systemic benefits for physiology and WFS resistance (Fig. 7), in contrast to organ-specific improvements reported in single-metabolite studies, such as pyruvic acid for liver health in Trachinotus ovatus [32] and DL-glutamine for intestinal morphology in Hoplias lacerdae [33]. Together, these findings show that our designed keystone metabolites exerted positive effects on both the gut and hepatopancreas, resulting in an overall protective response in shrimp. Because of their reproducibility and quality attributes, they are amenable to large-scale application. A limitation of our study is the reliance on semi-quantitative data from untargeted metabolomics to determine the ratio of keystone metabolites. Targeted LC–MS/MS with internal standards is required to precisely quantify the absolute concentrations and temporal dynamics of these metabolites in shrimp gut in future work.

Keystone metabolite supplementation recruits beneficial gut symbionts to enhance shrimp disease resistance

The gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in maintaining host health [8, 20, 34], and can be modified by diet. For example, in humans, high-fiber intake enriches butyrate-producing bacteria [35]. We confirmed B. subtilis, R. lacuscaerulensis and N. nitratireducens as the top discriminative taxa between MV and VI cohorts. Consistently, in vitro and in vivo experiments further confirmed that supplementation with pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine recruited these three probiotic symbionts (Fig. 6 and Table S6). The abundances of the three strains in healthy shrimp were higher than individuals suffering from WFS and other diseases (Fig. S9E and Table S11), indicating their key roles in disease resistance. Indeed, intensive studies have documented the benefits of Bacillus, Ruegeria and Nioella strains in shrimp health [36–39]. Genomic analyses revealed that these three strains possess essential genes for the metabolism of pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine (Fig. 6B). This dual ability to consume and produce these metabolites provides them a competitive advantage under supplementation and benefits the broader symbiont community. The SynCom efficiently protected shrimp from WFS in vivo (Fig. 6E). Mechanistically, B. subtilis produces antimicrobial peptides that antagonize pathogens and synthesizes tryptophan (Fig. 6B), whose derivatives alleviate gut inflammation and dysfunction [40, 41]. N. nitratireducens degrades recalcitrant nutrients, producing propionate and butyrate (Fig. 6B) that support immunity [42], gut development, and barrier integrity, thereby conferring colonization resistance [10]. R. lacuscaerulensis synthesizes B vitamins (Fig. 6B) that are essential nutrients and important antioxidants promoting shrimp growth [36]. The three strains in our SynCom collectively possess the genomic repertoire for pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine utilization (Fig. 6B). This shared metabolic requirement suggests potential niche overlap and competition, which may also reflect a functional redundancy that ensures community robustness under varying host conditions [43]. Indeed, the three strains also possess distinct CAZymes (Fig. S18), which could facilitate their niche differentiation in avoiding direct competition for carbon sources. The SynCom members dedicate distinct biological functions, suggesting functional “division of labor”. This is an important superiority of our designed SynCom over single strain, enabling ecological stability and adaptability to host physiology. Accordingly, the enriched three symbionts interacted positively with each other, which exerted strong and negative associations with the WFS-causing pathogens (Fig. 3E), indicating their synergistic role in WFS resistance. Their genomes also encoded regulatory elements such as phrC, sinR and envZ (Fig. 6B) that facilitated inter-strain “communication” [44, 45], thereby supporting their synergism and stability. Importantly, combining these strains with pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine enabled an 87.5% diagnostic accuracy in distinguishing healthy from diseased shrimp (Table S13), highlighting their indispensable roles in endowing shrimp disease resistance. The ratio of SynCom members was ascertained using their relative abundance in healthy shrimp, rather than absolute abundance. Despite this limitation, application of relative abundance to design SynCom is a pragmatic and common approach in synthetic ecology, as it captures the relative ecological weight of specific taxa under in situ conditions [46]. Particularly, the designed SynCom effectively recapitulates the protective phenotype (Fig. 6E), suggesting that the proportion of these functional strains is a key determinant of shrimp disease resistance. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that keystone metabolite supplementation recruits probiotic symbionts that strengthen gut barriers and host immunity, thereby protecting shrimp against Vibrio infection.

Keystone metabolite supplementation alleviates host inflammation via the gut microbiota–LXA4–AP-1 axis

There is growing evidence that the gut microbiota directly influences host metabolism [47]. For instance, gut Akkermansia strains regulate obesity by modulating lipid metabolism in humans [48]. Accordingly, keystone metabolite–induced changes in the microbiota may trigger cascading alterations in gut metabolites, thereby modulating host immunity. Consistent with this notion, significant differences in gut metabolic profiles were observed between MV and VI shrimp, with LXA4 emerging as the most significantly enriched metabolite in MV shrimp (Fig. S7B, D). LXA4 is an anti-inflammatory lipid mediator considered a “braking signal” in inflammation [49]. It has been shown that LXA4 alleviates inflammatory response by attenuating the activation of the JNK/MAPK pathway in mammals [50]. Our findings extend this understanding by demonstrating that LXA4 suppressed inflammatory responses through direct inhibition of AP-1 expression in invertebrate shrimp (Fig. 5E), a transcription factor located downstream of JNK. Conversely, elevated gut and hepatopancreas LXA4 levels promoted the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and IFN-α (Fig. S17).

The co-occurrence network further depicted that B. subtilis, R. lacuscaerulensis and N. nitratireducens were all significantly and positively correlated with gut LXA4 levels, whereas directly antagonizing the three WFS-causing pathogens (Fig. 3E). This ecological pattern was validated in vivo, where SynCom supplementation increased gut and hepatopancreas LXA4 levels and enhanced shrimp survival (Fig. 6E, F). As a by-product of AA metabolism, LXA4 synthesis is regulated by the AA pathway. Certain gut bacteria, such as Faecalibaculum rodentium, have been reported to directly participate in AA metabolism [51]. Similarly, whole-genome analysis showed that SynCom members harbored genes such as ephA, ggt, and gstB facilitating AA metabolism (Fig. 6B). Meanwhile, LXA4 suppressed the generation of PGE2 [52], a pro-inflammatory substance also derived from AA. Thus, our designed SynCom protects shrimp by directly suppressing pathogens and indirectly alleviating inflammation via LXA4 production.

Pyruvic acid generates ATP in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, which subsequently activates phospholipase A2 (PLA2) that promotes the release of AA [53]. Furthermore, the generated ATP fulfills the high energetic demands of 15-lipoxygenase, a pivotal and rate-limiting enzyme in the biosynthesis of LXA4 [22]. DL-glutamine serves as a precursor for glutathione that maintains the redox balance required for lipoxygenase function [54]. Correspondingly, the alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism pathway and the citrate cycle were significantly enriched in the MV group compared with the VI group (Fig. S14E). We further validated the causal role of LXA4 in keystone metabolite–mediated disease resistance. Blocking LXA4 synthesis in vivo significantly increased MSP shrimp mortality and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. Although SynCom strains remained enriched relative to controls, their protective effects were lost compared with MV1 shrimp (Figs. 5I and Fig. S16F–H). Therefore, keystone metabolites selectively recruit B. subtilis, R. lacuscaerulensis, and N. nitratireducens, but LXA4 is indispensable for their prophylactic efficacy. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that keystone metabolites confer protection against WFS through the recruitment of the SynCom–LXA4–AP-1 axis.

Conclusions

Findings from this study markedly advance our mechanistic understanding of the tripartite associations among keystone metabolites, the gut microbiome, and host immune responses in promoting disease resistance. We provide evidence that keystone metabolites selectively recruit beneficial strains—B. subtilis, N. nitratireducens and R. lacuscaerulensis—which enhance gut network stability, antagonize pathogens, and suppress pro-inflammatory responses, thereby collectively strengthening host disease resistance. These bacteria also harbor genes encoding SCFAs (including pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine) and antimicrobial peptides, supporting their functional roles in host protection and consistent with their enrichment in healthy individuals worldwide. A SynCom composed of the three strains in a 4:3:2 ratio replicates the protective efficacy of keystone metabolites. Mechanistically, both the metabolites and the SynCom elevate gut LXA4 levels, which in turn suppress the downstream pro-inflammatory transcription factor AP-1, thereby alleviating inflammation (Fig. 7). These findings provide a conceptual and mechanistic framework for translating microbial ecology into rational, precision-based disease control. The next step is to apply these keystone metabolites and SynCom formulations in field experiments to evaluate their performance under realistic aquaculture conditions. Extending such precisely designed biocontrol strategies—defined by mechanism, high efficacy, and reproducibility—offers compelling opportunities to strengthen host health in aquaculture and, more broadly, in other animals including humans.

Materials and methods

Identification of keystone metabolites in WFS shrimp

Untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed on shrimp naturally affected by WFS, as well as matched healthy controls collected from greenhouse ponds (67 m × 33 m × 1.2 m) in Ningbo, China. Metabolites were extracted from shrimp gut contents using methanol-assisted protein precipitation [55]. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry was performed with a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid (positive) or 5 mM ammonium acetate (negative), with methanol as phase B. The gradient program was 0–1.5 min, 2% B; 1.5–12.0 min, 2–100% B; 12.0–14.0 min, 100% B; 14.0–14.1 min, 100–2% B; 14.1–17.0 min, 2% B. Mass spectrometry was performed using an Orbitrap Q Exactive operating in positive and negative ion modes. The key parameters were configured as follows: spray voltage, 3.2 kV; capillary temperature, 320 ℃; and sheath/aux gas flow rate, 40/10 arbitrary units.

Raw data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard v3.0.8789 and processed with the “XCMS v3.2” package for peak detection, filtering, and alignment. Metabolites were classified using the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). After standardized treatment by autoscaling with mean-centering and scaling to unit variance, the minimized metabolic biomarkers distinguishing health status were identified using the Random Forest algorithm in the “randomForest” package. The top biomarkers were identified by their EC values in the network using the “NetCoMi” package, which quantifies a metabolite’s influence in the network based on its connectivity with neighboring nodes [56]. Metabolic biomarkers with significantly decreased EC in WFS shrimp were designated as keystone metabolites, as their reduction drove the network shift from healthy to diseased status. Accordingly, pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine were identified as keystone metabolites, and their supplementation ratio (1:2) was determined according to their relative proportions in the gut of healthy shrimp (Fig. 2 and Table S1).

Experiment 1: effects of keystone metabolites supplementation on shrimp response to WFS.

Cryopreserved Vf, Vc and Vt stored in 50% glycerol at − 80 ℃ were thawed at 4 ℃, streaked on thiosulfate citrate bile salts sucrose (TCBS) agar (HB4130, Hopebio, Qingdao, China), and incubated for 18 h at 28 ℃. Single colonies were transferred to liquid TCBS medium and cultured at 28 ℃ with agitation (160 rpm) for 12 h. Cells were harvested, washed three times with sterile saline, and adjusted to an OD600 of 1 (≈ 1 × 109 CFU/mL) using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV752N, Shanghai, China). Based on their relative proportions in the gut of WFS-affected shrimp, the three pathogens were combined into a cocktail at a 7:2:1 ratio [17].

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) L. vannamei post-larvae (N = 1440) were obtained from a breeding base in Ningbo, China, which were randomly distributed into 24 tanks (50 L; 60 shrimp per tank). The feeding trial was conducted in a static-water system. To eliminate epibionts, seawater was disinfected with calcium hypochlorite (20 mg/L) for 24 h, and neutralized with sodium thiosulfate (30 mg/L), followed by 1 week of continuous aeration under open conditions prior to use. The tanks were maintained under a 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod. Seawater temperature was controlled at 27.0 ± 1.0 ℃. Continuous aeration was performed to maintain dissolved oxygen levels at ≥ 5 mg L − 1. After 1 week of acclimatization with a basal commercial feed, shrimp were assigned to four treatments: CK, MS, MV and VI (six replicate tanks each). CK and VI shrimp were fed on basal feed, whereas MS and MV shrimp were fed on basal feed supplemented with pyruvic acid (P815676, Macklin, Shanghai, China) and DL-glutamine (D860074, Macklin, Shanghai, China) at 3% of body weight twice daily (09:00 and 18:00). After 14 days (0 dpi), MV and VI shrimp were challenged with the Vibrio cocktail (final density 1 × 107 CFU/mL; Fig. 2F). About 10% of the rearing seawater was exchanged every 3 days, with replenished Vibrio inoculum after each exchange to sustain the initial concentration (1 × 107 CFU/mL). Mortality was recorded every 12 h post-challenge. The trial was terminated at 9 dpi due to high mortality in the VI group (Fig. 2F).

Gut and hepatopancreas tissues were aseptically collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 ℃. Gut samples were processed for metagenomic and metabolomic analyses, whereas hepatopancreas samples were used for biochemical (both enzyme and ELISA assays) and transcriptomic analyses. Additionally, gut and hepatopancreas samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for histological observation.

Experiment 2: pharmacological inhibition of LXA4 in vivo

To validate the role of LXA4 in keystone metabolite–mediated disease resistance, shrimp 15-lipoxygenase was pharmacologically inhibited by PD146176 (SJ-MX1640, SparkJade, Jinan, China). A total of 720 SPF shrimp were randomly assigned to four groups (CK1, MV1, VI1, and MSP), each with six replicate tanks (30 shrimp per tank). MSP shrimp were fed on keystone metabolite–supplemented diets and injected with 15-lipoxygenase inhibitor PD146176 (1 μg/g) every 2 days. CK1 and VI1 shrimp were fed on basal feed, whereas MV1 shrimp received basal diet supplemented with keystone metabolites for 14 days. Subsequently, MSP, MV1 and VI1 shrimp were challenged with the Vibrio cocktail at 0 dpi (final density 1 × 107 CFU/mL; Fig. 5G). Shrimp mortality was recorded every 12 h post-challenge, and the trial was terminated at 9 dpi due to high mortality in the VI1 group (Fig. 5I). Gut and hepatopancreas samples were collected aseptically for histological, biochemical (ELISA assays), and metagenomic analyses (Fig. 5G).

Global distribution of gut B. subtilis, R. lacuscaerulensis, and N. nitratireducens between shrimp health statuses

We searched PubMed and Google Scholar (as of April 2025) using the keywords of “disease”, “L. vannamei”, “shrimp”, and “gut microbiota/microbiome”. Studies were included if raw 16S rRNA gene data (fastq format) and metadata specifying case or control status were publicly available. Fifteen studies covering eight different diseases were included (Table S10). For per-study analysis, amplicon reads were rarefied to the lowest sequencing depth within each study. To track the prevalence of R. lacuscaerulensis, N. nitratireducens, and B. subtilis across experiments, we implemented a targeted alignment strategy. First, 16S rRNA gene sequences were extracted from the whole-genome sequences of the three strains using Barrnap v0.9 (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap). These sequences were served as precise local references, rather than generic type-strain sequences from public databases. Subsequently, amplicon sequencing data from external studies were aligned against this custom reference database using BLASTN v2.10.0 [57]. To obtain high taxonomic resolution, only hits with an E-value < 1e − 5 and identity ≥ 99% were considered as matching our target strains.

Experiment 3: identification and isolation of gut keystone strains recruited by keystone metabolite supplementation

Gut symbionts were isolated from MV shrimp at 9 dpi, given that these enriched strains under metabolite supplementation and infection were likely to confer WFS resistance. Specifically, gut tissues were homogenized in 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and supernatants serially diluted and plated on Luria–Bertani (LB) agar. Colonies appearing after 3–7 days at 28 ℃ were purified by re-streaking on LB agar. Isolates were preserved in 50% glycerol at − 80 ℃. The 16S rRNA gene of each isolate was amplified using universal primers 27F/1492R and sequenced by Magigene Biotech (Guangzhou, China). Candidate strains showing 100% sequence identity with R. lacuscaerulensis, N. nitratireducens or B. subtilis were selected and whole-genome sequenced at an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Raw reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.39 [58] to remove adapters and low-quality bases. De novo assembly was performed using SPAdes v4.2.0 with default parameters [59] excluding contigs < 100 bp. Assembly quality was assessed with Quast v5.2.0 [60]. Genomes were annotated and visualized using Proksee [61], and gene prediction was performed with Prokka v1.2.0 [62]. CAZyme genes were annotated using the dbCAN2 meta server [63] against the CAZy database (http://www.cazy.org/).

Experiment 4: construction and validation of a SynCom in enhancing shrimp disease resistance

R. lacuscaerulensis, N. nitratireducens, and B. subtilis were consistently enriched, discriminative, and keystone strains in healthy shrimp compared with diseased individuals (Table S11). A SynCom was therefore constructed according to their ratio (relative abundance, 4:3:2) in the gut of healthy shrimp, rather than equal proportions. An in vivo validation experiment was performed to assess SynCom efficacy against WFS. In short, a total of 540 SPF shrimp were randomly divided into three groups (CK2, VI2 and SV), with six replicate tanks (30 shrimp per tank). The SV group was fed on our SynCom-supplemented diet (1 × 107 CFU/g diet) for 14 days before immersion challenge with the pathogenic cocktail. Treatments for CK2 and VI2 followed the same design as CK and VI in Experiment 1. Gut and hepatopancreas samples were collected aseptically for metagenomic, transcriptomic, and biochemical (ELISA assays) analyses. The experimental design was shown in Fig. 6D.

Testing the capacity of R. lacuscaerulensis, N. nitratireducens, and B. subtilis to metabolize keystone metabolites in vitro

Bacterial cultures were divided into two groups: M9 minimal salts medium (A1374401, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) inoculated with R. lacuscaerulensis, N. nitratireducens or B. subtilis (a) with or (b) without keystone metabolites (pyruvic acid and DL-glutamine; 5 μM). Cultures were incubated at 30 ℃ with agitation (160 rpm) for 24 h. Each treatment included three replicates. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, washed twice with sterile water, and weighed to determine bacterial biomass.

Enzyme activity assays

Hepatopancreas tissues (0.1 g) were thawed and homogenized with 0.4 mL pre-cooled normal saline using a freezer mill (JXFSTPRP-CL-BSC, Shanghai, China). Homogenates were centrifuged at 4 ℃, 5000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected for determination of immune (catalase, alkaline phosphatase, lysozyme) and digestive (pepsin, lipase) enzyme activities using commercial kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering, Nanjing, China).

Cytokines analysis

Hepatopancreas inflammatory cytokines were quantified by ELISA. Frozen tissues were washed with PBS, homogenized with a freezer mill, and centrifuged at 7500 rpm for 20 min at 4 ℃. TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10 and IFN-α levels were measured using commercial ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s instructions (Kete, Yancheng, China). Absorbance was read at 450 nm within 15 min using a microplate reader (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA), and cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

Histological observation

Gut and hepatopancreas samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 24 h, rinsed with 1 × PBS buffer. The tissues were then dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (50–95%), and embedded in paraffin. The tissue blocks were sectioned at 5 μm, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined by microscopy (NI-U, Nikon, Japan). Cell types and numbers in the hepatopancreas, as well as gut villus length, were quantified using ImageJ v1.54 g (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Detection of AP-1 and LXA4 level

Gut LXA4 level, hepatopancreas AP-1 activity, and LXA4 level were measured using ELISA kits (ZCIBIO, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Gut microbial metagenomic analysis

Total DNA from shrimp gut was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using TBS-380 and NanoDrop 2000, respectively. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced at an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (2 × 150 bp; Magigene Biotech, Guangzhou, China).

Raw sequences were quality filtered with Trimmomatic v0.39 (length > 30 bp, average base quality > 20) [58]. Host-derived contamination was removed using Bowtie2 v2.5.4 in sensitive mode [64]. Taxonomic profiles were generated with Kraken2 v2.1.3 based on its default bacteria database [65], and species relative abundances were calculated with Bracken v2.9 [66]. Remaining reads were de novo assembled using Megahit v1.2.9 with default parameters [67]. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from assembled contigs (> 300 bp) using Prodigal v2.6.3 [68]. Redundant ORFs were removed with CD-HIT v4.8.1 [69], producing a non-redundant gene catalog (unigenes, 95% identity, 90% coverage). Gene abundances were quantified using Salmon v0.11.3 [70], normalized by mapped reads and gene length. Functional annotation was performed with Diamond v2.1.11 [71] against the KEGG database. DFPs were identified using FDR < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|> 1 as thresholds [72]. DFPs were visualized using the iPath explorer.

Transcriptomic analysis of shrimp hepatopancreas tissues

Total RNA was extracted from hepatopancreas tissues using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). RNA concentration and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), respectively. mRNA was enriched with oligo (dT) magnetic beads and randomly fragmented into 100–400 bp segments. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Super RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, USA), and cDNAs were synthesized, PCR-amplified, adapter-ligated, and sequenced at an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Magigene Biotech, Guangzhou, China).

Raw RNA-seq reads were quality filtered with FastQC v0.11.8 [73] to remove adapter-containing reads, reads with > 10% undetermined nucleotides, and low-quality reads (Phred score < 20). Clean reads were mapped to the L. vannamei genome (NCBI Genome ID: ASM378908v1) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [74]. Reads mapped per gene were counted with FeatureCounts v2.0.3 [75]. The FPKM value was used to estimate transcript abundance, which eliminates the influence of different gene lengths and sequencing discrepancies [21]. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 v1.46.0 [76]. Functional enrichment of DEGs was assessed with clusterProfiler v4.14.6 [77], applying both gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R v4.2.1 [78] unless otherwise specified. Differences between groups (mean ± s.d.) were assessed using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA at P < 0.05. Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using the “ropls” package to compare the overall differences in metabolite distributions between groups. Differential metabolites were further selected based on the VIP score > 1 from the OPLS-DA model and univariate analysis, with statistical significance of P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change|> 1. Microbial diversity was calculated at the species level. Beta diversity was assessed by PCoA using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity. PERMANOVA was applied with the “adonis” function to quantify the effects of keystone metabolites, infection, and their interactions on gut microbiota variance. Functional diversity index (i.e., Shannon index) was calculated using the “vegan” package.

Co-occurrence networks were constructed using the “Hmisc” and “igraph” packages, only retaining strong (|r| > 0.8) and significant (P < 0.05) correlations, which were visualized in Gephi v0.10 [79]. Network stability was evaluated using modularity, vulnerability, connectivity, and the proportion of negative correlations [80]. Modularity quantifies the partitioning of a network into distinct, highly interconnected sub-communities or modules. Such compartmentalization is pivotal for network stability, as it facilitates the containment of localized perturbations within specific modules, thereby preventing systemic propagation across the entire community. Thus, high modularity is widely recognized as an indicator of increased stability [81, 82]. Ecologically, networks dominated by positive correlations (cooperation) are inherently fragile, because mutualistic dependencies could trigger cascading collapse upon keystone taxon loss. Conversely, negative correlations (competition) bolster stability due to the reduced inter-species reliance and enhanced resilience to external invasions [83]. Gene co-expression networks were constructed using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) [84] to identify immune-related modules. The minimum module size was set at 30 genes, and modules with eigengene correlations > 0.75 were merged. A key module most strongly associated with inflammatory cytokines was visualized in Cytoscape v3.9.1 [85]. Hub genes were identified using thresholds of weight > 0.35 and degree > 5. Then, hub genes were uploaded to Metascape [86] for enrichment analysis.

Multi-omics integration was performed using the “mixOmics” package [87] in R project. Consequently, an exploratory approach was adopted using the block.splsda() function without pre-specifying the keepX parameter [88]. To elucidate the interactions between omics blocks, multiple visualization approaches were employed—including circos plots (circosPlot()), sample scatterplots (plotDiablo()), and clustered image maps (cimDiablo()). A stringent correlation threshold of 0.7 was applied to isolate and highlight strong biological associations. PLS-PM [89] was applied to quantify associations among variables influencing disease resistance (defined by pathogen abundance and shrimp mortality). Mediation analysis was performed using the “mediation v4.5.0” package, with bootstrap testing (1000 Monte Carlo simulations) to assess causal interactions among gut microbiota (extracted from the microbial metagenomic analysis), metabolites (metabolomic data), and host gene expression (transcriptomic data) [18]. Graphs were generated using the “ggplot2” package and Graphpad Prism 10.
