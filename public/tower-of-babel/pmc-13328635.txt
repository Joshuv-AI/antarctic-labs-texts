Study on isolation pillar thickness optimization and slope stability control in hanging wall ore mining
Authors: Shuai Li, Liuyu Wang, Zhenyu Dan, Tubing Yin, Lifeng Yu, Zeming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aiming at the problems of easy disturbance to the overlying slope and difficulty in scientifically determining the thickness of the isolation pillar during the recovery of hanging-wall orebody under open-pit to underground mining conditions, this study takes Shizhuyuan Mine as the engineering background. FLAC3D numerical simulation and the strength reduction method (SRM) are adopted to conduct a comparative analysis of the stability response characteristics of the “slope-goaf-backfill” system under five working conditions with isolation pillar thicknesses ranging from 10 m to 50 m. The results show that although the safety factors of all working conditions meet the requirements for Grade I slope stability, the thickness of the isolation pillar exhibits a significant threshold effect on system deformation control: when the thickness increases to 30 m, the maximum system displacement is significantly reduced from the excessive level (> 80 mm) to approximately 34 mm, and the overall stability is obviously improved. However, the stability gain brought by further increasing the thickness gradually weakens. Under the recommended thickness, the stress distribution, displacement evolution, and plastic zone development of the goaf and backfill are all within the safe and controllable range. Considering both stability requirements and resource recovery rate, an isolation pillar thickness around 30 m can achieve a reasonable balance between safety and economy, which can provide a quantitative design reference for the safe recovery of hanging-wall orebodies under similar open-pit-underground coordinated mining conditions.

Introduction

With the continuous advancement of open-pit mines to deep levels, the gradual depletion of shallow resources, the rapid increase in stripping ratio, and the continuous rise in slope maintenance costs1, have made the coordinated mining from open-pit to underground an important direction to improve resource utilization efficiency and ensure the sustainable production of mines. During this transition process, the hanging-wall orebodies occurring below the final open-pit slope have become key successive resources.

However, hanging-wall orebodies are usually located below the final open-pit slope and within the stress influence range of high and steep slopes. Their underground mining activities will inevitably disturb the original mechanical balance of the slope-goaf system. If the control measures are improper, the mining disturbance may extend upward along the pillars and surrounding rocks, leading to progressive slope deformation, local damage, or even overall instability risks. Existing studies have shown that slope stability under hanging-wall orebody mining conditions is significantly sensitive to underground mining methods, mining sequences, and support structures2–5.

Focusing on the influence mechanism of hanging-wall orebody mining on slope stability, existing studies have systematically explored the mechanical response characteristics of the stope-slope system during mining and filling through numerical simulation and engineering case analysis6–8. Therefore, during the transition stage from open-pit to underground mining, how to realize the safe and efficient recovery of hanging-wall orebodies under the premise of ensuring the long-term stability of the open-pit slope has become one of the key engineering problems faced by such mines.

Regarding the research on isolation pillars, the core lies in reasonably determining their reserved thickness to improve resource recovery rate while ensuring structural safety. An excessively thick isolation pillar will result in resource waste, while an insufficient reserved thickness may lead to instability risks. Therefore, in engineering practice, methods such as the limit span theory, empirical formula method, and limit equilibrium method are often used to determine the reasonable thickness9. On this basis, some studies have quantitatively analyzed the stability and safe thickness of isolation pillars through numerical simulation, comprehensive index evaluation, and mechanical criteria combined with specific engineering conditions10–13.

In recent years, with the complexity of backfill mining technology and deep mining conditions, the research on isolation pillars has gradually developed towards multi-working conditions and refinement, such as the analysis of pillar stability under different filling conditions and stress environments14–17. In general, existing studies have provided useful references for the design of isolation pillar thickness, but most focus on underground stopes or single working conditions, and few systematically study them within the overall framework of the coordinated action of open-pit slope and underground mining disturbance. Their applicability under open-pit to underground conditions still needs further exploration.

Focusing on slope stability evaluation and mining disturbance mechanism, scholars at home and abroad have carried out a lot of research. As a traditional analysis method, the limit equilibrium method calculates the safety factor by satisfying the force and moment balance conditions, and has formed mature calculation systems such as the simplified Bishop method, simplified Janbu method, Morgenstern-Price method, and Spencer method18–20. However, such methods usually need to preset potential sliding surfaces, which are difficult to reflect the nonlinear mechanical behavior of rock mass materials and the progressive evolution process of slope failure, and have certain limitations under complex geological conditions and significant mining disturbance scenarios.

With the development of computational mechanics and numerical analysis technology, numerical methods such as the finite element method, finite difference method, and discrete element method have gradually become important means for slope stability research, which can simulate the goaf formation, surrounding rock deformation, and stress redistribution processes within the framework of continuous media or discrete structures.

Existing studies have combined numerical methods with SRM and widely applied them to slope stability evaluation in open-pit mines and complex geological conditions21–23. SRM was first introduced into slope stability analysis by Zienkiewicz et al., and later scholars such as Matsui and San, Zheng et al., Cheng et al., and Yu systematically expanded and verified the method under different constitutive models, calculation dimensions, and engineering conditions.

A large number of studies have shown that SRM does not need to preset sliding surfaces, can quantitatively solve the overall safety factor under heterogeneous rock masses, multi-stage disturbances, and complex boundary conditions, and has developed into one of the widely used slope overall stability evaluation methods in two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) numerical analysis24–27.

In recent years, with the development of computing power and numerical modeling technology, 3D slope stability analysis methods have gradually attracted attention. Relevant studies have pointed out that under engineering conditions with significant topographic undulations, complex spatial distribution of structures, or asymmetric loads, 3D analysis can more truly reflect the mechanical response characteristics of slopes by comparing 2D and 3D analysis results28–30.

However, due to the limitations of computational cost and modeling complexity, a large number of current studies are still carried out based on 2D assumptions, and directly applying 2D methods to 3D slope problems may lead to deviations in stability evaluation results. In addition, some so-called 3D analysis methods still rely on 2D analysis ideas in essence, and their ability to characterize real spatial effects is limited.

For open-pit mine engineering, existing studies have analyzed the impact of underground mining disturbance on open-pit slope response from 2D or 3D perspectives31–34, but most focus on a single link or local effect. In general, for the engineering scenario of hanging-wall orebody mining under open-pit slopes, which has significant 3D structural characteristics and strong mining disturbance effects, the law of stability evolution still lacks systematic and in-depth research.

Although relevant studies have made certain progress in slope stability analysis, isolation pillar design, and numerical simulation methods, most of them focus on underground mining or adopt 2D simplified methods, and fail to conduct an integrated 3D coupling analysis of open-pit slope and underground mining disturbance. There are still several urgent problems to be solved for the specific engineering scenario of hanging-wall orebody mining under open-pit slopes:

(1) Most existing studies analyze open-pit slope stability and underground mining disturbance separately, lacking an integrated 3D coupling research framework of slope-goaf;

(2) The design of isolation pillar thickness still mainly relies on empirical methods or single working condition analysis, lacking quantitative optimization basis based on multi-working condition and multi-index comparison, making it difficult to balance safety and resource recovery rate;

(3) Insufficient attention is paid to the dynamic evolution characteristics of the bearing system during sequential mining and filling, especially the instability risk during the stage when the load-bearing is transferred from the original rock pillar to the backfill, which lacks systematic research;

(4) There is a lack of a coordinated trade-off mechanism between safety and resource recovery rate, and no thickness optimization criteria that can directly guide engineering design have been formed.

To address the above problems, this study takes Shizhuyuan open-pit mine as the engineering background, constructs a 3D numerical model under real geological conditions, and combines the “two-step sublevel open-stope subsequent filling” process to systematically analyze the mechanical response characteristics of the slope-goaf system under different isolation pillar thickness conditions.

The main work and innovations of the study are reflected in the following aspects:

(1) Establish a 3D slope-goaf coupling analysis framework for open-pit to underground conditions, and systematically characterize the coordinated evolution characteristics of slopes and underground mining processes;

(2) Construct a dynamic simulation system covering the entire mining-filling process, and identify the key stability control links during the load-bearing transition stage;

(3) Propose a comprehensive evaluation idea of isolation pillar thickness based on multi-index response, providing a quantitative basis for the coordinated optimization of safety and resource recovery rate.

This study clarifies the essential differences from existing research by constructing a 3D slope-goaf coupling model and multi-index quantitative evaluation method. Relevant research can provide engineering references for slope stability control and safe recovery of hanging-wall orebodies during the open-pit-underground coordinated mining stage.

Mine overview and research methods

Mine overview

This study takes the tungsten polymetallic open-pit stope of Hunan Shizhuyuan Nonferrous Metals Co., Ltd. as the engineering background. The mining area is located in Suxian District, Chenzhou City, Hunan Province, with a typical mountainous landform and relatively simple regional strata and structures. The orebody is mainly hosted in skarn, a typical contact metasomatic rock formed by the interaction between magmatic rocks and carbonate rocks. The hanging wall and footwall surrounding rocks of the orebody are marble and granite, respectively.

The data and conclusions of the on-site mine investigation are obtained from field survey and in-situ investigation of the study mine. Field investigations show that the rock mass has good overall integrity, dense structure, and thin weathered layer, belonging to hard rock mass with high stability, which has certain natural advantages for slope stability. The final area of the open-pit mine is about 616,200 m².

With the mining extending to the final boundary, a high and steep artificial slope with a height of hundreds of meters and a slope angle of 45°-65° will be formed. Its stability is not only related to the safety of mining operations in the pit but also has an important impact on regional public safety and traffic operation because it is adjacent to the Gangan River and the main transportation roads of the mining area below. Therefore, the stability control of the overlying slope is one of the core requirements for the safe production of the mine.

Below the determined open-pit mining boundary, exploration has revealed a considerable scale of hanging-wall orebodies. This part of the resources has important economic value, but due to factors such as mining right red lines, stripping ratio, ecological constraints, and green mine construction, it cannot be recovered by expanding the open-pit boundary, and must rely on underground mining methods to achieve safe and efficient utilization.

Research methods and objects

To systematically carry out the research on the optimization of isolation pillar thickness during the recovery of hanging-wall orebodies under open-pit to underground conditions, the study is carried out in accordance with the technical route of “engineering background-model construction-numerical simulation-stability evaluation-engineering conclusions”, and its overall research ideas and analysis process are shown in Fig. 3.

Overall research ideas and analysis flow chart (Model Construction Drawn with 3DMine Mining Engineering Software, Version:3DMine 2014.12, URL: https://www.3dmine-cn.com/; Numerical Simulation Drawn with FLAC3D, Version: FLAC3D 6.00, URL: https://itascasoftware.com/products/flac3d/).

Under this framework, first, the research objects and engineering conditions are clarified, and a 3D engineering geological model including open-pit slopes, goafs, and backfills is constructed; then, through the numerical simulation of the entire process of sequential mining and subsequent filling, key response indicators such as stress, displacement, plastic zone, and safety factor are extracted; on the basis of multi-working condition comparative analysis, the impact of different isolation pillar thicknesses on system stability is comprehensively evaluated, and the reasonable isolation pillar thickness is determined accordingly. The specific research methods and objects are explained below.

(1) Model Construction and Mesh Generation.

The study adopts CAD-Rhino-FLAC3D three-dimensional coupling modeling technology. First, the slope geological section is drawn in CAD based on geological exploration data to clarify the rock stratum interface and orebody boundary; then, the geometric model is imported into the Rhinoceros platform for solid modeling, and the Griddle plug-in is used to divide hexahedral meshes to generate calculation units with regular shape and uniform quality.

The final model dimensions are: length 800 m in the X direction; height 500 m in the Z direction; thickness 20 m in the Y direction (along the slope strike). The Y-direction thickness is set to 20 m to weaken the influence of lateral boundaries on the core analysis area, make the model meet the plane strain assumption, and improve the solution efficiency while ensuring the calculation accuracy.

Simulation scheme design

To ensure the effectiveness and practicality of the study, considering the complexity and variability of open-pit mining technology, the following simplified assumptions are formulated during the modeling process:

(1) The rock mass is assumed to be an ideal elastoplastic body, and its strength and volume remain unchanged once the yield point is reached.

(2) The orebody and surrounding rock are regarded as locally homogeneous and isotropic materials.

(3) Considering the limitations of the finite element program, it is assumed that the site is not affected by tectonic activities, the in-situ stress is of the geostatic field type, the contact between various rock strata is conformable, and the interior of the rock strata is regarded as a continuous medium.

(4) The model does not consider the influence of groundwater activities, nor the influence of structural planes, fractures, and weak layers that may exist in the rock strata and orebody on stability.

(5) In the calculation, the selected load remains unchanged, does not change with the direction of the element, and always maintains the initial direction. The surface load is equal to the weight of the overlying rock stratum or backfill layer.

These simplified assumptions help optimize the efficiency of analysis and calculation, ensure that the study can be carried out efficiently under relatively simplified conditions, and at the same time ensure the reliability and applicability of the results.

Based on the above assumptions, the study designs five comparative schemes for isolation pillar thickness, namely 10 m, 20 m, 30 m, 40 m, and 50 m. The only variable is the thickness of the isolation pillar near the open-pit slope, and its geometric layout is shown in Fig. 6. The selected thickness range covers the common pillar sizes in engineering practice, which helps to reveal the influence of different thickness changes on the mechanical response and identify possible threshold effects.

Among these five schemes, the 30 m thickness is considered to have high engineering feasibility. Therefore, after completing the overall stability comparison, a special analysis is further carried out on the mechanical evolution of the goaf and backfill system under this scheme to ensure that the recommended thickness has sufficient safety reserves under sequential mining and filling conditions.

All schemes adopt the same sequential mining method, and combined with the evolution curve of the safety factor, representative key mining stages are selected for key research on stress, displacement, and plastic zone, so as to comprehensively evaluate the impact of isolation pillar thickness on the overall process stability.

Safety factor analysis based on SRM

The safety factor is a key indicator for evaluating the overall anti-sliding capacity of the slope, and its change with the mining process can reflect the disturbance and recovery process caused by underground mining and filling. The evolution law of the safety factor under different isolation pillar thickness schemes is shown in Fig. 7, and the typical characteristic values are shown in Table 4.

Fig. 7Evolution curves of slope safety factors under different isolation pillar thickness schemes.

Evolution curves of slope safety factors under different isolation pillar thickness schemes.

The simulation results show that with the isolation pillar set, the minimum safety factors of all schemes are in the range of 3.77–4.90, which are significantly higher than the design standard of 1.20–1.25 for Grade I slopes specified in the “Technical Code for Slope Engineering of Non-Coal Open-Pit Mines” (GB51016-2014)36. This indicates that the five thickness schemes all have sufficient safety reserves in terms of strength, and the overall anti-sliding stability meets the long-term service requirements.

It should be noted that all schemes have a common “safety factor trough” in Step 4. It can be found that the valley value of the safety factor appears at Step 4 in all schemes, which is a universal phenomenon. This stage corresponds to the complete formation of the first set of permanent pillars composed of low-strength backfill, which begins to replace the original rock pillars to bear the main supporting role. This characteristic is closely related to backfill stiffness, backfill strength, interface contact conditions, and filling timing.

Since the elastic modulus of the backfill is only about 0.23GPa, much lower than that of the surrounding rock (greater than 28GPa), the sudden drop in stiffness leads to obvious adjustment of the bearing system, stress redistribution to the surrounding rock mass, and a temporary weakening of the slope anti-sliding potential.

The low stiffness of backfill leads to temporary stress redistribution at the early stage of filling, resulting in a short-term drop in the safety factor. As the bearing structure stabilizes, the safety factor gradually recovers, which explains why this valley stage exists in all schemes.

Although the safety factor is still much higher than the lower limit of the specification at this time, from the perspective of the entire process, this stage is the most sensitive risk inflection point, and it is advisable to strengthen monitoring and early warning in engineering practice.

The isolation pillar thickness has an obvious interval characteristic for the improvement of system stability. In the range of 10–30 m, the minimum safety factor increases from 3.77 to 4.56, with an increase of about 21%, indicating that the pillar stiffness and bearing capacity increase significantly with the thickness at this stage, which is the main controlling factor for improving slope stability. When the thickness is further increased to 40 m and 50 m, the minimum safety factor only increases to 4.81 and 4.90, with an overall increase of less than 7.5%, showing a typical characteristic of diminishing marginal benefits.

From the mechanical mechanism, the thickening of the isolation pillar changes the stress flow path of the overlying rock mass of the goaf.

When the thickness is insufficient (≤ 20 m), the pillar is difficult to form a stable stress arch structure, and the goaf disturbance can penetrate the pillar and spread to the overlying slope, resulting in high sensitivity of the system stability.

when the thickness increases to 30 m, the cross-sectional stiffness of the pillar approaches the critical level, the stress arch structure is effectively developed, and the stress forms a relatively stable bypass channel between the pillar and the surrounding rock, which significantly weakens the mechanical coupling between the goaf and the slope; after further thickening to 40 m and 50 m, the stress arch structure is basically stable, and the further improvement of the bearing path is limited, so the gain of the safety factor slows down.

In summary, based on the comprehensive judgment of the evolution law of the safety factor and the reconstruction mechanism of the bearing system, 30 m can be regarded as the “safety efficiency critical point” of the isolation pillar thickness.

Deformation control analysis of surface displacement

The surface displacement of the slope is an important indicator reflecting the controllability of system deformation, and its evolution mode and final residual value are closely related to the long-term stability of the slope. Moreover, the displacement curves have not converged at the end of mining, indicating that the system is difficult to establish a new stable balance, and there are risks of cumulative deformation and delayed failure.

From the perspective of the displacement evolution mode, the 10 m and 20 m schemes show a “continuous growth type”, and the system cannot form a stable balance during the disturbance process; while the schemes with thickness of 30 m and above show a “rapid growth-platform convergence type”, indicating that the pillar and surrounding rock can effectively coordinate deformation, enabling the system to reach a new balance state in a timely manner in the middle and later stages of mining.

The displacement evolution curves in Fig. 8 show obvious differences among different schemes. For the 10 m and 20 m isolation pillar schemes, the displacement continues to increase without convergence, mainly because the pillar thickness is too small to form an effective stress-bearing structure. The low stiffness and weak disturbance resistance lead to continuous stress adjustment and large-scale deformation development.

In contrast, the displacement curves of schemes with a thickness of 30 m and above gradually tend to be stable and convergent. This is attributed to the reasonable stiffness matching between the isolation pillar and surrounding rock, which can effectively restrain the propagation of mining disturbance and form a stable stress path. With the increase of pillar thickness, the overall bearing system is gradually improved, and the deformation tends to converge under the action of self-stability and stress redistribution.

The above differences are closely related to stress paths, stiffness matching, and disturbance propagation mechanisms.

The above threshold characteristics can be attributed to the “stiffness matching mechanism” between the isolation pillar and the surrounding rock. When the pillar thickness is insufficient, its overall stiffness is low, and its ability to constrain the subsidence and lateral expansion of the goaf is limited. Mining disturbance cannot be effectively attenuated and is easily transmitted upward to the slope along the pillar, leading to continuous stress adjustment and persistent displacement amplification.

when the thickness reaches about 30 m, the pillar stiffness and surrounding rock strength achieve relative matching, forming a stable stress transmission path, and the disturbance is mainly dissipated and attenuated in the near field of the goaf, thereby significantly weakening the impact on the slope, making the displacement process controlled and finally converged; further thickening is still helpful to reduce displacement, but the improvement range has been significantly reduced, showing a “performance saturation” characteristic.

The comprehensive comparative analysis results of safety factor and displacement response show that: although the 10 m and 20 m isolation pillar thickness schemes meet the specification requirements in terms of safety factor, the deformation development of the slope and goaf is relatively significant, the overall deformation control ability is insufficient, and the stability safety reserve is limited, so they should be eliminated; the 40 m and 50 m thickness schemes are better in terms of stability and deformation control, but they lead to higher ore loss rate and lower resource recovery, obviously increasing resource waste and mining economy loss. Compared with the 30 m scheme, the stability improvement shows an obvious characteristic of diminishing marginal benefits.

Therefore, the determination of isolation pillar thickness can be regarded as a dual-objective trade-off between safety and resource economy. Under the engineering conditions of this study, the 30 m scheme not only satisfies stability and deformation control requirements, but also maintains higher resource recovery rate and lower ore loss rate, achieving the optimal balance between safety, deformation control and resource utilization efficiency. Thus, 30 m is recommended as the comprehensive optimal thickness of the isolation pillar.

Step 1 corresponds to the initial stress state; Step 4 corresponds to the sensitive period when the bearing system is transformed from the original rock pillar to the backfill pillar; Step 6 is the stage of large-scale mining with the strongest disturbance; Step 9 is the final stable stage after the completion of mining and filling. The analysis is carried out from three aspects: stress field, displacement field, and plastic zone to reveal the evolution process of the system bearing mechanism and the overall stability.

Discussion

Focusing on the optimization of isolation pillar thickness under open-pit-underground coordinated mining conditions, the results of multi-scheme numerical simulation show that the influence of isolation pillar thickness on the stability of the “slope-goaf-backfill” system has significant nonlinear characteristics. Its action mechanism is not simply linearly enhanced with the increase of thickness, but controls the mechanical response mode of the system by changing the bearing path, stress transfer mode, and overall stiffness matching relationship of the system.

When the thickness of the isolation pillar is small, the mining disturbance is difficult to be effectively attenuated in the near field of the goaf, and the disturbance is easily transmitted to the overlying slope along the pillar. The overall stiffness of the system is insufficient, and the slope deformation is highly sensitive to the mining process; with the increase of the isolation pillar thickness, its isolation effect and bearing capacity gradually increase, the stress arch structure is formed and stably developed, and the system stability and deformation control ability are significantly improved.

However, when the pillar thickness continues to increase, the stability improvement range decreases significantly, showing a typical characteristic of diminishing marginal benefits, indicating that simply increasing the pillar thickness to improve the safety reserve is not the optimal strategy.

From the perspective of the entire process, the dynamic conversion of the bearing system during sequential mining and filling is a key link affecting system stability. The numerical results show that during the transition from the original rock pillar to the backfill-surrounding rock cooperative bearing, stress redistribution and local deformation may increase temporarily, but under the condition of reasonable isolation pillar thickness, this adjustment process has good recoverability, and the system can re-establish a stable mechanical balance state after the backfill fully participates in bearing.

This indicates that the backfill pillar formation stage is a sensitive node in the evolution of system stability. In engineering practice, emphasis should be placed on the control of filling quality and the monitoring of deformation response at this stage to avoid the cumulative amplification of adverse disturbances.

Compared with existing studies, most current researches on the stability and thickness design of isolation pillars mainly focus on underground stopes, and the analysis methods are mostly limited to empirical formulas, limit equilibrium theory or 2D numerical models37–40, which rarely consider the 3D coupling effect of open-pit slope and underground mining disturbance. The research focus is usually on the stability of the pillar itself or local surrounding rock, lacking systematic consideration of the overall slope-goaf-backfill system.

For the recovery of hanging-wall orebodies under open-pit to underground conditions, although existing studies have explored the impact of underground mining on open-pit slope stability from a 2D or local 3D perspective, most of the work still separates slope stability analysis from pillar thickness design, lacking systematic research on the overall “slope-goaf-backfill” system within a unified 3D spatial framework, especially insufficient attention to the bearing path reconstruction and system stiffness matching mechanism during sequential mining.

To address the above deficiencies, under the specific engineering scenario of open-pit to underground mining, this study constructs a 3D numerical model based on real geological conditions, incorporates the isolation pillar into the overall bearing system of the “open-pit slope-goaf-backfill”, and reveals the threshold characteristics and mechanical mechanism of the influence of isolation pillar thickness on system stability through multi-thickness working condition comparison and full-process dynamic simulation.

The research results show that under the premise of meeting the overall stability requirements, there is a reasonable range of isolation pillar thickness that balances safety, deformation control, and resource recovery rate, providing a quantifiable engineering basis for the optimization of isolation pillar thickness during the open-pit-underground coordinated mining stage of hanging-wall orebodies.

It should be noted that the research conclusions are still subject to certain simplifying assumptions. The numerical model ignores groundwater, tectonic stress, structural planes, joints, and weak interlayers, which may cause deviations between simulation results and actual engineering stability. Additionally, dynamic disturbance, rock mass time-dependent behavior are not considered, and parameters are mainly from static tests.

To reduce these deviations, future research can gradually relax the above assumptions: conduct seepage-stress multi-field coupling analysis, introduce discrete fracture networks to characterize joints and weak layers, and combine on-site monitoring with dynamic simulation to optimize pillar thickness and improve stability evaluation accuracy.

Conclusions

Based on the open-pit-underground coordinated mining conditions of Shizhuyuan open-pit mine, this study uses FLAC3D numerical simulation to build a 3D mechanical model including open-pit slopes, underlying goafs, and backfills. It analyzes the overall system stability during hanging-wall orebody recovery. The results show continuous slope stress distribution, principal stress within rock mass strength, and no through plastic failure zone, verifying the model’s engineering applicability.

Taking isolation pillar thickness as the core factor, comparative analysis of different working conditions reveals: thin pillars lead to obvious mining disturbance transmission and large slope deformation. With increased thickness, system stiffness and isolation effect improve, stabilizing the slope. The 30 m scheme performs reasonably in stability and deformation control, with limited gains from further thickening.

Analysis of the 30 m thickness condition shows controllable stress, displacement, and plastic zone evolution of goafs and backfills during sequential mining and filling. No strength failure or plastic zone penetration occurs, and the bearing system smoothly transitions to backfill-surrounding rock cooperative bearing. The 30 m thickness balances stability, safety, and resource recovery, providing a reference for similar projects.
