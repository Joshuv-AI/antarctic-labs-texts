Pi-Transformer: A Physics-informed Attention Mechanism for Time Series Anomaly Detection
Sepehr Maleki, Negar Pourmoazemi

Abstract
Anomalies in multivariate time series often arise from temporal context and cross-channel coordination rather than isolated outliers. We present Pi-Transformer, a physics-informed transformer with two attention pathways: a data-driven series attention and a smoothly evolving prior attention that encodes temporal invariants such as scale-related self-similarity and phase synchrony. The prior acts as a stable reference that calibrates reconstruction error. During training, we pair a reconstruction objective with a divergence term that encourages agreement between the two attentions while keeping them meaningfully distinct; the prior is regularised to evolve smoothly and is lightly distilled towards dataset-level statistics. At inference, the model combines an alignment-weighted reconstruction signal (Energy) with a mismatch signal that highlights timing and phase disruptions, and fuses them into a single score for detection. Across five benchmarks (SMD, MSL, SMAP, SWaT, and PSM), Pi-Transformer achieves state-of-the-art or highly competitive F1, with particular strength on timing and phase-breaking anomalies. Case analyses show complementary behaviour of the two streams and interpretable detections around regime changes. Embedding physics-informed priors into attention yields a calibrated and robust approach to anomaly detection in complex multivariate systems. Code is publicly available at this GitHub repository 1 1 1 https://github.com/sepehr-m/Pi-Transformer .

The challenge.
Detecting anomalies in multivariate time series can be challenging because anomalies do not usually appear as simple outliers. They often depend on context, involve multiple variables, and change over time. Anomalies only show up when we look at the timing and relationships between variables together. Real-world systems work across several timescales. Rapid changes, gradual shifts, daily or seasonal patterns, and long-term trends all exist and interact. Anomalies may affect one timescale while leaving others untouched. This means detection needs models that can understand multi-scale timing

Variance levels can shift between different states, and unusual but harmless setups are common. A method that mistakes this variability for anomalies will trigger false alarms, while one that overlooks it might miss real failures. Therefore, the main challenge is to create anomaly detection systems that are sensitive to small changes yet strong enough to handle normal variability, while also being suitable for large-scale use.

Related work

Research on anomaly detection in multivariate time series has evolved over several generations. This, reflects the increased availability of sensor-rich data and the growing complexity of monitored systems. Early studies focused on statistical models. These were later expanded by traditional machine learning methods. Eventually, deep learning architectures became the dominant approach in the field. Recently, attention-based models have established new performance standards by capturing long-range dependencies. However, significant challenges still exist. We review these developments below, particularly emphasising attention-driven architectures that are most relevant to our approach.

Statistical models were the first tools for detecting anomalies in temporal data. Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models combine autoregressive lags with differencing and moving averages to make forecasts
[
13
]
. Anomalies are identified when observations significantly differ from predictions. In a similar way, Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) models
[
14
]
keep smoothed statistics and use fixed thresholds to detect anomalies. These methods are straightforward, easy to understand, and work well in stationary univariate situations like financial time series. However, their assumptions of linearity and stationarity do not hold in multivariate data. In such cases, temporal patterns can be nonlinear, noise levels can vary, and dependencies between sensors can offer important information.

Classical machine learning expanded anomaly detection beyond strict parametric assumptions. One-Class SVMs
[
15
]
learn separating hyperplanes in feature space to separate normal data from anomalies. Isolation Forests
[
25
]
use random partitions to find samples that can be isolated in a few steps, marking them as likely anomalies. These models are robust against noise and have been used in manufacturing and network monitoring. However, they struggle with capturing sequential structure. Anomalies that occur in relation to temporal context or the synchrony of different variables often go undetected when samples are considered as independent points.

Deep learning methods have changed the field by focusing on representation learning. Reconstruction-based approaches dominated the first wave. Autoencoders
[
16
]
compress data windows into latent embeddings and then reconstruct them, identifying anomalies through high reconstruction error. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
[
18
]
build on this framework with probabilistic modelling. They provide uncertainty estimates and likelihood-based scoring. OmniAnomaly
[
19
]
combines a VAE with stochastic recurrent flows to capture temporal uncertainty and achieves strong results on spacecraft telemetry benchmarks like MSL and SMAP. However, reconstruction-based detectors are difficult to calibrate. Rare but normal patterns can inflate error, while anomalies that can be somewhat reconstructed may show low error, leading to false positives and false negatives.

To solve these calibration problems, later work models normal behaviour more explicitly and depends less on raw reconstruction error. Likelihood-based scoring with VAEs assesses each window based on its reconstruction probability instead of point-wise error. This approach reduces alarms caused by variance
[
26
]
. GAN-based models like MAD-GAN
[
27
]
learn the regularities of multivariate temporal data through a generator-discriminator game and score anomalies using responses from the discriminator along with reconstruction discrepancies. Hybrid models combine the variational objective with adversarial training, such as VAEAT
[
28
]
, to improve detector performance under complex normal conditions and changes in data distribution. Another approach replaces explicit reconstruction targets with masked prediction in a self-supervised manner. The MAD task
[
29
]
randomly masks segments and trains a model to recover them, resulting in embeddings and losses that highlight unusual structures without needing labels. These methods clarify decision boundaries when reconstruction signals are unclear and improve stability under non-stationarity.

Forecasting methods use prediction error as the signal for anomalies. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks
[
17
,
30
]
predict future values based on past history. They flag large deviations as anomalies. For example, DeepAR
[
31
]
builds on this idea by producing full probabilistic forecasts. It detects anomalies when observations fall into low-likelihood areas. These methods work well when anomalies disrupt smooth dynamics, such as sudden changes in demand forecasting. However, they struggle with distribution shifts, needing re-training or recalibration when system behaviour changes. Additionally, forecasting models often mistake structural anomalies that remain predictable, such as phase-shifted oscillations, for normal behaviour. This happens because point-wise predictions may still remain accurate even with disruptions in underlying synchrony.

Attention-based methods represent a recent and significant development. TranAD
[
32
]
is one of the first detectors based on transformers. It combines a reconstruction goal with adversarial training. Its generator reconstructs windows, while a discriminator tells normal reconstructions apart from anomalous ones. This setup improves resistance to noise and unseen patterns. TranAD shows impressive results on large server datasets like SMD, highlighting the advantages of transformers in capturing long-range dependencies. However, it still faces the same calibration issues as earlier reconstruction-based models. In these cases, errors can reflect normal variability instead of true anomalies.

The Anomaly Transformer
[
20
]
advances the field by directly modelling association discrepancies. It introduces a Gaussian-kernel attention mechanism that compares associations between normal and abnormal points. It also uses a MinMax learning objective that highlights differences. This design makes it very sensitive to small structural changes in temporal dependencies, allowing it to achieve state-of-the-art results on several benchmarks. However, its focus on association strength as the main signal makes it prone to noise. Associations may weaken in benign high-variance situations, leading to false alarms. At the same time, anomalies that maintain partial associations may produce weak signals. Additionally, without clear guidelines on temporal invariants like phase synchrony, the model struggles to differentiate between significant disruptions and harmless variability.

DCdetector
[
11
]
represents a new approach, moving from reconstruction to contrastive learning. It features a dual-attention design that includes patch-wise and in-patch augmentations. This trains the model to create consistent representations for various views of normal data. By avoiding reconstruction losses, DCdetector reduces the chance of anomalies affecting the training goal. It also performs well on datasets like MSL and SMAP, especially for anomalies with subtle local signs. However, its channel-independent patching limits its ability to capture synchrony across variables when anomalies show up as coordination problems. Additionally, contrastive learning makes it sensitive to augmentation choices and increases computational demands.

Building on these foundations, several transformer extensions focus on stability, coverage, and efficiency. TransAnomaly
[
33
]
combines long-range context with probabilistic reconstruction by linking a transformer encoder with a variational decoder. This approach improves calibration under uncertainty. Memory-guided designs like MEMTO
[
34
]
keep standard prototypes in external memory. This helps reduce drift and strengthen recall during regime changes. Frequency- and channel-aware variants capture periodic links. For example, TimesNet
[
35
]
converts sequences into frequency-domain representations to model seasonality. Similarly, CATCH
[
36
]
uses frequency patching with channel awareness to highlight spectral anomalies. Patch-based alternatives substitute full self-attention with more efficient patch mixing. PatchAD
[
37
]
employs MLP-Mixer blocks for windowed patch processing. SimAD
[
38
]
demonstrates that simple dissimilarity scoring on patches can still perform well on standard benchmarks. Pre-training and sparsity have also been studied. AnomalyBERT
[
39
]
modifies bidirectional masked pre-training for transferable contextual embeddings. STformer
[
40
]
explicitly models joint spatial and temporal dependencies using multi-head patch attention across channels and time segments while stacking transformer encoder blocks for reconstruction-based detection. Rreconstruction similarity hybrids like RESTAD
[
41
]
cross-check evidence sources to address calibration issues. Practical retraining methods such as LARA
[
42
]
offer light, anti-overfitting refresh processes that help maintain performance during domain shifts.

In parallel, graph-based methods encode the inter-channel structure that sequence-only models might miss. Graph attention over sensor nodes directly models pairwise relations, as in G-TSAD
[
43
]
. GDN
[
44
]
learns dependency graphs from data to capture stable couplings in multivariate telemetry. Variational graph convolution combined with recurrence, as shown in DVGCRN
[
45
]
, supports this inductive bias by modelling uncertainty and temporal dynamics on the graph. Sparsity-promoting graph Autoencoders, like FuSAGNet
[
46
]
, foster compact latent graphs that reflect persistent dependencies. By framing the issue as dynamic graph forecasting, DyGraphAD
[
47
]
shifts detection to deviations from predicted, time-varying relationships. At the subsequence level, Series2Graph
[
48
]
converts time series into graph representations to enable density-based discovery of unusual patterns. These methods enhance transformer attention by introducing structural priors that stay stable during normal operation.

The shift from statistical models to transformers shows clear progress in capturing complex time dynamics, but a gap still exists. Most current methods struggle to distinguish when increased error signals indicate real structural problems versus harmless variability. They can model dependencies well, but they lack the built-in assumptions needed for time-related constants like phase synchrony or scale consistency that stay stable under normal conditions. To overcome this issue, Pi-Transformer introduces a dual-pathway attention mechanism. It combines a physics-informed prior with data-driven series attention. This inclusion of time-related constants offers a stable reference point, allowing for better and clearer anomaly detection in complex multivariate time series.

3
Methodology

3.1
Problem Formulation

We consider the problem of unsupervised anomaly detection in multivariate time series. Here, anomalies are defined as deviations from learned normal behaviour rather than being explicitly labeled examples. Let

\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}\},\qquad\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{C},

denote a multivariate sequence of length
T
with
C
channels (sensors). In many real-world situations, such as monitoring industrial equipment, large amounts of data are available from normal operation. However, failures are rare or absent. This limitation calls for an approach that learns the structure of normality without supervision and identifies deviations as potential anomalies.

A major challenge in anomaly detection is that anomalies in time series often depend on the temporal context and the coordination between channels. A reading may seem normal when viewed alone but could be anomalous relative to its recent history (contextual anomalies). Additionally, multiple sensors may each stay within normal ranges, but their phase relationships (the timing and synchronisation between channels) can reveal a fault. For instance, in rotating machinery, vibration sensors at different locations should maintain consistent phase offsets due to mechanical coupling
[
12
]
. A bearing fault may keep the amplitudes the same but distort these offsets
[
49
]
. In thermodynamic systems, the relationship between temperature and pressure sensors indicates efficiency. Disruptions in their synchronisation can signal degradation even before individual measurements exceed thresholds
[
50
]
.

We therefore, frame the unsupervised anomaly detection problem as modelling the nominal window distribution with a reconstruction model that couples to an explicit prior over temporal relationships. Given a dataset of windows taken from normal operation:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\{\mathbf{W}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times C}\}_{i=1}^{N},

Our goal is to learn parameters
\theta
so the model can reconstruct each window
\mathbf{W}_{i}
while also capturing two key properties of nominal sequences: (1) self-similarity across timescales, often defined by Hurst-related features, and (2) stable cross-channel phase relationships, which reflect coordinated system dynamics.

During inference, the model evaluates a test window
\mathbf{W}
against these learned patterns. Windows that cannot be reconstructed consistently with their expected self-similarity and phase structure receive high anomaly scores and are marked as anomalous. This approach changes anomaly detection from simple classification to discovering and monitoring the temporal patterns that define normal system behaviour.

3.2
Pi-Transformer

Anomaly detection in multivariate time series requires models that capture complex time-based relationships while being sensitive to slight deviations from normal behaviour. Although transformer architectures are powerful for sequence modelling, they often face challenges with time series data because they lack inductive biases suited for temporal structures. In real-world applications, sensor data from industrial settings often shows long-range dependencies and similar patterns on different scales. Standard attention mechanisms do not specifically account for these characteristics, as they treat relationships between time steps as equally unconstrained and fail to maintain consistency across scales or phases.

We tackle this issue by using reconstruction error as a sign of deviation from normal behaviour. We introduce a time-normalised prior-alignment weight to guide the interpretation of this error over time. Reconstruction error indicates whether the model can produce an observation based on its learned representation. However, on its own, it is not a reliable signal for anomalies. It can be influenced by harmless variability, such as changes in noise levels, shifts in variance, or rare but typical patterns, leading to false positives. On the other hand, some anomalies can be partially reconstructed, resulting in small errors. Small phase shifts or timing variations may increase error even if the underlying process has not changed, while coordinated anomalies might mask themselves by matching learned patterns. As a result, raw reconstruction error is not consistently adjusted across time, scale, or operating conditions, making it prone to both false alarms and missed detections.

To resolve these problems, Pi-Transformer uses a dual-pathway attention mechanism: a data-driven series attention learned directly from the data and a physics-informed prior attention that encodes expected timing relationships under normal conditions. Their agreement within each window is transformed, through a time-wise softmax, into prior-alignment weights that add up to one. Indices that remain phase-consistent with the prior get higher weights, while poorly aligned indices receive lower weights. Under normal conditions, the weights are fairly uniform, around
1/L
per index; however, near disruptions, they focus on the most phase-consistent indices. The resulting time-wise anomaly score, which we refer to as the Energy signal, is defined as

e_{i}\;=\;w_{i}\,r_{i},

(1)

where
r_{i}
is the reconstruction error at time
i
and
w_{i}
is the prior-alignment weight at the same time. Here
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
denotes the multivariate observation at time
i
, and
\widehat{x}_{i}
its reconstruction. Since the weights are normalised within each window, emphasis shifts towards positions that remain phase-consistent. Large Energy values arise either when reconstruction error spikes while timing remains nominal (amplitude/shape anomalies), or when timing is disrupted but neighbouring phase-consistent indices still carry elevated error (timing anomalies such as abrupt phase shift, variable lag, or jitter). To capture the temporal invariants outlined in Section
3.1
, we introduce the Pi-Transformer architecture (Figure
1
), a physics-informed encoder with a linear reconstruction head and a dual-pathway attention mechanism. Each window is processed by stacked Transformer layers that generate the two attentions, and the decoder reconstructs the input sequence from the resulting context representations.

The series attention follows the standard scaled dot–product formulation with a softmax over time:

S\;=\;\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

where

are learned projections of encoder features and

S_{i,\cdot}
indicates how strongly the model attends to each past index when estimating

x_{i}

The physics-informed prior attention shows which past indices are expected to contribute to each position under normal conditions. The encoder predicts time-based scale features, such as a Hurst field
H_{i}

\tau_{i}

These parameters are learned together and adjusted for smooth changes over time. They are also weakly distilled towards the dataset’s nominal statistics. A time-wise softmax creates a row-stochastic prior matrix

3.3
Training

Training the Pi-Transformer has two main goals. First, it aims to fit the nominal dynamics accurately enough to provide a calibrated reconstruction signal. Second, it seeks to learn a physics-informed prior attention that is stable and distinctly different from the data-driven series attention. The training objective combines the reconstruction quality with regularisation terms that stabilise the prior attention and keep a measurable difference from the series pathway. For simplicity, we denote the series and prior attentions as
S
and
P
respectively. These refer to their row-normalised distributions, which are the attention weights over past indices for each position
i
. These will later appear with specific layer and head indices in Eq. (
6
), but we are using shorthand here. The local difference between the two pathways at time index
i
is measured by a symmetric Kullback–Leibler divergence:

\Delta_{i}\;=\;\mathrm{KL}(S_{i}\,\|\,P_{i})\;+\;\mathrm{KL}(P_{i}\,\|\,S_{i}).

During training, we apply this divergence row-wise, without averaging across layers or heads, and without temperature scaling. This choice keeps mismatches at a detailed level, allowing each head and layer to provide unique alignment signals. It also prevents early smoothing that could weaken the teacher-student alternation. During inference, we gather the same divergences across layers and heads and scale them by a temperature factor (see Eq. (
6
)). If we drive this divergence to zero, one pathway would collapse onto the other. Instead, we use a coupled but non-collapsing update method. In the first pass, we adjust the series attention towards the current prior while treating the prior as fixed (using a stop-gradient). In the second pass, we adjust the prior towards the series while freezing the series. This teacher-student alternation promotes alignment supported by data but maintains a measurable gap that is important during inference. Formally, the two per-batch losses are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}\;-\;k\,\Big[\mathrm{KL}\!\big(S\,\|\,\mathrm{sg}(P)\big)+\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\mathrm{sg}(P)\,\|\,S\big)\Big]\;+\;\mathcal{R},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}\;+\;k\,\Big[\mathrm{KL}\!\big(P\,\|\,\mathrm{sg}(S)\big)+\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\mathrm{sg}(S)\,\|\,P\big)\Big]\;+\;\mathcal{R}.

(3)

Here
\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)
denotes the stop-gradient operator and
\mathcal{R}
collects regularisation terms applied to the prior. We backpropagate both losses sequentially in each iteration, so the series is nudged towards the prior in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and the prior towards the series in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, while the stop-gradient prevents trivial coalescence.

The reconstruction objective provides the main data-fidelity signal and sets the scale of test-time errors. For each window of length
L
with
C
channels, we minimise the mean squared error averaged over time and channels:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}\;=\;\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\big(x_{i,c}-\widehat{x}_{i,c}\big)^{2},

(4)

where
x_{i,c}
and
\widehat{x}_{i,c}
are the observed and reconstructed values for channel
c
at time
i
. This encourages the encoder to capture common nominal structure rather than memorising isolated fluctuations, yielding a calibrated baseline for time-wise errors.

To stabilise the physics-informed pathway, we regularise its parameters for smooth and interpretable evolution. Any time-wise scalar
\vartheta_{i}
of the prior (e.g., the Hurst field
H_{i}
or stiffness
\tau_{i}
) is penalised for rapid changes with a first-difference term:

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{smooth}}\;=\;\frac{1}{L-1}\sum_{i=2}^{L}\big(\vartheta_{i}-\vartheta_{i-1}\big)^{2},

(5)

Additional stabilisers are applied to the unnormalised prior scores (the pre-softmax values that generate
P
), together with an explicit smoothness penalty on
\tau_{i}
, and a weak distillation that nudges the predicted Hurst field towards dataset-level statistics estimated from nominal data. Collectively, these terms form
\mathcal{R}
and prevent brittle, rapidly fluctuating priors without enforcing a fixed analytic form.

We use Adam optimiser, apply gradient clipping, and use early stopping based on validation reconstruction loss. Hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, window length, and regularisation weights) are tuned on the validation split. This regime fits nominal data, stabilises the physics-informed prior, and preserves a controlled series–prior separation, which is exactly what is needed for the fusion of reconstruction evidence with phase-aware agreement at inference time (see Algorithm

3.4
Inference

At inference, the Pi-Transformer generates two time-wise quantities for each position
i
: (1) a reconstruction error
r_{i}
, which is the per-channel mean squared error averaged across channels (consistent with Eq. (
4
)), and (2) a phase mismatch
\Delta_{i}
that uses the same symmetric divergence applied during training, but is now averaged across layers and heads and adjusted by a temperature. Specifically,

\Delta_{i}\;=\;T\cdot\frac{1}{L_{\text{att}}H}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{\text{att}}}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\!\big(S^{(\ell,h)}_{i,\cdot}\,\|\,P^{(\ell,h)}_{i,\cdot}\big)+\mathrm{KL}\!\big(P^{(\ell,h)}_{i,\cdot}\,\|\,S^{(\ell,h)}_{i,\cdot}\big)\Big]\;,

(6)

where
S^{(\ell,h)}_{i,\cdot}
and
P^{(\ell,h)}_{i,\cdot}
denote the full row distributions (over past indices) of the series and prior attentions at time
i
in layer
\ell
and head
h
. This generalises the row-wise divergence
\Delta_{i}
of the training loss by averaging across layers/heads and introducing a temperature factor
T>0
that sharpens the distribution of alignment weights. The divergences
\{\Delta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
within each window are converted into prior-alignment weights
w
via a softmax over the negatives:

w_{i}\;=\;\frac{\exp\!\big(-\Delta_{i}\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}\exp\!\big(-\Delta_{j}\big)}\,,

(7)

which assigns larger weights to indices with stronger series–prior agreement. The Energy signal then follows Eq. (
1

Experimental analysis

We evaluate the Pi-Transformer on five widely used multivariate time-series anomaly detection benchmarks. The
SMD
(Server Machine Dataset) is collected over five weeks from 28 server machines at a large Internet company. It includes 38 metrics such as CPU load, memory usage, and network activity. The
MSL
(Mars Science Laboratory) dataset, provided by NASA, contains sensor measurements and actuator conditions for the Curiosity rover. The
SMAP
(Soil Moisture Active Passive) dataset, also from NASA, features satellite data on soil moisture levels and freeze/thaw states within the top 5 cm of Earth’s surface. The
SWaT
(Secure Water Treatment) dataset is collected from a water treatment testbed at Singapore University of Technology and Design. It covers 51 sensor and actuator metrics over 11 days. Finally, the
PSM
(Pooled Server Metrics) dataset comes from multiple eBay application servers. It includes 26 metrics such as CPU utilisation and memory usage, with 13 weeks of training data and 8 weeks of testing data that includes manually labeled anomalies. Together, these datasets encompass IT telemetry, cyber-physical systems, and mission-critical scientific instruments.

Competing methods include classical unsupervised detectors like LOF, OCSVM, IForest, and VAR, along with deep generative and recurrent models such as DAGMM, LSTM-VAE, OmniAnomaly, InterFusion, THOC, Deep-SVDD, BeatGAN, and LSTM. There are also Bayesian change detection methods via BOCPD and recent attention-based detectors like TS-CP2, Anomaly Transformer, and DCdetector. We report precision, recall, and F1-score using the standard point-adjust protocol for segment-level evaluation. All inputs are standardised per channel and split into overlapping windows of length

Across all five datasets, Pi-Transformer achieves state-of-the-art or near state-of-the-art F1 while maintaining a favourable balance of precision and recall. On datasets where anomalies primarily manifest as phase or timing disruptions, such as abrupt lags, seasonal misalignments, or jitter, the phase-mismatch stream
\Delta_{i}
rises sharply at the disruption, ensuring high recall. Even when prior-alignment weights dip at the exact breakpoint, muting the Energy signal at that index, the fused score continues to fire through the mismatch stream, thereby preventing false negatives. In contrast, on anomalies dominated by amplitude or shape changes under nominal timing,
\Delta_{i}
remains small, the prior-alignment weights stay close to uniform, and the Energy signal cleanly highlights the event. These complementary behaviours explain why the model simultaneously avoids false positives on benign variance spikes and retains sensitivity to phase-breaking disruptions.

Finally, hyperparameters shaping localisation behave as expected. The temperature
T
in Eq. (
6
) sharpens the prior-alignment softmax of Eq. (
7
), concentrating
w_{i}

With stride 1 overlap, projecting window-internal scores to the global timeline introduces a predictable lead effect that can be mitigated by right-edge attribution or aggregation across covering windows.

5
Conclusion

Multivariate time series anomaly detection is a challenging task because anomalies do not simply show up as straightforward outliers. Instead, they arise from complex time-based relationships and subtle structural changes that might only be noticeable in how sensors coordinate. To address this, we introduced the Pi-Transformer, a physics-informed transformer model that combines the expressive capacity of attention with inductive biases to capture temporal invariants. The main idea is to treat reconstruction error as a sign of deviation while adjusting this evidence with a prior-alignment weight based on the agreement between data-driven series attention and a physics-informed prior. This design offers a clear way to distinguish when reconstruction errors are significant and when they stem from harmless variance or model uncertainty. Concretely, the model produces an Energy stream and a phase-mismatch stream; at inference we normalise both and form a fused score
f_{i}
so the detector responds when either amplitude/shape evidence or a timing disruption is strong.

Testing on various benchmarking datasets showed that the Pi-Transformer achieves state-of-the-art or near state-of-the-art performance. The model is particularly good at finding phase-breaking anomalies, where traditional methods often fail, while still accurately detecting amplitude-driven anomalies. Case studies demonstrated how the prior-alignment weights focus attention on stable areas near disruptions, how the Energy signal peaks in locations with high evidence but not complete alignment, and how the fused score rises at onsets where mismatch spikes even if Energy is briefly muted. Ablation studies confirmed the importance of the physics-informed prior pathway, showing that removing it caused notable drops in recall and overall F1 scores.

While the Pi-Transformer shows clear improvements, it also suggests paths for future development. The current use of stride-1 overlapping windows creates a slight timing offset between detected peaks and actual anomaly boundaries. This could be reduced through different score aggregation methods or by adjusting scores across windows. Additionally, the model requires careful tuning of window length and temperature to balance accuracy against stability, which could benefit from adaptive or data-driven adjustments. Although the physics-informed prior is learned from data and regularised by smoothness and Hurst-related features, further investigation into domain-specific priors may improve interpretability and transferability to new areas.

The Pi-Transformer model shows that integrating physics-informed structure into transformer models provides an effective way to detect anomalies in multivariate time series. By combining reconstruction evidence with prior-informed alignment via a fused score, it improves detection accuracy while providing interpretable signals that clarify the time dynamics of complex systems. This approach lays the groundwork for creating more general models that unite learning ability with domain knowledge and emphasises the importance of principled biases in developing robust, reliable anomaly detection systems.
