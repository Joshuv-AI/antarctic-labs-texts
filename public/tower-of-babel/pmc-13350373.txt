Tricuspid Valve Regurgitation Manifesting in Gastrointestinal Symptoms
Authors: Ninoshka Pérez-Soto, Angélica Fregoso-Sánchez, Jorge Javier Miguel-González, Anna Greti Everding-Rodríguez, Alex Daniel Pacheco-Bouthiller, Benigno Ferreira-Piña, Adriana González-Martínez, Óscar Sergio Lomelí-Sánche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTricuspid regurgitation has a typical presentation coexisting with right heart failure symptoms and is challenging to diagnose, with extracardiac manifestations often overlooked.Case SummaryA 35-year-old woman had a history of mixed neurocardiogenic syncope. She received a pacemaker at age 11; 13 years later, she developed lead dysfunction. As pacing was no longer required, the leads were removed. She subsequently developed asymptomatic tricuspid regurgitation and experienced gastrointestinal symptoms, which were initially attributed to irritable bowel syndrome, with a low Gastrointestinal Quality of Life Index (GIQLI) score. Biological valve replacement improved gastrointestinal symptoms and GIQLI score. Later, a mechanical valve was implanted owing to bioprosthesis deterioration, resulting in further clinical and GIQLI score improvement.DiscussionSevere tricuspid regurgitation may cause visceral venous congestion, intestinal edema, dysbiosis, hypoperfusion, and inflammation, producing gastrointestinal symptoms.Take-Home MessageThis case emphasizes the importance of considering visceral venous congestion symptoms, such as gastrointestinal manifestations, in the assessment and management of severe tricuspid regurgitation.

History of Presentation

A 35-year-old woman had a history of pacemaker implantation at the age of 11 for mixed neurocardiogenic syncope. At age 22, the pulse generator was removed; however, the leads were abandoned owing to the lack of extraction equipment. Follow-up and evaluation for dysautonomia for lead extraction were performed at age 27; however, the patient decided not to undergo the procedure as she remained asymptomatic and postponed follow-up. A year later, the patient began experiencing gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms that included diarrhea, constipation, early satiety, and malabsorption problems. She consulted a gastroenterologist, who diagnosed irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and initiated treatment according to IBS guidelines. At age 29, she reported intermittent stabbing pain at the lead site, for which she returned for cardiology follow-up. Physical examination identified a heart murmur, no jugular venous distention or pulsatility, and no peripheral edema. Abdominal examination revealed no hepatomegaly or palpable hepatic pulsatility, and no ascites or splenomegaly were present. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed moderate to severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) caused by direct and continuous contact between the pacemaker lead and the posterior leaflet, resulting in incomplete leaflet coaptation. After extraction of leads, TR was caused not by prolapse or chordal rupture, but by restricted leaflet mobility secondary to chronic interaction with pacemaker leads. The patient continued to experience GI symptoms refractory to IBS treatment. During the 2-year follow-up, echocardiogram (Figure 1) and cardiac magnetic resonance showed severe TR and right atrial dilation with preserved right ventricular systolic function. At this point, it was suspected that the presentation of GI symptoms was caused by visceral venous congestion secondary to severe TR. Given the absence of cardiovascular symptoms, a Gastrointestinal Quality of Life Index (GIQLI) score was obtained as part of the evaluation. The GIQLI score was 120, indicating that the GI symptoms were affecting her quality of life (Figure 2). At 4 months after surgery, the patient reported no GI symptoms and a GIQLI score of 170, a significant improvement. The patient’s GI symptoms recurred 2 years after cardiac surgery, and she consulted the gastroenterologist and was again diagnosed with IBS. Owing to the lack of improvement, she consulted a cardiologist. An echocardiogram was obtained, which showed leaflet thickening suggestive of laminar thrombosis and/or pannus, massive TR, preserved right ventricular systolic function, with portal vein pulsed wave Doppler showing 66% pulsatility and an intrarenal vein inverted systolic/diastolic ratio, reflecting TR-related retrograde flow and increased visceral venous pressure (Figure 3). At this time, it was decided to perform valve replacement using a mechanical prosthesis. Before the mechanical valve replacement, a GIQLI score of 129 was obtained, reflecting the exacerbation of GI symptoms secondary to TR despite IBS management. At the postsurgical evaluation, a GIQLI score of 160 and complete improvement in the presentation of digestive symptoms were obtained.Figure 3Preoperative Transthoracic Echocardiogram With Bioprostatic Deterioration(A and B) Right ventricular inflow view with color Doppler demonstrates severe regurgitation of the bioprosthetic valve in the tricuspid position. Past Medical History

The patient’s past medical history included pacemaker implantation and appendectomy.

Differential Diagnosis

Generally, the differential diagnosis of TR is limited to similar pathologies, such as other valvular heart diseases. Initially, the cardiovascular symptoms were associated with abandoned leads in the right chambers; however, this was an incidental diagnosis based on echocardiographic findings, as the only cardiac manifestations the patient experienced were stabbing pains at the site of the abandoned leads and a holosystolic murmur. In most cases, symptoms are related to signs of right heart failure, such as ascites, hepatosplenomegaly, and peripheral edema. In this case, the symptoms aligned with venous return leading to congestion that manifested in an unconventional manner, affecting the intestinal loops rather than the hepatic system.

Investigations

Cardiovascular and GI investigations characterized symptom severity across disease phases. Intrinsic digestive disease was excluded by negative Helicobacter pylori testing, celiac serology, endoscopy, and colonoscopy. The GIQLI score was used longitudinally to quantify symptom burden. Initial echocardiography showed moderate to severe TR with preserved right ventricular function. During symptom worsening, portal vein pulsatility reached 66% with intrarenal vein flow reversal. Cardiac magnetic resonance showed right atrial dilation. Doppler normalization after surgery paralleled GIQLI score improvement, with deterioration recurring alongside venous congestion across sequential evaluations and treatment phases during long-term follow-up.

Management

Following GI symptoms and TR identification, the valve was replaced in 2022 with a bioprosthesis, leading to symptom resolution. Two years later, symptoms recurred; bioprosthesis deterioration prompted replacement with a mechanical valve, resulting in complete resolution. Both interventions were minimally invasive.

Outcome/Follow-Up

Complete improvement was observed when the patient underwent surgery to replace the tricuspid valve with a bioprosthesis, and subsequently with a mechanical prosthesis.

Discussion

This case illustrates that severe TR can manifest exclusively with refractory GI symptoms in the absence of classic cardiac symptoms, following rapid GI recovery after valve replacement, suggesting the relevance of venous congestion in her presentation. TR was associated with prolonged presence of abandoned pacemaker leads. The implantation of pacemakers could induce tricuspid valve dysfunction by interfering with its proper function, by causing noncoaptation, valve perforation, or damage to the subvalvular apparatus, which in some cases can worsen secondary TR. Valve damage after implantation is caused by inflammation and fibrosis, affecting valve mobility, annular dilatation, obstructions, infections, or lacerations. In many cases, TR symptoms include excessive fatigue, peripheral edema, and symptoms of right congestive heart failure. The isolated presentation of GI symptoms is uncommon and can delay diagnosis. Diuretics are usually the first-line treatment for TR, whereas surgical treatment is generally avoided owing to the associated surgical risks; however, valve repair is preferred to replacement in symptomatic patients with severe regurgitation or in cases where intervention of the left-sided valves is required.1 Nevertheless, the 2020 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease recommend surgery for asymptomatic patients with severe primary regurgitation, right ventricular dilation, or systolic dysfunction.

Other factors causing the patient's GI symptoms cannot be ruled out, such as the use of unreported medications. However, the lack of improvement with IBS treatment in the absence of intrinsic GI dysfunction and the progressive worsening of both TR and GI symptoms support that the severity of the TR was sufficient to generate visceral venous congestion.2, 3, 4 The increase in venous congestion raises renal venous pressure, which decreases the renal perfusion gradient and is therefore associated with progressive renal dysfunction.5 Similarly, this renal dysfunction can be extrapolated to the GI system,5 where the increase in venous pressure system is characterized by edema, intestinal wall damage, mucosal thickening, and impaired absorption due to retrograde volume overload.

The conceptual distinction between TR and heart failure in the illustrative model is intended for didactic purposes only. In clinical practice, TR, venous congestion, and right-sided heart failure represent interconnected manifestations of the same pathophysiological continuum. In this case, GI dysfunction reflected an early and predominant consequence of systemic venous congestion, preceding classic symptoms of overt right-sided heart failure. Venous congestion caused by TR can trigger hypoxia and ischemia in intestinal cells, promoting acidosis and leading to dysbiosis. This increases intestinal permeability, allowing the translocation of bacteria and metabolites such as trimethylamine oxide. Trimethylamine oxide is a proinflammatory molecule that triggers the release of cytokines and inflammatory mediators and is associated with increased cardiovascular mortality, heart failure, and a more pronounced prothrombotic state.6 Furthermore, acidosis activates and alters intestinal ion channels, such as NHE3, which are responsible for regulating sodium and hydrogen. This alteration results in increased volume overload on the cardiovascular system and worsens venous congestion. On the other hand, a decrease in beneficial bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids can increase intestinal permeability, allowing bacterial endotoxins to enter the bloodstream and trigger a proinflammatory state and vasodilation. High concentrations of endotoxins in the blood, resulting from decreased intestinal barrier function and venous congestion, can lead to poor elimination of bacterial products and activation of the inflammatory cascade. In our patient, the impaired absorption, abdominal distention, diarrhea, and constipation, in addition to their complete resolution after tricuspid intervention, which restored the blood flow, support the proposed mechanism in which venous congestion alters intestinal permeability, promotes dysbiosis, and triggers inflammatory pathways. This highlights the interplay between venous congestion and gut dysfunction. Whereas previous reports detail TR as an incidental diagnosis with typically cardiac manifestations, this case expands current knowledge related to isolated GI symptoms that remained undiagnosed until following a different clinical approach. The GILQI score could represent a useful tool beyond GI diseases.

Conclusions

GI symptoms refractory to conventional therapy may represent an atypical manifestation of severe TR, even in the absence of heart failure. In this case, complete resolution of digestive symptoms and GIQLI improvement after valve replacement demonstrated the direct impact of visceral venous congestion on GI function, despite the IBS treatment. GI disorders must be considered when making a TR diagnosis and are probably a criterion for further cardiac intervention.
