Molecular mechanisms and therapeutic strategies of glomerular cell senescence in diabetic kidney disease: from heterogeneity to precision intervention
Authors: Zhitao Zeng, Jiazhen Shang, Shouyu Chai, Baoze Ma, Xiaotian Han, Yifan Xu, Rong Wang, Zhimei Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) has emerged as a major cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) worldwide, with approximately 30%-40% of diabetic patients progressing to DKD and 25% eventually requiring renal replacement therapy. Glomerular cell senescence, a central pathological driver of disease progression, persists throughout the entire course of DKD and involves activation of the complement system, transcription factor regulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and DNA damage response (DDR). Despite shared senescence mechanisms, podocytes, endothelial cells, and mesangial cells exhibit distinct senescent heterogeneity. This manuscript explores the core mechanisms regulating glomerular cell senescence and their impact on renal filtration barrier injury, inflammation, and glomerulosclerosis, with a specific focus on the senescent characteristics and interactions of different types of glomerular cells. It also discusses senescence-related diagnostic biomarkers and epigenetic clocks. Our findings highlight the critical role of cell type-specific senescence in the pathogenesis of DKD and propose potential precision therapeutic strategies targeting cellular senescence, offering promising breakthroughs for preventing or alleviating the progression of DKD.

Introduction

As the most severe microvascular complication of diabetes, diabetic kidney disease (DKD) accounts for over 40% of newly diagnosed end-stage renal disease (ESRD) cases globally [1]. Epidemiological data indicate that 30%–40% of patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes will develop DKD within 10–20 years of diagnosis, and some will further progress to ESRD [2]. Current clinical approaches such as blood glucose control with sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, blood pressure regulation, and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade can improve short-term renal function indicators, but a large number of patients still inevitably progress to ESRD [3–5]. This therapeutic gap highlights the necessity of exploring new pathological mechanisms of DKD.

Research over the past decade has confirmed that cellular senescence is not a passive accompanying phenomenon of DKD, but rather a core pathological event driving disease progression [6, 7]. Under the persistent hyperglycemic environment of diabetes, intrinsic renal cells accelerate into a senescent state [8]. This senescence process involves multiple mechanisms, including telomere shortening, accumulation of DNA damage, epigenetic alterations, and mitochondrial dysfunction [9]. Senescent cells not only lose normal physiological functions, but also secrete various bioactive molecules that affect the local microenvironment, thereby promoting pathological changes in the kidney [10]. The most prominent feature of senescent cells is the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [11]. SASP includes various pro-inflammatory factors such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which act on surrounding cells through paracrine and endocrine pathways [12]. In the kidney, SASP can induce local inflammatory responses, promote fibrosis progression, damage vascular integrity, and may accelerate the senescence of neighboring cells through paracrine induction, forming a vicious “senescence–inflammation” cycle [13, 14]. This self-reinforcing pathological process may be an important factor driving the continuous progression of DKD.

The kidney is a highly heterogeneous organ, and glomerular endothelial cells, podocytes, and mesangial cells exhibit fundamentally different responses to hyperglycemia [15, 16]. This heterogeneity is directly manifested in the unique senescence patterns in DKD: glomerular endothelial cell senescence mainly drives early glomerular microangiopathy and impairs the filtration barrier; podocyte senescence is the core cause of proteinuria and glomerular filtration barrier dysfunction; mesangial cell senescence contributes to glomerulosclerosis [17]. In this process, these three cell types also exhibit pathological changes with significant heterogeneity (Table 1). Therefore, elucidating the specific senescence mechanisms of different renal cells is a prerequisite for developing precision treatment strategies for DKD. Undeniably, as the most abundant cell type in the kidney, renal tubular cell senescence plays a crucial role in renal fibrosis of DKD. In contrast, glomerular cell senescence can directly drive core pathological processes of DKD such as glomerular filtration barrier injury and glomerulosclerosis. In particular, research on the differences and connections among the senescence of the three types of glomerular cells may provide unique insights for our further understanding of the pathological mechanisms of DKD. Moreover, a considerable number of researchers have conducted very specific and detailed special elaborations on the mechanisms related to renal tubular cell senescence [18–20]. Overview of core molecular mechanisms of cellular senescence in DKD

Complement system activation and complement component 5a (C5a)-mediated senescence signaling pathway

As a key component of innate immunity, the complement system is abnormally activated in the early stages of DKD, serving as the initiation switch for cellular senescence [21]. Clinical studies have found that the expression of complement factor B (CFB), C3a, C5a, and other components is significantly elevated in the renal tissue of DKD patients, among which C5a is the critical mediator linking complement activation to cellular senescence [22]. C5a exerts cell-specific effects by binding to G protein-coupled receptors (GPCR) on the surface of renal parenchymal cells [23]. In podocytes, upon binding to Complement component 5a receptor 1 (C5aR1) on the podocyte surface, C5a activates the mitochondrial fission protein Drp1 via calcium signaling, promoting mitochondrial fragmentation. This subsequently induces decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced ATP production, and excessive generation of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), accompanied by the accumulation of the DNA damage marker 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine (8-O-dG) [24]. Studies have demonstrated that this effect is significantly exacerbated in the context of Klotho protein deficiency [25, 26]. In mesangial cells, C5a not only activates the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) pathway, promoting the expression of fibrotic factors such as TGF-β1, fibronectin (FN), and type I collagen [27], but also enhances the proliferative capacity of mesangial cells. Additionally, it regulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway: by upregulating β-catenin expression, C5a accelerates the transdifferentiation of mesangial cells into a SASP [28], leading to the sustained release of profibrotic factors and ultimately resulting in extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and the formation of senescent phenotypes. In glomerular endothelial cells, C5a primarily activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which downregulates the expression of the endothelial marker CD31 and upregulates the expression of the mesenchymal markerα-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). This induces endothelial cells to lose their barrier function and transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts [28]. Furthermore, C5a promotes the expression of inflammatory factors such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and TNF-α in endothelial cells, further exacerbating endothelial dysfunction [29].All C5a-mediated glomerular cell injuries exhibit typical senescent cell characteristics, and these injuries can further promote cellular senescence. In this process, the C5a-C5aR1 axis serves as a key switch triggering senescence-associated phenotypes, while ROS acts as an important intermediate mediator. ROS not only directly damages cells but also amplifies senescence signals through oxidative stress, forming a “ROS-senescence-fibrosis”vicious cycle.

Regulatory role of E2 transcription factor 1 (E2F1) transcription factor in cell cycle arrest

Abnormal regulation of the cell cycle is a core feature of senescence, and the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A (p16)/cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 A (p21)–retinoblastoma protein (RB)–E2F axis exhibits cell-specific activation patterns in DKD renal cells [30, 31]. The high-glucose environment can induce changes in the activity of the E2F1 transcription factor, leading to irreversible cell cycle arrest of glomerular cells [32, 33]. In the kidneys of diabetic mice and patients with DKD, the number of p16-positive senescent cells is significantly increased [34]. By inhibiting the activity of cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6), tumor protein p53 (p53)-p21-RB undergoes continuous dephosphorylation, which hinders the release of E2F family transcription factors and ultimately leads to cell cycle arrest [35, 36]. This core regulatory mechanism is universally present in the three types of glomerular cells, but the upstream triggering factors and activation patterns exhibit cell-specificity.

In podocytes, high glucose inhibits the antioxidant function of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) through phosphorylation of glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), resulting in the accumulation of ROS and DNA damage, and activating the p16/RB pathway; at the same time, high glucose also inhibits the insulin signaling pathway, enhancing E2F1-mediated cell cycle arrest [23, 37].In mesangial cells, advanced glycation end products (AGEs) activate the janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) pathway, inhibit autophagy, and upregulate p16 expression; angiotensin II (AngII) activates the p53/p21 pathway by shortening telomeres, collectively causing cells to arrest in the G0/G1 phase [38, 39].In endothelial cells, oxidative stress induces the expression of microRNA-200c (miR-200c), which activates p21 by inhibiting zinc finger e-box binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1) [40]; ROS mediated by NADPH oxidase 4 (Nox4) enhances p53 activity through the extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) pathway, triggering cell arrest in the G2/M phase [41].

Synergistic effects of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress

The feed-forward loop formed by mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress is the core driving force of cellular senescence in DKD, exhibiting unique characteristics in different renal cells [42]. High glucose regulates mitochondrial function through the Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1)/Forkhead Box Protein O1 (FOXO1) signaling pathway. When SIRT1 activity is inhibited, the acetylation level of FOXO1 increases, leading to mitochondrial overload and exacerbated oxidative stress [43]; meanwhile, activation of the Thioredoxin-Interacting Protein (TXNIP)/NOD-Like Receptor Thermal Protein Domain-Associated Protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome pathway further amplifies oxidative damage [44]. In glomerular cells, this synergy manifests in multiple ways.

In high glucose-induced cellular injury, podocytes exhibit mitochondrial fragmentation, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) accumulation, and metabolic disorders, driven by activation of the Ras Homolog Enriched in Brain 1 (Rheb1)/Mechanistic Target of Rapamycin (mTOR) pathway [45, 46], which reduces the phosphorylation level of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and impairs mitochondrial respiration, while peroxisome proliferator-activated receptorγ (PPARγ) reduces the clearance of damaged mitochondria by inhibiting autophagy, forming a “mitochondrial damage-oxidative stress” vicious cycle, exacerbating lipid metabolism disorders, and further increasing ROS production [47, 48]. Meanwhile, high-glucose-induced oxidative stress can also increase ROS levels via the DNA damage response protein 1 (REDD1) pathway, thereby promoting podocyte death [49]. Mesangial cells display the “Warburg effect,” where abnormal activation of pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) enhances glycolysis while suppressing mitochondrial respiration [50]. This metabolic reprogramming reduces antioxidant capacity, alters the local microenvironment by increasing lactate production, and promotes ECM deposition. Glomerular endothelial cells mainly show abnormal mitochondrial morphology, including mitochondrial swelling, cristae rupture, decreased membrane potential, and excessive ROS generation, which is closely related to Aquaporin 1 (AQP1)-mediated transmembrane transport of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). In this process, AQP1 transports extracellular H₂O₂ into the cell, forming an “oxidative stress–senescence” positive feedback loop with Nox4 activation [41, 51]; meanwhile, deficiency of spinster homolog 2 (SPNS2) protein leads to pyruvate metabolism disorders [52], further aggravating mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction. The exacerbation of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress occurring in DKD ultimately accelerates the onset and progression of cellular senescence. Additionally, the anti-aging protein Klotho plays a cell-specific role in mitochondrial regulation. Studies have found that its downregulation in glomerular endothelial cells aggravates oxidative damage [53], while in podocytes it alleviates mitochondrial injury by inhibiting the Protein Kinase C alpha(PKCα)/66 kDa SHC Adaptor Protein 1 (p66SHC) pathway [54], and has minimal effect on mesangial cells. The cell-specific role of Klotho protein also provides theoretical support for intervention strategies in DKD.

Molecular characteristics of DNA damage response and epigenetic modifications

Persistent activation of DDR and epigenetic remodeling together maintain the senescent phenotype of renal cells in DKD [55, 56]. High glucose induces telomere shortening and DNA double-strand breaks, activating the Ataxia-Telangiectasia Mutated Protein (ATM)/Ataxia-Telangiectasia and Rad3-Related Protein (ATR)–Checkpoint Kinase 1/2 (Chk1/2) – p53 signaling cascade in a cell-specific manner [57]. Podocytes preferentially activate the ATM–Chk2 pathway under high-glucose conditions, which is closely related to the formation of their anti-apoptotic phenotype [2]. Mesangial cells rely on the ATR–Chk1 pathway to regulate ECM-related gene expression [58]. In glomerular endothelial cells, high glucose induces telomere uncapping, leading to downregulation of the telomere-protective protein complex [59], and the accumulation of 3-nitrotyrosine (3-NT) induced by high glucose enhances DDR through the p53/p21 pathway [60].

Epigenetic modifications further refine cell-specific aging patterns. In the progression of DKD, podocytes exhibit alterations in histone modifications. Diabetes-related conditions reduce the expression of the histone methyltransferase Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 (EZH2) in podocytes, thereby decreasing the level of histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) [61].Studies have confirmed that H3K27me3 levels are generally decreased in podocytes from DKD patients and diabetic animal models. Moreover, depletion of EZH2 induces podocyte injury, oxidative stress, and proteinuria, which is directly associated with DKD progression.Decreased H3K27me3 levels sustainably regulate the expression of downstream genes, driving the long-term accumulation of podocyte injury and thereby accelerating the podocyte senescence process [61, 62]. Mesangial cells exhibit dysregulation of the miRNA profile. Studies have shown that miR-21 can induce mesangial cell cycle arrest by inhibiting cell division cycle 25 A (Cdc25a) and cyclin-dependent kinase 6 (Cdk6) [63], and the upregulation of miR-34a in diabetic patients is also closely associated with mesangial cell senescence [64, 65]. In glomerular endothelial cells, the decreased angiogenic capacity caused by potential hypermethylation of the vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) gene promoter region is also significantly associated with cellular senescence [66]. Furthermore, studies have confirmed that decreased expression of the key enzyme for H3K27me3, enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), leads to reduced enrichment of H3K27me3 in the promoter region of early growth response factor 1 (EGR1), lifting transcriptional repression of EGR1, thereby activating EGR1 and triggering the EGR1/TXNIP pathway to promote renal inflammation and fibrosis [44]. In addition, methylation abnormalities caused by inhibition of s-adenosylhomocysteine hydrolase (SAHH) have been shown to induce demethylation of the p16 promoter in mesangial cells and upregulate its expression, thereby promoting cellular senescence [44]. Recent studies have also reported that histone crotonylation can induce podocyte senescence via autocrine and paracrine pathways of interleukin-1beta (IL-1β) [67]; moreover, the long non-coding RNA LINC01806 can regulate the expression of kidney injury molecule 1 (KIM-1) and p53/p21/p16, activate the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, and promote glomerular cells to secrete SASP factors [68]. These molecular changes ultimately activate SASP, releasing large amounts of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factors, which exacerbate glomerular microenvironment disruption through paracrine effects [69].

Cell-specific aging characteristics and pathological impacts

Temporal characteristics in glomerular cell senescence

As previously established, glomerular endothelial cells, podocytes, and mesangial cells exhibit distinct senescent heterogeneity and core molecular mechanisms in diabetic kidney disease (DKD). Although the senescent phenotypes of different cells under high glucose stimulation show a seemingly sequential appearance, there is currently no research evidence indicating that these three cell types display obvious temporal characteristics during their senescence process [70, 71]. Presently, there is a lack of studies that simultaneously detect and compare the timing of appearance, accumulation rate, and degree of senescence markers in the three cell types within the same DKD model, especially dynamic models from early to late stages. Although some studies have suggested that during glomerular cell injury in DKD, glomerular endothelial cells and podocytes exhibit dysfunction and increased expression of senescence-related biomarkers as early as the early stage of the disease, with highly synchronous damage between the two [72]. Moreover, in the disease progression, hyperglycemia first impairs glomerular endothelial cells, leading to increased permeability, decreased nitric oxide (NO) production, and enhanced oxidative stress—changes that can be observed within 1–2 weeks in diabetic models [73]. Meanwhile, podocytes present structural and functional abnormalities such as foot process fusion and downregulated expression of slit diaphragm proteins, which have also been confirmed in renal biopsies of patients with early-stage diabetes [74]. In contrast, in previous studies on mesangial cells, obvious pathological changes in mesangial cells often occur significantly later than those in podocytes and endothelial cells [75]. However, this only suggests that there may be a certain sequential order in the process of cell injury in DKD, and more research is required to confirm the temporal characteristics of the senescence process itself.

Podocyte senescence: a key trigger for glomerular filtration barrier dysfunction

Podocytes are terminally differentiated epithelial cells that maintain the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier (GFB) [76]. Due to their lack of proliferative capacity, their senescence directly leads to irreversible GFB damage, making it a core cause of proteinuria in DKD [77] (Fig. 2).It has long been reported that podocytes exhibit foot process fusion (Fig. 1), disruption of slit diaphragm integrity, and significant downregulation of podocyte-specific functional markers nephrin, podocin, and Wilms tumor protein 1 (WT1) as early as the early stage of DKD [78, 79]. Moreover, clinical data have confirmed that the mean podocyte volume is positively correlated with the severity of proteinuria (Rs = 0.34, P = 0.02), and for each 1 standard deviation (SD) decrease in podocyte count, the risk of progressive CKD increases by approximately 61% [80], indicating that podocyte senescence is closely related to DKD progression.Fig. 1Glomerular cell-specific senescence progression in DKD

At the molecular level, podocyte senescence is characterized primarily by mitochondrial dysfunction and persistent activation of the DDR. Regarding DDR activation, podocytes accumulate large amounts of phosphorylated histone H2AX (γH2AX) during senescence, and deficiency of histone deacetylases 1/2 (HDAC1/2) further suppresses DNA repair enzyme activity, leading to sustained activation of the p53/p21 pathway [81]. Recent studies have identified adhesion G protein-coupled receptor GPR124 as a specific regulatory factor in podocyte senescence. Studies have shown that the expression of GPR124 receptor is significantly reduced in the renal biopsy tissues of diabetic model mice and patients, and its level is positively correlated with the glomerular filtration rate. GPR124 negatively regulates the phosphorylation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) by binding to vinculin; its deficiency triggers podocyte senescence and mitochondrial damage [57].

Due to the heterogeneity of senescence mechanisms, the three cell types exhibit distinct pathological phenotypes, collectively leading to glomerular cell loss and disruption of structural integrity, ultimately driving the progression of DKD.

Mesangial cell senescence: an important contributor to glomerulosclerosis

Mesangial cells maintain structural stability by synthesizing ECM and regulating glomerular blood flow. Their senescence is characterized by excessive ECM deposition and impaired contractile function, forming a key pathological basis for DKD-related glomerulosclerosis [82, 83] (Fig. 1). Pathologically, senescent mesangial cells are characterized by increased cell volume, nuclear pleomorphism, and elevated SA-β-Gal positivity rate [84, 85]. Meanwhile, the upregulated expression of α-SMA indicates their transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts [86]. Furthermore, uncontrolled ECM synthesis leads to massive deposition of type I, III, and IV collagen as well as fibronectin [87, 88], resulting in mesangial area expansion, compression of glomerular capillaries, and reduction of filtration area, thereby accelerating the progression of DKD (Fig. 2).Fig. 2The senescence-inflammation-fibrosis cycle driving DKD progression

The senescence-inflammation-fibrosis cycle driving DKD progression

The abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway during mesangial cell senescence is a core initiating factor triggering cell cycle arrest, a phenomenon that has been validated in naturally aging animal models and high glucose-induced mesangial cell in vitro experiments [89]. Its molecular cascadeinvolves crosstalk among multiple pathways and a tight regulatory network: its activation can 4plify signal transduction by forming a synergistic regulatory axis with the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), simultaneously engaging in crosstalk with the TGF-β/Smad pathway, and is coordinately regulated by the PXR-p53 axis. Senescent microenvironments such as serum from aged rats, high glucose stimulation, or TGF-β can activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [90, 91]. On one hand, pathway activation induces the upregulation of Wnt ligands including Wnt1, Wnt10b, and Wnt7a, which bind to Frizzled/LRP5/6 receptors on the cell membrane to inhibit cytoplasmic GSK-3β activity, prevent β-catenin phosphorylation and degradation, and promote its nuclear translocation, thereby initiating the transcription of downstream target genes [90–92]; on the other hand, high glucose can trigger superoxide burst by activating the Ras/Rac1 signal to further stabilize β-catenin and enhance pathway activity, while it can also potentiate the biological effects of TGF-β—activating TGF-β receptors to phosphorylate Smad3, which forms a synergistic signaling complex with β-catenin to amplify downstream cell cycle regulatory signals [93]. Additionally, p53 mediates the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through a dual mechanism: first, p53 directly binds to the promoter region of the Wnt7a gene to promote its transcriptional expression; second, p53 facilitates the assembly of the p-Smad3 complex, providing a synergistic basis for downstream signal transduction. In contrast, the pregnane X receptor (PXR) can colocalize with p53 in the nucleus and exert a physical interaction, inhibiting the binding of p53 to the Wnt7a promoter and thus blocking the abnormal activation of the Wnt7a/β-catenin pathway [91].

Following activation, the pathway may initiate cell cycle regulation through four core molecular cascades: First, the upregulation of transcription factors such as Snail and Twist, coupled with the inhibition of phosphorylation of PGC-1α, leads to reduced mitochondrial mass, disrupted cristae structure, and increased ROS production [94]. This further induces oxidative stress and DDR, upregulating the expression of the DNA damage marker γ-H2AX, thereby activating the p16INK4A and p53/p21 signaling axes—p16INK4A specifically inhibits CDK4/6, while p21 broadly inhibits multiple cyclin kinases including CDK2 and CDK4 [92]. Second, crosstalk with the RAS upregulates components such as renin and angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1), forming a positive feedback loop by inhibiting mitochondrial biogenesis (e.g., downregulation of PGC-1α) and increasing ROS production, which enhances the expression of p16INK4A and p21 to strengthen cell cycle arrest signals [90]. Third, the downregulation of miR-196a relieves its targeted inhibition of the CDK inhibitor p27kip1, leading to the accumulation of p27kip1 protein that specifically inhibits the activity of the CDK2/cyclin E complex [95], preventing cells from progressing from the G1 phase to the S phase. Finally, Smad3 activated by TGF-β acts synergistically with β-catenin to further upregulate the transcription of cell cycle inhibitors such as p16INK4A and p21, while the inhibitory Smad7 can block this synergistic effect by inhibiting Smad3 phosphorylation [96].

Ultimately, CDK inhibitors including p16INK4A, p21, and p27kip1 act synergistically to inhibit the phosphorylation of RB, maintaining its continuous binding to E2F transcription factors and suppressing their activity. This results in the failure of cells to initiate the transcription of DNA replication-related genes, ultimately leading to arrest in the G1 phase—an event that is a hallmark feature of mesangial cell senescence.

In addition, existing studies have found that the caveolin-1/p53 pathway plays a key role in mesangial cell senescence. High glucose can induce the upregulation of caveolin-1 expression, and experiments have confirmed that the upregulated caveolin-1 expression activates the p53/p21 pathway, leading to cell cycle arrest. After blocking caveolin-1, the SA-β-Gal positivity rate of mesangial cells treated with high glucose decreased from 88.31% to 30.73% [97], accompanied by prolonged telomere length and more regular cell arrangement. These results confirm that blocking this protein can inhibit senescence.

Endothelial cell senescence: the core driver of glomerular microvascular lesions

As the inner layer of the glomerular filtration barrier, glomerular endothelial cell senescence directly mediates early microvascular lesions in DKD and is an important cause of progressive decline in renal function [98, 99] (Fig. 2). Clinical studies have shown that the fenestration density of glomerular endothelial cells in DKD patients is positively correlated with the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (r = 0.77), and patients with reduced fenestral surface area of glomerular endothelial cells have significantly lower eGFR levels than normal individuals. In diabetic mouse models, the decreased fenestration density of glomerular endothelial cells is associated with the decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and the glomerular ultrafiltration coefficient (Kf) of diabetic mice is only 54% of that in normal mice [100]. Pathologically, senescent glomerular endothelial cells exhibit increased SA-β-Gal activity, upregulated expression of cell cycle inhibitors p16, p21, p19, and massive accumulation of DNA damage markers γH2AX, p53-binding protein 1 (53BP1)) [101]. Morphologically and functionally, loss of endothelial fenestrations and disruption of cell junction integrity lead to increased vascular permeability (Fig. 1), while impaired repair function of endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) results in reduced angiogenic capacity [102].

At the molecular mechanism level of aging, glomerular endothelial cell senescence is characterized by persistent activation of DDR and the paracrine effects of SASP. Single-cell sequencing analysis shows that endothelial cells undergo significant transcriptomic changes in the early stage of glomerular diseases [103]. Notably, senescent glomerular endothelial cells release various pro-damage factors via SASP,In addition to the signal molecules that act as messengers mentioned earlier, endothelin-1 (ET-1) and HMGB1 also form harmful signal transmission to podocytes through paracrine action, promoting podocyte foot process fusion and slit diaphragm damage [104, 105]. This endothelial cell–podocyte cross-talk is a key mechanism of early filtration barrier dysfunction in DKD.

It is not difficult to see from the aforementioned mechanisms that: in DKD, the heterogeneity of the three types of glomerular cells during senescence is remarkably significant. Podocytes, centered on mitochondrial dysfunction and sustained activation of the DDR, directly induce damage to the glomerular filtration barrier and proteinuria.Mesangial cells, relying on the abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and cross-regulation with fibrotic pathways, serve as a key driver of glomerulosclerosis.Glomerular endothelial cells, characterized mainly by DDR activation and the paracrine effect of the SASP, dominate early glomerular microangiopathy and reduce the eGFR. However, it is precisely the existence of this heterogeneity that makes us recognize the importance of precision intervention in DKD and the difficulty of its clinical translation.

Cross-talk between glomerular cells during senescence in DKD

Obviously, this senescence process is not isolated but tightly interconnected through a complex information exchange network, and these intercellular communication pathways are precisely the core regulatory hubs of the “senescence-inflammation-fibrosis” vicious cycle in DKD—senescence of one type of cell can directly induce senescence of other cell types through extracellular vesicles, SAS.0P factors, and other pathways, forming a cascade amplification effect [106, 107]. Specifically, the regulation of the vicious cycle and the induction of transcellular senescence by intercellular communication are mainly achieved through these major pathways: SASP factors such as plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM1) secreted by glomerular endothelial cells can bind to podocyte urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) or recruit macrophage infiltration through paracrine signaling [104, 108], which not only directly induces podocyte adhesion dysfunction and senescence but also triggers local inflammatory responses, and the inflammatory factors further accelerate mesangial cell ECM synthesis. Podocytes transmit signals to mesangial cells via the C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4)–stromal cell-derived factor 1α (SDF-1α) axis, SDF-1α can activate CXCR4 on mesangial cells, promoting mesangial cell proliferation and ECM synthesis [76, 109], thereby indirectly aggravating glomerulosclerosis, while the released IL-1β can amplify the senescence level of themselves and endothelial cells through autocrine and paracrine dual effects. Conversely, Slit homolog 3 (Slit3) and AGEs secreted by mesangial cells damage the integrity of the endothelial barrier and inhibit endothelial cell autophagy via the Receptor for Advanced Glycation End Products (RAGE)/STAT5 pathway [103, 110], accelerating their senescence. Meanwhile, Slit3 can bind to Robo receptors on the surface of podocytes/endothelial cells, thereby inhibiting the adhesive function of podocytes and impairing the endothelial barrier [103]. Similarly, the AngII secreted by it promotes ROS-induced telomere shortening by activating NADPH oxidase [111],and ultimately contributes to the senescence of neighboring cells. As key carriers, extracellular vesicles can encapsulate bioactive substances such as miRNAs and damaged proteins, shuttling between the three cell types—for example, in vitro experiments have confirmed that both glomerular endothelial cells and podocytes can secrete exosomes, and in vivo tracking technology has demonstrated the transfer of exosomes from one cell type to another. Exosomes derived from endothelial cells are capable of altering the gene expression of podocytes, and vice versa [112]. Notably, high glucose-stimulated endothelial cells deliver TGF-β1 mRNA and miR-200c-3p via exosomes, thereby inducing podocyte injury and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Meanwhile, mesangial cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) modulate podocyte function by transferring molecules such as TGF-β1[113, 114]. Similarly, podocyte-derived EVs can carry microRNAs including miR-221 to regulate downstream target cells [113].

In summary, glomerular cell senescence in DKD is not an isolated event but a networked process closely linked through extracellular vesicles, SASP factors, specific signaling axes, and other mediators. These intercellular communication pathways not only collectively drive the continuous amplification of the “senescence-inflammation-fibrosis” vicious cycle but also enable direct transcellular transmission of senescence signals. Intervening in these intercellular communication links may be a novel strategy to break the vicious cycle and treat DKD.

This figure reveals the pathological cascade of the “senescence-inflammation-fibrosis” vicious cycle initiated by diabetes/high glucose that drives DKD progression, clearly presenting the continuous mechanism from initiating factors to end-stage renal disease. Sustained high glucose stimulation first induces specific senescence of glomerular podocytes, mesangial cells, and endothelial cells, due to cell type heterogeneity, the senescence mechanisms are distinct; senescent cells further trigger a series of pathological injuries: podocyte injury leads to glomerular filtration barrier dysfunction, mesangial cell abnormalities cause excessive ECM deposition, and endothelial cell lesions induce glomerular microangiopathy; the aforementioned pathological changes stimulate senescent cells to secrete SASP factors, including pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), pro-fibrotic factors (TGF-β), and MMPs; finally, SASP factors act on surrounding glomerular cells through paracrine or endocrine pathways, further exacerbating cellular senescence and forming a self-reinforcing “senescence-inflammation-fibrosis” vicious cycle. The continuous progression of this cycle can lead to gradual aggravation of clinical symptoms such as albuminuria, glomerulosclerosis, and decreased GFR, ultimately progressing to ESRD.

Research progress on diagnostic biomarkers

Analysis of circulating senescence-related molecular profiles

Recent studies have shown that senescence markers in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) can serve as a non-invasive diagnostic tool for DKD [115, 116]. In patients with type 2 diabetes, the expression levels of the cyclin-dependent kinase Inhibitor 2 A (CDKN2A) gene and long non-coding RNA antisense non-coding RNA in the INK4 locus(ANRIL) are significantly reduced [117], and these molecules are highly likely to become specific biomarkers for assessing diabetes-related cellular senescence. In addition, the protein levels of p16INK4a, p21, and p53 show characteristic changes in circulating PBMCs of diabetic nephropathy patients [118], with p16INK4a expression positively correlated with the degree of renal function impairment [119]. Notably, persistent expression of p21 in renal tubular epithelial cells is associated with promoter demethylation and reduced expression of DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1). This epigenetic regulation may be an important mechanism underlying the “hyperglycemic memory” phenomenon in diabetic nephropathy, whereby endothelial cell senescence can continue to progress even after blood glucose returns to normal [120]. Furthermore, studies on human kidney tissue have found that the number of p21-positive cells is positively correlated with the degree of fibrosis, age, and decline in glomerular filtration rate [121], suggesting its potential value as a marker of disease progression. Multiple studies have also found that complement C5a promotes cellular senescence in DKD by mediating changes in the expression of cell cycle pathway-related genes, and its plasma level may also serve as a predictive indicator of disease progression.

Application of epigenetic clocks in DKD

Epigenetic modifications, particularly changes in DNA methylation patterns, provide new ideas for the early diagnosis of DKD. Studies have found that biological age estimation indicators based on DNA methylation levels, such as DNAmPhenoAge and DNAmAge Skin Blood Clock, are significantly associated with the decline in renal function in patients with DKD [122, 123]. During cellular senescence, the methylation status of the VEGFA and cystatin C genes also undergoes significant changes [66, 124]. These epigenetic markers not only reflect the degree of renal aging but are also closely related to the severity of microvascular lesions. In addition, whole-genome methylation analysis has shown that glomerular and tubular tissues in patients with DKD exhibit characteristic methylation profiles, which may occur before clinically detectable proteinuria appears [122, 125]. The epigenetic clock is an algorithmic model based on DNA methylation (DNAm) that integrates methylation measurement information from multiple CpG sites across the genome to quantify the difference between biological aging and chronological age, or directly assess the rate of biological aging and its relationship to health outcomes [126]. Current studies indicate that epigenetic clocks are directly associated with various age-related diseases and mortality risk, can serve as biomarkers of health risk, and can be used as effect indicators to verify whether interventions such as diet, exercise, and supplements can slow aging at the epigenetic level [127]. Compared with conventional aging biomarkers, DNA methylation-based algorithmic models offer inherent advantages including high standardization, minimal invasiveness, and cross-tissue applicability. However, most traditional epigenetic clocks are established based on the average DNA methylation levels of bulk tissues or peripheral blood, which inevitably masks the heterogeneity of aging across distinct cell types [127]. Recently, this field has achieved a pivotal breakthrough with the transition from bulk tissue-averaged profiling to single-cell resolution, which forms a dual coupling of technology and concept with the glomerular cellular aging heterogeneity emphasized in this review.

For instance, by leveraging the mitochondrial single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing technique, researchers have for the first time enabled the independent dissection of aging dynamics across distinct cell subpopulations within the same kidney tissue [128]. The study found that during renal aging, podocytes exhibit “positive selection” for mutated mitochondrial DNA, whereas renal tubular epithelial cells present “neutral drift”, which directly demonstrates that aging programs operate in fundamentally distinct modes among different cell types within the same organ [128]. In addition, the fluorescence lifetime imaging-based aging clock, developed with novel ultrasensitive molecular probes, can resolve aging heterogeneity at the single-cell level. It enables real-time monitoring of cellular senescence in living cells and intact live tissues without sample destruction, with a detection speed several days to even one week faster than that of conventional epigenetic clocks [129]. These technological advances pave the way for the future development of kidney-specific and even podocyte-specific epigenetic clocks, which can accurately distinguish physiological renal aging from hyperglycemia-induced pathological senescence, thus providing robust cellular-level evidence for the molecular subtyping and early warning of DKD.

Development of novel imaging biomarkers

Although current research on specific imaging biomarkers for renal aging is relatively limited, studies have explored the application value of radioactive tracers in assessing the progression of DKD. The novel positron emission tomography (PET) tracer N-(6-(1⁸F)Fluoropyridin-3-yl)glycine (6-[18F]FPyGly) has demonstrated good renal function assessment capability in a rat model of DKD, with its uptake significantly correlated with changes in glomerular filtration rate [130]. Its core advantage lies in its ability to noninvasively and dynamically reflect glomerular filtration function. In the field of molecular imaging, SA-β-Gal activity, as a marker of cellular senescence, has prompted the development of specific probes that may provide new tools for noninvasive evaluation of renal aging [131]. The core value of such probes is in transforming the molecular event of cellular senescence into a visual imaging signal, allowing quantification of the senescent cell burden in renal tissue through imaging signal intensity, thereby providing objective indicators for assessing the activation level of the “senescence–inflammation–fibrosis” axis in DKD. In addition, the co-localization of iron deposition with renal cell senescence suggests that magnetic resonance T2 imaging may become a potential imaging biomarker for evaluating senescence-related pathological changes in DKD [132]. Iron deposition is an accompanying feature of renal cell senescence in DKD, and T2 imaging can indirectly indicate the deterioration of the renal aging microenvironment through iron content, providing imaging evidence for predicting DKD prognosis.

Therapeutic strategies targeting cellular senescence in DKD

Complement inhibitors: blocking the initiation of senescence

Activation of the complement system plays a key role in the progression of DKD, and the C5a-mediated senescence signaling pathway has been shown to be closely related to glomerular cell senescence [133]. Recent studies indicate that inhibitors targeting the complement system can significantly delay the senescence process of glomerular cells in DKD models [134]. In experimental focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and mouse DKD models, complement inhibition interventions have demonstrated protective effects in reducing glomerular injury [135]. In complement factor B knockout mice under high-glucose conditions, the expression of KIM-1 in renal tubular epithelial cells is reduced, and the degree of renal interstitial fibrosis is attenuated [22], which indirectly ameliorates the glomerular microenvironment. In terms of clinical translational potential, while complement inhibitors have demonstrated promise in delaying glomerular cell senescence in DKD models and reducing urinary albumin levels in early clinical trials (NCT05265353) by blocking C5a-mediated senescence signalings [136] (Fig. 3), they carry risks of increased severe infections—this is because systemic suppression of the complement system impairs innate immunity—and have limited long-term renal protection data, while single-target inhibitors fail to cover heterogeneous complement activation pathways in all DKD patients. Senolytics: selective clearance of senescent cells

Alleviating the burden of senescent cells via selective induction of apoptosis in these cells represents a pivotal therapeutic direction for DKD. As a new class of therapeutic drugs, senolytics(senescent cell-clearing agents) have shown remarkable efficacy in preclinical studies of DKD [137]. Research indicates that senolytics can selectively clear senescent cells, reduce renal fibrosis, and improve renal dysfunction. In DKD models, senolytic treatment can reverse the senescent phenotype of renal tubular epithelial cells and improve renal function indicators [138]. In recent years, a pivotal paradigm shift has occurred in the clinical application of senolytics, advancing from the initial proof-of-concept phase—where efficacy was validated in animal models and manageable safety was preliminarily confirmed—to an advanced stage of clinical translation defined by the core hallmarks of long-term benefit, microenvironment remodeling, and multicenter validation.

With respect to clinical evidence, clinical trials of senolytics in patients with DKD have progressed from preliminary safety reports to studies with mature long-term follow-up data. For instance, in an early pilot clinical trial (NCT02848131), the “hit-and-run” dosing regimen of dasatinib plus quercetin (D + Q) was not only confirmed to reduce senescent cell burden and circulating senescence-associated secretory phenotype SASP factors in human adipose tissue and skin [139]; its long-term follow-up study further provided critical data on renal function changes and patient prognosis. The estimated completion date of this trial has been updated to 2026, indicating that investigators are actively collecting data on durable clinical outcome endpoints.

Regarding the mechanistic understanding, the beneficial effects of senolytics have been shown to extend far beyond their mere senescent cell clearance function. Recent preclinical studies in animal models have demonstrated that D + Q treatment not only improves renal function, alleviates glomerular and tubular injury, and mitigates fibrosis in DKD mice, but also significantly restores the renal levels of geroprotective factors including α-Klotho and Sirtuin-1. Furthermore, it indirectly inhibits paracrine senescence in adjacent cells by modulating the inflammatory microenvironment, including suppressing NF-κB activation and reducing macrophage infiltration [140]. These findings indicate that the therapeutic efficacy of senolytics is driven by the simultaneous integration of senescent cell clearance, tissue repair, and microenvironment remodeling.

Finally, with regard to clinical translation strategies, parallel clinical trials for diverse aging-related diseases are being accelerated through collaborative initiatives such as the Translational Geroscience Network, with the core goal of determining whether senolytics can be successfully translated into routine clinical practice [141]. This multicenter, cross-disease trial paradigm helps overcome the inherent limitations of single-disease trials, and provides a more robust framework for the precise stratification of DKD patient subtypes that may derive clinical benefit from senolytics treatment.

Klotho protein supplementation therapy

Klotho is a kidney-protective protein with anti-aging properties, whose expression is significantly reduced in DKD. Studies have shown that supplementation with recombinant Klotho protein (10 μg/kg/d) can alleviate kidney damage in DKD models, lower blood pressure and proteinuria, and inhibit RAAS activity [142]. Preclinical studies indicate that Klotho exerts protective effects through multiple mechanisms: first, it acts as a co-receptor for fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) to regulate phosphate metabolism [143]; second, it can alleviate renal fibrosis progression by inhibiting the TGF-β/Smad3 signaling pathway [144]; third, it can reduce oxidative stress in renal cells by activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway [145]. Although recombinant klotho (rKL) and its derived peptide KP6 exert renoprotective effects in DKD models by regulating phosphate metabolism and inhibiting fibrosis to alleviate kidney damage,lower blood pressure and proteinuria [144] (Fig. 3), they still face challenges including poor oral bioavailability that necessitates injection and reduces patient compliance, short half-lives that increase long-term treatment costs, risk of hypophosphatemia with excessive supplementation.

Application potential of regenerative medicine in the field of aging

Regenerative medicine offers innovative approaches for intervening in glomerular cell aging in diabetic nephropathy. With aging, the quantity and function of stem cells in the human body decline significantly, which is a key factor leading to organ dysfunction [146–148]. Recent studies have found that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can release anti-inflammatory factors and anti-aging signaling molecules through their secretome (Fig. 3), thereby suppressing the senescence-associated secretory phenotype of glomerular podocytes and endothelial cells, reducing macrophage infiltration and ECM deposition; furthermore, Lipalensel, a renal progenitor cell product, has achieved local colonization in the kidney via a biomaterial carrier. It has now entered Phase III clinical trials and is expected to directly replace senescent renal cells and reconstruct the glomerular microenvironment [149]. In addition, injecting experimental animals with anti-aging human mesenchymal progenitor cells enhanced by FOXO3 gene modification has shown strong anti-aging effects. MSC preparations have demonstrated the ability to slow the decline in eGFR in phase 1b/2a clinical trials [150]. Regenerative medicine has brought breakthrough potential for the treatment of renal aging and DKD. Its core value lies in breaking the limitation that traditional treatments can only provide symptomatic relief, instead shifting toward fundamental intervention. From the perspective of existing research, the secretome mechanism of MSCs avoids the colonization challenge of direct cell transplantation and enables the possibility of remote regulation of anti-inflammation and anti-senescence through paracrine signaling, making it particularly suitable for improving glomerular microenvironment disorders. Furthermore, the local colonization and replacement functions of renal progenitor cell products, as well as the targeted anti-senescence capability brought by FOXO3 gene-enhanced modification, further expand the intervention dimensions for renal aging.The application potential of regenerative medicine in the field of cellular senescence is undoubtedly enormous; yet it still faces challenges such as difficulties in achieving standardized cell sources, as well as issues concerning genetic safety, ethics, and other related aspects. However, we believe that all these issues will be resolved in the future; regenerative medicine will not only be fully applied in the field of renal aging, but also play an important role in systemic aging intervention.

Exploration of synergistic effects in combination therapy regimens

In senescence-targeted therapy, sufficient studies have confirmed that drugs with renal protective effects, including hypoglycemic drugs and RAAS inhibitors [151, 152], and non-pharmacological interventions such as exercise intervention and caloric restriction can delay the senescence process of renal cells and reduce renal tissue injury by alleviating inflammation and regulating metabolism [153]. Moreover, certain achievements have been made in drug application for DKD treatment (Table 2). Although the initial mechanisms of action of these therapeutic methods may be different, most of them have similar endpoints, which suggests that multi-target anti-senescence combined therapy may be feasible [139]. In addition, in current studies, due to the complexity of DKD, combined therapeutic regimens targeting different senescence pathways have also shown significant synergistic effects. Studies have shown that the combination of senolytics and apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1) inhibitors can more effectively improve the pathological changes of DKD than single therapy [55]; moreover, the combined application of complement inhibitors and FFAR4 agonists has also shown enhanced renal protective effects [154, 155]. Mechanistically, combined therapy can simultaneously target multiple key links of cellular senescence, including but not limited to the DDR, inflammatory factor secretion, and cell cycle regulation, which are important processes involved in cellular senescence [156, 157]. Treatments targeting glomerular cell senescence have demonstrated their respective effects in current studies. Based on the latest clinical studies and management consensus, the treatment of DKD has shifted from relying on a single drug to the combined use of renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi), sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (nsMRA) [158]. These three classes of drugs achieve a synergistic effect by targeting different pathophysiological mechanisms of DKD, such as hemodynamics, metabolism, inflammation, and fibrosis, thereby minimizing the residual cardiovascular and renal risks in patients. Thus combination therapy should become the focus of future research on anti-senescence therapy. How to enhance therapeutic efficacy while reducing impacts on target organs to achieve greater benefits remains a key focus of our future research. Current challenges and future directions

Translational gap between animal models and human disease manifestations

Although the animal models currently used in DKD research can simulate certain features of human disease, there remain significant differences in reflecting the molecular characteristics of human glomerular cell senescence [168]. Existing DKD models such as streptozotocin-induced mice and db/db mice cannot fully reproduce the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of senescence seen in human DKD. For example, in mouse models, p16-positive cells are mainly located in the glomerular mesangial area [169], whereas in human DKD, p16-positive cells are more enriched in glomerular endothelial cells [170]. Moreover, the development of human DKD takes decades, while animal models exhibit significant pathological changes within weeks. This time compression may obscure key molecular events related to endothelial cell senescence, such as the dynamic process of telomere uncapping. In the future, it will be necessary to develop models that more closely mimic human pathological features, such as kidney organoids and patient-derived xenografts, with a focus on simulating the senescence process of glomerular cells. This will enable precise elucidation of human-specific molecular mechanisms of glomerular cell senescence, allow accurate validation of senescence-related intervention strategies, enhance the reliability of clinical translation, ultimately promote the establishment of a precision medicine system for kidney disease classification and treatment guidance, and optimize clinical intervention plans.

Technical bottlenecks in cell-type-specific targeted delivery

Precise intervention targeting different cell types within the glomerulus faces major technical challenges. Existing studies have shown that although free fatty acid receptor 4 agonists have a specific protective effect on podocytes, there is a lack of effective delivery systems to achieve targeted drug delivery to specific cell populations in the glomerulus [103, 171].Cell type-specific surface markers revealed by single-cell sequencing such as GPR124 in podocytes provide potential molecular tags for targeted delivery, but how to efficiently cross the glomerular filtration barrier remains an unresolved technical problem. Current delivery systems struggle to achieve specific targeting of glomerular endothelial cells, with nanoparticle accumulation efficiency in the glomerulus being less than 10% [172]. The in vivo stability and drug-loading efficiency of exosome-based delivery systems still require optimization. Surface-functionalized delivery strategies based on glomerular endothelial cell-specific surface markers such as vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) and platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 (PECAM-1) may offer a breakthrough direction. Studies have shown that in the human kidney normothermic machine perfusion model, the accumulation rate of anti-PECAM-1 antibody-modified nanoparticles in glomerular and renal interstitial microvessels is 5–10 times higher than that of non-targeted nanoparticles [173]. Then, can conjugating complement inhibitors to anti-PECAM-1 antibodies also enhance the enrichment efficiency of the drug in glomerular endothelial cells? Therefore, nanocarrier and antibody conjugation technologies may become future breakthrough approaches for achieving targeted therapy and precise intervention in DKD.

Standardization issues in multi-omics integrated analysis and precision medicine strategies for individualized aging interventions

Although single-cell transcriptome technologies such as the single-cell senescence analysis platform can identify 72 mouse and 64 human cellular aging signatures, there is significant heterogeneity in the sequencing platforms and analytical workflows used by different laboratories [174]. While researchers have identified numerous specific markers of glomerular endothelial cell aging in DKD through single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and other techniques, the substantial heterogeneity in sequencing platforms and data analysis workflows across laboratories makes it difficult to achieve unified clinical validation of these markers. Therefore, establishing internationally recognized multi-omics data analysis standards, especially classification standards for glomerular microvascular cells, is key to advancing the field.

In terms of individualized aging interventions, the significant inter-individual differences in aging phenotypes among DKD patients are objectively present. Some patients are characterized by high Nox4 expression in glomerular endothelial cells [175], while others show mesangial cell Wnt pathway activation [176]. This heterogeneity results in limited effectiveness of “one-size-fits-all” treatments, with marked differences in clinical treatment responses, i.e., some patients experience no benefit or only minimal improvement, and in some cases, early intervention opportunities are missed, accelerating renal function deterioration. Moreover, such “one-size-fits-all” strategies often fail to achieve the expected therapeutic effect, and may instead increase the risk of adverse drug reactions, add to the patient’s medical burden, widen prognostic disparities, and raise the risk of ESRD. Therefore, in the future, we might consider integrating single-cell sequencing with machine learning to establish a DKD molecular classification system based on aging markers, for example, categorizing patients into endothelial aging-dominant, podocyte aging-dominant, and mesangial aging-dominant types, thus enabling a precision medicine model of classification-based diagnosis and treatment.

Conclusions

This review synthesizes the roles of glomerular cell senescence in the progression of DKD from a cell-type-specific perspective, focusing on podocytes, mesangial cells, and glomerular endothelial cells. Evidence indicates that cellular senescence constitutes an upstream, druggable programming layer governing the renal bifurcation among glomerular filtration barrier maintenance, inflammatory activation, and fibrotic remodeling. In podocytes, mitochondrial dysfunction and persistent DDR activation drive senescence-related filtration barrier impairment and proteinuria; in mesangial cells, abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and cross-talk with fibrotic signaling cascades promote glomerulosclerosis; in glomerular endothelial cells, DDR activation and SASP-mediated paracrine effects dominate early microvascular lesions and renal function decline. Operating in parallel and in concert across these cell types, these senescence mechanisms form an integrated pathological network that explains how sustained hyperglycemic stress is consolidated into irreversible DKD.

Therapeutically, we propose a precision senescence intervention framework that integrates cell-type-specific target selection, temporal windowing, and multi-modal combination strategies. Along the senescence initiation axis, complement inhibitors targeting the C5a-C5aR1 pathway should be prioritized to block early senescence triggers, with attention to balancing anti-senescence efficacy and immune homeostasis. Along the senescent cell clearance axis, selective senolytics (such as dasatinib-quercetin combinations) and PAI-1 inhibitors offer promising tools for reducing senescent cell burden, particularly when tailored to cell-type-specific senescence phenotypes. Along the regenerative regulation axis, Klotho supplementation and mesenchymal stem cell-based therapies provide complementary approaches to restore renal repair capacity and mitigate senescence-associated damage. Despite robust preclinical signals, translation remains limited by suboptimal cell-type-specific delivery systems, discrepancies between animal models and human disease heterogeneity, and the paucity of standardized multi-omics-based diagnostic and therapeutic monitoring tools.

Looking ahead, three priorities merit emphasis. First, integrate human DKD biopsy data with single-cell spatial omics to achieve clinical anchoring at the cell-locus-outcome level, clarifying human-specific senescence mechanisms and refining cell-type-specific molecular targets. Second, establish on-target efficacy biomarkers and develop kidney-specific delivery systems (such as PECAM-1-targeted nanoparticles or GPR124-conjugated agents) to enhance therapeutic precision and expand the therapeutic index while reducing off-target effects. Third, test rational combination strategies—including senolytics plus metabolic modulators, complement inhibitors plus anti-fibrotic agents, and regenerative therapies plus senescence blockers—in early, stratified clinical studies based on molecular subtypes of DKD.

With the maturation of cell-type-specific targeting technologies, standardized multi-omics analysis pipelines, and individualized classification systems, senescence-targeted interventions may enable disease modification of DKD by interrupting the inception of chronic glomerular damage during the early stages and, in established DKD, by slowing fibrotic progression while resetting renal immune and metabolic homeostasis. Meanwhile, regarding renal fibrosis, future research should further systematically integrate the crosstalk mechanisms of senescence between renal tubular cells and glomerular cells, fully improve the global senescence regulatory network of DKD, without being limited to local senescence.This approach not only holds promise for improving clinical outcomes in DKD patients but also provides a replicable precision medicine paradigm for other aging-related chronic kidney diseases.
