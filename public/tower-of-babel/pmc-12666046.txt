Complete blood count-derived biomarkers as indicators of systemic lupus erythematosus activity: a cross-sectional comparative study
Authors: Mariem Ajmi, Salma Riahi, Imen Ben Hassine, Asma Ben Cheikh, Yosra Dhaha, Donia Mbarki, Amel Rezgui, Jihed Anoun, Fatma Ben Fredj, Amina Bouatay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSLE is a chronic autoimmune disease with heterogeneous manifestations. This study evaluated the discriminative value of complete blood count-derived biomarkers, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), eosinophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (ELR), basophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (BLR), mean platelet volume and red cell distribution width (RDW), in identifying lupus flares.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted over 10 years in the internal medicine department of Sahloul Hospital. SLE patients were classified by disease activity using Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K): flare (SLEDAI-2K >6) or remission (SLEDAI-2K ≤6). A matched group of healthy controls was included. Comparisons were made between flare/remission and flare/controls. Discriminative performance of biomarkers was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic analysis, with sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp) and area under the curve (AUC) determined for the optimal cut-off values.ResultsThe study included 83 SLE patients (40 flares, 43 remission) and 85 controls, median age of 32 years and a sex ratio of 0.16. Compared with remission, flares had significantly higher NLR and RDW and lower ELR. The most discriminative biomarkers were RDW (AUC=0.708, cutoff ≥14.65, Se =75.9%, Sp =60.6%), NLR (AUC =0.636, cutoff ≥2.63, Se =72.4%, Sp =51.5%) and ELR (AUC =0.647, cutoff ≤0.0076, Se =74.4%, Sp =65%). Compared with controls, flares showed higher NLR, PLR, MLR, RDW and lower ELR and BLR. Best performances were RDW (AUC =0.864, cutoff ≥13.85, Se =89.7%, Sp =75.3%), NLR (AUC =0.822, cutoff ≥2.28, Se =79.3%, Sp =76.5%) and ELR (AUC =0.775, cutoff ≤0.029, Se =87.1%, Sp =70.3%). SLEDAI-2K correlated positively with NLR (r =0.228), PLR (r =0.238), RDW (r =0.303) and negatively with ELR (r =–0.317).ConclusionsThese biomarkers may serve as simple, accessible and cost-effective tools for detecting lupus flares, particularly in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Inflammation, Hematology

Introduction

SLE is a chronic autoimmune disease causing systemic inflammation and multisystem damage.1 Its global prevalence is estimated at 43.7 cases per 100 000 population, predominantly affecting women of childbearing age, with ethnic variations.2 Data on North African patients, especially Tunisians, remain scarce.3

Pathophysiologically, SLE arises from genetic, epigenetic, environmental and hormonal interactions leading to immune tolerance loss and immune complex formation responsible for tissue injury.4 5 Clinical manifestations are heterogeneous, ranging from mild to severe organ involvement, including haematological disorders often linked to disease activity.6

Given this variability, simple and reliable biomarkers are needed to assess disease activity. Several blood count–derived parameters, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), eosinophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (ELR) and basophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (BLR), have gained interest. Other indices like red cell distribution width (RDW) and mean platelet volume (MPV) are also explored as potential markers of systemic inflammation.

This study aimed to assess the value of these biomarkers in detecting SLE flares by determining their sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp) and threshold values as indicators of disease activity.

Patients and methods

This cross-sectional, single-centre study was conducted at Sahloul University Hospital between January 2015 and December 2024. Data were collected consecutively from the medical records of patients diagnosed with SLE, according to the criteria of the American College of Rheumatology, the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) and/or the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology.

Disease activity was assessed using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K), a validated composite score combining clinical and laboratory data, classifying activity as mild (0–4), moderate (5–9) or high (≥10).

Patients aged ≥18 years were classified into two groups: active flare (SLEDAI-2K >6 with clinical activity) or remission (SLEDAI-2K ≤6 with no clinical activity for ≥6 months). There is no universal SLEDAI cut-off, with previous studies using values >6, >8 or >10 depending on context. We adopted a threshold of >6, following Peirovy et al.7 Patients with conditions affecting the complete blood count (CBC) results were excluded, including other inflammatory diseases, malignancy, infection, recent transfusion (<3 months), chemotherapy or constitutional anaemia (eg, haemoglobinopathy, membranopathy, enzymopathy).

A control group of healthy volunteers, matched for age and sex, was recruited from blood donors after informed consent.

Clinical, biological and immunological data were meticulously gathered using a standardised form. CBC results were collected at hospital admission. Collected parameters included age, sex, disease duration, clinical manifestations, SLEDAI 2K score, CBC results (platelet count, leucocyte differential, RDW, MPV), biochemical parameters (urea, creatinine, liver enzymes, bilirubin, proteinuria, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C reactive protein (CRP), complement components C3 and C4, lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase) and SLE-specific autoantibodies. The latter included antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies (anti-dsDNA), anti-Smith antibodies (anti-Sm), anti-SSA/Ro, anti-SSB/La, anti-nucleosome and antiphospholipid antibodies (APL), including anticardiolipin antibodies, lupus anticoagulant (LA) and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies.

From 2015 to 2020, complete blood counts were performed using the Sysmex XN-1000 analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan), which combines impedance and optical detection based on fluorescence flow cytometry for white blood cell differentiation. Since 2021, analyses have been carried out using the Beckman Coulter DxH 900 analyser (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA), which uses a combination of impedance and multiangle light scattering technologies. Both instruments undergo regular internal quality control and participate in external proficiency testing to ensure data comparability.

The haematological indices derived from the CBC included the NLR, PLR, MLR, ELR and BLR. These were analysed as potential markers of systemic inflammation. Definitions of haematological abnormalities were as follows: lymphopenia = lymphocyte count <1.0×10⁹/L; anaemia = haemoglobin < 120 g/L in women and <130 g/L in men; thrombocytopenia = platelet count <150×10⁹/L; leucopenia = white blood cell count <4.0×10⁹/L.8 Reference ranges are detailed in the table 1.

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS V.20. The normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test (for non-parametric continuous variables), the Kruskal-Wallis test (for multiple comparisons) and the McNemar test (for paired categorical variables).

The correlation between haematological indices and SLE disease activity (SLEDAI 2K score) was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation test. The strength of the correlation was interpreted according to the correlation coefficient (r) as follows: values between 0 and 0.19 indicate a very weak or no correlation; 0.20–0.39, a weak correlation; 0.40–0.59, a moderate correlation; 0.60–0.79, a strong correlation and 0.80–1.00, a very strong correlation (r = correlation coefficient). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to determine the optimal cut-off values of the studied ratios, their Se, Sp and area under the curve (AUC). Diagnostic performance was classified according to the AUC values as follows: 0.90–1.00, excellent; 0.80–0.89, good; 0.70–0.79, moderate; 0.60–0.69, poor; 0.50–0.59, very low or non-discriminant and equal to 0.50, none (equivalent to chance). ORs were also calculated to estimate the strength of association between the ratios and active SLE flares.

A p value <0.05 was considered significant. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sahloul University Hospital.

Results

The studied population consisted of 83 patients with SLE, among whom 40 were experiencing a disease flare and 43 were in remission. A control group of 85 healthy individuals, matched for age and sex, was included (figure 1). The majority of SLE patients were female (88%) with a median age of 32 years. Age at diagnosis was comparable between the two groups, whereas disease duration was significantly longer among patients in remission (p=0.028).

Study population distribution: SLE patients and healthy controls.

The most frequent comorbidities were hypertension (18.1%), diabetes (13.3%) and cardiovascular diseases (12%). Clinically, the most common initial manifestations were articular (74.7%), cutaneous (42.2%), general (40%) and renal (33.7%).

Regarding autoantibodies, ANA tests were positive in 85.5% of patients. The most commonly detected autoantibodies were anti-dsDNA (66.3%), anti-nucleosome (43.4%) and anti-SSA/Ro (42.2%).

On the biological level, patients in flare exhibited significantly elevated inflammatory markers, including an increased ESR (p=0.05) and heightened CRP (p=0.014).

Further analysis revealed that patients in flare also had significant lymphopenia (p=0.009), more severe anaemia (p=0.02), reduced eosinophil counts (p=0.02), impaired renal function (urea p=0.001; creatinine p=0.007) and greater proteinuria (p=0.003). Additionally, RDW was significantly higher in patients during flares (p=0.01) (table 2).

Therapeutically, patients in remission were mainly treated with antimalarial drugs alone or combined with corticosteroids, whereas flare-phase treatments more frequently included corticosteroids, antimalarials and immunosuppressants.

Disease activity, assessed using the SLEDAI-2 K score, showed that 41% of patients had inactive disease, 26.5% had moderate activity, 19.3% had active disease and 13.2% had very active disease (figure 2).

Regarding inflammatory biomarkers, the NLR was significantly higher in patients during flare compared with those in remission (p=0.016) and controls (p<0.001). The PLR was significantly higher in patients during flare compared with controls (p<0.001), without a significant difference with the remission group (p=0.072). The MLR was also higher in patients during flare compared with controls (p<0.001), but without a significant difference between patients in remission (p=0.138). Conversely, the ELR was significantly lower in lupus patients during flare than in patients in remission (p=0.019) and controls (p<0.001). Similarly, the BLR was significantly lower during flare compared with controls (p=0.029), without a notable difference with remission (p=0.177). The RDW was significantly higher during flare compared with remission (p=0.011) and controls (p<0.001), independently of the presence of anaemia. In contrast, the MPV did not show a statistically significant difference between the compared groups (refer table 4).

ROC analyses identified optimal diagnostic thresholds to differentiate clinical states. For the flare/remission distinction, a NLR ≥2.63 (Se =72.4%, Sp =51.5%, AUC =0.636) and an RDW ≥14.65 (Se =75.9%, Sp =60.6%, AUC =0.708) were relevant. For differentiating flare/control, the most performant thresholds were RDW ≥13.85 (Se =89.7%, Sp =75.3%, AUC =0.864) and NLR ≥2.28 (Se =79.3%, Sp =76.5%, AUC =0.822), highlighting their diagnostic value. A PLR ≥128.31 and an MLR ≥0.28 showed lower performance but remained contributive to diagnosis. Conversely, ELR stood out as a potential remission marker, with thresholds at 0.0076 between flare and remission (Se =74.4%, Sp =65%, AUC =0.647), and 0.029 between flare and control (Se =87.1%, Sp =70.3%, AUC =0.775). BLR ≥0.013 was also associated with better Sp, favouring a non-active state (refer table 6).

Contingency table analysis revealed a significant association between some biomarkers and flare status: NLR (OR=10.38), RDW (OR=26.41), PLR (OR=4.12) and MLR (OR=6.13) were associated with an increased risk of flare, whereas ELR (OR=0.35) and BLR (OR=0.44) appeared associated with a decreased risk. The comparison between flare and remission also revealed a higher risk for NLR (OR=2.99) and RDW (OR=4.49), while ELR did not show a significant difference (OR=1).

In the multivariate models including age, sex, treatment and comorbidities, none of these variables were significantly associated with lupus flare occurrence for the NLR, MLR, ELR, BLR and RDW ratios, indicating that they did not act as confounding factors for these markers.

However, in the PLR model, treatment emerged as a significant confounding factor (p=0.016), suggesting that the observed association between PLR and disease flare may be partly explained by the effect of treatment.

Correlation between these biomarkers and clinical disease activity revealed significant positive correlation of NLR (r=0.228, p=0.042), PLR (r=0.238, p=0.035), and RDW (r=0.303, p=0.016) with the SLEDAI-2K score. Conversely, ELR presented a significant negative correlation with SLEDAI-2K (r=−0.317, p=0.004), while MLR, BLR and MPV did not show statistically significant correlation with this score.

Furthermore, the evaluation of these biomarkers as predictive factors for the type of lupus flare (cutaneous, articular, renal) did not reveal any statistically significant difference.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated haematological and inflammatory biomarkers in SLE and their potential role in identifying disease flares. The studied population included 83 SLE patients (40 in flare and 43 in remission) and 85 matched healthy controls. The majority of patients were young women, consistent with the known epidemiology of SLE. Clinically, the most frequent initial manifestations were articular, cutaneous and renal, while ANA and anti-dsDNA were the most prevalent autoantibodies.

Our results showed that patients in flare had significantly higher inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP), more pronounced cytopenias (anaemia, lymphopenia, eosinopenia) and impaired renal parameters compared with those in remission. Importantly, several haematological ratios derived from routine blood counts demonstrated significant differences across disease states. NLR, PLR and MLR were significantly elevated during flares, whereas ELR and BLR were reduced, suggesting opposite regulatory patterns depending on the disease phase. In addition, RDW was markedly higher during flare, independently of anaemia, whereas MPV did not differ significantly between groups.

Diagnostic accuracy analyses further emphasised the relevance of these markers. ROC curve results identified RDW and NLR as the most accurate indices for differentiating flare from control and flare from remission, with clinically meaningful thresholds. Contingency analysis confirmed that higher NLR, PLR, MLR and RDW were associated with increased risk of flare, while lower ELR and BLR were protective. Moreover, correlations with SLEDAI-2K scores highlighted a positive association of NLR, PLR and RDW with disease activity, and a negative association of ELR, reinforcing their role as activity markers.

In our study, the demographic profile of patients with SLE confirmed the predominance of young individuals, with a median age around 32 years, consistent with international data.9 SLE predominantly affects women, in line with the literature highlighting the strong female predominance linked to hormonal influences.10

Our study confirms the heterogeneity of initial SLE manifestations, with joint involvement being the most frequent (74.7%), primarily in the form of inflammatory polyarthralgia (72.3%), which aligns with the findings of Hoi et al and Salem et al.11 12 Haematological abnormalities were also frequent (67.5%), particularly lymphopenia (49.4%) and anaemia (48.2%), partially concordant with the results of Hoi et al and Salem et al.11 12 Malar rash was found in 42.2% of patients, a lower frequency than reported by Hoi et al, Sayhi et al and Ghedira, whereas renal involvement (33.7%) was comparable to Tunisian data from Salem et al and Ghedira.6 11 12 Rare manifestations such as myositis, endocarditis and retinal vasculitis (each 1.2%) highlight the clinical diversity and potential severity of lupus.

The immunological profile was marked by a high prevalence of autoantibodies, especially anti-dsDNA (66.3%), anti-nucleosome (43.4%) and anti-SSA/SSB antibodies, consistent with both global and Tunisian literature.13 14 The prevalence of APL (20.5% to 15.7%) fell within commonly reported ranges.15

Inflammatory markers, such as ESR and CRP, were significantly higher during active disease phases, in agreement with previous studies; however, their interpretation remains challenging due to the risk of confusion with infections.16 17

In our study, several haematological biomarkers were significantly associated with SLE activity, notably, NLR, RDW, PLR and MLR. NLR was significantly higher in patients with disease flare compared with controls, with an optimal threshold of 2.28, Se of 79.3% and Sp of 76.5%. These findings are consistent with those of Hossen et al and Abira et al, who reported similar thresholds between 2.2 and 2.4, although sensitivities and specificities varied across populations.18 19 NLR also correlated with SLEDAI, confirming observations by Wong et al that link elevated NLR with disease activity.20 21 However, its discriminatory power between flare and remission was more modest (AUC=0.636), possibly due to subclinical inflammation persisting during remission, a phenomenon also reported in cohorts from the Philippines and by Saha et al.22 23 RDW also proved to be a strong marker, with an AUC of 0.864 and an OR of 26.41 at a threshold of 13.85%, outperforming Haridy et al and close to Abira et al’s threshold of 15%.24 25 The significant correlation between RDW and SLEDAI in our study supports its role as a marker of inflammation, as confirmed by other independent studies.26 27 However, its discriminatory ability between active and inactive phases remained moderate (AUC=0.708), a limitation also noted in other studies.28

Regarding PLR, our results confirmed its elevation during active phases, with an optimal threshold at 128.31 and an AUC of 0.746. This performance is in line with findings by Lisnaedy and Intansari, who reported a similar threshold of 124 with moderate Se and Sp.29 Other studies, such as Amirpour et al, identified PLR as an independent predictor of disease activity, sometimes outperforming NLR due to its lower susceptibility to confounders such as infections or immunosuppressive therapy. However, some studies, notably one from Indonesia, proposed higher thresholds (203.85) with increased Sp but reduced Se, reflecting performance variability due to clinical and methodological differences.30 31

Although MLR was significantly higher during flares, it did not correlate with SLEDAI in our study, unlike findings from studies in India and in a cohort of 85 SLE patients, where a significant association was observed.23 This discrepancy may be due to methodological differences, sample size, population heterogeneity or treatments. Despite this, MLR showed acceptable performance in ROC analysis (AUC=0.749), consistent with its reported use in identifying active forms of lupus, particularly lupus nephritis.32

Conversely, some markers like ELR stood out for their ability to reflect disease quiescence. While there was no significant difference in ELR levels between SLE patients and controls overall, a low ELR threshold (0.029) effectively distinguished active disease phases, with an AUC of 0.775. This suggests that it may serve as a marker of remission, although research on this ratio is still limited. A prospective observational study found no correlation between ELR and other markers such as SLEDAI, CRP or ESR.33

Although MPV has been explored in the literature, its clinical utility is limited by various non-specific factors such as age and treatments.34 Some studies have reported inverse correlations between MPV and ESR, but these findings are inconsistent.35 BLR, despite being significantly elevated in active phases (p=0.029), showed poor diagnostic performance (AUC=0.382) and lacked correlation with SLEDAI, echoing the findings of Meena et al.36 However, some studies noted elevated BLR in specific manifestations such as vasculitis or myositis, suggesting a potential role in phenotypic characterisation rather than overall disease activity.37

Several studies have also investigated the relationship between haematological ratios and inflammatory biomarkers. Farouk et al reported strong positive correlations between NLR and ESR (r=0.621, p<0.001) and CRP, while Živković et al found similar positive associations. PLR was also positively correlated with ESR and CRP.38 39

In our cohort, these literature findings are largely consistent with our observations: NLR and PLR were positively correlated with CRP and ESR, supporting their role as inflammatory markers.

In conclusion, while haematological ratios such as NLR, PLR, MLR, ELR and BLR have been proposed as potential markers for specific manifestations of SLE, including nephritis, haematologic involvement and vascular disease, our study did not reveal significant differences among these biomarkers across various types of disease flares.19 40 This lack of distinction may be attributed to several factors, including our small sample size, the diverse clinical presentations of the participants, individual variability in inflammatory responses and the mitigating effects of immunomodulatory treatments. Such limitations are also recognised in existing literature, which emphasises the necessity for longitudinal, multicentre studies to more accurately evaluate the dynamics of these biomarkers over time and their relevance to particular clinical phenotypes.41

To better interpret the statistical associations observed in this study, it is essential to consider the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms linking haematological ratios and CBC-derived parameters to SLE activity. These indicators reflect the balance between innate and adaptive immune responses and the degree of systemic inflammation.

An increased NLR may indicate enhanced neutrophil activation, oxidative stress and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps, processes directly involved in endothelial and tissue injury during lupus flares.42 Conversely, lymphopenia, a frequent feature of active SLE, often driven by inflammation-induced apoptosis or corticosteroid therapy, contributes to elevated NLR values and reflects disease severity.43

The PLR integrates both inflammatory and thrombotic components, as activated platelets release pro-inflammatory mediators (CD40L, PF4) and interact with immune cells, promoting vasculopathy and lupus nephritis.44 The MLR reflects monocyte activation and differentiation into macrophages within inflamed tissues, linking it to chronic inflammation and organ damage.45

Although less studied, ELR and BLR may capture subtle shifts in immune regulation and cytokine signalling, particularly involving IL-5, IL-4 and type I interferon pathways, which play a central role in SLE pathogenesis. Exploratory studies suggest that eosinophils and basophils might modulate autoantibody production and T-helper cell polarisation, thereby influencing disease manifestations.45

Growing attention has also been given to the RDW, which reflects the heterogeneity of erythrocyte size. Its elevation may result from chronic inflammation, oxidative stress or ineffective erythropoiesis driven by cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Several studies have linked increased RDW to higher disease activity and more severe renal or cardiovascular involvement, highlighting its potential as a complementary inflammatory marker in SLE.46

Integrating haematological ratios derived from the CBC into SLE management offers a simple, cost-effective and accessible tool for monitoring inflammatory activity, complementing conventional markers. Despite their promise, their Se and Sp vary, highlighting the importance of interpreting them within a broader clinical framework. Our study contributes novel insights within the Maghreb context, with strengths including a comprehensive methodological approach, rigorous control of confounding factors and a representative hospital-based cohort, enhancing clinical relevance and applicability.

However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The small, monocentric sample size may have limited statistical power and generalisability. The partially retrospective data collection carries risks of bias and missing information, and the lack of longitudinal follow-up prevents assessment of dynamic changes in ratios over time. Furthermore, using two analysers with different methodologies may have introduced variability, while corticosteroid-induced lymphopenia could bias the calculated ratios. Finally, the absence of standardised definitions of disease flare complicates comparison across studies.

Future research should prioritise multicentre, longitudinal and integrative approaches, incorporating biological, immunological and genetic markers to validate these ratios as predictive tools, assess long-term prognosis and improve care for specific populations, including paediatric or atypical SLE patients.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the value of haematological biomarkers from CBC in assessing SLE activity. NLR, RDW and ELR were the most effective in distinguishing flare from remission. These markers are simple, inexpensive and widely available. PLR, MLR and BLR also correlated with disease activity, supporting the usefulness of this approach. Such biomarkers may complement conventional monitoring, particularly in resource-limited settings. However, their clinical use requires comprehensive evaluation, and their prognostic value should be confirmed in multicentre longitudinal studies including broader immunoinflammatory markers.
