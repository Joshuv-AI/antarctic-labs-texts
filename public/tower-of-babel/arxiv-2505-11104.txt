Multilevel Optimization: Geometric Coarse Models and Convergence Analysis ‡
Ferdinand Vanmaele † , Yara Elshiaty *, † , and Stefania Petra †

Abstract
. We study multilevel techniques, commonly used in PDE multigrid literature, to solve structured optimization problems. For a given hierarchy of levels, we formulate a coarse model that approximates the problem at each level and provides a descent direction for the fine-grid objective using fewer variables. Unlike common algebraic approaches, we assume the objective function and its gradient can be evaluated at each level. Under the assumptions of strong convexity and gradient L L -smoothness, we analyze convergence and extend the method to box-constrained optimization. Large-scale numerical experiments on a discrete tomography problem show that the multilevel approach converges rapidly when far from the solution and performs competitively with state-of-the-art methods.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider optimization problems of the form

(1.1)

\min_{y\in C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y),\qquad f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}^{2,1}(C),

where
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}^{2,1}(C)
represents the class of twice-differentiable,
\mu
-strongly convex functions with
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradients over a closed simple convex set
C
, such as a box. We assume that function
f
possesses an inherent “geometry”, meaning that it can be discretized at
multiple levels of resolution, forming a hierarchical structure.

We employ multilevel optimization, as introduced in
[
11
]
, to exploit this hierarchy. The core idea is to reformulate the problem on a coarse grid, referred to as the
coarse model
, where computations involve significantly fewer variables. During optimization on the fine grid, the search direction is determined using the coarse model. This approach not only reduces computational costs but also benefits from improved conditioning of the coarse-grid problems.

The classical model by Nash
[
11
]
constructs the coarse representation by linearly modifying the fine objective, evaluated on the coarse grid. The linear term incorporates
gradients
from both the coarse and fine objectives, using appropriate mappings between grid levels. Alternatively,
[
7
]
proposes a quadratic coarse model to approximate the fine objective by transferring its
Hessian
to the coarse grid via grid transfer operators.

Under the smoothness and convexity assumptions specified in (
1.1
),
[
7
]
demonstrates a composite convergence rate for their quadratic coarse model by combining linear and quadratic rates. In this work, we establish the relationship between the two coarse models, showing that both use first- and second-order information from the fine objective, which enables us to derive a similar convergence rate. Crucially, the Nash model
[
11
]
avoids the explicit computation of the fine objective’s Hessian on either the fine or coarse grid. This makes it particularly advantageous for large-scale problems, such as those encountered in imaging applications.

Related work.

Seminal contributions to unconstrained smooth multilevel optimization include
[
11
,
5
,
15
]
. These foundational ideas were later extended to address box-constrained and simplex-constrained optimization in
[
10
,
4
,
6
]
and further developed for more general nonsmooth convex optimization in
[
13
,
8
,
1
]
. Convergence results for the unconstrained case, under assumption (
1.1
), were established in
[
7
]
for a quadratic coarse model solely dependent on the objective
f=f_{0}
. In this work, we extend these results to the general coarse model proposed in
[
11
]
.

Two level optimization.

Coarse model.

The core idea is to define the
coarse grid model
, introduced in
[
11
]
,
analogous to a standard local approximation. It is defined at each step
k
based on the current iterate as

y_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in C\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}}\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle:=g(x)+\left\langle v_{k},x-Ry_{k}\right\rangle,\quad v_{k}:=R\nabla f(y_{k})-\nabla g(Ry_{k}),

where
\psi_{k}
is a linear modification of the coarse objective
g
. The linear term
v_{k}

represents the difference between the restricted gradient
\nabla f
evaluated at
y_{k}

and the gradient of the coarse objective
\nabla g
evaluated at
Ry_{k}
. Consequently, the model
incorporates both algebraic and geometric gradient information.

For the
initial
coarse-grid iterate
x_{k}:=Ry_{k}
, the gradient of the coarse model
satisfies

(2.2)

\nabla\psi_{k}(x_{k})=R\nabla f(y_{k}).

This property, known as the
first-order coherence condition
, ensures that a critical point
on the fine grid remains critical when transferred to the coarse grid.

The coarse model is designed to efficiently compute a descent direction using fewer coarse-grid variables.
The fine-grid update is then performed as

(2.3)

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=y_{k}+\alpha_{k}d_{k},\quad\alpha_{k}>0,\qquad d_{k}=P(x_{k}^{+}-Ry_{k}),

where
x_{k}^{+}
is an
approximate
solution to the coarse model (
2.1
)
satisfying
\psi_{k}(x_{k}^{+})<\psi_{k}(x_{k})
.
The step size
\alpha_{k}>0
is determined via a suitable
line search
strategy.

Relation to second order models.

For
x_{k}:=Ry_{k}
and
R=P^{\top}
, the coarse model (
2.1
) can be rewritten as

(2.4)

\psi_{k}(x)=g(x_{k})+\left\langle\nabla f(y_{k}),P(x-x_{k})\right\rangle+D_{g}(x,x_{k})

where the Bregman divergence is

\displaystyle D_{g}(x,x_{g})

\displaystyle=g(x)-g(x_{k})-\left\langle\nabla g(x_{k}),x-x_{k}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle x-x_{k},\nabla^{2}g(\tilde{x})(x-x_{k})\right\rangle

with
\tilde{x}=(1-t)x+tx_{k}
for some
{t\in[0,1]}
.
Thus, the coarse model resembles the quadratic approximation used in
single-level
optimization

(2.5)

q_{k}(y)=\left\langle\nabla f(y_{k}),y-y_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle y-y_{k},H_{k}(y-y_{k})\right\rangle,

where
H_{k}\succ 0
approximates
\nabla^{2}f(y_{k})
.
In contrast, the coarse model
\psi_{k}
incorporates first order information
of the fine objective
f
, and second order information of the coarse
objective
g
.

Alternative coarse model.

The authors in
[
7
]
define the coarse model as

(2.6)

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\langle R\nabla f(y_{k}),x-x_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle x-x_{k},\underbrace{R\nabla^{2}f(y_{k})P}_{:=Q_{k}}(x-x_{k})\rangle.

The key difference between (
2.4
) and (
2.6
) is that the latter
explicitly computes the fine-grid Hessian
\nabla^{2}f
at each iteration, which makes it impractical for
very large problems. However, the update in (
2.3
),
y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\alpha_{k}{d}_{k}

can be computed using a Newton-like direction
{d}_{k}=-PQ_{k}^{-1}R\nabla f(y_{k})
, which corresponds to
solving (
2.6
). This enables the authors to establish (sub)linear convergence and a
finite number of coarse correction steps. Similar results for the coarse model (
2.1
)
are presented in Section
3
.

Coarse correction descent.
In view of first-order coherence (
2.2
) of the coarse model
\psi_{k}
, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
g
is a convex function, then the update in (
2.3
) defines a descent direction for
f
at
y_{k}
.
Moreover, if in the
k
-th iteration, the search direction is given by
d_{k}=P(x_{\ast}-x_{k})
, where
x_{\ast}
is a solution of the coarse model (
2.4
), then

\left\langle\nabla f(y_{k}),d_{k}\right\rangle\leq\psi_{k}(x_{\ast})-\psi_{k}(Ry_{k})\leq 0.

Proof.

By (
2.4
) and

\psi_{k}(x_{k})=g(x_{k})
we have for arbitrary
x

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\psi_{k}(x_{k})+\left\langle\nabla f(y_{k}),P(x-x_{k})\right\rangle+\underbrace{D_{g}(x,x_{k})}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\geq\psi_{k}(x_{k})+\left\langle\nabla f(y_{k}),P(x-x_{k})\right\rangle,

where the last inequality follows by convexity of
g
.
∎

Coarse correction condition.

We adapt the criteria from
[
15
]
for
unconstrained
optimization

\displaystyle\|P^{\top}\nabla f(y)\|_{\ast}

\displaystyle\geq\kappa\|\nabla f(y)\|_{\ast}\quad\text{and}\quad\|P^{\top}\nabla f(y)\|_{\ast}>\epsilon,

where
\kappa\in(0,\min(1,\|P\|_{\ast}))
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.
These conditions prevent reliance on the coarse model when the direction
(x_{k}^{+}-Ry_{k})

is nearly zero.

Box constrained coarse model.

Given
x_{k}:=Ry_{k}
, the coarse model (
2.1
) can be extended to the box-constrained
problem
\min_{y\in[l,u]}f(y)
as follows

(2.7)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\psi_{k}(x)\qquad\text{subject to}\quad l_{k,P}\leq x\leq u_{k,P},

where the bounds are redefined as in
[
4
]

(2.8)

\displaystyle\left(l_{k,P}\right)_{j}

\displaystyle=x_{k,j}+\frac{1}{\|P\|_{\infty}}\max_{i=1,\dots n}\begin{cases}(l-y_{k})_{i},&P_{ij}>0,\\
(y_{k}-u)_{i},&P_{ij}<0,\end{cases}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\left(u_{k,P}\right)_{j}

\displaystyle=x_{k,j}+\frac{1}{\|P\|_{\infty}}\min_{i=1,\dots,n}\begin{cases}(u-y_{k})_{i},&P_{ij}>0,\\
(y_{k}-l)_{i},&P_{ij}<0,\end{cases}

and accommodates negative elements in
P
, as in cubic interpolation. The next result ensures that
feasibility is preserved under prolongation.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
4
, Lemma 4.3]
)
.

Let
l\leq y_{k}\leq u
and
x_{k}=Ry_{k}
. Let
P:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

l_{P,k}
and
u_{P,k}
from (
2.8
), (
2.9
). Then

\displaystyle l\leq y_{k}+P(x_{k}-x)\leq u,\qquad\forall x\in[l_{P,k},u_{P,k}].

To define a suitable coarse correction condition, we follow
[
4
, Note 2]
and set

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\|\Pi_{f}[y_{k}-\nabla f(y_{k})]-y_{k}\right\|_{\ast},\qquad b_{k}:=\left\|\Pi_{\psi_{k}}[x_{k}-\nabla\psi(x_{k})]-x_{k}\right\|_{\ast},

where
\Pi_{f}
and
\Pi_{\psi_{k}}
are Euclidean projection on the
constraint sets of
f
and
\psi_{k}
(
2.8
,
2.9
), respectively.
A coarse correction step is taken if

b_{k}\geq\kappa a_{k}

for some constant
\kappa\in(0,1)
.

The step size
\alpha_{k}

in the update (
2.3
) can be adapted to constraints using a projected line search, such as the Armijo rule along the projection arc
[
2
, pp. 227–230]
. However, by Lemma
2.2
, we can directly set

\alpha_{k}=1
.

3.
Rate of Convergence

In this section, we show that Algorithm
1
achieves a linear convergence rate and requires only finitely many coarse correction steps, under the assumption of gradient Lipschitz continuity and strong convexity of the objectives.

Sufficient decrease.

A key requirement is that the step size generated by the
Armijo condition
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle f(y_{k}+\alpha_{k}d_{k})

\displaystyle\leq f(y_{k})+\rho_{1}\alpha_{k}\left[\nabla f(y_{k})\right]^{\top}d_{k},\quad\rho_{1}\in(0,0.5),

must be bounded from below.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
15
, Theorem 3.3]
)
.

Suppose
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
M_{f}
. Let
\rho_{1}\in(0,0.5)
and
d_{k}
a descent direction at
y_{k}
. Then step sizes

\displaystyle\alpha\in[0,\hat{\alpha}],\quad\hat{\alpha}=\frac{2(\rho_{1}-1)d_{k}^{\top}\nabla f(y_{k})}{M_{f}\|d_{k}\|^{2}}.

satisfy (
3.1
). Furthermore, backtracking line search terminates with

\displaystyle\min\left(\alpha_{p},\frac{2\beta(\rho_{1}-1)d_{k}^{\top}\nabla f(y_{k})}{M_{f}\|d_{k}\|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{k}\leq\alpha_{\rho}

where
\beta
is the step size reduction parameter and
\alpha_{\rho}

the initial step size.

Coarse correction step.

Next, we provide a lower bound for the decrease in the function value at the fine grid after a coarse correction step (Algorithm
1
, Line
1
), under the following two assumptions.

Assumption 3.2
.

Let
y_{0}
denote the initial iterate. On the level sets
\mathcal{L}_{f}(y_{0})
and
\mathcal{L}_{g}({Ry_{0}})
, the fine and coarse objectives have Lipschitz continuous gradient with constants
M_{f}
and
M_{g}
, respectively, and are strongly convex with constants

m_{f}
and
m_{g}
.

Assumption 3.3
.

The coarse model (
2.1
) is solved exactly. In Algorithm
1
,
replace Line
1
with “Find
x_{k}^{\ast}=\argmin{\psi_{k}}
.”

Lemma 3.4

(Sufficient decrease by coarse correction)
.

Suppose Assumption
3.2
and
3.3
hold.
The coarse correction step
{d}_{k}
in Algorithm
1
then
leads to a reduction in function value

\displaystyle f(y_{k})-f(y_{k}+\alpha_{k}{d}_{k})

\displaystyle\geq\rho_{1}\beta\frac{m_{g}^{3}\kappa^{2}}{M_{f}M_{g}^{2}\omega^{2}}\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|_{\ast}^{2},

where
\rho_{1},\kappa
and
\beta
are defined
in Algorithm
1
and

\omega:=\max\left\{\|R\|,\|P\|\right\}
.

Remark 1
.

Compared to
[
7
, Lemma 3.1]
, we observe cubic terms
m_{g}^{3}
and quadratic terms
M_{g}^{2}
in the reduction lower-bound for a coarse correction step.
However, these constants pertain to the
coarse
model and may differ significantly – by several orders of magnitude – from those of the
fine
model.

Remark 2
.

The parameter
\kappa\in(0,1)
dictates when the algorithm
performs a coarse correction step. Choosing
\kappa
too small is
not desirable in terms of worst-case complexity. If
\kappa
is chosen
too large, coarse correction steps are less likely.

Linear rate of convergence.

Lemma
3.4
on the coarse correction step, together with the following assumption from
[
7
]
on the fine correction step (Line
1
in Algorithm
1
), will ensure sufficient decrease and a linear rate of convergence.

Assumption 3.5
.

For fine correction steps there exists a constant
\Lambda_{f}>0
such that

(3.2)

f(y_{k})-f(y_{k+1})\geq\Lambda_{f}\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|_{\ast}^{2}.

This holds for gradient descent, (inexact) Newton, and L-BFGS
[
15
]
.

Theorem 3.6

(Sufficient decrease for two level optimization)
.

Suppose Assumption
3.2
,
3.3
and
3.5
hold.
The step
d_{k}
in Algorithm
1
leads to a decrease in function value

\displaystyle f(y_{k})-f(y_{k+1})\geq

\displaystyle\min\left\{\Lambda_{f},\rho_{1}\beta\frac{m_{g}^{3}\kappa^{2}}{M_{f}M_{g}^{2}\omega^{2}}\right\}\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|_{\ast}^{2},

where the constant
\Lambda_{f}
depends on the fine correction step chosen.

Now the linear rate of convergence follows immediately.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by
Algorithm
1
and
y_{\ast}=\argmin f
. Assume
M_{g}^{2}\geq m_{g}^{3}
which holds for e.g.
M_{g}>1
,
m_{g}<1
. Then,

\displaystyle f(y_{k})-f(y_{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq(1-2m_{f}\Lambda)^{k}[f(y_{0})-f(y_{\ast})].

We denote the constant in Theorem
3.6
by
\Lambda
. As shown below, Algorithm
1
takes a finite number of coarse correction steps when
f
is coercive. We then have
\Lambda_{f}\equiv\Lambda
for
k
sufficiently large.

Number of coarse correction steps.
We show that Algorithm
1
only performs a finite number of coarse correction steps, depending on the distance of
y_{0}
to the exact solution
y_{*}
. We define the set diameter

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(y_{0})=\max_{y\in\mathcal{L}_{f}(y_{0})}{\|y-y_{\ast}\|}

and establish a sublinear convergence rate dependent on it.

Theorem 3.8

(
[
7
, Theorem 3.5]
Sublinear convergence rate)
.

Let
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by
Algorithm
1
. Then

\displaystyle f(y_{k})-f(y_{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}^{2}(y_{0})}{\Lambda}\frac{1}{2+k}.

Using this result and a criterion for fine correction steps, we determine an upper bound on the number of coarse correction steps. Specifically, if
f
is coercive and
\mathcal{R}(y_{0})

is bounded, Algorithm
1
performs only finitely many coarse corrections. The convergence rate then matches that of the fine correction method for sufficiently large

k
. However, numerical experiments show that incorporating coarse corrections significantly improves performance compared to using fine corrections alone; see Section
4
for details.

Lemma 3.9

(
[
7
, Lemma 3.6]
)
.

No coarse correction step will be taken by Algorithm
1

when
\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\omega}
, where
\omega=\max\left\{\|P\|,\|R\|\right\}

and
\epsilon>0
is user-defined.

Lemma 3.10

(
[
7
, Lemma 3.7]
)
.

No coarse correction step will be taken after

\left(\frac{\omega}{\epsilon}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathcal{R}^{2}(y_{0})}{\Lambda^{2}}-2\qquad\text{iterations.}

Remark 3
.

The smaller the choice of
\epsilon>0
, the more coarse correction
steps will be taken. Depending on the descent method for the fine correction steps, the choice of
\epsilon
could be different.
For example, a good choice for Newton’s method with
\|\cdot\|_{2}
and
d_{k}=-\left[\nabla^{2}f(y_{k})\right]^{-1}\nabla f(y_{k})

is
\epsilon=3\omega(1-2\rho_{1})m_{f}^{2}/M_{f}

[
7
, p.15]
.

Remark 4
.

In Section
2
, we discussed the box-constrained coarse model (
2.8
,
2.9
), which depends on the current fine-level iterate
y_{k}
. For functions where the Hessian’s condition number varies across the domain (e.g., that involve the Bregman divergence), this poses challenges for establishing convergence rates unless the coarse constraints can be uniformly bounded.

An alternative approach is to approximate the active set of constraints using projected gradient descent and apply the unconstrained multilevel method to the inactive set. The step size
\alpha_{k}

should be adjusted to ensure feasibility of the iterates. If the active set changes, gradient projection iterations can be used to update it as needed
[
2
]
. Exploring such extensions is left for future work.

4.
Experiments

We compare Algorithm
1
to basic (projected) gradient descent and the state-of-the-art quasi-Newton method L-BFGS
[
3
,
16
]
, which handles box constraints. The comparison includes the proposed coarse models (
2.1
) and (
2.7
), as well as the algebraic coarse model (
2.6
). Tests are conducted on both
unconstrained
and
box-constrained
variational models for discrete tomography. Whereas the relevant objectives are not strongly convex, Algorithm
1
exhibits the same behavior as predicted for strongly convex functions in Section
3
.

Data setup.
We reconstruct a large scale image
y
(
n=1024\times 1024
) subsampled using a tomographic projection matrix

A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
generated with ASTRA-toolbox.
Parallel beam projections are taken at equidistant angles between

0
and
\pi
, with a 10% undersampling rate at the fine grid.

Unconstrained optimization.

We first consider the unconstrained problem

(4.1)

\displaystyle\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y):=\frac{1}{2}\|Ay-b\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda L_{\rho}(Dy),\quad\lambda=0.1,\quad\rho=0.01,

where
L_{\rho}
is the Pseudo-Huber function defined as
L_{\rho}(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sqrt{\rho^{2}+a_{i}^{2}}-\rho)
, and
D
is the discretized image gradient using forward differences.
The objective
f
has a Lipschitz continuous gradient but lacks strong convexity without addition of a
\mu\|\cdot\|_{2}
regularizer (which offered no improvement in our experiments.)

Lipschitz constants.

We analyze the grid-dependent smoothness of the geometric coarse model (
2.1
) compared to the algebraic coarse model (
2.6
). Ignoring the linear term in (
2.1
), the Lipschitz constant of
\psi_{k}
equals that of the objective
f
(
4.1
) discretized on the respective grid. Similarly, ignoring the linear terms in
\varphi_{k}

We consider the bound constrained problem

5.
Conclusion

In this work, we established precise convergence results for an unconstrained multilevel
method using Nash’s classical geometric coarse model
[
11
]
. The derived
convergence rate applies without explicitly incorporating the Hessian of the fine-grid
objective as done in
[
7
]
, which our approach avoids. We extended
the method to box-constrained problems and applied it to a discrete tomography problem.
Despite severe undersampling, the method converges rapidly when far from the solution,
outperforming state-of-the-art techniques. However, near the solution, methods with
superlinear convergence, such as L-BFGS, prove more effective.
