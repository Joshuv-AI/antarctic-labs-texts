Early postoperative functional exercise adherence and its association with 3-month hip functional recovery among older adults after femoral neck fracture surgery: a prospective observational study
Authors: Binzhou Wu, Qiyu Chen, Wenjun Pan, Endian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults recovering from femoral neck fracture surgery transition rapidly from supervised inpatient mobilisation to increasingly self-managed exercise. This study examined before-discharge early postoperative functional exercise adherence, factors associated with adherence, and its association with 3-month hip functional recovery.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted among 230 adults aged 60 years or older who underwent surgery for femoral neck fracture. Functional exercise adherence, kinesiophobia, rehabilitation self-efficacy, social support and pain intensity were assessed before discharge. Hip functional recovery was evaluated using the Harris Hip Score (HHS) at 3 months. Descriptive statistics, group comparisons, Spearman correlation analysis and multivariable linear regression were used.ResultsThe mean age was 71.5 ± 4.1 years, and 52.6% of participants were women. The mean before-discharge functional exercise adherence score was 49.9 ± 6.9, indicating a moderate level. Adherence was negatively correlated with kinesiophobia (r = -0.753, p < 0.001) and pain intensity (r = -0.399, p < 0.001), and positively correlated with rehabilitation self-efficacy (r = 0.754, p < 0.001), social support (r = 0.893, p < 0.001) and 3-month HHS (r = 0.838, p < 0.001). In adjusted analyses, kinesiophobia, rehabilitation self-efficacy, social support, pain intensity, osteoporosis status, documented rehabilitation guidance and a documented post-discharge rehabilitation plan were associated with adherence. Adherence was positively associated with 3-month HHS (B = 0.842, p < 0.001).ConclusionsBefore-discharge early postoperative functional exercise adherence was moderate and was associated with psychological, social and continuity-of-care factors. Higher adherence was associated with better 3-month hip function, but the observational design does not establish causality. Multidisciplinary rehabilitation support, with nurses contributing assessment, symptom management, education and care coordination, should prioritise modifiable barriers and continuity across discharge.

Background

Femoral neck fracture is a major source of disability and healthcare use among older adults. The global incidence and prevalence of hip fracture increased substantially between 1990 and 2021 [1], and hip fracture remains associated with considerable mortality and healthcare utilisation [2]. Functional recovery after surgery varies across mobility, self-care and activities of daily living [3, 4] and is influenced by pre-fracture function, comorbidities, cognitive status, postoperative complications and rehabilitation exposure [5, 6]. Thus, successful surgery and survival do not necessarily indicate satisfactory functional recovery.

Clinical practice guidelines recommend early mobilisation, progressive strengthening, balance training, walking practice and function-oriented rehabilitation after hip fracture [7]. Rehabilitation guidelines for older patients after hip fracture further emphasise coordinated multidisciplinary management and continued exercise-based rehabilitation beyond the acute hospital phase [8]. Randomised trials and systematic reviews suggest that home-based and digitally supported rehabilitation may be associated with better adherence and selected functional outcomes after hip fracture surgery [9–11]. However, the potential value of these programmes depends on whether patients actually perform the prescribed exercises with sufficient regularity, intensity and duration.

Existing studies indicate that adherence to rehabilitation after hip fracture is partial, dynamic and influenced by patient, programme and contextual factors [12–15]. However, early functional exercise adherence before discharge has received less focused attention in patients with femoral neck fracture. In addition, psychological barriers, behavioural confidence, social resources and continuity-of-care factors have rarely been examined together, and the association between early adherence and a clinically interpretable short-term outcome such as the HHS remains insufficiently clarified.

Potentially modifiable mechanisms include pain-related fear, self-efficacy and social support. The fear-avoidance model proposes that pain-related fear can promote movement avoidance and disability [16], while kinesiophobia can be assessed using the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia [17, 18]. Rehabilitation self-efficacy reflects confidence in performing rehabilitation tasks [19] and may contribute to functional change after hip fracture [20], consistent with self-efficacy theory [21]. Social support may provide reassurance, supervision and access to rehabilitation resources and has been associated with rehabilitation outcomes after hip fracture surgery [22].

This question is directly relevant to nursing because nurses repeatedly assess pain, movement-related fear and readiness for activity; reinforce safe mobilisation; observe exercise participation; educate patients and caregivers; and coordinate the transition from ward-based care to home recovery within a multidisciplinary team. The present study therefore used a theory-informed prospective observational design to: (1) describe before-discharge early postoperative functional exercise adherence; (2) identify factors associated with adherence; and (3) examine the association between adherence and 3-month hip functional recovery.

Methods

Study design and reporting guideline

A prospective observational cohort study was conducted to examine before-discharge functional exercise adherence and its association with 3-month hip functional recovery. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [23]; the completed checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1. Ethical approval was obtained before participant recruitment.

Conceptual framework

The conceptual framework was an analytical framework used to guide variable selection rather than a previously validated causal model. It integrated the fear-avoidance model, self-efficacy theory and social support theory. Kinesiophobia and pain intensity were considered potential barriers to adherence, whereas rehabilitation self-efficacy, social support, documented rehabilitation guidance and a post-discharge rehabilitation plan were considered potential facilitating factors. Adherence was then examined in relation to 3-month HHS. Because this was an observational study, the framework did not assume causal or mediating pathways.

Setting and participants

Participants were recruited consecutively from the Department of Orthopaedics of the Second Affiliated Hospital & Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, China, between January and December 2025. All participants underwent surgical treatment for femoral neck fracture and received routine postoperative care according to the department’s clinical pathway. The research team did not assign or standardise rehabilitation exposure for study purposes.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) femoral neck fracture confirmed by clinical and imaging examinations; (2) surgical treatment with internal fixation, hemiarthroplasty or total hip arthroplasty; (3) age ≥ 60 years; (4) stable postoperative condition and ability to communicate and complete questionnaires independently or with assistance; and (5) informed consent and agreement to follow-up. The age threshold was prespecified because Chinese legislation and health policy commonly define older persons as citizens aged 60 years or older. Exclusion criteria were: (1) a documented severe cognitive or psychiatric disorder, or inability after standardised explanation and assistance to understand study instructions or provide reliable questionnaire responses; (2) pathological or malignant tumour-related fracture or multiple trauma; (3) severe pre-existing hip dysfunction, hemiplegia, paraplegia or long-term bedridden status; and (4) a postoperative complication documented by the treating team that made routine mobilisation or functional exercise medically unsafe or impossible during the index admission. No formal cognitive screening instrument was administered.

Routine rehabilitation programme and standard postoperative care

This prospective observational study evaluated rehabilitation delivered as routine clinical care and did not impose a study-specific intervention. Weight-bearing restrictions and medical precautions were determined by the treating orthopaedic team. Rehabilitation professionals, when involved, assessed mobility and advised progression, while ward nurses reinforced prescribed activities, monitored symptoms and safety, and provided patient and caregiver education. Core activities commonly included ankle-pump exercises, quadriceps and gluteal isometric contraction, bed mobility, transfer practice, progressive standing and walking with an assistive device, and range-of-motion exercises within postoperative precautions. The exact frequency, duration and progression were individualised according to surgical procedure, weight-bearing status, pain, medical stability and patient tolerance rather than fixed by the research protocol.

Routine postoperative care also included pain assessment and analgesic management, wound and neurovascular observation, venous thromboembolism prevention, fall prevention, nutritional support, complication surveillance and discharge education. A post-discharge rehabilitation plan, when documented, included verbal or written exercise advice, weight-bearing and safety precautions, caregiver instructions and follow-up arrangements. Receipt of inpatient rehabilitation guidance and a post-discharge plan was recorded from the medical and nursing records as a real-world care-exposure variable. Because delivery was not mandated by the study, some participants had no documented guidance or plan; the specific reasons were not collected systematically.

Study size

Study size was estimated using Cohen’s effect-size framework for multiple linear regression [24]: N = [(Z1-α/2 + Z1-β)² / f²] + k + 1, where k is the number of candidate predictors. With a two-sided α of 0.05, 80% power, a conservative small effect size (f² = 0.05) and approximately 10 candidate predictors, the minimum sample was 168. Allowing for 15% loss to follow-up or incomplete data yielded an adjusted minimum of 198 participants.

Measures

Functional exercise adherence was assessed before discharge using the Functional Exercise Adherence Scale for Orthopaedic Patients, which contains 15 items across physical exercise adherence, psychological exercise adherence and active learning adherence. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, producing a total score of 15–75; higher scores indicate better adherence [25]. The total score was analysed as a continuous variable. Internal consistency in the present sample was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.936).

Kinesiophobia was assessed using the 17-item Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK), with items rated on a 4-point scale and higher scores indicating greater fear of movement or reinjury [17, 18]. Rehabilitation self-efficacy was assessed using the 12-item Self-Efficacy for Rehabilitation Outcome Scale (SER) [19]. Internal consistency was acceptable for the TSK (Cronbach’s α = 0.740) and excellent for the SER (Cronbach’s α = 0.940). Both scores were analysed as continuous variables.

Social support was measured using the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), which assesses objective support, subjective support and support utilisation [26]; internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.920). Postoperative pain intensity was assessed using the 0–10 Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) [27]. The HHS was used to assess hip functional recovery at 3 months. It combines pain (44 points), function (47 points), absence of deformity (4 points) and range of motion (5 points), producing a total score from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better hip function [28]. All scale scores were retained as continuous variables; categorical cut-offs were not used in the revised analyses because universally accepted low, moderate and high thresholds were not available for all measures.

Data collection

Baseline demographic data and postoperative clinical information were collected by trained nurse researchers with experience caring for patients with hip fracture, using patient interviews and medical-record review. Before discharge, the same research team administered the adherence scale, TSK, SER, SSRS and NRS. For participants with reading difficulty, items were read aloud using standardised wording and responses were recorded without suggestive explanation.

At 3 months, the prespecified follow-up outcome was hip function measured with the HHS. Assessment was preferentially completed during outpatient review by a trained evaluator experienced in postoperative hip assessment. When an in-person visit was not feasible, video-based assessment was used for observable functional and range-of-motion items; telephone contact was used primarily for patient-reported items and to arrange video or outpatient assessment. Assessors were not formally blinded to participants’ earlier rehabilitation status. The psychosocial measures and adherence scale were not repeated because the study was designed to relate before-discharge factors to a later functional outcome rather than to model post-discharge trajectories.

Quality control

All research personnel received standardised training in eligibility screening, questionnaire administration, neutral interviewing, scale scoring, data abstraction and HHS assessment. Additional consistency training was used for the HHS because it includes clinician-rated functional and range-of-motion components and was administered through more than one assessment mode. Completed questionnaires were checked immediately, data were entered independently by two researchers and discrepancies were verified against source documents.

Participant flow and missing data

A complete-case approach was used for the primary analyses. Participants who declined participation, withdrew, could not complete the 3-month follow-up, or lacked the baseline adherence score or 3-month HHS were not included in the final analytical sample; missing primary outcomes were not imputed. Numbers and reasons at each stage are reported in Fig. 1. A formal baseline comparison between analysed and non-analysed participants was not possible because complete baseline questionnaire data were unavailable for many individuals who were not included.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital & Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University before data collection (approval number: 2024-K-251-02). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives. Data were anonymised using study identification numbers and stored securely for research purposes only.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0. Categorical variables were summarised as n (%) and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. Descriptive continuous results are presented to one decimal place to improve clinical readability, while inferential estimates retain appropriate statistical precision. Differences in adherence across participant groups were examined using independent-samples t tests or one-way analysis of variance. Spearman correlation analysis examined associations among adherence, TSK, SER, SSRS, NRS and HHS scores. Multivariable linear regression identified factors associated with adherence and examined the adjusted association between adherence and 3-month HHS. Candidate covariates were selected a priori from the analytical framework and clinical relevance. Regression coefficients, standard errors, standardised beta values, 95% confidence intervals and p values were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs); values > 5 were interpreted as possible moderate multicollinearity and values < 10 as not indicating severe multicollinearity. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Use of artificial intelligence tools in manuscript preparation

During manuscript preparation, the authors used ChatGPT (OpenAI) for language polishing, formatting and reference organisation. The authors reviewed and edited all generated content and take full responsibility for the accuracy and integrity of the manuscript.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 352 older adults with femoral neck fracture were assessed for eligibility. Fifty-three were excluded at initial screening: 35 were younger than 60 years, 10 were managed conservatively without surgery and 8 had pathological or tumour-related fractures. Of the 299 who passed initial screening, 69 were not included in the final analysis because of psychiatric disorder, severe cognitive impairment or communication difficulty (n = 8), multiple trauma or combined injuries (n = 6), severe pre-existing functional impairment (n = 12), severe postoperative complications preventing routine exercise (n = 9), declined participation, withdrawal or incomplete follow-up (n = 15), or unrecoverable missing baseline or 3-month HHS data (n = 19). The final complete-case sample comprised 230 participants, exceeding the estimated minimum sample size (Fig. 1). Their mean age was 71.5 ± 4.1 years; 109 (47.4%) were men and 121 (52.6%) were women (Table 1).

The multivariable model for adherence was statistically significant (F = 313.64, p < 0.001; adjusted R² = 0.932). TSK, SER, SSRS and NRS scores, osteoporosis status, documented rehabilitation guidance and a documented post-discharge rehabilitation plan were associated with adherence (Table 5). These coefficients represent adjusted associations and should not be interpreted as causal effects.

Discussion

Main findings

This prospective observational study found moderate before-discharge functional exercise adherence and a mean 3-month HHS of 69.2, indicating that short-term recovery remained incomplete at the group level. kinesiophobia, rehabilitation self-efficacy, social support, pain intensity, osteoporosis status, documented rehabilitation guidance and a post-discharge rehabilitation plan were associated with adherence. Adherence was also positively associated with 3-month HHS. These findings identify clinically relevant correlates and potential risk markers, but they do not demonstrate that changing adherence or any associated factor will necessarily cause better functional recovery.

Functional exercise adherence after femoral neck fracture surgery

The moderate mean adherence score should not be interpreted as clinically unimportant simply because some participants achieved acceptable short-term function. The mean HHS was close to the conventional boundary between poor and fair recovery, and an average score can mask substantial individual variation. Early adherence may therefore be useful as a behavioural signal of who requires additional assessment and support. Nevertheless, adherence was measured only before discharge, and the study cannot determine whether it persisted during the subsequent 3 months.

Early mobilisation and weight-bearing are associated with favourable in-hospital outcomes after hip fracture [29], and participation in exercise programmes can be influenced by symptoms, physical capacity and patient engagement [30]. The present results therefore support assessing barriers before discharge rather than assuming that standard information alone will be sufficient.

Kinesiophobia and pain as barriers to adherence

Kinesiophobia was one of the strongest negative factors associated with adherence. Patients with higher TSK scores had lower adherence scores, and adherence decreased stepwise from low to high kinesiophobia categories. A structured scoping review of psychological factors after hip fracture suggests that psychological constructs may influence recovery beyond the effect of surgery and physical rehabilitation alone [31].

Pain intensity was also negatively associated with adherence. Although postoperative pain is expected after hip fracture surgery, insufficiently controlled pain may reinforce fear of movement and reduce willingness to participate in functional exercise. Nurses should therefore assess pain together with exercise readiness and provide anticipatory analgesic planning before mobilisation.

Rehabilitation self-efficacy and social support as facilitating factors

Rehabilitation self-efficacy was positively associated with adherence. Patients with stronger confidence in completing rehabilitation tasks may be more willing to attempt progressive exercises, communicate difficulties and persist with home-based training. Social support also showed a strong positive association with adherence. Expert and family-based support for exercise after hip fracture has been shown to provide encouragement, modelling and reinforcement [32].

The strong correlation between SSRS and adherence suggests that support may work through several mechanisms, including emotional reassurance, supervision of exercise performance, fall-prevention assistance and access to rehabilitation resources. In the HHS regression model, social support was no longer independently associated with HHS after adherence was included. This may suggest that adherence partly captures the behavioural pathway through which support contributes to functional recovery; however, this interpretation remains exploratory.

Importance of rehabilitation guidance and post-discharge planning

Documented inpatient rehabilitation guidance and a documented post-discharge plan were associated with higher adherence. Transitional care programmes aim to bridge hospital and home rehabilitation, although effects vary with intensity, follow-up and patient characteristics [33]. In the present observational study, these variables reflected real-world care rather than randomly assigned interventions. The findings support consistent documentation and multidisciplinary transition planning, but they do not establish that the observed differences were caused by the guidance itself.

Multicomponent home-based rehabilitation and supervised exercise may support selected functional outcomes after hip fracture [34, 35]. Within such pathways, physicians and rehabilitation professionals determine medical precautions and specialised rehabilitation progression, while nurses reinforce safe activities, assess pain and fear, educate patients and caregivers, and coordinate follow-up. Rehabilitation planning should therefore extend beyond discharge through clearly assigned multidisciplinary responsibilities.

The positive association between osteoporosis and adherence was unexpected and requires cautious interpretation. It may reflect greater previous contact with healthcare services, greater awareness of fracture risk or differential exposure to education, rather than a direct effect of osteoporosis on behaviour. The unequal subgroup sizes and absence of detailed data on previous osteoporosis management also leave residual confounding possible. This finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires replication.

Functional exercise adherence and hip functional recovery

Functional exercise adherence was positively associated with 3-month HHS. This temporal association is clinically plausible because strengthening, transfer practice, walking and daily-activity training are core components of recovery, and home-based rehabilitation may reduce physical disability after hip fracture [36]. Tele-rehabilitation may also extend supervision after discharge [37]. However, the observational design, single early adherence measurement and residual confounding preclude causal conclusions.

The observed association should be interpreted with caution. The VIF values for adherence and social support in the HHS model indicated moderate multicollinearity, reflecting the close conceptual relationship among support, adherence and functional recovery. Although these VIF values were below the common threshold for severe multicollinearity, coefficient estimates may still be unstable when highly related behavioural and social variables are entered into the same model. Patient-perspective research shows that recovery after hip fracture involves not only physical training but also information needs, confidence rebuilding and negotiation of perceived risk [38, 39]. Future studies with raw longitudinal data should conduct sensitivity analyses, repeated adherence assessment, mediation analysis or structural equation modelling to clarify the pathways linking psychosocial resources, rehabilitation behaviour and functional outcomes.

Implications for nursing practice

The findings distinguish potentially modifiable targets from non-modifiable or uncertain risk markers. Pain, movement-related fear, low rehabilitation self-efficacy, weak support and gaps in transition planning are reasonable priorities for assessment and multidisciplinary intervention. Osteoporosis status should currently be used only as a signal for further assessment because its association may reflect confounding. Screening should be followed by communication with the orthopaedic and rehabilitation team rather than by isolated nursing action.

The term “nurse-led rehabilitation” has therefore been replaced with “nurse-coordinated rehabilitation support within multidisciplinary care.” Rehabilitation professionals should design and progress specialised exercise programmes, while nurses contribute continuous pain assessment, identification of kinesiophobia, reinforcement and return demonstration of prescribed activities, achievable short-term goals, caregiver engagement and discharge coordination. Motivation and supportive coaching are relevant to post-hip-fracture activity [40], and positive expectations may support recovery [41].

Strengths and limitations

This study integrated psychological factors, social support, rehabilitation care variables and HHS into a single analytical framework. The prospective measurement of HHS at 3 months and the use of established instruments strengthened the examination of factors associated with adherence and functional recovery.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-centre study, which may limit generalisability. Second, adherence was self-reported and susceptible to recall and social-desirability bias. Third, complete-case analysis excluded participants who withdrew, were lost to follow-up or had missing primary outcomes. Because complete baseline questionnaire data were unavailable for many non-analysed individuals, their characteristics could not be compared reliably with those of the final sample; attrition bias may therefore have influenced the estimates. Fourth, severe cognitive impairment was operationalised using clinical documentation and ability to complete study procedures rather than a formal cognitive screening instrument. Fifth, assessors were not formally blinded to baseline rehabilitation status, creating potential detection bias. Sixth, adherence, kinesiophobia, self-efficacy, social support and pain were not reassessed after discharge, preventing analysis of recovery trajectories or time-varying relationships. Seventh, routine rehabilitation frequency and intensity were individualised and were not measured in sufficient detail, and reasons for absent documented guidance or discharge plans were not systematically recorded. Eighth, important potential confounders, including pre-fracture autonomy, frailty, detailed comorbidity and medication profiles, length of stay, discharge destination and rehabilitation intensity, were not captured consistently enough for adjusted analysis. Ninth, remote HHS assessment may introduce measurement variation, particularly for range-of-motion items. Finally, moderate multicollinearity between social support and adherence may have reduced coefficient stability. These limitations mean that the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

Conclusions

Before-discharge functional exercise adherence among older adults after femoral neck fracture surgery was moderate. Kinesiophobia, rehabilitation self-efficacy, social support, pain intensity, osteoporosis status, documented rehabilitation guidance and a documented post-discharge rehabilitation plan were associated with adherence. Higher early adherence was associated with higher HHS at 3 months, but causality cannot be inferred.

Early adherence assessment may help identify patients who require additional support, even when average short-term recovery appears acceptable. Multidisciplinary rehabilitation should prioritise modifiable barriers, with nurses supporting pain and fear assessment, education, activity reinforcement, caregiver engagement and continuity across discharge, while rehabilitation professionals direct specialised exercise prescription and progression.

Future multicentre studies should use repeated adherence and psychosocial assessments, standardised measures of rehabilitation dose, more complete confounder data and sensitivity analyses for missing data. Intervention studies are required before concluding that targeted improvement of adherence leads to better hip function.
