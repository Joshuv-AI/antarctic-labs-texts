Evaluation of Multi‐Stakeholder Perspectives on the Preparedness of Ghana's Animal Health Services for African Swine Fever Control: A Cross‐Sectional Study

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAfrican Swine Fever (ASF) is a highly fatal viral disease of pigs that has persisted in Ghana since its first introduction in 1999, causing recurrent outbreaks with devastating socioeconomic consequences for livelihoods and food security. Despite control measures implemented by Ghana's veterinary services, outbreaks continue, reflecting critical gaps in national preparedness and institutional support. This study assessed the preparedness of Ghana's animal health services to manage and control ASF.MethodsA mixed‐method of cross‐sectional design was employed, combining structured questionnaires and semi‐structured interviews across three geographic belts of Ghana. Participants included veterinary officers, pig farmers, policy makers, and community animal health workers. Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and exported into Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 27 for analysis.ResultsFindings revealed partial preparedness: strong centralized diagnostic capacity but weak regional infrastructure and limited farmer engagement. ASF awareness varied, with persistent misconceptions about zoonotic potential and cultural beliefs undermining prevention. Institutional support was inconsistent where over 70% of farmers reported no cooperative support and 80% received no government assistance during outbreaks. Biosecurity compliance was irregular, constrained by financial limits and absent incentives, leading to under‐reporting. Farmers and field officers lacked epidemiological literacy, and highly centralized surveillance caused delays in field investigation and laboratory confirmation, with low responsiveness at regional and community levels.ConclusionAfrican Swine Fever's impacts extend beyond farm‐level losses to livelihood collapse, psychosocial distress, and pork market disruption. Though not zoonotic, its control demands a One Health approach integrating veterinary, environmental, and socioeconomic dimensions through decentralized diagnostics, stronger farmer–vet partnerships, compensation systems, and psychosocial resilience strategies.What's KnownAfrican swine fever is a highly lethal viral disease of pigs with no vaccine or treatment, bringing huge economic and livelihood losses around the world.Since the first detection in 1999, ASF outbreaks have occurred periodically in Ghana, pointing to the deficiencies in disease surveillance, biosecurity, and institutional coordination.Rapid diagnostics, compensation schemes, and strong veterinary governance structures have been effective control mechanisms in other regions, for example, Europe.What's NewThis study offers a multi‐stakeholder assessment of Ghana's ASF preparedness, integrating perspectives from veterinary officers, policymakers, and farmers in a holistic approach not previously reported in ASF literature for Ghana.It also, therefore, identifies psychosocial impacts and livelihood collapse among farmers and proposes incorporating psychosocial recovery and One Health principles into national ASF preparedness frameworks.Results indicate partial preparedness: strong central diagnostics but weak regional infrastructure, as well as limited farmer awareness and institutional support.What Are the Clinical Implications of This Study?The strengthening of decentralized diagnostics and surveillance, including rapid field tests and regional laboratory capacity, is of great importance for early detection and containment.Improved reporting and compliance will occur by implementing farmer–veterinary partnerships, compensation mechanisms, and biosecurity incentives.Molecular monitoring, psychological support, and multisectoral coordination added to Ghana's ASF Strategy would build long‐term resilience and align national control with international standards for a One Health approach.

Introduction

African swine fever (ASF) is a deadly viral disease of both wild and domestic pigs, caused by the African swine fever virus (ASFV), a large double‐stranded DNA virus belonging to the Asfarviridae family [1]. Due to inefficient vaccines or treatment for ASF, controlling the disease relies heavily on strong preventive measures. These include maintaining strict biosecurity, timely detection, culling infected animals, safely disposing of carcasses, and imposing movement restrictions, among others [2, 3].

The virus is transmitted by contact with an infected animal, contaminated feed, fomites, or via vectors such as soft ticks. Highly virulent strains could cause mortality rates as high as 100% in naïve herds. According to Galindo & Alonso [1] and WOAH [4], ASF has caused significant losses on the global economy; for sub‐Saharan Africa, ASF is continuous in threatening the food security of households and rural livelihoods, particularly for smallholder pig farmers who are often unable to recover once the outbreak recurs, as they lack the financial capacity to do so [5].

Despite early preventive measures after its 1999 introduction, ASF has remained endemic in Ghana [6, 7]. The 2021 outbreaks revealed two circulating ASFV genotypes, reflecting complex disease dynamics [3]. Outbreaks have caused high mortality and disrupted trade, with about 900 pigs culled in a 2018 Central Region incident [8]. Insufficient compensation funding limited containment, eroding trust in veterinary authorities and weakening compliance, leading some farmers to conceal sick pigs or improperly dispose of carcasses, causing heavy losses and pork market instability [4, 7]. Beyond economic costs, ASF carries social costs, as pig production is a key livelihood and protein source for many Ghanaian households [9].

Although the Veterinary Services Directorate (VSD) is mandated to oversee surveillance, outbreak response, and enforcement, little evidence demonstrates the operational effectiveness of these systems. The literature reveals critical gaps undermining national preparedness: economic effects are often reported but rarely quantified rigorously, leaving policymakers with limited data on the disease's true impact, while surveillance is often delayed and incomplete, hindering early detection [10]. Farmers’ attitudes toward prevention and cooperation with interventions remain poorly characterized, particularly where absent compensation schemes encourage concealment [11]. Further challenges, including limited diagnostic capacity, shortages of trained veterinary personnel, inadequate rapid‐response funding, and the lack of a comprehensive strategy, constrain Ghana's ability to manage outbreaks effectively.

Despite progress in understanding the viral epidemiology and genetic diversity of ASFV in Ghana, the socio‐economic dimensions of outbreaks and the resilience of the country's animal health services remain poorly characterized. This study assessed the effects, preparedness, and control strategies for ASF across Ghana, examining institutional support, diagnostic and surveillance capacity, and the socioeconomic and psychosocial impacts of outbreaks through multiple stakeholder perspectives. Addressing these gaps is essential to reducing ASF's devastating impact on livelihoods, food security, and rural economies.

Methodology

Study Design

A mixed‐methods, cross‐sectional study design was used to assess the effects, preparedness, and control strategies for ASF across Ghana, combining structured questionnaires and semi‐structured interviews to capture both quantitative trends and qualitative insights from pig farmers, veterinary officers, researchers, and policymakers.

Study Area

This study covered three geographical belts: the Forest/Middle Belt (Ashanti, Eastern, and Bono regions), the Savannah/Northern Belt (Northern, Upper East, Upper West, and North East regions), and the Coastal/Southern Belt (Greater Accra, Central, and Volta regions), enabling a diverse sample across varied socio‐economic and infrastructural conditions within Ghana's pig sector.

Selection of Participants for the Study

In May 2025, the FAO supported the Government of Ghana in developing a national ASF strategy to guide disease prevention and control. A 15‐member expert team from the Veterinary Services Directorate of the Ministry of Food and Agriculture, the Wildlife Division of the Forestry Commission, academia and research institutions, and FAO drafted the initial strategy, aligned with the continental strategy developed by the African Union Inter‐African Bureau for Animal Resources (AU‐IBAR).

A multi‐stakeholder validation workshop then convened the core institutions alongside representatives from the Ghana Health Service, Environmental Protection Agency, National Development Planning Commission, National Disaster Management Organisation, Ministry of Lands and Rural Development, and Regional Veterinary Offices from five affected regions (Central, Eastern, Upper East, Upper West, and Volta). Private sector and industry actors also participated, including the National Pig Farmers Association, the Women's Pig Farmers Association (Wa), the Western Region Pig Farmers Association, and the Ashanti Pig Farmers Association. Respondents were selected from these institutions and associations.

Study Population and Sampling

Purposive sampling ensured regional representation, with participants selected for direct engagement with the pig value chain or policy‐making and a minimum of 1 year of industry experience.

Data Collection Instruments

Data were collected using a 26‐item structured questionnaire across four thematic sections: (1) demographic information; (2) government ASF control policies and effectiveness; (3) farm‐level impacts, psychosocial effects, and coping strategies; and (4) intersectoral collaboration, guideline existence, and stakeholder cooperation.

Reliability and Validity

Internal consistency was assessed and a Cronbach's alpha value of 0.77 was obtained, which indicated acceptable reliability. This suggested that the research instrument consistently measured the constructs of interest for this study. The questionnaire was also pre‐tested among five respondents and revised to ensure validity.

Data Collection Procedure

Data collection was done in May 2025. Structured questionnaires were administered through face‐to‐face interviews by trained research assistants, with each interview taking approximately 10–15 min. Participants who consented to in‐depth interviews were engaged separately in face‐to‐face interviews to capture more detailed qualitative data. The voluntary nature of participation was explained to all of the participants, and confidentiality was guaranteed.

Data Analysis

Questionnaire data were cleaned in Microsoft Excel and exported into SPSS Version 27. Demographic characteristics, knowledge levels, effects, and preparedness were summarized using descriptive statistics such as frequencies and percentages. Qualitative interview data were analyzed thematically, with transcripts coded for six recurring themes: ASF knowledge and epidemiology, socio‐economic and livelihood impacts, diagnosis and surveillance, control and prevention, public health and perceptions, and challenges facing farmers and veterinary authorities.

Ethical Consideration

Participation was voluntary, with written and verbal informed consent obtained before data collection, alongside assurance of confidentiality and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Data were securely stored and accessible only to the research team. Administrative approval was obtained from the Veterinary Services Directorate (VSD), Ministry of Food and Agriculture, Ghana (reference VSD/DO/123/25).

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics

The ASF survey included 30 respondents, mostly male (80.0%) and highly educated, with 87.0% holding university degrees. Participants were fairly evenly distributed across age groups: 35–55 and above 55, each comprising 30%, while 10.0% were aged 25–35. Most had long pig‐sector experience, with 66.7% involved for over 10 years and 23.3% for 6–10 years. Respondents comprised farmers (40.0%), veterinary officers (33.3%), policymakers (13.3%), and CAHWs (13.3%). Of those reporting farm size (n = 11), 54.6% ran medium farms (51–500 pigs), 27.3% small farms (under 50), and 18.2% large farms (over 500) (Table 1).

Awareness of African Swine Fever and Its Impacts

The survey revealed mixed awareness of ASF control measures and policies: 34.5% low, 37.9% medium, and 27.6% high. Government response was rated moderately effective by 60.0% and ineffective by 40%, with only 20% receiving direct support, including disinfectants, fuel allowances, and PPE. Among those who experienced ASF directly, 50% reported very high farming impacts. Regarding psychological effects, 65.0% reported no family impact, while 35.0% indicated stress and other psychological consequences (Table 2).

Institutional and Sectoral Support for African Swine Fever

Institutional and sectoral support for ASF control was weak. Diagnostic capacity was rated poorly equipped by 41.4% and very well prepared by only 13.8%. Biosecurity compliance was inconsistent (51.7% sometimes, 34.5% rarely, 13.8% never). While 72.4% reported no cooperative support, the same proportion had received prior ASF training. Farmer–veterinary engagement was occasional (72.7%), ASF guidelines were absent (76.7%), transporter/trader cooperation was lacking (53.3%), and slaughterhouse monitoring was absent (90.0%). Sector operation was rated low by 53.3%, citing financial constraints, coordination gaps, and low awareness. Suggested improvements: farmer compensation, biosecurity subsidies, stronger veterinary staffing, and disease‐free zoning (Table 3).

Knowledge and Epidemiology

Both farmers and veterinary officers consistently described ASF as a viral disease with no cure or vaccine. As one farmer explained,Yeah, my knowledge about it is that it's a disease that is viral. And there is no drug, there's no vaccine as of now.(Farmer 1)

Yeah, my knowledge about it is that it's a disease that is viral. And there is no drug, there's no vaccine as of now.

Veterinary officers frequently emphasized the role of wildlife reservoirs in transmission, particularly warthogs, red river hogs, and soft ticks:The wild swine species… are carriers of the virus. Being carriers, they also carry some parasites, the soft ticks, that could also transmit the virus to domestic pigs.(Veterinary epidemiologist)

The wild swine species… are carriers of the virus. Being carriers, they also carry some parasites, the soft ticks, that could also transmit the virus to domestic pigs.

Farmers, in contrast, often attributed outbreaks to pig movements between northern and southern Ghana under free‐range systems:Most of this swine flu normally comes from the northern region… the disease normally spreads from there before it even gets to the southern part.(Farmer 2)

Most of this swine flu normally comes from the northern region… the disease normally spreads from there before it even gets to the southern part.

Both groups recognized ASF's endemic presence in Ghana since the first outbreak in 1999, and many noted a seasonal pattern, with outbreaks more common in the dry season (January–June) and after rains, when improperly buried carcasses contaminate waterbodies. As one officer explained,Mostly it's rampant in the dry season… and when the rain starts… carcasses not buried are washed out, infecting animals.(Laboratory scientist)

Mostly it's rampant in the dry season… and when the rain starts… carcasses not buried are washed out, infecting animals.

Diagnosis and Surveillance

Farmers showed familiarity with ASF's clinical signs, reporting dullness, loss of appetite, reddish ears, nose bleeding, and sudden mortality. As one farmer recalled,When it happens, the first thing you see is that they don't eat, they don't drink… you see reddish… the ears… and blood oozing from the nose.(Farmer 1)

When it happens, the first thing you see is that they don't eat, they don't drink… you see reddish… the ears… and blood oozing from the nose.

Veterinary officers noted that Ghana has strong diagnostic capacity at the national BSL‐3 laboratory, but regional labs remain under‐resourced.We have a grant lab, BSL‐3 lab… they are capable of diagnosing because they have the reagents, the human capacity, the resources… but other labs don't have enough resources.(Laboratory scientist)

We have a grant lab, BSL‐3 lab… they are capable of diagnosing because they have the reagents, the human capacity, the resources… but other labs don't have enough resources.

The Policy Officer, however, emphasized that diagnostic services are largely centralized, with most samples sent to Accra:Mostly, they always send it to the lab. And previously, they always said they were sending the sample to Accra for them to diagnose the disease.(Policy Officer 1)

Mostly, they always send it to the lab. And previously, they always said they were sending the sample to Accra for them to diagnose the disease.

Participants called for rapid field test kits and district‐level laboratories to improve early detection:I certainly would have been advocating for rapid test kits at the field level, so that the field veterinarian… can immediately do the test at the field.(Veterinary Officer) Every district should have a lab where they can even detect some of these cases. Preventing the disease is way better.(Veterinary Officer)

I certainly would have been advocating for rapid test kits at the field level, so that the field veterinarian… can immediately do the test at the field.

Every district should have a lab where they can even detect some of these cases. Preventing the disease is way better.

Control and Prevention

Biosecurity was consistently identified across all respondents as the most important prevention measure in the absence of treatment and vaccines. Farmers described good practices including fencing, disinfecting visitors, limiting feed sources, and restricting access by butchers:Most of the time, when visitors are coming, we make sure they are disinfected… most of the time we don't allow butchers to even come inside.(Farmer 3)

Most of the time, when visitors are coming, we make sure they are disinfected… most of the time we don't allow butchers to even come inside.

Movement restrictions and buffer zones were also suggested, particularly to prevent spread from free‐range farms in northern Ghana:Stop moving those pigs to the southern part… I think it's one of the major things that they need to work on.(Farmer 2)

Stop moving those pigs to the southern part… I think it's one of the major things that they need to work on.

At the regional level, ASF control relied on coordination between veterinary services and local authorities.Normally with the regional office, we have a regional veterinary doctor… we go to the regional coordinating council… and then we go as a team to these farmers.(Veterinary Officer)

Normally with the regional office, we have a regional veterinary doctor… we go to the regional coordinating council… and then we go as a team to these farmers.

Sensitization activities also included value chain actors such as bushmeat traders and hunters:We have brought bushmeat traders and hunters together to train and sensitize them on how their trade can lead to disease spread.(Veterinary Officer)

We have brought bushmeat traders and hunters together to train and sensitize them on how their trade can lead to disease spread.

Despite these efforts, participants highlighted policy gaps. No ASF‐specific national policy exists, and management relies on broader animal health legislation such as the Diseases of Animals Act (1961), meat distribution regulations, and wildlife conservation laws:For now, no specific policy for ASF.(Policy Officer 1) We are just basing on the Diseases of Animal Act 1961 to control ASF because it's one of the priority diseases for veterinary services.(Policy Officer 2) We also rely on animal importation out of Ghana… so we are using those regulations to contain the disease.(Policy Officer 3)

For now, no specific policy for ASF.

We are just basing on the Diseases of Animal Act 1961 to control ASF because it's one of the priority diseases for veterinary services.

We also rely on animal importation out of Ghana… so we are using those regulations to contain the disease.

Socioeconomic and Livelihood Impacts

Farmers described ASF as catastrophic for household livelihoods:Pig rearing is a source of livelihood for so many people… when it affects your farm, the only remedy is that they have to just wipe everything out.(Farmer 1)

Pig rearing is a source of livelihood for so many people… when it affects your farm, the only remedy is that they have to just wipe everything out.

Impacts extended beyond farms to family well‐being:You will not be able to have resources to take care of your kids in school, and in the long run, it affects your family as well.(Farmer 2)

You will not be able to have resources to take care of your kids in school, and in the long run, it affects your family as well.

Several farmers recounted abandoning pig production altogether after severe outbreaks: “My farm population was about 750 pigs… I lost almost 360 pigs. The veterinary officer told me to just stop operations. Since that time, I stopped operating that pig farm” (Farmer 3).

Veterinary officers emphasized that ASF's effects were not only individual but also national, contributing to pork shortages, higher prices, and greater reliance on imports. As one farmer summarized,Indirectly, it will affect the pork supply in the market… which is going to even make the price shoot up.(Farmer 3)

Indirectly, it will affect the pork supply in the market… which is going to even make the price shoot up.

Public Health and Perceptions

Veterinary officers clarified that ASF is not zoonotic, but acknowledged widespread public misconceptions. Farmers noted that radio misinformation discouraged pork consumption:Most of the people don't understand the African swine fever… someone sitting on the radio will advocate that… people shouldn't take pork.(Farmer 1)

Most of the people don't understand the African swine fever… someone sitting on the radio will advocate that… people shouldn't take pork.

Cultural and spiritual explanations also circulated, with some linking pig deaths to biblical curses. As one farmer recounted,Because most people believe that the way they are dying, because of the curse, Jesus Christ cast some demons into this. That's why sometimes the demons come, and they are dying. And most of them will refuse to eat pork again.… people don't want to buy because they are afraid, they must eat and die.(Farmer 2)

Because most people believe that the way they are dying, because of the curse, Jesus Christ cast some demons into this. That's why sometimes the demons come, and they are dying. And most of them will refuse to eat pork again.… people don't want to buy because they are afraid, they must eat and die.

These misconceptions reinforced stigma toward pig farmers, who were often viewed as socially undesirable:When you are even around a pig, they see you as someone who… doesn't even like society.(Farmer 1)

When you are even around a pig, they see you as someone who… doesn't even like society.

Challenges for Farmers and Veterinary Authorities

Veterinary officers highlighted systemic challenges such as understaffing and limited capacity for farm visits. One noted,If the number of people who are working in the district is enough, and every day at least two or three visits with the proper PPE… they can detect the disease earlier so that they can prevent it from spreading.(Veterinary Officer)

If the number of people who are working in the district is enough, and every day at least two or three visits with the proper PPE… they can detect the disease earlier so that they can prevent it from spreading.

They also reported that some farmers deliberately concealed sick animals to avoid culling losses:Some of these farmers… don't even want you to know that the animals are suffering from that disease. Some of them will either hide them or go and sell them at a cheaper price.(Veterinary Officer) Another confirmed, “If you don't report, then you end up selling off the meat… it has a consequence of spreading.(Laboratory scientist)

Some of these farmers… don't even want you to know that the animals are suffering from that disease. Some of them will either hide them or go and sell them at a cheaper price.

Farmers, meanwhile, stressed that the absence of compensation was a central barrier to reporting outbreaks:None of the government institutions even supported me, even to compensate me or something like that. So, it's really hurting.(Farmer 3)

None of the government institutions even supported me, even to compensate me or something like that. So, it's really hurting.

Discussion

This study offers insights into Ghana's animal health system preparedness for ASF, highlighting the intersection of farmers’ knowledge, institutional capacity, and evolving viral epidemiology. Farmers aged 25 to 45 dominated the sample, reflecting a dynamic sector but also vulnerability, as less experienced farmers may perpetuate risky husbandry. The marked gender imbalance, with men comprising 80% of respondents, reflects broader West African livestock ownership patterns [12, 13] and signals a missed opportunity to engage women and youth in ASF prevention.

The awareness of ASF was uneven, with only 28% of farmers demonstrating high knowledge, and widespread misconceptions about indirect transmission. This agrees with studies in Côte d'Ivoire [12] and Europe [14], where awareness was high in principle but epidemiological literacy remained weak. Such gaps are critical, as ASF may initially present with atypical signs, delaying recognition and control [15, 16]. Recent molecular epidemiology complicates this further: retrospective evidence showed co‐circulation of Genotype I and pandemic‐derived Genotype II in Ghana in 2021 [3], with Genotype II becoming dominant by 2022 through strains related to Nigerian RV502 and Estonia‐2014, carrying unique multigene family deletions [17]. Coexistence may confuse clinical presentation, as Genotype I typically causes fewer acute forms while Genotype II is linked to severe peracute disease [15]; 2019, making clinical interpretation harder for farmers and frontline officers and raising the stakes for accurate recognition and surveillance.

Diagnostic systems in Ghana are constrained by delayed sample transport, limiting the effectiveness of central laboratories [18]. This is particularly concerning in the context of diverse viral genotypes with overlapping but distinct clinical profiles. Sánchez‐Vizcaíno et al. [15] pointed out the need for differential diagnosis between ASF and other hemorrhagic diseases, such as Classical Swine Fever and Porcine Reproductive and Respiratory Syndrome, that have not been previously described in Ghana. These complications can become more complex with genotype variation. In this regard, recommendations for portable PCR assays and oral‐fluid sampling [19] could provide critical support to aid Ghana's decentralized veterinary infrastructure. A recent Ghanaian study by Faburay et al. [20], demonstrated pooled oral fluid sampling can provide early warnings and complement centralized laboratory testing.

Farmers reported weak institutional support during ASF outbreaks, with 80% receiving no assistance. This agrees with Enyetornye et al. [21] in Ghana and Kouakou et al. [12] in Côte d'Ivoire, who found that limited compensation mechanisms suppress reporting and encourage outbreak concealment. In contrast, successful eradications in Belgium and the Czech Republic were underpinned by rapid response, structured compensation, and robust coordination [19]. Ghana's centralized BSL‐3 diagnostic capacity is a strength, but the lack of resourced regional and district laboratories undermines early detection, especially in resource‐poor African settings [22]. Genomic evidence reflects this fragility: pandemic‐derived Genotype II was retrospectively confirmed in Ghana only after outbreaks had devastated farms [17]. Moreover, poorly surveilled informal pig and pork movements from Burkina Faso and Côte d'Ivoire enable silent cross‐border transmission [23], with implications for West Africa, where uncoordinated surveillance can facilitate Genotype I and II outbreaks [24]. Over the past 5 years, ASF has persisted in Ghana, with over 60% of farmers reporting at least one outbreak, often attributed to informal pig trade and human‐mediated transmission [15, 21, 25]. However, molecular evidence reveals greater complexity: Genotype I, with a longer history in Ghana, showed greater diversity in 2021 than the newly introduced Genotype II [3]. Thus, while farmer practices help maintain the virus, ASFV also undergoes local evolution, potentially increasing resilience and complicating control [26, 27]. This underscores the need for Ghana to sequence current strains to understand spread, evolution, and virulence. The coexistence of Genotypes I and II raises recombination risk, as documented in China [28], highlighting the urgency for robust surveillance—strengthened diagnostics and early warning systems integrating routine clinical monitoring and farmer reporting networks [3].

Farmer practices like swill feeding and selling pigs during outbreaks reflects a gap between awareness and behavior. These are well‐documented drivers of ASF transmission globally [19] and, in Ghana, they intersect with institutional weaknesses and viral evolution to sustain the disease. The socioeconomic consequences, herd collapse, farm abandonment, and psychosocial strain mirror from Uganda [29]. Penrith et al. [30] and Enyetornye et al. [21] noted that inadequate compensation fosters concealment and informal meat sales, similar to our findings. The pig farmers emphasized the psychosocial effects of the disease, aligning with a study by Cochran et al. [19], who recommended integrating psychosocial resilience into preparedness frameworks.

In furtherance, biosecurity practices remain inconsistent due to economic barriers and weak enforcement; as noted globally, enforcement without incentives is rarely sustainable [19, 31]. Misconceptions including beliefs that ASF is zoonotic or spiritually caused further erode confidence in control measures. European experience shows that transparent, culturally sensitive communication can restore trust [30], a lesson directly applicable to Ghana.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Ghana's preparedness and response to African swine fever (ASF) remain partial, marked by strong central diagnostic capacity but weak regional infrastructure, low epidemiological literacy, and limited institutional support. Comprehensive genomic sequencing of current ASFV strains is urgently needed to track spread, evolution, and virulence. ASF's effects extend beyond farm‐level losses to livelihood collapse, psychosocial distress, and pork market dislocation. Though not zoonotic, ASF is best managed through a One Health approach integrating veterinary, environmental, and socioeconomic dimensions. Ghana's future resilience will depend on implementing the national ASF strategy, prioritizing rapid decentralized diagnostics, economic incentives for on‐farm biosecurity, farmer to veterinary participatory approaches to build trust and compliance, and psychosocial recovery support for affected farmers.

Limitations

This study used workshop‐based purposive sampling, drawing experts and stakeholders from institutions involved in Ghana's ASF management and strategy development. The relatively small number of participants may not capture the full diversity of views among professionals in this field. Self‐reported responses from farmers and officers can be subject to recall or social desirability bias, particularly regarding biosecurity compliance and institutional performance. The study used both quantitative and qualitative data, but no direct farm‐level observations verified reported control methods.
