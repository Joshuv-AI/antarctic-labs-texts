Influence of genetic variants and omega-3 fatty acids on acute myocardial infarction: findings from a prospective cohort study
Authors: Haozhang Huang, Xiaozhao Lu, Sau Van Nguyen, Shiqun Chen, Jin Liu, Yong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpidemiological studies have indicated that elevated levels of omega-3 fatty acids may reduce the risk of acute myocardial infarction (AMI). In contrast, genetic studies and randomized controlled trials have not succeeded in corroborating this protective association. Subsequently, we applied a series of genetic analyses to uncover pleiotropic loci shared between omega-3 levels and AMI. Background

The potential cardiovascular benefits of omega-3 fatty acids have garnered substantial scientific interest. Epidemiological studies have consistently indicated that elevated levels of omega-3 fatty acids may reduce the risk of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [1–3]. The beneficial effects of omega-3 fatty acids include positive influences on lipoprotein metabolism and modulation of inflammatory, oxidative, thrombotic, vascular, and arrhythmogenic factors associated with cardiovascular disease [4]. However, numerous observational cohort studies, genetic investigations, and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have not consistently validated this protective association, resulting in ongoing debate concerning the role of omega-3 fatty acids in cardiovascular health [5–10].

In numerous genome-wide association studies (GWAS), the rs10096633 locus within the lipoprotein lipase (LPL) gene has been linked to variations in omega-3 fatty acid levels [11, 12]. The role of LPL in the development of cardiovascular diseases is increasingly acknowledged, as it plays a pivotal role in lipid metabolism and the regulation of triglyceride levels [13, 14]. Furthermore, other genetic variants, such as the rs964184 polymorphism in the ZNF259 locus, have been associated with higher plasma triglyceride levels and an increased risk of coronary heart disease [15–17]. Gaining insight into these genetic factors may not only enhance our understanding of the molecular pathways underlying the development of AMI but also elucidate the mechanisms contributing to the seemingly paradoxical relationship between omega-3 fatty acid levels and AMI risk. Moreover, this knowledge could facilitate risk stratification for cardiovascular disease, including the establishment of targeted screening programs.

This study aims to clarify how specific genetic variants associated with omega-3 fatty acid levels influence their relationship with the incidence of AMI, providing insights that could lead to the development of improved preventive strategies in cardiovascular health.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed to clarify the relationship between omega-3 fatty acid levels and the incidence of AMI through the application of genetic data and statistical methodologies. The primary objective is to identify causal and pleiotropic genetic variants that modulate omega-3 fatty acid levels, as well as to assess how these variants impact the risk of AMI.

Identification of causal loci modulating omega-3 fatty acids in AMI

Data sources and genetic instruments for GWAS

To identify genetic variants associated with omega-3 fatty acid levels, summary statistics derived from GWAS were utilized. Specifically, the omega-3 fatty acid measurements in this study were obtained through a comprehensive analysis by Borges et al., which utilized high-throughput Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) metabolomics to assess 249 metabolic traits. This analysis was conducted on a subset of 115,078 participants from the UK Biobank at recruitment. The data were obtained from a single blood sample (with no batch effects) and were expressed in absolute concentrations (mmol/L) as well as ratios. The measurements were performed using Nightingale Health’s metabolic biomarker profiling platform, which is known for its precision in metabolic assessments (https://www.nightingalehealth.com). A reproducible GWAS of omega-3 fatty acids was conducted by Kettunen et al., who utilized an NMR metabolomics platform to quantify 123 metabolite measures across 14 European cohorts, encompassing 24,925 individuals [11]. The GWAS data regarding Plasma omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid levels, including eicosapentaenoate (EPA; 20:5n3), docosahexaenoate (DHA; 22:6n3), alpha-linolenic acid (ALA; 18:3n3), and docosapentaenoate (DPA; 22:5n3), were aggregated from 8,299 individuals within the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA) cohort [18]. Data concerning coronary heart disease (n = 184,305) and AMI (n = 171,875) were obtained from the CARDIoGRAMplusC4D consortium [19]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) demonstrating genome-wide significance (P ≤ 5 × 10− 8) within a 1-Mbp region and r² < 0.001 in relation to omega-3 fatty acids were utilized as instrumental variables. An F-statistic less than 10 was considered insufficiently informative for further analysis [20], and the Steiger filtering test was implemented to address reverse causality [21]. As a result of this threshold, a limited number of instrumental variables for EPA, DHA, ALA and DPA were identified, prompting the adoption of a more permissive threshold of P < 1 × 10− 5.

Two-sample Mendelian randomization to assess causality

Within the field of two-sample MR, the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method is acknowledged as the principal analysis approach for assessing causal associations. To substantiate the primary results, additional analyses were conducted utilizing the MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted model methods. Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate SNP heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy. The examination of heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test in conjunction with the IVW and MR-Egger approaches, resulting in no significance findings, indicated by a P-value exceeding 0.05. MR-Egger intercept tests were employed to investigate horizontal pleiotropy; a P >0.05 indicated the absence of significant horizontal pleiotropy, suggesting that the genetic variants primarily influence the target trait. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding FADS-region variants (FADS 1, FADS 2, FADS 3, and within 100 kb) [22, 23]. Furthermore, we conducted a multivariable MR analysis, adjusting for LDL-C, Lp(a), and ApoB, with data derived from the UK Biobank Nealelab (https://www.nealelab.is/uk-biobank).

Associations among each SNP were analyzed using the Wald ratio method. Loci associated with omega-3 fatty acid levels, which exhibited a significant correlation with AMI (Wald ratio P < 0.05) were identified as potential causal loci.

Transcriptomics MR for validation of causal loci

We further validated potential causal loci through Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) analysis, employing GWAS focused on AMI along with expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data to assess the associations between identified top SNPs and gene expression levels. The potential causal loci were designated as the leading SNP corresponding to each gene. For eQTL expression data, we utilized genomic expression datasets from the GTEx V8 project encompassing whole blood, adipose tissue, blood vessels, and heart samples, as well as eQTLGen consortium data for blood analysis. SNPs exhibiting strong linkage disequilibrium, characterized by an r² value exceeding 0.9, were excluded, along with top-associated cis-eQTLs within a 1 Mb upstream and downstream region of the target gene where the minor allele frequency (MAF) surpassed 0.01. Expression probes with eQTL P ≤ 5 × 10⁻⁸ were selected as the most significantly associated variants. Furthermore, we performed the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test to examine potential linkage within the observed associations. A P_HEIDI value less than 0.01 indicates that the identified association may stem from two distinct genetic variants exhibiting high linkage disequilibrium [24, 25]. Finally, if a potential causal locus was associated with an increased risk of AMI in at least one tissue (P_SMR < 0.05 and P_HEIDI >0.01), it was classified as a validated causal locus.

Exploring pleiotropic loci between Omega-3 fatty acids and AMI

Local genetic correlation and heritability analysis

Linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) was used to assess genetic correlations between omega-3 fatty acids and AMI [26]. We used precomputed linkage disequilibrium (LD) from the 1000 Genomes Project specifically for SNPs in the HapMap 3 set [27]. Using LDSC, we estimated trait heritability and determined genetic correlations based on European ancestry data. We further evaluated local SNP heritability and genetic correlations utilizing heritability derived from summary statistics (ρ-HESS) [28]. In this analysis, we identified approximately 1,703 LD-independent regions, each measuring approximately 1.5 MB in size. Local heritability and genetic interrelations were calculated using data from the 1000 Genomes Project. Nominal significance was defined as P < 0.05, while Bonferroni correction was applied to establish the threshold for statistical significance at P correction < 0.05.

Cross-trait Meta-analysis of GWAS and colocalization: identifying shared risk loci

In order to identify shared risk SNPs that influence omega-3 fatty acids and exhibit significant genetic correlations with AMI, we conducted two cross-trait meta-analyses: multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) and cross-phenotype association test (CPASSOC) [29–31]. Significant SNPs uncovered in both MTAG and CPASSOC analyses were classified as those demonstrating P < 5 × 10− 8. We identified distinct SNPs through PLINK “clumping,” using following parameters: –clump-p1 5e-8, –clump-p2 1e-5, –clump-r2 0.2, –clump-kb 500. Novel loci were defined as independent SNPs lacking linkage disequilibrium (LD r2 >0.2 within 1000-kb windows) with significant SNPs originating from the initial single-trait GWAS. Colocalization analysis was performed using coloc, with a locus considered colocalized if the probability for H4 (PPH4) exceeded 0.75. We prioritized shared pleiotropic loci that achieved genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10− 8) in the cross-trait meta-analyses, located within regions identified by ρ-HESS, and satisfying the criterion of PPH4 >0.75.

Assessing the effect of genetic polymorphisms in prospective cohort study

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large-scale cohort study (∼500,000 participants aged 40–69 at the time of enrollment). Participants of European descent were determined to be eligible for inclusion in this analysis, culminating in a final sample size of 472,374 individuals. A total of 453,244 participants were systematically excluded based on the following criteria: (1) a documented history of AMI (N = 11,329); (2) missing records related to omega-3 fatty acids, use fish oil supplements or not, LDL-C, Lp (a), ApoB and follow-up information (N = 269,365); (3) the absence of records corresponding to the genotyping procedure and sample quality control (N = 983); (4) inconsistencies between genetic and reported sex, X-chromosomal aneuploidy, and kinship (N = 422). Consequently, a total of 190,275 participants were retained for the final analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Grouping was performed according to the median omega-3 fatty acid level. We employed full information maximum likelihood to address missing data, under the assumption that the missing data were randomly distributed. To handle missing baseline values before their incorporation in the analysis, we utilized the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method, leveraging the MICE R package.

To assess the impact of omega-3 fatty acid levels on the risk of AMI, we employed various statistical methodologies, including restricted cubic splines (RCS) and Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis. In the RCS analysis, we adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariates, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, education level, Townsend deprivation index, physical activity, healthy diet score, LDL-C, Lp(a), ApoB, hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), fish oil supplements, and statin use. Additionally, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to estimate adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). This model consisted of three levels of adjustments: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and BMI), and Model 3 (further adjusted for smoking status, education level, Townsend deprivation index, physical activity, healthy diet score, LDL-C, Lp(a), ApoB, hypertension, diabetes, CKD, fish oil supplements, and statin use).

We obtained genotyping data for the identified causal and pleiotropic loci from the UK Biobank. In instances where no corresponding SNP was available, proxies were selected based on maximum LD. To assess dose–response effects, stratification analyses were performed based on genotypes, examining whether genetic polymorphisms impacted the associations between omega-3 fatty acid levels and AMI risk. Furthermore, interaction analyses were conducted to investigate the moderating effects of genetic factors on the associations, utilizing Model 3 as the foundation while controlling for the initial ten genetic principal components.

For interaction analyses, we applied multiple comparison correction, using a significance threshold of P < 0.05/SNP count. For all other analyses, P-values < 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. All statistical tests were two-tailed and performed using R software version 4.3.3.

Results

Causal loci of omega-3 fatty acids in AMI

Comprehensive details regarding the IVs are documented in Supplementary Tables 1–2. The Two-sample MR analysis revealed that an increase in levels of genetically predicted omega-3 fatty acids correlates with an elevated risk of AMI (IVW-odds ratio [OR] = 1.11, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.03–1.19, P = 0.004). Furthermore, no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Egger intercept: P > 0.05) (refer to Fig. 1A-B). In sensitivity analyses, we excluded FADS-region variants, specifically two SNPs (rs2269928, rs2072114). The results remained consistent, with omega-3 fatty acids still associated with an increased risk of AMI (IVW-OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.07–1.30, P = 0.001). The replicable MR analysis yielded consistent results (IVW-OR = 1.22, 95% CI = 1.04–1.43, P = 0.012) (see Supplementary Table 3). However, it is important to note that after adjusting for LDL-C or ApoB, the association between omega-3 levels and AMI risk disappeared, and the direction of the effect reversed (OR < 1, P > 0.05). In contrast, when adjusting for Lp(a), the results remained unchanged (see Supplementary Table 4).

A similar robust relationship was identified for DPA (OR = 1.19, 95%CI = 1.01–1.42, P = 0.042, P pleiotropy > 0.05). Other sensitivity analyses also support this conclusion (Supplementary Tables 5–6). Although DHA suggested a marginally increased risk for CAD and AMI, and ALA exhibited a marginally decreased risk for CAD and AMI, these associations did not reach statistical significance (IVW-P > 0.05).

Utilizing the Wald ratio method, seven single SNPs were identified that strongly correlated with omega-3 fatty acids (P ≤ 5 × 10− 8, r2 < 0.001, kb = 10,000) and demonstrated associations with alterations in AMI risk, all indicating a positive correlation (see Fig. 1C).

As shown in Fig. 2, genes such as HNF1A, LPL, and ADAM10, along with MCAN, exhibited consistent risk effects on AMI in blood samples (β_SMR > 0, P_SMR < 0.05, P_HEIDI > 0.01). In contrast, other target genes did not reveal significant associations with AMI. Furthermore, no significant associations were identified between any of the target genes and AMI in other tissues, including adipose tissue, blood vessels, and cardiac samples. In conclusion, four causal loci related to omega-3 fatty acids were identified: rs7970695, rs10096633, rs12914626, and rs964184.

Shared pleiotropic loci between omega-3 fatty acids and AMI

The levels of omega-3 fatty acid exhibited a positive association with the incidence of AMI, characterized by a genetic correlation (rg) of 0.109 (standard error [se] = 0.040, P = 0.010) (refer Supplementary Table 7). In our cross-trait meta-analyses, 10 shared independent SNPs attained genome-wide significance concerning the combined phenotype of omega-3 fatty acids and AMI (see Supplementary Table 8). Following the exclusion of SNPs that exhibited linkage disequilibrium with significant SNPs identified in the original single-trait GWAS, we pinpointed 8 novel pleiotropic SNPs: rs11591147, rs10455872, rs186696265, rs112875651, rs76722925, rs174567, rs1077835, and rs429358 (illustrated in Fig. 2A). The mapping of these variants revealed specific associations with various genes: rs11591147 is located within the PCSK9 gene; rs10455872 and rs186696265 are associated with LPA; rs112875651 maps to RP11-136O12.2; rs76722925 correlates with SLC18A1; rs174567 relates to FADS2; rs1077835 is associated with LIPC; and rs429358 is linked to the APOE gene. Moreover, rs11591147, rs10455872, rs186696265, and rs112875651 presented evidence of colocalization (PPH4 > 0.75). After applying multiple corrections, a significant local correlation was observed within a specific genomic region, ranging from chr6: 160,580,497 to 162,169,564, between omega-3 fatty acid levels and AMI (P = 1.45E-06) (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, five additional genomic regions exhibited nominal significance in their association with the combined phenotype (such as chr 8: 126410917–128659111, P = 0.009) (refer to Supplementary Table 9).

In conclusion, analyses incorporating ρ-HESS, cross-trait meta-analysis, and colocalization analysis underscored two novel loci: rs10455872, situated in LPA, was associated with decreased omega-3 fatty acid levels, thereby increasing the risk of AMI. In contrast, rs112875651, located in RP11-136O12.2, was linked to increased omega-3 fatty acid levels while also amplifying the risk of AMI.

Prospective cohort study concerning the effect of genetic polymorphisms

Baseline characteristics

The analysis encompassed a total of 190,275 participants (Fig. 3A), with a mean age of 56.6 years (SD = 8.0), and 55.1% of female. Among these individuals, 6,509 (3.4%) were diagnosed with AMI over a median follow-up period of 13.7 years (interquartile range: 12.9–14.4 years). The baseline characteristics, categorized according to median omega-3 fatty acid levels, along with comprehensive definitions of variables, are presented in Supplementary Tables 10–11. Participants within the high omega-3 fatty acid group exhibited a median omega-3 level of 0.65 (interquartile range [IQR] = 0.57–0.78), in contrast to a median level of 0.38 (IQR = 0.31–0.44) observed in the low omega-3 fatty acid group. Those in the high omega-3 cohort tended to be older and included a higher proportion of females. Furthermore, this group demonstrated higher educational attainment, enhanced socioeconomic status, and superior dietary quality score. However, participants in the high omega-3 group also reported a higher prevalence of hypertension, stroke, and CKD, alongside with elevated levels of HDLC, LDLC, and TG. Moreover, this cohort showed an increased likelihood of receiving antihypertensive and lipid-lowering treatments. Despite these associated risks, the high omega-3 group exhibited a lower incidence of stroke and diabetes, as well as reduced levels of glycated hemoglobin and Lp(a).

Relationship between Omega-3 fatty acids and AMI

A non-linear dose-response relationship was identified between decreasing omega-3 fatty acid levels and an elevated risk of AMI (Fig. 3B). Kaplan-Meier survival analyses indicated that lower concentrations of omega-3 fatty acid levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of AMI (P = 0.009). Elevated levels of omega-3 fatty acids were linked to a reduced risk of myocardial infarction after comprehensive adjustment for confounding factors (aHR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.81–0.90, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 12).

Influence of genetic polymorphisms on phenotypic variation

The frequencies of homozygous genotypes for all SNPs are comprehensively presented in Supplementary Table 13. Specifically, the genotype rs964184 GG comprises 1.7%, rs964184 CG constitutes 23.1%, and rs964184 CC accounts for 75.2%. Furthermore, Fig. 3C illustrates the decline in omega-3 fatty acid levels that correlates with an increasing number of rs964184 C alleles; analogous trends are observed for other SNPs.

Figure 4 presents the estimation of interaction between genetic polymorphisms and omega-3 fatty acid levels regarding AMI risk. Notably, only rs964184 [ZNF259] exhibited a statistically significant interaction with omega-3 fatty acid levels regarding AMI risk (P-interaction = 0.008). The most pronounced protective effect was observed in individuals possessing the rs964184 GG genotype (aHR = 0.62, 95% CI = 0.41–0.94, P = 0.023). In contrast, a reduction in the protective effect was noted among individuals carrying the rs964184 CG (aHR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.73–0.90, P < 0.001) and rs964184 CC genotypes (aHR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.82–0.92, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 14).

Other genotypic variations, specifically rs10455872, rs10096633, and rs261339 (which serve as proxies for rs12914626), also influenced outcomes; however, these did not achieve statistical significance for interactions. Importantly, within the rs10455872 G group, Kaplan-Meier analysis suggested no significant difference in AMI risk between high and low omega-3 fatty acid level groups (P = 0.520). In contrast, within the rs10455872 AA group, a statistically significant difference was identified (P = 0.021). In the rs10455872 GG group, the aHR was determined to be 0.28 (95% CI = 0.02–3.73, P = 0.502). Similar patterns were observed for rs10096633, while the protective benefit associated with rs261339 T appeared diminished.

Discussion

This study elucidates the complex and seemingly paradoxical relationship between omega-3 fatty acid levels and the incidence of AMI. Specifically, our findings from the two-sample MR analyses indicate that a genetically determined increase in omega-3 levels is associated with an elevated risk of AMI. The positive genetic correlation between omega-3 fatty acids and AMI further highlights the complexity of this relationship. However, in the multivariable MR analysis, we observed that the positive association disappeared, suggesting that the previously observed genetically causal relationship may not be robust. Despite this, the UK Biobank cohort suggests that lower omega-3 fatty acid levels are associated with an increased risk of AMI. We hypothesize that some of the causal loci identified in the two-sample MR analysis, or shared loci, may be contributing to a false-positive result. Furthermore, we suggest that different genetic variants might influence omega-3 levels and AMI risk in distinct ways across genotypes, which may explain the observed discrepancies. This association may pertain to our identification of several genetic loci, including rs964184, rs10096633, rs12914626, and rs261339, which are linked to omega-3 fatty acid metabolism.

A significant genetic variant contributing to this paradox is the rs964184 polymorphism situated in the ZNF259 locus. The ZNF259 gene encodes for zinc finger protein 1 (ZPR1), which is essential for transcriptional regulation and cell cycle processes. Previous studies have extensively elucidated its association with lipid metabolism, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes [15, 32–35]. Notably, the rs964184 SNP has been significantly linked to elevated TG levels, and its association with cardiovascular diseases has been well-established [36, 37]. Brautbar et al. indicated that within a population exhibiting atherogenic dyslipidemia, common SNPs and haplotypes in the APOA5-ZNF259 region are strongly associated with the response to combination therapy involving statins and fenofibric acid, particularly regarding high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and apolipoprotein A1 levels [37]. This observation suggests that genetic variations in the ZNF259 region may influence the outcomes of pharmacological treatment. However, prior research has yet to identify the pleiotropic effects of the rs964184 variant, which could influence the benefits of omega-3 fatty acids in preventing AMI. In our study, individuals possessing the rs964184 GG genotype exhibited significant protection against AMI, whereas those with the CC genotype demonstrated a reduction in this protective effect. These findings highlight the critical mediating role of genetic factors in determining the cardiovascular implications of omega-3 fatty acids, suggesting that personalized approaches incorporating genetic profiling may enhance the efficacy of omega-3 supplementation in the prevention of cardiovascular disease. However, the STRENGTH trial (the Long-Term Outcomes Study to Assess Statin Residual Risk with Epanova in High Cardiovascular Risk Patients with Hypertriglyceridemia) showed no reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events with EPA + DHA supplementation and was halted early due to futility [5]. This should be interpreted cautiously, as this trial was constrained by stringent enrollment criteria that often target high-risk subgroups, which could inadvertently amplify the influence of certain genetic variations leading to null or negative overall trial outcomes. This apparent discrepancy with our cohort findings may stem from the fact that large observational cohorts, such as the UK Biobank, involve expansive sample sizes with more balanced distributions of genotypes across diverse populations, potentially diluting or averaging out genotype-specific effects. In addition, there may be other confounding factors such as genetic ancestry which may differ between these studies.

The rs964184 variant may influence lipid metabolism pathways, resulting in alterations to triglyceride levels and lipoprotein profiles. Individuals possessing the CC genotype may experience suboptimal lipid profiles [38], which can attenuate the cardioprotective effects typically associated with omega-3 fatty acids. Omega-3 fatty acids are recognized for their anti-inflammatory attributes, and genetic variations such as rs964184 may modify the expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines or receptors, thereby affecting the inflammatory response in individuals with varying genotypes and modulating the protective effects of omega-3s against AMI. Furthermore, the zinc finger protein family may contribute to gene transcription and cell signaling pathways that are vital for cardiovascular health [39, 40]. Variation in this gene could potentially influence cellular responses to omega-3 fatty acids, resulting in cardio protection among genotypes.

In summary, the rs964184 polymorphism may determine the efficacy of omega-3 fatty acids and contribute to the varying therapeutic responses observed in various populations. We hypothesize that the rs964184 variant within the ZNF259 region, along with other related genetic variations, may partially explain why some individuals experience non-significant benefits from omega-3 supplementation. It is important to note that these causal inferences regarding the role of omega-3 fatty acids and the rs964184 variant in AMI risk are derived solely from genetic instruments such as Mendelian randomization. Further research is imperative to investigate existing clinical trial datasets and to undertake prospective, randomized controlled trials aimed at discerning whether these genetic variations can reliably identify individuals most likely to benefit from omega-3 supplementation in reducing the risk of AMI.

Limitation

While this study offers essential valuable insights into the genetic underpinnings of omega-3 fatty acid levels and their association with AMI risk, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the reliance on observational data and genetic associations limits our ability to fully account for environmental factors and lifestyle factors that may influence both omega-3 levels and AMI risk. And while the interaction P for rs964184 has passed the multiple comparison threshold, it remains at the edge of significance and thus, this conclusion should be interpreted with caution. Secondly, the study population was limited to individuals of European ancestry, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups with different genetic backgrounds. Further studies in diverse populations are warranted to validate these associations. Finally, the complex interplay between genetic polymorphisms, environment exposures, and phenotypic expressions requires deeper investigation to clarify the mechanisms linking omega-3 fatty acids to AMI risk.

Conclusions

The ZNF259 rs964184 variant appears to negatively affect omega-3 fatty acid concentrations, potentially reducing their cardioprotective effect against myocardial infarction. Future clinical studies should incorporate genetic factors and investigate the underlying mechanisms to better understand these gene-nutrient interactions.
