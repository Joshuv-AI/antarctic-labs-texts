On the Role of Transformer Feed-Forward Layers in Nonlinear In-Context Learning
Haoyuan Sun, Ali Jadbabaie, Navid Azizan

Abstract
Transformer-based models demonstrate a remarkable ability for in-context learning (ICL), where they can adapt to unseen tasks from a few prompt examples without parameter updates. Notably, recent research has provided insight into how the Transformer architecture can perform ICL, showing that the optimal linear self-attention (LSA) mechanism can implement one step of gradient descent for linear least-squares objectives when trained on random linear regression tasks. Building upon this understanding of linear ICL, we investigate ICL for nonlinear function classes. We first show that LSA is inherently incapable of solving problems that go beyond linear least-squares objectives, underscoring why prior solutions cannot readily extend to nonlinear ICL tasks. To overcome this limitation, we investigate a mechanism combining LSA with feed-forward layers that are inspired by the gated linear units (GLU) commonly found in modern Transformer architectures. We show that this combination empowers the Transformer to perform nonlinear ICL, specifically by implementing one step of gradient descent on a polynomial kernel regression loss. Furthermore, we show that multiple blocks of our GLU-LSA model implement block coordinate descent in this polynomial kernel space. Our findings highlight the distinct roles of attention and feed-forward layers, demonstrating that the feed-forward components provide a mechanism by which Transformers gain nonlinear capabilities for ICL.

1
Introduction

In recent years,
large language models
(LLM) based on the Transformer architecture
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
have achieved remarkable successes across numerous domains such as computer vision
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
)
, speech recognition
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
, multi-modal data processing
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
, and human reasoning tasks
(
OpenAI 2023
)
.
Beyond these practical achievements, Transformers have also demonstrated the extraordinary ability to perform
in-context learning
(ICL)
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Wei et al. 2022b
;
Min et al. 2022
)
.
In ICL, a model adapts to different tasks by leveraging a
prompt
containing a short sequence of examples without requiring updates to its parameters.

The study of in-context learning has attracted significant attention in recent literature.
Formally, given a prompt composed of input-label pairs and a query,
(x_{1},f(x_{1}),x_{2},f(x_{2}),\dots,x_{n},f(x_{n}),x_{\textsf{query}})
, a Transformer model is said to learn a function class
\mathcal{F}
in context if it can accurately predict
f(x_{\textsf{query}})
for previously unseen functions
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
Empirical studies by
Garg et al. 2022
have demonstrated the efficacy of Transformers in performing in-context learning for a range of function classes such as linear functions, decision trees, and ReLU neural networks.

Building on these insights,
Akyürek et al. 2023
and
Von Oswald et al. 2023
established that Transformers can learn linear functions in context by implicitly implementing gradient descent over a linear regression objective on the prompt’s examples and provided a construction that facilitates such a mechanism.
Extending this perspective, subsequent theoretical analysis by
Ahn et al. 2024b
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Mahankali et al. 2024
showed that under suitably defined distributions over the prompts, the optimal single-layer
linear self-attention
(LSA) mechanism can effectively implement one step of gradient descent.

Despite these advancements, the theoretical understanding of ICL for nonlinear function classes remains underdeveloped.
Prior theoretical explanations primarily focus on the attention layers of Transformers.
However, the role of feed-forward layers in ICL tasks, particularly for nonlinear functions, has been underexplored and may be key to a complete understanding.
In this work, we demonstrate that the feed-forward layers in Transformers may represent a crucial yet overlooked component to advance the theoretical understanding of ICL for nonlinear functions.
As a representative case, we show that using standard feed-forward layer architectures, such as those based on the
gated linear units
(GLU), enables Transformers to learn quadratic and polynomial functions in context, illustrating a broader significance for feed-forward layers within Transformer models.

Our contributions.

In this work, we investigate the pivotal role of feed-forward layers in enabling Transformers to learn nonlinear functions in context.
Specifically, we show that Transformers with alternating linear attention and feed-forward layers can learn quadratic and higher-order polynomial functions in context by implicitly implementing gradient descent updates with respect to a collection of nonlinear features.
Our results overcome the inherent limitations of attention-only mechanisms in nonlinear ICL, identify key properties of optimal layer weights, and present scalable constructions for nonlinear ICL with deeper models.
The key contributions of our paper are as follows:

\blacksquare

We establish the crucial role of feed-forward layers in enabling in-context learning of nonlinear target functions. In Section
3.1
, we show that when the target functions are nonlinear,
no
deep linear self-attention (LSA) network can achieve lower in-context learning loss than a linear least-squares predictor.
Further, in Section
3.2
, we draw inspiration from modern Transformer architectures and show that a Transformer block consisting of one GLU-like feed-forward layer and one LSA layer can implement one step of gradient descent with respect to a quadratic kernel.

\blacksquare

In Section
3.3
, we analyze the Transformer model featuring the LSA-GLU mechanism.
We derive optimal conditions for the weights within both the GLU-like feed-forward layer and the attention layer.
In particular, we show that the optimal choice of the feed-forward layer must correspond to a kernel function that computes all possible quadratic features.
Furthermore, we show that the optimal attention layer implements gradient descent on the kernel feature space with a preconditioner approximately equal to the negative inverse of the features’ covariance matrix.

\blacksquare

In Section
4
, we describe several challenges with the initial construction in Section
3.2
and propose a deeper Transformer model to overcome them, yielding improved results for learning quadratic functions in-context.
Furthermore, in Section
4.2
, we highlight that the same approach can be easily generalized to handle more complex nonlinear tasks, including learning higher-order polynomial functions in context, by distributing the computation of kernel functions across multiple layers.
We support our analysis throughout this section with extensive numerical experiments.

Related works.

This section highlights the most directly related works in the literature; a more detailed survey of the literature can be found in Appendix
A
.

While prior work has primarily focused on the roles of attention layers in in-context learning, the significance of feed-forward layers has been largely overlooked.
For instance,
Zhang et al. 2024b
showed that incorporating an MLP feed-forward layer allows for implementing gradient descent with learnable
nonzero
initialization.
However, the results of this work are still limited to linear ICL.
To the best of our knowledge,
Zhang et al. 2024b
provides the only prior theoretical analysis exploring a potential mechanism by which Transformer feed-forward layers contribute to ICL.

While the theoretical analysis of in-context learning for nonlinear function classes has gathered significant attention in the literature, the existing works remain limited.
First,
Cheng et al. 2024
shows that attention endowed with certain nonlinear activation functions can implement gradient descent on a function space induced by this nonlinearity.
While this result allows for learning a range of nonlinear functions, it requires a non-standard form of attention.
Another line of work
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024b
)
studies how multi-head softmax attention can learn target functions that are represented by a
fixed
nonlinear feature function.
Both of these works impose some significant limitations on the input distribution.

Huang et al. 2024
assumes that the inputs
x_{i}
are drawn from an orthonormal set of vectors, while
Yang et al. 2024b
assumes the list of
x_{i}
’s is a fixed dictionary identical across all prompts. In contrast, our work assumes inputs are drawn i.i.d. from the space of all real vectors, a significantly less restrictive assumption.

2
Problem Setting

2.1
In-context learning

We are interested in the in-context learning problem, where a
prompt
consists of a sequence of input-label pairs
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
along with a query
x_{\textsf{query}}
, where
y_{i}=f(x_{i})
for an unknown
target function

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Given this prompt, our goal is to make a prediction
\hat{y}
so that
\hat{y}\approx y_{\textsf{query}}=f(x_{\textsf{query}})
.
In this paper, the prompt is represented as a matrix
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{(\bar{d}+1)\times(n+1)}
:

Z=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1&1\\
x_{1}&x_{1}&\cdots&x_{1}&x_{n+1}:=x_{\textsf{query}}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{n}&0\end{bmatrix},

(1)

where the zeros have dimension
\bar{d}-d-1
and the bottommost zero hides the unknown quantity
y_{\textsf{query}}
that we seek to predict.
In contrast to prior works (e.g.
Ahn et al. 2024a
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Mahankali et al. 2024
), we include a row of ones to accommodate inhomogeneity in the nonlinear target function
f
. Also, we introduce rows of zeros to embed the input in a higher-dimensional space.

We sample the prompts by first drawing i.i.d. Gaussian inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
.
In this work, we consider random
quadratic
target functions of the form:

\displaystyle f(x)=w_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}x_{i}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq d}w_{ij}x_{i}x_{j},\quad\text{where }w_{i},w_{ij}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

For a symmetric matrix
M
, we define
\mathrm{svec}(M)
as the vector obtained by flattening
M
but which does not repeat the off-diagonal entries and scales them by a factor of 2.
And let
\mathrm{smat}(\cdot)
be the inverse of this operation.
Then, in compact notation, we equivalently write:

f(x)=\begin{bmatrix}1&x\end{bmatrix}W\begin{bmatrix}1\\
x\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{for }\mathrm{svec}(W)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),

where
W
is a
(d+1)\times(d+1)
random matrix and
\mathrm{svec}(W)
has length
\binom{d+1}{2}+(d+1)=\binom{d+2}{2}
.
Given this class of target functions, we set
y_{i}=f(x_{i})
.
Then, prompts
Z
are sampled based on the randomness in the inputs
x_{i}
and the coefficients
w
of the target quadratic function.

2.2
Transformer architecture

Given a prompt
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}:=\mathbb{R}^{(\bar{d}+1)\times(n+1)}
, the
Transformer
model is a sequence-to-sequence mapping
\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
that consists of self-attention and feed-forward layers:

\blacksquare

Attention Layer:
The most crucial component of Transformers is the
self-attention
mechanism
(
Bahdanau et al. 2015
)
, which can be written as

\hskip-5.0pt\mathsf{Attention}(Z)=Z+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{W_{V}}ZM\,\sigma\bigg((\mathbf{W_{K}}Z)^{\top}(\mathbf{W_{Q}}Z)\bigg).

Here,
\sigma
is the
softmax
function,
\mathbf{W_{Q}},\mathbf{W_{K}},\mathbf{W_{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
are learnable matrix parameters, and
mask matrix
M=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{I}_{n},0)
erases the attention score with the last column, which contains
x_{\textsf{query}}
, due to the asymmetry that
y_{\textsf{query}}
is not part of the prompt.
Following the conventions established by
Von Oswald et al. 2023
, we consider a variant called
linear self-attention
(LSA), which omits the softmax activation and reparameterizes the weights as
\mathbf{P}:=\mathbf{W_{V}},\mathbf{Q}:=\mathbf{W_{V}}^{\top}\mathbf{W_{Q}}
:

\mathsf{attn}(Z)=Z+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{P}ZM\,\bigg(Z^{\top}\mathbf{Q}Z\bigg).

(2)

We note that LSA is practically relevant —
Ahn et al. 2024b
found that the optimization landscape of LSA closely mirrors that of full Transformers.
Furthermore, there has been significant progress on adopting linear attention for more efficient LLMs
(
Dao and Gu 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024a
)
.

\blacksquare

Feed-forward Layer:
The original Transformer model
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
employs a
multi-layer perceptron
(MLP) for the feed-forward layer.
However, recent studies have shown that variants of the
gated linear unit
(GLU) offer improved performance in large language models
(
Shazeer 2020
)
.
A GLU is the element-wise product of two linear projections and was first proposed in
Dauphin et al. 2017
.
A commonly used variant is the
SwiGLU
layer, where one of the projections passes through a
swish
nonlinearity.
In this paper, we consider the simplest GLU variant without any nonlinear activations, which is referred to as a “bilinear” layer
(
Mnih and Hinton 2007
)
.
For matrix parameters
\mathbf{W}_{0},\mathbf{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}\times\bar{d}}
, we define

\mathsf{bilin}(Z)=Z+\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}_{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\end{bmatrix}Z\right)\odot\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}_{1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\end{bmatrix}Z\right),

(3)

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product.
And for computing this layer, we mask the last row because of the asymmetry that the final label
y_{\mathsf{query}}
is absent from the prompt.
For the rest of the paper, we refer to (
3
) as the
bilinear feed-forward layer
to avoid any ambiguity.

For a Transformer
\mathsf{TF}
, let
Z^{(\ell)}_{\mathsf{TF}}
(or simply
Z^{(\ell)}
when the Transformer is clear from the context) denote the output of its
\ell
th layer.
In this paper, we examine two types of Transformers.

\blacksquare

A
linear Transformer
consists solely of LSA layers.
A linear Transformer
\mathsf{TF_{lin}}
with
L
layers is defined as:

Z^{(0)}_{\textsf{lin}}=Z,\quad Z^{(\ell+1)}_{\textsf{lin}}=\mathsf{attn}(Z^{(\ell)}_{\textsf{lin}}),\quad\ell=0,\dots,L-1.

\blacksquare

A
bilinear Transformer
consists of alternating LSA and bilinear feed-forward layers.
A bilinear Transformer
\mathsf{TF_{bilin}}
with
2L
layers follows the forward rule:

\displaystyle Z^{(0)}_{\textsf{bilin}}=Z,\;\;Z^{(2\ell+1)}_{\textsf{bilin}}=\mathsf{bilin}(Z^{(2\ell)}_{\textsf{bilin}}),Z^{(2\ell+2)}_{\textsf{bilin}}=\mathsf{attn}(Z^{(2\ell+1)}_{\textsf{bilin}}),\quad\ell=0,\dots,L-1.

We note that this differs slightly from the canonical definition of Transformers in that we have swapped the order of the attention and feed-forward layers.
This choice is to simplify the exposition of our construction in Section
3.2
and does not affect the expressive power of the Transformer.

2.3
In-context learning objective

We now define the objective of the in-context learning problem.
Given an
L
-layer Transformer
\mathsf{TF}
, we let its prediction
\hat{y}_{\mathsf{TF}}
be the
(\bar{d}+1,n+1)
th entry of its final layer, i.e.
\hat{y}_{\mathsf{TF}}=Z_{\mathsf{TF}}^{(L)}[\bar{d}+1,n+1]
.
Then, its
in-context learning loss
is the expected squared error with respect to the distribution of the prompts
Z
:

\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{TF})=\mathbb{E}_{Z}[(\hat{y}_{\mathsf{TF}}+y_{\textsf{query}})^{2}],

(4)

so that the Transformer’s prediction
\hat{y}_{\mathsf{TF}}
approximates
-y_{\textsf{query}}
.
Our objective is to find Transformers that minimize this loss
\mathcal{L}
.

3
Solving Quadratic In-context Learning

Recent literature has explored the ability of linear self-attention (LSA) mechanisms to learn linear function classes in context.
In this section, we demonstrate that LSA is inherently limited when it comes to nonlinear in-context learning (ICL).
To address this limitation, inspired by modern Transformer architectures, we study a mechanism that combines LSA with bilinear feed-forward layers, enabling the model to solve quadratic ICL through kernel regression.
Our approach is quite general, and as we show in Section
4.2
, it can be extended to handle ICL of higher-degree polynomials.

3.1
Limitations of linear self-attention in nonlinear ICL

Prior works
(
Mahankali et al. 2024
;
Ahn et al. 2024a
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
have shown that a single layer of linear self-attention (LSA) can learn linear functions in context by implementing one step of gradient descent with respect to a linear least-squares objective.
Formally, the optimal one-layer LSA model that minimizes the linear ICL loss has weights

\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{d\times d}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\mathbf{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

(5)

and its prediction given prompt
Z
can be expressed as

\hat{y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{\textsf{query}}^{\top}\Gamma x_{i}y_{i}.

This is equivalent to the prediction obtained after one step of preconditioned gradient descent with zero initialization and linear regression objective
L(w)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i}(w^{\top}x_{i}+y_{i})^{2}
.

Notably, to learn linear functions in context, the optimal attention weights are sparse.
Thus, a natural question arises: can we leverage these unused weights in LSA to learn richer classes of functions in context?
The following proposition answers this question by showing that, even with multiple layers, a linear Transformer consisting of only LSA layers
cannot
effectively learn a nonlinear target function in context.

Proposition 1
.

Consider any fixed target function
f
and inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
.
The in-context learning prompts are sampled according to the form as described in (
1
).
Then, for any linear Transformer
\mathsf{TF_{lin}}
(regardless of the number of layers), its expected prediction loss satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{x}[(\hat{y}_{\mathsf{TF}_{\mathsf{lin}}}+y_{\textsf{query}})^{2}]\geq\min_{\alpha,\beta}\mathbb{E}_{x}[(\left\langle x_{\textsf{query}},\beta\right\rangle+\alpha+y_{\textsf{query}})^{2}].

This result reveals the inherent limitation: a linear Transformer’s prediction performance is bounded by that achieved by solving linear regression, preventing it from effectively learning a nonlinear target function in context.
For a complete proof of this result, please see Appendix
B
.

3.2
Quadratic ICL with bilinear feed-forward layers

In order to overcome the limitation of LSA in handling nonlinear functions, drawing inspiration from modern Transformers, we study a mechanism that augments the linear Transformer architecture with bilinear feed-forward layers.
In particular, we consider two layers in a bilinear Transformer and define a
bilinear Transformer block

\mathsf{BTFB}
as the composition of a bilinear feed-forward layer followed by a linear self-attention layer, i.e.,

\mathsf{BTFB}(Z):=\mathsf{attn}\circ\mathsf{bilin}(Z).

In a
2L
-layer bilinear Transformer
\mathsf{TF_{bilin}}
, there are
L
bilinear Transformer blocks.
We claim that for sufficiently large embedding dimension
\bar{d}
and context length
n
, a single bilinear Transformer block can learn quadratic target functions in context.

Theorem 2
.

Given the quadratic in-context learning problem as described in Section
2.1
and embedding dimension
\bar{d}=\binom{d+2}{2}
, there exists a bilinear Transformer block
\mathsf{BTFB}
such that

\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{BTFB})=\frac{(d+2)(d+1)+d}{2n}\in O(d^{2}/n).

A formal proof of this statement can be found in Appendix
C
.
At a high level, we can achieve this bound in two steps.

\blacksquare

The bilinear feed-forward layer computes quadratic features, expanding the input space to capture every quadratic term in the target function.

\blacksquare

The labels
y_{i}
’s are then linear in these quadratic features, which allows the attention layer to solve for a least-squares objective in context.

Step 1:
To understand how the bilinear feed-forward layer computes the necessary quadratic terms, we recall the definition (
3
).
Specifically, we denote
Z^{+}:=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{W}_{0},0)Z\odot\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{W}_{1},0)Z
.
Let
\mathbf{W}_{0,j}
be the
j
th row of
\mathbf{W}_{0}
, then we have

Z^{+}[i,j]=(\mathbf{W}_{0,j}x_{i})\cdot(\mathbf{W}_{1,j}x_{i})=x_{i}^{\top}({\mathbf{W}_{0,j}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{1,j})x_{i},

(6)

which is quadratic in
x_{i}
.
Then, it is not difficult to see that there exist bilinear layer weights where

\displaystyle\textsf{bilin}(Z)=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{x}_{1}&\bar{x}_{2}&\cdots&\bar{x}_{n}&\bar{x}_{\textsf{query}}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{n}&0\end{bmatrix},\text{ with}

(7)

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i}=(1,x_{i}[1],\dots,x_{i}[d],x_{i}[1]^{2}-1,\dots,x_{i}[1]x_{i}[d],x_{i}[2]^{2}-1,\dots,x_{i}[2]x_{i}[d],\dots,x_{i}[d]^{2}-1),

which contains every quadratic monomial in
x_{i}
. So, the label
y_{i}
is linear in
\bar{x}_{i}
.

Step 2:
Given the bilinear layer that implements (
7
), the subsequent attention layer receives a prompt consisting of input-label pairs
(\bar{x}_{i},y_{i})
, where
y_{i}
is linear in
\bar{x}_{i}
.
With the same choice of weights as in (
5
), the LSA layer performs linear regression on pairs
(\bar{x}_{i},y_{i})
, and thus the final prediction of the bilinear Transformer block can be written as

\hat{y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{x}_{\textsf{query}}^{\top}\Gamma\bar{x}_{i}y_{i}.

We note that this solution corresponds to solving for kernel regression with a kernel function
\kappa(x_{\textsf{query}},x_{i})=\bar{x}_{\textsf{query}}^{\top}\Gamma\bar{x}_{i}
.
Therefore, the bilinear layer enables a bilinear Transformer block to express this kernel space, effectively implementing gradient descent on kernel regression.

3.3
Analysis of the optimal bilinear Transformer block

In Section
3.2
, we showed that a bilinear Transformer block learn quadratic functions in-context.
In this section, we investigate what choices of the Transformer weights can minimize the quadratic ICL loss.
Our analysis can be broken down into two steps:

\blacksquare

We show that the kernel function in (
7
) generates a minimal representation of the quadratic features. Hence, our choice of the bilinear layer in Section
3.2
is optimal (up to change of coordinates).

\blacksquare

Given this choice of the bilinear layer, we find attention weights that approximately minimize the ICL loss. We show that our solution converges to the negative inverse of the features’ covariance as the context length
n\to\infty
.

Step 1:
To show that the collection of features generated by the kernel function (
7
) is minimal, we construct an inner product space in which the features computed by (
7
) are linearly independent.
This argument is formalized as follows:

Lemma 3
.

Given random vector
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define an inner product on random variables as
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle:=\mathbb{E}_{x}[uv]
. Then, the following set of
\binom{d+2}{2}
random variables

\left\{v_{ij}\vphantom{x^{2}}\right\}_{0\leq i\leq j\leq d}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=j=0,\\
x[j]&\text{if }i=0,j\in\{1,\dots,d\},\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x[i]^{2}-1)&\text{if }i=j\in\{1,\dots,d\},\\
x[i]x[j]&\text{if }1\leq i<j\leq d,\end{cases}

(8)

are orthonormal under this inner product space.

Note that (
8
) forms a basis for the span of the features in (
7
).
Consider a bilinear layer whose output has the form

\textsf{bilin}(Z)=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{x}_{1}&\widetilde{x}_{2}&\cdots&\widetilde{x}_{n}&\widetilde{x}_{\textsf{query}}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}.

This implements a kernel mapping
x\mapsto\widetilde{x}
, and then
\widetilde{x}
is a
\bar{d}
-dimensional random variable.
Therefore, we can think of
\widetilde{x}
as a collection of
\bar{d}
vectors in this inner product space of random variables.
Then, we can apply a linear algebra argument to lower bound the ICL loss whenever
\widetilde{x}
does not span (
7
).

Proposition 4
.

Given the quadratic ICL problem as described in Section
2.1
,
we consider a bilinear Transformer block
\mathsf{BTFB}
whose bilinear layer implements a kernel mapping
x\mapsto\widetilde{x}
.
For the inner product space defined in Lemma
3
, if the orthogonal projection of
\widetilde{x}
onto the span of (
8
) has dimension
\widetilde{d}<\binom{d+2}{2}
, then the in-context learning loss is lower bounded by

\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{BTFB})\geq\binom{d+2}{2}-\widetilde{d}.

Therefore, the optimal bilinear Transformer block must have a bilinear layer that implements the same kernel as (
7
), up to an invertible linear map on the inner product space.
The full proof of these statements can be found in Appendices
D
and
E
.

Remark 1
.

As an immediate consequence of Proposition
4
, if the prompt’s embedding dimension
\bar{d}
is less than
\binom{d+2}{2}
, then the ICL loss is lower bounded by
\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{BTFB})\geq\binom{d+2}{2}-\bar{d}.

Remark 2
.

Our technique used to show this statement is general and applies to any choice of feed-forward network.
Therefore, the lower bound above is another fundamental limitation of the self-attention mechanism.

Step 2:
Recall from Section
3.2
, we discussed that the label
y_{i}
’s are linear in the features
\bar{x}_{i}
’s, and therefore the attention layer can be thought of as implementing linear ICL on this kernel feature space.
However, to find the optimal attention weights, we cannot directly appeal to the prior literature such as
Ahn et al. 2024a
because the distribution of
\bar{x}_{i}
is
not Gaussian
.
While there is no closed-form solution in our setting, we can still approximate the optimal weights.
And similar to the linear ICL setting, the optimal attention weights converge to the negative inverse covariance of the feature vectors
\bar{x}_{i}
’s in the limit as the prompt length
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 5
.

Consider a linear Transformer block
\mathsf{BTFB}
and fix its bilinear layer to correspond the kernel
x\mapsto\bar{x}
as described in (
7
).
Then, the optimal choice of self-attention layer that minimizes the in-context learning loss
\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{BTFB})
has weights

\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{d\times d}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\mathbf{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma^{\star}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

where
\Gamma^{\star}
satisfies

\left\lVert\Gamma^{\star}+\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}\bar{x}^{\top}]^{-1}\right\rVert_{F}\leq n^{-1/2}\sqrt{\frac{(d+2)(d+1)+d}{d+2-\sqrt{d^{2}+4d}}}\in O\left(\frac{d^{3/2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

When the prompt length
n
is large, the optimal weight
\Gamma^{*}
closely approximates the negative inverse of the feature covariance matrix
\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}\bar{x}^{\top}]^{-1}
.
We also note that
\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}\bar{x}^{\top}]^{-1}
is the limit of the Gram matrix

Therefore, given sufficiently many prompt examples, the optimal attention layer in the bilinear Transformer block approximates the ordinary least-squares solution with respect to the quadratic features.
The proof of this result can be found in Appendix

4
Deep Bilinear Transformers

While our results in Section
3.2
present an approach for learning quadratic functions in context with a single bilinear Transformer block, this initial approach faces several challenges.
First, we note that the computational complexity of the Transformer block scales quadratically in the prompt’s embedding dimension
\bar{d}
.
As shown in Proposition
4
, the embedding dimension
\bar{d}
must be on the order of
\Theta(d^{2})
, which leads to prohibitively expensive computations.
Second, one Transformer block only implements one step of gradient descent, but it is desirable to perform multiple steps to achieve better accuracy.
To address these shortcomings, in Section
4.1
, we introduce a
deep
bilinear Transformer, in which each Transformer block performs one step of block-coordinate descent on a quadratic kernel regression.
Notably, this approach can be easily generalized to higher-degree polynomial function classes, which we shall investigate in Section
4.2
.
To verify these observations, we also perform various numerical experiments on these deep bilinear Transformers.

4.1
Deep bilinear Transformers as block-coordinate descent solvers

To address the aforementioned limitations of a single bilinear Transformer block, we consider a
deep
bilinear Transformer network by stacking together multiple bilinear Transformer blocks.
In particular, we show that in a deep bilinear Transformer, we can spread out the calculations required for any number of kernel space gradient descent updates over multiple layers of the network.
Thus, we can solve the quadratic ICL task with embedding space
\bar{d}
less than the total number of features
\binom{d+2}{2}
.

Recall from Section
3.2
that a single bilinear Transformer block corresponds to one step of preconditioned gradient descent with respect to a quadratic kernel.
In particular, for the weights
w\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{d+2}{2}}
, we define the quadratic function:

\hskip 20.00003ptf(x;w)=\begin{bmatrix}1&x\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{smat}(w)\begin{bmatrix}1\\
x\end{bmatrix}.

Then, given the prompt’s examples
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})
, the Transformer’s prediction corresponds to a gradient descent step with some preconditioner
\Gamma

\displaystyle\hat{y}=f(x^{\textsf{query}};w^{+}),\quad w^{+}=\Gamma\nabla_{w}[L(w)]_{w=0},\quad L(w)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x_{i};w)+y_{i})^{2}.

This equivalence requires the bilinear layer to output all quadratic features.
If the embedding dimension is insufficient to fit all of them, we instead construct bilinear layers that only output a subset (block) of these monomials.
Then, the subsequent attention layer selectively performs a partial gradient descent update for a kernel regression over a subset of quadratic monomials.
This means that the bilinear Transformer across all of its layers implements block-coordinate descent step by step.

Specifically, we define a
block-coordinate descent
update, where for blocks

b_{\ell}

(9)

4.2
Learning higher-order polynomials in-context

In the previous section, we showed that a deep bilinear Transformer can split up the calculations of the full kernel function over multiple layers.
We extend this intuition and highlight that the same construction can be used to compute a kernel generating higher-order features.
Let
\bar{x}_{i}
be the feature vectors generated by the first bilinear feed-forward layer.
Then, by the same argument as (
6
), the second bilinear feed-forward layer with weights
\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{0},\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{1}
compute features of the form

(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{0,j}\bar{x}_{i})\cdot(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{1,j}\bar{x}_{i})=\bar{x}_{i}^{\top}\left({{\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{0,j}}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{1,j}\right)\bar{x}_{i},\quad j=1,\dots,\bar{d}.

Since
\bar{x}_{i}
’s are quadratic in the original input
x_{i}
’s, the output of the second bilinear feed-forward layer can compute features up to degree 4.
Thus, a deep bilinear Transformer is capable of learning higher-order polynomials in context.

The differences between the two models confirm our proposed mechanism through which bilinear Transformers can learn higher-order features in context.

To validate this mechanism of composing features over multiple layers, we recall that the inputs
x_{i}
are embedded into the prompt in the form of
[1,x_{i},\mathbf{0}]\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
, where the first
d+1
coordinates contain the original inputs and the next
\bar{d}-d-1
coordinates can be used to store the additional features.
If we partition the weights of a bilinear layer as

\mathbf{W}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{i}&\mathbf{B}_{i}\\
\mathbf{\widetilde{W}}_{i}&\mathbf{C}_{i}\end{bmatrix},\mathbf{\widetilde{W}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\bar{d}-d-1)\times(d+1)},

then
\mathbf{A}_{i},\mathbf{B}_{i}
are used to overwrite the space intended for the original inputs, and
\mathbf{B}_{i},\mathbf{C}_{i}
use the additional features from the previous layers to compute new features.
If we set
\mathbf{A}_{i},\mathbf{B}_{i},\mathbf{C}_{i}
all to be zero, so that

\mathbf{W}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
\mathbf{\widetilde{W}}_{i}&0\end{bmatrix},\mathbf{\widetilde{W}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\bar{d}-d-1)\times(d+1)},

(11)

then successive bilinear layers can only use the original inputs to calculate the new features.
Therefore, if our construction accurately reflects how the bilinear Transformers perform ICL, then this sparse version of bilinear feed-forward layers would prevent us from learning higher-order polynomials in-context.

To demonstrate this idea, we conduct an experiment where the target function is a cubic over 4 variables:

\displaystyle f(x;w)=w_{0}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{4}w_{i}x[i]+w_{12}x[1]x[2]+w_{23}x[2]x[3]

\displaystyle+w_{34}x[3]x[4]+w_{123}x[1]x[2]x[3]+w_{234}x[2]x[3]x[4],

where all of the coefficient
w
’s are drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
We consider 10-layer bilinear Transformers with either the dense (
3
) or the sparse (
11
) versions of the bilinear feed-forward layers.
We set the parameters to be
n=100
,
d=4
, and
\bar{d}=15
.
We note that there are 35 distinct monomials in the cubic function class of 4 variables, but we consider a cubic with just 10 terms.

As shown in Figure
2
, a bilinear Transformer with dense (
3
) bilinear layer weights significantly outperforms its counterpart with only sparse (
11
) bilinear layer weights.
Therefore, the bilinear Transformer with sparse bilinear layers fails to effectively learn the cubic target function in context because the sparse design inhibits the composition of higher-order features.
This observation matches up with our proposed role of the bilinear feed-forward layers in higher-order ICL.
Furthermore, the sparse bilinear Transformer can still learn quadratic features in context, which is why it still outperforms a linear Transformer of similar size.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we investigated the crucial role of feed-forward layers in enabling Transformer models to perform in-context learning (ICL) of nonlinear functions.
We first showed that previous theoretical analyses focusing on linear Transformer architectures are insufficient for learning nonlinear function classes in context.
To overcome this limitation, we studied a bilinear Transformer model composed of alternating linear self-attention and bilinear feed-forward layers inspired by modern Transformer architectures.
Using the quadratic function class as an illustrative example, we showed that a bilinear Transformer can solve nonlinear ICL tasks by implementing gradient descent over a kernel space generated by the feed-forward layers.
We further demonstrated the scalability of this approach to learn higher-order polynomials in context — underscoring the expressive power of our approach and advancing the theoretical understanding of nonlinear ICL in Transformers.

Several directions for future research may emerge from this study.
One promising avenue is to investigate how different choices of nonlinear feed-forward layers could affect Transformers’ ICL capabilities.
In this paper, we focused our analysis on the GLU-like bilinear feed-forward layers, while other types of feed-forward layers can be used to induce kernel functions of different properties.
Moreover, in our construction of the deep bilinear Transformer in Section
4.1
, there are many possible ways to split the calculations of the kernel function.
It remains an open question whether there is a “preferred” ordering of the features across the layers.

Appendix A
Extended Related Works

Motivated by the successes of the Transformer architecture, there has been tremendous progress in understanding its algorithmic power.
Independent of the studies on ICL, many works sought to characterize the expressivity of Transformers from an approximation-theoretic perspective.
These studies established that Transformers can effectively approximate a wide range of functions and algorithms (e.g.,
Pérez et al. 2021
,
Wei et al. 2022a
,
Giannou et al. 2023
,
Olsson et al. 2022
,
Bai et al. 2024
,
Edelman et al. 2022
,
Reddy 2024
).

In the context of ICL, numerous empirical studies have examined the Transformer’s capabilities to learn a variety of tasks and settings in context, including linear regression
[
Garg et al. 2022
]
, discrete boolean functions
[
Bhattamishra et al. 2024
]
, representation learning
[
Guo et al. 2024
]
, reinforcement learning
[
Lin et al. 2024
]
, and learning causal structures
[
Nichani et al. 2024
]
.
To explain these observations,
Akyürek et al. 2023
,
Dai et al. 2023
,
Li et al. 2023
proposed that Transformers can learn in context by implementing learning algorithms.
In particular, for linear regression,
Von Oswald et al. 2023
proposed that Transformers implement gradient descent with zero initialization and provided a simple construction for a single-layer linear attention model.
Subsequently,
Ahn et al. 2024a
,
Mahankali et al. 2024
,
Zhang et al. 2024a
,
Wu et al. 2024
showed that the optimal single-layer linear self-attention with respect to some suitably chosen in-context learning loss closely corresponds to the construction proposed by
Von Oswald et al. 2023
.
Additionally,
Chen et al. 2024
extended the analysis of linear ICL to softmax self-attention, where they found the optimal softmax attention implements a similar mechanism as the linear attention setting.

Our paper is closely related to this body of research and we draw several inspirations from it.
We highlight some of these ideas and how our work improves upon these existing results:

\blacksquare

Section 5 of
Mahankali et al. 2024
showed that even for nonlinear target functions, the optimal one-layer LSA minimizing the in-context learning loss corresponds to solving a linear least-square problem. Our result in Section
3.1
extends this conclusion to an arbitrary number of LSA layers.
Additionally, we propose a solution to this limitation by incorporating feed-forward layers.

\blacksquare

Proposition 2 in
Von Oswald et al. 2023
suggested a similar idea to ours that the feed-forward layers in a transformer implement a kernel trick, but did not prove the optimality of this construction, nor did it provide examples beyond a one-dimensional input. In contrast, we prove the optimality of this approach in Sections
3.2
–
3.3
and offer a scalable construction in Section
4
to more effectively apply deep Transformer models to learn nonlinear functions in-context.

While prior work has primarily focused on the roles of attention layers in in-context learning, the significance of feed-forward layers has been largely overlooked.
For instance,
Zhang et al. 2024b
showed that incorporating an MLP feed-forward layer allows for implementing gradient descent with learnable
nonzero
initialization.
However, the results of this work are still limited to linear ICL.
To the best of our knowledge,
Zhang et al. 2024b
provides the only prior theoretical analysis exploring a potential mechanism by which Transformer feed-forward layers contribute to ICL.

While the theoretical analysis of in-context learning for nonlinear function classes has gathered significant attention in the literature, the existing works remain limited.
First,
Cheng et al. 2024
shows that attention endowed with certain nonlinear activation functions can implement gradient descent on a function space induced by this nonlinearity.
While this result allows for learning a range of nonlinear functions, it requires a non-standard form of attention.
Another line of work
[
Huang et al. 2024
,
Yang et al. 2024b
]
studies how multi-head softmax attention can learn target functions that are represented by a
fixed
nonlinear feature function.
Both of these works impose some significant limitations on the input distribution.

Huang et al. 2024
assumes that the inputs
x_{i}
are drawn from an orthonormal set of vectors, while
Yang et al. 2024b
assumes the list of
x_{i}
’s is a fixed dictionary identical across all prompts. In contrast, our work assumes inputs are drawn i.i.d. from the space of all real vectors, a significantly less restrictive assumption.
Lastly, on the more empirical end,
He et al. 2025
conducted extensive studies of multi-head softmax attention models for various ICL tasks.
The discoveries from this work reveal some potential avenues for extending the theoretical analysis of softmax attention for nonlinear ICL.

where we used our claim above on the 3rd and 4th line, and we used that fact that

Therefore, the population loss of the attention network’s prediction cannot be better than the best linear model over the original prompt.

Note that, by the theorem statement, the span of

’s have dimension

.

Let
P_{2}
be the orthogonal projection onto the the span of

’s.
Then, for any fixed
w
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(y_{n+1}+\left\langle\beta,\widetilde{x}_{n+1}\right\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\beta}\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq d}\left(\bar{w}_{ij}+\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\mathbb{E}[v_{ij}\widetilde{x}_{n+1}[k]]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\beta}\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq d}\left(\bar{w}_{ij}+\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\mathbb{E}[v_{ij}\widetilde{x}_{n+1}[k]]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\vec{w}-P_{2}(\vec{w})\right\rVert^{2}.

Now we take the expectation over
w
and let
\mathrm{Cov}[\vec{w}]=\Sigma
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[\min_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(y_{n+1}+\left\langle\beta,\widetilde{x}_{n+1}\right\rangle\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[\left\lVert\vec{w}-P_{2}(\vec{w})\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left((I-P_{2})\vec{w}\vec{w}^{\top}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}((I-P_{2})\Sigma),

where we used the fact that
P_{2}
is idempotent.
Since
\Sigma
is positive semi-definite, we have

\mathrm{tr}((I-P_{2})\Sigma)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\Sigma^{1/2}(I-P_{2})(I-P_{2})\Sigma^{1/2}\right)=\left\lVert(I-P_{2})\Sigma^{1/2}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

And due to the matrix norm identity
\left\lVert AB\right\rVert_{F}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{F}\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
, we have

\left\lVert I-P_{2}\right\rVert_{F}\leq\left\lVert(I-P_{2})\Sigma^{1/2}\right\rVert_{F}\cdot\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\rm op}.

Since

can be written as:

\begin{bmatrix}d+1&\mathbf{1}_{1\times d}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{1}_{d\times 1}&2\mathbf{I}_{d}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}_{\bar{d}-d-1}\end{bmatrix},

where the top-left

\bar{w}_{00},\bar{w}_{11},\dots,\bar{w}_{dd}
.
The eigenvalues of this top-left submatrix are

with corresponding eigenvectors

[d,1,\dots 1],[-1,1,\dots 1],[0,-1,1,0,\dots],[0,-1,0,1,0,\dots],\dots,[0,-1,0,\dots,1].

Therefore, the minimum eigenvalue of
\Sigma
is 1.
It follows that

\mathrm{tr}((I-P_{2})\Sigma)=\left\lVert(I-P_{2})\Sigma^{1/2}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\geq\left\lVert I-P_{2}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\binom{d+2}{2}-\widetilde{d},

where the last equality follows from the fact that
I-P_{2}
is also an orthogonal projection matrix.
We conclude that

\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{BTFB})\geq\binom{d+2}{2}-\widetilde{d}.

Z^{(2\ell-1)}
.
Denote

Z^{(2\ell-1)}=:\begin{bmatrix}X&x_{q}\\
Y&y_{q}\end{bmatrix},

where the
X\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}\times n},Y\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n},x_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
and
y_{q}\in\mathbb{R}
.

\displaystyle Z^{(2\ell)}

\displaystyle=Z^{(2\ell-1)}+\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbf{P}^{(\ell)}Z^{(2\ell-1)}\right)\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\left({Z^{(2\ell-1)}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{(\ell)}Z^{(2\ell-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=Z^{(2\ell-1)}+\frac{1}{n}\begin{bmatrix}0_{d\times d}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X&x_{q}\\
Y&y_{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\left(\begin{bmatrix}X^{\top}&Y^{\top}\\
x_{q}^{\top}&y_{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma^{(\ell)}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X&x_{q}\\
Y&y_{q}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=Z^{(2\ell-1)}+\frac{1}{n}\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
Y&y_{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X^{\top}&V^{\top}\\
x_{q}^{\top}&y_{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma^{(\ell)}X&\Gamma^{(\ell)}x_{q}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=Z^{(2\ell-1)}+\frac{1}{n}\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
Y&y_{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X^{\top}\Gamma X&X^{\top}\Gamma^{(\ell)}x_{q}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}X&x_{q}\\
Y+\frac{1}{n}YX^{\top}\Gamma^{(\ell)}X&y_{q}+\frac{1}{n}YX^{\top}\Gamma^{(\ell)}x_{q}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&\cdots&x_{n+1}\\
x_{1}[\ell\%d]\cdot x_{1}&x_{2}[\ell\%d]\cdot x_{2}&\cdots&x_{n+1}[\ell\%d]\cdot x_{n+1}\\
y^{(\ell)}_{1}&y^{(\ell)}_{2}&\cdots&y^{(\ell)}_{n+1}\end{bmatrix},

where

\displaystyle y_{i}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=y_{i}^{(\ell-1)}+\left\langle\bar{x}_{i}^{(\ell)},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\Gamma^{(\ell)}}^{\top}\bar{x}_{i}^{(\ell)}y_{i}^{(\ell-1)}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=(1,x_{i},x_{i}[\ell\%d]\cdot x_{i}).

Now we compare
y^{(\ell)}
against the iterates of block coordinate descent through an induction argument.
Let

f(x;W)=\begin{bmatrix}1&x\end{bmatrix}W\begin{bmatrix}1\\
x\end{bmatrix}.

Note that for any
j\in b_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\partial w_{j}\left(\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(x_{i},W^{(\ell-1)})+y_{i}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(x_{i},W^{(\ell-1)})+y_{i}\right)\widetilde{x}_{i}[j]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y^{(\ell-1)}_{i}\widetilde{x}_{i}[j]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y^{(\ell-1)}_{i}\bar{x}^{(\ell)}_{i}[j],

where we use
\widetilde{x}_{i}
to denote the collection all quadratic monomials on
x_{i}
and note that
\bar{x}^{(\ell)}_{i}=\widetilde{x}_{i}[b_{\ell}]
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle y_{i}+f(x_{i},W^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=y_{i}+f(x_{i},W^{(\ell-1)})-\left\langle\bar{x}_{i}^{(\ell)},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta^{(\ell)}\bar{x}_{i}y^{(\ell-1)}_{i}\right\rangle,

which by induction is equal to
y_{i}^{(\ell)}
if we set
\eta^{(\ell)}=-{\Gamma^{(\ell)}}^{\top}
.
So we are done.
∎
