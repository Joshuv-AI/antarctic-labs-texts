Introduction

Accidental ingestion of foreign bodies (FB) is common, but intestinal perforation occurs in less than 1% of cases[1]. Among these, wooden toothpicks are particularly dangerous because of their sharp shape and radiolucency, which make diagnosis difficult and increase the risk of perforation, abscess, or peritonitis[2].

Toothpick perforations most frequently involve the ileum or colon, whereas jejunal perforation is exceedingly rare and often presents as an acute abdomen, mimicking other surgical emergencies[3]. We report a case of generalized peritonitis caused by a toothpick perforating the proximal jejunum, and we discuss the diagnostic and therapeutic considerations based on a review of the literature.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[6].

Case presentation

A 45-year-old man with a history of laparotomy for appendicular peritonitis 30 years earlier presented with severe, diffuse abdominal pain and vomiting for 24 hours. The patient was hemodynamically stable: with a blood pressure of 135/78 mmHg, a heart rate of 86 bpm, a respiratory rate of 18 breaths per minute, a temperature of 37.2°C, and an oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Physical examination revealed a rigid, tender abdomen with generalized contracture. The patient was not taking any regular medications. Urine output was preserved. Given the history of a prior laparotomy and chronic smoking, the initial differential diagnosis included adhesive small-bowel obstruction and a perforated peptic ulcer.

Laboratory studies revealed leukocytosis with elevated inflammatory markers and an increased serum creatinine level, consistent with acute kidney injury. An abdominal X-ray did not demonstrate free subdiaphragmatic air suggestive of pneumoperitoneum and showed no air–fluid levels or radiological signs of bowel obstruction. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT with intravenous contrast demonstrated pneumoperitoneum with a linear hyperdense foreign body traversing the small-bowel wall, consistent with a toothpick perforation (Fig. 1). Initial management included rapid fluid resuscitation with intravenous crystalloids, placement of a nasogastric tube, and initiation of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics targeting intra-abdominal pathogens in the context of suspected gastrointestinal perforation; specifically, a third-generation cephalosporin combined with metronidazole. A diagnostic laparoscopy revealed generalized purulent peritonitis and dense adhesions in the upper abdomen. At 70 cm from the duodenojejunal flexure, a wooden toothpick perforated the proximal jejunal loop on its antimesenteric border (Fig. 2). Because of the dense adhesions and diffuse contamination, conversion to a midline laparotomy was necessary. After the extraction of the toothpick, the perforation margins were debrided, and the defect was sutured with interrupted absorbable sutures. A thorough peritoneal lavage was performed, and a Redon drain was placed. The postoperative course was uneventful, with oral fluids resumed on day 2 and the patient discharged on day 4.

Discussion

Toothpick ingestion is an uncommon event, with an estimated annual incidence of fewer than 3000 cases worldwide[1]. Most swallowed foreign bodies pass spontaneously; however, wooden toothpicks pose a unique risk due to their shape, rigidity, and tendency to become embedded in the mucosa. Perforation of the gastrointestinal tract by a toothpick occurs in less than 0.1% of ingestions, but once it happens, the consequences can be severe, including abscess formation, generalized peritonitis, or even death[2].

The small bowel represents a rare site of perforation, accounting for less than one-fifth of all reported toothpick injuries[3]. Within the small bowel, the ileum is more frequently affected than the jejunum, likely because of its narrower lumen and angulated course. Jejunal perforation, as in our case, is exceptional and has been reported only in a few isolated cases since the first CT-documented jejunal perforation by Strauss in 1985[7].

This case highlights how a toothpick perforation can mimic more common causes of acute abdomen, particularly in patients with prior abdominal surgery. It also reinforces the value of contrast-enhanced CT in identifying subtle linear foreign bodies and the importance of considering this diagnosis even when ingestion is not reported.

Clinically, the presentation is often misleading. Many patients do not recall swallowing a toothpick, making the diagnosis particularly challenging. The resulting peritoneal irritation may mimic peptic ulcer perforation, appendicitis, or diverticulitis[8]. The time between ingestion and presentation can range from a few hours to several months, explaining the variability in presentation—from localized peritonitis to generalized sepsis[9]. In our patient, the combination of a previous laparotomy and an acute abdomen further complicated preoperative suspicion.

Radiological identification is critical yet difficult. Plain abdominal radiographs are rarely diagnostic because wood is radiolucent. CT is the most accurate tool, although the toothpick may still be overlooked if it is not hydrated or not aligned favorably with the imaging plane[10]. Thin-slice CT with multiplanar reconstruction increases detection sensitivity. In our case, the linear hyperdense foreign body seen on CT allowed preoperative identification, facilitating early surgery. In low-resource settings where cross-sectional imaging may not be readily available, diagnosis can be delayed, potentially increasing the risk of complications and affecting outcomes.

Treatment is almost always surgical. When peritonitis or perforation is confirmed, prompt exploration and removal of the foreign body are mandatory. A laparoscopic approach is feasible in selected localized perforations, but conversion is often necessary in the presence of diffuse contamination or adhesions from prior operations[11]. Simple suture repair after refreshing the margins is appropriate when the bowel is viable; resection is reserved for necrotic or multiple perforations.

The prognosis is favorable if the diagnosis is timely, but mortality can reach 10% in cases of delayed diagnosis[12]. Mortality is related to the progression toward generalized peritonitis, sepsis, and subsequent multi-organ failure. Early recognition is therefore essential and relies on quick clinical assessment, rapid imaging, and the immediate initiation of resuscitation measures, including intravenous fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics[13]. Timely surgical intervention remains the cornerstone for preventing severe septic complications and improving overall outcomes.

Management requires a multidisciplinary approach, involving early assessment and stabilization, accurate radiological diagnosis, and timely surgical intervention. Perioperative support from anesthesia and, when necessary, intensive care is essential.

To better understand the clinical and radiological profile of these rare events, we reviewed well-documented cases of jejunal and ileal perforations caused by ingested toothpicks from 1985 to 2025. The main findings, summarized in Table 2, highlight that most patients are middle-aged men who present with an acute abdomen or peritonitis. CT is diagnostic in only half of cases, and surgery (either laparoscopic or open) is the mainstay of treatment, with good overall outcomes when performed promptly. Conclusion

Small-bowel perforation by a swallowed toothpick is an extremely rare but serious event. Jejunal location is exceptional. Diagnosis is often delayed due to a nonspecific presentation and the radiolucent nature of the toothpick. Early CT evaluation and prompt surgical intervention remain the keys to successful management and a favorable outcome.
