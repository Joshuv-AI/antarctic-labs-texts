Bacterial Carriage and Invasive Infections Among People Who Inject Drugs: Clinical and Molecular Epidemiology in an Australian Prospective Cohort
Authors: Lucy O Attwood, Daniel O’Keefe, Jessica A Wisniewski, Nhu Q Doan, Bek Petrovic, Mariel Beiers, Olga Vujovic, Stephanie J Curtis, Peter Higgs, Jane Hawkey, Joseph S Doyle, Trisha N Peel, Anton Y Peleg, Paul Dietze, Andrew J Stewardson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople who inject drugs are at increased risk of invasive bacterial infections. We aimed to determine the prevalence and molecular epidemiology of Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes carriage in this population.MethodsWe performed a cross-sectional survey of carriage among people who inject drugs in Melbourne, Australia. Participants provided a combined throat-anterior nares and an axillae swab for S. aureus and S. pyogenes culture. Univariable analysis identified factors associated with S. aureus and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) carriage. Isolates underwent whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and were integrated in comparative genomic analysis with carriage and invasive disease isolates identified in 3 geotemporally linked studies of persons who both do and do not inject drugs.ResultsWe enrolled 305 participants between June 2022 and March 2023. We identified carriage with S. aureus in 40% (122/305), MRSA in 3% (9/305), and S. pyogenes in 1% (3/305) of participants. Recent incarceration was associated with MRSA carriage (OR 6.05, 95% CI 1.55–25.20). Whole WGS analysis identified multiple unrelated sequence types (STs), but S. aureus ST12 and ST398 were overrepresented in carriage and invasive disease isolates from people who inject drugs. We identified multiple clonal groupings (ie, isolates whose core genomes differed by ≤25 single-nucleotide polymorphisms) among carriage and invasive isolates.Conclusions S. aureus, but not S. pyogenes, carriage is common among people who inject drugs in Melbourne, with MRSA carriage associated with incarceration. While isolates from people who inject drugs in Melbourne were genetically diverse, the presence of clonal groupings and overrepresentation of specific lineages among people who inject drugs suggests that epidemiological dynamics may differ from those in the broader population.

Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes can colonize human skin without causing clinical disease. However, carriage can increase the risk of developing invasive infections, such as infective endocarditis [1, 2]. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) from North America and Europe has demonstrated transmission of S. aureus among people who inject drugs [3–5]. Outbreaks among injecting networks of invasive S. pyogenes infections (also known as invasive group A Streptococcus (iGAS)), have also been reported [6–9].

Understanding the molecular epidemiology of these organisms among people who inject drugs is essential to understand transmission and inform targeted interventions. We aimed to determine the prevalence of S. aureus and S. pyogenes carriage among a sample of people who inject drugs in Melbourne, Australia and to identify key carriage risk factors. Further, we aimed to characterize the molecular epidemiology of S. aureus and S. pyogenes from people who inject drugs by comparing these isolates with isolates from 3 geographically matched studies of people with and without a history of injecting drug use. We hypothesized that we would identify clonal transmission among people who inject drugs.

METHODS

Study Design, Population, and Procedure

To determine S. aureus and S. pyogenes carriage prevalence and risk factors, we conducted a cross-sectional survey with a sample of people who inject drugs in Melbourne, Australia. Melbourne is one of Australia's largest metropolitan areas with a population of 5.35 million people [10]. Participants were drawn from the SuperMIX study between June 2022 and March 2023. SuperMIX is a prospective cohort study of people who inject drugs that involves annual interviews with participants [11]. SuperMIX participants ≥18 years who reported injecting drug use within the previous 6 months were invited to participate.

Participation involved once-off collection of swabs at participants' SuperMIX interviews. Participants self-collected a combined throat-bilateral anterior nares swab and unilateral axillae swab (2 swabs collected per participant) [12] using ESwab (Copan Diagnostics). SuperMIX fieldworkers provided participants with written and verbal instructions (Supplementary Appendix 1.1). We linked results from these swabs with the same participant SuperMIX interview, administered at the time of sample collection, to collect potential carriage risk factor data. Risk factors were selected based on pre-existing literature [4, 6] and included fixed factors from baseline interviews, such as sex, and self-reported time-varying factors such as past 12-month incarceration. When assessing participants accommodation, we employed definitions utilized in the SuperMIX interviews [11]. We defined “unstable housing” as boarding house, supported accommodation (crisis or medium term), squatting, homeless/street or couch surfing. We defined “homeless' as rough sleeping (such as street or parks).

To characterize the molecular epidemiology of S. aureus and S. pyogenes, we compared carriage isolates from SuperMIX participants with isolates from 3 additional studies (see below and Supplementary Table 1). These studies included superficial and invasive isolates from people with and without a history of injecting drug use. SuperMIX participants contributed only carriage isolates unless participants had been independently recruited into one of the additional studies.

Identifiable data were available for participants included in the EMU study and the Microbiology Isolate Repository (Supplementary Table 1), allowing us to identify individuals who contributed isolates across these studies. No identifiable data were available for SuperMIX participants. However, because each SuperMIX participant provided 2 swabs (combined throat-bilateral anterior nares and unilateral axillae), up to 2 isolates could be obtained from a single participant. Where 2 isolates from a participant met the repeat-isolate exclusion criteria described below, 1 isolate was removed as a repeat.

Microbiologic Analysis of Carriage

Microbiologic analysis was performed at The Alfred Infectious Diseases microbiological laboratory. Colonies exhibiting morphologies consistent with S. aureus or S. pyogenes were subjected to species-level identification using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Confirmed isolates were preserved in 20% glycerol-HIB stocks for long-term storage. Additional methods are provided in detail in Supplementary Appendix 1.2.

Statistical Methods

We aimed to recruit 300 SuperMIX participants, estimating a S. aureus carriage proportion of 30%, with a confidence interval of 24.9%–35.5% [13]. Continuous variables are summarized as median with interquartile range and ordinal variables as count with percentage. Carriage with S. aureus (both total and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA)) and S. pyogenes is reported as prevalence of total population sampled during the study recruitment period. We computed 95% confidence intervals using the Clopper–Pearson method. We used univariable logistic regression to identify risk factors for S. aureus and MRSA carriage. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Molecular Epidemiologic Analyses

We compared the carriage isolates from SuperMIX participants with isolates from 3 studies restricted to similar geographical location (metropolitan Melbourne) and time. (1) S. aureus isolates from anterior nares and inguinal swabs from a trial recruiting patients undergoing elective joint-replacement surgery, assumed to be people who do not inject drugs (n = 81) [14]. (2) Superficial and invasive S. aureus and S. pyogenes isolates from people who inject drugs collected as part of a prospective cohort study (n = 46) [15]. (3) S. aureus and S. pyogenes isolates recovered from bloodstream infections at Alfred Health (n = 176). The medical records of patients from which these bloodstream isolates had been obtained were reviewed to confirm injecting drug status of participants (n = 19) [16]. Alfred Health is a major public health service located close to the central business district of Melbourne. It has a primary catchment of 770 000 people and functions as one of Victoria's key tertiary referral hospitals, providing services including trauma, intensive care, cardiothoracic surgery, and infectious diseases [17]. Further information on these studies is provided in Supplementary Appendix 2.

We defined superficial isolates as both asymptomatic carriage screening swabs and noninvasive clinical specimens (eg, wound swabs). Invasive isolates were those from sterile sites (including, but not limited to, blood culture, tissue culture, joint aspirate or abscess fluid collected in surgery under sterile conditions). We defined current injecting drug use as self-reported injecting drug use within the past 6 months.

Genomic Analysis

WGS was performed on S. aureus and S. pyogenes isolates cultured from carriage swabs and samples provided from comparator studies. Short read sequencing libraries were prepared using the DNA Library Prep Kit, Tagmentation (Illumina), and 150 bp paired-end sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina). The presence of mecA was used to predict MRSA [18]. For S. aureus isolates, PathogenWatch was used to construct a neighbor-joining tree based on pairwise single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) distances from the core genome alignment of these isolates [19, 20]. A species-wide neighbor-joining phylogeny was generated using all S. aureus genomes. Separate PathogenWatch analyses were subsequently performed for the dominant sequence types (STs) identified among people who inject drugs, generating ST-specific core genome alignments and neighbor-joining phylogenies used to examine fine-scale genomic relatedness within each lineage. Phylogenetic trees were visualized with iTOL [21]. Additional methods are provided in detail in Supplementary Appendix 1. All genome sequences are available in the public archives; a full list of their accessions is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Across all 4 studies, we excluded isolates as repeats if they originated from the same participant, belonged to the same ST and had an identical phenotypic profile, including the same antimicrobial resistance pattern and the same clinical classification (invasive vs superficial). When more than 1 isolate met these criteria, a single isolate was retained at random, as there was no consistent clinical or temporal characteristic that justified prioritizing one isolate over another. Two isolates from the same participant were retained if they differed in any of these characteristics or they were collected during separate admissions at different time points. Genetic relatedness of S. aureus isolates was determined based on pairwise SNP distances between isolates of 25 SNPs or less [22, 23]. Genetic relatedness of S. pyogenes isolates was determined based on pairwise core genome SNP distances between isolates of 10 SNPs or less [8].

Ethical Considerations

SuperMIX participants provided written informed consent and were reimbursed $50 for their time. Ethics approval was granted by the Alfred Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (Project 692/21), including for utilization of isolates from comparator studies. The full ethical considerations for those studies have been described previously [14–16]. The ASAP Access Committee reviewed and endorsed the study protocol prior to utilization of study isolates.

RESULTS

Participants

Between June 2022 and March 2023, 305 participants were enrolled from the SuperMIX cohort. The median age was 41 years (IQR 31–48), 206 (67.5%) were male and 27.5% reported unstable housing (84/305) (Table 1). More than a third of participants (117/385, 38.4%) reported reusing their own needles in the past month.

SuperMIX Carriage

Staphylococcus aureus

Carriage with S. aureus was identified in 40% of SuperMIX participants (122/305). Among participants with S. aureus carriage, 50.8% (62/122) were identified by throat-anterior nares swab alone, 16.4% (20/122) by axillae swab alone and 32.8% (40/122) by both swabs. MRSA was detected in 9 participants (3.0%), all on throat-anterior nares swabs. Information on the concordance of paired swabs from SuperMIX participants is provided in Supplementary Appendix 2.

Table 1 summarizes the univariable associations between risk factors and carriage with S. aureus (methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) or MRSA) and MRSA. Incarceration within the past 12 months was significantly associated with carriage with MRSA (OR 6.05, 95% CI 1.55–25.20). No other risk factors were significantly associated with carriage.

Streptococcus pyogenes

Carriage with S. pyogenes was detected in 3 unique SuperMIX participants (1.0%). Due the low prevalence of S. pyogenes carriage, risk factor associations were not modeled.

Staphylococcus aureus

Figure 2 demonstrates the phylogeny for all S. aureus genomes. We found 75 distinct STs of S. aureus, which included 25 novel STs across 28 genomes (7.4%). Thirteen participants had 2 isolates included in this analysis (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 2). We identified genomes from people who inject drugs across 42 distinct STs, demonstrating significant diversity. Six STs accounted for over half (203/380, 53.4%) of S. aureus genomes; ST5, ST398, ST30, ST12, ST15, and ST97 (Figure 3).

The identification of mecA occurred in 43 genomes (11.3%), representing MRSA. MRSA genomes were found across 17 unique STs, of which ST22 was the most common (10/43, 23.2%). Of the MRSA genomes, 13 (30.2%) were from people who inject drugs, with 3/13 (23.1%) ST93. Further information about the spa-type and detection of antimicrobial resistance genes for S. aureus isolates can be found in Supplementary Table 2.

There was a higher proportion of invasive isolates from people who inject drugs in ST12 and ST398 than from people without a history of injecting drug use (18.4% vs 0.75% P < .001% and 31.6% vs 11.2% P = .005, respectively) (Figure 3). There was a higher proportion of superficial isolates in ST12 from people who inject drugs compared to isolates obtained from people without evidence of injecting drug use (12.5% vs 1.4%, P = .008).

ST-specific phylogenetic analyses were performed for the dominant STs identified among people who inject drugs (ST12, ST398, and ST5). These analyses demonstrated that the median pairwise SNP distance was 71 (range 0–208 SNPs) for ST12 genomes, 139 (range 1–223) for ST398 genomes and 284 (range 0–411) for ST5 genomes. S. aureus isolates from people who inject drugs were distributed across the full diversity of STs. Within this diversity, we identified several clonal groups of highly related genomes. The largest of these were in ST12 and ST398, comprising 17 genomes from 16 separate participants (ST12) and 13 genomes from 12 separate participants (ST398) who inject drugs (Figure 4A and B). We also identified groups of closely related genomes in ST5 (Figure 4C). Across STs, some superficial and invasive isolates from people who inject drugs exhibited high genomic similarity (≤25 SNPs), highlighting that carriage and invasive isolates can arise from closely related subclonal groups with minimal genetic divergence. Participants with 2 isolates included in analysis (participants A–M, Supplementary Table 2) predominantly demonstrated genomes from 2 different STs. Streptococcus pyogenes

S. pyogenes isolates demonstrated diversity, with 11 emm-types and 12 STs identified across the 20 genomes (Table 2). The most common was emm77/ST572 (5/20, 25.0%). When applying functional classification of emm genotypes, genomes were classified into 7 emm-clusters among which E4 was the most common (9/20, 45.0%) [24]. emm77 and emm101 were only identified in genomes from participants with a history of injecting drug use (8/20, 40%). Genomic analysis of emm77 demonstrated a median SNP pairwise distance of 9 (range 4–41 SNPs). We identified a small cluster of 5 genomes with <10 SNPs indicating a potential transmission cluster among emm77 S. pyogenes isolates in people who inject drugs.

DISCUSSION

Among a community sample of people who inject drugs in Melbourne, Australia, S. aureus carriage was common (122/305, 40%), whereas S. pyogenes carriage was infrequent (3/305, 1%). Recent incarceration was the only factor significantly associated with MRSA carriage. Although S. aureus and S. pyogenes isolates from people who inject drugs were genetically diverse, specific S. aureus lineages were overrepresented among this population. Furthermore, several superficial and invasive isolates demonstrated high genomic similarity. These findings suggest epidemiological dynamics that may differ from the broader population.

We found that recent incarceration was associated with MRSA carriage among people who inject drugs, highlighting the importance of infection prevention and harm reduction measures in prisons [25]. Carriage with S. aureus is a recognized risk factor for subsequent infection and has been shown to precede invasive disease [26]. People who inject drugs experience a disproportionately high burden of S. aureus invasive infections, likely due to frequent skin puncture, skin and soft-tissue infections, and potential contamination of injecting equipment, all of which may facilitate bacterial entry into the bloodstream [26, 27]. Clinical outcomes associated with MRSA infection, including morbidity, mortality and hospital length of stay, are often worse than those associated with methicillin-susceptible S. aureus [28]. Globally, MRSA remains one of the leading contributors to antimicrobial resistance mortality [29]. The first year following MRSA detection has been associated with the highest risk of infection, underscoring the importance of reducing MRSA carriage in high-risk groups [30]. In this context, targeted S. aureus decolonization for individuals recently released from prison may warrant future evaluation as a potential adjunct to established harm reduction strategies [1, 31]. Such approaches would likely need to be embedded within broader harm reduction strategies in prison and postrelease to reduce the burden of invasive S. aureus infections among people who inject drugs.

Although injecting drug use is a recognized risk factor for invasive S. pyogenes infection [6, 32], S. pyogenes carriage was infrequent among our cohort. This finding is consistent with previous Australian studies demonstrating low carriage prevalence among asymptomatic individuals [33]. One potential explanation for this finding is suboptimal self-collection technique. However, our participants were given clear written and verbal instructions, and sample collection was supervised by field workers. Furthermore, prior studies have demonstrated comparable sensitivity between self-collected and healthcare-collected throat swabs for symptomatic S. pyogenes detection, albeit using PCR rather than culture [34]. An explanation for the discordance between low prevalence of carriage and relatively high incidence of infection with S. pyogenes is that wound carriage, rather than oropharyngeal carriage, may be more relevant in people who inject drugs. Injecting-related wounds and skin and soft-tissue infections may provide a direct portal of entry for S. pyogenes [6], potentially occurring independently of detectable throat carriage.

Analysis of S. aureus and S. pyogenes isolates from people who inject drugs in this study demonstrated substantial diversity, with isolates distributed across a wide range of STs and emm-types. This broad distribution likely represents circulation of strains that are present within the wider community rather than expansion of a small number of dominant clones. These findings differ from several previous genomic studies that reported more discrete clusters of S. aureus or S. pyogenes among people who inject drugs [3–6, 8, 9]. However, many of these studies described extended periods between cases, had small sample sizes or lacked comparator groups [35], making it difficult to delineate small outbreaks from general population transmission [36]. The diversity observed in our study is more consistent with the diversity demonstrated among S. aureus invasive isolates from people who inject drugs in New York [22]. Large urban populations and high access to harm reduction services in Melbourne and New York may reduce the likelihood of identifying dominant transmission clusters, while simultaneously maintaining circulation of multiple lineages [22, 37].

We observed that among both carriage and invasive disease isolates, S. aureus ST12 and ST398 were overrepresented among people who inject drugs compared to other individuals. Previous genomic studies have reported overrepresentation of specific S. aureus lineages among invasive infections in this population [5, 22]. We observed a similar enrichment among carriage isolates. This suggests that differences in circulating strains may contribute to the epidemiology of invasive disease among people who inject drugs, alongside behavioral and exposure-related risk factors [38]. ST398 has increasingly been recognized as a human-adapted methicillin-susceptible lineage capable of causing both carriage and invasive disease [26, 39]. Previous studies from the United States have reported overrepresentation of MSSA ST398 among invasive infections in people who inject drugs [5], while our findings demonstrate its presence among both carriage and invasive isolates in Melbourne. Similarly, although ST12 MSSA is reported relatively infrequently in Australia and globally [40–42], we identified 38 MSSA ST12 isolates across multiple participants and specimen types. The presence of multiple spa-types within this lineage suggests local genetic diversity rather than expansion of a single clone, highlighting an unexpectedly high burden of ST12 MSSA in Melbourne. Continued genomic surveillance will be important to determine whether these observations represent broader regional trends or a local epidemiological phenomenon.

Although this study was not designed to determine whether carriage directly preceded infection, the identification of highly related carriage and invasive isolates supports a biologically plausible pathway linking carriage and invasive disease. This interpretation is consistent with the broader literature demonstrating that S. aureus carriage is an important risk factor for subsequent infection in several high-risk populations [1, 13, 26, 43]. Our findings therefore support the possibility that circulating carriage strains may contribute to invasive disease. These findings suggest that both exposure-related factors, including injecting practices that facilitate bacterial inoculation and differences in distribution of circulating bacterial lineages may contribute to infection risk among people who inject drugs [5, 22, 38].

The potential for transmission within injecting networks reinforces the importance of preventing direct inoculation of bacteria in people who inject drugs. Nearly, 40% of our participants reported reusing their own needles or syringes in the past month. Multiple mechanisms may contribute to invasive infection risk, including direct inoculation of bacteria during injecting practices, contamination of injecting equipment, and entry through injecting-related skin breaches, in addition to transmission within social or injecting networks [5, 38]. Sociodemographic factors such as unstable housing may further increase risk by limiting opportunities for sterile injecting and timely wound care [3, 36, 44, 45]. Improving access to housing, sterile injecting equipment, wound care services and harm reduction programs remains essential for reducing the burden of invasive bacterial infections among people who inject drugs [27, 38, 46].

There are limitations to our analysis. First, the low prevalence of MRSA and S. pyogenes within our cohort precluded analyses of risk factors for S. pyogenes carriage and meant our findings with regards to MRSA lacked precision. SuperMIX participants self-collected swabs, which may have reduced sensitivity due to suboptimal technique. However, participants received detailed verbal and written instructions (Supplementary Appendix 1.1), and prior studies show comparable sensitivity between self-collected and healthcare-collected swabs for S. aureus and S. pyogenes [34, 47]. We acknowledge that MRSA detection may have been higher with groin screening [12], but swabs were designed to be feasible in public settings during interviews and axillae swabs were considered more acceptable. To maximize sensitivity, we requested sampling from multiple sites (nose, throat and axillae), consistent with evidence that multisite screening improves MRSA detection [12]. We also note that the reported unadjusted odds ratios represent associations rather than causal relationships. Second, determining injecting drug use status for comparator isolates relied on accurate documentation in medical records. There is a risk of misclassification bias if this stigmatized, illegal activity was not disclosed. Injecting status was not collected as part of the ASAP trial. We assumed that the prevalence of injecting drug use among patients undergoing elective joint replacement in a private hospital was sufficiently low to justify categorizing this group as not currently injecting drugs. This may result in some misclassification, but likely minimal, given only 0.2% of the Australian population aged 14 years or more report injecting drugs within the previous year [48]. Third, this study was conducted in a single city with broad access to harm reduction services, which may limit generalizability to other settings. Fourth, we lacked data on participants' social networks and injecting relationships, limiting our ability to directly investigate transmission pathways among individuals with closely related isolates.

CONCLUSION

Among people who inject drugs in Melbourne, S. aureus carriage was common and recent incarceration was associated with MRSA carriage. Although isolates were genetically diverse, S. aureus ST12 and ST398 were disproportionately represented among people who inject drugs, with several superficial and invasive isolates demonstrating high genomic similarity. These findings reinforce that distinct bacterial populations may circulate within this population and highlight the importance of harm reduction strategies aimed at reducing bacterial transmission and opportunities for direct inoculation during injecting practices.
