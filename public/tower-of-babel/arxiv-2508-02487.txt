Commit Stability as a Signal for Risk in Open-Source Projects.
1 st Elijah Kayode Adejumo, 2 nd Brittany Johnson, 3 rd Mariam Guizani

Abstract
Open source software (OSS) generates trillions of dollars in economic value and has become essential to the technical infrastructures that power organizations worldwide. As these systems increasingly depend on OSS, understanding the evolution of these projects is critical. While existing metrics provide insights into project health, one dimension remains understudied: project resilience, the ability to return to normal operations after disturbances such as contributor departures,security vulnerabilities and bug report spikes. We hypothesize that stable commit patterns reflects underlying project characteristics such as mature governance, sustained contributors, and robust development processes, that enable resilience when facing disruptions. These stability patterns can inform risk assessment, essential for dependency evaluation in safety-critical systems, health forecasting, and maintainer burnout prevention. Building on the Composite Stability Index (CSI) framework, we empirically validate commit frequency patterns across 100 highly ranked repositories. Our findings reveal that only 2% of repositories exhibit daily stability, 29% achieve weekly stability, and 50% demonstrate monthly stability, while half remain unstable across all temporal levels. Programming languages, blockchain application were the most stable across our dataset. We identified two exemplary repositories that achieved stability at all three granularity, whose governance models, Continuous Integration (CI) cadence, and release policies could serve as reference frameworks. Additionally, we observed that large yearly commit throughput does not necessarily correlate with stability. Beyond commits, stability can be enriched with issue‑resolution times, PR merge rate, and community‑engagement metrics, and by studying the exemplar projects in our dataset. These additions will broaden resilience assessment and sharpen stability‑based risk assessment.

Building on this foundation, we hypothesize that stable commit patterns could serve as proxy for risk assessment in open source software.

III
Methodology

Our research aims to evaluate how stable repository patterns can inform risk assessment in open-source software. While our broader framework encompasses multiple stability indicators including commit history, issue resolution time, pull request merge rates, and community engagement metrics, this work focuses specifically on commit pattern stability as a foundational component of OSS risk assessment. Our experiment is guided by the following research questions:

RQ
1

To what extend do highly ranked OSS projects display stable commit
rhythms daily, weekly, and monthly?

RQ
2

How does commit stability evolve as granularity expands across daily, weekly, and monthly?

RQ
3

Does commit stability differ significantly across application domains?

III-C

Commit Stability Measure (RQ1)

We empirically evaluate the applicability of the conceptual framework proposed by Destefanis et al.
[
16
]
. Following inspiration from control theory, Destefanis et al.
[
16
]
proposed a conceptual framework making the parallel between a stable manufacturing process yielding more predictable product quality and a stable software development rhythm yielding more predictable maintenance and evolution.

They defined the commit frequency function as follows:

c(t)=\frac{N_{C}\!\bigl(t,\,t+\Delta t\bigr)}{\Delta t},

(1)

Where
N_{C}\!\bigl(t,\,t+\Delta t\bigr)
represents the volume of commit within the time interval
\!\bigl(t,\,t+\Delta t\bigr)
. For our experimentation, we defined the interval to be within the last 5 years. A repository is classified as stable
[
16
]
when:

\left|\frac{dc(t)}{dt}\right|\;\leq\;\alpha_{c},\quad\forall\,t\in[t_{0},\,t_{0}+T],

(2)

The parameter
\alpha_{c}

establishes a maximum threshold for acceptable fluctuations in commit activity over a period
[t_{0},\,t_{0}+T]
. When this threshold is surpassed, the repository loses its stable classification. The boundary value
\alpha_{c}

[
16
]
is specified as:

\alpha_{c}=\frac{\sigma_{\text{daily commits}}}{\mu_{\text{daily commits}}}\leq 0.5.

(3)

Granularity variants

To examine stability at different levels of granularity, we
instantiate Eq. (
1
) at three window sizes:

\Delta t=\begin{cases}1\text{day}&\!\!:\;c_{d}(t)\\
7\text{days}&\!\!:\;c_{w}(t)\\
30\text{days}&\!\!:\;c_{m}(t)\end{cases}

and re‑apply the stability test in
Eq. (
2
) with the same coefficient‑of‑variation
threshold (Eq. (
3
),
\alpha_{c}\!=\!0.5
) at each granularity.
A repository is therefore labeled

daily‑stable
,
weekly‑stable
, or
monthly‑stable

when the inequality holds for
c_{d}(t)
,
c_{w}(t)
, or
c_{m}(t)
,
respectively.
The three labels allow us to study how stability classifications

vary across granularity
.

Target normalizer for the commit metric

Destefanis et al.
[
16
]
map each raw stability
signal onto
[0,1]
with the
triangular normaliser
in the CSI framework this
mapping is applied to four components

\{c,i,p,a\}
—
commit frequency, issue resolution,
pull‑request merge rate,
and
community activity
.

In this study, we focus on the
commit component

(
k=c
).
Following the original thresholds, we set the

target
and
tolerance
to

\mu_{c}=0.25
and
\sigma_{c}=0.25
; that is, we consider a
Coefficient‑of‑Variation (CV) of 0.25 to be ideal, and we tolerate
deviations up to
\pm 0.25
:

\phi_{c}(x)=\begin{cases}1-\dfrac{|x-0.25|}{0.25},&0\leq x\leq 0.50,\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus a repository with monthly

\text{CV}=0.25
receives the maximum stability score

\phi_{c}=1
, while any CV outside the corridor

[0.00,\,0.50]
is mapped to
\phi_{c}=0
(treated as unstable at that
granularity).

III-D

Stability assessments across different granularity (RQ2)

To assess how the stability score evolve after applying the target normalizer described in section
III-C
across the three granularity (daily, weekly, monthly), we evaluate the rate of change function. That is for every repository i we hold three stability scores:

\phi_{d,i},\phi_{w,i},\phi_{m,i}
, where
\phi_{d,i}=\text{daily stability score}
,
\phi_{w,i}=\text{weekly score}
, and
\phi_{m,i}=\text{monthly score}

To reveal the stability evolution as the window widens, we compute two stepwise deltas:

Step 1: Daily
\rightarrow
Weekly,

\displaystyle\Delta_{dw,i}

\displaystyle=\phi_{w,i}-\phi_{d,i}

(4)

This reveals how much the stability score rises (or falls) when we aggregate commits from 1 day into 7‑days.

Step 2: Weekly
\rightarrow
Monthly

\displaystyle\Delta_{wm,i}

\displaystyle=\phi_{m,i}-\phi_{w,i}

(5)

This reveals additional change when we further aggregate those 7‑day buckets into 30‑day window.

III-E

Stability differences across open source application domains (RQ3)

Building on Jansen’s observation that metric semantics drift between projects domains
[
20
]
, we interrogate the universality of the commit‑stability corridor across diverse software application domains. To systematically analyze domain-specific patterns, we employed a two-stage classification approach for our dataset of 100 repositories.

We first utilize the vast knowledge base of Large Language Model (ChatGPT-o3) for automated classification of our dataset into different domains using the following one-shot prompt: “Please classify the following into application domains, an example could be front-end/UI”.
This approach was chosen to minimize classification bias while allowing the model to leverage its training on software development documentation and repository metadata
[
21
]
.
To ensure classification accuracy, we conducted manual validation of the LLM-generated classifications across the 100 repositories. Where there were classification doubts we referenced the repository’s documentation.

This domain-based classification enabled us to assess whether the commit-stability patterns generalizes across different software application domains.

IV-C

Stability differences across open source application domains (RQ3)

The LLM-aided classification produced 25 domains across the 100 repositories evaluated. Following manual review, we corrected misclassifications and merged one repository into the appropriate category, resulting in 24 final domains. We utilized monthly stability scores to reveal stable patterns across domains, as monthly granularity captured the majority of repositories, with over 50 repositories demonstrating stability at this temporal granularity.

Our findings reveal that all repositories in core infrastructure domains specifically
programming languages and compilers

(rust, node, typescript, definitelytyped, go)
are stable at the monthly granularity level. Both
blockchain
related repositories in our dataset
(bitcoin and ethereum)
achieved a very high stability score (
>
0.90). This is likely due to the substantial economic value and critical infrastructure role these systems support, necessitating conservative development practices. Conversely,
Front-end/UI Frameworks & Libraries
,
Data Visualization and Analytics
domains exhibited the lowest stability, with 8 of 11 repositories classified in this domain showing instability at the monthly granularity.

Finally, our analysis revealed that stability was achieved across 16 of the 24 application domains, with several domains containing both stable and unstable repositories. This suggests that stability may be repository-specific rather than domain-determined, with factors such as economic importance, infrastructure criticality, or organizational maturity potentially serving as better predictors of stability. V
Use Cases & Discussion

Our findings introduce a new dimension for assessing open source software risk, extending beyond technical considerations to social factors. The stability metrics can identify projects with controlled activity patterns while revealing risks related to maintainer sustainability and project governance. Notably, stable commit patterns reflect underlying project characteristics such as mature governance, sustained contributors, and robust development processes, which enable resilience when facing disruptions. This suggests that commit stability serves as a proxy for predicting a project’s ability to weather challenges. We discuss use-case scenarios and future research directions.

V-A

Safety-Critical Software using Open Source Software (OSS)

The integration of open source software into the development of safety-critical software has been an areas investigated in the past. Investigation has been around the what types or domain of open source software are currently been used for safety-critical development
[
6
,
7
]
such as medical devices, automotive controllers etc. Beyond assessing feature fit and license, they need confidence that the library or tooling will be reliably maintained over the long term and not pose undue risk.
Our stability-informed risk assessment across multiple temporal granularities provides a nuanced evaluation framework by empirically examining the regularity and predictability of a project’s maintenance rhythm.
For instance, while both Rust (29,759 commits/year) and Homebrew-cask (40,078 commits/year) demonstrate high development activity over five years, their stability patterns differ markedly: Rust exhibits stability across all temporal granularities, whereas Homebrew-cask remains unstable across all granularities. This demonstrates that commit volume alone is insufficient and the regularity and predictability of development patterns are equally critical for risk assessment.

V-B

Maintainer Sustainability

Maintainers and contributors are central to open source software success
[
22
,
23
]
, yet they face significant challenges including stress and burnout from managing project demands
[
24
,
25
]
. Our stability-informed assessment offers a novel approach for projects to monitor their commit patterns and identify unhealthy trends before they escalate.
For instance, a sudden increase in weekend or late-night commits by a lead maintainer could cause stability scores to decline, serving as an early warning signal. This metric could trigger proactive community interventions, such as recruiting additional contributors or encouraging maintainers to take breaks before burnout occurs. By providing quantitative indicators of development stress patterns, our approach enables data-driven community health management.

V-C

Contributor Opportunities

Contributors are essential to OSS success and sustainability, yet identifying suitable contribution opportunities remains challenging
[
26

27
]
. Our stability-informed assessment offers a novel approach to this problem by highlighting projects with stable commit patterns, which may indicate mature development processes and greater receptiveness to new contributors.
Projects demonstrating consistent, predictable development rhythms often have established workflows, clear governance structures, and active maintainer engagement, factors that facilitate successful contributor onboarding
[
28
]
.

VI
Future Work

VI-A

Multi-Signal Resilience Index

While commit patterns provide valuable insights, they represent only one dimension of project health. A comprehensive stability-informed risk assessment requires multiple indicators, as suggested by
[
16
]
, including issue resolution times, pull request merge rates, and community engagement metrics.

Future work will extend our stability analysis framework to incorporate these additional signals, including GitHub Discussions board utilization and engagement patterns
[
29
]
. By combining commit stability with these complementary metrics, we aim to develop a multi-signal resilience index that provides a more holistic assessment of open source project sustainability and risk.

VI-B

Utilizing Exemplars Repositories

Our analysis thus far have shown two exemplars (Rust and Nixpkgs) our next steps include qualitative deep‑dive into the “always‑stable” repositories whose commit rhythms remain steady at daily, weekly, and monthly scales. Understanding their public governance documents, CI/workflow definitions, and tagged release history. We will also interview three or four core maintainers from each project to understand the intent and social norms behind those rules.
