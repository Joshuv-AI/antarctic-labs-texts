Tension band wiring versus suture anchoring for inferior pole patellar fractures: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yen-Yun Cheng, Ting-Yu Tu, Kai-Cheng Lin, Chun-Yu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInferior pole patellar fractures present surgical challenges due to their comminuted nature. Tension band wiring (TBW) is a commonly used fixation technique. However, it is associated with complications such as hardware prominence and soft tissue irritation. Suture anchor (SA) fixation has been proposed as an alternative to TBW that aims to reduce hardware-related complications. This study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes of TBW and SA fixation for inferior pole patellar fractures.MethodsA literature search was conducted in the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Scopus databases in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines to identify studies comparing TBW and SA fixation for inferior pole patellar fractures. Outcome data were collected, including operative time, intraoperative blood loss, functional scores (Bostman score, Insall–Salvati ratio, and range of motion), fracture union time, and postoperative complications. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. Pooled analyses were performed using risk ratios and mean differences with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsA total of 5 retrospective cohort studies involving 227 patients treated with TBW (n = 121) and SA fixation (n = 106) were included in the analysis. The SA group demonstrated higher Bostman scores and a greater range of motion than the TBW group (p < 0.05). No significant differences in operative time, intraoperative blood loss, Insall–Salvati ratio, or fracture union time were observed between the two groups (p > 0.05). SA fixation was associated with a lower postoperative complication rate than TBW (2 vs. 23, p < 0.05).ConclusionTBW and SA fixation for inferior pole patellar fractures were associated with comparable clinical and radiological outcomes. SA fixation demonstrated modest improvements in functional scores and lower complication rates. However, the clinical relevance of these differences remains uncertain, and caution should be exercised when interpreting the results. Further high-quality prospective studies are needed to better evaluate the effectiveness of these techniques.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06932-y.Keywords: Tension band wiring, Suture anchoring, Internal fixation, Inferior pole patellar fracture, Meta-analysis

Introduction

The patella serves as a biomechanical amplifier of quadriceps force during knee extension. Patellar fractures account for approximately 1% of all skeletal injuries [1] and are commonly caused by excessive tension through the extensor mechanism or direct trauma. Inferior pole patellar fractures are typically extra-articular and involve the lower quarter of the patella. These fractures account for approximately 18% of all patellar fractures and pose significant surgical challenges due to their high degree of comminution, which often compromises the stability of standard fixation techniques [1]. Surgical treatment is recommended for displaced inferior pole fractures to restore the integrity of the extensor mechanism [2]. Various surgical techniques have been widely adopted in clinical practice, including partial patellectomy, tension band wiring (TBW), and suture anchor (SA) fixation [3]. Treatment selection is based on fracture pattern and comminution severity.

TBW is the most widely used surgical technique for patellar fractures [4]. It converts anterior tensile forces generated by the quadriceps into compressive forces at the articular surface. This procedure involves inserting two K-wires perpendicular to the fracture line and applying a low-gauge metal wire in a figure-of-eight configuration. Despite its widespread use, TBW is associated with complications, including hardware prominence, soft tissue irritation, and fixation failure, resulting in a high implant removal rate (approximately 31.6%) [5]. These challenges prompted the development of alternative fixation methods.

SA fixation has emerged as an alternative to TBW to reduce hardware-related complications while maintaining adequate fracture stability. This technique has been increasingly adopted worldwide [6–10]. This approach involves inserting SAs loaded with nonabsorbable sutures into the proximal patellar fragment, securing the inferior fragments with sutures, and reattaching the patellar tendon for reinforcement. This technique has demonstrated satisfactory fracture union and clinical outcomes [6–10].

Despite the widespread use of TBW and SA techniques, the available literature is limited to small, single-center studies with relatively small sample sizes. Additionally, studies evaluating SA fixation have employed heterogeneous surgical techniques, and several studies lack sufficient statistical power to detect significant differences. Therefore, this meta-analysis aimed to synthesize the available evidence, quantitatively compare clinical outcomes between TBW and SA fixation, and provide more robust evidence to inform surgical decision-making.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251027740 on April 7, 2025, before the formal initiation of the literature search and study selection process. The search strategy and raw results are presented in Supplement 1.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Scopus databases to identify all relevant human studies (observational and experimental) published up to April 13, 2025, using the following keywords and Medical Subject Headings terms: ((Patella OR Patellar) AND Pole AND (Inferior OR Distal) AND (Fractures, Bone OR Fracture)) AND (Suture Anchors OR Anchor) AND (Tension Band OR Wire OR Wiring). No filters or restrictions were applied during the search process. The study population included patients with inferior pole patellar fractures treated surgically with either TBW or SA fixation. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-RCTs were considered eligible. The reference lists of all retrieved studies were manually reviewed to identify any potentially eligible articles. The search results are presented in Supplement 2.

Study eligibility

After the initial literature search, the study selection process was conducted independently by two reviewers, which involved removing duplicates, screening titles and abstracts for relevance, performing full-text reviews of studies meeting the preliminary eligibility criteria, and assessing the methodological quality of each included study. Full-text articles were reviewed when abstracts did not provide sufficient data for inclusion. Any disagreements between the two reviewers during the selection process were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) fracture location not explicitly identified as an “inferior” or “distal pole” patellar fracture, (2) any study design other than RCTs and cohort studies, (3) unavailability of full-text access, (4) patients’ age < 18 years, (5) evaluating only a single surgical intervention without a comparative group, and (6) gray literature, including conference abstracts, dissertations, and preprints, due to lack of peer review and insufficient methodological or outcome data for reliable quantitative synthesis. Additionally, studies that did not report a minimum follow-up period of 6 months for assessing clinical and functional outcomes were excluded. Radiographic fracture union was evaluated within the same follow-up period.

Data extraction

Relevant data, including study characteristics, patient demographics, and surgical procedure details, were extracted from eligible studies independently by two reviewers. Furthermore, six outcomes were extracted and categorized into three main groups: (1) clinical outcomes: operative time and intraoperative blood loss, (2) functional outcomes: Bostman score, Insall–Salvati ratio, and range of motion (ROM), and (3) radiological outcomes: fracture union time. Postoperative complications were recorded separately. Any disagreements between the two reviewers during data extraction were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when consensus could not be reached.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [12]. The NOS evaluates three domains: selection, comparability, and exposure/outcome. The selection domain assesses the representativeness of the case and control groups, clarity of the selection criteria, and comparability of the baseline characteristics. The comparability domain assesses whether key confounders, including age, sex, and lifestyle factors, are adequately controlled using appropriate statistical methods. The outcome domain assesses the clarity and accuracy of outcome measurements, completeness of follow-up, and handling of missing data or loss to follow-up. The maximum total score is 9, with studies scoring ≥ 7 considered high quality.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Dichotomous outcomes were pooled using the Mantel–Haenszel method and presented as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), whereas continuous outcomes were pooled using the inverse variance method and presented as mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs. When continuous outcomes were reported as medians and interquartile ranges, these values were converted to means and standard deviations (SDs) using validated mathematical formulas. A random-effects model was used for all pooled analyses. The random-effects model was chosen over a fixed-effect model to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity, such as variations in surgical expertise, fixation materials, and postoperative rehabilitation protocols across different global institutions. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, which quantifies the proportion of total variation across studies that is due to heterogeneity rather than chance [13]. Forest plots were generated for all pooled outcomes. In cases where meta-analysis was not feasible due to inconsistent outcome reporting, results were synthesized following the Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis guidelines [14]. Publication bias was not assessed using funnel plots or formal statistical tests as these tests are considered unreliable for meta-analyses containing < 10 studies. All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan 5.3 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Clinical outcomes

Operation time

Operation time, with SDs and 95% CIs, was reported in four studies (SA: 78 patients; TBW: 86 patients; Fig. 2A). The mean operation time was 48.95 min in the SA group and 60.21 min in the TBW group. However, no significant difference was observed between the two groups (MD = − 10.50 min; 95% CI, − 23.69 to 2.68; I2 = 97%; p = 0.12).

A Operation time. B Intraoperative blood loss. C Bostman score. D Insall–Salvati ratio. E Range of motion. F Fracture union time. G Complications

Intraoperative blood loss

Intraoperative blood loss, with SDs and 95% CIs, was reported in two studies (SA: 42 patients; TBW: 43 patients; Fig. 2B). The mean blood loss was 20.72 mL in the SA group and 22.48 mL in the TBW group. No significant difference was observed between the two groups (MD = − 0.94 mL; 95% CI − 2.56 to 0.69; I2 = 0%; p = 0.26).

Functional outcomes

Bostman score

The Bostman score, with SDs and 95% CIs, was reported in four studies (SA: 78 patients; TBW: 86 patients; Fig. 2C). The mean score was 27.98 in the SA group and 26.50 in the TBW group. The Bostman score was significantly higher in the SA group than in the TBW group (MD = 1.46; 95% CI, 0.14–2.77; I2 = 81%; p = 0.03).

Insall–Salvati ratio

The Insall–Salvati ratio, with SDs and 95% CIs, was reported in three studies (SA: 71 patients; TBW: 81 patients; Fig. 2D). The mean ratio was 0.914 in the SA group and 0.898 in the TBW group. No significant difference was observed between the two groups (MD = 0.01; 95% CI − 0.01 to 0.03; I2 = 0%; p = 0.32).

ROM

ROM, with SD and 95% CIs, was reported in the five studies (SA: 106 patients; TBW: 121 patients; Fig. 2E). The mean ROM was 128.35° in the SA group and 125.78° in the TBW group. ROM was significantly greater in the SA group than in the TBW group (MD = 2.96°; 95% CI, 0.27–5.66; I2 = 53%; p = 0.03).

Radiological outcomes

Fracture union time

Fracture union time, with SDs and 95% CIs, was reported in four studies (SA: 83 patients; TBW: 98 patients; Fig. 2F). The mean union time was 13.50 weeks in the SA group and 13.93 weeks in the TBW group. No significant difference was observed between the two groups (MD = 0.02 weeks; 95% CI − 0.44 to 0.48; I2 = 0%; p = 0.93).

Complications

Postoperative complications were reported in the five studies (SA: 106 patients; TBW: 121 patients; Fig. 2G). Postoperative complications occurred in 2 patients in the SA group (fixation failure, n = 1; mild anchor prolapse, n = 1) and in 23 patients in the TBW group (fixation failure, n = 3; wire fracture, n = 2; implant allergy, n = 1; nonunion, n = 2; and skin irritation, n = 15). SA fixation was associated with a significantly lower risk of complications than TBW (RR = 0.24; 95% CI 0.07–0.77; I2 = 0%; p = 0.02).

Discussion

Although TBW and SA fixation are widely used, no previous systematic review or meta-analysis has directly compared these approaches. This meta-analysis showed that TBW and SA fixation are effective modalities for treating inferior pole patellar fractures. SA fixation was associated with significantly lower complication rates and modestly superior functional outcomes. However, no statistically significant differences in operative time or intraoperative blood loss were observed between the two modalities.

Functional recovery was evaluated using three distinct parameters: Bostman score, Insall–Salvati ratio, and ROM [15, 16]. The SA group showed significant improvements in the Bostman score and ROM of approximately 1.5 points and 3°, respectively. Considerable statistical heterogeneity in these outcomes was observed (I2 = 81% for Bostman score and 53% for ROM). This variance may be due to divergent postoperative weight-bearing protocols and the fact that the included studies did not explicitly distinguish between active and passive ROM measurements. Furthermore, inconsistencies in the timing of assessment across the cohorts limited the feasibility of a more granular pooled analysis.

The clinical relevance of these functional findings must be evaluated against the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). Although recent studies have established MCID thresholds for patellar fractures using the knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (7.0 for pain and 11.8 for ADL) [17], no MCID values have been validated specifically for the Bostman score or ROM in this fracture subtype. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting the observed functional gains, as they may fall below the threshold of perceptible clinical benefit.

The analysis of operation time revealed considerable statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 97%). This high degree of variance reflects the clinical heterogeneity inherent in orthopedic trauma, particularly diverse fracture comminution patterns and varying surgeon proficiency with the emerging SA technique, as well as methodological differences in the definition of “operative time” across global institutions. A random-effects model was used for all pooled analyses to rigorously account for these variations. This approach incorporates within-study and between-study variance, providing a more conservative and generalizable estimate of the treatment effect than a fixed-effect model.

The functional improvements observed with SA fixation may be related to reduced implant-related soft tissue irritation. TBW often involves prominent metallic hardware that can cause discomfort and limit rehabilitation. However, pain and soft tissue irritation were not directly assessed. Therefore, this explanation remains speculative. Radiological outcomes showed a similar trend with slightly shorter fracture union times in the SA group, although this difference was not statistically significant. Furthermore, SA fixation was associated with lower postoperative complication rates than TBW. Although SA fixation appears to reduce implant-related morbidity, more standardized reporting of complication severity and long-term follow-up is required.

The small number of studies precluded meaningful subgroup or sensitivity analyses. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting the results. Inferior pole fractures pose unique surgical challenges because of small, often comminuted fragments. Accurate fixation is essential for preventing malunion, impingement, and anterior knee pain [18, 19].

Historically, partial patellectomy was used for small fragments. However, extensive evidence highlights the essential role of the patella in knee function [20]. Functional outcomes after partial patellectomy are often comparable to those of small retained fragments and are generally suboptimal [21]. Therefore, this approach is now less commonly employed.

TBW remains the most widely used technique. It provides stable fixation by counteracting quadriceps tensile forces while preserving vascular supply to the articular surface. Generally, TBW achieves better outcomes than partial patellectomy even in comminuted fractures [15]. However, it frequently requires secondary surgery due to hardware-related complications, including skin irritation, wire breakage, and migration [3, 22]. Kumar et al. [23] reported that approximately one in three patients required implant removal, which can lead to refracture, infection, joint stiffness, cartilage damage, or chronic pain [24].

Nonabsorbable suture fixation has been proposed as an alternative technique to achieve comparable outcomes while avoiding hardware removal. TBW and suture fixation have shown similar clinical and radiological outcomes [22], supporting the potential of suture-based techniques as a viable option. However, these techniques are less widely adopted.

Increasing attention has been paid to SA fixation for open reduction and internal fixation of patellar fractures. Shi et al. [25] reported that SA fixation is associated with early rehabilitation, effective fracture healing, and reduced joint stiffness. With advances in SA techniques, including double-row suture bridges [26] and suture bridge constructs for comminuted inferior pole fractures [7], fixation strength has improved. Additionally, SA fixation can be combined with additional materials, such as K-wires, to enhance stability [10, 27]. Potential limitations of SA fixation include infection, anchor pullout, and higher costs.

SA fixation in geriatric patients with osteoporosis may be limited by concerns about anchor pullout [28]. Biomechanical studies have shown that bone mineral density affects implant stability [29–31], and loosening or displacement may increase the risk of implant failure [30]. Therefore, alternative surgical strategies may be necessary in these populations.

Emerging trends in patellar fracture management include the use of nonmetallic fixation constructs, which aim to maintain stability while minimizing implant-related complications [32, 33]. Adjunctive orthobiologic therapies, such as platelet-rich plasma and mesenchymal stem cells, have been explored [34]. However, evidence remains limited and does not directly affect comparisons between TBW and SA fixation. Additionally, transosseous suture techniques, including the Krackow technique and separate vertical wiring, have demonstrated satisfactory outcomes (35–37) .

While recent systematic reviews have explored alternative fixation strategies, a significant gap remains in the literature regarding a direct, head-to-head statistical synthesis between the traditional metallic TBW gold standard and the emerging SA technique. Although Kim et al. [37] provided a valuable analysis comparing Krakow transosseous pullout sutures to separate vertical wiring, they focused on bone tunnel-based methods rather than the specific anchor-to-bone interface used in SA fixation. Many existing meta-analyses have diluted the specific clinical profile of SAs by aggregating them with various other nonmetallic or transosseous methods. This study addresses this distinct clinical gap by providing a focused analysis of the contemporary dilemma between TBW and SA fixation. By pooling data from the most recent cohorts, this study offers the statistical clarity necessary to compare complication profiles and objective functional scores, data that were previously too heterogeneous for definitive consensus. Our findings complement the work of Kim et al. [37] by reinforcing the viability of nonmetallic alternatives and demonstrating that SA fixation provides comparable clinical outcomes to TBW with a significantly lower risk of hardware-related complications. This targeted comparison isolates a fixation subset that has not previously been analyzed as a standalone group, thereby providing surgeons with evidence-based guidance for this specific technological transition.

This study has some limitations. All included studies were retrospective cohort studies, limiting causal inference and introducing potential confounding and selection bias. Variations in surgical indications, rehabilitation protocols, and timing of outcome assessments complicate comparisons. Outcomes were assessed across different time periods without adjustment methods, such as propensity score matching. The substantial heterogeneity in several outcomes reflects differences in surgical technique, surgeon experience, and rehabilitation. Although the exclusion of gray literature may have increased publication bias, it ensured the inclusion of peer-reviewed data. Furthermore, the included studies lack standardized, high-resolution postoperative radiographic data, which restricts the ability to perform a more granular assessment of fracture reduction quality and bone healing. Finally, cost considerations could not be systematically assessed due to inconsistent reporting.

Conclusion

SA fixation provided modest improvements in functional outcomes and a lower complication rate than TBW while achieving comparable clinical and radiological outcomes. These findings indicate that SA fixation is a viable alternative to TBW in selected patients. This study provides preliminary evidence to inform surgical decision-making. Further prospective randomized trials are needed to confirm these findings and better identify the relative advantages and limitations of each technique.
