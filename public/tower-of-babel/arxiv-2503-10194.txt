Surrogate modeling of resonant behavior in scattering problems through adaptive rational approximation and sketching Thanks: This research was funded in part by the Austrian Science Fund (FWF) projects 10.55776/F65 and 10.55776/P33477 (IP).
Davide Pradovera, Ralf Hiptmair, Ilaria Perugia

Abstract
This paper describes novel algorithms for the identification of (almost-)resonant behavior in scattering problems. Our methods, relying on rational approximation, aim at building surrogate models of what we call “field amplification”, defined as the norm of the solution operator of the scattering problem, which we express through boundary-integral equations. To provide our techniques with theoretical foundations, we first derive results linking the field amplification to the spectral properties of the operator that defines the scattering problem. Such results are then used to justify the use of rational approximation in the surrogate-modeling task. Some of our proposed methods apply rational approximation in a “standard” way, building a rational approximant for either the solution operator directly or, in the interest of computational efficiency, for a randomly “sketched” version of it. Our other “hybrid” approaches are more innovative, combining rational-approximation-assisted root-finding with approximation using radial basis functions. Three key features of our methods are that (i) they are agnostic of the strategy used to discretize the scattering problem, (ii) they do not require any computations involving non-real wavenumbers, and (iii) they can adjust to different settings through the use of adaptive sampling strategies. We carry out some numerical experiments involving 2D scatterers to compare our approaches. In our tests, two of our approaches (one standard, one hybrid) emerge as the best performers, with one or the other being preferable, depending on whether emphasis is placed on accuracy or efficiency.

Introduction

This work is concerned with the study of resonating behavior arising in wave-scattering problems, which are modeled in the frequency domain through the Helmholtz equation. It is well known from Fredholm theory that, as long as the proper
radiation conditions
are enforced for closure far away from the scatterer(s), scattering problems admit a unique solution at any nonzero
real
frequency. However, the well-posedness of the problem is sometimes at risk as a consequence of what we informally call “almost resonances”, namely, frequencies where the inf-sup constant of the problem becomes dangerously close to 0.

From a spectral viewpoint, such almost resonances can be interpreted as the “
real-axis
shadows” of the actual resonances that scattering problems display in the
complex
plane. As we discuss below in more detail, such complex resonances with nonzero imaginary parts are the solutions of certain nonlinear eigenvalue problems. Phenomenologically speaking, they correspond to a resonating behavior caused by the addition of a negative dissipation coefficient, i.e., a positive
reaction
term, to the wave equation. This ultimately leads to a blow-up in the scattered field.

Almost-resonance problems are usually exacerbated by large frequencies, since, in the large-frequency regime, the complex resonances of the scattering problem get arbitrarily close to the real axis. Thus, the behavior of the scattered wave can truly get close to resonance. A good understanding of these phenomena is paramount in many fields, e.g., when resonance is sought as a way to increase the power of a signal or, on the opposite end, when one tries to avoid resonance in the context of making a physical system more stable or well-behaved. This justifies our interest in this problem.

Incidentally, we also mention that almost resonances have important consequences on the
stability
of the scattering problem, e.g., when solved in approximated form by a numerical technique. As such, spotting almost resonances may be of use in understanding the behavior of numerical methods, e.g., in terms of convergence of iterative linear-system solvers.

At almost-resonating frequencies, the scattered wave may be rather large (in some norm) even for small incident waves. This issue may be equivalently stated in terms of a large operator norm (in suitable spaces) of the
solution operator
of the scattering problem, which, at fixed frequency, maps incident wave to scattered wave. In this work, we use this norm, under the label of “
field amplification
”, as a quantitative measure of almost-resonating behavior.

For a given scattering problem, our ultimate task is evaluating the field amplification over a prescribed range of frequency values. A key observation is that the cost of evaluating field amplification over a whole frequency range may be rather high, especially if both spatial- and frequency-domain discretizations are fine. Indeed, one is required to assemble the discrete solution operator and then find its norm (e.g., by SVD) at each frequency belonging to a grid that spans the target frequency range.

To lessen this burden, we rely on
surrogate modeling
, which suggests to replace the expensive high-fidelity scattering model with a more parsimonious low(er)-fidelity surrogate. The construction of such surrogate (the so-called “training” phase) is generally not cheap, since it must involve several evaluations of the high-fidelity model. However, it must be carried out only once, independently of the desired number of frequency-domain samples. Ignoring further training costs for simplicity, the complexity of a surrogate-based method for approximating scattering problems is often
\mathcal{O}\left(c_{\textup{high-fidelity}}n+c_{\textup{surrogate}}N\right)
, with
n
denoting the numbers of training samples and
N
denoting the number of target frequency evaluations. Compared to the “brute force” approach where the high-fidelity model is sequentially evaluated, computational time can be saved as long as the sampling is parsimonious (
n\ll N
) and the surrogate is efficient (
c_{\textup{surrogate}}\ll c_{\textup{high-fidelity}}
).

In recent years, surrogate modeling has often been applied with the objective of reducing the simulation costs linked to scattering problems
[
20
,
32
,
36
,
6
,
27
,
16
,
12
,
7
]
. In order to build an effective surrogate, a careful balance usually has to be struck between
a priori
knowledge of the high-fidelity problem’s structure and
a posteriori
information obtained from data. Both
a priori
and
a posteriori
information must be leveraged in the quest for an efficient method, i.e., keeping
n
and
c_{\textup{surrogate}}
low while achieving an acceptable approximation accuracy. The
a priori
knowledge on the problem is crucial for this, since it is what enables the selection of the “approximation class” within which the surrogate is then sought through a data-driven strategy. In the context of surrogate modeling, the approximation class often “makes or breaks” the effectiveness of a numerical algorithm.

As an example of this, we mention
[
12
]
, whose specific target is the approximation of field amplification just like in this work. The authors of
[
12
]
achieve efficiency with their surrogate-based method by a careful combination of adaptive sampling (keeping
n
low) and piecewise-polynomial approximation of the reciprocal of the field amplification (keeping
c_{\textup{surrogate}}
low).

However, the effectiveness of the algorithm in
[
12
]
is ultimately limited by the fact that the chosen approximation class only partially exploits the structure of the high-fidelity problem. Indeed, the method exploits the piecewise-smooth nature of the field amplification but not the meromorphy of the underlying scattering problem, cf.
Section

3
. In this work, we try to remedy this, by developing methods that better profit from both features. To this aim, we rely on some valuable results from operator theory to draw a key connection between our approximation target, namely, field amplification, and a certain nonlinear eigenvalue problem induced by the scattering problem’s differential operator.

A key aspect of our work is that, in building a surrogate for the field amplification, we assume that only
purely real
wavenumbers can be considered and sampled. Fundamentally, we do this because field amplification is a real quantity, related to real wavenumbers, so it should be possible to approximate it by sampling real wavenumbers only. However, and from a more practical viewpoint, non-purely-real wavenumbers are sometimes out of reach due to limitations in the numerical software that is used to discretize the scattering problem in the first place, and ultimately to compute field amplification. Indeed, adding an imaginary component to the wavenumber is arguably nonphysical, so that, by design, it may not be allowed by, e.g., finite- or boundary-elements libraries.

We stress that the focus of this paper is on the derivation and empiric validation of numerical algorithms. We conduct some mathematical analysis, but have to point out that some aspects of our new algorithm are still beyond the scope of rigorous theory.

2
Boundary-integral equations

We consider a general scattering problem in
d
dimensions, with
d\in\{2,3\}
, modeled through the Helmholtz equation with outgoing Sommerfeld radiation conditions

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{\textup{tot}}-(kn)^{2}u_{\textup{tot}}=0\quad&\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\left.\nabla(u_{\textup{tot}}-u_{\textup{inc}})\right\rvert_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}-\textup{i}kn_{\infty}\left.(u_{\textup{tot}}-u_{\textup{inc}})\right\rvert_{\mathbf{x}}=o\left(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\frac{1-d}{2}}\right)\quad&\textup{as }\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\to\infty.\end{cases}

(1)

The unknown is the
total wave

u_{\textup{tot}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
,
\textup{i}=\sqrt{-1}
is the imaginary unit, and
u_{\textup{inc}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is a given
incident
wave. Moreover,
n:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the index of refraction, which is assumed to attain a constant value inside and outside of a suitable bounded, Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Without loss of generality, by a scaling argument, we can set
n=1
outside
\Omega
, so that

n(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}n_{\textup{i}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\quad&\textup{for }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
1\quad&\textup{for }\mathbf{x}\notin\Omega,\end{cases}.

(2)

Hence, the boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
represents an interface between two materials with different, but uniform, acoustic properties. Generalizations to non-constant
n_{\textup{i}}
are possible.

In the following, we will sometimes explicitly denote the dependence of waves
u_{\bullet}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
on the wavenumber
k\in\mathbb{R}
, through the notations
u_{\bullet}(k)
or
u_{\bullet}(\mathbf{x};k)
.

Under the assumption that the incident wave satisfies the Helmholtz equation with index of refraction
1
, i.e.,

-\Delta u_{\textup{inc}}-k^{2}u_{\textup{inc}}=0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{d},

(3)

we can equivalently cast the problem in
transmission
form:

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{\textup{i}}-(kn_{\textup{i}})^{2}u_{\textup{i}}=0\quad&\textup{in }\Omega,\\
-\Delta u_{\textup{s}}-k^{2}u_{\textup{s}}=0\quad&\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\\
u_{\textup{i}}=u_{\textup{s}}+g_{D}\quad&\textup{on }\Gamma,\\
\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{i}}=\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{s}}+g_{N}\quad&\textup{on }\Gamma,\\
\left.\nabla u_{\textup{s}}\right\rvert_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}-\textup{i}ku_{\textup{s}}(\mathbf{x})=o\left(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{\frac{1-d}{2}}\right)\quad&\textup{as }\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\to\infty.\end{cases}

(4)

Above, we have introduced
\nu
, the outward-pointing normal to
\Gamma
, as well as

u_{\textup{tot}}(\mathbf{x};k)=:\begin{cases}u_{\textup{i}}(\mathbf{x};k)\quad&\textup{for }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
u_{\textup{s}}(\mathbf{x};k)+u_{\textup{inc}}(\mathbf{x};k)\quad&\textup{for }\mathbf{x}\notin\Omega,\end{cases}

The jump data
(g_{D},g_{N})\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma)\times H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
are defined based on the incident wave, through Dirichlet and Neumann trace operators, namely,

g_{D}=\left.u_{\textup{inc}}\right\rvert_{\Gamma}\quad\textup{and}\quad g_{N}=\left.(\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{inc}})\right\rvert_{\Gamma}.

It is known that, under the above assumptions, with the addition of smoothness requirements on
\Gamma
and
u_{\textup{inc}}
, the scattering problems in
Eqs.

1
and
4
admit weak solutions
u_{\textup{tot}}\in H_{\textup{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
(u_{\textup{i}},u_{\textup{s}})\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{\textup{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega})
, respectively, for all nonzero wavenumbers
k\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

[
12
]
.

2.1
Second-kind boundary-integral equations

Closely following
[
12
, Section 2]
and
[
17
, Section 1.3]
, we rewrite the transmission problem
Eq.

4
as a boundary-integral equation. We leverage representations of
u_{\textup{i}}
and
u_{\textup{s}}
in terms of
layer potentials
, via the free-space Green’s function for the Helmholtz operator
\Delta+\kappa^{2}\mathcal{I}
, namely,

G(\mathbf{x};\kappa):=\begin{cases}\frac{\textup{i}}{4}H_{0}(\kappa\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)\quad&\textup{if }d=2,\\
\frac{1}{4\pi\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|}\exp(\textup{i}\kappa\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)\quad&\textup{if }d=3,\\
\end{cases}

with
H_{0}
the index-0 Hankel function of first kind. Note, in particular, that the Green’s function depends on
\kappa
in a very
nonlinear
but
holomorphic
way. The (unique) solution of
Eq.

4
admits a layer potential representation
[
12
, (5)]

\begin{cases}u_{\textup{i}}=-\mathsf{V}(kn_{\textup{i}})\big[\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{i}}|_{\Gamma}\big]-\mathsf{K}(kn_{\textup{i}})\big[u_{\textup{i}}|_{\Gamma}\big]\quad&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{\textup{s}}=\mathsf{V}(k)\big[\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{s}}|_{\Gamma}\big]+\mathsf{K}(k)\big[u_{\textup{s}}|_{\Gamma}\big]&\text{in }\mathbb{R}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\end{cases}

(5)

with the “single-layer” and “double-layer” integral operators defined in a parameter-dependent way as

\mathsf{V}(\kappa):H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)\ni\psi\mapsto\int_{\Gamma}G(\cdot-\mathbf{y};\kappa)\psi(\mathbf{y})\textup{d}S(\mathbf{y})\in H_{\textup{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(6a)

\mathsf{K}(\kappa):H^{1/2}(\Gamma)\ni\psi\mapsto-\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu(\mathbf{y})}G(\cdot-\mathbf{y};\kappa)\phi(\mathbf{y})\textup{d}S(\mathbf{y})\in H_{\textup{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(6b)

Applying Dirichlet and Neumann traces to the single- and double-layer potential from
Eq.

6
yields four
boundary-integral operators
, whose integral representations read

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{V}(\kappa)\psi&:=\int_{\Gamma}G(\cdot-\mathbf{y};\kappa)\psi(\mathbf{y})\textup{d}S(\mathbf{y}),\\
\mathcal{K}(\kappa)\phi&:=-\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu(\mathbf{y})}G(\cdot-\mathbf{y};\kappa)\phi(\mathbf{y})\textup{d}S(\mathbf{y}),\\
\mathcal{K}(\kappa)^{*}\phi&:=-\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu(\mathbf{x})}G(\cdot-\mathbf{y};\kappa)\phi(\mathbf{y})\textup{d}S(\mathbf{y}),\\
\mathcal{W}(\kappa)\phi&:=-\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu(\cdot)}\partial_{\nu(\mathbf{y})}G(\cdot-\mathbf{y};\kappa)\phi(\mathbf{y})\textup{d}S(\mathbf{y}).\end{aligned}

(7)

See, e.g.,
[
28
, Section 3.3]
. These are the building blocks for the second-kind single-trace boundary-integral equation satisfied by the Dirichlet and Neumann traces of the solution of the scattering transmission problem
Eq.

4
. More precisely, applying both trace operators to the representation formula
Eq.

5
, appealing to jump relations
[
28
, Section 3.1.1]
, and taking into account transmission conditions, we obtain

\mathcal{A}(k)\begin{bmatrix}u_{\textup{i}}|_{\Gamma}\\
\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{i}}|_{\Gamma}\end{bmatrix}=\mathcal{B}(k)\begin{bmatrix}g_{D}\\
g_{N}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

with the compound boundary-integral operators (
\mathcal{I}
being the identity operator in
L^{2}(\Gamma)
)

\mathcal{A}(k):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{K}(kn_{\textup{i}})-\mathcal{K}(k)&\mathcal{V}(k)-\mathcal{V}(kn_{\textup{i}})\\
\mathcal{W}(k)-\mathcal{W}(kn_{\textup{i}})&\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{K}(kn_{\textup{i}})^{*}-\mathcal{K}(kn_{\textup{i}})^{*}\end{pmatrix}

and

\mathcal{B}(k):=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{K}(k)&\mathcal{V}(k)\\
\mathcal{W}(k)&\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{K}(k)^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

The former operator
\mathcal{A}(k)
is bounded on
[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}

[
12
, Lemma 1]
. Other properties of this formulation of the scattering problem, including its well-posedness, are discussed in
Section

3
. Another key feature of
Eq.

8
is the nonlinear but holomorphic dependence of the two above operators on the wavenumber
k
.

Remark 2.1
.

In several cases of practical interest,
(g_{D},g_{N})
contains Dirichlet and Neumann traces on
\Gamma
of a field
u_{\textup{inc}}
that locally solves the Helmholtz equation with unit refraction index, cf.
Eq.

3
. One can verify that such boundary data is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{B}(k)
with unit eigenvalue
[
8
, Theorem 2.6]
, i.e.,

\mathcal{B}(k)\begin{bmatrix}g_{D}\\
g_{N}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{D}\\
g_{N}\end{bmatrix}.

As such, one may sometimes simplify
Eq.

8
by replacing
\mathcal{B}(k)
with the identity operator.

2.2
Galerkin boundary-element discretization

We consider the boundary-integral equation
Eq.

8
in
[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}
and use the
L^{2}(\Gamma)
inner product to cast it into a variational form, which is the starting point for Galerkin discretization. Next, we pick a finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{X}_{h}
of
L^{2}(\Gamma)
, with
N_{h}=\dim\mathcal{X}_{h}
, and use
\mathcal{X}_{h}^{2}
as trial and test spaces. Then we choose a basis
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{h}}
of
\mathcal{X}_{h}
, which induces a basis
\{[\psi_{i},0]^{\top}\}_{i=1}^{N_{h}}\cup\{[0,\psi_{i}]^{\top}\}_{i=1}^{N_{h}}
of
\mathcal{X}_{h}^{2}
. We use this latter basis to convert
Eq.

8
into a
(2N_{h})\times(2N_{h})
linear system of equations

\textup{A}(k)\mathbf{u}=\textup{B}(k)\mathbf{g},

(9)

where the vector
\mathbf{g}
is defined as the coefficients (with respect to the chosen basis) of the
[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}
-orthogonal projection of
[g_{D},g_{N}]^{\top}
onto
\mathcal{X}_{h}^{2}
. Moreover,
Eq.

9
features the matrix
\textup{B}(k)
, defined entry-wise as

\textup{B}(k)_{ij}:=\begin{cases}\Big(\big({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{K}(k)\big)\psi_{j},\psi_{i}\Big)\quad&\text{if }i\leq N_{h}\text{ and }j\leq N_{h},\\
\Big(\mathcal{V}(k)\psi_{j-N_{h}},\psi_{i}\Big)\quad&\text{if }i\leq N_{h}\text{ and }j>N_{h},\\
\Big(\mathcal{W}(k)\psi_{j},\psi_{i-N_{h}}\Big)\quad&\text{if }i>N_{h}\text{ and }j\leq N_{h},\\
\Big(\big({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{I}+\mathcal{K}(k)^{*}\big)\psi_{j-N_{h}},\psi_{i-N_{h}}\Big)\quad&\text{if }i>N_{h}\text{ and }j>N_{h},\\
\end{cases}

with
(u,v)
denoting the
L^{2}(\Gamma)
-inner product, as well as
\textup{A}(k)=\textup{M}+\textup{B}(k)-\textup{B}(kn_{\textup{i}})
, with
M
being the
[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}
-mass matrix, defined entry-wise as

\textup{M}_{ij}:=\begin{cases}\big(\psi_{j},\psi_{i}\big)\quad&\text{if }i\leq N_{h}\text{ and }j\leq N_{h},\\
\big(\psi_{j-N_{h}},\psi_{i-N_{h}}\big)\quad&\text{if }i>N_{h}\text{ and }j>N_{h},\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

(10)

Note that we are glossing over some “variational crimes” committed through the inevitable use of numerical quadrature. We refer to
[
28
, Chapter 5]
for details on this issue and a comprehensive analysis.

Once the vector
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{2N_{h}}
solving
Eq.

9
has been found, approximations of the Dirichlet and Neumann traces can be recovered through linear combinations of the basis functions:

\displaystyle u_{i}|_{\Gamma}\approx(u_{\textup{i}})_{h}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{h}}(\mathbf{u})_{j}\psi_{j}\quad\textup{and}\quad\partial_{\nu}u_{i}|_{\Gamma}\approx(\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{i}})_{h}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{h}}(\mathbf{u})_{N_{h}+j}\psi_{j}.

The
boundary-element method
(BEM) relies on a particular construction of the discrete space
\mathcal{X}_{h}
built by first triangulating
\Gamma
and then using piecewise polynomial spaces on the resulting triangulation. Details are given in
[
28
, Chapter 4]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In
Remark

2.1
, we have mentioned how one can sometimes simplify the boundary-integral formulation by replacing the operator
\mathcal{B}(k)
with the identity. In the corresponding discrete formulation, one must replace
\textup{B}(k)
with the mass matrix

3
Scattering spectrum and field amplification

The well-posedness of the boundary-integral formulation
Eq.

8
and of its BE discretization
Eq.

9
may be determined by a Fredholm-alternative argument
[
28
, Section 2.1.4]
, based on the observation that, for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{A}(k)
is a compact perturbation of the identity operator in
[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}
. This makes it an operator of index zero, see, e.g.,
[
30
,
28
,
12
]
. Thanks to the injectivity of
\mathcal{A}(k)
for any nonzero
k
, one can conclude that
Eq.

8
is well-posed for all nonzero wavenumbers
k
. From
[
28
, Section 4.2.3]
we learn that, provided that
\mathcal{X}_{h}
possesses sufficiently good
L^{2}(\Gamma)
-approximation properties, the discretized boundary-integral equation
Eq.

9
is also well-posed.

The homogeneous version of the continuous and discretized boundary-integral equations

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(k)\begin{bmatrix}u_{\textup{i}}\\
\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{i}}\end{bmatrix}=0\quad\textup{and}\quad\textup{A}(k)\mathbf{u}=0,

(11)

obtained by setting
g_{D}=g_{N}=0
, has some relevance on its own. Indeed, it can be interpreted as a
nonlinear eigenvalue problem
, with
k
and
[u_{\textup{i}},\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{i}}]^{\top}
playing the parts of eigenvalue and eigenfunction, respectively. This problem has been extensively studied, and many of its theoretical properties have been explored before. For instance, eigenvalues must have negative imaginary parts
[
23
, Appendix A]
, with the exception of
0
, which admits the constant eigenfunction
[u_{\textup{i}},\partial_{\nu}u_{\textup{i}}]^{\top}\equiv[1,0]^{\top}
. Moreover, as we show in the next section, the set of eigenvalues is discrete and has no finite accumulation points.

3.1
Some properties of the solution operator

Most results presented in this section apply regardless of whether the infinite-dimensional boundary-integral formulation
Eq.

8
or its BE discretization
Eq.

9
is considered. As such, we employ the symbol
\mathcal{L}(k)
to denote either
\mathcal{A}(k)
or
\textup{A}(k)
.

We first recall Keldysh’s theorem for the inverse of a parameter-dependent operator, generalizing
[
14
, Theorem 2.8]
to the operator setting. See also
[
11
, Chapter 5]
and references therein for more details.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{C}
be compact. Let
\mathcal{L}(k)
be an operator-valued function of
k
, holomorphic (with respect to
k
) over
K
. We assume that, for all
k\in K
,
\mathcal{L}(k)
is a Fredholm operator of index zero over some Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
, e.g.,
(\mathcal{L}(k)-I):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is compact, with
I
the identity operator over
\mathcal{X}
. If
\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}
exists for at least one value of
k\in K
, then

\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{i,j}}{(k-\lambda_{i})^{j}}+\mathcal{H}(k)\quad\textup{for all }k\in K\setminus\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}.

(12)

Above, we have introduced several new objects, which we characterize as follows:

•

\Lambda_{K}=\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}\subset K
, whose elements are assumed pairwise distinct without loss of generality, is the
finite
set of values of
k\in K
where
\mathcal{L}(k)
is not invertible.

•

For each
i
,
m_{i}\geq 1
is the length of the longest
chain of generalized eigenvectors
corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
. The positive integer
m_{i}
represents the
order
of
\lambda_{i}
as a
pole
of
\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}
, in a complex-analysis sense.

•

For each
i
and
j
,
\mathcal{P}_{i,j}
is an operator whose range lies within the generalized eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
; for each
i
,
\mathcal{P}_{i,m_{i}}
is nonzero.

•

\mathcal{H}(k)
is an operator-valued function, holomorphic (with respect to
k
) over
K
.

Note, in particular, that
\Lambda_{K}
and
\mathcal{H}(k)
are
K
-dependent.

Remark 3.2
.

Keldysh’s theorem is founded on complex analysis and operator theory. Specifically, the “chains of generalized eigenvectors”, which allow the definition of the pole orders
m_{i}
, are the nonlinear extension of “Jordan chains”, which play a pivotal role in linear eigenvalue problems for non-normal (non-diagonalizable) matrices. In the framework of
Theorem

3.1
, we call
\{v_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m-1}\subset\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
a length-
m
chain of generalized eigenvectors associated to
k=\lambda
for
\mathcal{L}(k)
if

\sum_{n=0}^{j}\left.\left(\partial_{k}^{j-n}\mathcal{L}(k)\right)\right|_{k=\lambda}v_{n}=0\quad\textup{for all }j=0,\ldots,m-1.

Since the boundary-integral operators
Eq.

7
and their BE discretizations appearing in
Eq.

9
depend on
k
in a holomorphic way, the above theorem applies to both infinite- and finite-dimensional cases, by defining
\mathcal{X}=[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}
and
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{C}^{2N_{h}}
, respectively.

As already mentioned,
\mathcal{L}(k)
is invertible for all nonzero wavenumbers
k
with non-negative imaginary part. As a consequence,
\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}
exists for at least one value of
k\in\mathbb{C}
, and
Theorem

3.1
states that
\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}
is
meromorphic
over
\mathbb{C}
.
By applying
Theorem

3.1
over compact subsets of the upper half of the complex plane, one can conclude that
\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}
is well-defined and holomorphic there. In addition, the set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
of wavenumbers
k
such that
\mathcal{L}(k)
is not invertible cannot have any finite accumulation points.

Based on these two properties, a simple analytic-continuation argument shows that, given a real interval
[k_{\textup{min}},k_{\textup{max}}]\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, there exists a finite-thickness “strip”

\{z\in\mathbb{C},k_{\textup{min}}\leq\real(z)\leq k_{\textup{max}},-\varepsilon\leq\imag(z)\leq\varepsilon\}

(13)

where
\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}
is well-defined and holomorphic, as long as
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small. Specifically,
(-\varepsilon)
must be larger than the imaginary part of any eigenvalues
\lambda
whose real part is within the chosen wavenumber range.
However, in most settings, one can show that eigenvalues get arbitrarily close to the real line
[
23
]
, so that the above-mentioned “strip of holomorphy” must necessarily get thinner as one moves to larger (in magnitude) real wavenumber ranges.

This last feature can be equivalently stated in terms of possibility of approximation. Given a real interval, the thickness of the strip of holomorphy is directly related to the “ease” with which
\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}
can be approximated by polynomials. Roughly speaking, the rate of convergence of the error achieved, e.g., by approximating
\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}
with Chebyshev polynomials, improves as the strip of holomorphy becomes thicker. For large(r) wavenumbers, the thinness of the strip of holomorphy behooves one to discard polynomials altogether, resorting to other approximation strategies instead. In particular, given the meromorphy of
\mathcal{L}(k)^{-1}
, approximation by
rational functions
appears natural, cf.
Section

4.1
.

3.2
Field amplification

We are finally ready to properly introduce the quantity whose approximation is the target of our work, to which we refer as “field amplification”
\phi=\phi(k)
. We informally define it as the (operator) norm of the solution operator of the scattering problem, mapping incident data to the solution field. More rigorously, employing the boundary-integral form with the simplification described in
Remark

2.1
, we define

\phi(k)=\left\|\mathcal{A}(k)^{-1}\right\|_{[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}\to[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}}=\sup_{(g_{D},g_{N})\in[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\left\|\mathcal{A}(k)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}g_{D}\\
g_{N}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}}\bigg/\left\|\begin{bmatrix}g_{D}\\
g_{N}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}}.

(14)

As the name suggests, field amplification measures how large the norm of the scattered solution field can be, with respect to that of the incident field. This quantity is of great importance in several settings, e.g., to study the desired or undesired resonant behavior of physical systems. Moreover, field amplification has an impact on the numerics of solving scattering problems, since it is related to the conditioning of the BE problem. If
\phi(k)
is large, significant errors may be incurred due to round-off or other inaccuracies in the data, when numerically computing the scattered field. For instance, knowledge of
\phi
(even if approximate) allows one to draw quantitative conclusions on the magnitude of perturbations in the scattered field, resulting from perturbations in the incoming wave. This is of particular interest, e.g., in uncertainty quantification.

Computing
\phi
in practical settings demands a discretization of the infinite-dimensional space
[L^{2}(\Gamma)]^{2}
. As such, we replace the analytic field amplification
Eq.

14
with its discrete counterpart, which we still denote by
\phi
with an abuse of notation:

\phi(k)=\left\|\textup{A}(k)^{-1}\textup{M}\right\|_{\textup{op},\textup{M}}:=\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{h}}\setminus\{0\}}\left\|\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{A}(k)^{-1}\textup{M}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}\Big/\left\|\textup{M}^{1/2}\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2},

with
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
denoting the Euclidean norm and
M
denoting the mass matrix, cf.
Section

2.2
. Recall that we are casting the discrete problem as described in
Remark

2.2
, thus justifying the right-multiplication of the resolvent operator by the mass matrix. By the change of variable
\mathbf{v}\mapsto\textup{M}^{-1/2}\mathbf{v}
, we conclude that

\phi(k)=\left\|\textup{C}(k)\right\|_{\textup{sp}},\quad\text{given }\textup{C}(k)=\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{A}(k)^{-1}\textup{M}^{1/2},

(15)

with
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\textup{sp}}
representing the (Euclidean) spectral norm, i.e., the largest singular value of a matrix.

We note that, in practice, it is sometimes common to use a Cholesky factor of
M
rather than its square root: given
\textup{M}=\textup{R}^{*}\textup{R}
, one may define
\textup{C}(k)=\textup{R}\textup{A}(k)^{-1}\textup{R}^{*}
. Whether this is done or not is irrelevant here.

Remark 3.3
.

If it is
not
possible to ignore the operator
\mathcal{B}(k)
and the matrix
\textup{B}(k)
, cf.
Remarks

2.1
and
2.2
, one can define
\phi
incorporating it:
\textup{C}(k)=\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{A}(k)^{-1}\textup{B}(k)\textup{M}^{-1/2}
. Since
\textup{B}(k)
is smooth with respect to
k
, our discussion below applies also in this case.

By meromorphy of
\textup{A}(k)^{-1}
, which follows from
Theorem

3.1
, the matrix
\textup{C}(k)
is also meromorphic over
\mathbb{C}
with respect to
k
, with poles coinciding with those of
\textup{A}(k)^{-1}
. As a consequence,
\phi
, by continuity of the spectral-norm operator, remains bounded over all compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Lambda
, with
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}
being the set of all wavenumbers
k
such that
\textup{A}(k)
is singular. However, this stops being true if one moves toward an eigenvalue, as the following proposition states.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
\textup{A}(\lambda)
is singular, i.e.,
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Let
m=m(\lambda)\in\mathbb{N}
be the length of the longest chain of generalized eigenvectors associated to
\lambda
, cf.
Remark

3.2
. As
k\to\lambda
, there exist
\psi_{\lambda}>0
, depending only on the spectral projector
\textup{P}_{1,m}
, cf.
Theorem

3.1
, such that

\phi(k)=\psi_{\lambda}\left|k-\lambda\right|^{-m}+\mathcal{O}\left(\left|k-\lambda\right|^{1-m}\right).

(16)

Proof.

Let
\textup{C}(k)
be defined as in
Eq.

15
.
Theorem

3.1
can be applied to
\textup{A}(k)
near
\lambda
. By
Theorem

3.1
, we may choose a neighborhood
K
small enough that
\lambda
is the only pole of
\textup{A}(k)^{-1}
inside it. This leads to

\textup{C}(k)=\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{A}(k)^{-1}\textup{M}^{1/2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{P}_{1,j}\textup{M}^{1/2}}{(k-\lambda)^{j}}+\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{H}(k)\textup{M}^{1/2},

which is valid over the punctured
K\setminus\{\lambda\}
. By the triangular inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\phi(k)=\left\|\textup{C}(k)\right\|_{\textup{sp}}\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{P}_{1,m}\textup{M}^{1/2}}{(k-\lambda)^{m}}\right\|_{\textup{sp}}+\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{P}_{1,j}\textup{M}^{1/2}}{(k-\lambda)^{j}}+\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{H}(k)\textup{M}^{1/2}\right\|_{\textup{sp}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{P}_{1,m}\textup{M}^{1/2}\right\|_{\textup{sp}}}{\left|k-\lambda\right|^{m}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\left|k-\lambda\right|^{1-m}\right),

(17)

with the last step following from
\textup{H}(k)=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
by holomorphy of
H
over
K
. The claim then follows by setting
\psi_{\lambda}=\left\|\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{P}_{1,m}\textup{M}^{1/2}\right\|_{\textup{sp}}
, which is strictly positive by
Theorem

3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Identifying the length
m(\lambda)
of the longest chain of generalized eigenvectors associated to an eigenvalue
\lambda
is generally a difficult problem. To simplify our work, we will assume that no non-trivial solution exists, so that all poles
\lambda
have order
m(\lambda)=1
:

\phi(k)=\frac{\psi_{\lambda}}{\left|k-\lambda\right|}+\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)=\frac{\left\|\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{P}_{1,1}\textup{M}^{1/2}\right\|_{\textup{sp}}}{\left|k-\lambda\right|}+\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)\quad\textup{for }k\sim\lambda\in\Lambda.

(18)

This case is typical in practical settings, e.g., since round-off noise makes all poles’ orders 1.

Remark 3.6
.

It is crucial to note that
Proposition

3.4
provides a link between the field amplification function
\phi
defined in
Eq.

15
and the nonlinear eigenvalue problem
Eq.

11
. The singularities
\lambda_{i}
of
\phi
, whose existence is proven by
Theorem

3.1
, are located at all solutions to eigenproblem
Eq.

11
. However, solving the nonlinear eigenvalue problem is
not enough
to characterize the field amplification. This is because of the additional smooth term
\textup{H}(k)
, which affects
\phi
but is invisible to the eigenvalue problem.

The above results allow us to conclude that, although
\phi
is bounded over any closed interval
K\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, if the strip of holomorphy of
\textup{C}(k)
is too narrow, cf.
Eq.

13
, then almost-resonances will appear, in the form of “spikes” in
\phi
, cf.
Eqs.

16
and
18
. This is confirmed by our numerical examples in
Section

6
.

Now, recall that, in this work, we face the task of approximating
\phi
over the frequency range
K
. To ease the approximation effort, it is crucial to identify the set of eigenvalues that limit the strip of holomorphy of
\textup{C}(k)
, which we denote by
\Lambda_{K}
. Here we assume that
\Lambda_{K}
contains the
M_{K}
eigenvalues that lie closest to
K
, with
M_{K}
a large enough number, roughly corresponding to how many local maxima (the above-mentioned “spikes”)
\phi
has in
K
.

By identifying the elements of
\Lambda_{K}
and understanding their effect on
\phi
, one may circumvent some of the main issues due to the limited regularity of
\textup{C}(k)
. In the next section, we describe how one can rely on rational approximation to achieve this, leveraging the meromorphic structure of the problem.

4.1
Rational approximation

In order to justify rational approximation for field amplification, we take
Theorem

3.1
as starting point. Therein, it is shown that the resolvent
\textup{A}(k)^{-1}
is a matrix-valued meromorphic function of the wavenumber
k
. As such, it is possible to
efficiently
approximate it
to arbitrary accuracy
via
matrix-valued rational functions
, which, in this context, we define as the ratio of a matrix-valued polynomial and a scalar polynomial. For instance, using the extremely popular
barycentric
interpolatory rational form
[
24
]
, we may approximate
\textup{C}(k)
as

\textup{C}(k)\approx\textup{L}(k):=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{K}}\frac{w_{i}\textup{C}(\overline{k}_{i})}{k-\overline{k}_{i}}\bigg/\sum_{i=1}^{n_{K}}\frac{w_{i}}{k-\overline{k}_{i}}.

(19)

Note how the rational approximation consists of a linear combination of samples
\{\textup{C}(\overline{k}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{K}}
at arbitrary (distinct) locations
\{\overline{k}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{K}}\subset\mathbb{C}
, weighted by
scalar
-valued rational functions

\left\{k\mapsto\frac{w_{i}}{k-\overline{k}_{i}}\bigg/\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n_{K}}\frac{w_{i^{\prime}}}{k-\overline{k}_{i^{\prime}}}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{K}}.

One commonly refers to the scalar numbers
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{K}}\subset\mathbb{C}
, which represent the degrees of freedom of the rational approximation, as
barycentric weights
. Depending on the specific rational-approximation scheme, these are determined by solving a suitable data-fit or optimization problem.

Thanks to their meromorphic structure with respect to
k
, scattering problems have often been effectively dealt with by rational approximation in the surrogate-modeling community. See, e.g.,
[
16
,
4
,
27
,
6
]
. Even the extremely general “reduced basis” method
[
7
]
(which fundamentally has no links to rational functions) sometimes yields a rational surrogate when applied to the Helmholtz equation.

Remark 4.1
.

In
Algorithm

2

\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{K}}
. How this is done depends on which rational-approximation method was chosen. Generally, this step consists of solving a suitable optimization problem. For instance, in set-valued AAA
[
21
]
and minimal rational interpolation
[
5
]
, one finds the barycentric weights by solving a certain least-squares problem involving a Loewner-like matrix of size
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{h}^{2}n_{K}\right)\times n_{K}
. Note, in particular, how the number of rows of the Loewner matrix scales with
N_{h}^{2}
as a consequence of the unraveling of the samples from
(2N_{h})\times(2N_{h})
matrices into vectors of size
4N_{h}^{2}
. The cost of solving this least-squares problem is
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{h}^{2}n_{K}^{3}\right)
, the scaling which is reported in
Algorithm

2
as reference. More advanced strategies based on QR factorization may reduce this cost to
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{h}^{2}n_{K}^{2}\right)

[
5
]
.

In terms of algorithmic efficiency, it is crucial to assume that
n_{K}\ll N_{K}
. Indeed, if this is not the case, a surrogate model approximating
\phi
over the target wavenumbers
\{k_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{K}}
is just as complex as
\phi
itself. As such, rational approximation loses its effectiveness, and a direct evaluation of
\{\phi(k_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{K}}
as in

Moreover, for simplicity, from now on we will assume that
n_{K}\ll N_{h}
, i.e.,
M_{K}\ll N_{h}
. In practical settings, this is a reasonable assumption if the “spatial” complexity of the scattering problem dominates the “spectral” complexity of the problem.

Remark 4.2
.

Our assumption that
M_{K}\ll N_{h}
is often true in practice, especially in cases where a high spatial resolution is needed for good accuracy of the BEM discretization, e.g., if the scatterer has a complicated geometry. Still,
M_{K}\gtrsim N_{h}
may happen if the problem has an extremely complex spectrum, cf. the numerical results in
Section

6
. In that case, rational approximation is less beneficial at reducing the complexity of computing
\phi
, since
n_{K}>M_{K}\gtrsim N_{h}
. Specifically, sampling becomes the main bottleneck of any method for approximating
\phi
in this case.

Under the above assumptions,

\textup{L}(k)
. To achieve that, one may replace the cubic-cost SVD with a small number

of power-method iterations, achieving
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{h}^{2}\log N_{h}\right)

. In this way, one can decrease the surrogate-evaluation cost to
c_{\textup{surrogate}}=\mathcal{O}\left(N_{h}^{2}(n_{K}+\log N_{h})\right)
and the total complexity to
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{h}^{3}n_{K}+N_{h}^{2}n_{K}^{3}+N_{h}^{2}(n_{K}+\log N_{h})N_{K})\right)

\phi

(2N_{h})\times(2N_{h})

. This can be improved, as we proceed to describe.

4.2
Compression by randomized power method

The randomized power method (RPM) falls within the family of
randomized
methods in computational linear algebra, where randomness is leveraged to reduce the computational cost of linear-algebra operations, e.g., matrix compression
[
15
,
35
]
and approximating vector norms
[
9
,
22
]
. In randomized linear algebra, a large number of operations on deterministic vectors is replaced with a smaller number of operations on random vectors, introducing a small but nonzero probability of failure in the process.

The RPM can be applied in the specific case of spectral-norm approximation. Consider a matrix
\textup{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, a random vector
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, e.g., with i.i.d. normal Gaussian entries, and an arbitrary number of iterations
q\geq 0
. We can approximate

\left\|\textup{C}\right\|_{\textup{sp}}\approx\left\|\textup{C}\right\|_{\textup{sp},\textup{RPM},q}:=\frac{\left\|\textup{C}(\textup{C}^{*}\textup{C})^{q}\mathbf{b}\right\|}{\left\|(\textup{C}^{*}\textup{C})^{q}\mathbf{b}\right\|}.

(20)

This is simply the square root of the estimate for the largest eigenvalue of
\textup{C}^{*}\textup{C}
that one gets after
q
iterations of the well-known power method with starting vector
\mathbf{b}
.

Standard results in basic computational linear algebra show the convergence of
\left\|\textup{C}\right\|_{\textup{sp},\textup{RPM},q}
to
\left\|\textup{C}\right\|_{\textup{sp}}
as
q\to\infty
. In general, a minimum number of iterations
q\sim\log N
(the so-called “burn-in period”) is needed to guarantee a good approximation accuracy
[
29
,
22
]
. For simplicity, from here onward, we use the above result to prescribe the scaling
q\sim\log N
, so that the cost of computing
\left\|\textup{C}\right\|_{\textup{sp},\textup{RPM},q}
is
\mathcal{O}\left(N^{2}\log N\right)
.

As previously suggested, a straightforward application of RPM can speed up the spectral norm evaluations in
Algorithms

1
and
2
, since its
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{h}^{2}\log N_{h}\right)
cost is an improvement over the
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{h}^{3}\right)
cost of SVD.

However, we can employ RPM in a different way, leading to further computational savings. The main idea, rather than building a rational approximation of a matrix and then applying the RPM to it, is to apply the RPM first and then build rational approximations of the two vectors appearing in the RPM approximation, cf.
Eq.

20
. More rigorously, one may construct two rational functions
\bm{\xi}_{0}
and
\bm{\xi}_{1}
, approximating the two
\mathbb{C}^{2N_{h}}
-valued functions

\textup{C}(k)^{p}\left(\textup{C}(k)^{*}\textup{C}(k)\right)^{q}\mathbf{b}\approx\bm{\xi}_{p}(k):=\sum_{i=1}^{\widehat{n}_{K}}\frac{w_{p,i}\textup{C}(\overline{k}_{i})^{p}\left(\textup{C}(\overline{k}_{i})^{*}\textup{C}(\overline{k}_{i})\right)^{q}\mathbf{b}}{k-\overline{k}_{i}}\bigg/\sum_{i=1}^{\widehat{n}_{K}}\frac{w_{p,i}}{k-\overline{k}_{i}},

(21)

for
p\in\{0,1\}
. This leads to the procedure outlined in
Algorithm

.

5
Hybrid surrogate modeling

In the previous sections, we have discussed two surrogate-modeling-based methods for approximating
\phi
, which follow rigorous function-approximation and computational-linear-algebra theory. In this section, we describe an alternative approach, which approximates
\phi
following more heuristic arguments. Thanks to some drastic simplifications, the upcoming “hybrid” method, described in
Section

5.3
below, is extremely efficient. Indeed, it builds a surrogate model using roughly as few samples as
Algorithm

2
, with an extremely low surrogate-evaluation cost
c_{\textup{surrogate}}=\mathcal{O}\left(n_{K}\right)
.

However, the heuristic step that leads to an enhanced efficiency also causes a decrease in accuracy. Specifically, although our upcoming method provides an approximation that converges to the exact
\phi
, cf.
Proposition

5.2
, such convergence is slow. For this reason, it is more useful to think of our hybrid approach as building a “rough” approximation of
\phi
. What still justifies our interest in such method is that (i) the approximation retains good accuracy near almost-resonating wavenumbers and (ii) a higher accuracy can still be achieved if one is willing to pay a higher (but still competitive) cost for building the surrogate.

In order to derive our hybrid algorithm, we start from
Remark

3.5
and apply
Eq.

18
near all troublesome poles within
\Lambda_{K}
. Ignoring bounded
\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
-terms, we obtain

\phi(k)=\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|\textup{C}(k)\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}\approx\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{M_{K}}\frac{\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{P}_{i,1}\textup{M}^{1/2}\mathbf{v}}{k-\lambda_{i}}\right\|_{2},

(23)

with
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M_{K}}
being an enumeration of
\Lambda_{K}
. Note, in particular, that, since the eigenspaces are not pairwise orthogonal
[
2
]
, we cannot simplify the above expression through the Pythagorean theorem.

Moreover, we observe that, in order to use
Eq.

23
directly, we need to compute the (oblique) spectral projectors
\textup{P}_{i,1}
. This not only is computationally expensive, but also requires taking samples of
\textup{C}(\cdot)
at non-purely-real wavenumbers, which is impossible in our framework. As such, we propose to avoid handling such projectors, by looking at a more manageable
scalar
expression, obtained by applying the triangular inequality:

\phi(k)\lessapprox\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},0}(k):=\sum_{i=1}^{M_{K}}\sup_{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|\frac{\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{P}_{i,1}\textup{M}^{1/2}\mathbf{v}}{k-\lambda_{i}}\right\|_{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{K}}\frac{\psi_{\lambda}}{\left|k-\lambda\right|},

(24)

recalling the definition
\psi_{\lambda_{i}}=\left\|\textup{M}^{1/2}\textup{P}_{i,1}\textup{M}^{1/2}\right\|_{\textup{sp}}
.

At this point, one might note that
Eq.

24
does not avoid the computation of the spectral projectors
\textup{P}_{i,1}
, since they appear in the definitions of the scalar quantities
\psi_{\lambda_{i}}
. Moreover, the approximation relies on the unknown eigenvalues
\Lambda_{K}
. We now describe how these issues can be circumvented, through a combination of rational approximation and linear algebra, by approximating the set
\Lambda_{K}
(
Section

5.1
) and by fitting the expansion coefficients
\psi_{\lambda_{i}}
from data (
Section

5.2
).

5.1
Identifying the poles

Approximating the eigenvalues of a nonlinear eigenproblem is a common task in computational linear algebra. Solvers based on contour integration
[
3
]
or (local) linearizations
[
14
]
are quite popular. Unfortunately, linearizations are not very effective for our problem, due to the extremely nonlinear (in
k
) nature of the BE matrices. Moreover, contour integration is unavailable since, by assumption, we cannot sample the BE matrices at non-real wavenumbers. As a feasible alternative, we use rational approximation once more, similarly to
[
25
]
. Specifically, we start by computing a rational approximation of either the BE matrix
\textup{L}(k)\approx\textup{C}(k)
, a one-sided sketch
\bm{\xi}(k)\approx\textup{C}(k)\mathbf{b}
, with
\mathbf{b}
being a random vector, or a two-sided sketch
\zeta(k)\approx\mathbf{b}_{1}^{*}\textup{C}(k)\mathbf{b}_{2}
, with
\mathbf{b}_{1}
and
\mathbf{b}_{2}
being random vectors. Then we find an approximation of
\Lambda_{K}
by computing the set of poles of
\textup{L}(k)
,
\bm{\xi}(k)
, or
\zeta(k)
. Crucially, such pole-finding requires non-real computations of the rational approximation only, without involving any evaluation of the BE matrices at non-real wavenumbers.

In summary, given
n_{K}
samples, in this way we can approximate
\Lambda_{K}
with complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{h}^{3}n_{K}+N_{h}^{\eta}n_{K}^{3}\right)
, with
\eta\in\{0,1,2\}
depending on what kind of sketching is used, if any. Two-sided sketching yields the most efficient algorithm (
\eta=0
) and is the alternative chosen, e.g., in
[
6
]
. However, in our tests, we use one-sided sketching (
\eta=1
) because of two reasons:

•

it has lower cost than the non-sketched option (
\eta=2
) at (empirically) no accuracy reduction;

•

the vector-valued nature of
k\mapsto\bm{\xi}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{2N_{h}}
affords us the convenience of using the
adaptive
rational-approximation tools from
[
5
,
26
]
, which cannot be applied to the scalar-valued
k\mapsto\zeta(k)
.

Remark 5.1
.

In using rational approximation for pole-finding, one can fall in a common pitfall, related to the appearance of so-called “spurious poles”. Spurious poles are poles of the rational approximation that do not correspond to any pole of the original function. These may appear even in exact arithmetic
[
1
, Chapter 6.1]
, and are automatically placed by the rational-approximation algorithm at locations that are “convenient” to improve the approximation quality. As it so often happens, round-off noise exacerbates this issue in numerical settings. When identifying
\Lambda_{K}
by the set of poles of a rational approximation, one would ideally want to filter out any spurious poles.

Unfortunately, the spurious-pole filtering suggested by the above remark is one of the hardest tasks in rational approximation. Some (more or less) heuristic strategies for this exist
[
24
,
6
]
, flagging poles as spurious based on few additional samples of the target function. Unfortunately for our purposes, such additional samples must be placed near the poles. Due to the complex nature of the poles, cf.
Section

3
, this is impossible in our setting, where evaluations of the BE matrices are constrained to the real axis. There are also other techniques that flag poles as spurious if the corresponding
approximate residue
, i.e., the residue in a Heaviside expansion of the rational surrogate, is small. However, we were not able to apply these ideas effectively in our experiments, since we have often found spurious residues with even larger magnitude than non-spurious ones. For these reasons, in our algorithm we choose to approximate
\Lambda_{K}
either by the set of
all
surrogate poles

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}^{\textup{all}}:=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\sum_{i=1}^{n_{K}}\frac{w_{i}}{\lambda-\overline{k}_{i}}=0\right\},

cf.
Eq.

19
, or by the subset of all those surrogate poles that are closest to some
k\in\{k_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{K}}
, i.e.,

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}^{\textup{close}}:=\left\{\lambda\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}^{\textup{all}}:\exists k\in\{k_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{K}}\textup{ such that }\left|\lambda-k\right|\leq\left|\lambda^{\prime}-k\right|\forall\lambda^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}^{\textup{all}}\right\}.

(25)

The rationale behind taking the subset
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}^{\textup{close}}\subset\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}^{\textup{all}}
, rather than
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}^{\textup{all}}
itself, is that the latter set often includes a certain amount of spurious poles, located
at some distance
from
K
. Indeed, there are theoretical grounds for expecting at least some of the spurious poles to cluster on a relatively large Bernstein ellipse around the sampling interval
K

[
31
]
. Numerical evidence for this, as well as a discussion of the effects that spurious poles have on the resulting approximation of
\phi
, are included in
Section

6
.

5.2
Assembling the approximate field amplification

Now, assume that
\Lambda_{K}
has been well approximated as
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}=\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M_{K}^{\prime}}
by the above rational-approximation-based strategy, where, with an abuse of notation, we are denoting the surrogate poles as
\lambda_{i}
. The number of approximate eigenvalues satisfies
M_{K}^{\prime}\leq n_{K}-1
and should ideally be close to
M_{K}
. We face the task of computing the coefficients
\psi_{\lambda}
in the approximate expansion
Eq.

24
, with
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}
replacing
\Lambda_{K}
. To this aim, we choose to use the following approach based on
collocation
.

We start by observing that the coefficients
\{\psi_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}}
can be interpreted as the degrees of freedom appearing in an approximation of
\phi
that uses a linear combination of the basis functions
\{\left|\cdot-\lambda\right|^{-1}\}_{\lambda\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}}
. As long as the real parts of the approximate poles are pairwise distinct, any set of
M_{K}^{\prime}
distinct
collocation points

\{\widetilde{k}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M_{K}^{\prime}}\subset\mathbb{R}
is “unisolvent” with this basis, in the sense that the data-fit problem

\textup{find }\{\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}}\textup{ such that }\phi(\widetilde{k}_{i})=\sum_{\lambda\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}}\frac{\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda}}{\left|\widetilde{k}_{i}-\lambda\right|}\quad\textup{for all }i=1,\ldots,M_{K}^{\prime}

(26)

admits a unique solution. As such, it is possible to find approximations
\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda}\approx\psi_{\lambda}
by sampling the exact
\phi
at the
M_{K}^{\prime}
distinct points
\{\widetilde{k}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M_{K}^{\prime}}
, and then solving
Eq.

26
. In this way, one can sidestep the complicated projectors
\textup{P}_{i,1}
from
Eq.

24
.

Leveraging common ideas from the radial-basis literature, one may improve both approximation accuracy and numerical stability by placing the collocation points at the locations where the basis functions attain their respective maxima. See, e.g.,
[
34
]
. In our setting, this happens at
\widetilde{k}_{i}:=\real\lambda_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,M_{K}^{\prime}
.

Also stemming from the radial-basis literature is the idea of enriching the radial basis with some additional basis functions, e.g., polynomials up to a certain (low) degree. To account for the increased basis size, one must either add linear constraints or include additional samples. Although the former approach is more efficient, we choose to follow the latter approach since the additional samples help us achieve a higher accuracy. We introduce
n_{o}\geq 0
additional samples
3
3

3

The subscript “o” stands for “oversampling”.

\{\widetilde{k}_{i+M_{K}^{\prime}}\}_{i=1}^{n_{o}}\subset\mathbb{R}
, e.g., at uniformly spaced locations over
K
. Then we fix an arbitrary basis
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{b}}
, with
0\leq n_{b}\leq n_{o}
, and we construct the approximation

\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},n_{b}}(k)=\sum_{\lambda\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}}\frac{\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda}}{\left|k-\lambda\right|}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{b}}\alpha_{j}\psi_{j}(k).

(27)

The expansion coefficients can be easily found by solving the data-fit problem

\phi(\widetilde{k}_{i})=\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},n_{b}}(\widetilde{k}_{i})\quad\textup{for all }i=1,\ldots,M_{K}^{\prime}+n_{o},

(28)

either exactly (if
n_{b}=n_{o}
) or in a least-squares sense (if
n_{b}<n_{o}
).

While the accuracy of the basic approximation
\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},0}
hinges on the accuracy of the approximate expansion
Eq.

24
, the addition of extra terms can aid in overcoming this limitation. Indeed, since the coefficients of
\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},n_{b}}
are found by fitting
exact
values of
\phi
, the extra terms can reduce the “modeling mismatch” incurred in the scalar heuristic approximation
Eq.

24
. Based on this, we can conclude that
\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},n_{b}}

converges
to the exact
\phi
as
n_{b}\to\infty
, in the following sense.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
be compact. Let
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of functions
\psi_{j}:K\to\mathbb{R}
, and assume that any continuous positive function can be approximated by the family
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
to arbitrary precision in the
L^{\infty}(K)
-norm:

\forall\mu\in C(K;\mathbb{R}_{>0})\quad\lim_{n_{b}\to\infty}\inf_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{b}}}\sup_{k\in K}\left|\mu(k)-\sum_{j=1}^{n_{b}}\alpha_{j}\psi_{j}(k)\right|=0.

(29)

Then, if
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}\cap K=\emptyset
, one may choose the coefficients
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{b}}
in
Eq.

27
so that
\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},n_{b}}
converges to
\phi
in the
L^{\infty}(K)
-norm as
n_{b}\to\infty
.

Proof.

As discussed in
Section

3
, the spectrum of the scattering problem satisfies
\Lambda\subset\{0\}\cup\{z\in\mathbb{C},\imag z<0\}
and has no finite accumulation points. As such, the interval
K
has nonzero distance from
\Lambda
. Accordingly, the field amplification
\phi
is bounded uniformly over
K
by
Theorem

3.1
. Recalling definition
Eq.

15
, the continuity of
\phi
can be easily deduced by combining the facts that (i) the matrix
\textup{A}(k)^{-1}
depends continuously on
k\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Lambda
, cf.
Theorem

3.1
, and (ii) the spectral norm is continuous as a
\mathbb{C}^{(2N_{h})\times(2N_{h})}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
function.

If no surrogate pole
\lambda\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}
lies in
K
, the sum
\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},0}
, cf.
Eq.

24
, is a continuous function. As such, the claim follows by applying
Eq.

29
to the difference
\mu=\phi-\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},0}
, which is also continuous over
K
.
∎

Note that the “universal-approximation property”
Eq.

29
, which is sufficient for convergence, is satisfied by many common hierarchical bases, e.g., polynomials of increasing degree, the Fourier basis, or the piecewise-linear finite element basis on nested meshes. Low-regularity bases, e.g., the last above-mentioned option, are recommended for higher numerical stability, due to the low smoothness of
\phi
. In addition, we remark that the condition
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{K}\cap K=\emptyset
is generally satisfied in practice due to round-off noise. If this is not true, it suffices to “nudge” any real poles by a small imaginary shift
(-\varepsilon\textup{i})
,
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
.

Moreover, it is important to note that the convergence predicted by
Proposition

5.2
will inevitably be slow, due to the low regularity of
\phi
, which is continuous but not differentiable, due to the usual presence of kinks, as showcased, e.g., in
[
12
]
and in our numerical tests in
Section

6
. Such kinks are a simple consequence of the non-differentiability of the spectral norm. As such, we expect a convergence of order at most
\mathcal{O}\left(n_{b}^{-1}\right)
.

Remark 5.3
.

Proposition

5.2
may be adapted to show that a suitable superposition of the basis functions
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{b}}
converges to
\phi
as
n_{b}\to\infty
at the same rate
even without the additional term

\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},0}
. The justification for including this latter term is providing a better “starting point” for the surrogate, which already includes a fairly good approximation of any almost-resonant behavior.

There exists an alternative to approximation
Eq.

27
. Rather than using the triangular inequality to obtain the
sum
of the fractions in
Eq.

24
, we can simply take their
maximum
, in some sense following
Proposition

3.4
:

\phi(k)\approx\widetilde{\phi}_{\max,n_{b}}(k):=\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{K}}\frac{\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda}}{\left|k-\lambda\right|}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{b}}\alpha_{j}\psi_{j}(k).

(30)

The numerators
\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda}
may be found, e.g., with a heuristic collocation-based approach, through a direct sampling of
\phi(\widetilde{k})
at
\widetilde{k}=\real\lambda
:

\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda}:=\left|\real\lambda-\lambda\right|\phi(\real\lambda)=\left|\imag\lambda\right|\phi(\real\lambda)\quad\textup{for any }\lambda\in\Lambda_{K}.

By construction, this simple choice yields
\phi(\real\lambda)=\widetilde{\phi}_{\max,0}(\real\lambda)
for any
\lambda\in\Lambda_{K}
, as long as the maximum in
Eq.

30
is attained at
\lambda
, i.e., whenever no approximate pole “hides” any other approximate pole.

Furthermore, the coefficients
\alpha_{j}
may be found by imposing additional interpolation conditions, either exactly or in a least-squares sense:

\phi(\widetilde{k}_{i})=\widetilde{\phi}_{\max,n_{b}}(\widetilde{k}_{i})\quad\textup{for all }i=M_{K}^{\prime}+1,\ldots,M_{K}^{\prime}+n_{o}.

This procedure has the same complexity as the “sum-based” one.

The benefit of the expression
\widetilde{\phi}_{\max,0}
, compared to
\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},0}
, is that it is
not
globally smooth, but only piecewise differentiable. As our numerical results in
Section

6
show, this feature allows
\widetilde{\phi}_{\max,n_{b}}
to better mimic the regularity of the exact field amplification
\phi
, which is also only piecewise smooth. We still have convergence.

Corollary 5.4
.

Proposition

5.2
also holds with
\widetilde{\phi}_{\max,n_{b}}
in place of
\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},n_{b}}
.

Proof.

The proof of
Proposition

5.2
still applies, since
\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{max},0}
, cf.
Eq.

30
, is continuous over
K
.
∎

Numerical results

In this section, we perform numerical tests to assess the effectiveness of our proposed methods on some 2D benchmarks. For the BE discretization, we use continuous piecewise-linear polynomials for both Dirichlet and Neumann traces. Our algorithms are coded in Python 3.10, with wrappers to interface the code with a C++ implementation of the P1-BEM, compiled with
c++
11.4.0. For reproducibility, our code is made available at
https://github.com/pradovera/hybridScatteringAmplification.git
, where further information on software is also provided. All tests are performed on a machine with a 6-core 12-thread 3.5-GHz Intel
®
Xeon
®
processor. The BEM implementation takes full advantage of the multi-threaded architecture through OpenMP. Our tests involve the three scatterers displayed in
Fig.

1
.

Disk, C-shaped, and kite scatterers.

6.1
Disk scatterer

We consider a simple unit-disk scatterer
\Omega=B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
with contrast
n_{\textup{i}}=\sqrt{20}
, cf.
Eq.

2
. We target the approximation of the field amplification over the wavenumber range
K=[1,3]
, over a uniform grid with
N_{K}=1001
frequency points. The BEM discretization uses an 11
th
order quadrature rule to approximate the integral operators on
N_{h}=200
“panels”. (Other resolutions
N_{h}\in\{50,100\}
were also tested with similar results.) The number of RPM iterations used for estimating
\phi
is
q=\lfloor 10(1+\log N_{h})\rfloor=70
. For rational approximation of matrix- and vector-valued functions, we employ an
hp
-greedy version of minimal rational interpolation, as proposed in
[
27
]
, with a tolerance of
1\%
on the relative rational-approximation error in the Frobenius norm.

Spectral features

In

Appendix

\phi

In
Fig.

Algorithm

2
as gray circles. We can see that the rational poles provide a fairly good approximation of some exact eigenvalues. The not-so-high accuracy is a straightforward consequence of the fact that the rational approximation is built based only on real-axis information. Accordingly, some errors are incurred in the estimation of the eigenvalues’ locations, especially for those with larger (in magnitude) imaginary parts.

We can also observe how the rational approximation introduces an error in the eigenvalues that lie closest to the real axis, cf. the eigenvalues with negative imaginary parts smaller than
10^{-4}
. This might seem surprising, since the real information should do a better-than-average job at identifying “almost real” eigenvalues. However, the (apparently) large errors are, in fact, rather small, magnified by the logarithmic scale. These are just consequences of the BEM discretization: refining the BEM mesh leads to a more accurate approximation of the almost-real eigenvalues.

Moreover, we can see that one of the rational poles,
\lambda\approx 3.16+0.07\textup{i}
, has positive imaginary part. This is a non-physical eigenvalue, but should not be necessarily considered spurious, cf.
Remark

5.1
. Indeed, rational approximation sometimes relies on unstable poles to improve approximation accuracy. It is commonly suggested to either keep such non-physical poles, to “flip” them
[
13
]
, i.e., change the sign of their imaginary parts, or to remove them
[
18
]
. Four more spurious poles can also be observed in
Fig.

2
(top), along a band with imaginary part between
-1
and
-0.2
.

Starting from the case

A partial explanation for this effect can be found by looking at the shape of the

\widetilde{\phi}_{\textup{sum},0}

We also test the hybrid algorithm with

Remark 6.1
.

As a further comparison, we also ran the algorithm introduced in

Moreover, the method from

]
was worse than our best approaches, namely,
Algorithms

2
and
4

\phi
. This is a consequence of the fact that, by design, the method from
[

targets only the peaks of the field amplification, neglecting regions where the behavior of

7
Conclusions

In this work, we have introduced several methods for approximating the field amplification
\phi
related to scattering problems. Through rational approximation, we are able to exploit the problem’s meromorphic structure to great effect, leading to considerable computational savings. Among the presented algorithms, the ones that show the greatest potential are
Algorithms

2
and
4
.

•

Algorithm

2
performs rational approximation of the BEM-discretized solution operator, achieving great accuracy at the cost of a super-quadratic surrogate-evaluation cost due to the need to evaluate the spectral norm of large matrices.

•

The “hybrid” method from
Algorithm

4
targets the field amplification more directly, through a scalar interpolatory approach. This reduces the evaluation cost at the price of some accuracy. The hybrid method uses rational approximation as a tool to greatly improve the approximation quality and efficiency, by enriching the interpolation basis with some additional rational terms. This strategy ultimately enables a modest approximation accuracy at extremely low cost.

Both rational and hybrid approaches yield excellent results in our three test cases, especially when looking at the identification of the locations of the almost-resonances, namely, the spikes in the field amplification. In our view, it is important to note how well all our approaches perform, despite their very limited “intrusiveness”: none of the four methods presented requires access to the structure of the BEM operators, namely, to their specific frequency dependence. This feature is what ultimately enables applying our methods to a problem whose parameter dependence is so intrinsically nonlinear. Moreover, we highlight how our methods are able to succeed at approximating field amplification despite being limited to sampling real wavenumbers only.

Concerning future research directions, we note that the field amplification has been defined in terms of
surface potentials
on the interface
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
. In some cases, it might be of greater interest to consider
volume
field amplifications, e.g., measured in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
-metric:
\phi_{\Omega}(k):=\sup_{u_{\textup{inc}}\neq 0}\left\|u_{\textup{tot}}(k)\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}/\left\|u_{\textup{inc}}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
, cf.
Eq.

1
. In our setting,
\phi_{\Omega}
may be computed via the representation formula
Eq.

5
, which provides an
invertible
extension operator from surface potentials to Helmholtz solutions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In principle, our discussion and our techniques should apply also to
\phi_{\Omega}
. However, in such case the approximation task is much more challenging, since (i) the wavenumber-dependent extension operator makes
\phi_{\Omega}
depend on
k
in a more complicated way than
\phi
, and (ii) building and inverting the extension operator is difficult in itself. For these reason, further analysis and implementation work remain to be done.

Appendix A
Exact scattering eigenvalues on the disk

Let
J_{\nu}
and
H_{\nu}
denote Bessel and Hankel functions of the first kind of index
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
, respectively. Using polar coordinates, the eigenfunctions of the scattering problem for the unit disk
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
with refraction index
n_{i}
are of the form

u_{\nu}(r,\theta)=\begin{cases}\alpha J_{\nu}(n_{\textup{i}}\lambda r)e^{\textup{i}\nu\theta}\quad&\textup{if }r<1,\\
\beta H_{\nu}(\lambda r)e^{\textup{i}\nu\theta}\quad&\textup{if }r>1,\end{cases}

using the integer
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
to index the spectrum. The above scalars
\lambda
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
solve the following nonlinear eigenvalue problem:

\textup{find }\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\textup{ and }(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\textup{ s.t.}\begin{pmatrix}J_{\nu}(n_{\textup{i}}\lambda)&H_{\nu}(\lambda)\\
n_{\textup{i}}\lambda J_{\nu}^{\prime}(n_{\textup{i}}\lambda)&\lambda H_{\nu}^{\prime}(\lambda)\end{pmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\
-\beta\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{0}.

The above
2\times 2
transcendental system encodes compatibility conditions of Dirichlet and Neumann traces across the interface
\Gamma
. In our numerical tests, we solve this nonlinear eigenvalue problem by using Beyn’s method
[
3
]
.
