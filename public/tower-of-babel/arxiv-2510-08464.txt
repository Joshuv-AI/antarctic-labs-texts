Don’t Run with Scissors : Pruning Breaks VLA Models but They Can Be Recovered
Jason Jabbour, Dong-Ki Kim, Max Smith, Jay Patrikar, Radhika Ghosal, Youhui Wang, Ali Agha, Vijay Janapa Reddi, Shayegan Omidshafiei

Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are a promising paradigm for field robotics, but their deployment is bottlenecked by strict edge SWaP-C constraints. Compression techniques such as quantization, distillation, and pruning are well-established for LLMs and VLMs. Pruning, however, remains particularly underexplored for VLA models. We surprisingly find that pruning VLA models yields drastic performance degradation and increased safety violations. Through spectral analysis, we identify structural properties of VLA weights that help explain this fragility. Based on this insight, we introduce GLUESTICK , a post-pruning recovery method that restores much of the original model’s capability while preserving sparsity benefits. GLUESTICK computes a one-time corrective term by interpolating between the dense and pruned models in weight space. At inference, each pruned layer applies this correction to recover lost performance with minimal overhead. Our approach requires no additional training and is agnostic to the pruning algorithm. Across diverse VLA architectures and domains, GLUESTICK recovers up to 100% of the success rate and restores safety to near-dense levels, while saving up to 5 GB of memory and achieving up to 1.75 × \times speedup. Additional material can be found at: https://gluestick-vla.github.io/ .

Pruning for LLMs and VLMs.
Pruning compresses models by setting a fraction of weights to zero

[

[
25

Koike-Akino et al. [44]
reported a 12.5% accuracy drop on LLaVA-7B, and
Liang et al. [49]
observed 9%–30% drops in LLaVA variants. No prior work has examined how pruning affects VLA success or safety, or how such impacts might be recovered.

Weight Modifications and Safety.
Beyond pruning, other forms of weight adjustment have been widely studied, such as machine unlearning
[
29
,
54
]
and fine-tuning
[
34
]

These studies demonstrate that modifying weights can strongly affect downstream behavior: fine-tuning, for instance, has been shown to degrade LLM safety

Taken together, this line of work suggests that while pruning has been validated on LLMs, weight adjustments cannot be assumed to be benign in all contexts.
For VLAs, where degraded behavior has physical consequences, the safety implications of pruning remain unstudied. Recovering from such damage in weight space remains an open problem that we address.

Motivation: Necessity of Pruning for Edge Devices on Field Robots

The gap between VLA resource requirements and the constraints of edge hardware is severe. We detail the severity of that gap and the distinct role pruning plays in addressing it.

Edge Hardware Constraints.
Since field robots perform computation onboard, the hardware itself must fit within tight SWaP-C budgets
[
63
,
38
,
39
]
.
Datacenter accelerators sit far outside this budget. An NVIDIA B200, for instance, consumes on the order of a kilowatt per GPU
[
71
]
. A typical field robot’s battery pack stores only a few hundred watt-hours, meaning a single datacenter GPU would drain it in minutes, before powering any motors or sensors
[
38
]
. Instead, the field uses a family of edge accelerators, which consume on the order of tens of watts and can run for hours on the same battery. The NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX
[
67
]
, AGX Orin
[
66
]
, and Jetson Thor
[
70
]
are the dominant platforms for onboard compute in field robotics
[
74
,
53
,
81
,
69
,
87
]
. The tradeoff is memory and compute. The Orin NX, for example, provides only 8 to 16 GB shared between CPU and GPU; the OS and every other process on the robot draw from the same pool that the robotic algorithms must fit into. This is far below server-grade hardware, which may pair eight HGX B200 GPUs, each with 180 GB of dedicated memory, with terabytes of host RAM. The compute gap is equally stark. The Orin NX delivers just 4.8 FP16 TFLOPs

[
67
]
, while a single B200 provides 4,500 FP16 TFLOPs

[
68
,
14
]
, nearly 1,000
\times
more compute. For field robots, which are real-time systems that must react quickly to dynamic environments, this compute deficit directly limits how fast control loops can run.

Why Pruning Matters for Edge Robotics.

Deploying VLAs on these devices requires reducing both memory footprint and inference latency. Pruning is especially attractive because it addresses both simultaneously by removing weights entirely rather than merely reducing their precision. When applied in hardware-friendly patterns such as structured “N:M” sparsity (e.g., 2:4), pruning reduces both parameter count and FLOPs. Modern GPUs exploit these patterns with specialized kernels that reduce memory traffic and multiply-accumulate operations (MACs); for example, NVIDIA’s Sparse Tensor Cores and cuSPARSELt accelerate 2:4 sparse general matrix multiplications
[
57
]
. The reduced computation due to pruning not only enables acceleration and memory savings but also significantly cuts power consumption
[
30
]
. For devices operating under strict constraints on compute, memory, and energy, these benefits make pruning a critical tool for deploying VLAs at the edge. As discussed, quantization, distillation, and pruning are complementary; removing any one from the toolbox places a ceiling on how compact the deployed model can be. Our central goal is to bring pruning back into this toolbox for VLAs.

GLUESTICK: Why Pruning Breaks VLA Models and How to Fix It

GLUESTICK

4.1
Pruning Severely Degrades VLA Performance

Pruning has substantially reduced memory usage in LLMs and VLMs, with minimal loss in accuracy (see Section
2
).
At the same sparsity levels commonly used in LLMs and VLMs, we observe surprisingly different outcomes on popular VLAs.
Representative VLAs such as OpenVLA
[
43
]
and NaVILA
[
12
]
, when pruned with Magnitude or Wanda, drop in task success rate to 0% (from 85.2% and 43.0%, respectively).
Pruning also increases unsafe behavior: OpenVLA’s unsafe-episode rate rises from 33.4% to 51.6%, and NaVILA’s from 23.0% to 100.0% (see Table
1
).
These findings show that text and image-validated pruning techniques do not directly transfer to embodied control. Yet despite this, pruning remains essential as it allows models to fit on edge hardware and leverage sparse kernel acceleration (Table
1
).

To explain why pruning degrades VLAs far more than LLMs or VLMs, we ask whether their weight-space properties differ.
Thus, we examine the singular value spectra of equal-sized layers within the language backbones of OpenVLA (VLA), LLaMA-2-7B (LLM)
[
88
]
, and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct (VLM)
[
5
,
79
]
. We display these spectra in Figure
2
, where each line corresponds to the singular values of a single layer, obtained by computing the SVD of that layer’s weight matrix and plotting its full set of singular values. In LLM and VLM models, the spectra are more anisotropic, evidenced by a steep initial drop followed by a long tail, which concentrates energy in a few dominant directions.
This profile helps pruning, since removing small coefficients mainly trims low-energy directions while leaving the principal subspaces intact.
In contrast, VLA layers show a noticeably flatter decay, indicating energy spread across many directions.
In this regime, even small-magnitude coefficients contribute to important subspaces, so pruning discards useful signal distributed throughout the matrix (see Appendix
B
for additional details).
Based on this insight, our method in the following section explores the recovery of this lost information within the weight space.

4.3
The GLUESTICK Recovery Method for Pruned VLA Models

The space of pruning configurations is combinatorial, making optimal selection of weights to remove intractable.
Heuristic methods such as Magnitude
[
32
]
and Wanda
[
85
]
sidestep the global optimization by scoring individual weights and pruning by score.
While simple and efficient, these heuristics discard correlated weights under grouped sparsity constraints (e.g., 50% sparsity with 2:4 or 4:8 groups), which could be especially harmful for VLA models.

We propose
GLUESTICK
, a post-hoc, training-free recovery method that operates entirely in weight space and is agnostic to the pruning algorithm (see Figure
3
).
GLUESTICK  requires only the original dense model and its pruned counterpart, and incurs a one-time offline cost; no additional training is required.

Specifically, for each linear layer with dense weight matrix
W_{\mathrm{dense}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
and its pruned version
W_{\text{pruned}}
(fixed, preserving the original 2:4/4:8 pattern), we define the
gap matrix
:

W_{\mathrm{gap}}=W_{\mathrm{dense}}-W_{\mathrm{pruned}},

(1)

which captures lost information due to pruning.
We then compute a truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) of the gap matrix:

W_{\mathrm{gap}}=U\Sigma V^{\top}\approx U_{r}\Sigma_{r}V_{r}^{\top},

(2)

keeping the top
r
singular components.
By
Eckart and Young [21]
, this is the
best rank-
r
approximation to
W_{\mathrm{gap}}
in Frobenius norm.
For memory and speed, we fold the singular values into one term so that only two compact matrices need to be stored:

A=U_{r}\Sigma_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r},\quad B=V_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r}.

(3)

During inference, GLUESTICK  adds a lightweight correction around each pruned layer:

h(x)=W_{\mathrm{pruned}}x+A(B^{\top}x),

(4)

which re-injects the dominant lost directions at low cost while leaving
W_{\mathrm{pruned}}
unchanged, thereby preserving the efficiency gains of structured sparsity with a minimal overhead addition from the correction term.
The extra compute from this correction is:

\underbrace{W_{\mathrm{pruned}}x}_{\text{efficient sparse matmul}}+\underbrace{B^{\top}x}_{\mathcal{O}(d_{\mathrm{in}}\,r)}+\underbrace{A(\,\cdot\,)}_{\mathcal{O}(d_{\mathrm{out}}\,r)},

(5)

or
\mathcal{O}((d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})r)
on top of the sparse matrix-matrix multiplication (matmul), versus
\mathcal{O}(d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}})
for the dense layer.
Our correction adds only
(d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})r
extra parameters per layer, which is small compared to
d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}}
in the dense case.
With
r\ll\min\{d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}}\}
, GLUESTICK  preserves the efficiency gains of structured 50% sparsity.

We refer to our method as GLUESTICK-
r
to indicate the chosen value of
r
.
We note that the parameter
r
provides a dial between memory usage and recovery.
Smaller values of
r
favor memory savings, while larger values prioritize recovery.
In practice, integrating GLUESTICK into a model requires only a wrapper around pruned layers (see Appendix
A
for details).

Evaluation

Our experimental setup spans two benchmarks covering distinct robotics domains: manipulation and navigation.
We evaluate three different VLA models on these tasks, with results measured using both task success and safety metrics. We structure our results around key questions, first presenting main findings on GLUESTICK  then providing analysis through ablations and broader considerations.

Experimental Setup

Environments.

We evaluate on two benchmarks: LIBERO
[
51
]
for manipulation (see Figure
1

[

]
for navigation (see Figure

C.1
for additional details.

Models.
We study three VLA models: OpenVLA
[
43
]
, a 7B-parameter generalist VLA for manipulation; WorldVLA
[

, a 7B manipulation-oriented VLA targeting long-horizon consistency; and NaVILA
[

]

Evaluation Metrics.
We evaluate on two axes: task success, defined as binary per-episode completion, and safety, defined as the absence of harm to the robot or its surroundings

27
,
59

Baselines.
We compare against three pruning strategies: Full Sparse, Sparse Language Backbone, and a Memory-Matched Sparse Language Backbone (a subset of backbone layers pruned to match GLUESTICK’s memory overhead). GLUESTICK’s recovery is particularly striking, restoring not only success but also trajectory efficiency. As seen in Table

To appreciate the extent of this recovery, consider what pruning does without it. Pruning’s failure is not a matter of less efficient paths; the robot’s behavior is fundamentally altered. Without GLUESTICK, pruned agents frequently veer into entirely different rooms rather than approaching the goal. Q2:

How well does GLUESTICK restore the safety of pruned VLAs?

Across both manipulation and navigation, GLUESTICK preserves the safety profile of the dense policy, remaining near parity despite aggressive pruning.
Tables
3
and
5
report changes in unsafe-episode rate relative to the dense baseline in manipulation and navigation, respectively.
GLUESTICK yields
89\%
and
100\%

These results indicate that GLUESTICK restores dominant weight-space directions that carry both task-relevant and safety-critical signal, thereby preserving the safety of VLA models.

Q5:

How compatible is GLUESTICK with other compression methods?

GLUESTICK composes well with other compression techniques. To demonstrate this, we combine
Int8
quantization with pruning and GLUESTICK. The results in Table
7
show that quantization has minimal impact on the success rate of both dense and sparse models; however, pruning degradation remains severe even after quantization. GLUESTICK recovers performance to 62.2% even on top of quantization, achieving our goal of making pruning a viable tool in the VLA compression toolbox.

We also show that GLUESTICK can be combined with VLA-specific compression techniques such as token pruning. Token pruning reduces the number of input tokens, while GLUESTICK operates on pruned weights, making the two complementary rather than competing. We ran experiments combining GLUESTICK with VLA-Pruner
[
55
]
on NVIDIA Thor and found that their speedups compound: GLUESTICK’s 1.48
\times
speedup rises to 1.64
\times
, with an additional 0.12 GB of VRAM savings (VLA-Pruner targets latency rather than memory reduction). VLA-Pruner costs a 7% decrease in success due to token removal. This further shows GLUESTICK’s composability.

Is GLUESTICK agnostic to the pruning algorithm and sparsity pattern?

To test whether GLUESTICK generalizes beyond the Wanda 2:4 setting used in our main results, we apply it to a Magnitude-pruned language backbone of OpenVLA under both 2:4 and 4:8 sparsity and evaluate on LIBERO Spatial (see Table

We evaluate GLUESTICK’s capability to recover the performance of a pruned VLM under various sparsity ratios in Table

Q9:

How does GLUESTICK perform on real-world robots?

We evaluate GLUESTICK on two real-world robotic platforms, a Unitree G1 humanoid for manipulation and a Boston Dynamics Spot for navigation. For manipulation, our setup (Figure
5
, left) deploys GR00T N1.7
[
72
]
on the G1, fine-tuned to pick up an apple and place it in a bin. We prune both the vision and language backbones of the VLA using Magnitude pruning at 50% 2:4 sparsity. We run 10 trials per model and apply GLUESTICK-1000 to all pruned layers, reporting results in Figure

Interestingly, in nearly every episode, the pruned model exhibited self-collisions, collisions with the table, containment breaches in which the end effector moved beyond the table’s edges, or a combination of these. We also observed the arms twisting into contorted, unnatural poses. In contrast, GLUESTICK fully restores safety and reaches 60% success.

For navigation, we record observations from a real Spot robot maneuvering through a real office environment (Figure

We prune NaVILA using Wanda at 50% 2:4 sparsity and replay the recorded observations through each model, measuring trajectory deviation, defined as the cumulative L1 difference in linear and angular velocity commands relative to the Full Dense model. GLUESTICK reduces this deviation by 50% relative to the pruned model (Figure

6
Conclusion

We presented the first systematic study of pruning VLA models and showed that pruning severely degrades both task success and safety. To address this, we introduced GLUESTICK, a training-free, pruning-agnostic, and practical post-pruning recovery method that reintroduces lost directions due to pruning, restoring performance and safety while retaining efficiency. Importantly, because our approach is independent of the pruning algorithm, it can be applied as a universal recovery step as new pruning strategies continue to emerge. We hope this work lays the foundation for compression techniques that make VLAs deployable on resource-constrained robots.

Limitations.

We designed GLUESTICK with a single hyperparameter so that applications can tune it based on their needs, but this introduces an inherent trade-off between success, memory, and latency. We also applied a uniform rank (
r
) across all layers. Future work includes reducing this trade-off and exploring adaptive rank selection across layers for further efficiency and recovery.

Appendix C
Experimental Setting

C.1
Environments

Manipulation.

We evaluate on LIBERO
[
51
]
, a benchmark designed to test embodied manipulation skills inspired by human activities (see Figure
1
, right). LIBERO tasks provide agents, embodied as a Franka Panda arm, with natural language instructions and visual observations of the environment. The benchmark comprises four task suites: LIBERO-Spatial (same objects, varied layouts), LIBERO-Object (same layout, varied objects), LIBERO-Goal (varied task goals), and LIBERO-Long (long-horizon tasks). Our manipulation evaluation covers all 10 tasks from each of the four LIBERO suites, with each task repeated 50 times, resulting in 2,000 total episodes.

Navigation.

We evaluate on the VLN-CE-Isaac benchmark
[
12
]
, which simulates legged robots (e.g., the Unitree Go2 quadruped and the H1 humanoid) traversing indoor environments to reach goal locations (see Figure
1
, left). Agents receive image observations and natural language instructions that can involve long-horizon, compositional reasoning (e.g., “walk toward the French doors and turn left, pass the kitchen area, and wait at the end of the hallway near the painting”). The robot executes velocity commands (e.g., move forward 0.75 m, turn right 15°), which are generated by a VLA model.
VLN-CE-Isaac builds on VLN-CE
[
45
]
, which itself is based on the Habitat simulator
[
83
]
. Habitat provides photorealistic 3D environments and physics-based simulation for embodied AI, moving beyond the original VLN task that used MatterPort3D panoramas represented as discrete navigation graphs
[
4
]
. Unlike the graph-based setting, Habitat supports continuous actions and realistic perception, allowing agents to navigate freely in 3D space. However, Habitat does not simulate robot embodiment. For instance, agents can move through unrealistic gaps (e.g., 10 cm between two sofas) that would be infeasible for legged robots. VLN-CE-Isaac inherits this Habitat-based formulation but extends it to physically simulated robots in Isaac Sim, enabling evaluation on platforms such as the Unitree Go2 quadruped and Unitree H1 humanoid. This provides a comprehensive benchmark of the full navigation pipeline, from high-level language understanding to low-level motor control. We evaluate on 100 randomly selected scenes from the 1,077 available in the VLN-CE-Isaac benchmark.

Hardware.

Unless stated otherwise, experiments used an NVIDIA L40S GPU with 48 GB of VRAM.

C.2
Evaluation Metrics

C.2.1
Success

Success captures whether the agent achieves the stated goal; we report binary per-episode success where an episode is successful if the agent completes the objective, and unsuccessful otherwise.

C.2.2
Safety

Safety captures whether the agent achieves its goal without causing harm to itself or the environment. Following prior robotics safety work
[
20
,
27
,
59
]
, we operationalize safety as the absence of harm caused to the robot or its surroundings. We monitor robot- and environment-centered risks.

Safety in Navigation.

For navigation tasks, we use collisions as the primary safety metric.
A collision is recorded whenever the agent outputs actions that cause the robot to make unintended contact with objects in the environment.
This measures the agent’s ability to move purposefully without endangering itself or its surroundings.

Safety in Manipulation.

For manipulation tasks, we introduce a set of five safety metrics that capture risks to both the robot and the environment:

•

Joint limit violations:

Occur when the agent outputs actions that drive joint angles close to or beyond their mechanical limits, which can cause long-term wear or physical damage to the robot’s actuators.

•

Arm collisions:

Measured when any part of the robot arm (excluding the end effector) makes unintended contact with the environment, potentially harming both the robot and external objects.

•

Object velocities:

We track the velocities of manipulated objects as a proxy for physical stability, penalizing outcomes where objects are flung, dropped, or otherwise move unsafely.

•

End-effector containment:

We enforce that the end effector remains within a bounded three-dimensional workspace region.
This ensures that the robot’s actions stay localized and prevents dangerous or uncontrolled motions outside of its designated operating zone.

•

Whole-body containment:

Similarly, we verify that the robot’s entire body remains within a global containment region.
Exiting this region can represent unsafe configurations or uncontrolled movement, posing risk to both the platform and its environment.

An episode is deemed
unsafe
whenever it violates one or more of the defined safety metrics.
We use the following thresholds: joint limit violations occur if a joint comes within 0.1% of its limits;
object motion is unsafe if velocity exceeds 1.0 m/s;
the robot body is unsafe if more than 1% extends outside the containment region;
and the end effector is unsafe if more than 5% extends beyond containment.
Containment regions are computed from the ground-truth dataset.

Models

OpenVLA.
OpenVLA

WorldVLA.
WorldVLA

[

[

[
50

[
52

C.4
Baselines

We consider three pruning strategies: (i) Full Sparse, where all linear layers except the model head are pruned with 50% 2:4 structured sparsity using Wanda; (ii) Sparse Language Backbone, where only the language backbone is pruned; and (iii) a Memory-Matched Sparse Language Backbone, which prunes a subset of backbone layers while maintaining 50% 2:4 sparsity in each pruned layer, to provide a fair comparison to GLUESTICK’s memory overhead. We use a 2:4 structured sparsity across the main experiments, as this level of pruning is necessary to realize improvements from hardware-efficient sparse kernels. We choose Wanda as our base pruning algorithm because it represents the state of the art and is computationally efficient, requiring minimal cost during pruning.

D.3

We measure edge inference latency on an NVIDIA Jetson Thor and an
NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX. Latency is reported as mean

end-to-end inference time corresponds to producing one full 7-DoF
action; OpenVLA does not perform action chunking, so a single
inference comprises both the prefill of the multimodal prompt and the
autoregressive decode of all 7 action tokens. For WorldVLA, we use
the default chunk size of 25 actions and report the wall-clock time
to produce the entire chunk (prefill plus autoregressive decode of
all 25 actions). All values are averaged over 100
timed iterations after 3 warm-up iterations.

We do not rely on
torch.sparse
; instead, we expose NVIDIA’s

E.2
Calibration Set Selection

The influence of calibration data on pruning outcomes in robotics remains largely underexplored. To investigate its impact, we studied how the calibration set choice affects Wanda’s baseline pruning performance before applying GLUESTICK. Specifically, we pruned the language backbone of OpenVLA with Wanda and evaluated on LIBERO Spatial. We provided Wanda with 15K states from the LIBERO fine-tuning corpus. We then constructed a smaller but more targeted calibration set consisting of 3.5K states within a 5% window around the first gripper-closing event (Figure
10
). Our experiments show that the more targeted calibration set improved the pruned language backbone’s success rate on LIBERO Spatial from 31.2% to 33.4%, a gain of about 2%. Interestingly, the smaller calibration set yielded a slightly higher success rate.

E.3
Singular Value Selection

We ask whether there is an optimal criterion for selecting singular values. All results reported in this paper use the top-
r
singular components ranked by magnitude. To test alternatives, we conducted an experiment where, for GLUESTICK-200 on a fully sparse language backbone, singular values were instead chosen uniformly at random. In this setting, the model recovered only
\sim
10% of the task success achieved by magnitude-based selection. This indicates that the choice of singular values is highly influential, with selecting the largest components by magnitude playing a central role in effective recovery. However, different singular values may capture complementary subspaces in weight space, and future work could explore whether alternative selection criteria better preserve metrics such as safety.

E.4
Activation-Aware Variant of GLUESTICK

We explored an activation-aware variant of GLUESTICK, in which input activations steer which components of the gap are recovered. Using Wanda’s calibration activation norms
c_{j}=\|X_{j}\|_{2}
and
D=\mathrm{diag}(c)
, we scale the gap matrix as
\widetilde{W}_{\mathrm{gap}}=W_{\mathrm{gap}}D
, take its top-
r
SVD, and undo the scaling via
V_{r}=D^{-1}\widetilde{V}_{r}
. This changes the recovered subspace: the cosine similarity between the two corrections is 0.86 at rank 500 and 0.78 at rank 200. However, the variant performs slightly worse on LIBERO Spatial, reaching 59.0% vs. 60.2% (rank 500) and 47.6% vs. 49.0% (rank 200). Its offline cost is nearly identical (2.375 vs. 2.366 s/layer, excluding Wanda’s already-computed calibration activations), and its inference cost is unchanged (parameters, VRAM, and latency). Other activation-aware selection criteria remain a direction for future work.

E.5

In the navigation domain, small deviations in the robot’s trajectory have minimal impact on final success. By contrast, manipulation tasks require fine-grained, centimeter-level control. This fundamental difference results in significantly different error tolerances between the two domains.

E.7
Weight-Only Decomposition

A natural question is why pruning is necessary at all if weight matrices could instead be compressed directly through low-rank decomposition. In other words, why not compress weights directly with SVD and avoid pruning altogether? In principle, one could replace each dense layer with an SVD approximation, storing only the top-
r
singular components. To test this, we conducted experiments where OpenVLA weights were approximated at various SVD ranks (without pruning). On LIBERO Spatial, this setting achieved a
0\%
success rate across nearly all experiments, indicating that low-rank approximations alone are insufficient to preserve the functionality of VLA models. This suggests that the pruned weight matrix itself retains valuable structure that cannot be captured by low-rank SVD alone. GLUESTICK leverages this by preserving the pruned weights (and structured sparsity benefits) while using SVD only to reintroduce lost directions, nudging the model back toward a more performant region of weight space.

Appendix F
Discussion

F.1
GLUESTICK on Long-Horizon Tasks

In our main results, GLUESTICK recovers less on LIBERO Long than on LIBERO Spatial, which might suggest that it struggles with long-horizon tasks. However, the dense models themselves already perform relatively poorly on LIBERO Long (
\sim
55%), and pruning degrades them even more severely. For example, pruned WorldVLA no longer produces valid action tokens (Table
14
). To test whether horizon is the limiting factor, we evaluate OpenVLA-OFT
[
42
]
, a stronger VLA, where GLUESTICK improves success on LIBERO Long from 61.2% to 84.4%. This suggests that the gains of GLUESTICK depend more on the strength of the underlying VLA than on task horizon. Furthermore, VLN-CE-Isaac is itself a long-horizon benchmark, averaging 1828.2 steps per episode compared to 391.1 for LIBERO Long (
4.7\times
longer), yet GLUESTICK fully recovers success there (Table
4
). Together, these results suggest that GLUESTICK is not inherently limited by task horizon.

F.2
GLUESTICK and LoRA Differences

While both GLUESTICK and LoRA introduce low-rank matrices, they address fundamentally different problems and operate in distinct ways. LoRA is designed for efficient fine-tuning, whereas GLUESTICK is designed for post-pruning recovery. Although both methods use low-rank matrices, these components are derived very differently. LoRA’s low-rank matrices must be
learned
through gradient-based training over a dataset, while GLUESTICK requires
no training at all
. Instead of learning parameters, GLUESTICK computes the exact gap between the dense and pruned weight matrices and constructs a low-rank approximation of this gap.
Although both methods add a term to the layer’s output at inference time, the source of the information they inject is entirely different. LoRA injects information learned from data during fine-tuning, whereas GLUESTICK injects information derived analytically from the dense–pruned gap. There is no overlap in how the low-rank components are obtained or what problems they are intended to solve.
