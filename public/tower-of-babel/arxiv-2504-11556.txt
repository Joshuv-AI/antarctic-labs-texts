Alec Metsch

Abstract
We consider the optimal transportation problem on a globally hyperbolic spacetime with a cost function c c , which corresponds to the optimal transportation problem on a complete Riemannian manifold where the cost function is given by the squared Riemannian distance. Adapting a proof of Bernard [ 2 ] and building upon methods of weak KAM theory, we will establish the existence of C l ​ o ​ c 1 , 1 C_{loc}^{1,1} optimal pairs for the dual optimal transport problem for probability measures along displacement interpolations.

The proof follows an approach inspired by weak KAM theory, specifically the work of Bernard

is a Tonelli Hamiltonian on a compact manifold

. Let

\displaystyle T_{t}u(x):=\inf\{u(y)+h_{t}(y,x)\mid y\in M_{T}\},\

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{t}u(x):=\sup\{u(y)-h_{t}(x,y)\mid y\in M_{T}\},

Figalli

From now on until the appendix, let
(M,g)
always denote a globally hyperbolic spacetime. We refer to the appendix for a brief review of the basic concepts of Lorentzian geometry. Let
h
be any complete Riemannian metric, and let
\tau
be a function satisfying (

\displaystyle L:TM\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},\ L(x,v):=\begin{cases}(d_{x}\tau(v)-|v|_{g})^{2},\ &\text{if }v\in{\cal C}_{x},\\
\\

Note that

is smooth only on
\operatorname{int}({\cal C})
, where
{\cal C}:=\{(x,v)\in TM\mid v\in{\cal C}_{x}\}
. To begin, we introduce some basic notations and results.

Notation 2.1
.

If not otherwise specified, a curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is always assumed to be absolutely continuous. When we say that
\gamma
is causal, we always mean future-directed causal, i.e.
\dot{\gamma}(t)\in{\cal C}_{\gamma(t)}
for a.e.
t\in[a,b]
. We use the notion timelike only for
C^{1}
-curves and always mean future-directed timelike, i.e.
\dot{\gamma}(t)\in\operatorname{int}({\cal C}_{\gamma(t)})
for all
t\in[a,b]
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
action
of a curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is defined by

\mathbb{L}(\gamma):=\int_{a}^{b}L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\,dt\in[0,\infty].

Observe that if
\mathbb{L}(\gamma)
is finite, then
\gamma
must be causal (recall that
0
is causal in our definition). The converse implication does not hold.

Definition 2.3
.

(a)

A curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is called a
minimizer
if, for any other curve
\tilde{\gamma}:[a,b]\to M
with the same start- and endpoint as
\gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}(\gamma)\leq\mathbb{L}(\tilde{\gamma}).

(b)

For
t>0
, we denote by
\Gamma^{t}
the set of all causal minimizers
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
.

Lemma 2.4
.

The second derivative along the fibers,
\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial v^{2}}(x,v)
, is positive definite at each point
(x,v)\in\operatorname{int}({\cal C})
.

Proof.

This is an easy calculation that was already carried out in
[
15
]
, Corollary 7.17.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Every timelike minimizer
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
satisfies, in local coordinates, the Euler-Lagrange equation

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))=\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t)),\ t\in[a,b].

Observe that this holds only for timelike curves since
L
is not differentiable on the entire set
{\cal C}
.
Since the second derivative along the fibers of
L
is positive definite on
\operatorname{int}({\cal C})
, it follows that there exists a smooth (local) Euler-Lagrange flow
\phi_{t}
on
\operatorname{int}({\cal C})
. It is well-known that the speed curve
(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))
of any timelike minimizer is part of an orbit of the flow, cf.
[
6
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

A causal curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is a minimizer if and only if
\gamma
is a maximizer for the Lorentzian lenght functional and
L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))
is constant.

In particular, every minimizer is smooth, and for any
(x,y)\in J^{+}
and
t>0
, there exists a minimizer connecting
x
with
y
in time
t
.

Sketch of proof.

For a detailed proof, we refer the reader to
[
15
]
, Proposition 3.6. Consider the Lagrangian

\displaystyle L^{\prime}:TM\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},\ L^{\prime}(x,v):=\begin{cases}d_{x}\tau(v)-|v|_{g},\ &\text{if }v\in{\cal C}_{x},\\
\\
+\infty,\ &\text{else.}\end{cases}

Using Hölder’s inequality, one can easily check that a causal curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is a minimizer for
L
if and only if it is a minimizer for
L^{\prime}
and
L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))
is constant a.e.

However, since

\mathbb{L}^{\prime}(\gamma)=\tau(\gamma(b))-\tau(\gamma(a))-\ell_{g}(\gamma)

(
\ell_{g}
denoting the Lorentzian length), it is obvious that
\gamma
is a minimizer for
L^{\prime}
if and only if
\gamma
maximizes the Lorentzian length functional. This proves the first part of the lemma.

Since any two points
(x,y)\in J^{+}
can be joined by a maximizing geodesic (see
[
17
]
, Chapter 14, Proposition 19), which can be reparametrized smoothly such that
L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))
is constant, it follows that there always exists a minimizer (for
L
) connecting
x
with
y
.

The fact that every minimizer is smooth simply follows from the well-known fact that any maximizing curve for the Lorentzian length functional is a pregeodesic (
[
16
]
, Theorem 2.12). Hence, every minimizer
\gamma
is a reparametrization of a geodesic such that
L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))
is constant. Thus,
\gamma
is smooth.
∎

as

(iv)

The flow, when restricted to the interior

is the Euler-Lagrange flow. This means that if

Proof.

For a detailed proof (except for (iii) and (iv)), we refer the reader to

Because of part (iv) of the above proposition we will denote both the Euler-Lagrange flow as well as the flow from the proposition with

Corollary 2.10
.

The set of causal minimizers
\Gamma^{t}
is a closed subset of
C([0,t],M)
. In particular,
\Gamma^{t}
is a complete and separable metric space.

Proof.

Let
(\gamma_{k})\subseteq\Gamma^{t}
be a sequence converging to
\gamma\in C([0,t],M)
.
We need to show that
\gamma\in\Gamma^{t}
.

Since
\gamma_{k}
are minimizers, we have
\gamma_{k}(s)=\pi\circ\phi_{s}(x_{k},v_{k})
for
x_{k}:=\gamma_{k}(0)
and
v_{k}:=\dot{\gamma}_{k}(0)
. As
\gamma_{k}\to\gamma
, we have
(x_{k},\gamma_{k}(t))\to(\gamma(0),\gamma(t))
and the latter lies in
J^{+}
, since
J^{+}
is closed. By continuity of
c_{t}
on
J^{+}
, we obtain

\displaystyle C:=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}c_{t}(x_{k},\gamma_{k}(t))<\infty.

Now, using (
1.1
), we see that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle|v_{k}|_{h}\leq d\tau(v_{k})\leq 2L(x_{k},v_{k})^{1/2}=2t^{-1/2}c_{t}(x_{k},\gamma_{k}(t))^{1/2}\leq 2t^{-1/2}C^{1/2},

where we have used that
L(\gamma_{k}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{k}(s))
is constant.
Thus, the sequence
(x_{k},v_{k})
is relatively compact in
TM
, and for a subsequence that we do not relabel,
(x_{k},v_{k})\to(x,v)\in{\cal C}
as
k\to\infty
. By continuity of the flow, we clearly have

\displaystyle\pi\circ\phi_{s}(x,v)=\gamma(s)\text{ for all }s\in[0,t]\text{ such that the first curve is defined.}

If
v=0
, then the first curve is defined for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
due to Proposition
2.8
(ii). If
v\neq 0
, then
s\mapsto\pi\circ\phi_{s}(x,v)
is inextendible. Thus,
\phi_{s}(x,v)
must be defined for all
s\in[0,t]
and
\gamma(s)=\pi\circ\phi_{s}(x,v)
for all these
s
. In particular,
\gamma
is smooth, and due to the continuity of
\phi
, it holds
\gamma_{k}\to\gamma
in
C^{1}([0,t],M)
. Then we conclude the proof as follows:
By the continuity of
c_{t}
on
J^{+}
,

\displaystyle c_{t}(x,\gamma(t))=\lim_{k\to\infty}c_{t}(x_{k},\gamma_{k}(t))=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{L}(\gamma_{k})=\mathbb{L}(\gamma).

Thus,
\gamma
minimizes
\mathbb{L}
and, hence, belongs to
\Gamma^{t}
.

In particular,
\Gamma^{t}
is complete and separable as
C([0,1],M)
is a complete separable space.
∎

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
(x_{k})
and
(y_{k})
be sequences with
y_{k}\in J^{+}(x_{k})
converging to
x
and
y
, respectively. Let
t_{k}>0
with
t_{k}\to t\in(0,\infty)
. For each

, suppose that
\gamma_{k}:[0,t_{k}]\to M
is a minimizer connecting
x_{k}
with
y_{k}
. Then, for a subsequence,
\gamma_{k}
can be extended to smoothly to
[0,t]
(if
t_{k}<t
) and
\gamma_{k}:[0,t]\to M
converges in the
C^{1}
-topology to a minimizing curve
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
connecting
x
with
y
.

Proof.

The same proof as above shows that, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in[0,t_{k}]
,

\displaystyle|\dot{\gamma}_{k}(s)|_{h}\leq d\tau(\dot{\gamma}_{k}(s))\leq 2L(\gamma_{k}(0),\dot{\gamma}_{k}(s))^{1/2}

\displaystyle=2t_{k}^{-1/2}c_{t_{k}}(\gamma_{k}(0),\gamma_{k}(t_{k}))^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}t_{k}^{-1/2}c_{t_{k}}(x_{k},y_{k})^{1/2}.

In particular, the sequence of curves
\gamma_{k}
is equi-continuous.

Now, let
\varepsilon>0
. Since the curves are equi-continuous and
(\gamma_{k}(0))=(x_{k})
is relatively compact, the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem gives that
(\gamma_{k})
is precompact in

. The preceding lemma and its proof tell us that, along a subsequence that we do not relabel,
\gamma_{k}
converges in the

-topology to a minimizing curve

.

Iterating this proof for the sequence
\varepsilon_{m}=1/m\to 0
and extracting a diagonal sequence, it follows that there exists a curve
\gamma:[0,t)\to M
such that
\gamma_{k}
converges to
\gamma
in the

-topology on any compact subinterval. In particular, thanks to Proposition

,

\displaystyle\gamma(s)=\pi\circ\phi_{s}(x,\dot{\gamma}(0))\text{ for }s\in[0,t)\text{ and }\gamma_{k}(s)=\pi\circ\phi_{s}(x_{k},\dot{\gamma}_{k}(0))\text{ for }s\in[0,t_{k}].

However, since the sequence
\gamma_{k}
is equi-continuous, it follows that
\gamma
is uniformly continuous, so that
\gamma
can be extended to
[0,t]
. Thus, by Proposition
2.8
(iii), the orbit
\phi_{s}(x,\dot{\gamma}(0))
is defined up some

is open, also
\phi_{s}(x_{k},\dot{\gamma}_{k}(0))

can indeed be extended smoothly on

converges to

-topology. Using the continuity of

as a map from

and the fact that

∎

Exploiting the fact that the Lorentzian distance is locally semiconvex on
I^{+}
(
[
14
]
, Proposition 3.4) and, as a consequence, the fact that
c=c_{1}
is locally semiconvex on
I^{+}
, the following theorem extends a well-known result from the theory of Tonelli Lagrangians (see e.g.
[
7
]
, Proposition 4.19) to our setting. We keep the proof and the notation similar. Do not confuse the following notation
{\cal C}
with the the future cone.
For definitions and further properties on semiconcavity, see Subsection
6.1
in the appendix.

Theorem 2.12
.

The mapping

{\cal C}:(0,\infty)\times I^{+},\ (t,x,y)\mapsto c_{t}(x,y),

is locally semiconcave. If
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
is a minimizing curve with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(t)=y
, then

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}c_{t}(x,y),-\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}(0)),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y,\dot{\gamma}(t)\right)\in\partial^{+}{\cal C}(t,x,y).

(2.1)

Moreover, the set of all super-differentials is given by the closure of the convex hull of covectors of the form (
2.1
).
In particular,
{\cal C}
is differentiable at some point
(t,x,y)
if and only if there is a unique minimizing curve connecting
x
with
y
in time
t
.

Proof.

We have

c_{t}(x,y)=c(x,y)/t
for
(x,y)\in I^{+}
and
t>0
. Moreover, by Lemma
2.6
, minimizing curves for
c_{t}
are precisely affine reparametrisations of the form
[0,t]\ni s\mapsto\gamma(s/t)
of minimizing curves
\gamma
for
c
. Hence, it suffices to check that
c
is locally semiconcave on
I^{+}
, that

\displaystyle\partial^{+}{c}(x,y)=\overline{\operatorname{conv}\bigg\{\left(-\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}(0)),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y,\dot{\gamma}(1))\right)\bigg\}},

(2.2)

(where we take all minimizing curves
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
connecting
x
with
y
),
and that
c
is differentiable at
(x,y)\in I^{+}
if and only if there exists a unique minimizer connecting
x
with
y
(in time
1
).

Using the explicit formulation for
c
, the local semiconvexity follows easily from the well-known fact that the Lorentzian distance function
d
is locally semiconvex on
I^{+}
(see
[
14
]
, Proposition 3.4). An elementary proof, based on the methods in
[
8
]
, can be found in
[
15
]
, Proposition 7.13. Moreover, as a byproduct of the proof in
[
15
]
, one has

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}(0)),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y,\dot{\gamma}(1))\right)\in\partial^{+}c(x,y)

(2.3)

for all minimizing curves
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
connecting
x
with
y
. Since
\partial^{+}c(x,y)
is easily seen to be closed and convex, the inclusion
\supseteq
in (
2.2
) follows.
Next, recall the well-known fact that the set of super-differentials of a locally semiconcave function is the closure of the convex hull of reaching gradients (see
[
4
]
, Appendix A). This means that
\partial^{+}c(x,y)
is the closure of the convex hull of all covectors
(p,q)\in T_{x}^{*}M\times T_{y}^{*}M
for which there exists a sequence of differentiability points
(x_{k},y_{k})\in I^{+}
for
c
that converge to
(x,y)\in I^{+}
and such that

(p,q)=\lim_{k\to\infty}d_{(x_{k},y_{k})}c\text{ in the topology of the cotangent bundle.}

Let
\gamma_{k}:[0,1]\to M
be minimizing (necessarily timelike) curves connecting
x_{k}
with
y_{k}
. Since
c
is differentiable at
(x_{k},y_{k})
, there must be a unique super-differential, so that (
2.3
) gives

d_{(x_{k},y_{k})}c=\left(-\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x_{k},\dot{\gamma}_{k}(0)),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y_{k},\dot{\gamma}_{k}(1))\right).

Since the sequences
(x_{k})
and
(y_{k})
are relatively compact, it follows from the above corollary that, up to some subsequence,
\gamma_{k}
converges in the
C^{1}
-topology to some minimizing curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
. In particular,
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(1)=y
and

\dot{\gamma}(0)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\dot{\gamma}_{k}(0),\ \dot{\gamma}(1)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\dot{\gamma}_{k}(1).

Since
(x,y)\in I^{+}
,
\gamma
is necessarily timelike. Therefore,

(p,q)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(-\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x_{k},\dot{\gamma}_{k}(0)),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y_{k},\dot{\gamma}_{k}(1))\right)=\left(-\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}(0)),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y,\dot{\gamma}(1))\right).

This proves (
2.2
). Since differentiability is equivalent to unique super-differentiability, the last part of the theorem follows from (
2.2
) and the fact that, if
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are two minimizing curves connecting
x
to
y
and
{\cal L}(x,\dot{\gamma}_{1}(0))={\cal L}(x,\dot{\gamma}_{2}(0))
, then
\dot{\gamma}_{1}(0)=\dot{\gamma}_{2}(0)
since
{\cal L}
is a diffeomorphism. Thus, since both minimizers
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
have the same position and the same speed at
t=0
, we must have
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
.
∎

.

Since minimizing curves are precesily certain reparametrizations of maximizing geodesics, it follows that

Definition 2.14
.

The

\displaystyle T_{x}M\to T_{x}^{*}M,\ v\mapsto g(v,\cdot).

We also call

Remark 2.15
.

It is not hard to check that
\operatorname{int}({\cal C}_{x}^{*})
is the image of
\operatorname{int}({\cal C}_{x})
under the the canonical isomorphism given above. Here, we take the interior w.r.t to the subspace topology in
T^{*}_{x}M
resp.
T_{x}M
.

It is easy to see that the dual future cone
{\cal C}_{x}^{*}
(resp.
\operatorname{int}({\cal C}_{x}^{*})
) is characterized by the fact that
pv\leq 0
(resp.
<0
) for all

.

Definition 2.16
.

The
Legendre transform
of
L
is defined as the map

\displaystyle{\cal L}:\operatorname{int}({\cal C})\to T^{*}M,\ (x,v)\mapsto\left(x,\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,v)\right).

Lemma 2.17
.

The Legendre transform is a diffeomorphism as a map from
\operatorname{int}({\cal C})
to
T^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}^{*}
.

Proof.

Since the second derivative along the fibers of
L
is positive definite, the Legendre transform is injective and a diffeomorphism onto its image, thanks to the inverse function theorem. Thus, the only remaining task is to verify that the image of
{\cal L}
is precisely the complement of the closed dual future cone. If
v\in\operatorname{int}({\cal C}_{x})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,v)(v)=2L(x,v)>0.

By the characterisation of
{\cal C}_{x}
given in Remark
2.15
, it follows that
{\cal L}(x,v)\in T^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}^{*}
. Conversely, let
(x,p)\in T^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}^{*}
. Then there is
v_{0}\in{\cal C}_{x}
with
pv_{0}>0
. Then we have

\displaystyle p(\lambda v_{0})-L(x,\lambda v_{0})=\lambda pv_{0}-\lambda^{2}L(x,v_{0})>0\text{ for small }\lambda>0.

(2.4)

Thus,

\displaystyle H(x,p):=\sup_{v\in{\cal C}_{x}}(pv-L(x,v))>0.

Moreover, using the notation of Definition
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle pv-L(x,v)\leq|p|_{h}|v|_{h}-(|p|_{h}+1)|v|_{h}+C(|p|_{h}+1)\xrightarrow{|v|_{h}\to\infty}-\infty.

Therefore the continuity of the map
{\cal C}_{x}\to\mathbb{R},\ v\mapsto pv-L(x,v),
shows that there is
v_{1}\in{\cal C}_{x}
such that

\displaystyle 0<H(x,p)=pv_{1}-L(x,v_{1})\leq pv_{1}

In particular we must have
pv_{1}>0
and
v_{1}\neq 0
. We show that
v_{1}\in\operatorname{int}({\cal C}_{x})
. Indeed, if this was not the case then
v_{1}\in\partial{\cal C}_{x}\backslash\{0\}
. Then, working in an orthonormal basis of
T_{x}M
, it is easy to check that there is
v_{2}
close to
v_{1}
with

\displaystyle pv_{2}-L(x,v_{2})>pv_{1}-L(x,v_{1}).

So,
v_{1}\in\operatorname{int}({\cal C}_{x})
and since
L(x,\cdot)
is smooth on the open future cone, we obtain by differentiation

\displaystyle 0=p-\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,v_{1})\Rightarrow p={\cal L}(x,v_{1}).

This ends the proof of the lemma.
∎

Definition 2.18
.

The
Hamiltonian
is the map

H:T^{*}M\to\mathbb{R},\ H(x,p):=\sup_{v\in T_{x}M}pv-L(x,v).

Remark 2.19
.

The proof of the above lemma shows that, for
(x,v)\in\operatorname{int}({\cal C})
, we have

H(x,{\cal L}(x,v))={\cal L}(x,v)(v)-L(x,v).

Since the Legendre transform
{\cal L}:\operatorname{int}({\cal C})\to T^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}^{*}
is a diffeomorphism, it follows that
H
is smooth on
T^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}^{*}
. Moreover, it is well-known that the Euler-Lagrange flow
\phi_{t}
on
\operatorname{int}({\cal C})
is conjugated, via
{\cal L}
, to the Hamiltonian flow
\psi^{H}_{t}
on
T^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}^{*}
. Here,
T^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}^{*}
is equipped with its natural symplectic structure.

3
Lax-Oleinik semigroup

Definition 3.1
.

The
forward Lax-Oleinik semigroup
is the family of maps
(T_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, defined on the space of functions
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
by

\displaystyle T_{t}u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\ T_{t}u(x):=\inf\{u(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid y\in M\},

where we use the convention
-\infty+\infty:=+\infty
.
The
backward Lax-Oleinik semigroup
is the family of maps
(\hat{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, defined on the space of functions
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
by

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{t}u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\ \hat{T}_{t}u(x):=\sup\{u(y)-c_{t}(x,y)\mid y\in M\},

where we use the convention
\infty-\infty:=-\infty
.
If
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is any function, we also write

\displaystyle Tu:

\displaystyle\ [0,\infty)\times M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\ (t,x)\mapsto T_{t}u(x),

and we use the analogous notation
\hat{T}u
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that, according to our convention,

\displaystyle T_{t}u(x)=\inf\{u(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid y\in J^{-}(x)\}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{t}u(x)=\sup\{u(y)-c_{t}(x,y)\mid y\in J^{+}(x)\}

This eliminates the need to specify a convention for the sum
\mp\infty\pm\infty
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The Lax-Oleinik semigroups indeed form a semigroup, meaning that for
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
t,s\geq 0
, we have the identities
T_{t+s}u=T_{t}(T_{s}u)
and
\hat{T}_{t+s}u=\hat{T}_{t}(\hat{T}_{s}u)
.

Proof.

This follows as in
[
6
]
, where the result is proven for Tonelli Lagrangians on a compact manifold. The only property required in the proof is that, for
x,y\in M
and
t,s\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle c_{t+s}(y,x)=\inf_{z\in M}c_{t}(y,z)+c_{s}(z,x).

The inequality “
\leq
” follows directly from the definition of the minimal action. The reverse inequality holds because, for any pair
(y,x)\in M\times M
, we can find a minimizer
\gamma:[0,t+s]\to M
that connects
y
to
x
. We then set
z:=\gamma(t)
.
∎

The following lemma is well-known but we provide a proof since we have not encountered this exact formulation in a work where the minimal action can attain the value
+\infty
. A very similar situation is presented in
[
1
]
, Definition 6.1.2.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
t>0
, we have
\hat{T}_{t}(T_{t}u)\leq u
and also
T_{t}(\hat{T}_{t}u)\geq u
.

Proof.

Let
x\in M
. We have

\hat{T}_{t}(T_{t}u)(x)=\sup_{y\in J^{+}(x)}\inf_{z\in J^{-}(y)}u(z)+c_{t}(z,y)-c_{t}(x,y)

Choosing
z=x
, we obtain
\hat{T}_{t}(T_{t}u)(x)\leq u(x)
. The argument for the second inequality is similar.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

For Tonelli Lagranians on a compact manifold
N
, it is well known that
Tu
is locally semiconcave for any continuous function
u
, as stated in
[
6
]
. This result can be extended to the case of Lipschitz functions on non-compact manifolds, see the proof of Theorem 3.3 (4) in
[
10
]
. We will extend this result (under additional assumptions) to the Lorentzian case in the next section. Even in the case for Tonelli Lagrangians on a non-compact manifold and arbitrary functions
u:N\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, it was shown in
[
7
]
, Theorem 6.2(iii), that if
Tu
is finite at some point
(T,X)\in(0,\infty)\times N
, then
Tu
is locally semiconcave on
(0,T)\times N
.

However, such a result cannot be generalized to the Lorentzian case: Consider a function
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and two points
x_{0},y_{0}\in M
such that
u(x_{0})
and
T_{1}u(y_{0})
are finite. The only information we have about
Tu
is on the set
(0,1)\times(J^{+}(x_{0})\cap J^{-}(y_{0})
. Nonetheless, one might still hope that
u
is at least locally semiconcave on the interior of this set.

Our main result in this section asserts that the Lax-Oleinik evolution
Tu
is locally semiconcave on an open neighbourhood of the relative interior of the graph of calibrated curves. For the precise formulation, see below. Let us note that our proofs utilize several ideas from
[
7
]
, Theorem 6.2, and we have adapted both the notation and the formulation of the results.

Theorem 3.6
.

(i)

Let
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
be any function, and let
y_{0}\in I^{+}(x_{0})
and
t_{0}>0
. Suppose that
u(x_{0})
and
T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})
are finite. Additionally, assume that

T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})=u(x_{0})+c_{t_{0}}(x_{0},y_{0})
. Let
\gamma:[0,t_{0}]\to M
be a minimizer connecting
x_{0}
with
y_{0}
and let
t_{1}\in(0,t_{0})
. Then the Lax-Oleinik semigroup evolution

is locally semiconcave on a neighborhood of
(t_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))
.

(ii)

Let
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
be any function, and let
y_{0}\in I^{-}(x_{0})
and
t_{0}>0
. Suppose that
u(x_{0})
and
\hat{T}_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})
are finite. Additonally, assume that

\hat{T}_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})=u(x_{0})-c_{t_{0}}(y_{0},x_{0})
. Let
\gamma:[0,t_{0}]\to M

However, it is important to note that each of these lemmas has a corresponding version for the backward Lax-Oleinik semigroup.

(b)

Lemma 3.8

(
[
7
]
, Theorem 6.2)
.

Let
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
be any function, and let
x_{0},y_{0}\in M
and
t_{0}>0
. Suppose that
u(x_{0})
and
T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})
are finite. Then the function
Tu
is locally bounded on the set
(0,t_{0})\times(J^{+}(x_{0})\cap J^{-}(y_{0})).

Proof.

Let
K\subseteq(0,t_{0})\times(J^{+}(x_{0})\cap J^{-}(y_{0}))
be a compact set. The definition of the Lax-Oleinik semigroup yields

\displaystyle T_{t}u(x)\leq u(x_{0})+c_{t}(x_{0},x),\ (t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times M.

By continuity of the mapping
(0,\infty)\times J^{+},\ (t,x,y)\mapsto c_{t}(x,y)
, and compactness of
K
it follows that
Tu
is bounded above on
K
by

\displaystyle u(x_{0})+\sup_{(t,x)\in K}c_{t}(x_{0},x).

(3.1)

On the other hand, by the same argument, we have for
(t,x)\in K

\displaystyle T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})\leq T_{t}u(x)+c_{1-t}(x,y_{0})\leq T_{t}u(x)+\sup_{(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})\in K}c_{1-\tilde{t}}(\tilde{x},y_{0}).

(3.2)

Combining the inequalities (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we arrive at the following bounds for
T_{t}u(x)
,
(t,x)\in K
:

\displaystyle T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})-\sup_{(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})\in K}c_{1-\tilde{t}}(\tilde{x},y_{0})\leq T_{t}u(x)\leq u(x_{0})+\sup_{(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})\in K}c_{\tilde{t}}(x_{0},\tilde{x})

Since, by assumption,
u(x_{0})
and
T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})
are finite, the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 3.9

(
[
7
]
, Proposition 7.5)
.

Let
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

y_{0}\in J^{+}(x_{0})
and
t_{0}>0

u(x_{0})
and

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
be any function, and let
y_{0}\in I^{+}(x_{0})
and
t_{0}>0
. Suppose that
u(x_{0})
and
T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})
are finite. Additionally, assume that

T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})=u(x_{0})+c_{t_{0}}(x_{0},y_{0})
. Let
\gamma:[0,t_{0}]\to M
be a minimizer connecting
x_{0}
with
y_{0}
.
Then the function
Tu
is differentiable at
(t_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))
for any
t_{1}\in(0,t_{0})
.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.9
we know
T_{t_{1}}u(\gamma(t_{1}))=u(x_{0})+c_{t_{1}}(x_{0},\gamma(t_{1}))
. Thus, by part (i) of the above lemma, the Lax-Oleinik evolution
Tu
is super-differentiable at
(t_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))
. Moreover, by Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.4
, we have for all
t\in(0,t_{0})
and all
x\in M
that

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{t_{0}-t}T_{t_{0}}u(x)=\hat{T}_{t_{0}-t}T_{t_{0}-t}T_{t}u(x)\leq T_{t}u(x),

and, utilizing the fact that
\gamma
is a minimizer, it is straightforward to verify that equality holds at
(t_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))
. Moreover, one checks that

\hat{T}_{t_{0}-t_{1}}T_{t_{0}}u(\gamma(t_{1}))=T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})-c_{t_{0}-t_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1}),\gamma(t_{0})).

Thus, by (ii) of the above lemma, the mapping

(t,x)\mapsto(t_{0}-t,x)\mapsto\hat{T}_{t_{0}-t}T_{t_{0}}u(x)

is sub-differentiable at

as the composition of a sub-differentiable function with a smooth function. These facts easily imply that also

Remark 3.12
.

The results presented so far are are well-known in the case for Tonelli-Lagrangians, and the proofs are essentially the same. Of course, there is no counterpart for the assumption
y_{0}\in I^{+}(x_{0})
. The next Lemma is borrowed from
[
7
]
, Theorem 6.2, where the result is stated for Tonelli-Lagrangians. The proof is essentially the same, but we provide it here again for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
u:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
be any function, and let
y_{0}\in I^{+}(x_{0})
and
t_{0}>0
. Suppose that
u(x_{0})
and
T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})
are finite. Suppose that
\delta>0
and
(t_{1},x_{1})\in\mathbb{R}\times M
are such that

\displaystyle[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}+\delta]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(x_{1})\subseteq(0,t_{0})\times(J^{+}(x_{0})\cap J^{-}(y_{0})).

Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
with
2\varepsilon<\delta
, such that, for all
(t,x)\in[t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t}u(x)=\inf\{T_{s}u(y)+c_{t-s}(y,x)\mid(s,y)\in[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(x_{1})\}.

Notation 3.14
.

Throughout this paper, we denote by
B_{r}(x)
the open ball of radius
r
centered at
x
, either w.r.t. the complete metric
h
on
M
(when
x\in M
), or w.r.t. the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(when
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
).

Proof.

Denote
K:=[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}+\delta]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(x_{1})
and choose
\delta/2>\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{(\delta-\varepsilon)^{2}}{12\varepsilon}>2\sup_{(t,x)\in K}|T_{t}u(x)|+1.

(3.6)

The finiteness of the supremum on the right-hand side follows from Lemma
3.8
.
Let
(t,x)\in[t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon]\cap\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})
be given, and let
0<\eta<1
be arbitrary. We can find
y\in M
such that

\displaystyle u(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\leq T_{t}u(x)+\eta.

Now, let
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
be a minimizer with
\gamma(0)=y
and
\gamma(t)=x
.
Since
\gamma
is a minimizer, we have for
s\in[0,t]

\displaystyle c_{t}(\gamma(0),x)=c_{s}(\gamma(0),\gamma(s))+c_{t-s}(\gamma(s),x).

Substituting this into the inequality above gives

\displaystyle u(y)+c_{s}(y,\gamma(s))+c_{t-s}(\gamma(s),x)\leq T_{t}u(x)+\eta.

Using the fact that, by definition of
T_{s}u(\gamma(s))
, we have
T_{s}u(\gamma(s))\leq u(y)+c_{s}(y,\gamma(s))
, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{s}u(\gamma(s))+c_{t-s}(\gamma(s),x)\leq T_{t}u(x)+\eta.

(3.7)

Now, we pick
s
such that
(s,\gamma(s))\in\partial([t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(x_{1}))
. This is possible since
(0,\gamma(0))\notin[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(x_{1})
(because
t_{1}-\delta>0
), and
(t,\gamma(t))=(t,x)\in[t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})
). It follows that either
s=t_{1}-\delta
or
d_{h}(\gamma(s),x_{1})=\delta
. We want to show that in both cases
s\leq t_{1}-2\varepsilon
.

In the first case, this holds since
\varepsilon<\delta/2
.

Now, let us turn to the second case, where
d_{h}(\gamma(s),x_{1})=\delta
. The triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle d_{h}(\gamma(s),x)\geq d_{h}(\gamma(s),x_{1})-d_{h}(x_{1},x)\geq\delta-\varepsilon.

Due to (
1.1
), the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle c_{t-s}(\gamma(s),x)\geq\frac{d_{h}(\gamma(s),x)^{2}}{4(t-s)}\geq\frac{(\delta-\varepsilon)^{2}}{4(t-s)}.

Inserting this into (
3.7
) gives

\displaystyle T_{s}u(\gamma(s))+\frac{(\delta-\varepsilon)^{2}}{4(t-s)}\leq T_{t}u(x)+\eta.

Comparing this with (
3.6
) shows that
t-s\geq 3\varepsilon
, so that
t_{1}-s\geq 2\varepsilon
. Thus, in both cases, we have
s\leq t_{1}-2\varepsilon
.

In particular, we have proven that

\displaystyle\inf\{T_{s}u(y)+c_{t-s}(y,x)\mid(s,y)\in[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(x_{1})\}\leq T_{t}u(x)+\eta.

Since
\eta
was arbitrary, the proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

The preceding lemma shows that, in the case of a Tonelli-Lagrangian system, using the same notation as above (with
h_{t}
replacing
c_{t}
for the minimal action), the Lax-Oleinik evolution of
u
is locally semiconcave on the set
(t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon)\times B_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})
. The reason is that the family of functions
(t,x)\mapsto h_{t-s}(x,y)
, as
(s,y)
varies over the compact set
[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(x_{1})
, is uniformly locally semiconcave on this set in the sense of
[
8
]
, Definition A15. Consequently, the infimum (which, by the lemma, coincides with
T_{t}u(x)
) is indeed locally semiconcave (
[
8
]
, Proposition A16).

In the Lorentzian case, however, the difficulty arises from the fact that the family of mappings
(t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon)\times B_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\ (t,x)\mapsto c_{t-s}(x,y)
, as
(s,y)
varies over the compact set

, is not uniformly locally semiconcave. In fact, these functions are not even continuous or everywhere finite. We only know that

is locally semiconcave on

.

Thus, in order to obtain uniform semiconcavity results, we have to show that, locally for

, the infimum in the definition of

can be taken over all

that remain uniformly bounded away from

can be chosen uniformly bounded away from

. This is the content of the following lemma.

(t,x)\in[t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t_{1}))
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t}u(x)=\inf\{

\displaystyle T_{s}u(y)+c_{t-s}(y,x)\mid

\displaystyle(s,y)\in[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(\gamma(t_{1})),\ d(y,\gamma(t_{1}))\geq\varepsilon\}.

(3.13)

Proof.

By the foregoing lemma, we know that there exists
\varepsilon_{1}<\delta/2
such that,
for all
(t,x)\in[t_{1}-\varepsilon_{1},t_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1}))
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t}u(x)=\inf\{

\displaystyle T_{s}u(y)+c_{t-s}(y,x)\mid

\displaystyle(s,y)\in[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon_{1}]\times(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(\gamma(t_{1}))\backslash B_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1})))\}.

Thus, it suffices to prove that there exists
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
such that, for
(t,x)\in[t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t_{1}))
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t}u(x)<

\displaystyle\inf\{T_{s}u(y)+c_{t-s}(y,x)\mid

\displaystyle(s,y)\in[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon_{1}]\times(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(\gamma(t_{1}))\backslash B_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1}))),\ d(y,\gamma(t_{1}))<\varepsilon\}.

We argue by contradiction and assume that we find a sequence
(t_{k},x_{k})\to(t_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))
and, for each
k
, a pair

\displaystyle(s_{k},y_{k})\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}-2\varepsilon_{1}]\times(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(\gamma(t_{1}))\backslash B_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1})))\text{ such that }d(y_{k},x_{k})\to 0

and such that

\displaystyle T_{s_{k}}u(y_{k})+c_{t_{k}-s_{k}}(y_{k},x_{k})-\frac{1}{k}\leq T_{t_{k}}u(x_{k}).

(3.14)

Of course we must have
y_{k}\in J^{-}(x_{k})
for large
k
.
Since
Tu
is continuous at
(t_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))
, the latter converges to
T_{t_{1}}u(\gamma(t_{1}))
as
k\to\infty
.
Since
\gamma
is
Tu
-calibrated, we have

\displaystyle T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})=T_{t_{1}}u(\gamma(t_{1}))+c_{t_{0}-t_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1}),y_{0}).

(3.15)

On the other hand, by the semigroup property of
Tu
it also holds that

\displaystyle T_{t_{0}}u(y_{0})\leq T_{s_{k}}u(y_{k})+c_{t_{0}-s_{k}}(y_{k},y_{0}).

(3.16)

Now, from (
3.14
),(
3.15
) and (
3.16
) we obtain

\displaystyle T_{s_{k}}u(y_{k})+c_{t_{k}-s_{k}}(y_{k},x_{k})-\frac{1}{k}\leq

\displaystyle T_{t_{k}}u(x_{k})-T_{t_{1}}u(\gamma(t_{1})+T_{t_{1}}u(\gamma(t_{1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle T_{t_{k}}u(x_{k})-T_{t_{1}}u(\gamma(t_{1})+T_{s_{k}}u(y_{k})

\displaystyle+c_{t_{0}-s_{k}}(y_{k},y_{0})-c_{t_{0}-t_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1}),y_{0}).

Rearranging this inequality leads to

\displaystyle c_{t_{k}-s_{k}}(y_{k},x_{k})+c_{t_{0}-t_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1}),y_{0})\leq T_{t_{k}}u(x_{k})-T_{t_{1}}u(\gamma(t_{1})+c_{t_{0}-s_{k}}(y_{k},y_{0}).

Since the metric
h
is complete and due to the construction of our sequence,
(s_{k},y_{k})
is precompact. Thus, we can assume that, along a subsequence that we do not relabel,
(s_{k},y_{k})\to(s_{1},y_{1})\in[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon_{1}]\times(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(\gamma(t_{1}))\backslash B_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1})))
.
Since
J^{+}
is closed, it follows
y_{1}\in J^{-}(\gamma(t_{1}))
, and by the continuity of the Lorentzian distance,
d(y_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))=0
. Taking limits in the above inequality and regarding the continuity of
Tu
at
(t_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))
, we conclude

\displaystyle c_{t_{1}-s_{1}}(y_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))+c_{t_{0}-t_{1}}(\gamma(t_{1}),y_{0})\leq c_{t_{0}-s_{1}}(y_{1},y_{0}).

Since the reverse inequality always holds, this must be an equality. This implies that the curve obtained by concatenating a minimizer from
y_{1}
to
\gamma(t_{1})
with the curve
\gamma_{|[t_{1},t_{0}]}
is still a minimizer. However, the first curve is not timelike, since
d(y_{1},\gamma(t_{1}))=0
, and non-trvial (
y_{1}\neq\gamma(t_{1})
(this is where we need the last lemma)). The second curve, on the other hand, is timelike because
(\gamma(t_{1}),y_{0})\in I^{+}
. This contradicts Lemma
2.6
, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 3.18
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem 3.6.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Let
\delta>0
be such that

\displaystyle[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}+\delta]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(\gamma(t_{1}))\subseteq(0,t_{0})\times(J^{+}(x_{0})\cap J^{-}(y_{0})).

We can then apply the preceding lemma. Thus, there exists
\varepsilon<\delta/2
such that, for all
(t,x)\in[t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\varepsilon}(\gamma(t_{1}))
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t}u(x)=\inf\{

\displaystyle T_{s}u(y)+c_{t-s}(y,x)\mid

\displaystyle(s,y)\in[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon]\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\delta}(\gamma(t_{1})),\ d(y,\gamma(t_{1}))\geq\varepsilon\}.

Due to the continuity of the Lorenzian distance, it readily follows that there exists
\varepsilon>r>0
and a compact set
K
such that
B_{r}(\gamma(t_{1}))\times K\subseteq I^{+}
and such that, for all
(t,x)\in[t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon]\times B_{r}(\gamma(t_{1}))
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t}u(x)=\inf\{T_{s}u(y)+c_{t-s}(y,x)\mid s\in[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon],\ y\in K\}.

(3.17)

Since
{\cal C}
is locally semiconcave on
(0,\infty)\times I^{+}
it follows that the mapping

\{(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid t>s\}\times I^{+}\to\mathbb{R},\ (t,s,x,y)\mapsto c_{t-s}(x,y),

is locally semiconcave. Thus, the family of functions

\displaystyle(t_{1}-\varepsilon,t_{1}+\varepsilon)\times B_{r}(\gamma(t_{1}))\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle(t,x)\mapsto T_{s}u(y)+c_{t-s}(y,x),\ (s,y)\in[t_{1}-\delta,t_{1}-2\varepsilon]\times K,

is uniformly locally semiconcave in the sense of
[
8
]
, Definition A15. Thus, the infimum (if finite) is also locally semiconcave (
[
8
]
, Corollary A14). However, the infimum is, by (
3.17
), precisely

T_{t}u(x)
. This completes the proof.
∎

4
Lax-Oleinik evolution for
C^{1}
Lipschitz functions

In this section, we aim to study the Lax-Oleinik evolution for
C^{1}
Lipschitz functions. As already mentioned in Remark
3.5
, it is known that for a Tonelli-Lagrangian on a possibly non-compact manifold
N
, the Lax-Oleinik evolution of a Lipschitz function
f
is locally semiconcave. This follows from the fact that, locally in
(t,x)
, the infimum in the definition of
T_{t}f(x)
can be taken over a compact set. Therefore, since the mapping
(0,\infty)\times N^{2}\to\mathbb{R},\ (t,y,x)\mapsto h_{t}(y,x)
is locally semiconcave, also the family of mappings

(0,\infty)\times N,\ (t,x)\mapsto f(y)+h_{t}(y,x),\ y\in K,

is uniformly locally semiconcave, implying that their infimum is locally semiconcave (
[
8
]
, Proposition A16). In the Lorentzian case, the same issue as explained in Remark
3.15
arises, since the functions
c_{t}(x,y)
are not even finite or continuous. Again we have to show that, locally in
(t,x)
, the
y
in the infimum in the definition of
T_{t}f(x)
can be taken uniformly bounded away from
\partial J^{-}(x)
. Under additional assumptions, we will prove that the Lax-Oleinik evolution of a
C^{1}
Lipschitz function is locally semiconcave. Let us now state the precise formulation of our main result:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be
C^{1}
and Lipschitz. Let
x_{0}\in M
, and suppose that there exists
r>0
such that for every
x\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{r}(x_{0})
,
d_{x}f\in T_{x}^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}_{x}^{*}
.

Then, the function
Tf
is locally semiconcave on
(0,\infty)\times B_{r}(x_{0})
.
Furthermore, if
x\in B_{r}(x_{0})
and
t>0
, there is
y\in M
such that
T_{t}f(x)=f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)
, and we necessarily have
y\in I^{-}(x)
. If
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
is a minimizer with
\gamma(0)=y
and
\gamma(t)=x
then
(\partial_{t}c_{t}(y,x),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}(t)))
is a super-differential for
Tf
at
(t,x)
.
Finally,
Tf
is differentiable at
(t,x)
if and only if the there is a unique minimizing curve
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
with
\gamma(t)=x
such that
T_{t}f(x)=f(\gamma(0))+c_{t}(\gamma(0),x)
.

Recall (Notation
3.14
) that
B_{r}(x_{0})
denotes the open ball w.r.t. the complete metric
h
. As in Section 2, we need some preliminary steps for the proof. We first give the following definition.

Definition 4.2
.

For
K>0
let
C(K)>0
such that

\displaystyle L(x,v)\geq K|v|_{h}-C(K)\text{ for all }(x,v)\in{\cal C}.

Such a constant exists thanks to the uniform superlinearity of
L
on
{\cal C}
(due to (
1.1
)).

The proof of the next lemma is similar to the proof of Proposition 3.3 in
[
10
]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be an
L
-Lipschitz function. Then
Tf
is everywhere finite. Given
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times M
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)=\inf\{f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid d_{h}(y,x)\leq C(L+1)t\},

and the infimum is attained.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)<\inf\{f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid d_{h}(y,x)>C(L+1)t+1\}.

(4.1)

Proof.

It is clear that
T_{t}f(x)\leq f(x)<\infty
for all
(t,x)
. If
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times M
, and if
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
is a causal curve with
\gamma(t)=x
, we have

\displaystyle f(\gamma(0))+\int_{0}^{t}L(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))\,ds

\displaystyle\geq f(\gamma(0))+\int_{0}^{t}L|\dot{\gamma}(s)|_{h}\,ds-C(L)t

\displaystyle\geq f(\gamma(0))+Ld_{h}(\gamma(0),\gamma(t))-C(L)t

\displaystyle\geq f(x)-C(L)t.

Taking the infimum over all possible causal curves, we obtain
T_{t}f(x)\geq f(x)-C(L)t
, proving the first part of the lemma.

Moreover, if
\varepsilon>0
and
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
is a curve with
T_{t}f(x)\geq f(x)+\int_{0}^{t}L(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))\,ds-\varepsilon
, then
\gamma
must be causal and the same computation as above yields

\displaystyle f(x)\geq T_{t}f(x)\geq f(x)+d_{h}(\gamma(0),x)-C(L+1)t-\varepsilon.

Hence,
d_{h}(\gamma(0),x)\leq C(L+1)t+\varepsilon
. This proves (
4.1
) (for
\varepsilon=1
). In particular, any minimizing sequence
(y_{k})
for
T_{t}f(x)
is necessarily precompact and converges to some
y\in M
with
d_{h}(y,x)\leq C(L+1)t
. By the continuity of
f
, the closedness of
J^{+}
, and the continuity of
c_{t}
on
J^{+}
, we obtain

T_{t}f(x)=f(y)+c_{t}(y,x).

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz function. Then
Tf
is lower semi-continuous. Moreover, if
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times M
and the infimum in the definition of
T_{t}f(x)
is attained at some
y\in I^{-}(x)
, then
Tf
is continuous at
(t,x)
.

Proof.

Let
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times M
, and let
(t_{k},x_{k})
a sequence converging to
(t,x)
. For each
k
, choose
y_{k}\in J^{-}(x_{k})
such that

T_{t_{k}}f(x_{k})=f(y_{k})+c_{t_{k}}(y_{k},x_{k}).

By the preceding lemma, the sequence
(y_{k})
is relatively compact. Thus, by passing to a subsequence if necessary, we can assume that
y_{k}\to y
. Since
J^{+}
is closed,
(y,x)\in J^{+}
. This, together with the continuity of
c
gives

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}T_{t_{k}}f(x_{k})=f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\geq T_{t}f(x).

(4.2)

Since this holds true for any sequence, this proves the lower semi-continuity.

Now, suppose that the infimum in the definition of
T_{t}f(x)
is attained at some point
y\in I^{-}(x)
. For large
k
, it follows that
y\in I^{-}(x_{k})
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle T_{t_{k}}f(x_{k})\leq f(y)+c_{t_{k}}(y,x_{k})\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)=T_{t}f(x).

Together with (
4.2
), this proves the continuity at
(t,x)
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be
C^{1}
and
L
-Lipschitz. Let
x_{0}\in M
and suppose that
d_{x_{0}}f\in T_{x_{0}}^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}_{x_{0}}^{*}
, i.e. there exists
v\in{\cal C}_{x_{0}}
with
d_{x_{0}}f(v)>0
. Given
t_{0}>0
, there exists a constant
\delta>0
and a neighbourhood
U
of
x_{0}
such that, for every
x\in U
and
t\geq t_{0}
, it holds

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)<\inf\{

\displaystyle f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid

\displaystyle d_{h}(y,x)>C(L+1)t+1\text{ or }d_{h}(y,x)<\delta\}.

(4.3)

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
, we know that for all
(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times M
, the following holds:

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)<\inf\{f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid d_{h}(y,x)>C(L+1)t+1\}.

Thus, it suffices to prove that there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
x_{0}
and
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)<\inf\{f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid d_{h}(y,x)<\delta\}

for
x\in U
,
t\geq t_{0}
.

Let
v\in\operatorname{int}({\cal C}_{x_{0}})
such that
d_{x_{0}}f(v)>0
(such a
v
exists by the assumption
d_{x_{0}}f\in T_{x_{0}}^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}_{x_{0}}^{*}
). Let
(V,\phi)
be a chart around
x_{0}
and let
w:=d_{x_{0}}\phi(v)
and
z_{0}:=\phi(x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is easy to see that there exist two constants
r,T>0
such that
B_{r+T|w|}(z_{0})\subseteq\subseteq\phi(V)
,

\displaystyle\inf_{z\in B_{r+T|w|}(z_{0})}d_{z}(f\circ\phi^{-1})(w)=:M>0,

(4.4)

and such that, for
z\in B_{r}(z_{0})
, the curve

\displaystyle[0,T]\to M,\ s\mapsto\phi^{-1}(z-Tw+sw),

is timelike. In particular,
\phi^{-1}(z-sw)\in I^{-}(\phi^{-1}(z))
sor
0<s\leq T
.

Let
z\in B_{r}(z_{0})
and
t\geq t_{0}
. For
0<s\leq T
, denoting by
C_{1}
a Lipschitz constant of
\tau\circ\phi^{-1}
on
B_{r+T|w|}(z_{0})
, we have

\displaystyle c_{t}(\phi^{-1}(z-sw),\phi^{-1}(z))\leq\frac{(\tau(\phi^{-1}(z))-\tau(\phi^{-1}(z-sw)))^{2}}{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}^{2}s^{2}|w|^{2}}{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}^{2}s^{2}|w|^{2}}{t_{0}}.

Using (
4.4
) and the mean value theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle f(\phi^{-1}(z-sw))+c_{t}(\phi^{-1}(z-sw),\phi^{-1}(z))

\displaystyle\leq f(\phi^{-1}(z))-sM+\frac{C_{1}}{t_{0}}s^{2}|w|^{2}.

(4.5)

Now, pick
0<s\leq T
such that
-sM+\frac{C_{1}}{t_{0}}s^{2}|w|^{2}<0
. Then, if
\delta>0
is such that

|f(y)-f(x)|<-sM+\frac{C_{1}}{t_{0}}s^{2}|w|^{2}\text{ for all }x,y\in B_{2\delta}(x_{0}),

inequality (
4.5
) implies

\displaystyle T_{t}f(\phi^{-1}(z))<\inf\{f(y)+c_{t}(y,\phi^{-1}(z))\mid d_{h}(y,\phi^{-1}(z))\leq\delta\}

for
z\in B_{r}(z_{0})
such that and
\phi^{-1}(z)\in B_{\delta}(x_{0})
. This proves (
4.3
) for
U:=\phi^{-1}(B_{r}(z_{0}))\cap B_{\delta}(x_{0})
.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be
C^{1}
and
L
-Lipschitz. Let
x_{0}\in M
and suppose that
r>0
is such that for every
x\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{r}(x_{0})
,
d_{x}f\in T_{x}^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}_{x}^{*}
, i.e. there is

.

Given
t_{0}>0
, there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for every

Proof.

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{r}(x_{0})

∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be
C^{1}
and
L
-Lipschitz. Let
x_{0}\in M
and suppose that
r>0
is such that for every
x\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{r}(x_{0})
,
d_{x}f\in T_{x}^{*}M\backslash{\cal C}_{x}^{*}
.

Given
t_{1}>t_{0}>0
, there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that for every
x\in B_{r}(x_{0})
and
t_{1}\geq t\geq t_{0}
we have

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)<\inf\{f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid d_{h}(y,x)>C(L+1)t+1\text{ or }d(y,x)<\delta\}.

Proof.

Recall that, from the above corollary, there exists
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that for
(t,x)\in[t_{0},\infty)\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{r}(x_{0})
we have

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)<\inf\{

\displaystyle f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid

\displaystyle d_{h}(y,x)>C(L+1)t+1\text{ or }d_{h}(y,x)<\delta^{\prime}\}.

(4.7)

Let us assume, by contradiction, that there is a sequence
(t_{k},x_{k})\in[t_{0},t_{1}]\times B_{r}(x_{0})
and a sequence
(y_{k})
with
\delta^{\prime}\leq d_{h}(y_{k},x_{k})\leq C(L+1)t_{k}+1
and
d(y_{k},x_{k})\to 0
such that

\displaystyle f(y_{k})+c_{t_{k}}(y_{k},x_{k})\leq T_{t_{k}}f(x_{k})+1/k.

(4.8)

In particular,
y_{k}\in J^{-}(x_{k})
.
By the completeness of
M
w.r.t. the metric
h
, it follows that, along a subsequence that we do not relabel,
x_{k}\to x\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muB\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{r}(x_{0})
,
y_{k}\to y\in\partial J^{-}(x)\backslash\{x\}
and
t_{k}\to t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
.

We claim that

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)=\min\{f(z)+c_{t}(z,x)\mid z\in\partial J^{-}(x)\backslash\{x\}\}.

(4.9)

In fact, if
Tf
is continuous at
(t,x)
, then it follows from (
4.8
) that

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)=f(y)+c_{t}(y,x).

Since
y\in\partial J^{-}(x)\backslash\{x\}
, this proves (
4.9
) in the first case.
In the second case, assume that
Tf
is not continuous at
(t,x)
. By Lemma
4.3
and (
4.7
), there exists
z\in J^{-}(x)\backslash\{x\}
attaining the infimum in the definition of
T_{t}f(x)
. However, due to the fact that
Tf
is discontinuous at
(t,x)
, Lemma
4.4
implies that
z\in\partial J^{-}(x)\backslash\{x\}
, proving the claim. We have proved that there always exists a minimizer in (
4.9
), which we will denote by
z\in\partial J^{-}(x)
.

Let
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
be a causal null geodesic connecting
z
with
x
. Choose
\xi\in T_{z}M
with
g(\dot{\gamma}(0),\xi)>0
. Let
\xi:[0,t]\to TM
the parallel vector field along
\gamma
with
\xi(0)=\xi.
Consider the vector field
\tilde{\xi}(s):=(t-s)\xi(s)
. We can now pick a smooth variation
c:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\times[0,t]\to M
of
\gamma
such that its variational vector field is
\tilde{\xi}
and such that
c(r,t)=\gamma(t)=x
for all
r\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
. Our goal is to show that, for
r
small enough,

\displaystyle f(c(r,0))+c_{t}(c(r,0),x)<f(z)+c_{t}(z,x),

contradicting the definition of
z
and, thus, proving the claim.

We compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}g(\partial_{s}c(r,s),\partial_{s}c(r,s))

\displaystyle=2g\bigg(\frac{\nabla}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}\frac{\partial c}{\partial s}(r,s),\frac{\partial c}{\partial s}(0,s)\bigg)

\displaystyle=2g\bigg(\frac{\nabla}{ds}\frac{\partial c}{\partial r}(0,s),\dot{\gamma}(s)\bigg)

\displaystyle=2g\bigg(\frac{\nabla}{ds}((t-s)\xi(s)),\dot{\gamma}(s)\bigg)

\displaystyle=-2g(\xi(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))

\displaystyle=-2g(\xi(0),\dot{\gamma}(0))=:-2a<0,

where in the step to the last line we used the fact that
\xi
and
\dot{\gamma}
are parallel along
\gamma
.
Thus, Taylor expansion and the fact that
\gamma
is a null geodesic yield

\displaystyle g(\partial_{s}c(r,s),\partial_{s}c(r,s))\leq-|\dot{\gamma}(s)|^{2}_{g}-2ar+O(r^{2})\leq-ar

(4.10)

for small values
r>0
. In particular,
c(r,\cdot)
is either (future-directed) timelike or past-directed timelike for small
r
.
Since
z\neq x
and
z\in J^{-}(x)
we must have
z\notin J^{+}(x)
. Thus
z\approx c(r,0)\notin J^{+}(x)
for small
r
, so that
c(r,\cdot)
is in fact (future-directed) timelike for small
r
.
This, together with

\displaystyle L_{g}(c(r,\cdot))\geq t\sqrt{ar},

as follows from (
4.10
), gives

\displaystyle d(c(r,0),x)\geq t\sqrt{ar}.

On the other hand, since
\partial_{r}c(0,0)=t\xi
, it holds
d_{h}(c(r,0),z)\leq 2t|\xi|_{h}r
, if
r
is small.

Recall that
L
is a Lipschitz constant for
f
. It follows for small
r>0
that

\displaystyle f(c(r,0))+c_{t}(c(r,0),x)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle f(z)+Ld_{h}(c(r,0),z)+\frac{(\tau(x)-\tau(c(r,0))-d(c(r,0),x))^{2}}{t}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle f(z)+2Lt|\xi|_{h}r+\frac{\Big([\tau(x)-\tau(z)]+[\tau(z)-\tau(c(r,0))]-[d(c(r,0),x)]\Big)^{2}}{t}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle f(z)+2Lt|\xi|_{h}r+\frac{\Big(\tau(x)-\tau(z)+2\operatorname{Lip}(\tau)t|\xi|_{h}r-t\sqrt{ar}\Big)^{2}}{t}

In the last line, we abused the notation
\operatorname{Lip}(\tau)
to denote a Lipschitz constant of
\tau
in a small neighbourhood of
z
. For small values of
r
, the sum of the last two quantities in the bracket is less than
-r^{3/4}
. Thus,

\displaystyle f(c(r,0))+c_{t}(c(r,0),x)\leq f(z)+2Lt|\xi|_{h}r+\frac{(\tau(x)-\tau(z)-r^{3/4})^{2}}{t}

Since
z\in\partial J^{-}(x)
, it holds
c_{t}(z,x)=(\tau(x)-\tau(z))^{2}/t
. Then, factoring out gives

\displaystyle f(c(r,0))+c_{t}(c(r,0),x)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle f(z)+2Lt|\xi|_{h}r+c_{t}(z,x)-\frac{2}{t}(\tau(x)-\tau(z))r^{3/4}+\frac{r^{3/2}}{t}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle f(z)+c_{t}(z,x)+\left(2Lt|\xi|_{h}r-\frac{2}{t}(\tau(x)-\tau(z))r^{3/4}+\frac{r^{3/2}}{t}\right).

Clearly, this quantity is less than
f(z)+c_{t}(z,x)
, if
r
is small enough (note that at this point we use
z\in J^{-}(x)\backslash\{x\}
since then
\tau(x)-\tau(z)>0
). This is a contradiction to the definition of
z
.
∎

We are now in the position to prove Theorem
4.1
. With the tools we have in hand by now, the proof is similar to the proof of Corollary 4.23 in
[
7
]
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
t_{1}>t_{0}>0
be given. The foregoing lemma states that there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
x\in B_{r}(x_{0})
and
t_{1}\geq t\geq t_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t}f(x)=\inf\{f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)\mid d_{h}(y,x)\leq C(L+1)t+1,\ d(y,x)\geq\delta\}.

One argues as in the proof of Theorem
3.6
to show that
Tf
is locally semiconcave on
(t_{0},t_{1})\times B_{r}(x_{0})
, and, hence, locally semiconcave on
(0,\infty)\times B_{r}(x_{0})
.

If
x\in B_{r}(x_{0})
and
t>0
are given, and
y\in M
is such that
T_{t}f(x)=f(y)+c_{t}(y,x)
, then the above proposition tells us that necessarily
y\in I^{-}(x)
. Let
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
be a (timelike) minimizer connecting
y
to
x
. It is a consequence from Lemma
3.10
that a super-differential for
Tf
at
(t,x)
is given by

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}c_{t}(x,y),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(y,\dot{\gamma}(t))\right).

If
Tf
is differentiable at
(t,x)
, then there exists a unique super-differential. Given two minimizers
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}:[0,t]\to M
with
\gamma_{1}(t)=\gamma_{2}(t)=x
such that both
\gamma_{1}(0)
and
\gamma_{2}(0)
attain the infimum in the definition of
T_{t}f(x)
, they must be timelike. Hence, from what we just proved,

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}c_{t}(\gamma_{1}(0),x),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t))\right)\in\partial^{+}(Tf)(t,x),\

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}c_{t}(\gamma_{2}(0),x),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}_{2}(t))\right)\in\partial^{+}(Tf)(t,x).

In particular, both super-differentials have to coincide. Since the Legendre transform is a diffeomorphism by Lemma
2.17
, it follows that
\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t)=\dot{\gamma}_{2}(t)
. The unique integrability of the Euler-Lagrange flow implies
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
.

Conversely, assume that there exists exactly one minimizing curve
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
with
\gamma(t)=x
such that
T_{t}f(x)=f(\gamma(0))+c_{t}(\gamma(0),x)
. Let
y:=\gamma(0)
. Since
Tf
is locally semiconcave in a neighborhood of
(t,x)
, it is differentiable almost everywhere and the set of super-differentials at
(t,x)
is given by the closure of the convex hull of reaching gradients:

\displaystyle\partial^{+}(Tf)(t,x)=\overline{\operatorname{conv}\left\{\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(\partial_{t}c_{t_{k}}(y_{k},x_{k}),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x_{k},\dot{\gamma}_{k}(t_{k}))\right)\right\}},

where we take all sequences
(t_{k},x_{k})\in(0,\infty)\times B_{r}(x_{0})
converging to
(t,x)
such that
Tf
is differentiable at
(t_{k},x_{k})
. Here,
y_{k}:=\gamma_{k}(0)
and
\gamma_{k}:[0,t_{k}]\to M
is the unique minimizing curve with
\gamma_{k}(t_{k})=x_{k}
such that
T_{t_{k}}f(x_{k})=f(y_{k})+c_{t_{k}}(y_{k},x_{k})
.

Now, let
(t_{k},x_{k})
be such a sequence. Note that, by the foregoing proposition, we have
d_{h}(y_{k},x_{k})\leq C(L+1)t+2
and
d(y_{k},x_{k})\geq\delta
for sufficiently large
k
and some
\delta>0
. Hence, along a subsequence that we do not relabel,
y_{k}\to\tilde{y}\in I^{-}(x)
. Due to the continuity of
Tf
at
(t,x)
it follows that
T_{t}f(x)=f(\tilde{y})+c_{t}(\tilde{y},x)
. We are now in position to apply Corollary
2.11
. Let
\tilde{\gamma}:[0,t]\to M
be the minimizing (timelike) curve guaranteed by the corollary, and note that
\tilde{\gamma}(0)=\tilde{y}
,
\tilde{\gamma}(t)=x
and
T_{t}f(x)=f(\tilde{\gamma}(0))+c_{t}(\tilde{\gamma}(0),x))
. In particular, by assumption, we must have
\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma
and
\tilde{y}=y
. Then, clearly,
\partial_{t}c_{t_{k}}(y_{k},x_{k})\to\partial_{t}c_{t}(y,x)
as
k\to\infty
. Moreover, by the corollary,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x_{k},\dot{\gamma}_{k}(t_{k}))\to\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}(t))\text{ as }k\to\infty.

Thus, the super-differential
\partial^{+}(Tf)(t,x)
reduces to the singleton
\{(\partial_{t}c_{t}(y,x),\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}(x,\dot{\gamma}(t)))\}
, that is,
Tf
is differentiable at
(t,x)
.
∎

5
Application to Optimal transport

In this section we want to prove our main theorem. We start with the basic definition and well-known facts, also to fix our notation.

Let
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in{\cal P}(M)
be two Borel probability measures. A
coupling
of
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
is a Borel probability measure
\pi\in{\cal P}(M\times M)
such that its marginals are
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, that is,

\forall B\subseteq M\text{ Borel }:\mu_{0}(B)=\pi(B\times M)\text{ and }\mu_{1}(B)=\pi(M\times B).

In other words,
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
are the push-forward measures of
\pi
w.r.t. the projections
(x,y)\mapsto x
and
(x,y)\mapsto y
.
The set of couplings is denoted by
\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
and the
total cost
for the cost function
c
is defined as

\displaystyle C(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\inf\bigg\{\int_{M\times M}c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)\mid\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\bigg\}.

A coupling is said
optimal
if it minimizes the right-hand side of the above expression. It is a standard result in the theory of optimal transportation, using some compactness arguments (Prokhorov’s theorem), that there always exists an optimal coupling.

From now on
, fix
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
as above. We assume that
C(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})<\infty
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
dynamical optimal coupling
is a Borel probability measure
\Pi
on the metric space of minimizing curves
\Gamma^{1}
such that
(e_{0},e_{1})_{\#}\Pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
is an optimal coupling.

Here, for
t\in[0,1]
, the map
e_{t}
is defined by
e_{t}(\gamma):=\gamma(t)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
is an optimal coupling, then there exists a dynamical optimal coupling
\Pi
with
(e_{0},e_{1})_{\#}\Pi=\pi
.

Proof.

We borrow the proof from
[
18
]
, 7.16. Consider the map

(e_{0},e_{1}):\Gamma^{1}\to J^{+},\ \gamma\mapsto(\gamma(0),\gamma(1)).

Then
(e_{0},e_{1})^{-1}(x,y)
is compact for all
(x,y)\in J^{+}
thanks to Corollary
2.11
. Moreover, both
\Gamma^{1}
and
J^{+}
are complete separable metric spaces thanks to Corollary
2.10
and the closedness of
J^{+}
. Thus, a well-known selection theorem (see
[
5
]
) states that there exists a measurable map

S:J^{+}\to\Gamma^{1}
such that
S(x,y):[0,1]\to M
is a minimizing curve connecting
x
to
y
.
Since the optimal coupling
\pi
must be supported on
J^{+}
(here we use the fact that
C(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
is finite),
S
is defined
\pi
-a.e., hence we can define the measure

\Pi:=S_{\#}\pi.

It is easy to check that this measure is indeed a dynamical optimal coupling with
(e_{0},e_{1})_{\#}\Pi=\pi
.
∎

Now,
for the remainder of this section
, let
\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
be an optimal coupling, and let
\Pi
be a dynamical optimal coupling associated with
\pi
, i.e.
(e_{0},e_{1})_{\#}\Pi=\pi
. We define the curve of probability measures

\displaystyle[0,1]\to{\cal P}(M),\ t\mapsto\mu_{t}:=(e_{t})_{\#}\Pi.

(5.1)

This curve is also referred to as
displacement interpolation
.
For
0\leq s<t\leq 1
, the probability measure
\pi_{s,t}:=(e_{s},e_{t})_{\#}\Pi
clearly couples
\mu_{s}
with
\mu_{t}
. In fact, one can easily prove as in
[
18
]
, Theorem 7.21 (i)
\Rightarrow(ii)
, that it is an optimal coupling (for
\mu_{s}
and
\mu_{t}
) in the optimal transportation problem associated to the cost function
c_{t-s}
.

The following two definitions are adapted (with slight modifications) from
[
8
]
, Definition 2.1 and 2.2.

Definition 5.3
.

A pair of functions
\varphi,\psi:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is called
c
-subsolution
if

\displaystyle\psi(y)-\varphi(x)\leq c(x,y)\text{ for all }x,y\in M.

Here, we use the convention
\pm\infty\mp\infty:=-\infty
.
Equivalently(!),
\psi\leq T_{1}\varphi
.

Definition 5.4
.

We say that a
c
-subsolution is
(c,\pi)
-calibrated
if

\displaystyle\psi(y)-\varphi(x)=c(x,y)\ \text{ for }\pi\text{-a.e. }(x,y).

Remark 5.5
.

(i)

Note that this definition differs from the one in
[
8
]
since we do not assume
\psi
and
\varphi
to be Borel and
\varphi
and
\psi
may attain the value
-\infty
and
\infty
, respectively.

(ii)

By definition, in the Riemannian case,
\varphi
and
\psi
map to
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, respectively. The equivalence between the definition of a
c
-subsolution and the requirement
\psi\leq T_{1}\varphi
is then trivial. However, with our conventions, it is not difficult to check the equivalence.

However, there is no equivalence in being calibrated and satisfying
\psi=T_{1}\varphi
. For instance, if
\varphi,\psi\equiv\infty
, we have
\psi=T_{1}\varphi
, but
(\varphi,\psi)
is not
(c,\pi)
-calibrated.

Next,
for the remainder of this chapter
, suppose that
(\varphi,\psi)
is a
(c,\pi)
-calibrated pair and that
\pi(I^{+})=1
. Then the set

\displaystyle\{(x,y)\in I^{+}\mid\psi(y)-\varphi(x)=c(x,y)\}

(5.2)

is of full
\pi
-measure (in particular,
\psi(y),\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{R}
for all these
(x,y)
!).

In
[
15
]
it was shown that, under suitable conditions on the measures
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, there always exists a
(c,\pi)
-calibrated pair, and any optimal coupling must be concentrated on
I^{+}
.

Hence, there exists a Borel set
A\subseteq\Gamma^{1}
consisting of timelike minimizers on which
\Pi
is concentrated and such that

\displaystyle\psi(\gamma(1))-\varphi(\gamma(0))=c(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))\text{ for all }\gamma\in A.

Indeed, denoting by
B\subseteq I^{+}
a Borel subset of (
5.2
) of full
\pi
-measure, we can set
A:=(e_{0},e_{1})^{-1}(B)
. Note once again that
\psi(\gamma(1)),\varphi(\gamma(0))\in\mathbb{R}
for all
\gamma\in A
.
By definition, the measure
\mu_{t}
is then concentrated on the set

\displaystyle A_{t}:=\{\gamma(t)\mid\gamma\in A\}\subseteq M.

Using the definitions, the following lemma is well-known and easy to prove, see
[
18
]
, Lemma 7.36.

Lemma 5.6
.

For any
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
the pair

\displaystyle\varphi_{s}(x):=\inf_{z\in M}\varphi(z)+c_{s}(z,x)\text{ and }

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(y):=\sup_{z\in M}\psi(z)-c_{1-t}(y,z).

is a
c_{t-s}
-subsolution. Moreover, if
\gamma\in A
, then

\displaystyle\psi_{t}(\gamma(t))-\varphi_{s}(\gamma(s))=c_{t-s}(\gamma(s),\gamma(t)).

Proof.

For a proof, see
[
18
]
. The lemma also follows from the following more general statement (whose proof is similar).
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\gamma\in A
and
s,t\in[0,1]
.

(i)

If
0\leq\tau\leq 1-s
and
0\leq r\leq s+\tau
then

\hat{T}_{r}\varphi_{s+\tau}(\gamma(s+\tau-r))=\varphi(\gamma(0))+c_{s+\tau-r}(\gamma(0),\gamma(s+\tau-r)).

(ii)

If
0\leq\tau\leq t
and
0\leq r\leq 1-t+\tau
then

\ T_{r}\psi_{t-\tau}(\gamma(t-\tau+r))=\varphi(\gamma(0))+c_{t-\tau+r}(\gamma(0),\gamma(t-\tau+r)).

(iii)

If
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\geq 0
are such that
s+\tau_{1}\leq t-\tau_{2}
then
the pair
(\hat{T}_{\tau_{2}}\varphi_{s+\tau_{2}},T_{\tau_{1}}\psi_{t-\tau_{1}})
is still
(c_{t-s},\pi_{s,t})
-calibrated.
Additionally, if
\gamma\in A
,

T_{\tau_{1}}\psi_{t-\tau_{1}}(\gamma(t))-\hat{T}_{\tau_{2}}\varphi_{s+\tau_{2}}(\gamma(s))=c_{t-s}(\gamma(s),\gamma(t)).

Proof.

The proof boils down to elementary properties of the Lax-Oleinik semigroups and the fact that
\psi(\gamma(1))-\varphi(\gamma(0))=c(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))
. The details can be found in subsection
6.2
.
∎

Using Theorem
3.6
(i) and the fact that
T_{1}\varphi(\gamma(1))=\varphi(\gamma(0))+c(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))
, it is not difficult to show that, for
s\in(0,1)
,
\varphi_{s}
is locally semiconcave on a neighborhood of
A_{s}
. Similarly, by Theorem
3.6
(ii), for
t\in(0,1)
, the function
\psi_{t}
is locally semiconvex on a neighborhood of
A_{t}
. While we do not require this result, the following lemma establishes a similar statement.

Corollary 5.8
.

Fix
0<s<t<1
. For
0<\tau\leq 1-s
the function
\hat{T}_{\tau}\varphi_{s+\tau}
is locally semiconvex on a neighbourhood of
A_{s}
.
For
0<\tau\leq t
the function
T_{\tau}\psi_{t-\tau}
is locally semiconcave on a neighbourhood of
A_{t}
.

Proof.

If
\gamma\in A
, then from part (i) of the preceding lemma (applied to
r=s+\tau
and to
r=0
) and the fact that
\gamma
is a minimizer,

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{s+\tau}\varphi_{s+\tau}(\gamma(0))=\varphi_{s+\tau}(\gamma(s+\tau))-c_{s+\tau}(\gamma(0),\gamma(s+\tau))

Hence, we can apply Theorem
3.6
(ii) to
u:=\varphi_{s+\tau}
,
x_{0}:=\gamma(s+\tau)
,
y_{0}:=\gamma(0)
and
t_{0}:=s+\tau
, proving that
\hat{T}_{\tau}\varphi_{s+\tau}
is locally semiconvex in a neighbourhood of
\gamma(s)
. Since
\gamma
was arbitrary, this proves the first claim. The second statement can be derived similarly. Indeed, part (ii) of the above lemma applied to
r=1-t+\tau
and
r=0

yields

T_{1-t+\tau}\psi_{t-\tau}(\gamma(1))=\psi_{t-\tau}(\gamma-\tau))+c_{1-t+\tau}(\gamma(t-\tau),\gamma(1)).

The claim follows from Theorem
3.6
(i) with
x_{0}:=\gamma(t-\tau)
and
y_{0}:=\gamma(1)
and
t_{0}:=1-t+\tau
.
∎

Let us recall our main result (with a slightly different formulation and with an additional property) in this paper (Theorem
1.3
):

Theorem 5.9
.

Fix
0<s<t<1
. There exists a pair of functions
\Phi_{s},\Psi_{t}:M\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
that is
(c_{t-s},\pi_{s,t})
-calibrated and such that
\Phi_{s}
is
C_{loc}^{1,1}
on a neighbourhood of
A_{s}
while
\Psi_{t}
is
C_{loc}^{1,1}
on a neighbourhood of
A_{t}
.

In addition,
\Phi_{s}=\varphi_{s}
on
A_{s}
and
\Psi_{t}=\psi_{t}
on
A_{t}
.

For the proof we need some preparation. Let us first prove a corollary concerning the dual optimal transporation problem (Theorem
1.1
). Let us denote the total cost of the optimal transportation problem associated with the cost function
c_{t-s}
by
C^{t-s}
.

Corollary 5.10
.

Fix
0<s<t<1
and assume that
\varphi\in L^{1}(\mu_{0})
and
\psi\in L^{1}(\mu_{1})
. Then
\Phi_{s}\in L^{1}(\mu_{s})
,
\Psi_{t}\in L^{1}(\mu_{t})
and they are optimal for the dual problem of optimal transportation associated with the cost function
c_{t-s}
, i.e.

C^{t-s}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})=\int_{M}\Psi_{t}(y)\,d\mu_{t}(y)-\int_{M}\Phi_{s}(x)\,d\mu_{s}(x).

Proof.

Since the pair
(\Phi_{s},\Psi_{t})
is
(c_{t-s},\pi_{s,t})
-calibrated, it is enough to show that
\Phi_{s}\in L^{1}(\mu_{s})
and
\Psi_{t}\in L^{1}(\mu_{t})
.
Since
\mu_{s}
is concentrated on
A_{s}
and
\Phi_{s}
is
C_{loc}^{1,1}
on some open neighbourhood of
A_{s}
, it follows that
\Phi_{s}
is
\mu_{s}
-measurable. Similarly,
\Psi_{t}
is
\mu_{t}
-measurable.

Recall that we denote by
\Pi
our dynamical optimal coupling and that
\mu_{t}=(e_{t})_{\#}\Pi
and
\mu_{s}=(e_{s})_{\#}\Pi
.
For
\gamma\in A
we have by the theorem and Lemma
5.7

\Psi_{t}(\gamma(t))=\psi_{t}(\gamma(t))=\varphi(\gamma(0))+c_{t}(\gamma(0),\gamma(t))=\varphi(\gamma(0))+tc(\gamma(0),\gamma(1)).

Thus, the change of variables formula implies

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\Psi_{t}|\,d\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma^{1}}|\Psi_{t}(\gamma(t))|\,d\Pi(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma^{1}}|\varphi(\gamma(0))|\,d\Pi(\gamma)+t\int_{\Gamma^{1}}c(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))\,d\Pi(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{M}|\varphi|\,d\mu_{0}+tC(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})<\infty.

This shows that
\Psi_{t}\in L^{1}(\mu_{t})
and, similarly, one proves that
\Phi_{s}\in L^{1}(\mu_{s})
.

Thus, we can integrate the identity
\Psi_{t}(y)-\Phi_{s}(x)=c_{t-s}(x,y)
, which holds
\pi_{s,t}
-a.e., and obtain that
(\Phi_{s},\Psi_{t})
is an optimal pair for the dual formulation.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.9

As mentioned above, to prove Theorem
5.9
we need some preliminary results. The following proposition extends the results on the regularity of solutions to the Hamilton-Jacobi equation presented in
[
2
]
, and it plays the key role in the proof of our main theorem. We split the proof (as given in

6.1
Semiconcave functions and boundedness in

In this section, we review some basic properties of semiconcavity and semiconvexity, as well as other related definitions. For proofs and additional results, we refer the reader to Appendix A in

8
]
.

Definition 6.1
.

(a)

A function
f:U\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
defined on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
super-differentiable
(resp.
sub-differentiable
) at
x\in U
with
super-differential
(resp.
sub-differential
)
p\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
if
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}
and

\displaystyle f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle+o(|y-x|)

\displaystyle(\text{resp. }

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle+o(|y-x|)).

(b)

A function
f:U\to\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbb{R}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
defined on an open subset
U\subseteq N
of a smooth manifold
N
is said to be
super-differentiable
at
x\in U
with
super-differential

p\in T_{x}^{*}N
if
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}
and if, for one (or any) chart
(\phi,V)
of
U
around
x
, the function
f\circ\phi^{-1}
is super-differentiable at
\phi(x)
with super-differential
p\circ d_{\phi(x)}\phi^{-1}
.
We denote by
\partial^{+}f(x)
the set of all super-differentials at
x
.

A similar definition holds for sub-differentiable functions, whose sub-differentials at
x
are denoted by
\partial^{-}f(x)
.

Definition 6.2
.

(a)

A function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
defined on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
K
-semiconcave
if, for all
x\in U
, there exists
p\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
such that

\displaystyle f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle+K|y-x|^{2}\ \forall y\in U.

(6.1)

(b)

A family of functions
f_{i}:U\to\mathbb{R}
,
i\in I
, defined on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
uniformly semiconcave
if the constant
K
in definition (a) can be chosen uniformly for all functions
f_{i}
.

(c)

A function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
defined on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
locally semiconcave
if, for every
x\in U
, there exists an open neighborhood
V\subseteq U
around
x
such that
f
is
K
-semiconcave on
U
for some
K>0
.

(d)

A function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
defined on an open subset
U\subseteq N
of a smooth manifold
N
is said to be
locally semiconcave
if, for each chart
(\phi,V)
of
U
, the function
f\circ\phi^{-1}
is locally semiconcave.

(e)

A family of functions
f_{i}:U\to\mathbb{R}
,
i\in I
, defined on an open subset
U\subseteq N
of a smooth manifold
N
is said to be
uniformly locally semiconcave
if, for each
x\in U
, there exists a chart
(\phi,V)
of
U
around
x
such that the family of functions
f_{i}\circ\phi^{-1}
,
i\in I
, is uniformly semiconcave.

(f)

All of the above definitions also apply to semiconvexity by replacing
f
with
-f
.

Remark 6.3
.

(a)

It is shown in
[
8
]
, Corollary A14, that the infimum of a uniformly locally semiconcave family of functions is locally semiconcave, provided the infimum is finite.

An analogous result holds for semiconvex functions, replacing the infimum with the supremum.

(b)

Note that, in the situation of part (a) of the above definition, inequality (
6.1
) is only required to hold for some
p\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
, and necessarily
p\in\partial^{+}f(x)
. However, the inequality holds for any other
q\in\partial^{+}f(x)
as well (for the same
K
).

(c)

It is well-known that locally semiconcave and locally semiconvex functions are locally Lipschitz, see Lemma A5 in
[
8
]
.

Definition 6.4
.

(a)

A family of
C^{2}
functions
f_{i}:U\to\mathbb{R}
,
i\in I
, defined on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
bounded in

if

\sup_{i\in I,\ x\in U}\left\lVert D^{2}f_{i}(x)\right\rVert<\infty.

(b)

A family of
C^{2}
functions
f_{i}:U\to\mathbb{R}
,
i\in I
, defined on an open subset
U\subseteq N
of a smooth manifold
N
is said to be
locally bounded in
C^{2}
if, for each
x\in U
, there exists a chart
(\phi,V)
around
x
such that the family of maps
f_{i}\circ\phi^{-1}
,
i\in I
, is bounded in
C^{2}
.

Remark 6.5
.

(a)

Note that, similar to the situation in
[
8
]
, in part (b) of the above definition, we require the property to hold for only one chart. It is not necessarily true that the property holds for all charts.
We refer to Example A12 in
[
8
]
, which justifies the definition of uniform local semiconcavity.

(b)

It is clear from the definition that a family of functions that is locally bounded in
C^{2}
is uniformly locally semiconcave and semiconvex. In particular, the finite supremum (resp. infimum) of a family of functions that is locally bounded in
C^{2}
is locally semiconvex (resp. semiconcave).

Definition 6.6
.

(a)

For an open subset

\displaystyle\psi_{t^{\prime}}(\gamma({t^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle\geq\psi(\gamma(1))-c_{1-{t^{\prime}}}(\gamma({t^{\prime}}),\gamma(1))

\displaystyle=\varphi(\gamma(0))+c(\gamma(0),\gamma(1))-c_{1-{t^{\prime}}}(\gamma({t^{\prime}}),\gamma(1))

\displaystyle=\varphi(\gamma(0))+c_{t^{\prime}}(\gamma(0),\gamma({t^{\prime}})).

This proves our claim. Now, by the semigroup property and Lemma
3.4
,

\displaystyle T_{r}\psi_{t-\tau}(\gamma(t-\tau+r))

\displaystyle=T_{r}\hat{T}_{r}\psi_{t-\tau+r}(\gamma(t-\tau+r))

\displaystyle\geq\psi_{t-\tau+r}(\gamma(t-\tau+r))

\displaystyle=\varphi(\gamma(0))+c_{t-\tau+r}(\gamma(0),\gamma(t-\tau+r)).

On the other hand, by definition of the Lax-Oleinik semigroup, we have

\displaystyle T_{r}\psi_{t-\tau}(\gamma(t-\tau+r))

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{t-\tau}((\gamma(t-\tau))+c_{r}(\gamma(t-\tau),\gamma(t-\tau+r))

\displaystyle=\varphi(\gamma(0))+c_{t-\tau}(\gamma(0),\gamma(t-\tau))+c_{r}(\gamma(t-\tau),\gamma(t-\tau+r))

\displaystyle=\varphi(\gamma(0))+c_{t-\tau+r}(\gamma(0),\gamma(t-\tau+r)).

(iii)

Since
t-s\geq\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{t-s}(\hat{T}_{\tau_{2}}\varphi_{s+\tau_{2}})=T_{\tau_{1}}\circ T_{t-s-\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}\circ T_{\tau_{2}}\circ\hat{T}_{\tau_{2}}(\varphi_{s+\tau_{2}})\geq T_{\tau_{1}}\circ T_{t-s-\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}(\varphi_{s+\tau_{2}})

Moreover, since
\psi\leq T_{1}\varphi
, also
\hat{T}_{1}\psi\leq\hat{T}_{1}T_{1}\varphi\leq\varphi
. Hence,

\displaystyle T_{t-s-\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}(\varphi_{s+\tau_{2}})

\displaystyle\geq(T_{t-s-\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}\circ T_{s+\tau_{2}})\circ(\hat{T}_{t-\tau_{1}}\circ\hat{T}_{1-t+\tau_{1}})(\psi)

\displaystyle=T_{t-\tau_{1}}\circ\hat{T}_{t-\tau_{1}}\circ\psi_{t-\tau_{1}}

\displaystyle\geq\psi_{t-\tau_{1}}.

Both equations combined show that
T_{t-s}(\hat{T}_{\tau_{2}}\varphi_{s+\tau_{2}})\geq T_{\tau_{1}}\psi_{t-\tau_{1}}
. But this means that the pair
(\hat{T}_{\tau_{2}}\varphi_{s+\tau_{2}},T_{\tau_{1}}\psi_{t-\tau_{1}})
is a
c_{t-s}
-subsolution.

The other two claims follow from part (i) and (ii).

∎

6.3
Review of Lorentzian geometry

Definition 6.9
.

A
spacetime

(M,g)
consists of a smooth and connected manifold (Hausdorff and second-countable) and a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor field
g
of constant signature
(-,+,...,+)
. Moreover,
M
is time oriented, i.e. there exists a smooth global vector field
X:M\to TM
such that, for each
x\in M
,
g_{x}(X(x),X(x))<0
.

Definition 6.10
.

A vector
v\in T_{x}M
is called
timelike, spacelike, or lightlike
if

\displaystyle g_{x}(v,v)<0,\ g_{x}(v,v)>0\text{ or }g_{x}(v,v)=0\text{ and }v\neq 0.

Timelike and lightlike vectors as well as the null-vector are referred to as
causal
. A causal vector
v
is said to be
future-directed
(resp.
past-directed
) if
g_{x}(v,X(x))\leq 0
(resp.
\geq 0
).
For
x\in M
, we denote by
{\cal C}_{x}\subseteq T_{x}M
the set of all future-directed causal vectors. Then,
\operatorname{int}({\cal C}_{x})
consists of all future-directed timelike vectors, while
\partial{\cal C}_{x}
consists of the future-directed lightlike vectors and
0
. We also set
{\cal C}:=\{(x,v)\in TM\mid v\in{\cal C}_{x}\}
.

Definition 6.11
.

We say that a locally absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:I\to M
, with
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
an interval, is
future-directed causal
if
\dot{\gamma}(t)
is future-directed causal for almost every
t
.

A
C^{1}
-curve
\gamma:I\to M
, with
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
an interval, is called future-directed timelike if
\dot{\gamma}(t)
is future-directed timelike for every
t
.

Definition 6.12
.

Two points
x,y\in M
are said to be
causally related
(resp.
chronologically related
) if there exists a future-directed causal (resp. timelike)
C^{1}
-curve connecting them. In this case, we write
x<y
(resp.
x\ll y
). We write
x\leq y
if
x=y
or
x<y
.
The relations
>,\gg
and
\geq
are defined analogously.

We define the
chronological future/past
and
causal future/past
of a point
x\in M
as follows:

\displaystyle I^{+}(x):=\{y\in M\mid y\gg x\},

\displaystyle I^{-}(x):=\{y\in M\mid y\ll x\},

\displaystyle J^{+}(x):=\{y\in M\mid y\geq x\},

\displaystyle J^{-}(x):=\{y\in M\mid y\leq x\}.

We also set

\displaystyle J^{+}:=\{(x,y)\in M\mid y\in J^{+}(x)\}\text{ and }I^{+}:=\{(x,y)\in M\mid y\in I^{+}(x)\}.

Remark 6.13
.

An equivalent definition can be given by also considering absolutely continuous causal curves. The equivalence of these definitions is established in
[
16
]
, Theorem 2.9.

Definition 6.14
.

The
length
of an absolutely continuous future-directed causal curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to M
is defined by

\displaystyle L(\gamma):=\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{-g(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))}\,dt\in[0,\infty).

The
(Lorentzian) distance function
or
time seperation
is the function
d:M\times M\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, defined such that for
x<y
, the distance
d(x,y)
is the supremum of
L(\gamma)
over all absolutely continuous (or
C^{1}
, see
[
16
]
) future-directed causal curves connecting
x
with
y
, and such that
d(x,y)=0
if
x\nless y
.

A curve
\gamma
connecting
x
with
y>x
is said to be
(length) maximizing
if
L(\gamma)=d(x,y)
.

In this paper we focus on globally hyperbolic spacetimes, which are defined as follows:

Definition 6.15
.

A spacetime
M
is said to be
globally hyperbolic
if there is no causal loop (i.e. no closed future-directed causal curve), and if for any
x,y\in M
, the intersection
J^{+}(x)\cap J^{-}(y)
is compact.

Proposition 6.16
.

Let
M
be a globally hyperbolic spacetime. Then the distance function is real-valued and continuous. Moreover, for any points
x<y
, there exists a length maximizing geodesic connecting
x
and
y
.
Additionally, the set
J^{+}
is closed.

Proof.

See
[
17
]
, Chapter 14, Proposition 19 and Lemma 22.
∎

Remark 6.17
.

It is often helpful to fix an arbitrary complete Riemannian metric on
M
, which we will denote by
h
.
Recall that, according to the Whitney embedding theorem, every manifold can be embedded in some
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a closed submanifold. Since closed submanifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are complete w.r.t. the induced metric, we can define a complete Riemannian metric on
M
by pulling back this metric.

We will denote the norm of a vector
v
(w.r.t.
h
) by
|v|_{h}
. We also denote
|v|_{g}:=\sqrt{|g(v,v)|}
.

Remark 6.18
.

Since
M
is globally hyperbolic, the proof of Theorem 3 in
[
3
]
and the subsequent discussion, along with Corollary 1.8, show that there exists a smooth manifold
N
and a diffeomorphism
M\cong\mathbb{R}\times N
, such that the projection
\tau:\mathbb{R}\times N\to\mathbb{R},\ x\cong(t,z)\mapsto t,
satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle d_{x}\tau(v)\geq\max\bigg\{2|v|_{g},|v|_{h}\bigg\}

for all causal vectors
v\in{\cal C}_{x}
. This function is called a
splitting
.

For further references on Lorentzian geometry, we refer the reader to
[
17
]
.
