Noninvasive Speech Restoration After Total Laryngectomy Using Surface Electromyography: Pilot Study
Authors: Naresh K. Kaushal, Daniel C. Comstock, Peter C. Belafsky, Adolfo Ramirez‐Aristizabal, Lee M. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo demonstrate the feasibility of restoring intelligible, own‐voice speech after total laryngectomy using noninvasive surface electromyography.MethodsOne post‐laryngectomy patient and one healthy reference speaker silently articulated a closed‐vocabulary corpus of 499 dates and times while surface electromyographic (sEMG) signals were recorded from a 20‐electrode face, jaw, and neck montage. A decoder mapped sEMG into speaker‐independent speech tokens, which a public token‐to‐speech stack converted into audible speech; for the patient, the output was re‐rendered in his own pre‐surgical voice using approximately 8 min of audio recorded before surgery. A per‐subject procedure identified a reduced electrode subset, and performance was evaluated by five human listeners.ResultsThe system produced intelligible speech in the patient's own pre‐surgical voice. Mean word error rate by human listeners was 20.2% (95% CI 11.0%–31.5%) for the patient and 14.9% (6.8%–24.9%) for the reference speaker. Electrode‐selection analysis indicated that roughly half of the recorded electrodes captured 95% of the multi‐channel signal (9 of 20 for the patient, 11 of 18 for the reference), supporting more compact hardware. Single‐trial recordings showed coordinated muscle activity tracking each silently articulated word despite the absence of phonation.ConclusionNoninvasive sEMG‐based speech restoration is feasible after total laryngectomy. By decoding muscle activity into a speaker‐independent representation, the approach sidesteps the absence of post‐surgical own‐voice audio and remains applicable to patients unable to phonate. Unlike existing alternatives (electrolarynx, esophageal speech, tracheoesophageal puncture), it can also restore the patient's own pre‐surgical voice, offering a high‐performing, low‐complication path toward clinical use.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Total laryngectomy results in the complete, sudden, and irreversible loss of voice, profoundly affecting quality of life, social integration, and mental health. Impaired speech communication leads to social isolation, depression, and a host of negative psychological sequelae [1, 2]; patients with laryngeal cancer face a suicide risk six times higher than the general population [3, 4].

Currently available speech restoration options are limited. The electrolarynx produces robotic‐sounding speech with word correct rates averaging only 50%–60% and as low as 16% [5, 6], especially in noisy environments [5]; consequently, 87% of electrolarynx users report perceived stigma [6]. A second common option, esophageal speech, severely limits fluency and breath patterns. Even the most state‐of‐the‐art option, tracheoesophageal puncture (TEP), is frequently complicated by tissue degradation and pneumonia [3, 7]. Furthermore, all three approaches may be contraindicated depending on feeding tube reliance or extent of chemoradiation [8]. And critically, none of these approaches restores an individual's own‐voice quality, which is closely tied to identity and self‐expression [9, 10], contributing to persistent depression and reduced quality of life even in patients with partially restored speech [10].

Surface electromyography (sEMG)‐based silent speech decoding has advanced considerably in individuals with healthy articulators [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. However, post‐laryngectomy patients pose distinct challenges: there is no residual voice to provide ground‐truth audio for training. Methods effective in healthy speakers often rely on paired recordings of each utterance in both silent and vocalized speaking modes [12, 18], a form of supervision that is fundamentally unavailable post‐laryngectomy. Additionally, post‐surgical radiation toxicity commonly affects the remaining articulators [19, 20], producing large, idiosyncratic individual differences in muscle activation patterns. Prior sEMG work in this population includes binary utterance classification by Rameau and colleagues [21], continuous‐word recognition on a 2500‐word vocabulary in eight patients by Meltzner and colleagues [22], and word recognition followed by text‐to‐speech synthesis in a matched‐voicebank voice by Vojtech and colleagues [23]. To our knowledge, no prior sEMG study has decoded directly to audible speech without an intermediate text‐recognition stage, nor rendered the output in the patient's own pre‐surgical voice.

The purpose of this pilot study is to demonstrate the feasibility of using noninvasive sEMG to restore naturalistic, own‐voice speech in a patient with total laryngectomy. We decode sEMG signals into continuous synthesized speech using a computational decoder and benchmark performance using word error rate (WER). We also examine, in an exploratory single‐subject analysis, which electrode locations carry the most informative signal for this patient in order to motivate future studies of more comfortable hardware form factors.

Materials and Methods

The study enrolled one post‐laryngectomy patient and, as a reference, one healthy adult male speaker. Five additional participants served as human transcribers of the synthesized speech. All subjects were fluent in English.

Post‐Laryngectomy Patient

The patient was a 60‐year‐old male who underwent total laryngectomy surgery following severe dysphagia, approximately 2 months before participating in the sEMG recordings. Approximately 17.5 years earlier, he had received radiation therapy for laryngeal cancer, the long‐term tissue effects of which precluded use of an electrolarynx. After surgery, he had been fitted with a TEP and was wearing a plug to occlude the puncture site at the time of the study; the TEP valve had been removed prior to the recording session due to leaking, aspiration, and infections, leaving him completely unable to produce audible vocalizations. Residual articulator function at enrollment was good for the lips, adequate but limited for the jaw due to scar tissue, and very limited for the tongue due to scar tissue. At the time of enrollment, his only means of linguistic communication was typing.

Benchmark Reference Speaker

A 52‐year‐old healthy adult male was recorded under an identical protocol to provide a non‐clinical reference for system performance.

Human Transcriber Panel

Five additional participants of mixed gender, aged 23–25, served as human transcribers of the synthesized speech. All were native English speakers with self‐reported normal hearing.

Ethics and Consent

All participants were ≥18 years of age; sEMG participants additionally had self‐reported adequate vision. Research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the University of California, Davis Institutional Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent and consent for publication of de‐identified data.

Data Collection

Surface EMG was recorded from 20 active electrodes on the face, jaw, and neck (Brain Vision actiCHamp Plus, 5000 Hz, ground on the left earlobe) as shown in Figure 1. Full skin‐prep and electrode‐setup details are in the Supporting Information.

Electrode placement for laryngectomy patient. (a) Anterior view: Neck electrode placement. (b) Lateral view: Facial electrode placement.

Electrode placement followed the closed‐vocabulary protocol of Gaddy and Klein [18]. The corpus consisted of 499 phrases composed of dates and times (Table 1). Participants silently articulated each prompt (mouthing the words without phonation) and pressed the Enter key to advance, with sEMG and prompt‐onset events synchronized via the Lab Streaming Layer (LSL) protocol [24]. Each session lasted approximately 1 h and 20 min from electrode application through completion of the 499‐phrase set, which each phrase articulated once. Of the 499 utterances, 461 were used for training, 19 for validation, and 19 were held out as a fixed test set.

Per‐Subject Electrode Selection

For each subject, channel selection used two complementary analyses: principal component analysis (PCA) on windowed‐RMS features to estimate the intrinsic dimensionality of the multi‐channel sEMG (i.e., how many channels are needed), and an unsupervised greedy sequential feature selection (SFS) procedure to identify which specific electrodes to retain (procedural detail in the Supporting Information Methods). For the patient, PCA placed the intrinsic dimensionality near seven components and SFS crossed the 95% reconstruction threshold at nine channels, which were retained for decoding as shown in Figure 2. For the reference speaker, after excluding two low‐correlation outlier channels, PCA placed the intrinsic dimensionality near 8 components and SFS crossed the same threshold at 11 channels. Both reductions support a more compact, clinically tolerable montage than a full 20‐channel array. We emphasize that the electrode subsets reported here are subject‐specific and exploratory: with one patient and one reference speaker, the selection should not be read as a generalizable placement rule.

Patient's nine SFS‐selected electrodes (lateral view, left side).

Decoding Pipeline

The decoding pipeline maps sEMG from the SFS‐selected electrodes (9 for the patient, 11 for the reference speaker) into audible speech in two stages: (i) a decoder model predicts a sequence of speaker‐independent speech tokens (HuBERT units [25]) from sEMG, and (ii) the predicted token sequence is converted to audio by the publicly available Generative Spoken Language Modeling (GSLM) unit‐to‐speech stack (Tacotron‐2 [26] followed by the WaveGlow vocoder [27]). The decoder architecture follows Gaddy and Klein [12], with HuBERT‐token output following the Chang lab [28, 29]. Figure 3 summarizes the end‐to‐end system. Full architectural and training detail is provided in the Supporting Information Methods; only the elements relevant to interpreting the clinical results are summarized here.

End‐to‐end sEMG‐to‐own‐voice pipeline.

The choice of speaker‐independent speech tokens as the intermediate target is a deliberate design response to the post‐laryngectomy setting and parallels the approach used in recent intracortical speech BCI work [28]: Because post‐laryngectomy patients cannot phonate, no paired silent‐vocalized recordings can be collected to train a patient‐specific acoustic model; using speaker‐independent speech tokens as the decoder target decouples ‘what was said’ from any individual's vocal characteristics. The core decoder and the WER values reported below require no patient audio at any stage. The own‐voice rendering described next is a separable module engaged only when pre‐surgical recordings are available.

Own‐Voice Synthesis

The WaveGlow output was re‐rendered in Patient's pre‐surgical voice using the Coqui YourTTS multi‐speaker text‐to‐speech model [30], conditioned on a speaker embedding fit from approximately 8.12 min of pre‐surgical audio (in this case recorded by UC Davis researchers; however, such audio is often available in voicemails and personal recordings). Re‐rendering proceeds via an intermediate text‐extraction step on the WaveGlow output (Google Speech‐to‐Text [31]); any errors at this step affect only the personalized waveform and not the WERs reported below, which are computed on the pre‐personalization WaveGlow output. The personalized waveform is presented qualitatively as a feasibility demonstration of own‐voice restoration; replacing the text intermediary with a direct unit‐to‐speaker‐conditioned vocoder is a planned extension.

Inter‐Rater Reliability

Across the five human transcribers, per‐utterance correct‐word ratios were strongly correlated for the healthy reference speaker (mean pairwise Pearson r=0.83) and moderately correlated for the laryngectomy patient (r=0.48), indicating that transcribers tended to agree on which utterances were easier or harder to decode.

Discussion

This pilot study demonstrates the feasibility of restoring intelligible, naturalistic speech in a patient with total laryngectomy using a noninvasive sEMG‐based system, and shows that the synthesized output can be rendered in the patient's own pre‐surgical voice. Three prior sEMG studies have addressed laryngectomy: Rameau and colleagues [21] demonstrated binary utterance classification (86.4% accuracy); Meltzner and colleagues [22] achieved continuous‐word recognition on a 2500‐word vocabulary across eight patients (10.3% mean WER); and Vojtech and colleagues [23] demonstrated word recognition followed by text‐to‐speech synthesis in a matched‐voicebank voice. The present work differs from these in two respects: it decodes sEMG directly to audible speech without an intermediate text‐recognition stage, and it renders the output in the patient's own pre‐surgical voice rather than a substitute voice. It is worth drawing an explicit distinction here between two components of the system. The core decoder targets a speaker‐independent intermediate representation (HuBERT tokens) and is trained without requiring any audio of the patient's own voice; it is therefore directly applicable to any post‐laryngectomy patient, regardless of whether pre‐surgical recordings exist. The WER values reported above are measured on this generic‐voice output. Voice personalization is a separate, optional downstream step that, when pre‐surgical audio is available, additionally renders the output in the patient's own voice. In the present case approximately 8 min of pre‐surgical recordings supplied by the patient enabled this personalization, but it is not required for the core decoder to function.

Several recent studies have demonstrated sEMG‐based silent speech in healthy speakers. Meltzner and colleagues reported 8.9% WER for sEMG‐to‐text recognition on a 2200‐word vocabulary using purpose‐built sEMG hardware [11]. Gaddy and Klein synthesized audible speech from sEMG in a healthy speaker on both closed and open vocabularies [12, 18], but their training procedure requires paired recordings of each utterance in both silent and vocalized speaking modes. That paired‐vocalization supervision is fundamentally unavailable in the post‐laryngectomy setting; the speaker‐independent HuBERT tokens used here are a deliberate response to that constraint. The present study complements these healthy‐speaker results by extending sEMG silent speech to a clinical population in whom voice production is lost and in whom large inter‐individual differences arise from both etiology and treatment.

A second observation concerns the channel‐reduction analysis. Using PCA to estimate intrinsic signal dimensionality and SFS to identify which electrodes to retain, we found that 9 of 20 channels for the patient and 11 of 18 for the reference speaker were sufficient to recover 95% of the multi‐channel signal. This has direct implications for hardware miniaturization: roughly half the recorded electrodes carry the bulk of the input information, supporting a substantially more compact montage without meaningful information loss. In a clinical population where post‐surgical and radiation‐related tissue changes produce large individual differences in muscle activation topology, per‐subject channel selection is likely necessary, as the appropriate electrode count is a property of the individual rather than a fixed design parameter. With recordings across a larger cohort, a complementary question becomes tractable: whether a compact fixed‐location subset generalizes across most patients, which is a prerequisite for a manufacturable wearable that balances scalability and per‐patient adaptability. Wu and colleagues' 10‐electrode neck‐only “necklace” montage [32] illustrates one direction this could take.

The clinical motivation for pursuing this line of work was reinforced by the patient's own description of life after laryngectomy. In a pre‐study interview, he characterized himself as “a very verbal communicator” and described the impact of voice loss as “this has crushed me.” At enrollment, his only communication channel was typing, with which he reported being “not even remotely satisfied,” and his electrolarynx use was contraindicated by post‐surgical radiation tissue effects. Asked to describe his ideal assistive device, he stated that it “would give me the ability to communicate coherently with others, not a robotic modulated sound.” These remarks anchor the two design choices that distinguish this study from prior sEMG silent‐speech work: (i) targeting continuous, naturalistic speech output rather than utterance classification, and (ii) rendering the output in the patient's own pre‐surgical voice rather than a generic synthetic voice.

These emerging technologies are also relevant to the patient‐provider conversation about treatment course. Many laryngeal cancer patients face a difficult decision between total laryngectomy and more aggressive chemoradiation regimens that are perceived as “organ‐preserving” but carry lower survival rates [33]. Knowledge of available speech‐restoration options is therefore important for providers making recommendations and for patients making informed decisions [34]. This is particularly relevant for approaches such as noninvasive silent speech, where iterative algorithmic and hardware improvements over time can be expected to expand the range of patients for whom restoration is practical.

Limitations

Consistent with the feasibility framing of this pilot study, the present results are based on a single laryngectomy patient, and replication across a larger and more clinically diverse cohort is a planned next step. The corpus is a closed‐vocabulary set of dates and times, which is appropriate for an initial demonstration; extension to open conversational speech is a natural follow‐up. The voice‐personalization stage currently includes an intermediate text‐based step, and the WER values reported here are computed on the pre‐personalization output and are therefore not affected by errors at that step; replacing the text stage with a direct unit‐to‐speaker‐conditioned vocoder is a planned extension. Recordings come from a single session, and longitudinal stability of the decoder across days and across electrode re‐application is a target for follow‐up evaluation. The human transcriber panel was small and within a narrow age range (five listeners aged 23–25); inter‐rater concordance was nonetheless high (mean pairwise Pearson r=0.83 for the reference speaker and r=0.48 for the patient), and recruitment of a broader listener panel is planned.

Future work could improve robustness by combining sEMG with complementary noninvasive signal modalities (such as video or strain sensors), and by replacing the text‐based voice‐rendering step with a direct unit‐to‐speaker‐conditioned vocoder. For severe cases in which surface signals are inadequate, invasive brain‐computer interfaces represent a longer‐term alternative; recent work has demonstrated rapid text decoding [35], multimodal speech and avatar synthesis with pre‐injury voice personalization [28], and direct voice synthesis [36] from intracortical recordings, although these approaches remain neither indicated nor regulated for routine use at present.

Conclusions

This study serves as an initial step toward understanding and harnessing sEMG for speech synthesis after total laryngectomy. We also report a single‐subject characterization of the most informative electrode sites for this patient, with the long‐term goal of motivating per‐patient hardware that improves comfort and ease‐of‐use. For total laryngectomy patients in particular, the application of this technology promises high‐performance, low‐complication own‐voice speech restoration that is adaptable over time, in contrast to other current approaches. With further developments, sEMG silent speech promises to improve social integration, work productivity, and quality‐of‐life for tens of millions of individuals.

Ethics Statement

Research was conducted in accordance with the principles embodied in the Declaration of Helsinki and in accordance with the University of California, Davis Institutional Review Board Administration protocol 2078695‐1. All participants provided written informed consent. Consent was also given for publication of the deidentified data by all participants. Participants were healthy volunteers and were selected from any gender and all ethnic and racial groups. Subjects were aged 18 or above, were able to fully understand spoken and written English, and were capable of following task instructions. Subjects had no skin conditions or wounds where electrodes were placed. Subjects were excluded if they had uncorrected vision problems or neuromotor disorders that prevented them from articulating speech. Children, adults who were unable to consent, and prisoners were not included in the experiments.
