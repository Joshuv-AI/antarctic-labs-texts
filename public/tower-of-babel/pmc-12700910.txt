Optimization of EDM parameters for enhanced machining of X37CrMoV5-1 tool steel using taguchi method and regression analysis
Authors: Kapil Surani, Hiteshkumar Patel, Chandrakant Sonawane, Choon Kit Chan, Saurav Dixit, Subhav Singh, Mohd Aamir Mumtaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrical Discharge Machining (EDM) is a vital non-traditional process for shaping hard-to-machine alloys used in precision tooling applications. This study experimentally investigates the machining performance of X37CrMoV5-1 tool steel under varying EDM parameters: peak current (Ip), pulse-on time (Ton), duty cycle (DC), and gap voltage (Vg). Experiments were conducted using the Taguchi L9 orthogonal array, and regression models were developed to predict Material Removal Rate (MRR) and Tool Wear Rate (TWR). The results revealed that peak current had the most dominant influence on both responses, contributing approximately 64.3% to MRR and 57.8% to TWR variation. The optimal parameters for maximizing MRR were found to be IP = 9 A, TON = 155 µs, DC = 28%, and Vg = 50 V., while the optimal conditions for minimizing TWR were IP = 3 A, TON = 210 µs, DC = 19%, and Vg = 50 V. The machining conditions achieved an MRR of 5.8538 mm3/min and a TWR of 0.0535 mm3/min, representing a 27.5% improvement in MRR and 21.3% reduction in TWR compared to previously reported studies on similar tool steels. The regression models showed high accuracy with R2 values exceeding 0.987, confirming their suitability for performance prediction. Overall, the study demonstrates that controlled adjustment of Ip and Ton significantly enhances EDM efficiency for X37CrMoV5-1 tool steel.Keywords: EDM, X37CrMoV5-1 steel, Taguchi (DOE), Regression analysis, Process InnovationSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The development of extremely durable materials, which are challenging to manufacture but extensively employed in the nuclear, aviation, and many other industries, is a result of technological and industrial advancement. The development of new metals, ceramics, and composite substances with superior thermo-mechanical properties was observed during the development of material engineering. These exotic materials are machined using conventional machining techniques or electric discharge machining1. Electrical discharge machining (EDM) is an unconventional technique used to machine electrically conductive metals. EDM can be used to create complicated geometries with excellent surface finish and geometric accuracy2. In theory, material removal in EDM is caused by a series of properly regulated sparks that ignite under the condition of a dielectric liquid surrounding the work material and tool. The spark creates a small plasma channel with temperatures approaching 12,000 °C, melting or ablation of the material. Many subsequent sparks are produced by the complete elimination of materials3. Owing to its inherent benefits, EDM is widely used in industrial applications. Examples of these industries include the automobile and aerospace industries, which use EDM to manufacture tools and molds, and the medical sector, which uses it to generate implants and surgical tools4.

EDM is a sophisticated process involving multiple parameters and mechanical processes. The majority of EDM investigations have concentrated on the key variables that impact machining efficiency, including peak current, pulse-on time, pulse-off time, gap voltage, and duty cycle. The following performance measures are typically used to analyze EDM: material removal rate (MRR), electrode wear ratio (EWR), tool wear rate (TWR), surface roughness (SR), microhardness, and surface topology5. As the machining variables are closely related to the measures listed above, a significant number of studies have been conducted to further understand and assist future improvement. Numerous materials, even those from the same alloy class, behave independently during the EDM processes. Considerable studies and analyses are required to process specified alloys, electrode materials, and process variables.

In a significant investigation by Amoljit Singh Gill et al.6 machined H11 die steel using a tungsten tool electrode produced by the powder metallurgy (PM) technique and found that the alloying of the H11 die steel increased the degree of microhardness by 83 percent. The outermost layer produced using the PM tool exhibited no signs of microcracks. In their investigation of the hybrid aluminum metal matrix composite’s (AMMC) efficiency, Velusamy Mohankumar et al.7 found that a greater pulse-on time and a greater current improved the MRR because they enhanced sparking vitality, but they additionally raised the electrode wear. Obratanski et al.8 experimented with EDM to machine 60CrMoV18-5 steels. The investigators concluded that pulse-on current had the highest effect on MRR; however, pulse-on time and open-circuit voltage had little and ambiguous effect. Van Tron Tran et al.9 analyzed three EDM outcome characteristics (MRR, EWR, and SR) to optimize the input variables for manufacturing AISI P20 steel. The study found that current (I) had the greatest impact on MRR, EWR, and SR, accounting for approximately 92–96% of the total contribution. Singh et al.10 studied the effect of input variables on the drill rate and tool wear rate in micro-hole drilling on Ti-6Al-4 using a brass electrode. A high current leads to increased discharge energy, leading to higher melting rates and drill rates. Increased discharge energy, along with higher spark energy, leads to faster melting along with short drilling periods. Surani et al.11 investigated the effects of the addition of silicon carbide (SiC) powder to the dielectric on the surface quality of the TZM molybdenum superalloy. Adding SiC particles of proper dimension and quantity to the dielectric liquid improves the MRR and machined surface finish, as well as the accuracy in dimensions. Muthuramalingam et al.12 studied the EDM of AISI 304 steel using different tool materials and optimized the gap voltage, current, and duty factor using the Taguchi L9. Their results showed that electrical parameters significantly affected machining, and optimal settings were identified through multiresponse analysis. Surani et al.13 compared powder-mixed EDM with conventional EDM on TZM-molybdenum superalloys using a response surface approach. The PMEDM technology, which uses SiC powder immersed in a dielectric liquid, outperformed the standard EDM in terms of MRR. Lim et al.14 examined the solidification microstructures of various tool metals during EDM machining. This work provides an excellent illustration of how steels behave differently during EDM, demonstrating the need for a thorough and in-depth investigation of this topic. In a more recent study, Le et al.15 and Danish et al.16 examined the processing of steels using powder-mixed EDM using tungsten carbide powder and hydroxyapatit powder. Finally, research has been done on the EDM processing of AISI D2 tool steel by Amreeta R. Kaigude et al.17 and Bachchhav et al.18. It is simple to conclude the scientific value and practical significance of studies on EDM machining of steel alloys from the preceding comprehensive overview of the literature, particularly for alloys with significant commercial applications4.

The scientific literature that is currently available makes it clear that many investigators have published findings when utilizing various tool materials for different difficult-to-cut materials; however, further research is needed to understand the performance qualities of X37CrMoV5-1 steel during EDM. It has excellent durability, high thermal conductivity, resilience to high temperatures, high hardness, and low susceptibility to surface cracking19. It is appropriate for both cold work and plastic applications, specifically die-casting and light metal extrusion tools, forging dies, hot shear blades, and hot extrusion tools. Muthuramalingam et al.25 studied EDM of AISI 202 steel using brass electrodes and different pulse generators. Energy pulses reduced tool wear by 13.1% and improved the surface finish, with an open-circuit voltage having the most significant impact.

The present study investigates the EDM process of X37CrMoV5-1 steel, focusing on the effects of key machining parameters like peak current, pulse-on time, duty cycle, and gap voltage on material removal rate (MRR) and tool wear rate (TWR). A Taguchi DOE was implemented to systematically vary these input parameters, and regression analysis was used to correlate them with the outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive study has yet examined EDM of X37CrMoV5-1 steel across a wide range of industrially relevant parameter levels, capturing both low- and high-energy discharge regimes. This study addresses this gap by optimizing parameters for maximum MRR and minimum TWR using single-objective optimization, aiming to provide practical guidelines for industrial applications such as die and mold manufacturing, tooling, and high-performance component production. The novelty of this work lies in its systematic and quantitative investigation of the influence of discharge energy density on MRR and TWR for X37CrMoV5-1 steel, a high-hardness tool steel with limited EDM studies reported, with the ultimate goal of improving machining efficiency, tool life, and surface quality in real-world manufacturing scenarios.

Design of experiments

There are efficient statistics and DOE techniques that, aside from the most basic full factorial DOE that evaluates every feasible factor combination, allow the deduction of accurate data from fewer experiments. One of these techniques is the orthogonal array (OA) technique used in the Taguchi DOE technique44.This is implied by the word “orthogonal” to indicate that the array columns are balanced. For a system to be considered balanced, it must first have a similar set of levels in every column and a similar number of level combinations between any two columns. Four input variables with three levels each were considered in this investigation. Using the L9 orthogonal array and the Taguchi DOE approach, the required number of tests was reduced from 27 to 9. Figure 3a illustrates the relationship between MRR, Ip, and Ton, with the colour gradient representing MRR values, where lighter shades indicate higher material removal rates. The observations revealed that an increase in Ip significantly enhanced the MRR, demonstrating its dominant influence on the machining process. While an increase in Ton also contributes to an increase in the MRR, its effect is comparatively less pronounced than that of IP. Additionally, the nonlinear trend observed in the plot suggests possible interactions between these parameters, indicating that their combined influence on the MRR may not be strictly additive and could involve complex dependencies. The observed dominance of the peak current (IP) in influencing both MRR and TWR can be attributed to the fundamental physics governing the EDM process. In EDM, material removal occurs because of a series of rapid electrical discharges between the tool and workpiece, which create localized high-temperature plasma channels50. The peak current directly determines the intensity of each discharge spark and, hence, the amount of thermal energy delivered to the workpiece surface during each pulse. As Ip increases, the energy density within the plasma channel increases sharply, resulting in higher temperatures, typically exceeding 10,000 °C, and more intense localized melting and vaporization of the material. This leads to a greater material removal per discharge, which explains the significant influence of Ip on the MRR. Moreover, the increased thermal loading at higher Ip values also contributed to enhanced tool wear, thereby affecting the TWR. These effects highlight the central role of the peak current in controlling the energy dynamics and material response in EDM, particularly for hard-to-machine tool steels such as X37CrMoV5-1.

Figure 3b shows the variation in MRR with respect to Ip and Duty Cycle (DC), with the color gradient depicting different MRR values, where brighter shades correspond to higher material removal rates. The analysis indicates that the MRR increases significantly with a higher Ip, reinforcing its strong influence on the machining process. In contrast, DC exhibits a comparatively smaller effect on the MRR, suggesting that variations in the duty cycle do not substantially alter the material removal rates. Unlike the previous plot, the relationship in this case appears more linear, indicating a more consistent and predictable increase in the MRR as these parameters change.

The pronounced influence of the peak current (IP) highlights its critical role in determining the thermal energy released during each discharge event. As Ip increased, the intensity of the spark, and consequently the energy delivered to the material, also increased, leading to deeper and wider craters formed per discharge. This directly enhances the material removal per unit time. On the other hand, the duty cycle (DC), which defines the ratio of the pulse-on time to the total cycle time, primarily influences the frequency of discharge rather than its intensity. While a higher DC allows for more discharges within a given timeframe, the magnitude of the energy per spark remains constant at a given IP and TON. As a result, the influence of DC on MRR is less significant than that of IP. This observation aligns with the EDM process physics, wherein the energy per pulse (governed mainly by Ip and TON) plays a more dominant role in material erosion than the discharge frequency alone. Hence, the linear behavior seen in Fig. 3b reaffirms that while both parameters contribute, IP remains the primary driver of energy input and material removal in the EDM machining of X37CrMoV5-1 steel.

The 3D surface plot, as shown in Fig. 4a, analysis of the relationship between MRR, IP, and Vg reveals that IP plays a dominant role in influencing material removal rates. As IP increases, MRR increases significantly, highlighting its strong impact compared to Vg. In contrast, variations in Vg have a minimal effect on MRR, as indicated by the relatively flat surface along the Vg axis, suggesting that Vg is not a critical factor in determining the material removal efficiency51. Furthermore, the observed non-linear trend between IP and MRR implies that while MRR initially increases sharply with IP, the rate of increase gradually slows at higher current levels. These findings indicate that to achieve optimal MRR, IP should be maintained at higher values, whereas Vg can be adjusted within moderate ranges without significantly affecting material removal. Therefore, optimizing IP is a more effective approach than varying Vg for enhancing the machining performance.

These findings suggest that Ip plays the most crucial role in influencing the TWR, whereas TON, DC, and Vg exhibit minimal effects. The 3D surface plot, as shown in Fig. 6a, of TWR versus IP and TON reveals that TWR increases significantly with IP, confirming the regression analysis findings, where Ip was identified as the most influential factor. In contrast, TWR exhibited minimal sensitivity to TON, as variations in TON did not lead to significant changes in TWR, aligning with its high p-value in the regression analysis. Additionally, the curved surface suggests potential interaction effects, indicating that, at higher IP values, Ton may have a slight influence on the TWR. This non-linear relationship highlights the need for further analysis that incorporates interaction terms. From a process mechanism perspective, the dominance of IP on the TWR can be explained by the direct correlation between the discharge current and the thermal stress imposed on the tool electrode. A higher IP results in a higher energy concentration during each discharge, not only accelerating the melting and vaporization of the workpiece material, but also subjecting the electrode to intense localized heating. This causes increased electrode wear owing to melting, micro-cracking, and even spalling under rapid thermal cycling. In contrast, TON primarily controls the duration of the discharge rather than its intensity. When TON increases within a moderate range, the extended discharge duration may not necessarily increase wear unless combined with a high IP owing to the self-limiting nature of plasma expansion and energy saturation. This explains the observed curvature in the plot, where the effect of TON on the TWR becomes slightly more noticeable at higher current levels. Overall, the surface behavior emphasizes that IP is the principal driver of tool wear, with TON exhibiting a secondary interaction-dependent effect in the EDM of X37CrMoV5-1 steel.

Figure 6b illustrates the variation in TWR with IP and DC. TWR generally increases with IP, as a higher current leads to a greater energy input per discharge, resulting in more aggressive tool erosion owing to intense thermal loading. In contrast, TWR exhibits a non-linear relationship with DC, where moderate DC values tend to minimize tool wear, while extremely high or low DC values contribute to increasing TWR, suggesting the presence of an optimal DC range for reducing tool wear. Additionally, the interaction between Ip and DC shows that a combination of high IP and high DC maximizes TWR, leading to accelerated tool degradation. Therefore, achieving a balance between these parameters is crucial for minimizing the tool wear while ensuring machining efficiency. From a mechanistic standpoint, IP governs the energy intensity of each discharge event, and as IP increases, the localized temperatures at the tool–workpiece interface increase significantly, often exceeding the melting point of the tool material, leading to crater formation, melting, and microfracture of the tool surface. This explains the steady rise in TWR with increasing IP. Duty cycle (DC), on the other hand, defines the percentage of time during which the discharge is active within one pulse cycle. A very high DC reduces the off-time, resulting in insufficient cooling intervals between discharges. This causes excessive heat accumulation at the tool interface, which leads to thermal softening, wear by diffusion, and electrode deformation. Conversely, a very low DC reduces the number of discharge events per unit time, prolonging the machining cycle and increasing the thermal gradients, which can also degrade tool performance. Hence, a moderate DC allows sufficient energy transfer while maintaining thermal stability. The combined effect of a high IP and high DC intensifies tool wear through continuous high-energy discharges and poor thermal recovery, as evidenced by the peak in the TWR at these extremes. This complex interaction underscores the importance of jointly optimizing both parameters to maintain tool integrity and overall process stability during EDM of X37CrMoV5-1 steel.

The experimental results (Table 8) demonstrate clear variations in the Tool Wear Rate (TWR) under different combinations of peak current (IP), pulse-on time (TON), duty cycle (DC), and gap voltage (Vg). The TWR ranged from a minimum of 0.0224 mm3/min to a maximum of 0.1769 mm3/min, indicating a strong dependence on the input parameters, particularly Ip.

At lower current settings (Ip = 3 A), such as in the combination IP = 3 A, TON = 210 μs, DC = 19%, and Vg = 50 V, the TWR was observed to be minimal (0.0224 mm3/min). This suggests a more stable and uniform erosion pattern on the tool surface, which is attributed to lower spark energy and reduced thermal load. In contrast, at a higher current (IP = 9 A), especially in the combination IP = 9 A, TON = 100 μs, DC = 19%, and Vg = 40 V, the TWR spiked to 0.1769 mm3/min, indicating intense material removal from the electrode. Such high wear is likely due to the aggressive discharge energy causing crater formation, pitting, and localized melting on the tool surface, which are typical wear patterns observed under elevated thermal stress conditions.

Additionally, the data reveal that intermediate values of DC (26%–28%) tend to moderate TWR (e.g., 0.0499–0.0673 mm3/min), likely due to more consistent sparking and better heat dissipation. As TON increases, a mild increase in TWR is observed, although it is not as pronounced as that with IP. This suggests that longer discharge durations contribute to deeper thermal penetration and gradually enlarge the wear zones on the tool. The interaction between the parameters leads to both linear and non-linear wear behaviours, with excessive energy leading to uneven tool degradation. Overall, the tool wear patterns inferred from the experimental data suggest that optimized moderate settings of IP and TON help maintain uniform wear and reduce irregular erosion, and thus contribute to sustainable machining by lowering electrode consumption and minimizing replacement frequency.

Surface morphology analysis of machined surface

The surface morphology of X37CrMoV5-1 tool steel machined by EDM under the optimized parameters (Sample: IP = 9A: TON = 155 µs, Duty Cycle = 28%, Gap Voltage = 50 V) was examined using SEM at different magnifications (100 × , 500 × , 1000 × , and 2500 ×). SEM analysis was performed on Sample, corresponding to the highest material removal rate (MRR), to assess the impact of aggressive EDM conditions on surface integrity. At low magnification 100 × , Fig. 9a, the machined surface exhibits an overall uniform distribution of craters and recast layer formation, indicating the localized melting and rapid solidification typical of EDM processes. As the magnification increases to 500× and 1000× , Figs. 9b and c, finer features become visible, including micro-cracks, pitting, and uneven surface protrusions, which are primarily influenced by the relatively high pulse-on time (TON = 155 µs) causing more intense thermal loading. These microstructural changes can compromise surface integrity, leading to increased roughness and potential stress concentration sites. At 2500 × magnification, Fig. 9d, the detailed view of the recast layer and micro-voids confirms the role of the selected duty cycle (28%) and gap voltage (50 V) in controlling the energy input and sparking stability; a moderate duty cycle combined with the chosen gap voltage results in controlled material removal, yet still produces observable heat-affected zones. Overall, the SEM images highlight that the EDM parameters strongly affect surface integrity: higher TON increases crater size and recast layer thickness, whereas the duty cycle and gap voltage influence surface uniformity and the density of micro-cracks. Optimizing these parameters is crucial to balance material removal rate and surface quality, minimizing defects that could affect the tool’s functional performance.

Conclusion

The present study investigates the influence of key process parameters, including peak current (IP), pulse-on time (TON), duty cycle (DC), and gap voltage (Vg), on response factors such as the Material Removal Rate (MRR) and Tool Wear Rate (TWR) during EDM machining of X37CrMoV5-1 steel. A regression analysis was performed to evaluate the significance and impact of each parameter on the machining responses, and the Taguchi method was employed to optimize the process parameters for improved performance. In addition to MRR and TWR, the surface roughness and recast layer thickness are critical parameters in EDM, particularly for tool and die applications.
