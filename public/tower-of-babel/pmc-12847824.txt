Intelligent path control of autonomous AGVs in flexible manufacturing systems: a reinforcement learning approach
Authors: Siddharth Umakarthikeyan, S Priyadharshini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) play a crucial role in material handling and transportation in automated manufacturing, enhancing efficiency and accuracy. However, traditional AGVs follow predefined paths, limiting flexibility in dynamic industrial environments with unpredictable material movement and human activity. This paper introduces a hybrid navigation strategy for autonomous AGVs using a dynamic programming approach integrated with artificial intelligence techniques to enhance autonomy and flexibility. The approach utilizes a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to determine optimal, collision-free paths by considering predefined path costs based on distance and dynamically updating travel times. The AGV selects the best route using this updated network. Once an optimal path is identified, the Timed Elastic Band (TEB) planner guides the AGV to intermediate goal points. For obstacle avoidance, the AGV classifies detected objects as static or dynamic using LiDAR scan data, enabling real-time reactive navigation. Extensive simulations and hardware experiments validate the effectiveness of the proposed method in single AGV path planning. Additionally, in multi-robot material handling, updating the network improves the coordination of multiple AGVs, preventing collisions and optimizing shop floor efficiency. The results demonstrate that this dynamic programming-based approach significantly enhances autonomous AGV navigation in industrial settings.Keywords: Intelligent path planning, Dynamic programming algorithm, Natural navigation, LiDAR mapping, Flexible manufacturing system (FMS), Automated guided vehicles (AGVs)Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Traditional man-powered material handling in modern automated industrial systems cannot satisfy increasing production requirements. To increase operational efficiency, many automated guided vehicles (AGVs) transport the materials from the material storage units to the production lines and from the production lines to the warehouse units in manufacturing processes1–5. Utilization of AGVs can reduce logistics costs and also lower the mistakes and inefficiencies caused by humans. It also increases the repeatability of material handling tasks and ensures the timely delivery of goods between the assigned locations6–9. Flexible manufacturing systems typically consist of production plants, raw material storage, and warehouse areas, all of which contain numerous key locations that an autonomous system will utilize to navigate safely and transport goods. Figure 1 illustrates the movement of AGVs as they travel between different points within the shopfloor environment to deliver the goods. In the production area of a flexible manufacturing system, there are both dynamic and static obstacles that must be taken into consideration. The primary goals of the AGVs within this environment are to detect and circumvent these obstacles, allowing them to navigate towards a specific goal point successfully.

Model schematic representation of FMS environment with autonomous AGVs.

Line-following AGVs have been introduced in many industries, such as the automotive manufacturing sector and are generally guided through a facility by a physical line such as magnetic tape, inductive wire or painted line. Generally, the physical lines are placed on the floor, and the AGV is equipped with a sensor that focuses on the line on the floor10,11. The sensor measures the left and right offset from that physical line and controls the AGV’s trajectory using that information. This line-following technique is simple and does not require high processing power, but it has many drawbacks. It needs a significant investment of time and effort to install the physical lines throughout the work floor and also involves intensive work if the path of the AGV needs to be altered in the future. In scenarios requiring coordinated movement of multiple AGVs, such as fleets, management becomes difficult in this approach due to the complex installation and coordination processes. Another significant drawback with the physical lines is that they require maintenance due to the fact that they get detached from the floor over time. Tag-following navigation is another advancement of line-following navigation, but it has similar drawbacks to the line follower navigation.

To operate without the guiding facility, LiDAR scan data-based navigation has been introduced in the AGVs, where the LiDAR scan data helps to estimate the presence of obstacles and free space. The navigation algorithm also uses encoder data from the AGV motors and estimates the AGV position12,13. The major drawback of this navigation technique is that it fails in the dynamic and semi-static environments that the manufacturing units usually have. In such implementations, the AGV pose estimate from the wheel encoder accumulates error over time which after a certain point in the AGV motion results in a wrong prediction of the AGV pose estimate. Using Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) approaches, as a further improvement to this technique, the LiDAR scan data and the encoder odometry value are fused to minimize the error in the AGV pose prediction14,15. This sensor fusion technique enhances the robot pose estimate but does not provide a solution for feature extraction in dynamic and semi-static environments.

One approach to determining the dynamic and semi-static obstacles in the environment is to make use of 3D cameras. 3D camera-based visual odometry generation and Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) techniques have been introduced for AGVs, where a camera sensor captures both RGB and depth images and sends them to the processing unit where a map is built. The RGB-D image is converted into point cloud data, and feature extraction and matching are done to estimate the robot’s pose. During the initial map-building process, feature matching is done frame-by-frame to build the 3D map16–21. The major disadvantage of this technique is that the high computational complexity increases the latency of the AGV to react to an obstacle when the robot is in motion. This is particularly true for large environments such as a manufacturing shopfloor, which may lead to crashing the AGV with the obstacles. Usually, obstacle characteristics, such as position, shape, movement speed, and direction, are determined by incorporating a vision sensor module. Image processing generally requires high computational resources to provide obstacle characteristics to the AGV to avoid obstacles without any latency. The vision sensors can work up to a specific limit depending on the available light. In an industrial environment, there is no guarantee of a fixed illumination level critical for the optimal operation of these vision sensors. Due to cameras’ computational intensity and illumination susceptibility, determining moving and static obstacles based on LiDAR sensing is more appropriate and needs to be developed. The author, therefore, proposes a LiDAR-based obstacle feature extraction method that can reliably detect dynamic obstacles and their movement behaviour. This method requires less computation and can help AGVs react to uncertainty more effectively.

From the perspective of path planning, conventional path planning algorithms such as A* and Dijkstra’s algorithms plan a robot’s path by considering its current position, target location, and available free spaces, generating a complete obstacle-free solution for the robot to reach the goal point. However, these algorithms may not guarantee passing through a desired set of waypoints, as the path is constructed based on the costs calculated. Although the algorithms can deal with dynamic obstacles, the eventual path generated by these algorithms might have a huge spatial variation. However, from the perspective of material movement inside a manufacturing shop floor, an algorithm should provide a path with less spatial variation and allows for travel through certain via points and prevents entering certain places. These path planners do not provide such capability. An approach such as dynamic programming can be made use of in this particular context to reduce the variation in the paths by specifying key points along the trajectory for the AGV. Dynamic Programming (DP) is used to break down complex problems into smaller subproblems and solve them systematically to find an optimal path. In this study, a Dynamic Programming-based path selection approach is introduced to plan the initial path of the robot to reach the goal points via the desired waypoints. Previous research has made use of DP-based path planning algorithms for multiple AGVs operating in a fleet when there are no obstacles present. However, path planning using DP for environments with static and dynamic obstacles is challenging due to the uncertainty of the obstacle movement22. DP has also been used to create optimized path-finding graphs for self-navigating vessels based on ship-trajectory data23. DP has also been used in coordinating multiple AGVs in scenarios such as collisions. However, in these cases, it is restricted only to scheduling and not explicit path planning24,25 proposed an approach to avoiding static and moving obstacles on various road types. One drawback of this particular work is that the AGV does not differentiate between static and dynamic obstacles and therefore deals with both obstacles in a similar manner. This may not be an effective way to handle the unpredictability of moving obstacles. A similar path-planning approach26 relying on the deep neural network has also been proposed. However, this work also does not distinguish between static and dynamic obstacles, which can lead to poor performance in unpredictable scenarios.

Considering the computational complexity and better control of AGV’s path selection, this paper proposes a novel hybrid navigation technique based on a dynamic programming approach which is suitable for static and dynamic obstacles. The proposed algorithm identifies the movement characteristics of the obstacle and reacts accordingly to handle the unpredictability. The main contribution of this research article is an intelligent path-planning algorithm that can recognize dynamic obstacles and allow for different responses compared to static obstacles whilst ensuring that the AGV passes through specified key points within virtual boundaries in the environment. The algorithm also attempts to follow the original plan with the least amount of variation in dynamic scenarios. The proposed algorithm can use real-time information to predict the future movements of the dynamic obstacles and adjust the planned path accordingly, thereby better handling their unpredictable movements. The study also addresses the scenario where there may be no valid path for the AGV to reach a goal using the original plan provided by the dynamic program. In such cases, the algorithm provides a solution for the AGV to still reach the goal by rerunning the DP and obtaining the sequence of key points that generate the least-cost path from the current node. A Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS) operating with multiple AGVs for handling the materials around the plant is considered for this study. The shopfloor has a set of intermediate node points (marked as key points, KPs) which serves as the via points for the AGV to plan its path appropriately and ensure a safe and controlled trajectory generation for transporting the materials from the storage to the production line and further to the warehouse areas. The primary step in the proposed technique involves selecting a minimum cost path for the AGV, knowing its current state with respect to the target location, computed using the dynamic programming approach. The AGV uses LiDAR scan data alone to make decisions and avoid obstacles. To extract the required obstacle characteristics with less computational complexity, this work proposes a LiDAR scan-based obstacle feature extraction method.

The cost of the path associated between two key points in the overall path of the AGV is updated considering significant factors such as the distance between KPs, the time taken to travel between KPs, the frequency of dynamic changes between KPs and the preferred path. Trajectory tracking is accomplished by implementing a Model Predictive Controller (MPC). Once the AGV’s initialization is done and the optimal path has been identified, the AGV starts its motion accordingly based on the control input.

Obstacle detection based on LiDAR data received by the AGV is accomplished similarly to the work implemented in27. The AGV classifies the identified obstacle as either static or dynamic based on the laser scan time history. If the identified obstacle is dynamic such as moving humans, other dynamic vehicular movements etc., the AGV stops its motion until the obstacle completely moves away from a certain threshold range. If a static obstacle is identified, the AGV uses the TEB planner to find a path to ensure that the AGV can operate within a virtual boundary. If no such path is found, the algorithms mark any path that goes outside the virtual boundary as a no-valid-path, sets the cost to the maximum, navigates the AGV to the previously reached KP and starts calculating a new optimal path from that KP. Using this approach, the AGV can easily decide to react in an intelligent manner to an obstacle instead of calculating a new path at all times and also control the AGV’s path selection instead of planning the path in any free available space. The proposed path planning approach navigating from KP to KP also addresses the problem of accumulation of errors in the localization estimate by correcting the accumulated errors periodically upon reaching each key point.

Dynamic programming

Dynamic programming (DP) provides a recursive approach to compute the optimal value function and policy for a Markov Decision Process (MDP). In this work, DP is employed to evaluate the cost-to-go between key points in the shop-floor graph and to derive the optimal high-level navigation policy for the AGV. DP relies on Bellman equations for value propagation and iterative optimization, as summarized below.

Reinforcement learning–driven adaptive cost update

Although DP provides the basis for global path evaluation, the proposed framework extends it into a reinforcement learning (RL) formulation by treating the shop-floor key-point (KP) graph as a finite Markov Decision Process (MDP). Each KP is modeled as a state , transitions correspond to actions , and the nominal edge cost is mapped to a reward .

The contribution of this work lies in online reward adaptation rather than relying on a static, predetermined cost map. Transition rewards are dynamically updated using real-time perception signals, including: (i) static or dynamic obstacle classification from the LiDAR module, (ii) obstacle motion direction and persistence, (iii) corridor congestion and repeated blocking, and (iv) backtracking or near-conflict events. These adjustments act as reward-shaping signals, enabling the AGV to update its long-horizon decisions based on environmental feedback. which allows the AGV to refine its policy whenever new information is observed. This transforms the classical DP solver into an online, tabular RL agent capable of adapting to dynamic changes in the environment. This module outputs an updated key-point sequence, while the TEB planner computes the continuous, dynamically feasible trajectory between successive KPs. This results in a hierarchical navigation architecture, where the RL layer governs global decision-making and TEB handles local motion optimization. This hierarchical formulation differentiates the proposed method from traditional DP and TEB approaches by providing adaptive, feedback-driven policy refinement rather than fixed-cost planning.

LiDAR-based obstacle feature extraction

The DDTMR deploys the DP approach to compute an optimal path based on cost. Key points (KPs), along with the cost associated with it, are generated between the start and the end point thereby creating a roadmap. Once the roadmap of the DDTMR is updated, the KPs of the path are fed to the system as the intermediate goal points for its travel. Upon reaching each intermediate KP, the system records the same in its memory. The stored memory about the KP status will be completely cleared once the trip is completed. The DDTMR starts its travel by acquiring the sequential intermediate KPs as its goal points. At any point in time, the cost for DDTMR opting a path can be modified for any usual happenings in manufacturing lines, such as maintenance activity happening in a particular area, any accident or emergency in a particular location of the manufacturing map so that it can be ensured that the DDTMR does not pass through any of the above-mentioned areas. The DDTMR is equipped with a LiDAR sensor at its front. The primary function of the LiDAR sensor is to provide the scan data which is used to identify and analyze the nature of the obstacle present in the DDTMR’s path and to update the map of its working area continuously.

Timed elastic band path planner

The Timed Elastic band (TEB) path planner algorithm generates smooth, collision-free paths for autonomous robots or vehicles to follow40,41. It considers the time the robot takes to follow the path, allowing the planner to stretch or compress the path as needed to avoid collisions or meet other constraints. The planner outputs the final, optimized path for the robot to follow, along with instructions on how the robot should execute the path (e.g., speeds, accelerations, etc.) to stay on track and avoid collisions. While the TEB planner provides a dynamically feasible and collision-free local trajectory, it does not by itself determine the long-horizon sequence of regions or corridors the AGV should traverse. Industrial shop floors contain strict topological constraints (aisles, intersections, one-way routes), and the global planner must ensure that the AGV always moves within these allowable regions. For this reason, TEB is integrated with a DP–based global planner that selects the optimal key-point sequence, while TEB refines each segment into a smooth, kinematically feasible trajectory. This hierarchical DP + TEB combination is preferred over end-to-end learning or purely classical methods because it achieves a superior balance of safety, interpretability, dynamic feasibility, and real-time performance in structured industrial environments. In this work, a modification is made in the traditional cost function to include an obstacle avoidance parameter in the DDTMR path generation. When a static obstacle is found in super-critical regions, the DDTMR switches to this modified TEB path planner. The planner first receives input about the DDTMR’s current position and orientation, the desired position and orientation and also receives information about any obstacles or other constraints the DDTMR must avoid or navigate around. The planner then generates a set of possible paths that the robot could follow to reach the end position, considering the DDTMR’s motion constraints and the environmental constraints imposed by obstacles, no-go zones, etc. The planner evaluates each possible path to determine which of these is the best in terms of smoothness, efficiency, and collision-free operation. It uses a cost function to assess the quality of each path and selects the one with the lowest cost. The general form of the cost function used by this modified Timed Elastic Band (TEB) planner is shown in Eq. (41).

In Eq. 41, the terms on the right-hand side represent different factors that the planner considers when generating a trajectory, including distance to goal, smoothness of planned path, local obstacle avoidance, velocity and acceleration of the DDTMR. , , , , and are the weights that control the relative importance of each factor in the cost function. If distance to goal is the main concern for selecting a path, weight will be higher than the other weights. Similarly other weight factors can be chosen to emphasis the importance of the other factors suitably. The obstacle avoidance term in Eq. 41 is a weighting factor that represents the importance of avoiding collision with obstacle relative to other factors in the DDTMR’s path planning. This term is computed based on the obstacle information obtained from the sensor where the distance to the nearby objects is used for its computation. By minimizing the cost function, the modified TEB planner generates a feasible and optimal trajectory for the robot to follow while considering the various factors specified by the cost function.

The planner then adjusts the timing of the selected path as needed to ensure that the robot can follow it safely and smoothly, which may involve stretching or compressing the path in time to avoid collisions or to meet other constraints. Once the DDTMR detects a static obstacle in its critical region, the planner evaluates its current surroundings and provides an optimal path, the DDTMR checks whether it can avoid the obstacle and navigate in the same direction it was moving in earlier or head back to the previous KP and replan its path again. For this purpose, the waypoints of the path generated by the TEB planner are accessed and compared with coordinates of the current position and orientation of the DDTMR to determine whether the plan will move the DDTMR in the same direction it was travelling earlier. If the planner finds a suitable path within the permissible region, the DDTMR adopts it, avoids that obstacle and follows the generated path. Suppose the path formed by the planner takes the DDTMR in the other direction to overcome the obstacle present or the path generated by the planner lies outside the permissible region, the DDTMR registers that there is no feasible path to overcome the current obstacle, and the cost of that particular path is updated with a very high value so that other DDTMRs will not adopt this path. The DDTMR makes a 180-degree turn after updating cost and provides the TEB planner target position as the latest successful key point. Upon reaching the latest successful key point, DDTMR initializes the dynamic programming approach to calculate a least-cost path and navigate accordingly. The notification of the cost updating can be passed to the centralized network by which the blocking is informed. Once the block is cleared and the cost is updated, DDTMRs may opt for that path if needed.

AprilTag-based trailer localization

AprilTag-based localization uses fiducial markers consisting of black and white squares with unique binary patterns to determine the pose of a camera relative to a known pattern or tag through computer vision algorithms. In this work, AprilTags are used to keep track of the trailer’s position with respect to the DDTMR to which it is attached. To ensure that a trailer travels safely within a designated area and avoids collisions with obstacles, its trajectory is monitored and analyzed continuously. A simulation camera sensor is mounted on the rear end of the DDTMR facing the trailer. Two AprilTags are placed on the front side of the trailer body towards the DDTMR as shown in Fig. 6a. The raw image captured by the camera is processed using a computer vision algorithm to estimate the relative pose between the DDTMR and the trailer. Transformations Tct1 and Tct2 shown in Fig. 6b represent the pose of tags 1 and 2 relative to the camera module and transformations Tt1b and Tt2b represent the transformations between the tags and the trailer body center of mass Cmn. With use of two AprilTags, it is ensured that the system can detect the trailer’s location even during larger turns since one of the tags remains visible in the camera’s range.

Computational complexity analysis

To evaluate computational efficiency, the time and space complexity of the proposed planner was analyzed and compared against state-of-the-art algorithms. For the global DP module, path computation between key points requires constructing a multistage graph with edges. Dynamic programming with cost updates therefore operates in time and space, where is the number of key points. The RL component operates at the local decision level and updates Q-values with each state–action transition, which is per step, and thus negligible compared to the DP complexity. In contrast, RRT* exhibits an expected time complexity of for graph expansion with nnn sampled nodes, but requires repeated sampling to converge toward an optimal solution, leading to high variance in planning time. DWA evaluates mmm discrete velocity samples at each control cycle, yielding complexity per step, but scaling poorly as mmm increases for high-resolution velocity spaces.

Conclusion

The present study proposes an intelligent path planning strategy for an autonomous DDTMR in a flexible manufacturing system which is validated through simulations and experiments. The main goal of the suggested path planning approach is to decrease the computational complexity of the industrial-grade material handling AGVs in choosing a nearly optimal path with limited onboard sensors. A nearly realistic manufacturing plant environment, complete with static and dynamic obstacles, was created using CoppeliaSim models. The proposed path planning strategy was built in Python and integrated with ROS. The comparison study was performed considering the standard path planning strategies such as A*, Dijkstra and TEB. From the simulation results, it is found that the A* path planner sometimes get stuck in local minima, resulting in suboptimal paths. Using the Dijkstra algorithm, the path search becomes computationally expensive and also creates knots in the path, especially when dealing with dynamic environments where the graph needs to be updated frequently. The TEB planner provides smoother paths however there is no guarantee that it will stay within the virtual boundary. Under these circumstances, implementation of the proposed strategy guarantees both smoothness and operation within the boundaries. The results demonstrate that the DDTMR follows the reference path more closely than other path-planning strategies. It also indicates that the DDTMR follows the reference trajectory in almost all conditions, by ensuring the DDTMR’s safety by avoiding collisions with obstacles and preventing entry into non-permissible areas. Obstacle characteristics are determined robustly and its impact on movement behaviour was tested. The results indicate that appropriate velocity factors are generated for such movements thereby enabling the DDTMR to perform the task assigned. Currently, AGVs in industrial settings rely on multiple sensors, either individual or fused, to detect obstacles and to overcome them, which increases their cost of purchase and maintenance. However, the proposed method provides a consistent, reliable, and low computational effort solution for the DDTMR to reach its goal regardless of the nature of the obstacles, with only a LiDAR sensor onboard. The proposed path planning strategy also ensures a tighter control of the DDTMR movement with respect to the plan generated by it. It is found that the trailer in these particular scenarios stays within the bounds prescribed for the vehicle to operate. However more studies with different DDTMR speeds are necessary to conclude on the limits up to which the trailer will still satisfy the virtual boundary constraint. One future direction for this work is to extend the proposed path planning strategy to a swarm of DDTMRs, assigning priority-based paths. Path selection process can be further enhanced using information available from multiple DDTMRs in the working area with information available from each one of them being used for updating the map in a centralized cloud-based storage. Although the experimental validation in this work focuses on an AGV–trailer configuration, the proposed DP–RL–TEB hierarchy is kinematics-agnostic at the planning level and can be applied to heterogeneous robot fleets. Only the local TEB parameters require platform-specific tuning, while the global DP planner, RL-based cost adaptation, and coordination mechanisms remain unchanged.
