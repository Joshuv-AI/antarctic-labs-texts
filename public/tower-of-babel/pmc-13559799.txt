Prolonged fever in ESBL-producing Salmonella Typhi in Lebanon: Diagnostic challenges and the possible role of occult low-level carbapenemase activity
Authors: Soha Fakherddine, Rana Ibrahim, Ali Houmani, Hassan Akouch, Abbas Fadel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Salmonella Typhi remains a global health concern, particularly in travelers returning from endemic regions. We report a 32-year-old male with persistent fever after travel to Iraq. Initial investigations were inconclusive, but cultures later identified ESBL-producing S. Typhi resistant to ceftriaxone, fluoroquinolones, and TMP-SMX, but susceptible to meropenem. Fever persisted despite meropenem and resolved only after azithromycin was added. Standard testing showed no carbapenem resistance, suggesting possible undetected low-level carbapenemase activity. This case highlights the need for heightened clinical suspicion, use of advanced diagnostics, and consideration of combination therapy in prolonged typhoid fever, especially in returning travelers from high-resistance regions.

Introduction

Typhoid fever is a systemic infectious disease caused predominantly by Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi (S. Typhi) and, less frequently, by Salmonella enterica serovars Paratyphi A, B, and C (S. Paratyphi A, B, and C) [1]. It remains an important public health challenge in many low- and middle-income countries and continues to represent a significant cause of prolonged febrile illness among travelers returning from endemic regions [2,3]. Patients commonly present with prolonged fever, headache, anorexia, abdominal discomfort, and nonspecific constitutional symptoms [3]. Because these clinical manifestations overlap with numerous infectious, inflammatory, and neoplastic disorders, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is frequently performed during the evaluation of fever of unknown origin or suspected intra-abdominal pathology. CT plays an important complementary role by identifying bowel involvement, narrowing the differential diagnosis, and detecting complications such as perforation, abscess formation, and lymphadenopathy [[4], [5], [6]].

The radiological findings of typhoid fever are not pathognomonic. Segmental mural thickening involving the terminal ileum and cecum, homogeneous mural enhancement, mild mesenteric lymphadenopathy, and splenomegaly may also be encountered in Crohn disease, intestinal tuberculosis, lymphoma, adenocarcinoma, Yersinia enterocolitis, and other infectious ileocolitides [[4], [5], [6]]. Therefore, imaging findings should always be interpreted together with the clinical history, travel exposure, laboratory investigations, and microbiological confirmation [3,[4], [5], [6]].

Without proper treatment, typhoid fever can be life-threatening. While current mortality rates are estimated to be below 1%, they ranged from 10% to 20% before the advent of antibiotic therapy [7]. Antibiotics have long been the cornerstone of typhoid fever treatment. However, multidrug resistance (MDR), characterized by resistance to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and co-trimoxazole, initially emerged in the 1970s. Since then, resistance to fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins, and azithromycin has steadily increased [8]. Extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Salmonella Typhi was first identified in Pakistan in 2016 and is characterized by resistance to first-line antibiotics, fluoroquinolones, and third-generation cephalosporins [9].

In this report, we describe a patient with ESBL-producing Salmonella Typhi presenting with prolonged fever in whom abdominal CT demonstrated nonspecific ileocecal wall thickening and mesenteric lymphadenopathy. The case emphasizes the complementary role of imaging in directing further diagnostic investigations, excluding alternative diagnoses and complications, and illustrates the radiologic differential diagnosis of ileocecal disease. Although persistent fever despite meropenem therapy prompted consideration of occult resistance mechanisms, low-level carbapenemase activity remains a hypothesis because molecular confirmation was not available.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old Lebanese man with a history of appendectomy and a giant cell tumor of the left knee treated with multiple surgeries, the most recent being a left total knee replacement six years earlier, as well as previous radiotherapy, presented to our hospital with a 14-day history of intermittent low-grade fever associated with fatigue, cough, lethargy, and anorexia. Over the three days preceding admission, the fever progressively increased, reaching 39.5°C. The patient had returned from Iraq two weeks before presentation.

On admission, the patient was conscious and cooperative and complained of fever and headache. Vital signs revealed a temperature of 38.3°C, heart rate of 93 beats/min, blood pressure of 120/70 mmHg, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Lung auscultation demonstrated good bilateral air entry without focal abnormalities. There were no gastrointestinal or urinary symptoms. Examination of the left knee revealed no swelling, erythema, tenderness, or limitation of movement. The patient denied recent consumption of unpasteurized dairy products or contact with ill individuals.

Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated normal white blood cell and platelet counts, with an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration of 2.5 mg/dL. Influenza and COVID-19 PCR assays were negative, as were Brucella and Widal serological tests. Chest radiography was unremarkable. Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest demonstrated two small subpleural nodules (7 mm and 4 mm) within the left lower lobe without focal consolidation, pleural effusion, or mediastinal abscess. Multiple small mediastinal lymph nodes without pathological enlargement and mild splenomegaly (13.1 cm) were also identified. Two sets of blood cultures and a urine culture were obtained before initiation of empirical ceftriaxone (2 g/d). Procalcitonin measured on hospital day 2 was negative. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the left knee demonstrated an intact prosthesis without joint effusion, marrow edema, peri-prosthetic fluid collection, or imaging evidence of osteomyelitis.

On hospital day 3, the patient developed recurrent low-grade fever, prompting repeat blood cultures. Laboratory investigations continued to show a normal complete blood count, while CRP increased to 12.5 mg/dL. Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated segmental circumferential mural thickening involving the ileocecal region, measuring approximately 4.7 × 3.7 cm, with homogeneous mural enhancement and adjacent mildly enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes, findings suggestive of an inflammatory or infectious ileocecal process (Fig. 1). No bowel obstruction, free intraperitoneal air, drainable abscess, or significant free fluid was identified. Given the nonspecific imaging appearance, the principal radiological differential diagnoses included infectious ileocolitis, Crohn disease, intestinal tuberculosis, lymphoma, and cecal neoplasm. Because of persistent fever and the abnormal abdominal imaging findings, antimicrobial therapy was escalated to piperacillin–tazobactam on hospital day 7. On hospital day 11, blood cultures confirmed ESBL-producing Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, resistant to ceftriaxone, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and levofloxacin but susceptible to carbapenems. Despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy and progressive laboratory improvement, including declining CRP levels and normalization of leukopenia and thrombocytopenia by hospital day 16, the patient remained persistently febrile. HIV serology was negative. Repeat contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated no interval progression of the ileocecal inflammatory changes and no evidence of bowel perforation, abscess formation, or other intra-abdominal complications.

On hospital day 19, the patient developed severe headache. Non-contrast CT of the brain demonstrated no acute intracranial abnormality, mass lesion, hemorrhage, or imaging evidence of cerebral abscess. Azithromycin (1 g/d) was added to meropenem therapy. Following initiation of combination therapy, the patient became afebrile, and repeat blood cultures confirmed microbiological clearance (Fig. 3). Histopathological examination of the colonoscopic biopsy specimens demonstrated chronic lymphocytic inflammatory changes without evidence of malignancy. Additional immunohistochemical analysis excluded lymphoma. The patient was discharged on hospital day 27 in good clinical condition, with complete resolution of fever, normalization of inflammatory markers and complete blood count, and no evidence of treatment-related complications. At follow-up 10 days after discharge, he remained asymptomatic with normal laboratory findings.

Discussion

Typhoid fever continues to be prevalent in many low- and middle-income countries because of inadequate sanitation, unsafe drinking water, and poor food hygiene [3]. In 2017, the global case fatality rate was estimated at approximately 0.95%, with higher mortality reported among children, elderly individuals, and populations living in resource-limited settings [2]. The burden of disease remains particularly high throughout Asia, including Pakistan, India, Indonesia, Vietnam, China, and Iraq, where increasing antimicrobial resistance has complicated disease management [7,8,[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]].

In Lebanon, typhoid fever remains a notifiable disease. Between 2000 and 2008, the Ministry of Public Health reported more than 6000 cases, with an average of approximately 600 cases annually [[16], [19]]. Although previous Lebanese studies demonstrated relatively low resistance rates to third-generation cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones [20], increasing regional travel and dissemination of MDR and XDR Salmonella Typhi strains have substantially increased the likelihood of encountering resistant infections.

Radiological findings and differential diagnosis

The principal educational value of this case lies in the radiological evaluation of nonspecific ileocecal abnormalities in a patient presenting with prolonged fever. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated segmental circumferential mural thickening involving the ileocecal region associated with mild mesenteric lymphadenopathy. Although these findings are not specific for typhoid fever, they significantly narrowed the differential diagnosis and guided further investigations, ultimately leading to colonoscopy and microbiological confirmation.

Segmental ileocecal wall thickening is a common imaging finding with a broad differential diagnosis. Inflammatory bowel disease, particularly Crohn disease, typically demonstrates asymmetric mural thickening, skip lesions, mesenteric fat proliferation, and fistulous complications. Intestinal tuberculosis frequently involves the ileocecal region but is more commonly associated with necrotic lymph nodes, ascites, and peritoneal involvement. Primary lymphoma generally presents as marked circumferential bowel wall thickening with bulky lymphadenopathy while preserving the bowel lumen, whereas adenocarcinoma usually manifests as focal irregular asymmetric thickening producing luminal narrowing. Infectious ileocolitis, including typhoid fever, more commonly demonstrates relatively symmetric mural thickening with reactive mesenteric lymphadenopathy and inflammatory changes, although substantial overlap exists among these entities [[4], [5], [6]].

In the present case, CT findings alone were insufficient to establish the diagnosis. However, the patient's recent travel history, persistent fever, CT evidence of ileocecal inflammation, and subsequent colonoscopic findings appropriately directed clinicians toward enteric fever, which was ultimately confirmed by blood culture. This case therefore emphasizes that CT should be considered a complementary diagnostic tool capable of narrowing the differential diagnosis and excluding complications rather than providing definitive diagnosis.

Several reports have documented the emergence of MDR and XDR Salmonella Typhi in Pakistan, India, Iraq, the United States, and other countries [7,8,[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. The present isolate demonstrated resistance to ceftriaxone, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and fluoroquinolones while remaining susceptible to carbapenems.

Despite microbiological susceptibility to meropenem, the patient remained febrile for several days before clinical improvement occurred following the addition of azithromycin. Delayed defervescence has previously been described in patients with MDR and XDR typhoid fever and does not necessarily indicate microbiological treatment failure [21,22].

One possible explanation for the prolonged clinical response is the presence of low-level carbapenemase production that escaped routine phenotypic detection. However, because molecular testing for carbapenemase genes (including blaKPC, blaNDM, and blaOXA-48) was not performed, this hypothesis cannot be confirmed and should be interpreted cautiously. Alternative explanations include delayed intracellular bacterial clearance, host immune response, or the natural course of severe enteric fever despite microbiologically appropriate therapy [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]].

Previous studies have suggested that combination therapy may benefit selected patients with MDR or XDR typhoid fever. Ghodbole et al. recommended combining carbapenems with an intracellularly active antimicrobial agent in severely ill patients [28]. Similarly, several published case reports described successful treatment using carbapenem–azithromycin combinations [28,29]. Conversely, Qureshi et al. demonstrated no significant difference in fever clearance time between meropenem monotherapy and combination therapy [29]. Therefore, the clinical benefit observed after azithromycin initiation in our patient should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of superiority but rather as an observation within a single case report.

Although newer agents such as tigecycline and fosfomycin have demonstrated promising in vitro activity against resistant Salmonella Typhi isolates [30,31], carbapenems remain among the most reliable therapeutic options for severe ESBL-producing or XDR infections. Appropriate antimicrobial stewardship remains essential to preserve their effectiveness.

Finally, increasing implementation of typhoid conjugate vaccines has been predicted to substantially reduce the burden of antimicrobial-resistant typhoid worldwide [32]. Continued surveillance, antimicrobial stewardship, and improved laboratory diagnostic capacity remain essential to limit dissemination of resistant Salmonella Typhi strains.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic complexity of MDR/XDR- typhoid fever, particularly in patients with persistent fever despite appropriate antimicrobial treatment. The potential role of undetected low-level carbapenemase activity warrants further investigation and emphasizes the importance of incorporating advanced molecular diagnostics into routine clinical practice. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in returning travelers from endemic regions and consider empirical carbapenem therapy when clinically indicated. In selected cases, combination therapy and prolonged treatment durations may be necessary. Strengthening local laboratory capacity, antimicrobial stewardship, and surveillance systems is essential to effectively manage and contain MDR infections. Moreover, further clinical trials are needed to evaluate the efficacy of combination therapy versus monotherapy to inform future treatment guidelines.
