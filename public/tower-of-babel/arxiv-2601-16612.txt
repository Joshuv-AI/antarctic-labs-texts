Abstract
The most common instruments currently measuring active/reactive energy and power quality indicators are smart energy meters. Unfortunately, the verification of such meters is currently performed under ideal conditions or with simple signal models, which do not recreate actual states occurring in the power grid and do not ensure the verification of the properties of their signal chains. This paper presents challenges in the proper metrological verification of smart energy meters. It presents existing legal and normative requirements and scientific research directions regarding these meters. Selected test results are presented, which show that although the tested meters meet the normative and legal requirements because they have been approved for sale, numerous imperfections in the signal and measurement chains of the analyzed instruments are revealed for the selected test signal. On the basis of the presented research results, further directions of research in the field of smart energy meters have been determined.



I
Introduction

Energy meters are among the most widely used meters. Currently designed and implemented meters are analog-digital electronic devices with advanced capabilities, classified as advanced metering infrastructure (AMI) systems or smart energy meters. The AMI energy meters directly measure voltages and currents. These data are used to determine and record energy values and other quantities, such as active and reactive power, root-mean-square (rms) values of voltage and current, frequency, total harmonic distortion (THD), selected harmonics, and short- and long-term flicker indicators. AMI energy meters constitute a distributed source of information in the power grid, enabling, among other things, the acquisition of information on energy flows and power quality (PQ) at customer supply points. Knowledge of the status of the power grid is particularly important in the presence of distributed energy sources, and AMI energy meters are, in practice, indispensable diagnostic tools. This discontinuity results from the large number of measurements and the need to conduct them at the supplier-customer connection. It is difficult to imagine performing measurements with a similar number of power quality analyzers. Furthermore, some customers’ power lines are designed in such a way that it is impossible to perform measurements at the supplier-customer connection. The widespread use of such meters and competition among manufacturers force the drive for low prices. The complexity of AMI energy meters (software and hardware) and the drive for low prices are somewhat contradictory. It is especially important to remember that any AMI energy meter performance imperfections can occur in a very large number of cases. Due to the dispersion of meters, detecting malfunctions is difficult. This requires exceptional care when designing and performing comprehensive smart energy meter tests. This paper presents laboratory selected test results for smart energy meters from various manufacturers, which demonstrate significant discrepancies in the measurements of signals representing states occurring in the actual power grid. The test results demonstrate the need to develop a new verification procedure for smart energy meters, whose implementation of which will increase the reliability of measurements performed by such meters.

Given the limited number of pages in this paper, the results were limited to assessing the compliance of selected parameters that define the quality of the power from smart energy meters. On the one hand, a simple test signal allows for the analytical calculation of reference indicators, and based on the known properties of the source used, it is possible to determine the uncertainty budget of the set value, which is particularly important from a metrological perspective. On the other hand, the readings of advanced equipment, such as a class A analyzer with a specific inaccuracy, can be used as a reference value and compared with the readings obtained for smart energy meters. If the confidence intervals for the unknown measured quantities overlap, it can be assumed that the readings of the tested smart energy meters are correct. The presented results show that in practice there are discrepancies in the readings that significantly exceed the permissible limit values of selected parameters in the power network, while the analyzed test signal for the selected

IV
Conclusion

Currently, smart energy meters (AMI energy meters) are among the most commonly used active/reactive energy meters and power quality indicators. Due to their widespread use, they are much less expensive than advanced measurement instruments, such as class A power quality analyzers. As a result, smart energy meters can exhibit significant imperfections in their measurement and signal chains. The paper points out that currently marketed smart energy meters are tested under ideal conditions or with simple test signals, which do not recreate the actual states occurring in modern power grids or allow for the effective identification of imperfections in their measurement and signal chains. It is worth noting that the growing share of renewable energy sources and electrification in modern power grids often leads to the simultaneous occurrence of multiple disturbances of power quality. Despite these conditions, measuring devices are expected to provide reliable measurements. This paper presents results for an example test signal. Although it does not perfectly recreate the actual conditions that occur in modern power grids, it allows for the identification of imperfections in the measurement and signal chains of the smart energy meters, which may occur under real-world operating conditions with the simultaneous occurrence of multiple disturbances in power quality disturbances. Due to its simple functional description, the selected test signal can be used for metrological verification of the measurement results. The presented research results demonstrate the necessity and challenges in finding the smallest possible number of test signals that will enable accurate metrological verification of AMI smart energy meters, and thus increase the reliability of these meters’ measurement results in real-world conditions.
