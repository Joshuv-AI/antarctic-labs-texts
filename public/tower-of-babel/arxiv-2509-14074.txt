Generalizations of the Crossing Lemma
Géza Tóth HUN-REN Alfréd Rényi Institute of Mathematics, Budapest, Hungary Budapest University of Technology and Economics, SZIT, Budapest, Hungary geza@renyi.hu

Abstract
The crossing number of a graph is the minimum number of crossings over all of its drawings on the plane. The Crossing Lemma, proved more than 40 years ago, is a tight lower bound on the crossing number of a graph in terms of the number of vertices and edges. It is definitely the most important inequality on crossing numbers. We review some generalizations and applications of the Crossing Lemma.



0.1
Introduction

The
crossing number

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)
of
G
is the minimum number of edge
crossings over all drawings of
G
on the plane.
It has a fascinating history and lots of applications in theory and practice.
It started with Turán’s Brick Factory problem in 1944, also known
as Zarankiewicz’s problem
Guy 69
.
Determining or even estimating the crossing number is a notoriously difficult problem
GJ 83
;
Sch 18
,
and it has a huge number of practical and theoretical applications
Szé 97
;
DLM 19
in
VLSI design, geographic information systems, graph drawing, computer graphics,
and many other fields.
Even the crossing numbers of complete graphs
are not known, despite the huge number of results, attempts, improvements

BLS 19
;
HH 62
;
ACFM+ 12
;
ADFM+ 20
.
The Crossing Lemma of Ajtai, Chvátal, Newborn, Szemerédi and independently
Leighton, 40 years ago was a real breakthrough.
It is a general lower bound on the crossing number,
it states that any simple graph with
n
vertices and
e\geq 4n
edges has crossing number
at least
ce^{3}/n^{2}
for some
c>0
.

Székely
Szé 97
and then others observed that crossing numbers, in particular the Crossing Lemma,
also has important
applications in the theory of incidences
ST 01
, additive number theory
Ele 97
,
in the analysis of geometric algorithms
Dey 98
and many other fields.

In this paper we survey some results related to the Crossing Lemma and its generalizations.
It Section 2 we review the history and improvements of the Crossing Lemma.
It is easy to see that
it does not hold for multigraphs in general, not even in any weaker form.
In Section 3
we investigate its generalizations for multigraphs under different conditions on the drawing.
In Section 4 we try to figure out, how far can we go with such a generalization, that is, what could be the weakest condition which implies some Crossing Lemma type statement.
Finally, in Section 5 we introduce some other versions of the crossing number, the pair-crossing number and the odd-crossing number, their relationships, and generalizations of the Crossing Lemma for these crossing numbers.

0.2
The Crossing Lemma

In a
drawing
of a graph (or multigraph)
G
, vertices are represented by points and edges
are represented by curves connecting the corresponding points.
If it does not lead to confusion, the points (curves), representing the vertices (edges) are also called vertices (edges).
We assume that in a drawing an edge cannot contain a vertex in its interior, if two edges have a common point, then it is a common endpoint or a proper crossing, and three edges do not cross at the same point.
For any graph
G
,
n(G)
and
e(G)
denote its number of vertices and edges, respectively.
A graph (resp. multigraph), together with its drawing, is called a
topological

graph (resp. multigraph).

The most important, fundamental result on crossing numbers is the Crossing Lemma,
first proved by Ajtai, Chvátal, Newborn, Szemerédi, and
Leighton,
40
years ago

ACNS 82
;
Lei 83
.

Theorem 0.2.1
(Crossing Lemma)

For any simple graph
G
, if

e(G)\geq 4n(G)
then

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq\frac{1}{64}\frac{e(G)^{3}}{n(G)^{2}}.

The original proofs were quite complicated and gave a much worse constant in the bound.
The following beautiful proof is attributed
to Chazelle, Sharir, and Welzl and appeared in
”Proofs from THE BOOK”
AZ 99
.
For the proof we need the following easy but very important statement (see e.g.
PT00b
).

Observation 1

For any simple graph
G
, in a drawing with exactly
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)
crossings,
(i) no edge crosses itself, (ii)
two edges have at most one common point.

Proof

(i) Suppose that an edge
\alpha
crosses itself at point
p
.
Reverse the orientation of the part of
\alpha
between the two occurrences of
p
. Then
p
becomes a self-touching point. And now we can get rid of the touching by a slight perturbation at
p
.
This way e get a drawing
with fewer crossings, which is a contradiction.
See Figure
1
.

(ii) Suppose that two edges
\alpha
and
\beta
have two common points,
p
and
q
.
One of them can be a crossing or a common endpoint, the other one is a crossing.
Then swap the parts of the edges between
p
and
q
. Again, we get a drawing
with fewer crossings, which is a contradiction. See Figure
1

Reducing the number of crossings.

Proof
(Proof of the Crossing Lemma)

The proof goes in two steps.

1. First we show that for any graph
G

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq e(G)-3n(G).

(1)

This can be proved by induction on
e(G)
. If
e(G)\leq 3n(G)
, then the statement holds trivially.
Suppose that
e(G)>3n(G)
and we have already proved the statement for smaller values of
e(G)
.
It is well-known consequence of Euler’s formula that a planar graph of
n
vertices has
at most
3n-6
edges.
Therefore,
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)>0
. Remove an edge
\alpha
from
G
which contains a crossing.
Then
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G-\alpha)+1
and by the induction hypothesis,

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G-\alpha)\geq e(G)-1-3n(G)
. Combining them, the statement follows.

2.
Consider a drawing of
G
with
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)
crossings. Take a random induced subgraph

G^{\prime}
of
G
by selecting each vertex independently with probability
p
(whose value is determined later).
We obtain a random subset of vertices
U\subset V(G)
and let
G^{\prime}
be the
subgraph induced by
U
.
Draw the edges of

G^{\prime}
as they were drawn in the original drawing.
The number of vertices and edges of
G^{\prime}
,
n(G^{\prime})
and
e(G^{\prime})
are random variables, and

E[n(G^{\prime})]=pn(G)
,

E[e(G^{\prime})]=p^{2}e(G)
since each edge of
G
survives with probability
p^{2}
.
Let
X(G^{\prime})
be the number of crossings of
G^{\prime}
in the obtained drawing.
Then clearly
X(G^{\prime})\geq{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G^{\prime})
. By Observation
1
, only independent edges can cross in the original optimal drawing of
G
, consequently, also in
G^{\prime}
.
A crossing is present in the drawing of
G^{\prime}
if all four corresponding endpoints are selected and the probability of this is
p^{4}
. (A crossing of two edges with a common endpoint would be present with probability
p^{3}
.)
Therefore,
E[X(G^{\prime})]=p^{4}{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)
. By
1
,
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G^{\prime})\geq e(G^{\prime})-3n(G^{\prime})
. Taking expectations, we get

p^{4}{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)=E[X(G^{\prime})]\geq E[{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G^{\prime})]\geq E[e(G^{\prime})]-3E[n(G^{\prime})]=p^{2}e(G)-3pn(G)

so
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq e(G)/p^{2}-3n(G)/p^{3}
.
Set
p=4n(G)/e(G)
, by assumption
p\leq 1
.
Substitute in the last formula and we get

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq\frac{1}{64}e(G)^{3}/n(G)^{2}.

This bound cannot be improved apart
from the value of the constant.
That is, there is a constant
c
with the following property.
For any
n
,
e
,
e\leq\binom{n}{2}
,
there is a graph
G
with
n(G)=n
,
e(G)=e
and
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\leq ce^{3}/n^{2}
.
The easiest example is — very roughly — the following.
Take the disjoint union of
n^{2}/2e
complete graphs, each of
2e/n
vertices.
This graph has approximately
n
vertices,
e
edges. Each component can be drawn such that any two edges cross at most once, and components can be separated from each other.
Hence
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\leq(n^{2}/2e)((2e/n)^{2}/2)^{2}/2=e^{3}/n^{2}
.

There is a better example by Pach and Tóth
PT 97
. Suppose that
n\gg e\gg n^{2}
, that is,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}e/n=\infty
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{2}/e=\infty
.
We define a graph
G
of
n
vertices and
e
edges, together with its drawing.
Let

V(G)
be a set of
n
points arranged in
a slightly perturbed unit square grid
of size
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
, so that the points
are in general position.
Let
r=\sqrt{2e/\pi n}
, so that
r^{2}\pi=2e/n
.
Connect two points by a straight-line segment if and only if
their distance is at most
r
. Then,
n(G)=n
, the degrees of almost all vertices are close to
r^{2}\pi
, so

e(G)\approx nr^{2}\pi/2=e
. One can approximate the number of crossings, which is an upper bound for the crossing number,
by an integral.
It turns out that

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\leq\left(8/9\pi^{2}+o(1)\right)e^{3}/n^{2}<\left(0.09006+o(1)\right)e^{3}/n^{2}.

(2)

In fact, the calculation in
PT 97
includes a quadruple integral and there was an error in it, which was corrected in

PRTT 06
. Recently, Czabarka et al.
CSSW 20
found essentially the same bound with a much simpler and more general construction.
Take
n
random points independently on the sphere. Connect a pair of points by the geodesic (the shortest connecting curve, which is an arc on a great circle)
if it has length at most
r
. If we set the value of
r
properly, the expected number of edges is
e
and the expected number of crossings is
(8/9\pi^{2}+o(1))e^{3}/n^{2}
. It is not hard to argue that there is an instance
where both the number of edges and crossings are close to their expected values.

From the other direction there were several improvements. In general, for a better constant we have to pay with
a stronger condition in place of
e\geq 4n
.
First, in the proof above we can set
p=4.5n/e
but then we have to assume that
e\geq 4.5n
.
We obtain the following statement:

\mbox{if }\ e(G)\geq 4.5n(G)\ \mbox{then }\ {\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq\frac{1}{60.75}\frac{e(G)^{3}}{n(G)^{2}}.

The next improvement was in
PT 97
.
The main idea is that equation (
1
) can be replaced by a stronger one. In equation

we only use that once a graph has more than

edges, then there is an edge with a crossing on it.
But if the number of edges is a little bit more, then there will be an edge with
two

For any

, a graph is called

Let

denote the maximum number of edges of a

-planar graph of

vertices. Clearly,

m_{0}(n)=3n-6
.
It was shown in

that

m_{1}(n)=4n-8
for

and

m_{2}(n)\leq 5n-10
which is tight for infinitely

n
,

m_{3}(n)\leq 6n-12
and
m_{4}(n)\leq 7n-14
.

Therefore, for any graph

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq(e(G)-3n(G))+(e(G)-4n(G))+(e(G)-5n(G))

+(e(G)-6n(G))+(e(G)-7n(G))=5e(G)-25n(G).

By Observation
1
, all crossings are between independent edges.
Using this inequality in the probabilistic argument in the proof of the Crossing Lemma we get the following.

Theorem 0.2.2
(
PRTT 06
)

For any simple graph

, if

e(G)\geq 7.5n(G)

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq\frac{1}{33.75}\frac{e(G)^{3}}{n(G)^{2}}
.

Ten years later, it was shown by Pach, Radoičić, Tardos and Tóth

that

m_{3}(n)\leq 5.5n-11
, which is tight apart from an additive constant. Using this bound, we already get an

This implies the following result,

(

For any simple graph

Note that in each case we could apply Obseration

For larger

m_{k}(n)=\Theta(\sqrt{k}n)

.

So we do not know the optimal constant in the Crossing Lemma, but

Let

denote the minimum crossing number of a

Verifying a conjecture of

(

If

It is not known whether the condition

can be replaced by the weaker condition

. If the answer is yes, then an easy calculation shows that

.
We call the constant

As we have seen, the value of

We have

,

.

. They also proved the existence of a midrange crossing constant for many other graph classes.

0.3
Multigraphs

The Crossing Lemma does not hold
for multigraphs in general. To see this,
just take two vertices and arbitrarily many parallel edges connecting them, with no crossings, or just take one vertex and many loops.
So it is important to understand, under what conditions on the drawings
does the statement of the Crossing
Lemma, or a similar statement holds for multigraphs.
For simplicity, we assume that there are no loops, but there could be parallel edges in the multigraphs considered in this section.

The first such attempt to generalize the Crossing Lemma for multigraphs
was to limit the multiplicity of parallel edges
PT 97
;
Szé 97
;
Mat 02
.
In this case we have an easy, weaker, but tight bound.
But this condition does not really give an insight.

Theorem 0.3.1
(Crossing Lemma for multigraphs with bounded edge-multiplicity)

For any multigraph
G
, if any two vertices are connected by at most
m
edges and

e(G)\geq 4mn(G)
then

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq\frac{1}{64}\frac{e(G)^{3}}{mn(G)^{2}}.

Proof

Consider a drawing of
G
with
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)
crossings. Take a random induced subgraph

G^{\prime\prime}
by the following random process. Let
p=4mn/e
which is at most
1
, by the assumption.
First
select each vertex independently with probability
p
.
We obtain a random subset of vertices
U\subset V(G)
and let
G^{\prime}
be the
subgraph induced by
U
.
Now for each pair of adjacent vertices,
u,v\in U
, de the following.
Keep each of the edges between
u
and
v
with probability

1/m
, and delete the others, in such a way that these events are mutually exclusive.
The resulting random graph is
G^{\prime\prime}
.
The edges of

G^{\prime\prime}
are drawn as they were drawn in the original drawing.
The number of vertices and edges of
G^{\prime\prime}
,
n(G^{\prime\prime})
and
e(G^{\prime\prime})
are random variables, and

E[n(G^{\prime})]=pn(G)
,

E[e(G^{\prime})]=p^{2}e(G)/m
since each edge of
G
survives with probability
p^{2}/m
.
For the number of crossings
X(G^{\prime\prime})
of
G^{\prime\prime}
in the obtained drawing we have

X(G^{\prime})\geq{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G^{\prime})
.
The probability that a crossing is present in the drawing of
G^{\prime}
is
p^{4}/m^{2}
, so

E[X(G^{\prime\prime})]=p^{4}/m^{2}{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)
. Since
G^{\prime\prime}
is a simple graph, by (
1
),

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G^{\prime\prime})\geq e(G^{\prime\prime})-3n(G^{\prime\prime})
, so

p^{4}/m^{2}{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)=E[X(G^{\prime\prime})]\geq E[{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G^{\prime\prime})]

\geq E[e(G^{\prime\prime})]-3E[n(G^{\prime\prime})]=p^{2}/me(G)-3pn(G)

and we get that

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)\geq\frac{1}{64}\frac{e(G)^{3}}{n(G)^{2}m}.

For
m=1
, Theorem
0.3.1

gives the Crossing Lemma, but as
m
increases, the bound is
getting weaker. Still, it is also
tight up to
a constant factor.
Moreover, it was shown by Ágoston and Pálvölgyi
ÁP 22

that Theorem

0.3.1

holds with the same constant as the the original Crossing Lemma, in the following sense.
Let
\kappa(n,e,m)
denote the minimum crossing number of a
multigraph
G
with
n
vertices, at least
e
edges, and edge multiplicity at most
m
.
In particular,
\kappa(n,e,1)=\kappa(n,e)
.

Theorem 0.3.2
(
ÁP 22
)

For any
m\geq 1
,

m^{2}\kappa\left(n,\lfloor e/m\rfloor\right)\leq\kappa(n,e,m)\leq m^{2}\kappa\left(n,\lceil e/m\rceil\right).

Proof

For the upper bound on
\kappa(n,e,m)
, take a simple graph with
n
vertices,

\lceil e/m\rceil

edges drawn with
\kappa\left(n,\lceil e/m\rceil\right)
crossings.
Now replace each edge by at most
m
parallel edges, drawn very close to the original edge.
This way we can get a multigraph of
e
edges and edge multiplicity
at most
m
. Each original crossing corresponds to at most
m^{2}
new crossings, so we have

\kappa(n,e,m)\leq m^{2}\kappa\left(n,\lceil e/m\rceil\right)
.

Now we prove the lower bound on
\kappa(n,e,m)
.
Two parallel edges are called
homotopic
if they are homotopic in the vertex-punctured plane.
That is, the closed curve formed by their union is contractible to a point.
Take a multigraph
G
with
n
vertices,
e
edges, edge mutiplicity at most
m
, drawn with

\kappa(n,e,m)
crossings. Let
u,v
be two vertices and take all
uv
edges.
Let
\alpha
be one with the minimum number of crossings. Redraw all other parallel edges
very close to
\alpha
. We did not increase the number of crossings, so we still have

\kappa(n,e,m)
crossings. Do it with all pairs of vertices. Now we have a drawing with

\kappa(n,e,m)
crossings such that parallel edges are homotopic.

An edge is called
full
if it has multiplicity
m
.
Suppose that
\alpha
and
\beta
are not parallel,
\alpha
is
not full, and it has at most as many crossings
as
\beta
. Remove
\beta
and add an edge parallel to
\alpha
, drawn very close to it.
We obtain another multigraph with the same number of edges, edge multiplicity at most
m
, and we did not increase the number of crossings. Therefore, we still have
\kappa(n,e,m)
crossings. By a repeated application of this step, we can obtain a multigraph
G^{\prime}
with
n
vertices,
e
edges, edge mutiplicity at most
m
, drawn with

\kappa(n,e,m)
crossings, such that the number of non-full edges is less than
m
, and they are all parallel.

Now delete all non-full edges and keep only one of each parallel
m
-tuples of the the remaining edges.
Let
G_{1}
be the obtained simple graph. It has
n
vertices,
\lfloor e/m\rfloor

edges and in its drawing each crossing corresponds to
m^{2}
original crossings. Therefore,

m^{2}\kappa\left(n,\lfloor e/m\rfloor\right)\leq\kappa(n,e,m)
.

It was suggested by Bekos, Kaufmann, and Raftopoulou

that the dependence on the
multiplicity might be eliminated if we restrict our attention to special
classes of drawings. In particular, it is natural to assume that each ”lens” enclosed by two
parallel edges contains a vertex.

Definition 1

A drawing of a multigraph
G
in the plane is called

–
separated
if any pair of parallel edges
form a simple closed curve and there is at least one vertex in its interior and exterior,

–
single-crossing
if any two independent edges cross at most once,

–

if adjacent edges do not cross.

It turned out that these conditions are sufficient to generalize the Crossing Lemma in some form.
The first such result is by Pach and Tóth

Theorem 0.3.3
(

Suppose that

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(M)\geq c\frac{e(G)^{3}}{n(G)^{2}}

Later it turned out that the ”single-crossing” condition can be dropped.

Theorem 0.3.4
(
KPTU 18

Suppose that
M

e(M)\geq 4n(M)

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(M)\geq c\frac{e(G)^{3}}{n(G)^{2}}

.

Theorem 0.3.5

We can do it such that any two edges cross at most twice. This multigraph has

Suppose now that

Take roughly

0.4
Non-homotopic drawings

What is the ”weakest possible” condition that guarantees many crossings in terms of the number of edges?
We certainly have to rule out the trivial
example from the previous section, that is, arbitrarily many loops or parallel edges, without crossings.
A natural candidate is that we do not allow that an edge can be continuously deformed into another edge
without passing through vertices.
More precisely,
consider the drawing on a vertex-punctured plane (but it could be the sphere as well)
that is, vertices are removed, and we assume that no two parallel edges are homotopic.

In this case there is again a positive answer, shown by Pach, Tardos and Tóth
PTT 22
.
A multigraph
G
, drawn in the plane, punctured at the vertices,
with no homotopic edges is called a
non-homotopic topological multigraph
. If it is clear from the context, we do not make a notational distinction between
a non-homotopic topological multigraph and its underlying abstract multigraph.
The
crossing number

of a non-homotopic topological multigraph

is simply the number of edge crossings in its drawing.
Define the function
{\rm cr}(n,m)
as the minimum crossing number
of a non-homotopic multigraph with
n
vertices and
m

Theorem 0.4.1
(
PTT 22
)

The crossing number of a non-homotopic topological multigraph

with
n>1
vertices and
m>5n
edges satisfies
{\rm cr}(G)\geq\frac{1}{40}\frac{m^{2}}{n}
. That is,
{\rm cr}(n,m)\geq\frac{1}{40}\frac{m^{2}}{n}
.

This bound is tight up to a polylogarithmic factor.

Theorem 0.4.2
(
PTT 22
)

For any

,
m>4n
,

with
n
vertices and
m
edges
such that its crossing number satisfies

{\rm cr}(G)\leq 100\frac{m^{2}}{n}\log_{2}^{2}\frac{m}{n}
. In other words,

{\rm cr}(n,m)\leq 100\frac{m^{2}}{n}\log_{2}^{2}\frac{m}{n}
.

intersects
1/12n

(

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\rm cr}(n,m)}{m^{2}}=\infty.

Explicitly, we have

\frac{{\rm cr}(n,m)}{m^{2}}=\begin{cases}\Omega\left(\frac{(\log m/\log\log m)^{1/6}}{n^{8}}\right)&\text{for $m>4n$},\\
\Omega\left({\log^{2/3}m}\right)&\text{for fixed $n$.}\end{cases}

(4)

Proof
(Proof of Theorem

Let

, where

are distinct points in the plane, and let

be also fixed. Assume without loss of generality that
a_{i}=(i,0),\;1\leq i\leq n,
and
x=(0,-1)
. An

-loop
is a (possibly self-crossing)
oriented path in
S
from
x
to
x
, i.e., a continuous function

with
f(0)=f(1)=x

The homotopy group of

is the free group
F_{n}
generated by
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
, where
g_{i}
can be represented by a triangular
x
-loop around

, for example the one going from
x
to
(2i-1,1)
, from here to
(2i+1,1)
, and then back to
x
along three straight-line segments; see
LS 77
.

We define an

to be a polygonal
x
-loop with intermediate vertices

(1,\varepsilon_{1}),\,(2,\varepsilon_{2}),\,\dots,\,(n,\varepsilon_{n}),\,(n+1,-1),

in this order, where each
\varepsilon_{i}\;(1\leq i\leq n)
is equal either to
1/2
or to
-1/2
. There are
2^{n}
distinct elementary loops, depending on the choice of the
\varepsilon_{i}
. Each of them represents a distinct homotopy class of the form
g_{i_{1}}\cdots g_{i_{t}}
, where the indices form a strictly increasing sequence. By making infinitesimal perturbations on the interior vertices of the elementary loops, we can make sure that every pair of them intersect in at most
n-1

. See Fig.

.

If

, and we are done.

In the case

, we consider all

-loops which can be obtained as the product (concatenation) of

elementary loops. Unfortunately, some of these concatenated

0.5
Other crossing numbers

The crossing number of a graph
G
is usually defined as
“the minimum number of edge crossings in any drawing of
G

in the plane”
BL 84
.
But
this definition can be interpreted in
several different ways. Sometimes it is assumed that in
a drawing two edges have at most one common point, which is a crossing or a common endpoint (
WB 83
;
Bie 91
). Many authors do not
make this assumption (
Tut 70
;
GJ 83
;
SSSV 97
). If two edges are allowed to cross several
times, we may count their intersections with multiplicity or
without
AS 14
. We can impose some further
restrictions on the drawings (e.g., the edges must be
straight-line segments
Jen 71
, or polygonal paths of
length at most
k

BD 93
). No matter what definition
we use, the determination of the crossing number of a graph
appears to be an extremely difficult task (
GJ 83
;
Bie 91
). In fact, we do not even know the value of
any
of the above quantities for the complete
graph
K_{n}
with
n
vertices and for the complete bipartite
graph
K_{n,n}
with
2n
vertices, for large
n
.

In his famous book about crossing numbers, Schaefer
Sch 18
lists more than 100 different versions of the crossing number.
The systematic study of different versions of the crossing number and their relationships started with the work of
Pach and Tóth
PT00b
;
PT00a
. See also the dynamic survey of Schaefer
Sch 12
.

The most commonly used version of the crossing number is simply called the
crossing number
of a graph
G
,
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)
, is the minimum number of crossings
(crossing points)
over all drawings of
G
. (Here we assume that no three edges cross at the same point, or these crossings are counted with multiplicity, and not edge goes through a vertex.)
The
pair-crossing number
,
{\mbox{\sc pcr}}(G)
, is the minimum number of pairs of crossing edges
over all drawings of
G
.
As we have seen in Observation
1
,
in an optimal drawing for
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)
, any two edges cross at most once.
Therefore,
it is not easy to see the difference between these two definitions.
Indeed, there was some confusion in the literature between these two notions,
until the systematic study of their relationship. Clearly,
{\mbox{\sc pcr}}(G)\leq{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)
for every
G
, and
in fact, we cannot rule out the possibility, that
{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)={\mbox{\sc pcr}}(G)

for every graph
G
. Undoubtedly,
it is the most interesting open problem in this area. Any progress would be a great step in understanding the crossing number.
From the other direction, after several steps, the best known bound is

{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G)=O({\mbox{\sc pcr}}(G)^{3/2}\log{\mbox{\sc pcr}}(G))

Sch 18
.

The
odd-crossing number
,
{\mbox{\sc ocr}}(G)
, is the minimum number
of pairs of edges that cross an
odd
number of times,
over all drawings of
G
.
Clearly, for every graph
G
,

{\mbox{\sc ocr}}(G)\leq{\mbox{\sc pcr}}(G)\leq{\mbox{\sc cr}}(G).

This definition was partly inspired by the weak Hanani-Tutte theorem

;

which states that if

is planar, that is,

.
It was shown in

that for

, then

There are examples where

and

From the other

{\mbox{\sc pcr}}(G)<2{\mbox{\sc ocr}}(G)^{2}

Theorem 0.5.1
(Crossing Lemma for other crossing numbers)

Let

{\mbox{\sc cr}}_{-}(G)

{\mbox{\sc pcr}}_{+}(G)

{\mbox{\sc pcr}}(G)

{\mbox{\sc pcr}}_{-}(G)

{\mbox{\sc ocr}}_{+}(G)

{\mbox{\sc ocr}}(G)

For the proof we need the Strong Hanani-Tutte theorem

Theorem 0.5.2
(Strong Hanani-Tutte theorem,

)

If a graph

In other words,

Proof
(Proof of the Crossing Lemma for other crossing numbers.)

The proof is very similar to the proof of

0.2.1

It is enough to prove the statement for the smallest one,
{\mbox{\sc ocr}}_{-}(G)

1. Just like in the original proof, we can prove by induction on

{\mbox{\sc ocr}}_{-}(G)\geq e(G)-3n(G).

(9)

Suppose that

By the Strong Hanani-Tutte theorem, we have

{\mbox{\sc ocr}}_{-}(G)>0

use the induction hypothesis.

2.
Consider a drawing of
G
with

independent odd-crossing pairs of edges. Take a random induced subgraph

of
G
by selecting each vertex independently with probability
p
.
We obtain a random subset

and let

from the original drawing of
G

We have

E[n(G^{\prime})]=pn(G)
