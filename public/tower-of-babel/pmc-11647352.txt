Patients achieving low lupus disease activity state, systemic lupus erythematosus disease control or remission showed lower rates of organ damage during longitudinal follow-up: analysis of the Hopkins Lupus Cohort
Authors: Jacob Hunnicutt, Mary Elizabeth Georgiou, Anna Richards, Holly Quasny, Laurence Magder, Daniel Goldman, Michelle A Petri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOne key target of treating patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is to prevent organ damage. This analysis quantified the association between time spent in four specific SLE low disease activity (LDA) states and organ damage rate.MethodsThis retrospective real-world data analysis (GSK Study 207168), undertaken to help contextualise the BLISS-BELIEVE clinical trial, included adults with SLE enrolled for≥1 year in the Hopkins Lupus Cohort and treated with standard therapy in a specialist care centre between 1987 and 2019. LDA states (Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS), disease control, clinical and complete remissions) were defined using SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI)/Physician Global Assessment scores, prednisone-equivalent dose and medication use criteria combinations. Time spent in each LDA state was expressed as a percentage of total follow-up (0%; >0–<25%; 25–49%; 50–74%; ≥75%). Pooled logistic models were used to estimate adjusted rate ratios (aRR) between time spent in LDA states and organ damage rate (assessed using the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/American College of Rheumatology Damage Index (SDI)).ResultsOverall, 1632 patients experienced 1246 organ damage events. Follow-up time (calculated from days of follow-up) totalled 9841.1 person-years. At baseline, the mean (SD) SLEDAI score was 2.8 (3.3) and the mean (SD) SDI score was 1.7 (1.9). Organ damage rates were lower in patients who achieved an LDA state versus those who did not. Rates decreased with increasing time spent in each LDA state. Even a small percentage of time (>0–<25% vs 0%) spent in an LDA state was associated with reduced damage (aRR (95% CI): LLDAS, 0.75 (0.61, 0.91); disease control, 0.80 (0.68, 0.93); clinical remission, 0.73 (0.60, 0.88); complete remission, 0.80 (0.68, 0.93)).ConclusionsRegardless of definition, achieving and maintaining a low disease activity state was associated with reduced organ damage in patients with SLE.Keywords: Autoimmune Diseases; Autoimmunity; Epidemiology; Glucocorticoids; Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) low disease activity states, such as Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS), disease control and remission, have been used in previous studies to evaluate the effectiveness of SLE therapies and have been shown to be associated with reductions in disease flares and organ damage accrual, as well as improvements in patient-reported outcomes.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease characterised by immune system dysfunction, leading to diverse clinical manifestations, and increased morbidity and mortality.1 Persistent disease activity and SLE flares lead to irreversible organ damage.2 3 Antimalarials, corticosteroids and non-targeted immunosuppressants have been the mainstays of SLE treatment.4 However, higher cumulative corticosteroid use is associated with an increased risk of organ damage and early mortality.5 6 Therefore, to prevent disease complications and minimise organ damage, the ideal treatment target is attaining a state of remission that requires the absence of any clinical activity with minimal corticosteroid dose.7

An international Definitions Of Remission In SLE (DORIS) task force developed a framework for defining remission8; however, many alternative remission definitions are used across clinical and observational studies in SLE.9 Regardless of definition, remission is often achieved by only a small number of patients within a short time frame (eg, 52 weeks) with conventional standard therapy. Consequently, the less restrictive and more realistically attainable Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) was developed and validated by Franklyn et al.10 Remission and LLDAS endpoints have been included in various SLE trials.11,13

Previous studies demonstrated that achieving remission and/or LLDAS is associated with a lower probability of organ damage accrual and flares in patients with SLE,14,20 along with lower mortality rates and better health-related quality of life.14 16 The earlier these states are achieved, the better the chance of positive clinical outcomes.9 Furthermore, the longer the patient remains in remission/LLDAS, the better the long-term organ damage protection.7 20 21

However, within a typical trial with a follow-up period rarely exceeding 1–2 years, there is limited opportunity to link treatments of interest with more distal outcomes, such as organ damage events. Therefore, longitudinal cohort studies that use similar low disease activity criteria to those included in the clinical trials are needed to provide greater context to the link between low disease activity states (ie, LLDAS, disease control or remission) and organ damage risk. Further, few studies have considered how the association between increasing time in different low disease activity states of interest and organ damage may vary by patient subgroups. This is important given disease activity and organ damage rates observed in practice vary by race/ethnicity and other patient characteristics.20 22 23

In this study, using recent data from the Hopkins Lupus Cohort and definitions of LLDAS, disease control, clinical remission and complete remission used in prior clinical trials,11 13 we quantified the association between specific SLE low disease activity states and the organ damage rates during follow-up.

Methods

Study design

This observational retrospective analysis (GSK Study 207168) used real-world data from the Hopkins Lupus Cohort prospective, longitudinal, single-centre study, in which>2000 patients with SLE were recruited from 1987 to 2019, to help contextualise the findings from the BLISS-BELIEVE clinical trial. The index date (start of follow-up period) was defined as 1 year after enrolment into the Hopkins Lupus Cohort. Patients were followed at quarterly standard of care (SOC) visits by one provider.

Patients and follow-up

Patients with SLE (diagnosed according to the revised American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) criteria24) were enrolled in the Hopkins Lupus Cohort and received standard therapy at a single specialist care centre in Baltimore, Maryland, USA. Eligible patients were ≥18 years of age at enrolment, enrolled in the Hopkins Lupus Cohort for≥1 year (ensuring sufficient time to assess time spent in each low disease activity state; patients had varying SLE disease duration lengths before entry into cohort) and had≥1 follow-up visit within 365 days of the index date. Patients missing key variables needed to define their exposure, outcomes or confounders were excluded.

Patients were followed from the index date until the earliest of the following: >365 days between clinical visits, death or study end (September 2020). Patients censored due to a>365-day gap between clinical visits were followed until 90 days after their last recorded visit based on the assumption that events that occurred in that period would likely be recorded in the study database.

Variables

SLE low disease activity states

For each person-month of follow-up, patients were classified as being in specific low disease activity states (LLDAS, disease control, clinical remission and complete remission) based on SLE disease activity measured at the most recent clinic visit. Disease activity was evaluated at each quarterly SOC visit using various versions of the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI), Physician Global Assessment (PGA; 0–3 Visual Analogue Scale) and medication use. The definitions of low disease activity states are summarised in table 1. Briefly, LLDAS was defined as SLEDAI ≤4 (with no increase in any SLEDAI components since the previous visit); PGA ≤1; prednisone-equivalent dose≤7.5 mg/day; biologics and other immunosuppressants allowed. Disease control was defined as SLEDAI ≤2; prednisone-equivalent dose≤5 mg/day; immunosuppressants (other than biologics) not allowed; biologics allowed. Clinical remission was defined as clinical SLEDAI (cSLEDAI)=0 (excluding serological components, that is, anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) positivity and hypocomplementaemia); prednisone-equivalent dose≤5 mg/day; biologics and other immunosuppressants allowed. Complete remission was defined as SLEDAI=0; prednisone-equivalent dose≤5 mg/day; biologics and other immunosuppressants allowed. Any individuals not achieving one of these definitions (eg, individuals with PGA>1, SLEDAI>4) were classed as having a high disease activity state.

The definitions for low disease activity states broadly align with definitions implemented in the recent BLISS-BELIEVE trial and post hoc analyses of belimumab clinical trials.11 12 The definitions for SLE low disease activity states allowed the use of biologics and other immunosuppressants (except for the definition of disease control), to align with prior DORIS recommendations to focus on on-treatment endpoints, given they are more attainable in the context of trials and clinical research.10 21 However, supplementary analyses of disease control and remission without biologics and other immunosuppressants allowed, and remission with biologics and other immunosuppressants allowed, were also conducted to ascertain the impact of immunosuppressive regimens in achieving these disease activity states (defined in footnotes of online supplemental table 1 and figure 1).

Analyses were performed in the overall population (primary objective) and subgroups stratified by key demographic and clinical characteristics of interest (secondary objective).

Percentage of prior time in specific SLE low disease activity states

The percentage of prior time spent in specific low disease activity states was a time-varying exposure. The percentage of prior time was updated during each follow-up month based on details about a patient’s disease activity and medication use, collected at the quarterly SOC study visits and assessed from cohort enrolment through each follow-up month:

Further detail on how patients’ percentage of time in an SLE low disease activity state was determined is shown in online supplemental figure 2.

At 12 months post-enrolment, and each month thereafter, the percentage of prior time spent in each low disease activity state of interest was estimated for each eligible person-month of follow-up, per the following categories: 0%; >0–<25%; 25–49%; 50–74%; ≥75%.

Organ damage

Organ damage was assessed during follow-up at each 3-monthly visit using the SLICC/ACR Damage Index (SDI) to identify damage events after the index date.25 26 The SDI ranges between 0 (no damage) and 47 points (maximum damage) and captures permanent, irreversible damage in patients with SLE across 12 organ systems: ocular, neuropsychiatric, renal, pulmonary, cardiovascular, peripheral vascular, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, skin, endocrine, gonadal and malignancies.25 For each month of follow-up, a new organ damage event was defined as any new damage recorded on the SDI. Multiple organ damage events occurring in the same month were conservatively counted as a single event because the exact dates of onset were unknown.

Patients could contribute with multiple organ damage events to the analysis during follow-up if they occurred in different months.

Demographic and clinical subgroups of interest

Key subgroups of interest were defined according to the following patient characteristics: self-reported race/ethnicity (white, black African ancestry, other) at cohort enrolment, SLE disease duration at index (<2 years, 2–6 years, >6 years), presence of prior organ damage at index based on SDI scores (0, 1, ≥2), presence of biopsy-confirmed lupus nephritis (LN)27 at index (yes/no) and SLEDAI score at index (0, 1–2, 3–5 and≥6; and<10 and ≥10).

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics included the variables used to define the key demographic and clinical subgroups described above, as well as additional demographics including age at index as a continuous variable and sex (male, female). SLE disease activity was described using multiple disease activity scores, including the Safety of Estrogens in Lupus Erythematosus National Assessment – SLE Disease Activity Index (SELENA-SLEDAI), cSLEDAI (excluding serological components, is, anti-dsDNA positivity and hypocomplementaemia) and PGA scores based on the most recently available assessment preceding the index date. Organ system involvement was captured based on each patient’s most recently available SLEDAI score (eg, mucocutaneous, renal). SLE biomarkers included low complement component (C)3 (yes/no), low C4 (yes/no) and anti-dsDNA positivity (yes/no) based on data collected between enrolment and the index date. Concomitant SLE medication use at index included the use of antimalarials (yes/no), immunosuppressants (yes/no), biologics (yes/no) and corticosteroids (average daily prednisone-equivalent doses captured using different cut-off points (≥5 mg/day, ≥7.5 mg/day, ≥10 mg/day)).

To further describe disease activity and treatment, and for the statistical modelling approach described below, a patient’s SLE disease activity and treatment at enrolment were categorised into mutually exclusive groups: high activity, high treatment; high activity, low treatment; low activity, high treatment; and low activity, low treatment.28 Low versus high activity was defined using the PGA and SELENA-SLEDAI score (low activity: PGA<1 and SLEDAI≤2; high activity: all patients not meeting the definition of low activity); low versus high treatment was defined using prednisone dose and immunosuppressant use (low treatment: prednisone<5 mg/day and no immunosuppressant use; high treatment: all patients not meeting the definition of low treatment).

Statistical analysis

For baseline characteristics, categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages; continuous variables were described using means and SD.

During follow-up, and for each low disease activity state of interest, the frequency and percentage of patients ever achieving the low disease activity state as well as the proportion of overall follow-up months, originally calculated in days, that patients spent in the low disease activity state, were estimated.

For each low disease activity state of interest and by the percentage of prior time spent in the disease activity state, the number of new organ damage events, person-years of follow-up and crude rate of organ damage per 10 person-years were reported. Pooled logistic models were then used to estimate adjusted rate ratios and 95% CIs between the percentage of prior months in specific low disease activity states and the organ damage rate during follow-up (referent: 0% of prior time in the low disease activity state). The unit of analysis for these calculations was based on each person-month of follow-up, with patients considered at-risk for the entire month if they contributed≥1 day of follow-up to that month. The analyses were adjusted for the following potential confounders: age, sex, race/ethnicity, baseline disease activity and treatment at index, SLE duration at index, LN at index and prior organ damage at index.

For subgroup analyses, the same approach described above was implemented, but results were stratified by demographic and clinical subgroups of interest. All subgroup analyses were descriptive in nature. For the subgroup analyses, a variable was omitted from the adjusted model if it was included in the stratified outcome.

Patients and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this analysis.

Results

Patient population

Of 2658 patients enrolled in the Hopkins Lupus Cohort, 1632 patients were included in the analysis. Eligible patients were predominantly women (91.9%, n/N=1500/1632); the mean (SD) age was 38.5 (13.0) years and 39.5% of patients were of black African ancestry (table 2). At index, 22.2% of patients had LN, and the most frequent SLEDAI organ system involvement prior to index included cutaneous (16.9%) and renal (12.4%). At index, the mean (SD) duration of SLE was 6.1 (6.9) years and the mean (SD) SLEDAI score was 2.8 (3.3); further, 66.2% of patients were using antimalarials, 26.7% were receiving immunosuppressants and 25.9% had an average prednisone-equivalent corticosteroid dose≥10 mg/day. Only 0.2% of patients were receiving biologics at index.

Time spent by patients in SLE low disease activity states of interest

Overall, patients had a total of 9841.1 person-years (117 850.9 person-months) of follow-up. Patients spent 55.0% of the total study follow-up time (64 866 months) in LLDAS, 42.4% (49 948 months) in disease control, 49.6% in clinical remission (58 398 months) and 34.8% (41 002 months) in complete remission (table 3).

The time spent in disease control without the allowed use of biologics and other immunosuppressants was the same as that achieved with the allowed use of biologics but not the use of other immunosuppressants (online supplemental table 1). Time spent in clinical remission and complete remission without the allowed use of biologics and other immunosuppressants (26.9% and 20.4% of follow-up time, respectively) was lower than that spent in clinical remission and complete remission with allowed use of biologics and other immunosuppressants (49.6% and 34.8% of follow-up time, respectively).

Subgroup analyses

When stratified into patient baseline characteristics subgroups, the percentage of follow-up spent in LLDAS was numerically higher in white patients (62.10%) versus patients of black African ancestry (46.35%), patients with SLE disease duration<2 years (59.25%) versus longer disease duration (2–6 years (54.15%); >6 years (50.55%)), patients with no organ damage at index (SDI score=0 (58.52%)) or SDI score of 1 (58.01%) versus SDI score of≥2 (50.84%) and patients without LN at index (57.55%) versus those with LN (45.33%) (figure 2). Similar trends were observed for disease control and clinical and complete remission (online supplemental figures 3–5). Similar trends were also seen when patients were stratified by SLEDAI scores at index, with patients having a greater percentage time in LLDAS with SLEDAI scores of 0 (67.65%) or 1–2 (57.57%) versus 3–5 (44.88%) or≥6 (41.83%), and in patients with SLEDAI<10 (55.71%) versus ≥10 (43.47%) (online supplemental figure 6).

The proportion of patients achieving low disease activity states was greater in patients with low disease activity and/or treatment at baseline versus high disease activity or treatment (online supplemental table 2). Rates of organ damage were similar across baseline disease activity and treatment states, with numerically lower rates reported in patients achieving remission versus disease control or LLDAS (online supplemental figure 7A–D).

In all subgroups, longer durations of follow-up time spent in any of the SLE low disease activity states were generally associated with lower new organ damage rates (figure 2 and online supplemental figures 3–5, 7), following similar trends to the overall analysis (figure 1).

Discussion

In this study, new organ damage rates were lower among patients with SLE with longer durations of LLDAS, disease control or remission compared with those who spent less time in these low disease activity states. Even a small percentage of time (>0–<25%) spent in any of the low disease activity states was associated with a notable reduction in new organ damage rates during follow-up. Similarly, new organ damage rates decreased as the time spent in the low disease activity states increased. These results validate previous studies demonstrating an association between prolonged LLDAS/remission and reduced organ damage accrual.10 21 28 29 Additionally, this study highlights the importance of low disease activity and remission as meaningful targets in clinical trials and practice by leveraging more extensive cumulative follow-up data (>9000 person-years) and demonstrates consistent benefits across several definitions of low disease activity states and important patient subgroups, providing further context to results observed in the recent BLISS-BELIEVE clinical trial, which used a disease control definition that was adapted for this analysis to assess low disease activity attainment.30

The reduction in new organ damage rates observed in this study was similar between the assessed states. Although remission is considered the ultimate goal of SLE treatment, the other low disease activity states of LLDAS and disease control demonstrated benefits for organ damage sparing and may be more attainable as endpoints within the typical clinical trial duration.8 10 31 This is supported by two previous Hopkins Lupus Cohort studies, where the median time to LLDAS was 1.1 years overall,22 whereas the median time to DORIS clinical remission and clinical remission on treatment (with allowed use of biologics and other immunosuppressants) was 8.7 years and 1.8 years, respectively.28

Despite very few patients receiving biologics at index in this study (0.2%), a high proportion of patients achieved LLDAS, disease control or clinical and complete remission with the allowed use of biologics or other immunosuppressants during follow-up, likely due to low SLE disease activity at index (mean SLEDAI score of 2.8 and PGA score of 0.7). There was a lower risk of new organ damage seen when the remission definitions included an allowance of immunosuppressants compared with no allowance. Furthermore, as demonstrated by a previous real-world analysis, the durability of remission endpoints with conventional standard therapy is limited, with most patients relapsing to higher disease activity within months.28 Although ever achieving a low disease activity state was associated with benefits in organ damage reduction, increased time in each disease activity state was associated with further benefits. This highlights the need for new treatment regimens to help patients quickly and durably achieve low disease activity and remission. As noted in the 2023 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology recommendations, prompt initiation of biologics, such as belimumab or anifrolumab, should be considered to control the disease and facilitate corticosteroid tapering or discontinuation.31 Therefore, further studies are required to evaluate the effect of biologics on attaining and maintaining low disease activity states.

This study also confirmed the benefit of low disease activity states across different patient subgroups. Although some subgroups were previously analysed (race/ethnicity and prior organ damage),20 22 other subgroups, including disease duration and LN status, were not. Importantly, patients of black African ancestry (vs white patients), patients with SLE disease duration≥2 years (vs<2 years), more prior damage at index (SDI score≥2 vs 1 or 0) or LN at index (vs no LN at index) spent less time on average in the assessed low disease activity states. The observed differences between patients of black African ancestry versus white patients are consistent with a prior Hopkins Lupus Cohort study20 and the increased disease prevalence, greater disease severity (including renal involvement) and higher mortality reported in the literature.32,34 The underlying reasons for this are not clear from our study. Potential factors include genetics, environmental pollutants and social determinants of health such as income, educational attainment, neighbourhood-level characteristics, healthcare access and discrimination.35 36 Given disparities in disease prevalence and burden, and the general benefit of low disease activity regardless of race/ethnicity, it is critical that patients of black African ancestry and other racial/ethnic minorities receive care and therapy that help achieve low disease activity states. Further, racial/ethnic minority patients should be given more opportunities to be included in clinical research, especially since non-white patients have been historically under-represented in trials.37 More broadly, structural factors (eg, societal, economic, political) need acknowledgement and addressing to curtail the perpetuation of these disparities.

In this study, patients with longer disease duration at baseline spent less time in low disease activity states. This is consistent with prior results from the Hopkins Lupus Cohort that found a shorter SLE disease duration since diagnosis was associated with reduced time to achieving LLDAS.22 It was postulated this may be because patients with longer disease duration have more established SLE manifestations and more difficult disease to control.22 However, this association has not always been consistent across studies,38 and some ambiguity remains.

Patients with more organ damage at baseline (SDI≥2) had a lower percentage of follow-up in SLE low disease activity states in this study than patients with lower baseline disease activity (SDI of 1 or 0). Pre-existing organ damage in SLE is associated with further damage accrual and increased mortality.5 39 More severe underlying disease could drive this association, though other factors, including prolonged high-dose corticosteroid use, may also contribute. The mechanistic pathways linking prior organ damage to future damage are incompletely understood, and further research is needed.

Patients with LN at baseline had less time in any of the assessed low disease activity states. Patients with LN are known to have higher morbidity, mortality and healthcare-related costs versus those without LN.40 41 Time in low disease activity states may be particularly important in LN given potential renal-specific benefits. For instance, in adult patients from the Hopkins Lupus Cohort, increased time in LLDAS was previously found to be associated with a reduced risk of developing end-stage renal disease.20 Another study reported paediatric patients with LN who achieved LLDAS during the first 6 months of initial treatment had lower subsequent renal flare rates and damage accrual.42

Unsurprisingly, patients were also more likely to achieve a low disease activity state with low disease activity and/or low treatment at baseline. This finding aligns with a study assessing the effect of baseline disease activity and treatment on time to remission, with longer times for achievement seen for patients with high disease activity and/or high treatment.28

Importantly, for all patient characteristics considered, when examining the association between disease activity states and organ damage, we observed similar trends in benefit between time spent in each disease activity state and reduced organ damage accrual, as documented in the overall cohort. Although the subgroup analyses had smaller sample sizes and should be interpreted carefully, these results support using these low disease activity states as treatment targets for clinical trials and routine practice.

Together, the results of this study extend the findings of a previous observational study by Petri and Magder20 through the analysis of a larger population and with longer follow-up, as well as the inclusion of disease control as a low disease activity state and more extended analysis by the inclusion of additional patient characteristic subgroups. The findings add to a growing body of evidence that a patient spending more time in low disease activity states, be it remission, LLDAS, disease control or another definition of low activity, is broadly associated with reduced organ damage accrual, mortality, flares and medical costs, and better quality of life.8 14 21 However, there are some limitations. We focused on a subset of low disease activity states definitions, but there are many other definitions used across clinical trials and observational studies. Additionally, a SLEDAI score of>2 to denote high activity was used to align with previous analyses of the Hopkins Lupus Cohort28 ; however, SLEDAI scores of>4 and ≥10 have been used to denote active and highly active SLE, thus some patients may have been classified as ‘low active’ had a different threshold been applied. The observational nature of the study means unmeasured or residual confounding cannot be ruled out as a potential explanation for the reported results. Compared with a previous Hopkins Lupus Cohort study,20 we treated several potential confounders as time-fixed at index rather than time-varying. Nonetheless, the results were similar despite differing operationalisation of confounders across both studies. Furthermore, because more recent data were available, we leveraged a larger sample size with longer follow-up. As patients had variable follow-up periods, the impact of a percentage of time in a given low disease activity state will differ between patients; however, analysing the actual time patients spent in a low disease activity state was beyond the scope of this study. Changes in low disease activity states were based on changes in disease activity and medication use, which could only be captured at study visits. However, this is also a limitation of other observational SLE studies. It has previously been shown that the majority of patients who are in LLDAS are also in remission43; therefore, it is likely that a number of patients in this study who achieved LLDAS also achieved remission. This suggests that some of the benefits seen with LLDAS may have been driven by the protective effect of remission; however, as our analysis did not stratify patients achieving LLDAS by their remission status, the effect of remission on organ damage in patients with LLDAS cannot be differentiated in this study. Additionally, the limited use of biologics in this study (0.2%) presents challenges in interpreting results when analysing definitions of disease control with the allowed use of biologics but not other immunosuppressants and without the allowed use of biologics and other immunosuppressants, which might hinder a comprehensive evaluation of their effect on the study’s endpoints. The Hopkins Lupus Cohort is restricted to patients treated in a single specialist centre; thus, generalisability on certain aspects of this study may be limited. However, this cohort has a strong representation of white and non-Hispanic black patients. Finally, as this was not an incident cohort, disease activity levels prior to entry into the Hopkins Lupus Cohort were unknown; however, subgroup analyses by disease duration showed similar results among patients with less disease duration at index (<2 years) compared with the overall analysis.

In conclusion, achieving LLDAS, disease control and remission, and increasing amounts of time spent in these disease activity states were associated with reduced organ damage rates. This was observed consistently in the overall population and across several patient subgroups. Compared with previous studies, the subgroup analyses in the current study used larger sample sizes and a more extensive follow-up time and considered additional patient subgroups. Differences in outcomes across patient subgroups in our study further emphasise the importance of optimally managing those at a higher risk to achieve LLDAS, disease control and, ultimately, remission. These findings support using these low disease activity states as meaningful patient and clinician treatment targets in clinical trials, real-world studies and clinical practice.
