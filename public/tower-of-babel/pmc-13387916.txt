ABSTRACT
ObjectivePositive α‐synuclein seed amplification assay (SAA) is a biomarker found in most people with Parkinson's disease (PD). We explored if free‐water (FW) imaging detects microstructural differences in the brains of patients with early PD with SAA+ or SAA– status.MethodsWe studied patients with PD with baseline diffusion imaging and α‐synuclein SAA data from the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI). We compared FW, FW corrected fractional anisotropy (FAT), and clinical characteristics between SAA+ and SAA– groups. We also applied the Automated Imaging Differentiation for Parkinsonism (AIDP) at baseline to classify PD versus atypical parkinsonism, stratified by SAA status.ResultsAmong 462 participants (41 SAA– and 421 SAA+), individuals with SAA+ had hyposmia and shorter motor symptom duration before baseline magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). AIDP identified PD in 92.4% (n = 427, 91.6% had SAA+) and classified 7.6% as atypical parkinsonism (n = 35, 85.7% had SAA+). At baseline, SAA+ individuals had lower FW in the superior cerebellar peduncle, compared to SAA– (pFDR < 0.05). No significant differences in FAT were found between groups.InterpretationPositive α‐synuclein SAA was associated with focal microstructural differences but did not distinguish broader diffusion MRI (dMRI) changes across FW and FAT metrics. These findings indicate that molecular confirmation of synuclein aggregation (via SAA) provides limited stratification of neurodegeneration detected by FW imaging in early PD. ANN NEUROL 2026;100:295–304

The α‐synuclein seed amplification assay (SAA) is a promising diagnostic tool in Parkinson's disease (PD) and related synucleinopathies. One of the pathological hallmarks of PD is the accumulation of aggregated, misfolded α‐synuclein in the substantia nigra and extranigral regions. 1 , 2 The α‐synuclein SAAs (via real‐time quaking‐induced conversion [RT‐QuIC] and protein misfolding cyclic amplification [PMCA]) 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 accurately detect the presence of aggregated α‐synuclein in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and other tissues, with sensitivity ranging from 85 to 93% and specificity ranging from 95 to 98% for individuals with clinically diagnosed PD. 5 , 6 , 7 Recent studies showed application of CSF α‐synuclein SAA in identifying individuals from at‐risk groups, including rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder and prodromal PD. 5 , 8

CSF α‐synuclein SAA positivity may be lower in certain PD populations. Siderowf et al (2023) 5 reported overall 87.7% sensitivity of CSF α‐synuclein SAA in PD participants from Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI), where all participants also had an abnormal dopamine transporter (DaT) single photon emission computerized tomography (SPECT). 9 CSF α‐synuclein SAA positivity varied by genetic (eg, LRRK2) and clinical features (eg, olfaction). Sensitivity was highest (98.6%) for sporadic PD with typical hyposmia but decreased progressively in those with normosmia and/or LRRK2 PD. 5 Studies have also found conflicting associations among α‐synuclein SAA, motor parkinsonism, Hoehn and Yahr stage, and PD disease duration. 3 , 4 , 10 It remains unclear whether different pathologies/mechanisms underly people with PD having positive versus negative CSF α‐synuclein SAA, despite shared PD clinical phenotypes. Although CSF α‐synuclein SAA positivity indicates the presence of α‐synuclein aggregation, it does not currently provide information about the cause of this protein aggregation or, critically, the underlying disease mechanism. 6

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) is a promising noninvasive, in vivo technique to examine microstructural changes in neurodegenerative disorders. An advanced diffusion processing technique called free‐water (FW) imaging uses a 2‐compartment model to decompose extracellular and tissue‐specific diffusion properties. This approach is more sensitive to microstructural effects in grey and white matter compared to conventional diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), which does not account for non‐Gaussian partial volume effects and is susceptible to tensor estimation bias. 11 FW imaging has proven sensitive to early disease effects in the absence of volumetric atrophy changes using conventional anatomical imaging. 12 , 13 FW in the posterior substantia nigra is elevated in PD compared with controls, 12 and has proven to be a promising progression imaging marker, increasing over 1, 2, and 4 years in de novo PD. 14 , 15 More recently, FW imaging combined with machine learning (Automated Imaging Differentiation for Parkinsonism [AIDP]) has provided an automated and highly accurate approach for differentiating PD from other neurodegenerative forms of atypical parkinsonism (multiple system atrophy parkinsonian type [MSAp]; progressive supranuclear palsy [PSP]). 16 , 17 In this study, we use PPMI's comprehensive clinical and biomarker dataset to: (1) compare baseline FW‐based imaging metrics in people with PD who are CSF α‐synuclein SAA‐positive versus SAA‐negative, and (2) evaluate the concordance between AIDP PD classification and CSF α‐synuclein SAA status.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study uses data from the PPMI cohort. PPMI is a longitudinal, observational, multicenter, international natural history study. By tracking progression of clinical features, imaging outcomes, and biologic and genetic markers, the overall goal of PPMI is to identify markers of disease progression across all stages of PD to accelerate PD therapeutic trials (symptomatic and disease modification). PPMI recruits participants in the United States and approximately 50 sites worldwide. Detailed information about PPMI study design, inclusion/exclusion criteria, informed consent, and demographic data, can be found on the website (https://www.ppmi-info.org/).

Participants

All participants were initially identified from PPMI's PD cohort enrolled between July 2010 and November 2024. The clinical diagnosis was made by site investigators who are movement disorder specialists and confirmed by a central consensus committee review. This study included de novo PD of sporadic or genetically defined origin (eg, GBA and SNCA) with a positive DaT‐SPECT scan. We excluded participants with PRKN and LRRK2 variants, as up to one third of LRRK2‐associated parkinsonism cases with nigral degeneration lack pathologic α‐synuclein. 18 Similarly, the pathologic hallmark of Lewy bodies is not a consistent feature of PRKN‐linked PD. 19 We queried all patients from PPMI within the PD cohort with quality baseline dMRI (≥30 directions) and CSF α‐synuclein SAA data. Other PPMI cohorts (ie, participants who are prodromal; participants with parkinsonism but with scans without evidence of dopamine deficiency [SWEDD]; or healthy controls) were not included.

Figure 1 details the cohort selections for this study's primary objectives. Whereas a total of 500 participants met our initial selection criteria, we excluded 9 individuals due to inconclusive or inconsistent baseline SAA results. Among the 491 participants, 24 with LRRK2 variants and 4 with PRKN variants were further excluded, and one was excluded due to inadequate FW imaging quality. The final baseline cohort consisted of 41 participants with negative α‐synuclein SAA (“SAA–”) and 421 with positive α‐synuclein SAA (“SAA+”). For the second aim, we used the baseline cohort (total n = 462) in an automated FW imaging AIDP approach to classify PD versus atypical MSAp/PSP parkinsonism (see Fig 1).

All experimental procedures were performed in ethical accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the institutional review board at each PPMI site, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Study Evaluations

In PPMI, all participants undergo a comprehensive battery of clinical testing, neuropsychological evaluation, imaging, and biospecimen collection at baseline. Clinical assessments pertinent to this study include Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS‐UPDRS) parts II (motor aspects or experiences of daily living) and III (motor examination; recorded in the off‐state for treated participants) for parkinsonism, and Montreal Cognitive assessment (MoCA) for global cognitive screen. Biomarker and genetic assessments included CSF α‐synuclein SAA, University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT), sporadic versus PD genetic variant, and dMRI.

CSF α‐synuclein SAA is performed with Amprion's protocol as described previously. 5 , 20 According to the PPMI Data User Guide, 2 SAA methods were used: “Amprion‐αS‐SAA” (150‐hour method) and “Amprion‐24 h‐αS‐SAA” (24‐hour method, which uses a reduced volume of recombinant α‐synuclein and a shortened assay length; https://www.ppmi-info.org/sites/default/files/docs/PPMI%20Data%20User%20Guide.pdf). These 2 methods correspond to distinct PPMI projects – samples included in “Project 155” were analyzed using the “Amprion‐αS‐SAA” method, whereas those in “Project 237” used the “Amprion‐24 h‐αS‐SAA” method. The main binary outcome for α‐synuclein SAA result is positive versus negative. The highest raw fluorescence from 3 replicates (wells) is used to establish the assay output for each sample. A sample is deemed positive for α‐synuclein SAA if all 3 replicates are positive, whereas a sample is deemed negative if zero or only one replicate were positive. A sample is deemed inconclusive if 2 replicates are positive, at which point a second‐level criterion within the algorithm compared the average maximum fluorescence from inconclusive samples (eg, inconclusive samples would be deemed negative if replicates have highly variable or low average maximum fluorescence). 5 , 20 In samples tested with “Amprion‐24 h‐αS‐SAA,” based on the value of the maximum fluorescence, additional determination would be made as to whether the case is “Lewy body disease (LBD)‐like” (type 1), “MSA‐like” (type 2), or undetermined. CSF α‐synuclein SAA data are available through the PPMI website (https://www.ppmi-info.org/access-data-specimens/download-data).

dMRI Acquisition

All participants underwent 3T MRI. Axial diffusion weighted images (with or without multiband) were acquired (baseline SAA– [n = 41] and SAA+ [n = 421] cohorts – Siemens = 369, GE = 59, Philips = 34) using a whole‐brain echo planar sequence with the following parameters: gradient directions = 31 to 66, b‐value = 1,000, TR = 500 to 17,000 ms, TE = 61 to 107 ms, in‐plane voxel size of 1.0 to 2.5 mm in x‐ and y‐dimensions, and slice thickness = 2.0 mm (no gap).

dMRI Analysis

The dMRIs were processed using the FMRIB Software Library (fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/), Advance Normalization Tools (stnava.github.io/ANTs/), and custom UNIX shell scripts. The dMRI processing pipeline was automated and previously validated. 13 , 17 Scans were corrected for distortions from eddy currents and head motion with affine transformations. The gradient directions were rotated to reflect these corrections, and non‐brain tissue regions were removed from the scans. The b0 and FA images were calculated using DTIFIT. We applied an established 2‐compartment model to estimate the FW of the extracellular compartment and FW‐corrected fractional anisotropy (FAT) of the tissue compartment for each voxel. 13 , 14 , 21 The primary outcomes were FW and FAT across 60 regions of interest (ROIs), many of which have shown a high sensitivity to PD. 17 FW and FAT maps were calculated using custom written MATLAB R2020b (The Mathworks) codes. 14 To adjust for the effect of different scanner manufacturers (ie, Siemens, GE, and Philips), all diffusion data were harmonized using the ComBat batch‐effect correction tool. 22

Regions of Interest (Gray and White Matter)

Sixty ROIs were collated in Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space based on prior studies and validated dMRI pipelines. 16 , 17 We included 17 regions in the basal ganglia (anterior substantia nigra, posterior substantia nigra, subthalamic nucleus, globus pallidus, putamen, and caudate nucleus), midbrain, thalamus, corpus callosum (premotor corpus callosum, prefrontal corpus callosum, pedunculopontine nucleus, red nucleus, and thalamus), and cerebellum (middle cerebellar peduncle, superior cerebellar peduncle, inferior vermis, dentate nucleus, cerebellar lobule V, and cerebellar lobule VI; Fig 2). We also used 43 white matter tracts based on probabilistic tractography from previous work, including subthalamo‐pallidal, nigrostriatal, corticostriatal, sensorimotor area, cerebellar, parietal, occipital, frontal, and prefrontal commissural tracts. 23 , 24 Baseline FW and FAT were calculated for each region and tract separately for each participant.

Machine Learning and AIDP

AIDP is an artificial intelligence (AI)‐powered imaging software that uses a standard dMRI scan and automated FW image processing with machine learning to differentiate atypical MSAp and PSP parkinsonism from PD. 16 The input features to the model include FW and FAT from 132 brain regions and tracts, as well as age and sex of the participant. The software uses a 2‐tiered classification system to predict the class of the patient, which includes a tier 1 classification for atypical PSP + MSA parkinsonism versus PD and a conditional tier 2 classification for PSP versus MSA. In the current study, we applied AIDP to baseline diffusion scans for the full PD cohort (n = 462; see Fig 1). We sought to examine the concordance between tier 1 AIDP classification of PD and CSF SAA positivity. We report the proportion of SAA+ and SAA– patients within each AIDP predicted class. Data were analyzed using Python, version 3.9.13 (Python Software Foundation).

Statistical Methods

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between groups were subjected to either independent samples t tests (for continuous variables) or chi‐square tests/Fisher's exact tests (for categorical variables). For imaging variables (FW and FAT), the distributions of the dependent variables were evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk test for normality and the homogeneity of covariance were tested using the Box's M‐test.

To maximize the sample size of SAA+ for comparison with the smaller SAA– cohort in PPMI, the propensity score weighting was applied to balance individual differences in background characteristics. The propensity scores were estimated using a probit regression model with SAA status as the dependent variable, and sex and age as predictors. To ensure adequate balance, the standardized mean differences (SMDs) for the covariates were checked (SMD < 0.1). The distributions of age and sex across the SAA+ and SAA– groups were examined before and after match weighting in the full sample.

For baseline comparisons of imaging variables (FW and FAT), 1‐way analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were conducted using full matching weights, adjusting for age and sex. The significance threshold for all analyses was set at 0.05. The p values were corrected using the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). 25 All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.2.0; R Core Team [2022], www.R-project.org/) and R software packages (tidyverse, version 1.3.1 26 ; nlme, version 3.1.157 27 ; ggplot2, version 3.3.6 28 ; rstatix, version 0.7.0, 29 and MatchIt, version 4.7.2 30 ).

Results

Baseline Cross‐Sectional Cohort Clinical Characteristics

We included 41 PD participants that were CSF α‐synuclein SAA– and 421 that were CSF α‐synuclein SAA+ for primary cross‐sectional baseline comparisons (see Fig 1). Baseline olfactory dysfunction (based on UPSIT) was significantly worse in individuals with SAA+ (Table 1). There were no significant baseline differences in age, sex, ethnicity, race, MDS‐UPDRS‐II or III, Hoehn and Yahr stages, or total MoCA scores between SAA+/− cohorts. Participants with SAA– had longer duration of PD motor symptoms (p = 0.02), but there were no group differences in PD diagnosis duration or exposure to dopaminergic treatments before baseline MRI (see Table 1).

Discussion

We tested FW imaging to determine if it is associated with underlying synuclein pathology based on CSF synuclein SAA results. FW imaging has been sensitive to neurodegenerative microstructural changes within the substantia nigra in PD. 12 , 14 , 15 Recently, FW imaging was also used to quantify longitudinal disease‐specific information useful to distinguishing neurodegeneration in PD from atypical parkinsonian syndromes including PSP and MSAp, based on clinical and neuropathologically confirmed diagnoses. 17 , 31 We sought to investigate how CSF‐based α‐synuclein SAA, which has a high sensitivity for diagnosing PD, would be associated with microstructural changes in participants with PD and abnormal baseline DAT‐SPECT. First, we showed high concordance of PD diagnosis predicted by AIDP that were also associated with SAA positivity, in this clinical PD cohort identified from PPMI. Second, the current data revealed limited associations between CSF α‐synuclein SAA results to FW imaging. The presence of decreased FW in the SCP (a PD‐related gray matter region) at baseline in people with PD and SAA+ was a novel association.

Using the AIDP approach, 16 most clinically diagnosed PD participants from PPMI were classified as PD based on FW imaging, with 92% also testing CSF synuclein SAA positive. AIDP predictions were aligned with baseline SAA+ “type 1” (LBD‐like) profiles, previously validated against pathologically confirmed PD. 16 Of the 7.6% (35/462) classified by AIDP as having atypical parkinsonian syndromes, most were SAA+ (30/35, 86%). Nearly half of these were predicted as PSP (16/35, 46%), and the remaining as MSA (including one with SAA+ “type 2” profile). Among SAA– individuals, AIDP predicted MSA in 3 and PSP in 2. Synuclein SAA positivity in PSP‐predicted cases (based on AIDP) may reflect PSP‐type tau cross‐reactivity or presence of Lewy body co‐pathology, 32 , 33 , 34 whereas SAA negativity in MSA‐predicted cases may relate to distinct synuclein strains (eg, differences in fluorescence intensity, aggregation kinetics, etc.). 35 , 36 Future longitudinal and postmortem studies are needed to investigate the progression of microstructural changes (FW and FAT) and underlying pathology in SAA+ versus SAA– individuals.

A robust finding in PD is the presence of elevated FW in the substantia nigra, particularly the posterior portion, which continues to increase over time in early PD. 12 , 14 , 15 The lack of FW differences in the substantia nigra between SAA+ versus SAA– is perhaps not surprising, as the majority were classified as PD with positive DaT‐SPECT and 92% identified as PD in AIDP analysis. We identified limited but significant dMRI differences between participants with SAA+ versus SAA– in a region implicated in PD pathogenesis. Specifically, SAA+ participants demonstrated lower baseline FW in the SCP, compared to SAA– participants. Prior studies have reported structural and functional changes involving the cerebellum (including microstructural alterations involving cerebellar peduncles) in early PD, potentially contributing to the pathophysiology of gait/locomotor, axial and non‐motor symptoms. 37 , 38 The biological basis for lower baseline FW in the SCP among SAA+ participants remains uncertain. One speculative possibility is that early extracellular synuclein accumulation may precede neurodegeneration, potentially influencing FW measures before tissue loss leads to the expected FW increase. This hypothesis requires further testing. Overall, more validation and longitudinal follow‐up are needed to determine whether the association between SAA positivity and these selective regional changes in early PD are biologically meaningful. The absence of more widespread associations between CSF α‐synuclein positivity and dMRI metrics (ie, no significant FAT differences between SAA+ and SAA– in any ROI) suggests that SAA status alone may not stratify substantial neurodegeneration detectable by FW imaging in early PD.

In another recent study on radiological markers of CSF α‐synuclein aggregation in PD, lower FA was reported in the left fronto‐occipital fasciculus in patients with PD with SAA+ compared to PD SAA–. 39 Lower FA from a single compartment model has also been associated with elevated FW in a 2‐compartment model. Further investigation of the PD‐SAA+ group in the study by Droby et al (2025) 39 indicated that the DTI finding was driven by patients with GBA1‐PD, who had significantly lower FA values compared with sporadic PD cases. The study by Droby et al (2025) 39 and our current study differ fundamentally in analytical methods. The FW imaging used a 2‐compartment modeling, versus Droby et al's conventional DTI approach. Using the FW and FAT measures, we explicitly separate the fractional volume of freely diffusing water molecules within the voxel, from the corrected FA value (FAT; ie, after FW component is eliminated). 40 We have previously shown the importance of finding the FW parameter and corrected diffusion tensor that best fit the acquired signal in PD, overcoming limitations of conventional DTI. 15 , 40 Other sources of variability between our studies include differences in ROIs (eg, Johns Hopkins University Diffusion Tensor Imaging based WM atlas 39 ; vs our 60 PD ROIs created in MNI space based on prior studies and validated dMRI pipeline 16 , 17 ), as well as white versus gray matter analyses.

Olfactory dysfunction was the main clinical feature distinguishing SAA+ versus SAA– participants. At baseline, the SAA– group had a mean UPSIT raw score of 33.5 ± 4.0, corresponding to an estimated >30th UPSIT percentile for age‐ and sex‐adjusted norms (≥50 years), consistent with normosmia. 41 In contrast, the SAA+ group showed significantly lower UPSIT scores (mean raw score = 21.7 ± 7.0), corresponding to <15th percentile and consistent with hyposmia. 41 This pattern aligns with prior studies linking hyposmia to synuclein pathology. 42 , 43 The groups differed in motor symptom duration before initial MRI, but not in time since formal PD diagnosis. There were no significant differences in scan intervals or dopaminergic medication exposure at baseline. More study is needed to clarify the relationship among α‐synuclein SAA and motor severity, medication exposure, disease stage, and duration. 3 , 4 , 10 Longer‐term longitudinal studies will also be important to determine whether clinical trajectories diverge over extended follow‐up in SAA+ and SAA– patients.

This study has several limitations. PD participant inclusion from the PPMI dataset was primarily based on clinical diagnosis (and an abnormal DaT‐SPECT), which has lower accuracy especially in early PD disease of <5 years’ duration (eg, probable PD diagnosis at first visit was 71% accurate in a clinicopathological study 44 ). Additionally, accuracy of DaT‐SPECT varies depending on PD disease stage and certainty of diagnosis (eg, sensitivity ranging from 79–100% in people with possible or probable PD). 45 , 46 Further validation is needed to extend the current analyses to larger cohorts of pathologically confirmed PD with CSF SAA status. Future studies would benefit from investigating influence(s) of medications and co‐pathologies (eg, vascular, Alzheimer's disease‐related changes, etc.), as well as mechanisms beyond α‐synuclein pathology, on our FW imaging results. Future studies should also consider groups at‐risk for PD to better understand the interaction of dMRI changes, α‐synuclein SAA positivity, and disease staging. It will also be important to evaluate potential differences between sporadic and genetic PD/parkinsonism (eg, LRRK2‐ and PRKN‐associated PD, which were excluded here), in relation to dMRI and synuclein SAA status. Finally, our current study has limited generalizability due to inclusion of predominantly male, White, non‐Hispanic individuals.

In conclusion, α‐synuclein positivity in early PD was associated with focal FW imaging differences in disease‐relevant regions but did not distinguish widespread patterns of neurodegeneration. These findings suggest that molecular confirmation of α‐synuclein pathology (CSF SAA) provides limited stratification of early microstructural changes detected by dMRI in PD. AIDP performed well across SAA groups, supporting the application of FW imaging in identifying PD‐related neurodegeneration. More longitudinal study will be important to determine whether people with PD and SAA– versus SAA+ represent biologically and clinically distinct trajectories.
