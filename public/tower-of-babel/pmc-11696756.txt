Gallium Electrowinning in Complex Solutions Produced by a Zinc Hydrometallurgy System
Authors: Jie Wang, Fupeng Liu, Bin Liao, Chunfa Liao, Wei Zhang, Haofeng Tan, Dongfeng Hu, Caigui Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Gallium metal is scattered within Fankou lead–zinc ore, and the existing zinc hydrometallurgy process includes a leaching–extraction–electrowinning sequence to recover the gallium metal. However, impurities from lead–zinc ore have many adverse effects on the gallium electrowinning process such as strong hydrogen evolution reaction and low mass transfer rate, which lead to low current efficiencies and poor quality cathode gallium during gallium electrowinning process. In order to achieve efficient separation and recovery of gallium from zinc hydrometallurgy system, the effects of Al, Zn, and OH– impurities on gallium electrowinning were systematically investigated in this study. The research results indicate that the Al impurity did not affect the cathode gallium product, but do affect the current efficiency of the gallium electrowinning process. The Zn impurity had a greater impact on the gallium electrowinning process than the other impurities, and deposition of Zn at the cathode led to a low current efficiency and directly affected the quality of the cathode gallium product. When the Zn concentration exceeded 1 g/L, the purity of the cathode gallium product was reduced to approximately 99%. Both excessively low and high OH– concentrations had negative effects on the gallium electrowinning process, and the maximum value of current efficiency of gallium electrowinning is 54.14% when the alkali concentration was 120 g/L NaOH. The optimized main conditions for gallium electrowinning provide suggestions for realization of gallium production in zinc hydrometallurgy system.

Introduction

Scattered metallic gallium is widely used in semiconductors and alloy materials. The gallium compounds GaAs and GaN are commonly used for the fabrication of optoelectronic devices and optoelectronic integrated circuits. Gallium halides have high catalytic activities and are often used as catalysts for polymerization and dehydration reactions.1,2 Alloy materials composed of gallium and other metals are used as components for engines and actuators. The extensive use of gallium and its compounds in semiconductors, optoelectronic devices, smart materials, and the medical and health fields has driven increasing demand for gallium.3,4 However, gallium is rarely found in nature as a standalone deposit and is often associated with ores such as bauxite and sphalerite in trace amounts.5 Therefore, it is generally considered a byproduct when extracting other metals and is recycled accordingly. Currently, approximately 90% of global gallium comes from the alumina production process.6 The recovery of gallium from secondary resources such as zinc smelting slag, fly ash, bauxite residue, and electronic waste has also gained widespread attention. However, due to the low contents of gallium in these resources, the processes for gallium recovery are relatively complex and costly.7

Due to its sulfur affinity, gallium mainly exists in solid solutions of sulfide minerals containing Fe2+ and Zn2+, such as sphalerite. Due to the similarity in crystal structures and covalent bonding, gallium is more easily incorporated into the sphalerite lattice, and the gallium grade in some zinc sulfide concentrates can reach 0.01%.8 In the conventional zinc hydrometallurgy process, most of the gallium enters the calcines, with a small portion entering the flue dust. Over 95% of the gallium in the calcines exists in the lattice of zinc ferrite.9 However, it is difficult to dissolve zinc ferrite with conventional leaching processes, so most of the gallium is retained in the leaching residue. Subsequent roasting processes typically cause gallium to enter the slag. As a result, gallium is dispersed in zinc leachants, leachates, flue dust, and slag, so it challenging to concentrate it and achieve efficient recovery.10 The oxygen pressure leaching process introduced by Sherritt in Canada improved gallium recovery. In this process, gallium and other metals (e.g., zinc, iron, aluminum, etc.) are leached into the solution with a two-stage countercurrent oxygen pressure leaching process. Then, the gallium is precipitated into the gallium–germanium replacement residue with zinc powder.11 Next, the gallium-rich leachate is obtained by pressure leaching of the replacement residue. Afterward, a gallium-rich solution is obtained by extraction/reverse extraction and other methods. Furthermore, through neutralization, alkali dissolution, removal of impurities, and other operations, gallium-rich electrolytes are obtained. However, there are still small amounts of impurities in the electrolyte that are difficult to remove completely. Finally, the gallium-rich electrolyte is electrowinned to obtain the gallium products.12

There has been relatively little research on gallium electrowinning for zinc hydrometallurgy systems. On the one hand, the recovered gallium is usually a byproduct of other production processes, so impurities, mostly heavy metal ions, are often present during the gallium enrichment process. Removal of these impurities in acidic systems is difficult, and direct electrowinning often leads to simultaneous deposition of impurities, resulting in impure cathode gallium and difficulty in meeting product standards.13−15 On the other hand, the current efficiency of gallium electrowinning under acidic conditions is low, so industrial-scale production is difficult. Several studies have shown that in chloride salt systems, the pH gradually increases as gallium electrowinning progresses, which leads to the formation of solid GaOCl on the cathode and hinders gallium deposition.16 Since gallium is an amphoteric metal, its compounds also exhibit amphoteric properties. Electrowinning of gallium in alkaline solutions allows easier control of the reaction rate and deposition rate during the electrowinning process, resulting in better quality control.17 Therefore, in actual production, alkaline neutralization and dissolution of gallium-rich materials are commonly used, and sodium sulfide is added to remove heavy metal impurities and obtain a relatively pure gallium-rich solution. This achieves the extraction of gallium metal in an alkaline system. Moreover, the gallium electrowinning kinetics in alkaline solutions are favorable, as gallium has a high reaction rate, which increases the speed and efficiency of the electrowinning process and results in a higher current efficiency.18−21

In industry, a mixed solution of NaOH and NaGaO2 is commonly used as the electrolyte for preparing gallium metal. The standard electrode potential of Ga/GaO2– in alkaline solution is −1.3 V, which is much more negative than the hydrogen evolution potential.22 Therefore, during gallium electrowinning, there is a strong hydrogen evolution side reaction, which reduces the current efficiency of the electrowinning process. Additionally, if the concentration of gallium in the electrolyte is low (1–3 g/L), electrolytic of gallium metal is controlled by the mass transfer steps, resulting in very low current efficiencies for gallium electrowinning. Varadharaj and Rao used sodium amalgam as a cathode to electrolyze a sodium aluminate solution containing gallium and greatly improved the current efficiency of gallium deposition.23 However, industrialization of mercury electrolysis is difficult due to the large amount of mercury used, which can be harmful to human health. Dorin et al. used a copper rotating disk electrode to electrolyze a synthetic Bayer solution with a gallium concentration of 3.2 × 10–3 mol/L and achieved a current efficiency of 11%.24 Chongsi used a gallium-plated cathode to obtain a current efficiency of 5.06% for gallium electrowinning at a current density of 1000 A/m2 in a NaGaO2 electrolyte with a gallium concentration of 1 g/L and a temperature of 22 °C.25 It is evident that low current efficiencies have hindered the development of gallium electrowinning.

In addition, the importance of impurity removal and purification during the entire gallium production process cannot be ignored. A high impurity content affects the electrowinning process and intensifies the hydrogen evolution side reaction and also decreases the quality of the gallium product. For example, if the vanadium ion content is too high, the electrode may experience anode burning, making it difficult to carry out the electrolytic deposition process.26 To improve the current efficiency of gallium electrowinning, high overpotential metals or metal alloys can be used as cathode materials to suppress the hydrogen evolution side reaction.27 Additionally, enhancing the mass transfer process improves the current efficiency of gallium electrowinning. After the cathodic activation treatment, the surface roughness of the stainless-steel electrode increases, providing additional active sites for gallium deposition, which accelerates gallium electrowinning and improves the current efficiency.28−30 In summary, the interaction of gallium and impurity metals in the gallium electrowinning process of zinc hydrometallurgy system is still unclear, resulting in the difficulty for achieving the efficient recovery of gallium in the zinc hydrometallurgy system.

In this work, the effects of various parameters on gallium electrowinning were first investigated by using thermodynamics analysis and experimental study. Then, the behavior of impurity ions on gallium electrowinning was explored. And the formation mechanism of cathode Ga was discussed finally. By clarifying the behavior of impurities during gallium electrowinning and the parameters that directly affect the current efficiency, this paper provides clear research ideas and directions for gallium electrowinning, as well as theoretical support and guidance for gallium electrowinning production processes.

Experimental Methods

An electrochemical workstation (CHI760E) produced by Shanghai Chenhua Instruments Co., Ltd., was used in the gallium electrowinning experiments. A SUS304 stainless steel electrode (1 cm × 1 cm) was used as the working electrode and counter electrode, and a Hg/HgO electrode served as the reference electrode in the three-electrode system, where the standard electrode potential of Hg/HgO electrode is 0.098 V. A certain volume of gallium-rich solution or preelectrolyte was first measured and added to a magnetic stirrer and stirred at a speed of 150 rpm to weaken the effect of concentration polarization on the experiment. The above solutions were used to carry out gallium electrowinning investigations in a three-electrode system using an electrochemical workstation. The effects of temperature (20–60 °C), current density (0.05–0.25 A/cm2), and time (0–14 h) on the gallium electrowinning process were investigated by controlling the one-factor-variable method, in which the current density was controlled by using a multicurrent step module in the electrochemical workstation. In addition, the effect of electrolyte such as composition (NaOH concentration, Ga concentration), impurity ions (Al, Zn) on the gallium electrowinning process was investigated. The optimum conditions for some process parameters and the electrowinning behavior of impurity ions were investigated by comparing and analyzing the current efficiency, yield and cathode gallium quality.

Analysis and Characterization

The multicurrent step technique electrochemical workstation (CHI760E) was applied to monitor the cathode potential in real time, record the data, and plot the curves during the gallium electrowinning process. All the potential values measured were calibrated by using the following equation: Evs RHE = Evs Hg/HgO + 0.098 + 0.059pH. The concentrations of gallium before and after electrowinning and the impurity content of the cathode gallium were analyzed with a TR788-DGS-III inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP–AES 7400, HORIM). The main phase of cathode gallium was identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD; PANalytical X’Pert Pro Powder, Almelo, The Netherlands) using a Cu Kα radiation source with a 40 kV acceleration potential and current of 40 mA. The XRD diffractograms were analyzed by using Jade 6.0 software. The micromorphology and elemental distribution of the cathode gallium were determined via SEM–EDS (MIRA 3 LMH, TESCAN Brno, S.r.o., Brno, Czech Republic).

Electrowinning of Complex Gallium-Rich Electrolytes

Effect of Temperature

Figure 2 investigates the effect of temperature (20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C) on the gallium electrowinning process, where the choice of temperature is based on the melting point of gallium metal (29.78 °C).22 The results in Figure 2a indicated that temperature had a significant impact on the cathode potential. At the beginning of the electrowinning process, the cathode potentials are difficult to stabilize in a short period of time, resulting sharp potential peaks at the beginning of all curves in Figure 2a. The cathode potentials in all of the curves initially increased and subsequently stabilized at higher temperatures, resulting in more positive stable cathode potentials. Moreover, the overpotential for hydrogen evolution decreased with increasing temperature. Higher temperatures increased the likelihood of hydrogen gas evolution at the cathode. Therefore, the temperature should be reduced to avoid hydrogen evolution. Figure 2b shows that both excessively high and low temperatures are unfavorable for gallium electrowinning. At a temperature of 30 °C, the current efficiency and yield reached their maximum values of 52.69% and 24.97%, respectively. Low temperatures resulted in gallium deposition on the cathode surface without automatic detachment, which affected the current efficiency and collection of gallium products. Increasing the deposition temperature appropriately accelerated mass transfer in the solution, facilitated gallium deposition, and improved the current efficiency. However, when the temperature was too high, the dissolution rate of gallium dripping from the cathode increased, resulting in the loss of gallium. Based on these findings, the optimal temperature for electrowinning was 30 °C.

Effect of the Current Density

The effect of different current densities on gallium electrowinning is displayed in Figure 3. These results suggested that as the current density increased, the stable cathode potential became more negative. Within a certain range, increases in the current density led to more negative cathode potentials, which favored gallium electrowinning. However, an excessively high current density resulted in a sharp increase in the tank voltage and a more pronounced hydrogen evolution reaction, leading to a decreased current efficiency. The gallium electrowinning yield increased with increasing current density, but the current efficiency initially increased and then decreased. When the current density was appropriately increased, diffusion replenished the metal ions in a timely manner, which minimized ion depletion in the solution and improved the current efficiency. A maximum current efficiency of 67.46% was achieved at a current density of 0.10 A/cm2. The subsequent decrease was due to the depletion of GaO2– in the electrolyte near the cathode Ga generation region when the current density was too high. Diffusion did not replenish the GaO2– ions in time, leading to rapid depletion. This triggered the deposition of impurity ions and intensified the hydrogen evolution reaction, resulting in a decreased current efficiency. These results are supported by Figure 3b. Therefore, a lower current density is recommended, but extremely low current densities are not suitable because they result in lower production efficiency. Based on the comprehensive analysis of these results, a current density of 0.10 A/cm2 was deemed optimal.

Effect of different current densities on gallium electrowinning (a) cathode potential–time diagram (b) current efficiency–current density diagram (T = 30 °C, V = 30 mL, t = 2 h).

Effect of Time

Figure 4 shows the concentration versus current efficiency changes at 2 2 h intervals. The results show that as the time increased, both the gallium concentration and current efficiency gradually decreased. When the gallium concentration decreased from 65.25 to 6.98 g/L, the current efficiency decreased from 59.64% to 13.25%. The decrease in current efficiency was more significant in the first 6 h, indicating a higher current utilization efficiency at high gallium concentrations. From 6 to 14 h, as the gallium concentration decreased, additional electrical energy was used for water electrolysis, while the rest was used for impurity deposition, resulting in a decrease in the current efficiency. As the electrowinning time progressed, the gallium concentration gradually decreased, but the current efficiency also decreased with decreasing gallium concentration. Therefore, a cyclic electrowinning method was considered, in which a large volume of solution was used to achieve external circulation during the electrowinning process, thus maintaining the gallium concentration within a certain range and improving the current efficiency. As shown by the results in Table 3, at a current density of j = 0.25 A/cm2, the current efficiency in 14 h was 28.80%, while at a current density of j = 0.10 A/cm2, the current efficiency in 14 h was 47.16%. Therefore, a lower current density led to a higher current efficiency, which is consistent with the results in Figure 3b.

Gallium Electrowinning from Impurity-Free Electrolytes

Effect of NaOH Concentration

As shown in Figure 5a, increases in the NaOH concentration shifted the cathode potential in the positive direction and caused fluctuations. Both the current efficiency and yield initially increased and then decreased with increasing alkalinity, indicating that extremely low or high alkalinity hindered gallium electrowinning. This occurred because at very low alkalinity, the solution had poor conductivity, resulting in a low current efficiency. The addition of sodium hydroxide increased the ionic strength of the solution and reduced the solution resistance, thereby enhancing the current efficiency. Combined with the potential–pH diagram in Figures 1 and 5a, it can be seen that the equilibrium potential for the hydrogen precipitation Reaction 2 shifts negatively as the solution pH rises with increasing NaOH concentration. However, the actual cathodic potential is positively shifted the closer to the equilibrium potential. The hydrogen precipitation overpotential decreases, leading to an intensification of the hydrogen precipitation reaction and a gradual decrease in the current efficiency. Therefore, when the NaOH concentration was increased appropriately, the current efficiency increased. However, when the NaOH concentration exceeded 120 g/L NaOH, the current efficiency started to decrease. This occurred because the continuous increase in alkalinity reduced the overpotential required for hydrogen evolution, which intensified the hydrogen evolution reaction and led to a decrease in the current efficiency. Additionally, excessively high alkalinity can lead to gallium resolubilization and result in losses. When the NaOH concentration was 120 g/L NaOH, the current efficiency and yield reached their maximum values, which were 54.14% and 22.34%, respectively. Therefore, a gallium electrowinning process with an alkaline concentration of 120 g/L NaOH is considered appropriate.

Effect of the Gallium Concentration

The effect of different gallium concentrations on gallium electrowinning is shown in Figure 6. The results indicated that with increasing concentration, the cathode potential shifted negatively, and the overall cathode potential became more negative, while the current efficiency gradually increased, and the yield gradually decreased. At low concentrations resulting in a decreased current efficiency. When the concentration was 5 g/L, the current efficiency was only 8.74%. At high concentrations, concentration polarization occurs, so higher concentrations limit the increase in current efficiency. At 65 g/L gallium, the current efficiency was 54.14%. Therefore, with decreasing gallium concentrations, the current efficiency also decreased. With the concentration of gallium decreasing from 65 to 20 g/L, the decreased current efficiency was less obvious. However, once the gallium concentration in the solution was lower than 20 g/L, the current efficiency decreased significantly, which increased the power consumption and posed a considerable safety hazard due to the large amount of hydrogen gas produced. Thus, the results in Figures 4 and 6 show that the actual gallium electrowinning process required an outlet for discharging liquid gallium at the bottom of the electrolyzer to prevent gallium loss due to redissolution, and the gallium concentration during the gallium electrowinning cycle should be controlled at approximately 20–30 g/L.

Effect of Impurity Ions on Gallium Electrowinning

Effect of the Aluminum Impurity

The effect of the aluminum concentration on gallium electrowinning was investigated with a gallium concentration of 65 g/L, a current density of 0.25 A/cm2, an electrolyte volume of 30 mL, an electrowinning temperature of 30 °C, an electrowinning time of 2 h, and an alkalinity of 120 g/L, and the results are shown in Figure 7. With increasing aluminum concentration, the cathode potential became more negative, and the presence of AlO2– led to greater fluctuations in the cathode potential, resulting in an increased hydrogen evolution reaction. When there was no AlO2– present, the current efficiency and yield of gallium electrowinning reached their maximum values of 54.14% and 22.34%, respectively. With increasing aluminum concentration, the current efficiency and yield of gallium electrowinning were affected to some extent. The Ga concentration of the postelectrowinning solution is generally around 5 g/L. However, since aluminum is present in the electrolyte, the aluminum concentration is high. Therefore, calcium oxide is first used to remove Al from the postelectrowinning solution, and then the dealuminaed postsolution will be further returned to the alkaline dissolution and preparation of gallium-rich electrolyte step in order to achieve the recycling of the process.

Effect of the Zinc Impurity

The effect of the zinc concentration on gallium electrowinning is presented in Figure 8. The results indicated that the introduction of zinc impurities led to a more negative cathode potential in the gallium electrowinning process, and the cathode potential continued to decrease with increasing zinc concentration, which intensified the cathodic hydrogen evolution reaction. Moreover, since the deposition potential (Figure 1a) for zinc in alkaline systems is similar to that of gallium, zinc deposited and accumulate on the cathode, resulting in poorer quality for the cathode gallium. The results in Figure 8b also indicate that introduction of the zinc impurity changed the current efficiency for gallium electrowinning, and the current efficiency decreased with increasing zinc concentration. Based on the distribution of zinc in the cathode gallium shown in Table 4, zinc did exist in the cathode gallium, which was consistent with the previous analysis. When the zinc concentration in the solution was 0.96 g/L, the purity of the cathode gallium was significantly reduced, and subsequent purification of crude gallium was required through acid pickling and other steps.31 Therefore, impurity Zn needs to be removed as much as possible at the front end of the gallium electrowinning process by adding sulfides.

Distribution of Zinc Impurities in the Cathode Gallium

According to the SEM images in Figure 9a–d, gallium formed spherical particles on the cathode surface during the electrowinning process, while the zinc impurity formed flake particles on the cathode and gallium surface. The SEM images in e,f showed that the gallium particles grew and that the particle sizes increased with increasing electrowinning time. When the electrowinning time was 120 s, the gallium particles exhibited surface fracture and particle collapse, and the gradually formed gallium particles were clustered into irregular shapes. The elemental surface distribution diagram in Figure 10 showed that the distribution of the zinc impurity in the cathode gallium was not uniform, and the elemental correlation was not high; however, most of the zinc existed on the surfaces of the gallium particles.

Discussion on the Mechanism of Cathode Ga Formation

Cathode gallium was solidified, crushed and sieved into gallium powder for XRD analysis. Since the melting point of gallium is 29.78 °C, the test temperature needs to be lower than 29.78 °C. The XRD pattern of cathode gallium is shown in Figure 11. As can be seen from Figure 11, the diffraction peaks of cathodic gallium correspond to the standard card of gallium (PDF no. 05-0601). Based on previous research and experimental results, the mechanism for cathode Ga formation through electrowinning and redox reactions is shown in Figure 12. For gallium-rich solutions containing impurities, mechanical stirring can be used to accelerate the mass transfer rate and to make the mass transfer mode convective. During this process, the electrolyte, cathode and anode form in different areas, and the metal ions (e.g., Ga, Zn) migrate to the cathode area and form metal particles on the cathode surface, while water is reduced to hydrogen gas at the cathode. In the anode region, OH– migration dominates, and the main reaction at the anode surface is the oxidation of OH– to produce oxygen gas.
