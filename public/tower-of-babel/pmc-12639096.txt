Physical activity modifies the association between ambient air pollution and comorbid depression and disability in activities of daily living
Authors: Yuquan Chen, Yanwei You, Jiarong He, Qi Zhang, Yuechuan Zhang, Wenjia Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to explore the effects of physical activity, ambient air pollutants, and their interactions on the risk of depression, ADL disability, and their comorbidity. The study followed 11,875 adults aged 45 and older from China. Time-varying Cox proportional hazards regression models, adjusted for year, month, season, and spatial autocorrelation, city, demographic, health-related behavioral, and socioeconomic factors, were used to analyze the relationships between pollutants and various health outcomes. Over a median follow-up of 7 years, 2,227 participants developed depression (18.8%), 212 developed ADL disability (1.8%), and 183 developed comorbidity (1.5%). Throughout the follow-up period, the annual average concentration of PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3 and NO2, had a median value of 25.2 (IQR 18.2, 33.0) µg/m³, 43.9 (IQR 31.6, 58.8) µg/m³, 76.2 (IQR 53.3, 103.0) µg/m³, 88.6 (IQR 82.0, 97.9) µg/m³, and 26.0 (IQR 19.4, 33.5) µg/m³, respectively. For every 10 µg/m³ increase in pollutants, the risk of comorbid depression and ADL disability increased significantly: PM1 by 33.3% (aHR = 1.33, 95% CI 1.24–1.43), PM2.5 by 16% (aHR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.11–1.20), PM10 by 7% (aHR = 1.07, 95% CI 1.05–1.10), O3 by 14% (aHR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.08–1.20), and NO2 by 44% (aHR = 1.44, 95% CI 1.33–1.55). Physically active individuals had significantly reduced risks of comorbidity compared with non-physically active ones by 10.2% for PM2.5 and 6.1% for PM10. Higher air pollution levels are associated with increased risks of depression and comorbid depression and ADL disability. However, physical activity may mitigate these risks for these two conditions.Keywords: Air pollutants, Depression, Activities of daily living disability, Comorbidity, Physical activitySubject terms: Physiology, Health care, Risk factors

Introduction

Depression and activities of daily living (ADL) disability are highly prevalent conditions, especially in China. Depression is a psychological condition marked by ongoing emotions of sadness, despair, a lack of interest or enjoyment, sleep irregularities, and additional symptoms1. Nowadays, depression has become a major mental health issue for middle-aged and older adults, with a prevalence of 22.6% (95% CI: 18.9–26.7%) for this population in China in a meta-analysis2. Activities of Daily Livings (ADLs) include everyday tasks that people carry out independently to take care of themselves, such as eating, getting dressed, bathing, and moving around3. ADLs are a vital measure for evaluating physical activity and an individual’s ability to independently manage their daily routines4–6. ADL disability, therefore, indicates difficulties or limitations in performing these essential tasks due to health issues or physical functional impairments7. According to a study covering the years 2011 to 2018, the prevalence of ADL disability among Chinese older adults increased from 10.2% in 2011 to 10.5% in 20188. Numerous studies have shown a robust link between depression and ADL disability9,10. Depression often occurs alongside ADL disability, and the existence of depressive symptoms is a significant predictor of the future onset of ADL disability, and vice versa11,12. A cross-sectional study involving 5,863 older individuals reported an association between ADL disability and its specific areas, such as personal care, transfer, and medical care, with depression13. However, research on the comorbidity of the two conditions remains limited, especially concerning external environmental risk factors and preventive measures.

Many studies have indicated that depression is influenced by external factors like environmental conditions, and air pollution is a severe risk factor for depression-related cases, leading to major depression14,15. A recent study in South Korea suggested that exposure to air pollution, whether short-term or long-term, increases the risk of developing depression15. Similarly, some studies have shown a relationship between air pollution and ADL disability. For example, a study based on 15,973 older residents of 866 counties and cities in China indicated that air pollution increased the odds of ADL disability16. However, most of these studies are cross-sectional, with very few cohort studies providing higher-level evidence. Compared to research on depression, detailed exploration of the relationship between air pollution and ADL disability is scarce, and studies examining air pollution’s effects on comorbid depression and ADL disability are lacking. Furthermore, these studies do not comprehensively examine how different air pollutants (such as particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, ozone, etc.) vary in their associations with these outcomes, which is crucial for developing targeted prevention and intervention strategies17.

Physical activity (PA) has recently gained increasing attention as a novel approach to preventing and combating depression and ADL disability. Numerous meta-analyses have demonstrated that various forms of PA, including sports and exercise, effectively alleviate depressive symptoms and prevent other mental health issues18,19, with moderate-certainty evidence supporting this association20. Simultaneously, there are also many studies that reveal the significant benefits of PA in preventing and alleviating the onset and progression of ADL disability21–24. However, few studies have indicated whether PA can play a significant role in reducing the risk of comorbidity of these two conditions. In particular, no scholars have explored the impact of PA on the relationship between air pollutants and comorbid depression and ADL disability25,26. If PA can offset the harmful effects of air pollutants on depression, ADL disability, or their comorbidity, promoting PA remains beneficial. On the other hand, if air pollution significantly diminishes the health benefits of PA, measures should be taken to reduce pollution exposure during outdoor activities or to improve air quality, ensuring that people can safely engage in PA27,28.

Methodology

Data source

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is overseen by the National Development Institute at Peking University29. CHARLS aims to collect and analyze health, socioeconomic, and demographic information of individuals aged 45 and older living in various communities throughout China. Initiated in 2011, the first CHARLS survey was conducted through household interviews using a comprehensive method that combined multi-stage, stratified, and clustered sampling across 150 counties in 28 provinces and cities nationwide. Follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020, adding further socio-demographic data, along with physical, biological, and health assessments. The Biomedical Ethics Review Committee at Peking University (IRB00001052-11015) granted ethical approval for CHARLS, and all participants provided informed consent.

Data extraction

In this research study, subjects were tracked using their unique ID over five distinct waves of the survey conducted in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. At baseline, 13,900 participants were included. Exclusions were made for those classified as having ADL disability (n = 59) and depression (n = 440) at the 2011 baseline. There were no missing data for ADL disability or depression at baseline (n = 0). Because most time-varying covariates in CHARLS change slowly, we imputed sporadically missing observations using the Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) method30. LOCF preserves each participant’s longitudinal trajectory without adding artificial between-person variability, and sensitivity analyses based on complete-case data and multiple imputation produced comparable estimates. Consequently, the LOCF imputation method was utilized to estimate the missing covariate data for educational level, residence, marital status, medical insurance, income, employment status, season and spatial autocorrelation, city, interview time, smoking status, sleep duration, physical activity and alcohol consumption. For gender and age variables, data reported in any wave were used to logically infer the corresponding data for other waves. All other covariates were filled in using the 2011 baseline data, as most conditions remained stable or showed minimal changes over time31. After applying LOCF imputation to partially missing time‑varying covariates, we checked for any residual missingness. Finally, we excluded 21 participants whose city-level air-pollutant exposure could not be assigned in any survey wave, and 1,505 participants who had no observed value for at least one covariate across all five waves. Because the LOCF approach requires at least one non-missing observation to carry forward, these individuals could not be imputed. This yielded a final analytic sample of 11,875 participants Fig. 1.

Exposure variables: air pollutants (PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3, and NO2)

High-resolution NO₂ exposure data were obtained from the China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) datasets (https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html). Specifically, we used 1 km-resolution annual NO2 grids for January 2019-December 202032–35 and 10 km-resolution annual NO2 grids for January 2010 – December 201835. In addition, 1 km-resolution data for PM1, PM2.5, PM10, and O3 covering January 2010-December 2020 were obtained from the same CHAP source. This dataset is recognized as a reliable and comprehensive source of air pollution data for China and has been widely utilized in numerous related studies36–38.

Because exposure levels vary from year to year, annual pollutant concentrations were treated as time-varying exposures. Residential location in CHARLS is available only at the city level. For each survey wave, we linked every participant to the previous calendar year’s city-wide mean concentration (µg/m3) of each pollutant—assigned at the geographic centroid of their city of residence—to treat annual pollutant levels as time-varying exposures and capture a one-year lag effect39–41. For example, the annual average concentrations of the five air pollutants for 2010 were used to correspond with the 2011 wave data in CHARLS.

Outcome variable: ADL disability, depression, and comorbidity

The Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (Katz ADL)42 was employed to evaluate the older adults’ competence in performing daily tasks. The CHARLS questionnaire covered 6 essential areas: feeding, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, and bathing. For each of these activities, respondents could choose from 4 options: (1) “No, I have no trouble”; (2) “I struggle but can still manage”; (3) “Yes, I struggle and need assistance”; and (4) “I am unable to do it”. Individuals who needed assistance or were unable to complete one or more of these 6 ADL tasks were classified as having an ADL disability. In contrast, those who managed all 6 activities independently were considered capable of performing their ADLs without assistance.

The depression levels of the selected individuals were assessed utilizing the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CESD-10). This scale has shown reliability and validity among Chinese older adults in many previous studies43,44. This concise version evaluates the frequency of certain moods on a scale from 0 to 3, where 0 signifies “rarely or never,” 1 denotes “occasionally,” 2 indicates “sometimes,” and 3 means “often or always.” Reverse scoring was applied to positive items. The CESD‑10 items were administered by trained interviewers during face‑to‑face, computer‑assisted personal interviews (CAPI) conducted on tablet computers. The scores from all 10 items were totaled to generate a depressive score, representing each participant’s overall depression status. The depression severity score ranged from 0 to 30, where higher scores indicated more intense depressive symptoms. To identify depression, a threshold CESD-10 score of 10 or above was used for the participants44.

Based on the presence of depression and ADL disability, all participants were stratified into four groups: neither depression nor ADL disability, depression only, ADL disability only, and comorbid depression and ADL disability.

Interaction variable: physical activity

The survey aimed at evaluating the PA levels of participants was from the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). Throughout the five survey waves from 2011 to 2020, participants were asked about their involvement in PA sessions lasting a minimum of 10 min each. These activities were divided into categories such as vigorous (carrying heavy loads, digging, farming, doing aerobics, cycling quickly, or biking with cargo), moderate (carrying lighter items, cycling at a regular pace, mopping, practicing tai chi, or walking briskly), and low-intensity activities (walking to work or home, or walking for leisure and exercise). The survey also collected data on the frequency of these activities, ranging from once to seven times a week, and their daily duration, categorized into segments (10 to 30 min, 30 min to 2 h, 2–4 h, and > 4 h).

For consistency in analysis and due to the lack of specific time durations in the questionnaire, we used a median value approach as adopted by other scholars45,46. Thus, the time segments were interpreted as follows: more than 10 min but less than 30 min as 20 min, over 30 min but under two hours as 75 min, between two and four hours as 180 min, and more than four hours as 240 min. The total weekly duration scores for each level of PA were calculated by multiplying the number of days the activity was performed by the median time spent on that activity per day. The overall score for physical activity volume (PAV) was expressed using the metabolic equivalent (MET)47. It was computed as follows: PAV equals 8.0 times the score for weekly vigorous PA duration plus 4.0 times the score for weekly moderate PA duration plus 3.3 times the score for weekly low-intensity PA duration. According to the IPAQ guidelines, a total PAV below 600 MET-minutes per week was classified as physical inactivity (PI)46,48.

Confounders

Confounders were selected based on prior research and included the following: (1) demographic factors, including age group (< 70 years old, 70–80 years old, ≥ 80 years old), gender (male or female), educational level (illiterate, primary school and below, middle school and high school, technical school and above), residence (urban or rural), marital status (married or unmarried); (2) health-related behavior factors, including smoking status (still smoking, former smoking, and never smoking), alcohol consumption (current drinking, non-drinking), sleep duration (short, nighttime sleep duration < 7 h; normal, nighttime sleep duration 7–9 h; long, nighttime sleep duration > 9 h) and physical activity (physical inactivity and physically active); (3) socioeconomic variables, including medical insurance (NMI; UEMI; URMI; URRMI and NCMI), income (based on the quintiles of personal annual income for the five waves of the 10,301 participants included, specifically: Quintile 1, personal annual income of 0 to 2,880¥; Quintile 2, personal annual income of 2,880¥ to 11,000¥; Quintile 3, personal annual income of 11,000¥ to 21,900¥; Quintile 4, personal annual income of 21,900¥ to 41,000¥; Quintile 5, personal annual income of greater than 41,000¥); employment status (unemployed, agricultural job, non-agricultural job ) and city.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics (wave 2011) of study participants were summarized using frequency (n) and percentage (%), categorized by neither depression nor ADL disability, depression only, ADL disability only, and comorbid depression and ADL disability. Owing to the large sample size of included variables, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to evaluate the normality of the annual average concentrations of various pollutants49,50. For normally distributed data, the mean and standard deviation (SD) were reported. For data with a skewed distribution, the median along with the 25th and 75th percentiles were presented.

Significant variations in the annual average concentrations of air pollutants across survey waves made it challenging to define consistent exposure, potentially introducing immortal time bias51. To address this, Cox proportional regression with time-varying exposure and confounders was performed to mitigate this bias. This regression analysis was employed individually for each air pollutant to investigate the association between air pollutant exposure and depression risk, ADL disability risk, and comorbid depression and ADL disability, with survey waves used as the timescale. According to the previous studies52,53, several models were constructed as follows: Model I was a crude model, unadjusted for any factors. Model II adjusted for age (continuous variable) and gender. Model III further adjusted for socioeconomic status (SES) factors, including education level, residence, marital status, medical insurance, income, employment status, and city. It should be noted that the city was modeled using random intercepts to account for potential between-city variability. Model IV added adjustments for behavioral factors to Model 3, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, and PA. Simultaneously, adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated and reported. It should be noted that the analyses above were based on single air pollutant models. All reported aHRs and their CIs were based on comparisons with neither depression nor ADL disability.

To assess non-linearity in the relationship between air pollutant exposure and depression, ADL disability, and their comorbidity risk, two Cox proportional hazards models with time-varying exposure were fitted: one with a linear term for each air pollutant and another with a natural spline term (degrees of freedom = 3) for air pollutants, both based on Model IV. A likelihood ratio test was then conducted to compare the two models, and a significant P-value from this test indicated evidence of non-linearity.

Subsequently, sensitivity analyses were conducted based on Model IV controlling for all confounding factors, which included multiple pollutant models, including double-pollutant and triple-pollutant models. In addition, another sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the effect of NO2 by restricting the analysis to the period of 2010–2018, where data quality remained consistent across all years. This analysis allowed us to evaluate whether the effect estimates of NO2 during this period were consistent with the estimates derived from the full dataset (2010–2020). Simultaneously, we evaluated the interactions between each air pollutant exposure and PA levels (physical inactivity and physically active) and conducted interaction comparisons under different models using the Wald test. Finally, subgroup analyses were performed based on these stratifications, controlling for all confounding variables.

Stata 16.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA) and R software (version 4.3.2) were used for all statistical analyses and images drawing. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant among all analyses.

Results

Characteristics of included participants

The final analytic sample comprised 11,875 participants, of whom 9,253 (78.0%) remained free of depression and ADL disability, 2,227 (18.8%) developed depression only, 212 (1.8%) developed ADL disability only, and 183 (1.5%) experienced both conditions over a median follow-up of 7 years (IQR 4–9 years). Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of included participants. Compared to the neither depression nor ADL disability group, only participants with depression only were more likely to be female, younger, have a technical school education level or higher, be unmarried, reside in rural areas, never smoking, not drinking, have URRMI and NCMI medical insurance, fall within the lowest income quintile of 0 to 2,880¥, did agricultural job, having short sleep duration, and be physically inactive. Similarly, participants with ADL disability only were more likely to be female, older, illiterate or technical school and above, be unmarried, former smoking, not drinking, have UEMI, URRMI and URMI medical insurance, fall within the lowest income quintile of 0 to 2,880¥, unemployed, having long sleep duration, and be physically inactive. Lastly, the comorbid depression and ADL disability were more likely to be female, 70–80 years old, have a technical school education level or higher, be unmarried, reside in rural areas, never or former smoking, not drinking, have URRMI and NCMI medical insurance, fall within the lowest income quintile of 0 to 2,880¥, unemployed, having short and long sleep duration, and be physically inactive. Baseline comparisons showed that the excluded group did not differ meaningfully from the analyzed cohort in all covariates, or pollutant exposure (all P > 0.10).

Associations of ADL disability, depression and comorbid them with air pollutant exposure

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test shows that the annual average concentrations of all air pollutants do not follow a normal distribution (P < 0.05). Therefore, during the entire follow-up period, the annual average concentrations of PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3 and NO2 were reported as medians (25th percentile, 75th percentile): 25.2 (18.2, 33.0) µg/m³, 43.9 (31.6, 58.8) µg/m³, 76.2 (53.3, 103.0) µg/m³, 88.6 (82.0, 97.9) µg/m³, and 26.0 (19.4, 33.5) µg/m³, respectively. After adjusting for all confounders (Model IV), compared with neither depression nor ADL disability group, each ten-unit increase in the annual average concentration of PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3 and NO2 (10 µg/m3), corresponded to an increased risk of only depression by 14% (aHR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.12–1.17), 6% (aHR = 1.06, 95% CI 1.05–1.07), 3% (aHR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.04), 5% (aHR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.06) and 19% (aHR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.16–1.22) higher risk of depression only, respectively (Table 2); each ten-unit increase in the annual average concentration of PM2.5, corresponded to an increased risk of only ADL disability by 4% (aHR = 1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.08), while no significant association was observed between PM1, PM10, O3, NO2 and ADL disability only. Similarly, for each ten-unit increase in the annual average concentration of PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3 and NO2 (10 µg/m3), corresponded to an increased risk of comorbid depression and ADL disability by 32% (aHR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.23–1.41), 15% (aHR = 1.15, 95% CI 1.11–1.20), 7% (aHR = 1.07, 95% CI 1.04–1.09), 13% (aHR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.07–1.19) and 43% (aHR = 1.43, 95% CI 1.32–1.54), respectively. Figure 3 displays the natural cubic splines of the annual average exposure concentration of all pollutants and the risk of depression only, ADL disability only and comorbid depression and ADL disability, respectively. The P-nonlinearity for PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3, NO2, and the risk of depression only was < 0.001, 0.001, < 0.001, 0.001, 0.002, respectively, indicating a linear relationship between all five air pollutants and the risk of depression only. Simultaneously, the P-nonlinearity for PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3, NO2, and the risk of ADL disability only was 0.834, 0.015, 0.338, 0.124, 0.112, respectively, indicating only a linear relationship between PM2.5 and the risk of ADL disability only. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between depression, ADL disability, and their comorbidity in the context of air pollution, particularly with precise exposure to various specific air pollutants. Simultaneously, the strong evidence of this study is supported by 9 years of longitudinal tracking data from a nationally representative sample of 11,875 participants. Our study demonstrates that heightened exposure to a spectrum of air pollutants, namely PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3, and NO2, is significantly correlated with an elevated risk of depression, as well as comorbid depression and ADL disability. However, it is only PM2.5 that shows a significant association with an increased risk of ADL disability. After adjusting for all confounding variables, NO2 was identified as having the strongest effect on depression risk only or comorbid depression and ADL disability, followed by PM1, PM2.5, O3, and PM10, highlighting their individual contributions to depression or comorbidity risk. Additionally, PA can reduce the risk of depression related to PM1, PM2.5, O3 and NO2 by 6%, 3%, 3% and 11%, respectively; and can offset the ADL disability risk caused by exposure to PM1 and O3, rendering it no longer statistically significant; and can reduce the risk of comorbid depression and ADL disability related to PM2.5 and PM10 by 10.2% and 6.1%, respectively.

Our results are consistent with many other relevant studies. Firstly, Yang et al. conducted a study involving 389,185 participants across multiple cities and found a significant association between long-term exposure to air pollution and increased rates of depression54. Similarly, in a Rome-based cohort of 1,733,331 adults free of mental disorders at baseline, Nobile et al. found that each interquartile-range increase in PM2.5 (1.13 µg m⁻³) was associated with a 1% higher incidence of depression (HR = 1.135, 9% CI 1.086–1.186)55. Furthermore, for ADL disability, Ren et al. examined a cohort of 6,216 older individuals and reported that chronic exposure to air pollution was correlated with a higher risk of developing ADL disability56. However, no previous studies have thoroughly explored the relationship between specific air pollutants and the risk of their comorbidity. This study is the first to investigate this issue in detail. This study produced robust statistical associations between exposure to PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3 and NO2 and the risks of depression, ADL disability, and their comorbidity. Although causal inference cannot be drawn, the observed interaction with physical activity indicates that maintaining regular PA could be a feasible personal strategy to partially offset these risks. According to the results of our study, compared to sedentary behavior, PA is the most cost-effective means to reduce a range of health impacts caused by exposure to various air pollutants. Research has shown that PA can alleviate the depressive effects of air pollution. For example, a study on 22 868 students and demonstrated that individuals engaging in moderate to high levels of physical activity experienced fewer depressive symptoms related to air pollution exposure compared to those with lower levels of physical activity57. Regarding the benefits of PA in alleviating ADL disability in the context of air pollution, Jiang et al. followed 4 537 older adults and concluded that physical activity significantly attenuated the negative impact of air pollution on physical function37. Our results are the first to indicate that PA is beneficial in reducing the risk of comorbidity.

The exact mechanism linking air pollutants, depression, and ADL disability still remains unclear. As for the benefits of PA, its positive effects on health have been demonstrated by many relevant studies. Mikkelsen et al. investigated the mechanisms through which PA alleviates depression and found that regular exercise increases the levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which promotes neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity. These changes are believed to counteract the neurotoxic effects of air pollution, thereby reducing depressive symptoms58. In the context of ADL disability, Chen et al. studied the biological mechanisms by which PA mitigates functional decline. Their research indicated that physical activity enhances muscle strength and cardiovascular health, which are critical for maintaining functional independence. Additionally, PA was shown to reduce systemic inflammation and improve endothelial function, both of which are negatively impacted by air pollution and contribute to ADL disability59.

However, there is still a lack of research explaining the mechanisms behind the development of these two conditions and their comorbidity in the context of air pollution. Based on our study and existing literature, we propose several hypotheses regarding the mechanisms through which PA may mitigate the harmful effects of air pollutant exposure: One potential mechanism is the regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Chronic exposure to air pollution can dysregulate the HPA axis, leading to increased levels of stress hormones such as cortisol, which contribute to depression. Regular physical activity helps to regulate the HPA axis, maintaining balanced cortisol levels and reducing stress-related depressive symptoms. This regulatory effect can counteract the impact of air pollution on mental health and reduce the risk of depression. Another important mechanism is the improvement of mental health through behavioral factors associated with physical activity. Engaging in physical activity often involves social interaction, structured routines, and goal-setting. These behavioral aspects can provide additional mental health benefits, such as increased social support, a sense of accomplishment, and improved self-esteem. These psychological benefits may enhance resilience to air pollution related stressors, thereby mitigating adverse effects on mental health and functional ability.

There are several limitations in this study. First, due to the absence of specific addresses, air pollutant exposure was estimated at the city level, potentially causing misclassification by disregarding variations within cities. This limitation might not have altered the direction of the relationship between air pollution and hypertension but could have biased the point estimate and increased the range of uncertainty. Previous studies, including the bootstrap measurement‑error analysis by Xue et al. demonstrated that city-level PM2.5 assignment tends to slightly overestimate the association with depressive symptoms, as correction for within-city variability yielded a stronger effect estimate60. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data presents inherent limitations, such as the potential for overestimation or underestimation due to social desirability bias, recall bias, and other influencing factors. Lastly, despite adjusting for a wide array of potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. However, we still need to admit that the strengths of this study include its longitudinal design, a large and nationally representative sample from 28 provinces across mainland China, and a relatively long follow-up period (median follow-up of 7 years, IQR 4–9 years). Furthermore, the study investigates the impact of multiple specific air pollutants on the risk of depression, ADL disability and their comorbidity, which was not conducted in previous relevant studies. Last but not least, physical activity was classified as a dichotomous variable, with physically active versus physically inactive groups based on the WHO and IPAQ guidelines61,62. The WHO defines sufficient physical activity as at least 150 min of moderate-intensity or 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity per week61,62. This binary approach simplifies the assessment of physical activity’s impact on health outcomes, aligning with standard public health frameworks that distinguish between adequate and inadequate physical activity levels. While this categorization provides clarity, it does not capture the full spectrum of physical activity intensity, potentially limiting the nuanced understanding of its effects on depression and ADL disability in the context of air pollution exposure.

Our findings carry substantial implications for health policy and future research. Policymakers should recognize the distinct impacts of different air pollutants on depression and ADL disability when establishing air quality standards and public health initiatives. Targeted measures to decrease exposure to harmful pollutants such as NO2, PM1, PM2.5, O3, and PM10 in at-risk populations could help lower the risk of depression, ADL disability and comorbid them. Moreover, encouraging moderate PA among middle-aged and older adults is essential, fostering a societal culture that prioritizes physical fitness. Even in settings with elevated air-pollution exposure, our findings suggest that engaging in regular physical activity may help attenuate the pollutant-related risks of depression, ADL disability, and their comorbidity. Accordingly, pursuing air-quality improvements together with initiatives that encourage physical activity could serve as complementary, modifiable approaches to lessen these burdens in middle-aged and older adults.”

Conclusion

Our study identifies a significant positive association between exposure to PM1, PM2.5, PM10, O3, and NO2 and the risk of depression, and comorbid ADL disability and depression among Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Among the pollutants examined, NO₂ showed the strongest association with increased risks of depression and comorbid ADL disability and depression, whereas PM10 displayed the comparatively weakest association. However, it is only PM2.5 that shows a significant association with an increased risk of ADL disability. Among physically active individuals, the associations between air pollutant exposure and depression (PM₁, PM₂.₅, O₃, NO₂), ADL disability (PM₁, O₃), and their comorbidity (PM₂.₅, PM₁₀) were substantially attenuated compared to physically inactive individuals. Implementing targeted policies and interventions to reduce air pollution, alongside promoting regular PA, could potentially lower the risk of depression, ADL disability and comorbidity of them, and support healthy aging.
