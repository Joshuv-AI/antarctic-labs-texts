Bidirectional optoelectronic switching in protein by photoinduced phase transition
Authors: Guangdong Zhou, Xiuxia Wang, Xuesen Xie, Ye Zhou, Bai Sun, Yang Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protein polymers conduct electricity due to the delocalization of π electrons along the conjugated main chains, but their photoconductivity often shows unidirectional tunability. We report a protein polymer with bidirectional optoelectronic conductivity because of reversible light intensity–dependent secondary structural transformation. The protein device shows a positive photocurrent under high light intensity (2.22 to 3.18 μW·μm−2 and the wavelength of 405-nanometer illumination) illumination and a negative photocurrent under low light intensity (0.32 to 1.91 μW·μm−2). Under low light intensity light, the helix structures untwist into a low-conductivity β-turn because only partial hydrogen bonds are damaged by insufficient low-energy excitons and Joule heat, whereas high-intensity light drives a transition to a high-conductivity β sheet because most hydrogen bonds are destructed by high-energy excitons and sufficient Joule heat. The tunable low-conductivity state arises from an increase in β-turn content coupled with a decrease in β sheet content, while the inverse transition enables the high-conductivity state. This bidirectional conductivity enables the fibroin-based device to perform in-sensor computing for real-time perception of multiple object motions.

INTRODUCTION

Polymers derive their conductivity primarily from π-electron delocalization along the conjugated main chain, this delocalization can be further enhanced by doping with conductive ions (1–3). In conventional conductive polymers such as polyaniline (4, 5), polythiophene (6, 7), and polypyrrole (8), efficient charge transport occurs along the polymer chains. This transport follows two distinct mechanisms, high conductivity is achieved through long-range–ordered charge transport in well-aligned domains (1, 6, 9), in contrast to the limited conductivity resulting from charge hopping in disordered, defect-containing regions (10, 11). However, these mechanisms cannot achieve bidirectionally tunable conductivity because the π-electron delocalization effect only builds a positive conductance response under external stimuli such as electric fields, magnetic fields, and light irradiation.

Fibroin, an important polymer, offers rich secondary structures (α helix, β sheet, β-turn, random coil, etc.) and abundant chemically active groups (-OH, -COOH, and -NH2) for functionalization (12–14). In its native state, the protein’s electronic properties are governed by a high proportion of β sheet configurations, which establish extensive π-π stacked domains functioning as charge trapping centers to modulate carrier transport (15, 16). This highly ordered framework, stabilized by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, is the key structural component (16). Extensive research over the past decade has focused on modulating polymer conductivity. One common strategy involves incorporating conductive nanoparticles (e.g., metal nanoparticles, carbon dots, and nanotubes) or mobile ions into the protein network to expand charge transport pathways and increase β sheet density, thereby enhancing conductivity (15, 17–20). Unlike inherently conductive polymers (21, 22), native fibroin is intrinsically insulating, a property that can be substantially altered through protein network engineering (23–25). The mechanical deformation of secondary structures (e.g., stretching, twisting, and rotation) induced by electric fields or light is a strategy to enhance charge transfer efficiency in modified fibroins, consistently yielding a positive conductance response, a behavior common to most polymers (19, 23, 24, 26, 27). In contrast, bidirectional optoelectronic switching is essential for fully in-sensor processing of dynamic information, along with its underlying physical mechanism, representing a notable gap in the current study of conductive polymers.

In this work, we demonstrate a light intensity–dependent bidirectional optoelectronic switching mechanism in fibroin polymers, featuring both positive photoconductance (PPC) memory response and negative photoconductance (NPC) memory response. Triggered by low-intensity 405-nm illumination, the fibroin polymer exhibits an NPC as α helix untwists into low-conductivity β-turns. High-intensity light, conversely, drives a transition to high-conductivity β sheets, yielding a PPC. This bidirectional photoresponse enables a frame-difference computational method that suppresses static background noise, permitting efficient real-time detection of multiple moving objects in complex environments.

A two-terminal device was fabricated with a ∼150-nm protein polymer sandwiched between a ∼20-nm F-doped SnO2 top electrode and a ∼100-nm Au bottom electrode to investigate the fibroin-based polymer’s conductivity (Fig. 1C). These devices (200 μm in diameter) were integrated on a flexible protein substrate to form a free-standing pixel array. The electrical analog resistive switching memory and its underlying secondary-structure mechanism are demonstrated in Fig. 1, D to G. Typical current-voltage relation (I-V) curves from dc voltage sweeps (0 → 1.0 → 0 → -1.0 → 0 V) reveal analog switching memory with linearly tunable conductance states, enabling high-precision computing (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, the switching behavior remained stable over 104 bending cycles, confirming excellent mechanical flexibility (Fig. 1E) (12). By applying successive voltage scans (0 → 0.5 V) and reading at 10.0 mV, 32 distinct conductance levels were achieved and retained for 104 s, demonstrating nonvolatility (Fig. 1F and fig. S2A). This behavior contrasts with the unstable digital switching observed in pure fibroin devices (fig. S2B), indicating that the hydrogen-bonding network formed between the fibroin backbone, polyglycerol-3, and 5,6-dihydroxyindole is crucial for enabling the analog memory characteristics.

Figure 1G schematically illustrates an electrical switching mechanism, which couples a secondary structural change with electron trapping and detrapping processes. Notably, residual ions (e.g., Li+, concentration of ∼60 mg/kg after 48-hour dialysis) would introduce trap sites. At initial state, the fibroin polymer device is at high-resistance state (HRS).

Under positive voltage, electrons are detrapping from trap centers, thus introducing charged trap centers that acted as the dominated factor to set the device to low-resistance state (LRS). Conversely, under negative voltage, electron injection neutralizes these centers, resetting the device to HRS (29, 30). The dynamic trapping and detrapping of electrons, coupled with the electric field force, disrupt hydrogen bonds in protein networks. Notably, the π-π stacked regions of β sheets facilitate electron mobility between molecules, akin to electron transmission in metallic materials (15, 16). This electric field effect promotes the breakdown of hydrogen bonds in α helices and other structures, leading to the formation of highly conductive β sheets or low-conductivity β-turns. Under low electric fields, the increase in β sheets and decrease in β-turns enhance the set process. Under high fields, the concurrent rise in both β sheets and β-turns (due to protein chain breakage under high filed) largely offsets their individual contributions. Despite this, the fibroin device exhibits consecutive switching memory behavior, suggesting that electron trapping/detrapping remains the dominant role. Nevertheless, the field-induced accumulation of β sheets enables continuous memory formation, endowing the protein polymer device with analog switching behavior and a progressively enlarged memory window.

Figure 1 (H to K) demonstrates the positive and negative photocurrent responses of the fibroin-based polymer device, along with its optoelectronic switching mechanism. Figure 1H presents the I-V curves under dark conditions, low-intensity illumination (1.91 μW·μm−2, 2.0 s), and high-intensity illumination (3.18 μW·μm−2, 2.0 s). The results reveal that the fibroin-based polymer device exhibits a higher photocurrent response (PPC effect) under high-intensity 405-nm light illumination (3.18 μW·μm−2, 2.0 s) and a lower photocurrent response (NPC effect) under low-intensity illumination (1.91 μW·μm−2, 2.0 s) compared to its dark current value. The device can be sequentially set to a high-conductance state by the PPC stimulation (3.18 μW·μm−2, 2.0 s) and reset to a low-conductance state by the NPC stimulation (1.91 μW·μm−2, 2.0 s) (Fig. 1, I and J). This light intensity–dependent PPC and NPC effect enables the protein-polymer device to optically update its conductance states in a manner analogous to retinal cells with tunable synaptic plasticity (fig. S3). When shorten the 405-nm light pulse width from 2.0 s to 10 ms, the NPC and PPC effect shows a good cyclic endurance (more than 1500 cycles), indicating that building precise conductance states is feasible for optoelectronic device based on the protein polymer (figs. S4 and S5). This short timescale (10 ms) NPM and PPM response ascribes to the fast photophysical/photochemical processes (e.g., exciton generation, charge redistribution, and proton transfer) within femtosecond-to-picosecond timescale, the breakage and reconfiguration of the hydrogen-bonding networks within tens of picoseconds timescale, and conformation change within microseconds (31), building an identical structure transformation in the protein polymer when prolongs light illumination.

Figure 1K schematically illustrates the light-induced secondary structure transformation mechanism. Specifically, the generation of β sheet, which contributes to high conductivity, leads to the PPC effect, while the formation of β-turn, which results in low conductivity, causes the NPC effect. Note that the light can provide energy to excite electron and Joule heat. The Joule heat is not the dominant driving force for the conformational transitions in this system, but it can facilitate the secondary structure transition. The overall heating (300 to 440 K) by oven and localized Joule heating by 405-nm laser irradiation are different in terms of thermal distribution and nonequilibrium characteristics (table S1). Overall heating builds homogeneous thermal effect that mostly induces the changes tending toward thermodynamic equilibrium because of low heat gradient, resulting in low internal structure aggregation (32). For localized heating, it has higher heat gradient and tends to thermodynamic nonequilibrium state (33), thus driving nonequilibrium structure change and high-density aggregation (figs. S6 and S7, and table S2).

Under low-intensity illumination, the low energy results in an insufficient electron generated from the photoactive 5,6-dihydroxyindole groups. This energy deficit leads to only partial untwisting of the α helix, resulting in the formation of the β-turn structure. Therefore, under low intensity (0 ∼ 1.91 μW·μm−2), the helix untwists to form a β-turn structure, endowing the fibroin-based polymer with low conductivity and thus inducing the NPC effect.

Under high-intensity light illumination (2.22 ∼ 3.18 μW·μm−2), the α helix thoroughly untwists its helical structure to form the β sheet structure with an abundant π-electron region that allows for the free transmission of electrons, enabling the PPC effect. In addition, the light-driven electron detrapping from trap centers would cause a positive photocurrent response, thus contributing a positive photocurrent response.

The low conductivity is dominated by the formation of β-turn under low light intensity illumination, whereas the high conductivity originates from the generation of β sheet under high light intensity illumination. Notably, the light-driven electron detrapping differs from the electric-driven detrapping. The electrons driven by the electric field have consistent transmission direction and higher kinetic energy; thus, the electric switching is dominated by the trapping and detrapping process. In contrast, the light-induced detrapping electrons generally exhibit random transmission direction and have high probability in deexcitation recombination after light stimuli; thus, the optical switching is dominated by the secondary structure conversion. This device not only allows electrically triggered conductance updates but also enables light-based full conductance updates (PPC for increasing conductance and NPC for decreasing conductance).

Figure 2D is extracted from Fig. 2C over 200 ns under various electric fields. This reveals that the hydrogen bonds in protein network are destroyed by the electric field and then trigger structure phase change. The β sheet component, which features a π-π stacked region enabling free electron transmission among molecules and thus contributes to higher conductivity, progressively increases. In contrast, the β-turn component, which is linked to lower conductivity, along with the α helix and random coil, progressively decreases within the electric field range of 0.2 to 0.7 mV/Å. The high-conductivity β sheet generation endows the protein polymer devices with an accumulative conductivity, thus building large memory window for these analog switching behaviors.

Light intensity–dependent bidirectional photoconductivity

The concentrations of secondary structures were quantified using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 3, A to C). The mole concentrations in the amide I region (1600 to 1700 cm−1) were deconvoluted from the FTIR spectra of the fibroin polymer. Under dark conditions, all secondary structure components including α helix, β sheet, β-turn, random coil, and side chains were identified within the amide I region (Fig. 3A). Under low-intensity illumination (1.91 μW·μm−2, 2 s), the concentration of β-turns (low conductivity) increases, while that of β sheets (high conductivity) decreases. This trend is completely reversed under high-intensity light (3.18 μW·μm−2, 2 s), with β-turns decreasing and β sheets increasing (Fig. 3, B and C). This reciprocal change directly links the structural composition to the conductive response, a rise in β-turn content coupled with a drop in β sheet content produces the NPC effect, and the opposite change generates the PPC effect. In sharp contrast, pure fibroin exhibits no significant change in β sheet or β-turn content under identical illumination conditions (fig. S8 and table S3), highlighting the critical role of polyglycerol-3 and 5,6-dihydroxyindole in enabling the bidirectional photoresponse of the fabricated polymer. To further verify above results, the secondary structure transformation is investigated by the circular dichroism spectroscopy, showing the same change trend under low- and high-light intensity illumination (figs. S9 and S10, and table S4).

Figure 3D presents the evolution of secondary structure mole ratios in the fabricated fibroin polymer under light illumination with intensities ranging from 0 to 3.18 μW·μm−2. As the intensity rises from 0 to 1.91 μW·μm−2, the contents of both β sheet and α helix decrease markedly, whereas the β-turn content shows a continuous increase. The side chain and random coil components exhibit only minor and stochastic fluctuations. As the light intensity increases further from 2.22 to 3.18 μW·μm−2, the trends for these structures are reversed. The β sheet and α helix contents increase, while the β-turn content decreases. This reciprocal relationship directly links structure to its function. The decrease in conductive β sheets and the increase in β-turns under low-intensity light (0 to 1.91 μW·μm−2) yield the NPC effect. Conversely, the opposite structural conversion under high-intensity light (2.22 to 3.18 μW·μm−2) generates the PPC response, thereby directly accounting for the bidirectional photoconductance (table S5).

To directly verify the secondary structure-governed bidirectional conductivity, we performed current-sensing atomic force microscopy (CS-AFM) on protein polymer/Au samples, defining current flowing from the Au electrode to the Pt probe as positive (Figure 3E). The measurements reveal a gradual enhancement of the localized sensing current as the light intensity increases from 2.22 to 3.18 μW·μm−2 (Fig. 3F). This rising current is consistent with the increased conductivity (PPC effect) from the light-induced secondary structure conversion, specifically the growth of β sheet content and the concomitant decrease of β-turns. Conversely, when the intensity is reduced from a preset high level (3.18 μW·μm−2) to lower values (0.32 to 1.91 μW·μm−2), the initially high current is progressively suppressed. This suppression corresponds to the NPC effect, driven by the opposite structural change, a decrease in β sheets, and an increase in β-turns. All CS-AFM measurements were performed under a constant bias voltage of 10.0 mV. The observed NPC and PPC effects thus collectively demonstrate that the photoconductance of the protein polymer film is governed by internal conversions among its secondary structures, a process that is critically dependent on the light intensity.

It is recognized that 405-nm illumination introduces Joule heating, which can potentially cause protein denaturation. To quantify this thermal contribution, we performed in situ thermography on the protein polymer film under 2-s light pulses. The results show that the central temperature rises as the intensity increases from 0.32 to 3.18 μW·μm−2 (Fig. 3G). This substantial temperature increase indicates that thermal effects possibly play a significant role in the observed secondary structure transformations (16, 29, 34, 35).

Mechanism of positive and negative tunable photoconductivity

The secondary structure conversion is dominantly driven by the photoactive 5,6-dihydroxyindole monomer and the α helix. In the 5,6-dihydroxyindole monomer, the electron densities of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) are distributed across the entire molecule, albeit with an asymmetric character that limits electron delocalization (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the 5,6-dihydroxyindole polymer exhibits a strong absorption peak at 420.63 nm (fig. S12A), indicating that 405-nm light can efficiently excite its electrons, promoting intermonomer charge transfer. Consequently, this polymer serves as the primary exciton generator, initiating secondary structural changes in the fibroin matrix and directly modulating its conductivity.

The analysis of the polyglycerol-3 polymer reveals that both HOMO and LUMO orbitals are localized at two terminal sites, leading to similarly confined electron clouds (Fig. 4B). This configuration implies that electronic excitations are intrinsically localized to internal sections of the polymer chain. An identical orbital localization pattern is found in the β sheet and β-turn structures (Fig. 4, C and D), suggesting that electron transitions from HOMO to LUMO also occur solely within the internal β sheet and β-turn structures without extending to another monomer.

In sharp contrast to the photoactive 5,6-dihydroxyindole, the polyglycerol-3 polymer, β sheet, and β-turn structures exhibit absorption maxima only at 160 to 180 nm (fig. S12, B to D), confirming their optical inactivity at the 405-nm excitation wavelength. Consequently, the primary photoexcitation event occurs within the 74 to 81 polypeptide segment of the fabricated fibroin polymer. Upon 405-nm laser illumination, electrons in this segment are excited, undergoing a transition from orbital no. 266 to orbital no. 280, which facilitates intermonomer electron transfer. Crucially, this electronic excitation induces significant changes in the φ (C−1-N-CA-C) and ψ (N-CA-C-N+1) dihedral angles of the 74 to 81 segment, as revealed by ground-state versus excited-state calculations (table S6). As these angles are the most conformationally sensitive parameters in proteins, their perturbation directly drives the secondary structural transitions, such as untwisting, elongation, and distortion.

For the α helix, two key absorption orbitals have been identified: the no. 266 orbital (HOMO), primarily localized on the carbon-terminal carboxyl group, and the no. 280 orbital (LUMO), predominantly associated with the branched benzene ring (Fig. 4E). These orbitals establish a charge-transfer pathway for photoexcited electrons, as confirmed by the 405-nm absorption peak of the 74 to 81 peptide segment (fig. S12E), which exhibits the strongest light response among all analyzed regions.

To further investigate these findings, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted on the α helix molecule. The results indicate that under increasing light intensity (from 0 to 3.18 μW·μm−2), the α helix undergoes structural changes, including untwisting, stretching, and conversion to a 310-helix (blue) and a turn structure (Fig. 4F). These structural transformations play a critical role in the secondary structure conversion of the fibroin polymer films. However, note that the fabricated fibroin polymer film exhibits an optical bandgap (Eg-photon) of 2.43 eV, whereas the pure fibroin protein has a larger Eg-photon of 3.41 eV (fig. S13). The electrochemical bandgap (Eg-ec) based on cyclic voltammetry of the pure fibroin film and protein polymer film is consistent with the Eg-photon results (fig. S14). This difference suggests that the 405-nm light alone cannot excite electrons in the pure fibroin film. Instead, the secondary structure conversion is driven by the combined effects of the light-active sites (5,6-DHI monomer and α helix monomer) and the thermal energy. To observe the secondary structure conversion in the fabricated fibroin polymer under light illumination, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted at varying light intensities (0 to 3.18 μW·μm−2) for 200 ns (Fig. 4G). The results reveal that the secondary structures, including the β-turn (white), 310-helix (light pink), bridge β sheet (light red), extended β sheet (red), and α helix (crimson), undergo dynamic real-time changes at specific light intensities (fig. S15).

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted on the α helix to elucidate the structural dynamics underlying the photoresponse. The simulations demonstrate that rising light intensity (0–3.18 μW·μm−2) induces significant structural changes, such as untwisting, stretching, and transitions to a 310-helix and turn (Fig. 4F). These alterations are integral to the secondary structure conversion mechanism in the fibroin polymer films, thereby underpinning the observed optoelectronic functionality. Notably, the optical bandgap of the fabricated fibroin polymer is 2.43 eV, which is substantially smaller than the 3.41 eV gap of the pure protein (fig. S13). This bandgap narrowing means that the 405-nm photon energy can effectively excite the electrons from the fabricated fibroin material, whereas it is insufficient for the pure fibroin. Consequently, the structural transformation is initiated by the excitation of light-active components (5–6-dihydroxyindole and α helix) in conjunction with thermal effects, rather than being a direct result of exciting the pure protein matrix. To probe the dynamics of light-induced secondary structure conversion, we performed molecular dynamics simulations on the fabricated fibroin polymer under light intensities ranging from 0 to 3.18 μW·μm−2 over 200 ns (Fig. 4G). The simulations capture the real-time evolution of the secondary structures, marked by dynamic changes in β-turns (white), 310 helices (light pink), bridge β sheets (light red), extended β sheets (red), and α helices (crimson) at specific illumination thresholds (fig. S15).

Fig. 4H quantifies the evolution of secondary structure concentrations derived from the results in Fig. 4G, which tracked structural changes over 200 ns under light intensities from 0 to 3.18 μW·μm−2. Positive and negative values indicate an increase or decrease in a given component, respectively. As the light intensity rises from 0.32 to 1.91 μW·μm−2, the proportion of the low-conductivity β-turn component increases steadily, while the proportions of the high-conductivity β sheet and α helix components decrease. This shift toward a higher β-turn/low β sheet ratio under low-intensity illumination (0.32 to 1.91 μW·μm−2) directly correlates with the observed NPC memory effect. In contrast, when the light intensity increases from 2.22 to 3.18 μW·μm−2, the β-turn and α helix components decrease, while the β sheet content shows a continuous increase (Fig. 4H and table S7). This rapid rise in conductive β sheets, coupled with the decrease in β-turns, drives the PPC memory response under high-intensity illumination. Under these conditions, the detrapping of electrons from defect centers by the light illumination further enhances the photocurrent. Thus, the PPC effect arises from the synergistic effect of forming conductive β sheet pathways and the concomitant release of trapped charges. Despite this, the deexcitation recombination between the photogenerated electrons and the charged trap centers (corresponding to the trapping) largely reduces the contribution on conductivity. Therefore, the bidirectional conductivity is primarily dominated by the light-induced phase conversion.

Real-time perception multiple moving objects based on bidirectional photoconductivity

Motion perception is crucial for intelligent systems such as autonomous driving, navigation, and smart homes (36, 37). Figure 5 demonstrates the NPC and PPC memory effects for perceiving multiple moving objects. The underlying principle is illustrated in Fig. 5A. the PPC memory enhances feature pixels, while the NPC memory suppresses noisy background pixels. Figure 5C illustrates the architecture for multiple moving object perception, which integrates both PPC and NPC memory functionalities within a single protein-based optoelectronic cell. It notes that the NPC and PPC are not preassigned to specific regions of a single image frame. Instead, pixel-level adaptive photoresponse modulation is guided by motion-region information extracted via an interframe difference method (fig. S16). The system processes an input video stream (30 fps) over a time interval Δt, comprising frames from t0 to tn The processing pipeline involves three key steps: First, a specific frame (fn) is projected onto the pixel array and encoded via the PPC memory effect; second, the image contrast is enhanced by simultaneously applying NPC-based background denoising and PPC-based feature amplification; last, a differential operation is performed between adjacent frames (fn − fn−1) or across different frames (fn − fx) to extract motion information by subtracting the static background. To achieve final noise suppression and feature highlighting, a binarization operation is performed on the differential frame. This step critically relies on the observation that the interframe photocurrent gap (ΔI) is minimal and stable for background pixels but significant and dynamic for feature pixels, even after initial contrast enhancement. Pixels with ΔI above a threshold are set to “1” (moving object), and others to “0” (background), resulting in a clear segmentation of multiple moving objects. This process effectively eliminates residual background noise and sharply delineates the moving features, enabling precise perception of multiple objects.

The YOLO algorithm provides high-speed, end-to-end object detection suitable for real-time visual perception (fig. S17). We compared the performance of the conventional YOLO algorithm with our heterogeneous architecture, which synergizes YOLO with NPC/PPC-based in-sensor preprocessing, for detecting multiple moving objects (e.g., pedestrians and vehicles; Fig. 5, D and E, and movies S1 to S3). In our architecture, each video frame is first preprocessed in real-time through a sequence of optical operations: NPC-based denoising, NPC/PPC-based contrast enhancement, interframe differencing, and threshold-based binarization. The resulting frames are then fed into the YOLO convolutional network for forward propagation and final classification. Whereas the conventional YOLO achieves a precision of only 33% on the real-time video (Fig. 5F), the NPC/PPC preprocessing–enabled YOLO architecture achieves a markedly higher precision exceeding 91%. This significant improvement underscores the critical role of in-sensor computing in enhancing complex visual tasks. Under processing the same intelligent task (300-frame images), the NPC/PPC-enabled YOLO system exhibits the highest perception accuracy (95%) than the conventional YOLO and background subtraction algorithm (fig. S18). Compared with conventional digital preprocessing based on histogram equalization, the real-physical driving YOLO system also exhibits higher efficiency (fig. S19).

DISCUSSION

The bidirectional photoconductivity of the engineered fibroin polymer critically depends on the intensity of 405-nm light, which supplies both electronic excitation energy and Joule heat. Within the β sheet structure, the benzene rings of adjacent peptide chains form extensive π-π stacked regions, enabling metallic-like electron delocalization and efficient intermolecular charge transport. In stark contrast, the β-turn structure features a compact hydrogen-bonding network with short bond lengths (0.28 to 0.31 nm) and linear angles (160° to 170°), which severely restricts the pathways for electron or ion movement. Consequently, an increase in β sheet content elevates conductivity, while an increase in β-turn content diminishes it, providing the structural basis for the tunable PPC and NPC effects.

The underlying mechanism shares a common origin: the light- and electric field–responsive behaviors both stem from the competition between hydrogen-bonding stability and external energy input. Native fibroin is insulating because its β sheets and β-turns are locked in a rigid hydrogen-bonded network. The incorporation of photoactive 5,6-dihydroxyindole is crucial, as it absorbs photons to generate excitons. Under low-intensity light (0 to 1.91 μW·μm−2), the limited exciton density and kinetic energy result in incomplete helix untwisting, favoring the formation of the low-conductivity β-turn. In contrast, high-intensity light (2.22 to 3.18 μW·μm−2) provides ample excitons and substantial Joule heating, which collectively break hydrogen bonds thoroughly, enabling complete helix unfolding and subsequent formation of the high-conductivity β sheet. Similarly, under an electric field, the analog resistive switching memory emerges from the synergistic effect of charge trapping/detrapping at defect sites and the continuous, field-induced accumulation of β sheet content.

When the background ΔI is elevated by disturbances and its amplitude remains lower than that of actual moving targets, the ΔI falls below the PPC activation threshold, yielding no false enhancement. Simultaneously, the NPC mechanism continuously suppresses background regions to low conductance level, further attenuating the propagation of residual interference. The hardware-intrinsic nonlinear mapping from ΔI amplitude to conductance state endows the system with immunity against modest increases in background ΔI.

However, when the background ΔI is comparable to that of the target moving objects, it requires other methods to handle such abnormal background ΔI. In this case, the adaptive threshold via ΔI histogram analysis is necessary. Otsu’s method dynamically computes an optimal threshold to separate background and moving object ΔI clusters after frame differencing. This threshold adaptively adjusts the ΔI-to-light intensity mapping (e.g., raising PPC trigger level), preserving separation without heavy computation. Furthermore, gradient-domain frame differencing offers a viable alternative. By applying lightweight Sobel-like kernels to extract gradient magnitude from each frame and performing differencing in this feature space, edge and texture information is emphasized, which remains stable under global illumination changes and shadow regions. This approach cancels common-mode illumination variations without requiring a background model and is inherently robust to slow lighting drifts.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protein polymer synthesis and optoelectronic measurements

The protein polymer is synthesized according to the method given by (20) and (29). The cocoon was boiled in the Na2CO3 solution with a concentration of 0.02 M for 30 min to remove sericin and then cleanly washed. Raw fibroin protein cellulose was obtained after dissolving into 9.3 M LiBr solution at 60°C for 3 hours and dialyzed for 48 hours. The protein polymer was synthesized when the 0.003 g of glycerin and the 0.003 g of melanin were orderly added into 1 ml of raw fibroin protein. The flexible protein polymer substrate was obtained after the primeval modified fibroin protein polymer solution thermally processing at 97°C for 3 hours and then natural cooling to room temperature at the moisture ambient with relative humidity of 65%. The electrodes were fabricated by sputtering and the protein polymer function layer was spin-coated at 4500 rpm for 30 s. A photoelectric test platform including laser (405-nm cobolt), oscilloscope (UNI-T, UPO7072Z), probe station (Lakeshore, TTPX), semiconductor analyzer (B1500A, Keysight), and electrochemical workstation (CHI, 760E) was carried out for the optic-electric measurements. The morphology structure, ultraviolet (UV) spectra, infrared spectra, and in situ sensing current were characterized by the field emission scanning electron microscopy (7100), UV-visible spectrometer (SHIMADZU, UV-2700i), FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50), and atomic force microscope (AFM, BY3000).

Molecular dynamics calculation

Absorption spectrum calculation. Absorption properties of individual molecules were calculated by the quantum chemistry method based on the Gaussian 16 B01 to examine the adsorption of the 5,6-DHI polymer (CAS: 25656-67-1), which initial structure was manually built through Discovery Studio Visualizer and structurally optimized under the 6-311++g(2d, 2p) group using the B3LYP method of density functional theory. The molecule charge and multiplicity were 0 and 1, respectively. The absorption spectrum of the optimized 5,6-DHI polymer was calculated by the CAM-B3LYP method under the same group. A simulated α-h unit with a size of 60 Å by 47 Å by 56 Å and 4 Glu74-Phe107 peptide chains was employed to conduct the dynamic simulations.

Construction of molecular force field

The molecular force field of the Pg-3 (CAS: 56090-54-1) was directly generated by CGenFF process, while the molecular force field of the 5,6-DHI polymer was obtained by using VMD’s ffTK plug-in combined with quantum chemical calculations. The optimized 5,6-DHI polymer, single-point energy, and Hessian matrix were calculated by the MP2 method, while other calculations such as atomic charge, single-point energy of water molecules, and so on were carried out by the Hatree Fock method.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Considering the amino acid sequence of silk protein (Uniport access code: P05790) is too long to implement calculation, the front 356 residues were selected to build the fibroin structure and its three-dimensional coordinates (PDB ID: 3UA0) were obtained for the N-terminal part of filaments by crystallographic experiment (38). Swiss-Model was used to refill all residues based on homology modeling, yielding a reasonable fibroin structure with 126 sequences. Also, the post amino acid residues from 127 to 356 were modeled based on the classical theoretical model (PDB ID: 2SLK) that includes three-layer β sheet structure (39). The option on the front and post residues was based on the important role of N-terminal in fibroin structural assembly and mechanical properties and the repeated three-layer β sheet structure as the most abundant component of the silk protein sequence. Stable protein polymer model is composed of two stable fibroin structures, 24 5,6-DHI polymer molecules, 30 Pg-3 molecules, 6 Br− ions, 14 Li+ ions, and 12 TIP3P water molecules. All dynamics simulation calculations were performed using the dynamics software package NAMD 2.14, and the force field parameters of silk fibroin protein were derived from the CHARMM36m protein parameter set. Periodic boundary conditions were used in the molecular dynamics simulation, and the short-range interaction including the van der Waals effect and electrostatic effect was set to be 12 Å. To avoid the system instability caused by sudden energy change, a switching function was used in the range 10 to 12 Å to gradually reduce the potential energy to zero. The atomic pair radius for the short-range interaction was set to be 14 Å, and the PME method was used to calculate the long-range electrostatic effect. Room temperature (T = 298 K) and atmospheric pressure (P = 1.01 × 105 Pa) was acted as the beginning condition for the dynamic simulation. In the whole dynamic simulation system, 50,000 steps of conjugate gradient minimization were firstly performed to eliminate the unreasonable contact in the initial model, and then the dynamics of canonical ensembles were simulated for 10 ns at 298 K. The pressure control was turned on to enable the dynamic simulation to run for 200 ns. The protein polymer composite system was heated from 298 to 338 K at an increasing rate of 2 K/ns. The integral step in the kinetic simulation was set to 2 fs, and all the chemical bonds containing hydrogen atoms were restricted by SHAKE rule.

Optimized YOLO for multiple objects motion detection

We demonstrate the detection and perception of multiple moving objects in complex real-world scenarios. The experimental data consist of multiobject motion processes captured in real-world settings. To achieve multi-object motion detection based on optically controlled positive and negative photoconductive effects, a neuromorphic computing framework was constructed to emulate the signal processing mechanism of the human retina. By simulating the ON/OFF antagonistic shunting function of retinal bipolar cells, the light intensity–dependent positive and negative photoconductive current effects were incorporated into an interframe difference algorithm. The PPC effect was used to enhance pixel signals in moving target regions, while the NPC effect was used to suppress and erase background noise, thereby improving feature contrast and enabling dynamic target detection.

Subsequently, the interframe difference algorithm enhanced by positive and negative photoconductive processing was integrated with a YOLO model, forming a collaborative algorithmic architecture to enable the perception of multiobject motion. The preprocessed interframe difference data were fed into an optimized YOLO neural network for neuromorphic inference simulation. In this fused architecture, the optical preprocessing unit acts as a front-end bionic layer dedicated to motion feature enhancement and noise filtering, which significantly reduces data complexity and redundancy for subsequent processing by the convolutional neural network within YOLO. The results show that in the frame data preprocessed by the dual-mode photoconductive mechanism, the contrast between moving targets and the background is markedly enhanced. This allows the YOLO network to identify genuine moving targets more accurately during convolutional feature extraction, bounding box regression, and object classification while effectively suppressing false detections of static objects. System performance simulations quantitatively demonstrate the notable advantages of this hybrid architecture: Under complex multiobject motion scenarios, it achieves a recognition accuracy of more than 91% (compared to only 33% with traditional algorithms) and a processing frame rate exceeding 30 fps. These results fully underscore the considerable potential of this neuromorphic computing paradigm, which integrates bioinspired optoelectronic hardware with neural network algorithms-in enabling high real-time, high-precision motion perception.
