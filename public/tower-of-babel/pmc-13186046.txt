Classification of indeterminate and off-target cell types within human kidney organoid differentiation
Authors: Sean B Wilson, Jessica M Vanslambrouck, Angela Murphy, Drew R Neavin, Joseph E Powell, Sara E Howden, Melissa H Little
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWhile proposed to recapitulate embryonic patterning, classifying cellular states present within human pluripotent stem cell-derived organoids is challenged by the lack of early human embryo reference datasets, formation of “off-target” states and the potential to form cellular states not present in vivo. A lack of cell classification can overlook such non-specific endpoints. This study describes cell classification across a temporal factorial single-cell transcriptomic profile of kidney organoid differentiation analyzed at five time points across 27 days. While this should pattern through intermediate mesoderm to kidney, we observed anticipated cellular states as well as off-target cell types representing both embryological mispatterning and mesodermal populations with no clear in vivo equivalent. The resulting transcriptional profile will assist in defining how off-target populations arise in vitro, ultimately refining kidney organoid protocols for therapeutic outcomes.Subject areas: stem cells research, developmental biology, transcriptomics

Introduction

The directed differentiation of human pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) has been used to form multicellular organoid models of various human tissues,1 with most protocols following a stepwise differentiation analogous to commitment during mammalian development. While such organoid models represent a major opportunity for accurate human disease modeling and advanced tissue engineering strategies,2,3 the resulting organoids frequently contain unanticipated, unclassified, or “off-target” cell types.4,5,6 The temporospatial complexity of embryonic development is such that accurately recapitulating this in vitro is likely to result in cellular states that reflect patterning to an adjacent or “off-target” endpoint, the formation of an intermediate cell type not previously characterized or the formation of a completely artificial transcriptional state unique to the in vitro culture conditions. Unassigned cell types have been routinely reported in most existing kidney organoid protocols.6,7 Classification of these cell types can be even more challenging. While transcriptional atlases of human fetal tissues are rapidly increasing in depth,8,9 considerable gaps exist. In addition, while many bioinformatic tools exist for cell classification,10,11,12 selecting the appropriate reference for comparison with an in vitro culture system is also problematic. Even when robust datasets exist, unassigned identities can be observed due to in vitro cellular states for which no reference exists.

There are several published protocols for the differentiation of human PSC to kidney organoids containing patterning and segmenting nephrons.7,13,14,15 Most kidney organoid protocols are regarded as mimicking gastrulation with commitment to mesoderm via canonical Wnt activation, followed by patterning to IM and induction of nephron formation. Initial patterning can be performed in 2D culture, micro-aggregates or Matrigel sandwich cultures (reviewed in Little and Combes7). Consequently, the first week of such protocols is similar to the protocols selected for other mesodermal (heart,16 blood,17 and muscle18) and neuromesodermal (NMP,19 neural crest,20 and neuromuscular organoids21) cell types. Indeed, the presence of neural, muscle and other mesodermal off-target cell types in kidney organoids has been described previously,4,22,23,24 as has considerable variation between batch, cell line, and laboratory. While not surprising, the coincident formation of such inappropriate cellular states and their variable presence between differentiations will decrease the endpoint utility with respect to the kidney.

In this study, we classify the cellular identity of all cells present within a comprehensive single-cell RNA-sequencing transcriptional profiling of a commonly applied kidney organoid protocol across 27 days from starting human PSC to kidney organoids. A factorial screen addressing developmental shifts in early mesodermal patterning, the protocol variations included 21 conditions to more comprehensively investigate the source of mispatterning within the canonical protocol. As such, this maximizes the potential sources of predictable embryonic variation while continuing to include the optimal canonical protocol. Prediction of cell identity was achieved with reference to available atlases from mouse and human, including both in vivo and in vitro. Across the time course, 53 individual cellular identities were defined, providing additional clarity to previously unassigned cell types in this protocol. Using this classification, a reanalysis of single-cell profiling previously published using this protocol revealed the presence of the same off-target and indeterminate cellular states, suggesting that these arise irrespective of starting cell line or laboratory. Finding and identifying off-target populations within in vitro-derived tissues has major implications for advancing approaches for future therapeutic applications.

Results

Multifactorial time course of kidney provides broad transcriptomic landscape for cell identity projection

The screen was designed to capture sample information at the end of each different media composition along the protocol, which we designated as “stages” (Figure 1A). The Takasato protocol was divided into four stages, making single-factor permutations across the first three stages to investigate 21 protocol variations. Stage 1 included 3, 4, or 5 days exposure to 7 μM CHIR prior to stage 2. At stage 2, we investigated the original Takasato protocol and how variations of FGF9, WNT, BMP, and RA signaling would impact patterning and organoid development, at each of the three initial CHIR exposures (Figure 1A). Technical replicates were pooled together while Hash-Tag Oligo (HTO) barcoding was used to multiplex samples for optimal cell sample collection (Figure 1A). Organoids had clearly developed nephron structures by day 27 in almost all conditions, an example being the control conditions showing all nephron segmentations (Figure 1B). Following quality control filtering of the scRNA-seq results, 150, 957 cells were retained across the entire time course, providing the basis of a multifactorial transcriptomic atlas of kidney organoids at four key time points throughout differentiation (Figure 1C, Table 1). Prior to comparing the outcome of each parallel condition, the developing cell types were annotated. Due to the distinct transcriptional profiles between stages, the cell type annotation was performed on each stage independently. While this annotation was more accurate at later organoid stages that have been extensively studied, less-investigated earlier intermediate time points required deeper analyses.

Incomplete primitive streak differentiation evident during stage 1

The process of gastrulation during human development is poorly characterized by single-cell transcriptomics, posing a challenge for cell classification within stage 1. In vitro, gastrulation is mimicked via the epithelial to mesenchymal transition from pluripotency to mesoderm. This first stage of differentiation was collected on days 3, 4, and 5 of culture in the presence of CHIR, a GSK3β inhibitor mimicking canonical WNT signaling and used to initiate mesodermal commitment. Transcriptomic analyses have previously described a primitive streak (PS) identity at the end of stage 1.15,23

Projecting cell age onto a UMAP of all stage 1 cells, age-defined groupings followed a chronological transition (Figure 1D), confirmed using pseudotime trajectory analysis (Figure 1E). Attempting to agnostically classify these cells, we investigated if reference-based classification with published datasets could identify the most similar cell type for each cell. The Tyser25 (in vivo; human), Tanigawa26 (in vivo; mouse), Minn27 (in vitro; human), and Diaz-Cuadros28 (in vitro; human) reference datasets were used to classify the derivation of PS and trunk mesodermal patterning. Label-transfer and machine learning tools (scPred,29 SCINA,30 SciBet,31 scClassify,32 and scAnnotatR33) were combined with these references classify our data. However, these reference-based annotations were highly variable irrespective of the dataset or the R-package applied (Figure S1A). Interestingly, batch correction34 using Harmony,35 CSS,36 or fastMNN37 improved but did not remove the batch effect of the human-derived single-cell references (Figure S1A). A Pearson’s correlation using pseudobulk transcriptomes of all references, using all DEGs identified or only the top 25 DEGs, from each individual analysis was still unable to clarify definitive identity. However, a transcriptomic comparison of all shared genes between references after a batch-correction on the pseudobulked data defined some correlation between references and stage 1 clusters (Figure S1B). The use of disparate nomenclature between studies made the cross-assignment of identity between references difficult. As such, applying the models to our data, the results were difficult to interpret. While stage 1 populations were generally regarded as mesodermal lineage, the population identity was inconsistent (Figure S2A). We therefore investigated the differential expression of key gastrulation stage markers.

The two smallest clusters expressed pluripotent markers EPCAM, POU5F1, and SOX2, with one cluster also expressing differentiation markers SP5, MIXL1, FGF8, and FGF17, indicating iPSC-like and neuromesodermal progenitors (NMPs), respectively (Figure 1F). The larger remaining clusters expressed PS markers alone and could be divided into three groups, giving five populations at this stage (Figure 1G). The iPSC and NMP clusters contained cells of all three ages, indicating these populations persisted despite continued CHIR exposure (Figure 1H). Pseudotime projection confirmed cells were transitioning from the 3c population to 5c, decreasing POU5F1 and T expression and increasing MIXL1, MSX2, and TBX6 with age (Figures 1E and 1I). As these populations all expressed the key markers for posterior primitive streak (pPS), they were distinguished as early_pPS, late_pPS_1, and late_pPS_2 based on their position along the correlating pseudotime-age axis.

The presence of cells that had not fully committed to pPS indicated that there had been incomplete differentiation from the CHIR addition. The proportion of cells remaining in an iPSC-like state was 0.22% at 3 days and 0.18% at 5 days (Figure 2D). iPSC-like cells have previously been identified in maturing gastruloids.38 In contrast, early_pPS consisted of >95% 3 days cells, while late_pPS_2 had >50% 5 days cells and almost no 3 days cells. This demonstrated that a population of cells are either unable to differentiate in response to CHIR, or there is an asynchronous differentiation with lagging cells continuing to incompletely respond ever after 5 days of CHIR-mediated differentiation. These cells were not present by stage 2.

Stage 2 contains a heterogeneous mix of mesodermal and neural cell identities

Patterning from pPS to intermediate mesoderm (IM) was hypothesized to be the most critical window for influencing endpoint cell types. In the original Takasato protocol, this is performed in 2D culture with IM induced via the addition of FGF9.15,39 The combined stage 2 dataset contained 44,282 cells following 4 days of treatment post-CHIR at 7, 8, or 9 days of culture. An attempt at reference-based classification for stage 2 populations was again challenging. The expected outcomes of nascent mesoderm (NM) or IM could be seen if only focusing on one classifier, SciBet (Figures S2A and S2B). While this was the most reliable outcome, there was still a large overlap between clusters for identities (Figure S2C). These outcomes indicated we were indeed generating the expected cell types; however, the lack of clarity in the classifications led us to rely on the standard process of dimensional reduction, clustering, and differential gene expression analysis to reliably annotate the cell types present.

Kidney-deriving IM populations are known to express PAX8, PAX2, and GATA3, while nephron progenitor cells (NPCs) themselves are defined by SIX2 expression.40 Plotting the expression distribution of these and markers of other potential cell populations shows the heterogeneity of cell types present at this stage (Figure 2A). The overlapping expression of PAX8 and SIX2 indicated there were definitive kidney lineage cells present. However, the localized expression of neural (SOX2) and muscle (MYL9 and ANXA1) precursors indicated “off-target” populations could be identified at this time point, as could paraxial mesoderm (PM) markers TCF15 and MEOX2 (Figure 2A). These genes were among the top DEGs in some clusters (Figure 2B). PAX8+CITED1+ IM (IM-1 and IM-2), TCF15+MEOX2+ PM, FGF8+SP5+ posterior presomitic mesoderm (pPSM) and SOX2+ PAX3+ neural progenitor (Neural-Prog) were identified (Figures 2A and 2B). All clusters except Neural-Prog were enriched for FOXC1 and FOXC2 indicating a commitment to mesoderm41,42 (Figure 2B). At this point, we could identify cells that did not have more specific lineage or population markers, which were labeled mesoderm-1, -2, and -3 (Figure 2C).

Investigating the 3, 4, and 5 days CHIR variations of the original Takasato protocol showed the majority cell population in all three conditions was “on-target” IM-1, while IM-2 was also well represented (Figure 2D). However, there was a considerable population of unspecified mesoderm; more than 25% in all three samples (Figure 2E). The proportion of Neural-Prog cells decreased as CHIR duration increased, while the pPSM cells were present almost only in the 5 days CHIR condition (Figure 2E). There were very few PM cells in these differentiations, while no evidence of lateral plate mesoderm was found. This showed that even in the most characterized protocol giving rise to kidney organoids, variable “off-target” cell types could be identified prior to 3D organoid generation.

Optimization of DevKidCC to accurately predict kidney and non-kidney cell types in complex in vitro datasets

After the 2D culture to stage 2, all cells were dissociated and reaggregated to form a 3D micromass and cultured at an air-media interface (Transwell culture).15 In the subsequent 5 days of culture, during which FGF9 remains present, NPCs should arise and begin transitioning to early nephron (EN) via a mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition.24 Classification of this stage, containing distinguishable kidney cell types, was initially performed using DevKidCC,6 a classification tool containing a hierarchal set of binary models trained on a multi-sample human fetal kidney (HFK) reference. Based on scPred,29 the tool DevKidCC6 uses three classification tiers to provide a definitive identity to a cellular state in a HFK.

Initial application of DevKidCC showed that a substantial component of cells did not match cells present in vivo (classified as unassigned) (Figure 3A). Of the 34,386 cells collected at stage 3, 52.1% were classified with a kidney lineage while 47.9% remained unassigned. The 52.1% of kidney cells subset into 31.5% nephron lineages, while 20.6% were mesenchymal lineages. No cells were defined as endothelial while five cells of ureteric epithelial identity were classified. Plotting each lineage separately displayed distinct groupings of nephron, NPC, and stroma (Figure 3A). However, large overlapping populations of NPC-like and unassigned cells were also present. DevKidCC6 had previously assigned the term NPC-like to cells with a transcriptional congruence similar to NPC, but below the default threshold of transcriptional congruence (set at 0.7) and lacking expression of PAX26 (Figure 3B). Cells identified as NPC-like within this stage displayed a broad distribution across stroma and unassigned domains suggesting that this classification represented an artifact. The large proportion of unassigned cells at stage 3 also suggested that, during this transition from IM to nephron, this classification tool was suboptimal. The expression of known nephron and non-nephron markers indicated that, while the NPC and nephron lineages had been accurately identified, there were false negatives in the unassigned cells (Figure 3B). Further, the population of SOX2 and PAX3 expressing off-targets had been classified heterogeneously as nephron, NPC-like or unassigned (Figure 3B).

To readdress this, the stage 3 data were clustered into 12 distinct populations using k-means (Figure 3C) with each cluster subsequently reclassified using DevKidCC (Figure 3D). In many clusters, unassigned and NPC-like cells were the most prevalent identity. Within clusters 0, 2, 8, 11, and 12, most cells were classified as nephron or NPC identity. These clusters expressed IM and metanephric mesenchyme (MM) markers CITED1, PAX2, and PAX8 and were thus classified as NPC-1, Early_Nephron, and UrEp based on their DevKidCC identity (Figure 3E). The remaining clusters expressed non-nephron markers such as PDGFRA, ZIC1, and PRRX1 (Figure 3B). Interestingly, PAX8 was expressed across a broad range of cells, including coexpression with ZIC1, SOX2, and PAX3 populations (Figure 3B). One cluster was enriched for the neural markers SOX2 and EPHA4, indicating these were a neural off-target population (Figure 3E). A cluster expressed MYL1 strongly in a few cells, alongside TCF15, PAX3, and SCX indicating a muscle-progenitor population we describe as myotome (Figure 3E). Clusters 1, 3, and 10 displayed a similar DEG expression profile and included cells designated as stroma by DevKidCC (Figure 3D). Mesenchymal genes PDGFRA and COL3A1 were expressed, as was the muscle lineage determinant PRRX1, but no other indicative markers of a kidney-specific population (Figure 3E). These were annotated as Early_Stroma and advanced mesoderm (AdvMeso-1/2) (Figure 3F). A mix of gene expression is present in clusters 5 and 7. Weak expression of PAX8, SIX2, and ZIC1 is present in cluster 5, with PRRX1, SOX2, and PAX3 in cluster 7 (Figure 3E). This profile predicts that these cell populations are similar to mesoderm and neural crest, respectively.

Within the original Takasato protocol conditions, the most prevalent populations were “on-target” cell types of Early_Nephron and NPC-1. Early_Stroma, and the closely related AdvMeso-2 populations were strongly represented in the 5 days CHIR condition, while the Early_Neuron and IM-3 populations were greatly enriched in the 3 days CHIR condition. Other populations were much rarer within the Takasato protocol, with their presence in this dataset derived from other test conditions (Figures 3G and 3H).

These analyses suggested that lineage divergence between nephron and non-nephron occurs during stage 3 of differentiation and enabled cell identities to be assigned to all clusters (Figure 3F). These included “on-target” cell types (nephron, NPC, and stroma), a proliferating nephron cell type (pNPC), unspecified or ambiguous mesenchymal populations (IM-3, AdvMes-1/2/3), and “off-target” populations (Neural_Crest, Early_Neuron, and myotome).

Development of neural and muscle “off-targets” in later organoids

At stage 4 (day 27 organoids), 59,631 cells were collected for single-cell profiling. As this stage resembles a trimester 2 HFK,15 it is optimal for DevKidCC classification. The nephron lineages (NPC, nephron, and UrEp) were clearly distinguished from the mesenchymal (stroma and NPC-like) and unassigned cell types (Figure 4A). A single endothelial cell (Endo, not shown) and few UrEp cells were present, which agrees with previous studies.4,6,23,43,44,45 We repeated the process of k-means clustering and analysis of DevKidCC per cluster, for more accurate classification (Figure 4B). This method identified nephron (clusters 3, 5, 6, 7, and 13), stromal (clusters 0, 9, 12, and 14), and unassigned (clusters 1, 2, 4, 8, 10, and 11) cell types (Figure 4C).

Differential gene expression showed EPCAM and NPHS1 expression marking the nephron and podocyte populations, respectively, while PDGFRA is enriched in the stromal population (Figure 4D). Previous studies have shown the presence of neural and muscle cell types in kidney organoids.4,5,22,23,24,46 The top DEGs in the unassigned clusters related to neural (SOX2, STMN2, and SOX10) and muscle (PAX3, PAX7, and MYOG). A number of populations expressed variations in neural and glial markers including ZIC1, ZIC2, SOX2, and MSX1. In particular, one population expressed ATF3, a marker of ependymal cells (Figures 4D and 4E), not previously identified in kidney organoid studies. The pigment-expressing cells (MLANA and PMEL) are most likely Schwann cells as they also express neural crest and glial markers (S100B and SOX10) (Figures 4E and S3). These have previously been labeled as melanocytes in other studies.4,5 Much of the stromal population co-expressed mesenchymal genes, PDGFRA, COL3A1, COL9A1, and PRRX1, alongside OGN known both as a regulator of fibrillogenesis but also as an inducer of osteoblast differentiation47 (Figure 4E).

Given the large proportion of non-kidney cell types present at this stage, label transfer using the tool Azimuth48 was utilised to compare all cells at stage 4 with a human fetal atlas containing cell types from 15 organs between 72 and 129 days post-conception.9 When investigating the distribution of maximum similarity scores at stage 4, all “on-target” cluster cells showed highest correlation with a kidney-metanephric and kidney-ureteric bud classification (Figure S4A). However, for most cells the predicted score was <0.5 (Figure S4B) indicating only a weak similarity to any population within the reference. It was interesting to see that stromal populations had very little correlation to the kidney-stromal reference, while podocytes (Pod) showed no correlation with kidney identity within this comparison (Figures S4A and S4B). When comparing the off-target populations to the reference, many cells retained the strongest correlation to kidney-metanephric although most cells had less than 0.5 mapping score (Figures S4C and S4D). The gene expression of key markers from the “off-target” populations correlate with the label transfer, providing confirmation of these in vitro cell identities (Figure 3E). Combining the DevKidCC and differential expression profiles enabled an in-depth classification of both “on-target” and “off-target” cell types in the kidney organoid dataset (Figure 4F).

Subsetting only the cells generated in the 3, 4, and 5 days CHIR variants of Takasato protocol removed almost all the neural and glia-ependymal cell types, indicating these had arisen from a different variation. The most abundant “off-target” population was glia-1, which had a weaker neural signature than other glial populations while maintaining some PDGFRA/COL3A1 expression. The 5 days CHIR condition was depleted of neural cell types, instead enriched for “on-target” stromal populations, indicating the longer CHIR improved early population specification toward the mesodermal lineages (Figures 4G and 4H).

These data demonstrated the large proportions and varieties of off-target cell types in kidney organoids. Further, the comparison between marker gene and label transfer analyses highlight the difficulty in reference-based identity assignment of in vitro data onto in vivo references. After a month of differentiation, the majority of cells taken on a transcriptional profile similar enough to an in vivo cell type for classification; however, this is still a gap between in vivo and in vitro cell type specification that is reflected in the “off-target” populations not represented in the in vivo organ.

Off-target populations vary in data from previous studies

The observation of “off-target” cell types within the Takasato15 protocol has been reported previously.4,5,6 A direct comparison was made between the transcriptional profile of classified “off-target” cells in this study with published data from two prior studies using the same protocol (Subramanian et al. and Wu et al.)4,5 and a more recent modification which reports enhanced proximal tubule maturation (Vanslambrouck et al.)46 (Figure 5A). These published protocols used distinct cell lines but collected data at similar time points to stage 3 and stage 4. Based on the prior observation of neural “off-targets,” the Wu dataset also contained transcriptional data from cultures treated with an inhibitor of BDNF shown to reduce neural commitment (Wu_TB).

Using Seurat, datasets from each publication were labeled based on the highest matched cell type using the anchor label transfer method. The most mature organoids from each study (compared to stage 4) showed distinct differences in the distribution and proportion of “off-target’ markers. Subramanian5 organoids contained more distalized nephrons and fewer neural subsets (Figures 5A and S4E). All lines, batches ,and treatments included in the Wu4 dataset also contained the cell types described in our study (Figure 5A). Conversely, the data from Vanslambrouck46 lacked almost all neural and muscle “off-targets,” although was previously shown to contain a cartilage population not detected using the atlas in the current study (Figure 5B). The distribution of maximum probability scores for all ‘off-target’ cells in these samples, separated by individual conditions per study, confirmed that most cells were above 0.5 (50%) score for similarity to their assigned identity indicating true transcriptional congruence and not artificially forced mapping (Figure 5B). The decrease in neural cell types, both in the Vanslambrouck (Van_D27) and Wu inhibition of BDNF (Wu_TB_ D26) impacted all neural subtypes (Figure 5B). Less prevalent “off-target” populations in our dataset (Ependymal, Schwann, neurons, and myocytes) were identified in some but not all conditions (Figure 5B).

This analysis was repeated for the stage 3 samples in each of these studies (Figure 5C). At stage 3, there were predictable differences in cell projections between samples based on the protocol used in the study. The Vanslambrouck day 13 sample was maintained in a nephron progenitor expansion media, which is reflected in the increase in higher scores for IM-3 and NPC-1 at this time. In contrast, the other studies had initiated nephrogenesis and had higher proportions of differentiated cell types, including the ambiguous mesoderms AdvMeso-1 and AdvMeso-2 (Figure 5C).

The consistent generation of off-target cell types shows that a spectrum of cellular heterogeneity is inherent in the differentiation protocol. These can be manipulated via targeted changes to optimize nephron maturation46 or inhibit unwanted genetic programs.4 The Takasato organoids in this study are comparable to previous datasets. Further, the atlas as a whole provides valuable information that can be used to advance kidney organoid differentiation.

Discussion

This study represents a comprehensive temporal single-cell transcriptional atlas across in vitro differentiation from pluripotency through mesoderm patterning to kidney in one iPSC line. The purpose of this manuscript was to provide a classification of cellular identity for all cells present within an in vitro differentiation to human kidney. What was observed were distinct “off-target” cell states present alongside kidney cell types, even within the most well characterized protocol. The classification of all 150,957 cells into 53 distinct identities within this atlas will serve as a valuable resource for the kidney organoid community, enabling a thorough dissection of where and why current kidney organoid protocols are imperfect and serving as a reference upon which modifications of the protocol can be compared.

In humans, the permanent kidney arises from posterior IM, with this in turn arising from the pPS. In the mammalian embryo, the IM gives rise to three pairs of nephron-containing organs, the pronephros, mesonephros, and metanephros. More anterior IM, regarded as exiting the tailbud at an earlier time point, gives rise to the pronephros, mesonephros, and nephric duct while posterior IM forms the metanephros. Across the mediolateral axis of the mammalian embryo, the mesoderm is patterned from paraxial through intermediate to lateral plate mesoderm. By performing a complete temporal factorial screen based around the most commonly used kidney organoid protocol, this study enabled an examination of how well the in vitro differentiation matched in vivo patterning. The differentiation was based around the canonical Takasato protocol together with growth factor variations in early mesodermal patterning to investigate how this widened the field of commitment. The objective was to classify the cellular identity of all cells present across time and condition. This allowed an evaluation of all “on-” and “off-” target cell types present across time. As anticipated, nephrons were present at both immature (NPC-2, EN, EDT, EPT, and EPod) and mature (DT, LOH, PT, PEC, and Pod) stages, minor populations of ureteric lineages (UTip, UIS, and UOS) in the shorter CHIR variants and populations of kidney-specific stroma (CS, MS, and MesS) throughout. A thorough investigation of NPC-like and unassigned cells within the organoids identified populations of neural (neuron and glial subtypes) and muscle (myocyte and satellite-cells) while some undecided cells do retain an NPC-like identity.

scRNA-seq is often analyzed by reducing the dimensional information from thousands of cells and genes into tens of component dimensions in an attempt to remove noise and home in on the patterns of gene expression that explain biological context.49 Cells are then grouped using clustering algorithms based on these lower dimensions prior to visualization, often after further dimensional reduction.50,51 While appropriate for the definitive assignation of identity to mature distinct populations, this is problematic with developing tissue and more so with an in vitro model of a developing tissue. Hence, the central challenge in this study was the accurate assignment of cellular identity in the absence of a ground truth. This was a problem both early, where data on gastrulation and early mesoderm patterning was limited, and late, with the presence of “off-target” populations with no clear in vivo counterparts. We found that trying to directly compare whole or even partial transcriptomes to the few available reference datasets for stages 1 and 2 was difficult due to the variability in the transcriptional profiles reported and the nomenclature used in each study. Regardless of the method used for reference-based classification, the results were unreliable. It proved more effective to rely on selected expressed genes to provide the best fit to a reference population. This illustrated that there is room for improvement in reference-based classification of early development.

At later stages, initial classification deployed DevKidCC.6 This tool is based on an organized set of binary models trained on in vivo HFK data to provide a robust and unbiased prediction kidney cellular identity, enabling direct cross comparison between datasets.6 When applied to stages 3 and 4, the classification of nephrons was highly accurate, but there was increased margin of error with stroma and unassigned (“off-target”) populations. Our analysis also suggested that the classification of NPC-like was an in vitro artifact and should be reconsidered. Classification based on DEG, Pearson’s correlation, and label transfer using Azimuth with a general human developmental reference enabled a more comprehensive assignation of likely cell identity for all non-nephron cells at these stages. Again, it should be noted that the sparsity of scRNA-seq data available, use of arbitrary clustering and the presence of likely in vitro transitional cell states will remain a challenge to definitive cell classification.

We describe here the presence of unexpected cell types within the in vitro system, and some evidence of plasticity throughout the differentiation process, including NMPs and their derivatives. The NMP population, co-expressing SOX2 and the mesodermal marker Brachyury/T, are reported to give rise to both the spinal cord and the PM, the latter forming the somites. While NMP do arise via gastrulation, there is not regarded to be an association between NMP, or PM, with formation of the IM. However, more recent studies have proposed the NMP to be a common progenitor for neural, PM, and IM while lineage tracing in the fish has suggested nephron formation from the PM.52,53 A recent study of spatial transcriptional identity during kidney development proposed the presence of plasticity in the identity of individual nephron cell types present during in vivo development based upon the surroundings of these cells with respect to their neighbors.54 Indeed, this concept of developmental plasticity is now of significant interest in dynamic landscape analysis of cell differentiation.55 Hence, some of the intermediate cellular states described here may well genuinely reflect options available during normal development, but rarely cataloged. A greater understanding of the dynamic landscape of cellular identity in vitro and in vivo will be valuable in improvements to directed differentiation protocols.

Limitations of the study

The major limitation of this study is the use of a single iPSC line for the generation of single-cell data.

Method details

Kidney organoid differentiation

iPSCs were initially seeding at a density of 7 x 104 cells into 6-well culture plates was then cultured with variations based on the Takasato protocol15 modified to use TeSR-E6 medium59 (STEMCELL technologies). Briefly, cells were cultured in 1.5mL of TeSR-E6 with 7μM CHIR99021 (Tocris) for 3-5 days. Then cells were cultured in 2mL of TeSR-E6 in a control condition of 200 ng/L FGF9 (bio-techne; 273-F9-025) and 1g/ml of heparin sodium (Fisher Scientific, SIG-H4784-250MG) fully described in this study. Between Stage 1 and Stage 2 the growth factors provided included the previously published 200 ng/ul FGF9 or one of the following alternatives: 100ng/ml FGF9; 100ng/ml FGF9 + 7 M CHIR; 100ng/ml FGF9 +LDN193189 (SMAD inhibitor); 100ng/ml FGF9 + AGN193109 (pan-RAR inhibitor) or no growth factors. Between Stage 2 and 3, replicates of 100ng/mL FGF9 cultures included the addition of 50ng/ml GDF11. All growth factor modifications were designed to widen the mesodermal trajectory in a mediolateral or rostrocaudal fashion to mimic variations between differentiations. For the purposes of this study, all endpoint cells at each timepoint have been pooled irrespective of variations in growth factor condition. Cells were then dissociated and resuspended into a dense 2 x 105/μl cell paste with TeSR-E6. Each organoid was generated by transferring 1 μl onto the top, air-facing side of a TranswellTM membrane (Corning), with six organoids per Transwell. This deposition method mirrors printed extrusion of cell paste.44 A pulse of WNT signalling was provided using 1.1mL of 5μM CHIR in TeSR-E6 for 1 hour. After 1 hour, cells were cultured with 1.1mL of TeSR-E6 with one of two growth factor combinations for 5 days, before being cultured in TeSR-E6 only to day 27. Media was changed every second day unless changing to a new media composition on a set protocol day. Samples were collected at the end of the four stages of differentiation as defined by changes in media composition, with the final organoids collected at day 27 of culture.

Design and execution of kidney organoid single-cell transcriptional profiling

The kidney organoid protocol utilised was first presented in Takasato et al.15 and then refined to use TESR-E6 minimal basal media.24,58 The most commonly used kidney organoid protocol,7 differentiation progresses from pluripotency through 4 stages; mesodermal induction (posterior primitive streak), IM commitment, nephron formation via a mesenchyme to epithelial transition and finally patterning and segmentation of nephrons within a renal stroma.7,15,23 This study was designed to capture samples at all four stages of differentiation for the standard kidney organoid protocol while also examining the impact of variations in the duration of initial mesodermal patterning (3, 4 or 5 days of CHIR; 3c, 4c, 5c) and the impact of additional signaling pathway agonists (Wnt, GDF and FGF signaling) and antagonists (inhibition of RA and BMP) on final organoid cellular composition (Figure 1A). These growth factors were selected to interrogate the impact of rostro-caudal and mediolateral shifts in differentiation on both on-target and off-target cell type specification.

All experiments were performed simultaneously using the same iPSC line to minimise batch effects. Further, each unique condition was differentiated into three separate cultures, pooling the three replicates at the point of single-cell capture to include technical variability. Finally, each sample was labelled using hash-tag oligos60 (HTOs) for sample multiplexing (Figure 1B). Following quality control, 150,957 single cells were processed for analysis. Integration, dimensional reduction, and diffusion mapping (Figure 1C) showed stages 1 and 2 to be nearly unique populations, while stages 3 and 4 had a clearer transition as organoids matured (Figure 1D).

scRNA-seq capture including Hash-Tag Oligo sample barcoding

Kidney organoids were dissociated to single cell, incubated with Totalseq-A antibodies (Biolegend; Cat# 394601, 394603, 394605, 394607, 394609, 394611, 394613, 394615) for sample hashing each samples collection day, pooled and super-loaded onto the 10x Genomics Chromium Controller (10x Genomics) to capture single cells. Briefly, 3x technical replicates for each sample were dissociated using TrypLE for 3 minutes, pooled and diluted in TEsR-E6 and centrifuged at 300 × g for three minutes. The cell pellets were resuspended in 100 μL cold Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS with 2% fetal bovine serum). Then, 2 μL of Totalseq-A hashing antibody was added and the cells were gently pipetted to mix before incubating for 20 min on ice. Cells were then washed twice with FACS buffer by centrifuging at 300 × g for 5 min, discarding the supernatant and resuspending the cell pellet in 100 μL cold FACS buffer. Approximately 40,000 cells were loaded onto the Chromium Single Cell Chip B (10x Genomics; Catalog Number: PN-1000073) to capture 20,000 single cells with the Gel Bead Kit V3.0 (10x Genomics; Catalog Number: PN-1000075). GEM generation, barcoding, cDNA amplification, and library construction were performed according to the 10x Genomics Chromium User Guide (CG000183). Libraries were prepared with the Single Cell 3’ V3.0 Library and Gel Bead Kit (10x Genomics; Catalog Number: PN-1000075). The samples collected from stages 1, 2 and 3 occurred across six days, each capture multiplexed up to eight unique conditions and all were sequenced on the same run. The final collection of stage four cells occurred separately, multiplexing either 3 or 4 conditions to allow for greater cell numbers per condition for the more complex “mature” organoids. This design allowed us to maximise our capacity for cell capture per condition while minimising the potential batch effects that would impair downstream analysis.

13 unique scRNA captures were collected across the protocol. 7 captures were completed in the first 14 days of the protocol and were sequenced in the same batch, while the 6 captures at day 27 were sequenced in a second batch. HTO library sequencing was performed on the NextSeq 500/550 using a NextSeq 60 cycles kit (Illumina, 20416695) while the mRNA sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 using a NovaSeq S4 230 cycles kit (Illumina, 20363961).

Use of R packages for reference-based cell classification

5 tools were used with data from each reference to further interrogate cellular identity within stages 1 and 2. SCINA30 uses the expression of gene sets within a data point to predict similarity to cell types, annotating cells above a particular level. scAnnotatR33 trains a classification model based on enriched gene sets to predict identity. scClassify32 generates cell-type hierarchies based on ensemble learning and sample size estimation. SciBet31 uses an entropy test to identify sets of genes enriched in a reference that provides significant association with cellular identity, using these to predict identity in a query. scPred29 uses machine learning to train classifiers for annotated references based on shared principal components and eigenvalues. DevKidCC6 utilises a set of pre-trained binary classifiers in a three-tier hierarchy of classification levels to robustly identify fetal kidney cell types in single cell data sets.

Azimuth label transfer

A Seurat object was imported to the shiny application hosted at https://app.azimuth.hubmapconsortium.org/app/human-fetus after which the resulting files were downloaded for further analysis using R. This method uses the label transfer method applied in Seurat which generates a confidence score ranged from 0 to 1, with 1 being maximal similarity that quantifies how confidently each cell from a query dataset can be assigned to a cell type in a reference dataset. It's derived from a matrix of probabilities where the highest probability for each cell's match to a reference cluster is extracted as its matching confidence score.

Immunofluorescence assay of kidney organoids

At day 27, kidney organoids were fixed using 4% PFA in PBS for 20 minutes, before washing 3x in PBS and storing at 4 degrees Celsius. For immunostaining, organoids were first blocked using 10% donkey serum in PBS + 0.5% Triton-X for 2 hours at room temperature. Organoids were then washed 3x in PBS then incubated in primary or secondary antibody solution overnight at 4 degrees Celsius. Following each incubation, organoids were washed 3x in PBS for 30 minutes at room temperature. Immunofluorescence staining was performed using the following antibodies: Rabbit anti-MAFB (Sigma-Aldrich #HPA005653), Mouse anti-HNF4A (Life Technologies #MA1-199), Mouse anti-EPCAM-488 (Biolegend #324210), Goat anti-GATA3 (R&D systems #AF2605)), Rabbit anti-GFAP (DAKO #Z0334), Mouse anti-S100β (Sigma-Aldrich #S2532), Alexa Fluor anti-Rabbit 568 (Fisher Scientific #A10042), Alexa Fluor anti-Mouse 405(Abcam #ab175659), Alexa Fluor anti-Goat 647 (Fisher Scientific #A21447). Organoids were imaged using an Andor Dragonfly spinning disk confocal microscope.
