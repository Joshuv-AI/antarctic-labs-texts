TWGFD: an integrative platform for tetraploid wheat gene family analysis
Authors: Qinglin Ke, Tingting Li, Yiting Su, Han Lin, You Xu, Mengxing Wang, Yihan Li, Xiaojun Nie, Licao Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gene families, primarily are composed of transcription factors, comprise evolutionarily conserved orthologs derived from common ancestors, characterized by shared sequence homology, structural domains, and fundamental roles in biological processes including development, growth, and environmental adaptation. As the immediate progenitor of hexaploid bread wheat (Triticum aestivum), tetraploid wheat constitutes both a vital industrial crop and a critical genetic reservoir, harboring numerous agronomically important stress-resistance traits. Systematic characterization of its gene families is imperative for elucidating functional and evolutionary mechanisms, yet dedicated analytical resources remain unavailable. To address this gap, we developed the inaugural Tetraploid Wheat Gene Family Database (TWGFD; http://triticumgfdb.com), integrating 92 gene families encompassing 11,781 wild emmer (T. dicoccoides) and 13,357 durum wheat (T. durum) genes. This multi-omics platform delivers eight analytical modules: gene annotations, structural architectures, cis-regulatory elements, conserved protein motifs, phylogenetic relationships, expression profiles, ortholog identification, and nucleotide variation analysis. TWGFD's interactive interface enables gene retrieval, BLAST alignment, and bulk data downloads. This resource accelerates research on wheat domestication, adaptive evolution, and agronomic trait discovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13063-5.

Background

Common wheat (Triticum aestivum) is a cornerstone of global food security, with annual production exceeding 620 million tons to supply 20% of human caloric intake and serve as the primary staple for > 35% of the world’s population [1]. Projected demographic growth necessitates a > 60% increase in wheat production by 2050, yet domestication has imposed severe genetic bottlenecks that constrain crop diversity [2]. Wild relatives harbor extensive untapped genetic variation, offering critical opportunities for trait introgression and crop improvement [3]. This species originated through two hybridization events. The first occurred 0.36–0.50 million years ago between Triticum urartu (AA) and a close relative of Aegilops speltoides (SS) [4], generating wild emmer wheat (Triticum dicoccoides, AABB). Primary domestication selected for non-shattering spikes, yielding cultivated emmer (Triticum dicoccum, AABB), while secondary domestication eliminated hulled grains to produce free-threshing durum wheat (Triticum durum, AABB) [5]. Subsequently, hybridization between tetraploid wheat and Aegilops tauschii (DD) ~ 8,000–10,000 years ago established hexaploid common wheat [6]. Tetraploid wheat bridges wheat evolution, domestication, and polyploidization [5]. As one of humanity’s earliest cultivated cereals, its domestication predates common wheat by millennia. Through its contribution of two-thirds of the hexaploid genome comprising the A and B subgenomes, tetraploid wheat provides essential agronomic traits including large grain size, elevated protein content, disease resistance, and abiotic stress tolerance, rendering it indispensable for breeding [7]. Through long-term evolution, tetraploid wheat has developed diverse variants, specifically the wild species T. dicoccoides, the early domesticated form T. dicoccum, and the improved cultivar Triticum durum, which collectively represent an ideal model system for investigating cereal evolution and artificial selection dynamics within the Triticeae tribe [8].

Gene families originate from duplication events that generate homologous members distributed as clustered or dispersed chromosomal loci [9]. Their expansion occurs via whole-genome duplications or specific small-scale mechanisms including tandem, segmental, and transposon-mediated replications [10]. Initial genetic redundancy from these events becomes resolved through neo-functionalization or sub-functionalization during adaptive divergence [11]. Members of a given family typically encode functionally related proteins with conserved domains and exhibit analogous sequence architectures. Notably, the majority function as transcription factors, regulating essential plant processes including metabolism, development, signaling transduction, and environmental stress responses [12]. Recent advances in tetraploid wheat research have identified key gene families through integrated phylogenetic, structural, and expression analyses. In T. durum, functionally characterized families include GASA [13], Catalase [14], sulfate transporters [15], AP2/ERF [16]. T. dicoccoides studies further elucidate MADS-box [17], U-box [18], ZIP [19], and NAC [20] families, highlighting their roles in stress adaptation and developmental regulation.

The emergence of "Breeding 4.0", driven by cloud computing, artificial intelligence, and high-throughput sequencing, relies on integrated multi-omics data [21]. To systematically exploit multi-omics data for identifying key agronomic trait genes and improving complex crop traits, researchers have established gene family databases across multiple species. Representative databases such as MGFD [12], PlantGF [22], BGFD [23], and PlantTFDB [24–27], exemplify this trend. However, no comprehensive platform currently integrates and analyzes all available tetraploid wheat gene families. To address this critical gap, we developed the Tetraploid Wheat Gene Family Database (TWGFD; http://triticumgfdb.com) (Fig. 1). This resource integrates 92 gene families encompassing 25,138 genes, specifically 11,781 from T. dicoccoides and 13,357 from T. durum. It consolidates eight core analytical modules covering gene characteristics, structure, cis-acting elements, conserved domains, phylogeny, expression profiles, orthologs, and variants. The platform further supports functional utilities including search tools, sequence alignment, database documentation, and data download capabilities. Construction and content

Data collection, gene family identification, and physicochemical property analysis

Amino acid sequences of T. dicoccoides and T. durum were retrieved from Ensembl Plants (https://plants.ensembl.org). Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) corresponding to target protein domains were acquired from the InterPro database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). Pfam IDs corresponding to target protein domains are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Candidate genes were identified using HMMER v3.0 with default parameters. Functional annotations of these candidates were further verified through National Center for Biotechnology Information—Conserved Domain Database (NCBI-CDD; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/), Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool (SMART; http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/), and InterPro. The physicochemical properties, including molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), were calculated using ExPASy ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Chromosomal localization data were extracted from genome annotation files. Orthologs in Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa were identified using InParanoid v8.0 [28] with default parameter..

Gene structure, cis-regulatory element, and conserved motif analysis

Gene structures were visualized with Gene Structure Display Serve v2.0 (GSDS; http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) based on gene transfer format annotations [29]. Putative cis-acting regulatory elements in promoter regions (1.5 kb upstream of coding sequences) were predicted using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/plantcare/html/) [30]. Element distribution patterns were profiled and visualized as heatmaps using the R package pheatmap. Conserved protein motifs were identified with Multiple EM for Motif Elicitation (MEME; https://meme-suite.org/meme/) under these parameters: maximum motifs = 8, width range = 6–250 residues, and no repetition constraints [31].

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction

Full-length protein sequences were aligned using Clustal X v1.83 with default parameters. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed in MEGA X via the neighbor-joining method [32], with branch support assessed by 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Orthologous relationships among A. thaliana, O. sativa, T. aestivum, T. dicoccoides, and T. durum were analyzed using InParanoid v8.0 and visualized with Circos v0.65 [33].

To estimate evolutionary rates of homologous genes, codon-based alignments were generated from amino acid and corresponding mRNA sequences using PAL2NAL v14 (http://www.bork.embl.de/pal2nal/) [34]. The codeml package in PAML v4.9 was employed to calculate nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates, as well as Ka/Ks ratios [35]. Divergence times (T) were estimated using the formula T = Ks/2λ, where λ represents the mutation rate (6.5 × 10–9 substitutions per site per year).

Expression profiling and nucleotide variation analysis

Publicly available RNA-seq datasets encompassing diverse tissues, developmental stages, and stress conditions were curated from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra). The collection included 42 T. dicoccoides and 68 T. durum samples across 17 BioProjects (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Table 3). Raw SRA files were converted to FASTQ format using parallel-fastq-dump (https://github.com/rvalieris/parallel-fastq-dump). Quality control was performed using Trimmomatic v0.36 with the following parameters: TRAILING = 3, LEADING = 3, SLIDINGWINDOW = 4:5, and MINLEN = 90. Processed reads were aligned to reference genome assemblies (T. dicoccoides WEWSeq v.1.0 and T. turgidum Svevo v1) via HISAT2 v2.1.0. Transcript abundance was quantified as transcripts per kilobase per million mapped reads (TPM) using StringTie v2.1.3 [36]. Differential expression patterns were visualized with the R package pheatmap based on log2(TPM + 1)-transformed expression values.

Whole-genome resequencing data from 107 tetraploid wheat accessions (33 T. dicoccoides, 25 T. dicoccum, and 49 T. durum; Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Table S5) were processed as follows. Quality-filtered reads were aligned to the T. dicoccoides WEWSeq v.1.0 reference genome using BWA-MEM v0.7.13 with parameters -k 32 -M -R [37]. PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). Variants were called per sample with GATK v4.0 HaplotypeCaller [38], followed by joint genotyping and stringent filtration based on criteria: QD < 2.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < − 8.0. Biallelic SNPs were further filtered with VCFtools v0.1.14 under thresholds of minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05, missing rate < 0.1, and depth within 1/3 × to 3 × mean coverage [39]. Functional annotation of variants was performed using SnpEff v4.3 [40], categorizing exonic SNPs as synonymous or nonsynonymous and classifying noncoding variants by genomic features such as splice sites, UTRs, and introns. Complementary variant data from Wheat VMAP 1.0 (Genome Sequence Archive: CRA001951; https://bigd.big.ac.cn/gsa) were integrated for cross-validation.

Database infrastructure and development

The TWGFD platform (http://triticumgfdb.com) was deployed on a Tencent Cloud server running CentOS v7.9 (8-core CPU, 64 GB RAM, 500 GB storage), using Nginx v1.25.0 as a reverse proxy. The front-end interface was developed with HTML5, Bootstrap5, and JavaScript, while back-end operations used PHP v5.6. Processed data were stored in a MySQL v5.7.22 database, accessed via custom PHP scripts for dynamic queries.

For sequence homology analysis, BLAST v2.11.0 databases were built from T. dicoccoides and T. durum coding sequence (CDS) datasets. Homology searches were supported by the ViroBlast toolkit (https://els2.comotion.uw.edu/product/viroblast) [41]. These BLAST search results are presented within the database frontend as interactive visualizations using embedded HTML widgets, facilitating user-driven exploration of sequence homology relationships.

Utility and discussion

Overview of TWGFD

The Tetraploid Wheat Gene Family Database is accessible at http://triticumgfdb.com. It integrates 92 gene families (41 transcription factor families and 51 non-transcription factor families), encompassing 11,781 genes from wild emmer wheat and 13,357 genes from durum wheat. Within wild emmer wheat, the leucine-rich repeat receptor-like protein kinases (LRR-RLK) family is the largest (1,086 members) while Whirly is the smallest (4 members). In durum wheat, F-Box constitutes the largest family (1,475 members) and ATG8 the smallest (3 members). The database homepage provides a full gene family list for browsing and selection, supplemented by a search function in the navigation bar (Fig. 2). Each gene family features a dedicated homepage with a concise description and eight integrated modules: Gene Family Member, Gene Structure, Cis-acting Element, Conserved Motif, Phylogenetic Tree, Expression Pattern, Orthologous Gene, and Nucleotide Variation. Gene family member module

This module lists all family members with identifiers, chromosomal locations, gene lengths, and protein lengths. Individual gene pages present information through three dedicated sections: (1) Basic information detailing exon count, chromosomal position, gene length, protein length, molecular weight, pI, and GRAVY; (2) Orthologous genes listing IDs and symbols of verified orthologs in A. thaliana and O. sativa; and (3) Sequences providing downloadable FASTA-formatted protein and CDS files.

Gene structure module

Intron–exon organization provides insights into gene structure diversification and phylogenetic relationships. Interactive schematics display exons (black-purple boxes), 5′ UTRs (light green boxes), 3′ UTRs (dark green boxes), and introns (black lines), with a scale bar indicating sequence length. Annotations are downloadable in GFF format for customized analysis.

Conserved motif module

Evolutionary relationships and functional insights are provided through conserved motif distributions. Eight motifs identified by MEME are represented by distinct colored boxes, with consensus sequences displayed below. A scale bar enables precise motif localization.

Cis-acting element module

To elucidate transcriptional regulation, promoter cis-elements were systematically annotated. Results for 42 functionally significant element types are visualized in categorized heatmaps, highlighting roles in organ development, hormone response, light signaling, and stress adaptation. These annotations support predictions of gene involvement in specific biological pathways. Raw cis-element abundance data per promoter are downloadable.

Phylogenetic tree module

Unrooted phylogenetic trees, reconstructed using the Neighbor-Joining method from full-length protein alignments with 1,000 bootstrap replicates, reveal evolutionary relationships. Interactive visualization facilitates analysis of gene clustering patterns. Users may download FASTA-formatted alignments for custom tree reconstruction.

Gene expression module

Spatiotemporal and stress-responsive expression profiles quantified using TPM values provide functional insights. The database integrates wild emmer wheat data encompassing 40 tissues or stages from two developmental series and two conditions under a single stress treatment, along with durum wheat data comprising 16 tissues or stages from two developmental series and 52 conditions across 12 stress treatments, totaling 110 samples derived from 17 studies. Expression patterns are visualized via heatmaps, with raw TPM values downloadable. This module is complemented by direct access to our companion Triticeae Expression Database (TriticeaeExpDB: https://www.triticeaeexpdb.cn), a sister resource developed by our research group. This integrated platform enables comprehensive expression profiling with tools for differential expression analysis, enrichment analysis, and co-expression network construction.

Orthologous genes module

Orthologs between A. thaliana and O. sativa were identified using the InParanoid v8.0 algorithm. This method employs a stringent, graph-based clustering approach that initiates with reciprocal best BLAST matches between the complete protein sequences derived from the genomes of the two species. High-confidence orthologs constitute 28.7% of wild emmer genes relative to A. thaliana and 37% relative to O. sativa. For durum wheat, these values are 28.7% and 35.8% respectively, consistent with phylogenetic distances. Ka/Ks analysis revealed evolutionary mechanisms: ratios < 1 indicate purifying selection, = 1 neutral evolution, and > 1 positive selection. Ka/Ks values for wild emmer relative to A. thaliana ranged from 0.001 to 0.745 with a mean of 0.041, while those relative to O. sativa ranged from 0.001 to 1.456 with a mean of 0.176, indicating differential evolutionary constraints. Orthologous relationships among wild emmer, durum, and common wheat are provided, enabling studies on selection pressures during tetraploid wheat domestication and polyploidization.

Nucleotide variation module

Genetic variation was characterized using resequencing data from 107 tetraploid wheat accessions. Annotated variants include genomic positions and distributions, downloadable in VCF format. These resources support marker-assisted selection, genome-wide association studies for agronomic traits, and research on domestication and adaptive evolution.

Database tools and functionality

To enhance user experience and facilitate data exploration, the TWGFD incorporates several integrated tools accessible via the navigation menu: Search, BLAST, Introduction, and Download.

Search

TWGFD features a versatile Search function. Users can query gene families or individual genes by entering the full family name or specific gene ID. An advanced search interface enables filtering based on criteria such as chromosome number, protein length, or exon number. Search results provide hyperlinks to detailed gene information pages (Fig. 4A-B).

BLAST

The BLAST tool allows users to identify putative homologous genes within TWGFD for their query sequences. Users can input nucleotide or amino acid sequences in FASTA format directly or upload text files. TWGFD supports five core BLAST algorithms (BLASTN, BLASTP, BLASTX, TBLASTN, and TBLASTX) against dedicated gene libraries for both wild emmer and durum wheat. An advanced interface allows customization of search parameters, including E-value threshold, word size, and maximum number of hits (Fig. 4C-D).

Introduction

This section provides a dropdown menu linking to the TWGFD Materials and Methods module. Direct links redirect users to original data sources and the websites of software tools used in the analyses. A comprehensive workflow schematic is also displayed, detailing the full analytical pipeline with specific parameter configurations (Fig. 4E).

Download

The Download page offers comprehensive access to TWGFD data resources. Users select either wild emmer or durum wheat and then choose the desired gene family from a dropdown list. Available datasets for download include gene structures (GFF format), promoter sequences (TXT format), phylogenetic trees (FASTA format of alignments), expression profiles (TXT format), orthologous gene information (TXT format), and nucleotide variants (VCF format). Datasets are generated and downloaded upon clicking the "Generate" button (Fig. 4F).

Case study

To demonstrate the utility of the TWGFD for evolutionary and functional genomics studies in wheat, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the mitochondrial transcription termination factor (mTERF) gene family in T. durum, designated as TtmTERFs. Utilizing the family member module within TWGFD, we identified 176 TtmTERF genes within the durum wheat genome. Gene length varied substantially, ranging from 67.42 kb (TRITD6Bv1G004320) to 324 bp (TRITD1Bv1G217810). Corresponding protein lengths spanned from 649 amino acids (TRITD2Bv1G120740) to 107 amino acids (TRITD1Bv1G217810). Calculated MW averaged 37.91 kDa, with a minimum of 12.18 kDa (TRITD1Bv1G217810) and a maximum of 73.28 kDa (TRITD2Bv1G120740). The pI ranged from 7.24 (TRITD6Bv1G158040) to 10.63 (TRITD2Bv1G244580), with a mean pI of 9.40 (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table S6). Chromosomal localization revealed a non-uniform distribution across 14 chromosomes (Fig. 5B). Chromosome group 6 harbored the highest density, with 63 members on 6B and 33 members on 6A. Phylogenetic analysis based on protein sequences retrieved from TWGFD resolved evolutionary relationships among the TtmTERF members (Fig. 5C). Examination of gene structure showed that TtmTERFs possess, on average, 1.53 exons, with 128 members (72.7%) containing only a single exon, indicative of a predominantly intron-less structure. Analysis of exon–intron organization revealed that phylogenetically related members often exhibit similar gene structures. Conserved motif analysis via MEME identified 8 significant motifs (motifs 1–8). Motif 1 corresponds to the core mTERF functional domain. The predominant motif arrangement among TtmTERFs was motif 5 + motif 7 + motif 3 + motif 1 + motif 2. Crucially, motif composition and organization also exhibited conservation within specific phylogenetic clades (Fig. 5D), further supporting shared evolutionary histories and potentially similar functions.

Cis-regulatory element analysis of the 1.5 kb upstream promoter regions of the TtmTERF genes revealed a total of 39 distinct cis-element types associated with diverse biological processes (Supplementary Table S7). Key elements implicated in development included AC-I (14 copies), AC-II (12 copies), and the CAT-box (127 copies). Elements associated with stress response encompassed LTR (involved in cold response; 111 copies), the WUN-motif (wound response; 44 copies), and MBS (drought response; 97 copies). Hormone-responsive elements were abundant, including ABRE for ABA signaling (413 copies); AuxRR-core (25 copies) and TGA-element (79 copies) for auxin signaling; CGTCA-motif/TGACG-motif for MeJA signaling (326 copies); and ERE (35 copies), GARE (92 copies), P-box (43 copies) for GA signaling. Elements responsive to SA included the TCA-element (72 copies). The widespread presence of these diverse regulatory elements strongly suggests putative functional roles for TtmTERFs in multiple signaling pathways and stress adaptation mechanisms in durum wheat.

TtmTERF expression patterns were assessed using transcriptomic data within TWGFD (Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S8). Average expression levels during grain development at 3, 5, 11, 16, 21, and 30 days after pollination (DAP) were 1.70, 1.42, 1.31, 1.18, 0.60, and 0.34, respectively (Fig. 5E). This pattern indicates significantly higher expression during early and mid-grain development stages (3–16 DAP) followed by a pronounced decrease during later stages (21–30 DAP). Notably, a 28-member tandem duplication cluster was identified within a ~ 3.15 Mb region (approximately 11.54 Mb—14.69 Mb) on chromosome 6B. Expression profiling within this cluster revealed considerable functional divergence (Fig. 5E). For instance, genes such as TRITD6Bv1G004770, TRITD6Bv1G004660, TRITD6Bv1G004050, and TRITD6Bv1G004480 exhibited broad, high expression across multiple tissues, whereas TRITD6Bv1G004690, TRITD6Bv1G004320, TRITD6Bv1G004030, and TRITD6Bv1G004560 displayed negligible expression in all tested conditions. This divergence suggests distinct evolutionary fates following duplication, potentially including sub-functionalization, neo-functionalization, or pseudogenization. Analysis of TtmTERF responses to abiotic stress revealed specific candidates potentially involved in heat tolerance. For example, TRITD6Bv1G177140, virtually unexpressed under control conditions, was induced by heat stress in both grains and leaves. Similarly, TRITD6Av1G015120 expression in leaves increased 2.32-fold under heat stress. Collectively, these transcriptomic data provide valuable insights into the potential biological functions of TtmTERFs and highlight candidate genes for subsequent functional validation studies.

Discussion

Recent advances in genome sequencing have become primary drivers for genetic and breeding improvements in wheat and related cereals [42]. Substantial progress in tetraploid wheat genome assembly has been achieved through enhanced algorithms such as the TRITEX workflow, coupled with innovative sequencing technologies including PacBio HiFi, 10 × Genomics, chromosome conformation capture, and BioNano optical mapping [5, 43, 44]. These newly released tetraploid wheat genome assemblies facilitate functional genomics, genetic analysis, and breeding applications.

To maximize the utility of multi-omics data for elucidating biological functions and molecular mechanisms, dedicated databases for collecting, storing, and curating genomic information are essential. The completion of the A. thaliana genome initiated an era of whole-genome scale gene family identification [45]. Publicly accessible databases catalog approximately 70 transcription factor families, including ARGIS [46, 47], RARTF [48], and DATF [49]. Compilation resources such as PlnTFDB [50], PlantTFDB v4.0 [27], GFDP [9], MGFD [12], and PlantGF [22] further expand gene family coverage across plant species.

While these resources initially provided valuable information, many have remained static for over a decade and lack integration with continuously updated genomic and multi-omics datasets. Existing databases primarily focus on gene family identification and basic characterization, omitting functionally critical data such as expression patterns and variation atlases. Moreover, their rigid frameworks preclude seamless incorporation of emerging omics data. Systematic integration of heterogeneous multi-omics information within a unified framework is imperative to support contemporary wheat genomics and breeding research.

To address these limitations, we developed the TWGFD with the following features: This first-of-its-kind resource comprehensively characterizes 92 essential gene families across T. dicoccoides and T. durum, encompassing 11,781 and 13,357 members respectively, constituting the most extensive collection for tetraploid wheat gene family analysis. TWGFD integrates eight functional modules per family, including member annotation, gene structure, cis-acting elements, conserved domains, phylogenetic relationships, expression profiles, orthologous relationships, and nucleotide variation. Curated data from Ensembl Plants, Pfam, Expasy, PlantCARE, and NCBI enable unified analyses. Transcriptome datasets incorporate 17 BioProjects encompassing developmental, biotic, and abiotic stress responses, facilitating biological function prediction. The database further integrates population-level resequencing data from 107 tetraploid wheat accessions, enabling characterization of functional genes relevant to agronomic traits. Cross-linking to our TriticeaeExpDB enhances interoperability. TWGFD is publicly accessible with intuitive search capabilities including keyword queries, BLAST alignment, and bulk data download.

Future development will focus on four key areas: First, expanding coverage to novel gene families as the tetraploid wheat pangenome landscape evolves. Second, incorporating emerging data types including epigenetic modifications, proteomic profiles, and single-cell transcriptomes to elucidate regulatory mechanisms at cellular resolution. Third, establishing genotype–phenotype associations through integrated phenotypic datasets to enable predictive breeding. Fourth, developing dynamic visualization tools and online analytical modules for user-customized analysis.

In summary, TWGFD provides a foundational platform for candidate gene discovery, functional characterization, and molecular breeding in tetraploid wheat. This comprehensive database bridges the gap between rapidly accumulating multi-omics data and practical applications, particularly benefiting researchers without specialized bioinformatics expertise. Continuous expansion of gene family annotations, incorporation of novel multi-omics datasets, and development of analytical tools will ensure TWGFD remains a vital community resource for advancing wheat genomics research.

Conclusions

The rapidly expanding volume of multi-omics data in tetraploid wheat represents a valuable resource for gene family research. To harness this information, we developed the TWGFD. This comprehensive platform integrates detailed annotations for 25,138 members spanning 92 gene families from T. dicoccoides and T. durum. TWGFD incorporates user-friendly tools, enabling researchers without specialized bioinformatics expertise to conduct gene family analyses. Serving as a central resource, TWGFD will facilitate the identification of candidate genes and functional exploration of key agronomic traits for researchers and breeders. Given the accelerating pace of omics data generation, we will implement regular updates incorporating diverse multi-omics data, particularly epigenomic, proteomic, and metabolomic datasets, to maintain the platform's comprehensiveness and currency. Furthermore, additional web-based analytical tools tailored for tetraploid wheat gene family studies will be developed and integrated into TWGFD.
