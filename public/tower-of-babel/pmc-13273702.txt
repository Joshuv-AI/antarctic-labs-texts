Interpreting Postdiagnosis Toenail Metal Measurements in Childhood Nephrotic Syndrome
Authors: Lei Wang, Zhiui Li, Liang Zhong, Dongxiang Zheng
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with interest the report by Robinson et al.1 Their study addresses a question that frequently concerns families of children with nephrotic syndrome and shows that overt toxic-range metal exposure was uncommon in this Canadian cohort. We respectfully suggest, however, that the negative associations may merit a narrower interpretation.

Toenail metals generally reflect exposure over the months before clipping, not remote exposure at disease onset, and biomarker validity remains agent-specific and incompletely standardized.2,3 In this study, clippings were obtained once, at a median of 2.1 years after diagnosis.1 Because steroid resistance and frequent relapsing or steroid dependence are early-course phenotypes, these analyses may be better suited to later disease-course associations than to etiologic or early prognostic inference.

In addition, the handling of below-detection values may matter. All values below the method detection limit were set to the detection limit itself.1 Fixed substitution may bias regression estimates when nondetects are common, and recent mixtures methodology suggests that below–limit of detection handling can affect weighted quantile sum analyses.4,5 This is relevant here because several analytes had substantial censoring.

Finally, only 109 of 298 children had sufficient residual material for inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry of nonmercury metals after mercury testing.1 Recent analytical work suggests that low-mass toenail inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry and planned sample partitioning can preserve biospecimens for multiple assays, while still requiring attention to intraperson variability.3 We therefore think that the main message is strongest as evidence of low toxic-range exposure at sampling in this cohort, whereas repeated or near-diagnosis biomonitoring with censoring-aware analyses would more directly address whether clinically relevant associations exist in childhood nephrotic syndrome.
