Progressive fibrotic interstitial lung diseases in India: national challenges and implications for global health policies
Authors: Satish Sasikumar, Swati Patidar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterstitial lung diseases (ILDs) constitute a heterogeneous group of diffuse parenchymal lung disorders characterized by varying degrees of inflammation and fibrosis. Global estimates indicate that the incidence of ILDs has risen by over 50% in the recent years. In India, epidemiological data remain limited and inconsistent due to heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria and regional disparities, impeding accurate burden estimation and policy prioritization.Main textAmong ILDs, progressive fibrotic forms, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in particular, cause substantial morbidity and mortality. Many ILDs progress to irreversible fibrosis, leading to declining lung function, impaired quality of life and shortened survival. Timely and precise diagnosis of progressive fibrotic (PF)-ILDs is imperative to facilitate early therapeutic intervention and potentially slow down fibrotic progression. Diagnostic evaluation necessitates high-resolution computed tomography, histopathological examination and multidisciplinary team consensus, all of which are often unavailable or prohibitively expensive outside tertiary care centres in India. Therapeutic options are confined primarily to two antifibrotic agents approved for IPF, which modestly attenuate disease progression but do not significantly improve survival in patients. Access to these therapies is limited by cost, lack of proper insurance coverage and non-uniform healthcare infrastructure. Clinical presentation is often delayed owing to low disease awareness and referral gaps. Although, pulmonary fibrosis following novel coronavirus disease 2019 has attracted recent attention, it constitutes a minor fraction of the PF-ILD burden. Despite the high morbidity and mortality associated with PF-ILDs, these conditions are inadequately represented in India’s health policy instruments, research agendas and funding priorities.ConclusionsThe burden of PF-ILDs in India demands an urgent policy response, which involves integrating ILDs into national non-communicable disease frameworks, expanding diagnostic capacity in secondary care, improving affordability and access to antifibrotic drugs and supporting multicentre registries for surveillance and research. Addressing these gaps could reduce disease impact domestically and provide a model for other low- and middle-income countries confronting similar challenges in tackling fibrotic lung diseases.Keywords: Chronic respiratory disease, Epidemiology, Health policy, Indian healthcare sector, Interstitial lung disease, Public health, Pulmonary fibrosis

Introduction

Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs), also known as diffuse parenchymal lung diseases, encompass a large group of heterogeneous disorders that are mostly characterized by inflammation and fibrosis (scarring and hardening) in the lung parenchyma [1, 2]. In addition, these conditions can also typically affect the peripheral airways, arteries and epithelial and endothelial linings [3]. Among more than 200 known ILDs, the world is becoming increasingly concerned about the fibrosing types that cause progressive and irreversible lung damage, leading to high patient mortality rates [2, 4, 5]. Over the years, various strategies for grouping ILDs have helped clinicians make reasonably optimal diagnoses and select effective therapeutics for these lung diseases [2, 3, 6]. The number of global cases of ILDs is on the rise and recent global epidemiological analyses have confirmed increasing incidence and prevalence trends across diverse populations, likely driven by greater awareness, improved diagnostic capabilities and an ageing demographic. These differences emphasize the need for comprehensive global surveillance to understand the true epidemiological burden and facilitate context-specific healthcare planning [7–12].

Similarly, in India, the absence of authoritative epidemiological studies on ILDs has led to an underrepresentation of the actual number of cases, causing the diseases to remain unrecognized as a public health priority [13–15]. Hence, we have reviewed the studies on ILDs in India and their outcomes and limitations. Further, we propose strategies through which national authorities can effectively orchestrate the available resources to manage the latest increase in the number of cases of ILDs in the population.

Classification of interstitial lung diseases: an overview

ILDs were classified for the first time in 1969 using traditional histopathology-based methods, and by 2002, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) and multidisciplinary team approaches were included in the classification criteria. This was followed by the addition of molecular and genetic disease markers, and grading of variations in the natural progression of these lung diseases, improving the classification process in 2013 [2, 3, 6]. Recently, Kreuter et al. proposed that ILDs can be broadly classified into four major categories on the basis of their aetiology and characteristics [4]. The first category includes ILDs with known causes, such as connective tissue disease (CTD), pneumoconioses resulting from occupational exposure to dust or particulates and drug-induced ILDs, whereas the second category includes cases of unknown causes (idiopathic), such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and idiopathic non-specific interstitial pneumonitis. The third group encompasses granulomatous ILDs, such as sarcoidosis and hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), which involve the formation of non-caseating granulomas (clusters of activated immune cells such as macrophages and T lymphocytes without necrosis) in the lungs. The last category comprises various rare types of ILDs, including lymphocyte interstitial pneumonitis and pleuro-parenchymal fibroelastosis, which are characterized by distinct pathological features and clinical manifestations [4, 16] (Fig. 1). Although ILDs are known to manifest in older adults, they also occur in infants with dissimilar aetiologies and are referred to as children’s interstitial and diffuse lung disease (chILD) syndrome [17]. Interestingly, another recent study alternatively classified ILDs on an aetiological basis into four categories, namely, lung-only, systemic disease-related, exposure-related and vascular disorders. However, this classification system involves the grouping adult and paediatric ILDs into one class and advocates the use of a comprehensive data-driven diagnostic algorithm along with an integrative multiomics approach to make a precise diagnosis and prognosis of ILDs [3]. The evolution from morphology-based to multimodal, molecularly informed classifications has direct clinical implications. It facilitates better identification of disease subtypes, can guide targeted therapies, enable earlier intervention and improve patient-specific treatment planning. Nevertheless, even with advanced classification schemes, the diagnosis and management of ILDs still demand multidisciplinary evaluation integrating clinical, radiological and histopathological findings [2, 3, 18].

Fatal interstitial lung diseases

Among the known ILDs, pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a serious, debilitating and fatal condition that affects the alveoli, pulmonary interstitium and airways [2, 4]. Again, those cases of IPF, where the underlying cause(s) remains unknown, appear to be among the more common and unpredictable types of PF [19]. IPF is characterized by shortness of breath, a dry and hacking cough, tiredness, aching joints and muscles, inadvertent weight loss and clubbing of fingers [20–22]. The diverse range of probable causes of PF include exposure to hazardous substances, a genetic predisposition or sequelae to viral infections and autoimmune disorders [2, 18, 23]. At the cellular level, pathological attributes such as traction bronchiectasis/bronchiolectasis (dilatation of bronchi and bronchioles caused by scarring within the lungs leading to distortion of these airways) and consequent subpleural honeycombing with basal dominance and fibroblastic foci become evident. Furthermore, excessive deposition of extracellular matrix proteins, such as collagen and fibronectin leads to scarring and stiffening of the lung parenchyma, eventually distorting the lung architecture [23–26]. Without proper and timely medical intervention, the hardened lung tissues become dysfunctional, leading to a respiratory decline in patients who ultimately die within 2–3 years of detection [20, 24, 27].

Importantly, the clinical phenotype termed progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF), which encompasses both IPF and progressive fibrosing forms of non-IPF ILDs, is associated with very high mortality and short median survival. Large national and multicentre cohorts have shown that patients with PPF exhibit a median survival of only ~2.9–3.7 years after progression onset, comparable to or only slightly better than IPF [28–30]. In addition, patients with PPF show significantly higher mortality risk compared with non-progressive ILDs as observed in the Shanghai single-centre cohort study, where higher HRCT scores [hazard ratios (HR) 1.684] and systolic pulmonary artery pressure >36.5 mmHg (HR 3.619) were independently associated with increased mortality [31]. Reflecting its significance, the 2022 international clinical practice guideline jointly issued by the American Thoracic Society (ATS), European Respiratory Society (ERS), Japanese Respiratory Society (JRS) and Latin American Thoracic Association (ALAT) formally recognized PPF as a distinct entity with major prognostic and therapeutic implications [32]. Interestingly, approximately 20% of patients diagnosed with pulmonary sarcoidosis develop lung fibrosis and progress to stage IV sarcoidosis, indicating that inflammatory ILDs can evolve into fibrosing ILDs, leading to a significant increase in both morbidity and mortality [2, 33]. Another study revealed that approximately 18–32% of individuals diagnosed with ILDs other than IPF are susceptible to developing progressive fibrosing phenotypes, and in these cases, mortality occurs anywhere between 61 and 80 months after the onset of symptoms in patients [5].

Epidemiology, diagnosis and management of interstitial lung diseases

Global and regional epidemiology of interstitial lung diseases

According to the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBDS) in 2019, ILDs and sarcoidosis accounted for 3.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) (estimated sum of years of life lost due to premature death and those lived with disability) worldwide, including 4.7 million prevalent cases and 24.2 million new cases [34]. Furthermore, a study published in 2024 reported that the BRICS countries (namely, Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa) collectively had 11.4 million cases of ILDs in 2019, highlighting the growing burden in these regions [35]. A staggering 51% surge in the global incidence of ILDs occurred, from 207.2 in 1990 to 313.2 cases per 100,000 in 2019 [36, 37].

Focusing on northern parts of India, the crude estimates of the incidence and prevalence of ILDs were 10.1–20.2 and 49.0–98.1 per 100,000 individuals, respectively, while the standardized national prevalent burden of these diseases has been projected to be 0.45–0.89 million [38]. Determining the exact epidemiology of ILDs is inherently difficult, particularly in regions such as the BRIC countries (Brazil, Russia, India and China), where limited access to high-resolution chest imaging, variability in lung function testing and disparities in healthcare infrastructure hinder timely and accurate diagnosis. These obstacles, combined with differences in study methodologies, contribute to inconsistencies in reported outcomes [8, 13, 18]. Reflecting this variability, large multicentre efforts from India have yielded divergent results: while some registries identified HP as the most frequent ILD subtype (accounting for 48.2%, 47.3% and 32.2% of cases), other studies conducted in India using different cohorts and study designs highlighted sarcoidosis as the leading diagnosis (42.2% and 37.3%) across different time periods [8, 38–42] (Table 1). Adding to this body of evidence, a large single-centre series from northern India (n = 884) reported HP (35.9%) and connective tissue disease–related ILD (30.9%) as the leading subtypes, with reticulations and traction bronchiectasis being the predominant HRCT findings [43].

A recent study reported that rheumatoid arthritis–associated ILD (RA-ILD) was the most common subtype of connective tissue disease–related ILD, accounting for 40.3% (252/620) of enrolled patients. In this cohort, non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) was the predominant pattern on HRCT, found in 59.3% (327/551) of patients for whom chest HRCT data were available [44]. Behera et al. reported the prevalence of 19.72% RA-ILD in their cohort and the predominant HRCT pattern was usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP)/probable UIP (53.84%), while NSIP accounted for 46.15% [45]. This contrasts with the findings of Santhanam et al., who observed RA-ILD to be the most frequent connective tissue disease–associated ILD (CTD-ILD), with NSIP being the predominant pattern. The discrepancy may reflect differences in patient selection, disease duration, referral patterns or sample size across studies. Importantly, Agarwal et al. in a cross-sectional analysis of 100 CTD-ILD patients from a tertiary care centre in western India, confirmed that RA (26%) and systemic sclerosis (21%) were the most common CTDs associated with ILD, with NSIP (42%) and UIP (21%) as the leading HRCT patterns and a strikingly high prevalence of pulmonary hypertension (40%) [46] (Table 2). Recent data also suggest that the pandemic linked to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID- 19) caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) modestly altered the aetiological spectrum of ILDs in India. In a 136-patient cohort, post-COVID ILD contributed to 8% of the total caseload, ranking as the fourth most common cause after idiopathic interstitial pneumonitis, HP and CTD-ILD, with 55% (11/20) of the new-onset cases arising in patients who had experienced severe COVID-19 illness [47, 48]. Taken together, these Indian cohorts underscore the significant burden of RA-ILD and CTD-ILD more broadly, highlighting heterogeneity in radiological patterns and the clinical challenge posed by pulmonary hypertension. Since antifibrotic therapy has shown greater benefit in progressive fibrosing ILDs with UIP-like patterns, as demonstrated in the INBUILD trial [49], systematic characterization of CTD-ILD subtypes in India is essential to optimize treatment strategies. These discrepancies reflect the impact of geography, methodology and time period on reported ILD patterns in India.

Against this backdrop of heterogeneous national data, retrospective analyses across different regions of India, spanning from the late 1970s to the 2010s, consistently highlighted idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) as a frequent subtype of ILD, though its relative burden varied over time and between centres. The earliest hospital-based series from northern India (Chandigarh) and western India (Mumbai) in the 1970s–80s already described fibrosing alveolitis/IPF as the leading diagnosis among ILD cases [50, 51]. Subsequent clinicopathological studies from northern India (Delhi and Chandigarh), including bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and spectrum analyses, further supported the predominance of IPF into the 1990s and early 2000s [52, 53]. Evaluations from southern India (Vellore) also documented IPF as the most common idiopathic diffuse parenchymal lung disease (DPLD), distinct from the secondary forms [54]. Tertiary care data from western India (Mumbai) in the following decade again underscored IPF as a leading ILD entity [55]. More recent reports from eastern India (Kolkata and other centres) confirmed IPF as a major contributor but also revealed geographic variation, with HP and connective tissue disease–related ILDs emerging prominently in some cohorts [56, 57]. Taken together, these studies illustrate that while IPF is repeatedly reported as a dominant ILD subtype in India, there are significant interregional and temporal differences in the distribution of ILD subtypes, highlighting both diagnostic challenges and possible environmental and occupational influences [13]. In comparison, as summarized by Kaul et al., IPF accounts for 11.5–38.6% of ILD cases in Europe, with lower proportions in population-based or incident cohorts and higher proportions in tertiary referral cohorts and in prevalent cohorts. In North America, IPF ranges from 22.5–51.9% across studies. Among incident ILD cases, CTD-ILD varies from 7.2% in Belgium to 34.8% in Saudi Arabia [8]. Such regional differences directly shape diagnostic emphasis and treatment strategies—from detailed exposure histories in HP-endemic areas to early antifibrotic use in IPF-dominant regions [8, 9].

Evolving spectrum of interstitial lung diseases

Although India has historically lagged in ILD research, recent studies provide important region-specific insights. Notably, the ILD-India registry, which is one of the first and largest multicentre analysis of ILDs (1084 patients across 27 centres) in India, unequivocally demonstrated that these diseases are more prevalent in the region than previously appreciated (Table 1). While the overall spectrum of ILDs aligns broadly with patterns observed in Western countries, the registry’s comprehensive data collection has brought to light critical insights that advance our understanding of disease distribution at the national level [14, 39]. Although certain factors such as regional case representation, limited lung biopsy availability and reliance on preliminary clinical diagnoses present inherent challenges, these limitations reflect the current realities of clinical practice and do not undermine the registry’s foundational value [39, 58, 59]. Furthermore, Singh et al. identified HP as the most common ILD in India, strongly associated with exposures to avian and domestic environmental antigens, followed by CTD-ILDs; notably IPF was less prevalent than in Western populations (Table 1) [8, 39]. These constraints emphasize the need for, and support the validation of, accessible diagnostic tools like transbronchial cryobiopsy in Indian centres [15, 39, 58–60]. Corroborating these discussions, a tertiary-clinic analysis from eastern India reported a steady rise in outpatient registrations for DPLD from 104 cases in 2009 to 204 cases in 2019, and an increase in relative outpatient share from 4.36% to 6.9% over the same period, underscoring the need for standardized surveillance and diagnostic frameworks to determine the true national burden [57]. It is important to note that, at the time of data collection, consensus guidelines existed only for IPF, whereas formal diagnostic frameworks for PPF and non-IPF fibrosing ILDs were still evolving, which understandably influenced diagnostic precision [9, 61–63]. Taken together, these landmark studies not only provide a robust foundation but also signal an urgent need for further comprehensive, standardized and regionally representative research to refine our knowledge of ILDs in India [15, 63]

To address this gap, public health researchers in India should leverage insights from recent multi-ethnic studies conducted in Greater Paris, France, employing stringent and standardized diagnostic criteria to generate robust epidemiological data for each ILD subtype nationwide [38, 64]. Additionally, reference can be made to the largest epidemiological investigation of ILDs in Asia, undertaken in Hong Kong, which utilized a territory-wide validated electronic health record system. This comprehensive study, spanning over 16 years, remains the longest longitudinal analysis worldwide, providing valuable data on prevalence, incidence, survival and risk factors associated with ILDs [65]. Finally, it is imperative to explore potential regional variations in environmental exposures, occupational hazards, rates of lung infections and use of prescription or non-prescription drugs, as these factors may contribute to the heterogeneity observed in epidemiological findings on ILDs across different parts of India.

Impact of coronavirus disease 2019 on pulmonary fibrosis

Global deaths from ILDs and pulmonary sarcoidosis rose by 166.63% between 1990 and 2019 (that is, more than a 2.5-fold increase over this period) [7]. Alarmingly, GBDS has projected that the number of deaths due to ILDs will markedly increase in the coming decades, largely attributed to a rise in the incidence of PF [4, 66]. In this context, compared with several types of cancer, IPF, which is arguably the most prevalent and incurable form of PF and has poorer survival rates, could considerably increase the number of mortalities in the population [24, 67, 68]. Notably, the reported global prevalence of IPF varies widely, ranging from 7 to 1,650 cases per 100,000 individuals [9].

Recently, as a matter of concern, a significant increase in the number of cases of PF was recorded during the COVID-19 pandemic caused by SARS-CoV-2, which ravaged people in the years 2020–2023 [48, 69, 70]. In patients who recovered from COVID-19, PF was identified as a debilitating sequela that can sometimes cause permanent lung damage [71, 72]. This long-term effect of COVID-19 suggests that SARS-CoV-2 can have a profibrotic effect and trigger lung fibrosis, as observed in human herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7), HHV-8, cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infections [23, 71, 73]. Prior outbreaks of PF from SARS-CoV and MERS-CoV showed prevalence of 62% and 33%, respectively [69, 74, 75].

Persistent post–COVID-19 ILD/PF occurs in only a minority of survivors, but given the massive scale of the pandemic, even small proportions represent a considerable clinical burden. A systematic review estimated post-COVID-19 pulmonary fibrosis (PCPF) in 7% of patients, while a large nested case-control study from Israel showed that the odds of a new diagnosis of PF were 1.8 times higher overall and more than nine times higher after severe COVID-19 [76, 77]. A multicentre radiographic study found persistent fibrotic ILD with functional impairment in 5.6% of patients at six months, though estimates remain highly variable depending on disease severity, imaging definitions and follow-up duration [78, 79]. Prevalence of PCPF varies in countries such as China, Egypt, Iran, the USA, Italy and the United Kingdom (UK); the UK reports 9.3%, while one study from China reports up to 84.15% [69]. A meta-analysis by Hama Amin et al. estimated overall PCPF prevalence at 44.9%, higher in males (53.8%) than females (46.2%), and 2.88 times more common in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), indicating comorbidities affect prevalence [69, 80]. As post-COVID-19 effects rise, PF poses an mounting challenge globally [71, 72].

The data from India remain limited, but available reports suggest post-COVID-19 ILD represents a modest proportion of cases, with a disproportionate burden among relatively younger patients, mostly aged between 41-60 years, recovering from severe illness [47]. Further, a review by Udwadia et al. emphasizes the need for careful, long-term follow-up of multiple cohorts of post-COVID-19 survivors with ILDs, incorporating serial lung function assessments and imaging [81]. Although some investigators have expressed scepticism regarding the long-term clinical significance of PCPF in India [82], recent reviews note clinical similarities with IPF, suggesting that early recognition, structured long-term follow-up with functional and imaging evaluations and consideration of antifibrotic or immunosuppressive therapy may be warranted, even in the absence of standardized regimens [83, 84].

As of 2 February 2025, over 777.4 million confirmed cases of COVID-19 and more than 7 million deaths have been reported globally. During the 28-day period from 6 January to 2 February 2025, India reported 23 new COVID-19 cases, ranking just after Thailand with 1,884 cases and Indonesia with 65 cases among Southeast Asian countries [85]. With ongoing COVID-19 cases worldwide, understanding the incidence of PCPF remains important [69, 71]. However, the exact causal link between COVID-19 and PF is not yet clear [71]. Diagnosis involves chest imaging, pulmonary function tests (PFTs) and lung biopsy. Pulmonary fibrosis may increase the risk of acute exacerbations in COVID-19 patients with existing ILD, affecting mortality [86].

While reports from other countries provide valuable insights, early clinical experience and anecdotal observations in India suggest some uncertainty remains regarding the prevalence and clinical significance of PCPF [69, 81, 82, 87, 88]. This underscores the need for robust, India-specific research and comparative studies with countries such as China to better understand regional epidemiology and outcomes. Given India’s diverse clinical landscape, such tailored research is essential to develop effective diagnostic and management strategies. Furthermore, greater emphasis should be maintained on preventable and treatable non-IPF causes of ILD, which continue to have significant clinical relevance.

Diagnosis of interstitial lung diseases

From a clinical perspective, the differential diagnosis of ILDs such as IPF, idiopathic non-specific interstitial pneumonia (iNSIP), fibrosing hypersensitivity pneumonitis (fibrosing HP) and PF associated with systemic diseases requires a panoramic evaluation of signs and symptoms due to the complex nature of these diseases. In this context, iNSIP involves uniform lung inflammation and fibrosis, unlike patchy fibrosis seen in IPF, and fibrosing HP refers to HP with established lung fibrosis. Pulmonary fibrosis associated with systemic diseases is lung scarring caused by interstitial lung involvement in autoimmune connective tissue disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic sclerosis [2–4, 20, 21]. On the basis of the presenting symptoms, a patient suspected of having an ILD is subjected to a thorough clinical evaluation, which includes an accurate physical examination. Sometimes, auscultation reveals a crackling sound such as the “opening of Velcro” within the lungs, which is called sclerosophonia and may be an early indication of IPF [4, 89, 90]. An exhaustive aetiological evaluation is essential in suspected ILD cases. This involves a comprehensive history-taking to document the onset, progression, and pattern of symptoms; assessment of smoking habits; and detailed inquiry into occupational (for example, asbestos, silica, metal dust) and environmental exposures (for example, birds, moulds, farming) [2, 4]. A meticulous drug history is obtained to identify medications known to cause ILD, such as amiodarone, methotrexate, bleomycin or nitrofurantoin. Family history is reviewed for evidence of familial PF or related genetic disorders. Screening for systemic diseases includes eliciting signs and symptoms suggestive of connective tissue disorders [for example, Raynaud’s phenomenon, joint pain (arthralgia), rash], vasculitis or sarcoidosis. Infectious causes such as tuberculosis (TB), atypical pneumonias and chronic fungal infections are considered when relevant. Laboratory investigations include autoimmune serologies (antinuclear antibodies, rheumatoid factor, cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies and myositis-specific antibodies), inflammatory markers and HP antigen panels [2, 4, 18, 40, 63, 91, 92].

Radiodiagnosis involves X-ray-based high-resolution non-contrast thin-section computed tomography to visualize the characteristic UIP pattern in the preliminary stages [2, 4, 89]. Serial PFTs, which include spirometry, oximetry, forced vital capacity (FVC), diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and a 6-minute walk test (an exercise-based evaluation), are conducted to monitor signs of worsening lung physiology as a result of disease progression [92–94]. For severe and advanced cases of PF, endoscopic techniques such as bronchoscopy for retrieving bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and lung tissue biopsy are often needed to confirm the diagnosis of these lung disorders [4, 89]. An Indian retrospective study demonstrated that a multidisciplinary team approach obviated the need for biopsy in most patients with DPLD, achieving 80% clinico-radiological concordance when compared with histopathology, though NSIP remained less reliably classified without biopsy [95]. Follow-up data from the ILD-India registry identified the Modified-Gender Age and Physiology (M-GAP) score, past history of TB, smoking history, presence of honeycombing on computed tomography and the ability to perform spirometry as key predictors of survival in patients with ILD [41]. Beyond survival metrics, patient-reported outcomes are increasingly recognized as critical endpoints; in a South Indian cohort of 159 patients with ILD, health-related quality of life was most impaired in the ‘breathlessness and activity domain’ with FVC, 6-minute walk distance and exercise-induced desaturation identified as independent predictors [96]. Focusing on patient-centred outcomes, a recent pre-post study demonstrated that a four-week home-based pulmonary rehabilitation programme using minimal resources significantly improved both functional capacity and quality of life in Indian ILD patients [97].

Emerging evidence suggests that proactive monitoring of respiratory health, coupled with screening for telomere-related and surfactant-related genes, may help detect early signs of fibrosis and support timely intervention [92, 98, 99]. Additionally, novel serum and BAL biomarkers hold promise for identifying patients at elevated risk for progression of PF [6]. In an Indian prospective cohort, elevated Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6) and surfactant protein D (SP-D) [high-molecular-weight, lung-specific glycoproteins produced by type II alveolar cells and elevated in ILDs] independently predicted ILD progression and mortality, and combined biomarker models with conventional measures markedly improved prognostic accuracy [100]. However, these approaches are not yet endorsed by major clinical guidelines and warrant further validation.

To complicate things further, individuals with ILDs can show symptoms that overlap with respiratory illnesses such as asthma, pneumonia, bronchitis, COPD and, in some cases, even lung cancer, often leading to misdiagnosis [101–103]. Nearly 55% of people with ILDs have reported one or more misdiagnoses and 38% reported two or more misdiagnoses with a median delay of 11 months to two or more years in some cases for a final diagnosis of the specific type of ILD [101]. IPF, one of the most extensively studied ILDs, was historically and in some regions continues to be initially misdiagnosed as TB, particularly in TB-endemic countries like India [13, 104]. Such misdiagnoses often lead to dire consequences in patients due to inappropriate treatment strategies and delayed medical care [102, 104].

In India, where delayed diagnosis of ILDs is prevalent, early detection is paramount. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients presenting with progressive dyspnoea, dry cough or characteristic auscultatory findings such as Velcro crackles. PFTs including spirometry, DLCO and HRCT are essential for early evaluation, even in resource-constrained settings. Screening should be prioritized in high-risk populations including individuals with occupational or environmental exposures, systemic autoimmune disorders or familial PF [63, 105]. Emerging tools such as biomarkers obtained from body fluids and targeted genetic testing could facilitate earlier recognition of ILD; however, their availability in India is still limited. Early recognition enables timely initiation of antifibrotic or immunomodulatory therapy, potentially slowing disease progression and improving clinical outcomes [63, 106–108].

Therapeutic landscape, governmental policies and economics

With regard to available drug-based treatments, the Food and Drug Administration, USA (USFDA) has approved only two antifibrotic drugs, namely pirfenidone and nintedanib, which delay instead of reversing the progression of IPF [13, 109, 110]. Since these drugs decrease the risk of acute deterioration in lung function, early diagnosis of IPF is crucial for prompt treatment and prolonging the survival of affected individuals. In the context of drugs for treating ILDs other than IPF, nintedanib has recently been approved for treating progressive fibrosing ILDs (PF-ILDs) and systemic sclerosis-associated ILD (SSC-ILD) [110, 111].

Importantly, management of fibrosing ILDs carries broader health policy implications, as timely diagnosis, equitable access to antifibrotics and structured supportive care can alter disease trajectory and reduce mortality. However, access and affordability remain uneven worldwide. In the Asia Pacific region, high antifibrotic drug costs, limited insurance coverage and absent pharmacoeconomic frameworks severely restrict availability [112]. By contrast, European cost-effectiveness analyses, such as a Belgian model, indicate that despite its high price, nintedanib may be cost-saving when compared with pirfenidone and represents a good value when included in reimbursement schemes [113]. Real-world claims-based data from the USA further show that antifibrotic therapy lowers respiratory-related hospitalization costs, underscoring its potential to reduce economic burden on health systems [114].

In India, access to antifibrotic therapy remains constrained by high out-of-pocket expenditure, limited insurance penetration and uneven availability beyond tertiary centres. Although the introduction of generics has reduced costs, significant financial barriers persist [13, 112, 115]. Generic listings by TradeIndia for pirfenidone (200 mg tablets) vary widely, with box prices observed from Indian Rupees (INR) 120 to INR 630. At the standard IPF maintenance dose (approximately 2,400 mg/day, which equals 12 tablets of 200 mg), this translates to a patient cost of roughly INR 2,880–15,120 per month [equivalent to United States Dollars (USD) 33–172] and INR 34,560–181,440 per year (equivalent to USD 393–2,062), depending on the supplier and formulation (conversion at ~INR88 = 1USD, September 2025) [116, 117]. Similarly, generic listings for nintedanib (150 mg capsules) on TradeIndia vary by supplier, with box prices observed from INR 748 to INR 3,500. At the standard IPF maintenance dose (150 mg twice daily), this corresponds to a patient cost of roughly INR 1,496–7,000 per month (equivalent to USD 17–80) and INR 18,201–85,167 per year (equivalent to USD 207–968), depending on the brand and pack size [118, 119]. India’s per capita net national income for the financial year 2024–25 is INR 205,324, which is approximately USD 2,470 per year (or approximately USD 205 per month) [120], making this generic therapy constitute a substantial proportion of monthly income. Unsurprisingly, financial burden contributes to treatment discontinuation in real-world cohorts [62, 115, 121], and prescribing patterns are strongly influenced by affordability, with pirfenidone still more frequently prescribed than nintedanib, often driven by economic considerations, even in patients whose clinical phenotype or guideline-based evidence would suggest nintedanib as the preferred choice [115, 122]. This situation contrasts with European and North American settings, where reimbursement mechanisms mitigate cost barriers, a position supported by pharmacoeconomic analyses confirming the long-term cost-effectiveness of antifibrotics [113, 114].

In addition to economic barriers, real-world tolerability concerns have been reported in India; in a prospective cohort of 30 patients with IPF, 80% were unable to tolerate the standard pirfenidone dose of 2400 mg/day, with a lower median tolerated dose of 1800 mg/day, although adverse effects were mostly mild and manageable without having to discontinue the use of this drug [122, 123]. Similarly, a larger retrospective Indian cohort (n = 113) reported that only 24 patients with IPF and other fibrosing ILDs tolerated the full 2400 mg/day dose, with the majority tolerating lower doses (1200–1800 mg/day) and better tolerability was observed in males with greater height and body surface area, while adverse effects were mostly mild and not dose-limiting [124]. Real-world prescribing practices from India also point to systemic challenges: in a prospective study of 77 ILD patients in northern India, HP was found to be the most common subtype, with nearly one-quarter experiencing treatment-related adverse drug reactions, mainly gastritis and hepatitis [125]. These findings emphasize the dual need for careful environmental exposure assessment to avoid misdiagnosis and for strengthened pharmacovigilance frameworks tailored to ILD management in India. These health-system barriers in India, coupled with delays in diagnosis, highlight the pressing necessity for policies promoting affordable access to antifibrotic therapies, their integration into public insurance frameworks and the development of novel therapeutic strategies [5, 126].

In the case of non-IPF fibrosing ILDs, recent studies have shown that immunomodulatory corticosteroids remain the preferred first-line therapy in many subtypes. Depending on the underlying aetiology, additional immunosuppressants such as mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, methotrexate, cyclophosphamide or rituximab are frequently employed, particularly in connective tissue disease–associated ILDs, chronic HP and sarcoidosis. Choices for second- and third-line therapy remain diverse in practice and are guided by ILD subtype, patient comorbidities and physician experience [5, 20, 127]. The only feasible treatment option thus far has been replacing diseased lungs with those from a healthy and matched human donor; however, the cost of standard-of-care (a legal obligation for healthcare professionals to provide the committed level of care to the patients) for the transplant event can range from 16,748 USD to 361,959 USD [128, 129]. This wide disparity in cost depends on factors such as the type of lung disease, the number of transplants performed at the hospital each year, the use of mechanical life support, the severity of illness before and after the transplant and the different methods of assessing costs by various healthcare sectors [128]. These differences show that economic evaluations are complex and necessitate careful analysis of all aspects when assessing the costs and benefits of lung transplantation [128, 130].

The feasibility of lung transplantation in India is limited by constrained capacity, late referral and socioeconomic barriers, with only a small fraction of patients with end stage ILDs undergoing the procedure. This is primarily due to limited donor availability, low organ donation rates and the clustering of transplant programmes in a few high-volume tertiary centres in India such as the Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (Chandigarh), Apollo Hospitals (Chennai) and Fortis Hospitals (Delhi) [131–133]. According to the Indian Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation registry, 475 lung transplants had been performed in India up to March 2023. A recent report by National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation (NOTTO), India documented 197 lung transplants in 2023, with 228 procedures by December 2024 [134–136].

Interstitial lung disease accounts for the majority of referrals, with IPF being the leading indication, highlighting the rising burden of fibrotic lung diseases as a key driver for transplantation [130, 137]. India’s deceased organ donation and transplant activity, including lungs, has historically lagged behind global leaders: in 2019, Spain had 2,302 donors [49.6 per million population (pmp)] and 419 lung transplants (9 pmp), the United States had 11,870 donors (36.1 pmp) with 2,759 lung transplants (8.4 pmp), while India recorded only 715 donors (0.5 pmp) and 114 lung transplants (0.1 pmp). Although modest gains have been achieved with over 18,900 organ transplants, including 228 lung transplants, in 2024, the deceased organ donation rate in India remains below one per million population. This persistent disparity highlights the urgent need to expand organ donation programmes and to strengthen transplant infrastructure nationwide [131–133, 136, 138], reflecting challenges such as limited public awareness, cultural beliefs, prolonged pre-transplant waiting times and logistical hurdles in organ retrieval and transport [89, 131–133, 139].

In India, 1-year survival following lung transplantation has been reported as 78% in a multicentre cohort, with 3-year survival around 70% [132]. This cohort included patients from Chennai, Bengaluru, and Mumbai, with ILDs such as IPF and HP as the most common indication (66%), followed by bronchiectasis and pulmonary hypertension. A single-centre study reported a 1-year survival of 85.6%, including bilateral, single and heart–lung transplants, reflecting outcomes at a high-volume centre with 132 transplants over 3 years [130]. While multicentre outcomes remain modestly lower than international benchmarks, the high-volume centre’s results are comparable to global averages. Attawar et al. reported international 1-year and 5-year survival rates of approximately 85% and 59%, respectively, for adult lung transplant recipients since 2010 [139, 140]. According to the 2025 International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) Registry, 1-year post-transplant survival has improved over time, reaching 88.4% for transplants during 2018–2023, reflecting progressive gains in short-term outcomes worldwide [141].

Common post-operative complications include primary graft dysfunction, cytomegalovirus reactivation, multidrug-resistant bacterial and fungal infections and financial barriers to sustained immunosuppression, while patients remain at risk of life-threatening hyperacute rejection (within 24 h), acute rejection (within a year), chronic lung allograft dysfunction and opportunistic infections, necessitating intensive follow-up and management [130, 135, 136, 139]. Taken together, limited procedural volume, donor scarcity, infection-related mortality and the cost of long-term care restrict lung transplantation as a population-level solution for IPF in India and emphasize the need for earlier referral pathways, expansion of transplant capacity and strengthened perioperative infection control and long-term support systems [130, 134, 137, 139, 142]. Thus, the high cost of lung transplants, systemic constraints and general resistance to organ donation are major barriers to developing this kind of treatment modality in populous Asian countries such as India and China [89, 130, 143–146]. Therefore, governmental policies are needed both in developing and economically weak countries to help patients afford the cost of a lung transplant, promote organ donation and develop dedicated transplant infrastructure [130, 143, 145]. Pulmonary fibrosis is frequently accompanied by several comorbid conditions, which include gastroesophageal reflux disease, emphysema, obstructive sleep apnoea and coronary artery disease that independently contribute to the patient’s clinical burden. Additionally, complications such as pulmonary hypertension and an increased risk of lung cancer can arise secondary to the underlying fibrotic process [4, 18, 147–149]. Both comorbidities and complications pose challenges for disease management and may significantly impair quality of life [18, 149, 150]. Supportive measures such as long-term oxygen therapy, pulmonary rehabilitation and management of comorbidities are integral to improving ILD patient outcomes [149, 151].

From an economic perspective, the growing number of patients with ILDs, including PF, may place additional demands on India’s healthcare system, where specialized hospitals, expertise and advanced diagnostic facilities are concentrated mainly in metropolitan areas and major cities. This uneven distribution can contribute to delayed diagnosis of ILDs in some regions [13, 139]. While PCPF cases continue to be reported, the broader focus should remain on strengthening public health strategies for the long-term management of ILDs as a whole, ensuring equitable access to diagnosis and care while maintaining an appropriate emphasis on the relative burden of PCPF [71, 150].

Major challenges in dealing with interstitial lung diseases in India

Globally, research on fibrotic lung diseases, including ILDs and PF, has attracted significant investment, amounting to billions of dollars. Despite decades of rigorous research, substantial gaps remain in understanding the underlying mechanisms, pathogenesis, diagnosis and treatment of these diseases [111]. In India, considerable progress has been made in research on ILDs and PF, with many studies conducted by prominent institutions [13]. Despite these efforts, India still lags behind due to insufficient budgetary allocation, modest research initiatives, inadequate medical infrastructure and limited collaborative networks on ILDs [14, 15, 152]. It is important to note that the concept of progressive fibrosing ILDs (PF-ILDs) and the more recently formalized classification of PPF are relatively new and, as stated earlier, together represent a subset of ILDs with a progressive fibrotic phenotype [2, 21, 49]. While research on PF-ILDs/PPF is still evolving worldwide, India’s focus remains on strengthening the broader research landscape for ILDs and PF. To emerge as a major global partner in this field, India needs to further enhance its research strategies and investments targeting all aspects of ILDs, including the recently recognized area of PF-ILDs/PPF [2, 13–15, 21].

With 18% of the world's population, India accounted for an alarmingly high share (32%) of the global burden of DALYs attributed to chronic respiratory diseases [153]. Despite the substantial burden of respiratory diseases in the Indian population, recent reports reveal that research on chronic respiratory diseases (including ILDs) has received comparatively less funding [152]. However, it is worth mentioning that the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS), India, which released INR 80.96 billion as grants for research in the period of 2012–2017, has recently incorporated a component for studying chronic respiratory diseases [154, 155]. Nonetheless, the focus on funding pulmonary health research within the overall programme remains relatively limited and is often overshadowed by the importance given to research on other non-communicable diseases, such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes [153].

Pulmonary fibrosis has already been recognized as a life-threatening disorder in several national organisations worldwide, including the National Organization for Rare Disorders (NORD) in the United States, the European Organisation for Rare Disorders (EURORDIS) and the Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health (CADTH). In addition, international rare disease resources, such as Orphanet, have incorporated PF as well as other ILDs in their database. Although the Organization for Rare Diseases India (ORDI) has recently listed both IPF and ILD as rare diseases in its registry, many other ILD subtypes have not yet been included [156–160]. Thus, despite this acknowledgment from major countries worldwide, PF and other ILDs were excluded from the National Policy for Rare Diseases (NPRD) until 2021 in India, and no new policy in this regard has surfaced thus far [161]. This necessitates immediate action and policy amendments in India to rectify the situation and ensure proper recognition and treatment of ILDs, especially PF, after the recent COVID-19 pandemic. By incorporating PF along with other ILDs on the list of emerging health issues, India can prevent these diseases from remaining unnoticed, take appropriate remedial measures and provide proper management and care for affected individuals.

One of the major challenges in treating ILDs is the high rates of failure of clinical trials owing to insufficient therapeutic target validation in early discovery and preclinical research, which is the most expensive phase in drug development [111, 162]. Therefore, leading pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies worldwide have expanded their drug discovery efforts by employing computational chemistry, advanced physics-based molecular modelling with deep learning and artificial intelligence (AI). For instance, compounds designed using AI have shown efficacious inhibition of the collagen-activated proinflammatory receptor tyrosine kinase discoidin domain receptor 1 (DDR1), which is a crucial protein expressed on epithelial cells in fibrotic tissues [163, 164]. Notably, phase I clinical trials of ISM001-055 (also known as INS018_055), an in silico proposed drug, as a potential treatment for IPF have been completed in New Zealand [162, 163, 165].

Although notable pharmaceutical companies in India, such as Cipla and Glenmark Pharmaceuticals Limited, have successfully manufactured generic medications for PF [166–168], there is still a pressing need for large-scale research and clinical trials to develop novel and effective therapeutics for ILDs. Hence, as part of the healthcare reforms for enhancing the well-being of its citizens, the Government of India is actively attracting foreign direct investments and undertaking initiatives, such as Aatmanirbhar Bharat (which translates to self-reliant India) in 2018, which aimed to enhance domestic production as a part of the self-sustenance initiative to provide affordable medical care and facilities to its citizens [169]. In this context, the India-based pharmaceutical sector should capitalize on recent advances in national policy to intensify their research ventures by using the power of AI and other advanced technologies to produce new-generation drugs against these lung diseases.

On an encouraging note, India has emerged as a favoured destination for conducting clinical trials of new drugs against ILDs owing to its heterogeneous patient pool and escalating costs for organising trials in developed markets [169, 170]. The need for approval to conduct these clinical trials has been reasonably rationalized in advanced policies in India, such as the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019. Strategic policies to promote India as a clinical trial hub also encompass the approval of new drugs from global trials, which include Indian patients, and the establishment of animal testing and biocompatibility testing laboratories [169].

Conclusions

A significant rise in ILD cases is a growing concern for India, compounded by delayed or incorrect diagnosis and limited treatment strategies, all of which often contribute to higher mortality.

The way forward

India has the potential to address these challenges and advance ILD research through the following measures:

Prioritized funding and infrastructure—Government agencies could issue priority calls to fund research on systematized epidemiological data, AI-based therapies, personalized medicine, artificial organ development and machine learning-based patient monitoring. India could implement the abovementioned strategies and skilfully steward available resources to improve the quality of life of patients and reduce disease burden in the community. These efforts could lead India to emerge as an Asian hub for advanced research, which includes clinical trials to treat ILDs. In fact, some of the recommendations proposed herein could be adopted, evolved and implemented by other nations for dealing with similar public health exigencies. Finally, by extending the benefits of these painstaking efforts beyond the national borders, India could remarkably position itself as a front-runner in global research for improved diagnosis and treatment of these complex lung disorders. Since few data are available on the epidemiology of ILDs, particularly in developing countries and since the prevalence and incidence of these lung disorders have been increasing across various regions [13, 18], this study has global implications.
