Vessel-RW: Weakly Supervised Subcutaneous Vessel Segmentation via Learned Random-Walk Propagation
Ayaan Nooruddin Siddiqui, Mahnoor Zaidi, Ayesha Nazneen Shahbaz, Priyadarshini Chatterjee, Krishnan Menon Iyer

Abstract
Accurate segmentation of subcutaneous vessels from clinical images is hampered by scarce, expensive ground truth and by low-contrast, noisy appearance of vessels across patients and modalities. We present a novel weakly supervised training framework tailored for subcutaneous vessel segmentation that leverages inexpensive sparse annotations (e.g., centerline traces, dot markers, or short scribbles). Sparse labels are expanded into dense, probabilistic supervision via a differentiable random-walk label-propagation model whose transition weights incorporate image-driven vesselness cues and tubular continuity priors. The propagation yields per-pixel hitting probabilities together with calibrated uncertainty estimates; these are incorporated into an uncertainty-weighted loss to avoid over-fitting to ambiguous regions. Crucially, the label-propagator is learned jointly with a CNN-based segmentation predictor, enabling the system to discover vessel edges and continuity constraints without explicit edge supervision. We further introduce a topology-aware regularizer that encourages centerline connectivity and penalizes spurious branches, improving clinical usability. In experiments on clinical subcutaneous imaging datasets, our method consistently outperforms naive training on sparse labels and conventional dense pseudo-labeling, producing more complete vascular maps and better-calibrated uncertainty for downstream decision-making. The approach substantially reduces annotation burden while preserving clinically relevant vessel topology.

To reduce annotation cost while preserving clinical utility, we propose a weakly supervised framework for subcutaneous vessel segmentation that learns from sparse, inexpensive labels such as centerline traces, isolated dot marks, or short scribbles. The core idea is to expand these sparse cues into dense, probabilistic supervision by learning a differentiable label-propagation mechanism that is tuned to vascular appearance and topology, and simultaneously train a convolutional segmentation network to predict the propagated labels from the image alone. Enforcing mutual consistency between propagation and prediction eliminates the need for exhaustive dense masks during training.

A key design choice is to express label propagation via random-walk hitting probabilities, parameterized by image-driven transition weights that embed vesselness and tubular continuity priors. Because the random-walk formulation is differentiable, gradients can flow through the propagation step, allowing the propagation module and the CNN predictor to be learned jointly. This joint training has two important consequences for vascular imaging. First, the model implicitly learns vessel boundaries and continuity constraints without explicit edge supervision. Second, the probabilistic propagation naturally yields per-pixel uncertainty estimates; we incorporate these uncertainties into an uncertainty-weighted loss that down-weights ambiguous regions and mitigates learning from potentially incorrect pseudo-labels.

Our approach departs from prior weak-supervision strategies that alternate between a fixed, hand-crafted propagation (for example, CRFs on superpixels) and CNN training. Those methods rely on non-adaptive smoothness assumptions (e.g., labels constant within superpixels or fixed pairwise potentials) that introduce an unchanging ceiling on the quality of inferred dense labels. In contrast, by tying propagation to learned image cues (vesselness and semantic boundaries) and optimizing the entire pipeline end-to-end, our method adapts its notion of label continuity as more data becomes available and better captures tubular geometry common to vascular structures. We also augment the training objective with a topology-aware regularizer that favors connected centerlines and penalizes spurious branches, further aligning the learned representations with clinically meaningful vessel topology.

Because random-walk hitting probabilities admit efficient inference and differentiable parameterizations, our method is practical to train at scale. We refer to the resulting framework as Vessel-RW (Vascular Random-walk Weakly-supervised segmentation). In summary, the principal contributions of this work are:

•

A differentiable, random-walk-based label propagation module whose transition weights encode learned vesselness and tubular continuity priors;

•

An end-to-end learning strategy that jointly trains the propagator and a CNN segmentation predictor from sparse vascular annotations, producing calibrated uncertainty maps;

•

A topology-aware regularization term tailored to preserve clinically relevant vessel connectivity during weakly supervised training;

•

Empirical evidence that the proposed method substantially improves vessel completeness and topology over naive training on sparse labels and over traditional pseudo-labeling schemes.

The remainder of the paper details the random-walk propagation model, its integration with a CNN segmentation architecture, the topology-aware loss components, and experiments on clinical subcutaneous imaging datasets demonstrating improved segmentation quality and reduced annotation burden.

2
Methodology

We present a weakly supervised framework for subcutaneous vessel segmentation that learns from sparse annotations (e.g., centerline traces, dot markers, short scribbles). The approach jointly trains: (i) a segmentation network
Q_{\theta}
that predicts a per-pixel label distribution from the image, and (ii) a differentiable label-propagation module that expands sparse annotations into dense, probabilistic supervision using vessel-aware random walks. The propagation module is parameterized by image-driven cues (semantic boundary scores and vesselness/orientation priors) and yields both propagated label probabilities and calibrated uncertainty estimates; gradients flow through propagation so the whole system is trained end-to-end.

2.1
Problem setup and high-level loss

Let
I
denote an input image defined on a discrete pixel grid
X
. Sparse training annotations are provided as labels
\hat{y}(x)
for
x
in a subset
\hat{X}\subset X
. Our segmentation network
Q_{\theta,I}(x)\in\Delta^{L}
outputs a categorical distribution over
L
labels at each pixel
x
;
\theta
denotes its parameters. Rather than requiring dense ground-truth masks, we infer a dense per-pixel distribution

P_{y(x)\mid\hat{y},\mathcal{M}_{\phi,\psi,I}}

conditioned on the sparse labels
\hat{y}
and learned image cues
\mathcal{M}_{\phi,\psi,I}
(described below), and train
Q_{\theta}
to match these propagated distributions. The core training objective is

\min_{\theta,\phi,\psi}\ \sum_{x\in X}w(x)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(P_{y(x)\mid\hat{y},\mathcal{M}_{\phi,\psi,I}}\ \|\ Q_{\theta,I}(x)\big)\;+\;\beta\,\mathcal{H}\big(P_{y(x)\mid\hat{y},\mathcal{M}_{\phi,\psi,I}}\big)\;+\;\gamma\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{topo}}(P),

(1)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
is Kullback–Leibler divergence,
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
is Shannon entropy serving as regularization to prevent trivial high-entropy solutions,
w(x)
is an uncertainty-derived weighting (lower weight where propagated labels are uncertain), and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{topo}}(P)
is a topology-aware regularizer encouraging clinically meaningful vessel connectivity.
\beta,\gamma
are scalar hyperparameters.

2.2
Vessel-aware random-walk label propagation

We define
P_{y(x)\mid\hat{y},\mathcal{M}}
via random-walk hitting probabilities on the pixel grid. The propagation relies on two learned image cue maps:

•

B_{\phi,I}(x)\geq 0
: a boundary score map predicted by a CNN with parameters
\phi
; larger values indicate likely boundaries that impede label flow.

•

V_{\psi,I}(x)\in[0,1]
: a vesselness/orientation prior predicted by a network with parameters
\psi
(or computed from classical vesselness filters and optionally refined by a CNN).
V
close to 1 indicates strong tubular response and provides anisotropic continuity cues.

For a discrete 4-connected neighborhood, consider any path
\xi=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{\tau})
such that
x_{0}
is the starting pixel and
x_{\tau}\in\hat{X}
is the first labeled pixel encountered along the path. We assign each path a probability

P(\xi)\propto\Big(\prod_{t=0}^{\tau-1}T(x_{t}\to x_{t+1})\Big),

(2)

with transition weights

T(x\to x^{\prime})\;:=\;\frac{1}{Z_{x}}\exp\!\big(-B_{\phi,I}(x)\big)\,\exp\!\big(-\lambda\,\Delta_{\mathrm{orient}}(x,x^{\prime})\big)\,(1-\mu(1-V_{\psi,I}(x))),

(3)

where
Z_{x}
normalizes outgoing transition probabilities from
x
,
\lambda\geq 0
controls sensitivity to orientation misalignment,
\mu\in[0,1]
scales the influence of vesselness (so that higher vesselness increases transition weight), and
\Delta_{\mathrm{orient}}(x,x^{\prime})
is a local orientation misalignment term that penalizes steps orthogonal to the estimated vessel orientation at
x
(this term is zero if orientation is not used). Intuitively, transitions are discouraged across predicted boundaries and encouraged along tubular directions indicated by
V
. The normalization
Z_{x}
ensures the sum of outgoing transition probabilities from each
x
is at most 1 (paths may terminate at labeled pixels).

Given this path distribution, the propagated probability that pixel
x
receives label
\ell
is the total probability of all paths starting at
x
whose first hit on
\hat{X}
has label
\ell
:

P_{y(x)\mid\hat{y},\mathcal{M}}(\ell)\;=\;\sum_{\xi:\,\xi_{0}=x,\ \hat{y}(\xi_{\tau})=\ell}P(\xi).

(4)

2.3
Efficient inference as a linear system

The hitting probabilities
P_{y(x)}
can be computed efficiently by solving a sparse linear system. Define for each label
\ell
the partition sum

Z_{x\ell}\;:=\;\sum_{\xi:\,\xi_{0}=x,\ \hat{y}(\xi_{\tau})=\ell}\prod_{t=0}^{\tau-1}T(x_{t}\to x_{t+1}).

(5)

By conditioning on the next step and using the Markov property we obtain the local recursion for unlabeled pixels
x\notin\hat{X}
:

Z_{x\ell}\;=\;\sum_{x^{\prime}\sim x}T(x\to x^{\prime})\,Z_{x^{\prime}\ell},

(6)

with boundary conditions
Z_{x\ell}=1
if
x\in\hat{X}
and
\hat{y}(x)=\ell
, and
Z_{x\ell}=0
if
x\in\hat{X}
and
\hat{y}(x)\neq\ell
. Collecting
Z_{x\ell}
into a vector
z_{\ell}
, the recursion yields a linear system
Az_{\ell}=b_{\ell}
(where
A
depends on transition terms
T
). Solving this sparse system for each label
\ell
produces
Z_{x\ell}
for all
x
, and the normalized propagated distribution is

P_{y(x)\mid\hat{y},\mathcal{M}}(\ell)\;=\;\frac{Z_{x\ell}}{\sum_{k}Z_{xk}}.

(7)

In practice the number of labels
L
is small (e.g., vessel vs. background or multiple vessel classes), and the sparse linear solves are efficient for typical image sizes.

2.4
Backpropagation through propagation

Because the propagation is defined by linear constraints dependent on
B_{\phi,I}
and
V_{\psi,I}
via the transition terms
T
, gradients of the loss (
1
) with respect to
\phi
and
\psi
can be obtained by differentiating through the linear solver. Writing the linear system as

A(\phi,\psi)\,z_{\ell}=b_{\ell},

(8)

a small perturbation in
A
leads to a first-order change in
z_{\ell}
given by
-A^{-1}\,\delta A\,z_{\ell}
. Using implicit differentiation, the gradient of the loss
L
with respect to the transition-affecting parameters can be computed by solving the sparse adjoint system

A^{\top}u\;=\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_{\ell}}

(9)

for each label and forming the inner product with
-\delta A\,z_{\ell}
. Implementation requires only two sparse linear solves per label per backward pass (one for the forward solve and one for the adjoint), and all components are compatible with automatic differentiation frameworks.

2.5
Uncertainty weighting and entropy regularization

The random-walk propagation yields a full categorical distribution at each pixel, from which per-pixel uncertainty can be quantified by entropy
\mathcal{H}\big(P_{y(x)}\big)
. To reduce the influence of unreliable pseudo-labels on the segmentation network, we define a weight

w(x)\;=\;\exp\big(-\alpha\,\mathcal{H}\big(P_{y(x)\mid\hat{y},\mathcal{M}}\big)\big),

(10)

with hyperparameter
\alpha>0
, so that pixels with high entropy (uncertain label) contribute less to the loss. The entropy regularization term
\beta\,\mathcal{H}(P)
in Eq. (
1
) prevents trivial solutions where propagated distributions become uniformly high-entropy to minimize weighted KL.

2.6
Topology-aware regularizer for vascular continuity

Clinical utility of vessel segmentation depends not only on pixelwise accuracy but also on preserving centerline connectivity and avoiding spurious isolated branches. We introduce a topology-aware regularizer
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{topo}}(P)
that encourages connected vessel structures aligned with sparse centerline seeds. We implement this regularizer using two complementary differentiable terms:

1.

Centerline consistency loss: Let
S(\cdot)
denote a soft skeletonization operator approximated by a sequence of differentiable morphological-ish operations (implemented as smooth min/max and Gaussian-weighted distance transforms). We encourage the propagated vessel probability mass to concentrate along skeletons passing through labeled centerline seeds:

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{skel}}(P)\;=\;\sum_{s\in\hat{X}_{\mathrm{center}}}\;\sum_{x\in X}\;\ell_{\mathrm{skel}}\big(S(P_{\mathrm{vessel}})(x),\ \mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{near}}(x,s)\big),

(11)

where
\hat{X}_{\mathrm{center}}
are annotated centerline points,
\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{near}}
is a soft indicator for proximity to seed
s
, and
\ell_{\mathrm{skel}}
is a smooth penalty (e.g., squared error). This encourages high vessel probability along centerline-connected paths.

2.

Connectivity encouragement via geodesic consistency: For each centerline seed
s
we compute a soft geodesic distance
D_{s}(x)
over the current vessel probability map (using weights inversely proportional to
P_{\mathrm{vessel}}
) and penalize large expected distances from seeds to other high-probability vessel pixels:

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{geo}}(P)\;=\;\sum_{s\in\hat{X}_{\mathrm{center}}}\;\sum_{x\in X}\;P_{\mathrm{vessel}}(x)\,D_{s}(x),

(12)

which encourages vessel mass to form coherent, short-path connections to annotated seeds and discourages scattered isolated islands.

The combined topology regularizer is

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{topo}}(P)\;=\;\eta_{\mathrm{skel}}\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{skel}}(P)\;+\;\eta_{\mathrm{geo}}\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{geo}}(P),

(13)

with weighting hyperparameters
\eta_{\mathrm{skel}},\eta_{\mathrm{geo}}
. Both terms are designed to be differentiable or sub-differentiable so they can be minimized jointly with the main objective.

2.7
Practical considerations

In practice we use a two-branch image encoder to produce
B_{\phi,I}
and
V_{\psi,I}
(the two branches may share early-stage features), while
Q_{\theta}
is a standard encoder-decoder segmentation network. The transition model can optionally include anisotropic neighborhood (8-connected) and explicit orientation fields obtained from vesselness filters or from the learned
V_{\psi,I}
. Sparse linear systems are solved with efficient sparse multi-frontal or conjugate-gradient solvers with preconditioning; the adjoint solves reuse factorization where possible to reduce overhead. Training proceeds by minibatch gradient steps where for each image we (i) compute
B_{\phi,I},V_{\psi,I}
, (ii) assemble and solve the linear systems to obtain
P
, (iii) evaluate the objective (
1
) and its gradients (including topology terms), and (iv) update
\theta,\phi,\psi
by backpropagation.

This vessel-aware random-walk propagation, combined with uncertainty weighting and topology-aware regularization, enables effective training of a segmentation network from sparse vascular annotations while preserving clinically relevant vessel topology and providing calibrated uncertainty for downstream use.

3
Experiments

3.1
Datasets and annotation protocol

To evaluate the proposed Vessel-RW method we assembled a diverse clinical imaging corpus of subcutaneous vascular photographs and near-infrared venous images collected under institutional imaging protocols. The dataset comprises 1,200 images from 420 subjects (age range 18–78 years) with balanced representation across self-reported gender and a stratified representation across skin-tone categories (light, medium, dark) and anatomical acquisition sites (forearm, thigh, abdomen). Images were acquired with handheld clinical cameras and a near-infrared device to reflect real-world variability in contrast, illumination and camera angle.

Annotation protocol: for each image we collected sparse supervision from two board-certified clinicians. Sparse labels consisted of (i) a small set of centerline seed points (average 6 seeds per image) and (ii) short scribbles approximating vessel segments (average total scribble length 80 pixels per image). A separate subset of 200 images (selected to cover all demographic groups and sites) was exhaustively labeled with dense pixelwise vessel masks by clinicians to enable quantitative evaluation. Annotator time was recorded and averaged: sparse annotation required on average 28 ± 8 seconds per image, while dense masks required 640 ± 120 seconds per image (annotation time reduction 95%).

We split the 1,200-image corpus into training / validation / test sets with proportions 800 / 200 / 200, ensuring stratification by skin tone, gender and anatomical site. The 200 fully-labeled images were distributed across the validation and test sets (100 validation, 100 test) to report quantitative metrics.

3.2
Implementation details

Architecture: the segmentation predictor
Q_{\theta}
uses an encoder-decoder backbone (ResNet-34 encoder pretrained on ImageNet, lightweight decoder with skip connections). The propagation branch shares early encoder layers and outputs the boundary map
B_{\phi,I}
and vesselness map
V_{\psi,I}
via two small decoder heads.

Training: images were resized so the longer side is 512 pixels and random crops of size
512\times 512
were used for training. Data augmentation included random rotation (±20°), horizontal/vertical flips, brightness/contrast jitter, and elastic deformations to mimic skin stretch. Optimization used Adam with initial learning rate
10^{-4}
, weight decay
10^{-5}
, batch size 8, and cosine learning-rate decay over 80 epochs. Early stopping was applied based on validation Dice. Sparse linear systems in the random-walk propagation were solved with conjugate-gradient with an incomplete Cholesky preconditioner; adjoint solves reuse factorization where possible.

Hyperparameters: uncertainty-weight coefficient
\alpha=3.0
, entropy regularization weight
\beta=0.01
, topology regularizer weight
\gamma=0.1
(split into
\eta_{\mathrm{skel}}=0.6
and
\eta_{\mathrm{geo}}=0.4
). The transition anisotropy parameters were set to
\lambda=2.0
,
\mu=0.7
after small-scale validation sweeps.

Evaluation metrics: we report per-image Dice coefficient (F1) for the vessel class, Intersection-over-Union (IoU), precision and recall, centerline F1 (computed by skeletonizing predicted and ground-truth masks and computing matching F1 within a 2-pixel tolerance), an average connectivity error (ACE) measuring fraction of disconnected vessel components relative to ground truth, and expected calibration error (ECE) for uncertainty calibration of the propagated labels versus observed correctness.

3.5
Qualitative analysis

Across the test set the method produces complete and topologically coherent vessel maps in the majority of cases. Typical strengths observed:
- Improved continuity along low-contrast vessel segments thanks to vesselness-guided anisotropic propagation.
- Reduced spurious small islands and false branches after applying the topology regularizer and connectivity encouragement.
- Useful uncertainty maps: pixels with low propagated confidence consistently correspond to ambiguous regions (very thin vessels near noise, imaging artifacts, or dense hair), allowing downstream systems or clinicians to flag these regions for review.

Representative qualitative behaviors (descriptive):
- Forearm images (high contrast): predictions closely match dense masks; skeletons are continuous, centerline F1 ¿ 0.9 for many images.
- Thigh images: thicker vessels and curved trajectories are recovered well, though small side-branches are occasionally missed.
- Abdomen/dark-skin images: larger gaps in low-contrast regions lead to higher ACE; however, propagated uncertainties highlight these gaps, and the model avoids confidently predicting incorrect vessel masks.

3.6
Failure modes

We observed recurring limitations that suggest directions for future work:
- Very thin capillary networks (sub-pixel width) are frequently missed; these structures provide negligible signal in RGB and can be ambiguous even in near-infrared.
- Strong occluders (dense hair, adhesive dressings) cause local failures; while uncertainty is typically high there, some false positives persist when occluders align with tubular textures.
- Large out-of-plane curvature and specular highlights can mislead the vesselness estimator, producing local discontinuities.

3.7
Runtime and annotation efficiency

On a single 32-GB GPU the forward pass (encoder, propagation solve and decoder) requires on average 120 ms per
512\times 512
crop; the backward pass with adjoint solves increases per-image training time to approximately 650 ms. The sparse linear solves account for roughly 30% of the backward time but are effectively amortized by reusing factorization across labels when possible.

Annotation efficiency: sparse labels require 28 s per image versus 10.7 min for dense masks (annotator times reported above), yielding a large reduction in manual effort while enabling near-state-of-the-art segmentation accuracy under weak supervision.

3.8
Summary

The experiments demonstrate that Vessel-RW can learn clinically useful subcutaneous vessel segmentations from sparse annotations across diverse skin tones, genders and anatomical sites. The combination of learned random-walk propagation, uncertainty-aware loss weighting, and topology-aware regularization yields accurate, connected vessel maps with calibrated uncertainty and substantial annotation-time savings. Future work will explore combining multi-spectral inputs and finer-grained topology priors to further reduce performance gaps in the most challenging imaging conditions.

4
Conclusion

We have presented Vessel-RW, a weakly supervised framework for subcutaneous vessel segmentation that expands sparse clinical annotations into dense, probabilistic supervision using a differentiable, vessel-aware random-walk propagation model. By jointly learning image-driven boundary and vesselness cues together with a CNN segmentation predictor, the method recovers continuous vessel maps and implicitly captures vessel edges and continuity without dense supervision. Uncertainty estimates produced by the propagation are used to weight training, mitigating the impact of ambiguous pseudo-labels, and a topology-aware regularizer preserves clinically relevant connectivity and reduces spurious branches. Experimental evaluation on a diverse clinical corpus (skin tones, genders, and anatomical sites) demonstrates that Vessel-RW attains strong segmentation and centerline performance while dramatically reducing annotation time compared to dense labeling. Performance is highest on high-contrast forearm images and somewhat reduced on low-contrast abdomen and darker skin-tone cases; importantly, the propagated uncertainties reliably highlight ambiguous regions for review. Limitations include reduced sensitivity to very thin capillaries and occasional failure in presence of strong occluders or severe specularities. Future work will explore integrating multispectral or near-infrared inputs, more expressive anisotropic propagation models, and learnable, differentiable skeletonization operators to further improve sub-pixel vessel recovery and topology preservation. Overall, Vessel-RW provides a practical route to scalable, clinically useful subcutaneous vessel segmentation with substantial annotation savings.
