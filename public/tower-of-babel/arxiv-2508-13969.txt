Towards multi-purpose locally differentially private synthetic data release via spline wavelet plug-in estimation
Thibault Randrianarisoa ??, Lukas Steinberger ??, Botond Szabo ??

Abstract
We develop plug-in estimators for locally differentially private semi-parametric estimation via spline wavelets. The approach leads to optimal rates of convergence for a large class of estimation problems that are characterized by (differentiable) functionals Λ ⁡ ( f ) \Lambda(f) of the true data generating density f f . The crucial feature of the locally private data Z 1 , … , Z n Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n} we generate is that it does not depend on the particular functional Λ \Lambda (or the unknown density f f ) the analyst wants to estimate. Hence, the synthetic data can be generated and stored a priori and can subsequently be used by any number of analysts to estimate many vastly different functionals of interest at the provably optimal rate. In principle, this removes a long standing practical limitation in statistics of differential privacy, namely, that optimal privacy mechanisms need to be tailored towards the specific estimation problem at hand.

1
Introduction

Differential privacy
Dwork 2006
;
Dwork et al. 2006
;
Evfimievski, Gehrke and
Srikant 2003
was conceived 20 years ago as a once-and-for-all defense against any kind of possible data privacy breach. Already in these early references the idea of synthetic data release by private histograms was envisioned. It means that a new, and in some sense artificial, data set is (randomly) generated from sensitive original data such that these new data can be made public without any concern for privacy violation but contain similar statistical information. In the last decade, however, mathematical statisticians have demonstrated in a multitude of papers
(cf.
Acharya, Sun and Zhang 2021
;
Butucea et al. 2020
;
Duchi, Jordan and Wainwright 2018
;
Lalanne, Garivier and
Gribonval 2023
;
Rohde and Steinberger 2020
;
Steinberger 2024
, to mention only a few)
that statistically optimal privacy mechanisms have to be tailor made for the specific inference problem at hand. In other words, no single private data release mechanism can be simultaneously optimal for many different estimation problems.

To give a very simple example, in the local paradigm of DP, where there is no trusted curator available, when original sensitive iid data are
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and the goal is moment estimation (that is,
\theta={\mathbb{E}}[X^{k}]\in{\mathbb{R}}
) on a sample space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq[-M,M]
, we can achieve the optimal dependence of the risk on sample size
n
and the privacy budget
\alpha
by simple local Laplace addition

Z_{i,k}=X_{i}^{k}+Lap\left(\frac{\alpha}{2M^{k}}\right)

and then averaging
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i,k}
.
If an analyst has retrieved, say,
Z_{i,1}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, for mean estimation, then the privacy budget
\alpha
for each of those
n
data owners is already used up. If, at a later point, she decides to also estimate the second moment, then there are only two possible alternatives: either she queries
n
new data owners to obtain a new sanitized sample
Z_{i,2}
,
i=n+1,\dots,2n
, which may be expensive, or she can try to use the
Z_{i,1}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, also for the new estimation task. The latter option is, indeed, feasible by a deconvolution approach, but it is clearly statistically much less efficient than using the
Z_{i,2}
. Notice that if the analyst had first retrieved
Z_{i,2}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, and then later decided to estimate also the mean, then, depending on the distribution of
X_{i}
, this task may be impossible altogether without querying new data owners. For instance, if
\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1)=(\theta+1)/2=1-\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=-1)
then
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{i}]=\theta\in[-1,1]
but the distribution of
X_{i}^{2}=1
does not depend on
\theta
.

In light of the above, it would be highly desirable to query the
n
data owners only once to obtain a sanitized synthetic data set
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
which can subsequently be used in a statistically optimal way by analysts for arbitrary inference tasks. In this paper we provide a proof of concept that this can, indeed, be done for a large class of possible inference problems.

A natural approach is that of (semiparametric) plug-in estimation. If the sensitive data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are iid from some smooth density
f\in\mathcal{F}
on
[0,1]
, then we let
\Lambda:\mathcal{F}\to{\mathbb{R}}
denote some functional of interest. We construct a local privacy mechanism that turns
X_{i}
into a private view
Z_{i}
on some appropriate space
\mathcal{Z}
, such that these sanitized data can be used to compute a density estimator
\hat{f}_{n}
for which the plug-in rule
\Lambda(\hat{f}_{n})
achieves the (private) minimax rate for estimating
\Lambda(f)
(among all locally differentially private estimators of
\Lambda(f)
and not only the plug-in rules). This is shown to hold for a large class of differentiable functionals
\Lambda
. In other words, no matter what the estimation task formalized by the functional
\Lambda
is, the analyst can use the synthetic data
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
to construct a minimax optimal estimator. Since
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are generated only once and protect
\alpha
local differential privacy of data owners, there is no limit to the number of analysts or to the number of estimation tasks each analyst can solve.

1.1
Related results

Needless to say, the locally private density estimation problem has been treated before by several authors
Duchi, Jordan and Wainwright 2018
;
Kroll 2019
;
Butucea et al. 2020
;
Györfi and Kroll 2023
;
Kroll 2021
;
Kroll 2022
;
Schluttenhofer and
Johannes 2022
. However, not every private density estimator
\hat{f}_{n}
is suitable for multi-purpose synthetic data release in the sense that it is optimal for a large class of estimation problems. First of all, if we want to allow also for functionals
\Lambda
that involve derivatives of the true density
f
(such as Fisher-Information; see Section
8
for a list of examples), then the estimated density
\hat{f}_{n}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
should also be smooth, ruling out the classical histogram approach. Smoothness can be obtained, for instance, via kernel density estimation
\tilde{f}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(X_{i}-t)
, where
K_{h}(x)=\frac{1}{h}K(\frac{x}{h})
. However, it is not a trivial task to combine the kernel density approach with differential privacy. The problem arises from the fact that we can not simply add noise to the original data
X_{i}
without running into the deconvolution problem mentioned above. An optimal randomization procedure should rather be applied to
K_{h}(X_{i}-t)
. This is straightforward by Laplace noise addition if the point
t
is fixed. However, for estimation of the entire density, which is essential for multi-purpose data release, we should publish the whole randomized trajectory
t\mapsto Z_{i}(t)=K_{h}(X_{i}-t)+\Xi(t)
, where
\Xi
is an appropriate stochastic process.
Kroll 2019
explored this approach, but achieved only
approximate
local differential privacy.

Another practical difficulty with the kernel density idea is data storage and numerical differentiation. As we can never hope to generate and store the entire function
Z_{i}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
on a server (recall that
X_{i}
has to remain hidden), we would have to a priori discretize and therefore limit the precision any analyst can achieve by specifying a grid of values
Z_{i}(t_{1}),\dots,Z_{i}(t_{N})
. This situation is markedly different from, e.g., using a projection type approach where only sanitized (wavelet) coefficients need to be stored and differentiation could even be done analytically by the analyst.

Optimal rates for nonparametric density estimation under privacy constraints with a wavelet approach were obtained in
Butucea et al. 2020
. However, they use a specific wavelet basis and it is unclear whether it is simultaneously optimal for estimation of derivatives and point evaluations of the density, required for our analysis.
Goldstein and
Messer 1992
developed a framework to extend nonparametric density estimators to semiparametric plug-in estimators and obtained optimal rates, but they only considered kernel estimators, which are not suitable for our purpose.

1.2
Our contribution

For the reasons mentioned above, we here follow a projection type approach with a B-spline basis as a practical and natural choice for estimation of a smooth density and its derivatives (cf. Section
5
). More precisely, if
B_{k,j_{n}}
are the B-spline functions with equispaced knot sequence of step
2^{-j_{n}}
and degree
d
, we estimate
f
by

\hat{f}_{n}^{(j_{n})}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{2^{j_{n}}+d}\bar{Z}_{k}^{(j_{n})}B_{k,j_{n}}(t),

(1.1)

where
\bar{Z}_{k}^{(j_{n})}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i,k,j_{n}}
and
Z_{i,k,j_{n}}=e_{k,j_{n}}(X_{i})+W_{i,k,j_{n}}
for appropriate Laplace random variables
W_{i,k,j_{n}}
and functions
e_{k,j_{n}}
. Even though this locally private procedure achieves optimal rates on atomic functionals (e.g., point evaluations of the density), it does not perform well enough for estimation of smooth functionals (e.g., functionals of integral form such as moments or integrals of functions of
f
, etc.) due to excessive noise accumulation
(the latter was also observed by
Duchi, Jordan and Wainwright 2018
, Section 5.2.2., who proposed a more refined noise addition scheme to fix the problem at least for density estimation in
L^{2}
)
. It turns out, however, that with the structure of a multiresolution analysis we can efficiently distribute noise across resolution levels, leading to a locally private density estimator that achieves optimal rates on both smooth as well as atomic functionals. See Remark
6.4
in Section
6
for more details on this issue. Thus, in order to widen the class of estimation problems which can be solved by our synthetic private data, we extend the B-spline approach to spline wavelets
Jia 2006
(cf. Section
6
for details) and consider an estimator of the form

\hat{f}_{n}^{(j_{n})}(t)=\sum_{j=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\bar{Z}_{jk}\tilde{\psi}_{jk}(t),

(1.2)

where
Z_{ijk}=\psi_{jk}(X_{i})+W_{ijk}
and
\bar{Z}_{jk}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{ijk}
.

Extending results by
Goldstein and
Messer 1992
we prove optimal rates of convergence and adaptation for locally private plug-in estimators based on spline wavelets.

1.3
Practical advantages of the multi resolution analysis and adaptation

Notice that there is another fundamental problem with the private estimation approach in (
1.1
) that is resolved by the wavelet estimator (
1.2
). In order to achieve the optimal rate of convergence the tuning parameter
j_{n}
has to be chosen in dependence of the smoothness of the true data generating density (and in general also in dependence on the functional
\Lambda
we are trying to estimate). In the notation of (
1.1
), our multi-purpose synthetic data should therefore consist of
\mathbf{Z}^{(j_{n})}=(\bar{Z}_{1}^{(j_{n})},\bar{Z}_{2}^{(j_{n})},\dots,\bar{Z}_{2^{j_{n}}+d}^{(j_{n})})
, for some appropriate choice of
j_{n}
. For any given
j_{n}
, these data can be released such that
\alpha
-local differential privacy is protected. However, we cannot use the exact same data in order to adaptively choose
j_{n}
. To do that we would actually need to try different values of
j_{n}
(e.g., model selection or Lepski’s method) and fit models based on
\mathbf{Z}^{(j)}
, for
j\in\mathcal{J}
, say, resulting in the need for further locally private data generation. This comes at the price of a privacy loss. If
\mathbf{Z}^{(j)}
can be generated with a privacy parameter
\alpha_{j}
and
\alpha
is the overall privacy budget, then we have to make sure that
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\alpha_{j}\leq\alpha
. This is achieved, for instance, by setting
\alpha_{j}=\alpha/|\mathcal{J}|
, but of course estimation based on
\mathbf{Z}^{(j)}
with tuning parameter
j
and privacy budget
\alpha
will lead to a faster rate than estimation based on the privacy budget
\alpha/|\mathcal{J}|
. Typically the loss is polynomial in
|\mathcal{J}|
, which is itself of logarithmic order
|\mathcal{J}|\asymp\log(n)
. Hence, the price of adaptation of this private estimation scheme is at least another poly-logarithmic term and we would also have to a priori specify the grid of values
\mathcal{J}
which would subsequently be fixed for all data analysts.

This additional poly-logarithmic cost of adaptation arising from the need of repeated private data generations may not seem to be a big issue theoretically, given that in certain cases there is the price of a logarithmic factor anyways, even when original data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are observed. Furthermore, it is an open question whether local differential privacy necessarily causes a logarithmic price for adaptation
(cf.
Butucea et al. 2020
, Section 6)
. However, with a wavelet basis (
1.2
) the situation is a bit more convenient, at least from a practical point of view. In fact, we can release all the randomized data
\bar{Z}_{jk}
,
j\in\{j_{0}-1,j_{0},\dots,j_{n}\}
,
k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}
, up to arbitrary
j_{n}
, at a constant privacy budget of
\alpha
(cf. Proposition
6.1
). Hence, we can pick a privacy level
\alpha
and a very large number
j_{max}
, say, and release
\bar{Z}_{jk}
,
j\in\{j_{0}-1,j_{0},\dots,j_{max}\}
,
k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}
, to protect
\alpha
local differential privacy. Now any data analyst can subsequently use these data to compute (
1.2
) as long as
j_{n}\leq j_{max}
. In particular, they can also compute (
1.2
) and derivatives thereof for different values of tuning parameters
j\in\mathcal{J}
, say, as long as
\max\mathcal{J}\leq j_{max}
. See Section
7
for further details, where we pick
j_{max}=(\log n)/3
.

1.4
Organization of the paper

In Section
2
we give a detailed description of the statistical model, the set of estimation problems characterized by a functional
\Lambda
and the privacy constraint that needs to be satisfied. We also collect our basic definitions and notations. Section
3
contains private minimax lower bounds for our class of functionals
\Lambda
and for the class of all locally private estimators, which we aim to achieve with our plug-in approach. In Section
4
we provide some background on B-splines and associated wavelet bases and summarize the approximation theoretic facts needed to prove our main results. Sections
5
and
6
contain the main results on optimality of our synthetic data release and plug-in estimators. Section
5
deals with a simpler B-spline estimator that is, however, only optimal on atomic functionals. Section
6
then introduces the spline wavelet approach, which is subsequently shown to be optimal on atomic and smooth functionals. The optimal choice of resolution level
j_{n}
for estimation of atomic functionals, however, depends on the unknown smoothness of the data generating density
f
. Thus, we introduce a fully data driven choice of
j_{n}
in Section
7
. Finally, in Section
8
, we list a few examples of common statistical functionals
\Lambda
and show how they fit into our general private estimation framework. The proofs are deferred to the Appendix.

2
Notations and model assumptions

The original, possibly confidential, data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are assumed to be independent and identically distributed random variables with Lebesgue density
f
on
[0,1]
and we want to estimate
\Lambda(f)
for some functional
\Lambda
(not necessarily linear) while ensuring local differential privacy. We denote by
P_{f}
the associated probability measure on the sample space
([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]))
and by
\mathbb{P}_{f}=P_{f}^{n}
the corresponding product measure. We assume in the following that, for
p
a nonnegative integer,
f\in C^{p}[0,1]
the set of continuous functions with
p
or more continuous derivatives. Also, for
1\leq q<\infty
and
\lambda=(\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{p})
a vector of finite measures on
[0,1]
, we note

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{(q,p,\lambda)}=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|f^{(j)}|^{q}d\lambda_{j}\right)^{1/q},

and define
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{(\infty,p,\lambda)}
similarly when
q=\infty
, replacing integrals with essential supremums. When
\lambda
is the vector of Lebesgue measures, we drop the dependence on this vector in the previous display and write
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{(q,p)}
for simplicity. We also introduce

\mathcal{B}_{(q,p)}(M)\coloneqq\left\{f\in C^{p}[0,1]\colon\ \left\lVert f\right\rVert_{(q,p)}<M\right\}

the ball of radius
M
in this metric. In the following, we restrict our attention to densities satisfying

f\in\mathcal{W}_{p}\coloneqq\left\{f\in C^{p}[0,1]:\ f\geq 0,\ \int_{0}^{1}f=1\right\}\ \cap\ \mathcal{B}_{(\infty,p)}(M),

(2.1)

for some unknown
M>0
.
Also, we consider functionals
\Lambda
that have the following expansion for
0\leq m<p
and
\lambda
a vector of finite measures depending on
\Lambda
but not
f
,

\Lambda(f+h)=\Lambda(f)+T_{f}(h)+O(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}_{(2,m,\lambda)}),

(2.2)

for
f\in\mathcal{W}_{p}
,
h\in C^{p}[0,1]
with
\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{(\infty,m)}
small enough and
T_{f}
(the functional derivative of
\Lambda
, depending on
f
) a bounded linear functional on
C^{p}[0,1]
. As argued in
Goldstein and
Messer 1992
, the Hahn-Banach theorem and the Riesz representation theorem imply that

T_{f}(h)=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\int_{0}^{1}h^{(j)}d\mu_{j},

(2.3)

where
\mu_{j}
is a finite signed Borel measure on
[0,1]
(possibly depending on
f
). From Lebesgue’s decomposition theorem, the finite measures
\nu
we consider here can all be decomposed as

\nu=\nu_{\text{cont}}+\nu_{\text{sing}}+\nu_{\text{pp}},

where
\nu_{\text{cont}}
is the absolutely continuous part,
\nu_{\text{sing}}
is the singular continuous part and
\nu_{\text{pp}}
is the pure point part (a discrete measure with a countable set of atoms). We restrict ourselves to situations where the pathological singular continuous part is null,
\nu_{\text{sing}}=0
, which will be the case in our practical examples. We also assume that all absolutely continuous measures have bounded densities from now on.

Assumption 1
.

We assume the finite signed measures
\mu_{j}
in the derivative (
2.3
) and
\lambda_{j}
in the remainder term of (
2.2
) are composed of only absolutely continuous parts, with Lebesgue densities bounded by quantities depending only on
M
as in (
2.1
), and discrete parts. Also, we assume that the total mass they assign to
[0,1]
is upper bounded by a quantity depending only on
M
.

In the following, we consider differentiable functionals
\Lambda
(of order
m
), whose decomposition (
2.2
) satisfies that for any
j\leq p
, the nonnegative measure
|\mu_{j}|=\mu_{j}^{+}+\mu_{j}^{-}
, where we use the Jordan decomposition
\mu_{j}=\mu_{j}^{+}-\mu_{j}^{-}
(recall that these measures may depend on
f
), satisfies

\underset{f\in\mathcal{W}_{p}}{\sup}\ |\mu_{j}|([0,1])<\infty.

(2.4)

This implies that the decomposition is uniform over
\mathcal{W}_{p}
in the sense that

\underset{f\in\mathcal{W}_{p}}{\sup}\left\lVert T_{f}\right\rVert_{p}\coloneqq\underset{f\in\mathcal{W}_{p}}{\sup}\quad\underset{\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{(\infty,p)}\leq 1}{\sup}\left|T_{f}h\right|<\infty.

(2.5)

Among these, we can identify the smooth functionals of order
m
for which
T_{f}(h)=\int_{0}^{1}h\ \omega_{f}
, for all
f\in\mathcal{W}_{p}
, and where the bounded measurable function
\omega_{f}
depends on
f
only and satisfies

\underset{f\in\mathcal{W}_{p}}{\sup}\left\lVert\omega_{f}\right\rVert_{\infty}<\infty.

(2.6)

We also study the atomic functionals of index
s
,
0\leq s\leq p
, which are such that for all
h\in C^{p}[0,1]
,
T_{f}
can be represented as

T_{f}(h)=\sum_{j=0}^{s_{f}}\int_{0}^{1}h^{(j)}d\mu_{j,f}

with
\mu_{s_{f},f}
having a discrete component
\delta_{s_{f},f}
, and
s=\max_{f\in\mathcal{W}_{p}}s_{f}
being the largest integer for which such representation is valid. In the following,
C_{p},C_{f},C_{p,f}
, etc. denote constants depending on the subscript parameters only, and whose value may change from line to line.

Finally, we focus on the local paradigm of differential privacy, where data owners can generate sanitized views of their sensitive information “on their local machine” without sharing the original information with anybody else. For a privacy budget
\alpha>0
, we write
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}
for the set of all Markov kernels
Q
from
([0,1],\mathcal{B}([0,1]))
to some measurable space
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{G})
such that

Q(A|x)\leq e^{\alpha}Q(A|x^{\prime}),\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{G}\text{ and for all }x,x^{\prime}\in[0,1].

(2.7)

Using such a privacy mechanism
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}
, data owners can independently generate sanitized versions
Z_{i}
of their sensitive information
X_{i}
by
Z_{i}\thicksim Q(\cdot|x)
with
x=X_{i}
. In other words,
Q(\cdot|x)
denotes the conditional distribution of
Z_{i}
given
X_{i}=x
and the marginal distribution of
Z_{i}
is given by
QP_{f}:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Q(\cdot|x)P_{f}(dx)
. Also notice that
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are iid according to
QP_{f}
and the conditional distribution of
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
given
X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{n}=x_{n}
is given by
Q(\cdot|x_{1})\times\dots\times Q(\cdot|x_{n})
, which satisfies the conventional definition of (central) differential privacy as in
Dwork et al. 2006
. Notice that
Q(\cdot|x)\ll Q(\cdot|x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in[0,1]
and hence (
2.7
) can be verified by checking whether

\frac{dQ(\cdot|x)}{dQ(\cdot|x^{\prime})}(z)\leq e^{\alpha}.

We write
{\mathbb{E}}
and
\mathbb{P}
to denote expectation and probability with respect to the joint distribution of all the private and public data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
,
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
. To stress dependence on
Q
and
f
, we sometimes also write
{\mathbb{E}}_{QP_{f}}
or simply
{\mathbb{E}}_{f}
, if the privacy mechanism
Q
is clear from the context.

3
Minimax lower bounds

In this section we prove private minimax lower bounds of estimation for large classes of functionals
\Lambda
over
\mathcal{W}_{p}
. In subsequent sections we then show that these lower bounds can be achieved by our plug-in estimators. Notice that the collection of estimators considered in our minimax lower bounds is the collection of all measurable functions of the sanitized data
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
rather than just the plug-in rules.

We begin with a general private minimax lower bound of parametric order that holds for estimation of any differentiable and non-constant functional
\Lambda
on a convex subset
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{p}
. Notice that
\mathcal{W}_{p}
itself is convex. The proof of the following result is deferred to Appendix
A
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix
n,p\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\alpha>0
and a convex set
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{p}
. Let
\Lambda:\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable as in (
2.2
). If
\Lambda
is not constant on
\mathcal{W}
, then there exist constants
c,C>0
depending only on
\Lambda
and
\mathcal{W}
, such that the minimax-risk of locally private estimation is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha}}\inf_{\hat{\Lambda}_{n}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{W}}{\mathbb{E}}_{QP_{f}}\left[|\hat{\Lambda}_{n}-\Lambda(f)|\right]\geq c\left(n(e^{\alpha}-1)^{2}\right)^{-1/2},

provided that
n(e^{\alpha}-1)^{2}\geq C
. Here, the second infimum is over all estimators
\hat{\Lambda}_{n}
that take the sanitized data
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\thicksim}}QP_{f}
as input.

For large
\alpha
the bound in Theorem
3.1
can become arbitrarily small. Notice, however, that the classical lower bounds without a privacy constraint derived by
Goldstein and
Messer 1992
also apply here. Simply condition on the original data
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
and use Jensen’s inequality to get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{QP_{f}}\left[|\hat{\Lambda}_{n}-\Lambda(f)|\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left[|\hat{\Lambda}_{n}(Z)-\Lambda(f)|\Big|X\right]\right]\geq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\hat{\Lambda}_{n}(Z)\Big|X\right]-\Lambda(f)\right|\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{f}\left[\left|\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}-\Lambda(f)\right|\right],

where
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}
is an estimator of
\Lambda(f)
that takes the original data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\thicksim}}f
as inputs.

In order to prove a tighter lower bound of nonparametric order, we need to make sure that our statistical model
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{p}
does not degenerate to a parametric class. The following notion is an adaptation of Definition 3.4 of
Goldstein and
Messer 1992
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{p}
and
\Lambda:\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable as in (
2.2
) and atomic of index
s
. We say that
(\mathcal{W},\Lambda)
is
non-degenerate
if for all
x_{0}\in[0,1]
there exists a compactly supported function
\psi\in C^{p}({\mathbb{R}})
with
\psi^{(s)}(0)\neq 0
and a positive constant
\delta_{0}>0
, such that the following holds true: For all
f_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
there exists
\eta_{0}>0
, such that for all
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0})
and all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
the function

f_{1}(x):=f_{0}(x)+\eta\delta^{p}\psi((x-x_{0})/\delta),\quad x\in[0,1],

belongs to

4.1
Splines

For a knot sequence

\mathbf{\xi}\coloneqq{\left\{\xi_{1}\leq\dots\leq\xi_{l}\right\}},\quad l\in\mathbb{N},

in

or identically equal to

(convention: fractions with denominator

have value
0
).
We also recall that this definition guarantees that (see Section 1, Theorem 3 of

)

\frac{dB_{k,d,\mathbf{\xi}}}{dx}(x)=d\left[\frac{B_{k,d-1,\mathbf{\xi}}(x)}{\xi_{k+d}-\xi_{k}}-\frac{B_{k+1,d-1,\mathbf{\xi}}(x)}{\xi_{k+d+1}-\xi_{k+1}}\right],\quad d>1.

From this, we deduce that

is

\int_{0}^{1}B_{k,d,\xi}(x)dx=\int_{\xi_{k}}^{\xi_{k+d+1}}B_{k,d,\xi}(x)dx=\frac{\xi_{k+d+1}-\xi_{k}}{d+1},

as the support of

is

\frac{\xi_{k+d+1}-\xi_{k}}{(d+1)^{2}}\leq\int_{\xi_{k}}^{\xi_{k+d+1}}B_{k,d,\xi}(x)^{2}dx\leq\frac{\xi_{k+d+1}-\xi_{k}}{d+1},

(4.3)

the upper bound coming from

Lyche, Manni and Speleers 2018

states that the B-splines define a local partition of unity

\sum_{k=1}^{l-d-1}B_{k,d,\xi}(x)=1,\qquad x\in[0,1].

(4.4)

\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi}\coloneqq B_{k,d,\xi}/\left\lVert B_{k,d,\xi}\right\rVert_{L^{2}[\xi_{1},\xi_{l})}

over the intervals

Definition 2

The following theorem describes the link between frames and Riesz bases.

Theorem 4.2

A sequence

Theorem 4.3

Given a sequence

•

\left\{v_{k}\right\}

6
Spline wavelet plug-in estimator

In this section we introduce an alternative estimator, along with a new privacy mechanism, that will allow us to obtain both, the optimal rate of Theorem
5.2
on atomic functionals and the parametric rate for smooth functionals. In the following, we consider wavelet estimators defined using a spline wavelet basis on the compact space
[0,1]
. Given the compact support property of
\psi,\psi_{i}
in (
4.13

j\geq j_{0}
and

3d+1
non-zero wavelets at level
j
and point
x
. Indeed, there are
d
boundary wavelets (
\psi_{j,i}^{\text{left}}
near

and
\psi_{j,i}^{\text{left}}
near

j\geq j_{0}
and
2^{j_{0}}\geq 2d+1
) and, among the inner wavelets, at most
2d+1
are non-zero at any point in the unit interval.
For some level
j_{n}
to be defined later and

\alpha>0
,

and

Z_{ijk}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{0})}}(X_{i})+\sigma_{\alpha,j_{0}-1}Y_{i(j_{0}-1)k},&\quad\text{if }j=j_{0}-1,k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1},\\
\psi_{j,k}(X_{i})+\sigma_{\alpha,j}Y_{ijk},&\quad\text{if }j\geq j_{0},k\in\mathcal{M}_{j},\end{cases}

(6.1)

where
Y_{ijk}
are independent Laplace distributed random variables with parameter 1, and

\sigma_{\alpha,j_{0}-1}=\frac{4(d+1)^{2}}{\alpha}2^{j_{0}/2},\quad\sigma_{\alpha,j}=\frac{4(3d+1)\max(\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{\infty},(\left\lVert\psi_{i}\right\rVert_{\infty})_{i=-1,\dots,-d})}{\alpha}\frac{a}{a-1}j^{a}2^{j/2},

(6.2)

for
j\geq j_{0}
. We will see that the spline degree

only affects the constants but not the rates. Thus, for multi-purpose synthetic data release we will choose a large value of

such that the conditions below are satisfied. We show in the following result that this defines
\alpha
-differentially private data.

Proposition 6.1
.

The privacy mechanism defined by (

) and (

) is
\alpha
-locally differentially private in the sense of (
2.7
) irrespective of the value of
j_{n}
.

By definition, for fixed

, the conditional Lebesgue density of
Z_{i}=(Z_{ijk})_{j_{0}-1\leq j\leq j_{n},k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}
given
X_{i}=x_{i}
can be written as

\displaystyle\prod_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\alpha,j_{0}-1}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left|\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{0})}}(x_{i})-z_{i(j_{0}-1)k}\right|}{\sigma_{\alpha,j_{0}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\alpha,j}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left|\psi_{j,k}(x_{i})-z_{ijk}\right|}{\sigma_{\alpha,j}}\right).

An application of reverse and ordinary triangle inequality leads to

\displaystyle\frac{dQ(\cdot|x_{i})}{dQ(\cdot|x_{i}^{\prime})}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}\exp\left(\frac{\left|\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{0})}}(x_{i}^{\prime})-z_{i(j_{0}-1)k}\right|-\left|\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{0})}}(x_{i})-z_{i(j_{0}-1)k}\right|}{\sigma_{\alpha,j_{0}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\exp\left(\frac{\left|\psi_{j,k}(x_{i}^{\prime})-z_{ijk}\right|-\left|\psi_{j,k}(x_{i})-z_{ijk}\right|}{\sigma_{\alpha,j}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}\frac{\left|\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{0})}}(x_{i}^{\prime})\right|+\left|\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{0})}}(x_{i})\right|}{\sigma_{\alpha,j_{0}-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\frac{\left|\psi_{j,k}(x_{i}^{\prime})\right|+\left|\psi_{j,k}(x_{i})\right|}{\sigma_{\alpha,j}}\right).

We recall that for any
x
in the unit interval there are at most
d+1

\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{0})}}

\displaystyle\frac{dQ(\cdot|x_{i})}{dQ(\cdot|x_{i}^{\prime})}(z)

Remark 6.4
.

So far, we obtained results for smooth and atomic functionals with plug-in estimators (
6.3
) based on spline wavelets. However, for plug-in estimators (
5.2
) based on splines we only proved convergence rates for atomic functionals. The reason is that the spline estimator does not offer a good enough control of the variance coming from the Laplace noise in the privacy mechanism (
5.1
). An inspection of the proof of Theorem
6.3
shows that parametric rates are achieved in the smooth case because the term
V_{2,j_{n}}
is upper-bounded by a constant free of
n
. This is true when spline wavelets are used because they leverage the smoothness of
\omega_{f}
in that
\left|\langle\omega_{f},\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle\right|
is decreasing exponentially fast in
j
(see (
4.2.2
)). An equivalent expression for

with spline-based estimators would be

V_{2,j_{n}}=2\sigma_{\alpha,j}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{j_{n}}+d}T_{f}(\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{n})}})^{2}=2\sigma_{\alpha,j}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{j_{n}}+d}\left|\langle\omega_{f},\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{n})}}\rangle\right|^{2}.

Since the normalized B-splines
\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{n})}}
form a frame with bounds independent of
n
, the above display is lower bounded by
C\sigma_{\alpha,j}^{2}\left\lVert\omega_{f}\right\rVert^{2}=\tilde{C}\alpha^{-2}2^{j_{n}}\left\lVert\omega_{f}\right\rVert^{2}
. Therefore, this term cannot be bounded, and B-splines cannot leverage the assumed regularity of
\omega_{f}
to counterbalance the variance of the Laplace noise. Finally, we note that it is independent of the scaling chosen for the B-splines, since multiplying
\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{n})}}
by a constant

results in
e_{k,j_{n}}
in (
4.8
) being replaced with
e_{k,j_{n}}/c_{n}
and
\sigma_{\alpha,j_{n}}
being replaced with
\sigma_{\alpha,j_{n}}/c_{n}
in Proposition
5.1
. Therefore, the above upper bound would remain unchanged.

7
Adaptation with spline wavelets

As seen in Theorem
6.2
, the optimal threshold is
2^{j_{n}}\asymp(n\alpha^{2}\log^{-2a}n)^{\frac{1}{2p+2}}\wedge n^{\frac{1}{2p+1}}
for atomic functionals (not depending on the smoothness
s
of the functional). This, however, depends on the regularity of the unknown truth
f_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}
, which is typically not known in practice. Therefore, one has to provide a data driven choice of this tuning parameter which results in optimal convergence rates for all smoothness classes, simultaneously. For this we adapt Lepski’s method for this privacy constrained setting.

We start by deriving some bounds following from the non-adaptive analysis. First, as a direct consequence of (
B.11
),

\displaystyle\big(\tilde{\psi}_{jk}^{(s+1)}(x)\big)^{2}\leq\tilde{C}_{d,s}2^{j(2s+3)}

(7.1)

for some universal constant
\tilde{C}_{d,s}
not depending on
x\in[0,1]
. Furthermore, in view of (
B.6
), we have for all
s\in\{0,1,..,\lfloor p\rfloor\}
that

\displaystyle\|(f-P_{V_{j}}f)^{(s)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}\leq C_{p,d}2^{-(p-s)j}\|f^{(p)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}=:B(j,f,s,p).

(7.2)

Also note that

\displaystyle\|(f-P_{V_{j}}f)^{(s)}\|_{L_{2}[0,1]}\leq\|(f-P_{V_{j}}f)^{(s)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}\leq B(j,f,s,p).

In view of (
B.8
) and (
B.9
), note that for all
s\in\{0,1,..,\lfloor p\rfloor\}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j})^{(s)}-{\mathbb{E}}(\hat{f}_{n}^{j})^{(s)}\|_{L^{2}[0,1]}^{2}\lesssim n^{-1}2^{2js}(2^{j}+2^{2j}j^{2a}\alpha^{-2}).

(7.3)

Then the optimal, oracle choice of the thresholding parameter is

\displaystyle j_{n}^{*}=\min\{j\in\mathcal{J}:B(j,f,s,p)^{2}\leq n^{-1}2^{2js}j(2^{j}+2^{2j}j^{2a}\alpha^{-2}),\forall s\in\{0,1,..,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\},

(7.4)

where
\mathcal{J}=\{1,...,j_{\max}\}
with
j_{\max}=(\log n)/3
.
1
1

1

We note that this upper bound follows from the assumption
p\geq 1
. If this assumption is relaxed one has to choose a larger
j_{\max}
.
Furthermore, note that we have an extra
j
multiplier on the right hand side, which will result in a logarithmic factor loss in the proof. Finally, note that the above
j_{n}^{*}
satisfies that

2^{j^{*}_{n}}\asymp(n\alpha^{2}\log^{-(1+2a)}n)^{1/(2+2p)}\wedge(n/\log n)^{1/(1+2p)}

(7.5)

and as a consequence

\displaystyle n^{-1}2^{2j_{n}^{*}s}\big(2^{j_{n}^{*}}+2^{2j_{n}^{*}}(j_{n}^{*})^{2a}\alpha^{-2}\big)\lesssim(n/\log n)^{-\frac{p-s}{2p+1}}\vee(n\alpha^{2}\log^{-(1+2a)}n)^{-\frac{p-s}{2p+2}}.

(7.6)

This oracle choice
j_{n}^{*}
, however, depends on
B(j,f,s,p)
which in turn depends on the unknown regularity
p
. Hence we consider a data driven choice for the optimal threshold
j^{*}_{n}
. We do not consider an empirical version of the supremum norm as it would provide an additional logarithmic factor, while we are interested in pointwise and
L_{2}
-concentration rates in the first place. Furthermore, controlling the variance in supremum norm of our estimator is technically highly challenging. In our proof one of the key step is to achieve the optimal concentration rate for arbitrary, unspecified point
x\in[0,1]
. Since the estimator can not depend on the unknown
x
, we introduce a fine enough grid and define the estimator on it. This turns out to be sufficiently accurate approach to provide optimal estimation rate at arbitrary point
x
.

Let us introduce the grid
x_{t}=t/M_{n}
,
t=0,...,M_{n}
, for
M_{n}\gtrsim n^{4/3}
and define the estimator as

\displaystyle\hat{j}_{n}=\min\Big\{j\in\mathcal{J}:\,

\displaystyle\|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j}-\hat{f}_{n}^{l})^{(s)}\|_{L_{2}[0,1]}^{2}\vee\max_{x_{t},\,t=0,...,M_{n}}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j}-\hat{f}_{n}^{l})^{(s)}(x_{t})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tau n^{-1}2^{2ls}l(2^{l}+2^{2l}l^{2a}\alpha^{-2}),\qquad\forall l>j,\,l\in\mathcal{J},\forall s\in\{0,1,..,\lfloor p\rfloor\}\Big\},

(7.7)

with
\tau=C_{0}c_{a,p,s,L,\psi}
and
C_{0}
as in Lemma
7.1
, for a large enough universal constant
c_{a,p,s,L,\psi}
to be specified in the proof.
Then we define our adaptive estimator as

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n}(x)=\hat{f_{n}}^{\hat{j}_{n}}(x).

(7.8)

The theorem below shows that this estimator achieves the minimax concentration rate both pointwise and for the
L_{2}
-norm.

Lemma B.2

For any integers

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{(\infty,m)}\leq 2\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{(2,m+1)}.

the last inequality coming from a second application of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
Finally, this proves that

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{(\infty,m)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\left\lVert f^{(i)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m}\left\lVert f^{(i)}\right\rVert_{L_{2}[0,1)}+\left\lVert f^{(i+1)}\right\rVert_{L_{2}[0,1)}

. From (

(B.5)

From the first inequality in Theorem

(B.6)

Variance of
\hat{f}_{n}
: We now bound the variance of the estimator for
L_{2}
and pointwise convergence. Let’s write

\displaystyle K_{j_{n}}(x,y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}\mathbf{B}_{k,d,\xi^{(j_{0})}}(x)\tilde{\psi}_{j_{0}-1,k}(y)+\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(x)\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(x)\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(y).

The variance of the estimator, by independence, is given by the sum of the variance coming from the Laplace terms and the variance coming from the empirical spline wavelet coefficients. For any absolutely continuous measure
G
with bounded Lebesgue density
g
and
P_{n}
the empirical measure of the
X_{1}\dots,X_{n}
, we have by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\lVert\hat{f}_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{f}\hat{f}_{n}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{f}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}K_{j_{n}}(x,y)dP_{n}(x)-\int_{0}^{1}K_{j_{n}}(x,y)dP_{f}(x)\right)^{2}g(y)dy

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}2\frac{\sigma_{\alpha,j_{0}-1}^{2}}{n}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{j_{0}-1,k}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G)}^{2}+\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}2\frac{\sigma_{\alpha,j}^{2}}{n}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty}n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{f}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{j=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(X_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(y)\right)^{2}dy

\displaystyle+2n^{-1}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty}\left[\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}\sigma_{\alpha,j_{0}-1}^{2}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{j_{0}-1,k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\sigma_{\alpha,j}^{2}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right],

where we bounded the variance the second-order moment in the last inequality.
We note that all the functions
\psi_{j,k}
and
\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}
are orthogonal for different resolution levels
j\neq j^{\prime}
, since
S_{j}^{-1}
maps into
W_{j}
and the
W_{j}
’s are mutually orthogonal. Then, the integral in the upper bound above is equal to

\sum_{j=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(X_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(y)\right)^{2}dy.

Let’s fix
j
between
j_{0}-1
and
j_{n}
. We recall that
\psi_{j,k}=S_{j}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}
for some operator
S_{j}
with
\left\lVert S_{j}\right\rVert\leq B_{d}
(for the induced
L_{2}
operator norm), where
B_{d}
is a quantity that depends on
d
only. Let’s write
\mathbf{T}_{X}=(\dots,\psi_{j,k}(X),\dots)^{T}
where
k
runs over
\mathcal{M}_{j}
and
G_{\tilde{\psi},j}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{\psi}_{j,l}(y)\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(y)dy\right)_{lk}
the Gram matrix of those dual wavelets in
W_{j}
. For an arbitrary orthonormal basis
\mathcal{O}=(O_{q})_{q\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}
of
W_{j}
(or
V_{j_{0}}
if
j=j_{0}-1
), we can also write
C_{j}
the matrix whose columns are formed by the coordinates of the
\psi_{j,k}
in
\mathcal{O}
(i.e.,
(C_{j})_{q^{\prime}k}=\langle O_{q^{\prime}},\psi_{j,k}\rangle
), and
\bar{S}_{j}^{-1}
the matrix of the endomorphism
S_{j}^{-1}
in
\mathcal{O}
(i.e.,
(\bar{S}_{j}^{-1})_{qq^{\prime}}=\langle O_{q},S_{j}^{-1}O_{q^{\prime}}\rangle
) which has spectral norm bounded by
A_{d}^{-1}
by (
4.12
). We then have

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\psi}_{j,l},\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\langle O_{q},\tilde{\psi}_{j,l}\rangle\langle O_{q},\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{q}\sum_{q^{\prime}}\langle O_{q^{\prime}},\psi_{j,l}\rangle\langle S_{j}^{-1}O_{q^{\prime}},O_{q}\rangle\sum_{q^{\prime\prime}}\langle O_{q^{\prime\prime}},\psi_{j,k}\rangle\langle S_{j}^{-1}O_{q^{\prime\prime}},O_{q}\rangle,

since
\tilde{\psi}_{j,l}=\sum_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\langle O_{q^{\prime}},\psi_{j,l}\rangle S_{j}^{-1}O_{q^{\prime}}
.
Using the fact that
S_{j}^{-1}
is self-adjoint, we can then check that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(X_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(y)\right)^{2}dy

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}_{X_{1}}^{T}G_{\tilde{\psi},j}\mathbf{T}_{X_{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}_{X_{1}}^{T}C_{j}^{T}(\bar{S}_{j}^{-1})^{T}\bar{S}_{j}^{-1}C_{j}\mathbf{T}_{X_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq A_{d}^{-2}\mathbf{T}_{X_{1}}^{T}C_{j}^{T}C_{j}\mathbf{T}_{X_{1}}

\displaystyle=A_{d}^{-2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(X_{1})\psi_{j,k}(y)\right)^{2}dy.

(B.7)

We also recall that for any
x\in[0,1]
and fixed

, there are at most
C_{d}
non-zero wavelets
\psi_{j,k}(x)
. We denote by

the set of indices of these non-zeros. Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality twice on the second line below (once for the integral, once for the expectation)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(X_{1})\psi_{j,k}(y)\right)^{2}dy

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{f}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in I_{j}(X_{1})}\psi_{j,k}(X_{1})\psi_{j,k^{\prime}}(X_{1})\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{j,k}(y)\psi_{j,k^{\prime}}(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{j},\\
|k-k^{\prime}|\leq C_{d}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{j,k}(y)^{2}dy\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{j,k^{\prime}}(y)^{2}dy

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{j},\\
|k-k^{\prime}|\leq C_{d}\end{subarray}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{d}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}2^{j}.

Summing these terms, we obtain

\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty}n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{f}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{j=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(X_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(y)\right)^{2}dy\leq C_{d}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}n^{-1}2^{j_{n}}.

Since the elements of the wavelet basis are normalised,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}\sigma_{j_{0}-1}^{2}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{j_{0}-1,k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\sigma_{j}^{2}\left\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}\sigma_{\alpha,j_{0}-1}^{2}A_{d}^{-2}+\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\sigma_{\alpha,j}^{2}A_{d}^{-2}

\displaystyle=C_{d}\alpha^{-2}\left[\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}2^{j_{0}}+\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}j^{2a}2^{j}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\alpha^{-2}j_{n}^{2a}2^{2j_{n}}.

In conclusion,

\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\lVert\hat{f}_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{f}\hat{f}_{n}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G)}^{2}\leq C_{d}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty}n^{-1}(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}2^{j_{n}}+2^{2j_{n}}j_{n}^{2a}\alpha^{-2}).

(B.8)

From (
4.1
), the
q
th derivative of
\hat{f}_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{f}\hat{f}_{n}
for
0\leq q\leq d-1
is also a spline, and denoting by
\mathbf{b}(X_{1})^{(q)}
the coefficients in the relevant normalized B-spline basis, we have
\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\lVert\mathbf{b}(X_{1})^{(q)}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{d}2^{2qj_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\lVert\mathbf{b}(X_{1})\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}
. Using Theorem
4.1
and the fact that
\hat{f}_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{f}\hat{f}_{n}\in V_{j_{n}+1}
, we obtain a bound on
\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\lVert\mathbf{b}(X_{1})\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}
similar to the one in (
B.8
) (more precisely, we reproduce the above proof with
G
the Lebesgue measure and

and then use Theorem
4.1
), up to a constant depending on
d
. Using Theorem
4.1
again, we deduce

\mathbb{E}_{f}\left\lVert\hat{f}_{n}^{(q)}-\mathbb{E}_{f}\hat{f}_{n}^{(q)}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G)}^{2}\leq C_{d}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty}n^{-1}(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}2^{j_{n}(1+2q)}+2^{2j_{n}(1+q)}j_{n}^{2a}\alpha^{-2}).

(B.9)

We now control the pointwise variance. For any
x\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}(\hat{f}_{n}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{f}\hat{f}_{n}(x))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{f}\left(\sum_{j=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(X_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+2n^{-1}\left[\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j_{0}-1}}\sigma_{j_{0}-1}^{2}\tilde{\psi}_{j_{0}-1,k}(x)^{2}+\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\sigma_{j}^{2}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)^{2}\right].

(B.10)

The above expectation can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}\left(\sum_{j=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(X_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{j=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(y)\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)\right)^{2}dy

\displaystyle=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{j=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(y)\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)\right)^{2}dy.

For a fixed
x\in[0,1]
, the function
w\colon\ y\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)\psi_{j,k}(y)
is the element
w
of
W_{j}
(or
V_{j_{0}}
if
j=j_{0}-1
) satisfying
\langle w,\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle=\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)
. Indeed, we know that
w=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\langle w,\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}\rangle\psi_{j,k}
and the
\psi_{j,k}
form a basis (see below Theorem
4.3
). This is the reproducing kernel property of an RKHS. According to Theorem 5 of
Rakotomamonjy and
Canu 2005
(or Remark 6 therein),
W_{j}
is indeed a reproducing kernel Hilbert space as, for any

,

\left\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)\psi_{j,k}\right\rVert_{L_{2}}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}|\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)|\left\lVert\psi_{j,k}\right\rVert_{L_{2}}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}|\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)|<\infty.

Theorem 7 from
Rakotomamonjy and
Canu 2005
explicits the form of the kernel which is equal to

\displaystyle K_{j}\colon[0,1]^{2}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(s,t)

\displaystyle\longmapsto K_{j}(s,t)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(s)\psi_{j,k}(t),

and the symmetry of the kernel ensures that

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(s)\psi_{j,k}(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(t)\psi_{j,k}(s).

We can then write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(y)\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)\right)^{2}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(x)\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(y)\right)^{2}dy

\displaystyle\leq A_{d}^{-2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(x)\psi_{j,k}(y)\right)^{2}dy,

where the inequality was proved in (
B.2
) already. Using that
\left\lVert\psi_{j,k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{d}2^{j/2}
,
\left\lVert\psi_{j,k}\right\rVert_{L_{2}}=1
and for any
x\in[0,1]
at most
C_{d}
wavelets
\psi_{j,k}
are nonzero, we deduce from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(x)\psi_{j,k}(y)\right)^{2}dy\leq C_{d}2^{j}.

Using that
\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}=\sum_{l}\langle\tilde{\psi}_{j,k},\tilde{\psi}_{j,l}\rangle\psi_{j,l}
(see below Theorem
4.3
) and
\mathbf{T}_{x}=(\dots,\psi_{j,k}(x),\dots)^{T}
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)^{2}=\sigma_{j}^{2}(G_{\tilde{\psi},j}\mathbf{T}_{x})^{T}G_{\tilde{\psi},j}\mathbf{T}_{x}=\sigma_{j}^{2}\mathbf{T}_{x}^{T}G_{\tilde{\psi},j}^{2}\mathbf{T}_{x},

where
G_{\tilde{\psi},j}
is the Gram matrix already used above. As above in (
B.2
), we can bound
\mathbf{T}_{x}^{T}G_{\tilde{\psi},j}^{2}\mathbf{T}_{x}
by
A_{d}^{-2}\mathbf{T}_{x}^{T}G_{\psi,j}^{2}\mathbf{T}_{x}
for
G_{\psi,j}
the Gram matrix of the wavelets in
W_{j}
(instead of the dual wavelets). As the wavelets have compact support, this second Gram matrix is band-limited, with at most
C_{d}
nonzero elements around the diagonal, and its non-zero elements are bounded in absolute value by
1
by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Therefore, for any nonzero eigenvector
\mathbf{z}
of
G_{\psi,j}
associated to eigenvalue
\lambda
and taking the index
k
of its largest element in absolute value, so that

|\lambda||\mathbf{z}_{k}|\leq\sum_{l}\left|(G_{\psi,j})_{k,l}\right||\mathbf{z}_{l}|\leq C_{d}|\mathbf{z}_{k}|.

As a consequence, this matrix has its eigenvalues bounded in absolute value by
C_{d}
and we can conclude that

\left\lVert G_{\tilde{\psi},j}\mathbf{T}_{x}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell^{2}}\leq A_{d}^{-2}C_{d}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{T}_{x}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell^{2}}.

Using again that for any
x\in[0,1]
at most
C_{d}
wavelets
\psi_{j,k}
are nonzero and these are bounded by
C_{d}2^{j/2}
, this implies

\sigma_{j}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}(x)^{2}\leq A_{d}^{-2}C_{d}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\psi_{j,k}(x)^{2}\leq C_{d}\sigma_{j}^{2}2^{j}.

From (
4.1
), we know that any element of
V_{j_{n}+1}
is
(d-1)
–differentiable. Therefore, for
q\leq d-1
, we can introduce
\mathbf{T}_{x}^{(q)}=(\dots,\psi_{j,k}^{(q)}(x),\dots)^{T}
and generalize our previous notations. By linearity, we also have

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}^{(q)}(x)^{2}=\left\lVert G_{\tilde{\psi},j}\mathbf{T}_{x}^{(q)}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell^{2}}\leq A_{d}^{-2}C_{d}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{T}_{x}^{(q)}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell^{2}}.

Using the fact that the wavelets have compact support and hence at most
C_{d}
wavelet derivatives
\psi_{j,k}^{(q)}
are nonzero at any point
x
(since it is the case for the
\psi_{j,k}
), and since these are bounded by

according to (

), there exists a constant

such that

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j,k}^{(q)}(x)^{2}\leq A_{d}^{-2}C_{d}\left\lVert\mathbf{T}_{x}^{(q)}\right\rVert^{2}_{l^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2^{j}}\right)}\leq C_{d,q}2^{(1+2q)j}.

(B.11)

We can conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\hat{f}_{n}^{(q)}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{f}\hat{f}_{n}^{(q)}(x)\right]^{2}\leq C_{d,q,M}n^{-1}(2^{j_{n}(1+2q)}+2^{2j_{n}(1+q)}j_{n}^{2a}\alpha^{-2}).

(B.12)

In the end, we obtain

\displaystyle\max\left(\mathbb{E}_{f}|\hat{f}_{n}^{(q)}(x_{0})-f^{(q)}(x_{0})|^{2},\ \mathbb{E}_{f}\left\lVert\hat{f}_{n}^{(q)}-f^{(q)}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G)}^{2}\right)\leq

\displaystyle C_{d,q,M}\left[2^{-2j_{n}(p-q)}+n^{-1}(\alpha^{-2}j_{n}^{2a}2^{j_{n}(2+2q)}+2^{j_{n}(1+2q)})\right].

(B.13)

With the condition on

, we can bound

\max\left(\mathbb{E}_{f}|\hat{f}_{n}^{(q)}(x_{0})-f^{(q)}(x_{0})|^{2},\ \mathbb{E}_{f}\left\lVert\hat{f}_{n}^{(q)}-f^{(q)}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(G)}^{2}\right)\leq C_{d,q,M}(n\alpha^{2}\log^{-2a}n)^{\frac{-2(p-q)}{2p+2}}\vee n^{\frac{-2(p-q)}{2p+1}}.

Semiparametric rates for
\Lambda(\hat{f}_{n})
: We conclude as at the end of the proof of Theorem
5.2
, and obtain

\underset{f\in\mathcal{W}_{p}}{\sup}{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|\Lambda(f)-\Lambda(\hat{f}_{n})|\leq C_{p,s,M}\left[(n\alpha^{2}\log^{-2a}n)^{-\frac{(p-s)}{2p+2}}\vee n^{-\frac{(p-s)}{2p+1}}\right].

\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}C_{0}^{2}c_{a,d}^{2}\alpha^{-2}2^{2\ell(s+3)}\ell^{2a+2}\leq C_{0}^{2}\tilde{c}_{a,d}^{2}\alpha^{-2}2^{2l(s+3)}l^{2a+2}
.
Therefore, by combining the above displays with (
C.10
), we get for
l<j_{\max}=(\log n)/3
and
M_{n}\gtrsim n^{4/3}
that for large enough constant
\tilde{c}_{a,d,s}
and
C_{0}\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}W_{s,\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{C_{0}^{2}\tilde{c}_{a,d,s}2^{2l(s+1)}l^{2a+1}}{n\alpha^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(M_{n}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{M_{n}}|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}W_{s,\ell}(x_{t})|^{2}\geq\frac{C_{0}c_{a,d,s}^{2}2^{2l(s+1)}l^{2a+1}}{n\alpha^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{P}\Big(M_{n}^{-2}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}W_{s,\ell}^{(1)}(x)|^{2}\geq\frac{C_{0}^{2}\tilde{c}_{a,d}^{2}2^{2l(s+3)}l^{2a+2}}{\alpha^{2}M_{n}^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\leq M_{n}4e^{-(C_{0}/2)l}+2e^{-(C_{0}/2)j}\leq 4(M_{n}+1)e^{-(C_{0}/2)j},

where in the second line we used that
M_{n}^{2}\geq n^{8/3}\gg n2^{4l}

concluding the proof of (
C.16
).

Finally, we prove (
C.18
). Note that in view (
C.11
) and (
7.1
),

\displaystyle\sup_{x}|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}V_{s,\ell}^{(1)}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{x}|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}V_{s,\ell,i}^{(1)}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{d}c_{\psi}\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}2^{\ell/2}\max_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}\sup_{x}|\tilde{\psi}_{\ell k}^{(s+1)}(x)|\leq 2c_{d,s,\psi}2^{l(s+2)}.

Furthermore, similarly to (
C.19
),

\displaystyle\|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}V_{s,\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\leq M_{n}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{M_{n}}|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}V_{s,\ell}(x_{t})|^{2}+M_{n}^{-2}\sup_{x\in[0,1)}|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}V_{s,\ell}^{(1)}(x)|^{2}.

By combining the above displays with (
C.7
) for the design points
x_{1},...,x_{M_{n}}
, we get for
l\leq j_{\max}=(\log n)/3
and large enough constant
\tilde{c}_{d,s,\psi}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}V_{s,\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\geq C_{0}\frac{\tilde{c}_{d,s,\psi}2^{l(2s+1)}l}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{t=1}^{M_{n}}\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\sum_{\ell=j}^{l}V_{s,\ell}(x_{t})\Big|\geq\frac{c_{d,\psi,L}^{2}2^{l(s+1/2)}\sqrt{C_{0}l}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\leq M_{n}2e^{-(C_{0}/2)l},

where the first inequality holds for
M_{n}^{2}\geq n^{8/3}\gg n2^{3l}
. ∎

C.2
Proof of Theorem
7.2

We distinguished the cases
\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}
and
\hat{j}_{n}>j_{n}^{*}
, and deal with them separately. We start with the former one and consider the
L_{2}
norm first. Then by triangle inequality, in view of the definition of
\hat{j}_{n}
in (
7.7
), the upper bound for the variance in (
7.3
), the projection error in (
7.2
) and the upper bound for the mean squared error at the oracle
j_{n}^{*}
given in (
7.6
), we get that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{f}\|(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}}-f)^{(s)}\|_{L_{2}[0,1]}1_{\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{f}\|(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}}-\hat{f}_{n}^{j_{n}^{*}})^{(s)}\|_{L_{2}[0,1]}1_{\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}}+{\mathbb{E}}_{f}\|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j_{n}^{*}}-f)^{(s)}\|_{L_{2}[0,1]}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau n^{-1/2}2^{j_{n}^{*}s}(2^{j_{n}^{*}}+2^{2j_{n}^{*}}(j_{n}^{*})^{2a}\alpha^{-2})^{1/2}+{\mathbb{E}}_{f}\|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j_{n}^{*}})^{(s)}-{\mathbb{E}}_{f}(\hat{f}_{n}^{j_{n}^{*}})^{(s)}\|_{L_{2}[0,1]}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\|(f-P_{V_{j_{n}^{*}}}f)^{(s)}\|_{L_{2}[0,1]}

\displaystyle\lesssim(n\alpha^{2}\log^{-(1+2a)}n)^{-\frac{p-s}{2p+2}}\vee(n/\log n)^{-\frac{p-s}{2p+1}}.

Next we consider the point-wise error. Let us take an arbitrary
x\in[0,1)
and let us denote the nearest grid point to
x
by
x_{t}
. Then by triangle inequality

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}}-f)^{(s)}(x)|1_{\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}}-f)^{(s)}(x_{t})|1_{\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}}+|f^{(s)}(x)-f^{(s)}(x_{t})|

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}})^{(s)}(x_{t})-(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}})^{(s)}(x)|1_{\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}}.

(C.20)

We deal with the three terms on the right hand side separately. The upper bound of the first term follows the
L_{2}
case above

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}}-f)^{(s)}(x_{t})|1_{\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}}-\hat{f}_{n}^{j_{n}^{*}})^{(s)}(x_{t})|1_{\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}}+{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j_{n}^{*}}-f)^{(s)}(x_{t})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau n^{-1/2}2^{j_{n}^{*}s/2}(2^{j_{n}^{*}}+2^{2j_{n}^{*}}(j_{n}^{*})^{2a}\alpha^{-2})^{1/2}+{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j_{n}^{*}})^{(s)}(x_{t})-{\mathbb{E}}(\hat{f}_{n}^{j_{n}^{*}})^{(s)}(x_{t})|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+|(f-P_{V_{j^{*}_{n}}}f)^{(s)}(x_{t})|

\displaystyle\lesssim(n\alpha^{2}\log^{-(1+2a)}n)^{-\frac{p-s}{2p+2}}\vee(n/\log n)^{-\frac{p-s}{2p+1}}.

Then, for the second term, in view of
f\in W^{p}
, for
p\geq s+1
, we get that

\displaystyle|f^{(s)}(x)-f^{(s)}(x_{t})|\leq|x-x_{t}|\|f^{(s+1)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}\lesssim M_{n}^{-1}.

Finally, for the third term, note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}})^{(s)}(x_{t})-(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}})^{(s)}(x)|1_{\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\leq j_{n}^{*}}{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j})^{(s)}(x_{t})-(\hat{f}_{n}^{j})^{(s)}(x)|1_{\hat{j}_{n}=j}

\displaystyle\leq\|x-x_{t}\|\sum_{j\leq j_{n}^{*}}{\mathbb{E}}_{f}\sup_{z\in[x,x_{t}]}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j})^{(s+1)}(z)|1_{\hat{j}_{n}=j}.

(C.21)

Noting that
\|x-x_{t}\|\leq 1/M_{n}
it remained to give an upper bound for the second term on the right-hand side of (
C.21
). Also, for any
j\leq j_{n}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[x,x_{t}]}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j})^{(s+1)}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}\|\tilde{\psi}_{\ell k}^{(s+1)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}|\bar{Z}_{\ell k}|.

In view of (
7.1
), (
B.10
) and (
B.11
)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\leq j_{n}^{*}}{\mathbb{E}}_{f}\sup_{z\in[x,x_{t}]}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j})^{(s+1)}(z)|1_{\hat{j}_{n}=j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}\|\tilde{\psi}_{\ell k}^{(s+1)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|\bar{Z}_{\ell k}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}\|\tilde{\psi}_{\ell k}^{(s+1)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{f}\bar{Z}_{\ell k}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}\|\tilde{\psi}_{\ell k}^{(s+1)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}\Big(\frac{\sigma_{\alpha,\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\frac{\sigma_{\alpha,\ell}+1}{\sqrt{n}}2^{\ell/2}\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}\|\tilde{\psi}_{\ell k}^{(s+1)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j_{n}^{*}}\frac{\sigma_{\alpha,\ell}+1}{\sqrt{n}}2^{\ell(s+2)}\lesssim j_{n}^{*1+a}2^{j_{n}^{*}(s+5/2)}n^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\log n)^{c_{a}}\Big((n\alpha^{2})^{\frac{(s-p)+3/2}{2+2p}}+n^{\frac{(s-p)+2}{1+2p}}\Big),

(C.22)

for some constant
c_{a}>0
.
Hence, the right hand side of (
C.21
) is bounded from above by
(\log n)^{c_{a}}\big((n\alpha^{2})^{\frac{(s-p)+3/2}{2+2p}}+n^{\frac{(s-p)+2}{1+2p}}\big)M_{n}^{-1}
for
M_{n}\gtrsim n
and
p\geq 1
(recall that we assumed that
p\geq s+1
). Therefore, by substituting the above three upper bounds into (
C.20
), we arrive at

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}^{\hat{j}_{n}}-f)^{(s)}(x)|1_{\hat{j}_{n}\leq j_{n}^{*}}\lesssim(n\alpha^{2}\log^{-2a}n)^{-\frac{p-s}{2p+2}}\vee n^{-\frac{p-s}{2p+1}}.

It remained to consider the event
\hat{j}_{n}>j_{n}^{*}
. Then in view of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, assertions (
7.3
) and (
7.5
), Lemma
7.1
and the definition of
j_{n}^{*}
, for large enough choice of
C_{0}
(in the definition of
\tau
), we get the following upper bound for the
L_{2}
-error

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{f}\|(\hat{f}_{n}-f)^{(s)}\|_{2}1_{\hat{j}_{n}>j_{n}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J},\,j>j_{n}^{*}}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}_{f}\|(\hat{f}_{n}^{j}-f)^{(s)}\|_{2}^{2}}P_{f}(\hat{j}_{n}=j)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J},\,j>j_{n}^{*}}n^{-1/2}j^{1/2}2^{js}(2^{j/2}+2^{j}j^{a}\alpha^{-1})\sqrt{M_{n}j_{\max}}e^{-(C_{0}/2)j}

\displaystyle\lesssim n^{-1/2}e^{-(C_{0}/2-(2+1/2)\log 2)j_{n}^{*}}=o\Big(\frac{1}{n(\alpha\vee 1)^{2}}\Big).

Finally, we deal with the point-wise case. Note that similarly to (
C.22
)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{f}((\hat{f}_{n}^{j}-f)^{(s)}(x))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{f}((\hat{f}_{n}^{j})^{(s)}(x))^{2}+f^{2}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell}}\|\tilde{\psi}_{\ell k}^{(s)}\|_{L_{\infty}[0,1]}\Big(\frac{\sigma_{\alpha,\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)+f^{2}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell=j_{0}-1}^{j}2^{\ell(s+1)}\lesssim 2^{j(s+1)}.

Then, in view of the definition of
j_{n}^{*}
, for large enough choice of

,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{f}|(\hat{f}_{n}-f)^{(s)}(x)|1_{\hat{j}_{n}>j_{n}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J},\,j>j_{n}^{*}}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}_{f}((\hat{f}_{n}^{j}-f)^{(s)}(x))^{2}}P_{f}(\hat{j}_{n}=j)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J},\,j>j_{n}^{*}}\sqrt{M_{n}j_{\max}}2^{j(s+1)/2}e^{-(C_{0}/4)j}

\displaystyle=o\Big(\frac{1}{n(\alpha\vee 1)^{2}}\Big).

∎
