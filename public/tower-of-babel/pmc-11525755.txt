Spontaneous recovery of postsurgical progressive cervical spine kyphosis following intramedullary spinal cord tumor resection in a 4-year-old boy: illustrative case
Authors: Eve Michaud, Rakan Bokhari, Christine Saint-Martin, Neil Saran, Roy W R Dudley
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDPostsurgical kyphosis is relatively common in children who have undergone resection of intramedullary spinal cord tumors. Progressive kyphosis almost always requires instrumentation and fusion surgery, which can delay or interfere with adjuvant oncological treatments and can deleteriously impact the long-term performance status of the patient.OBSERVATIONSHere, the authors report a case of near-complete spontaneous recovery (i.e., without spinal fusion surgery) of postsurgical progressive cervical spine kyphosis following intramedullary spinal cord tumor resection and discuss the potential factors that may have contributed to this positive outcome.LESSONSThis case serves as a reminder that spontaneous recovery from postsurgical progressive cervical spine kyphosis can occur and that some patients (i.e., those without neurological deficits) can be monitored closely, with a watch-and-wait approach, before subjecting them to additional surgical risks, delays in other treatments, and potential morbidity. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24187 Keywords: intramedullary spinal cord tumor, laminectomy, laminotomy, kyphosis, spontaneous recoveryABBREVIATIONS: CNS = central nervous system, CSF = cerebrospinal fluid, CT = computed tomography, DEM = deep extensor muscle, DLGNT = diffuse leptomeningeal glioneuronal tumor, EVD = external ventricular drain, IMSCT = intramedullary spinal cord tumor, IONM = intraoperative neuroelectrophysiological monitoring, MEK = mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase, MRI = magnetic resonance imaging, VP = ventriculoperitoneal, WHO = World Health Organization.

Intramedullary spinal cord tumors (IMSCTs) are relatively rare, accounting for less than 10% of all central nervous system (CNS) tumors.1–3 However, they are more common in children than in adults, representing 35% of all spinal tumors in the pediatric population.1–4 Treatment usually involves resection via a multilevel laminectomy or osteoplastic laminotomies, as well as a myelotomy, in addition to adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy in some cases, depending on histopathological and molecular diagnoses and extent of resection.1–2, 4–6 Surgery within the delicate confines of the spinal cord is not without risk but is usually well tolerated in terms of neurological function with the assistance of modern microsurgical techniques and intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring.1–2, 4–6 However, postlaminectomy/postlaminoplasty (i.e., postsurgical) spinal deformity, particularly progressive kyphosis, deleteriously impacts the long-term outcome for many of these patients and is more common in children, with some studies reporting kyphosis in 100% of this population, a younger age being a significant risk factor.1–5, 7 Progressive postsurgical kyphosis requires further surgery in the form of spinal instrumentation and fusion, which can complicate and/or delay the oncological treatments in addition to increasing the risk of further morbidity. Indeed, long-term modified McCormick Scale grades and Karnofsky Performance Statuses were found to be worse in children requiring postlaminectomy fusion.1, 4

Here, we report a case of near-complete spontaneous recovery from postsurgical progressive cervical spine kyphosis following IMSCT resection, obviating the need for spinal fusion. We discuss the potential reasons for this surprising outcome. This case serves as a reminder that, while seemingly rare, spontaneous recovery from postsurgical progressive cervical spine kyphosis can occur. Thus, some patients can be monitored closely to watch for signs of spontaneous recovery before subjecting them to the risks and potential morbidity of spinal fusion surgery.

Illustrative Case

A 4-year-old boy presented with a 2-month history of headache, vomiting, and malaise. His full physical and neurological examination was normal except for papilledema. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed acute hydrocephalus with transependymal edema (Fig. 1A and B), and on the very inferior skull base images, a cystic change was seen in the upper cervical spinal cord (Fig. 1C), which prompted an MRI scan of his entire spinal cord. The scan revealed a large intramedullary multiloculated cystic and gadolinium-enhancing tumor expanding the cervical spinal cord and extending from C2 to C6 (Fig. 1D–F); there was no cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space remaining at these levels. In addition, there was extensive leptomeningeal sugarcoated gadolinium enhancement up and down the spinal cord (Fig. 1E), consistent with tumor dissemination. We speculated that the patient’s hydrocephalus was nonobstructive related to tumor dissemination, but we wondered whether the expansile mass at the upper spinal cord could have also been interfering with CSF circulation. Furthermore, the leptomeningeal dissemination and relatively short history raised a concern that this might be a very malignant tumor with an extremely poor prognosis. Therefore, instead of directly opting for a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt, we elected to place an external ventricular drain (EVD) to sample his CSF for cytology while discussing the next steps with our neuro-oncology colleagues and the patient’s parents.

Following these multidisciplinary discussions and negative CSF findings and based on the apparent extensive dissemination of the tumor, we recommended an open biopsy and tumor debulking with intraoperative neuroelectrophysiological monitoring (IONM). The goals were to obtain a histopathological and molecular-genetic diagnosis to plan further treatment and to decrease the chance of further tumor growth causing neurological deficits. After opening the skin and fascia, we dissected the deep extensor muscles (DEMs) off the spinous processes and laminae of C2–5. We partially removed the spinous process of C5 to gain access to the C5–6 intralaminar space and performed osteoplastic laminotomies (i.e., laminoplasty) of C2–5. We did not believe that we had to open the C6 lamina because only a cystic (i.e., nonnodular, nonenhancing) part of the tumor occupied this lower extent. Only the medial facet joints were exposed bilaterally, with complete preservation of the joint capsules. Following a standard midline myelotomy, we opened into the cyst (which decompressed the spinal cord) and performed conservative debulking of only the grossly abnormal-looking tissue, which still allowed us to remove approximately 80% of the tumor (Fig. 1G). This decision was guided by the disseminated nature of the tumor disease and the fact that the patient was completely neurologically intact preoperatively. After closing the dura, the laminae were sutured back at their respective levels, the C5 spinous process was sutured to the C6 spinous process, and the muscles were reapproximated back to the spinous processes. The IONM was stable throughout the case, and he woke up without new deficits. Histopathological and genetic analysis revealed a diffuse leptomeningeal glioneuronal tumor (DLGNT) with tandem duplication of 7q34 leading to a BRAF fusion and a codeletion of 1p and 19q. This is a rare CNS neoplasm of slow progression that was added to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of CNS tumors in 2021; it has not been given a WHO grade yet.8 Patients diagnosed with these tumors are predominantly males around the ages of 5–8 years old.9, 10–11 Radiologically, DLGNTs are described as cystic or nodular hyperintense lesions with leptomeningeal enhancement in the brainstem and spinal cord region on MRI, consistent with our case.9, 12–14

We could not wean the patient’s EVD without the return of hydrocephalus signs and symptoms. Therefore, a right-sided VP shunt was placed. One week after the surgery, follow-up spine MRI showed a greatly reduced cystic component, a decreased mass effect, and no obvious complications. Four months later, however, his spine MRI revealed significant cervical spine kyphosis (Fig. 1H) centered at the C4 level with associated spinal canal stenosis and, in retrospect, atrophy of the DEMs (Fig. 1I); he remained asymptomatic with a normal neurological examination. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the cervical spine (Fig. 2A) revealed a loss of the C5 spinous process remnant that had been sutured to the C6, consistent with bone reabsorption at this level. However, the C2, C3, and C4 levels were fused, and the lamina and spinous process of C2 were fused to the spinous process and lamina of C3.

Due to the significant kyphosis, we referred him to our pediatric orthopedic spine surgery colleagues. Short-term follow-up cervical radiographs were obtained, revealing interval worsening of the kyphosis (Fig. 2B), and the patient was scheduled for cervical spine fixation surgery to prevent further deformity and potential neurological injury. While awaiting surgery, the patient was started on chemotherapy (vincristine and carboplatin) because of tumor progression seen on the MRI. Subsequent MRI (Fig. 2E) performed 4 weeks later (i.e., 6 months after surgery) showed that his kyphosis was improving despite the absence of any cervical brace or collar; therefore, his surgery was put on hold. Continued follow-up imaging revealed further improvement, and 8 months after the surgery for his IMSCT, his kyphosis had spontaneously resolved (Fig. 2F). Cervical radiography at 14 (Fig. 2C) and 21 (Fig. 2D) months remained stable, and eventually 3.5 years later, his repeat spine MRI (Fig. 2G) continued to demonstrate stable recovery of the kyphosis, despite continued severe radiological progression of tumor throughout his brain and spinal cord (Fig. 2F). While bony morphological changes were noted in the anterior vertebral bodies from C3 to C5 after surgery (likely secondary to mechanical overload of the anterior column in a growing spine related to the cervical kyphosis) and given his young age, we are expecting some resolution of these changes with the off-loading of the anterior column, as the kyphosis has reverted to lordosis. His DLGNT progressed more in terms of brain and spine dissemination than in terms of local spinal cord progression despite multiple rounds of chemotherapy and mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MEK) inhibitors. However, he remains without focal neurological deficits, and he has never undergone radiotherapy. His parents were very happy and relieved that he did not need a fusion surgery for his spine.

Discussion

Observations

Postsurgical kyphosis is a relatively common complication of IMSCT surgery, particularly in young children.1–5, 7 Here, we have presented a rare case of spontaneous recovery from progressive kyphosis. This provides an opportunity to discuss both the risk factors for progressive postsurgical kyphosis in children and potential protective features that might allow for spontaneous recovery, as well as the interplay between these juxtaposed influences.

Specifically in the pediatric population, Yao et al.1 found that preoperative spinal deformity, an increasing number of resections, an age younger than 13 years, a tumor-associated syrinx, and surgery involving the thoracolumbar junction were risk factors for progressive spinal deformity following the resection of IMSCTs. Meanwhile, Ahmed et al. found that preoperative kyphosis and laminectomy and laminoplasty performed on increasing levels were independently associated with progressive postsurgical kyphosis in children requiring fusion following IMSCT resection.15 Studies in adults found that surgeries above C5, especially those involving detachment of the DEMs (i.e., semispinalis cervicis and the semispinalis capitis) from the spinous process of C2, as well as postsurgical atrophy of the same muscles, were risk factors for postsurgical kyphosis.16–18 What is unique in cases involving resection of IMSCTs is that the effect of surgery not only is disruptive to the cervical tension band but can also be restorative to extensor muscle function. As the mass effect from the spinal cord lesions can cause neuromuscular compromise, which interferes with paravertebral muscle strength, surgery may improve on this.1

In relation to these previously described risk factors, our patient was indeed quite young, only 4 years old. It is hypothesized that a young age is associated with a greater risk of postsurgical kyphosis due to the incompletely ossified vertebral bodies, increased cartilaginous components within the bony elements, increased ligamentous laxity, and more horizontally oriented facets, combined with a rapidly growing spine in children.1–5 Furthermore, while our patient did not present with kyphosis, he did have a cervical IMSCT extending over 4 levels with a very large cyst that might have already begun to compromise paravertebral muscle strength, making him more susceptible to postsurgical kyphosis.5 Due to the extensive upper cervical spinal cord location of his tumor, we were forced to open the laminae from C2 to C5, dissect the DEMs off the spinous process of C2, and include C2 in his osteoplastic laminotomy. Despite our efforts to replace the laminae from C2 to C5, his eventual postoperative CT imaging revealed a loss of the C5 posterior elements due to bone resorption of the small spinous process remnant that we had preserved during the surgery. Finally, postoperatively, he developed DEM atrophy. We believe that, combined with the patient’s immature, growing spine, this perfect storm of risk factors allowed for relatively early progressive kyphosis, which rightly prompted the recommendation for surgical fixation.

The question remains as to how, despite this combination of risk factors, he was able to recover spontaneously. As stated above, this is an extremely rare outcome in this situation. We only found one similar report in the literature. Gonda et al.5 presented the case of an 11-year-old boy who developed cervical spine kyphosis after the resection of an IMSCT spanning C4 to T4. The kyphosis was corrected with the use of a cervical collar and some lifestyle modifications (i.e., less time spent in neck flexion while on the smartphone).

Understanding why spontaneous recovery is rare requires some understanding of cervical spine biomechanics. The normal lordotic alignment of the cervical spine results in the cranial center of gravity passing posterior to the lordotic apex and the instantaneous axis of rotation, with axial loads mostly distributed to the posterior elements.19 This serves to maintain lordosis and forward gaze with minimal strain on the paraspinal muscles. As lordosis decreases, the cranial center of gravity shifts forward and eventually lies anteriorly in the kyphotic spine. At this point, the posterior elements are no longer in compression but experience tensile forces from the anteriorly shifted cranial center of gravity. The weight of the head now pushes the spine into further kyphosis, and the muscles eventually lose mechanical advantage. A vicious cycle is now in place, with kyphosis begetting further kyphosis (Fig. 3).20, 21

However, our patient experienced spontaneous correction without the use of a cervical brace or collar and without lifestyle modifications. The literature suggests potential explanations that may apply to our patient’s spontaneous recovery. First, as mentioned above, surgery in the upper cervical spine (i.e., above C5) appears to increase the risk of postsurgical kyphosis, particularly in adults. However, overall (and directly studied in children), the cervical spine is less susceptible than the thoracic spine to this complication, probably at least partly due to the fact that the cervical spine already starts off in a “protective” lordotic alignment, whereas the normal baseline alignment for the thoracic spine is, in fact, kyphosis.1, 5 Second, early surgery (< 1 month from presentation) for IMSCTs was found to be a protective factor against postsurgical kyphosis.1, 5 This is particularly interesting in our case because our patient did not present with weakness secondary to his spinal cord tumor but instead presented with headaches from hydrocephalus due to tumor dissemination. This is analogous to a brain tumor patient presenting with seizures before the tumor itself has caused neurological deficits, prompting early surgery. The early presentation in our case may have protected the patient from developing profound paravertebral muscle weakness and atrophy. Similarly, multiple surgeries increase the risk of postsurgical kyphosis,1 but our patient underwent only one surgery for his IMSCT. Indeed, there was no intention of going back to attempt gross-total resection because this tumor was widely disseminated at presentation.

Another potential factor that assisted with his spontaneous recovery was the use of osteoplastic laminoplasty with care to preserve the facets. Three of those 4 levels were fused, which provides attachment to paraspinal muscles and assists them in recovering their normal mechanics, as opposed to remaining suspended had we not replaced the laminae. Although the role of osteoplastic laminotomy, as opposed to laminectomies, remains controversial in this context, the literature suggests that the former approach could have a protective effect against postsurgical kyphosis.2–4 Although our patient had an eventual gap at the C5 spinous process level, all other aspects of his laminotomies had fused again laterally, with the addition of the vertical posterior C2–3 fusion. Obviously, the posterior spine bony fusion developed slowly and was seen at 4 months postsurgery, just before his spontaneous recovery began to be seen on radiographs obtained in preparation for his proposed fixation surgery. We speculate that his early, single surgery prevented severe neuromuscular compromise and paravertebral muscle weakness/atrophy, and once the mass effect of his large cyst had been removed, he was able to begin the recuperation of any subtle paravertebral weakness; meanwhile, his laminotomies fused and prevented further kyphosis. In short, we hypothesize that his laminotomies were fusing while any paraspinal muscle weakness was recovering.

Lessons

In summary, we speculate that the combination of early relief from the spinal cord mass effect and paravertebral muscle recovery, in addition to his laminotomy fusion, was enough to overcome the perfect storm of risk factors described above and likely allowed for his spontaneous recovery. It is interesting to note that his chemotherapy was also started around the same time his spontaneous recovery began, but at that point, he no longer had a large mass of tumor in his spinal cord; the chemotherapy was started more for his progressive disseminated disease. While we cannot rule out an additional protective effect of his chemotherapy against his progressive kyphosis, we think that this is highly unlikely, especially since his chemotherapy trial of vincristine and carboplatin failed and his chemotherapy regimen was eventually switched to vinblastine and MEK inhibitors. This highlights one more interesting point about this case: despite unrelenting progression and dissemination of his tumor throughout his brain and along his spinal cord, the postsurgical kyphosis was able to recover and did not recur in more than 3 years of follow-up.

Of course, any lessons learned from this report must be tempered by the fact that it was only a single case; it is still possible that if 10 cases were seen like this, the other 9 would require spinal fusion because of relentless progressive kyphosis. In addition, while we have a follow-up of more than 3 years, the true test of long-term stability would require a 5- to 10-year follow-up and perhaps into adulthood. Despite these limitations, the fortunate and unexpected outcome for our patient provides practical points (i.e., potential modifiable risk factors) that must be kept in mind by the neurosurgeon and the orthopedic surgeon encountering similar cases in the future. These include the following: 1) operating early for IMSCTs if possible, particularly when there is a significant mass effect from the tumor itself and/or its cyst or syrinx, in the hopes of avoiding paravertebral muscle weakness and atrophy; 2) exposing and opening the smallest possible number of laminae (i.e., if there is no need to reach both the superior and inferior extents of an IMSCT cyst, there is no need to remove additional laminae to expose both ends); 3) performing osteoplastic laminotomies as opposed to laminectomies provides the best chance of postoperative posterior element fusion and muscle reattachment; 4) if significant risk factors are present, such as young age, presurgical kyphosis, or multiple surgeries, consider keeping the patient in a cervical collar for at least 6–8 weeks postsurgery to allow for recovery of paravertebral weakness and muscle atrophy (although we did not do this); 5) follow such patients closely both with regular clinic visits to watch for signs of neck pain and/or neurological deficits and with imaging such as cervical spine lateral radiographs in a standing or sitting position at 4–6 weeks postoperatively, in addition to the spinal MRI that is required for tumor follow-up; 6) be aware that while rare, some patients who appear to be developing progressive postsurgical kyphosis may be able to recover spontaneously and can be given the chance to do so, with a close and cautious watch-and-wait approach before committing to spinal fixation. In addition, in this case, the patient’s deformity remained flexible (as seen on cervical extension radiography), meaning that if he eventually needed surgery, a straightforward posterior instrumented fusion would have sufficed. However, waiting is probably not ideal if the deformity starts getting to the point that a more extensive surgery is required.

In conclusion, this case of a young boy with a disseminated spinal cord IMSCT who had near-complete spontaneous recovery of postsurgical progressive kyphosis without the need for spinal fixation surgery provides important lessons regarding risk factors and potential protective factors associated with this common complication of IMSCT surgery. It serves as a reminder that if the balance of protective factors versus risk factors is potentially in favor of recovery, such patients should be given a chance to avoid spinal fixation surgery, with a close and cautious watch-and-wait approach as long as no neurological deficit occurs.
