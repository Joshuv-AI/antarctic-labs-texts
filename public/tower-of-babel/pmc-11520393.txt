Implications for household health expenditure in China’s ageing population: based on Red Herring hypothesis
Authors: Xuyang Du, Hualin Wei, Xianbo Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The rising healthcare costs due to population aging present a complex issue, with debate centering on whether these costs are driven by aging or end-of-life care. This study examines healthcare expenditures in Chinese households using data from the 2005 and 2018 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey. By applying the Heckman select model and a two-part model, the research innovatively includes time to death, income, social security and health level in the benchmark regression in order to validate the recent some new Red Herring hypothesis. The findings show that time to death is the primary determinant of healthcare expenditures, while the effect of aging is minimal. Income, social security, and health status also significantly influence health expenditure, but they do not function as Red Herring variables.

Introduction

With the global economy progressing and advancements in living conditions and medical technology, life expectancy has steadily increased while mortality rates have declined [1]. This demographic shift has led to a critical challenge: population aging. As the population ages, the surge in healthcare expenditures has placed significant economic pressure on both households and governments [2]. Older individuals face more chronic health issues and weakened immune systems, leading to frequent and costly medical services [3–5]. This increased demand for healthcare creates a financial strain on families, who must cover rising costs for medication, regular doctor visits, and long-term care [6]. At the government level, population aging necessitates substantial investments in healthcare infrastructure, medical technology, and personnel, further heightening financial burdens [7]. Addressing the impact of aging-related healthcare costs has therefore become a pressing issue.

China is facing an especially severe challenge in managing the financial burden of healthcare expenditures driven by its rapidly aging population, which is placing enormous pressure on both households and the government [8]. According to the 2010 Chinese Population Census data, among individuals aged 65 and older, 49% rely on family support, 20% on labor income, and only 25% receive pensions [9]. This income structure leaves many families struggling to cope with rising healthcare costs, especially as the health of elderly family members deteriorates and medical expenses escalate [10]. From 2005 to 2015, the proportion of elderly individuals using outpatient services in China increased from 27 to 49%, while the prevalence of chronic illnesses surged by 31.1% [11]. Moreover, the share of elderly health expenditures as a percentage of GDP rose from 2.1% in 1993 to 3.4% in 2013, reflecting the growing demand for medical care among the aging population [11]. China’s out-of-pocket healthcare costs account for 50.61% of total health expenditure, far exceeding international averages, highlighting insufficient government support in healthcare coverage [12]. To meet the rising demand for medical services, the government is forced to allocate significant resources towards building more healthcare facilities, procuring advanced equipment, and training healthcare professionals, further straining public finances [13]. With the continued rise in the elderly population and a slowing economic growth rate, the financial burden of healthcare on both households and the government is becoming increasingly unsustainable.

The factors driving health expenditures remain a topic of significant debate. In response to this, the research question of this study is: What is the main factor driving the rise in health expenditure for the elderly? To tackle issues of multicollinearity and endogeneity, this research will use the two-part model and the Heckman selection model, based on cross-sectional data from the 2005 and 2018 waves of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS).

Literature review

Factors affecting health expenditures

In examining the factors that drive healthcare expenditures, a prevalent perspective highlights population aging as a significant contributor. As individuals age, their health deteriorates, leading to an increased demand for medical services [14–17]. For instance, health reports from Serbia indicate that elderly individuals undergo more frequent laboratory and radiological examinations, outpatient visits, extended hospitalizations, and increased consumption of medications [18]. Age-related health deterioration is a common trend, and studies consistently show a positive correlation between aging populations and rising healthcare costs. Corti’s analysis of cross-sectional microdata from 2015 using OLS regressions confirmed that the elderly group’s medical expenditures were significantly higher than other age groups, with a higher adjusted R-squared value indicating a substantial age-related impact [19]. Similarly, Lopreite & Mauro’s B-VAR model and impulse response analysis of Italy revealed that age, rather than life expectancy or per capita GDP, had a more pronounced effect on health expenditures [20]. In Japan, Tamakoshi & Hamori used panel data and the fully modified ordinary least squares method to demonstrate a positive association between the proportion of elderly individuals and per capita real health expenditure, further solidifying the link between aging and rising healthcare costs [21]. In South Africa, Tunzi & Simo-Kengne employed time series data to explore the impact of population aging, finding that the elderly dependency ratio, alongside income, is a key driver of public health expenditure [22]. In conclusion, these studies collectively highlight aging as a critical factor influencing healthcare costs, but other variables such as income and healthcare access also play crucial roles.

Red Herring hypothesis

In the debate on factors driving healthcare expenditures, age is commonly viewed as a key contributor. However, Zweifel propose that the remaining time to death is the primary factor influencing the rise in healthcare costs, challenging the significance of age [23]. This perspective, known as the Red Herring Hypothesis, has gained support from subsequent studies [24–27]. Zweifel further affirmed, using the Heckman and two-part model, that age loses significance in influencing health expenditures when considering time to death. This suggests that aging is not a decisive factor in healthcare expenditures, where the crucial role is played by time to death [28].

Other studies also support this hypothesis. About one-quarter of total health expenditures are reported to occur in an individual’s final year [29, 30]. In Switzerland, Werblow validated the Red Herring hypothesis using extensive data, emphasizing that age is not the determiner, but time to death holds pivotal importance [31]. Colombier extended this analysis using Swiss macroeconomic data from 1960 to 2012, confirming the significance of time to death and identifying health technology progress and per capita GDP as additional influential factors [32]. Neglecting proximity to death may lead to an overestimation of health expenditures by 10–16% [27]. For OECD countries, Palangkaraya & Yong analyzing OECD countries, discovered that considering proximity to death can reverse the correlation between population aging and health expenditures, suggesting an overestimation of aging’s impact [33]. They argue that while the influence of aging may be overstated, the role of modern technology may be underestimated. Coory demonstrated, using historical data from 26 OECD countries, that population aging does not severely impact health expenditures [34]. Both quantitative and qualitative studies suggest that population aging has a limited contribution to health expenditure. A Age serves as a precipitating factor, while proximity to death emerges as the predominant demographic determinant influencing health expenditures [2, 35].

Recent Red Herring hypothesis

Recent developments in the research on the Red Herring Hypothesis have expanded its scope beyond the proximity to death, bringing factors such as medical technology [32], health insurance coverage [36], and health level [37] into the spotlight as potential key drivers of healthcare expenditures. Some scholars challenge the robustness of these findings, highlighting potential shortcomings in the econometric methodologies employed [38–40]. Most research on the Red Herring hypothesis uses dummy binary variables to indicate whether an individual died within a given year, with ‘1’ indicating death and ‘0’ survival [41]. Control variables, such as gender, urban-rural status, and medical insurance, are also often binary, which, as Williams explains in Econometric Analysis, can result in severe multicollinearity problems, particularly when discussing model specification errors and variable selection [42]. Hill, in Principles of Econometrics, also highlights this issue, emphasizing its importance in models dealing with categorical data [43]. Johnston and DiNardo in Econometric Methods provide some solutions, such as aggregating data to reduce the number of dummy binary variables [44].

Nevertheless, household income has been largely overlooked. According to the life-cycle theory, the elderly have no income and rely solely on savings accumulated during their younger years [45]. In developed welfare states, older adults typically have sufficient health insurance to cover basic medical and living expenses, and much of the existing research on the Red Herring uses survey data from Europe, particularly Switzerland, thus negating the need to consider the role of income. However, for developing countries entering an aging phase, where the Chinese government struggles to afford comprehensive high-level medical and retirement insurance, the family-based elderly care model is especially prevalent [46]. In such contexts, household income becomes a significant factor affecting elderly healthcare expenditures and should be considered. Consequently, the validation of the Red Herring hypothesis remains an active field of inquiry.

Given these complexities, the Red Herring Hypothesis has faced challenges, particularly with respect to issues like multicollinearity and endogeneity, both of which complicate the reliability of findings [38–40]. As a result, more recent studies propose new dimensions of the Red Herring Hypothesis, suggesting that health status and income may represent additional “Red Herrings,” each exerting its own influence on elderly healthcare expenditures [34, 38, 39]. However, variations in datasets and methodologies have led to inconsistencies in results across studies.

This paper aims to fill these gaps by conducting regression analysis on a unified dataset, systematically testing each of the recent Red Herring Hypotheses variables while addressing potential multicollinearity and endogeneity issues. The objective is to produce robust results and uncover the key determinants of health expenditures.

This study holds both academic and practical significance. Academically, it contributes to the literature by critically reviewing and empirically validating recent extensions of the Red Herring Hypothesis, while also incorporating the roles of elderly income and insurance coverage. From a practical perspective, the study identifies the key determinants of healthcare expenditures, offering well-founded policy recommendations to mitigate the escalating financial pressures on households and governments due to rising healthcare costs in the context of population aging.

Methodology

Data

The Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) is conducted by the Center for Healthy Ageing and Development at Peking University, serving as a crucial resource for aging and health research in China. As CLHLS started recording health expenditures in 2005 and the latest data extends to 2018, this study utilizes cross-sectional data from CLHLS surveys in 2005 and 2018.

Dependent variable

Log of health expenditure: In CLHLS, health expenditure is measured as the total RMB amount spent by elderly individuals in a year. Due to the large values and non-normal distribution, a logarithmic transformation is applied. For HEP of 0, it is kept as 0 and no logarithmic treatment is taken.

Independent variables

AGE: The age of the interviewee at the year of the interview.

AGE2: To test whether age has a non-linear relationship on healthcare spending, AGE squared AGE2 was added to the regression [28].

GEN: In the context of an aging population, considering gender differences is crucial. Therefore, the variable ‘GEN’ has been included in the regression analysis, where ‘1’ represents male and ‘0’ represents female [28].

AGE_GEN: The variable ‘AGE_GEN’ is the product of ‘AGE’ and ‘GEN’, created to estimate the impact of medical expenditures across different ages and genders. This interaction term allows for the analysis of how gender modifies the effect of age on healthcare spending [28].

TTD: TTD refers to the number of years between 2005 and the time of death of the interviewee [28, 47]. It is a continuous variable measured in years. For individuals who are still alive by the end of the study, the ‘Time to death’ variable is treated as censored data, representing the time from the last survey date to the end of the observation period.

lnINC: Considering that most developing countries, including China, lack the comprehensive universal insurance systems found in Western countries, families still bear the financial responsibilities for elderly expenses [46]. Therefore, per capita family income plays a significant and often overlooked role in elderly healthcare expenditures. In the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), family income per capita is measured as the total annual expenditure in RMB by elderly individuals. Due to the large values and non-normal distribution of this data, a logarithmic transformation is applied to normalize the distribution for analytical purposes.

INS: Most related literature employs dummy binary variables to indicate whether an individual possesses insurance [25, 33]. However, to reduce the number of dummy binary variables in the regression, this study introduces ‘INS’ as a count of the types of insurance an individual holds. The variable ‘INS’ ranges from 0 (no insurance) to 7, encompassing various forms of coverage including pension, private old age insurance, public medical insurance, cooperative medical insurance, basic medical insurance, severe disease insurance, and life insurance.

HL: It represents self-rated health level, where the values range from 1 to 4, indicating health levels from low to high.

UR: Given the substantial disparities in living standards and healthcare quality between urban and rural areas in China, an urban-rural binary variable is incorporated into the regression analysis to control these differences, where 1 represents urban and 0 represents rural [47].

EDU: Individuals with different levels of education tend to exhibit varying lifestyles and attitudes towards healthcare [47]. Therefore, educational attainment should also be included in the regression to control for this variation.

Instrumental variables

DRK: Extensive research indicates that alcohol consumption is associated with various negative health outcomes, including morbidity, mortality, and disability [48–50]. However, there is no direct relationship between alcohol consumption and the level of medical expenditures [51]. Therefore, it serves as a suitable instrumental variable for the Heckman selection model.

FDRK: For individuals who consume alcohol, the frequency of drinking has varying impacts on personal health outcomes [52]. However, the relationship between drinking frequency and health expenditures is complex and multifaceted [53]. Thus, it is well-suited as an instrumental variable for the Heckman selection model.

SMK: Smoking status significantly influences multiple health-related indicators [54]. However, smoking status does not affect medical expenditures [55]. Therefore, it serves as a suitable instrumental variable for the Heckman selection model.

FSMK: Among smokers, increased smoking frequency is associated with more severe health impacts, particularly regarding stroke and lung diseases [56, 57]. However, this does not directly determine the amount of health expenditures. Therefore, it is appropriate as an instrumental variable for the Heckman selection model.

EXC: Exercise is closely related to improved health outcomes, including reduced risks of cardiovascular diseases and diabetes [58, 59]. However, the impact of exercise on health expenditures is indirect and potential [60]. Thus, it serves as a suitable instrumental variable for the Heckman selection model.

OCP: Occupational type is significantly related to various health indicators, especially concerning occupational diseases and mental health [61]. However, this relationship does not have a direct impact on health expenditures. Therefore, it is well-suited as an instrumental variable for the Heckman selection model.

Empirical equations

The Red Herring hypothesis has long been challenged due to issues of multicollinearity and endogeneity [38, 41, 65]. To address these concerns, this paper employs a two-part model to mitigate multicollinearity and then applies the Heckman selection model for robustness checks to address endogeneity, thereby enhancing the reliability of the regression results [28]. In the two-part model, the first stage uses a logit model to estimate the probability of incurring healthcare expenditures, while the second stage uses OLS to estimate the amount of these expenditures [66]. By modeling these stages separately, the two-part model reduces interference between variables, thereby alleviating multicollinearity concerns. The Heckman selection model, on the other hand, addresses sample selection bias by combining the selection equation (logit) with the outcome equation (OLS) through the inverse Mills ratio, effectively correcting for endogeneity caused by the interaction between the dependent and independent variables [38].

This paper initially conducts a benchmark regression using only demographic variables concluded from most relevant studies [23, 31, 36, 40, 47]. Then, in order to test the Red Herring hypothesis related to time to death, as well as to explore the existence of new Red Herring variables, lnINS, HL, INS, and TTD are sequentially added into the regression analysis in Model 2. Furthermore, the coefficient of 0.909 for urban-rural status indicates that urban residents incur much higher healthcare costs than their rural counterparts [47]. Lastly, education has a positive impact on healthcare expenditures, with a coefficient of 0.039, implying that individuals with higher education levels tend to spend more on healthcare [47].

In testing the Red Herring hypothesis, the regression results show that after introducing TTD into the benchmark model, the significance of AGE and AGE2 disappears, while TTD exhibits a significant negative effect on healthcare expenditures. This suggests that the farther an individual is from death, the lower their healthcare costs. The insignificance of AGE and AGE² further supports the Red Herring hypothesis, indicating that time to death, rather than age, is the primary determinant of healthcare expenditures [28, 65].

In testing the recent extensions of the Red Herring hypothesis, we observe that when lnINC, HL, and INS are individually introduced into the benchmark model, AGE remains significant in all results except for result 4, with little change in its coefficient. Discussion

Similar to most studies, without considering other influencing factors, an increase in age not only leads to a higher probability of medical expenditures but also increases the amount spent on healthcare [21, 22, 67]. However, after incorporating the time-to-death variable, the impact of age on healthcare spending is no longer significant, consistent with much of the research and supporting the Red Herring hypothesis [18, 27, 28, 31, 33, 38, 40].

The findings of this study reveal that, contrary to the assertions made in the existing literature [36], the recent Red Herring Hypotheses related to health status, income, and health insurance do not result in a diminished significance of age’s impact on healthcare expenditures when these variables are included in the regression model [36]. Therefore, health status, income, and health insurance cannot be classified as new Red Herring variables. However, both the Heckman selection model and the two-part model consistently demonstrate that these factors exert significant influence on healthcare expenditures, indicating that while they are important, they are not the primary determinants. This observation aligns with the notion that higher-income individuals tend to incur greater healthcare expenses due to their financial capacity to pursue costlier treatments or access advanced healthcare facilities [68, 69]. Additionally, this can be simply explained by Keynesian consumption-saving theory, which posits that as people’s incomes increase, consumers will rationally allocate the additional income, correspondingly increasing personal consumption [70]. Regarding health insurance, the results indicate that having a greater variety of insurance types correlates positively with healthcare expenditures. This can be interpreted as individuals with more extensive health insurance coverage being more likely to seek timely medical attention, thereby reducing economic barriers to accessing healthcare services [1, 71]. Health insurance serves as a protective mechanism, facilitating individuals in affording necessary medical treatments, preventive care, and routine check-ups [72].

Regarding other control variables, education and urban-rural differences consistently demonstrate a significant impact on healthcare expenditures across all results. As anticipated, healthcare spending among urban residents is significantly higher than that of their rural counterparts [38, 47]. This disparity can be attributed to the generally superior healthcare infrastructure and increased accessibility of medical services in urban areas, which enables residents to utilize healthcare services more frequently, thus elevating their expenditures [73]. Additionally, the cost of living in urban areas is generally higher than in rural regions, which may further contribute to an overestimation of urban residents’ healthcare expenses [74]. Concerning education, individuals with higher educational attainment typically possess greater health literacy, allowing them to make more informed health decisions and seek timely medical assistance [75]. They are more likely to understand the importance of preventive care and regular health check-ups, thereby demonstrating a greater willingness to invest in healthcare.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper aims to exploring the drivers of healthcare expenditure. Given that population aging is a rapidly developing global issue, particularly concerning in developing countries recently, this paper integrates the characteristics of income inequality, low levels of social security, and family-based elder care prevalent in developing countries. Using CLHLS microdata in 2005 and 2018, we employ Heckman select model and two-part model to scrutinize the determinants influencing household health expenditure, aiming to identify strategies to alleviate the health fiscal burden associated with population aging in China.

Empirical results confirm the validity of the Red Herring hypothesis in the context of China, emphasizing the dominant role of time to death over age in influencing individual healthcare spending. Additionally, income, insurance, and health status are not new red herring hypothesis variables, although they also have a significant impact on health care spending. Finally, education and urban-rural also significantly affect individual health care expenditures.

Based on these empirical findings, due to the existence of the Red Herring effect, policymakers should focus on the time until death rather than age to alleviate the financial pressure of healthcare expenditures in China. To achieve this, specific policy recommendations could develop and implement early intervention strategies targeting at-risk populations. This can involve regular health screenings and preventive care initiatives aimed at identifying and addressing health issues before they escalate. By focusing on preventive measures, individuals can potentially delay the onset of severe health conditions, thereby extending the time until death and reducing associated medical expenses. Instead of implementing blanket healthcare funding for older adults without considering their specific health status. Additionally, considering the significant disparities between urban and rural healthcare in China, as well as the important role of education in influencing healthcare expenditures, policymakers should encourage urban physicians to engage in outreach and service in rural areas. Specifically, this can be achieved through the organization of regular medical clinics and health education seminars, aimed at enhancing the health literacy of rural residents. Such initiatives not only improve access to healthcare services for rural populations but also reduce the frequency and costs associated with medical visits by addressing health issues proactively. Moreover, these programs foster urban physicians’ understanding of rural health needs, enabling them to respond more effectively. This approach can contribute to a more equitable and efficient healthcare system, effectively narrowing the gap in medical resources between urban and rural areas while simultaneously lowering overall healthcare expenditures.
