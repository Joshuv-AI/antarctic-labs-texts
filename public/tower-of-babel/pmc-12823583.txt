Quality evaluation of Capitatae Fructus from different geographical regions in China
Authors: Hairong Zhong, Qing Xia, Ying Li, Linchang Tubi, Aji Lege, Kelong Aluo, Jinsong Ren, Xiaoli Eqi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCapitatae Fructus (CF), an ethnomedicinal resource with both edible and medicinal values in Southwest China, has an incomplete quality evaluation system, and the regulatory mechanisms of environmental factors on its bioactive component accumulation remain unclear. In this study, 37 CF batches from 6 producing areas in Sichuan and Yunnan Provinces were analyzed. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used to establish a fingerprint with good precision and reproducibility and conduct multi-component quantitative analysis, combined with hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), Correlation analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), and hierarchical partitioning (HP) to investigate CF’s chemical characteristics and environmental regulation patterns. Results showed an HPLC fingerprint of CF was successfully constructed, with 15 common peaks matched and 5 key bioactive compounds (gallic acid, hyperoside, isoquercitrin, quercitrin, quercetin) identified. HCA classified the samples into two distinct clusters: the first cluster covers low-, medium-, and high-altitude regions, among which the gallic acid contents (L26, L27) and quercetin content (L28) from Miyi County (low-altitude area) are higher than those of other sample batches. In contrast, the second cluster, concentrated in medium-altitude regions, generally exhibits lower contents of key components. Results from PCA show that sample L28 (Miyi County, low-altitude area) has the highest comprehensive score, which further confirms this altitude-related distribution characteristic of the compounds in CF. Correlation analysis was conducted to examine relationships between the 5 key compounds, showing that gallic acid was significantly positively correlated with hyperoside (r = 0.46, P < 0.01), and hyperoside was extremely significantly positively correlated with isoquercitrin (r = 0.91, P < 0.001). HP analysis was used to eliminate multicollinearity among environmental factors, confirming annual total solar radiation and annual cumulative ultraviolet radiation as core regulatory factors for CF component accumulation. Among the 5 compounds, gallic acid and hyperoside exhibited the highest sensitivity to environmental changes (R² = 0.76 and 0.71, respectively). Based on the above findings, Miyi County was identified as a high-quality producing area for CF, with gallic acid and hyperoside confirmed as the core quality indicators of CF. East longitude was determined to be the main environmental factor affecting CF component accumulation, while Annual Total Solar Radiation, Annual Cumulative Ultraviolet Radiation, and Annual Total Solar Radiation Difference were the synergistic driving factors. This study initially established a quality evaluation system for CF, providing support for its standardized development and sustainable utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32517-6.Keywords: Capitatae Fructus, HPLC fingerprint, Quantitative analysis, Quality evaluation, Environmental regulationSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Capitatae Fructus (CF), commonly known in Chinese as “Shanlizhi” due to its lychee-like appearance, refers to the dried mature fruit of Dendrobenthamia capitata (Wall.) Hutch.—a species of the genus Dendrobenthamia in the Cornaceae family1. From a taxonomic perspective, Dendrobenthamia has a clear historical affiliation, its species were once classified under the broader genus Cornus, and thus D. capitata was historically recorded as Cornus capitata Wall.; the genus Dendrobenthamia was later recognized as an independent genus1. Currently, Cornus capitata is universally regarded as a synonym of Dendrobenthamia capitata in academic circles, a classification that clarifies the systematic status of this species.

Biogeographically, the genus Dendrobenthamia exhibits a distinct geographical differentiation pattern, with its species divided into two major lineages: the East Asian lineage and the North American lineage2. As a key representative species of the East Asian lineage, D. capitata is mainly distributed in the East Asian region3, where China serves as its core distribution area. Specifically, natural populations of D. capitata in China are widely scattered across Southwest China (including Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou provinces, and Tibet Autonomous Region) as well as some other areas with suitable climates, typically growing in mixed forests at elevations of 1,300–3,150 m1. This wide geographical distribution, coupled with diverse environmental conditions (e.g., variations in altitude, temperature, and precipitation), has fostered abundant natural germplasm resources of D. capitata in China.

CF has a long history of dual application as food and medicine in Southwest China: fresh fruits, harvested between September and November, are consumed directly or used for winemaking and vinegar brewing, while dried fruits are processed into traditional medicinal materials. Though CF contains numerous hard seeds and minimal pulp, it is rich in proteins, vitamins, and dietary fiber, conferring significant nutritional value. Its medicinal use is particularly prominent in the traditional Yi medicine system—with the earliest documentation found in the Ming Dynasty’s Shuangbai Yi Medicine Book, which records its anthelmintic effect4. In recent years, CF has been officially included in the Sichuan Provincial Standards for Chinese Medicinal Materials (2022 Edition for Tibetan, Yi, Qiang, and Miao medicinal materials), marking its transition from a traditional ethnomedicinal resource to a recognized material in modern medicinal practice.

Some plants of Cornaceae family can be made as preserves and sweets5. Notably, a systematic research foundation has been laid for the chemical compounds of Cornaceae family: existing studies indicate that it contains various bioactive compounds, including polyphenols, flavonoids, and anthocyanins5,6. which provide an important material basis for explaining the pharmacological effects of species in Cornaceae family. However, research on D. capitata remains notably limited. This is especially true for systematic exploration of its fruit (hereafter referred to as CF): only a small number of modern studies have focused on the chemical compounds of different parts of D. capitata, and the isolated compounds primarily include flavonoids7, polyphenols8, triterpenoids9, and organic acids10. Among these, the research on the biological activities of flavonoids is particularly noteworthy: quercetin and isoquercitrin isolated from its branches and leaves both exhibit significant antiplasmodial activity, with half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC₅₀) of 155.7 µg/mL and > 277.8 µg/mL, respectively7. In traditional applications, D. capitata fruit is often used for anthelmintic purposes (e.g., against roundworms). From this, it can be inferred that the quercetin and isoquercitrin in CF may be the core material basis for its roundworm-killing effect. Studies have shown that the polyphenol content in D. capitata fruit is strongly correlated with its antioxidant activity, demonstrating its potential therapeutic value in treating diseases related to oxidative stress11. Therefore, flavonoids in D. capitata fruit are closely related to its traditional anthelmintic efficacy, while polyphenols are the core carriers of its antioxidant activity; these two types of compounds together constitute the key material basis for CF to exert its medicinal value.

Capitatae Fructus (CF) boasts abundant germplasm resources Although relevant research on CF has been gradually carried out in the agricultural, food, and pharmaceutical sectors—for instance, research on its application in wine-making, jam production, and tobacco flavoring—systematic studies on its quality evaluation and regulatory mechanisms remain insufficient. This research gap has hindered the effective utilization and development of CF resources.To address these limitations, this study collected 37 batches of CF samples from six counties across Sichuan and Yunnan provinces in China.

This study will first establish an HPLC fingerprint and a multi-component quantitative method for key compounds of Capitatae Fructus (CF). Subsequently, it will explore the effects of environmental factors (covering macro-geographic and micro-climatic types) on the accumulation of these key compounds, and screen potential quality control indicators for CF.Overall, the study is intended to build a multi-component quantitative evaluation system for CF, lay a theoretical foundation for identifying high-quality CF production areas, and further provide support for the scientific, standardized and sustainable development and utilization of this ethnomedicinal resource.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

From 2021 to 2022, a total of 37 batches of Capitatae Fructus (CF) samples were collected from six county-level regions across Sichuan and Yunnan provinces, China. These regions included Xichang City, Huili County, Dechang County, and Miyi County (in Sichuan Province), as well as Shangri-La and Shilin County (in Yunnan Province). Detailed information about the sample sources is provided in Table S1.

Prior to sampling, plots were established based on pre-investigated D. capitata distribution, with quantities adjusted according to local resource abundance: at least 3 representative 20 m × 20 m plots were set up in Xichang City, Miyi County, and Shangri-La, while 2 such plots were established in Dechang County and Shilin County (due to relatively limited resource availability). All plots adhered to uniform habitat criteria; adjacent plots within each region were spaced no less than 1 km apart to avoid microhabitat overlap and ensure sample independence. Within each plot, healthy adult CF plants were randomly selected, with a straight-line distance over 50 m between any two sampled plants to reduce microhabitat correlation interference.Notably, CF blooms from May to June, and its fruits reach maturity from September to November. All CF samples were harvested during this mature period (September–November), with fruits evenly picked from the upper, middle, and lower canopy layers of each plant to avoid heterogeneity caused by uneven development. After removing impurities, the samples were dried at 55 °C, ground, and passed through a No. 3 sieve (50 mesh) for subsequent analysis.

These samples were authenticated as the dried mature fruit of Dendrobenthamia capitata (Wall.) Hutch. var. (original variety) by Associate Professor Li Ying of the College of Pharmacy and Food Science, Southwest Minzu University, with the photograph of its original plant shown in Fig. 1. The voucher specimens were stored under the number 513424-210926-001-LY at the Sichuan Provincial Qiang-Yi Medicinal Resources Protection and Utilization Technology Engineering Laboratory.

Sample preparation and chromatographic analysis

Preparation of reference standard solutions

Accurately weighed amounts of five reference standards—gallic acid, hyperoside, isoquercitrin, quercitrin, and quercetin—were each dissolved in methanol to prepare stock solutions at concentrations of 1.039, 1.024, 1.055, 1.030, and 1.030 mg/mL, respectively. Subsequently, 1 mL, 7 mL, 3 mL, 3.5 mL, and 4 mL of the above stock solutions (in the order of gallic acid, hyperoside, isoquercitrin, quercitrin, and quercetin) were accurately pipetted into a 25 mL volumetric flask. The volumetric flask was then made up to volume with methanol and thoroughly mixed. This yielded the mixed reference standard solution.

Preparation of sample solutions

Precisely 1.0 g of powdered CF (passed through a No. 3 sieve, 50 mesh) was accurately weighed and placed into a conical flask with a stopper. After adding 25 mL of methanol, the flask was weighed, and the sample was subjected to ultrasonic extraction in a water bath (ultrasonic frequency: 40 kHz; power: 500 W; extraction temperature: 30 °C) for 30 min. Following extraction, the solution was cooled to room temperature, and methanol was added to adjust the solution to its original weight, compensating for solvent evaporation during ultrasonication. The final mixture was shaken thoroughly and filtered through a 0.45 μm microporous membrane. The resulting filtrate was used for HPLC analysis.

HPLC-PDA chromatographic conditions

Chromatographic analysis was performed using a Waters 2998 HPLC system equipped with an Agilent C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (A) and 0.3% phosphoric acid aqueous solution (B), with a gradient elution as follows: 0–20 min, 10% acetonitrile; 20–30 min, 10%→18% acetonitrile; 30–42 min, 18% acetonitrile; 42–43 min, 18%→22% acetonitrile; 43–60 min, 22%→28% acetonitrile; 60–61 min, 28%-36% acetonitrile; 61–75 min, 36%→60% acetonitrile. Detection was carried out at 310 nm, with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min and a column temperature of 35℃.The injection volume was 10 µL.

HPLC fingerprint analysis and validation

Method validation was conducted according to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (ChP 2020, Volume IV). Precision was assessed by injecting the same sample solution (L23) six consecutive times. Stability was evaluated by analyzing the same standard solution at 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 h. Repeatability was examined by preparing six independent replicates of the L23 sample.

Chlorogenic acid (Peak 11) was selected as the reference peak. Relative retention time (RRT) and relative peak area (RPA) of the common peaks across all samples were calculated, and their relative standard deviations (RSDs) were used for method validation.

Fingerprint evaluation was conducted using the Similarity Evaluation System for Chromatographic Fingerprint of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Version 2012 A). Chromatograms of the 37 batches were imported into the software, with sample L23 serving as the reference fingerprint (Fig. 2B). The median method was applied with a time window width of 0.2, followed by multi-point correction and mark peak matching according to standard protocols12.

The numbers indicate the 15 common peaks for identification: (4) Gallic acid, (11) Hyperoside, (12) Isoquercitrin, (13) Quercitrin, (15) Quercetin.

Quantitative determination of five major compounds

Reference standards of quercetin (RP200910, > 98.91%), isoquercitrin (RP210506, > 98.91%), and quercitrin (RP200602, > 98.91%) were purchased from Chengdu Medison Technology Co., Ltd. Gallic acid (1512025, > 98%) and hyperoside (MUST-21042514, > 98.65%) were obtained from Chengdu Kangbang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and Chengdu Mansite Biotechnology Co., Ltd., respectively.

Quantitative analysis of gallic acid, hyperoside, isoquercitrin, quercitrin, and quercetin in CF samples was performed by HPLC under the chromatographic conditions and sample preparation procedures described in Sect. 2.2.1–2.2.3.

Calibration curves for the five compounds were established using linear regression analysis, plotting peak area (Y) versus concentration (X, µg/mL). Method validation included tests for precision, repeatability, stability, and accuracy: Precision was evaluated by six consecutive injections of the same sample. Repeatability was assessed by preparing and analyzing six independent replicates of the same sample in parallel. Stability was tested by analyzing the same sample solution at 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 h.

To assess accuracy, a recovery test was conducted. Known quantities of the five standards were spiked into six 0.1 g powdered CF samples. Recovery rates and RSD values were calculated to confirm the reliability of the method.

Statistical analysis

The data of climatic factors were obtained from the WorldClim database (https://www.worldclim.org/data/biocli m.html#google_vignette)13. Based on the longitude and latitude of 37 sampling sites, monthly-scale data for 8 environmental factors (covering the fruit ripening period, i.e., the year prior to sample collection) were acquired: air temperature (at 2 m), surface temperature, relative humidity, wind speed (at 10 m), precipitation, sunshine duration, ultraviolet radiation intensity, and total solar radiation intensity. For the analysis, the first four factors were used in their original monthly format, specifically Monthly Average Air Temperature at 2 m (MAAT), Monthly Average Surface Temperature (MAST), Monthly Average Relative Humidity (MARH), and Monthly Average Wind Speed at 10 m (MAWS). The latter four factors were converted from monthly data to annual totals, specifically Annual Total Precipitation (ATP), Annual Total Sunshine Duration (ATSD), Annual Cumulative Ultraviolet Radiation (ACUR), and Annual Total Solar Radiation (ATSR). Additionally, Longitude (Lon), Latitude (Lat), and Altitude (Alt) were recorded in situ using GPS during sampling.

All indicators were subjected to normality testing and visualization using Origin 2025 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Violin plots and boxplots were also generated in Origin 2025 to illustrate the distribution and variation of each component. PCA was carried out and plotted in 3D using Origin 2025 to explore sample distribution and component contribution patterns. HCA with heatmap visualization, Pearson correlation analysis with corresponding visualization of the correlation matrix14, and HP were all conducted in R (version 4.3.2), using the ‘ComplexHeatmap’ package, “glmm.hp” package for these respective analyses15,16; figures were using the “ggplot2” package13.Separately, correlation analysis and collinearity diagnosis among environmental factors were performed using SPSS (version 26).

Results

HPLC fingerprint analysis

Method validation

The accuracy, stability, and repeatability of the HPLC fingerprinting method were evaluated by calculating the relative standard deviations (RSDs) of the relative retention time (RRT) and relative peak area (RPA) of the common peaks. The method demonstrated good precision, with RSDs for RRT and RPA ranging from 0.04% to 0.16% and 0.62% to 3.08%, respectively. The repeatability was also satisfactory, as indicated by RSDs of 0.04%–0.18% (RRT) and 0.57%–2.51% (RPA) in six independently prepared replicates. Regarding stability, the RSDs of RRT and RPA over a 24-hour period were below 0.20% and 2.36%, respectively, confirming the analytical stability of the samples within 24 h. These results demonstrate that the developed HPLC method is precise, stable, and reproducible, and is therefore suitable for fingerprint analysis of CF samples.

Establishment and similarity evaluation of CF fingerprints

The fingerprint chromatograms of 37 CF batches were analyzed using the Similarity Evaluation System for Chromatographic Fingerprint of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Version 2012 A). Sample L23 was selected as the reference chromatogram. The similarity values of all tested samples ranged from 0.620 to 0.992 (Table 1).

Identification of common peaks

Using the method described above, a total of 15 common peaks (F1–F15) were matched across the HPLC chromatograms of the 37 CF batches (Fig. 2A). Building on previously reported chemical compounds of CF from relevant literature, this study identified 5 compounds by comparing the retention times and photodiode array (PDA) spectral data of the sample peaks with those of corresponding reference standards (Fig. 2C). These identified compounds include 1 phenolic acid (F4: gallic acid) and 4 flavonoids (F11: hyperoside, F12: isoquercitrin, F13: quercitrin, F15: quercetin).

Ariations of five compounds in CF by geographic region and elevation

To visually dissect the regulatory roles of geographic region (macro-scale factor) and elevation (micro-scale factor) in the accumulation of the five compounds, a cluster heat map was constructed (Fig. 5). From the perspective of geographic region-related clustering, samples from different regions (Xichang, Dechang, Miyi) did not form independent clusters. For instance, Xichang samples (L13, L16, L8) were scattered in both major groups: L13 and L16 belonged to the first group, while L8 was in the second group; similarly, Miyi samples (L26, L28, L17) were split between the first group (L26, L28) and the second group (L17). This cross-regional distribution indicates that the overall impact of geographic region on the accumulation pattern of the five compounds is relatively weak, failing to generate region - specific component characteristics.

HCA heatmap illustrating variations in the contents of five compounds among CF samples, jointly grouped by elevation and geographic region.

In terms of component clustering, hyperoside, isoquercitrin, quercitrin, and quercetin clustered together, which is consistent with their shared classification as flavonoid compounds. In contrast, gallic acid, a phenolic acid, formed a distinct separate cluster, highlighting the chemical category-based differentiation in accumulation patterns.

Further HCA of the 37 CF batches identified two major groups, with elevation emerging as the key driver of group differentiation as evidenced by the distinct clustering of samples from different altitude ranges. When the CF samples were clustered into two groups, the altitude distribution and compound contents of the two groups showed obvious different trends: the first group covered a wide altitude range, including samples from low altitude (< 2000 m, such as L26 and L28 from Miyi County), medium altitude (2000–2300 m, such as L13 and L16 from Xichang) and high altitude (> 2300 m, such as L24 from Dechang County). Within their respective altitude ranges, the contents of specific compounds in this group were the highest. Among them, L26 at low altitude had the highest gallic acid contents, L28 at low altitude had the highest hyperoside and quercetin contents and L24 at high altitude had the highest isoquercitrin content. The second group was concentrated in the medium-altitude (2000–2300 m) area, specifically L8 from Xichang and L17 from Miyi, and the contents of compounds in this group were generally low. L8 had the lowest isoquercitrin and quercetin contents, and L17 had the lowest quercitrin content. Therefore, the cluster heatmap reveals that altitude exerts a significant and directional regulatory role on the differentiation of CF sample groups and the content of their characteristic compounds.

In Fig. 6, a correlation heatmap combined with Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed significant positive relationships among the five compounds in CF. Gallic acid was positively correlated with hyperoside (r = 0.46, P < 0.01). Hyperoside showed significant positive correlations with both quercitrin and isoquercitrin (r = 0.66, P < 0.001). Isoquercitrin was strongly positively correlated with quercitrin (r = 0.76, P < 0.001). Additionally, quercitrin exhibited a significant positive correlation with quercetin (r = 0.55, P < 0.001). These significant positive correlations confirm potential coordinated accumulation among the compounds—likely due to shared upstream metabolic pathways (e.g., the phenylpropanoid pathway for flavonoids and phenolic acids)—wherein increased content of one compound tends to accompany higher levels of the others.

PCA of five compounds in CF for chemical profile evaluation

To further integrate the information of these interrelated compounds and comprehensively assess the chemical profile of CF, PCA was conducted, with results summarized in Table S2 and illustrated in Fig. 7. The first three principal compounds (PC1, PC2, and PC3) explained 58.35%, 19.13%, and 17.25% of the total variance, respectively, cumulatively accounting for 94.73% of the variability. This indicates that these three compounds sufficiently capture the main chemical composition of CF.

3D diagram of PCA analysis of five compounds of CF.

The factor loading matrix revealed the relationships between the original variables and the principal compounds. Specifically, PC1 was strongly associated with hyperoside, isoquercitrin, and quercitrin; PC2 was primarily related to phenolic acids, particularly gallic acid; and PC3 mainly reflected the contribution of quercetin.

Using the established PCA model and following the method outlined in ref.17, eigenvectors for each compound were calculated. These eigenvectors were converted to loadings, which represent the correlation coefficients between individual compounds and the principal compounds. The absolute values of the loadings directly reflect the contribution intensity of each compound to the corresponding principal component—larger absolute values indicate stronger contributions (Table S2). Based on these loadings, weights were assigned to derive the scoring formulas for the three principal compounds (H1, H2, H3). The contents of the five compounds were standardized using SPSS (version 26). These standardized values were then input into the respective scoring formulas to generate individual component scores for each sample. Finally, the comprehensive score (H0) was calculated by weighting each principal component according to its contribution ratio to the total variance.The scoring formulas were as follows:

Relationship among compounds and environmental factors

To quantify the independent regulatory effects of different environmental factors on the accumulation of the five compounds in CF, this study employed HP to evaluate the impacts of environmental factors on the contents of these five compounds (Fig. 8). The results indicated that the influence magnitude of environmental factors varied significantly among different compounds. From the perspective of model explanatory power (R²), gallic acid (R²=0.76) and hyperoside (R²=0.71) were significantly regulated by environmental factors, suggesting that environmental factors had strong explanatory power for the content variation of these two components. In contrast, isoquercitrin (R²=0.43), quercitrin (R²=0.24), and quercetin (R²=0.39) had relatively low R² values, indicating that environmental factors had weak overall explanatory power for their content variation.

Further screening of core driving factors based on the criterion of “independent effect > 10%” showed that the core driving factors for gallic acid were ATSR, ACUR, ATSD, and LON; while the core driving factors for hyperoside were ATSR, ACUR, and ATSD. For isoquercitrin, quercitrin, and quercetin, there were no dominant environmental factors with an independent effect exceeding 10%, so it was inferred that their content variation was more dependent on the plant’s own genetic characteristics or uninvestigated environmental factors in this study (e.g., soil factors).

In the HCA, gallic acid (L26), hyperoside (L28), and quercetin (L28) from Miyi County at low altitude showed altitude dependence. However, in the HP analysis, altitude had no significant effect on various components. The core reason for this discrepancy was the extremely significant negative correlation between altitude and east longitude (r=-0.213, P < 0.001) with strong collinearity. The actual influence of east longitude on gallic acid was confused with the effect of altitude, which further led to the difference between HCA and HP results.

Discussion

As the core distribution area for East Asian lineage, China harbors four natural germplasm resources, with Dendrobenthamia capitata (Wall.) Hutch.(D. capitata) being one of them1,18. However, due to the scattered growth of these plants, the development and utilization of their resources lag far behind those of the North American lineage. D. capitata fruit (Capitatae Fructus, CF) is traditionally used as a medicinal material to treat ascariasis19,20.

In this study, 37 batches of CF samples from different geographical origins were collected, and an HPLC fingerprint was established. A total of 15 common peaks were matched, with similarity values ranging from 0.620 to 0.992 (Table 1). This indicates significant differences in the component stability of the samples, which are obviously affected by geographical environments. Variations in the peak areas of common peaks in the fingerprint are the core manifestation of differences in chemical characteristics; therefore, 5 compounds were identified based on these common peaks, namely gallic acid, hyperoside, isoquercitrin, quercitrin, and quercetin. A simultaneous quantitative determination was further conducted, and their contents were found to be in the ranges of 0.0078 ~ 0.1556 mg/g, 0.0043 ~ 0.2194 mg/g, 0.0209 ~ 0.0705 mg/g, 0.0254 ~ 0.0849 mg/g and 0.0005 ~ 0.0119 mg/g, respectively. Furthermore, among the 15 common peaks, several remarkable ones (e.g., F1, F3, F7) are highly likely to be bioactive substances of CF; however, their chemical structures have not yet been identified due to limitations in current analytical conditions. These unknown compounds will be further characterized using multiple complementary analytical methods (such as UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy) in subsequent studies.

Results from both hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA) indicated that the chemical composition of CF exhibits an altitude-dependent characteristic. For instance, the sample L24 collected from Dechang County (high altitude) had the highest content of isoquercitrin (0.0705 mg/g); in contrast, the sample L26 from Miyi County (low altitude) showed the highest gallic acid content (0.1556 mg/g), while the sample L28 (also from Miyi County, low altitude) had the highest contents of hyperoside and quercetin, which were 0.2194 mg/g and 0.0119 mg/g, respectively.

When exploring the regulatory effects of environmental factors on the accumulation of 5 compounds in CF, this study first conducted correlation analysis and multicollinearity diagnosis. The results revealed significant multicollinearity among environmental factors such as annual total solar radiation (ATSR), annual cumulative ultraviolet radiation (ACUR), and annual total precipitation (ATP) (Table S3). For example, high-altitude areas are often associated with strong ultraviolet radiation and low temperatures, leading to high information overlap between factors. This multicollinearity can interfere with the determination of “the independent effect of a single factor” in traditional regression analysis, making it impossible to accurately quantify the actual contribution of each environmental factor to component accumulation.

Therefore, this study adopted HP analysis. This method can eliminate the interference of multicollinearity between factors by gradually decomposing the total explained variance, and directly quantify the independent explanatory power of individual environmental factors on the variation in component contents, thereby enabling more accurate identification of the core environmental factors regulating the accumulation of each component. HP results showed that the 5 compounds exhibited significant differences in their responses to environmental factors. This regulatory difference may stem from the environmental response specificity of the chemical structure of each component and the key enzymes involved in their biosynthesis. For instance, hyperoside (β-D-galactose) and isoquercitrin (β-D-glucose)—two flavonoids—differ in their glycosyl groups, and the corresponding glycosyltransferases have significant differences in sensitivity to ultraviolet radiation: the galactosyltransferase of hyperoside is more sensitive to mild ultraviolet radiation and becomes inactivated through oxidative stress21. The synthesis of phenolic acids such as gallic acid is directly related to solar radiation; in particular, ultraviolet-B (UV-B) radiation can induce the accumulation of phenolic sunscreens, enhancing the plant’s protective effect against UV radiation22. Thus, the increased content of gallic acid serves as a defense mechanism against ultraviolet radiation23.

The HP analysis indicated that gallic acid and hyperoside were the most sensitive to environmental changes (with R²=0.76 and 0.71, respectively), and ATSR, ACUR, ATSD and LON were the core driving factors for these compounds (especially the independent effects of LON for gallic acid is >10%). This explains why the contents of gallic acid and hyperoside in low-altitude production areas of Miyi County were significantly higher in the cluster analysis: it is not altitude itself that drives the accumulation, but rather the fact that low-altitude areas in Miyi correspond to higher LON (as altitude and east longitude show an extremely significant negative correlation, r=-0.213, P < 0.001, Table S3), and the LON gradient further correlates with suitable ATSD (r=-0.828, P<0.001), ACUR(r=-0.854, P<0.001) and ATSR(r=-0.827, P<0.001) levels, ultimately promoting the synthesis of these two compounds. In mountainous ecosystems, altitude gradient is a key factor affecting plant secondary metabolism, primarily due to the synergistic changes in temperature and ultraviolet radiation caused by altitude variation24. Generally, as altitude increases, UV-B radiation intensifies and temperature decreases25. However, HP analysis in this study clarifies that altitude does not exert a direct independent regulatory effect on the 5 compounds; the “altitude-related variation” of components observed in cluster analysis is essentially a confounding effect caused by the strong collinearity between altitude and east longitude. The real driving force behind component accumulation is east longitude (and its associated radiation factors), while altitude only appears to be correlated due to its spatial co-variation with east longitude. This correction avoids overestimating the independent regulatory role of altitude and reveals the true environmental driver of compound accumulation in CF. Therefore, the actual environmental factors affecting the content changes of gallic acid and hyperoside (the two core components most sensitive to the environment) in CF are centered on LON, along with ATSR, ACUR, and ATSD—all of which exhibit a strong correlation with LON.

Combined with the results of correlation analysis, gallic acid showed a significant positive correlation with hyperoside (r = 0.46, P < 0.01), and hyperoside exhibited extremely significant positive correlations with isoquercitrin (r = 0.91, P < 0.001) and quercitrin (r = 0.66, P < 0.001). These findings indicate a synergistic accumulation effect among the compounds. Based on the results of HP, hyperoside and gallic acid are the most sensitive to ATSR and ACUR (with R² values of 0.71 and 0.76, respectively), and the contents of isoquercitrin and quercitrin increase synchronously with the increase in hyperoside content. Therefore, using hyperoside as the core component, combined with gallic acid, can not only directly reflect the regulatory effect of the environment on sensitive compounds but also indirectly reflect the response of the overall chemical composition of CF to the environment through their synergistic relationship—making this combination more representative than a single component.

As plant secondary metabolites, flavonoids have been reported to possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and anthelmintic activities26–28. To date, more than 30 types of flavonoids—including hyperoside, isoquercitrin, and quercetin—have been isolated and identified from D. capitata. Notably, quercetin and isoquercitrin exhibit strong antimalarial activity7. It has been confirmed that similar flavonoid compounds in plants such as Zanthoxylum (prickly ash) and Punica granatum (pomegranate) exert anthelmintic effects by disrupting parasite cell membranes and inhibiting metabolism28. Given that quercetin and isoquercitrin in CF also possess antimalarial activity—with partial overlap in their target mechanisms with anthelmintic effects (e.g., mitochondrial damage)—it is inferred that these flavonoids may serve as the potential material basis for the traditional use of CF in treating ascariasis. Meanwhile, prior research has demonstrated a strong correlation between gallic acid content and antioxidant activity in D. capitata providing evidence for gallic acid’s functional relevance to CF’s biological properties11. Beyond this functional link, both gallic acid and hyperoside possess three key attributes critical for quality control indicators: good stability, reliable detectability, and proven effectiveness. The effectiveness of these two compounds is supported by two lines of evidence: first, their potential association with CF’s traditional medicinal efficacy (e.g., anthelmintic effects), and flavonoids are important secondary metabolites in plants which determine fruit quality due to their essential contribution to fruit color, antioxidant capacity and nutritive value6. Second, the verified pharmacological activities of structurally similar compounds in related plant species. Given these characteristics, gallic acid and hyperoside collectively meet the criteria for quality assessment of CF and can therefore be proposed as potential quality control indicators29.

Environmental factors form a complex system that includes geographical, climatic, and edaphic (soil) factors, and they are key determinants influencing the quality of medicinal materials. In this study, only the regulatory mechanisms of geographical and climatic factors on known chemical compounds were explored, and the sample scope did not cover all distribution areas. In future research, we will expand the sample scope to further investigate the effects of edaphic (soil) factors on these compounds, integrate multiple detection methods to identify unknown compounds, and conduct additional pharmacodynamic experiments for verification.

Conclusions

To clarify the chemical characteristics and environmental regulation mechanisms of Capitatae Fructus(CF), this study conducted a systematic investigation using 37 batches of CF samples from 6 producing areas in Sichuan and Yunnan provinces, combining High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) fingerprinting, Hierarchical Partitioning (HP) analysis, and correlation analysis. The results showed that the established HPLC fingerprint could be effectively used to characterize the chemical properties of CF, and a total of 5 compounds were identified, among which hyperoside and gallic acid could serve as core quality control indicators for CF.

East longitude (LON) was the core geographical factor affecting the accumulation of CF components, while Annual Total Solar Radiation (ATSR), Annual Cumulative Ultraviolet Radiation (ACUR), and Annual Total Solar Radiation Difference (ATSD) — all of which showed a strong correlation with east longitude — were the key factors jointly driving component accumulation. Altitude itself had no direct and independent regulatory effect on component accumulation. Based on the above results, it is recommended that Miyi County be regarded as a high-quality producing area, and “gallic acid ≥ 0.15 mg/g and hyperoside ≥ 0.20 mg/g” be used as the core indicators for screening high-quality CF. For the artificial cultivation of CF, priority should be given to areas with suitable east longitude and sufficient sunlight, rather than simply low-altitude areas. This provides scientific support for the standardized development and utilization of CF resources.
