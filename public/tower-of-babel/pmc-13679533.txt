Introduction

Contemporary warfare and prolonged humanitarian emergencies operate under the assumption of an efficient pharmaceutical supply chain; one capable of delivering appropriate antibiotics before the clinical consequences of resistance become irreversible. This assumption fails in protracted conflicts, areas under siege, and chronically resource-limited settings where logistical disruptions, import restrictions, and infrastructure collapse interrupt antibiotic availability for weeks or months at a time.1,2 In these environments, antibiotics become rationed commodities, and clinicians are forced to manage infections with depleted stocks while simultaneously confronting wound colonizing pathogens that have evolved under sustained antimicrobial pressure. The global burden of bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR) was estimated at 1.27 million attributable deaths in 2019.3 This figure is disproportionately borne by low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and conflict-affected populations, where MDR infections coincide precisely with the weakest pharmaceutical supply system.4,5 ESKAPE pathogens—Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp. are the dominant cause of hospital-acquired infections in resource-limited and combat environments, and PDR isolates are increasingly reported from Ukrainian conflict casualties.6,7 Resistance in these conflict-associated isolates is dominated by a small set of mechanisms that is carbapenemase production, efflux pump overexpression, and outer-membrane impermeability that together render most first- and second-line antibiotics ineffective and underlie the clinical urgency for alternative therapeutic strategies.8

Bacteriophages (phages), viruses that selectively infect and lyse bacteria, offer a fundamentally different therapeutic paradigm. Unlike conventional antibiotics, phages are self-replicating: they amplify at the infection site in proportion to the bacterial burden and theoretically reach their highest concentration precisely where and when they are needed.9,10 This property, combined with the capacity for local isolation from environmental sources and a production timeline measured in days rather than years, makes phage therapy uniquely suited to logistically constrained settings.1

In this narrative review, we synthesize the mechanistic, clinical, and operational evidence for bacteriophage therapy as a living medicine strategy for health systems operating under siege conditions. We deliberately include Eastern European and Ukrainian-language sources to address the geographic publication bias that has historically marginalized the most extensive phage therapy clinical datasets. This review advances beyond previous summaries2,7,11 by integrating the PhageSP UKR conflict-zone isolation model, a systematic analysis of phage-driven plasmid loss in MDR pathogens, and a structured assessment of point-of-care production feasibility.

Methods

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

This review was conducted in accordance with narrative review methodology and reported following adapted SANRA (Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles) criteria. A systematic search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, and grey literature sources (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO IRIS, preprint servers bioRxiv/medRxiv) for articles published from 1 January 2001 to 1 April 2026. The search was supplemented by manual reference list screening of included articles and deliberate inclusion of non-English language publications from the Eastern European phage therapy literature to reduce geographic bias.

Search terms (title/abstract): (bacteriophage OR phage therapy OR phage cocktail) AND (antimicrobial resistance OR multidrug resistant OR ESKAPE OR carbapenem-resistant) AND (conflict zone OR resource-limited OR combat wound OR trauma infection OR besieged OR humanitarian). Separate searches were conducted for bacteriophage pharmacokinetics, phage-biofilm interaction, phage immune synergy, PhageSP Ukraine, and bacteriophage regulation.

Inclusion criteria: (i) human clinical studies, case series, compassionate – use reports; (ii) in vitro or animal studies directly informing clinical translation; (iii) operational or programme reports from phage therapy institutions; and (iv) regulatory and policy analyses. Studies published prior to 2001 were included selectively if they represented foundational or otherwise irreplaceable clinical evidence. Conference abstracts and non-peer-reviewed sources were excluded unless they described unique clinical or operational datasets unavailable elsewhere.

Outcome of interest: Bacteriophage therapeutic efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics, biofilm disruption, resistance trade-off mechanisms, and logistical deployment feasibility in resource-limited or conflict settings.

The Biology of Self-Amplification

Phage Replication Kinetics and in vivo Amplification

Bacteriophage pharmacokinetics differs fundamentally from that of conventional antimicrobials. Standard drugs follow first-order elimination kinetics: concentration decreases monotonically after administration regardless of therapeutic effect. Phages, by contrast, replicate within their bacterial hosts and can increase in concentration in tissues containing actively dividing susceptible bacteria, a phenomenon termed in situ amplification.12 Early in vivo studies demonstrated 100-to-1000-fold increases in phage titre at infection sites within 6–18 hours of a single intravenous or topical dose.13 This concentration-at-target behaviour is particularly relevant in wound settings, where phages administered to the infection site encounter a dense bacterial bioburden capable of supporting sustained replication.

In the absence of susceptible bacteria, phage titres decline as they are cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system and kidney filtration, with plasma half-lives ranging from 4 hours to several days depending on phage morphology and surface charge.12,13 Bacterial presence critically alters these kinetics; phage administered orally or topically have been observed to achieve systemic distribution to organs not directly targeted, presumably via bacterial-dependent amplification at mucosal surfaces.14 These data argue for adaptive dosing regimens calibrated to bacterial burden rather than fixed pharmacokinetic schedules. Pharmacodynamic parameters derived from in vitro and murine studies suggest phage therapy is most effective when the multiplicity of infection (MOI) exceeds a threshold sufficient to prevent resistant mutant outgrowth.15 Cocktails of two to four complementary phages targeting the same pathogen have demonstrated improved bacterial clearance compared with single-phage preparations, primarily by reducing the probability that resistant mutants can simultaneously evade all phage species in the mixture.7,15

Phage—Immune System Synergy

Early conceptualization of phage therapy modelled phages as acting in isolation to achieve bacterial clearance. Subsequent experimental and clinical evidence has substantially revised this view. Mathematical modelling by Leung and Weitz demonstrated that phage-innate immune synergy can reduce bacterial clearance times by 70–90% compared with either agent acting alone, largely because phages reduce bacterial numbers below a threshold density at which neutrophil and macrophage killing become more efficient.16 This synergy has been replicated in murine bacteremia and lung infection models using P. aeruginosa and S. aureus.13,17

Clinically, this synergy has implications for patient selection. Phage therapy appears to achieve its greatest efficacy in immunocompetent hosts or in combination with immunostimulatory supportive care; in profoundly immunocompromised patients, reduced bacterial killing by innate effectors may blunt the synergistic effect.18 This caveat does not preclude phage therapy in immunocompromised populations—several compassionate-use cases in transplant and oncology patients have demonstrated clinical benefit—but underscores the importance of concurrent immune optimization and suggests that outcomes data stratified by immune status are urgently needed.

A related and distinct limitation is host adaptive immunity: prolonged or repeated phage administration can elicit neutralizing antibody responses against phage capsid proteins, which may progressively reduce circulating and tissue phage titres over a treatment course and constrain the duration over which a given phage preparation remains effective.19,20

Pharmacokinetic and Pharmacodynamic Considerations for Clinical Protocols

Available pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data from human compassionate-use cases, the PhagoBurn trial, and animal models inform an emerging framework for clinical dosing. Phages administered intravenously at doses of 109–1011 plaque-forming units (PFU) have achieved detectable plasma concentrations within 15 minutes of infusion and systemic distribution to deep tissue compartments within 2–4 hours.21 Topical application to wound beds at 108–1010 PFU/mL has shown equivalent or superior local concentrations compared with intravenous routes for superficial wound infections.22,23 The PhagoBurn Phase I/II randomised trial noted subtherapeutic phage concentrations at the wound surface in approximately one-third of participants after 7 days, attributed to inadequate initial dose titration relative to the measured bacterial burden.22 This finding emphasises that dose-finding studies analogous to those routinely performed for conventional antibiotics remain a critical unmet need in phage therapy development.

Beyond the specific dose-titration failure observed in PhagoBurn, the phage therapy field more broadly lacks consensus on core dosing parameters: the number of doses a patient should receive, the target phage concentration per administration, and the total duration of a treatment course all vary substantially across published case series and compassionate-use protocols, with no standardised regimen established for any indication. Optimal route of administration is similarly unresolved — topical, intravenous, intraprostatic, oral, and aerosolised delivery have each been reported effective in different clinical contexts (Table 1), but head-to-head comparisons are lacking, and route selection in practice is typically guided by anatomical accessibility of the infection site rather than by pharmacokinetic evidence of superiority. Resolving these parameters through structured dose-ranging and route-comparison studies is a prerequisite for moving phage therapy from personalised compassionate use toward standardised clinical protocols.

Representative Clinical Studies and Compassionate-Use Cases of Bacteriophage Therapy in Humans (Selected)

Clinical Evidence: From Compassionate Use to Combat

Compassionate-Use Outcomes and Case Series

The clinical evidence base for bacteriophage therapy is primarily derived from compassionate-use cases and small case series, owing to the historical regulatory barriers to formal clinical trials discussed in Regulatory Landscape and the Path Forward.1,27 Table 1 summarizes representative clinical experiences.

Representative cases from the compassionate-use literature further illustrate the therapeutic potential. Johri et al24 described a patient with chronic bacterial prostatitis refractory to four antibiotic courses who achieved clinical resolution following intraprostatic phage cocktail administration.24 These case reports, while methodologically limited, consistently demonstrate two clinically significant observations: (i) phage therapy can achieve pathogen clearance in infections where antibiotics have definitively failed; and (ii) the adverse-effect profile in human compassionate-use has been consistently mild, with no severe systemic reactions attributable to phage preparations in reported cases.1,7

The Eliava Institute Legacy and Eastern European Phage Programmes

The Eliava Institute of Bacteriophage, Microbiology, and Virology in Tbilisi, Georgia, established in the 1920s under George Eliava, represents the largest continuous clinical phage therapy programme in the world.27 Decades of Soviet and post-Soviet clinical deployment—primarily for gastrointestinal, wound, and urological infections—have generated safety data on millions of patient exposures, constituting a longitudinal safety record that no prospective trial has yet replicated in size.27 Sybesma et al25 conducted an in vitro evaluation of Eliava phage cocktails against urinary tract infection (UTI) isolates from Belgian patients, demonstrating lytic activity against approximately two-thirds of clinical strains, including several isolates resistant to first-line antibiotics.25 These findings provide evidence that Eastern European phage products, developed against locally circulating strains, retain partial but clinically meaningful activity against Western pathogen populations.

The deliberate inclusion of Eastern European sources in this review is not a minor bibliographic gesture. The systematically larger compassionate-use datasets from Georgia, Poland, and Russia contain the only longitudinal safety signals currently available for phage therapy in human populations, and their exclusion from Western literature syntheses has created a misleadingly sparse evidence base.

Phage Therapy for ESKAPE Pathogens and MDR Wound Infections

ESKAPE pathogens dominate MDR wound infections in combat and humanitarian settings.7,26 Klebsiella pneumoniae (carbapenem-resistant, NDM-1-producing), Acinetobacter baumannii (extensively drug-resistant, XDR), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PDR) are the pathogens most frequently isolated from Ukrainian conflict casualties and from wound infections in LMICs experiencing protracted conflict.6,11 Phages targeting each ESKAPE species have been characterized in the literature.

Several phage cocktails demonstrate lytic activity against XDR and PDR isolates with no conventional antibiotic susceptibility.7,9 The clinical translation gap between laboratory susceptibility and bedside efficacy remains a key challenge; phage-bacterial interactions are strain-specific, and a phage active in vitro against a reference strain may exhibit reduced or absent activity against clinical isolates with modified receptor profiles.28 This heterogeneity reinforces the value of local isolation and the point-of-care production model described in The Logistics of Point-of-Care Phage Production.

The PhageSP UKR Initiative: Rapid-Response Isolation Against MDR and PDR Pathogens

The PhageSP UKR (Phage Support Ukraine) consortium represents a model for rapid-response phage therapy in an active conflict setting.6 Initiated in 2022 in response to the surge of MDR wound infections among Ukrainian military casualties, the programme established a framework for local phage isolation directly targeting circulating pathogen populations, bypassing the dependency on established phage product libraries.

Key reported findings from the PhageSP UKR initiative include:

Isolation success: Phages demonstrating lytic activity against MDR and PDR

K. pneumoniae sequence types ST39, ST307, ST395, and a clonal group CG10146, first detected in Moscow, Russia and subsequently linked to conflict-associated isolates across Eastern Europe, were successfully isolated from environmental samples in proximity to Ukrainian medical facilities.6

Phage-resistance tradeoffs: In vitro observations in a single K. pneumoniae strain noted that approximately one-third of phage-resistant mutants arising during phage exposure concurrently lost the NDM-1-carrying hybrid resistance plasmid, restoring antibiotic susceptibility; the remaining resistant mutants retained the plasmid but carried other mutations. This finding described in further detail in Phage-Driven Resistance Trade-OBs and Antibiotic Re-Sensitization—suggests that phage selection pressure can exploit a fitness trade-off between phage-receptor expression and resistance plasmid maintenance.6,29

Operational model: The programme established a workflow for phage isolation, whole-genome sequencing, safety assessment, and formulation achievable within a comparable timeframe to published personalized phage delivery pipelines, which report cumulative sample-to-treatment times ranging from roughly 8 to 23 days depending on pipeline optimization — substantially shorter than the months to years required for conventional antibiotic development.30 This pipeline, like the PhageSP UKR workflow, begins with de novo environmental phage isolation rather than deployment from a pre-characterized stock. The PhageSP UKR model illustrates the critical principle that phage therapy does not require centralised pharmaceutical infrastructure: environmental phage isolation from hospital sewage and water sources, combined with rapid microbiological characterisation, can generate locally tailored therapeutic agents within operationally relevant timescales.

Biofilm Disruption in Trauma Wounds

Biofilms—structured bacterial communities embedded in a self-secreted extracellular polymeric matrix—represent the dominant infection phenotype in trauma and combat wounds.31 The extracellular matrix reduces antibiotic penetration by 10- to 1000-fold, creates metabolically quiescent persister cells refractory to bactericidal agents, and provides a physical scaffold for horizontal gene transfer of resistance determinants.32,33 Conventional antibiotic therapy is therefore systemically limited in biofilm-associated wound infections.

Phages interact with biofilms through complementary mechanisms. Phage tail fibres can bind to biofilm-associated bacterial surface receptors and initiate lytic infection within the matrix; additionally, some phages encode biofilm-degrading enzymes (depolymerases) that directly degrade the extracellular matrix, facilitating deeper phage penetration and exposing bacteria to both phage and host immune cells.23,34 A systematic review covering 80 in vitro and in vivo studies of phage-biofilm interactions concluded that:

The Logistics of Point-of-Care Phage Production

Low-Cost Production and Local Manufacture

The economic and logistical advantages of phage therapy in resource-limited settings arise from several key properties. First, self-replication means that a seed stock of 108 PFU—producible at negligible material cost from environmental isolation—can be expanded to therapeutic quantities through a single passage in a suitable bacterial host culture; no chemical synthesis, animal-derived biologics, or complex fermentation infrastructure is required.37,38 Second, phage production does not require the cold-chain logistics that constrain antibiotic delivery in low-resource settings; phages are stable in simple aqueous buffers, and many preparations tolerate ambient temperature storage for days to weeks.21

Téllez-Isaias et al analysed phage production economics in agricultural applications—where cost constraints are comparably stringent to conflict-zone medicine—and estimated production costs of under US$1 per therapeutic dose for locally produced phage cocktails at modest scale.38 While human therapeutic production incurs additional safety testing costs, the fundamental production economics remain orders of magnitude more favourable than synthetic antibiotic manufacturing.

Phage-Driven Resistance Trade-OBs and Antibiotic Re-Sensitization

A mechanistically important and clinically relevant benefit of phage therapy derives from the evolutionary trade-offs imposed by phage predation on bacteria. To develop resistance to phage, bacteria must typically mutate or downregulate the surface receptor used by the phage for host recognition. For MDR K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii, the phage receptors frequently include outer membrane proteins (OMPs), lipopolysaccharide (LPS) components, and capsular polysaccharides that also function as structural determinants of virulence and, in some cases, as mediators of antibiotic impermeability.28,29 Mutants that downregulate these structures to avoid phage entry consequently exhibit reduced virulence and, in several experimental systems, increased susceptibility to antibiotics that target the now-modified outer membrane.29

This trade-off is not universal, however. Chis et al5 showed that although phage resistance in E. coli mediated by loss of the TolC efflux pump or LPS modification frequently restored antibiotic susceptibility, some phage-resistance mutations pleiotropically increased antibiotic resistance instead, indicating that the direction of the trade-off is mutation- and receptor-specific rather than guaranteed.19 Under combined phage-antibiotic selection in biofilm-associated Pseudomonas aeruginosa, phage resistance evolution has similarly been shown to correlate with increased antibiotic resistance, driven by biofilm growth acting as a generalized resistance mechanism favoured specifically under combination treatment.39 These findings caution against assuming phage-driven re-sensitisation as a reliable or general therapeutic mechanism, reinforcing the strain- and context-specific framing adopted throughout this review.

The PhageSP UKR and related studies have extended this observation to plasmid-encoded resistance. A subset of phage-resistant bacterial mutants arising under phage pressure have been shown to concurrently lose NDM-1 metallo-β-lactamase-encoding plasmids, restoring susceptibility to carbapenems.6 The mechanism likely involves the fitness cost of maintaining multiple genetic elements (phage resistance mutation and large conjugative resistance plasmid) simultaneously, with selection favoring plasmid loss when phage predation is no longer overcome by antibiotic resistance.29 Trejo-Hernández et al40 further demonstrated that pseudo lysogeny-mediated evolutionary trade-offs in phage-resistant Cutibacterium acnes mutants included simultaneous reduction in virulence factor expression, suggesting the mechanism extends beyond Gram-negative pathogens.40 Taken together, these data support the concept of phage therapy as a directed evolutionary tool—not merely a bactericidal agent, but a selective pressure that can systematically reduce both pathogen resistance and virulence (Figure 3).

Regulatory Landscape and the Path Forward

The University of California San Diego (UCSD) Center for Innovative Phage Applications and Therapeutics (IPATH) has established a clinical program focused on personalized phage therapy, including phage selection and preparation for patients with difficult-to-treat bacterial infections. Its experience demonstrates the potential for integrating individualized phage therapy into clinical practice. Würstle et al30 One of the principal barriers to phage therapy advancement is the absence of harmonized regulatory pathways. Standard regulatory frameworks for pharmaceuticals were designed for chemically defined, homogeneous molecular entities; phage preparations are inherently heterogeneous biologics with variable host range, pharmacokinetics depending on the target pathogen, and the property of evolving in situ during treatment.41,42

Specific regulatory challenges include:

Standardisation

Each phage preparation differs from others in host range, pharmacokinetics, and antigenic profile, making the conventional “one product-one approval” model impractical for the personalized phage therapy paradigm.41

Clinical Trial Design

Randomised controlled trials require standardized interventions; personalized phage therapy—in which the phage cocktail is matched to the specific patient isolate—is fundamentally incompatible with double-blinded standardization.22,42 The PhagoBurn trial, the most rigorous published RCT, encountered precisely this tension; the subtherapeutic dose concentrations observed in participants were attributable to an unanticipated 1000- to 10,000-fold loss of phage titre during cocktail manufacturing and storage — not a failure of dose selection or personalization — a stability issue that triggered a mid-trial protocol amendment.22

Manufacturing Scale

Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) requirements for biological products are resource-intensive and may be disproportionate for preparations intended for single-patient compassionate use.21

Proposed and implemented solutions include adaptive licensing frameworks—such as the Belgian magistral phage regulation—that allow patient-specific phage preparations to be produced by hospital pharmacies under simplified oversight.37,43 The European Medicines Agency has issued guidance acknowledging the need for phage-specific regulatory approaches.42,44 In conflict and austere settings, the most pragmatic near-term pathway is recognition of bacteriophage preparations under humanitarian use provisions, analogous to the compassionate-use exemptions already employed in Belgian, French, and Georgian practice.27,41

Broader Considerations: Safety, Ethics, and Equity

Safety Profile and Potential Risks

The adverse-effect profile of bacteriophage therapy in human compassionate-use experience has been consistently reassuring, with no severe systemic reactions attributable to phage preparations reported in published case series.1,7 Mild, transient fever has occasionally been reported following intravenous administration and is attributed to endotoxin contamination from insufficient purification rather than to the phage particles themselves; this underscores the importance of rigorous endotoxin testing in any production protocol.21

A risk that merits explicit acknowledgement is generalized transduction: the capacity of some phages to inadvertently transfer bacterial DNA—including resistance genes—between bacterial strains during the lytic cycle.34 Strict exclusion of transducing phages through genomic screening is therefore a mandatory component of any therapeutic phage characterisation workflow. The PhageSP UKR safety assessment protocol incorporates whole-genome sequencing precisely for this purpose.6

Equity of Access and Dual-Use Considerations

The logistical accessibility argument for phage therapy in conflict zones must be balanced against realistic implementation barriers. The published personalized phage pipeline timelines cited above, while operationally impressive, requires access to basic microbiology laboratory infrastructure (biosafety cabinet, centrifuge, incubator, gel electrophoresis), sterile consumables, and trained microbiological personnel— resources that may be unavailable in the most austere conflict or humanitarian settings. Investment in pre-positioned phage biobank networks—repositories of characterised phages targeting the most prevalent conflict-zone pathogens—would substantially reduce the point-of-care production burden and deserves prioritisation by humanitarian health organisations.11,26

Dual-use concerns, while theoretical in the current therapeutic context, warrant acknowledgement: the same local isolation and amplification technology that enables therapeutic production could in principle be adapted for harmful purposes. This risk is mitigated by the requirement for biosafety infrastructure and trained personnel and by the inherent species-specificity of phage, which limits off-target potential compared with broad-spectrum pathogens.

Discussion

This review synthesises evidence across four domains—phage biology, clinical evidence, operational logistics, and regulatory science—to make the case that bacteriophage therapy is not a futuristic alternative to antibiotics but a pragmatically deployable intervention for health systems under siege. Several specific advances position this synthesis beyond prior reviews in the field.2,7,11

First, the PhageSP UKR conflict-zone isolation model provides the most operationally detailed proof of concept for rapid point-of-care phage production in an active conflict, demonstrating that the transition from environmental sample to clinical-grade preparation is achievable within 8–23 days using modest laboratory infrastructure. Second, the convergent evidence for phage-driven plasmid loss and antibiotic re-sensitisation—documented in K. pneumoniae, NDM-1 producers, and most recently in acnes—suggests that phage therapy provides mechanistic benefits that extend beyond direct bactericidal activity and may actively reverse resistance, creating a therapeutic compounding effect when combined with antibiotics. Third, the systematic inclusion of Eastern European clinical sources—historically marginalised in Western reviews—provides a larger and more balanced evidence base than is available from Western compassionate-use reports alone.

The principal limitation of the current evidence base is the absence of prospective randomised trials adequately powered to assess clinical endpoints in conflict or resource-limited settings. The PhagoBurn trial remains the only published randomised trial and found bacterial burden reduction to be significantly slower with phage therapy than standard of care (HR 0.29, p=0.018), a result attributable to an unanticipated loss of phage titre during manufacturing rather than a failure of the therapeutic approach itself; it does not establish efficacy but does establish proof of feasibility and a favorable safety signal (fewer adverse events and septic complications than standard of care).22

Two further clinical hurdles deserve comparable weight. First, prolonged or repeated phage administration can elicit neutralizing antibody responses against phage capsid proteins, progressively reducing circulating and tissue phage titres over a treatment course and constraining the therapeutic window before adaptive immunity limits phage access to the infection site — a limitation with direct implications for the dosing schedules and treatment durations proposed elsewhere in this review.45,46 Second, achieving pharmaceutical-grade endotoxin purification outside centralized manufacturing facilities remains a significant technical barrier for field or point-of-care phage production, given that residual endotoxin, not the phage particles themselves, accounts for the mild febrile reactions reported in compassionate-use series.21

Extrapolating from compassionate-use cases to population-level recommendations requires caution, as publication bias towards positive outcomes is well recognised in the compassionate-use literature.

The highly personalised nature of phage therapy—where the optimal phage or cocktail is matched to a patient’s specific bacterial isolate—is fundamentally incompatible with the conventional parallel-group randomised controlled trial paradigm, which assumes a uniform intervention across participants. Methodological innovation is therefore essential: N-of-1 adaptive designs, platform trials with response-adaptive randomisation, and basket-type frameworks that stratify enrolment by pathogen-phage susceptibility profiles rather than anatomical site of infection offer more appropriate architectures for generating rigorous evidence.46 Concurrently, artificial intelligence and machine learning are beginning to accelerate the phage-characterisation bottleneck that currently limits clinical deployment. Graph neural networks trained on phage-host protein interaction networks can predict host-range extension with accuracy exceeding traditional sequence-homology approaches, while large-language-model-assisted genome annotation pipelines are shortening the time from environmental isolation to annotated, deployable sequence from weeks to hours.47 Integration of these computational tools into PhageSP-type production workflows could substantially compress the identification-to-administration timeline, making personalised phage therapy operationally viable even in time-critical conflict-zone scenarios.48,49

Immunocompromised patients—common in combat casualty and humanitarian emergency populations due to malnutrition, polytrauma, and concurrent infections—may experience attenuated phage-immune synergy and deserve dedicated clinical study. This review therefore supports pragmatic phage deployment for confirmed MDR infections in conflict settings while simultaneously advocating for accelerated clinical trial programmes in these environments.8,21

Conclusions

Bacteriophage therapy represents a biologically rational, mechanistically distinctive, and logistically feasible intervention for health systems besieged by MDR infections in the absence of reliable antibiotic supply chains. The self-replicating, self-dosing, locally isolatable nature of phages maps uniquely onto the constraints of conflict-zone and resource-limited medicine. The PhageSP UKR model demonstrates that the operational barriers to point-of-care phage production are surmountable with modest microbiological infrastructure, and the mechanistic evidence for phage-driven resistance reversal introduces a therapeutic dimension absent from any conventional antimicrobial.

Critical evidence gaps remain: adequately powered randomised trials in conflict settings, structured adverse-event registries, studies stratifying outcomes by immune status, and dose-finding data to prevent the PhagoBurn under-dosing phenomenon field-deployable endotoxin purification methods suitable for austere settings, and a clearer understanding of neutralizing antibody-mediated phage clearance during prolonged or repeated administration. Addressing these gaps requires targeted investment in three areas: (i) pre-positioned phage biobank networks maintained by international humanitarian health organisations; (ii) adaptive regulatory frameworks that enable compassionate and emergency use while building toward formal trial data; and (iii) international consensus protocols for point-of-care phage production quality assurance in austere settings.

The burden of proof for pragmatic deployment in the context of otherwise untreatable infections with high immediate mortality should not require the same evidentiary threshold as prophylactic use in the general population. The existing evidence base— spanning basic mechanistic science, compassionate-use clinical data, decades of Eastern European programme experience, and the PhageSP UKR conflict-zone model— is sufficient to justify carefully monitored, protocol-governed deployment under humanitarian use provisions while the formal trial evidence continues to accrue.
