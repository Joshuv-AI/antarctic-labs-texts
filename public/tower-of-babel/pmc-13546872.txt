Heart Transplant in LATAM: experience, outcomes and perspectives
Authors: Lorena Montes, María Alejandra Gómez-Gutiérrez, Monica Gilbert, Fabio Gaiotto, Martín Antelo, Franz Soplopuco, Daniel Absi, Luis Eduardo Echeverria, Gustavo Prieto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart failure is a global health challenge with significant impacts on quality of life and healthcare systems. Heart transplantation remains the gold standard for select patients with end-stage heart failure, yet access in Latin America is limited by economic constraints, systemic barriers, and inequities in infrastructure. Despite contributing less than 7% of global Heart transplant procedures, LATAM has made important strides, with countries like Argentina demonstrating success in increasing donor rates through presumed consent legislation. However, disparities in donor availability, centralized heart transplant centers, and survival outcomes persist. This article reviews LATAM's historical contributions, current state, and challenges in heart transplant, identifying critical barriers such as the absence of unified transplant registries, limited access to advanced technologies like LVADs and ECMO, and a shortage of specialized training programs. Cultural attitudes and misconceptions surrounding organ donation further compound these issues. Solutions discussed include establishing a regional transplant registry to improve data transparency, leveraging regional collaboration to expand donor pools, and implementing public awareness campaigns to address cultural barriers. With concerted efforts in education, legislation, cost reduction, and technology integration, LATAM has the potential to significantly improve heart transplant access and outcomes. This transformation would save countless lives and position the region as a key contributor to global advancements in heart transplant.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is recognized as a worldwide public health epidemic with an estimated prevalence of 64 million people globally, accounting for 9.91 million years lost due to disability (YLDs).1, 2. It carries severe implications on patients' quality of life, leading to recurrent hospital admissions and functional impairment, often preventing individuals from maintaining productive lives. The global economic burden of HF is estimated at $346 billion annually,1 with the United States alone incurring over $40 billion in direct healthcare costs, primarily driven by hospital admissions.3 Similar economic burdens are reported across Europe, highlighting the importance of effective management strategies to alleviate this strain.1, 4

The primary etiologies of HF include ischemic heart disease and arterial hypertension, and the condition is associated with five-year mortality rates as high as 56%.5 Heart transplantation (HTx) continues to be regarded as the optimal treatment for select patients with end-stage heart failure, offering the potential for improved survival and quality of life.6 Advances in nearly every aspect of HTx, including improved candidate selection and reduced complications, have substantially enhanced outcomes. By one to three years post-transplant, functional status remains high, with approximately 90% of survivors able to engage in normal daily activities.7

Despite this, access to HTx is unevenly distributed, particularly between developed and developing regions. Latin America (LATAM), home to over 652 million people spanning 20 countries and 13% of the Earth’s surface, faces significant challenges in achieving equitable access to advanced cardiovascular care. Many countries in LATAM are classified as low- or middle-income economies, limiting their ability to develop and sustain robust transplant programs.8 According to the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT), LATAM contributes less than 7% of the global HTx volume, with wealthier countries in the region performing the majority of transplants while others struggle to build and sustain viable transplant programs.9 Closing these gaps will require strategic initiatives aimed at improving healthcare financing, optimizing resource allocation, and fostering regional collaboration. By addressing these challenges, LATAM could expand its contribution to the global HTx landscape and ensure more equitable access to life-saving therapies across the region.10

Despite the growing number of HTx in LATAM, we still face important obstacles. Currently, the region lacks a unified transplant registry. While we often look to countries with extensive transplant experience for guidance, their contexts and challenges vary significantly from ours. In order to improve, we need to know where we stand. This article aims to fill this gap by providing a comprehensive overview of the LATAM HTx experience and hopes to encourage the creation of an official network and registry.

Methods

Data for this study were obtained from multiple complementary sources. Between January 2024 to december 2025, information was collected through direct communication with cardiac surgeons, cardiologists, transplant coordinators, and medical directors from heart transplant centers across Latin America. Communications were conducted by telephone, email, and virtual meetings. All unpublished institutional data included in this report were shared with the permission of the corresponding centers, as many of these data are not available through official public registries or published sources.

Additional information was obtained from publicly available reports issued by national transplant organizations and governmental transplant agencies, including those from Argentina, Brazil, and Colombia. A structured literature search was also performed using PubMed and the Cochrane Library to identify publications reporting heart transplantation activity in Latin America. However, published literature on this topic remains scarce.

Historical perspective

The first interhuman HTx was performed by Christiaan Barnard on December 3, 1967 in Cape Town, South Africa and the news quickly spread in international media.11 LATAM soon joined the pioneering efforts. The very next day, in Brazil, Dr. Euryclides de Jesus Zerbini began preparations for cardiac transplantation. He was able to perform the first HTx in LATAM on March 26, 1968. The patient survived for 28 days but unfortunately died from refractory acute rejection. Dr. Zerbini went on to perform transplants on two other patients: one that survived for over a year, while the other lived for 60 days.11

The LATAM journey continued with Dr Miguel Bellizi in Buenos Aires, Argentina. He was able to perform two HTx, the first one in May/ 1968 to a patient that survived for only 4 days. The second HTx patient died in the immediate postoperative period. Bellizi faced harsh criticism arguing the infrastructure and training was insufficient to support such procedures.12 Later, in June of 1968, Dr Jorge Kaplan Meyer from Chile performed the third HTx in LATAM.13 By the end of 1968, 102 HTx had been performed worldwide.5

In the 1970s, enthusiasm started to fade as surgeons faced high organ rejection rates and early mortality. However, in 1980, the introduction of cyclosporine as an immunosuppressant effective at avoiding chronic rejection, along with the use of endomyocardial biopsy for early diagnosis, reignited interest in the procedure.5, 6 This revival also extended to LATAM, where transplant programs were reactivated, and the first HTx were performed in countries such as Colombia14 and Mexico.5 Even with these advances, in 1990, the number of HTx performed around the world stabilized due to limited availability of donor hearts.15 (Figure 1).Figure 1Timeline of HxTx in LATAM.

Summary

Consent legislation should therefore be understood as an enabling framework rather than a determinant of donation performance. Countries with comparable legal models show markedly different donation rates, indicating that outcomes depend on context as well as the organizational infrastructure built around the law: systematic identification and referral of potential donors in intensive care units, availability of trained in-hospital donor coordinators, protocolized brain death diagnosis and donor management, continuous audit of the donation process, and sustained public trust in the health system and in equitable organ allocation. The Brazilian experience illustrates this point, as the reversal of presumed consent followed public mistrust and operational difficulties rather than a failure of the legal principle itself. For LATAM, this implies that legislative reform should be accompanied by investment in donor coordination networks, ICU donation pathways, and transparent communication strategies; otherwise, opt-out legislation risks remaining a formal provision with limited practical impact.

Donor characteristics

While donation after brain death (DBD) remains the main source for HTx, donation after circulatory death (DCD) is gaining global traction and has the potential to substantially expand the donor pool.33 Successful implementation in countries like the UK and Australia, where DCD accounts for up to 30% of HTx, has demonstrated comparable outcomes to DBD, supported by optimized retrieval and preservation protocols.34, 35

In LATAM, early efforts to implement DCD protocols initially focused on non-cardiac organs, particularly kidneys, with structured programs based on Maastricht Category III (controlled asystole) emerging in Argentina.36, 37 Recently, this landscape changed with the first reported pediatric heart transplantation using controlled DCD at the Hospital Italiano de Buenos Aires, and the first in adults in Hospital Cordoba, in Cordoba, marking the initial experience of cardiac DCD transplantation in the region and signaling a shift in donor utilization strategies.38

In response to donor scarcity and limited access to cutting-edge technologies, transplant teams in LATAM have developed creative, pragmatic approaches to expand donor eligibility. Teams from the region have pioneered pragmatic approaches to maximize available resources. One notable example is the use of extended-criteria donors, including a reported case in Colombia where a donor with moderate aortic stenosis was accepted. To address the valvular disease, the transplant team performed a bench aortic valve replacement using a fresh pulmonary homograft from the explanted heart of the recipient, a technique inspired by the Ross procedure39 and a case at another center in Colombia where a donor with severe aortic stenosis received a bioprosthetic valve before procurement.40 These examples reflect the resourcefulness and clinical courage found in the region.

Situation to transplant

Waiting list mortality

Given the challenges surrounding HTx in these countries, it is crucial to address the mortality rates on the waiting list (WL), calculated here based on the total number of patients ever active on the WL in 2021 for each country. In the United States, during 2021, there were 7659 patients ever active on the WL, with 248 deaths, resulting in a mortality rate of 3.2%.41 For the same year, Eurotransplant reported the following mortality rates among its member countries: Austria had a rate of 5.6%, followed by Belgium at 8.3%. Croatia, Hungary, Luxembourg, and Slovenia reported no deaths on the WL (0.0%). Germany recorded the highest mortality rate at 14.3%, while the Netherlands reported a rate of 6.3%.42

In LATAM, Argentina reported 31 deaths out of 309 patients (10.0%), while Brazil recorded 106 deaths among 821 patients (12.9%). In Chile, 11 of the 43 patients died (25.6%), and Colombia reported 6 deaths out of 124 patients (4.8%). Cuba had 2 deaths among its 6 patients on the WL (33.3%), the highest mortality rate, while Ecuador recorded no deaths among its 6 patients. Mexico reported 4 deaths out of 87 patients (4.6%), Paraguay had 4 deaths out of 17 patients (23.5%), and Uruguay reported 10 deaths out of 68 patients (14.7%)41 These disparities likely reflect unequal access to bridging therapies like left ventricular assist devices (LVADs), delayed referrals, geographic inaccessibility, and systemic barriers to timely care.

Recipient characteristics

HF in LATAM presents distinct challenges shaped by systemic barriers and population-specific factors. Access to guideline-recommended pharmacological therapies is often limited by sociopolitical and economic constraints, increasing the risk of progression to advanced stages.43 Additionally, unique regional etiologies require tailored management strategies to optimize transplant outcomes.

Due to the global shortage of donors, long-term circulatory support has emerged to bridge patients to transplantation, guide decision-making, or serve as destination therapy. According to the ISHLT registry, use of LVADs as a bridge to HTx now exceeds 50% worldwide.10 Advances in durable and reliable mechanical circulatory support allow these devices to extend patients’ survival time as they await a HTx.43

Despite technological progress, financial and logistical barriers in LATAM limit access to LVADs, depriving many patients of therapies that could extend survival and enable transplantation. Although all countries with active HTx programs have access to ECMO, Ecuador and Uruguay depend solely on it, lacking other temporary or long-term support devices. The Berlin Heart X-Core is accessible in Brazil, Argentina, and Paraguay. The CentriMag, a widely used temporary mechanical circulatory support device, is available in Colombia, Chile, Argentina, Mexico, Brazil, Peru, and Costa Rica. The Impella device, though effective, is only accessible in Brazil, Panama, Chile, and Venezuela, and its use is limited by its cost and strict indications. The HeartMate 3 provides essential long-term support for advanced heart failure and is available in Colombia, Chile, Argentina, Mexico, Brazil, Perú and Costa Rica. However, its use is limited by high upfront costs that exceed $150,000 USD,43 ongoing maintenance requirements, and the need for a specialized team for implantation and long-term management. Recently a new durable LVAD the CorHeart 6 was implanted in Colombia, as the first in the Americas.44

Regarding the criticality of recipients before transplant, with direct communication with cardiologists, Cardiovascular surgeons and ICU specialists from each country and analyzing their data, we could establish the following data: 100% of reported cases in Panamá were classified as urgent. Chile had 49% categorized as urgent/emergent, while Ecuador, Colombia, and Argentina reported 70%, 68%, and 71% of patients in the same categories, respectively. Additionally, the data on patients bridged with mechanical circulatory support revealed the average duration in countries where this information was available. Argentina reported an average of 12 days on Short-Term Ventricular Assist Device-Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (sVAD-ECMO); Colombia had an average of 30 days on sVAD-ECMO; Perú reported 20 days on sVAD-ECMO; and Chile averaged 40 days on sVAD-ECMO.

A distinctive feature of HF in LATAM is the high burden of Chagas disease, with an estimated 6 million infected and 30,000 new cases annually.45, 46, 47. Despite consistently poorer outcomes, no pharmacological trials have been specifically designed for this population, as most studies exclude Chagas patients.48, 49 Clinical management is further complicated by arrhythmias, myocarditis, RV (Right Ventricular) dysfunction, and fibrosis, making HTx the only accepted surgical option for refractory cases. While Trypanosoma cruzi reactivation remains a concern, HTx has demonstrated favorable long-term survival outcomes in patients with Chagas cardiomyopathy.46 The lack of targeted trials highlights an urgent research gap requiring region-specific evidence.

Despite these results, long-term post-transplant care remains a major barrier to success in LATAM. Sustaining graft function depends on lifelong adherence to immunosuppressive therapy and consistent access to medications. However, coverage is often limited, supply chains are fragile, and geographic disparities persist, particularly in public health systems54 This inconsistent access increases the risk of rejection and graft failure, disproportionately affecting socioeconomically vulnerable and rural populations.54, 55, 56

Adherence to immunosuppression is similarly influenced by a range of modifiable factors. A systematic review in adult HTx recipients identified psychological comorbidities, lack of social support, and complex dosing regimens as key predictors of nonadherence, which in turn correlated with increased rates of acute rejection, coronary allograft vasculopathy, and late mortality55 These findings highlight the importance of developing region-specific strategies that ensure consistent access to medications, strengthen patient education, simplify treatment regimens, and provide long-term psychosocial and logistical support.

Challenges

Organ shortage

The shortage of organs for donation is a global challenge. The number of individuals awaiting transplants continues to rise, surpassing available supply and resulting in prolonged waiting periods, often with fatal consequences for some patients.21, 57 In LATAM, the problem is further aggravated by legislative variability regarding donor consent, limited healthcare coverage, unequal access to technology, and logistical barriers such as geographic isolation and transportation difficulties.

Cultural beliefs and widespread misconceptions continue to hinder public acceptance of organ donation in LATAM, including misleading interpretations about circulatory and brain death, fears of body disfigurement, and different religious beliefs, despite the endorsement of almost all religions on donation, as an altruistic act. These attitudes are reinforced by distrust in the healthcare system and sensationalized media portrayals of organ trafficking. Addressing these barriers through culturally sensitive education and myth-busting communication is essential to improve donor registration and acceptance.58, 59, 60 Limited awareness of how to register as an organ donor further exacerbates the shortage in LATAM.61 Even among those willing to donate, unclear guidance on the process creates a gap between intention and action. Public health campaigns that offer accessible, step-by-step information are key to translating support into actual donor registration

Health systems

The diverse and fragmented healthcare systems across LATAM pose significant challenges to collaboration and standardization in transplant protocols. Political instability in the region often goes hand in hand with inconsistent healthcare policies, leading to unpredictable coverage and access to care. Delays in reimbursing healthcare providers exacerbate challenges in the procurement and distribution of high-cost medications, which are essential both for optimizing pre-transplant patient conditions and for managing post-transplant care.62 For example, in Ecuador, critical immunosuppressive drugs are currently facing distribution delays, which complicates post-transplant care and patient outcomes.63

Centralization

Advanced HF programs in LATAM are highly centralized, with most transplant centers and key diagnostic services located in major urban areas. While this facilitates specialized care, it creates major barriers for patients in rural or remote regions, where long travel distances and limited transportation access delay timely evaluation and treatment. Critical support services, such as immunology laboratories for compatibility testing, are also centralized and may take weeks to process results, further slowing transplant workflows. Addressing these issues requires improving regional access without dispersing the technical expertise on which transplant outcomes depend.62

Accordingly, centralization of surgical activity and decentralization of access are complementary rather than opposing strategies. Consolidating transplant procedures in high-volume centers improves outcomes through standardization, concentrated expertise, multidisciplinary team continuity, and optimized use of scarce resources. What must be decentralized is not the operation itself but the pathway around it: early identification of patients with advanced heart failure, initial evaluation and timely referral from regional cardiology services, shared post-transplant follow-up through structured co-management protocols with local teams, and locally accessible immunosuppression monitoring and drug dispensing. Such a model would allow LATAM to concentrate surgical experience in a limited number of centers while distributing evaluation and follow-up across a broader network of regional institutions, reducing the geographical and socioeconomic barriers that currently determine which patients reach the waiting list. Telemedicine, standardized referral criteria, and formal agreements between transplant centers and regional hospitals are the practical instruments to build such networks, and cross-border collaboration between countries with low transplant volumes and established regional centers could extend the same logic beyond national borders.

Costs and access to technology

The high cost of advanced medical technologies remains a major barrier for HTx programs in LATAM. Devices like LVADs and ECMO are often unaffordable for many institutions, limiting their availability. These technologies also reach the region with significant delays, leaving programs reliant on outdated or less effective equipment. This combination of high cost and limited access contributes to stark disparities in the use of modern circulatory support systems.43 Also new technologies such as organ ex vivo preservation, are nowadays currently unavailable in most countries due to economic and logistical constraints, leaving our donor pool expansion very limited.

Training

Cardiovascular institutions equipped with the infrastructure and specialized personnel required to support HTx teams and manage LVADs are scarce in LATAM. The lack of specialized training centers creates a significant reliance on international education, which involves substantial costs and logistical hurdles. Beyond surgical skills, training requires a comprehensive approach, including the preparation of multidisciplinary teams such as cardiologists and heart failure specialists. However, financial constraints, travel requirements, and language barriers limit access to LATAM health professionals. This disparity in training directly affects the quality and sustainability of transplant programs across LATAM.5, 43, 46 Establishing regional training initiatives and fostering local collaborations are essential steps to address this issue effectively.

Registry and reporting

As previously mentioned, LATAM lacks a unified registry for HTx, and reporting is not mandatory. Unfortunately, there appears to be a lack of commitment to HTx data collection. For instance, in Argentina, 21 of 27 transplant centers (78%) report their data, while in Chile, none of the 8 centers (0%) currently report to the ISHLT. In Brazil, only 6 out of 38 centers (16%) submit data, and in Colombia, just 2 of 9 centers (22%) report. In Peru, 1 out of 3 centers (33%) provides information. This significant gap in data reporting to the ISHLT undermines efforts to accurately assess and improve transplant outcomes across the region. Moreover, in large global registries, LATAM is often categorized as "other," grouped together rather than being recognized as a distinct region.64., 65 In contrast, Europe benefits from cross-border transplant networks that allow for organ sharing between countries. LATAM lacks a comparable system. Base on the findings of this review, we emphasize the urgent need for a coordinated regional strategy to improve data collection and reporting across Latin America. Rather than establishing an entirely independent registry, a pragmatic and sustainable approach may be to strengthen collaboration among national transplant societies, transplant centers, governmental agencies, and regional scientific organizations while promoting harmonization with internationally recognized standards, such as those adopted by the ISHLT Registry. Such collaboration would facilitate standardized data collection, enable benchmarking across countries and with international programs, improve research capacity, and provide a stronger evidence base to guide healthcare policies and resource allocation. Ultimately, developing a collaborative regional network would not only enhance the visibility of heart transplantation in Latin America but also foster continuous quality improvement and greater integration of the region into the global transplant community.

Future perspectives

HTx in LATAM tells a story of resilience and progress, but it is also a reminder of how far we still need to go. The challenges are multifaceted, but so are the opportunities. Moving forward requires us to take an honest look at the gaps mentioned before and address them with practical, region-specific solutions.

Efforts to improve access to technology, develop specialized training, and promote culturally relevant public education all converge on a shared objective: improving survival and long-term outcomes for patients with advanced heart failure. Rather than replicating models from other regions, LATAM must define its own approach, grounded in local realities, clinical expertise, and institutional collaboration. The necessary components already exist in many countries.

In our opinion, the most transformative step would be creating a unified LATAM transplant registry. Such a system would enable better data collection, facilitate policy development, and allow for outcome benchmarking across the region. It would also serve as the foundation for future inter-country organ sharing and research collaboration, giving LATAM a stronger voice in global transplant science. With sustained efforts across education, legislation, infrastructure, training, technology, and regional alignment, LATAM can move towards a more just and effective transplant ecosystem, one where access is no longer a privilege, but a standard of care.
