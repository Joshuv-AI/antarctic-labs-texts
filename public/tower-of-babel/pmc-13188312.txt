Ensuring flexible optical intubation skills using a portable virtual reality simulator – a validation study
Authors: Mingjuan Sun, Xiaomei Guo, Qiong Qin, Huimin Liu, Hongyan Fang, Chengyun Wu, Zheng Wang, Lars Konge, Rong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnesthesiologists must be able to perform flexible optical intubation (FOI) in challenging cases, but the skill is difficult to master and cannot be adequately taught using the traditional apprenticeship model. Mastery learning simulation-based training ensures that all trainees acquire basic competence in a safe environment before progressing to supervised performance on patients. However, mastery learning training programs require a simulation-based test with strong validity evidence and a defensible pass/fail standard.MethodsWe developed a FOI test on a portable virtual reality (VR) flexible bronchoscopy simulator, consisting of 12 challenging cases, and administered the test to 15 novices and 15 experienced anesthesiologists. Performance metrics were automatically recorded by the simulator, and all five sources in Messick’s contemporary validity evidence framework were explored.ResultsInternal consistency reliability was Cronbach’s α = 0.89. Generalizability theory analyses found that six cases were required for formative assessment (reliability > 0.7) and 10 cases were required for summative assessment (reliability > 0.8). An independent samples t-test showed that the test could significantly discriminate between novices and experienced anesthesiologists, p < 0.001. A credible pass/fail standard of 34.4% was established using the contrasting groups’ standard setting method. This standard resulted in two false positives (i.e., novices that passed the test) and one false negative (i.e., experienced that failed the test).ConclusionThe simulation-based FOI test based on 12 challenging adult airway cases on a VR portable flexible bronchoscopy simulator demonstrated strong validity evidence. The test can be used for both formative (i.e., feedback) and summative (i.e., certification) assessment and thereby support a mastery learning simulation-based training program for FOI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03809-9.Keywords: Flexible Optical Intubation (FOI), Virtual Reality Simulator, Mastery Learning, Validity Evidence, Skill Assessment

Introduction

Flexible optical intubation (FOI) is a very useful procedure in case of difficult airways where traditional intubation using a laryngoscope is dangerous or not possible [1, 2]. FOI is often done in time-critical situations where creating a secure airway is top priority [3]. However, mastering the procedure can be challenging, particularly in high-pressure situations [4]. Traditionally, training followed the apprenticeship model where a resident performs the procedure supervised by a consultant [4, 5]. Unfortunately, this traditional approach can put the patients at risk because they end up bearing the burden of the trainees’ learning curve.

Several studies have shown that simulation-based training (SBT) of FOI offers a patient-safe and relatively stress-free learning environment [6–8]. A challenge in SBT is that all trainees learn at different paces making it impossible to ensure that everybody is competent after a training course with a fixed duration. Mastery learning solves this by requiring trainees to keep practicing until they reach a predefined level of competence [9, 10].

Supervisors are traditionally needed to assess competency levels of their trainees, but they are often biased and can be too busy for repeated assessment in a mastery learning training program. Virtual reality (VR) simulators provide automatic quality metrics that can be used for feedback during training, but we cannot automatically assume that these metrics are good enough to ensure end-of-training proficiency [11]. A standardized test with evidence of validity including a credible pass/fail standard is a prerequisite for setting up a mastery learning training program [12].

The aim of the study was to develop and gather validity evidence for a FOI test based on a portable VR simulator and to establish a credible pass/fail criterion.

Materials and methods

The development of and the administration of the test were done in the Comprehensive Simulation Experiment Center of Clinical Skills of Yunnan University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Kunming, China. Validity evidence was gathered for all five sources in Messick’s contemporary framework of validity, i.e., content, response process, internal structure, relationship to other variables, and consequences [13].

Development of the exam

The ORSIM bronchoscopy simulator was used for testing. It consists of three main components: a replica video bronchoscope, a desktop sensor that registers all movements of the bronchoscope inside the simulated airways, and a dedicated laptop with software that generates the virtual airways of the different included virtual patients. The user can progress through a range of upper and lower airway scenarios receiving instant feedback as well as scores generated from built-in metrics. The simulator also allows recording and play-back. For the study, all 12 cases in the adult upper airway disorders were included: airway trauma, angioneurotic oedema, carcinoma of the tongue, distorted larynx, epigenetic cyst, false cord cyst, gross papilloma, macroglossia, retropharyngeal abscess, severe epiglottitis, unexpected difficult airways, and vestibular lesion. The inclusion of all 12 scenarios allowed us to determine the difficulty and discriminatory ability of each scenario and use these empiric data to exclude scenarios that were too easy, too difficult, or without discriminatory ability.

Three experts performed a pilot test where they completed all 12 cases. Based on their performances, it was determined that the maximum time allowed for each case in the exam is 7 min.

Gathering of validity evidence

Two groups were invited to participate in the study. Convenience sampling was used: Novices were residents from different departments at the First Affiliated Hospital of Yunnan University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, who were familiar with standard intubation, but had no prior experience with FOI or flexible bronchoscopy. Experienced participants were specialists in anesthesiology from different hospitals in Kunming, who had performed at least 100 FOIs independently. A questionnaire regarding basic demographic data and experience was used in the recruitment process (Gender, age in years, years of experience as doctor, number of intubations performed (approx.), number of FOI performed (had to be zero for novices and 100 + for experienced), and number of flexible bronchoscopies performed). (Appendix 1) Group sizes of more than 10 are necessary to assume normal distribution of data in educational studies and allow us to use the statistical methods needed [14]. We chose to include 15 participants in each group to have reasonable statistical power while ensuring that the final test would be clinically and practically relevant for future testing of FOI competence. Our sample size (n = 30) was aligned with current practices in the validation field where a systematic review of 498 validity studies in surgical simulation found a median sample size of 28 participants [15]. Participants all signed informed consent and were informed of the scoring method for each case.

Before the exam, participants watched a short, pre-recorded video. The video was recorded for the purpose of demonstrating how to use the simulator, focusing on showing how to hold and operate the scope, and demonstrating one procedure. After watching the video, participants were allowed up to five minutes to be familiarized with the simulator using a warm-up case (not included in the test) for a maximum of 5 min.

All participants had to complete a FOI in all 12 scenarios according to the order of cases shown on the simulator. During the exam, a technician, who was knowledgeable about the simulator but was not trained in clinical medicine, was on site to assist with selecting scenarios. The technician provided technical support if needed but offered no assistance or advice regarding performing the FOIs (Fig. 1).

One of the participants performing a simulated flexible optical intubation on the virtual reality simulator. The research assistant in the picture prepared each case on the simulator and noted the automatically generated metrics, but did not offer any help or guidance during the test

The study intended to explore the validity of the ORSIM bronchoscopy simulator’s built-in automated grading system, so it used metrics recorded by the simulator during participants’ FOI attempts as primary outcome measures. These metrics include Procedure duration in seconds, Collisions Avoidance in percentage, and Final score in percentage.

Statistical analysis

An item analysis was done to explore the difficulty and the discriminative abilities of each of the 12 cases.

Validity evidence for internal structure: Internal consistency reliability was calculated as Cronbach’s alpha across all 12 cases. Generalizability theory was used to perform a G-study and 12 Decision (D) studies to explore the test’s absolute measures reliability when based on 1 to 12 cases.

Validity evidence for relationship to other variables: The discriminative ability for each of the 12 cases was explored by independent samples t-tests. A repeated measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the total test results of novices and experienced.

Validity evidence for consequences: The contrasting groups’ standard setting method was used to establish a credible pass/fail score for the total test score [16]. The means and standard deviations of the two groups are plotted and the intersection point marks the pass/fail standard. The consequences of this standard were explored by counting the number of false negative (i.e., experienced failing the test) and false positives (i.e., novices passing the test). Finally, the pass/fail rates of the two groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test.

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 29.0.1.0 and generalizability studies were done using the G-String IV software. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

We developed a test of FOI skills based on a portable VR simulator, and established solid validity evidence including a credible pass/fail standard by administering the test to 15 novices and 15 experienced anesthesiologists.

The ability to perform FOI in case of difficult airways is an essential skill in clinical practice, however, existing research investigating the extent of FOI training has found that, while a vast majority of participating residents (97%) acknowledge its importance, only a small proportion of participants believe that opportunities to practice FOI are adequate (7%) [17]. A possible solution to this problem is simulation-based training that can improve trainees’ proficiency of clinical skills, thereby reducing medical errors. The effectiveness of simulation-based training is not only reflected in FOI training, but also in gastroscopy, chest tube insertion, epidural anesthesia, etc [18–20]. To ensure the quality of training, standardized tests with solid evidence of validity, including credible pass/fail standards, is a prerequisite for establishing mastery-learning training programs [10]. The findings of this study can be used to provide guidance for FOI testing, and to provide a marker of competency for any simulation-based FOI training course, in order to help ensure adequate training outcomes [21].

VR simulators often include many different patient cases in their software, and it can be difficult to provide solid arguments for which cases to include in a test, i.e., validity of evidence for content. We chose to explore all 12 difficult airway cases on the ORSIM portable bronchoscopy simulator allowing us to base final selection on empiric data from 30 participants. Surprisingly, all 12 cases demonstrated adequate difficulty and good discriminatory ability (Table 2) meaning that they can all be used for future training and testing. For practical reasons, it is possible to shorten the test to six cases (feedback, formative assessment) or 10 cases (end-of-training testing, summative assessment) while maintaining adequate reliability i.e., validity evidence for internal structure. As shown in Table 2, the difficulty levels of the 12 cases vary greatly, and this information can be used to plan future training programs. Trainees could be instructed to start with easier cases and advance to more difficult as they proceed along their learning curve [21].

A test’s ability to discriminate between participants with different levels of experience is an important part of the validity argument i.e., validity evidence for relationship to other variables. Our experienced participants performed better than novices in all cases which is seldom the cases in simulation-based validity studies where it is often necessary to exclude some cases and simulator metrics [22, 23].

All cases and metrics in the simulator were effective for testing FOI skills in difficult airways. However, the pass/fail standard of 34.4% still resulted in a false negative (i.e., an experienced anesthesiologist that failed the test) and two false positives (i.e., two novices that passed the test). This may be related to how the simulator is designed to recognize a specific, fixed pathway. Experienced participants can complete an FOI through multiple pathways in clinical practice, such as from the left or right sides, or through the middle or lower parts, of the epiglottis. They presume that the technique that leads to a successful FOI in a clinical setting would be correct on a simulator; therefore, when they go off a pathway outside of the simulator’s pre-defined pathway, they do not attempt correcting because the pathway of their selection would be acceptable in clinical practice. In contrast, novices do not have experiences to serve as reference points, and for this reason, they rely on feedback they receive from the simulator to find the pathway, leading to false positives where a participant ends up receiving scores not corresponding to his or her actual technical competency, by figuring out how to “beat the simulator” instead of understanding how to pass through anatomical features.

We believe that this phenomenon could allude to future upgrades for simulation-based FOI training. For example, a larger variety of pathways which would lead to successful FOIs should be included. Moreover, evaluation could place more emphasis on the quality and thoughtfulness of decisions, instead of sole reliance on proximity to a pre-defined pathway.

At the same time, it must be recognized that maneuvering the flexible scope to the trachea is not the only skill required to master FOI. Anesthesiologists also need to assess the airway, decide the most suitable tracheal intubation method according to airway and patient condition, and use the correct angle and amount of force during the procedure [24]. Ideally, trainees can further practice these important skills after learning how to use the bronchoscope, and demonstrating their basic skills on this validated test.

One limitation of our study is that the sample size was limited to 30 participants. Although the study was sufficient to produce statistically significant results, we need to acknowledge that developing a test with sufficient validity evidence is only the first, albeit necessary, step to FOI training and full mastery of the technique. Future research must be done to show that standardized simulation-based training programs transfer to improved clinical performance and reduce adverse consequences in trainees’ early clinical practice.

Researchers have shown that simulation-based training improve the trainees’ FOI skills [7, 25]. However, there is a lack of a comprehensive overview of evidence on FOI skills training and assessment [4, 26], and there is no evidence-based consensus on educational strategies which sheds lights on an important gap in airway management training. This study does not explore the efficacy of FOI training, but the standardized and valid end-of-training test can none-the-less help fill the gap by ensuring that all trainees have basic FOI skills after finishing a simulation-based training program that has not been fully scientifically explored yet.

Conclusions

A newly developed test consisting of all 12 cases in adult difficult airways on the ORSIM portable VR simulator has solid evidence of evidence. Performance of six cases can provide reliable feedback to the trainee and credible certification can be based on 10–12 cases. The current test can be used to establish simulation-based training curricula following the recommended mastery-learning approach where all individual trainees demonstrate basic skills on a simulator before proceeding to supervised training on patients.
