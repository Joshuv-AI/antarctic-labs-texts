Ozone-modified potato starch as a gelatin alternative in dairy desserts: Optimization and evaluation of textural, sensory, and rheological properties
Authors: Akram Khezri, Mohammad Ali Sahari, Mohsen Barzegar, Hassan Ahmadi Gavlighi, Mohsen Asghari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gelatin is widely used in dairy desserts; however, plant-based alternatives are increasingly gaining popularity. In this study, potato starch was modified using ozone as an environmentally friendly oxidizing agent. The modification was combined with thermal preheating and controlled starch concentration, and optimization was performed using response surface methodology (RSM) to obtain an optimized starch sample with minimum syneresis and maximum hardness. Subsequently, the complete replacement of gelatin with ozone-modified starch (MS), in combination with κ-carrageenan (C), was investigated in a semi-solid dairy dessert. Different formulations containing MS (0.25–1.25%) and C (0.5–1.0%) were evaluated in terms of color, texture, syneresis, sensory attributes, and rheological properties. The optimized sample (MS 1.25%–C 0.5%) exhibited a hardness of 0.477 N, cohesiveness of 0.65, and hydrogel strength of 0.33 N, closely resembling the gelatin control. Color differences (ΔE) ranged from 29.64 to 31.40, with slightly lower L∗ values than the control (72.57). Increasing MS concentration reduced syneresis, making its behavior similar to the control (3.79%). Sensory evaluation indicated comparable flavor among all samples, with only minor differences in texture and color. Rheological measurements showed that the optimized dessert exhibited higher storage modulus (G′ ≈ 615 Pa) and loss modulus (G″ ≈ 75 Pa) than the control, predominantly elastic behavior (G′ > G″), and pseudoplastic viscosity with controlled shear-thinning behavior. These findings demonstrate that the combination of MS and C can serve as an effective and environmentally friendly alternative to gelatin, producing a stable, elastic, and sensorially acceptable dairy dessert.

Introduction

Desserts, particularly milk-based products, are highly popular among different age groups due to their nutritional properties (Lin et al., 2025). Typically, dairy desserts are composed of milk, sweeteners, and gelling agents of either animal or plant origin. Among these agents, gelatin is one of the most widely used hydrocolloids in the food industry owing to its unique functional properties. However, religious restrictions, as well as vegetarian and vegan dietary preferences, have limited its use in certain populations, prompting researchers to explore alternative gelling agents (Mahamud et al., 2023; Said, 2020).

Carrageenan, a sulfated polysaccharide extracted from red seaweed, is one such alternative. It forms a reversible gel in hot water. Different types of carrageenan generate various textures: κ-carrageenan typically forms a firm and brittle gel, whereas ι-carrageenan produces a soft and elastic gel (Al-Baarri et al., 2018). In warm solutions, κ-carrageenan chains exist in a random-coil conformation; upon cooling, they transform into an ordered double-helix structure, which subsequently aggregates to form a three-dimensional network. This network is stabilized by positively charged ions such as K+ or Ca2+, which act as cross-linking bridges between the negatively charged sulfate groups. In dairy systems, calcium ions (Ca2+) naturally present in milk serve as ionic bridges, promoting gel network formation. In dairy desserts, κ-carrageenan is particularly effective due to its electrostatic interactions with casein, whereby its negatively charged sulfate groups interact with positively charged casein proteins (Geonzon et al., 2020).

Another commonly used hydrocolloid in dairy desserts is starch, which is valued for its role in gel formation and texture enhancement. Starch gelatinizes at approximately 60 °C and subsequently forms a gel through retrogradation (He et al., 2023). Among various starch sources, potato starch is preferred because of its superior gelation capacity, high clarity, and compatibility with colloidal systems (Lin et al., 2025; Park et al., 2024). However, native potato starch exhibits several drawbacks, including low thermal stability, reduced clarity compared with modified starches, and poor water-holding capacity, which results in increased syneresis. Therefore, starch modification is necessary to improve its functionality. Various modification techniques are employed to enhance starch properties, including physical methods (e.g., heating, microwave, and ultrasound treatments), chemical processes (e.g., oxidation, cross-linking, and etherification), enzymatic treatments, and genetic approaches (He et al., 2023).

Among these methods, oxidation is particularly effective because it converts hydroxyl groups into carbonyl and carboxyl groups, thereby increasing starch polarity and hydrophilicity. This modification enhances water-retention capacity and reduces syneresis. Furthermore, oxidation improves the mechanical strength of starch hydrogels, making it a promising approach for starches intended for use in dairy desserts (Khezri et al., 2025). In this context, ozone oxidation has gained attention as a novel, environmentally friendly process that does not require chemical reagents (Satmalawati et al., 2020).

The present study aimed to optimize ozone-modified potato starch to achieve maximum hydrogel strength and minimum syneresis by considering three key variables: preheating time, starch concentration, and ozonation duration, using RSM. The optimized starch was subsequently incorporated with different levels of κ-carrageenan in a dairy dessert formulation as a potential complete substitute for gelatin.

Materials and methods

Materials

Native potato starch was sourced from Alvand Starch Industries. The starch, derived from the Agria cultivar, was characterized by a moisture content of 15.6%, amylose content of 24%, purity of 99.49%, total ash content of 0.3% (dry matter basis), negligible total fiber and lipid contents, and protein content of 0.21%. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that the hydrodynamic radius of amylose ranged from 30 to 50 nm, whereas that of amylopectin ranged from 100 to 150 nm. Gelatin (bloom strength 240) was procured from Sahand Tabriz Company, κ-carrageenan (E407; GPI, Canada) was used as received, pasteurized milk with 3% fat content was obtained from Pegah Tehran Company, and both sugar and vanilla were purchased from the local market.

Starch preparation and modification

Potato starch was thermally pretreated at 130 °C and subsequently ozonated in a cylindrical glass reactor using an Ozonica Series OZ-50 ozone generator. Oxygen (≈99% purity) was supplied at a flow rate of 2 L/min to generate ozone, and starch slurries were treated under previously optimized conditions, as described in our earlier study (Khezri et al., 2025). After treatment, the samples were decanted, dried in an air-circulating oven at 35 °C to approximately 12% moisture content, and sieved through a 250 μm mesh.

Experimental design

To evaluate the effects of preheating time, starch concentration, and ozonation time on potato starch modification, a face-centered central composite design (FCCCD) was employed within the RSM framework, including six center points. The independent variables were preheating time (X1), starch concentration (X2), and ozonation time (X3), each investigated at three coded levels: low (−1), medium (0), and high (+1) (Table S1). The actual values were 0, 2, and 4 h for preheating time; 10%, 15%, and 20% for starch concentration; and 30, 60, and 90 min for ozonation time. A total of 20 experimental runs were performed, including replicates at the center points. The optimization criteria focused on maximizing hydrogel strength and minimizing syneresis. Quantitative optimization was conducted using a multiple-response approach, and validation experiments were performed under the predicted optimal conditions to confirm the expected responses.

Evaluation of starch hydrogel strength

Hydrogel strength of the starch hydrogels was determined according to the method of Lima et al. (2021) using a texture analyzer (H50 KS, Hounsfield, England) equipped with a 50 kg load cell. Samples were compressed with a cylindrical probe (12 mm diameter) at a speed of 1 mm/s to a penetration depth of 10 mm.

Syneresis

Syneresis was evaluated by storing the prepared samples at 4 °C for specific periods (7 days for starch gels, and 7 and 30 days for dairy desserts) after preparation and molding. To measure the amount of liquid released from the gel structure under gravitational force, a gel sample of known initial mass was placed on a pre-weighed filter paper positioned in a funnel above a collection container. After 24 h of drainage, the gel was carefully separated from the filter paper, and its final mass was recorded without the filter paper. The amount of expelled liquid collected in the container was also weighed. For starch gels, a 10% (w/v) suspension was prepared and heated in a boiling water bath for 30 min under constant stirring. Dairy dessert characterization

Dairy dessert preparation

The formulations of the treatments were prepared according to Table 1, with the sample containing 100% gelatin serving as the control. All ingredients were thoroughly mixed and heated in a water bath at 100 °C for 15 min with continuous stirring. The dessert mixtures were poured into plastic containers (5 cm in diameter and 2 cm in height), allowed to cool at room temperature, and then stored at 4 °C for 24 h prior to further analyses.

Textural profile analysis (TPA)

Using the method described by Yusof et al. (2019), the textural characteristics of the dairy desserts were evaluated using a Brookfield texture analyzer equipped with a 10 kg load cell. Samples were compressed using a TA5 cylindrical probe (12.7 mm diameter, 35 mm length) to 40% of their original height, with a trigger force of 5 g and a compression speed of 0.5 mm/s. The time interval between the two compression cycles was 5 s (Yusof et al., 2019).

Gel strength

Using the same method (Yusof et al., 2019), hydrogel strength was measured with a Brookfield texture analyzer fitted with a 10 kg load cell and a flat-bottomed TA5 cylindrical probe (12.7 mm diameter, 35 mm length). The crosshead speed was set at 1 mm/s. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Color analysis

Color measurements of the samples were carried out using a ColorFlex colorimeter (HunterLab Inc., Reston, VA, USA).

Syneresis properties

Syneresis was determined according to Section 2.4.2 by measuring the initial gel weight and the amount of water released, using Equation (1), at storage intervals of 7 and 30 days.

Sensory analysis

Thirty untrained panelists (comprising university students and experienced factory personnel), familiar with the general characteristics of dairy desserts, were recruited from Tarbiat Modares University and a gelatin dessert manufacturing company. Prior to evaluation, the panelists received verbal instructions from the researcher regarding the scoring procedure and the sensory attributes to be assessed. Samples were evaluated in triplicate for texture, flavor, color, and overall acceptability using a 5-point hedonic scale (1 = very poor, 5 = very good), with higher scores indicating greater preference (Yilmaz-Ersan et al., 2019).

Regarding ethical considerations, all procedures performed in this study involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees. The study protocol was strictly reviewed and officially approved by the Ethics Committee of Tarbiat Modares University under the ethical approval code IR. MODARES.REC.1401.005. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Furthermore, the participants were assured that their personal data would remain completely anonymous and strictly confidential, and that they had the right to withdraw from the evaluation at any time without any consequences.

Dynamic rheological measurements

Small amplitude oscillatory measurements

Dynamic rheological measurements were performed using a controlled stress/strain rheometer (MCR 502, Anton Paar, Austria) equipped with a parallel-plate geometry (25 mm diameter) and a gap of 1.000 mm. Samples were carefully placed on the rheometer plate, and excess material was removed using a spatula. Prior to oscillatory measurements, all samples were equilibrated at the initial test temperature for 5 min (Nikolaou et al., 2025).

Strain sweep measurements

To determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR), strain sweep tests were conducted over a shear strain range of 0.01–100% at ambient temperature (25 °C) and a fixed frequency of 1.59 Hz (10 rad/s). A strain value of 0.01%, within the LVR, was selected for all subsequent dynamic measurements. Storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), yield stress, and loss tangent (tan δ) were determined from these tests (Nikolaou et al., 2025).

Frequency sweep measurements

Frequency sweep tests were carried out at a constant strain of 0.01% (within the LVR) over a frequency range of 0.01–100 rad/s. Dynamic rheological parameters, including storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), and loss tangent (tan δ), were obtained and analyzed for all samples. The frequency dependence of the dynamic moduli was further evaluated by fitting the Power Law model:(2)G′=k′.ωn′(3)G″=k″.ωn″where k′ and k″ are consistency constants, n′ and n″ are frequency exponents, and ω is the angular frequency (Nikolaou et al., 2025).

Apparent viscosity

The apparent viscosity of the dairy dessert samples was measured using a controlled-stress rheometer (MCR 502, Anton Paar, Austria) equipped with a parallel-plate geometry (25 mm diameter, 1 mm gap). Samples were carefully placed at the center of the lower plate, and excess material was removed from the edges. The sample temperature was maintained at 25 °C using the rheometer's Peltier temperature control system.

Steady shear measurements were performed over a shear rate range of 1–200 s−1. Shear stress (τ) and shear rate (γ) were recorded, and the apparent viscosity (η) was calculated as:(4)τγ=η

The measured data were used to plot apparent viscosity versus shear rate curves, and the flow behavior of the samples was analyzed, including the presence of pseudoplastic (shear-thinning) behavior (Biglarian et al., 2022).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in triplicate, and the results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using JMP Statistics 13.2.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test, was applied to determine significant differences among samples at p < 0.05. The control dessert sample consisted of 100% gelatin. To evaluate similarity among samples based on textural and sensory attributes, agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC) was performed. Calculations and clustering plots were generated using Excel with the XLSTAT add-in. This approach enabled the grouping of samples into distinct clusters based on Euclidean distances and was used to identify samples most closely resembling the control.

The hydrogel strength model showed similar trends across both optimization approaches, although numerical values varied slightly depending on the multi-response optimization criteria. These differences can be attributed to variations in the relative prioritization of optimization objectives. According to the regression equation, ozonation time (X3) exerted the strongest positive effect on hydrogel strength. Moreover, interaction effects such as X1X2 and X1X3 were significant. The strong negative interaction between preheating time and ozonation time indicates that, under certain conditions, their combined effect may reduce hydrogel strength.

Modeling results also indicated that the effect of starch concentration on the responses differed between systems (Fig. 1a). In gummy candy formulations, low starch content simultaneously improved hardness, clarity, and solubility, whereas higher starch levels exerted a negative effect. In contrast, in dairy desserts, starch concentration generally had a negative effect: although increased starch content enhanced hydrogel strength, it also promoted syneresis. Since the optimization objective was to simultaneously maximize hydrogel strength and minimize syneresis, the fitted model exhibited a negative linear effect of starch concentration. Furthermore, the effect of ozonation on the hydrogel strength differed between the two systems. In gummy candy, the linear coefficient associated with ozonation was negative at short treatment times and became positive at longer durations, whereas in dairy desserts, the coefficient was predominantly positive. This discrepancy can be attributed to the dual effect of ozone: on one hand, ozone induces starch chain scission and reduces molecular weight, which may decrease hydrogel strength; on the other hand, it introduces polar functional groups that enhance intermolecular interactions, thereby improving hydrogel strength and reducing syneresis within the matrix.

As shown in Fig. 1b, increasing ozonation time significantly enhanced gel hardness, consistent with the positive X3 coefficient in the regression model. This result underscores the critical role of ozonation time in strengthening the gel matrix. As previously reported, ozonation promotes the formation of carbonyl and carboxyl groups in starch, which enhance water retention and gel stability through increased hydrogen bonding (Lima et al., 2021; Pandiselvam et al., 2019; Sumardiono et al., 2021). The positive coefficient of X1 and the response surface depicted in Fig. 1c further indicate that preheating exerts a reinforcing effect on gel properties. Mild preheating (e.g., at 130 °C) can densify the starch structure, thereby contributing to enhanced hydrogel strength (Deng et al., 2022).

However, the negative interaction between preheating time and ozonation time indicates that simultaneous application of both factors at high levels may diminish, or even reverse, their positive effects (Fig. 1d). This trend is also evident in the response surface plots. Overall, the model suggests that controlled increases in ozonation time combined with mild preheating can effectively strengthen the gel structure, whereas excessive treatment may compromise the integrity of the gel network. As shown in Fig. 1e and as noted above, partial starch degradation during preheating may increase the availability of reactive sites for oxidation by ozone. With respect to starch concentration, higher levels appear to provide more favorable conditions for forming a stronger gel network, because increasing starch concentration raises the number of polymer chains in the system, thereby enhancing intermolecular interactions and yielding a more robust network (Huang et al., 2024).

Syneresis of starch hydrogel

Syneresis is one of the most important indicators of starch hydrogel stability in dairy desserts, reflecting the ability of the gel network to retain water. Reducing syneresis through optimization of starch composition and processing conditions results in a more uniform texture and improved product shelf life. Several studies have attributed reduced syneresis to ozonation treatment (Fig. 2a). The large negative coefficient for X3 indicates that increasing ozonation time (and thus the extent of oxidation) substantially decreased syneresis. This reduction is attributed to carboxyl group formation, which can inhibit amylose retrogradation and introduce negative charges, thereby enhancing water-holding capacity and limiting water release (Cahyana et al., 2018; Handarini et al., 2020). These findings are consistent with Handarini et al. (2020), who reported that ozonation of arrowroot starch, in combination with thermal and oxidative modification, improved water retention and gel stability.Fig. 2Response surface plots showing the effects of modified starches on syneresis. Furthermore, a study on cassava starch reported that the combination of preheating and ozonation may facilitate hydrogen bonding between carbonyl and carboxyl groups in amylose and amylopectin chains, thereby enhancing gel network integrity and hydrophilicity and minimizing syneresis (Lima et al., 2021). According to the regression equation, the quadratic term for ozonation time suggests that the decreasing trend persists even at high treatment durations; increased oxidation and higher levels of hydrophilic carbonyl and carboxyl groups further reduce syneresis. Moreover, ozonation decreases apparent amylose content and cleaves amylopectin branches, which may reduce retrogradation and consequently lower syneresis (Castanha et al., 2017; Pulgarín et al., 2023).

According to the regression equation and as illustrated in Fig. 2b, the positive coefficient of starch concentration indicates that increasing starch concentration increases syneresis. As indicated above, both preheating time and ozonation time reduced syneresis; however, high starch concentration, likely due to increased medium viscosity and denser packing, may diminish the effectiveness of these treatments within the starch matrix. Moreover, at higher starch levels, enhanced interactions among starch components and closer proximity of amylose and amylopectin chains may promote retrogradation, resulting in greater water release from the gel structure (Pulgarín et al., 2023).

Fig. 2c presents the three-dimensional response surface illustrating the effect of preheating time on syneresis. The negative coefficient in the regression equation indicates that increasing preheating time reduced syneresis. Preheating has been reported to strengthen intermolecular bonds in the amorphous regions of starch granules, limiting amylose leaching during gelatinization and improving water retention within the amylose–amylopectin matrix, thereby reducing syneresis (Hoover and Manuel, 1996). In addition, thermal treatment alone has been shown to increase starch water-holding capacity by promoting granule swelling (Bae and Lee, 2018).

According to the regression equation and as illustrated in Fig. 2d, the interaction between starch concentration and ozonation time (X2X3) was positive, indicating that increasing both factors increased syneresis. High starch concentration (dense packing) combined with prolonged ozonation, which shortens starch chains (Castanha et al., 2017), may destabilize the hydrogel network and ultimately increase water release.

According to Fig. 2e and the regression equation, the interaction between preheating time and ozonation time (X1X3) was also positive. As discussed previously, preheating promotes starch gelatinization, whereas prolonged exposure to ozone can weaken and degrade the starch structure. Excessive application of both factors may weaken the gel network, reduce water-holding capacity, and consequently increase syneresis.

Model prediction evaluation

Based on the software analysis, the optimal conditions for achieving maximum hydrogel strength and minimum syneresis were identified as 35 min of preheating, 10% starch concentration, and 90 min of ozonation. These conditions were selected as the optimal treatment and were applied in subsequent experiments. The developed model demonstrated acceptable accuracy in predicting syneresis and hydrogel strength responses under the optimal conditions. Predicted and observed values for both responses were compared using a paired t-test, which showed no significant difference (p > 0.05), indicating the validity and reliability of the model.

Under the optimal conditions, the prediction error percentages for syneresis and hydrogel strength were 2.26% and 1.61%, respectively, indicating low error and strong model performance in simulating the experimental data. Therefore, the proposed model can reliably predict the examined responses and can be effectively used to optimize the starch modification process (Table S6).

Characterization and evaluation of produced dairy dessert properties

The effects of different levels of carrageenan (C) and ozone-modified starch (MS), as well as their interaction, on the dairy dessert were investigated. The control sample (containing gelatin) was not included in the experimental design because the objective of the factorial design and associated statistical analysis was to evaluate the effects of carrageenan and modified starch as gelatin substitutes. Because the control formulation differs fundamentally due to the presence of gelatin, direct comparison with the experimental treatments using Tukey's test was not appropriate; therefore, the control was evaluated separately.

Color results of the produced dairy dessert

Analysis of variance revealed that carrageenan (C), modified starch (MS), and their interaction significantly affected all measured color parameters (L∗, a∗, and b∗) (p < 0.0001). For the lightness index (L∗), C was the key determinant. Increasing the C level significantly enhanced lightness, with the highest L∗ observed in the MS 0.25–C 1.0 treatment and the lowest in MS 0.25–C 0.5. This can be explained by the formation of a dense carrageenan network in the presence of milk calcium, which reduces light scattering and increases white light reflection. Conversely, increasing MS slightly decreased L∗, as starch granules absorb part of the incident light. Compared to the gelatin-based control (L∗ = 72.57), the MS-C samples were slightly darker, likely because the homogeneous dissolution of gelatin in water reduces turbidity and maximizes light reflection (Sow et al., 2018).

Regarding the redness (a∗) and yellowness (b∗) indices, MS exerted the greatest influence. Increasing C while decreasing MS resulted in lower redness and higher yellowness. Consequently, the lowest a∗ and highest b∗ values were both observed for MS 0.25–C 1.0, whereas the inverse was true for MS 1.25–C 0.5. Although previous studies reported minor changes in a∗ in starch-based dairy desserts (Naseer et al., 2021) the reduction in redness here was consistent with the increase in lightness, which likely masked red tones. Additionally, altered starch dispersion modified light absorption. The increase in b∗ is likely attributable to the inherent color of carrageenan (Wei et al., 2025), while higher MS levels increased opacity and lowered perceived yellowness (Su et al., 2024). Compared with the gelatin control (b∗ = 2.78), all treatments showed higher b∗ values, with MS 1.25–C 0.5 being the closest (15.24).

Total color difference (ΔE) values, calculated relative to the gelatin control, indicated pronounced color differences for all combinations, ranging from 29.64 (MS 0.75–C 1.0) to 31.40 (MS 0.25–C 0.5). These differences are primarily attributed to the darker appearance (lower L∗) of the C × MS treatments relative to gelatin and the substantial increase in b∗ caused by carrageenan. Similar trends have been reported in studies where hydrocolloid addition altered light scattering and colorimetric properties, leading to increased ΔE values (Su et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025).

A notable observation in this study is the apparent discrepancy between the high instrumentally measured color differences (ΔE>29) and the sensory evaluation results, which indicated no significant reduction in color acceptability for the optimal formulation. This divergence primarily stems from the substantial differences in the L∗ and b∗ parameters between the opaque C-MS samples and the inherently translucent gelatin control. The C-MS matrices, characterized by increased opacity, enhance light scattering and reduce visual contrast. Consequently, the standard ΔE formula, which assigns a high mathematical weight to changes in lightness (ΔL), registers large deviations. However, the human eye does not necessarily perceive these changes as undesirable color defects within an opaque matrix (Pathare et al., 2013). In such turbid food systems, instrumental color measurements often exhibit poor correlation with sensory perception. Ultimately, consumers perceive the plant-based dessert not as a defective, discolored gelatin, but rather as a distinct, creamy-style product with its own acceptable visual appeal.

Hydrogel strength is a key indicator of three-dimensional network integrity and initial resistance to fracture, reflecting the force required to deform or rupture the gel structure. This measurement represents the bond strength and structural cohesion and provides a rapid metric for comparing formulations. Texture profile analysis (TPA) of the produced dairy dessert

As shown in Table 4, C, MS, and their interaction significantly affected dessert hardness (p < 0.0001). Increasing C increased hardness, with the highest value observed at 1.0% C (2.73 N) and the lowest at 0.5% C (1.26 N). In contrast, increasing MS decreased hardness. The interaction effect indicated the highest hardness in MS 0.25–C1.0 (3.27 N) and the lowest in MS 1.25–C0.5 (0.477 N). These findings indicate that, even in the presence of carrageenan, higher MS levels reduced hardness. The gelatin control (2G) exhibited a hardness of 0.31 N, which was lower than that of the C–MS combinations.

κ-Carrageenan, owing to its anionic sulfate groups and interactions with milk proteins, can contribute to the formation of a stronger three-dimensional network (Lu et al., 2025). At low MS levels, the effect of C on hardness predominated; however, higher MS levels may reduce hardness due to the increased presence of polar carbonyl and carboxyl groups, which enhance water retention and may weaken the gel network. In addition, interactions among MS carboxyl groups and carrageenan sulfate groups may introduce competition and electrostatic repulsion under certain conditions, thereby partially weakening network integrity. Calcium ions present in milk may partially offset these effects by strongly promoting carrageenan gelation. Specifically, divalent calcium ions (Ca2+) can screen the electrostatic repulsions and form calcium bridges between the negatively charged sulfate groups of carrageenan, facilitating the aggregation of carrageenan helices and reinforcing the structural network. Similar trends have been reported in carrageenan–oat gum dairy desserts, in which hardness decreased with increasing oat gum content, with corresponding changes in gumminess and chewiness (Zarzycki et al., 2019).

It is worth noting that the hardness of the final composite desserts (Table 4) was substantially lower than that of the pure starch hydrogel (Table S2). This significant difference is primarily because the pure hydrogel was formulated with a much higher starch concentration (10%), whereas the final desserts contained a lower starch content distributed within a complex matrix of milk and sugar, naturally yielding a softer gel structure.

C significantly affected cohesiveness (p < 0.0001), which increased at higher C levels. MS had no significant main effect, whereas the C × MS interaction was significant (p = 0.0008). The highest cohesiveness was observed in MS 1.25–C 1.0 (0.65), and the lowest in MS 1.25–C 0.5 (0.43). Overall, trends in hardness and cohesiveness were broadly aligned. Carrageenan generally enhanced both attributes, reducing fragmentation and supporting network integrity. Lower syneresis in MS 1.25–C 1 indicated improved water retention, suggesting that retained moisture may act as a plasticizer and enhance phase cohesion. At higher C levels, a more continuous network may form, promoting more uniform dispersion of modified starch and retained water, thereby increasing the energy required for fracture and improving cohesiveness.

Gumminess was significantly affected by C, MS, and their interaction (p < 0.0001). Increasing C increased gumminess from 0.652 N (0.5% C) to 1.675 N (1.0% C). In contrast, increasing MS decreased gumminess from 1.518 N (0.25% MS) to 0.742 N (1.25% MS). The interaction effect showed that higher C with lower MS yielded the highest gumminess (MS 0.25–C 1.0 = 1.985 N), whereas MS 1.25–C 0.5 produced the lowest (0.205 N). Because gumminess is calculated as the product of hardness and cohesiveness, its pattern largely mirrors those attributes (Bourne, 2002).

Springiness was not significantly affected by C (p = 0.2973), MS (p = 0.2772), or their interaction (p = 0.3024). The maximum and minimum springiness values were observed in MS 0.75–C 0.5 (7.70 mm) and MS 1.25–C 0.75 (4.27 mm), respectively. Although differences were not significant, the addition of C and MS generally tended to increase elasticity.

Chewiness and resilience were significantly affected by C, MS, and their interaction (p < 0.0001). Resilience was the highest at 1.0% C and decreased with decreasing C. The highest resilience was observed in MS 1.25–C 1.0 (0.285), whereas the lowest was observed in MS 1.25–C 0.5 (0.195). The gelatin control exhibited the highest resilience (0.44).

Gel strength of the produced dairy dessert

Hydrogel strength results indicated significant main effects of C and MS on this parameter (p < 0.0001) and a significant C × MS interaction (p = 0.0055p = 0.0055p = 0.0055). Increasing C from 0.5% to 1.0% markedly increased hydrogel strength, whereas increasing MS from 0.25% to 1.25% decreased it. The highest hydrogel strength was observed in MS 0.25–C 1.0 (1.54 N), and the lowest in MS 1.25–C 0.5 (0.33 N). The gelatin control exhibited a hydrogel strength of 0.270 N.

In carrageenan gels, junction zones form via double helices, whereas gelatin networks form via triple helices. The number and nature of junction zones influence gel properties: gelatin gels are typically softer and more elastic, whereas carrageenan gels are generally firmer (Lu et al., 2025). Increasing C increases network density and resistance to deformation, thereby increasing gel strength. Conversely, higher MS levels may weaken the network because carbonyl and carboxyl groups increase hydrophilicity and water retention, which can reduce structural rigidity. The significant C × MS interaction suggests that specific combinations of these ingredients can produce gels with deformation behavior closer to that of gelatin.

TPA hardness and hydrogel strength were positively and strongly correlated (r = 0.986), indicating that both methods capture closely related aspects of network rigidity and may be used complementarily to describe dairy dessert texture.

Deformation and flexibility, as indicators of resistance to deformation and elasticity, were not significantly affected by C or MS alone but were significantly affected by their interaction (p = 0.0201). C significantly affected flexibility (p < 0.0001), and MS also had a significant effect (p = 0.0269). Increasing C slightly increased deformation and flexibility, whereas increasing MS slightly reduced both, potentially due to the formation of a more brittle network. The maximum deformation was observed at 0.75–1.0% C and 0.75% MS (3.997 mm), whereas the minimum was observed in MS 0.75–C 0.5 (3.960 mm). The gelatin control exhibited 3.935 mm. Maximum flexibility was observed in MS 1.25–C 1.0 (0.485), and the minimum in MS 1.25–C 0.5 (0.325), whereas the gelatin control exhibited the highest flexibility (0.915). Collectively, these results indicate that the combination of carrageenan and modified starch can functionally replace gelatin in dairy desserts.

Normalized composite indices were used to assess textural similarity to the control. The treatment containing 0.5% C and 1.25% MS showed the closest textural resemblance to the gelatin control.

The highest syneresis was observed in MS 0.25–C 1.0 (8.82%), and the lowest in MS 1.25–C 0.5 (4.32%). Compared with the gelatin control, carrageenan–modified starch gels exhibited greater water release; however, increasing MS resulted in behavior more closely approaching that of gelatin.

After 30 days, the overall trend remained similar; however, the effect of C changed, and higher levels of C resulted in a relative increase in syneresis. The highest and lowest syneresis values were observed in MS 0.25–C 1 (13.78%) and MS 1.25–C 0.5 (7.88%), respectively, whereas the control sample exhibited 3.79%. The control sample exhibited remarkably low syneresis (1.98%and 3.79% after 7 and 30 days, respectively). While gelatin-based dairy desserts can be susceptible to higher Syneresis for instance, formulations utilizing lower gelatin concentrations (0.3%) and have been reported to exhibit syneresis values exceeding 4.5% after 7 days (Lin et al., 2025) the superior water-holding capacity in our control sample is directly attributed to the specific formulation. The incorporation of high-bloom gelatin (240 Bloom) at a significantly higher concentration (2%) forms a highly dense and rigid three-dimensional polymeric network. Furthermore, the high sucrose content (15%) tightly binds free water through extensive hydrogen bonding, while milk proteins act as active fillers within the gel matrix. Together, these factors effectively prevent the structural contraction of the matrix and restrict water mobility, leading to enhanced stability against Syneresis.

These results indicate that modified starch plays a key role in water retention and syneresis reduction, whereas increasing C may render the gel network more prone to contraction over time, leading to greater water release during storage. As carrageenan concentration increases, gel contraction and solvent expulsion may also increase in the resulting firmer gels. Moreover, the enhanced water-holding capacity of modified starch can be attributed to hydrogen bonding involving carbonyl and carboxyl groups introduced during oxidation, which contributes to network integrity and water retention within the gel matrix. Overall, the combined effects of C and MS suggest that lower carrageenan levels favor higher water-holding capacity, whereas higher MS levels yield gels with minimal syneresis. Across both storage periods, MS 1.25–C 0.5 most closely resembled the gelatin control. In addition, the reduced apparent amylose content of modified starch may contribute to decreased syneresis and improved stability (Castanha et al., 2019).

Rheological properties of the optimized sample compared to the control

To evaluate the rheological behavior of the optimized dairy dessert and compare it with the gelatin-based control, strain sweep, frequency sweep, and steady shear tests were conducted for both formulations. Measurements were performed within the LVR to assess gel network structure, stability, and flow behavior. Variations in storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″), loss factor (tan δ), and apparent viscosity were analyzed and are discussed below.

Strain sweep analysis

Based on the strain sweep results, changes in G′ and G″ within the strain range of 0.0001–1% were evaluated to determine the LVR and gel network stability. For both samples, G′ remained nearly constant at low strains (up to approximately 0.01%), indicating a stable three-dimensional structure and predominantly elastic behavior. This range corresponds to the LVR, in which the gel structure remains intact without rupture or disruption (Fig. 5a).Fig. 5Rheological properties of gelatin-controlled (2G) and optimized (0.5C–1.25MS) dairy dessert samples. Rheological properties of gelatin-controlled (2G) and optimized (0.5C–1.25MS) dairy dessert samples. (a) Variation of storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) as a function of shear strain (γ) at 25 °C, (b) frequency sweep profiles of G′ and G″ at a constant strain of 0.01, (c) viscosity as a function of shear rate).

At strains above 0.01%, both samples exhibited a gradual decrease in G′ and a concurrent increase in G″, indicating the onset of network disruption. However, the decline in G′ was less pronounced for the optimized sample and occurred more gradually, suggesting higher structural stability in the nonlinear region. In contrast, the gelatin control exhibited a sharper decrease in G′, indicating earlier network breakdown.

Across the full strain range, G′ values were considerably higher for the optimized sample than for the control (approximately twofold higher within the linear region). Specifically, at a strain of 0.01%, the G′ of the optimized sample was measured at 434.68 Pa, compared to 162.27 Pa for the gelatin control indicating the formation of a stronger and more cohesive gel network. This behavior may be attributed primarily to the interactions involving ozone-oxidized starch, κ-carrageenan, and milk proteins. In particular, hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between oxidized starch functional groups (e.g., carbonyl and hydroxyl groups) and the anionic carrageenan groups, as well as interactions with κ-casein, may contribute to network reinforcement and increased resistance to shear deformation.

Analysis of tan δ showed that, within the linear region, values were approximately 0.08 for the control and 0.14 for the optimized sample, indicating predominantly elastic behavior in both formulations. With increasing strain, tan δ gradually increased and then rose sharply beyond approximately 0.01%, consistent with network breakdown. The smaller and more gradual increase in tan δ for the optimized sample, relative to the gelatin control, suggests improved capacity to accommodate deformation without abrupt structural failure.

Overall, although both samples exhibited an LVR up to approximately 0.01% strain, the formulation containing oxidized starch and κ-carrageenan showed greater structural stability beyond this limit. These results indicate that replacing gelatin with ozone-modified starch and κ-carrageenan can yield a gel with higher strength, greater resistance to applied strain, and more stable viscoelastic behavior.

Similar trends have been reported in studies examining oxidized potato starch in milk protein systems, in which G′ increased and gel network stability improved. These effects were attributed to hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between carbonyl groups in oxidized starch and functional groups in casein, which strengthened the network and reduced deformation-induced breakdown (Ahsan et al., 2024). In addition, hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions have been reported to play key roles in strengthening gels formed by casein and κ-carrageenan, leading to denser, more homogeneous networks and reduced syneresis (Lu et al., 2025). These findings are consistent with the present results.

Frequency sweep analysis

A frequency sweep test at a constant strain of 0.01% was performed for the control sample (2G) and the optimized sample (MS 1.25-C 0.5) to evaluate gel viscoelasticity (Fig. 5b). The optimized sample exhibited higher G′ values than the control across the entire frequency range. Specifically, G′ of the control increased from 21.7 Pa at low frequencies to 285 Pa at intermediate frequencies, but decreased at high frequencies, suggesting brittleness of the gelatin network. In contrast, G′ of the optimized sample increased from 278 Pa to 1123 Pa and did not decrease at high frequencies, indicating a more stable and reinforced gel network due to the incorporation of ozone-modified starch and κ-carrageenan.

Analysis of G″ showed that the optimized sample also exhibited higher values than the control at most frequencies. In the control, G″ was relatively high at low frequencies, decreased at intermediate frequencies, and increased sharply at very high frequencies, consistent with increased viscous dissipation and network instability at high frequencies. In the optimized sample, G″ remained higher than that of the control at intermediate and high frequencies, suggesting improved capacity to dissipate energy while maintaining network integrity.

Tan δ further highlighted differences between the formulations. In the control, tan δ was close to 1 at low frequencies and increased to >1.4 with increasing frequency, suggesting viscous-dominant behavior and reduced network stability at high frequencies. In the optimized sample, tan δ remained <0.2, indicating predominantly elastic behavior and improved network stability, despite the higher G″ values.

Overall, these results indicate that the combination of ozone-modified starch and κ-carrageenan reinforced the gel network, enhanced elastic strength, and improved energy dissipation capacity. Similar effects have been reported for systems containing κ-carrageenan and oat gum, which strengthened gel structure and improved viscoelastic properties (Zarzycki et al., 2019). In contrast, the gelatin gel network exhibited a more brittle, semi-viscoelastic character, and the variations observed in G′ and G″, together with the increase in tan δ at high frequencies, reflect its comparatively weaker mechanical stability. Therefore, ozone-modified starch can serve as an effective alternative to gelatin in dessert formulations, improving both mechanical performance and the gel network stability.

For further analysis, the G′ and G″ data were fitted to the Power Law model over an angular frequency range of 0.215–31.6 rad/s to extract gel network parameters, as described in Equations (2) and (3). The parameters analyzed included G′ (storage modulus, Pa), G″ (loss modulus, Pa), k′ and k″ (hydrogel strength constants, Pa. sn), n′ and n″ (dimensionless frequency exponents), and ω (angular frequency, rad/s). To perform this, the natural logarithms of the experimental data were plotted, and linear regression was applied. The detailed linearized equations (Eqs. S1-S6) are provided in the Supplementary Material.

For the optimized sample, G′ fitting yielded a low frequency exponent (n′ = 0. 0899, R2 = 0. 9942), indicating minimal frequency dependence and reflecting the stability of the gel network. Its high hydrogel strength constant (k′ = e6.419 ≈ 615 Pa) indicates high structural strength. The loss modulus (G″) of the optimized sample yielded n′′ = 0.0937, k′′≈75 Pa, and R2 = 0.8039. Although this R2 value is lower than that of G′, it reflects the inherent variability associated with measuring the much smaller viscous component in a highly elastic gel rather than a poor model fit. Overall, these parameters indicate limited energy dissipation and predominantly elastic behavior, reflecting a strong, stable gel network with low sensitivity to frequency variations.

For the gelatin control, G′ fitting showed a higher frequency exponent (n′ = 0.1453, R2 = 0.8804), indicating greater frequency dependence, while its network strength (k′ ≈ 191 Pa) was lower compared with the optimized formulation. The loss modulus (G″) of the control (n″ = 0.1908, k″ ≈ 17.7 Pa, R2 = 0.6557) indicated limited energy dissipation; however, the gel network remained brittle and sensitive to frequency changes.

Viscosity analysis

The viscosity of the gelatin control (2G) and the optimized sample (MS 1.25-C0.5) was evaluated as a function of shear rate (Fig. 5c). Both samples exhibited non-Newtonian, shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) behavior, with viscosity decreasing as shear rate increased. At low shear rates (1–20 s−1), the control sample showed considerably higher viscosity than the optimized sample. At 1 s−1, viscosities were 93013 Pa s and 76304 Pa s for the control and optimized samples, respectively, indicating greater resistance to initial flow and a more fragile gelatin network in the control. The lower viscosity of the optimized sample at low shear rates implies improved flowability, facilitating shaping and consumption.

At high shear rates (>100 s−1), viscosities of both samples converged to similar values, with the optimized sample ranging from 698 to 922 Pa s and the control from 552 to 1041 Pa s. These results indicate that the optimized sample possesses a more stable network with controlled viscosity reduction under high shear, whereas the gelatin control is more sensitive to shear variations and exhibits greater structural fragility. Overall, incorporation of ozone-modified starch and κ-carrageenan reduced low-shear viscosity, improved flowability, and enhanced viscosity stability at high shear rates, rendering the optimized formulation rheologically superior.

Conclusion

In this study, potato starch was modified using ozone in combination with preheating and controlled starch concentration, and the optimal formulation was identified using RSM based on minimal syneresis and maximal gel hardness. Complete replacement of gelatin with MS in combination with C was then evaluated in a dairy dessert system. The results demonstrated that MS and C levels significantly influenced color, texture, syneresis, sensory attributes, and rheological behavior. The optimized formulation (MS 1.25–C 0.5) exhibited textural properties comparable to the gelatin control, with a hardness of 0.477 N, cohesiveness of 0.65, and hydrogel strength of 0.33 N, compared with 0.31 N, 0.62, and 0.27 N for the control, respectively. Color differences (ΔE) ranged from 29.64 to 31.40, with slightly lower lightness values than the gelatin control (L∗ = 72.57). Syneresis decreased markedly with increasing MS content, from 8.82% to 4.32% after 7 days and from 13.78% to 7.88% after 30 days, with the optimized sample exhibiting behavior closest to the control (3.79%). Sensory evaluation showed comparable flavor across samples, with only minor differences in texture and color. Rheological analysis revealed that the optimized sample had higher G′ (≈615 Pa vs. 191 Pa) and G″ (≈75 Pa vs. 17.7 Pa), predominantly elastic behavior (G′ > G″), and shear-thinning viscosity. Overall, the combination of ozone-oxidized starch and κ-carrageenan effectively replaced gelatin in dairy desserts, yielding a stable, elastic product with acceptable sensory quality. This formulation represents a plant-based, environmentally friendly alternative to gelatin, suitable for vegetarian consumers and individuals with religious restrictions on gelatin consumption.
