Dual antiviral and anti-inflammatory effects of BAY 11-7082 in SARS-CoV-2 infection via inhibition of the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway
Authors: Po-Chih Wu, Meng-Chang Lee, Ming-Sian Wu, Chun-Hsi Tso, Jia-Chuan Hsu, Der-Yuan Wang, Hao-Ai Shui, Chi-Tun Tang, Chia-Wen Shih, Chin-Mao Hung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe continued emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants and persistent inflammatory complications of COVID-19 highlight the urgent need for therapeutics with both antiviral and anti-inflammatory properties. Despite intensive global efforts, no approved antiviral therapy with these dual functions has yet been developed, representing a significant gap in current COVID-19 treatment strategies. In this study, we identify BAY 11-7082 (BAY) as a dual-action compound that inhibits SARS-CoV-2 replication and the production of virus-induced pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including IL-6, IL-8, CXCL1, and CXCL2. BAY predominantly exerts its antiviral activity at the post-entry stage of the viral life cycle. Mechanistically, BAY potentially interacts with SARS-CoV-2 NSP14 and inhibits virus-induced phosphorylation and degradation of IκBα, suppressing NF-κB activation through the IKK-IκBα signaling axis. Furthermore, BAY exhibits potent antiviral activity against multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (VOCs). Collectively, these findings support the potential of BAY as a dual-action therapeutic candidate, combining antiviral and anti-inflammatory effects, against SARS-CoV-2 and its emerging variants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48699-6.Keywords: BAY 11-7082, SARS-CoV-2, Antiviral therapy, Anti-inflammatory, NSP14, Canonical NF-κB signaling pathwaySubject terms: Diseases, Drug discovery, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) belongs to the Coronaviridae family, which includes a broad range of coronaviruses that infect both humans and animals, including SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV. Since its emergence, the COVID-19 pandemic, caused by SARS-CoV-2, has resulted in more than 770 million confirmed cases and over 7.1 million deaths worldwide (as of August 2025). Although vaccination efforts have reduced transmission and disease severity, the rise of new variants and long-term complications of COVID-19 highlight the persistent need for effective treatments1,2. Numerous studies indicate that dysregulated immune responses and persistent inflammation following SARS-CoV-2 infection contribute to the development of post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC) or long COVID—a chronic condition characterized by debilitating and long-term symptoms3,4. Elevated inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), have been linked to the development and persistence of these symptoms5,6. However, current antiviral agents show limited effectiveness in managing inflammation-related complications following SARS-CoV-2 infection7–9. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify therapeutic agents with dual antiviral and anti-inflammatory properties to address unmet medical needs.

Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) is a key transcription factor in the regulation of host immune responses during viral infections. However, many viruses have evolved diverse mechanisms to manipulate NF-κB signaling, which in turn promotes immune evasion and drives disease progression. A common strategy employed by upper respiratory viruses, such as influenza virus and respiratory syncytial virus, is the activation of NF-κB-driven inflammatory responses during the early phase of infection, while simultaneously suppressing antiviral signaling mediated by type I (IFN-I) and type III (IFN-III) interferons to evade immune surveillance10–12. Similarly, single-cell transcriptomic analysis of SARS-CoV-2-infected lung epithelial cells shows a shift toward increased NF-κB-driven inflammatory gene expression, accompanied by downregulation of interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3)-mediated antiviral genes13. Moreover, analyses of postmortem lung tissues and peripheral blood cells from patients with severe COVID-19 indicate markedly reduced or undetectable expression of IFN-I and IFN-III14,15. Interestingly, targeting NF-κB signaling—through the inhibition of target genes such as IL-8 and IL-32 or suppression of the NF-κB subunit RelA/p65—has been shown to effectively reduce SARS-CoV-2 replication and protein expression13,16. SARS-CoV-2 viral proteins can further modulate viral activity by regulating host NF-κB signaling. For instance, the non-structural protein 14 (NSP14) interacts with inosine-5′-monophosphate dehydrogenase 2 (IMPDH2) to activate NF-κB, thereby enhancing viral infection17. Although the precise mechanisms by which SARS-CoV-2 exploits NF-κB signaling remain incompletely understood, the dual role of NF-κB in viral replication and inflammatory responses makes it a promising therapeutic target.

The inhibition of NF-κB signaling has been considered a potential therapeutic strategy for SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, several representative NF-κB inhibitors currently used or investigated for COVID-19 treatment show notable limitations. For example, dexamethasone suppresses NF-κB activation by interfering with its binding to DNA response elements, and has been used to mitigate COVID-19-induced cytokine storms. Nonetheless, it has been shown that dexamethasone offers no significant benefit to patients not receiving respiratory support18, and high-dose corticosteroid treatment has been associated with delayed viral clearance19,20. Similarly, IKK inhibitors such as parthenolide and ML120B effectively suppress NF-κB activation but do not appear to inhibit SARS-CoV-2 replication21. These findings suggest that representative NF-κB inhibitors continue to exhibit limitations in effectively targeting SARS-CoV-2 infection.

BAY 11-7082 (BAY) is an NF-κB pathway inhibitor that exerts broad anti-inflammatory effects by targeting multiple signaling nodes22–24. Previous studies have demonstrated that BAY inhibits influenza virus-induced cytokine expression and viral mRNA synthesis, leading to reduced viral replication and viral titers25,26. Given the central role of NF-κB as a common host target across viral infections, BAY’s dual antiviral and anti-inflammatory properties make it a highly promising candidate to combat SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, its therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms, particularly in live infection models, remain largely unknown and warrant further investigation.

In this study, we demonstrate that BAY exhibits both antiviral and anti-inflammatory effects against SARS-CoV-2 infection by suppressing the virus-induced canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, and maintains its antiviral efficacy across various variants, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic candidate for COVID-19.

Results

BAY exhibited antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 infection without cytotoxic effects

Host transcriptomic responses to SARS-CoV-2 share a high degree of concordance with those induced by other respiratory viruses, including influenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus, and rhinovirus27. Building on this observation, our preliminary analyses in an A549 cell model of influenza A virus (IAV)-induced inflammation indicated that BAY treatment partially attenuated virus-induced transcriptional changes. Notably, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that these changes were significantly inversely correlated with host pathways implicated in SARS-CoV-2 infection and COVID-19 adverse outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 1), supporting the potential of BAY as a therapeutic candidate for COVID-19.

To evaluate the antiviral potential of BAY against SARS-CoV-2, A549 cells engineered to overexpress human ACE2 (A549-ACE2) were used, as parental A549 cells exhibit low ACE2 expression and are poorly permissive to viral infection28. Cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that BAY did not affect the viability of A549-ACE2 cells at concentrations up to 40 μM following 24- and 48 h (h) treatments (Fig. 1A), defining a non-toxic concentration range. Within this range, BAY treatment significantly reduced both intracellular and extracellular SARS-CoV-2 viral RNA levels at both 24 h and 48 h post-infection (Fig. 1B). Consistently, immunoblot analysis revealed a dose-dependent reduction in SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein levels, which was further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 1C,D). Similarly, BAY reduced both viral RNA and protein levels in Calu-3 cells at non-cytotoxic concentrations (Supplementary Figs. 2A–C).

We next assessed the effects of BAY on the production of infectious SARS-CoV-2 particles. Viral titer reduction assays demonstrated that BAY markedly decreased viral titers in the culture supernatants at both 24 and 48 h post-infection (Fig. 1E). At 48 h post-infection, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) and half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of BAY in A549-ACE2 cells were determined to be 7.5 ± 1.3 and 61.6 ± 2.9 μM, respectively, yielding a selectivity index (SI) of 8.2 (Fig. 1F). In Calu-3 cells, BAY exhibited an IC50 of 5.7 ± 1.6 μM and a CC50 of 32.6 ± 1.2 μM, with a resulting SI of 5.7 (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that BAY exhibits potent antiviral effects against SARS-CoV-2 infection, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic candidate.

BAY exerted its antiviral effects primarily at the post-entry stage of the viral life cycle

To identify the stage of the SARS-CoV-2 replication cycle affected by BAY, A549-ACE2 cells were subjected to BAY treatment at specific time points relative to viral infection. Given that a full replication cycle of SARS-CoV-2 is estimated to occur within approximately 7 to 24 h29, our experiments focused on the period within the first 24 h post-infection to minimize potential confounding effects from secondary rounds of infection. The treatment regimens included: (i) virus control (vehicle only), (ii) continuous BAY treatment, (iii) host cell pre-treatment, (iv) viral pre-incubation with BAY, (v) co-treatment, and (vi) post-infection treatment, as illustrated in Fig. 2A. Post-infection treatment with BAY markedly decreased viral RNA and protein levels, as demonstrated by real-time qPCR and immunoblot results (Fig. 2B,C). Viral titer assays further demonstrated a significant reduction in infectious virus production (Fig. 2D), with a similar extent of viral titer reduction as that observed for continuous treatment. Overall, these results suggest that BAY exerts its antiviral effects primarily at the post-entry stage of the SARS-CoV-2 life cycle, rather than through inhibition of viral entry or direct impairment of viral infectivity.

BAY reduced SARS-CoV-2-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine production

To investigate whether BAY exerts anti-inflammatory effects during the early phase of infection, A549-ACE2 cells were infected with SARS-CoV-2 and treated with BAY or vehicle control, with uninfected cells serving as mock controls. Early SARS-CoV-2 infection in lung epithelial cells triggers robust induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including IL-6, IL-8, CXCL1, and CXCL2, which are strongly associated with hyper-inflammation and disease severity in COVID-1930,31. We therefore examined whether BAY modulates the expression of these pro-inflammatory mediators in infected cells at 24 h post-infection. Real-time qPCR analysis demonstrated that BAY treatment dose-dependently inhibited SARS-CoV-2-induced upregulation of IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL10 transcripts (Fig. 3A). Consistently, the elevated levels of IL-6, IL-8, CXCL1, and CXCL2 in the culture supernatants of SARS-CoV-2-infected cells were significantly suppressed by BAY treatment (Fig. 3B). Together, these results demonstrate that BAY attenuates SARS-CoV-2-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine production, highlighting its potential to mitigate acute infection-associated hyper-inflammatory responses.

Transcriptome analysis identified BAY-mediated modulation of host cell responses to SARS-CoV-2 infection

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying BAY’s antiviral and anti-inflammatory effects, transcriptome analysis was performed to examine the host cell responses to BAY treatment during SARS-CoV-2 infection. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that SARS-CoV-2-infected samples were clearly separated from mock samples along PC1, which accounted for 89.3% of the variance. Among BAY-treated infected samples, the 10 µM group clustered closely with vehicle-treated infected samples, whereas the 20 µM group showed a modest but consistent shift in PCA space (Fig. 4A). These observations suggest a potential concentration-dependent influence of BAY on host transcriptional responses. Differential expression analysis identified 4,787 genes significantly altered upon viral infection (p < 0.01, absolute log2 fold change ≥ 1), including 3,160 upregulated and 1,627 downregulated genes. BAY treatment attenuated a subset of virus-induced transcriptional changes in infected cells. Among the 3,160 genes upregulated by SARS-CoV-2, 528 were downregulated upon BAY treatment, whereas 102 of 1,627 virus-downregulated genes showed increased expression relative to vehicle-treated infected cells (Fig. 4B). Notably, compared with mock cells, SARS-CoV-2 infection led to upregulation of multiple NF-κB target genes, while most type I and type III interferon (IFN)-related genes were minimally or not induced (Supplementary Figs. 3A,B). Accordingly, BAY treatment significantly suppressed the expression of NF-κB-responsive genes, including IL-6, CXCL1, CXCL2 and NFKB1 (Fig. 4C).

Building on the transcriptomic findings, comparative gene ontology (GO) analysis further demonstrated that BAY modulated virus-induced perturbations in biological processes, particularly those associated with inflammation, immune regulation and viral replication (Supplementary Fig. 4). Consistently, representative genes from virus-induced gene sets associated with “inflammatory response” and “TNF-α signaling via NF-κB” are presented in the heatmap to illustrate gene expression changes (Fig. 4D). Importantly, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) provided statistical evidence for pathway-level downregulation, revealing that BAY broadly suppressed virus-induced inflammation-related signaling pathways, notably “TNF-α signaling via NF-κB” and “IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling” (Fig. 4E). Compared with vehicle-treated infected cells, BAY treatment was associated with higher enrichment of pathways involved in core cellular functions, including oxidative phosphorylation, mTORC1 signaling, glycolysis, and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 4E). Together, these results indicate that BAY suppresses virus-induced inflammatory responses and modulates host metabolic and signaling pathways in infected cells. Mechanistically, the transcriptomic changes suggest that BAY exerts its antiviral and anti-inflammatory effects, at least in part, through modulation of NF-κB signaling.

Potential binding of BAY to the SARS-CoV-2 NF-κB activator NSP14

Based on the transcriptomic results, we further investigated whether BAY’s dual inhibitory effects are mediated by targeting viral components that modulate NF-κB signaling. To explore this, we focused on SARS-CoV-2 proteins, which are known to strongly activate NF-κB. Among these, NSP14 was selected as a candidate target because it has been identified as a potent NF-κB activator and plays an essential role in viral RNA replication32–34. It is a bifunctional enzyme comprising an N-terminal exoribonuclease (ExoN) domain and a C-terminal N7-guanine methyltransferase (N7-MTase) domain, with the MTase domain being essential for both NF-κB activation35 and viral RNA capping36,37.

To determine whether BAY directly interacts with NSP14, we performed molecular docking analysis to evaluate their potential interactions. Docking results suggest that BAY may adopt a pose within a binding cavity located in the N7-MTase domain. This cavity is formed by β-sheet motifs (β9, β11 and β12) and surrounding helical regions, placing BAY in proximity to residues C309-R310, Q313, D331-N334, A337-I338, and L383-C387 (Fig. 5A,B). The docking model predicts several potential interactions, including a possible hydrogen bond with W385, with Q313 potentially participating in hydrogen bond formation, and A337 possibly interacting through a π-alkyl interaction. Additionally, residues within the β9 and β11 motifs are predicted to contribute to van der Waals contacts (Fig. 5B). Notably, many of these predicted interacting residues overlap with those previously identified as being involved in NF-κB activation34,38 and methyltransferase activity39. To further validate the direct interaction between BAY and NSP14, we performed a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA). Lysates from SARS-CoV-2-infected A549-ACE2 cells were incubated with BAY or vehicle control, and subsequently subjected to a temperature gradient ranging from 37.5 to 55.0 °C to assess the thermal stability of NSP14. Immunoblot analysis revealed that BAY treatment increased NSP14’s thermal stability, with detectable protein signals persisting at higher temperatures compared with the vehicle control. Band intensities were quantified and normalized to the lowest temperature point (37.5 °C, set to 100%), and the resulting thermal stability curves were fitted to estimate T50, defined as the temperature at which the normalized NSP14 signal decreased to 50%. The estimated T50 values were 42.68 ± 0.18 °C for the vehicle control and 45.87 ± 0.56 °C for the BAY-treated group, corresponding to a ΔT50 of + 3.19 ± 0.51 °C (mean ± SD, n = 3, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5C,D). These findings suggest a potential interaction between BAY and NSP14, raising the possibility that BAY may modulate NSP14-mediated functions.

BAY inhibited SARS-CoV-2-induced NF-κB activation by targeting the IKK-IκBα axis

Building on this observation, we next assessed BAY’s potential to modulate NF-κB signaling in SARS-CoV-2-infected cells. A549-ACE2 cells were infected with virus and subsequently treated with BAY or vehicle control to examine the effects on NF-κB signaling activity at 24 h post-infection. Immunoblot analysis revealed that the increase in NF-κB phosphorylation triggered by SARS-CoV-2 infection was dose-dependently suppressed by BAY, without altering total NF-κB protein levels (Fig. 6A,B). Since NSP14 has been shown to interact with components of the IKK complex, including IKKα and IKKβ, to promote canonical NF-κB signaling activation34, the effect of BAY on this upstream regulatory step was subsequently examined. Immunoblot analysis revealed that BAY treatment effectively reduced virus-induced IκBα phosphorylation, thereby preventing its ubiquitination-dependent degradation and restoring total IκBα levels (Fig. 6C,D).

To further investigate whether BAY-mediated inhibition of IκBα phosphorylation affects NF-κB nuclear translocation, subcellular fractionation followed by immunoblot analysis was performed. BAY treatment markedly reduced virus-induced NF-κB nuclear translocation, with no significant changes in cytoplasmic expression levels (Fig. 6E,F). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the proportion of NF-κB-positive nuclei in infected cells was significantly decreased following BAY treatment (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these findings indicate that BAY inhibits virus-induced NF-κB activation by preventing IκBα phosphorylation and degradation, highlighting the IKK-IκBα axis as a promising therapeutic target against SARS-CoV-2 infection.

BAY exhibited potent antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 variants

The continuous emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants carrying extensive mutations, particularly in the spike protein, poses a challenge to current antiviral strategies40. To evaluate whether the antiviral effects of BAY extend across different SARS-CoV-2 lineages, we examined its activity against several representative variants of concern (VOCs), including Alpha (B.1.1.7), Beta (B.1.351), Gamma (P.1), Delta (B.1.617.2), and a sublineage of Omicron (BA.1.17.2). A549-ACE2 cells were infected with each variant and treated with serial dilutions of BAY for 24 h. BAY treatment markedly reduced intracellular viral RNA levels in all tested variants in a dose-dependent manner, with comparable IC50 values ranging from 4.7 to 6.3 μM across lineages (Fig. 7). These findings demonstrate that BAY retains potent antiviral activity against a broad spectrum of SARS-CoV-2 variants, including immune-evasive strains such as Omicron.

To better understand the molecular basis of BAY’s cross-variant antiviral activity, we compared NSP14 sequences among major SARS-CoV-2 VOCs and seven other coronaviruses. Sequence alignment of the NSP14 N7-MTase domain revealed that residues predicted to be proximal to BAY are fully conserved across all major VOCs and remain highly conserved among other pathogenic coronaviruses, such as MERS-CoV, HCoV-OC43, and HCoV-NL63 (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, the functional relevance of this conservation—specifically, whether BAY can modulate NSP14-mediated functions and exert antiviral activity in other coronaviruses—remains to be addressed. Overall, our findings highlight BAY as a promising candidate for the development of effective therapies against SARS-CoV-2 and its emerging variants.

Discussion

The development of therapeutic agents with both antiviral and anti-inflammatory properties continues to be a critical unmet need in COVID-19 management. Increasing evidence indicates that immune dysregulation during the early stage of infection plays a pivotal role in disease progression41,42. Individuals with severe COVID-19 exhibit significantly elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines compared to those with mild disease43. While combination therapies employing separate antiviral and anti-inflammatory agents have been shown to reduce viral burden and ameliorate lung injury in SARS-CoV-2-infected animal models44, a single compound capable of simultaneously exerting both effects has yet to be identified. To our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize BAY as a compound with dual antiviral and anti-inflammatory activities against SARS-CoV-2 infection. BAY significantly reduced viral replication and suppressed virus-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine expression (Figs. 1–3). Time-of-addition analyses indicated that the antiviral activity of BAY is primarily exerted at a post-entry stage of the viral life cycle. No inhibitory effect was observed under pre-treatment or entry-associated conditions (Fig. 2), suggesting that BAY does not interfere with viral attachment or entry. Importantly, BAY treatment markedly decreased intracellular viral RNA levels, accompanied by a reduction in viral protein expression (Fig. 1B,C), supporting an effect on viral RNA accumulation or replication-associated processes, which may consequently reduce downstream viral protein production. Although BAY exhibits antiviral activity at a post-entry stage, the specific step affected—such as viral genome uncoating, viral RNA transcription, replication complex formation, or viral RNA processing—requires further mechanistic dissection. Overall, the dual actions of BAY in suppressing viral replication and modulating host-mediated inflammatory responses highlight its therapeutic potential for the treatment of COVID-19.

Early SARS-CoV-2 infection is characterized by a phenotype of elevated inflammation and attenuated interferon responses. Consistent with previous reports13,45, our transcriptomic analysis revealed that SARS-CoV-2 infection in A549-ACE2 cells significantly altered host gene expression networks, characterized by the upregulation of NF-κB-driven pro-inflammatory genes (e.g., IL6, IL8, NFKB1) and concurrent suppression of interferon-stimulated genes (e.g., IFNL) (Supplementary Fig. 3B). In contrast, some studies using intestinal epithelial cell models have reported upregulation of type I and III interferon-related genes following SARS-CoV-2 infection46. These discrepancies are most likely explained by differences in experimental conditions, including cell types, viral inoculum, and the timeline of sample collection. Despite such variation in cytokine responses, sustained activation of NF-κB can be observed across SARS-CoV-2 infection models. This selective activation of NF-κB is a conserved strategy shared by other viruses, including HIV-147, Epstein-Barr virus48, and IAV26,49. Therefore, therapeutics targeting the NF-κB signaling pathway hold significant potential not only to alleviate acute infection-induced hyper-inflammation but also to serve as a crucial broad-spectrum antiviral intervention.

The precise mechanisms by which SARS-CoV-2 manipulates NF-κB signaling during the early stage of infection are not yet fully understood. Previous studies showed that systematic overexpression of individual SARS-CoV-2 proteins, combined with NF-κB reporter assays, identified several viral proteins, including NSP14, ORF3a, and ORF7a, as NF-κB activators34,50–52. Among these viral proteins, NSP14 is translated directly from the ORF1b region of the incoming viral genomic RNA (gRNA), independent of subgenomic RNA (sgRNA) intermediates53. This direct translation enables the rapid and early functional engagement of NSP14, creating an advantage for viral replication. Moreover, the resulting temporal advantage may facilitate immune evasion, as NSP14 not only suppresses IFN-I responses by inhibiting IFN-β expression and blocking IRF3 nuclear translocation54–56, but also promotes NF-κB-driven inflammatory signaling. Mechanistically, NSP14 directly interacts with the IKK complex components (IKKα and IKKβ)34 and/or the NF-κB essential modulator (NEMO)38 to promote canonical NF-κB signaling. BAY is a well-established inhibitor of the IKK-IκBα-NF-κB pathway and can directly suppress host NF-κB signaling independently of viral infection. This is reflected by the stabilization of IκBα levels in uninfected cells (Fig. 6C,D). Upon SARS-CoV-2 infection, BAY selectively inhibited IκBα phosphorylation and degradation, thereby reducing NF-κB nuclear translocation (Fig. 6E,F) and suppressing virus-induced NF-κB activation. Molecular docking and CETSA results suggest a potential interaction between BAY and NSP14, supporting a role in subsequent NF-κB regulation (Fig. 5). Moreover, the antiviral effects of BAY may further limit virus-driven NF-κB activation. Accordingly, the observed anti-inflammatory effects of BAY can be attributed to the combination of: (i) direct inhibition of host NF-κB signaling; (ii) potential modulation of NSP14-mediated NF-κB activation; and (iii) reduced viral replication, which may additionally limit virus-driven inflammatory signaling. The relative contributions of these pathways cannot be definitively determined with the current data and thus warrant further investigation.

Molecular docking analysis suggests that BAY may occupy a putative groove within the NSP14 N7-MTase domain, delineated by the β-sheet surface comprising strands β9, β11, and β12 (Fig. 5A,B). Notably, residues D331 and G333 within the conserved DIGNPK motif (β9) have been implicated in maintaining the local conformation of NSP14 and in promoting NSP14-mediated NF-κB activation34. Several residues predicted to contact BAY overlap with those previously validated as functionally relevant to NSP14-mediated NF-κB activation34. In addition, a recently identified NSP14 inhibitor (compound C10) features a sulfonamide moiety at the reported R1 position that is structurally similar to the vinyl sulfonyl group of BAY57. C10 has been proposed to occupy a region adjacent to the site predicted for BAY, with several residues implicated in its interaction with the SAM-binding pocket predicted to be near residues that may contact BAY. Although CETSA and docking analyses suggest potential BAY-NSP14 engagement, they do not definitively demonstrate that BAY’s antiviral or anti-inflammatory effects are mediated through NSP14. Future studies employing NSP14 pocket-site mutants, together with direct assessment of BAY’s impact on SAM-dependent methyltransferase activity, will be necessary to further evaluate whether BAY functionally engages NSP14-mediated functions.

Many viruses manipulate host metabolism to evade immunity and facilitate their replication. Dysregulated metabolism can lead to uncontrolled inflammatory responses, as observed in severe COVID-19 patients58. Among these, NF-κB signaling is widely considered to be closely linked to cellular metabolism, as its persistent activation during viral infection can drive metabolic shifts that favor viral replication59. In interpreting BAY-associated enrichment patterns, it should be noted that the present study did not include an uninfected BAY-only transcriptomic control, which limits attribution of the observed signatures to BAY baseline effects and/or infection-dependent changes. Nevertheless, in infected cells, BAY treatment was associated with higher enrichment of key metabolic pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, mTORC1 signaling, glycolysis, and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 4E). The enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation-related genes in BAY-treated cells suggests a potential shift in cellular energy metabolism, which could be associated with antiviral responses. The observed enhancement of mTORC1 signaling is indicative of potential roles in protein synthesis, cellular repair, and immune activation60. Notably, the apparent upregulation of fatty acid metabolism is particularly intriguing, as this pathway has been reported to be suppressed during SARS-CoV-2 infection in both in vitro models and clinical COVID-19 patients61. Modulating lipid metabolism has been shown to impede viral replication, as evidenced by treatment with orlistat, a lipase inhibitor, or by silencing fatty acid synthase (FASN)62. Moreover, viral replication depends on host lipid droplet turnover, and pharmacological inhibition of lipolysis using agents such as tetrahydrolipstatin (THL) or atorvastatin markedly reduces viral spread63. Collectively, these findings suggest that BAY is associated with enhanced antiviral defenses through modulation of host metabolic functions and inflammation, and that it holds translational potential as a host-directed therapy.

In summary, BAY acts as a dual-function compound, simultaneously reducing SARS-CoV-2 replication and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Mechanistically, BAY inhibits SARS-CoV-2-induced NF-κB activation, which may involve direct suppression of host NF-κB signaling and potential modulation of virus-mediated NF-κB activation via the IKK-IκBα signaling axis (Fig. 8). Additionally, BAY’s retained antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 variants further underscores its potential as a therapeutic candidate for COVID-19.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

A549 cells overexpressing human ACE2 (A549-ACE2) (#101005; National Institute for Biological Standards and Control (NIBSC), UK) were cultured in Ham’s F-12 (Kaighn’s) medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM glutamine, and 200 μg/mL hygromycin B. Calu-3 (HTB-55; American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), USA) and Vero E6 (CRL-1586; ATCC) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS. All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere in the presence of 5% CO2, and all of them tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Viruses

This study used the following six SARS-CoV-2 viral strains: the original strain (BetaCoV/Taiwan/4/2020; Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (TCDC), Taipei, Taiwan), Alpha strain (lineage B.1.1.7) (NR-54011; BEI Resources, Manassas, VA, USA), Beta strain (lineage B.1.351) (NR-54009; BEI Resources), Gamma strain (lineage P.1) (NR-54982; BEI Resources), Delta strain (lineage B.1.617.2) (hCoV-19/Taiwan/1144/2021; TCDC), and Omicron strain (lineage BA.1.17.2) (hCoV-19/Taiwan/16804/2021; TCDC). All procedures involving live viruses were conducted in a biosafety level 3 facility following the biosafety regulations. Viral stocks were propagated and amplified in Vero E6 cells, and viral titers were determined before subsequent use.

Chemical compounds and antibodies

BAY 11-7082 (BAY), (E)3-[(4-Methylphenyl)sulfonyl]-2-propenenitrile, (#B5556; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and stock solutions were aliquoted and stored for subsequent use. The antibodies used for Western blotting (WB) and immunofluorescence (IF) and their respective working dilutions are listed as follows: mouse anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein (#33717; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; WB, 1:1,000), mouse anti-dsRNA (#76651; Cell Signaling Technology; IF, 1:500), rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2 N protein (#86326; Cell Signaling Technology; WB, 1:1,000), rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2 NSP14 (#ab286920; Abcam, Cambridge, UK; WB, 1:1,000), rabbit anti-phospho-NF-κB (p65) (#3033; Cell Signaling Technology; WB, 1:1,000), mouse anti-NF-κB (p65) (#6956; Cell Signaling Technology; WB, 1:1,000), rabbit anti-NF-κB (p65) (#8242; Cell Signaling Technology; IF, 1:500), mouse anti-phospho-IκBα (#9246; Cell Signaling Technology; WB, 1:1,000), rabbit anti-IκBα (#4812; Cell Signaling Technology; WB, 1:1,000), rabbit anti-GAPDH (#5174; Cell Signaling Technology; WB, 1:1,000), mouse anti-GAPDH (#97166; Cell Signaling Technology; WB, 1:1,000), rabbit anti-Lamin B1 (#ZRB1143; Sigma-Aldrich; WB, 1:1,000), goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (#7074; Cell Signaling Technology; WB, 1:2,000), goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (#7076; Cell Signaling Technology; WB, 1:2,000), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse IgG (#A-21202; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; IF, 1:500), and Alexa Fluor 647 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (#A-31573; Invitrogen; IF, 1:500).

Cell cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic effect of BAY was evaluated by the CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay (#G7571; Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Briefly, cells were seeded on culture plates one day before treatment and exposed to serial dilutions of BAY for a specified duration. The cell viability assay was then performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Raw data obtained from three independent experiments were used to calculate the 50% cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of BAY through a nonlinear regression method in GraphPad Prism (version 5.0; https://www.graphpad.com/).

Cellular infections with fully replicative SARS-CoV-2

A549-ACE2 or Calu-3 cells were seeded in 6-, 12-, or 24-well tissue culture plates at densities of 6 × 105, 2 × 105, or 1 × 105 cells per well, respectively, one day prior to infection. Cells were infected with SARS-CoV-2 at the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI) for 1 h. The inoculum was then removed, and cells were treated with BAY until sample collection. For experiments analyzing inflammatory responses in host cells, the inoculum was retained. Supernatants were collected for viral titration or cytokine analysis, and cell lysates were harvested for viral RNA, cytokine gene expression, or protein analysis.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription qPCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA was extracted using an EZ1 Advanced XL automated nucleic acid purification system (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Total RNA from cell lysates was extracted using an EZ1&2 RNA Tissue Mini Kit (#959034; Qiagen), and viral RNA from culture supernatants was extracted using an EZ1&2 Virus Mini Kit v.2.0 (#955134; Qiagen). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for each kit. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (#11766050; Invitrogen). The cDNA was used as a template for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) with gene-specific primers and the LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master kit (#0407516001; Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Cycle threshold (Ct) values were determined using a LightCycler 480 system (Roche Diagnostics), and relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH or HPRT using the 2−ΔΔCT method64. To quantify viral RNA in the supernatants, the relative expression levels of viral RNA in the tested samples were measured and normalized to the quantified national standards65. The primer sequences used in this study were provided in Supplementary Table 1.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. These libraries were then sequenced on a NovaSeq X platform (Illumina). Filtered reads were aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome (GENCODE GRCh38.p14 primary assembly) using Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR, version 2.7.11b; https://anaconda.org/bioconda/star)66, and transcript abundance was quantified using Salmon (version 1.11.4; https://anaconda.org/bioconda/salmon)67, with a transcriptome file generated through GENCODE GRCh38.p14 annotation using GffRead (version 0.12.7; https://anaconda.org/bioconda/gffread). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (version 1.44.0; https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html) in R (version 4.4.1; https://cran.r-project.org/)68. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.01 and an absolute log2 fold change of ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Heatmaps were generated using pheatmap (version 1.0.13; https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap) to visualize the expression patterns of selected gene lists across different conditions. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using Fast Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (fgsea, version 1.30.0; https://github.com/ctlab/fgsea/) to identify enriched pathways from the MSigDB Hallmark gene sets (h.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt)69.

Assessment of antiviral effectiveness

The antiviral activity of BAY was assessed as the minimal concentration required to achieve 50% inhibition (IC50) of either the viral infectious titer or the viral RNA content. For viral infectious titer determination, Vero E6 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well tissue culture plates containing DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and incubated overnight prior to infection. Virus-containing supernatants harvested from DMSO- or BAY-treated cells were subjected to tenfold serial dilutions (ranging from 10−1 to 10−8), after which 100 µL of each dilution was inoculated onto Vero E6 cells for 5 days. Cytopathic effects (CPE) were observed under a phase-contrast microscope, and TCID50 values were calculated using the Reed-Muench method70. For determination of viral RNA content in variant viruses, viral RNA was extracted from virus-containing supernatants as mentioned above, and subjected to RT-qPCR analysis. IC50 values were calculated from three independent experiments using nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism (version 5.0).

Subcellular fractionation

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions of A549-ACE2 cells infected with SARS-CoV-2, with or without BAY treatment, were isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions using a CelLytic NuCLEAR Extraction Kit (NXTRACT; Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were harvested, pelleted, and resuspended in the 1 × lysis buffer containing 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and a protease inhibitor. IGEPAL CA-630 solution was then added to a final concentration of 1%, and samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g to separate the cytoplasmic fraction. The nuclear pellets were subsequently incubated on ice in the nuclear extraction buffer and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min (min) to collect the nuclear fraction.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

CETSA was performed as previously described71. In brief, SARS-CoV-2-infected A549-ACE2 cells were collected and inactivated by ultraviolet (UV) radiation (2 × 105 μJ/cm2)72. The cell lysates were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles, and the soluble fraction was separated from cell debris by centrifugation at 20,000 × g at 4 °C for 20 min. The lysates were then divided equally into two aliquots and incubated with 20 μM BAY or a diluent control at room temperature for 30 min. The resulting samples were further divided into 50 μL aliquots in PCR tubes, and were heated individually at different temperatures for 3 min (Veriti Thermal Cycler, Applied Biosystems, USA), followed by cooling to 25 °C for 3 min. The heated samples were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were transferred to new tubes for subsequent immunoblot analysis.

Immunoblot analysis

Cell lysis was achieved using RIPA lysis buffer (#20-188; Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor (#PPC1010; Sigma-Aldrich). A Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (#23200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to quantify protein concentration for equal loading. During this process, a sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer (#1610747; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) containing 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol was added, and the samples were heated to denature proteins. The resulting proteins were then separated using 4–15% Mini-PROTEAN TGX precast gels (#4561084; Bio-Rad Laboratories) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (#1620177; Bio-Rad Laboratories). After blocking with 5% (w/v) milk in PBS, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Subsequently, the membranes were washed with PBS supplemented with 0.1% Tween 20 and incubated with horseradish-peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, followed by signal development using an Amersham ECL WB Reagent (#RPN2106; Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Alternatively, an Amersham ECL WB Select Reagent (#RPN2235; Cytiva) was used to enhance weak signals. Finally, signals were visualized using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and quantified using the Image Lab Software (version 6.1; https://www.bio-rad.com/).

Immunofluorescence and signal quantification

Cells on 12-mm glass coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS solution, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100/PBS solution, and blocked with 2.5% BSA/PBS solution. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA/PBS solution. After washing, the samples were further probed with corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor dyes (Invitrogen) for 1 h. Finally, the samples were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, #MBD0015; Sigma-Aldrich), mounted with Fluoromount-G mounting medium (#00-4958-02; Invitrogen), and viewed under a ZEISS LSM 700 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with a 40 × oil immersion objective lens with a numerical aperture of 1.4. Raw images were processed and analyzed using the ZEISS ZEN 2.3 (blue edition) software (https://www.zeiss.com/).

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of the SARS-CoV-2 NSP14 receptor protein was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). The apo form of NSP14 (PDB ID: 7QGI) was used as a reference template for structural alignment and superimposition with five ligand-bound NSP14 complexes (PDB IDs: 8R7B, 5SKX, 7QIF, 8BWU, and 7R2V). Among these, the structure with the lowest RMSD value (PDB ID: 7QIF) was selected for molecular docking studies (Supplementary Table 2). The receptor was prepared with a sphere radius of 13.362844 Å, which was designed to encompass the ligand-binding pocket formed by the essential amino acid residues involved in NF-κB signaling activation within the NSP14 domain34. A molecular docking simulation was performed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2025 (https://www.3ds.com/) with the CDOCKER docking protocol. The receptor was kept rigid, while the ligand was docked flexibly into the binding pocket. The top-ranked docking pose was selected, and intermolecular interactions between the ligand and receptor were analyzed to identify key binding features.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for cytokine and chemokine analysis

Supernatants from SARS-CoV-2-infected cells, with or without BAY treatment, were first inactivated by UV exposure as described above. Prior to cytokine and chemokine measurements, a preliminary test was performed to determine the optimal dilution factor for each assay. CXCL1/GROα (#BMS2122; Invitrogen), CXCL2/GROβ (#EEL054; Invitrogen), IL-6 (#EH2IL6; Invitrogen), and IL-8/CXCL8 (#KHC0081; Invitrogen) were detected using ELISA kits in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using an automated microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1). The concentrations of cytokines and chemokines in each sample were determined from standard curves derived from known standards.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 5.0). At least three independent experiments were conducted, and all yielded similar results. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Between-group comparisons were conducted using an unpaired Student’s t-test, and multiple-group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni’s posttest. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, or ***p < 0.001.
