Evaluating continuous identity cognitive therapy for veterans with a recent suicidal episode: An open-label group pilot study
Authors: Yosef Sokol, Sofie Glatt, Sarah Andrusier, Chynna Levin, Caroline Boucher, Josephine Ridley, Clayton H. Brown, Yulia Landa, Shirley Glynn, Marianne Goodman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThere is a gap in effective recovery-oriented treatments for Veterans experiencing suicidal thoughts and/or behaviors, particularly those recovering from a suicidal episode. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of Continuous Identity Cognitive Therapy (CI-CT), a novel recovery-oriented psychotherapy for Veterans with a history of a recent suicidal episode, and to refine the intervention through an iterative development process. CI-CT integrates theories of personal identity and selfhood within a cognitive therapy framework. It aims to repair personal identity through the construction of a coherent, meaningful self-narrative connecting the present to a clear, detailed, realistic, and desired future self.MethodThree one-arm trials of CI-CT were conducted to evaluate feasibility and acceptability of the therapy. Trials were conducted iteratively, with each trial incorporating lessons and modifications from the previous one. Participants (N = 15 consented, with N = 12 initiating therapy and 11 completing the full intervention) were U.S. Veterans with a history of a suicide attempt or plan with intent within the past two years.ResultsCI-CT had high levels of feasibility and acceptability based on recruitment rates, attendance rates, low dropout rates, high completion rate of follow-up assessments, and participant feedback. In addition, there were high levels of measured client satisfaction and positive qualitative feedback.DiscussionThe high attendance and retention rates and positive Veteran feedback support further exploration and testing of CI-CT in a randomized clinical trial.Clinical trial registrationNCT04731519.

Introduction

Suicide is a major public health issue nationwide, and an especially pressing concern within the Veteran population. In 2022, suicide was ranked as the 11th leading cause of death in the United States with 49,369 deaths, representing an age-adjusted rate of 14.2 per 100,000 population [1]. This concern is particularly acute within the Veteran population. In the same year, 6407 Veterans died by suicide, with the age-adjusted suicide rate for Veterans being approximately 1.5 times higher than non-Veterans after adjusting for age and sex differences [2].

Beyond immediate safety concerns, Veterans navigating the post-acute suicidal episode (PASE) phase require support for long-term recovery. This period, varyingly described as the first 6 months to the first two years after the acute episode, where acute risk has subsided, but vulnerability persists [3,4], is often characterized by disruptions to personal identity—an individual's core sense of self, values, and roles. It can also diminish future self-continuity, which is the perceived connection between one's present and future self [5,6]. Consequently, theoretical frameworks that address these specific challenges are needed. Three such frameworks are particularly relevant: narrative identity (the life story we construct to make sense of ourselves), future self-continuity (FSC; the subjective perception of connection, similarity, and vividness between one's current self and anticipated future self), and personal recovery (the process of building a meaningful life even with persistent symptoms) [5,13,49]. These models suggest that developing a coherent life story, a positive and connected sense of future self, and hope, meaning, and empowerment are likely central to helping PASE individuals.

The treatment of suicidal thoughts and behaviors primarily follows clinical and risk-based models aimed at alleviating symptoms and reducing immediate risk [7]. Current evidence-based treatments, such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Suicide Prevention (CBT-SP; Stanley et al. [8]), Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT; Linehan [9]), and the Collaborative Assessment and Management of Suicidality (CAMS; Jobes [10]), provide critical strategies for managing acute risk, developing coping skills, and addressing underlying psychopathology. DBT, for instance, aims broadly to help individuals build a ‘life worth living’ and includes components addressing emotional regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness.

However, in practice, standard DBT protocols typically prioritize safety and foundational skills by focusing on achieving behavioral control (Stage 1) and, increasingly, processing the past, including the resolution of childhood trauma (Stage 2) [9,11]. More broadly, while vital, these established approaches often do not center on the reconstruction of personal identity, narrative meaning-making, or strengthening future self-continuity. These represent profound needs, especially during the PASE phase as research has consistently shown that identity disruption and diminished future self-continuity can lead to suicide related cognitions, including hopelessness, thwarted belongingness, and an inability to envision a viable future [[3], [4], [5], [6],12]. These identity and existential needs align closely with DBT's later stages (Stages 3 and 4), which focus on achieving greater personal growth and meaning [9]. While these later stages are conceptualized as part of DBT [9], they lack specific accepted protocols and widespread empirical validation in routine practice compared to Stages 1 and 2. [9]. The gap in later stage treatment availability may be clinically significant based on the persistently high rates of suicide attempts and rehospitalizations post-discharge [3,4] which suggest a need for comprehensive PASE care specifically targeting longer-term recovery processes.

Personal recovery

In contrast to clinical models focused primarily on symptom remission, the personal recovery movement prioritizes finding and maintaining meaning, purpose, hope, and a positive sense of self, even alongside persistent mental health symptoms [13]. In the context of suicidal thoughts and behaviors, this perspective involves shifting emphasis from solely mitigating risk factors towards increasing protective factors and the capacity to thrive despite potentially recurring suicidal thoughts [[14], [15], [16]]. The personal recovery goal expands beyond survival to flourishing, with increasing intrinsic motivation to live. While established treatments like CAMS (with its collaborative stance [10]), Safety Planning (with its strength-based focus [17]), and DBT (with its goal of building a life worth living [9]) incorporate recovery-oriented elements, their primary mechanisms are not explicitly designed to facilitate the multifaceted process of personal recovery defined by frameworks like CHIME (Connectedness, Hope, Identity, Meaning, Empowerment; Leamy et al. [18]; Sokol et al. [12]). Continuous Identity Cognitive Therapy (CI-CT) was developed to complement existing acute and skills-based interventions by specifically addressing this gap through offering a fully recovery-oriented approach focused on helping individuals construct a coherent and valued sense of self from present to future.

Identity and self-continuity

A coherent and positive sense of identity is increasingly recognized as a central component of personal recovery [18]. Recovery from a suicidal episode, in particular, may be closely linked to how an individual rebuilds and relates to their sense of self [15]. Individuals experiencing suicidal symptoms often report difficulties envisioning a positive future and may perceive their sense of self as fragmented or rapidly changing [5]. These difficulties can lead to a weakened sense of FSC [19]. Diminished FSC and disruptions to the broader construct of ‘continuous identity’ (CI; the sense of having a unified, persistent self over time) are relevant to suicide risk [6]. Lower FSC has been linked empirically to higher levels of depression, hopelessness, reduced meaning in life [21,22], suicidal ideation, and behavior [6,20]. Individuals with diminished FSC may struggle to see current difficulties within a broader life context or to make present choices that benefit their long-term well-being [6]. Identity challenges can be particularly salient for Veterans. While the transition from military to civilian life presents unique identity adjustments for some [23,24], broader identity issues related to service experiences, trauma, disability, moral injury, role changes, and aging can persist or emerge throughout a Veteran's lifespan [25,26]. Therefore, addressing identity coherence and fostering a positive future outlook may be especially pertinent in supporting recovery for Veterans who have experienced suicidal crises [29].

Continuous identity cognitive therapy

Continuous Identity Cognitive Therapy (CI-CT) is a novel, manualized, recovery-oriented group psychotherapy developed by the authors, first for Veterans with a Serious Mental Illness (SMI), and then adapted for Veterans with a history of suicidal thoughts and/or behaviors. [12,27]. It integrates principles from cognitive therapy with theories of personal identity, narrative psychology, and self-continuity. The core premise is that strengthening an individual's sense of a coherent, positive, and continuous self extending into the future can foster hope, meaning, and motivation for living, particularly during the PASE phase. CI-CT involves a structured yet collaborative process where participants construct a meaningful self-narrative bridging their present experiences with a desired, realistic future self. Key components aim to enhance FSC, clarify life values, develop a self-growth perspective, connect with positive future possibilities, and reframe current challenges within a broader life story context [12,27]. For instance, a Veteran struggling with hopelessness might be guided to identify a core personal value, such as ‘dependability,’ and then construct a narrative of how they can enact that value in a small, concrete way in the coming week (e.g., calling a friend when promised), thereby bridging their present to a future more aligned with their desired self. These components align closely with general personal recovery principles (CHIME; Leamy et al. [18]) and a suicide-specific recovery model known as COURAGE (Choosing life, Optimizing identity, Understanding one's self, Rediscovering meaning, Acceptance, Growing connectedness, and Empowerment; Sokol et al. [15]). The group format itself is intended to increase connectedness and mutual support, reinforcing members' sense of value and belonging, key aspects of universal therapeutic factors in group therapy [28].

An earlier 8-module CI-CT version (delivered in a VA intensive outpatient setting with four weekly, 3-h group sessions) showed feasibility and acceptability among Veterans with SMI, alongside promising preliminary effects on suicidal symptoms and related outcomes [12]. Feedback from that pilot, alongside extensive consultation with Veteran stakeholders and scientific mentors, prompted significant revisions to the intervention before this study. These revisions included expanding the planned structure to 12 sessions, explicitly incorporating elements targeting connectedness and other processes outlined in the COURAGE model [15], and refining workbook materials for clarity and relevance. Given these substantial modifications to the protocol and the shift in focus towards the specific recovery needs of Veterans in the PASE phase (a potentially distinct population from those with broader SMI diagnoses), the current study was undertaken as a necessary step in iterative treatment development.

This study

This paper describes the evaluation of the revised CI-CT protocol for PASE Veterans through a series of three iterative, one-arm open-label trials. Our primary objectives were to assess the feasibility and acceptability of delivering this manualized telehealth intervention to PASE Veterans. Secondary objectives included gathering data to further refine the CI-CT facilitator manual and Veteran workbook and conducting exploratory analyses of changes in clinical outcomes to inform future research. We hypothesized that CI-CT would demonstrate adequate feasibility and high acceptability based on pre-defined benchmarks for recruitment, retention, attendance, satisfaction, and implementation effort.

Methods

Study design

The study comprised two stages designed for the iterative development and feasibility testing of Continuous Identity Cognitive Therapy (CI-CT) for PASE Veterans. The first stage involved initial modification of existing CI-CT materials based on stakeholder feedback. The second stage consisted of three sequential, one-arm, open-label trials conducted over 10 months (October 2022 to August 2023) at the James J. Peters Department of Veteran Affairs Medical Center (JJPVAMC). Following the conceptual framework outlined by Bowen et al. [44] for feasibility studies and the importance of pre-defined success criteria as recommended by Thabane et al. [45], this study was designed to assess multiple facets of CI-CT's potential application before larger-scale efficacy testing. The primary objective of these trials was to assess the feasibility and acceptability of the revised CI-CT intervention delivered via telehealth. The iterative nature of the trials allowed for refinements to the protocol based on accumulating data and participant feedback. The first trial arm began as a closed cohort. Based on feedback and to improve recruitment flow, the subsequent two trial arms utilized a rolling admission model.

Ethics considerations

All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the JJPVAMC Institutional Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation in any study activities, including confirmation of their understanding of the study procedures, risks, benefits, and confidentiality. Participant confidentiality was maintained throughout the study; all data were coded and stored securely according to institutional and federal guidelines.

Development of materials pre one-arm trials

Prior to this study, an initial pilot of an earlier CI-CT version (8 modules delivered over four 3-h sessions) for Veterans with SMI indicated feasibility but suggested areas for optimization [12]. Key feedback included needing a slower pace and integrating concepts from the COURAGE model [15], particularly enhancing connectedness. This led to an initial plan to expand the intervention to 12 modules delivered over 12 weekly 90-min sessions. To further refine these planned 12-session materials before the current trials, we conducted two rounds of feedback collection, one with Veterans recruited via CloudResearch (N = 14 first round, N = 7 s round) who had self-reported histories of suicidal ideation or attempts, and another with scientific stakeholders (N = 6, including experts in suicide prevention, Veteran mental health, and psychotherapy development). Feedback focused on language sensitivity, exercise effectiveness, and therapy content, guiding revisions to the facilitator manual and Veteran workbook. Specific changes included adding physical goal reminders (e.g., values cards), rephrasing potentially triggering language, shortening specific exercises, tailoring examples, and refining terminology.

Material development during one-arm trials

Consistent with an iterative development approach, feedback from participants and facilitators was collected throughout the three one-arm trials. This feedback informed further modifications to intervention content, assessment tools, and delivery methods aimed at improving feasibility and acceptability in a practical clinical context. Post-trial, findings were reviewed with a Scientific Advisory Board (SAB) and Community Advisory Board (CAB) to finalize the protocol evaluated herein.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were Veterans receiving mental health services at the JJPVAMC, referred primarily by clinical providers or suicide prevention coordinators. Veterans were contacted via phone; up to three attempts were made before discontinuing contact. Consent and all assessment measures were collected via phone by the study coordinators. Inclusion criteria targeted Veterans considered to be in the post-acute suicide episode (PASE) stage. For this study, this high-risk post-crisis phase was operationalized as the two-year period following a suicide attempt or a plan with intent and preparatory behavior, supported by evidence that 93 % of suicide deaths and 88 % of reattempts occur within the first 2 years post-index attempt, with overall suicide mortality risk approximately 25 times higher than the general population [48]. Initially, this history was required within the past year to capture the more immediate post-acute phase. However, due to slower-than-anticipated recruitment during the initial trial phase, this criterion was expanded to include events within the past two years to achieve the target sample size necessary for assessing feasibility. While pragmatically necessary, this decision was also supported by the clinical rationale that CI-CT addresses identity disruption and recovery needs which often persist well beyond one year post-crisis. Nonetheless, this expansion introduces potential heterogeneity regarding time since the index suicidal event, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Inclusion Criteria: All participants met the following criteria: (1) minimum 18 years of age; (2) receiving mental health services at the JJPVAMC; (3) Veteran of the US Military; (4) resident of the NYC region; (5) access to a reliable device with internet/webcam for telehealth; (6) sufficient clinical stability for group therapy (as determined by the referring provider's clinical judgment); (7) sufficient medical stability (as determined by the referring provider's clinical judgment); and (8) elevated suicide risk with history of attempt or plan with intent/preparatory behavior. Exclusion Criteria: (1) active alcohol/opiate dependence requiring medically supervised withdrawal; (2) imminent suicidal/homicidal risk; (3) acute suicidal episode within the past week; (4) current high levels of acute suicidal ideation or intent, defined by a score >23.5 on the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSSI; representing two standard deviations above the mean in a clinical outpatient sample; Beck et al., [30]) or a score ≥5 on item 4 of the SBQ-R (indicating a high likelihood of making an attempt soon; Osman et al., [31]) (5) inability to speak English; (6) lack of capacity to consent; (7) unwillingness/inability to provide emergency contact; (8) inability to attend outpatient group; (9) current participation in another intervention RCT; or (10) insufficient interpersonal functioning for group therapy (assessed via referrer consultation and chart review).

Intervention: Continuous Identity Cognitive Therapy (CI-CT)

As described in the Introduction, CI-CT is a manualized, recovery-oriented group therapy focusing on identity reconstruction and future self-continuity for PASE individuals [27]. The version evaluated in these trials was delivered by two clinician co-facilitators via VA Webex. After each session, the PI (treatment developer) met with the other group facilitator to discuss treatment delivery, model adherence, and any specific clinical issues that arose during the session.

Based on pre-trial development, the intervention was initially planned as 12 weekly 90-min sessions. However, during the iterative one-arm trials (specifically, after the first arm), participant feedback regarding the 12-week commitment and logistical challenges with cohort-based starts prompted modifications. To enhance feasibility and accommodate rolling admission (allowing participants to join ongoing groups), the core intervention cycle was reduced to 10 weekly 90-min sessions. This involved integrating essential introductory/wrap-up elements into the remaining sessions or workbook materials. The shift to rolling admission aimed to improve recruitment flow and minimize delays for interested Veterans. Protocol deviations/modifications not related to iterative development

Beyond the planned iterative refinements described above and summarized in Table 5, several other deviations from the original study protocol ([27]; NCT04731519) occurred. As noted, the PASE inclusion window was expanded from one to two years. The primary screening tool shifted from the C-SSRS to a chart review combined with the BSSI/SBQ-R item 4 screen described above, with the C-SSRS administered at baseline; this was feasible as most referrals came from a prior study with overlapping criteria [32]. Assessment of ability to engage with workbook materials was done via the consent process rather than a separate task. Furthermore, due to data collection errors, only the “goal and success orientation” subscale items of the Recovery Assessment Scale (RAS) were consistently administered. Similarly, only item 4 of the SBQ-R was used for screening, precluding analysis of the full-scale score. These data collection issues did not affect the primary aims of assessing feasibility and acceptability but limited the scope of exploratory outcome analyses.

Protocol and procedure

Participants began the intervention within two weeks of providing informed consent. Baseline assessments (Timepoint (TP)-1) were completed pre-intervention. Follow-up assessments were completed at post-intervention (TP-2) and 1-month post-intervention (TP-3). This paper reports data from baseline (TP-1), post-intervention (TP-2), and the 1-month follow-up (TP-3, used for satisfaction measures), focusing on feasibility and immediate post-intervention exploratory outcomes. Participants were compensated for assessment completion ($45 for TP-1, $55 for TP-2, $60 for TP-3) but not for session attendance. The PI served as the primary facilitator. Sessions followed the manualized content, with qualitative feedback collected post-session.

Exploratory outcome measures

The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS; Posner et al. [33]) was used to measure the severity of suicidal ideation and behavior. It is regarded as a gold standard tool for suicide risk assessment [34]. The severity of ideation subscale is scored on a 6-point ordinal scale, ranging from 0 (no suicidal ideation) to 5 (active suicidal intent with a specific plan), capturing increasing levels of suicidal thought severity. The intensity of ideation subscale consists of six items—frequency, duration, controllability, deterrents, reasons for ideation, and overall intensity—each rated on an ordinal scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 25, reflecting the depth and persistence of suicidal thoughts. The behavior subscale categorizes suicidal and self-injurious behaviors, including actual suicide attempts, interrupted attempts, aborted attempts, preparatory acts, and non-suicidal self-injurious behavior, assessing their presence, frequency, and lethality rather than using a single ordinal scale.

Recovery Assessment Scale (RAS; Corrigan et al. [35]) Subscale. The ‘goal and success orientation’ subscale of the RAS was used to evaluate aspects of the personal recovery process (Corrigan et al., 2004). This subscale consists of several items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher mean scores indicating greater recovery in this domain.

Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS; Beck et al. [36]). The BHS, a 20-item dichotomous (true/false) self-report measure that assesses feelings about the future, loss of motivation, and expectations, was used to assess hopelessness. Total scores are created by reverse-coding appropriate items and summing the item scores, with higher scores indicating greater hopelessness (range 0–20). The BHS has demonstrated adequate reliability and concurrent validity predictive capacity for suicidality across psychiatric samples [[36], [37], [38], [39]].

Future Self Continuity Questionnaire (FSCQ; Sokol & Serper [19]). The FSCQ assesses individuals' temporal sense of personal identity from the present to the future in three areas: vividness, similarity, and positivity. There are 10 items with a 6-point response metric, with higher scores indicating increased FSC. Sample items include, “Do you like what you will be like 10 years from now?” and “How similar are your beliefs now to what they will be like 10 years from now?” The total FSCQ score is averaged from all items and subscale scores are calculated from the mean of associated items. Higher scores indicate increased FSC. Both the total FSCQ and the FSCQ components have high levels of internal reliability, test-retest scores, and convergent and discriminant validity [19].

Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire (INQ; Van Orden et al. [40]). The INQ, a 15-item assessment of thwarted belongingness (9 items; scores range from 9 to 63) and perceived burdensomeness (6 items; scores range from 6 to 42), was used to assess a sense of belonging and burden. Individuals provide self-report responses on a 7-point metric ranging from 1 (Not at all true for me) to 7 (Very true for me). Appropriate items are reverse-coded, and the total score is calculated from the summed item scores, with higher scores indicating greater levels of each. The INQ has demonstrated strong psychometric properties [40].

Lubben Social Network Scale-Revised (LSNS-R; Lubben [41]). The LSNS-R, a 12-item measure that assesses social support received by family, friends, and mutual supports, was used to assess social networks. Scores for each question range from zero to five, with zero indicating minimal social integration and five indicating substantial social integration. The total score is summed from all item scores (range 0–60), with higher scores indicating a greater level of social support and low risk for isolation. The LSNS-R has high internal consistency and has been used in clinical and non-clinical populations [41].

World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF; Whoqol Group [42]). The WHOQOL-BREF is a 26-item instrument assessing four domains of quality of life (QOL): physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environment. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Domain scores were transformed to a 0–100 scale by multiplying the mean domain score by 25 [(domain score - 4) × 25], per WHOQOL-BREF scoring guidelines, with higher scores indicating higher QOL. The WHOQOL-BREF has high internal consistency, test-retest reliability, discriminant validity, and construct validity across general, mental, and physical health domains [42].

Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ-8; Attkisson & Greenfield [43]). The CSQ-8 is an 8-item measure of patient-reported satisfaction with mental health care services. Items are rated on a 4-point response scale, with higher scores indicating a higher level of overall satisfaction with services. Example questions include “How would you rate the quality of the treatment you have received?” and “To what extent has our treatment met your needs?”

Qualitative feedback

Qualitative feedback on acceptability was collected via two methods. First, after each session, facilitators documented participant comments and reactions in structured debriefing notes. Second, as part of the TP-2 (post-intervention) assessment, participants were asked a series of open-ended questions about their experience, such as ‘What did you find most helpful about the CI-CT group?’ and ‘What suggestions do you have for improving the group?’ Feedback was descriptively summarized for acceptability metrics (i.e., % positive responses), consistent with pilot study guidelines for qualitative pilot data (e.g., Thabane et al. [45] ). Thematic content analysis was not pursued due to pilot scope, per Thabane et al.'s [45]guidelines.

Specific, pre-defined benchmarks for determining adequate feasibility and acceptability were established a priori, a critical component of pilot study design [45]. Adequate recruitment efficiency was set at ≥65 % of eligible Veterans approached agreeing to participate; adequate retention was defined as ≥75 % of enrolled participants completing at least 7 of 10 sessions (adjusted from 9/12 sessions due to protocol modification); adequate follow-up completion for the post-treatment assessment (TP-2) was ≥70 %; high participant satisfaction was indicated by a mean CSQ-8 score ≥20 and ≥ 75 % positive qualitative feedback; implementation ease was defined as total facilitator time ≤50 h per 10-session cycle. These benchmarks were developed based on common practices in psychotherapy pilot research and standards for high engagement in VA-based intervention studies. They aim for metrics indicative of strong potential for success in a larger trial (e.g., retention ≥75 % aligns with VA group therapy pilots showing high engagement as in Pless et al. [50]).

Exploratory outcome analyses were conducted using the R statistical language (version 4.4.2; R Core Team [51]) in RStudio [52]. Given the small sample size (N = 11 with pre/post data) and the primary feasibility focus, analyses were restricted to descriptive statistics (Means, SDs) for primary and secondary outcome measures at pre- (TP-1) and post-intervention (TP-2). Analyses used a complete case approach for participants with both TP-1 and TP-2 data (N = 11). To quantify the magnitude of observed changes, effect sizes (Cohen's d) were also calculated using the baseline standard deviation. Consistent with recommendations for pilot studies, other inferential statistics (e.g., t-tests) were not computed to avoid over-interpreting findings from an underpowered sample.

Treatment modifications

As noted in the Methods, the CI-CT protocol underwent iterative refinement before and during these trials. Pre-trial modifications focused on adapting materials based on stakeholder feedback (e.g., language, examples, adding facilitator guidance for managing distress). During the one-arm trials, further changes were implemented based on participant and facilitator experiences (detailed in Table 5). Key modifications included shortening session content to fit the 90-min timeframe, simplifying between-session exercises to improve adherence (while making them optional), adding summary takeaways within sessions, introducing a brief mindfulness exercise at the start of each session, and adjusting the core cycle length from the planned 12 sessions to 10 sessions to facilitate rolling admission and potentially reduce participant burden.

Feasibility and acceptability

CI-CT demonstrated strong feasibility and acceptability, meeting or exceeding pre-defined benchmarks across key domains. Regarding the Demand/Recruitment Efficiency criteria, of the 19 Veterans referred, 15 were eligible and all 15 consented (100 % consent rate among eligible). Fourteen completed baseline assessments. Recruitment efficiency based on eligible Veterans approached exceeded the ≥65 % benchmark. As for the Acceptability (Satisfaction, Follow-up) criteria, 11 participants (91.7 % of starters) completed the post-treatment (TP-2) assessment, exceeding the ≥70 % benchmark. Participant satisfaction was very high. The mean CSQ-8 score was 27.6 (SD = 2.9; range 23–31), substantially exceeding our a priori benchmark of ≥20. This score indicates an average item rating between ‘very satisfied’ and ‘extremely satisfied’ on the 4-point scale. Qualitative feedback was overwhelmingly positive: 100 % reported finding CI-CT helpful, 100 % rated the quality as “good” (N = 5) or “excellent” (N = 6), and 82 % (N = 9) felt “most of my needs have been met,” exceeding the ≥75 % positive feedback benchmark. Finally, regarding practicality, the delivery via VA telehealth platform was feasible using standard equipment and facilitator effort within expected limits.

Discussion

This study aimed to describe the iterative development process of Continuous Identity Cognitive Therapy (CI-CT) and primarily to assess its feasibility and acceptability for Veterans in the post-acute suicide episode (PASE) stage when delivered via telehealth. The findings strongly support CI-CT's feasibility and acceptability within this population and setting. Across three sequential open-label trials, the intervention met or exceeded pre-defined benchmarks for key feasibility indicators, including recruitment efficiency, participant retention and attendance, participant satisfaction, and implementation ease from the facilitators' perspective. The high levels of engagement, retention, and positive Veteran feedback suggest that CI-CT resonates with PASE Veterans and is a viable intervention approach warranting further investigation.

While the 45 h of facilitator time for the 10-session cycle met our feasibility benchmark (≤50 h), a pro-rated estimate for the full 12-session protocol is approximately 54 h. Although this slightly exceeds the benchmark, it remains within standard VA telehealth norms for group therapy (cf. [47]) and is considered feasible with minor streamlining in a future trial.

While the study was not designed or powered for efficacy testing [45], exploratory descriptive analyses of clinical outcomes were conducted to generate preliminary data for future research. These findings suggest potential signals of change, with pre-post effect sizes providing a preliminary indication of magnitude. A large effect size was observed for improvement in psychological quality of life (WHOQOL-D2, d = 0.93. Medium to medium-large effect sizes were found for reductions in hopelessness (BHS, d = 0.55), perceived burdensomeness (INQ-PB, d = 0.67, and improvements in recovery-related goal orientation (RAS subscale, d = 0.37) and environmental quality of life (WHOQOL-D4, d = 0.68). The trivial effect size for C-SSRS ideation intensity may suggest the intervention's core targets (e.g., identity, future outlook) impact broader affective states more immediately than the specific intensity of suicidal thoughts. These preliminary observations should be interpreted as indicating a need for rigorous testing in a controlled trial to determine CI-CT's actual clinical effects.

This study contributes to the field by demonstrating the feasibility of a novel intervention targeting identity disruption and personal recovery principles within suicide postvention care for Veterans. The focus on reconstructing a coherent self-narrative and enhancing future self-continuity addresses a potential gap, as existing treatments often focus primarily on acute risk management or specific skill deficits [12]. Furthermore, the study shows the significant value of an iterative development process incorporating direct stakeholder feedback. Modifications made in response to Veteran input, such as simplifying between-session exercises and adjusting session structure, helped refine the protocol and likely contributed directly to the high rates of acceptability, attendance, and retention observed.

Implications

Should CI-CT demonstrate efficacy in subsequent randomized controlled trials, its unique focus on identity and personal recovery would be a valuable addition to the continuum of suicide prevention services, potentially bridging the gap between acute crisis stabilization and longer-term mental health care. However, successful integration into routine clinical settings would require developing robust training and supervision protocols to ensure clinician competency and maintain fidelity to the model.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations inherent to its design. As an open-label, uncontrolled pilot study with a small sample size (N = 11 completers), it cannot establish the efficacy of CI-CT or rule out the influence of other factors (e.g., time, concurrent treatments) on observed changes. The primary aim was feasibility, and the findings should be interpreted accordingly. Data collection issues resulted in incomplete administration of the RAS and precluded analysis of the full SBQ-R, limiting the scope of exploratory outcome analyses. Another limitation pertains to the sample characteristics. The participant group primarily consisted of mid-life and older Veterans. Consequently, the findings cannot be directly applied to younger Veterans. In addition, concurrent mental health treatments were not systematically tracked. While this does not affect the primary feasibility and acceptability aims, it limits interpretation of the exploratory clinical outcomes. In addition, the protocol did not include systematically tracking the total number of unique referrals or the outcomes of all contact attempts (e.g., refusals, non-responses). This point precludes a full calculation of recruitment rates and limits our assessment of overall treatment demand. Similarly, a formal fidelity monitoring protocol was not implemented, though the PI served as the primary facilitator across all groups to ensure consistency. The recruitment strategy of drawing most referrals from a previous suicide prevention study introduces potential selection bias, as these Veterans may have been more amenable to research participation, and as there may be confounding effects from their participation in a prior intervention [32]. Future trials should take care to avoid using a large proportion of previous trial subjects.

Additionally, the protocol underwent modifications during the trials, including the adjustment from a planned 12-session format to a 10-session cycle to facilitate rolling admission. The 10-session cycle was a pragmatic adaptation during this feasibility pilot to facilitate rolling admission. However, based on the totality of participant and facilitator feedback that more time was needed to comprehensively cover the content of all modules, the full 12-session version of the CI-CT manual is considered the optimal dosage and will be used for future research.

Future research should prioritize evaluating CI-CT's efficacy via a randomized controlled trial, comparing it against an appropriate control condition, such as a carefully defined and monitored Veteran Affairs Medical Center (VAMC) Treatment as Usual (TAU), which includes established suicide prevention programs, to test its added value within the VA system. Such a trial should also aim to recruit a more diverse sample, including Veterans from different eras, backgrounds, and geographical locations. Further research could also compare different delivery modalities (e.g., telehealth vs. in-person) or explore CI-CT's applicability to other populations facing significant identity disruption and recovery challenges, such as individuals with chronic health conditions or Long COVID [46], where preliminary work is underway.

Conclusion

This study provides strong evidence for the feasibility and acceptability of CI-CT, a novel intervention emphasizing identity reconstruction and personal recovery, for PASE Veterans when delivered via telehealth. Despite limitations inherent in the pilot design, the high engagement, satisfaction, and retention rates, coupled with the successful iterative refinement of the protocol based on Veteran feedback, indicate that CI-CT is a promising approach. These findings support the progression to efficacy testing in a randomized controlled trial.
