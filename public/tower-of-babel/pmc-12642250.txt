A scoping review on medical students’ international migration: trends, determinants, and implications for global health workforce planning
Authors: Gerald Tze Zhen Ser, Zhi Sean Teng, Yady Zikry Yusni, Krisada Shen Yang Ooi, Zhi Yong Wong, Prashwin Singh Tejpal Singh, Wei-Han Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysician migration is a known global phenomenon, with the migration intention of medical students serving as early indicators of the trends in workforce mobility. Understanding the factors influencing their decision to migrate is crucial in the planning of human resources for health in a country and the sustainability of a healthcare system, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC). This scoping review synthesises current available evidence on the trends, determinants, and implications of medical student international migration.MethodsA systematic search was conducted on PubMed, Scopus, and Proquest from July 2013 to May 2025, following the PRISMA for Scoping Reviews framework. The selected articles included primary research investigating factors influencing migration intention among medical students. Articles were excluded if the study only explored on perception of migration. Included studies were assessed for quality according to Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal tool, and extracted factors were reclassified and regrouped into domains of Academic and Personal Development, Economic and Working Conditions, and Social and Living Environment factors.Results517 articles were screened, of which 33 articles were included, covering medical students from diverse geographical locations. Migration intentions were varied across regions, with studies from 6 countries each reporting that over 80% of sampled medical students intended to migrate. The most common push factors included income, working culture and environment, and career progression. The most frequently cited stay factors were family, financial, and patriotism.ConclusionMigration intentions were widely reported among medical students across different regions, though the proportions varied by country. This serves as a predictor and could indicate the evolving global trend of physician migration in the years to come. Financial prospects, career opportunities, and working conditions play critical roles in determining the migration intentions of medical students. Targeted retention strategies should be implemented especially in resource-limited settings to address brain drain, although at the same time achieving balance in ethical recruitment practices in high-income countries. Future research is warranted to study the longitudinal trends of migration intention and assess the impact of policies on retention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12960-025-01027-x.Keywords: Medical student migration, Workforce planning, Global health, Brain drain, Healthcare policy, Human resources for health

Background

The rapidly changing global demographic dynamics in the healthcare system is increasingly strained by medical workforce shortages, further intensified by the rising trend of healthcare professionals migrating across international borders [1, 2]. As the backbone of future healthcare systems, medical students play a pivotal role in shaping the global distribution of healthcare professionals. Their intentions to migrate post-graduation serve as early indicators of workforce mobility trends, underscoring the need to understand the factors driving these decisions [3]. A thorough analysis of these factors is essential for informed workforce planning and policy development, ensuring the long-term sustainability and resilience of healthcare systems worldwide.

Medical migration is part of a greater global health issue—the global healthcare workforce crisis, in which there is a global undersupply of healthcare professionals [4]. The question of whether medical migration is ethical remains debatable. According to the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the freedom to leave one’s country and free choice of profession are codified as human rights [5]. One may argue that at the societal level, medical migration can reduce access to basic healthcare in source countries already struggling with a shortage of healthcare professionals. This, in turn, may be seen as a violation of human rights, at least prima facie, as it compromises the fundamental right to healthcare [6].

Despite growing concern over medical workforce migration, past research showed fragmented insights, often limited to specific geographic regions or individual determinants. This lack of a comprehensive synthesis creates gaps in understanding broader global migration patterns [7]. Additionally, recent economic fluctuations, evolving healthcare policies, and shifting migration trends necessitate an updated review to capture contemporary influencing factors and their implications for healthcare workforce retention.

This article seeks to explore the key factors influencing medical students’ intentions to migrate after graduation through a comparative analysis across different countries and regions. Push factors are factors which promote students to migrate, while stay factors are factors which encourage students to stay or prevent them from migrating [8]. By systematically examining push and stay factors shaping migration decisions, this study aims to provide valuable insights that inform both policymakers and medical educators in their efforts to enhance workforce retention and equitable distribution.

Through a scoping review, this article consolidates global evidence on medical students’ intention to migrate, offering a refined classification of influencing factors. The findings will contribute to evidence-based policy recommendations aimed at mitigating physician shortages in source countries although promoting a more balanced and sustainable global healthcare workforce.

Methods

A scoping review according to the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines was undertaken to explore the factors influencing migration intention after medical school. The review was registered on Open Science Framework with the DOI (10.17605/OSF.IO/A5TMU).

Search strategy

The search was made on the 23rd of July 2023, with an updated search on 14th of July 2025, in PubMed, Scopus, and Proquest, filtering for studies published between the 1st of July 2013 to the 1st of May 2025. The databases were screened using the keywords “factor”, “influence”, “migration”, “emigration”, and “medical students” (search strategy in Supplementary Table 1). A few key papers were first selected for initial in-depth reading by the authors. A librarian was then consulted to understand the nature of the papers and the search strategies were developed collectively by the team after careful considerations.

Screening of literature

Two rounds of screening were done independently by two researchers (ZYW and PSTS) via Rayyan [9]. In case of disagreement, a third researcher was involved (GTZS). The literature was first screened by title and abstract according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The remaining full-text articles were then assessed for final inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study had to be primary research including medical students as the population of interest, containing findings pertaining to factors influencing emigration, published in English and in a peer-reviewed journal. Articles which only refer to the perception of migration by the students and not their interest in choosing it, and unpublished articles, abstracts, conference proceedings, or letters were excluded. No methodological criteria were applied for article selection, both qualitative and quantitative papers were considered. If the requested data was not available or further details were needed, the original study's authors were reached by email.

Extracted information included author, year of publication, research design, country, sample size, demographics, as well as factors promoting and withholding migration. The quality of the included studies was then assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool, and the studies included were categorised based on the quality of the study (Good, Moderate, Poor).

Study quality assessment

The JBI Critical Appraisal checklist was used to assess the quality of the included studies. Two review authors independently assessed the risk of bias and methodological quality in making decisions to include, exclude, or seek further information was reached for each of the studies. A total score would be converted to percentage and a study was considered low quality if it scored less than 50%, moderate quality if it scored between 50 and 70% and high quality if it scored above 70% [10]. Studies with “high” or “moderate” qualities were included. The breakdown of the quality assessment for each study has been included in Supplementary Table 2.

Classification of factors

The factors influencing migration that were extracted from the included papers were reclassified into push and stay factors. Push factors are defined as factors which promote students to migrate, while stay factors include factors which encourage students to stay or prevent them from migrating [8]. The extracted push and stay factors were thematically grouped into three broad domains—Academic and Personal Development, Economy and Working Conditions, and Social and Living Environment—based on common patterns observed across studies during a narrative synthesis of the findings. These domains were developed by the authors to facilitate clearer conceptual organisation, given the heterogeneity of study contexts and factor descriptions. The Academic and Personal Development domain includes factors relating to professional aspirations and motivation to further develop one’s skills, such as career progression, further training, etc. Economic and Working Conditions meanwhile includes factors revolving around workplace environment and financial status such as income, working culture and environment, etc. Finally, the domain of Social and Living Environment includes factors relating to one’s lifestyle, sociocultural and demographic background, such as patriotism, family, etc.

Role of the funding source

This study did not benefit from any funding.

Results

88 articles were identified through the PubMed database, 183 through the Scopus database, and 246 from the Proquest database. 416 articles remained after removing the duplicates. 355 articles were excluded based on their title and abstract, and 29 were excluded after full-text review. Reasons for exclusion were study population including medical graduates/junior doctors/other healthcare workers, not including factors influencing migration intention, etc. 33 articles were included in this scoping review (Fig. 1).

Majority of participants in most studies expressed migration intentions (20.8–89.6%), with rates exceeding 80% in 7 studies conducted in Romania, Egypt, Ireland, Serbia, Nigeria, and Kyrgyzstan. Of these countries, Ireland and Romania are classified as high-income countries (HICs); Egypt, Nigeria, and Kyrgyzstan as low- and middle-income countries (LMICs); and Serbia as an upper-middle-income country (UMIC). Notably, in countries where more than 1 study has been conducted, Pakistan, Ireland, Nigeria and Egypt consistently exhibited high migration aspirations among their medical students with more than 50, 60, 70 and 80% of them expressing such intentions respectively. Apart from Ireland being classified as a high-income country (HIC), Pakistan, Nigeria, and Egypt are lower-middle income countries (LMICs). While there is a wide variation in migration intention across world regions, 3 studies from the Middle East & North Africa have reported high migration intention rates, all above 60%.

Some studies identified specific destination countries, with Australia being the most common, among other countries like the United States of America (USA) and Germany. This comprehensive overview presents a foundation for understanding the demographic profile of the study populations and their migration aspirations, facilitating comparisons across different cultural and socioeconomic contexts. Push factors

Among all factors that influenced medical students to migrate after medical school, income in the economy and working conditions category was the most commonly reported factor overall, quoted by 24 out of the 33 included studies, while individual satisfaction in the academic & personal development category was the least commonly reported factor overall, explored in just 1 of the included study. Notably, there was no trend seen in comparison with the income status of the countries, as income was commonly examined as a push factor in all income statuses.

Within the academic & personal development category, career progression was most commonly reported in 18 of the studies included. This was followed by training quality in the similar category that was reported in 12 of the studies included.

Working culture was the next most prevalent factor in the economy & working conditions category after income, as seen in 19 studies.

Quality of life was the most commonly reported within the social & living environment category, quoted by 16 of the studies included followed by family as seen in 13 of the included studies.

Income and career progression were commonly reported as push factors concurrently, as seen in 16 studies from Greece, Croatia, Madagascar, Tanzania, Egypt, Serbia, Ireland, Nigeria, UK, Kyrgyzstan, Poland, Turkiye, Malaysia, and Italy. Additionally, of the 13 studies that have described family as a push factor, income was also highlighted in 11 of them.

In terms of national income status, studies from LICs and MICs have described the common push factors to be career progression, income, and working culture and environment. These include studies from Madagascar, Sri Lanka, Egypt, Serbia, Uganda, Pakistan, Kyrgyzstan, Malaysia, and Turkiye. Meanwhile, medical students from HICs like Greece and Ireland have highlighted quality of life to be a factor influencing medical students’ intention to migrate.

Stay factors

Family in the social and living environment category was the most common stay factor overall as reported in 13 of the included studies. In the same category, patriotism was the second most reported factor that influenced decisions to stay as reported in 7 of the included studies.

Financial factor was found to be the second most common overall stay factor and was the main factor reported in the economy and working conditions category, as seen in 9 studies. This was followed by working culture in the same category as reported by 4 of the included studies.

Qualifications and immigration process emerged as the primary obstacle to emigration within the academic and personal development category, as highlighted by 6 of the included articles.

Traversing across all three categories, personal satisfaction, career progression, research opportunities, and local infrastructure were the least commonly highlighted factors across all 33 studies included, with each of the factors above only quoted by 1 study each.

All 9 studies that have described financial reasons as a stay factor have also reported family as a factor that promotes medical students to stay. These include studies from Croatia, Sri Lanka, Egypt, Pakistan, Kyrgyzstan, Nigeria, and Turkiye.

Discussion

Overview

Medical graduates form an important role in the physician workforce pipeline, and their transition into clinical roles directly affects the availability and delivery of healthcare services, particularly in regions where there is a shortage in the healthcare workforce. The training of medical graduates is a long journey ranging between five to six years. It is also a costly process depending on the location of the medical school. The mean price of a 4 to 5-year medical education can range from USD 300,00 in USA (HIC) to USD284,058 in Thailand (UMIC) to USD 69,323 in Vietnam (LMIC) [44, 45]. In certain countries, subsidies for these hefty fees in public institutions are provided by the government for citizens using local taxpayers' funds [46]. When fresh medical graduates migrate immediately after graduation without contributing to the local healthcare system, it remains a loss to the source country economically and reduces the number of readily available manpower should there be no replacement sought. In countries where there is already an existing shortage of healthcare professionals, this may further worsen the issue and increase the burden on the healthcare system. Conversely, source countries may experience economic benefits through remittances from destination countries, which may serve to offset the costs of medical education or contribute to strengthening healthcare systems in the countries of origin.

Push factors

Among the 17 push factors analysed within this study which found income to be the most common followed by career progression and working culture, income and career progression may be closely linked. The desire for locations with higher income may be attributed to the likely lower starting pay for medical graduates early in their career. This is further aggravated by the location these doctors practice at, where doctors in middle-income countries (MICs) may receive relatively lower salaries when compared with their counterparts in more developed nations, as shown in Chaet et al., de Silva et al., Fouad et al., Gazibara et al., and Kabbash et al. [13, 15, 17, 19, 22, 47] Anastasiou et al. identified income was one of the main push factors for Greek medical students to emigrate. Notably, Greece has one of the lowest physician salaries in Europe, despite being the largest producer of medical graduates [11]. While some source countries provide a higher starting wage for doctors, the wage difference remains large, where the salaries in these source countries only remain about one-third the level of HICs like Australia and the USA [48]. Consequently, the lower income can lead to insufficient funds to cover living expenses, return student loan repayments, or achieve the desired standard quality of life as expected by doctors in developed countries. Additionally, the differences in currency strength may also play an important factor, where although two different nations have the same numerical value in salary, the amount after currency exchange may lead to a certain level of dissatisfaction with the amount of work contributed as compared to the financial remuneration [49]. For example, in Singapore, the starting salary for a junior doctor is SGD 5,000 (approximately USD 3,800), meanwhile juniors doctors of the same position in Malaysia takes home a salary of RM 5,000 (approximately USD 1,100) a month [49]. Healthcare workers, including doctors, have a right to migrate to a country where they receive better incentives. On the contrary, people in source countries who have lost many healthcare professionals due to brain drain also have a right to affordable and accessible healthcare in their home countries [50]. This remains a global challenge where policymakers and governments struggle to find a good balance.

In contrast from income which is an economical factor, another push factor includes career progression, which leads to medical graduates aspiring for opportunities such as training programmes to acquire specific skills and knowledge. As the training of specialists is more resource intensive as compared to training medical students, in countries with a high number of medical graduates but limited number of education and specialty training opportunities leads to medical graduates seeking alternative options overseas for their career progression, as shown in Fouad et al., Gouda et al., Nasir et al., and Santric-Milicevica et al. [17, 20, 24, 26, 51]. The poor quality of medical training and practice in source countries has been cited as a demotivating factor for retention, whereas the opportunity to access higher standards of training abroad is frequently reported as a key driver of migration among doctors [52]. This is supported by Gouda et al., Raza et al., Oyedokun et al., Sancak et al., and Teng et al. [20, 25, 38, 40, 42]. More importantly, graduates with a strong interest in highly niche specialities requiring cutting-edge research facilities and access to the latest technologies that may be limited in their home country may resort to searching for opportunities abroad. This leads to migration to countries with more established or emerging training programs to further develop their expertise and lead to the achievement of personal career goals.

Importantly, the differences in national income status may influence the types of push and stay factors influencing medical graduates from different countries. Medical graduates from LICs and MICs are often motivated by factors such as limited local opportunities for career progression, inadequate remuneration, and suboptimal working conditions, whereas those from HICs tend to prioritise lifestyle preferences. This hypothesis was supported by studies across Greece (Anastasiou et al. 2021), Madagascar (Chaet et al. 2021), Ireland (Cronin et al. 2019), Sri Lanka (de Silva et al. 2014), Egypt (Fouad et al. 2014, Kabbash et al. 2021), the Republic of Serbia (Gazibara et al. 2019), Uganda (Kizito et al. 2015), Pakistan (Nasir et al. 2018, Raza et al. 2022), Kyrgyzstan (Kasiev et al. 2024), Malaysia (Saw et al. 2024, Teng et al. 2024), and Turkiye (Sancak et al. 2023) [11, 13–15, 17, 19, 22–25, 36, 37, 40, 42].

Stay factors

Each study also reported the presence of students who did not intend to migrate, possibly due to a stronger influence of stay factors relative to push factors on their decision.

Among the top 3 stay factors, family was the most identified stay factor followed by financial factors and patriotism. Family socio-economic status (SES) strongly influences the students’ career decision-making. Staying close to their families might be important to support their families' financial needs constantly and it may deter them from deciding to migrate. This is supported by studies that have described both financial reasons and family as stay factors [12, 15, 17, 21, 24, 25, 36, 38, 40]. Moreover, there could also be a cultural influence, such as society being made up of large joint families that do not typically witness any migration of family members or the obligation of the younger generation to bear financial responsibility of their families once they have started working. Any deviation from such a norm may be seen as unacceptable and subsequently hinders any migration aspiration that medical graduates may have. This reiterates the fact that cultural influence and family are mutually inclusive factors. This hypothesis was supported by studies across Croatia (Bojanic et al. 2015), Sri Lanka (de Silva et al. 2014), Egypt (Fouad et al. 2014), Uganda (Kizito et al. 2015), Serbia (Santric-Milicevic et al. 2014), and most prominently Pakistan (Hossain et al. 2016; Nasir et al. 2018; Raza et al. 2022) where 3 such studies reported family as a stay factor [12, 15, 17, 21, 23–26]. However, it is likely that people from low SES also often express the desire to achieve greater financial stability than their current family. This leads us to the question of whether a job in a foreign country offering the prospect of greater financial gain would be a factor that is strong enough to challenge the cultural factors that hinder migration and subsequently persuade them to leave their families and move abroad.

Most certainly, finance is an important consideration as a demanding profession such as medicine requires adequate financial remuneration. Many studies even report finance as the most important determining factor when it comes to selecting a job. Finance was reported to be a stay factor across 3 countries: Croatia (Bojanic et al. 2015), Sri Lanka (de Silva et al. 2014), and Pakistan (Raza et al. 2022) [12, 15, 25]. Interestingly, finance was also a top push factor, and to report that finance was a stay factor would be contradictory to the importance of finance as a part of the decision-making process to migrate. Interestingly, a scoping review on physician migration found that the very factors attracting doctors to the United Kingdom (UK) were also cited by locally trained doctors as reasons for leaving [53]. This highlights how migration factors are highly contextual and relative; what constitutes a stay factor for some may simultaneously act as a push factor for others depending on their personal expectations. For example, it is uncertain if finance as a stay factor reflects the perception of medical graduates that they may be able to manage their finances more efficiently in their home countries, or perhaps earn more in their home countries through private practice or alternative sources of income, or their lack of awareness about the financial remuneration in healthcare sectors of other countries.

Factors such as language, race, religion, social background, local culture, and customs were least reported to be stay factors. This could be attributed to a growing understanding and tolerance of societal diversity among the younger generation [54]. Besides, it could be due to an interest in exploring various cultures in different countries. Importantly, leaving one’s home country does not translate into losing touch with his/her social and cultural roots. With the increasing diversity in various countries due to growing migrant populations, it is common to experience one’s own culture, customs, and language being practiced in another country. Medical professionals are also trained to be more culturally sensitive since medical school due to the nature of the profession that prioritises care over intolerance. Nevertheless, some studies report these factors as stay factors [55].

Burden of medical migration

The impact of brain drain causes a greater effect in lower-income source countries. This is where access to care is even more limited when the supply of professionals is unable to meet the growing demand of patient care required and the rise in healthcare budget is insufficient to improve career offers to doctors. On the other hand, high-income destination countries have the financial capabilities of importing international medical graduates to fill the void in critical workforce gaps and improve competency in care [56]. However, this places the healthcare system of these countries in a position of being over dependent on foreign skill force.

Strengths and strategies

Many strategies and interventions have been explored from both the source and destination countries to address the migration of healthcare professionals. From our results, the most important push factor for medical students’ migration intention is income. This coincides with findings on migration of healthcare professionals globally [57]. While narrowing or eliminating the pay gap between doctors in source and destination countries may seem like the obvious solution, most source countries—primarily LMICs—lack the financial capacity to implement such measures [6]. Source countries could integrate exit requirements such as compulsory service as part of a medical degree to halt medical graduates from migrating [58]. This strategy has been shown to be effective in several countries, including Puerto Rico and Thailand, where the implementation of compulsory service has successfully mitigated the issue of healthcare workers shortage, including in rural regions [59, 60]. Secondly, simple yet impactful implementations by source countries such as improving workplace culture, reducing unnecessary bureaucracy, eliminating workplace discrimination, bullying, or favouritism, and etc. could improve the workplace satisfaction of doctors, ultimately encouraging them to stay in the country [42]. On the other hand, destination countries should engage in ethical healthcare professional recruitment, which may help reduce the impact on source countries [6]. Moreover, since most medical education programs are publicly funded or heavily subsidised by the government, source countries risk bearing the cost of training healthcare professionals who ultimately choose to migrate and contribute to the workforce of destination countries [48]. Several suggestions have been proposed by the International Association of Employers, which included compensation or taxation by destination countries to reimburse education costs or support improvements in the healthcare systems in source countries [61]. An example of such has been implemented involving nurses, where Germany employs nurses from the Philippines and Tunisia, while providing remittances to boost the economy in the source countries [62]. Other forms of compensation measures recommended by the International Labour Organisation—such as scholarship and exchange of experts, joint ventures, investment in healthcare facilities in source countries, and support for the return and reintegration of healthcare professionals—should be given consideration [63]. Furthermore, in alignment with the World Health Organisation’s (WHO) Global Strategy on Human Resources for Health: Workforce 2030, destination countries—typically wealthier and more developed—should scale up health professional education to meet their own healthcare need, and reduce the dependency on international medical graduates [2]. Lastly, both source and destination countries should explore government-to-government agreements in the form of Bilateral Labour Agreements (BLAs) or Memorandum of Understanding (MOUs), as used in nurse recruitment. These formal agreements can facilitate ethical recruitment, protect migrants’ rights, and mitigate the negative impact on source countries [63].

This is a scoping review of the currently available literature. While physician migration has been well studied and described, studies involving medical students have not been reviewed extensively. While factors of physician migration play a role in the planning of human resources for health in a country, it is particularly important to study medical students’ intention to migrate too because this population will be the key determinant to the healthcare system within the coming years. It is important for policymakers and stakeholders to consider the migration intention and its key drivers among medical students to strategise and plan future healthcare policies. The population included in our review are from a diverse group of countries, and the most important factors of migration vary across regions due to geographical and sociocultural backgrounds. Additionally, the students included in our review were at their various stages of training. This allows our review to provide a good overview of the current global evidence on the intentions of medical students to migrate.

Limitations

There are several limitations in this study. While our review included all the push and stay factors mentioned in each study, we only studied the frequency of each factor in the included studies while the weightage and popularity of the factors were not considered. The review does not explore how gender and stage of training may shape migration intentions, due to a lack of disaggregated data and detailed analysis in the included studies. Additionally, this review focuses on the migration intentions of medical students rather than the experiences of physicians who have migrated. Future research should explore how perceptions such as idealised views of destination countries and underestimation of challenges like licensing and accreditation influence migration decisions. It should also examine the gap between the intention to migrate and actual migration. Finally, there is currently no validated and standardised classification on the migration factors, thus a different classification of the factors included in our review may produce varying results.

While the scoping review approach lacks the depth and statistical rigor of systematic reviews or meta-analyses, this review included studies with a great heterogeneity in study designs, settings, and populations. A scoping review would be the most appropriate choice to map and provide an overview of existing evidence and identify knowledge gaps which will guide future research directions.

Conclusion

Financial prospects, career advancement opportunities, and working conditions play important roles in shaping medical students' migration intentions, while family ties serve as a key factor for retention. The findings suggest that these considerations may play a role in shaping future migration patterns, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where the outflow of healthcare workers can have a disproportionate impact.

This review highlights the importance of considering these factors in the planning of human resources for health. Potential interventions such as competitive remuneration, structured training pathways, conducive and supportive working environment, and strategic workforce policies may promote retention of doctors. Future research is needed to explore the evolving migration intention trends of medical students and correlation between factors and the intention to migrate. The role of gender and year of training in shaping migration intention among medical students should also be studied. Additionally, the effectiveness of national policies aimed at balancing global mobility with the sustainability of local healthcare systems should be explored further.
