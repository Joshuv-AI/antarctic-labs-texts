Characterizing the Objective Time Spent on Health Care Activities in Clinic and at Home for Patients With Cancer
Authors: Gabrielle B. Rocque, Nicole Lynne Henderson, Helen M. Parsons, Katherine Brown, Zuofu Huang, Arjun Gupta, Deanna Teoh, Yingling Fan, Anne Blaes, J. Nicholas Odom, Rebecca Arend, Julian Wolfson, Rachel I. Vogel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhile the time burden of cancer care is increasingly recognized in oncology, existing research lacks the granularity needed to identify intervention targets. This study characterized time spent on cancer care at home and in‐clinic, including travel, waiting, and clinical tasks, assessing variations by treatment modality.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of two longitudinal cohorts. Cohort 1 included patients with metastatic breast or advanced ovarian cancer (n = 55) and Cohort 2 included patients with any cancer (n = 55) receiving systemic anti‐cancer therapy. Participants used a mobile application for 28 days that collected GPS and motion sensor data to infer locations. Daily surveys characterized activities and captured time spent on home‐based tasks.ResultsThe study population (N = 110) was predominantly female and White, receiving a mix of infusion and oral treatments. Participants recorded 597 out‐of‐home healthcare episodes. While median time receiving care per episode for lab‐only visits was short (10 min), visits combining labs and treatment had a median duration of 162 min (compared to treatment alone, median duration of 68 min). Patients on infusion therapies experienced higher time burdens per episode both in‐facility (median 125 min vs. 50 min for oral therapy) and at home for scheduling and symptom management (median 188 min vs. 102 min).ConclusionsPatients face substantial time burdens that extend beyond the clinical encounter. Waiting between stages, such as labs and treatment, makes combined out‐of‐home healthcare episodes disproportionately time‐consuming. These data facilitate a deeper understanding of the trade‐offs between different treatment modalities and care delivery models.

Introduction

The oncology community increasingly recognizes that patients spend substantial amounts of time on cancer care and associated out‐of‐home healthcare services [1, 2]. Early work evaluating time burden related to cancer, sometimes called time toxicity [3], found that while oncologists spend on average 20 min with an individual, women with metastatic breast cancer spent over on average 3.5 h on a typical healthcare episode and over 60 h in the initial 3 months traveling to and receiving routine cancer care [4]. In a broader cancer population, median total time spent on receiving outpatient care was 3.3 h per visit [5]. This time commitment is exacerbated when patients have to be seen with the health system at increasing frequencies, receive complex treatment regimens, or need multimodal care [4, 6]. When considering healthcare contact days with the health system, defined as days with in‐person healthcare outside the home (e.g., outpatient encounters such as blood draws, infusions, clinician visits, urgent care or emergency department visits, and inpatient stays in acute care hospitals and nursing and rehabilitation facilities), women with metastatic breast cancer spent approximately 30 days within their first year of treatment [7, 8], while patients with metastatic gastrointestinal cancer spent over 25% of their time in contact with the healthcare system [6].

This time spent on cancer care is burdensome for patients, with almost half of patients reporting a desire to minimize time burdens [9]. Patients express that while healthcare facility‐based care is often more burdensome than home‐based care, in‐person visits may be perceived as “value added” due to interaction with clinical teams. However, home‐based care can also be burdensome when time needed to complete tasks is unpredictable and/or when it adversely impacts care partners [3]. Importantly, time demands are often a key consideration when selecting treatment, particularly when considering clinical trials or treatments with marginal benefit [3, 10, 11]. Time considerations may also be particularly pertinent for individuals living a substantial distance from care, which has known adverse impacts on cancer stage at diagnosis, treatments received, quality of life and other outcomes [12]. In a study at the University of Alabama at Birmingham, 25% of patients lived greater than 1 h from their cancer care provider [13]. The need to travel is exacerbated in rural communities, where 20% of Americans live greater than 60 miles from a medical oncologist [14].

Despite this increasing recognition of the importance of time burdens in cancer care, data on time spent is often broad (e.g., days with healthcare contact, total hours) and does not provide the granularity needed to understand how the time spent on components of care (e.g., visits, labs, infusion) may be modified or coordinated. There are potential targets for minimizing time toxicity, such as improving coordination of care, selecting treatment modalities associated with more limited need for in‐person care, and transitioning delivery to a local healthcare center or the home. However, further evaluation with nuanced consideration of time spent on cancer care activities is needed to frame future strategies to mitigate time toxicity. The objective of this study was to leverage high‐resolution sensor and self‐reported survey data collected via a mobile application to characterize the granular components of time spent on cancer care, including travel, waiting, and clinical activities, both within healthcare facilities and in the home setting, and to identify how these time burdens vary by service type (e.g., labs, treatments) and treatment modality.

Methods

Study Design

This analysis combines participants from two longitudinal cancer patient cohorts that both assessed the objective time spent on various healthcare activities using a mobile application (Daynamica, described below). The two cohorts were established with distinct primary objectives: Cohort 1 focused on validating a mobile application's utility as a research instrument for capturing time burden; Cohort 2 prioritized assessing the feasibility and patient‐centered acceptability of the mobile application. Details of the approach to capture time and methods for Cohort 1 are described elsewhere [15]; methods for Cohort 2 are similar unless otherwise stated. In brief, participants completed a baseline survey to report demographic and treatment characteristics. Subsequently, they used the mobile application for 28 days, which leverages sensor data with daily surveys to capture time spent on cancer care. These data were analyzed to assess differential time by healthcare activity to generate hypotheses for future interventions that could reduce time. The Cohort 1 study was approved by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board (STUDY00016834) and the Cohort 2 study was approved by Advarra (SSU00246429); both were registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05708703, NCT06322992, respectively).

Study Population and Recruitment

Two study populations were included in this analysis. Cohort 1 included adults (age 18+) with a diagnosis of metastatic (Stage IV) breast cancer or advanced (Stage III or IV)/recurrent ovarian, primary peritoneal, or fallopian tube cancer receiving systemic anti‐cancer therapy within the University of Minnesota M Health Fairview system or the University of Alabama at Birmingham. Eligible patients were approached at the time of a scheduled clinic visit by dedicated research staff in collaboration with their healthcare provider between December 2023 and September 2024. Cohort 2 included adult (aged 18+) patients currently receiving treatment for any type and stage of cancer within the University of Minnesota M Health Fairview system. These participants were recruited via electronic medical record messages between May and September 2024 and completed an electronic consent process over the phone with a study coordinator. For both cohorts, participants were required to be able to read and write English. A smartphone was provided by the study team to participants who did not own a smartphone for the duration of the study. Participants were eligible at any point after their original diagnosis. Participants were compensated $180 for participation in Cohort 1 and $200 for participation in Cohort 2.

Data Collection

After study enrollment and consent, participants were asked to install the mobile application on their smartphone and use it for 28 days. The mobile application, Daynamica, is an established, publicly available application that leverages GPS location data, as well as data from other phone sensors (e.g., accelerometer, gyroscope) to infer a user's location and activity type. The app distinguishes (1) activities (e.g., picking up medications from the pharmacy), which take place at a specific location, and (2) trips, during which individuals move between activities. Participants label locations for the initial time at a given location (e.g., Home). From locations provided by the participant, the “Activity type” is inferred and the mode of transport for trips between locations was identified (car, walk, bike, etc.). Repeated visits to the same care location were automatically recognized and labeled.

When an individual is away from home, the activities are grouped into an episode. Each episode is labeled as being cancer‐related (“facility‐based” out‐of‐home or home‐based healthcare episodes) or not cancer related. For those cancer‐related, the app prompts participants to further clarify the type of care received by selecting all that apply: labs, treatment, clinic visit, research, pharmacy, imaging, other, and to estimate time spent waiting during that episode (no wait, < 15, 15–30, 30–60, > 60 min). These included both ambulatory and in‐patient encounters. At the end of each day, participants were asked to review, correct, and label any unlabeled episodes at the end of each day. Participants were also asked daily to complete a short (< 5 min) survey documenting the amount of time spent on any cancer‐related phone calls or activities (e.g., scheduling appointments, arranging help) during that day that were not otherwise reported.

Analysis

This analysis included only participants who engaged with the application for at least 7 days (including both activity data collection and end‐of‐day surveys) and recorded at least one out‐of‐home cancer healthcare related episode. We additionally excluded individuals who were participating in a therapeutic clinical trial and those from Cohort 2 who were not receiving systemic anti‐cancer therapy (such as those undergoing surgery or radiation alone). The consort diagram is shown in Figure S1. The outcomes of interest for this analysis were time spent on out‐of‐home healthcare activities including labs, imaging, clinic visits for cancer care, clinic visits for non‐cancer care, cancer treatment, non‐cancer treatments, hospitalizations, and waiting for services either alone or in combination with other services. We also evaluated time spent at home on healthcare including telehealth appointments, taking medications, symptom management, scheduling appointments, managing medical bills, and arranging for additional help related to cancer care. We combined time across care activities to identify total out‐of‐home care time (including waiting time), travel time, and total time spent on tasks at home per week for all weeks in which participants had data (all had at least 1 week of data).

Participant demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Activity data for each participant were generated by combining episodic data with the daily survey responses. We used descriptive statistics and visualizations to provide a detailed breakdown and analysis of time spent on out‐of‐home and at‐home cancer‐related tasks.

Results

Of the 144 participants enrolled in Cohort 1 and Cohort 2, the analytic sample for this study included 110 patients (55 from each Cohort). The median age of participants was 58 years old (range 26–85 years). The majority were female (80.0%), White (78.2%), and privately insured (57.3%). Almost half were working at the time of participation. Cancer types varied, with breast and ovarian being the most prominent cancer type. The cohorts differed in that Cohort 1 had a greater proportion of women (100% vs. 60%) due to being exclusively comprised of patients with breast and ovarian cancer, as well as having a greater proportion of Black participants (18.2% vs. 3.6%), individuals without any college education (30.9% vs. 16.4%), and governmental insurance coverage (Medicare, Medicaid, or TRICARE; 54.5% vs. 29.1%) than Cohort 2 (Table 1). Cohort 1 also had more patients treated for recurrent/progressive disease with infusion (intravenous or intramuscular) treatments. Compared to those on oral therapies, individuals on infusion therapies in this sample included a higher proportion of patients who were Black (14.3% vs. 0%), lacked college education (27.4% vs. 11.5%), unemployed (52.4% vs. 42.3%), and on governmental insurance (47.6% vs. 23.1%) (Table S1).

The 110 participants had 597 out‐of‐home healthcare episodes. Care time per episode ranged from 0 to 108 h, with the longest episodes including multi‐day hospitalizations. Figure 1 is a stacked bar plot that summarizes total time spent in each of the 597 episodes, decomposed into three components of the care process: (1) travel to and from appointments, (2) waiting for care, and (3) time receiving care. The median episode length was 112 min (1.9 h). Qualitatively, while there were outliers in individual components (e.g., greatest round‐trip travel time was 11 h; 13% of episodes included waits of > 60 min), these components were relatively consistent compared to the wider variability in treatment time. The individuals with the highest total time predominantly had increased treatment time as the largest component of total time.

Time spent on traveling, waiting, and receiving care for each out‐of‐home cancer‐related healthcare episode (597 episodes from 110 participants). Plot is truncated at 12 h.

When considering individual components, median care time varied by type of service per healthcare episode. The median care time was least for lab only visits (10 min), followed by cancer clinic visits (43 min), imaging (49 min) or treatment only visits (i.e., no lab or imaging, 68 min) (Table 2). The median wait time for single types (e.g., lab only, imaging only) of out‐of‐home healthcare episodes was 8 min (e.g., most participants reported waiting < 15 min). Importantly, when visits were combined, the time increased beyond what would be additive for individual components. This was particularly notable where labs were added to another out‐of‐home healthcare episode type: combined visits with labs + cancer clinic visits had a median duration of 75 min, labs + treatment visits 162 min, and labs + cancer clinic visit + treatment visit 191 min. In contrast, the median reported wait time for multi‐component visits only modestly increased.

Patients within this study also spent substantial time on healthcare activities at home (median 164 min). For the 49 patients who accessed telehealth (n = 108 telehealth visits), 22% were between 0 and 15 min, 32% between 15 and 30 min, 32% between 30 and 60 min, and 14% greater than 60 min. In addition to formal telehealth visits, patients spend substantial time at home on other cancer‐related healthcare activities. The median amount of time spent per week taking medicine was 60 min, managing symptoms was 55 min, and scheduling appointments was 8 min.

When considering mode of therapy, patients on infusion therapy spent more time on cancer‐related healthcare activities both out‐of‐the‐home (median 125 min per week) and at home (median 188 min per week) compared to those on oral therapies (median 50 and 102 min per week, respectively). Modest differences in these categories were observed with patients on infusion therapy having more travel time (median 45 vs. 28 min), time on symptom management (65 min vs. 37 min), and scheduling appointments (9 vs. 7 min) per week (Table 3). Findings were similar amongst the breast cancer only cohort, which was the most common cancer type in the dataset (data not shown).

Discussion

This analysis provides detailed estimates on both facility‐based and home‐based time spent managing and receiving cancer‐related healthcare activities. We demonstrated that the selection of treatments with less frequent schedules and use of oral therapies instead of IV therapies could influence time spent on healthcare, which should be considered in the context of other potential considerations such as toxicity and efficacy. This study found home‐based time burden might be greater for those on infusion‐based therapies overall due to symptom management and scheduling needs that appeared more time intensive than for those oral therapies. This provides further support that pharmaceutical developers should consider the potential for time toxicity and ability to administer medications at home in treatment development.

Our data highlight potential tradeoffs that can guide future interventions to minimize time toxicity. For example, there are choices about whether to do all out‐of‐home healthcare activities (laboratory and imaging tests, appointment, infusion visits) on the same day or spread across multiple days. Consolidation of the schedule has potential to minimize drive time but also may be associated with substantial wait time between activities as they may be scheduled at different times throughout the day and tests may require results to be posted prior to an appointment or infusion, resulting in substantial wait times. This likely contributes to the observed more‐than‐additive time increase for the addition of labs to other care components. Another potential burden‐reducing approach is to conduct laboratory and imaging tests locally to minimize travel time and clinic wait time in high volume cancer centers. Furthermore, this may avoid unnecessary travel for canceled treatment due to abnormal lab or imaging results. At the same time, this approach may lead to visits spread over multiple days or increase time spent traveling. Minimizing time toxicity through tailored scheduling will require a deeper understanding of patients' priorities and competing time demands.

The option for home‐based healthcare presents an additional option to minimize time toxicity. Our data supports the use of telehealth as a home‐based solution that can minimize drive and wait time. The use of telehealth is currently threatened by policy changes [16], and advocacy is needed to continue this option for patients. While telehealth and home‐based care are important options, some patients may prefer facility‐based care. There may be shifts in the perception of the quantity and quality of time spent face‐to‐face with their clinical team. In addition, touchpoints with the broader care team (e.g., social work, care coordinators) that benefit patients are often missing from telehealth appointments. Finally, receipt of care in the home setting can shift burden to caregivers, who fill in additional roles managing symptoms and treatments. Both patients and caregivers need education and support for successful home‐based care delivery, including the use of technology for video visits, management of symptoms, treatment administration, and navigating financial concerns. Furthermore, these activities can be time‐consuming, and interventions to simplify home‐based care to minimize burden are needed as well [17].

This study has several limitations. The participants were receiving care at only two institutions. Other cancer centers may have different workflows that would impact how long patients spend receiving services. Different catchment areas require patients to travel varying distances to receive care. However, this analysis does highlight potential tradeoffs that could be considered in new contexts. There is a possibility of measurement error if participants do not consistently carry their phones when leaving their home, resulting in missing data. Another limitation is that we do not analyze the perceived burden of these different types of time commitments. Understanding the perception of time spent in cancer‐related care is critical to inform interventions to minimize time burdens. The study was also only available to participants who spoke English, and those with a need for language translation may have different time burdens. Finally, due to the small sample size and study duration, we are not able to look at either time demands for individual medications or for the trajectory of a cancer journey.

Conclusions

Patients spend substantial time on their cancer care both within facilities and at home. Granular estimates of components can facilitate consideration of tradeoffs in where time is spent including same day vs. different day services, use of telehealth, and choice of oral vs. infusion therapies. Additional work is needed to develop and test interventions to reduce time toxicity.
