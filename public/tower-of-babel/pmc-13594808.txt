Phosphorus alleviates age-associated osteoporosis in laying hens by reshaping bone metabolism and modulating cecal microbial communities
Authors: Kai Guan, Yu Xiao, Changchun Xu, Haijun Zhang, Shugeng Wu, Zhigang Song, Sumei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis in aged laying hens poses a major constraint on long-term egg production system. This study investigated the efficacy and underlying mechanisms of dietary phosphorus (P) in alleviating age-associated osteoporosis. A completely randomized design was employed, with 300 Hy-Line Brown laying hens (58 weeks of age) randomly assigned to one of three dietary treatments: a low-phosphorus diet (LP, 0.12% non-phytate phosphorus [NPP]), a regular-phosphorus diet (RP, 0.32% NPP), or the LP diet supplemented with 10,000 FTU/kg phytase (PHY, 0.12% NPP). Each treatment comprised 10 replicates of 10 hens. Following a 2-week adaptation period, the experiment lasted for 12 weeks.The results showed that dietary P reduction or phytase supplementation had no significant effect (P > 0.05) on egg production performance or egg quality throughout the experimental phase. However, at the end of the trial, hens fed the LP diet exhibited significantly lower serum P concentration and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, along with reduced tibial P and magnesium (Mg) contents (P < 0.05), compared with the RP group. Conversely, serum calcium (Ca) level and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRACP) activity were significantly elevated in the LP group (P < 0.05). In contrast, phytase supplementation in the PHY group significantly increased serum ALP activity and tibial P and Mg contents relative to the LP group (P < 0.05). Notably, the PHY group also showed higher serum Ca concentration than the RP group (P < 0.05). Histomorphological examination revealed progressive cortical bone thinning accompanied by irregular erosion and increased demineralized areas in the tibiae of LP-fed hens, whereas only mild pathological changes were observed in the PHY group. Analysis of cecal microbiota indicated that dietary P modulation significantly altered microbial community structure: principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) showed distinct clustering for the LP and PHY group at week 8 and week 12, respectively (P < 0.05). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) further revealed that the LP group was enriched with pro-inflammatory taxa and microbes associated with nutrient metabolism stress. The RP group harbored a more complex and diverse microbial community, while the PHY group was characterized by an enrichment of beneficial bacteria involved in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production. Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated significant associations between specific bacterial genera and tibial health parameters. Genera such as Phascolarctobacterium, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, and Bacteroides were positively correlated with tibial mineral content and structural integrity, whereas Lactobacillus, Olsenella, and Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group showed negative correlations with these bone traits (P < 0.05).Collectively, these findings indicate that chronic P deficiency induces severe skeletal deterioration and gut microbiota dysbiosis in aged laying hens. Phytase supplementation partially counteracts the adverse effects of low-P diet by improving P utilization and reshaping the cecal microbiota toward a more beneficial composition. Nevertheless, long-term feeding of P-deficient diet even with phytase remains insufficient to fully preserve skeletal health. Therefore, ensuring adequate dietary P supply is essential for alleviating age-associated osteoporosis in aged laying hens, likely through integrated regulation of bone metabolic homeostasis and gut microbial ecology.Keywords: Aged laying hen, Phosphorus, Osteoporosis, Bone metabolism, Gut microbiota

Introduction

Osteoporosis in aged laying hens is an important nutritional and metabolic disease in current laying hen farming, directly related to the realization of the goal of an ultra-long laying period (500 eggs in 700 days). The main manifestations of osteoporosis are cessation of egg production, accelerated bone loss, and increased fracture rate. The incidence rate is 16% to 30%, and the mortality rate is as high as 35% (Fu et al., 2022; Whitehead, 2004; Zhao et al., 2020), which greatly affects the farming benefits. Due to the rapid bone turnover metabolism and unique medullary bone of laying hens, as the laying period extends, structural bones will gradually be absorbed (Fu et al., 2022; Lyu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2020). Particularly in aged laying hens, declining estrogen levels and diminished bone remodeling capacity lead to a significant increase in the incidence of osteoporosis (Lyu et al., 2023). Therefore, reducing the incidence of osteoporosis in aged laying hens is crucial for extending the laying period, representing a key challenge in current laying hen industry.

Phosphorus (P) is an essential mineral element for laying hens, crucial for maintaining bone integrity (Hansen et al., 1976). Its supply level is directly related to the occurrence and development of osteoporosis in laying hens. Yu et al. (2008) found that feeding low‑P diets to aged laying hens increases bone loss and induces osteoporosis. A series of recent studies on laying hens have further revealed that feeding low‑P diets during peak laying periods significantly reduces overall bone quality, disrupts skeletal homeostasis, and induces osteoporosis (Teng et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). Aged laying hens may be more sensitive to dietary P deficiency (Ren et al., 2023). Moreover, studies on broilers and rats have also demonstrated that adequate P supplementation effectively reduces the occurrence of osteoporosis (Cao et al., 2021; Katsumata et al., 2014), indicating that P plays a key role in maintaining skeletal homeostasis in animals, and appropriate dietary P can effectively lower the incidence of osteoporosis in laying hens. Studies have also shown that P participates in regulating the composition of the gut microbiota, directly or indirectly modulating bone metabolism by influencing the microbial communities colonizing the intestines (Borda-Molina et al., 2016; Li et al., 2022; Roth et al., 2022). Therefore, we hypothesize that P may alleviate age‑related osteoporosis in laying hens by modulating bone metabolism and gut microbiota. To test our hypothesis, this study evaluated the effects of dietary non-phytate phosphorus (NPP) levels and phytase supplementation on the laying performance, egg quality, calcium (Ca) and P metabolism, bone health, and cecal microbiota of aged laying hens. The findings will provide a scientific basis for rational P nutrition and osteoporosis prevention in aged laying hens.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

Animal procedures were approved by the management of the Animal Care and Use Committee of Institute of Feed Research, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (approval No. AEC—CAAS-20240301).

Birds and experiment design

In the present research, a completely randomized experimental design was used. A total of 300 58-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens, which had been uniformly fed a conventional laying diet from 17 to 58 weeks of age as per the Chinese Feeding Standard of Chicken (China, 2004), with metabolizable energy of 2700 kcal/kg, crude protein of 16.5%, and Ca and NPP levels of approximately 3.5% and 0.32%, respectively, were randomly assigned to three dietary treatments: a low-phosphorus diet (LP, 0.12% NPP), a regular-phosphorus diet (RP, 0.32% NPP), and the LP diet supplemented with 10,000 FTU/kg phytase (PHY, 0.12% NPP). Each treatment comprised 10 replicates with 10 hens per replicate. Experimental hens had similar initial body weight and laying rate across all the replicates. The Ca content of all diets was maintained at 3.5% by adjusting the inclusion levels of limestone and zeolite powder. The basal diet was of the corn-soybean meal type, following the nutritional requirements and standards published by the National Research Council (1994) and the Chinese Feeding Standard of Chicken (China, 2004). The feed composition and nutrient levels are presented in Table 1. Following a 2-week adaptation period, the experiment lasted for 12 weeks. The hens were housed in a three-story ladder cage system and exposed to 16 h of light daily. The environmental temperature and relative humidity in the chicken house were maintained at 21°C ± 2°C and 50% to 65%, respectively. Feed and water were provided ad libitum. Before the start of the trial, samples of feed ingredients and diets from each treatment were collected for analysis of Ca, P, and NPP content.

Sample collection

At the end of weeks 4, 8, and 12 of the feeding trial, two hens per replicate were randomly selected after a 12 h fasting period. Tibiae of the hens were imaged using a portable X-ray machine (AS/C200, Aoshi Testing Equipment Co., Ltd., Dongguan, China). Tibial status and intrinsic characteristics were evaluated based on the obtained radiographic images. Subsequently, blood samples were collected via wing vein puncture, and serum was immediately separated and stored at –20°C for subsequent analysis. After blood collection, the hens were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Tibial samples were obtained by manually removing adherent muscle and cartilage. Intact left tibiae were used for measuring bone properties and mineral composition and were stored at –20°C. The collected right tibiae were truncated in the middle of the bone mid-diaphysis, with the proximal parts fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histomorphological analysis. The cecal contents were collected in sterile containers for microbiome sequencing and stored at –80°C. To reduce individual biological variation, samples from selected hens in each replicate were pooled into one sample in equal ratios before analyses.

Laying performance and egg quality

Daily egg number, total egg weight, and biweekly feed consumption were recorded for each replicate. The egg production rate, average egg wight, average daily feed intake and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated. The FCR was calculated as the ratio of total feed intake to total egg weight. At the end of weeks 4, 8, and 12 of the feeding trial, four eggs were randomly selected from each replicate for egg quality analysis. Egg weight, yolk weight, and eggshell weight were measured using an electronic scale with an accuracy of 0.01 g. The yolk weight ratio (yolk weight / egg weight × 100%) and eggshell weight ratio (eggshell weight / egg weight × 100%) were calculated. The egg shape index was calculated as the ratio of the vertical length to the transverse length of the egg, measured with an electronic vernier caliper (Shanghai Shenhan Measuring Tools Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Eggshell thickness (blunt end, tip, and equator) and the eggshell breaking strength were measured by an Eggshell Thickness Gauge (ESTG-1, ORKA Food Technology Ltd., Ramat HaSharon, Israel) and an Egg Force Reader (EFR-01, ORKA Food Technology Ltd., Ramat HaSharon, Israel), respectively. The eggshell fracture toughness was calculated according to the formula established by Mabe et al. (2003), as follows:

Eggshell fracture toughness = 0.777 × [2.388 + (2.9934 × 6/R)] × (F/T3/2) where R is the equatorial radius of the egg (mm), F is the eggshell breaking strength (N), and T is the eggshell thickness (mm). Additionally, albumen height, yolk color, and Haugh unit (HU) were measured using the Egg Analyzer™ (EA-01, ORKA Food Technology Ltd., Ramat HaSharon, Israel).

Serum parameter analysis

Serum levels of Ca, P, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRACP) were measured using commercial kits purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols. All kits were colorimetric assays. The manufacturer's claimed intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV) were ≤ 4% and ≤ 6% for Ca, ≤ 2.3% and ≤ 3.1% for P, and ≤ 3% and ≤ 5% for ALP, respectively; for TRACP, the CV values were not directly provided in the product manual.

Bone parameter analysis

Frozen left tibiae were thawed at room temperature for 2 h, after which all soft tissues were removed. Tibial bone mineral content (BMC) and bone mineral density (BMD) were determined using a GE Lunar Prodigy Advance dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (GE Healthcare, USA). A daily quality-control scan using the manufacturer-supplied Lunar calibration phantom was performed before each session to standardize X-ray attenuation and BMD baselines.All bone parameters were quantified using the proprietary workstation software enCORE (version 18.0). Subsequently, tibial bone breaking strength (BBS) was measured following the three-point bending test using a texture analyzer (TMS-Pro, Food Technology Corporation, Sterling, VA, USA). In this test, the tibial shaft was positioned with the ventral side (facing the abdominal cavity) upward to receive the vertical load from the upper indenter, while the dorsal side was placed downward on two lower supports with a span of 40 mm. The indenter was driven downward at a constant speed of 2 mm/min until the specimen fractured, with the loading point at the mid-shaft. A load cell with a rated capacity of 1000 N was used. The tibiae were then defatted (petroleum ether, 48 h), oven-dried (105°C, 24 h), and ashed (muffle furnace, 550°C, 16 h) to obtain ash content as described in the study by Cao et al. (2021). Ash content was calculated as a percentage of fat-free dry weight. After wet digestion with HNO₃ and HClO₄, Ca, P, and magnesium (Mg) concentrations in the ash were determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (5110VDV, Agilent Technologies Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA).

Bone histomorphological analysis

The proximal right tibiae fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were decalcified, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Subsequently, the sections were dewaxed and stained with Masson's trichrome using a commercial kit (G1340, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Images were captured using an upright fluorescence microscope (DM300, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

16S rRNA sequencing of cecal microbiota

Total DNA was extracted from frozen cecal content using the E.Z.N.A. Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) following the manufacturer's instructions, and DNA concentration and purity were determined. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR products were extracted from a 2% agarose gel and the products were purified and quantified. Purified amplicons were subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to the standard protocol of Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw sequencing reads were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (Accession Number: PRJNA1420734).

Statistical analysis

Data other than cecal microbiota were analyzed by one-way ANOVA procedure and means were compared using Duncan's multiple range test with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The replicate was taken as an experimental unit for analysis. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Data were expressed as mean and pooled standard error of the mean (SEM).

Microbial data analysis was performed on the cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com) of Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. Alpha diversity indices (ACE, Chao, Shannon, and Simpson) were employed to evaluate microbial community richness and evenness. Beta diversity was assessed through principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) to compare differences in microbial community structure among groups. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) was applied to identify differences in microbial composition and determine statistically differential biomarkers across groups. Spearman correlation analysis was employed to reveal specific associations between bone parameters and genus-level bacterial taxa across all samples.

Tibial radiography and histomorphology

The X-ray images in Fig. 1 revealed that the tibiae in all groups appeared normal at various time points: the diaphyses were straight, bone density was uniform, and the cortical bone exhibited clear and regular contours. Furthermore, no bending, cracking, fractures, abnormal joint spaces, or periar­ticular swelling were observed.

Cecal microbiota

As shown in Table 7, the alpha diversity of the cecal microbiota was assessed using the Ace, Chao, Simpson, and Shannon indices. The results indicated no significant differences in alpha diversity among the groups during the experimental period (P > 0.05). Beta diversity analysis was employed to compare differences in microbial community structure among the groups. PCoA plots revealed that at week 4, samples from each group were fully aggregated, suggesting similar microbial community compositions across groups at this time point (P = 0.925, Fig. 3A). At week 12, compared with the LP group, the cecal microbial community composition in the PHY group showed significant changes (P = 0.037, Fig. 3C).

Discussion

Facing the dual challenges of increasingly scarce resources and growing environmental pressures, the laying hen industry is confronted with new developmental requirements, namely breeding "long-life" laying hens capable of reaching a production cycle of up to 100 weeks while producing 500 eggs during this period (Bain et al., 2016; Jing et al., 2021). However, osteoporosis in aged laying hens is considered a major threat to long-term rearing and cannot be overlooked in any discussion about extending the egg-laying cycle (Fu et al., 2024). This also implies that the nutritional requirements for "long-life" laying hens need to be re-evaluated. P is a key element constituting the mineral matrix of bones and plays a crucial role in maintaining skeletal integrity and strength (Ciosek et al., 2021). The addition of inorganic P in animal feed is closely related to the conservation of phosphate rock resources and environmental concerns (Cheng et al., 2020; Cordell et al., 2009). Regarding the P requirements of laying hens, studies have pointed out that there is room for further optimization of P levels in current laying hen diets (Jing et al., 2018; Rodehutscord et al., 2023). Research by Pagnussatt et al. (2025) indicated that reducing dietary NPP levels to 0.22% did not affect the production performance or egg quality of laying hens, while significantly increasing tibial Ca and P content from 26 to 41 weeks of age and enhancing eggshell thickness and breaking strength from 66 to 81 weeks of age. Research by Ren et al. (2020b) also confirmed that reducing dietary NPP levels to 0.12% had no negative impact on the egg production performance or egg quality of laying hens aged 40 to 55 weeks. Consistent with the aforementioned studies, the results of this research show that reducing dietary NPP levels to 0.12% did not cause significant adverse effects on the egg production performance or egg quality of laying hens aged 60 to 72 weeks. It should be noted that mild P deficiency may not affect production traits within months, as P can be mobilized from the bones. This increases the risk of skeletal diseases in hens during the egg-laying period and highlights the importance of considering skeletal development in requirement studies (Rodehutscord et al., 2023).

Bone is a mineralized connective tissue that performs fundamental functions such as movement, protection, and regulation of mineral homeostasis (Downey and Siegel, 2006; Florencio-Silva et al., 2015). It undergoes continuous remodeling through the coordinated actions of osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and osteoblast-mediated bone formation (Hadjidakis and Androulakis, 2006). Furthermore, as the primary reservoir for Ca and P in the body, bone maintains systemic homeostasis by dynamically storing and releasing these minerals. The balance between bone formation and bone resorption is essential for preserving bone mass stability and preventing net changes in bone mass after remodeling (Lyu et al., 2024). When dietary P supply is insufficient to meet the physiological demands of laying hens, the body mobilizes P reserves from the bones to maintain homeostasis. This process disrupts the balance of bone remodeling, ultimately leading to bone loss due to inadequate replenishment of the released P. Numerous studies have shown that serum Ca and P levels can reflect skeletal health (Teng et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). When hens develop metabolic bone disorders due to dietary deficiencies in Ca or P, abnormal concentrations of serum Ca and P are observed. In this study, serum P levels were significantly reduced in the LP group, accompanied by decreased Ca utilization, resulting in elevated serum Ca concentrations. Serum ALP concentration, a biomarker of osteoblast activity, decreased with reduced bone formation. Conversely, serum TRACP concentration, which reflects osteoclast activity, increased with accelerated bone resorption (Yao et al., 2011). In this study, serum ALP levels in the LP group were significantly lower than those in the RP and PHY groups, while serum TRACP levels were significantly higher than those in the RP group. This indicates enhanced bone resorption and suppressed bone formation activities in the LP group, placing the hens in a state of negative bone remodeling balance. It is noteworthy that, in most dietary groups, serum Ca, P, and ALP levels showed a gradual increasing trend from week 4 (64 wk) to week 12 (72 wk). This longitudinal elevation was not due to any pre‑experimental restrictive feeding, as all hens had been fed a standard diet before the experiment. Rather, it most likely reflects the natural physiological progression of aging laying hens with advancing age. As hens enter the late laying period, declining estrogen levels disrupt bone remodeling homeostasis, leading to an overall increase in bone turnover (Beck and Hansen, 2004; Yue et al., 2023). Consequently, circulating P and ALP levels rise slowly over time, accompanied by a corresponding increase in serum Ca.

For a long time, tibia quality has been used to assess the P requirements of poultry, as it serves as a more sensitive indicator of P adequacy than production performance (Ren et al., 2020a). A previous study indicated that hens fed a low-P diet (0.12% NPP) mobilized minerals from their bones to compensate for P deficiency, thereby compromising bone health, as evidenced by reduced tibial ash content, decreased stiffness, and diminished maximum load-bearing capacity (Sacakli et al., 2025). The findings of this study are consistent with that report, demonstrating that a low-P diet increases tibial fragility in laying hens. This effect was most pronounced at week 8 of the trial (68 wk), characterized by significant reductions in tibial BMD, BBS, and ash content. Based on fat‑free dry bone weight under ashing at 600 °C, the normal range of tibia ash content in laying hens aged 22 to 78 weeks is approximately 49%–61% (Jing et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2023; Sacakli et al., 2025). In the present study, the tibia ash content of the RP group fell at the lower end of this range, whereas that of the LP group was markedly below the values reported for most healthy hens of comparable age, indicating a severe deviation from normal mineralization levels. Previous studies have demonstrated that bone ash content is highly correlated with bone strength, and that changes in bone ash have an even greater impact on bone strength than equivalent changes in bone volume (Hernandez et al., 2001), with correlation coefficients between bone ash and BMC or BMD exceeding 0.9 (Onyango et al., 2003). In our study, the reduction in bone ash content in the LP group was accompanied by concurrent decreases in BMD and BBS, further confirming the direct negative effect of impaired mineralization on skeletal mechanical properties. Furthermore, regarding tibial mineral composition, the LP group exhibited significantly lower levels of tibia P and Mg compared to the RP group and the PHY group. P is a core constituent element of hydroxyapatite crystals. A decrease in its content leads to excessive carbonate substitution, reduced crystal size, and a lower Ca/P ratio in hydroxyapatite. This not only directly reduces the elastic modulus of bone tissue, but also increases crystal solubility, making bone more susceptible to resorption and erosion under physiological conditions (Amenta et al., 2018; Rodriguez‐Florez et al., 2015). Although tibial X-ray examinations did not reveal any obvious macroscopic structural abnormalities, further histomorphological analysis of the tibia using Masson's trichrome staining showed consistent thinning of the cortical bone thickness in the LP group. Additionally, more irregular erosions and demineralized areas were observed in the intracortical region. Such progressive damage to cortical bone structure typically indicates that severe bone resorption may have occurred in the hens, leading to a decline in bone quality.

In common feed ingredients such as corn and soybean meal, approximately two-thirds of the total P exists in the form of phytate, which is poorly utilized by poultry (Ravindran et al., 1994). Phytase is widely used in the feed industry due to its ability to hydrolyze phytate P in plant-based feeds, thereby reducing the need for inorganic P supplementation (Selle and Ravindran, 2007). However, studies conducted at similar dietary NPP levels have yielded somewhat inconsistent results. Research by Jing et al. (2021) indicated that supplementing the diet of laying hens aged 22 to 34 weeks with 1000 U/kg phytase allowed the NPP level to be reduced to 0.10% without affecting production performance, egg quality, bone quality, or plasma biochemical parameters, while significantly reducing P excretion. In contrast, research by Ren et al. (2023) found that supplementing the diet of laying hens aged 69 to 78 weeks, with an NPP level of 0.12%, with 1470 FTU/kg phytase had no significant impact on egg production performance, egg quality, or serum Ca and P levels, but led to significant reductions in tibial breaking strength, tibial ash content, and Ca and P levels. The discrepancies in research outcomes may stem from factors such as phytase efficacy, poultry breed, age, and other variables. With advancements in phytase production processes and reductions in production costs, excessive phytase supplementation no longer poses a significant economic burden, and studies on conventional dosage levels are already relatively comprehensive (Fernández et al., 2019; Park et al., 2024). In this experiment, laying hens aged 60 to 72 weeks were fed a diet with an NPP level of 0.12% supplemented with 10,000 FTU/kg phytase. The results showed that high-dose phytase supplementation partially alleviated nutritional stress in laying hens, but this effect gradually diminished after week 8 of the trial. This was reflected in a significant increase in serum Ca concentration, a declining trend in serum P levels, tibial BMD, and BMC, as well as minor pathological changes in tibial microstructure, although tibial mineral composition remained at a relatively favorable level. These findings indicate that long-term feeding of a low-P diet supplemented with high-dose phytase still cannot fully meet the P requirements of aged laying hens. Notably, the beneficial effects of phytase on improving the composition of the gut microbiota should not be overlooked.

Given the pivotal role of gut microbiota in nutrient metabolism and host health, and evidence that dietary P and phytase modulate intestinal microbial composition in poultry (Borda-Molina et al., 2016; Li et al., 2022; Roth et al., 2022), we characterized the cecal microbial community. PCoA revealed that treatment‑induced divergence in community structure intensified over time, with significant separation of the LP group at week 8 and the PHY group at week 12. At the phylum level, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes predominated across all groups, consistent with previous reports (Xu et al., 2023). LEfSe analysis revealed distinct enrichment patterns among treatments. In the LP group, P deficiency progressively favored taxa associated with inflammation and adaptive nutrient metabolism. At week 8, the opportunistic pathogen Desulfovibrio, known for its lipopolysaccharide production, was enriched (Zhou et al., 2024). By week 12, the enrichment of Spirochaetota, which may trigger gastrointestinal inflammation, was accompanied by increased abundances of Bacteroides and Prevotellaceae_UCG‑001, both reported to promote Ca and P absorption, as well as Succinatimonas, recognized for its high efficiency in carbohydrate metabolism (Tang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2022). In the RP group, the cecal community exhibited greater complexity. At week 8, enriched taxa included Brachyspira, which is potentially associated with intestinal infection, and unclassified_f__Oscillospiraceae, a taxon linked to short‑chain fatty acid (SCFA) production (Kang et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024). By week 12, this mixed pattern intensified, with concurrent enrichment of the potential pathogen unclassified_f__Synergistaceae, Mailhella (associated with hydrogen sulfide and odor production), the SCFA‑producing Lachnospiraceae_FCS020_group, and GCA‑900066575, which may promote bile acid degradation (Huang et al., 2019; Long et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025). In contrast, phytase addition to the low-P diet consistently steered the microbiota toward a beneficial profile. The PHY group showed enrichment of Erysipelotrichaceae, which participates in feed degradation and SCFA generation, at week 4 (Liu et al., 2021); of the beneficial Ruminococcus, which produces volatile fatty acids and lactic acid to inhibit pathogens, at week 8 (Li et al., 2024); and by week 12, of multiple SCFA producers including Firmicutes, Lachnospiraceae, Butyricicoccus, and CHKCI001 (Dai et al., 2022; Hamid et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025).

SCFAs (acetate, propionate, and butyrate) are key microbial metabolites that support intestinal development and play crucial roles in the skeletal system, influencing satellite cell homeostasis and skeletal muscle, and mitigating osteoporosis (Koh et al., 2016; Lyu et al., 2024). Correlation analysis between cecal microbiota and tibial parameters revealed that SCFA‑producing genera, including Phascolarctobacterium, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Bacteroides, unclassified_f__Oscillospiraceae, and norank_f__Muribaculaceae, were positively correlated with certain tibial parameters, consistent with the ability of SCFAs to inhibit osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption while preserving bone formation (Gao et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2023; Lucas et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025). Conversely, several genera, such as Lactobacillus, Ruminococcus_torques_group, Olsenella, unclassified_f__Prevotellaceae, Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group, unclassified_k__norank_d__Bacteria, and unclassified_f__Lachnospiraceae, showed negative correlations with certain tibial parameters. Notably, the negative correlation of Lactobacillus observed here contrasts with some previous reports, likely due to strain‑specific effects (Tang et al., 2025; Villa et al., 2017). Olsenella abundance is elevated in osteoporosis patients and positively linked to bone resorption markers (Qin et al., 2021), and Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group has been associated with synovial inflammation in knee osteoarthritis (Jie et al., 2025). Additionally, Desulfovibrio and norank_f__norank_o__Clostridia_UCG‑014 exhibited complex, time‑dependent correlations. For example, Desulfovibrio showed a positive correlation with tibial Ca content but negative correlations with tibial P and Mg contents, reflecting its dual roles in health and disease (Zhou et al., 2024).

We consider it necessary to acknowledge the inherent limitations of the 16S rRNA gene sequencing method employed in this study (Johnson et al., 2019). Although this approach is widely recognized for its robust capacity to characterize microbial community structure and detect changes in relative abundance, its taxonomic resolution is generally limited to the genus level, and it does not directly provide information on functional gene expression, metabolic pathway activities, or strain‑level diversity (Matchado et al., 2024). Nevertheless, this resolution is sufficient to address our primary research objectives, namely to identify significant alterations in cecal microbial composition across different dietary treatments and to explore their correlations with tibial parameters. In addition, baseline samples were not collected in this study, as the experimental design focused on cross‑sectional comparisons rather than longitudinal tracking. Rigorous randomization and a 2‑week adaptation period sufficiently controlled for initial differences, so the between‑group comparisons remain reliable. Future investigations will require deeper sequencing approaches, such as shotgun metagenomic sequencing, to achieve species‑level resolution and functional annotation, combined with metabolomic profiling of SCFAs and other microbial metabolites (Knight et al., 2018). Such multi‑omics strategies, supplemented with baseline sampling, would help to further elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the gut‑bone axis observed in the present study.

Conclusions

Although the low-P diet (0.12% NPP) did not significantly affect the egg production performance or egg quality of aged laying hens, it induced disorders in Ca and P metabolism, disrupted bone metabolic balance, and led to severe bone loss, along with causing structural dysbiosis of the gut microbiota. Supplementing the low-P diet with phytase (10,000 FTU/kg) partially alleviated the nutritional stress caused by P deficiency. In the short term, this maintained serum P levels, tibial BMD, and tibial ash content comparable to those of the RP diet (0.32% NPP). It also significantly increased tibial P and Mg contents and improved the composition of the cecal microbiota. However, long-term feeding still proved insufficient to maintain optimal skeletal health. Therefore, to safeguard the overall health of aged laying hens and mitigate skeletal health threats associated with extended laying cycles, appropriate P nutrition must be provided in the diet, which can alleviate age-associated osteoporosis by modulating bone metabolism and cecal microbiota.
