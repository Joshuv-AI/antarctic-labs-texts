Descending Aorta Perforation During Transseptal Puncture for Left Atrial Appendage Closure
Authors: Tommaso Bini, Stefania Quintiliani, Vladimir Makaloski, Roberto Galea, Lorenz Räber, Nicolas Brugger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAortic root injury during transseptal puncture (TSP) is a rare but well-recognized complication. However, injury to the descending aorta has not previously been described in this context.Case SummaryA 57-year-old woman underwent left atrial appendage closure. During TSP using the VersaCross Connect radiofrequency-powered system and a 15-F steerable sheath, an unrecognized perforation of the descending aorta occurred. Despite repeated transthoracic echocardiograms showing no pericardial effusion and initial hemodynamic stability, she subsequently developed a mediastinal hematoma and bilateral pleural effusion. Management required an emergency percutaneous thoracic endovascular aortic repair, followed by surgical evacuation of the hematoma.DiscussionExcessive tenting during radiofrequency-powered TSP, related to interatrial septum aneurysm, resulted in injury to the descending aorta with life-threatening consequences.Take-Home MessageRadiofrequency-powered systems for TSP have enhanced tissue penetration capability and should therefore be used with caution, particularly in the presence of an interatrial septum aneurysm and atypical echocardiographic windows.

Case Presentation

A 57-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation and recurrent bleeding under oral anticoagulation was referred to our center for percutaneous left atrial appendage (LAA) closure (LAAC). Her past cardiovascular medical history was notable for a transient ischemic attack in the presence of a grade III patent foramen ovale. In addition, multiple liver cysts were known. Apixaban 5 mg twice a day was discontinued 2 days before the intervention, and the procedure was performed according to the standard of care at our institution: conscious sedation and transesophageal echocardiographic (TOE) guidance using a X11-4t pediatric probe (Philips Healthcare) for better tolerability. Probably due to the cyst-related hepatomegaly, the TOE windows for the transeptal puncture (TSP) were unusual (Figure 1A), and after the first attempt to cross the interatrial septum using the VersaCross Connect radiofrequency-powered (RFP) system (Boston Scientific), the wire could not be visualized in the left atrium (LA). For this reason, the sheath was not advanced over the septum, and the wire was retracted under the assumption that it was inside the pericardium. Besides, on TOE, a new structure appeared interposed between the probe and the LA (Figure 1B, Video 1), raising suspicion of esophageal perforation, and the TOE probe was withdrawn. An urgent gastroscopy with a 10-mm endoscopic probe was performed under conscious sedation in the catheterization laboratory, which excluded an esophageal lesion or perforation. In consideration of the clinical situation with no PE despite administration of 3,000 IU heparin prior puncture, persistent hemodynamic stability, and an unremarkable gastroscopy, we decided to resume the LAAC procedure 40 minutes after the suspected puncture of the pericardium. Because of the atypical echocardiographic windows, a conventional adult TOE probe X8-2t (Philips Healthcare) was used for the rest of the intervention. The second TSP attempt using RFP was successful; the wire was advanced under TOE guidance, coiled appropriately, and positioned directly in the LAA without any interaction with adjacent structures. Based on the landing zone measurements obtained by 3-dimensional TOE, a Watchman FLX Pro 31 mm (Boston Scientific) was successfully implanted. After the intervention, no anticoagulant or antithrombotic therapy was given in view of the initial mispuncture, and the patient was monitored in the cardiological intermediate care unit (IMC), the control TTE revealed no new on-set PE, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable only reporting mild thoracic back pain. After 4 hours of IMC monitoring and multiple TTE ruling PE out (Figure 2), she was transferred to the ward with persisting low-intensity back pain. After mobilization, the patient experienced a syncopal episode due to marked hypotension and was therefore readmitted to our IMC for close monitoring. Serial blood tests revealed a rapid drop in hemoglobin levels from 99 g/L at the IMC admission to 80 g/L two hours later (hemoglobin level at hospital admission: 121 g/L). An urgent thoracoabdominal computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a large mediastinal hematoma compressing the esophagus as well a bilateral pleural effusion, caused by active arterial bleeding from the descending aorta (Figure 3). The CT did not demonstrate any evidence of active bleeding from the posterior wall of the LA. Management

The mediastinal hematoma management required an interdisciplinary approach involving both vascular and thoracic surgeons. As first-line intervention, a percutaneous endovascular aortic repair with a 100-mm-long thoracic stent graft was realized to cover the lesion and stop the hemorrhage (Video 2). Subsequently, bilateral thoracic drainages were placed to evacuate the hematoma.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Two days later, a follow-up CT scan showed no evidence of leak from the thoracic stent graft but demonstrated a persistent right-sided mediastinal hematoma, potentially jeopardizing the esophageal circulation, thus requiring an additional video-assisted thoracoscopic evacuation. After thoracoscopy, antithrombotic therapy was reinitiated, initially with prophylactic unfractioned heparin and subsequently replaced by clopidogrel 75/mg daily. Over time, the hemoglobin level rose, and the patient showed significant clinical improvement permitting to remove the thoracic drains 3 days after the last operation. She was discharged home 8 days after LAAC on clopidogrel monotherapy after an unremarkable control chest x-ray.

Discussion

TSP is an obligatory step during a wide range of left-sided percutaneous cardiac interventions, such as pulmonary veins ablation, mitral valvuloplasty, mitral valve transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, and LAAC. Its use has increased steadily over the past decade, and it is now widely performed by interventional cardiologists. Most interventions performed by these operators require TOE guidance, and TSP is therefore carried out under echocardiographic visualization, which markedly reduces the risk of injury to adjacent structures or inadvertent entry into the pericardial space. TSP represents a critical step during LAAC and can rarely results in severe, potentially life-threatening complications: operators must be fully aware of the associated risks.1

To our knowledge, it is the first report of accidental puncture of the descending aorta in the setting of TSP for any intervention including percutaneous LAAC, while perforation of the ascending aorta has been reported in few cases.2, 3, 4 Conventionally, an inferior-posterior puncture of the fossa ovalis is targeted during LAAC not only to facilitate the coaxiality between the delivery sheath and the LAA axis but also to reduce the risk of accidental puncture of the ascending aorta.5

A priori, it seems anatomically difficult to explain how the descending aorta could be perforated during a transseptal puncture. Based on the echocardiographic guidance images from the initial puncture and by recreating the same views using multiplanar reconstruction on the preprocedural CT, we could show that the pressure applied on the 15-F steerable sheath catheter, combined with an interatrial septum aneurysm (ASA), could have produced excessive tenting, allowing the catheter tip to reach the posterior wall of the LA (Figure 4). Probably the radiofrequency application then permits the wire to cross all 4, at this time, adjacent layers (interatrial septum, LA posterior wall, pericardium, and descending aortic wall). Atypical echocardiographic windows due to hepatomegaly, the use of a pediatric TOE probe with poor visualization of extracardiac structures because of its high frequency, the presence of an ASA (septal excursion 14 mm, thickness of the septum primum 2 mm), small atria (LA indexed volume 26 mL/m2), and the use of RFP together created a combination of factors that led to this otherwise highly improbable complication of TSP under TOE guidance.

Although the complication was successfully managed with implantation of a thoracic stent graft, its earlier recognition might have allowed a timely endovascular treatment, limiting blood loss as well as compression of adjacent organs, possibly sparing the patient a surgical evacuation of the hematoma. Implantation of an occluder as described previously in cases involving the ascending aorta would probably not have been possible given the difficulty in identifying and catheterizing the small lesion in the descending aorta.2, 3, 4 The loss of wire visualization after TSP under the assumption of a pericardial position and the repeated TTE examinations without PE both lead to a delayed diagnosis.

Retrospectively, resuming the procedure after the urgent gastroscopy and administering heparin were contraindicated. However, at that time there were no clinical sings suggestive of active bleeding, and continuation of the procedure after pericardial wire positioning is not unusual if the sheath was not advanced and no PE is observed.

The use of the RFP system VersaCross Connect is one of the multiple possible cofactors responsible for this severe adverse event but probably not the leading cause, as previous studies have reported comparable outcomes in terms of periprocedural complication rates regardless of the TSP system used. Nevertheless, the available evidence is limited, with small study populations and several methodological limitations.6, 7, 8 The benefits of RFP systems include a precise and stable puncture at the selected position of the septum, something which is more challenging with a conventional transseptal needle that often requires more force and some rotation to penetrate the septum resulting in a less controlled TSP. Notwithstanding this advantage of the RFP system, it has shown the potential to penetrate multiple structures in the presence of substantial tenting due to ASA and a small LA.

Conclusions

TSP for percutaneous intervention such as LAAC, especially under echocardiographic guidance, is a very safe procedural step but may rarely result in serious adverse events, and physicians should be aware of the potential risks. Our report describes the inadvertent puncture of the descending aorta resulting in mediastinal hematoma and pleural effusions, which required an interdisciplinary approach involving vascular and thoracic surgeons. Accidental puncture of surrounding structure during TSP may result in fatal complications. Its prompt recognition and immediate management are essential as clearly illustrated by the present case.
