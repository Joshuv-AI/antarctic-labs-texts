Post-marketing safety profile of bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide: a FAERS pharmacovigilance study with EudraVigilance validation and mechanistic investigation of pancreatitis
Authors: Yuxin Xie, Yawen Luo, Yinghua Chen, Guoqing Shi, Yihuai He, Xia Liu, Zhiwei Cui, Fan Zou, Chengyu Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBictegravir/Emtricitabine/Tenofovir alafenamide (BIC/FTC/TAF) has demonstrated strong efficacy and favorable tolerability as a first-line HIV treatment. However, there is a lack of long-term safety data from large-scale studies and real-world settings. This study aimed to evaluate the post-marketing safety of BIC/FTC/TAF using the FAERS database, with external validation in EudraVigilance, and to further explore the potential mechanisms underlying the pancreatitis signal.MethodsA retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis was conducted using data from FAERS from 2018 to 2025. deduplication, disproportionality analyses were performed using the reporting odds ratio and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network methods, with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. Sex-stratified analyses, time-to-onset analyses, and sensitivity analyses were additionally performed. Key findings were externally validated using EudraVigilance. Because pancreatitis emerged as an unexpected and clinically important signal, logistic regression, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and transcriptomic validation were further applied to explore its potential biological basis.ResultsA total of 5,815 adverse drug (ADE) reports were identified in FAERS, Reports were more frequently submitted for male patients. Several identified positive ADEs were consistent with the drug label, such as weight increase (n = 526, ROR 10.32), headache (n = 239, ROR 1.56), immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) (n = 113, ROR 76.26). Unexpected signals included “pancreatitis” (n = 51, ROR 4.37), “dysphagia” (n = 104, ROR 4.47), and “mental disorder (n = 33, ROR 3.3). Sex-stratified analyses showed more positive signals in males than in females, with differences in the signal spectrum between subgroups. Among 813 reports with valid time-to-onset data, most adverse events occurred within the first month, although approximately one-quarter were reported after 1 year. External validation in EudraVigilance identified 75 overlapping signals with FAERS, including weight increased, insomnia, dysphagia, diabetes mellitus, pancreatitis, and acute pancreatitis. Mechanistic analyses suggested that BIC/FTC/TAF-associated pancreatitis may involve inflammatory and metabolic pathways, particularly the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, with hub targets including NFKB1, AKT1, STAT3, and MMP9.ConclusionThis study expands the post-marketing safety profile of BIC/FTC/TAF by identifying both recognized and potentially emerging adverse event signals in real-world pharmacovigilance data. Cross-database replication supported the robustness of several findings, particularly metabolic, psychiatric, renal, hepatobiliary, and pancreatic signals. Although these findings are hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causality, they support continued clinical vigilance during both early and long-term BIC/FTC/TAF treatment, especially in patients with polypharmacy or metabolic vulnerability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03178-2.Keywords: Bictegravir/Emtricitabine/Tenofovir Alafenamide (BIC/FTC/TAF), Adverse drug events (ADEs), Pharmacovigilance, FAERS, EudraVigilance, Pancreatitis

Background

According to the Joint United Nations Programme on Human Immunodeficiency Virus/Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (HIV/AIDS), by 2023, an estimated 39.9 million individuals will live with HIV, with 1.3 million new infections. Despite a decline in HIV/AIDS-related deaths between 2000 and 2019, the rate of improvement slowed from 2019 to 2021 [1]. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has significantly improved HIV-1 prognosis. The latest guidelines recommend a regimen of two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) combined with an integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI). Bictegravir (BIC), a second-generation INSTI, has shown robust efficacy when combined with emtricitabine (FTC) and tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) [2]. In phase III trials, the BIC/FTC/TAF regimen achieved virological suppression comparable to dolutegravir (DTG) after 48 weeks and was non-inferior at week 144 [3, 4].

However, the safe use of medications necessitates attention to potential adverse drug events (ADEs). In a phase 3 clinical trial, treatment with BIC/FTC/TAF was generally well tolerated and most ADEs were mild to moderate in severity [5]. Another 5-year follow-up study also identified that the most common ADEs were headache (4.5%) and diarrhea (4.7%) [6]. Considering the long-term nature of BIC/FTC/TAF treatment, there is potential for some ADEs to worsen or for previously undetected safety issues to emerge. Of particular concern are ADEs not explicitly listed in the drug’s label, as these may be overlooked by both clinicians and patients during treatment.

The U.S. FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) is the world’s largest pharmacovigilance database, capturing spontaneous reports from diverse sources. FAERS serves as a valuable pharmacovigilance tool for detecting potential safety signals and identifying unexpected adverse drug events in real-world settings [7, 8]. However, because FAERS is a spontaneous reporting system, its analyses are subject to limitations such as under-reporting, reporting bias, and the potential for false-positive or false-negative signals. Therefore, findings derived from FAERS should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causal relationships. This study aimed to conduct a pharmacovigilance analysis using FAERS data to identify ADEs related to BIC/FTC/TAF, focusing on uncovering additional adverse events that may not have been detected in clinical trials. To further assess the robustness and external generalizability of the primary findings, we performed an external validation using the EudraVigilance database, a large independent pharmacovigilance system managed by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) [9]. Because pancreatitis emerged as an unexpected and clinically important signal, integrated network pharmacology to investigate its potential biological basis. This combined strategy was intended not only to improve signal detection and cross-database credibility, but also to generate biologically informed hypotheses that may support safer long-term use of BIC/FTC/TAF in clinical practice.

Methods

Data sources and preprocessing

We conducted a retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis utilizing the FAERS database. The FAERS database comprises seven primary datasets: demographic and administrative information (DEMO), drug details (DRUG), adverse drug reactions (REAC), patient outcomes (OUCT), report sources (RPSR), drug treatment initiation and termination dates (THER), and drug administration indications (INDI). These files were interconnected within the FAERS architecture using specific identification numbers, such as PRIMARYID (unique report identifier).

Considering that BIC/FTC/TAF received FDA approval for market release in February 2018, this study retrospectively analyzed adverse drug event (ADE) reports associated with BIC/FTC/TAF from January 1, 2018 (Q1 2018) to December 31, 2025 (Q4 2025). We identified ADE reports associated with BIC/FTC/TAF by searching for both its generic name (BIC/FTC/TAF) and its brand name (BIKTARVY). We strictly adhere to the official guidelines on data cleaning provided by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, ensuring the uniqueness of the reports [10]. In our study, we utilized CASEIDs (identification number for FAERS cases), FDA_DTs (date the FDA received the case), and PRIMARYIDs as key filters to remove duplicate records. We extracted the PRIMARYIDs, CASEIDs, and FDA_DTs fields from the DEMO file in the raw data and organized them. Specifically, when CASEID was identical, the most recent FDA_DT was retained. In cases where both CASEID and FDA_DT were identical, the report with the higher PRIMARYID was selected [11]. Furthermore, since the first quarter of 2019, each quarterly data package has included a list of deleted reports. Following data deduplication, reports are excluded based on the CASEID found in the deleted reports list [12]. In FAERS, drugs are classified into four categories: PS (primary suspected), SS (secondary suspected), C (concomitant), and I (interaction). We retained only those ADE reports classified as PS [13]. To assess the robustness and external generalizability of the primary analyses, we conducted an external validation using the EMA-managed pharmacovigilance database, EudraVigilance. EudraVigilance collects spontaneous reports of suspected ADEs from across the European Economic Area (EEA) and is one of the primary sources of pharmacovigilance data in Europe [14]. Validation data were obtained from the publicly accessible EudraVigilance platform (https://www.adrreports.eu/en/search_subst.html), including all available reports submitted from the inception of the database in 2002 through December 31, 2025. In accordance with EMA data disclosure policies, the public dataset includes only spontaneous ADE reports in which the drug of interest is designated as a suspected drug, excluding reports derived from clinical trials or reports in which the drug is recorded solely as a concomitant medication. In both databases, ADEs were classified according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA, version 28.1). MedDRA comprises five hierarchical levels, from lowest to highest: lowest level terms (LLTs), preferred terms (PTs), high-level terms (HLTs), high-level group terms (HLGTs), and system organ classes (SOCs) [15]. LLTs represent the most granular terms, often reflecting the verbatim expressions reported by healthcare professionals or patients. PTs serve as the standard terms for describing specific ADEs and are widely used to identify pharmacovigilance signals, enabling the evaluation of both documented and previously unlisted drug-related events. HLTs group related PTs based on similar clinical manifestations, facilitating more comprehensive signal detection. HLGTs further aggregate HLTs with common pathophysiological or etiological characteristics, allowing broader classification of related events. SOCs, the highest level, organize ADEs by organ system, disease etiology, or therapeutic intervention, providing a structured overview of safety data across the body system level. This hierarchical structure enables precise, consistent, and systematic evaluation of ADEs for pharmacovigilance and regulatory purposes [16]. Notably, within the outcome variable OUTC_COD, the code “OT” represents other serious, which correspond to Important Medical Events (IMEs) as defined by regulatory reporting standards.

Data analysis

In pharmacovigilance studies, disproportionality analysis is an essential method for detecting potential links between drugs and adverse events [17]. Signals refer to new or established associations between adverse events and drugs [18]. To enhance the reliability of our results, we employed both non-Bayesian [19]. Table 1 provides the 2 × 2 contingency tables and detailed formulas for the disproportionality analysis methods, along with the signal thresholds. Signals with larger values indicate a stronger association with drug use. To minimize the risk of false-positive results (type I errors), we applied the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons [20]. The formula is as follows: P-adjusted = P-original × n, where P-original represents the original P-value from the Chi-square test, P-adjusted is the Bonferroni-corrected P-value, and n is the total number of tests conducted. To improve the reliability of the study results, signals that met the predefined thresholds of both algorithms at the PT level and had an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were classified as positive PTs. ADEs related to drug indications were excluded to improve clarity. Signals not listed in the drug label were designated as “unexpected signals.”

Time-of-onset (TTO) analysis

The TTO of ADEs was calculated using the following formula: Onset time = event start date (EVENT_DT) – treatment start date (START_DT). Cases with missing or inaccurate dates were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, cases where the ADE onset date occurred before the start of BIC/FTC/TAF treatment were excluded. In our study, we employed the median, interquartile range (IQR), minimum, maximum, and Weibull shape parameter to comprehensively evaluate the TTO characteristics [21]. The cumulative incidence of ADEs associated with BIC/FTC/TAF was described using the Kaplan-Meier method [22]. The scale parameter (α) of the Weibull distribution test defines the distribution’s scale, while the shape parameter (β) determines its shape. A shape parameter β < 1, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) excluding 1, suggests a decrease in ADE frequency over time, indicative of early failure. If β is approximately 1 and its 95% CI includes 1, it suggests a random failure characteristic, where the AE rate remains constant over time. Conversely, a β > 1 with a 95% CI excluding 1 indicates an increase in ADE frequency over time, characteristic of wear failures [23]. Additionally, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess differences in the time to onset of adverse events across different SOC levels. The TTO analysis at the SOC level helps elucidate the temporal patterns and potential timing differences in the occurrence of adverse events across organ systems, thereby providing complementary evidence for clinical risk monitoring and medication safety management.

BIC/FTC/TAF-related DIP network construction

Among the unexpected signals identified, pancreatitis was selected for further mechanistic exploration because it represents a clinically serious adverse event with potentially life-threatening consequences. In addition, several case reports have previously suggested a possible association between BIC/FTC/TAF and acute pancreatitis, making it a biologically and clinically relevant target for mechanistic investigation. Therefore, network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses were conducted to explore the potential pathways underlying this signal. The chemical structure and SMILES notation of BIC/FTC/TAF (C[C@H](CN1C=NC2 = C(N = CN=C21)N)OC[P@@](= O)(N[C@@H](C)C(= O)OC(C)C)OC3 = CC = CC=C3.C1C[C@@H]2 C[C@H]1N3[C@H](O2)CN4C = C(C(= O)C(= C4C3 = O)O)C(= O)NCC5 = C(C = C(C=C5F)F)F.C1[C@H](O[C@H](S1)CO)N2C = C(C(= NC2 = O)N)F) were retrieved from the PubChem database. The SMILES notation was then analyzed using the PharmMapper and SuperPred databases to identify potential drug targets, resulting in 399 distinct targets after removing duplicates and normalizing entries with UniProt. To pinpoint DIP-associated targets, data from GeneCards (relevance score ≥ 1) and the online Mendelian inheritance in man (OMIM) database (using default value) were utilized. Overlapping targets between BIC/FTC/TAF and DIP were identified using R software, followed by further analysis through the STRING database to construct protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks. Hub genes were determined using Cytoscape 3.7.1 based on betweenness centrality. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the intersecting genes using the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4) in R, aiming to uncover biological pathways that may be involved in BIC/FTC/TAF-related DIP.

Molecular docking analysis

Crystal structures of the proteins encoded by the core target genes were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, www.rcsb.org/pdb/home/home.do). During the selection of PDB entries, preference was given to protein structures determined by X-ray diffraction with a resolution better than 2.0 Å. For molecular docking analysis, the target proteins and the SMILES structures of BIC/FTC/TAF were uploaded to the CB-Dock2 platform for docking calculations [24]. Binding energy serves as a key indicator of the stability of the protein-ligand complex, with lower binding energies corresponding to stronger affinity and higher likelihood of forming a stable complex. Binding energies below − 5.0 kJ/mol are generally considered indicative of strong molecular binding activity [25]. Among the generated docking conformations, the one with the lowest binding free energy was selected for visualization to illustrate the potential interactions between the core targets and BIC/FTC/TAF.

Validation of core target expression using transcriptome analysis

The dataset GSE194331 was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), retrieved on March 8, 2026. GSE194331 comprises RNA sequencing data from peripheral blood samples of patients with pancreatitis, including 87 patient samples categorized as mild (n = 57), moderate (n = 20), and severe (n = 10). In this study, we extracted peripheral blood gene expression data for 87 pancreatitis patients and 32 healthy controls to validate the expression of key target genes. The diagnostic potential of these targets was further evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Statistical analysis

All BIC/FTC/TAF-related data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.2.3 and Microsoft Excel 2019.

Results

Demographics and reporting trends of BIC/FTC/TAF-related ADEs

In this study, after removing duplicates, 5,815 BIC/FTC/TAF-related ADEs and 14,757 BIC/FTC/TAF-related PTs were identified (Fig. 1). Among reports with available sex information, those coded as male were more frequent than those coded as female (68.5% vs. 22.8%); however, sex was not available/was unspecified in a subset of reports, and therefore these proportions reflect the reporting composition rather than the sex distribution of exposed patients. Among the reports with known age information, the largest number of cases involved patients aged 18–65 years (2,835 cases). However, weight information was missing in 88.6% of the reports. The United States was the primary country of origin for these reports (4,192 cases, 72.1%). The primary reporters were submitted by health professionals (3,953 cases, 68.0%). Regarding ADEs outcomes, “other serious outcomes” were the most frequently reported, with 1,362 cases (23.4%), followed by “hospitalization-initial or prolonged” in 927cases (15.9%). The most common indication for BIC/FTC/TAF was HIV infection (3,006 cases, 39.5%). Apart from “product used for unknown indication” and missing reports, “prophylaxis against HIV infection,” “Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome,” and “HIV test positive” are also indications related to HIV treatment. Other reported indications, which may potentially fall outside the approved labeling, include hypertension (0.3%), hepatitis B (0.3%), and anxiety (0.2%). The presence of non-HIV indications in the FAERS database does not necessarily imply off-label prescribing, as indications recorded in spontaneous reporting systems may reflect comorbid conditions, incomplete coding, or reporter interpretation rather than the primary therapeutic indication of the drug. The number of reports of ADEs associated with BIC/FTC/TAF peaked in 2019 (n = 911), representing 15.7% of cases. The number of reports remained stable from 2020 to 2025. Demographic analysis of ADEs associated with BIC/FTC/TAF by sex stratification

Sex-stratified analyses were performed among reports with known sex information. This study further explored sex-stratified reporting. Among the 3,982 male and 1,327 female patients who submitted ADE reports, similarities were observed in age, weight, and the composition of the reported patient origins. For reporting countries, the top three for male were the United States (2,902 cases, 72.9%), France (151 cases, 3.8%), and Japan (111 cases, 2.8%), while for female, the top three were the United States (948 cases, 71.4%), France (102 cases, 7.7%), and Spain (23 cases, 1.7%). The primary reporters for both sexes were health professionals. Regarding BIC/FTC/TAF drug outcomes, excluding unspecified reports, the most common adverse event outcome for both sexes was classified as “other serious outcomes”. To better characterize the adverse events contributing most prominently to the detected signals, analyses at the HLGT and HLT levels were further integrated to provide a more detailed interpretation within the positive SOC categories. At the HLGT level, the strongest signals were observed for microbiology and serology investigations (ROR 24.63, IC025 4.35), gastrointestinal therapeutic procedures (ROR 9.84, IC025 2.91), and soft tissue neoplasms malignant and unspecified (ROR 14.80, IC025 2.81), while the most frequently reported HLGTs were medication errors and other product use errors and issues (n = 1,120), general system disorders nec (n = 936), and gastrointestinal signs and symptoms (n = 851). At the HLT level, the strongest signals were identified for virus identification and serology (ROR 42.23, IC025 5.07), retroviral infections (ROR 50.81, IC025 4.29), and gastrointestinal therapeutic procedures nec (ROR 21.04, IC025 0.68), whereas the most frequently reported HLTs were physical examination procedures and organ system status (n = 603), therapeutic and nontherapeutic responses (n = 429), and virus identification and serology (n = 404) (Supplementary Table 3). After excluding drug-unrelated PTs, FDA-approved indications, and disease progression-associated ADEs, our study identified 118 positive PTs (Supplementary Table 5). Next, we classified PTs with more than 20 cases. The PT signals were presented using forest plots and categorized according to whether they were listed in the FDA drug label (Fig. 2). Notably, the following PTs had a large of reported cases, and were consistent with the drug’s label, including weight increased (n = 526), headache (n = 239), insomnia (n = 163), vomiting (n = 159), and immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) (n = 113) (Fig. 3A). Additionally, we identified several signals that were not listed in the drug label. TTO analysis of BIC/FTC/TAF-related ADEs

We collected 813 (14.0%) valid TTO reports. As shown in Fig. 4A, most ADEs occurred within the first month after drug administration (n = 274, 33.7%). Notably, still 25.3% of ADEs occurred after 1 year of BIC/FTC/TAF treatment. The median TTO of ADEs was 80 days (IQR19-365). The results of Weibull distribution test suggested that the β was 0.61, and the upper limit of the 95% CI was 0.64, indicating that the incidence of ADEs decreases over time (Fig. 4B). We further analyzed the TTO at the SOC level (Fig. 4C). The results of the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated significant differences in the time to onset of adverse events across different system organ classes (Kruskal–Wallis test P = 8.0e-23). The median TTO for the longest-lasting SOCs, “neoplasms benign, malignant and unspecified (incl cysts and polyps)” was 344 days (IQR 106.5–344). In contrast, the median TTO for psychiatric disorders was the shortest, at 15.5 days (IQR 5-57.5).Additionally, we analyzed the temporal pattern of ADEs among reports with serious outcomes. The distribution across time intervals was comparable to that observed in the overall analysis, with most events occurring within 1 month after drug treatment (n 173, 33.53%), followed by those occurring more than 1 year later (n 125, 24.2%) (Fig. 4D).

External validation using the EudraVigilance database

Between 2018 and 2025, a total of 4558 ADE reports associated with BIC/FTC/TAF were identified in the EudraVigilance database, comprising 10,268 individual adverse events (Fig. 1). Most reports involved male patients (62.88%), and the 18–64-year age group accounted for the largest proportion of cases (53.47%). Similar to findings from FAERS, the majority of reports were submitted by healthcare professionals (88.15%), and 61.96% of patients experienced serious outcomes, with initial or prolonged hospitalization being the most frequently reported (18.34%). Geographically, reports from EEA (58.34%) slightly exceeded those from non-EEA (41.66%) (Fig. 6A). The annual number of reports peaked in 2022 (n = 796) and 2024 (n = 794), exceeding those of other years (Fig. 6B). At the SOC level, 10 SOCs met the predefined signal detection thresholds, including investigations (n = 1605, ROR 2.52 [2.39–2.66], IC025 1.12), psychiatric disorders (n = 1095, ROR 2.14 [2.01–2.28], IC025 0.92), infections and infestations (n = 681, ROR 1.29 [1.19–1.39], IC025 0.23), renal and urinary disorders (n = 383, ROR 1.45 [1.31–1.60], IC025 0.37), metabolism and nutrition disorders (n = 372, ROR 1.54 [1.39–1.71], IC025 0.45), hepatobiliary disorders (n = 225, ROR 1.57 [1.37–1.79], IC025 0.44), immune system disorders (n = 198, ROR 1.28 [1.11–1.48], IC025 0.14), surgical and medical procedures (n = 158, ROR 1.81 [1.55–2.12], IC025 0.61), pregnancy, puerperium and perinatal conditions (n = 75, ROR 1.43 [1.14–1.79], IC025 0.17), and congenital, familial and genetic disorders (n = 43, ROR 1.39 [1.03–1.87], IC025 0.02) (Fig. 6C). Further analysis of ROR-positive adverse events identified 140 positive PTs (Supplementary Table 8). Among events with ≥ 15 reports, 34 signals were not listed in the EMA drug label, including depression (n = 104, ROR 3.02, IC025 1.28), dysphagia (n = 71, ROR 4.71, IC025 1.81), renal failure (n = 60, ROR 1.76, IC025 0.42), nightmare (n = 56, ROR 10.07, IC025 2.76), gastrointestinal disorders (n = 51, ROR 2.79, IC025 1.03), and diabetes mellitus (n = 40, ROR 3.38, IC025 1.22) (Fig. 6D). In contrast, 22 detected signals were consistent with the EMA drug label, including weight increased (n = 556, ROR 20.99, IC025 4.14), IRIS (n = 133, ROR 85.45, IC025 5.43), insomnia (n = 125, ROR 3.44, IC025 1.48), anxiety (n = 85, ROR 2.00, IC025 0.67), and suicidal ideation (n = 60, ROR 4.12, IC025 1.59) (Fig. 6E). Comparison between FAERS and EudraVigilance revealed 75 overlapping PT signals, including weight increased, IRIS, insomnia, dysphagia, and flatulence. Notably, several signals—such as pancreatitis, acute pancreatitis, diabetes mellitus, and paranoia—were detected in both databases but are not currently described in the FDA or EMA drug labels. Additionally, database-specific signals were identified: FAERS-specific signals included headache, rash, vomiting, alopecia, myalgia, and hyperglycaemia, whereas EudraVigilance-specific signals included depression, anxiety, vertigo, osteoporosis, erectile dysfunction, and spontaneous abortion (Supplementary Table 9).

Logistic regression analysis of risk factors for BIC/FTC/TAF-associated pancreatitis

Signal detection in both the FAERS and EudraVigilance databases identified a statistically significant association between pancreatitis and the use of BIC/FTC/TAF. To further evaluate potential confounding factors associated with BIC/FTC/TAF-related pancreatitis, logistic regression analyses were conducted. In the FAERS database, 3,112 complete reports were included in the analysis. Univariate analysis showed that age, sex, and reporter type were not significantly associated with the occurrence of pancreatitis. Compared with reports originating from the United States, reports from France (OR 3.571, 95% CI 1.644–7.757, P = 0.001) and other countries (OR 2.208, 95% CI 1.197–4.073, P = 0.011) were associated with an increased risk of pancreatitis. In addition, compared with monotherapy, reports involving concomitant medications were associated with a higher risk of pancreatitis (OR 2.057, 95% CI 1.192–3.550, P = 0.01). In the multivariable logistic regression model, reports from France and those involving combination therapy remained significant risk factors for BIC/FTC/TAF-related pancreatitis (Table 6).

BIC/FTC/TAF-related DIP network construction

In this study, a total of 399 genes related to BIC/FTC/TAF were identified, consisting of 131 drug-related target genes from the SuperPred database and 290 from the PharmMapper database. Furthermore, 2244 genes associated with DIP were identified by combining 2149 target genes from GeneCards and 192 from OMIM. The overlap between these two gene sets revealed 208 potential target genes that may contribute to BIC/FTC/TAF-associated DIP (Fig. 7A). A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was built using the STRING database with a highest-confidence interaction score cutoff of 0.9. This network contained 207 nodes and 433 edges, demonstrating strong enrichment of protein interactions (P-value < 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁶). Twenty core targets within the network included SRC, CYP3A4, PRKACA, NFKB1, CDC25C, MAPK8, AKT1, ESR1, CCNA2, MMP9, RXRA, MAOB, CCL5, CYP19A1, CASP3, STAT3, TOP2A, GSTP1, TYMS, and CXCR4 (Fig. 7B). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the 208 intersecting genes revealed 2,371 significantly enriched terms (adjusted P-value < 0.05). Enriched biological processes primarily involved ligand-activated transcription factor activity, nuclear receptor activity, protein serine/threonine/tyrosine kinase activity, carboxylic acid binding, and RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding transcription factor binding. Cellular components were notably enriched for vesicle lumen, ficolin-1-rich granule lumen, membrane microdomain, and focal adhesion, while molecular functions were enriched for response to peptide, xenobiotic stimulus, lipopolysaccharide, and nutrient levels, positive regulation of kinase activity, and wound healing (Fig. 7C). KEGG pathway analysis identified 164 significantly enriched pathways, with the top five being PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, lipid and atherosclerosis, Ras signaling pathway, Rap1 signaling pathway, and proteoglycans in cancer (Fig. 7D). These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying BIC/FTC/TAF-associated pancreatitis and highlight potential therapeutic targets to mitigate this effect.

Molecular docking analysis

Twenty core targets identified through network pharmacology analysis were selected for molecular docking with BIC/FTC/TAF using the CB-Dock2 platform. The docking results are presented in Fig. 8A. The binding energies between BIC/FTC/TAF and all core targets were lower than − 5 kcal/mol, indicating favorable binding affinity between the drug and these targets. Among the top 10 core targets ranked by betweenness centrality, five targets showed particularly strong binding interactions with BIC/FTC/TAF (binding energy < − 7.5 kcal/mol), including CYP3A4 (− 8.7 kcal/mol), PRKACA (− 8.5 kcal/mol), MAPK8 (− 8.4 kcal/mol), CCNA2 (− 7.9 kcal/mol), and MMP9 (− 9.0 kcal/mol). Visualization of the docking conformations using Discovery Studio (Fig. 8B) revealed that BIC/FTC/TAF formed multiple hydrogen bonds with ARG105, ASN441, and ARG440 residues of CYP3A4, while π–alkyl interactions were observed with ALA484 and VAL313 and a π–sigma interaction with CYS442 (Fig. 8D). For PRKACA, hydrogen bonds were formed with ASN20 and ARG19, along with a π–cation interaction with GLU127 (Fig. 8E). In MAPK8, BIC/FTC/TAF formed hydrogen bonds with GLN37, GLU73, and ASN114 and a π–sulfur interaction with MET108 (Fig. 8H). In CCNA2, hydrogen bonds were observed with ALA183 and THR182, accompanied by a π–sulfur interaction with CYS117 and a π–sigma interaction with PRO271 (Fig. 8K). For MMP9, hydrogen bonds were formed with TYR423 and ALA191, together with a π–cation interaction with HIS401 (Fig. 8L). In addition, interactions between BIC/FTC/TAF and other core targets, including SRC (Fig. 8C), NFKB1 (Fig. 8F), CDC25C (Fig. 8G), AKT1 (Fig. 8I), and ESR1 (Fig. 8J), mainly involved hydrogen bonds, π–alkyl interactions, and van der Waals forces between the ligand and amino acid residues. The docking results for the remaining core targets ranked in the lower half of betweenness centrality are shown in Figure S2. Collectively, these findings indicate that BIC/FTC/TAF exhibits strong binding interactions with the core targets predicted by network pharmacology, further supporting the potential mechanistic involvement of these targets in BIC/FTC/TAF-associated pancreatitis.

Validation of core target expression based on RNA-seq data

To validate the expression patterns of the identified core targets, RNA-seq expression profiles from the GSE194331 dataset were analyzed, comparing blood samples from patients with pancreatitis and healthy controls. The differential expression of core targets between the pancreatitis and control groups is illustrated in Fig. 9A. As shown in Fig. 9B and C, several genes were significantly upregulated in the pancreatitis group compared with healthy controls, including NFKB1 (P < 0.05), PRKACA (P < 0.001), MMP9 (P < 0.001), RXRA (P < 0.001), CASP3 (P < 0.01), STAT3 (P < 0.01), and CXCR4 (P < 0.05), whereas SRC (P < 0.05), ESR1 (P < 0.01), and CCL5 (P < 0.001) were significantly downregulated. The area under the curve (AUC) values for distinguishing pancreatitis from healthy controls are presented in Fig. 9D and E. Notably, PRKACA (AUC = 0.721), MMP9 (AUC = 0.825), RXRA (AUC = 0.714), and CCL5 (AUC = 0.769) demonstrated strong discriminative performance between the two groups, suggesting that these core targets may serve as reliable diagnostic biomarkers for pancreatitis.

Discussion

Baseline information description

Our study found that ADEs were more frequent reported in males (68.5%) than in females (22.8%). It is important to note that the predominance of male reports in FAERS should be interpreted with caution. Since the spontaneous reporting system does not provide sex-stratified data on drug exposure denominators, the male-to-female ratio in this dataset may reflect the following factors: (i) the sex distribution of individuals receiving BIC/FTC/TAF treatment in the primary reporting regions. The majority of ADE reports related to BIC/FTC/TAF originate from the United States, where there is a significantly higher proportion of male HIV-infected patients compared to females; (ii) variations in prescribing patterns and indication combinations (including differences in key population characteristics and healthcare accessibility); and (iii) reporting-related biases. Furthermore, unmeasured clinical factors-such as disease stage/severity, comorbidities, concomitant medications, and incomplete sex documentation-may confound sex-stratified comparisons. Therefore, the higher proportion of male reports should be regarded as a reporting characteristic rather than evidence of higher risk among males. Additionally, patients aged 18–65 years were the most commonly reported group, aligning with the age group that has a high prevalence of HIV infection. The absence of weight information in 88.6% of the reports limits a comprehensive assessment of patient characteristics. The most common serious ADEs were “other serious outcomes” and “hospitalization-initial or prolonged”, indicating that the drug may lead to significant clinical consequences in some cases. The annual trend of ADEs shows a peak in reports in 2019, followed by a slight increase, suggesting that the use of BIC/FTC/TAF may be associated with a gradual rise in drug safety concerns.

Overall safety profile of BIC/FTC/TAF in real-world pharmacovigilance

In this study, BIC/FTC/TAF generated disproportionality signals across a broad range of SOCs, indicating that its post-marketing safety profile is more heterogeneous than that captured in pre-approval trials. Importantly, the most prominent SOC signals were not limited to classic organ toxicities, but were concentrated in investigations, injury, poisoning and procedural complications, and surgical and medical procedures, while highly reported HLTs also included laboratory- and monitoring-related terms. This pattern suggests that part of the signal burden may reflect intensive HIV monitoring, treatment-response assessment, and medication-use issues in routine care rather than direct drug-induced injury alone. Nevertheless, several high-frequency labeled PTs identified in our analysis, including weight increased, headache, insomnia, vomiting, and IRIS, were broadly consistent with the established safety profile of BIC/FTC/TAF [6]. Long-term randomized follow-up has shown that BIC/FTC/TAF maintains durable virologic suppression through 5 years, with rare discontinuation due to adverse events and overall favorable tolerability, supporting the external validity of our pharmacovigilance findings.

Signals consistent with the drug label

Among labeled events, weight increased (n = 526, ROR 10.32, IC025 3.17) was the most commonly reported ADE and deserves particular attention. The results of a five-year follow-up from two randomized trials showed that the median weight change from baseline to week 240 was + 6.1 kg [6]. Patients who switched from a DTG-based treatment and received 96 weeks of BIC/FTC/TAF treatment also experienced varying degrees of weight increase [26]. In a comparison of DTG + lamivudine and BIC/FTC/TAF for the treatment of newly infected HIV patients not receiving ART, BIC/FTC/TAF was associated with an increased risk of weight increase [27]. Importantly, weight increase can also increase the risk of hypertension or dyslipidemia. The use of INSTIs has been associated with hypertension, while TAF has been linked to dyslipidemia, both of which may elevate the risk of cardiovascular disease [28]. Recent studies have found that BIC can cause mitochondrial dysfunction in vitro, altering carbohydrate metabolism and glucose consumption in liver cells, and it also affects glucose tolerance and gluconeogenesis regulation in mice, which may contribute to weight increase [29]. Additionally, some INSTIs can directly affect adipose tissue by inhibiting lipolysis, leading to lipofibrosis and hypertrophy, which may contribute to metabolic disorders [30]. Therefore, when treating HIV patients with metabolic or cardiovascular diseases, it is important to consider the potential impact of body weight.

Based on PTs with more than 100 case reports, IRIS (n = 113, ROR 76.26, IC025 5.23) which is consistent with the drug label, demonstrated high signal values. IRIS refers to a group of clinical syndromes that occur in HIV-positive patients during immune reconstitution following the initiation of ART. One case report described a liver flare in a patient with newly diagnosed HIV and HBV co-infection, occurring during immune reconstitution syndrome 6 weeks after starting treatment with BIC/FTC/TAF [31]. Another HIV patient with hepatitis B and multidrug-resistant tuberculosis developed disseminated cryptococcosis while taking BIC/FTC/TAF and anti-tuberculosis drugs. His condition worsened during treatment with liposomal amphotericin B and fluconazole, and he was eventually diagnosed with IRIS [32]. However, in a double-blind, multicenter, randomized controlled phase 3 non-inferiority trial assessing the initial treatment of HIV-1 and hepatitis B co-infection, 1 of 121 participants in the BIC/FTC/TAF group reported a serious adverse event (cryptococcal meningitis due to IRIS [33]. Importantly, underdiagnosis of IRIS can lead to high early mortality [34]. Although BIC/FTC/TAF helps control HIV by boosting the immune system, preventing and managing IRIS requires early monitoring and timely treatment of potential infections.

Fanconi syndrome acquired (Acquired FS, n = 6, ROR 13.86, IC025 1.19) is a rare event. A patient with normal renal function one month before switching to TAF developed FS after the switch [35]. However, 28 patients who developed proximal renal tubular disease (PRT, FS) while taking tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) were followed up for 5 years after switching to TAF, which showed no recurrence of PRT and no significant changes in related markers [36]. Animal studies have shown that, compared to TDF, TAF treatment does not cause FS [37]. Although there is ongoing debate about whether BIC/FTC/TAF can cause acquired FS, drug interactions in clinical practice remain a potential cause of adverse reactions. Future evidence is needed to establish the potential causal relationship. Regular monitoring of kidney function, including blood creatinine, urinary protein, and blood phosphorus levels, during BIC/FTC/TAF treatment can facilitate early detection of FS.

It should be explicitly noted that all SOC-level findings in this study are exploratory results presented descriptively and are not labeled as statistically confirmed signals. Among HIV-infected populations, the incidence of AIDS-related cancers (such as AIDS-related sarcomas and non-Hodgkin lymphoma) and various non-AIDS-defining cancers is well recognized. Cancer risk is significantly influenced by immune status, co-infections with oncogenic viruses, and healthcare accessibility/screening practices [38]. Notably, in our TTO analysis, “neoplasms benign, malignant and unspecified (incl cysts and polyps)” exhibited the longest median TTO, consistent with the relatively long latent periods observed in many tumor processes. This suggests that the reported events may reflect underlying disease progression rather than acute drug effects. Therefore, the “tumor” reports observed in FAERS should be regarded as hypothesis-generating findings requiring validation through pharmacoepidemiological studies.

Interpretation of organ-specific and unexpected adverse event signals

The positive signals involving psychiatric disorders, particularly insomnia, are noteworthy. Although BIC/FTC/TAF appears to have a comparatively favorable neuropsychiatric profile within the INSTI class, sleep-related symptoms remain clinically relevant in both trials and real-world practice. The DOBINeuro trial reported improvement in sleep disorders after switching from DTG/ABC/3TC to BIC/FTC/TAF, suggesting that bictegravir may be better tolerated than some prior INSTI-based regimens [39]; however, persistent insomnia and broader mental complaints continue to be captured by post-marketing surveillance and may meaningfully affect adherence and quality of life. Our results therefore suggest that neuropsychiatric symptoms remain an important aspect of routine safety monitoring even when they do not frequently lead to treatment discontinuation.

Signals for renal and urinary disorders and hepatobiliary disorders also merit consideration. Overall, long-term trial data support the renal safety of BIC/FTC/TAF, and real-world cohort data have suggested generally favorable metabolic and hepatic tolerability [40]. However, rare but clinically important hepatic injury has been reported, including a published case of fulminant hepatic failure temporally associated with bictegravir exposure [41]. Accordingly, our findings do not contradict the generally favorable hepatic and renal safety profile of BIC/FTC/TAF, but they do indicate that closer laboratory surveillance remains warranted in patients with baseline kidney dysfunction, HBV coinfection, metabolic abnormalities, polypharmacy, or other hepatic or nephrotoxic risk factors.

A major contribution of this study is the identification of several unexpected unlabeled PTs. Pancreatitis (n = 51, ROR 4.37,IC025 1.63) and pancreatitis acute (n = 21, ROR 4.16,IC025 1.24) are important unexpected ADEs due to their potential for serious adverse consequences [42]. A 32-year-old male patient with AIDS presented with severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, nausea, vomiting, and heterogeneous pancreatic parenchymal structures on abdominal ultrasonography 45 days after starting BIC/FTC/TAF, and was diagnosed with mild drug-related acute pancreatitis, possibly related to the drug [43]. Another case report described a patient with diabetes and HIV who developed severe rhabdomyolysis and acute pancreatitis after starting BIC/FTC/TAF treatment [44]. Two randomized trials with a five-year follow-up also identified 1 case of pancreatitis attributed to BIC/FTC/TAF, occurring on day 572 [6]. Certain antiretroviral drugs used to treat HIV/AIDS, such as nevirapine, abacavir, and tenofovir [45], have been linked to increased pancreatitis risk and may trigger toxic reactions in the pancreas. As mentioned earlier, TAF treatment was found to cause abnormalities in blood sugar and lipids in animal experiments [37]. A recent study found that emtricitabine has no direct damaging effect on β-cell function and survival [46]. Our study also identified blood triglycerides increased (n = 24, ROR 6.49, IC025 1.83), viraemia (n = 46, ROR 139.84, IC025 4.71, EBGM05 119.51), and various types of infection signals as risk factors for the development of pancreatitis [47, 48]. These findings are concordant with our results and suggest that at least some pancreatic events may emerge in the setting of regimen-associated metabolic disturbance rather than purely idiosyncratic toxicity. Therefore, BIC/FTC/TAF should be used with caution in patients with a history of pancreatic disease. Dynamic monitoring of patients’ abdominal symptoms, abdominal imaging, and laboratory indicators can aid in early detection of pancreatitis and should include consideration of a potential drug-related cause.

When interpreting the pancreatitis signal in FAERS, several alternative explanations should be considered. First, tenofovir formulations exhibit distinct metabolic profiles: multiple randomized controlled trials and observational studies indicate that TAF is associated with a higher incidence of elevated lipid levels (including total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides) compared to TDF. Given that hypertriglyceridemia is a recognized trigger for acute pancreatitis [49], a potential indirect mechanism may be that TAF-related lipid elevations-combined with other risk factors (e.g., protease inhibitors, diabetes, alcohol consumption)-could increase susceptibility to pancreatitis in some patients. However, the absence of laboratory data (e.g., triglyceride levels) and exposure denominator data in FAERS precludes direct validation of this hypothesis. Secondly, pancreatitis historically associated with TDF and early antiretroviral regimens has been hypothesized to involve mitochondrial toxicity, frequently occurring during the initial phase of treatment. However, tenofovir-associated mitochondrial damage has been most consistently demonstrated in renal proximal tubules rather than the pancreas [50]. Given the lower systemic exposure of TAF [51], extrapolation of mechanisms from TDF to TAF should be undertaken with caution. In our TTO analysis, pancreatitis did not exhibit a distinct early-onset pattern, potentially challenging the notion of a purely early mitochondrial toxicity phenotype; however, underreporting of TTO data in FAERS warrants caution in interpretation. Thirdly, the occurrence of pancreatitis in HIV-infected individuals may be associated with underlying disease contexts, including opportunistic infections (such as cytomegalovirus) and tuberculosis-related immune reconstitution phenomena. These factors are difficult to reliably exclude in spontaneous case reports [52, 53]. Consequently, the findings of this study should be regarded as hypothesis-generating evidence. In clinical practice, prior to attributing pancreatitis to BIC/FTC/TAF therapy, other potential aetiologies (co-infections/immunorestoration inflammatory syndrome, cholelithiasis, alcohol consumption, and hypertriglyceridaemia) must be evaluated. This study did not model mechanisms for other unexpected signals (such as dysphagia or psychiatric disorders) due to the clinical heterogeneity of these phenotypes and their greater reliance on spontaneous reporting. In contrast, pancreatitis provides a more organ-specific endpoint with traceable mechanisms, facilitating hypothesis generation.

Sex differences in drug safety

The sex-stratified analysis showed more positive signals in males than in females, suggesting a degree of sex-related heterogeneity in the post-marketing safety profile of BIC/FTC/TAF. However, this difference should be interpreted cautiously, because it may reflect not only biological variation but also differences in treatment patterns, comorbidity structure, and reporting behavior. In females, the relatively strong signals for maternal exposure during pregnancy and weight increased are clinically plausible. Current pregnancy data indicate that bictegravir can maintain high rates of virologic suppression during pregnancy, and U.S. perinatal guidance states that first-trimester exposure has not been associated with an increased risk of congenital anomalies. Therefore, the prominence of pregnancy-related signals in females likely reflects true exposure and enhanced surveillance in this population rather than clear evidence of novel reproductive toxicity [54]. At the same time, female may be more vulnerable to INSTI-associated weight gain, which provides a biologically coherent context for the stronger female signals related to weight and, potentially, dysglycemia [55]. Female-specific signals such as diabetes mellitus, abortion spontaneous, and disseminated tuberculosis also require careful interpretation. The diabetes signal is supported by case-based literature on bictegravir-associated hyperglycemia, but spontaneous abortion and disseminated tuberculosis are much more likely to be influenced by pregnancy-related reporting intensity, baseline infection burden, or immune reconstitution phenomena than to directly indicate drug-specific toxicity.

In males, the appearance of signals such as blood triglycerides increased, nightmare, and muscle atrophy may suggest a profile more weighted toward metabolic and neuropsychiatric complaints, although direct evidence specific to bictegravir remains limited and these findings should be validated in cohort-based studies. Muscle atrophy (n = 16, ROR 5.57, IC025 1.43) is a male-specific PT. Although studies have shown that TAF use can lead to higher skeletal muscle mass and a higher skeletal muscle mass index [56], we must consider that certain groups, such as transgender women, are at higher risk for HIV infection [57]. There is a low likelihood of clinically relevant drug interactions between feminizing hormone therapy and ART [58]. Feminizing hormone therapy can lead to androgen imbalances, which may contribute to muscle wasting. Further research with a larger sample size is needed. Overall, our results support considering sex as an important factor in individualized safety monitoring, particularly with respect to pregnancy-related exposure, weight change, and glucose metabolism in female, and metabolic or neuropsychiatric symptoms in male.

Temporal characteristics of BIC/FTC/TAF-related adverse events

The Weibull distribution test showed that the incidence of ADEs gradually decreased with the length of treatment, which aligns with common drug tolerance mechanisms. Furthermore, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the TTO of ADEs after BIC/FTC/TAF use for each organ system. The median duration of ADEs related to psychiatric disorders was only 6 days, (IQR 3-27.25 days). This suggests that psychiatric ADEs are typically short-term, possibly due to the short-term effects of the drug on the neurotransmitter system. BIC/FTC/TAF may induce brief psychiatric symptoms through certain regulatory effects on the central nervous system. One study found that FTC inhibits GABA currents [59], while another study noted that FTC can reduce mitochondrial spare respiratory capacity in striatal nerve terminals [60]. Further stratification of the SOC data revealed significant heterogeneity in the timing of events across different systems. Psychiatric disorders showed the shortest median onset, which is consistent with the clinical observation that insomnia and related neuropsychiatric symptoms are often perceived and reported early after treatment initiation [39]. In contrast, late-onset categories such as neoplasm-related signals are more likely to be influenced by background disease burden, accumulated comorbidity, and follow-up duration than by straightforward direct causation. Likewise, the observation that death exhibited a wear-out pattern whereas weight gain, alopecia, pancreatitis, and renal failure were more consistent with random-failure pattern indicates that not all adverse events follow the same time-dependent trajectory [41, 61]. The later onset observed in older patients, fatal cases, and IME-related reports is also clinically plausible, as these scenarios are often associated with greater comorbidity, reduced organ reserve, and more complex exposure backgrounds. Taken together, our results suggest that BIC/FTC/TAF-related safety monitoring should incorporate both early vigilance for symptomatic complaints and long-term follow-up for metabolic, hepatic, renal, and serious medical event. Most importantly, considering the low reporting rate (14.0%) of TTO reports, special caution is required in interpreting our findings, but we still offer valuable references and insights for clinical monitoring of ADE timing.

External validation across pharmacovigilance databases

The EudraVigilance analysis substantially strengthens the robustness of our main findings. Despite differences in geography, regulatory context, labeling systems, and pharmacovigilance practices, both FAERS and EudraVigilance consistently detected signals in investigations, psychiatric disorders, renal and urinary disorders, and hepatobiliary disorders, as well as overlapping PTs such as weight increased, IRIS, insomnia, and dysphagia. Because EudraVigilance is the EMA-operated system for managing and analyzing suspected adverse reactions in the EEA, this cross-database overlap provides indirect support for the reproducibility of the observed signals. In particular, the recurrence of weight increased and IRIS across two large pharmacovigilance systems is highly consistent with long-term clinical and regulatory evidence [6].

Of particular interest, several signals not fully described in current FDA or EMA labeling, including diabetes mellitus, pancreatitis/acute pancreatitis, and dysphagia, were detected in both databases [61, 62]. Repetition of these events across independent pharmacovigilance systems suggests that they are unlikely to represent mere database-specific noise and instead should be regarded as candidate post-marketing risks requiring further epidemiologic evaluation. At the same time, database-specific signals were also observed, with FAERS more likely to highlight headache, vomiting, alopecia, and hyperglycemia, whereas EudraVigilance more readily captured depression, anxiety, nightmare, osteoporosis, and spontaneous abortion. Such discrepancies probably reflect regional differences in patient characteristics, reporter composition, event coding, and label content rather than fundamentally different toxicity profiles. Pregnancy-related signals in EudraVigilance should be interpreted especially carefully, because current pregnancy evidence and NIH perinatal guidance do not indicate increased congenital anomaly risk with bictegravir exposure.

Pancreatitis as a potentially important emerging safety signal

Among the unexpected findings, pancreatitis deserves special emphasis. It was detected in both FAERS and EudraVigilance, and its association with BIC/FTC/TAF remained of interest after further regression analyses. In FAERS, reports from France and the presence of concomitant medications were associated with higher odds of pancreatitis, whereas in EudraVigilance, reports originating from the EEA appeared protective relative to non-EEA reports. These findings suggest that the pancreatitis signal may be shaped not only by drug exposure itself but also by regional reporting differences and, importantly, by the context of polypharmacy. This interpretation is clinically plausible because drug-induced pancreatitis is often multifactorial, with contributions from co-medications, hypertriglyceridemia, gallstone disease, alcohol use, and other host-level risk factors. The recently published BIC/FTC/TAF -associated pancreatitis case, in which several metabolic and biliary risk factors coexisted, is highly consistent with this view [42]. It is worth noting that recent case reports have described acute pancreatitis following exposure to BIC/FTC/TAF, with patients also presenting with metabolic/ biliary tract risk factors; this is highly consistent with the findings of this study that ‘polypharmacy or complex medical histories increase the risk of pancreatitis’, and also suggests that BIC/FTC/TAF is more likely to act as a triggering or amplifying factor in high-risk hosts, rather than being the sole determining factor [62]. Secondly, our network pharmacology analysis revealed that the intersection of targets was enriched in pathways such as PI3K-Akt, Ras, Rap1, lipid and atherosclerosis; these pathways are closely associated with inflammatory amplification, immune cell activation, apoptosis/necrosis and lipid metabolism disorders in acute pancreatitis; Recent mechanistic studies have also consistently emphasized the key roles of the PI3K-Akt, NF-κB and STAT3 axes in the inflammatory cascade of pancreatitis, providing external support for the core nodes identified in this study, such as NFKB1, STAT3 and AKT1 [63]. Furthermore, molecular docking results suggest that BIC/FTC/TAF exhibit strong binding affinity with targets such as CYP3A4, PRKACA, MAPK8 and MMP9, whilst RNA-seq validation further revealed that NFKB1, PRKACA, MMP9, RXRA, CASP3, STAT3 and CXCR4 are upregulated in pancreatitis samples, with molecules such as MMP9 also demonstrating good discriminatory ability; previous studies have also suggested that MMP9 is involved in the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps, tissue destruction and inflammation amplification in acute pancreatitis, supporting its plausibility as a potential key mediator [64]. However, these findings should be interpreted as supportive mechanistic clues rather than proof of causality, because network pharmacology and docking are prediction-based approaches and the transcriptomic validation dataset reflects pancreatitis in general rather than BIC/FTC/TAF-specific injury. Overall, the totality of evidence in our study supports pancreatitis as a low-frequency but potentially credible emerging safety signal, especially in patients with concomitant medications and underlying metabolic risk.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it provides a comprehensive post-marketing safety evaluation of BIC/FTC/TAF using two large spontaneous reporting systems, FAERS and EudraVigilance, thereby improving the robustness and cross-regional reproducibility of the detected signals. Second, beyond conventional disproportionality analysis, we incorporated sex-stratified analyses, time-to-onset assessment, and external validation, which allowed a more refined characterization of heterogeneity in adverse-event reporting patterns. Third, for pancreatitis, we further combined multivariable regression, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and transcriptomic validation, which adds biological plausibility to an emerging safety signal that was consistently observed across both databases. These complementary approaches are particularly valuable because BIC/FTC/TAF is generally considered a durable and well-tolerated regimen in long-term clinical studies, and therefore systematic post-marketing evaluation is essential for identifying uncommon or delayed adverse events that may be underrepresented in trials.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, both FAERS and EudraVigilance are spontaneous reporting systems, and therefore are inherently subject to under-reporting, duplicate reporting, selective reporting, missing data, and variable report quality [65]. Second, these databases lack reliable denominators and detailed clinical information, such as duration and dose of exposure, baseline metabolic status, alcohol use, gallstone disease, laboratory trends, and comprehensive comedication histories, which limits adjustment for confounding and precludes estimation of true incidence. In addition, although sex-stratified signal detection was performed to explore potential differences in reporting patterns, these analyses should be interpreted cautiously because spontaneous reporting databases may contain missing or incomplete demographic information and lack reliable exposure denominators. Shu et al. [66]. Third, although the pancreatitis-related network pharmacology, molecular docking, and RNA-seq analyses support biological plausibility, they remain exploratory and cannot establish a direct mechanistic link between BIC/FTC/TAF exposure and pancreatic injury. Finally, while external validation in EudraVigilance strengthens signal credibility, differences in coding practice, label content, and pharmacovigilance systems between the United States and Europe may still influence the degree of cross-database concordance [67]. Fourth, some detected signals may reflect indication-related events, disease progression, surveillance intensity, or regional reporting practices, rather than direct drug toxicity [12]. Lastly, disproportionality analysis does not quantify risk or establish causality; rather, it only provides an estimate of the signal strength for disproportionate reporting, based solely on statistical significance [68].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study expands the real-world safety profile of BIC/FTC/TAF by identifying both recognized and potentially emerging adverse-event signals across multiple organ systems. In addition to confirming known events such as weight increased, insomnia, and IRIS, our analyses highlight several signals that warrant closer clinical attention, particularly metabolic abnormalities, psychiatric symptoms, renal and hepatobiliary events, and pancreatitis. The observed heterogeneity by sex and time to onset further suggests that BIC/FTC/TAF-associated adverse events are not uniformly distributed across patient subgroups or treatment duration. Importantly, the replication of multiple signals in EudraVigilance supports the robustness of the main findings, while the integrated analyses for pancreatitis provide preliminary mechanistic support for this potential risk. Overall, although BIC/FTC/TAF remains a highly effective and generally well-tolerated regimen, clinicians should maintain continued vigilance during both early and long-term treatment, especially in patients with polypharmacy or underlying metabolic vulnerability. Future prospective pharmacoepidemiologic and mechanistic studies are needed to confirm the causal relevance of the newly identified signals and to refine risk stratification in clinical practice.
