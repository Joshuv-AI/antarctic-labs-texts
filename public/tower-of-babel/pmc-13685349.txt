Relationship between hyperuricemia and cardiovascular risk among postmenopausal women in cameroon: a community-based study
Authors: Stéphane Fargeon Massin Mbouna, Jan René Nkeck, Néri Ngole Atabe, Stéphane Yves Mbana Bikoi, Raphael Kinyuy Ndze, Emeraude Sorelle Mbock, Jean Maximilien Tang, Danielle Cécile Tang-likeng, Claude Cyrille Ndoua Noa, Vicky Jocelyne Ama-moor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite the known association between hyperuricemia and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, the prevalence and epidemiology of hyperuricemia in postmenopausal women in Cameroon are not yet fully understood. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of hyperuricemia, identify its associated factors, and examine its association with cardiovascular risk markers among postmenopausal women living in Yaoundé.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Yaoundé from November 2024 to June 2025, involving 206 postmenopausal women. Biological measurements were performed at PRIMA Lab using the Cobas 6000®. Independent associated factors with hyperuricemia were analyzed using logistic regression. Association between hyperuricemia and cardiovascular risk markers was assessed, using chi2. The significance level was set at 5%. SPSS 28 was used for statistical analysis.ResultsOut of 206 postmenopausal women, the mean age was 63.5 ± 8.7 [48–87] years. The prevalence of hyperuricemia was 37.9%. Menopausal duration ≥ 14 years (OR = 2.8; P = 0.016), overweight/obesity (OR = 1.9; P = 0.030) and hypertension (OR = 2.0; P = 0.036). Hyperuricemia was significantly associated with non-HDL cholesterol ≥ 130 mg/dL (P = 0.001), LDL/HDL ratio (P = 0.001), WHO CVR score ≥ 10% (P = 0.018), and non-HDL/total cholesterol ratio (P = 0.045).ConclusionMore than one-third of postmenopausal women have been diagnosed with hyperuricemia. Long duration of menopause, overweight/obesity and hypertension were independently associated with hyperuricemia. Hyperuricemia was significantly associate with non-HDL cholesterol, LDL/HDL ratio, non-HDL/total cholesterol ratio and WHO CVR score.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06335-1.Keywords: Asymptomatic hyperuricemia, Cardiovascular risk, Post-menopause, Cameroon

Introduction

Hyperuricemia is defined as an elevated serum uric acid level, usually greater than 60 mg/L in women and 70 mg/L in men [1]. This condition is a prevalent metabolic disorder, with prevalence rates ranging from 2.6% to 36% on a global scale [2]. In the United States, this condtion concerns approximately 20% of general population [3]. A population-based study indicated a notably high prevalence of hyperuricemia in sub-Saharan Africa, showing that 34.8% of individuals in Nigeria and 20.6% in Senegal exhibit signs of this condition [4, 5].

It is well recognized that hyperuricemia is associated with numerous leading causes of mortality, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, various forms of cancer and chronic kidney disease [6]. Furthermore, in a large cohort, Bos et al. have demonstrated that individuals with serum uric acid levels exceeding 65 mg/L have a twofold increased risk of experiencing a myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke compared to those with levels below 44 mg/L [7]. Nevertheless, the relationship between asymptomatic hyperuricemia and cardiovascular disease is a subject of controversy in the literature. A body of research in the epidemiological field has demonstrated a correlation between hyperuricemia and cardiovascular risk [8, 9]. In addition, in vitro, excessive activity of xanthine oxidase, resulting from chronic inflammation, has been demonstrated to induce elevated uric acid levels, the major endogenous antioxidant molecule, and reduced nitric oxide availability. These phenomena are considered the primary catalysts of endothelial dysfunction [10]. Moreover, experimental studies have proposed that hyperuricemia could decrease the synthesis of nitric oxide directly via the inhibition of endothelial nitric oxide synthase [11]. However, a consensus has yet to be reached concerning the precise mechanism by which hyperuricemia contributes to the pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease (CVD). This relationship is a subject of debate, as evidenced by certain Mendelian randomization studies [12, 13].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), natural menopause is defined as 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea that is not attributable to other pathological or physiological causes [14]. In postmenopausal women, a decrease in estrogen levels leads to a reduction in its uricosuric effect, increasing the level of serum uric acid, raising the risk of gout and potentially cardiovascular diseases [15]. Conversely, the cessation of oestradiol synthesis in postmenopausal women has the potential to result in a reduction of vascular and cardioprotective functions of oestrogens. Consequently, menopause can be regarded as a pivotal period for both hyperuricemia and cardiovascular diseases. In China, a systematic review have repeatedly shown a considerably higher rate of hyperuricemia among postmenopausal women compared to women of childbearing age [16]. In Thailand, Techatraisak et al. found the prevalence of hyperuricemia in postmenopausal women to be 21.9% [17].

In two other Asian inception cohort studies, hyperuricemia has demonstrated a significant predictive value for several cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and diabetes mellitus [18, 19]. The sub-Saharan literature concerning hyperuricemia among postmenopausal is scary. Nonetheless, over the past two decades, the adoption of Western lifestyles might have contributed to promote the prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia in the sub-Saharan region [5]. A more profound comprehension of the relationship between hyperuricemia and cardiovascular risk events has the potential to facilitate the early identification and subsequent prevention of sudden cardiovascular diseases among postmenopausal women.

The objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of Hyperuricemia and to investigate its association with cardiovascular risk among postmenopausal women in Cameroon, a Central African nation, via a community-based investigation.

Methods

Study design and location

We conducted a cross-sectional study among postmenopausal women living in Yaoundé, the capital city of Cameroon. Yaoundé is an urban community consisting of seven districts, located at 3° 52′ north latitude and 11° 31′ east longitude in the Central Region. The city covers an area of 304 km² and has a population density of 9,095 inhabitants per km². Biochemical assays were performed at the Prima SARL laboratory and the Yaoundé Teaching Hospital Center. The study period spanned from November 1, 2024, to June 30, 2025.

Participants and minimal sample size

The study sample consisted in menopause women living in each of the seven districts of Yaoundé for at least three months. Initially, announcements about the study were shared through social media, television, and radio. Following this, volunteers were enrolled at various locations where women typically gather, including religious institutions such as churches and mosques, community associations, and marketplaces. According to the WHO guidelines, menopause is defined as 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea that is not attributable to other pathological or physiological causes. The participants in the study were postmenopausal women who provided their informed consent. The exclusion criteria were established according to a recent Japanese community-based study concerning the prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia [1]. To that end, postmenopausal women with any clinical or biological condition that is commonly recognized as directly influencing the secretion or excretion of uric acid were excluded from the study. Such conditions include, but are not limited to, prior or present chronic kidney impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate less than 60 ml.min.m− 2 at three-month intervals), past medical history of chronic gout, kidney stones, hemoglobinopathies, liver diseases, and biliary stones [18, 19]. Additionally, women who were on hypouricemic medications were also not included in the study. A series of diagnostic procedures were conducted at three-month intervals to evaluate the exclusion criteria. These selection procedures included biological assays, such as serum creatinine, liver enzymes, serum albumin, complete blood count, and blood smear analysis. Additionally, abdominal ultrasound and clinical examination were performed. Any biological abnormality of these parameters conducted to exclusion of volunteers. Additionally, participants with hemolyzed blood samples that could not be resampled were also excluded from the study.

The sample size was determined using the Cochrane’s formula specifically established for cross-sectional studies.

n = minimal size ; p = prevalence ; Z = standardized level of significance (1,96) ; e = standardized error (5%).

Based on the findings of He et al., which examined the prevalence of asymptomatic hyperuricemia among South Korean postmenopausal women (p = 15.3%), we determined the minimal sample size to be 199 participants [18].

Procedures and data collection

Following the acquisition of administrative authorizations from the pertinent health districts and ethical clearance, an invitation was extended to menopausal women residing in Yaoundé via social media, radio, and television. Each volunteer was informed of their rights and responsibilities through an information notice available in both official languages, English and French. The initial stage of the study involved the identification of exclusion criteria on two occasions over a period of three months. Prior to inclusion in the study population, all eligible participants completed an informed consent form. In the subsequent stage of the study, the collection of data was executed through the utilization of a sheet.

The relevant variables included sociodemographic characteristics, pregnancy history, purine diet evaluation, the duration of menopause, level of physical activity, blood pressure, body mass index, waist circumference, lipid profile, venous blood glucose, and cardiovascular risk score. The level of physical activity was assessed by means of the Marshall scale. The cardiovascular risk score was determined using the World Health Organization (WHO) cardiovascular risk score, which was adapted specifically for Western African origins, as delineated in the supplementary material 1. According to the International Diabetes Federation, abdominal obesity in women is defined as a waist circumference greater than 80 centimeters. Given the absence of standardized instruments in Cameroon, an indirect, semi-quantitative assessment of the purine diet score was undertaken, drawing upon the food frequency questionnaire developed by Cade et al. The contextualization of the questionnaire’s variables is elucidated in further detail in the supplementary material (see Sect. 2). The assessment of consumption frequencies was conducted on a daily, weekly, and monthly basis. Subsequently, these frequencies were assigned a numerical value ranging from 0 to 8, thereby generating a total score of 64 [20]. In the final study population, the following variables were measured: serum uric acid (SUA), venous blood glucose, and fasting lipid profiles.

Biological assays

The participants were subjected to testing following a 12-hour fast. They were seated with their elbows extended, following a rigorous aseptic procedure. As part of the medical examination, a blood sample was extracted from each subject using a Vacutainer® needle, in accordance with established protocols. This protocol permitted the collection of 10 milliliters of blood, which was subsequently divided into three distinct tubes: a dry tube for uric acid, serum creatinine and lipid profile testing, a fluorinated tube for venous blood glucose testing. The collected specimens were placed in a cooler and transported to the Prima SARL laboratory and the Univeristy Teaching Hospital laboratory and within two hours of collection. Upon arrival at the laboratory, the centrifugation process was initiated at a speed of 3,000 revolutions per minute for a duration of 10 min. The serum obtained was transferred to Eppendorf® tubes using micropipettes.

Serum uric acid levels were determined through the implementation of the uricase method. In the presence of uricase, uric acid undergoes a transformation into allantoin, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen peroxide. In a study examining the action of peroxidase on hydrogen peroxide, it was found that a red chromogen called quinoneimine is formed. The degree of absorption exhibited by this compound is directly proportional to the amount of uric acid present in the examined specimen. The readings were taken using a spectrophotometer set to a wavelength of 520 nanometers. Serum uric acid (SUA) levels were expressed in mg.dL− 1. Hyperuricemia was defined as a value above 6.0 mg.dL− 1. Serum creatinine was also measured by an enzymatic method. The glomerular filtration rate was evaluated by CKD-EPI formula. Volunteers with e-GFR less than 60 ml.min− 1.m2 at three-month intervals were excluded from the final sampling.

The Trinder method was employed to measure venous fasting blood glucose and total cholesterol. The high-density lipoprotein (HDL-c) was measured using the two-phase “selective detergent and accelerator” method, while triglycerides (TG) were assessed using the Fossati method, coupled with a Trinder reaction. All measurements were obtained using the Cobas 6000®, a fully automated biological analyzer manufactured in France in 2020. All these parameters were expressed in mg.dL− 1.

Statistical analysis

The data were entered and analyzed using SPSS software, version 27.0, also known by the acronym “Statistical Package for the Social Sciences”. In this study, quantitative variables were characterized by the mean and standard deviation (µ ± SD) or the median and interquartile range [quartile 25; quartile 75], depending on the normality of the distribution. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to ascertain the validity of the hypothesis concerning the normality of the distribution of quantitative variables. Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages. The prevalence of hyperuricemia was determined by calculating the proportion of postmenopausal women with serum uric acid levels exceeding 60 mg/L in relation to the entire study sample. Univariate analysis, employing either the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, has been utilized to identify factors associated with hyperuricemia. The selection of the appropriate test is contingent upon the frequencies present within the cross-tables. In instances where all frequencies were equal or greater than five, the chi-square test was employed. The odds ratio, with its confidence interval, was used to assess the direction and strength of the associations. Variables with a p-value less than 0.05 after univariate analysis, including age, duration of menopause, past history of hypertension, abdominal obesity, BMI, total cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, and physical inactivity, were entered into a logistic regression model. The objective of this multivariate analysis was to identify factors independently associated with asymptomatic hyperuricemia. The adjusted p-value and adjusted odds ratio were assessed at the conclusion of the multivariate analysis. A factor was identified as being independently associated with asymptomatic hyperuricemia when its p-value was less than 0.05 following multivariate analysis. The association between hyperuricemia and cardiovascular risk markers such as non-HDL/HDL ratio, LDL/HDL ratio, total cholesterol/HDL ratio, log(TG/HDL), and the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, was assessed using chi square test and Odds ratio. The significance level was set at 5%.

Quality management and safety

This study was carried out in a laboratory accredited by ASQUALAB. All aspects of the sample processing procedure were performed according to the ISO 15,189 standard. All abnormally low or high biological test results were repeated twice. The means of the three results were retained.

Results

The mean age of the subjects was 63.40 ± 8.72, with a range from 48 to 87 years. The predominant age group was between 55 and 64 years of age. The majority of the participants were married (37.9%), had received a secondary education (42.7%), and were of Bantu origin (68%). The mean age at menarche was 13.7 ± 1.95, with a range from 8 to 20 years. The mean duration of menopause was 13.5 years, with a range of 6 to 20 years and a maximum duration of 48 years. Hormone replacement therapy was administered to two (1%) participants. The study population included 25 participants (12.1%) with a history of diabetes and 56 participants (27.2%) with a history of hypertension. The majority of participants had a Charlson Comorbidity Index greater than 1 (98.5%). The majority of participants were sedentary (69.9%), with a mean duration of physical activity per week ranging from 30 to 60 min. The majority of participants (60.2%) exhibited a moderate frequency of consumption of a purine-rich diet. The mean systolic blood pressure was 139.21 ± 28.23 mm Hg, with a range from 88 to 231. The mean diastolic blood pressure was 89.20 ± 14.62 mm Hg, with a range from 50 to 134. The mean waist circumference was 96.43 ± 13.11 cm, ranging from 63 to 150. The proportion of abdominal obesity was 69.9%. The mean BMI was 28.25 ± 5.2 kg/m², with a range from 18.9 to 43.4. With respect to nutritional status, the majority of participants were classified as overweight (35.9%) and grade I obese (20.9%). The predominant metabolic abnormalities identified included total hypercholesterolemia (51.0%), hypertriglyceridemia (34.5%), and low HDL-cholesterol (22.8%). The prevalence of metabolic syndrome was 22.3% (see Table 1 below).

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of hyperuricemia among postmenopausal women was 37.9%. After univariate analysis, factors associated with hyperuricemia included older age (≥ 63 years), abdominal obesity, an LDL/HDL ratio ≥ 3.55, total hypercholesterolemia, elevated LDL levels, physical inactivity, and overweight/obesity. After multivariate analysis, factors independently associated with hyperuricemia were menopause duration more than 14 years (aOR 2.81, p = 0.015), a BMI of 25 kg/m² or higher (aOR 2.13, p = 0.030), and a history of hypertension (aOR 2.11 ; p = 0.040). Hyperuricemia was found to be significantly associated with an elevated LDL/HDL ratio (OR = 1.50, 95% CI 1.21–1.89, p = 0.001), non-HDL-C levels of ≥ 130 mg/dL (OR = 2.76, p = 0.001), and the non-HDL/TC ratio (OR = 1.14, p = 0.045). Additionally, the WHO CVD risk score was significantly higher in women with AH, with mean of 9.53 ± 5.67 vs. 7.16 ± 4.90 (p = 0.003).

The mean age in this study was 63.4 years, which is similar to the result reported by Leon et al. [21]. Leon’s research examined trends in the mean age of menopause in low- and middle-income countries, including Cameroon, from 1986 to 2019. Additionally, the mean serum uric acid level in our sample was comparable to that of 100 outpatients at the Bafoussam Regional Hospital, which reported a level of 54.7 ± 19.1 mg/L [22]. Both studies were conducted in urban centres in Cameroon. In contrast, the result obtained in this study is lower than the mean value of 94 mg/L reported by Ewenighi et al. in a study conducted in Nigeria [5]. This discrepancy can be attributed to the variation in methodologies employed. In the Nigerian study, the majority of the patients included were diagnosed with gout crisis. The study was conducted exclusively in the rheumatology unit. Notwithstanding, the sample size of the present study was greater than those of African studies. Moreover, these studies did not focus on postmenopausal women. Consequently, there is a possibility of persistent selection bias because patients with comorbidities, which could lead to hyperuricemia, were excluded. Consequently, we approach the interpretation of generalizations with a degree of caution. Moreover, the findings of this study are constrained to postmenopausal Cameroonian women residing in urban environments.

In the present study, the prevalence of hyperuricemia was found to be 37.9%. Alikor et al. have documented a 17.4% prevalence among rural populations in the southeastern region of Nigeria [23]. This discrepancy can be attributed to two potential factors. A decline in estrogen levels has been observed in menopausal women, which has been shown to result in a decrease in renal excretion of serum uric acid. Secondly, in the study of Alikor, the mean age was 41.3 years old compared to 63.4 years old in our study. In their study, the authors included volunteers aged 15 and above. Conversely, Alikor et al. have determined that the prevalence of AH was found to be significantly higher in women than in men among participants aged 50 years. In a similar vein, a systematic review by Ngandeu-Singwe et al. has revealed a comparable age-specific trend of increasing hyperuricemia rates among women, particularly in urban areas [24]. This observation can be attributed to several factors. A salient factor is the uricosuric effect of estrogen, which refers to its capacity to enhance the renal excretion of uric acid. A decline in estrogen levels following menopause can result in a decrease in uric acid excretion, leading to elevated serum uric acid levels [25]. A decline in glomerular filtration rates with age has been demonstrated, particularly in elderly individuals. The decline in renal function that is observed in this patient has been demonstrated to result in a decrease in uric acid excretion and, consequently, can lead to the development of hyperuricemia [26]. In the present study, subjects with renal impairment were excluded from the analysis in order to minimize confusion bias of altered renal function on the association between hyperuricemia and cardiovascular disease. Otherwise, volunteers with past history of chronic kidney disease (CKD) were excluded. As demonstrated in a substantial cohort of Kuwabara et al. in Japan, the presence of chronic kidney disease at the inception of the survey has the potential to compromise the efficacy of asymptomatic hyperuricemia in predicting cardiovascular events within a five-year timeframe [1]. Indeed, CKD has been identified as a distinct risk factor for both hyperuricemia and cardiovascular disease. Due to the nature of the cross-sectional study design, it was challenging to ascertain whether CKD manifested prior to the onset of hyperuricemia or vice versa. Therefore, we considered that CKD might represent a possible major confounding factor that was difficult to adjust for in this sample, despite its potential pertinence.

Additionally, some antihypertensive medications, such as thiazide diuretics, can raise uric acid levels [27]. This is particularly relevant in our study population, which consists of women with significant comorbidities, including hypertension (27%), some of whom are using hydrochlorothiazide, a thiazide diuretic [28]. Consistent with the findings of Bonsu et al. in Ghana, the prevalence of abdominal obesity was 92% among individuals aged 65 and over with a waist circumference exceeding 80 centimeters [29]. However, multivariate analysis revealed that postmenopause stage was not independently associated with abdominal obesity. As posited by numerous scholars, menopause frequently engenders anxiety regarding weight gain. Moreover, the body composition of postmenopausal women shifts towards a greater proportion of fat, resulting in weight gain, particularly in the lower abdominal region [30]. This finding may offer a potential explanation for the observed prevalence of 44% for metabolic syndrome in the present study, which aligns with the findings of a similar study conducted in Ghana.

After multivariate analysis, factors independently associated with Asymptomatic Hyperuricemia were menopause duration more than 14 years (aOR 2.81, p = 0.015), a BMI of 25 kg/m² or higher (aOR 2.13, p = 0.030), and a history of hypertension (aOR 2.11 ; p = 0.040). A biologically plausible mechanism is highlighted, showing the uricosuric effect of estrogen [25, 31]. Furthermore, adipose tissue has been demonstrated to contribute to elevated purine metabolism and possess the capacity to directly synthesize uric acid. Obesity has also been demonstrated to promote insulin resistance, which in turn reduces renal urate excretion. The relationship between weight loss and serum uric acid levels has been demonstrated in several studies, further substantiating a causal metabolic pathway [17]. Hypertension and hyperuricemia have been demonstrated to be associated through a number of pathophysiological mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, insulin resistance, and the utilization of antihypertensive medications, specifically calcium channel blockers and thiazide diuretics. According to the American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines, thiazide diuretics are considered a primary pharmaceutical treatment for hypertension within the Black population. These diuretics are understood to reduce proximal tubular excretion of uric acid, a key component in the management of hypertension [31]. Metabolic syndrome was not associated with hyperuricemia in the adjusted model, even though two of its components, BMI and hypertension, remained independently associated. This discrepancy is likely due to colinearity among the components of metabolic syndrome, which can weaken the overall effect of the composite variable after adjustment [32].

In this study, the WHO CVD risk score was found to be significantly higher among women with hyperuricemia, with a mean score of 9.53 ± 5.67, compared to 7.16 ± 4.90 in those without the condition (p = 0.003). These results suggest a potential link between hyperuricaemia and an increased cardiovascular risk. As indicated by research, total cholesterol, age, and blood pressure components of the WHO CVD score are recognized by the literature as independent factors of hyperuricemia [1]. This could suggest an indirect link between hyperuricemia and global cardiovascular risk. However, a cohort study involving 431,967 participants from the UK Biobank indicated that hyperuricemia significantly raised the risk of ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, and cardiovascular death [33]. Hyperuricemia was found to be significantly associated with an elevated LDL/HDL ratio (OR = 1.50, 95% CI 1.21–1.89, p = 0.001), non-HDL-C levels of ≥ 130 mg/dL (OR = 2.76, p = 0.001), and the non-HDL/TC ratio (OR = 1.14, p = 0.045). These findings align with previous research indicating that non-HDL-based indices effectively capture the full spectrum of atherogenic lipoproteins, including LDL, IDL, and VLDL remnants [34, 35]. Additionally, these indices have been shown to outperform LDL-C alone in predicting cardiovascular risk. This lends support that it could be exist a biological link between hyperuricemia and an atherogenic lipid profile. This result was consistent with several studies that showed elevated lipid-ratio indices are positively associated with hyperuricemia in other populations [36]. This is due to the strong correlation between increased lipid metabolism levels and insulin resistance, which promotes hepatic VLDL secretion and a dyslipidemic profile of small, dense LDL particles combined with low HDL levels.

In contrast, we found no significant association between hyperuricemia and the TC/HDL ratio, Log(TG/HDL), the non-HDL/HDL ratio, or the TyG index. This stands in contrast to the findings of certain studies, mainly conducted in Asian populations; In these studies, the TyG index have been shown to be positively associated with hyperuricemia [37]. There are several possible explanations for this discrepancy. Firstly, there exist several differences in body composition and dietary patterns between our sub-Saharan African sample and the Asian populations on which most of the studies about the link TyG and hyperuricemia have conducted [34–37]. Fezeu et al. in Cameroon have demonstrated that hypertriglyceridemia plays a minimal role in the prevalence of metabolic syndrome among the Cameroonian population [38]. In addition, Ama Moor et al. noted that low HDL cholesterol was the predominant dyslipidemia among patients of both sexes with high cardiovascular risk. Its prevalence of hypertriglyceridemia was considerably lower than that reported in Asian and Western literature [39]. Secondly, the possibility that, in postmenopausal women specifically, estrogen-related shifts in renal urate handling and lipid metabolism may attenuate the insulin-resistance-mediated pathway captured by triglyceride/glucose-based indices, compared with cholesterol-based indices such as non-HDL-C and the LDL/HDL ratio, which remained significant in our study. Finally, our relatively modest sample size compared to Asian cohorts might possibly might have reduced the ability for the statistical tests to detect significant differences.

The present study is subject to the following limitations. Firstly, the sampling did not include rural postmenopausal women and was based on voluntary enrollment. It is possible that both factors could have led to a reduction in the representativeness of the results. The selection procedure was constrained by several logistics, costs and transport challenges. Consequently, the collection and timely transport of biological assays from rural women was rendered infeasible. Additionally, another potential limitation concerning a very large external validity is that this study focused on asymptomatic hyperuricemia volunteers without renal impairment. Given the established relationship between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and both hyperuricemia and cardiovascular diseases, we choose to exclude CKD patients from the design [8]. As demonstrated in a recent Asian large cohort study, this decision was made in the aim to minimize the confusion bias of CKD [1]. This relationship, characterized by its ambivalent nature, has the potential to result in misinterpretations of the statistical analysis, particularly in a cross-sectional design with a modest sample size. The final aspect of limitation is cross-sectional design. Even it can identify an association between hyperuricemia and cardiovascular risk, it can’t ascertain the real and temporal implications of hyperuricemia in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases. Nonetheless, the result obtained in this study was consistent with the findings of in vitro and observational studies that examined the potential role of elevated uric acid levels in predicting cardiovascular events and related mortality [31–33].

Despite its limitations, this study was the first in the Central African region to explore this possible relationship among postmenopausal women. While our study cannot establish causality due to its cross-sectional nature, the observed differences highlight the need for longitudinal prospective studies in sub-Saharan settings.

Conclusion

The present study emphasizes the considerable prevalence of hyperuricemia among postmenopausal women in Yaoundé. Long duration of menopause, overweight and hypertension were factors independently associated with hyperuricemia. In addition, this study demonstrated a higher estimated cardiovascular risk score among women with hyperuricemia. A comprehensive approach, encompassing better lifestyle and low purine diet, is recommended for this specific population. Further prospective longitudinal studies are needed to ascertain the causality link between asymptomatic hyperuricemia and cardiovascular risk among postmenopausal women in Cameroon.
