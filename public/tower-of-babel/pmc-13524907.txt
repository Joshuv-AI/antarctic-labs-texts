Validation of point-of-care INR testing against standard laboratory methods in a Malaysian primary healthcare setting
Authors: Rosline Hassan, Salfarina Iberahim, Siti Suhaila Mohd Yusoff, Nor Khairul Putra Md Madi, Ahmad Zakwan Mustafa, Mohamad Fadli Mohd Yunus, Razan Hayati Zulkeflee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reliable INR monitoring is crucial for patients on warfarin to prevent thrombotic or bleeding events. This study evaluated the accuracy of the Wondfo POCT device compared to standard laboratory methods (Stago STA-R Max3 and ACL TOP 300) in a primary healthcare setting. The goal was to assess whether this point-of-care testing can provide timely and reliable results for better patient management in community-based clinics. In a prospective, cross-sectional study, samples from 74 patients on warfarin and 20 healthy controls were analyzed. Agreement was assessed using Bland-Altman plots, Deming regression, and CLSI POCT14-A criteria. Deming regression demonstrated strong linear correlations across all comparisons (r > 0.92, R² > 0.84). The mean bias for Wondfo (capillary) remained within CLSI POCT14-A agreement limits for all INR ranges when compared with both Stago and ACL TOP 300 platforms (all SE < AE). A very strong correlation was also observed between Wondfo venous and capillary results (r = 0.954, R² = 0.908). The study concludes that the Wondfo POCT device demonstrates acceptable agreement with standard laboratory testing within therapeutic ranges, thereby offering a viable alternative. It is a reliable and practical solution for rapid, decentralized INR monitoring, particularly in outpatient care, and can potentially improve patient management and safety.

Introduction

The management of patients on warfarin therapy relies heavily on accurate and timely monitoring of the international normalized ratio (INR)1–3. INR testing is essential for maintaining therapeutic efficacy while reducing the risk of serious hemorrhagic or thrombotic events. Traditionally, INR measurements are performed using standard laboratory methods, which require venipuncture and centralized analysis via either mechanical (viscosity-based) or photo-optical clot detection techniques2. While highly accurate, this approach often entails logistical challenges such as sample transportation, batch processing, and delayed turnaround times, potentially impacting clinical decision-making and patient convenience2.

INR point-of-care testing (POCT) offers a convenient and promising alternative for clinicians managing patients on warfarin therapy2. POCT devices offer rapid results at the patient’s bedside or in outpatient clinics, utilizing capillary blood samples and providing immediate feedback4. This swift accessibility holds the potential to streamline patient care, facilitate immediate dose adjustments, and improve patient adherence and engagement with their treatment4. Numerous studies globally have demonstrated the utility and reliability of POCT INR devices in various clinical settings2,4,5. Before introducing POCT into general practice, clinicians must be assured that their decisions are guided by reliable, accurate, and precise results comparable to gold standard conventional laboratory testing. Nevertheless, concerns regarding potential underestimation or overestimation of INR values highlight the need for rigorous comparative evaluation.

Despite the increasing global adoption of POCT technologies, there is a notable research gap concerning their performance and clinical utility in primary healthcare settings, particularly in Malaysia. Local patient demographics, common comorbidities, and the existing laboratory infrastructure may affect the comparative accuracy and agreement between POCT and standard laboratory INR methods6. For instance, patients with antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APLS) or lupus anticoagulant may exhibit falsely elevated INR values on photo-optical systems, while mechanical detection methods may be less susceptible7. Similarly, the absence of standardized reagent lots across laboratories, common in resource-limited settings, may introduce inter-laboratory variability8. Local dietary patterns (e.g., high intake of vitamin K-rich foods) and prevalent comorbidities such as chronic liver disease and renal impairment may also affect drug metabolism and thus INR stability, potentially amplifying discrepancies between POCT and laboratory methods7.

This study aims to evaluate the agreement and performance of point-of-care INR testing using the Wondfo Optical Coagulation Analyzer (OCG-102) in comparison with standard laboratory methods: the Stago STA-R Max3 (mechanical, viscosity-based detection) and the ACL TOP 300 (photo-optical clot detection), using both capillary and venous samples. The study seeks to determine the level of concordance among these three methods and assess the clinical utility of POCT INR in optimizing patient management within the local healthcare setting.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients population

This prospective, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Haemostasis Unit, Hematology Laboratory, and Primary Care Specialist Clinic of Hospital Pakar Universiti Sains Malaysia (HPUSM), an 800-bed tertiary care facility, between March and June 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Universiti Sains Malaysia (USM/JEPeM/KK/24100958) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. A total of 74 adult patients (≥ 18 years) receiving stable oral anticoagulant therapy with warfarin for at least three months were enrolled from the hospital’s outpatient anticoagulation clinic. Inclusion criteria comprised patients on warfarin under follow-up at the INR clinic, presenting with INR values within the study-specified range, and having INR measured by both point-of-care testing (POCT) and standard laboratory methods on the same day. Exclusion criteria included pregnant women and individuals with known bleeding disorders, as these conditions could impact the accuracy and interpretation of INR results. Patients with hypercoagulable states such as antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APLS) were not excluded; however, their results are interpreted with caution given the known potential for discordant INR values in this population. Additionally, 20 healthy blood donors were recruited as controls. Healthy controls were defined as adults with no known bleeding or clotting disorders, not on any anticoagulant or vitamin K-affecting medications, with no active infection, and with a baseline INR within the normal range (0.8–1.2). For each participant, demographic information (age, sex), underlying indications for anticoagulation, and INR values from both standard laboratory and POCT methods were recorded. The time differences between sample collection and result availability were also determined. The sample size (n = 74 patients) was determined pragmatically and exceeds the minimum of 40 samples recommended by CLSI EP09 for method-comparison studies. For the healthy control group (n = 20), the purpose was to characterize device performance within the normal INR range (0.8–1.2), for which this sample size is sufficient. Of the 74 INR measurement events analyzed, 62 represented unique participants; 12 records comprised repeat measurements from the same individuals obtained on different occasions, and were included in the method-comparison analysis but excluded from the demographic summary presented in Table 1.

INR measurement procedures

For each enrolled patient, INR was measured using two distinct methods during a single clinic visit:

Standard Laboratory Method (Reference method): Venous blood samples were collected by trained phlebotomists into 2.7 mL 0.109 M trisodium citrate tubes, with additional non-anticoagulated whole blood capillary samples obtained concurrently. Samples were immediately sent to the HPUSM Hematology Laboratory. INR was measured using the automated coagulation analyzers Stago STA-R Max3 (mechanical, viscosity-based detection, STA R max3-STA - NEOPTIMAL 10 reagent; Diagnostica Stago S.A.S., Asnières-sur-Seine, France), ACL TOP 300 (photo-optical clot detection; HemosIL RecombiPlasTin 2G reagent, Instrumentation Laboratory, Bedford, MA, USA), and Wondfo Optical Coagulation Analyzer (OCG-102; Wondfo coagulation test strips, Guangzhou Wondfo Biotech Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China), following the manufacturers’ instructions and routine laboratory quality control protocols.POCT Method (Test): Capillary blood was collected via fingertip using an Accu-Chek lancet and applied to the Wondfo Optical Coagulation Analyzer (OCG-102), with devices located at both the Primary Health Clinic and the Hemostasis Unit, Hematology Laboratory. Capillary blood for POCT was collected immediately following venous phlebotomy during the same clinic visit, ensuring a minimal time difference between sample types. POCT results were available within approximately three to five minutes of sample application, while standard laboratory results were available within approximately 45–60 min from sample receipt. Venous samples drawn into trisodium citrate tubes were also analyzed using the Wondfo OCG-102. The practice nurse performing POCT at the primary health clinic received practical training on patient handling and internal quality control to ensure safe and accurate testing. All POCT devices were subjected to daily quality control according to manufacturer recommendations. All patient samples were tested once on the Wondfo OCG-102 and once on the reference laboratory coagulation analyzer. The results obtained were used for method comparison analyses.

INR results were stratified into four predefined ranges for analysis: 1.0–2.5, 2.6–3.5, 3.6–5.0, and > 5.0. This classification allowed assessment of agreement between POCT and standard laboratory methods across clinically relevant therapeutic and supratherapeutic INR levels.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using OriginPro (Version 2024b, OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA) and Microsoft Excel software. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study population. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous data were reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). To assess the relationship between the two measurement systems, a Pearson correlation analysis was performed to determine the association between the POCT and standard laboratory INR values. A linear regression model was then fitted to further describe this relationship. For agreement analysis, a paired t-test was used to evaluate the average discrepancy between the POCT and conventional INR results. The Bland-Altman method was employed to visually analyze the agreement between the two systems, identifying the mean bias and the 95% limits of agreement. All statistical inferences were based on a 95% confidence interval, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Demographic data were summarized descriptively for the 62 unique participants, with continuous variables reported as mean ± SD and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study population

The demographic characteristics of both groups are summarized in Table 1. Among the 62 warfarin-treated patients, the mean age was 60.7 ± 15.1 years (median 62 years, range 24–87 years). The majority were female (61.3%, n = 38). The most common indication for anticoagulation was atrial fibrillation (AF; 61.3%, n = 38), followed by mechanical heart valve replacement (29.0%, n = 18), venous thromboembolism (VTE; 6.5%, n = 4), and other indications (3.2%, n = 2). The 20 healthy controls were younger (mean age 31.1 ± 7.9 years, median 32, range 21–45 years) and comprised 65.0% females.

Bland-Altman analysis showed good agreement between the POCT Wondfo Optical Coagulation Analyzer (OCG-102) and the laboratory method (Stago STA-R Max3), with a minimal mean bias of 0.04 and limits of agreement from − 1.00 to 1.08, apart from a few outliers especially at higher INR values (as shown in Fig. 1). Deming regression demonstrated a strong correlation (r = 0.922, R² = 0.849) with the equation Y = 1.045X − 0.144, indicating that POCT INR values were slightly higher than laboratory INR. Overall, the findings confirm good concordance between both methods, with minimal bias, acceptable limits of agreement, and strong correlation, supporting POCT INR as a reliable alternative to laboratory testing. Table 2 presents the method bias of the POCT device compared with the laboratory reference, stratified into three clinically relevant INR ranges based on CLSI POCT14-A guidelines: 1.0–2.5 (below therapeutic), 2.6–3.5 (therapeutic), and ≥ 3.6 (high INR, bleeding risk). The POCT INR demonstrated good overall accuracy across all ranges, with a slight positive bias at lower INR values (1.0–3.5), which is clinically acceptable, and a minor negative bias at higher values (≥ 3.6), which may be less favorable but remained within acceptable limits. Importantly, all categories met the CLSI POCT14-A performance criteria, as the system error was consistently below the allowable error (SE < AE). This indicates that the method’s bias is clinically acceptable within each specified INR range.

These findings confirm that both venous and capillary sampling are clinically reliable for INR testing with the Wondfo device, although a slight upward bias should be considered when using venous samples at lower INR ranges.

Discussion

Accurate measurement is critical for patient care; thus, POCT methods should be validated against standard assays before clinical implementation. This study set out to rigorously assess the agreement and performance of a POCT device (Wondfo) in comparison with two reference laboratory systems (Stago STA-R Max3 and ACL TOP 300) for INR measurement. Overall, our findings show that the POCT results were largely consistent with laboratory values, particularly results which were within the therapeutic range. The device demonstrated a strong linear correlation with both laboratory methods either by mechanical or optical, supported by high correlation coefficients (r > 0.92) and R² values (> 0.84). Nevertheless, Bland–Altman analysis indicated broad limits of agreement, with variability increasing notably at higher INR levels. Despite this, the device’s bias remained within the CLSI POCT14-A acceptance limits across all INR categories, though higher INR therapeutic ranges warrant careful interpretation.

Comparison of venous and capillary samples on the same POCT device also revealed excellent concordance (r > 0.95), underscoring its reliability across sample types in therapeutic monitoring. Clinically, the accuracy of the device is particularly critical within the INR range of 2.6–3.5, where small discrepancies could directly affect warfarin dose adjustments. Below INR 2.0, the risk of thrombosis increases, making precise measurement essential. Our analysis suggests that the POCT device performs reliably at subtherapeutic and therapeutic INR levels; however, its reduced accuracy above INR 3.6–4.0 highlights the need for cautious interpretation and possibly tailored usage guidelines, consistent with findings from previous studies by Yelland et al. and Jackson et al.9,10. Therefore, a confirmatory sample should be referred to the reference laboratory whenever a supratherapeutic INR is detected during a POCT visit. Our findings align with several key points from the existing body of literature. Bhat et al. (2020), which evaluated a different POCT device (Hemochron Signature Elite), also found a “good concord” between POCT and laboratory INR results, with a median bias of 0.071. This is very similar to our own findings. They also observed a drift or lack of reliability in the POCT results at higher INR values (≥ 4.7), which is a critical point of caution that our study also identified1.

The standard approach for INR monitoring in patients receiving warfarin therapy remains laboratory testing of venous blood, collected in citrated tubes, followed by plasma analysis after centrifugation. This process typically requires up to one hour from specimen collection to result reporting. In contrast, POCT provides a faster alternative, generating results within approximately five minutes from a finger-prick capillary sample. In this study, both venous and capillary samples were evaluated using the POCT device, demonstrating no significant differences and a strong correlation between the two sample types. These findings support the use of capillary blood obtained by finger prick for patient testing. The immediate availability of results enables timely adjustment of warfarin dosing, potentially reducing healthcare resource utilization and enhancing patient convenience11.

Moreover, the patient-centered benefits of POCT are further substantiated by the randomized controlled trial by Woods et al. (2004), which directly assessed patient preference for capillary versus venous blood sampling. Their results demonstrated a strong preference for capillary testing, with patients reporting significantly less pain and greater satisfaction12. Similarly, a comparison of capillary and venous plasma drug concentrations for various antipsychotics by Remmerie et al. (2016) found that capillary sampling was predictive of venous concentrations. By using a Deming regression analysis, they concluded that finger-stick sampling is an appropriate alternative to venous sampling13. This finding, from a different field of medicine, provides powerful and independent support for the central premise of the present study: that capillary blood obtained via a POCT device can reliably reflect the concentration of a substance measured in venous plasma.

Rudd and Dier (2010) directly compared different models of anticoagulation management and found that a pharmacist-managed service resulted in the lowest rates of hospitalization and emergency department visits related to anticoagulation therapy. They also noted significant financial savings14. This is a crucial finding that complements our study. Our research validates the clinical performance of the Wondfo POCT device itself, while the Rudd and Dier study provides the real-world evidence that using a device like the Wondfo within a specialized clinic model can reduce patient morbidity and healthcare costs. This holistic perspective—combining the device’s accuracy with the proven benefits of a specialized management model—underscores the complete value proposition of implementing POCT in a clinical setting.

Furthermore, the study by Garwood et al. (2008) highlights the critical importance of the anticoagulation management model itself. Their research showed a significant decrease in INR control and an increase in adverse medical events when patients were transitioned from a pharmacist-managed anticoagulation clinic to general physician care15. This finding directly relates to our study by highlighting the clinical context in which a POCT device provides maximum benefit. Our data demonstrates that the Wondfo POCT device is a clinically acceptable and convenient tool, while the Garwood study shows that a structured, specialized clinic is the most effective care model. Therefore, the convenience and accuracy of the Wondfo POCT device, when paired with a pharmacist-managed clinic, could be a powerful combination that leads to a higher quality of care and improved patient outcomes over the long term.

These findings support the overall conclusion that POCT devices are reliable across a broad range of INR values but may require laboratory confirmation when results fall within the supratherapeutic range. Similarly, the study by Bhat et al. underscored key advantages of POCT from the patient perspective, including shorter waiting times and preference for the less invasive fingerstick method, thereby strengthening the case for their integration into clinical practice. Collectively, the evidence suggests that POCT devices represent a clinically acceptable and patient-preferred alternative for routine monitoring, though their limitations at higher INR values necessitate structured verification protocols1. Despite these benefits, several challenges remain as POCT continues to expand. In contrast to central laboratory testing, POCT relies on single-use reagent formats rather than bulk reagents, resulting in a higher per-test cost compared with high-volume laboratory assays. Moreover, POCT systems may not fully account for pre-analytical, analytical, and post-analytical factors that can affect test accuracy. These vulnerabilities can introduce additional variables that compromise specimen integrity and increase the risk of false-positive or false-negative results16,17.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. The study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other patient populations or clinical settings. Furthermore, this was a cross-sectional study, and therefore, it does not provide insight into the long-term clinical outcomes of managing patients with the POCT device, such as its impact on time in therapeutic range or the incidence of bleeding and thrombotic events. While the sample sizes were adequate for the statistical analyses performed, a larger cohort would have provided greater statistical power and allowed for more robust subgroup analyses. Furthermore, the relatively small number of samples in the supratherapeutic INR range (> 3.5) limits the statistical robustness of conclusions drawn for this category; patients with INR values exceeding 3.5 were underrepresented in this cohort, and dedicated studies recruiting higher proportions of such patients are needed to better characterize POCT device accuracy at elevated anticoagulation levels.

Additionally, this study did not evaluate inter-device variability or lot-to-lot reagent variation for the Wondfo OCG-102, which may influence INR results across different device units or reagent batches. Furthermore, the comparative analysis was limited to two reference systems (Stago STA-R Max3 and ACL TOP 300), and validation against a broader panel of PT reagents, which may yield different ISI calibrations, was not performed. Despite the clinical advantages of POCT, including rapid turnaround, less invasive sampling, and patient convenience, the higher per-test cost of single-use POCT reagent strips compared with bulk laboratory reagents represents a significant practical consideration. Furthermore, as demonstrated in this study, supratherapeutic INR results (> 3.5) warrant confirmatory testing using a standard laboratory reference method, introducing an additional step and associated cost that may further limit cost-effectiveness in resource-limited or high-volume primary care settings. A formal cost-effectiveness analysis, incorporating turnaround time savings, reduced patient travel, and clinical outcomes, would be valuable before widespread deployment is recommended.

Furthermore, this study represents a single-point validation; periodic correlation between the Wondfo OCG-102 and the reference laboratory method, recommended at initial site implementation, with each new reagent lot, following device servicing, and at minimum every 6 months during routine use per manufacturer and CLSI POCT14-A guidelines, was not evaluated, and long-term device performance consistency therefore remains to be established. Finally, within-run duplicate measurements were not performed; therefore, within-run reproducibility of the Wondfo OCG-102 could not be formally assessed per CLSI POCT14-A guidelines, and this represents an additional limitation of the current study. Future studies should address these factors to further characterize the performance of the device across diverse laboratory environments.

Conclusion and future perspective

In conclusion, the Wondfo POCT device (OCG-102) consistently meets the clinically acceptable bias limits defined by the CLSI POCT14-A guidelines especially for within therapeutic INR ranges. A particularly significant finding is the excellent agreement between venous and capillary samples on the same POCT device, with a near-zero bias in the critical, high INR range. Clinical decision-making, especially regarding dose adjustment, depends not only on test results but also on the patient’s clinical history and recent circumstances. Assessing the limits of agreement is therefore essential, as it helps identify the threshold at which discrepancies between POCT and laboratory INR values could influence anticoagulant management. Reporting this measure in future method-comparison studies would provide valuable guidance for clinicians in optimizing patient care.
