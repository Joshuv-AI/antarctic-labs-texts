Association of Heterotopic Gastric Mucosa in the Upper Esophagus (HGMUE) with Pharyngolaryngeal Symptoms: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Xuanran Chen, Shunhai Zhou, Chaoyi Shi, Mingzhi Feng, GeSang ZhuoMa, Diyun Shen, Tianyue Wang, Jun Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeterotopic gastric mucosa in the upper esophagus (HGMUE) is considered to be accompanied by pharyngolaryngeal symptoms, whereas the association strength between HGMUE and pharyngolaryngeal symptoms remains controversial. This study assessed the strength of the association between HGMUE and pharyngolaryngeal symptoms using a meta-analytic approach.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CNKI databases were searched for relevant articles published between January 2010 and January 2024. The pharyngolaryngeal symptoms of chronic cough, dysphagia, hoarseness, and globus in patients with HGMUE were summarized. The pooled odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using a random-effects model. The exploratory analyses were also performed, including sensitivity and subgroup analyses.ResultsA total of 17 observational studies (1 cohort study and 16 cross-sectional studies) with 626,369 patients (2414 HGMUE patients and 623,955 non-HGMUE patients) were included in the meta-analysis. HGMUE was significantly associated with an elevated incidence of chronic cough (OR: 3.36; 95% CI 1.25–9.01; P = 0.02), dysphagia (OR: 1.58; 95% CI 1.12–2.25; P = 0.01), hoarseness (OR: 4.13; 95% CI 1.47–11.56; P = 0.007), and globus (OR: 2.41; 95% CI: 1.43–4.04, P < 0.001). The association between HGMUE and the risk of dysphagia was found to be potentially influenced by study design, sample size, country, and diagnostic method, whereas the association between HGMUE with the risk of globus was potentially affected by the study design and country.ConclusionHGMUE was significantly associated with chronic cough, dysphagia, hoarseness, and globus. HGMUE should be taken into consideration for patients with pharyngolaryngeal symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10620-024-08699-w.

Introduction

Heterotopic gastric mucosa of the upper esophagus (HGMUE), also known as “inlet patch (IP)” or “cervical inlet patch (CIP),” is an endoscopic pink lesion characterized by the presence of gastric mucosal tissue in the upper esophagus [1]. The prevalence of HGMUE on endoscopy has been reported to range from < 1% [1]–13.8% [2], having an even higher prevalence on autopsy of ≤ 70% [3]. HGMUE is frequently considered an asymptomatic lesion that does not require treatment [4]. However, extra-esophageal symptoms, especially pharyngolaryngeal symptoms, have also been reported to accompany this lesion [5]. The symptomatic mechanism of HGMUE remains unknown, and there are currently three prevailing views addressing its pathogenesis: (1) HGMUE is a remnant in the esophageal column embryo, which occurs due to squamous epithelial hypoplasia during fetal life [6]; (2) HGMUE is linked to gastroesophageal reflux and may share pathophysiology with Barrett’s esophagus. With regurgitant material irritation [3], chronic acid injury causes squamous epithelium to metaplasia into columnar epithelium [7, 8]. (3) HGMUE may develop from a cervical esophageal mucous gland cyst [9].

Pharyngolaryngeal symptoms include chronic cough, hoarseness, globus, dysphagia, or frequent throat clearing [5]. Globus is characterized by a continuous or sporadic, painless feeling of a lump or foreign body in the neck that lasts > 3 months [10]. In gastroenterology clinics, some 10% of patients presenting to the clinic are affected by globus [11]. Also, globus accounts for about 4% of ENT referrals [12]. Globus tends to be persistent, challenging to cure, and recurrent [13]. It has been reported that nearly 50% of patients with globus made multiple visits to the hospital, and the condition seriously affected the quality of their life [14]. Thus far, the etiology of pharyngolaryngeal symptoms has remained unclear, and most studies have suggested that they are part of the extraesophageal syndrome in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [15], but the rate of positivity of their 24-h pH monitoring is low [16]. In a study by Maldhure et al. [17], the frequency of laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) in patients with pharyngolaryngeal symptoms such as globus was about 47%. Among these patients, only 10% showed signs of GERD, suggesting that only a small percentage of pharyngolaryngeal symptoms were due to GERD. Recent studies have reported that the etiology of pharyngolaryngeal symptoms may be due to HGMUE’s constant secretion of associated acids [18, 19]. Consequently, it is necessary to further define the pathogenesis and etiology associated with pharyngolaryngeal symptoms.

In clinical practice, it is critical to determine whether HGMUE significantly influences the emergence of pharyngolaryngeal symptoms. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the strength of the association between HGMUE and pharyngolaryngeal symptoms of chronic cough, hoarseness, dysphagia, and globus.

Methods

Literature Search

PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CKNI, four databases available in Chinese and English language, were searched for relevant studies. Literature search period was from January 2010 to January 2024. Search items were as follows: “Cervical inlet patch”, “Esophageal Inlet Patch”, “Inlet Patch”, “Gastric Inlet Patches of the Cervical Esophagus”, “heterotopic gastric mucosa of the upper esophagus”, “gastric heterotopia of the proximal esophagus”, “heterotopic gastric mucosa” (Supplemental Table 1). There were no restrictions on publication language or data. Basic information of the included literature

Literature Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Two reviewers (SH. Z and CY. S) independently performed the literature search and study selection. Disagreements amongst reviewers were settled through group talks until a consensus was reached. Inclusion criteria for studies in our meta-analysis were: (1) participation open to the general population without any serious or urgent underlying disease; (2) interventions determined using upper endoscopic confirmation of the diagnosis or histologic evidence; (3) the prevalence of HGMUE and clinical symptoms (including, but not limited to, chronic cough, dysphagia, hoarseness, and globus) among the outcomes; (4) all study designs, including cross-sectional, cohort, and case–control studies, ought to be observational. Exclusion criteria were the following: (1) duplicate publication or poor quality of information; (2) lack of outcome metrics or insufficient raw data provided and fruitless requests; (3) genres such as reviews, abstracts, and commentaries; and (4) number of HGMUE cases < 5.

Data Extraction and Quality Evaluation

Two reviewers (SH. Z and CY. S) conducted the literature search and study selection separately, adhering to a standard procedure. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by group discussion until an agreement was achieved. The following information was taken from each article: first author’s name, presentation year, study type, study design, study location, age and sex of subjects, type and number of cases, adjustment for confounders, Odds Ratio (OR), and 95% confidence interval (95%CI). The methodology of the listed research was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [12]. The research evaluated case–control study exposure, comparability, and selection as well as the cohort studies’ selection, comparability, and results. The maximum rating for a study was 9 stars, and studies with > 6 stars were recognized as of high relative quality.

Statistical Analysis

The included literature was statistically analyzed using Review Manager 5.3 and STATA 14.0. Using OR as the effect indicator, the combined OR value and 95% CI were calculated, and the resulting OR values were tested for heterogeneity. The fixed effects model was used to assess I2 < 50% if P > 0. 05; the random effects model was used to combine the data if P < 0. 05, which suggested increased heterogeneity. Studies with a considerable degree of heterogeneity underwent sensitivity analysis. Considering that the studies were observational and that the effect measurements employed in each study were distinct, and to explore heterogeneity further, we conducted subgroup analyses of outcome-related variables (study design, sample size, study quality, and geographic region variables), which were considered at p-values < 0.05. The differences in categories were statistically significant. For the outcome variables of > 10 studies, inverted funnel plots were drawn to determine whether publication bias existed, and a quantitative assessment of publication bias was conducted utilizing the Egger and Begg method. The trim and fill approaches were used to account for publication bias where it was present.

Results

Included Studies

Among a total of 1812 documents retrieved from the initial screening based on the keywords, 753 duplicate documents were excluded, 965 irrelevant documents were excluded by title and abstract, 94 documents were screened in full text, finally resulting in 17 documents included in the analysis [2, 4, 5, 20–33] (Fig. 1). Among these, 4 were retrospective studies, and 13 were prospective studies. Each of the included literature was read in its entirety to extract information about the authors, year of publication, demographics of the study population, sample size, prevalence, and pharyngeal symptoms (chronic cough, dysphagia, hoarseness, and globus) in the literature (Table 1). Prevalence of HGMUE

Results of the Meta-Analysis

Prevalence of HGMUE

A total of 17 studies were included, comprising 626,369 people without HGMUE and 2414 patients with HGMUE. The overall prevalence of HGMUE was 3.6% (95% CI = 0.022–0.060); there was a substantial amount of heterogeneity (I2 = 100%; P < 0.0001), possibly due to study design according to the meta-regression analysis results. The total rate of HGMUE based on the study region was 3.7% (95% CI = 0.006–0.249) in Europe and the USA, and 3.6% (95% CI = 0.018–0.070) in Asia. The total prevalence of HGMUE according to study type was 0.5% (95% CI = 0.002–0.10) in retrospective research and 6.7% (95% CI = 0.045–0.101) in prospective research. According to the approaches for identifying CIP, the pooled prevalence of CIP was 2.2% (95% CI = 0.013–0.039) by biopsy and 4.7% (95% CI = 0.011–0.211) by microscopy. Depending on the choice of the endoscope, the overall prevalence of plain white light endoscopy was 2.4% (95% CI = 0.015–0.040), and the overall prevalence of magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (ME-NBI) was 8.2% (95% CI = 0.045–0.152), with one article [34] using both of these methods. Age was not substantially related to the incidence of HGMUE (P > 0.05); there was a significant correlation with gender, where men were significantly more affected than women (OR = 1.99, 95% CI = 1.48–2.68, P < 0.01, Table 2).

Association Between HGMUE and Pharyngeal Symptoms

Association Between HGMUE and Chronic Cough

The relationship between HGMUE and chronic cough was documented in five studies, which included 470 patients with HGMUE and 21,310 patients without HGMUE. A random-effects model detected considerable heterogeneity in the eligible studies’ results (P < 0.0001, I2 = 88%, Fig. 2a). The OR of the relationship between HGMUE and chronic cough was 3.36 (95% CI = 1.25–9.01, P = 0.02), suggesting a strong association between HGMUE and chronic cough. During each run, individual studies were eliminated from the analysis to conduct sensitivity analyses (Supplemental Fig. 1). Nonetheless, the calculated OR remained relatively constant, demonstrating the robustness of the meta-analysis findings. When Yu’s study [4] was removed, a strong association remained between HGMUE and chronic cough (OR = 1.65, 95% CI = 1.25–2.181, P = 0.0004), and the heterogeneity of the articles was significantly reduced (P = 0.27, I2 = 24%, Fig. 2b), using a fixed-effects model.Fig. 2a Forest diagram Meta-analysis of the relationship between HGMUE and chronic cough. b Forest diagram Meta-analysis of the relationship between HGMUE and chronic cough (Yu’s study [4] was removed)

Association Between HGMUE and Dysphagia

The relationship between HGMUE and dysphagia was documented in 13 studies, which included 2030 patients with HGMUE and 621,017 patients without HGMUE. There was considerable heterogeneity in the results among all eligible studies (P < 0.0001, I2 = 75%, Fig. 3) using a random-effects model. The OR of the relationship between HGMUE and dysphagia was 1.58(95% CI = 1.12–2.25, P = 0.01). The inverted funnel diagram did not indicate any appreciable publishing bias (Supplemental Fig. 2), and the quantitative assessment of publication bias conducted using the Egger and Begg approach revealed no discernible publication bias (p > 0.05, Supplemental Fig. 3). Sensitivity analysis was carried out for each run by taking each study out of the analysis one at a time (Supplemental Fig. 4). Association Between HGMUE and Hoarseness

The relationship between HGMUE and dysphagia was documented in 3 studies, which included 89 patients with HGMUE and 1484 patients without HGMUE. There was considerable heterogeneity of results among all eligible studies (P = 0.08, I2 = 61%, Fig. 4) using a random effects model. The OR for the association between HGMUE and hoarseness was 4.13 (95% CI = 1.47–11.56, P = 0.007). Association Between HGMUE and Globus

The relationship between HGMUE and globus was documented in 14 studies, which included 1862 patients with HGMUE and 514,694 patients without HGMUE. There was considerable heterogeneity in results among all eligible studies (P < 0.0001, I2 = 87%, Fig. 5) using a random-effects model. The OR of the association between HGMUE and globus was 2.41 (95% CI = 1.43–4.04, P = 0.0009), an inverted funnel plot suggested no significant publication bias (Supplemental Fig. 6), and the quantitative assessment of publication bias conducted using the Egger and Begg approach revealed no discernible publication bias (p > 0.05, Supplemental Fig. 7). Sensitivity analysis was carried out for each run by taking each study out of the analysis one at a time (Supplemental Fig. 8). Nonetheless, the calculated odds ratio remained relatively constant, demonstrating the robustness of the meta-analysis findings.

Subgroup Analysis

We found that there remained large heterogeneity across all 4 outcome variables. Based on the differences in the number of included papers and feasibility, we analyzed subgroups of publications of > 10 publications. The degree of association between HGMUE and pharyngolaryngeal symptoms was analyzed in subgroups based on study design, sample size, study quality, and geographic region variables for HGMUE that carried out the inclusion of > 10 publications, where chronic cough with hoarseness was not subgrouped for < 10 publications.

Subgroup Analysis of the Degree of Association Between HGMUE and Dysphagia

A total of nine publications prospectively analyzed the relationship between HGMUE and dysphagia and showed no apparent distinction between dysphagia and HGMUE (OR 1.19, 95% CI: 0.69–2.05, P = 0.52). Four studies in all performed a retrospective analysis of the relationship between HGMUE and dysphagia and demonstrated a significant association between HGMUE and dysphagia (OR = 2.21, 95% CI: 1.30–3.73, P = 0.003). Six studies in all the literature on the relationship between HGMUE and dysphagia with sample sizes > 1000 individuals demonstrated that HGMUE and dysphagia were significantly associated (OR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.33–3.12, p = 0.001). Seven studies in all the literature on the association between HGMUE and dysphagia with sample sizes < 1000 people demonstrated no noteworthy difference between HGMUE and dysphagia (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.55–2.00, P = 0.89). A total of 8 literature on the relationship between HGMUE and dysphagia within the geographic region of Asia proved a noteworthy association between HGMUE and dysphagia (OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.26–2.77, p = 0.002). A total of five publications on the relationship between HGMUE and dysphagia within the geographic region of Europe and the USA demonstrated no noteworthy difference between HGMUE and dysphagia (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.61–2.48, P = 0.57). Subgroup Analysis of the Degree of Association Between HGMUE and Globus

A total of 11 publications prospectively analyzed the relationship between HGMUE and globus, demonstrating a significant correlation between HGMUE and globus (OR = 2.40, 95% CI = 1.25–4.61, p = 0.008). Three studies conducted a retrospective analysis of the relationship between HGMUE and globus and demonstrated that HGMUE and globus did not differ significantly (OR = 2.46, 95% CI = 0.78–7.71, P = 0.12). Seven published studies on the connection between globus and HGMUE with sample sizes > 1000 individuals found a significant correlation between HGMUE and globus (OR = 2.97, 95% CI = 1.32–6.67, p = 0.008). Seven studies on the relationship between HGMUE and globus with a sample size of < 1,000 people demonstrated a significant correlation between HGMUE and globus (OR = 1.94, 95% CI = 1.06–3.57, P = 0.03). A total of 9 studies on the relationship between HGMUE and globus within the geographic region of Asia proved a significant correlation between HGMUE and globus (OR = 2.56, 95% CI = 1.33–4.95, P = 0.005). A total of five studies on the relationship between HGMUE and globus within the geographic region of Europe and the USA proved that HGMUE and globus did not differ significantly (OR = 2.12, 95% CI = 0.85–5.26, P = 0.11). Six studies examining the connection between globus and HGMUE using endoscopy as a diagnostic tool found a substantial correlation between the two (OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.06–3.36, p = 0.03). A total of eight publications on the relationship between HGMUE and globus whose diagnostic modality was biopsy proved a significant association between HGMUE and globus (OR = 2.72, 95% CI: 1.29–5.72, P = 0.008, Table 3b).

Discussion

This study conducted a large-scale meta-analysis of 628,783 participants (2414 patients with HGMUE and 626,369 non-IBS participants) from 17 studies to synthesize possible associations between pharyngeal symptoms and HGMUE. A significant difference in the prevalence of chronic cough, dysphagia, hoarseness, and globus was found between HGMUE and controls. Subgroup analysis of globus detected a significant correlation between HGMUE and globus in prospective studies and Asian populations.

The meta-analysis conducted by Yin et al. [35] is the only one with a similar direction to our study. Although they also detected a significant correlation between HGMUE and pharyngolaryngeal symptoms, they did not perform a corresponding sensitivity or subgroup analysis to explore the reasons for the highly heterogeneous results. Therefore, this study focused on investigating the degree of association between chronic cough, dysphagia, hoarseness, and globus in pharyngolaryngeal symptoms and HGMUE and analyzing the source of heterogeneity. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis that addressed the degree of association between pharyngolaryngeal symptoms and HGMUE, providing a source of evidence for identifying the source of pharyngolaryngeal symptoms, detecting lesions early, and targeting treatment. Moreover, the study included relevant articles published from 2010 to the present. We aimed to reduce the interference and lower the prevalence of clinically examined HGMUE compared to actual prevalence due to underdeveloped endoscopic and NBI examination techniques and an incomplete understanding of HGMUE.

The average HGMUE detection rate across the 17 publications in this meta-analysis was 3.6%. The reason for the significant difference in the detection rate of HGMUE in different papers might be the following: (1) most HGMUE are located in the upper esophagus, where the upper esophageal sphincter is repeatedly contracted, the patient’s pharynx is highly reactive, which makes it difficult to stay here during endoscopic examination and exit [36]; (2) as a result of the endoscopist’s experience and emphasis on focus, when the endoscopist is inexperienced, the detection rate of HGMUE tends to be reduced [1]; (3) the true prevalence and clinical importance of HGMUE may be underestimated because the clinical relevance of the disease has not been appreciated and the location of the lesion is frequently missed during endoscopy [24]. Our results showed that the detection rate of ME-NBI was higher than that of plain endoscopy, which may be because ME-NBI is superior to white-light imaging for HGMUE visualization as it can detect HGMUE by color differences [2]. Therefore, ME-NBI is more favorable for HGMUE detection than white light endoscopy. In our study, HGMUE detection rates were significantly higher in prospective than in retrospective studies, as endoscopists pay more attention to these lesions in prospective studies. Maconi et al. [1] and Azar et al. [37] have shown that detecting HGMUE depends on the endoscopist’s thorough probing of the proximal esophagus. When endoscopists consciously participate in prospective studies to detect HGMUE, they tend to spend more time examining the proximal esophagus.

According to several studies, HGMUE may be linked to reflux reflux disease and share a pathophysiology with Barrett’s esophagus [3]. ROSZTÓCZY et al. [38] noted a 69% co-prevalence between HGMUE and GERD, and that patients with HGMUE had significantly higher rates of acid and biliary exposure in the esophagus than the normal population, which is similar to esophageal manifestations of GERD. Thus, it was concluded that there is a probable association between GERD and HGMUE. However, there are fewer studies between HGMUE and GERD, and it is hard to confirm a clear association between them and the causal relationship. This requires prospective analysis with large samples and further proof through a Mendelian randomization study. Chatelain et al. [11] reported a case of HGMUE adenocarcinoma with the same immunohistochemical markers as Barrett’s esophagus, i.e., CK7/CK20, on the surface of the tumor and in the surrounding HGMUE, inferring that HGMUE adenocarcinoma had the same pathological mechanism as Barrett’s esophagus and that both were related to gastroesophageal reflux. Feurle et al. [39] compared HGMUE and Barrett’s esophageal epithelium by immunohistochemical testing and showed that both contain cells that secrete glucagon, growth inhibitors, serotonin antibodies, and pancreatic polypeptides, suggest a physiopathologic link between HGMUE and Barrett’s esophagus. Several studies [18, 29, 40] reported that biopsies of HGMUE revealed the presence of acid-producing mural cells in most HGMUE. The hoarseness, chronic cough, dysphagia, and globus that we studied may be due to the associated symptoms caused by the constant acid secretion by these acid-producing mural cells. However, there are no reliable and easy-to-use means to test the acid secretion capacity of HGMUE because the test is time-consuming and labor-intensive, the patients are uncomfortable, and the results do not reflect the actual situation due to the influence of gastric acid reflux. In a study by Maldhure et al. [17], the frequency of pharyngolaryngeal symptoms in patients with LPR was about 47%, and only 10% had evidence of GERD. De Bortoli et al. [41] reported a low incidence of gastroesophageal reflux in patients with pharyngolaryngeal symptoms on pondus hydrogenii (pH) examination. Another study in Malaysia [42] reported that GERD was identified by pH monitoring in only 25% of patients with chronic laryngitis. These findings suggest that 25–50% of patients with pharyngolaryngeal symptoms need to be evaluated for reasons other than GERD. According to research by Yu et al. [4], patients with GERD had a 40.07% prevalence of pharyngolaryngeal symptoms, while those with HGMUE had a 76.67% prevalence. This finding raises the possibility that HGMUE (especially in those without reflux symptoms) may be a separate source of pharyngeal discomfort, which is further supported by our findings where four pharyngolaryngeal symptoms, including chronic cough, dysphagia, hoarseness, and globus, were significantly associated with HGMUE.

Considerable heterogeneity was present in all the results of the degree of association in the article. We proved the robustness of the meta-analysis results by sensitivity analysis, and the qualitative and quantitative analysis of article bias by inverted funnel plot and Egger and Begg method proved no significant publication bias. However, the results should be further analyzed for other reasons. For example, in the analysis of the association between chronic cough and HGMUE, Yu et al. [4] described chronic cough as an “annoying or irritating cough”, which is different from chronic cough, and the heterogeneity could be reduced to 24% by removing the results of this study. In the analysis of the degree of correlation between hoarseness and HGMUE, because there were only three papers included in the literature, and in one of these studies [5]. HGMUE was only diagnosed endoscopically, the results showed a strong correlation, having a large impact on the overall results. Thus, differences in the assessment and judgment of symptomatology in different kinds of literature could significantly impact the experimental results; in meta-analyses with fewer studies, the results of particular research can significantly impact the overall results.

The mechanism through which HGMUE causes globus remains unclear. Previous studies have shown that HGMUE can produce acid by pH monitoring. It is also believed that acid produced by HGMUE has a role in the symptom progression of globus [18, 19]. Therefore, using proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) can inhibit acid secretion in HGMUE [19, 43], and treatment with PPIs can reduce symptoms of globus [44–47]. However, in one of these studies, PPIs were more prevalent in patients with globus, suggesting a lack of a control effect [44]. In one study, the globus symptoms were relieved by ablative treatment of HGMUE, but the procedure results suggested that there were no acid-producing mural cells in the patients’ HGMUE [44]. Therefore, whether the acid secreted by HGMUE produces globus remains to be investigated. Another view is that the globus may be produced by HGMUE-induced hyperbaric upper esophageal sphincter (UES). Reflex contraction of the UES is thought to be a respiratory defense mechanism triggered by stomach acid reflux. This contraction is thought to be responsible for generating globus [48, 49]. In general, the esophagus lumen is the most important site for mechanical or chemical stimuli, normally covered by a squamous epithelium. However, since the HGMUE is covered by columnar epithelium, it seems to be sensitive to stimuli [2]. Therefore, HGMUE may increase the ability of UES to contract in response to external stimuli. According to another study [2], patients with globus had a decreased rate of asymptomatic reflux. Thus, patients with esophageal insensitivity may not suffer from globus. It is also possible that the etiology of the hysteric globus is due to visceral hypersensitivity. According to a dynamic pH study [50], reflux did not always need to reach the pharynx to cause globus; in most cases, reflux was restricted to the distal esophagus in individuals with globus. When the esophagus is hypersensitive in patients with globus, a small amount of reflux of gastric contents in the distal esophagus can cause globus. It has been suggested that pharyngeal reflux may cause globus via the vagus nerve, which was confirmed by a study where acid perfusion in the distal esophagus elevated UES pressure, leading to globus [48]. The present study could only clarify a significant correlation between globus and HGMUE, and further experimental studies are needed to elucidate the mechanism of globus.

Our subgroup analysis of dysphagia revealed no significant association between HGMUE and dysphagia in the prospective study, while there was a significant relationship between the two in the retrospective analysis. In the sample size, a significant association between HGMUE and dysphagia existed in larger sample sizes (> 1000 individuals) and none in the opposite direction, which may be due to a particular bias in the study results caused by the smaller sample size. The association between HGMUE and dysphagia was more pronounced in Asian countries compared to European and American countries. A comparison of endoscopy directly revealed a significant association between HGMUE and dysphagia in biopsy. Through subgroup analysis, we identified multiple variables that influenced the study results, so more prospective, large-sample studies are needed to analyze the association between dysphagia and HGMUE further. Additional subgroup analysis of globus revealed a significant correlation between HGMUE and globus in the prospective study, whereas there was no significant association between them in the retrospective analysis, which could be because the inherent flaws in retrospective studies are prone to greater bias. The difference in the incidence of HGMUE between prospective and retrospective studies revealed that the lower detection rate of HGMUE in retrospective studies tends to cause underdiagnosis in patients with globus. The correlation between HGMUE and globus was more evident in Asian countries than in European and American countries, which may be due to ethnographic differences between Asian and European and American populations, or because of the smaller number of literature included in Europe and the USA, with a study by Neumann et al. [23] having the largest sample size with a positive result. There were no significant differences between HGMUE and globus in the retrospective analyses and countries in the European and American regions, which suggests that geography and type of study may influence the results and, therefore, should be further explored. The results of both subgroup analyses of diagnostic method and sample size revealed a significant correlation, both in endoscopy and biopsy and in sample size. There was also a clear correlation between globus and HGMUE.

The present study has the following limitations: (1) although this study involved a large number of cases (626,369 in total), sample representativeness and heterogeneity between studies may affect the generalizability of the findings. Designs, patient populations, diagnostic criteria, and symptom assessment methods may vary across studies, leading to differences in results; (2) most of the data included in this study were likely to be from retrospective studies, which are often subject to data recording and review bias. The analyses in this study were based on published literature, and unpublished data were not searched, so publication bias is unavoidable. (3) Although this study provides evidence of the association between HGMUE and pharyngolaryngeal symptoms, there is a lack of high-quality prospective studies to validate these findings. (4) There is limited information on chronic cough and hoarseness, with only five studies covering symptom analysis of chronic cough and three articles covering symptom analysis of hoarseness, so it was not possible to explore the extent to which these pharyngeal symptoms were associated with HGMUE through subgroup analysis. (5) Analyses were based on combined data provided in published articles, and individual data were not available, thus limiting more detailed analyses.

In conclusion, the results of this meta-analysis indicated a clear correlation between pharyngolaryngeal symptoms and HGMUE. After ruling out GERD, HGMUE needs to be considered in patients with pharyngolaryngeal symptoms, and attention should be focused on the esophageal inlet during gastroscopy. High-quality prospective case–control studies are still needed to accurately determine the correlation between pharyngolaryngeal symptoms and HGMUE, and whether HGMUE is similar to Barrett’s esophagus and related to GERD.
