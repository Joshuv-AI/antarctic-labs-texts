Accuracy, readability, and content coverage of AI-generated responses to questions on functional appliances
Authors: Serene A Badran, Snigdha Pattanaik, Sarah Jumaah, Abdulrahman Salmeh, Meena Al-Saadi, Taiba Al-Mizban, Nadia Al-Zaidi, Abdullah Hanifa, Mahmoud K AL-Omiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients increasingly rely on Large Language Models (LLMs) for health information, yet the accuracy and readability of AI-generated dental advice remain variable across different clinical domains and Artificial Intelligence (AI) models. This study therefore aimed to compare the readability, accuracy, and comprehensiveness of responses generated by four leading AI models (ChatGPT-4o Mini, ChatGPT-5, Google Gemini 2.5 Flash, and DeepSeek V3) to patient questions on functional appliances.MethodsThirty-eight frequently asked questions were identified using a structured Google search and categorized into three domains: “treatment fundamentals and general information,” “lifestyle and practical concerns,” and “appointments and long-term results”. Each question was independently answered by the four AI models. Readability was assessed using the Flesch–Kincaid tools. Accuracy and comprehensiveness were independently rated by two blinded orthodontists.ResultsAI-generated responses were generally accurate and comprehensive but difficult to read, requiring college-level literacy. ChatGPT-5 produced the lowest readability scores (most difficult-to-read responses; P < .001). Although Gemini 2.5 Flash achieved the highest comprehensiveness scores across all three domains, these differences were not statistically significant. Treatment-related questions yielded lower readability scores than lifestyle-related queries across all models (P < .001). No single model demonstrated superior performance across all evaluated domains.ConclusionAI-generated information on functional appliances was generally accurate and comprehensive but often exceeded recommended patient literacy thresholds. Readability must be considered alongside informational quality when deploying AI tools for patient education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08616-9.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Large Language Models, Health Literacy, Orthodontics, Functional Appliances, ChatGPT, Gemini, Accuracy, Readability

Background

The internet has become a primary source of medical information, with many patients seeking health-related information online before consulting a healthcare practitioner [1]. The emergence of large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek has further transformed access to medical and dental information [2]. Patients often use these models for quick conversational responses to health-related queries [3].

However, concerns regarding the accuracy and reliability of AI-generated content persist. LLMs are trained on large datasets containing both reputable medical literature and misleading public content [4] AI responses may include inaccurate information [2] and fail to comply with the standards of evidence-based health information [5], leading to anxiety, delayed professional consultation, or inappropriate self-management [6]. Even accurate information may be misunderstood if presented at an unsuitable readability level [7]. Ensuring AI output is both accurate and comprehensible is therefore critical [8].

AI models differ in design, training, and reasoning, leading to variability in output quality [9]. ChatGPT (OpenAI, San Francisco, California, USA) is known for fluency and context preservation [10]; DeepSeek V3 (DeepSeek, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) is notable for its open-weight/open-access design enabling on-site deployment on private servers for safer handling of sensitive patient data [10, 11]; and Google Gemini 2.5 Flash (Google DeepMind, London, UK) is a multimodal model optimized for rapid and resource-efficient responses [10].

Previous studies have assessed AI-generated dental information on various topics [5, 12, 13], but none have focused on functional appliances. Moreover, these studies have mostly concentrated on individual AI models, especially ChatGPT, assessing one or more outcome domains [2, 14]. Other studies have compared different versions of ChatGPT or benchmarked AI outputs against traditional search engines such as Google [15, 16]. The performance of AI models in answering patient queries has been shown to be variable depending on the LLM used [9, 14]. Moreover, a recent systematic review investigating the reliability of AI-generated information in dentistry has shown substantial variability in results depending on the investigated specialty [14]. This heterogeneity underscores the need to evaluate AI performance in under-researched clinical domains, rather than assuming generalizable performance of AI across all clinical areas.

One such unexplored specialized domain is orthodontic functional appliances, which are a diverse family of devices that harness natural oral and perioral soft tissues and musculature to guide jaw growth and correct malocclusions [17]. Despite their well-established efficacy, functional appliances may be perceived as unfamiliar and challenging by some patients and their parents due to associated eating difficulties, jaw pain, and psychological strain during the initial adaptation period [18–20]. Moreover, the success of functional appliance therapy is uniquely dependent on patients’ specific developmental age, compliance, and tolerance of physical discomfort. As such, the clarity and accuracy of patient-facing information are clinical prerequisites for successful treatment. Given this combination of clinical complexity, patient unfamiliarity, and adaptation difficulties, many families may turn to AI-powered chatbots for guidance, making an objective evaluation of AI output in this specific domain essential.

No study to date has evaluated the performance of AI in answering functional-appliance-related queries. Therefore, the aim of this study was to conduct a comparative evaluation of four leading LLMs (ChatGPT-4o Mini, ChatGPT-5, Google Gemini 2.5 Flash and DeepSeek V3) regarding accuracy, readability, and comprehensiveness of answers to questions on orthodontic functional appliances.

Methods

The study involved three stages (Fig. 1). A structured Google search was conducted on April 19, 2025, using Google Chrome browser with default settings to identify frequently asked questions (FAQs) about orthodontic functional appliances. The pre-defined search terms used were “orthodontic functional appliances FAQs” and “what are functional appliances in orthodontics”. The first three pages of the results were reviewed, as most users rarely exceed this range [2]. Websites with relevant and accessible FAQs were searched, resulting in a total of 222 questions retrieved from 29 websites. After removing promotional (n = 14), duplicate (n = 112), and irrelevant (n = 58) entries, 38 questions remained for analysis.

Study workflow

The 38 questions were categorized into three thematic domains for subgroup analysis: (1) Treatment fundamentals and general information, which included questions related to the purpose, process, and types of functional appliances (n = 16); (2) Lifestyle and practical concerns, covering practical daily management (eating, cleaning, and sports participation) and personal experiences (discomfort and speech adaptation) (n = 14); and (3) Appointments and long-term results, focusing on clinical follow-up, handling appliance-related issues, and what happens after the active treatment phase (n = 8). This categorization was performed independently by two authors, and inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, which demonstrated strong agreement (k = 0.88). Any initial discrepancies were resolved through group discussion to reach a final consensus. This classification enabled a detailed assessment of AI performance across specific thematic domains. The complete list of categorized questions is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

The following large language models (LLMs) were evaluated: ChatGPT-4o Mini and ChatGPT-5 (OpenAI), Gemini 2.5 Flash (Google), and DeepSeek V3 (DeepSeek). To ensure transparency, specific versions and chronological data were recorded: ChatGPT-4o Mini (released July 18, 2024) and DeepSeek V3 (released January 20, 2025) were queried on April 25, 2025. Meanwhile, Gemini 2.5 Flash (released June 17, 2025) and ChatGPT-5 (released August 7, 2025) were both queried on August 16, 2025.

Each question was entered verbatim to each AI model via their official web interface using the same network and device. Standard default LLM settings were maintained for all interactions to simulate a typical patient experience. Responses were generated using zero-shot prompting. To minimize the influence of browser history, conversational context, and algorithmic bias, each question was submitted individually within a new chat session and separate incognito browser tab (Google Chrome). Moreover, the task of question entry was shared equally between two authors to reduce operator fatigue and ensure procedural consistency.

To control assessor bias, responses were anonymized, randomized, and compiled for blinded assessment.

Accuracy and comprehensiveness

The Accuracy of Information (AOI) Index [21] comprises five domains: Factual accuracy, Corroboration, Consistency, Clarity and Specificity, and Relevance. Each item was scored from 0 (poor) to 2 (excellent), yielding a maximum total score of 10.

The information completeness and comprehensiveness of each AI response was evaluated on a 10-point scale, assessing whether each response adequately addressed all relevant aspects expected for the given question. A higher score would depict a more comprehensive response.

Both the accuracy (AOI) and the completeness/comprehensiveness of information were scored independently by two blinded orthodontists, and the average score was used for subsequent analysis.

Readability

Readability of the AI-generated responses was analyzed by two blinded assessors using the Flesch-Kincaid Reading Ease and Grade Level formulas via an online tool (https://charactercalculator.com/flesch-reading-ease/). Reading Ease scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores depicting easier readability. Grade Level indicates the average years of education required to understand the text, with lower scores denoting easier readability. Scores were categorized and interpreted following established Flesch–Kincaid thresholds (Table 1).

Intra-examiner and inter-examiner reliability

For inter-examiner reliability, both assessors scored the same 7 randomly selected responses. For intra-examiner reliability, each assessor re-scored these responses after a two-week interval. Reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. Normally distributed continuous data (FK Reading Ease scores) were reported as means, standard deviations, and 95% confidence intervals, while non-normally distributed and scale data (FK Grade Level, AOI Index, and comprehensiveness/completeness scores) were summarized as medians, interquartile ranges, and minimum–maximum values. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics v20.0, with significance set at a two-tailed α of 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals.

A mixed design factorial ANOVA (general linear model) was used to assess variations in FK Reading Ease scores of different AI models by question category, including interaction effects, with paired samples t-tests and Bonferroni post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. The assumptions for mixed design ANOVA were confirmed, including the FK Reading Ease score being a continuous and normally distributed variable as checked via Shapiro-Wilk (statistic = 0.962 and P = .214), skewness test (z score of skewness below 1.96) and Q-Q plots. Also, homogeneity of variance of the FK Reading Ease score across the between subjects factor (question category) was confirmed (Levene’s statistic = 1.229, P= .295). Furthermore, the equality of error variances across the repeated measures of the dependent variable (FK Reading Ease scores) was confirmed (Levene’s statistic = 1.518 (P= .233), 1.521 (P= .232), 0.648 (P= .529), and 1.028 (P= .368) for ChatGPT-4o Mini, DeepSeek V3, ChatGPT-5, and Google Gemini 2.5 Flash; respectively). In addition, the homogeneity of covariance matrices across all groups was confirmed (Box M statistic = 21.774, P= .618). Finally, sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test (Mauchly’s W statistic = 0.521, P= .001), and Greenhouse-Geisser correction was used to adjust for degrees of freedom (Epsilon = 0.681).

Friedman’s test was used to analyze group differences in AOI and comprehensiveness/completeness scores by AI model type. Wilcoxon-Signed ranks tests were then used for pairwise comparisons. Kruskal-Wallis test was used to analyze group differences in AOI and comprehensiveness/completeness scores by question category. Multivariate analysis for AOI and comprehensiveness/completeness scores was not applicable due to non-normal data distribution. Also, the multiple-comparison correction was not calculated for the non-parametric pairwise tests because all these tests were not significant and the P values for these tests were above 0.05. Associations among FK Reading Ease, AOI, and comprehensiveness/completeness scores were tested using Spearman’s rho correlation.

Effect sizes were calculated using η² for ANOVA test, Kendall’s W for Friedman tests, Cohen’s d for Bonferroni post hoc comparisons, and r for Wilcoxon-Signed ranks tests. The Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for the multiple-comparisons for the non-parametric and parametric pairwise tests.

A power calculation utilizing a post hoc power analysis via G*Power computer software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine University) was conducted. Repeated measures within-between interactions ANOVA approach with 4 measurements and 3 groups was utilized in this regard. The calculated power was 1.000 considering an effect size of 0.23, an alpha ≤ 0.05, and a sample size of 38 questions (152 observations).

Results

Internal consistency reliability

The internal consistency reliability of the AOI index, the Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.719; indicating adequate internal consistency reliability of the used AOI index for this study.

Inter- and intra-evaluator reliability

Inter- and intra-evaluator reliability for the AOI index and comprehensiveness/completeness scores were assessed using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC). Inter-evaluator ICC values were 0.754 for AOI scores and 0.822 for comprehensiveness scores, indicating good reliability and agreement for these investigations. Intra-evaluator reliability was similarly high, with ICC values of 0.856 for AOI scores and 0.852 for comprehensiveness scores, reflecting good consistency of repeated assessments.

Readability of generated responses

ChatGPT-4o Mini had the highest mean (i.e. easiest to read responses) FK Reading Ease scores (42.01 ± 10.74), followed by DeepSeek V3 (40.58 ± 8.82), Gemini 2.5 Flash (37.24 ± 11.13), and ChatGPT-5 (26.13 ± 13.91) in descending order (Table 2). The lifestyle and practical concerns category was associated with the highest mean FK Reading Ease scores (45.57 ± 10.65), followed by the appointments and long-term results category (43.49 ± 13.59), then the treatment fundamentals and general information category within each AI model (38.15 ± 8.45), except for ChatGPT-5 where the appointments and long-term results category was associated with the highest mean (30.61 ± 16.64) of FK Reading Ease scores (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Accuracy and completeness of generated responses

Table 6 presents the differences in AOI and comprehensiveness scores between different AI models. No significant differences in AOI scores were observed between different AI models (P = .349; Table 6; Fig. 3). Meanwhile, significant differences in comprehensiveness scores were found between AI models in general (P = .037, Table 6; Fig. 4). However, paired comparisons showed no significant differences in comprehensiveness scores between any two AI models (P > .05, Table 6). Furthermore, no significant differences in AOI (Kruskal Wallis test: X2 (2) = 5.373, P = .068) and comprehensiveness (X2 (2) = 1.608, P = .448) scores were found between different categories of questions.

Discussion

Laypeople now have many on-demand options for medical information, with ChatGPT being the most popular web-based tool for patients and health care professionals [22]. The present study confirmed that patients consulting AI for orthodontic functional-appliance queries are likely to receive information that is generally accurate and comprehensive, yet difficult to read, with variation depending on the model.

This disconnect between informational quality and readability is a key concern for patient education, especially when compared against literacy benchmarks. Average reading levels in North America are estimated at grade 8 or lower, with leading organizations recommending that patient education materials do not exceed grade 5–8 readability thresholds [23, 24]. Accordingly, difficult-to-read health information, even when scientifically accurate, may be misinterpreted by laypeople [6, 7].

Most AI models generally required college-level literacy, with ChatGPT-5 producing the most difficult-to-read responses. Although ChatGPT-5’s readability has not been previously investigated specifically in orthodontics, these findings align with observations reported for other AI models in the field [2, 4, 8, 25]. The discrepancy between models, specifically ChatGPT-5’s output (professional-level) versus ChatGPT-4o Mini’s (10th-grade to college-level) likely stems from differences in training data and model architecture. ChatGPT-5’s more recent, larger training corpus may be more heavily weighted toward highly technical academic and medical literature. Furthermore, its enhanced reasoning capabilities likely contribute to more intricate grammatical structures, longer sentences, and greater conceptual density. In contrast, ChatGPT-4o Mini appears more optimized for user-centric, everyday communication.

Readability also varied by question category. Treatment-related queries triggered more complex, professional-level language compared to the more accessible lay-oriented responses for lifestyle questions. This suggests that AI models may implicitly adapt their linguistic register based on the perceived technicality of the prompt, framing for healthcare professionals when prompted with technical questions about functional appliances.

Prompt engineering offers a promising strategy to mitigate these readability challenges. A recent study found that prompting ChatGPT with plain-language conversational questions, rather than keyword-only prompts, significantly improved readability [26]. Nevertheless, the resulting readability scores remained above recommended readability thresholds. Interestingly, a study evaluating prompt engineering with ChatGPT-3.5 and GPT-4 found that specifying reading level (8th-grade) and word limit (300 words) significantly improved readability, although at the expense of reduced accuracy and comprehensiveness [24]; highlighting an inherent trade-off that must be carefully managed.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess these specific model versions as patient information tools with respect to both accuracy and comprehensiveness of information. Our findings revealed that while Google Gemini 2.5 Flash and ChatGPT-5 showed slight trends toward higher comprehensiveness and accuracy, respectively, although these differences were not statistically significant after correcting for multiple comparisons. This suggests a convergence in performance among the latest generation of high-performing LLMs, corroborating previous research that reported comparable accuracy between ChatGPT and Gemini [8, 27, 28].

However, it is worth noting that while we found no statistically significant differences in comprehensiveness, previous reports have highlighted a tendency for Gemini to include additional elements such as physician recommendations, references, and graphical aids [28]. While such features were present in our sample, the overall depth of clinical information appears to have reached a similar threshold across all four models. A recent systematic review highlighted substantial variability in AI-generated responses depending on the AI-model, dental subtopic, and evaluation criteria [14]. Moreover, ChatGPT-4 exhibited better performance in knowledge-based orthodontic tasks than the compared models [14]. Within the present study, accuracy levels were found to be higher than those reported in the systematic review. These differences may be attributed to the use of standardized and blinded scoring by expert orthodontists, differences in the investigated models and model versions, and potential improvements in model performance over time compared with earlier studies included in the review. Moreover, our study focused on one clinical domain (functional appliances), reinforcing that AI performance is both model-dependent and domain-sensitive.

This study’s primary strength and novelty lie in addressing a critical “information gap” within the previously underexplored domain of orthodontic functional appliances; a field characterized by significant patient unfamiliarity and compliance challenges. Moreover, this study distinguishes itself by directly comparing four distinct leading LLMs, whereas previous dental AI studies have typically been limited to fewer models. By benchmarking the latest 2025/2026 generation of LLMs, the study provides a more comprehensive and current assessment of AI capabilities than earlier reports. The methodological rigor is further distinguished by the use of a standardized AOI index, blinded expert scoring, objective readability metrics, and thematic stratification, offering a more nuanced understanding of AI utility across different health-related question domains.

Nonetheless, limitations exist; expert scoring may retain an element of subjectivity, despite calibration and blinding. Furthermore, the study employed zero-shot prompting, which may not reflect real-world iterative use, wherein patients often engage in further questioning or clarification. Additionally, the use of English-only queries may limit the generalizability of these findings to non-English speaking populations or other linguistic contexts. Moreover, while the Flesch-Kincaid readability metrics provide a standardized and objective measure of linguistic complexity, they are limited by their focus on sentence and syllable length, failing to account for semantic clarity, medical jargon, or cultural factors that influence actual patient comprehension [29]. Lastly, the AOI index demonstrated a pronounced ceiling effect, with median scores clustered between 9.5 and 10 across all four AI models. While the index has proven useful in previous studies for detecting frank inaccuracies [21], its discriminatory capacity diminishes substantially when evaluating high-performing models that consistently produce clinically acceptable content. This ceiling effect has direct implications for interpreting our findings of no statistically significant differences in accuracy between models. Specifically, the absence of significant differences cannot be interpreted as evidence of equivalent performance; rather, it may reflect the instrument’s inability to detect subtle but potentially meaningful variations in accuracy among state-of-the-art LLMs. These variations might include nuanced differences in how models handle clinical uncertainty, or the precision of numerical claims (e.g., treatment duration ranges). Future studies should consider employing more sensitive instruments, such as domain-specific scoring rubrics with finer gradations (e.g., 5- or 7-point Likert scales), error categorization schemes that distinguish between minor imprecisions and clinically dangerous misinformation, or continuous outcome measures such as expert-rated deviation from evidence-based standards. Until such instruments are validated, researchers should interpret non-significant accuracy differences among high-performing LLMs with appropriate caution, recognizing that ceiling-constrained comparisons may lack the statistical power to detect clinically relevant distinctions.

Our findings carry multiple practical implications for the clinician’s evolving role in the digital age. As AI emerges as a significant adjunct for healthcare communication, clinicians may find themselves evolving from being the primary providers of information to acting as information facilitators who actively vet, interpret, and bridge the gap between AI-generated content, patient understanding, and clinical reality. This shift is driven by two factors. Firstly, while general-purpose LLMs serve as valuable educational adjuncts, they are not yet equipped to serve as substitutes for human clinicians. Recent research demonstrates that human practitioners remain significantly more accurate than chatbots in clinical and diagnostic decision-making, with specialists outperforming AI by a substantial margin [30]. Secondly, even when AI models are sufficiently accurate, the content often remains linguistically inaccessible; making a clinician’s guidance essential to ensure that accurate data translates into patient understanding [7].

In clinical practice, simply warning patients about AI limitations may no longer be sufficient. Safe integration requires improving prompting literacy among both clinicians and patients to ensure digital interactions are productive. For example, practitioners can proactively manage this by providing simple instructions for patients to append their queries, such as requesting an ‘8th grade reading level’ [24].

Ultimately, a promising solution may lie in the development of dedicated dental AI systems specifically engineered for patient communication. Developers and healthcare regulators must prioritize readability tuning and design dedicated ‘patient-mode’ output options within these platforms. This is particularly crucial given the notable communicative shortcomings of current models; specifically regarding linguistic accessibility, empathetic engagement, and citation accuracy [31]. Hardcoding parameters for appropriate reading levels and empathetic tone, alongside a training corpus restricted to trusted, evidence-based medical and dental sources, could provide a secure, reliable digital adjunct that enhances patient health literacy without compromising clinical safety.
