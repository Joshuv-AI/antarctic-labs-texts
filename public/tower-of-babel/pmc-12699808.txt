Multilevel analysis of improved water utilization and associated factors in Yemen: using 2013 DHS Yemen national dataset
Authors: Lamrot Yohannes, Mengesha Genet, Sisay Deme, Asmare Asrat Yirdaw, Mequannent Sharew Melaku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccess to clean drinking water is essential for public health and quality of life. Yemen faces a severe water crisis driven by scarcity, groundwater overexploitation, and socio-political instability. This study analyzed data from the 2013 Yemen Demographic and Health Survey (17,333 weighted samples from 8,794 households) to identify household and community factors influencing access to improved water sources. A multilevel logistic regression was applied to identify factors associated with the use of improved drinking water, considering variables with p-values < 0.05 as statistically significant. The results show that about 58.8% of households had access to improved water sources. Media exposure, larger household size, fewer under-five children, shorter water collection time, higher wealth status, urban residence, and lower community poverty levels were associated with higher odds of access to improved water. Regional disparities were observed across governorates. To enhance access to improved water sources, targeted interventions should prioritize infrastructure development, poverty reduction, and public awareness campaigns tailored to the specific needs of each community.Keywords: Determinants, Improved drinking water, DHS, Yemen

Background

Access to safe and reliable drinking water is a fundamental determinant of human health, productivity, and well-being, and it is central to achieving Sustainable Development Goal 6 (SDG6). The WHO/UNICEF joint monitoring program (JMP) reports that, although global coverage of improved drinking-water sources has expanded since 2000, large inequalities persist, and millions remain without basic access [1]. Unsafe or unimproved water sources contribute substantially to the global burden of disease, particularly diarrheal illnesses, child undernutrition, and preventable deaths, underscoring the urgency of ensuring equitable and sustainable water supply [2, 3]. In 2019, inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) services contributed to an estimated 2.5% of global deaths and 2.9% of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [4], with unsafe sanitation alone causing around 564,000 deaths primarily from diarrheal disease [5]. While an estimated 89% [6] of the world’s population has access to “improved” water sources, sustained and equitable access to safe water remains a major challenge, especially in low- and middle-income countries where water quality is a pressing public health concern [6–8].

Yemen is one of the most water-insecure countries in the world. At least half of the Yemeni population faces significant water quality challenges, and just 45% have access to essential hygiene products such as soap, resulting in a high burden of waterborne diseases and recurrent diarrheal outbreaks. The country has experienced the world’s largest cholera outbreak, with over 2.5 million suspected cases and 4,000 reported deaths between 2016 and 2022 [9, 10]. Long-standing structural water scarcity, rapid aquifer depletion, population growth, political instability, environmental stressors, and weak water governance have historically constrained service coverage [11–18]. Since 2015, protracted conflict and economic collapse have further devastated infrastructure, disrupted supply chains, and increased dependence on costly and unreliable water vendors [19–21]. These pressures have widened urban–rural disparities and deepened inequalities in access to improved water sources, leaving vulnerable households disproportionately reliant on unsafe alternatives [22]. In addition, the compounded effect of conflict and resource mismanagement has also disrupted agricultural activity, worsening both food and water security [23, 24]. Efforts to improve water supply and sanitation through emergency WASH interventions, rehabilitation projects, and innovative technologies such as solar-powered water systems and rainwater harvesting have been reported. However, gaps remain in understanding localized patterns of water access disparities and the interplay of household-level factors [25–27]. Household access to improved sources of drinking water varies depending on several factors, including the household’s residence, family size, household wealth index, and the attributes of the head of the household, including age, gender, education level, marital status, and employment status [28–31].

The 2013 Yemen National Health and Demographic Survey (YNHDS) provides the most recent nationally representative baseline of household access to water and sanitation prior to the escalation of conflict. According to the survey, just fewer than 60% [32] of households used an improved drinking-water source, with marked disparities across urban–rural residence, wealth, and geographic regions. While previous studies have documented general inequalities in water access in other low- and middle-income countries, including Yemen, there is limited evidence that explicitly identified household-and-community-level determinants of improved water utilization. Most prior work relies on descriptive statistics or single-level regression, which may mask the influence of contextual and structural factors. Our study addresses this gap by applying a multilevel multivariable logistic regression model. DHS data are hierarchically structured, with households nested within primary sampling units and governorates. Multilevel analysis allows for the quantification of both household-level factors (such as wealth, education, and household head) and contextual factors (including regional infrastructure, urbanization, and community-level water access) [33–35]. Moreover, by estimating the importance of each feature and visualizing their relative influence, this study offers policymakers actionable insights to better prioritize interventions. It also provides a methodological advancement over previous studies and establishes a nationally representative baseline for evaluating the compounded effects of Yemen’s ongoing crisis on water security.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and determinants of improved drinking-water source utilization in Yemen using the 2013 DHS dataset, applying a multilevel modeling approach. Specifically, this study assessed the level of improved water utilization at the national level and across different population subgroups, identified household-level factors influencing water utilization, and examined community-level variations to understand the broader contextual factors affecting water access. Findings from this study can inform equity-focused water policy, intervention, and program design in Yemen. In addition, the findings can help identify the most affected areas and guide efforts to ensure equitable access to safe drinking water across the country.

Methods and materials

Study design and area

The Republic of Yemen is located in the southwest corner of the Arabian Peninsula, a large landmass attached in the north to western Asia and surrounded by water on the other three sides, bordering the Arabian Sea, Gulf of Aden, and Red Sea, between Oman and Saudi Arabia. Yemen is one of the poorest countries in the Middle East and North Africa region. The survey is nationally representative, conducted every five years in each country, and provides comprehensive data on health as well as related characteristics such as population and nutrition. Its samples are usually selected based on a stratified two-stage cluster sampling technique. The country was divided into enumeration areas, or clusters, or communities. Stratification is achieved by separating each cluster into urban and rural areas. Thereafter a sample of households was selected from the chosen urban and rural enumeration areas. The DHS has datasets for different populations, such as men, women, children, births, and households. The sample collection consisted of 213 clusters from urban areas and 587 clusters from rural areas, with a total of 800 clusters, although 781 clusters were used for this study after removing ten unlisted households and nine additional clusters that had not been visited for security reasons. A comprehensive household listing was conducted within each cluster and used to select the households for the study in the second stage between November 10 and November 30, 2012. A sample of 24 households and 25 households was selected from each rural and urban cluster, respectively. This process was projected to yield a national sample of 17,351 households that had been successfully interviewed. After removing missing values, this study used a total of 17,333 weighted samples of households. Yemen has 22 administrative divisions called governorates (muhafazat). Furthermore, Yemen is divided into six zones based on political proximity. These governorates are further subdivided into 333 districts (muderiah), which are broken down into 1,996 sub-districts (Fig. 1).

Study area map of Yemen

Data sources

YDHS 2013 was accessed through authorized registration. The data was accessed from the DHS website (https://dhsprogram.com/data/dataset_admin/index.cfm) following an official online request and authorized access. The survey collects data regarding significant health indicators and health-related events, like having access to improved sources of drinking water. The data was downloaded from the DHS website with authorized access, which offers various datasets, including the Household Record (HR), Individual Record (IR), Child Record (KR), Men’s Record (MR), Personal Record (PR), and other country-specific datasets. For the purposes of this study, the Household Record (HR) dataset was used.

Sample size and sampling procedure

Participants in the study were chosen using a two-stage stratified cluster sampling procedure. Based on Yemen’s 2013 population and housing census, enumeration regions and clusters were chosen as the sampling units in the first stage, while homes were chosen as the units in the second. Additionally, stratification by rural and urban areas was employed to guarantee the sample’s representativeness. The Household Record (HR) dataset was used for this investigation, and it was cleaned to make sure it was appropriately prepared for the planned research. This entailed eliminating any missing values and eliminating families that were not interviewed, those that were not listed in the original plan, and those that were not visited because of security concerns. Finally, a total of 17,333 households were selected and included in this study.

Variables of the study

Outcome variables

Household access to improved sources of drinking water.

Explanatory variables

For this study, age, gender, marital status, educational level of the household head, household size, time to get water, wealth index, number of under-five children, tobacco smoking, chat chewing, health problem, working status, location of water source, media exposure, and residence. Community-level factors such as poverty, education, governorate, and community-level media exposure were included.

Data management and analysis

Data extraction, cleaning, recoding, and statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 14, utilizing the “Household Record (HR)” dataset from the 2013 YDHS. To ensure nationally representative estimates and correct for potential bias due to under-reporting or over-reporting, sample weights (v005/1,000,000) were applied throughout the analyses. Descriptive statistics were generated and presented as frequencies, percentages, text, tables, and figures to provide an in-depth overview of the sample characteristics and key variables. Both bivariable and multivariable multilevel logistic regression analyses were employed. In the bivariable analyses, individual-level predictors with p-values < 0.2 were selected for inclusion in the multivariable multilevel logistic regression. This step ensures that potentially important variables are retained for further evaluation while controlling for confounders. Statistical significance in the final multivariable model was determined using adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and a p-value < 0.05.

Model-building process

In this study, a multilevel logistic regression modeling approach was employed to analyze factors associated with access to improved drinking water sources, accounting for the hierarchical structure of the 2013 Yemen DHS data where households are nested within communities (clusters). This method is robust because it appropriately manages data clustering and captures variation at multiple levels (individual and community), thereby reducing bias and improving the precision of estimates compared to traditional single-level models [37].

Four sequential models were constructed to assess the influence of individual and community-level factors:

Null Model (Empty Model): Included no predictors and estimated the baseline variation in improved water access attributable to clustering at the community level. This model helped quantify the extent of between-cluster variation and served as a reference for subsequent models.

Each model was fit separately using maximum likelihood estimation, and the stepwise building enabled understanding of variance explained at different levels.

Model selection and fit

Model fit and parsimony were evaluated using multiple complementary criteria such as Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Log-Likelihood Ratio (LLR), Deviance (−2LL), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The model exhibiting the lowest values across these metrics was selected as the final model for interpretation, balancing fit and complexity.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted as a secondary data analysis using publicly available datasets from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) program. Because the data are publicly accessible, formal ethical approval and participant consent were not required for this analysis. Prior to data access, the principal authors registered the research topic and its significance with the MEASURE DHS Program and submitted a formal data access request through their website. Permission to download and utilize the dataset was subsequently granted by MEASURE DHS, ensuring compliance with their data use policies and ethical standards.

Determinants of improved water source utilization

In the multilevel multivariable logistic regression analysis, households with media exposure were 1.18 times more likely to access improved water sources (AOR 1.18; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.38) compared to households without media exposure. Families with 1 to 3 members had a 1.72 times higher likelihood of accessing improved drinking water (AOR 1.72; 95% CI: 1.47, 2.06) compared to households that have 7 or more members. Households that spent 30 min or less to collect water were 3.77 times more likely to use improved sources (AOR 3.77; 95% CI: 2.77, 5.13) compared to households that accessed water upon permission. Residents in urban areas were 3.38 times (AOR 3.38; 95% CI: 1.86, 6.14) more likely to access improved drinking water compared to those in rural settings. Households categorized as middle and rich were 1.77 times (AOR 1.77; 95% CI: 1.48, 2.11) and 1.80 times (AOR 1.8; 95% CI: 1.41, 2.30) greater than that of poor households, respectively. Communities with lower poverty levels were found to be 10.29 times (AOR 10.29; 95% CI: 5.06, 20.95) more likely to access improved drinking water than their counterparts. Households that have no or three under-five children in the household were more likely to access improved drinking water, with odds of 1.68 times (AOR 1.68; 95% CI: 1.20, 2.34) and 1.59 times (AOR 1.59; 95% CI: 1.15, 2.19) compared to households with four or more children. The governorate zones Janad, Tahama, and Aden were 3.25 (AOR 3.25; 95% CI: 1.40, 7.56), 3.17 (AOR 3.17; 95% CI: 1.33, 7.55), and 14.23 (AOR 14.23; 95% CI: 5.04, 40.21) times more likely to access improved drinking water compared to the Saba zone (Table 4) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study was aimed at investigating the determinants of improved water utilization in Yemen. About 58.82% of households in Yemen use improved water sources for drinking purposes, which indicates that a significant segment of the population could not have improved water sources for drinking, although lack of water is an underlying cause of many diseases related to poor sanitation. Yemen’s access to improved water sources is notably lower than the global average and several Middle Eastern and North African countries; for instance, in 2022, nations like Bahrain, Israel, Kuwait, and Qatar reported nearly universal access to safely managed drinking water [39, 40]. On the other hand, the finding of this study is higher than the findings from Sub-Saharan Africa [41]. This disparity can be attributed to several factors. Lower-income countries often lack the financial resources to invest in necessary water infrastructure, leading to reduced access to clean water, and regions with arid climates or challenging terrains may find it difficult to source and distribute water effectively. In addition, countries experiencing conflict or political unrest may see their water infrastructure damaged or neglected, further hindering access. Moreover, urban areas typically have better access to improved water sources compared to rural regions, where infrastructure development is often lagging [42, 43].

This study identified media exposure, number of household members, time to get water, wealth index, governorate, under-five children, residence, and community poverty as significant variables to predict improved water source use among households in Yemen.

Economic status is a well-established determinant of access to improved water sources. This study’s finding shows that middle- and rich-class households in Yemen were more likely to use improved water sources. In Yemen, particularly amid economic instability, households with fewer resources are more dependent on unsafe water sources. The finding is supported by previous findings in Ethiopia [44–48], Ghana [49, 50], Vietnam [51], Indonesia [52], and Zambia [53]. There is well-established evidence that shows wealth disparities significantly affecting water source usage, in which households with a middle and rich wealth index have the ability to install a water source in their household and afford transportation costs for access to improved sources away from their households.

Similarly, this study’s finding that revealed smaller households were more likely to use improved water sources aligns with global evidence. Research in Ethiopia found that households with fewer members were more capable of managing their water needs, while larger households often struggled to secure enough water, leading to reliance on unimproved sources [48, 54], and Cambodia [55]. In Nigeria, large family size has similarly been linked to reduced access to improved water, as the burden of collecting and managing water for many members becomes more challenging [56]. The logistical difficulties faced by larger families, particularly in rural Yemen, exacerbate their vulnerability to water scarcity and unsafe sources. As the number of family members increases, total household income and wealth may decrease. This eventually declines the household’s ability to afford an improved drinking water source. Contrary to this finding, findings from India revealed that households with bigger family sizes were more likely to have improved water access [42]. This could be explained as households with bigger families will have increased opportunities to fetch water, whether from nearby or far-flung water sources.

Time spent fetching water is a critical predictor of access to improved drinking water sources. This study’s finding that households within a 30-minute range were more likely to use improved water sources is supported by studies from Sub-Saharan countries, where proximity to water significantly increased the likelihood of using safer sources [57]. In addition, the finding of this study is supported by studies from Nigeria [58] and India [59]. In Yemen’s rural regions, where water scarcity and infrastructure challenges are severe, the time taken to collect water remains a critical barrier.

The urban-rural divide in water access is a global issue, consistent with the findings of this study in Yemen. Urban households are more likely to use improved drinking water compared to their counterparts. Research from sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia consistently shows that urban areas have better access to improved water infrastructure, including piped water and sanitation services [60]. In addition, the finding of this study is supported by studies from Ethiopia [45] and Vietnam [51]; a study in Brazil and Ghana found that rural households were more reliant on unimproved water sources due to lack of infrastructure investment [61]. This suggests urban households are spatially closer to facilities or services, whereas rural households are spatially dispersed. Installing a private facility is expensive in rural areas, whereas these are provided by local government or municipal bodies in urban areas.

Exposure to media has been shown to significantly impact the use of improved water, sanitation, and hygiene practices. This study revealed that households with media exposure were more likely to use improved water sources. Media can play a critical role in bridging the information gap, especially in areas where formal education and services are disrupted. Studies indicate that households with access to various media forms are more likely to utilize improved water sources and engage in better hygiene practices [62]. Hence, those who have media exposure would be encouraged to use improved water sources since they might be informed about the potential health benefits of improved water source utilization.

Community poverty is the other significant variable influencing the accessibility of improved drinking water. Communities with lower poverty levels were highly likely to access improved drinking water sources. Communities with lower poverty levels typically have greater economic resources to invest in water infrastructure. In contrast, poorer communities often rely on less reliable sources. Research indicates that economic disparities significantly affect access to clean water, with wealthier communities having better infrastructure and maintenance capabilities [20, 63]. Lower poverty levels often correlate with better public services and infrastructure development. In contrast, financially constrained communities may struggle to maintain existing facilities, leading to deterioration and reduced access over time. In Yemen, where water scarcity is a major challenge, community-level poverty exacerbates the problem by limiting infrastructure investments, reducing affordability, and increasing reliance on unsafe water sources.

The presence of children under five years old in a household significantly influences access to improved water sources in Yemen. Household size is a significant determinant of water demand and access. Smaller households or those with fewer dependents (such as no children under five or only 1 to 3 children) may have more manageable water needs and financial flexibility to invest in improved water sources. Studies have shown that household size positively correlates with water demand; larger households often face greater resource constraints, which can limit their ability to access improved sources [64, 65]. Smaller households may also prioritize quality over quantity when securing water. Households with young children, especially those under five years old, are more likely to prioritize improved water sources due to heightened awareness of the health risks associated with unsafe drinking water. However, the presence of too many dependents might strain financial resources, reducing the ability to consistently access improved sources [64, 66].

Divided governorate zones in Yemen were an influential factor in the accessibility of improved drinking water. Janad, Tahama, and Aden were more likely to access improved water sources as compared to the other zones. They have benefited from relatively better infrastructure development compared to Saba, Azal, and Hadramawt. Improved water supply systems, such as piped networks and boreholes, are more prevalent in these zones. For instance, projects funded by international organizations have focused on enhancing water supply systems in urban areas like Aden, leading to improved access for residents [67]. Additionally, geographical characteristics of these governorates play a crucial role in water availability. Janad and Tahama benefit from better rainfall patterns and groundwater recharge compared to the arid conditions found in Saba and Hadramawt. Moreover, the ongoing conflict in Yemen has disproportionately affected certain areas more than others. Zones like Saba and Hadramawt may experience greater instability or disruption of services due to conflict dynamics, leading to deteriorating infrastructure for water supply [68]. In contrast, areas that have maintained some level of stability may continue to improve their access to drinking water [21].

Limitations and strengths of the study

Despite the wide nature and high sample size of this study, the survey was conducted in 2013, which is relatively old. On the other hand, the study’s use of a nationally representative DHS dataset will improve the study’s generalizability.

Conclusion

In Yemen, almost seven out of ten households have improved water source access. This implies that a significant percentage of Yemen’s population had no improved water access; despite the World Health Declaration, everyone could have access to improved drinking water. Although there is a continued hot conflict in Yemen, the Ministry of Health, in collaboration with the government, should work to increase the access of improved drinking water for the citizens of Yemen. Through a comprehensive multilevel multivariable logistic regression analysis, media exposure, larger family size (seven or more members), the presence of no or three under-five children in the household, shorter water collection time (≤ 30 min), higher wealth index (middle and rich), urban residence, lower community poverty levels, and regional variations across governorates increase the likelihood of access to improved drinking water. To enhance access to improved drinking water in Yemen, the government and international organizations should invest in strengthening water infrastructure, particularly in rural areas. Targeted interventions are needed for vulnerable groups, including households with under-five children and those in poverty-stricken communities, to ensure equitable water access. Expanding media awareness campaigns can promote safe water practices and encourage investment in water treatment options. Addressing socioeconomic barriers through livelihood programs can improve household wealth and affordability of safe water. Additionally, strategic investments in rural water accessibility, such as boreholes and rainwater harvesting systems, are essential to bridge the urban-rural gap. Reducing water collection time by constructing nearby water points can significantly improve access, especially for women and children. Furthermore, integrating water accessibility programs into broader health, sanitation, and economic development strategies through multi-sectorial collaboration will enhance the overall impact.
