Interpretable machine learning with multimodal hearing data for diagnosing endolymphatic hydrops
Authors: Xu Liu, Qin Sun, Suming Shi, Wenqian Gao, Wuqing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the diagnostic value of electrocochleography (ECochG) for identifying endolymphatic hydrops (EH) and to investigate whether integrating multimodal auditory data from ECochG and pure-tone audiometry (PTA) into machine learning models could improve diagnostic accuracy and clinical interpretability.MethodsA prospective cohort of 78 patients (156 ears) with a strong clinical suspicion of EH was evaluated between March and June 2024. ECochG and PTA examinations were performed, and extracted parameters were used to develop and validate multiple machine learning models. Diagnostic performance was compared using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy.ResultsAfter excluding six ears with unrecordable ECochG data, 150 ears were analyzed. Models based solely on ECochG-derived features demonstrated limited diagnostic performance (maximum AUC = 0.61). When multimodal hearing data were incorporated, model performance improved substantially. The Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) model achieved an AUC of 0.92 and an accuracy of 0.73 for predicting the anatomical distribution of EH.ConclusionsIntegrating multimodal auditory data into interpretable machine learning models can markedly improve the diagnostic accuracy of endolymphatic hydrops, providing an objective framework to support early clinical diagnosis and management. However, this study is limited by a small sample size and has not yet been validated across multiple centers.Level of evidenceLevel 3.

Introduction

Electrocochleography (ECochG) is an electrophysiological technique used to record responses from the cochlea and auditory nerve. By placing electrodes in the external auditory canal, on the tympanic membrane, or near the round window, ECochG captures cochlear microphonic potentials, compound action potentials (AP), and summating potentials (SP) elicited by acoustic stimulation [1]. As an objective and minimally invasive test, ECochG provides detailed information about cochlear and auditory nerve function and plays an important role in the diagnosis and evaluation of various otologic disorders.

Clinically, ECochG is primarily applied to measure the SP/AP ratio, which is often elevated in Ménière’s disease (MD) and considered indicative of endolymphatic hydrops (EH)—the major pathological feature of MD [2, 3]. Inner ear MRI remains the only technique capable of visualizing EH in vivo, while ECochG serves as a widely used functional correlative test in the diagnostic workup [4], its sensitivity varies considerably, and its diagnostic reliability for EH requires further investigation. Some studies have reported that ECochG can distinguish affected from unaffected ears in MD patients [5]. while others have suggested that it may predict individual susceptibility to MD, assist in diagnosing semicircular canal dehiscence, and monitor inner ear status during surgery [3].

Previous studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between the degree of cochlear hydrops, the level of hearing loss, and ECochG findings [6]. One study reported an overall ECochG abnormality rate of 76.9% in MD, with positivity rates of 72.1%, 73.3%, and 81.3% across disease durations of 0–5, 6–10, and over 11 years, respectively [7]. The SP/AP amplitude ratio shows an average positivity rate of about 60%, while the SP/AP area ratio may yield higher sensitivity [8]. However, ECochG’s diagnostic value appears to be more closely associated with disease duration and hearing thresholds than with the number of symptoms [9]. Some evidence indicates that cochlear EH may not correlate with the SP/AP ratio, whereas vestibular EH does, possibly due to disrupted ionic homeostasis in the inner ear rather than hydrops itself [10]. In most patients, whether surgically or conservatively treated, elevated SP or SP/AP ratios remain stable over time, suggesting that hair cell dysfunction caused by EH-related fluid imbalance or other cochlear disturbances—rather than basilar membrane displacement—is the primary mechanism [11].

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has shown remarkable potential in analyzing medical data, offering valuable insights for clinical decision-making [12, 13]. Machine learning (ML) applications have rapidly expanded to disease prediction, diagnosis, and treatment planning, particularly in oncology and neurological research [14]. Therefore, by integrating multimodal data from ECochG and pure-tone audiometry (PTA), ML-based diagnostic models may substantially enhance diagnostic accuracy and efficiency for EH. Accordingly, this study prospectively evaluated the diagnostic efficacy of ECochG for detecting endolymphatic hydrops and developed machine learning models integrating ECochG and PTA data to provide a more robust and precise diagnostic tool for Ménière’s disease.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patients who visited our department between March 1, 2024, and June 30, 2024, presenting with vertigo or sudden sensorineural hearing loss and showing a high clinical suspicion of endolymphatic hydrops were included. All enrolled patients underwent inner ear gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), pure-tone audiometry (PTA), and electrocochleography (ECochG). Based on the results of gadolinium-enhanced MRI, patients were classified into the endolymphatic hydrops group and the non-endolymphatic hydrops group. According to established diagnostic criteria, the enrolled participants were classified by specific disorders, with the final diagnoses including Meniere’s disease [15], vestibular migraine [16], sudden sensorineural hearing loss [17], and idiopathic vertigo.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they exhibited poor-quality inner ear gadolinium-enhanced MRI images, unrecordable ECochG data, or declined participation in the clinical trial.

Ethics

The Ethics Committee of the Eye and ENT Hospital, Fudan University, approved this study (#2024099). The study did not have any adverse effects on de-identified subjects, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Pure-tone audiometry and electrocochleography protocol

Pure-tone audiometry

Bilateral pure-tone audiometry (PTA) thresholds were obtained from all enrolled patients at seven frequencies ranging from 0.125 to 8 kHz prior to intravenous gadolinium administration. The tests were performed in a sound-treated environment using standardized audiometric procedures, and the results were expressed as air conduction thresholds in decibels hearing level (dB HL).

Electrocochleography protocol

Electrocochleography (ECochG) was conducted in a soundproof room that met audiometric testing standards. During the examination, patients lay quietly on a treatment bed in a relaxed state and were not required to respond to auditory stimuli. Recordings were obtained using the Smart EP M010000 system (Intelligent Hearing Systems, USA). A tympanic membrane electrode served as the recording electrode, while an insert earphone delivered acoustic stimuli. The stimuli consisted of alternating polarity click sounds presented at an intensity of 95 dB nHL, with a stimulation rate of 7.10/s, a total of 1024 sweeps, and a recording bandwidth of 10–1500 Hz.

The tympanic membrane electrode was positioned directly on the tympanic membrane, the reference electrode was placed on the ipsilateral mastoid, and the ground electrode was affixed to the forehead. Responses were continuously monitored and recorded following acoustic stimulation. The obtained waveforms were analyzed and annotated by an experienced audiologist to identify the summating potential (SP) and action potential (AP) components for subsequent analysis.

MRI protocol and endolymphatic hydrops evaluation

MRI examinations were performed on a 3.0‑T scanner, all patients received a double dose (0.4 ml/kg of body weight) of Gd-HP-DO3A intravenously. MRIs were conducted 4 h post-administration, using a 32-channel phased-array receive-only coil on a Verio MRI system (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). T2-space and 3D-real-IR sequence MRIs were performed to acquire images. Based on the 3D-real-IR sequence of inner ear gadolinium-enhanced MRI, cochlear hydrops (CH) was classified into four grades according to the criteria described by Gürkov et al. [18], and as the criteria proposed by Bernaerts et al. [19], vestibular hydrops (VH)was also classified into four grades. The grade of endolymphatic hydrops was assessed by two otolaryngologists, both blinded to other patient information. In cases where their evaluations disagreed, the final assessment was determined after further discussion.

Data collection and processing

In this study, we collected the amplitude (Asp) and latency (Lsp) of the summating potential (SP), as well as the latency (Lap) and amplitude (Aap) of the action potential (AP). The duration between the SP and AP waves was calculated as (Lap – Lsp). In addition, we recorded both the amplitude ratio (SP/AP) and the area ratio (ASP/AAP) derived from the inverted SP and AP waveforms.To quantify interaural differences, we calculated the ratio of the SP/AP value in the test ear to that in the contralateral ear, denoted as SP/AP (asy). Similarly, the ratio of ASP/AAP between the test and contralateral ears was calculated and denoted as ASP/AAP (asy).

Furthermore, air conduction thresholds from pure-tone audiometry (PTA) were obtained at seven frequencies (0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 kHz) for both ears of all included subjects. These thresholds were recorded as T125, T250, T500, T1k, T2k, T4k, and T8k, respectively. The average air conduction thresholds at 500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, and 4 kHz were calculated and represented as the mean PTA value.

Machine learning

Machine learning models

Given the limited sample size, seven machine learning models were utilized for analysis, including logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), decision tree, random forest, light gradient boosting machine(lgb), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Naive Bayes (NB).Logistic regression is a classification technique in machine learning [20]. Support vector machine is a powerful method to build a classifier, aiming to create a decision boundary between two classes that enables the prediction of labels from one or more feature vectors [21]. SVM is a common supervised learning method widely applied in the biomedical field. Decision tree is a machine learning approach that encompasses various algorithms for decision tree generation, including ID3, C4.5, and CART [22]. Random forest refers to a classification method that utilizes multiple trees for sample training and prediction, representing an ensemble learning approach [23]. Light gradient boosting machine is a distributed gradient boosting framework rooted in decision tree algorithms with two novel techniques: gradient-based one-side sampling along with exclusive feature bundling [24].K-Nearest Neighbors is a simple, instance-based learning algorithm that classifies data points based on the majority class of their nearest neighbors in the feature space [25]. Naive Bayes is a probabilistic classifier based on Bayes’ theorem, assuming independence between features, often used for its simplicity and efficiency in text classification [26].

Cross-validation

Five-fold cross-validation was performed on the training dataset across seven machine learning models to determine the optimal model. Each model’s diagnostic performance was evaluated using both the training and test datasets. The final diagnostic model was then retrained using the entire training dataset to maximize its generalization capability.

Model evaluation and dataset partitioning

The diagnostic efficacy of the machine learning models for endolymphatic hydrops (EH) was evaluated using accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Owing to the limited sample size in this study, the dataset for the EH diagnosis task was divided into a training set and a test set in a 3:1 ratio. For the task of predicting the anatomical location of EH, the dataset was divided into a training set and a test set in a 2:1 ratio.

Implementation of an endolymphatic hydrops prediction tool

To develop a practical tool for estimating the probability of endolymphatic hydrops (EH), the pre-trained model was integrated into a web-based application and deployed on an internal server. This platform allows users to input air conduction hearing thresholds and ECochG test parameters to obtain an individualized probability estimate of EH.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the included population

A total of 78 patients (156 ears) were included in this study. The cohort comprised 50 patients(20 males and 30 females)diagnosed with endolymphatic hydrops (EH) and 28 patients (13 males and 15 females ) without EH. Among the non-EH group, 10 patients had vestibular migraine, 10 had sudden sensorineural hearing loss, and 8 had idiopathic vertigo. The mean age of the EH group was 49.48 ± 14.74 years, while that of the non-EH group was 45.39 ± 14.38 years. A Mann–Whitney U test indicated no significant difference in age between the two groups (Z = 1.360, P = 0.1737). Additionally, a Pearson chi-square test revealed no significant difference in sex distribution between the groups (χ² = 0.3039, P = 0.581).Six ears were excluded due to unrecordable ECochG data, resulting in 150 ears being analyzed.

Correlation between ECochG and pure-tone audiometry air conduction thresholds

As shown in Table 1, Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated that the SP/AP ratio was positively correlated with air conduction thresholds. The strongest correlation was observed at 2 kHz, followed by the pure-tone average (PTA) and 4 kHz (T4k). Across all frequencies, the correlation coefficients between SP/AP and air conduction thresholds ranged from 0.36 to 0.44, indicating a weak but statistically significant correlation. Similarly, the ASP/AAP ratio exhibited the highest correlation with the 2 kHz air conduction threshold (r = 0.38), followed by PTA and T4k. The correlation coefficients between ASP/AAP and air conduction thresholds ranged from 0.28 to 0.38, also reflecting a weak but statistically significant association. Overall, both ECochG-derived parameters (SP/AP and ASP/AAP) showed stronger correlations with PTA, T2k, and T4k than with other frequencies. These findings suggest that mid- to high-frequency hearing thresholds may serve as more sensitive indicators of cochlear dysfunction associated with endolymphatic hydrops.

As shown in Table 1, the SP/AP ratio obtained from ECochG demonstrated strongest correlation (r=0.33) with the overall degree of endolymphatic hydrops (EHL), compared to cochlear hydrops (CH) and vestibular hydrops (VH). Similarly, the ASP/AA Pratioalso correlated most strongly with EHL (r=0.26), while its correlations with VH and CH were weaker.

ECochG for endolymphatic hydrops

As shown in Table 2, the diagnostic performance of machine learning models based solely on ECochG features for detecting endolymphatic hydrops (EH) was generally suboptimal. The Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGB) model achieved the highest area under the curve (AUC) of 0.61. The k-nearest neighbor (KNN) model demonstrated the highest sensitivity (SEN = 0.50), while the support vector machine (SVM) model achieved the highest specificity (SPE = 1.00) and accuracy (ACC = 0.78).

Overall, the sensitivity of ECochG-based models was relatively low, ranging from 0.25 to 0.50, whereas specificity was acceptable, ranging from 0.52 to 1.00. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the LGB model yielded the highest AUC (0.61), while the decision tree model showed the lowest (0.43). The Precision–Recall (PR) curves in Fig. 1B further indicated that the svm model performed the best, whereas the decision tree model exhibited the poorest performance.

Machine learning models incorporating multimodal data for endolymphatic hydrops

To further enhance the diagnostic performance for endolymphatic hydrops (EH), patients’ pure-tone audiometry (PTA) air conduction thresholds were incorporated into the model training. As shown in Table 3, the inclusion of multimodal data substantially improved the diagnostic capability of the models. In the test dataset, the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGB) model achieved the highest area under the curve (AUC = 0.92) and sensitivity (SEN = 0.67). The support vector machine (SVM) model demonstrated the highest specificity (SPE = 0.96) and accuracy (ACC = 0.89).As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the LGB model yielded the highest AUC (0.92), whereas the decision tree model showed the lowest (0.69). The Precision–Recall (PR) curves in Fig. 2B further confirmed that the LGB model achieved the best overall performance, while the decision tree model exhibited the weakest.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that combining electrocochleography (ECochG) with pure-tone audiometry (PTA) and applying machine learning significantly improves the diagnostic accuracy for endolymphatic hydrops. The Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGB) model achieved the best performance (AUC = 0.92) and effectively predicted hydrops location. A web-based prediction tool was also developed, providing a practical approach for clinical application. Future multi-center studies with larger and more diverse datasets are needed to validate and further optimize this multimodal diagnostic framework.

Discussion

The findings of this study highlight the potential value of electrocochleography (ECochG) and pure-tone audiometry (PTA) in diagnosing endolymphatic hydrops, particularly when combined with machine learning models. Although the correlations between ECochG indicators (SP/AP, ASP/AAP) and air conduction thresholds were weak, they were statistically significant. The integration of multimodal hearing data, including ECochG and PTA, substantially enhanced the diagnostic performance of the machine learning models. Among them, the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGB) model achieved the best performance, with an AUC of 0.92. Furthermore, the LGB model based on diverse hearing data demonstrated an accuracy of 0.73 in predicting the location of endolymphatic hydrops. These results suggest that a multimodal approach integrating various audiometric data could serve as a more robust diagnostic tool in clinical practice.

In addition, this study developed a web-based tool capable of predicting the probability of endolymphatic hydrops in the affected ear using ECochG and PTA results. Previous studies have reported mixed success in using ECochG for diagnosing Ménière’s disease and related conditions, often citing variability in test outcomes and limited sensitivity as major limitations [4, 27–30]. The improved diagnostic performance achieved in this study—through the application of machine learning algorithms—addresses some of these challenges by enhancing both accuracy and reliability.

Feature importance analysis further revealed that SP/AP, SP/AP (asy), Lsp, ASP/AAP (asy), T125, T250, and T500 were key predictors for diagnosing endolymphatic hydrops. For predicting its location, important features included Lap–Lsp, SP/AP, SP/AP (asy), ASP/AAP, ASP/AAP (asy), T125, and T250. These findings emphasize that low-frequency hearing thresholds remain reliable indicators of endolymphatic hydrops. Moreover, ECochG parameters such as SP/AP [29, 31–33] and ASP/AAP [34] are continue to demonstrate diagnostic value. This study also introduced asymmetry features—SP/AP (asy) and ASP/AAP (asy)—representing interaural differences, which were found to enhance the detection of hydrops. Additionally, the latency of the SP wave (Lsp) contributed to hydrops diagnosis, while the interval between SP and AP waves (Lap–Lsp) aided in predicting the hydrops location.

In this study, the relatively low sensitivity indicates that the models trained solely on electrocochleography (ECochG) and audiometric data may result in certain missed diagnoses. This may be partly due to the limited sample size. In addition, our dataset included only audiometric data, without incorporating patients’ clinical symptoms or vestibular function test results. Furthermore, the negative sample group also included patients with vestibular migraine and sudden sensorineural hearing loss, who may present with hearing loss, adding to the diagnostic challenge. Although the sensitivity and specificity of ECochG remain subjects of debate, this method continues to be valuable in specific clinical contexts. For instance, in medical centers where inner ear gadolinium-enhanced MRI is unavailable, or in patients unable to undergo MRI due to physical constraints, ECochG-based diagnostic models may still provide meaningful insights.

The correlation between ECochG measurements and PTA thresholds in our study was relatively low, which may be explained by their different physiological bases, temporal characteristics, subgroup variability, and measurement noise. These factors together likely contribute to the limited association observed.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and the single-center design may limit the generalizability of our findings, and external validation is still needed. Second, the dataset was relatively homogeneous and lacked other relevant data types, which may affect the comprehensiveness of the analysis. Additionally, age remains an important factor influencing auditory function and may act as a potential confounder. In future studies, we plan to expand the sample size and conduct more detailed stratified analyses to better account for age-related effects and other confounding variables. Furthermore, integrating additional clinical information, such as medical history and vestibular function tests, could further enhance diagnostic accuracy. Multi-center studies with larger cohorts will also be essential to validate our models and improve their generalizability.
