Effect of breathing phase number on the 4D robust optimization for pancreatic cancer intensity modulated proton therapy
Authors: Xiaoying Fan, Shuting Wang, Weijie Li, Tengxiang Li, Yong Yin, Tianyuan Dai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeRespiratory movement, as one of the main challenges in proton therapy for pancreatic cancer patients, could not only lead to harm to normal tissues but also lead to failure of the tumor control, resulting in irreversible consequences. Including respiratory movements into the plan optimization, i.e. 4D robust optimization, may mitigate the interplay effect. However, 4D robust optimization considering images of all breathing phases is time-consuming and less efficient. This work aims to investigate the effect of the breathing phase number on the 4D robust optimization for pancreatic cancer intensity modulated proton therapy (IMPT) by examining plan quality and computational efficiency.MethodsA total of 15 pancreatic cancer patients were retrospectively analyzed. In this study, both anterior-fields and posterior-fields plans were created for each patient. For each plan, six four-dimensional (4D) robust treatment planning strategies with different numbers of respiratory phases and one three-dimensional (3D) treatment plan were created. Optimization of the plans were performed on all ten phases (10phase plan), two extreme phases (2phase plan), two extreme phases plus an intermediate state (3phase plan), two extreme phases plus the 3D CT (3Aphase plan), six phases during the exhalation stage (6Exphase plan), six phases during the inhalation stage (6Inphase plan) and 3D Computed Tomography (CT) scan image (3D plan), respectively. 4D dynamic dose (4DDD) was then calculated to access the interplay effect by considering respiratory motion and dynamic beam delivery. Plan quality and dosimetric parameters for the target and organs at risk (OARs) were then analyzed.ResultsCompared to the 4D plans, 3D plan performed terribly in terms of target coverage and organs at risk. Target dose in anterior-fields plan varied slightly among all six 4D treatment planning strategies. Both the 6Exphase and 6Inphase plans demonstrated performance that was comparable to the 10phase plan in target coverage, outperforming the other five plans for anterior-fields plan. It’s basically the same for the posterior-fields plan. The six strategies showed similar OARs sparing effect for both anterior-fields and posterior-fields plan. Compared with the 10phase plan, the average decline rates of the optimization time of the six plans of 2phase, 3phase, 3Aphase, 6Exphase, 6Inphase, and 3D were 73.26 ± 6.54% vs. 74.48 ± 6.63%, 65.80 ± 7.89% vs. 65.81 ± 9.58%, 54.67 ± 11.52% vs. 65.75 ± 9.58%, 42.14 ± 13.57% vs. 39.63 ± 16.93%, 37.72 ± 11.70% vs. 40.79 ± 13.62% and 75.52 ± 8.21% vs. 80.67 ± 5.62%, respectively (anterior vs. posterior). With the decrease of the number of phases selected for optimization, the decline rates increased, while the other dosimetry parameters generally showed a deterioration trend.ConclusionIn this study, a comprehensive evaluation of six 4D robust treatment planning strategies and one 3D treatment planning strategy for pancreatic cancer patients receiving IMPT was performed. The results showed that six 4D robust optimization strategies were comparable in common posterior field therapy. 2phase and 3phase (including 3Aphase) treatment planning strategies could replace the 10phase treatment planning strategy. It should be noted that patients with large motion amplitudes should receive special attention. The dosimetric performance of the 6Exphase and 6Inphase plans closely aligned with that of the 10phase plan in anterior fields. These plans offered a feasible alternative to 10phase treatment planning strategy by reducing optimization time while maintaining dose coverage of the target and protection of OARs. This research provides guidelines to reduce optimization time and improve clinical efficiency for pancreatic cancer IMPT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13094-9.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is a significant contributor to the global cancer-related mortality with a mortality rate nearly equal to its morbidity rate. Despite the rapid development of the treatment method, long-term survival rates remain low, typically ranging from 9 to 11% over a 5-year period [1]. Early detection of pancreatic cancer is challenging leading to the diagnosis at advanced stages. Radiation therapy involves the use of high-energy radiation to target and destroy tumor cells, slowing tumor growth. In the case of pancreatic cancer, radiotherapy plays a crucial role in various clinical treatment approaches, including postoperative adjuvant chemoradiotherapy and radical concurrent chemoradiotherapy. This treatment modality is essential in reducing patient symptoms, improving survival rates, and enhancing overall quality of life [2, 3].

However, radiotherapy for pancreatic cancer often comes with challenges due to the deep anatomical location of the pancreas in the abdominal cavity surrounded by organs like the duodenum and small intestine. This complicates the precise targeting of the tumor and sparing of the normal tissue. Proton therapy, a novel technique, offers the advantage of delivering prescribed doses to the tumor target with better spare of the surrounding tissues. This ability to minimize radiation exposure to normal tissues enhances therapeutic outcomes and reduces the risk of toxic side effects [4]. Research by Thompson et al. demonstrated that proton therapy, in comparison to traditional photon therapy, notably reduces medium and low doses to critical organs near the tumor site [5]. As a result, proton therapy is increasingly being utilized in the treatment of pancreatic cancer [6, 7].

Although proton therapy shows potential in the treatment of pancreatic cancer, challenges remain considering the impact of patients’ respiratory movement and dynamic proton beam delivery, i.e. interplay effect. The uncertainty of tumor movement significantly affects the actual radiation dose delivered to the tumor, potentially resulting in inadequate coverage of tumor target or severe damage to the nearby critical organs [8–10]. To mitigate the effects of motion uncertainty, incorporating tumor movement into the treatment planning optimization process was proposed. Four-dimensional (4D) optimization considering the dynamic anatomy changes of the tumor results in a 4D robust treatment plan [11–13]. However, 4D robust optimization, while performing better in mitigating the effects of motion uncertainty, is less efficient and time-consuming compared to traditional 3D optimization, limiting its clinical use.

This work aims to investigate if strategies considering a smaller number of optimization phases in pancreatic cancer IMPT treatment planning could maintain tumor target coverage and plan quality, thus saving time and enhancing efficiency. In this study, different numbers of respiration phases were selected for 4D optimization. Merits of the optimization results were evaluated through the 4D dynamic dose (4DDD), so as to select the optimization strategy that was closest to the 10phase plan optimization strategy. This study also explored the optimal number of optimization phases. This research provides guidelines to reduce optimization time and improve clinical efficiency for pancreatic cancer IMPT.

Materials and methods

Patients and contouring

Fifteen pancreatic patients were recruited from Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute. Treatment planning for these patients was then conducted with intensity-modulated proton treatment (IMPT) planning techniques. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) unresected pancreatic cancer patients; (2) No previous history of radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) change of radiotherapy regimen during radiotherapy; (2) combination of other malignant tumors. All patients were scanned with calm breathing using Philips Big Core CT (Phillips Medical Systems, 96 Highland Heights, OH) and reconstructed 4D CT image sets of 10 respiratory phases. The 50%phase is defined as the end of expiratory breathing phase (0%Ex). The thickness of the image scanning layer was 3 mm. Table 1 summarizes patient number, position, tumor location, volume, motion amplitude and field angle in this study. The clinical tumor volume (CTV) ranged from 16.27 to 690.97 cc, and the tumor movement amplitude (specified as the largest displacement of the tumor mass center in the three coordinate directions in all the phases of the 4D CT dataset) was between 0.938 and 10.662 mm.

The end-expiratory phase (0%Ex) in 4D CT served as the reference phase in this work [14]. The gross target volume (GTV) and organs at risk (OARs) were contoured in the RayStation11B(RaySearch Laboratories AB, Stockholm, Sweden)treatment planning system by the certificated radiation oncologists in our center on all phases of the 4D CT. CTV was determined to be the GTV plus a margin of 5 mm [15, 16]. Based on the deformation algorithm, CTVs on all other phases in 4D CT were registered to the reference phase (end of expiratory phase and 3D CT). The internal clinical target volume (ICTV) was then defined as the envelope of CTVs from all the breathing phases on the reference phase (target in 3D plan).

Treatment planning

The clinical beam model of ProBeam (Varian Medical Systems, CA, USA) used in the Proton Center of Shandong Cancer Hospital, with the nominal energies range of 70-240 MeV in spot scanning mode was adopted. The Monte Carlo algorithm is applied to calculate dose, and the dose grid is 3 × 3 × 3 mm3. The RayStation11B treatment planning system was used to design the two kinds of IMPT plans (using anterior or posterior fields [14] which is commonly utilized in clinical practice. To maintain consistency in results analysis, the other conditions of the plans from different angles were identically created. For beam setup, two fields were typically used. Two patients were planned with three fields due to the large tumor size. The beam angles for all 3D and 4D robust treatment plans were identical for the same patient with all patients positions of the Head First Supine (HFS). The relative biologic effectiveness (RBE) weighted dose was determined using a fixed RBE value of 1.1 for proton. A prescription dose of 45 Gy (RBE) for 25 fractions was administered to 95% volume of the CTV [12, 16, 17].

Robust optimization

The ion/spot was set to 10,000, and the uncertainties of the MC was set to 0.5% in RayStation11B. 4D robust optimization which considering the optimization function selected for robustness in the worst-case scenario algorithm [18], considering set S of scenarios was implemented using minimax optimization. The objective function was.

where X is the set of feasible variables (e.g., spot weights for IMPT), and d(x, s) is the dose distribution as a function of the variables x and the scenario s. Functions applied only to the nominal scenario can also be added to the objective. Moreover, nominal and robust constraints can be used in combination with formulation [18]. A CTV-based 3D robust optimization was used with universal beam, patient setup uncertainties were simulated by shifting the patient iso-center in the anterior–posterior (A–P), superior–inferior (S–I), and right-left (R–L) directions by 5 mm [19], range uncertainties were simulated by ± 3.5% [19, 20] The robust optimization functions were set as follows: The minimum dose of CTV was 45 Gy(RBE) with the weight of 100. The maximum dose of CTV was 47.25 Gy(RBE) with the weight of 100. The maximum dose of duodenum was 48 Gy(RBE) with the weight of 60. The maximum dose of small intestine was 45 Gy(RBE) with the weight of 60. For the plans with posterior fields, the mean dose of left/right kidney was 8 Gy(RBE) with the weight of 40 in additional.

Six 4D robust plans optimized with various selections of respiratory phases were designed: (1) 10phase plan: all phases of the respiratory cycle (10 phases in this study) were used for standard 4D optimization; (2) 2phase plan: the end of expiratory (0%Ex) and the end of inspiratory (100%In) phases were used for optimization [21]. (3) 3phase plan: two extreme phases and an intermediate phase were used for optimization. Three breathing phases of 0%Ex, 100%In and 60%Ex were used in this study. (4) 3Aphase plan: two extreme phases and the 3D CT were used for optimization. (5) 6Exphase plan: all exhalation phases, including 0%Ex, 20%Ex, 40%Ex, 60%Ex, 80%Ex, 100%In were used for optimization; (6) 6Inphase plan: all inspiratory phases, including 0%Ex, 20%In, 40%In, 60%In, 80%In, 100%In were used for optimization. Besides, the 3D robust plan considering the single reference phase (3D CT) was also conducted. The beam configuration and restriction function were the same for all plans.

4D dynamic dose (4DDD) calculation

A scheme for the process of 4DDD calculation is shown in Fig. 1. This study took into account the interplay effect, which involved the interaction between dynamic pencil beam delivery and respiratory movement. Utilizing the Monte Carlo (MC) dose algorithm in RayStation, an in-house script for 4DDD calculation was developed. Parameters in the script include: (1) energy layer switching time was 1s; (2) beam spot moving speed was 10 m/s; (3) the dose rate during the beam delivery was 800MU/s; (4) Total duration of respiratory cycle was 5s.

In order to evaluate the intra-fraction variability, the 4DDD were calculated with inter-fractional motion. The calculation of the dose distribution after inter-fraction variation used the same method as in 4DDD. Dynamic dose calculations were performed for four patients with two sets of 4DCT.

4D dose evaluation

For each patient (with six 4D robust plans and one 3D plan), all plans were normalized before running the script so that 100% of the prescription dose covered 95% volume of CTV. For CTV, the dose metrics were chosen as D98 (the prescribed dose received by at least 98% of the volume), D95 (the prescribed dose received by at least 95% of the volume), D2 (the minimum dose received by 2% of the volume), the target heterogeneity index (HI), as calculated by the formula:

Where Dpre represents the prescription dose. The HI ranged from 0 to 1, where 0 is the ideal value, the higher the HI, the worse the homogeneity [22].

The conformability index (CI):

Where TV95 and V95 represent the target volume receiving the 95% of the prescription dose and the total volume receiving at least 95% of the prescription dose, respectively. TV indicates the target volume. The CI ranged from 0 to 1, the smaller the CI, the better the conformation [23].

For OARs, the maximum dose to the duodenum, small intestine and spinal cord (Dmax) was treated as D2 in this work [23]. The mean dose of left and right kidneys was evaluated.

The time decline rate is calculated according to the formula below:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{T}}_{\text{d}\text{e}\text{c}\text{l}\text{i}\text{n}\text{e}}=\left({\text{T}}_{10\text{p}\text{h}\text{a}\text{s}\text{e}}-{\text{T}}_{\text{x}}\right)/{\text{T}}_{10\text{p}\text{h}\text{a}\text{s}\text{e}}$$\end{document}

Where T10phase represents the optimization time of the 10phase treatment planning strategy. Tx represents the optimization time of each treatment planning strategy (2phase, 3phase, 6Exphase, 6Inphase or 3D plan). The decline rate ranged from 0 to 100, the higher the value, the less of the time required for the optimization. All above metrics (except the rate of time decline) were also selected for patients with inter-fractional movement.

Statistical analysis was carried out using the SPSS software with the reference of 10phase plan. Wilcoxon signed-rank sum test was used to evaluate statistical differences between dose metrics. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the p value < 0.001 was considered more significant.

Discussion

4D robust optimization considers all respiratory phases to account for tumor deformation during movement. However, selecting more phases increases optimization time. Multiple sets of 4D CT images are needed for precise anatomical movement analysis [24]. Therefore, minimizing calculation load and optimization time is crucial. This study analyzed 15 cases, which had certain credibility with varied tumor size, location, and motion amplitude. Two types of IMPT plan were created utilizing anterior fields and posterior fields, respectively.

For the strategies using the anterior fields, optimizing the number of phases selected for the target improves dose coverage and uniformity significantly (p < 0.05). But for the posterior fields plans, these merits were similar. The result was consistent with the pencil beam passing through the anterior abdomen. The organs at risk (small intestine, etc.) which were irradiated have uncertain factors such as bowl movement, stomachic filling. The proton beam is very sensitive to the anatomy changes on its path. Therefore, anterior fields treatment was more affected by organ movement than posterior fields treatment. Posterior or posterior oblique fields are used for proton therapy, which place the luminal gastrointestinal organs at the distal end of the fields [25] .

Conversely, an increase in the number of phases selected for the target leads to poor conformality (CI) in two kinds of fields. This could be attributed to the inclusion of a wider range of tumor movement scenarios with varying amplitude and density changes. However, as tumor anatomical changes are not entirely predictable, this complexity can hinder optimization efforts. Further research is needed to explore the integration of tumor movement management and 4D optimization. Reduction in selected respiratory phases had a minimal impact on dose distribution in OARs. More normal tissue around tumor target will get irradiated as setup and range uncertainties were incorporated in the breathing phase-based optimization to mitigate the setup and range uncertainties during treatment. In cases of pancreatic cancer, the tumor location, size and the different angles of fields irradiation significantly influenced the dose to OARs, making organ protection a challenge in proton therapy [26].

To alleviate interplay effect, various methods have been suggested, including respiratory gating, tumor tracking, and breath holding [8, 27]. In addition to using 4D robust optimization in the study, it is also a good method to use iso-energy layer repeated scanning technology. The scanning technology performed multiple repeated scans on each energy layer [28]. Reasonable selection of the number of repeated scans based on the case situation can also alleviate the interplay effect [29]. The combination of iso-energy layer repeated scanning technology and 4D robust optimization will also be applied to subsequent studies. For large inter-factional breathing variation, the reduction of the breathing phase number in 4D robust optimization should be taken seriously. The combination of 4D robust optimization with other methods to alleviate inter-fractional motion treatment seems to be a promising approach. Adaptive radiotherapy techniques (ART) may also be used for tumor motion management.

This study focused on optimizing the treatment planning time by incorporating 4D robust optimization alongside 3D robust optimization. Patient setup uncertainties were simulated by shifting the patient iso-center in the anterior–posterior (A–P), superior–inferior (S–I), and right-left (R–L) directions by 5 mm, range uncertainties were simulated by ± 3.5%. The results obtained were based on the 10phase plan. The average time of this optimization method was 7971.8s. It was observed that full-time phase optimization can be time-consuming in clinical planning, as highlighted in a previous study by Buti et al. [30]. Therefore, reducing optimization time is crucial for practical clinical applications. In this study, cases with extreme tumor volume and extreme motion amplitude were analyzed separately according to patient characteristics (Fig. 4). When using the posterior field, the selection of motion management strategies for patients with large motion amplitude need to be noticed (selected > 7 mm in this study) to avoid unnecessary injury.

However, the study had limitations. The small number of cases (15) could not fully represent all patients. Because of the irregular changes in respiratory cycle, the inability of 4D CT images cannot capture all respiratory movements [24, 31]. Tumor movement also caused the irregular phase distribution. Additionally, the simulation in the study was simplified with regular beam delivery and fixed respiratory cycle initiation. Future research should carefully consider the combination of respiratory movement and beam delivery logfile for a more comprehensive analysis.

Conclusion

In this study, a comprehensive evaluation of six 4D robust treatment planning strategies and one 3D treatment planning strategy for pancreatic cancer patients receiving IMPT was performed. The results showed that the six 4D robust optimization strategies were similar in common posterior-fields plan, 2phase and 3phase (including 3Aphase) optimization could replace 10phase optimization, but attention should be paid to the scenario of large motion amplitude patients. The dosimetric performance of the 6Exphase and 6Inphase plans closely aligned with that of the 10phase plan in anterior-fields plan. These plans offered a feasible alternative to 10phase optimization by reducing optimization time while maintaining dose coverage of the target and protection of OARs. This research provides guidelines to reduce optimization time and improve clinical efficiency for pancreatic cancer IMPT.
