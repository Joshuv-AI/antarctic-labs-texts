Stress, diet, and aging: a comprehensive analysis of allostatic load in biological aging
Authors: Jun Liu, Song Zhou, Jihong Zhang, Shuyu Ke, Junming Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the global population continues to age, biological aging has emerged as a significant public health challenge. It is imperative to identify modifiable factors and underlying mechanisms that can promote healthy aging. Allostatic load (AL), a comprehensive indicator of chronic stress, is linked to numerous adverse outcomes in behavioral health. However, there is a paucity of comprehensive analyses and mechanistic investigations regarding the relationship between AL and biological aging across the entire population. This study seeks to elucidate the relationship between AL and biological aging, with a particular focus on examining the mediating role of the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII).MethodThis study analyzed data from NHANES (2001–2010 and 2015–2018) with 25,775 U.S. adults, excluding those with missing data. It measured AL using 8 biomarkers and assessed biological aging with four indicators: Phenotypic Age, Biological Age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge. The DII was calculated from 24-h dietary recall of 45 food items. Sociodemographic and clinical factors were covariates. Weighted linear and logistic regressions examined the complex relationship between AL, DII, and biological aging, with subgroup and mediation analyses exploring mediating role of DII in the AL-aging link.ResultA total of 21.56% of participants were in the high AL group, showing significantly higher DII scores and elevated biological aging indicators compared to the low AL group (P < 0.001). After adjusting for confounders, high AL was linked to faster biological aging, with a 2.08-year increase in Phenotypic Age and a 9.06-year increase in Biological Age. Odds ratios for elevated GOLD BioAge and Light BioAge were 1.60 and 2.16, respectively (P < 0.001). The AL-aging link was stronger in young/middle-aged adults, females, and non-obese individuals. DII was positively linked to all aging indicators, and exploratory mediation analysis suggested that DII may statistically account for a small proportion of the AL-aging association, with mediation proportions ranging from 0.33% to 5.45% (P < 0.001).ConclusionIn a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, elevated AL is significantly associated with accelerated biological aging assessed by multiple complementary indicators, and exploratory mediation analysis suggested that DII may partly account for part of this association. These findings underscore the importance of mitigating chronic physiological stress and reducing the inflammatory potential of the diet as strategies to promote healthy aging. Clinical assessment of AL and DII may provide valuable insights for identifying individuals at risk of accelerated biological aging and guiding targeted intervention measures to improve long-term health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13148-026-02210-0.Keywords: Biological aging, Allostatic load, Dietary inflammatory index, NHANES

Introduction

Biological aging, a progressive degenerative process distinct from chronological age, involves interconnected changes across multiple biological pathways, ultimately leading to cumulative cellular and tissue damage, organ functional decline, increased risk of age-related diseases, and shortened healthy lifespan [1]. As global aging accelerates, biological aging has emerged as a major public health challenge, making the identification of modifiable risk factors and underlying mechanisms critical for developing targeted interventions to promote healthy aging.

Allostatic load (AL), a composite measure that integrates biomarkers from various physiological systems, provides a comprehensive method for quantifying the cumulative physiological impact of chronic stress exposure [2]. AL serves as a distinctive framework for assessing and comprehending the health-related effects of stress by utilizing biomarkers that reflect the physiological dysregulation caused by prolonged stress exposure [3]. Chronic physiological stress can impair biological functions by persistently activating stress responses in the inflammatory, metabolic, neuroendocrine, and cardiovascular systems, resulting in high blood pressure, abnormal lipid levels, and systemic inflammation [4]. In a longitudinal evidence, elevated allostatic load was associated with poorer ageing outcomes [5]. Recently, an epidemiological study conducted by Child Health and Development Studies Disparities Study (DISPAR) sample reported a significant association between higher AL and biological aging among middle aged adults [6]. However, there remains a lack of comprehensive analyses focusing on the relationship between AL and biological aging among the whole population.

Dietary habits represent a significant variable that can modulate physiological response of specific person to stress. Empirical evidence indicates that high levels of perceived stress are correlated with increased total energy intake and a heightened propensity to consume pro-inflammatory foods [7]. Conversely, the adverse health consequences of an inadequate diet may contribute to elevated stress levels [8]. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) serves as a validated instrument for evaluating the inflammatory potential of common diet by analyzing the consumption of various nutrients with pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory properties [9]. Prior research has demonstrated that a pro-inflammatory dietary pattern, as reflected by a higher DII score, is linked to an increased risk of biological aging [10, 11]. Moreover, recent population-based studies have reinforced the direct positive correlation between DII and AL [12]. In light of these findings, we hypothesize that the DII may mediate the relationship between AL and biological aging.

Biological aging is a complex process characterized by various dimensions of physiological decline, each captured by distinct aging indicators. Phenotypic Age reflects the physiological status associated with mortality risk [13], while Biological Age is derived from DNA methylation patterns, recognized as one of the most precise aging proxies [14]. GOLD BioAge estimates “true age” in alignment with mortality risk through Gompertz’s law, and Light BioAge serves as a simplified biomarker-based aging index [15]. Employing multiple indicators facilitates a more robust and comprehensive evaluation of biological aging, thereby mitigating the bias associated with reliance on a singular measure [16].

Therefore, the aim of our study utilized nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) was to investigate the complex relationship between AL, DII, and biological aging, as measured by four complementary aging indicators (Phenotypic Age, Biological Age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge), with a particular focus on examining the mediating role of DII in the association.

Materials and methods

This study utilized data from the NHANES cycles spanning 2001–2010 and 2015–2018. Participants with missing data for key variables—including allostatic load status, Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), Phenotypic Age, Biological age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge—were excluded from the primary complete-case analysis. Additionally, individuals with missing covariates such as body mass index (BMI), poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), education level, hypertension, or diabetes were excluded. The participant selection process and reasons for exclusion are presented in Fig. 1. To evaluate potential selection bias, we compared the weighted characteristics of participants included in and excluded from the complete-case analysis. The NHANES study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics at the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. All adult participants provided written informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. In total, 25,775 participants were included in the final analysis.

Measurement of allostatic load

Our independent variable AL was derived from eight biomarkers: systolic blood pressure (mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (mmHg), pulse rate (beats per minute), body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), glycated hemoglobin (%), direct high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (mg/dL), total cholesterol (mg/dL), and serum albumin (g/dL). Values above the 75th percentile for all biomarkers were considered high risk, except for HDL cholesterol and serum albumin, for which values below the 25th percentile were defined as high risk [17]. Each biomarker classified as high risk was assigned a score of 1, whereas those classified as low risk received a score of 0. After summing the biomarker scores, each respondent received a total score ranging from 0 to 8. Subsequently, based on the obtained scores, respondents with a score ≥ 4 were classified as having high allostatic load, whereas those with a score < 4 were classified as having low allostatic load [18, 19].

Measurement of phenotypic age, biological age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge

Phenotypic age is a biological age indicator calculated based on clinically measurable biomarkers, reflecting the relationship between an individual’s physiological state and chronological age. It represents the average age of the population corresponding to that individual’s mortality risk. Phenotypic age is calculated using chronological age plus nine serum biomarkers through a Gompertz proportional hazards elastic net model. The serum biomarkers include albumin, creatinine, glucose, C-reactive protein, lymphocyte percentage, mean corpuscular volume, red cell distribution width, alkaline phosphatase, and white blood cell count [13]. Biological age refers to an indicator that measures the overall functional status of an organism compared to the expected level of a specific calendar age, reflecting its overall health status and degree of aging. It is primarily predicted based on DNA methylation patterns and is currently one of the most accurate methods [14]. GOLD BioAge is an optimal linear biomarker-derived age model constructed based on Gompertz’s law. GOLD BioAge utilizes the risk function of this law to jointly model calendar age and biomarkers, estimating the “true age” that matches mortality risk [15]. Light BioAge is a simplified version of GOLD BioAge, which estimates age using only three indicators: serum creatinine, blood glucose, and C-reactive protein.

Measurement of DII

Dietary intake data regarding the types and quantities of foods and beverages were obtained from a single 24-h dietary recall conducted at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC) and were used to calculate the DII [20]. Therefore, the DII in this study reflects the inflammatory potential of dietary intake reported for the recall day rather than habitual long-term dietary patterns. Briefly, a total of 45 food parameters were considered, with each parameter assigned specific DII scores based on its effects on six major inflammatory biomarkers (IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and CRP). To assess the inflammatory potential of a participant’s diet, all food-component-specific DII scores were summed to obtain the overall DII, where positive values indicate pro-inflammatory potential, negative values indicate anti-inflammatory potential, and a score of zero indicates no significant effect on inflammatory potential [21].

Measurement of covariates

Covariates included sociodemographic and basic physical condition data obtained through questionnaire surveys. Sociodemographic variables included sex, age, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, and family income-to-poverty ratio (PIR). Basic physical condition included BMI, hypertension status, and diabetes status. Race/ethnicity was categorized into five groups: Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, other Hispanic, and other race. Education level was classified into three groups: less than high school, high school graduate, and higher education. Marital status was divided into two groups: currently married and currently unmarried. Diabetes status and hypertension status were both dichotomized as yes and no.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R software following the guidelines provided by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Given that NHANES employs a complex multistage probability sampling design to select representative participants, we incorporated primary sampling units, stratification, and sample weights in our data analysis. Weighted analyses were conducted using the “survey” package in R statistical software to generate nationally representative estimates. This approach ensures that the research findings are generalizable to the non-institutionalized U.S. population while avoiding overestimation of statistical significance. According to NHANES guidelines, weight selection prioritized variables representing small population subgroups, and appropriate weights were applied accordingly. The primary analyses were based on complete cases. To assess potential selection bias, demographic, socioeconomic, clinical, dietary, and biological aging characteristics were compared between participants included in and excluded from the complete-case analysis.

This study first generated baseline analyses displaying the correlation of various indicators across different stress load statuses. Subsequently, an unadjusted model (Model 1) was established, followed by a minimally adjusted model (Model 2) including age, sex, BMI, race, marital status, education level, and income-to-poverty ratio (PIR). Finally, a fully adjusted model (Model 3) was constructed, further incorporating hypertension and diabetes-related data. Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analyses across various factors based on covariates. Given the multiple interaction tests performed across subgroup variables, subgroup analyses were considered exploratory. For each biological aging indicator, nine subgroup variables were assessed; therefore, a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P for interaction < 0.006, calculated as 0.05/9, was used to identify interaction findings that were robust to multiple-comparison correction. Interaction P-values between 0.006 and 0.05 were interpreted as nominally significant and hypothesis-generating. As a sensitivity analysis, missing values were imputed using a single-imputation procedure. Predictive mean matching was used for continuous variables, and logistic regression-based methods were used for categorical variables. All variables required for the primary analyses that contained missing values were included in the imputation procedure. The primary regression models were then repeated in the imputed dataset.

The associations between AL and phenotypic age and other indicators were validated through multiple linear regression analysis and logistic regression analysis, respectively. To explore the relationship between the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) and Phenotypic Age, Biological age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge, we conducted both continuous and categorical model analyses. It was confirmed that DII is positively associated with Phenotypic Age, Biological age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge. Mediation analysis was finally performed, and the results showed that DII may be a critical pathway linking AL to Phenotypic Age, Biological age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge.

Participant characteristics

A total of 25,775 eligible participants from NHANES 2001–2010 and 2015–2018 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The weighted characteristics of participants included in and excluded from the complete-case analysis are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Overall, 12,980 were females (51.15%), and 8,478 participants (32.89%) were older than 59 years. Participants were categorized into low AL (n = 20,218, 78.44%) and high AL (n = 5,557, 21.56%) groups. Compared with participants with low AL, those with high AL differed significantly in most demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical characteristics, except marital status (Table 1). The high AL group also had a higher mean DII score (1.22 ± 0.04 vs. 0.87 ± 0.03, P < 0.001) and higher levels of all four biological age indicators, including Phenotypic Age (64.63 ± 0.31 vs. 53.60 ± 0.26), Biological Age (56.14 ± 0.35 vs. 38.62 ± 0.22), GOLD BioAge (61.29% vs. 34.31% for positive status), and Light BioAge (84.73% vs. 52.63% for positive status), all with P < 0.001. The weighted characteristics of participants included in and excluded from the complete-case analysis are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Association of AL with phenotypic age, biological age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge

High AL was consistently associated with all four biological aging indicators in both logistic and linear regression analyses (Tables 2 and 3). In the fully adjusted model, high AL was associated with higher odds of elevated GOLD BioAge (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.42–1.80) and Light BioAge (OR = 2.16, 95% CI: 1.90–2.46) (both P < 0.001). High AL was also associated with higher Phenotypic Age (β = 2.08 years, 95% CI: 1.60–2.55, P < 0.001) and Biological Age (β = 9.06 years, 95% CI: 8.38–9.74, P < 0.001), with a significant trend (P for trend < 0.001). The primary regression analyses were repeated in the imputed dataset of 71,420 participants (Supplementary Table S2). The fully adjusted results were consistent with those from the complete-case analysis. High AL was associated with elevated GOLD BioAge (OR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.55–1.80), elevated Light BioAge (OR = 2.51, 95% CI: 2.30–2.73), higher Phenotypic Age (β = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.71–2.42), and higher Biological Age (β = 9.43, 95% CI: 8.90–9.96), with all P < 0.001. These findings supported the robustness of the primary results.

Overall, the positive associations between high AL and biological aging indicators were generally consistent across most subgroups.

After applying the Bonferroni-corrected threshold for interaction analyses, several interaction findings remained robust. For GOLD BioAge, age showed a significant interaction with AL (P for interaction < 0.001), with the strongest association observed in the 20–39-year group (OR = 2.04, 95%CI: 1.61–2.59). The interaction for education level was nominally significant (P = 0.009) but did not meet the corrected threshold. For Light BioAge, sex showed a significant interaction with AL (P for interaction < 0.001), with a stronger association in females (OR = 2.66, 95%CI: 2.20–3.20). The interaction for age was nominally significant (P = 0.013) but did not meet the corrected threshold. For Phenotypic Age, age remained a significant effect modifier after correction (P for interaction < 0.001), with the strongest association in the 20–39-year group (β = 4.01, 95%CI: 2.88–5.14). The interaction for BMI was nominally significant (P = 0.010) but did not meet the corrected threshold. For Biological Age, BMI showed a significant interaction with AL after correction (P for interaction < 0.001), with the strongest association among participants with BMI < 25 (β = 12.12, 95%CI: 9.69–14.55). The interaction for diabetes was not statistically significant after correction (P = 0.052).

Association of DII with phenotypic age, biological age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge

DII was positively associated with all four biological aging indicators in both continuous and categorical models (Tables 4 and 5). In the fully adjusted continuous model, each 1-unit increase in DII was associated with higher odds of elevated GOLD BioAge (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.05–1.10) and Light BioAge (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.09–1.14), as well as higher Phenotypic Age (β = 0.71 years, 95% CI: 0.60–0.81) and Biological Age (β = 0.26 years, 95% CI: 0.13–0.39) (all P < 0.001). Similar patterns were observed in quartile-based analyses, with the highest DII quartile showing the strongest associations compared with the lowest quartile.

Mediation analysis with DII as a mediator

Exploratory mediation analysis suggested that DII statistically accounted for a small proportion of the association between AL and biological aging indicators (Fig. 6). The estimated proportion of mediation was 5.45% for Phenotypic Age: β = 1.96, 95%CI: 1.49–2.43), 0.33% for Biological Age (β = 9.02, 95% CI: 8.35–9.70), 2.55% for GOLD BioAge (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.34–0.58), and 2.2% for Light BioAge (OR = 0.76, 95%CI: 0.63–0.89). These findings suggest that DII may explain a statistically significant but modest proportion of the association between AL and accelerated biological aging.

Discussion

This study utilized nationally representative data from NHANES 2001–2010 and 2015–2018 to systematically investigate the relationships between allostatic load (AL), the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), and four distinct biological aging indicators: Phenotypic Age, Biological Age, GOLD BioAge, and Light BioAge, within a substantial cohort of 25,775 U.S. adults. The principal findings indicated that elevated AL was consistently associated with accelerated biological aging across all four indicators, and exploratory mediation analyses suggested that DII may modestly account for part of this association. Furthermore, the strength of these associations varied among different demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical subgroups, offering novel insights into the intricate interactions between chronic physiological stress, dietary inflammation, and biological aging.

Initially, our results confirm and extend previous evidence by demonstrating a robust positive association between high AL and accelerated biological aging in a nationally representative population. Consistent with the findings from the Child Health and Development Studies Disparities Study (DISPAR) focusing on middle-aged adults [6], our current study expands the generalizability of this association to a broader age spectrum, highlighting that chronic physiological stress-induced dysregulation is a universal risk factor for premature biological aging beyond chronological age. The magnitude of this association is notable: after full adjustment for confounders including BMI, hypertension, and diabetes, high AL was associated with a 2.08-year increase in Phenotypic Age, a 9.06-year increase in Biological Age, and 1.60- and 2.16-fold higher odds of elevated GOLD BioAge and Light BioAge, respectively. This underscores the profound impact of cumulative physiological stress on multiple dimensions of aging, from clinical biomarker-based phenotypes to epigenetic regulation.

The subgroup analyses provided exploratory evidence that the association between AL and biological aging may differ across population groups. After applying the Bonferroni-corrected threshold for interaction analyses, age remained a robust effect modifier for GOLD BioAge and Phenotypic Age, sex remained a robust effect modifier for Light BioAge, and BMI remained a robust effect modifier for Biological Age. Other subgroup differences should be interpreted as nominal and hypothesis-generating. The stronger association between AL and GOLD BioAge/Light BioAge/Phenotypic Age in the 20–39 and 40–59 age groups compared to the > 59 age group suggests that young and middle-aged adults may be more susceptible to the aging-accelerating effects of chronic stress. This may be attributed to the relatively preserved physiological reserve in younger individuals, where the disruption of homeostatic balance by high AL leads to a more pronounced deviation from the expected aging trajectory [22]. Additionally, the stronger association in females for Light BioAge aligns with previous studies [23, 24], potentially due to hormonal fluctuations and differential susceptibility to metabolic and cardiovascular stressors. In addition, the enhanced association between AL and Biological Age/Phenotypic Age in participants with BMI < 25 may suggest that physiological dysregulation related to AL remains relevant even in non-obese individuals. However, because these subgroup analyses were exploratory and involved multiple comparisons, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in independent cohorts.

A novel exploratory finding of this study is that DII may partly account for the observed association between AL and biological aging. However, given the cross-sectional design, the temporal sequence among AL, DII, and biological aging cannot be established. Therefore, these mediation findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of a causal pathway. The mediation proportion varied across aging indicators, with the highest proportion (5.45%) observed for Phenotypic Age and the lowest (0.33%) for Biological Age. This discrepancy may reflect the distinct biological underpinnings of each aging metric: Phenotypic Age, derived from clinical biomarkers closely linked to mortality risk [25], is highly responsive to modifiable factors such as diet and physiological stress, as these factors directly alter metabolic, inflammatory, and cardiovascular parameters. In contrast, Biological Age, based on DNA methylation patterns [26], is a more stable epigenetic marker that may be influenced by long-term, cumulative exposures rather than short-term dietary changes captured by a single 24-h dietary recall. The statistically significant but modest mediation effect of DII suggests that dietary inflammatory potential may be one contributing factor in the association between AL and accelerated biological aging. However, the small mediation proportions indicate that DII is unlikely to be a dominant mediator. Meanwhile, reverse causation remains possible, because accelerated biological aging may increase physiological dysregulation or influence dietary behaviors. Longitudinal studies are required to clarify whether dietary inflammatory potential lies on the temporal pathway linking chronic physiological stress to biological aging. This finding is consistent with prior research demonstrating that pro-inflammatory diets are independently associated with premature aging [10, 11] and reinforces the importance of dietary modification as a potential intervention target.

The observed positive correlation between the DII and all four biological aging indicators, as demonstrated in both continuous and categorical models, underscores the significant role of dietary inflammation in the aging process. Specifically, each 1-unit increase in DII corresponded to a 0.71-year increase in Phenotypic Age and a 0.26-year increase in Biological Age. Furthermore, individuals in the highest DII quartile exhibited a 3.43-year increase in Phenotypic Age and had 1.44–1.68 times higher odds of elevated GOLD BioAge and Light BioAge compared to those in the lowest quartile. These findings build upon previous research by illustrating that dietary inflammation influences aging across various physiological dimensions, ranging from clinical phenotypes to epigenetic regulation, even after accounting for sociodemographic factors, BMI, and chronic diseases. The graded association between DII quartiles and biological aging suggests that even moderate reductions in dietary inflammatory potential may confer incremental benefits in delaying the aging process.

This study possesses several notable strengths. First, the use of nationally representative NHANES data with a large sample size (n = 25,775) ensures the generalizability of findings to the non-institutionalized U.S. adult population. Second, the inclusion of four complementary biological aging indicators allows for a comprehensive assessment of aging, mitigating the bias associated with reliance on a single measure [16]. Third, the rigorous statistical analysis, including adjustment for multiple confounders (sociodemographic factors, BMI, hypertension, diabetes) and incorporation of NHANES sampling weights, enhances the robustness of the results. Fourth, the exploratory mediation analysis provides preliminary evidence that dietary inflammatory potential may modestly contribute to the association between AL and biological aging, while also indicating that additional pathways are likely to explain most of this association.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal or temporal inference regarding the relationships among AL, DII, and biological aging. Although mediation analysis was used as an exploratory tool to examine whether DII may statistically account for part of the AL-aging association, the directionality of the AL-DII-aging relationship cannot be determined from these data. It is equally plausible that accelerated biological aging predisposes individuals to higher physiological dysregulation, higher AL, or poorer dietary choices, resulting in a higher DII. Therefore, the mediation results should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating findings rather than evidence of a causal mechanism. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether reducing AL or improving dietary inflammatory profiles can delay biological aging. Second, DII was calculated based on a single 24-h dietary recall, which is prone to within-person variation because day-to-day dietary intake can vary substantially. As a result, the DII derived from one recall day may not accurately capture an individual’s habitual long-term dietary inflammatory pattern and may introduce measurement error. This limitation is particularly important for the mediation analysis, because random error or misclassification in the mediator can attenuate or bias the estimated indirect effect. Therefore, the observed mediation proportion should be interpreted cautiously and may not fully reflect the contribution of long-term dietary inflammatory potential to the association between AL and biological aging. Future studies using repeated dietary recalls, food-frequency questionnaires, or longitudinal dietary assessments are needed to more accurately evaluate the mediating role of habitual diet.

Third, potential residual confounding factors, such as physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and psychological stress, were not fully adjusted for, as these variables may also influence AL, DII, and biological aging.

Fourth, the mediation effect proportions were small, particularly for Biological Age (0.33%), suggesting that the clinical relevance of DII as a mediator should be interpreted cautiously. Although the indirect effects were statistically significant, DII accounted for only a limited fraction, Other unmeasured mediators, such as oxidative stress, gut microbiota composition, telomere length, may also play important roles in the AL-biological aging pathway.

Fifth, subgroup analyses involved multiple interaction tests and should be interpreted cautiously. Although we applied a Bonferroni-corrected threshold to reduce the risk of false-positive interaction findings, this conservative approach may also increase the risk of false-negative findings. In addition, some subgroups (e.g., Other Hispanic, n = 1959) had relatively small sample sizes, which may limit the statistical power of subgroup analyses for these populations.

In conclusion, elevated AL was significantly associated with accelerated biological aging across multiple indicators in a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, and DII may partly explain this association in exploratory mediation analyses. These findings support the potential importance of chronic physiological stress and dietary inflammatory potential in biological aging, but causal and temporal relationships cannot be inferred from the present cross-sectional study. Public health interventions designed to alleviate chronic stress (e.g., workplace stress reduction, mindfulness programs) and to promote anti-inflammatory dietary patterns may contribute to delaying biological aging and decreasing the prevalence of age-related diseases. Clinically, the assessment of AL and DII could offer valuable insights into the risk of individual accelerated biological aging, thereby guiding targeted interventions to enhance long-term health outcomes.
