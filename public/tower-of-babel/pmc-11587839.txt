Dexmedetomidine inhibits ferroptosis and attenuates sepsis-induced acute kidney injury via activating the Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway
Authors: Jiao Huang, Yang Zhao, Xi Luo, Yunpeng Luo, Jiemei Ji, Jia Li, Jian Lai, Ziru Liu, Yuanyuan Chen, Yunan Lin, Jingchen Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe molecular mechanism underlying the protective effects of DEX against sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (SAKI) remains to be elucidated.MethodsWe established S-AKI models in vivo via CLP and in vitro with LPS-induced HK-2 cells.ResultsThe Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway was inhibited in S-AKI both in vitro and in vivo. The overexpression of Nrf2 inhibited LPS-induced ferroptosis by activating the SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway. DEX ameliorated kidney tissue damage, as determined by a decrease in BUN, Cr, and inflammatory factor levels, along with renal tubule vacuolation and inflammatory cell infiltration in S-AKI mice. Additionally, DEX treatment significantly ameliorated ferroptosis in S-AKI in vitro and in vivo, as indicated by an improvement in mitochondrial shrinkage and disruption of cristae, a decrease in iron, ROS, MDA, and 4-HNE levels, and an increase in GSH and GPX4 levels. Mechanistically, DEX treatment restored the reduction of Nrf2 expression and nuclear translocation in S-AKI, as well as, the levels of downstream SLC7A11, FSP1, and CoQ10. Knocking down Nrf2 in vitro and administering brusatol in vivo eliminated the protective effect of DEX against S-AKI.ConclusionsDEX mitigated ferroptosis and attenuated S-AKI by activating the Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway. Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by organ failure caused by body's abnormal response to infection [1] and accounts for about 20% of deaths worldwide [1, 2]. About 22–53% of septic patients suffer from sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (S-AKI) [3,4], which is associated with a 6–8-fold increase in in-hospital mortality [4], thus placing an immense burden on the healthcare system. No specific targeted therapeutic strategies or interventions are available for S-AKI [5]. Elucidating its pathophysiology and developing effective therapies are essential.

The development of S-AKI is highly complex and involves inflammatory responses, vasoconstriction, production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and cell death [3,6,7]. Renal tubule epithelial cells (RTECs) are the main site of pathogenic effectors of input [8,9], with dysfunction linked to ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis [10–12]. Ferroptosis, first proposed by Dixon et al. in 2012 [13]. It is a new type of regulated cell death marked by excess iron, accumulation of ROS, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial shrinkage with the disruption of cristae [13–15]. Studies have identified several ferroptosis pathways, including the system xc–-GSH-GPX4 pathway [13,16], the GTP cyclohydrolase-1 and tetrahydrobiopterin/dihydrobiopterin [17], and the ferroptosis inhibitory protein 1 (FSP1)-CoQ10 pathway [18–20]. Studies have shown the important role of ferroptosis in the pathophysiological processes of S-AKI [21–24].

Nuclear factor-erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is crucial for regulating oxidative stress by binding to antioxidant response elements (AREs) in the nucleus [25,26]. In a mouse model of S-AKI, irisin was reported to protect against S-AKI by increasing the expression of Nrf2 to inhibit ferroptosis [22]. Guo et al. [27] suggested that ginsenoside Rg1 can suppress ferroptosis by activating the FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in S-AKI. Nrf2 may target FSP1 to influence the progression of ferroptosis and disease [28–30]. Some studies have also found that activating the Nrf2/subunits solute carrier family 7 member 11(SLC7A11) pathway alleviates injury in cardiomyocytes, neurons, and lung tissue by inhibiting ferroptosis [31–34].

Dexmedetomidine (DEX) is a selective agonist of the α2-adrenergic receptor that has good anti-anxiety, sedative, and analgesic effects [35]. Although DEX has been reported to protect against S-AKI [35,36], its mechanism of action is not fully understood and requires further elucidation. Studies have indicated that DEX may protect against hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury and asthma by targeting the Nrf2 pathway [37,38]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway is involved in ferroptosis in S-AKI and that DEX activates this pathway for protection.

Methods

Ethics

The protocols received approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangxi Medical University (no. 202304001). All mouse and cell experiments were conducted following the guidelines established by the National Institutes of Health.

Cell culture and grouping

HK-2 cells (CL-0109, Procell, China) were cultured in a specialized full medium (Pricella, Wuhan, China) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, with daily medium changes. To establish S-AKI cell model, HK-2 cells were sequentially treated with 0, 0.1, 1, 10, or 100 μg/mL LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h to identify the optimal.

The cell experiments were conducted in four parts. In the first part, HK-2 cells were randomly divided to four groups: (A) Control, HK-2 cells were cultured for 24 h in a normal culture medium; (B) LPS, HK-2 cells were treated with 10 µg/mL LPS for 24 h in a normal culture medium [22]. (C) LPS + ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), where HK-2 cells were preincubated with Fer-1 (0.1 μM) for 2 h before being exposed to LPS for 24 h, as described in another study [22]; and (D) LPS + erastin, where HK-2 cells were preincubated with erastin (5 μM) for 2 h before exposure to LPS for 24 h, as described by Lu et al. [39].

In the second part, the HK-2 cells were randomly divided into four groups: (A) control, (B) LPS, (C) LPS + Nrf2 overexpression (OE) (pcSLenti-CMV-NFE2L2 -3xFLAG-PGK-Puro-WPRE3), and (D) LPS + vehicle (GL186 pcSLenti-CMV-MCS-3xFLAG-PGK-Puro-WPRE3). When the cells reached 60–70% confluence, a suitable volume of the viral vector (Obio Technology, Shanghai, China) was mixed with 3 mL of penicillin–streptomycin-free medium supplemented with 20 µL of polybrene (Obio Technology, Shanghai, China) and incubated for 6 h. The medium was then replaced with complete culture medium with puromycin and changed daily until the cells reached 90–100% confluence. The transfection efficiency was assessed via quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR).

In the third part, the cells were randomly assigned to three groups: (A) control, (B) LPS, and (C) LPS + DEX. To determine the optimal DEX concentration for protection, HK-2 cells were incubated with 0, 2.5, 5, 10, or 20 ng/mL DEX (Yangtze River Pharmaceutical Group, China) for 2 h, followed by 24 hours of LPS exposure [36, 40]. Eventually, the HK-2 cells were incubated with 10 ng/mL DEX for 2 h before they were exposed to LPS for 24 h, similar to Zhu et al. [40].

In the fourth part, the cells were randomly divided into five groups: (A) control, (B) LPS, (C) LPS + DEX, (D) LPS + DEX + small interfering RNA Nrf2 (siRNA-Nrf2), and (E) LPS + DEX + siRNA negative control (siRNA-NC). When the cells reached 60–70% confluence, 20 nM siRNA-Nrf2 (sense: 5′-GAGUAAGUCGAGAAGUAUU-3′, antisense: 5′-AAUACUUCUCGACUUACUC-3′; Qingke Biology; Beijing, China) or siRNA-NC (sense: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3′, antisense: 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAA-3′; Qingke Biology; Beijing, China) was mixed with 2 mL of penicillin–streptomycin-free medium supplemented with 5 µL of polybrene (Obio Technology, Shanghai, China) and incubated for 6 h. Afterwards, the medium was replaced with complete culture medium, and the intervention was performed according to the experiment. The transfection efficiency was assessed via qRT-PCR 24 h after intervention.

Animals

A total of 50 male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Animal Care Center of Guangxi Medical University and housed under standard feeding conditions. All mice were housed in a room with temperature regulation (22 ± 2 °C) and a 12-h light/dark cycle. The mice were given standard rodent feed and free access to water. The mice used for experiments were 6–8 weeks old and weighed 20–25 g.

Experimental design

A mouse model of S-AKI was established via CLP, following established methods [41, 42]. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane (RuiWoDe, Shenzhen, China). After disinfecting the skin, a 1 cm incision was made in the midline of the abdomen to expose the cecum. The cecum was ligated between its distal end and the base and punctured with a 21G needle to release some feces. The mice were euthanized 24 hours later via cervical dislocation under anesthesia [22, 43]. Blood and kidney tissue samples were subsequently obtained for further experiments.

In this study, all mice were randomly assigned to one of two experimental groups. In the first part, 15 surviving male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old; weight: 20–25 g) were randomly assigned to three groups: (A) Control, where the mice underwent an abdominal incision and suture without ligation and puncture of the cecum; (B) CLP, (C) CLP + DEX, where 50 μg/kg DEX (dissolved in 0.9% saline) was administered intraperitoneally 30 min before CLP [44].

In the second part, 20 surviving male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old; weight: 20–25 g) were randomly assigned to four groups: (A) Control, (B) CLP, (C) CLP + DEX, (4) CLP + DEX + brusatol. The mice were intraperitoneally injected with 2 mg/kg brusatol every two days for 10 days before CLP [45].

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The kidney injury score was evaluated based on the proportion of kidney tubule vacuolation, interstitial edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration: 0 = none, 1 = 0–20%, 2 = 20–50%, 3 = 50–70%, and 4 > 70% [30, 46]. The detailed method is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, USA) and SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., USA). The data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The differences between multiple groups were determined by performing one-way ANOVA. Tukey’s post hoc test was performed to compare differences between any two groups. Kaplan-Meier survival plots and log-rank tests were used to compare survival results across groups. All differences were considered to be statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Detailed methods are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Primers used for quantitative polymerase chain reaction are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Results

Ferroptosis contributed to LPS-induced cytotoxicity in HK-2 cells

Our results indicated that exposure to 1, 10, and 100 μg/mL LPS significantly increased LDH release in HK-2 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 1(A)). Additionally, 10 μg/mL LPS inhibited cell viability by 50% (Figure 1(B)), making it suitable for establishing an in vitro AKI model. [22]. CCK-8 assays revealed that Fer-1 reduced LPS-induced suppression of cell viability, whereas erastin exacerbated it (Figure 1(C)). While LPS treatment increased LDH release, Fer-1 treatment decreased it. In contrast, erastin treatment further aggravated LDH release in HK-2 cells (Figure 1(D)). TEM (Figure 1(E)) showed the mitochondrial membrane and mitochondrial ridge were intact and prominent in the control group, whereas the LPS group exhibited ferroptotic changes like mitochondrial shrinkage and disruption of cristae. These changes were ameliorated by Fer-1 but exacerbated by erastin. MMP assays indicated that LPS induced a decrease in MMP, while Fer-1 attenuated and erastin exacerbated this reduction (Figure 1(F–G)). In contrast, LPS treatment increased intracellular ROS and lipid ROS in HK-2 cells, which were ameliorated by Fer-1 and aggravated by erastin (Figure 1(H–K)). LPS also triggered iron overload and increased the levels of MDA and 4-HNE, which were decreased by Fer-1 but increased by erastin (Figure 1(L-O)). The levels of GPX4 and GSH were lower in the LPS group than in the control group but were restored in the LPS + Fer-1 group and further decreased in the LPS + erastin group (Figure 1(N–S)). These results suggested that ferroptosis contributed to LPS-induced cytotoxicity in HK-2 cells.

The Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway was downregulated in LPS-induced HK-2 cells

We performed qRT-PCR and WB assays to quantify the levels of expression of Nrf2, SLC7A11, and FSP1 in each experimental group. In the LPS group, the mRNA levels of these genes were significantly lower than in the control group; this reduction was reversed in the LPS + Fer-1 group but exacerbated in the LPS + erastin group (Figure 2(A)). Consistent with the PCR results, the protein levels of total Nrf2, nuclear Nrf2, SLC7A11, and FSP1 showed similar changes (Figure 2(B,C)). Similarly, the intracellular CoQ10 and NADH levels decreased after LPS treatment, which were increased in the LPS + Fer-1 group but decreased in the LPS + erastin group (Figure 2(D,E)). In contrast, LPS treatment increased the NAD+/NADH ratio; this ratio decreased in the LPS + Fer-1 group but increased in the LPS + erastin group (Figure 2(F)). These results indicated that the Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway was downregulated in LPS-induced HK-2 cells.

Nrf2 overexpression alleviated LPS-induced ferroptosis by activating the SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in HK-2 cells

To evaluate the anti-ferroptotic effect of Nrf2 via regulation of the SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in LPS-induced HK-2 cells, a lentivirus was used to overexpress Nrf2. QRT-PCR confirmed successful overexpression (Figure 3(A)). Compared with the LPS group, the LPS + Nrf2 OE group presented greater cell viability (Figure 3(B)) and lower LDH release (Figure 3(C)). Compared with those in the LPS group, the MMP in the LPS + Nrf2 OE group increased, the intracellular ROS and lipid ROS levels decreased, and the iron, MDA, and 4-HNE levels were lower, with higher GPX4 and GSH levels (Figure 3(D–O)). Additionally, in the LPS + Nrf2 OE group, the mRNA and protein levels of Nrf2, SLC7A11, and FSP1 were significantly greater than those in the LPS group, whereas the intracellular CoQ10 and NADH levels were lower and the NAD+/NADH ratio was greater (Figure 3(P–U)). These results suggested that Nrf2 overexpression alleviated LPS-induced ferroptosis by activating the SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in HK-2 cells.

DEX mitigated S-AKI in vitro and in vivo

We first assessed the protective effects of DEX in cellular models. The results of the CCK-8 assay indicated that 10 ng/mL and 20 ng/mL DEX treatment significantly increased the viability of HK-2 cells (Figure 4(A)). LDH assay suggested that these DEX concentrations decreased LDH release in HK-2 cells to a similar extent (Figure 4(B)). Therefore, we selected 10 ng/mL DEX for subsequent cell experiments, in accordance with the findings of another study [40]. The ELISA results revealed that DEX treatment decreased the levels of IL-1β and IL-6 (Figure 4(C,D)), indicating that DEX treatment ameliorated S-AKI in vitro. We subsequently established a CLP-induced S-AKI mouse model to further evaluate the protective effect of DEX in vivo. During 72 h follow-up after the CLP procedure, DEX treatment improved the survival rate from 8.33% to 36.36% (p < 0.05) (Figure 4(E)). H&E staining revealed kidney changes in the mice (Figure 4(F,G)). The control group presented intact renal tubular cells without lesions or inflammation. The CLP group exhibited changes associated with S-AKI, including tubular vacuolar lesions, cell swelling, and inflammatory cell infiltration, which were ameliorated by DEX treatment. In the CLP + DEX group, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), IL-1β and IL-6 levels were lower compared to the CLP group (Figure 4(H–K)). These findings indicated that DEX effectively mitigated S-AKI in vitro and in vivo.

Ferroptosis-related indicators were measured to evaluate the anti-ferroptotic effect of DEX through regulation of the Nrf2/SLC7A11FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in LPS-induced HK-2 cells. TEM images showed that DEX alleviated LPS-induced mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae disruption (Figure 5(F)). MMP assays indicated that DEX reversed the LPS-induced decrease in the MMP (Figure 5(G–H)). In addition, in the LPS + DEX group, the levels of intracellular ROS, lipid ROS, iron content, MDA and 4-HNE were reduced, whereas the levels of GPX4 and GSH were increased compared with those in the LPS group (Figure 5(I–R)). Next, the qRT-PCR and WB results revealed that Nrf2, SLC7A11, and FSP1 expression levels were significantly greater in the LPS + DEX group than in the LPS group (Figure 5(S–U)). Similarly, the CoQ10 and intracellular NADH levels were restored in the LPS + DEX group compared to their respective levels in the LPS group (Figure 5(V–W)), while the NAD+/NADH ratio was higher (Figure 5(X)). These results showed that DEX mitigated S-AKI by inhibiting ferroptosis by activating the Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in HK-2 cells.

DEX mitigated S-AKI by inhibiting ferroptosis by activating the Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in mice

In the S-AKI mouse model, the CLP group exhibited ferroptotic changes like mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae disruption, which were ameliorated by DEX treatment (Figure 6(A)). CLP increased ROS, iron, MDA, and 4HNE levels, whereas DEX reduced these effects (Figure 6(B–G)). GPX4 and GSH levels were decreased in the CLP group and restored with DEX treatment (Figure 6(F–K)). In the CLP + DEX group, the expression levels of Nrf2, SLC7A11, and FSP1 mRNAs and proteins, as well as intrarenal CoQ10 and NADH were higher; in contrast, the NAD+/NADH ratio was lower compared to the CLP group (Figure 6(L–Q)). These results suggested that DEX mitigated S-AKI by inhibiting ferroptosis by activating the Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in mice.

Nrf2 knockdown reversed the anti-ferroptotic effect of DEX on S-AKI by regulating the SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in HK-2 cells

To further elucidate the mechanism underlying the protective effect of DEX against S-AKI, we used siRNA-Nrf2 to knock down the expression of Nrf2 in HK-2 cells. Nrf2 expression was significantly lower in HK-2 cells, as determined by the results of qRT-PCR assays (Figure 7(A)). The LPS + DEX + siRNA-Nrf2 group had lower MMP (Figure 7(B,C)) but higher intracellular ROS, lipid ROS, iron, MDA, and 4HNE levels compared to the LPS + DEX group (Figure 7(D–K)). GPX4 and GSH levels were lower in the LPS + DEX + siRNA-Nrf2 group than in the LPS + DEX group (Figure 7(J–M)). In the LPS + DEX + siRNA-Nrf2 group, Nrf2, SLC7A11, and FSP1 mRNA and protein levels were lower, while intracellular CoQ10 and NADH levels were higher, and the NAD+/NADH ratio was lower compared to the LPS + DEX group (Figure 7(N–S)). These results indicated that knocking down Nrf2 reversed the anti-ferroptotic effect of DEX on S-AKI by regulating the SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in HK-2 cells.

Brusatol abolished the anti-ferroptotic effect of DEX on S-AKI by regulating the SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in mice

In the S-AKI mouse model, brusatol, an Nrf2 inhibitor, was used to abolish the protective effect of DEX. The survival rate in the CLP + DEX + Brusatol group was 18.18%, which was less than the 27.27% survival rate in the CLP + DEX group (Figure 8(A)). CLP induced increases in ROS, iron, MDA, and 4HNE levels, which were mitigated by DEX but reversed by brusatol (Figure 8(B–G)). In contrast, GPX4 and GSH levels were lower in the CLP + DEX + Brusatol group than in the CLP + DEX group (Figure 8(F–I)). Next, DEX treatment restored the mRNA and protein levels of Nrf2, SLC7A11, and FSP1, brusatol treatment reversed these effects (Figure 8(J–L)). The intrarenal CoQ10 and NADH levels were higher, whereas the NAD+/NADH ratio was lower in the CLP + DEX + Brusatol group than in the CLP + DEX group (Figure 8(M–O)). These results suggested that brusatol administration eliminated the anti-ferroptotic effect of DEX on S-AKI by regulating the SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in mice.

Discussion

S-AKI is a life-threatening complication with high morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients [3,47]. The underlying mechanisms remain unclear, highlighting the need for effective treatments. Multimicrobial sepsis caused by CLP is the most commonly used model because its progression and characteristics are similar to those of human sepsis [48]. CLP-induced S-AKI involves changes in blood and urine biomarkers and kidney histology, including elevated BUN, Cr, and inflammatory cytokines, as well as renal tubular vacuolar degeneration, cellular swelling, and inflammatory cell infiltration [22,30,49]. Therefore, in this study, we performed CLP to induce S-AKI in mice, as confirmed by increased IL-1β, IL-6, BUN, and Cr levels and S-AKI-related histological changes, which aligns with previous studies [22,23,30]. In vitro, 10 μg/mL LPS was selected to establish the S-AKI model, reducing cell viability by about 50%, which is consistent with other studies [22,50].

Ferroptosis involves the accumulation of intracellular ferrous and lipid peroxidation. It is characterized by mitochondrial shrinkage and the loss of ridge rupture [13–15]. Lipid peroxidation causes the polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes to produce lipid hydroperoxides and their derivatives MDA and 4-HNE [21]. GPX4 is crucial for inhibiting iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation [51,52]. GSH depletion can lead to iron-dependent accumulation of ROS, especially lipid ROS [52]. The antioxidants GSH and GPX4 are downregulated in ferroptosis [21,51,52]. In this study, S-AKI triggered ferroptosis by inducing iron overload, ROS accumulation, an increase in 4HNE and MDA levels, and a decrease in GPX4 and GSH levels in vitro and in vivo. However, administering Fer-1, a specific and potent ferroptosis inhibitor, alleviated LPS-induced ferroptosis in vitro in HK-2 cells. In contrast, erastin, a specific and potent ferroptosis inducer, aggravated these effects. These results strongly suggested that ferroptosis plays a key role in the development of S-AKI, which is consistent with previous studies [22,23,30].

System xc– is a cysteine/glutamate antiporter that plays a crucial role in regulating the degree of ferroptosis. SLC7A11, a key component of system xc–, is predominantly localized at the brush border membrane of renal tubules [51]. Inhibiting SLC7A11 exacerbates kidney injury by promoting ferroptosis, as reported in models, like chronic kidney disease, renal cell carcinoma, and cisplatin-induced AKI [53–55]. Additionally, regulating SLC7A11 by transcription factors significantly affects disease progression. Nrf2 is a key transcription factor that maintains the redox state by activating downstream target genes and preventing the accumulation of active substances [25,26,56]. During oxidative stress, Nrf2 dissociates from inactivated Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), translocates to the nucleus, binds to AREs to initiate gene transcription [25,26]. As the ARE gene regulates several metabolic pathways that are important for ferroptosis, including iron metabolism, GSH metabolism, and NADPH production, Nrf2 is an important regulator of ferroptosis [57–60]. In an ischemia-reperfusion-induced acute lung injury mouse model, Nrf2 and SLC7A11 levels were suppressed; overexpression of Nrf2 increased SLC7A11 levels, whereas silencing Nrf2 significantly decreased them [61]. In a hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury model (in vitro and in vivo), Nrf2 and SLC7A11 expression levels were lower, and knocking down Nrf2 substantially decreased SLC7A11 expression and reversed the anti-ferroptotic effects of dimethyl fumarate [62]. Similarly, in this study, Nrf2 and SLC7A11 mRNAs and proteins levels were reduced in S-AKI models both in vitro and in vivo, whereas Fer-1 mitigated and erastin exacerbated these reductions, respectively. Compared with the LPS treatment, the use of a lentivirus to overexpress Nrf2 in HK-2 cells resulted in significantly greater expression of Nrf2 and SLC7A11 mRNAs and proteins. Additionally, ferroptosis-related indicators, such as ion content, MDA, 4HNE, and ROS accumulation were restored, and the mitochondrial ultrastructure was improved in the LPS + Nrf2 OE group compared to that in the LPS group, suggesting that Nrf2 may target SLC7A11 to regulate ferroptosis in S-AKI.

FSP1, also known as apoptosis-inducing factor mitochondrial 2 (AIFM2), is an NAD(P)-dependent CoQ oxidoreductase [63,64]. Unlike GPX4, FSP1 does not rely on GSH [18–20,63,64]. It converts CoQ10 into an antioxidant, creating a radical-trapping antioxidant that prevents lipid peroxide buildup and suppresses ferroptosis [64]. The N-myristoylation of FSP1 facilitates its attachment to the cell membrane and is essential for converting oxidative CoQ10 to its reductive form [63,64]. Inhibiting the FSP1/CoQ10 pathway was found to exacerbate ferroptosis in models of subarachnoid hemorrhage [65] and retinal degeneration [66]. Guo et al. [27] found that ginsenoside Rg1 suppresses ferroptosis in S-AKI via the FSP1/CoQ10 pathway in HK-2 cells. Similarly, in this study, S-AKI was found to induce ferroptosis by decreasing FSP1, CoQ10, and NADH levels and increasing the NAD+/NADH ratio, which was reversed by Fer-1 treatment. Similar to SLC7A11, FSP1 regulation by transcription factors affects disease progression. Nrf2 may regulate the transcription of FSP1 by binding to its promoter region. In a sodium aescinate-induced nephrotoxicity model, Nrf2 was suppressed, while its overexpression activated the FSP1/CoQ10 pathway, alleviating ferroptosis [28]. In a KEAP1-deficient lung cancer cell model, FSP1 served as a key downstream effector of Nrf2 [67]. Zhang et al. [30] reported that LPS decreased CoQ10 levels in HK-2 cells, whereas Nrf2 siRNA reversed this reduction. Consistent with previous studies [28,30,67], we used a lentivirus to overexpress Nrf2 in HK-2 cells and found that compared to the LPS group, the LPS + Nrf2 OE group showed higher cell viability, lower levels of ferroptosis, and greater translocation of Nrf2 to the nucleus. Additionally, the overexpression of Nrf2 increased FSP1 expression while reducing intracellular CoQ10 and NADH levels compared with those in LPS group. These results suggested that increased FSP1 may increase CoQ10 and NADH consumption in HK-2 cells, as reported in previous studies [27,66]. Overexpression of Nrf2 increased the NAD+/NADH ratio compared to the LPS group, suggesting that increased FSP1 levels led to NADH consumption and metabolite (NAD+) production, aligning with previous studies [27,66]. To summarize, Nrf2 may target FSP1 to regulate ferroptosis in S-AKI.

In this study, we investigated the protective effect of DEX against S-AKI in vitro and in vivo. The results showed that DEX treatment effectively reduced IL-1β and IL-6 levels and mitigated histological pathological kidney injury, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [68,69]. Additionally, DEX treatment ameliorated mitochondrial shrinkage and disruption of cristae, attenuated the decrease in the MMP, restored the intracellular ROS, lipid ROS, iron content, MDA, and 4HNE levels, and increased the GPX4 and GSH levels compared to those in the LPS group. These results suggested that DEX protected against S-AKI by inhibiting ferroptosis.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which DEX counteracts ferroptosis in S-AKI, we designed experiments to determine whether DEX can activate Nrf2 and subsequently promote the expression of SLC7A11 and FSP1. The results of the docking experiment indicated a direct and strong interaction between DEX and Nrf2. DEX treatment significantly facilitated nuclear translocation of Nrf2 in vitro and in vivo, which was consistent with previous studies on the protective effects of DEX in asthma [37], and myocardial and hepatic ischemia/reperfusion [38,70,71]. Compared to the LPS/CLP group, DEX treatment significantly increased the levels of SLC7A11, FSP1, CoQ10, and NADH and decreased the NAD+/NADH ratio in vitro and in vivo. Knocking down Nrf2 expression using siRNA in HK-2 cells and using the Nrf2 inhibitor brusatol in mice eliminating the protective effect of DEX, as shown by downregulated Nrf2, SLC7A11, and FSP1 mRNA and protein levels. Additionally, knocking down of Nrf2 expression in HK-2 cells or brusatol treatment in mice increased CoQ10 and NADH levels compared with those in the LPS + DEX/CLP + DEX groups. These results indicated that inhibiting Nrf2 expression can reverse the increase in FSP1 levels after DEX treatment, thereby reducing CoQ10 and NADH consumption and increasing CoQ10 and NADH levels, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [27,66]. In contrast, Nrf2 knockdown in HK-2 cells or brusatol treatment in mice further reduced the NAD+/NADH ratio compared with that in the LPS + DEX/CLP + DEX group. These results suggested that inhibiting Nrf2 expression can reverse the increase in FSP1 levels after DEX treatment, subsequently leading to fewer NAD + metabolic products, which is in line with the findings of previous studies [27,66]. These findings suggested that DEX mitigated S-AKI by inhibiting ferroptosis, via activating the Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway.

Despite these findings, our study has several limitations. First, gene knockdown was not conducted in vivo. Furthermore, we did not use an alpha 2-adrenergic receptor antagonist to rule out the involvement of α2-adrenoceptors. However, we manifested that DEX exerted anti-ferroptotic effect by activating Nrf2.

In summary, our study revealed that the Nrf2/SLC7A11/FSP1/CoQ10 pathway is involved in the ferroptosis in S-AKI. DEX can protect against S-AKI by inhibiting ferroptosis by activating this pathway. The key findings are summarized in the graphical abstract (Figure 9). This study provides compelling evidence supporting the effectiveness of DEX in treating S-AKI and highlights potential therapeutic targets.
