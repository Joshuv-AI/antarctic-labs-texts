Bowel ischemia following extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR): case reports and narrative review
Authors: Yanzhu Chen, Shaoyan Mo, Minghan Deng, Hanwen He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) is a life-saving strategy for refractory cardiac arrest (CA), but the mortality rate is still high. Bowel ischemia is associated with the clinical outcomes, and few studies on bowel ischemia following ECPR have been reported.MethodsWe reported 2 cases of bowel ischemia following ECPR. And we searched literature to describe the incidence, clinical features, and outcomes of bowel ischemia following ECPR. Demographic features, risk factors, clinical features, diagnostic methods, treatment and outcome were analyzed.ResultsWe described 2 patients developed bowel ischemia with recurred hematochezia at different phases post ECPR, conservative treatments were not effective, one patient received colectomy after extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) weaning and recovered, and the other died without surgery. Our literature search found 3 publications. Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia(NOMI) was more common for bowel ischemia following ECPR, and the incidence of NOMI in ECPR patients seemed higher than that in conventional CPR patients. Postmortem findings demonstrated signs of bowel ischemia within hours of ECPR. The incidence of bowel ischemia following ECPR could not be identified due to the variability among the studies.ConclusionBowel ischemia may initiate early post ECPR, or occur insidiously during the therapeutic process, thus clinicians should have a high suspicion and low threshold for CT scan and endoscopy. Surgical timing for advanced bowel ischemia should be individualized.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12245-026-01327-6.

Introduction

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) has been increasingly accepted as a life-saving strategy for refractory cardiac arrest (CA), due to its benefit over conventional cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) on improving the survival rates and favorable neurological outcomes [1–3]. However, the mortality rate of ECPR is still quite high [1]. The mortality of ECPR is closely associated with the development of the post-cardiac arrest syndrome (PCAS) characterized by microcirculatory dysfunction, damage of the vascular endothelium, which can lead to multi-organ failure and death [4]. Among the organs, gastrointestinal (GI) tract has been paid little attention. According to previous studies, bowel ischemia is associated with the clinical outcomes [5, 6]. Currently, there is no standard for the management of bowel ischaemia after CA, and data on bowel ischemia following ECPR is limited [7]. This work aims to report 2 cases and a narrative review on bowel ischemia following ECPR, and discuss the characteristics of bowel ischemia following ECPR and therapeutic regimens, providing a better understanding of bowel ischemia following ECPR.

Materials and methods

Search strategies and study selection

Literature search was performed in PubMed and Cochrane Library. Papers published in English between January 1, 2016, to January 1, 2026, were included. The literature was searched using keywords of [(bowel ischemia OR ischemic bowel OR intestinal ischemia OR intestinal injury) AND (ecpr OR cardiac arrest OR resuscitation OR extracorporeal)]. After first evaluation of the paper, duplicates were removed. Full text papers were evaluated and selected by two independent authors (Fig. 1). All the authors approved this selection process.

Inclusion criteria

Case reports, case series, observational studies and animal experiment describing the incidence, clinical features, management and outcomes of bowel ischemia developed after initiation of ECPR were included in the narrative review. Clinical studies with patients < 18 years of age, and reviews were excluded. Demographic features, risk factors, clinical features, diagnostic methods, treatment and outcome were analyzed.

Case definitions

The diagnosis of bowel ischemia is confirmed according to the findings of computed tomography (CT), endoscopy, surgery and/or histomorphology. CT patterns include bowel-wall thickening or thinning with distension, lack of parietal enhancement, parietal pneumatosis, portal gas, and perforation [8]. The presentations of digestive endoscopy include: grade 1, mucosal oedema and erythema; grade 2, non-necrotic ulcerations on an oedematous mucosa; and grade 3, necrosis with grey-black mucosal discoloration [8].

Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia(NOMI) accounts for 20–30% of overall bowel ischaemia [9, 10]. The diagnostic criteria of NOMI include the absence of of mesenteric arterial or venous occlusion, ischemic and necrotic spots in non-consecutive segments, and ischemic changes without fibrin plugs in the small veins [9, 10].

Results

Case report

Case 1

A 52-year-old male with history of hypertension was admitted because of chest pain for 10 min, and a quick electrocardiogram suggested ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. After taking loading doses of aspirin, ticagrelor, and rosuvastatin, the patient underwent an emergency coronary angiography, which revealed that the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) was 100% occluded. During the procedure, the patient suddenly presented refractory CA, ECPR was started, and the resuscitation duration before ECMO initiation was 67 min. After ECMO initiation, the patient regained the spontaneous circulation, requiring norepinephrine at 0.2–0.3 µg/kg/min, and turned consciousness after 3 h. On day 2, bloody gastric fluid positive for occult blood was noted from the nasogastric tube, without obvious worsening in hemodynamics, which improved on day 3 after administration of proton pump inhibitor (PPI). Unfractionated heparin was initiated on day 3, maintaining activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) at 40–60 s. Enteral nutrition was initiated on day 4. Meanwhile, the patient’s hemodynamics and cardiac function gradually improved, and the parameters of ECMO support accordingly decreased. On day 7, the patient presented diarrhae, without fever or shock. On day 8, the patient had bloody stools accompanied by abdominal distention, hypotension (Supplemental Table 1). Bedside emergency colonoscopy revealed that the entire transverse colon and ascending colon were congestion and swelling, with scattered erosions and ulcers covered with yellow-white exudate and necrotic debris, and there was mucosal swelling, necrosis, discoloration and localized bleeding in the ascending colon near the cecum (Fig. 2A−2B). Gastroscopy did not show obvious ischemia or necrosis. A consequent abdominal computed tomography angiography (CTA) on day 8 showed thickening and edema in the walls of the ascending colon, transverse colon, and descending colon, lack of parietal enhancement in the wall of the ascending colon, and no obvious sign of arterial or venous occlusion was observed (Fig. 3A−3B). Considering the patient’s coagulopathy and the high risk of bleeding during ECMO support, we chose conservative management. The patient weaned from ECMO on day 11. However, the patient still had recurrent hematochezia. The patient was transferred to another hospital, and received right hemicolectomy on day 28. On day 41, hematochezia recurred again, and endoscopic hemostasis was administered, then the patient improved, and discharged on day 56. A six-month follow-up revealed that the patient was living a normal life.

Case 2

A 41-year-old male with history of smoking was admitted to the emergency department because of witnessed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) for 30 min, ECPR was initiated, and the resuscitation duration before ECMO initiation was 95 min. The patient recovered the spontaneous circulation after ECMO running for 1 h, still comatose, needing norepinephrine at 1.0–1.2 µg/kg/min. On admission to the ICU, bloody gastric fluid positive for occult blood was observed from the nasogastric tube, which resolved by day 2, and trophic enteral nutrition was initiated. Unfractionated heparin was intravenously pumped, maintaining APTT at 42–58 s. The patient’s hemodynamics and cardiac function improved gradually, and weaned from ECMO on day 9. On day 24, the patient started to have hematochezia, with decreased hemoglobin (Hb) level and unstable hemodynamics (Supplemental Table 1). Bedside emergency colonoscopy demonstrated extensive mucosal erosion, discoloration in the ileal mucosa near the cecum, with local blood clot adhesion, and the length of the lesion was approximately 15 cm, oozing was noted at the ischemia regions (Fig. 2C−2D). Abdominal CTA on day 24 revealed contrast extravasation in the terminal ileum without sign of arterial or venous occlusion (Fig. 3C-3D). The patient’s hemodynamics improved after conservative therapy, but deteriorated again on day 27, with bloody stools and a drop in Hb level. A second bedside colonoscopy revealed findings comparable to the first examination, without any exposed blood vessels. The patient’s relatives refused surgical treatment. The patient presented recurrent hematochezia with decreased Hb level despite endoscopic and drug hemostasis, and blood transfusion, and died on day 46.

Review on reports of bowel ischemia

Our literature search of databases (PubMed, and Cochrane Library) yielded 130 reports. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, related publications and references were included and analyzed, remaining 3 publications to be evaluated (Fig. 1).

The papers included 1 prospective study, 1 retrospective study and 1 case report, containing 100 patients treated with ECPR (Table 1). In Jana Smalcova’s study, a sub-study of the Prague OHCA study, 89 patients were treated with ECPR for refractory OHCA, and 46 (51.7%) patients presented signs of intestinal damage within 12 h of admission, being diagnosed as possible NOMI [11]. Besides, the incidence of possible NOMI in patients treated with ECPR was higher than those treated with standard CPR (51.7% vs. 16.5%) [11]. Martina Focardi and the colleagues reported postmortem findings from 10 patients who died after ECPR, and the average duration of ECMO support was 257.4 min (range from 35 min to 24 h) [12]. Bowel ischemic lesions were found in 8 (80%) patients, and extensive mucosal necrosis and haemorrhage were found in 70% patients [12]. Mary Huerter reported a case of occlusive acute mesenteric ischemia with onset on day 8 [13].

Research on bowel ischemia following ECPR

Discussion

Bowel ischemia negatively impacts the outcomes of ECPR patients, but few studies have been reported. To provide a better understanding, we present 2 cases of bowel ischemia following ECPR and a narrative review including 1 prospective study, 1 retrospective study and 1 case report, and discuss the clinical characteristics, diagnosis and treatment for bowel ischemia following ECPR.

The intestine is sensitive to systemic ischemia/reperfusion injury following CA and resuscitation [14]. According to Bjørn Hoftun Farbu’s report, the incidence of intestinal injury after non-traumatic CA was 82% to 100%, and the incidence of NOMI was 0.7% to 2.5% [5, 8]. CT scan showed 6.6% of OHCA patients had bowel ischemia within 24 h [6]. The incidence of bowel ischemia was 9% in patients receiving ECMO support for refractory cardiogenic shock or CA [15]. In our work, we report 2 cases and a narrative review on bowel ischemia following ECPR, involving 102 patients. However, due to the heterogeneity across the studies included in design, population, diagnostic criteria, and timing of assessment, an incidence of bowel ischemia following ECPR can not be established in this work, needing further investigations. In our two cases, CTA imaging show no evidence of arterial or venous occlusion, while colonoscopy demonstrate necrosis, fulfilling the diagnosis criteria of NOMI. Bowel ischemia following ECPR includes occlusive mesenteric ischemia and NOMI, and NOMI is more common, which is consist with previous studies [16, 17]. Bowel ischemia following ECPR is mainly contributed by the low-flow within the intestinal circulation and high-dose vasoconstrictor agents given during resuscitation [9, 18–20]. Meanwhile, ECMO support might increase the risk of bowel ischemia, but the exact mechanism has not been elucidated [11].

Nowadays, the diagnosis of bowel ischemia following ECPR is suspected mainly based on the clinical signs, confirmed by endoscopy, CT scan, and surgical exploration [8, 12]. The symptoms of bowel ischemia following ECPR are variable, including abdominal pain, vomiting, bleeding, diarrhea, abdominal distention, etc [8]. These symptoms are often not readily apparent, and patients receiving ECPR are usually unconscious or sedated, leading to bowel ischemia being overlooked [9]. The histology findings in patients treated with ECPR have showed that bowel ischemia may initiate within hours [12]. The two patients in our cases report presented with bowel ischemia manifesting primarily as hematochezia on day 8 and day 24, respectively. According to Marie Renaudier’s study, for patients with refractory shock, the delay between V-A ECMO initiation and mesenteric ischaemia (occlusive and non-occlusive) suspicion is four (2–7) days [15]. Therefore, diagnosis of bowel ischemia following ECPR early is challenging. Patients suffering from NOMI post OHCA have higher median lactate levels, a greater median base deficit and a lower median pH at admission [21]. Marine Paul and colleagues have pointed out that female sex, cardiovascular comorbidities, and factors reflecting the severity of the ischaemia/reperfusion syndrome (low flow duration, initial lactate, and inotropic score) are independently associated with NOMI [8]. However, these involved parameters are nonspecific, and no threshold of diagnostic relevance has yet been defined. Previous studies have showed that intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP) is associated with mesenteric ischemia [7, 22]. I-FABP is a small cytoplasmic protein specific to enterocytes, it will be released into plasma in response to intestinal injury after CA. However, the half-life of I-FABP is short, making its sensitivity for diagnosing acute mesenteric ischemia declines with distance from the ischemic event [23]. And the detection of I-FABP has not yet been widely implemented in clinical practice. Jonathan Tam’s study has revealed that early CT scan (4.1[2.7–5.7] hours after CA) can identify bowel ischemia in patients post OHCA [6]. MARIA A MAZZEI and colleagues have confirmed that multidetector CT can distinguish NOMI with reperfusion basing on the vessels futures, mesenteric, bowel and peritoneal CT findings, helping early diagnosis and decision for therapy, which is consist with Reiko Woodhams’s findings [10, 24]. CT scan can effectively reflect perfusion status and reveal early bowel ischemia. Given that CT is already a routine non-invasive tool, it can be used as a diagnostic tool for early detection of bowel ischemia. Despite being invasive, digestive endoscopy is feasible at the bedside, while CT scan necessitates transferring outside the ICU, thus digestive endoscopy may offer a good alternative for ECPR patients.

Reviewing the two cases reported in this work (Supplemental Table 1), both patients presented bloody gastric fluid soon after resuscitation, neither endoscopy of abdominal CT scan was performed until bloody stools were observed. Furthermore, the patient of our second case presented bloody stools on day 24, which is later than those reported in previous studies [11, 15]. No obvious abdominal signs prior to the bloody stools were observed, neither CT or colonoscopy was performed, thus we were unable to identify the time of onset of bowel ischemia for the second patient. According to previous findings, factors such as a second shock, vasoconstrictor agents, infection, enteral nutrition, and mechanical support (like ECMO, renal replacement therapy) after ECPR initiation may constitute a second hit to the gut [8, 11, 15]. Bowel ischemia following ECPR may initiate early after ECPR, or occur insidiously later. Accordingly, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion, and have a low threshold for CT scan and digestive endoscopy.

Bowel ischemia following ECPR can be fatal. The mortality rate of NOMI post OHCA is about 50%, and the mortality rate of bowel ischemia in refractory shock on VA-ECMO can be 100% [9, 15]. There is a paucity of reports on the therapeutic regimens for bowel ischemia following ECPR, especially for the treatment of NOMI [11]. Conservative treatment may be effective for bowel ischemia in an early stage [9, 15]. However, bowel ischemia is frequently overlooked due to its nonspecific clinical manifestations and laboratory abnormalities, leading to delayed recognition until advanced stages for which conservative treatments would have no place. In Anna Taieb’s retrospective study, bowel ischemia was the most common indication for ECMO patients receiving emergency abdominal surgery, 77% patients presented postoperative bleeding, and 20% patients needed reinvention for hemorrhage [25]. ECMO support may aggravate the coagulation disorders post ECPR, increasing the risk of bleeding for surgery, which makes the management of bowel ischemia more tough. In our case 1, conservative treatment was not effective for the patient, he had recurred hematochezia until hemicolectomy post-ECMO weaning. When surgery is indicated, the timing should be individualized based on surgical risk, hemodynamics, infection, coagulation, and ECMO support. Under certain conditions, it may be optimal to delay the surgery for NOMI until the patient achieves better statue, for example, after ECMO weaning.

Our study has several limitations. The studies included in this narrative review are variable in design, methods, and the studies are of small sample, no conclusion on the incidence, clinical characteristics can be inferred. Besides, in our cases, assessment and monitoring on GI tract are limited, failing to comprehensively reflect the onset and progression of bowel ischemia. Given the limited data from this work involving 2 cases and a narrative review of 3 publications, conclusions on diagnosis and treatment can not be generalized.

Conclusions

We present 2 cases of bowel ischemia following ECPR and a narrative review including 3 publications. The incidence can not be identified due to the variability among the studies. NOMI is more common for bowel ischemia following ECPR, and ECMO support might increase the risk of bowel ischemia. Bowel ischemia may initiate in an early phase post ECPR, or occur insidiously during the therapeutic process, clinicians should be vigilant for it. CT scan may be helpful for early diagnosis of bowel ischemia, and digestive endoscopy should not be delayed. Bowel ischemia following ECPR can be fatal, and surgery is important for the treatment of advanced bowel ischemia. The timing of surgery should be individualized based on the patient’s clinical condition. Given the small sample sizes and heterogeneity of the studies included in this work, larger-scale studies are needed.
