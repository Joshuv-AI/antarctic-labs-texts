Introduction

Fetus-in-fetu (FIF) is a rare congenital anomaly of embryogenesis in which a malformed or parasitic twin is enclosed within the body of its host[1]. The estimated incidence is approximately 1 in 500 000 live births, with a male predominance of nearly 2:1[2,3]. FIF represents an uncommon cause of abdominal mass and is classically distinguished from teratoma by the presence of a vertebral column, limb buds, or other organized organ systems.

The first case of FIF was described by Meckel in 1800, and in 1953, Willis defined it more precisely as a mass containing a vertebral axis along with other well-differentiated organs[4]. FIF is usually diagnosed in infancy, with a presumptive diagnosis made using ultrasonography, plain radiography, computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

We report a case of FIF in an infant, with the aim of highlighting its clinical features, the diagnostic and management challenges faced in low-resource settings, and the critical importance of timely referral to specialized centers for early detection and effective treatment.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE Guidelines 2025 criteria[6].

Case presentation

A 3-month-old boy presented to the outpatient clinic with complaints of irritability and progressive abdominal distention localized to the left upper quadrant since birth. On physical examination, the patient was vitally stable and afebrile. However, a well-defined, semi-solid mass measuring 8 cm × 9 cm was palpable in the left upper quadrant of the abdomen. It was immobile, non-tender, and exhibited fluctuation.

An abdomen contrast-enhanced CT scan following ultrasonography was performed, which showed a large, well-defined heterogeneous lesion in the left upper quadrant of the abdomen, extending into the left lumbar and epigastric regions, measuring 7 × 6 × 4.7 cm, with resultant displacement of the left kidney, spleen, and colon. The features were suggestive of either neuroblastoma or teratoma (Fig. 1). Laboratory investigations were within normal limits. Elective laparotomy was planned and discussed with the patient’s parents. Under general anesthesia, a lower midline incision was performed, and the peritoneal cavity was opened. An encapsulated retroperitoneal mass was identified on the left side of the abdomen, displacing the left kidney, spleen, and pancreas. Careful dissection allowed complete excision of the mass, which was sent for histopathological evaluation. Grossly, the specimen contained soft tissue, fat, fluid, and bony elements, including structures resembling a spine, ribs, facial bones, and upper limbs (Fig. 2). The histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of FIF. The postoperative period was uneventful. Oral feeding was started on the second postoperative day and was well tolerated. The patient was discharged in stable condition on the fourth postoperative day, and follow-up at 6 months demonstrated a good clinical outcome. Discussion

FIF is a rare congenital anomaly in which a parasitic monozygotic diamniotic twin is enclosed within the body of its host[1]. Typically, a single parasitic twin is identified, although multiple inclusions have also been reported. The exact pathogenesis remains unclear. It has been suggested that during the second to third week of embryonic development, abnormal ventral folding allows the inclusion of one twin within its sibling[7]. Persistent vitelline duct anastomosis during development has also been proposed as a contributing factor[8]. Another theory attributes FIF to unequal division of the totipotent inner cell mass of the developing blastocyst, resulting in the incorporation of a smaller cell mass within the host embryo[9].

Genetic marker studies, including blood group typing, sex chromosome analysis, protein polymorphisms, and DNA markers, have demonstrated genetic identity between the host infant and the fetiform mass, supporting its monozygotic origin[10,11].

Most FIF lesions are diagnosed in early childhood and occur predominantly in the retroperitoneum[2,12], although rare cases have been reported in the cranial cavity, liver, pelvis, scrotum, and mediastinum[4,5,13,14]. In our case, the lesion was retroperitoneal, which is consistent with the most common presentation.

Clinically, FIF is usually benign, with symptoms arising from mass effect. These may include abdominal distention, feeding difficulties, nausea, urinary problems, or respiratory compromise due to compression of adjacent structures. In our case, the patient presented with irritability and abdominal distention.

The differential diagnosis of FIF includes neuroblastoma, meconium pseudocyst, and teratoma[15]. These entities may present with calcifications or ossifications on imaging studies. In contrast, intra-abdominal FIF is typically encapsulated within a fluid-filled sac and attached by a single pedicle. Commonly identified structures include a vertebral column, dermal tissue, limbs, gastrointestinal segments, and portions of the central nervous system[16]. Rarely, gonads, adrenal glands, heart tissue, or primitive respiratory elements may also be present.

Radiological investigations, such as ultrasound, X-ray, CT scan, and MRI, are useful diagnostic methods for early diagnosis and favorable outcomes. Ultrasonography and plain radiography can detect suspicious masses but often cannot provide a definitive diagnosis. CT and MRI are more accurate in delineating the anatomy, confirming the presence of organized skeletal elements, and guiding surgical planning[17]. In the present case, ultrasonography and CT revealed a heterogeneous mass suggestive of neuroblastoma or teratoma, which was later confirmed as FIF after surgical excision and histopathological evaluation.

The mainstay of treatment for intra-abdominal FIF is complete surgical excision. Since histopathological confirmation is essential, removal of all components is necessary to rule out malignant transformation and reduce the risk of recurrence[17]. Postoperative follow-up, including screening with tumor markers such as alpha-fetoprotein and beta-hCG, is recommended, given the rare association of FIF with malignancy[18]. In our patient, laparotomy via a midline incision allowed complete excision of the mass, and histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of FIF. The postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient remained well at the 6-month follow-up.

To our knowledge, only one previous case of FIF has been reported from our center, making this the second documented case in Afghanistan. The country faces a critical shortage of pediatric surgical and pathological services, with almost no specialists available outside the capital, particularly in governmental hospitals. In such circumstances, congenital masses are often misdiagnosed or inadequately managed due to a lack of facilities. Our case underscores the importance of meticulous imaging and surgical planning while highlighting the urgent need to strengthen diagnostic and surgical capacity in low-resource environments. Improved awareness, early recognition, and timely referral are essential not only to determine the true incidence and prevalence of FIF in Afghanistan but also to improve outcomes for infants with complex congenital anomalies.

Conclusion

This case underscores the challenges of diagnosing and managing FIF in resource-limited settings. Raising awareness among healthcare providers at primary and secondary facilities, together with early recognition and timely referral, is essential not only for improving patient outcomes but also for establishing the true incidence and prevalence of this rare condition in Afghanistan and similar developing countries.
