Molecular Mechanisms of Growth and Salt Tolerance in Diverse Rice Cultivars Balanced by Rhodobium marinum RH-AZ
Authors: Cheng Xu, Yang Gao, Tao Tang, Xiao Xie, Junjie Chen, Deyong Zhang, Jing Peng, Qianjun Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalt stress severely limits rice productivity, and plant–microbial interaction offer a sustainable solution. This study investigated the molecular mechanisms by which Rhodobium marinum RH-AZ treatment enhances salt tolerance in salt-sensitive cultivar 9311 and moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931 rice cultivars. An integrated transcriptomic and physiological analysis revealed that RH-AZ treatment significantly alleviated salt-induced growth inhibition and oxidative damage in both cultivars. Crucially, the regulatory mechanisms exhibited distinct cultivar-dependent patterns. In the moderately tolerant cultivar 3931, RH-AZ treatment mobilized a broader array of stress-responsive genes and optimized the trade-off between growth and defense by modulating the IAA/ABA balance and fine-tuning JA/SA signaling. In contrast, the salt-sensitive cultivar 9311 showed a more limited transcriptional response with less effective hormonal regulation. Furthermore, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) identified a core module associated with diterpenoid biosynthesis as a key conserved pathway enhanced by RH-AZ traetment. Collectively, these findings suggest that RH-AZ treatment enhances rice salt stress adaptation by integrating basal defense potentiation, hormonal homeostasis fine-tuning, and secondary metabolism activation, with the moderately tolerant cultivar 3931 possessing a superior genetic plasticity to leverage these microbial stimuli.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00894-9.Keywords: Salt stress, Rhodobium marinum, RNA-seq, Plant hormones, Plant–microbial interaction

Introduction

Soil salinization, exacerbated by climate change and intensive agricultural practices, has emerged as a major threat to global food security, particularly impacting salt-sensitive crops such as rice. As a staple food for over half of the world’s population, rice (Oryza sativa L.) experiences multifaceted detrimental effects under salt stress, including osmotic imbalance, ion toxicity (primarily from Na+), and secondary oxidative damage (Ma et al. 2022; Yang and Guo 2018). These stressors collectively disrupt nutrient homeostasis, accelerate leaf senescence, and ultimately lead to severe yield losses (Golldack et al. 2014). Therefore, developing efficient and sustainable strategies to enhance salt tolerance remains a critical research objective in both rice biology and agricultural production.

Plants deploy a range of adaptive mechanisms to cope with salt stress, involving ion transporters, antioxidant systems, and hormonal regulation (Ahmad et al. 2010; Khan et al. 2018; van Zelm et al. 2020). Among these, the accumulation of osmoprotectants such as proline (Pro), polyols, and various sugars, along with the enhanced activity of antioxidant enzymes including CAT, APX, SOD, and POD, represents a classic physiological response to salinity. Proline accumulation helps maintain cellular osmotic balance, prevents water loss, and supports intracellular homeostasis essential for key biological processes. Recent studies have also indicated that proline exhibits antioxidant properties (Nguyen et al. 2021). In addition to this, antioxidant enzymes function to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals, thereby strengthening the plant’s ability to mitigate secondary oxidative damage induced by salt stress (Ahmad et al. 2010).

The dynamic regulation of hormone levels under salt stress represents a key mechanism for enhancing plant salt tolerance and growth characteristics. Accumulating evidence indicates that plant hormones not only regulate growth and development under optimal conditions but also play crucial roles in adapting to environmental stresses such as salinity, thereby assisting plants in acclimating to challenging growth conditions (Yu et al. 2020). Extensive studies have shown that plant hormones, as core signaling molecules governing the balance between growth and defense under stress, do not function in isolation. Instead, they form a sophisticated and efficient regulatory network that operates synergistically across different developmental stages, tissues, and environmental contexts (Figueroa-Macías et al. 2021; Verma et al. 2025; Waadt et al. 2022). Under salt stress, a significant interaction occurs between abscisic acid (ABA) and auxin (IAA). High salinity triggers ABA accumulation, which helps regulate osmotic balance and mitigate stress-induced damage (Ortiz-García et al. 2023). However, elevated ABA levels can disrupt normal IAA distribution and suppress plant growth and development (Zhang et al. 2006). Conversely, IAA helps maintain root development and the capacity for water and nutrient uptake through complex interactions with ABA (Lu et al. 2019). An increased IAA/ABA ratio is generally associated with improved salt tolerance of crops. Additionally, jasmonic acid (JA) and salicylic acid (SA) are involved in mediating defense responses and adaptive adjustments under salt stress. Their signaling pathways exhibit both interaction and antagonism, resulting in coordinated or competitive regulatory modes depending on genetic background and stress progression (Mur et al. 2006). Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that beneficial microorganisms can enhance host salt tolerance by modulating hormonal homeostasis (Zhang et al. 2025).

Salt tolerance in rice is genotype-dependent, and the identification and characterization of salt tolerance-related genes constitute a critical strategy for addressing salt stress. Considerable research effort has been devoted to identifying such genes, and hundreds associated with salt tolerance have been reported to date (Nakhla et al. 2021). Despite the large number of candidate genes identified, very few have been successfully applied in practical breeding programs, largely due to their limited efficacy under field conditions or undesirable effects on other beneficial agronomic traits (Swarup et al. 2021).

Plant-microbe interactions represent a frontier research area for enhancing crop stress resistance (Muhammad et al. 2024). Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) mitigate salt stress damage in plants through multiple mechanisms (Witzel et al. 2025). While Non-halophilic PGPRs have been extensively studied for salt stress mitigation, they often face challenges in survival and colonization under high-salinity field conditions (Etesami and Maheshwari 2018). In contrast, marine microorganisms possess innate metabolic adaptations to hyper-saline environments (Etesami and Glick 2020). Consequently, marine bacteria, such as Rhodobium marinum, represent a robust yet underexplored resource for enhancing crop resilience. However, the specific molecular mechanisms and regulatory networks orchestrating this marine halophile-induced salt tolerance remain largely uncharacterized. In particular, how plants with different genetic backgrounds achieve a dynamic balance between growth and defense during microbe-mediated salt adaptation has not been fully elucidated at the molecular level. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) provides a powerful approach to profile genome-wide expression changes in plants under specific conditions (Razzaque et al. 2019). When combined with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), which identifies clusters of genes with similar expression patterns and correlates them with phenotypic traits, this integrated methodology enables the discovery of potential hub genes (Ren et al. 2022). To date, the combination of RNA-seq and WGCNA has been extensively validated for elucidating salt adaptation mechanisms in major crops and model plants, including Oryza sativa L. (Zhu et al. 2019), Solanum lycopersicum L. (Wang et al. 2023), and Arabidopsis thaliana (Amrine et al. 2015).

Based on our previous research, Rhodobium marinum (RH-AZ), isolated from seawater, exhibits a high degree of salt tolerance, and its fermentation broth significantly enhances the salt tolerance and growth capacity of rice. The strain RH-AZ, originally identified as Rhodobium marinum, has been updated to Rhodobium marinum according to the latest taxonomic classification (Urdiain et al. 2008). This application of RH-AZ fermentation broth is hereafter designated as the RH-AZ treatment. Preliminary experimental results demonstrated that under salt stress, rice seedlings exhibited a 12.5% reduction in shoot length, an 18.7% decrease in shoot fresh weight, and a 23.3% decline in root fresh weight. In contrast, when the RH-AZ treatment was applied under the same salt stress conditions, the growth parameters of the rice seedlings remained comparable to those of the control group (Δ < 5%, P > 0.05) (Gao et al. 2025). Given the established roles of plant hormones in mediating salt stress responses and the observed growth-promoting and salt-tolerance effects conferred by RH-AZ, it is of considerable interest to investigate whether the fermentation broth enhances salt tolerance in rice by modulating phytohormone pathways. In this study, we utilized the salt-sensitive cultivar 9311 and the moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931 as experimental materials to analyze the endogenous accumulation of IAA, ABA, JA, and SA under different conditions. Furthermore, by integrating RNA-seq and WGCNA, we explored the molecular regulatory patterns influenced by RH-AZ treatment in these two rice cultivars under salt stress. This study contributes to elucidating the regulatory role of microorganisms in enhancing plant salt stress tolerance, thereby enriching the theoretical foundation for utilizing plant-microbe interactions to address soil salinization.

Materials and methods

Rice Cultivars and Microbial Strain

Seeds of rice cultivars 9311 (salt-sensitive) and 3931 (moderately salt-tolerant) were provided and preserved by the Hunan Plant Protection Institute, Hunan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Changsha, China). Seeds were surface-sterilized with 3% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) for 20 min, followed by thorough rinsing with sterile water. The sterilized seeds were then placed in beakers and germinated in the dark under humid conditions at 32 °C, with daily rinsing using sterile water. After radicle emergence approximately 2–3 mm, uniformly germinated seeds were transferred to 96-well hydroponic culture boxes filled with Hogland nutrient solution (LB0140; Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Rice seedlings were cultivated in a controlled climate chamber under a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod, at 26 ± 2 °C, and 70 ± 5% relative humidity. The nutrient solution was replenished every three days. Experimental treatments were initiated at the three-leaf stage.

The Rhodobium marinum strain RH-AZ was obtained from the stock culture collection in the laboratory. The strain was activated following the method described by Gao et al. (2025). The activated strain was cultured in a photosynthetic bacterial liquid medium (PBLM) containing 6% (w/v) NaCl. The bacterial concentration was adjusted to approximately 2 × 108 CFU/mL, as determined by the plate colony counting method. The bacterial broth was centrifuged at 6,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant, containing the RH-AZ fermentation broth, was collected and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Salt Stress Tolerance Assay

Three experimental treatments were established: Treatment A (control, Hoagland nutrient solution), Treatment B (Hoagland nutrient solution + NaCl + 2.5% (v/v) PBLM), and Treatment C (Hoagland nutrient solution + NaCl + 2.5% (v/v) RH-AZ fermentation broth). Genotype-specific NaCl concentrations were optimized according to preliminary phenotypic assays to ensure comparable physiological stress intensity, particularly similar relative growth inhibition, in both cultivars under Treatment B. The final NaCl concentration was set at 8‰ for the moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931 (Treatments B/C) and 6‰ for the salt-sensitive cultivar 9311 (Treatments B/C). This standardization was crucial to prevent varying stress baselines from obscuring the precise assessment of RH-AZ-mediated tolerance mechanisms. After three days of treatment, uniformly grown rice seedlings from each group were collected, rinsed with distilled water, gently blotted dry, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analyses, including RNA-seq, phytohormone assays, and real-time quantitative PCR (RTq-PCR) analyses.

RNA-seq and Transcriptomic Analysis

RNA-seq libraries were constructed from the cultivars 9311 and 3931, with three biological replicates per treatment, resulting in 18 libraries (2 varieties × 3 treatments × 3 replicates). After quantification with a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), the libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Total RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were conducted by Meiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw sequencing reads were quality-controlled and filtered using Fastp (v0.23.4) (Chen et al. 2018) to generate high-quality clean reads for downstream analyses. Read alignment and subsequent analyses were performed using the Oryza sativa indica reference genome assembly ASM465v1 (https://plants.ensembl.org/Oryza_indica/Info/Index). The clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.2) (Kim et al. 2015). The aligned reads were then assembled into transcripts using StringTie (v2.2.4) (Pertea et al. 2015) in a reference-guided approach. Gene expression levels were quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) based on read counts and transcript length. Gene abundance was estimated using RSEM (1.3.3) (Li and Dewey 2011). The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number PRJNA1365694.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the online tools available on the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://www.majorbio.com). Based on the raw read counts, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 package (v1.44.0) in R (v4.3.1) (Love et al. 2014) for the following comparisons: treatment A vs. B and treatment C vs. B, in 2 rice cultivars. DEGs with |log2FC| > 2 and an adjusted p-value (FDR) ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. These DEGs were visualized using scatter plots.Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed using Goatools (v1.3.3) and KOBAS (v3.0), respectively (Xie et al. 2011). A corrected p-value < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.Finally, heatmaps and hierarchical clustering analysis were generated using the Majorbio Cloud (http://www.majorbio.com) and OEBiotech (http://www.oebiotech.com) Cloud platforms.

Quantification of IAA, ABA, JA, and SA

The contents of ABA, IAA, JA, and SA in all samples were determined following a modified protocol reported by Luo et al. (2012). Frozen samples stored at -80 °C were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. For each sample, 0.1 g of the homogenized powder was weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube and extracted with 800 µL of 80% (v/v) methanol at 4 °C for overnight about 16 h. The extract was centrifuged at 4,800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the pellet was re-extracted with 800 µL of 80% (v/v) HPLC-grade methanol (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). The combined supernatants were vacuum freeze-dried. The resulting residue was reconstituted in 200 µL of 0.1 mol/L sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8). The solution was purified using a Sep-Pak C18 solid-phase extraction cartridge (Waters, USA). The cartridge was preconditioned with 200 µL of deionized water and then eluted with 1.4 mL of 80% (v/v) methanol. The methanol eluate was evaporated to dryness. The lyophilized powder was reconstituted in 40 µL of 50% (v/v) methanol and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using an Agilent 1260 Infinity HPLC system coupled to a G6460A triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA).

RT-qPCR Analyses

Gene-specific primers (Table S1) were designed with DNAMAN software (v9.0). RT-qPCR analyses were conducted using a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, California, USA) with TransStart Green qPCR SuperMix UDG (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) in a 20 µL reaction volume. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 5 s, 58 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 10 s. The EF-1α gene served as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method, with three biological replicates per treatment and three technical replicates per biological replicate.

Construction of the Co-expression Network

WGCNA was conducted on the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Genes with low expression levels (FPKM < 1) were excluded. A soft thresholding power (β) of 12 was chosen to achieve a scale-free network topology, with a model fit index (R²) greater than 0.85. The adjacency matrix was then transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). Module identification was performed using the dynamic tree-cut algorithm with the following parameters: deepSplit = 2, minModuleSize = 30, and mergeCutHeight = 0.25. Correlations between module eigengenes and traits were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. A correlation was considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. The strength of the correlation was qualitatively described as follows: r ≥ 0.7 was considered strong, 0.5 ≤ r < 0.7 was considered moderate, and r < 0.5 was considered weak. For downstream analysis, the module exhibiting the strongest positive correlation (> 0.9) with a specific trait of interest was identified. Finally, key modules associated with phytohormone responses were visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape (v3.9.1). Hub genes within these modules were subsequently identified based on a module membership (KME) threshold of greater than 0.9.

Statistical Analysis

Differences between two groups were assessed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons among three or more groups, data were analyzed to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). If the ANOVA indicated a significant difference, Duncan’s multiple range test was employed for post hoc comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM® SPSS® Statistics version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA), and all figures were generated using SigmaPlot version 14.0 (Systat Software, Inc., San Jose, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

RH-AZ Treatment Confers Salt Tolerance in Rice Cultivars 9311 and 3931

Seedlings of two rice varieties, 9311 and 3931, were cultivated under three distinct treatment conditions. After 72 h of treatment, differences in growth performance among the groups became apparent (Fig. 1). Compared to Treatment A, the growth of both rice varieties in Treatment B was inhibited, with a noticeable reduction in plant height. In contrast, seedlings in Treatment C, which involved the application of RH-AZ treatment, exhibited weaker growth than those in Treatment A, but performed better than those in Treatment B. Although the differences were not statistically significant at the 72-hour time point, they indicated that physiological distinctions among the plants had begun to emerge. Thus, this time point was selected for sampling to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which RH-AZ treatment enhances salt tolerance in rice seedlings.

RNA-seq, Quality Control, and Functional Annotation

We performed RNA-seq on samples from two rice varieties under different treatment conditions to investigate the mechanism by which RH-AZ treatment enhances rice growth under salt stress. Following sequence assembly and alignment to the reference genome, the raw read counts per sample ranged from 39.91 to 51.21 million, corresponding to 6.03–7.42 gigabases of sequence data. The average genome mapping efficiency was 94.54% with a range of 92.49–98.40%. High sequencing quality was further confirmed by an average GC content of 53.02% and an average Q30 score of 94.17% (Table S1). Principal component analysis (PCA) of the RNA-seq data revealed high reproducibility among biological replicates, with low intra-group variation, and clear separation by both treatment type and variety (Fig. 2A).

To investigate the transcriptional differences between the two rice varieties under different treatment conditions, we identified 12,410 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using DESeq2 (P < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 2). Compared to salt stress alone, RH-AZ treatment led to substantial changes in DEG profiles in both cultivars. The salt-sensitive cultivar 9311 exhibited significantly fewer DEGs (659 up-regulated, 1170 down-regulated) than the moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931 (2420 up-regulated, 3993 down-regulated) (Fig. 2B). Moreover, the number of DEGs specifically altered in response to RH-AZ application also differed markedly between the two varieties: 523 in 9311 compared to 5696 in 3931 (Fig. 2C, D). These results indicate that RH-AZ treatment induces dynamic transcriptomic reprogramming in rice under salt stress, with the response varying considerably depending on the genetic background and inherent salt tolerance of the cultivar.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis categorized the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) into biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) groups. We compared the top 20 enriched GO terms between salt-sensitive rice 9311 and moderately salt-tolerant rice 3931 under two conditions: Treatment B-vs-Treatment A, Treatment C-vs-Treatment B. Distinct differences in GO enrichment patterns were observed between the two cultivars. In the salt-sensitive 9311, DEGs were significantly enriched in protein phosphorylation (GO:0006468) under both Treatment B and Treatment C (Fig. 3A and C). In the moderately salt-tolerant 3931 under Treatment B, DEGs were enriched in oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491) and small molecule binding-related GO terms (Fig. 3B). Under Treatment C, DEGs in 3931 were enriched in glycosyltransferase activity (GO:0016757) and protein phosphorylation (GO:0006468) (Fig. 3D). Notably, the number of protein phosphorylation-related DEGs was substantially higher in the cultivar 3931 than in the cultivar 9311. These findings suggest that RH-AZ treatment modulates salt tolerance in rice varieties with different genetic backgrounds through differential signaling and metabolic reprogramming.

KEGG pathway analysis revealed that under salt stress, DEGs in salt-sensitive 9311 were mainly enriched in pathways related to secondary metabolite biosynthesis (Fig. 4A), while those in moderately salt-tolerant 3931 were enriched in secondary metabolite synthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, and MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 4B). Under treatment C, DEGs from both varieties were enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling, and secondary metabolite synthesis. Notably, the cultivar 3931 showed a higher number of DEGs involved in plant hormone signal transduction than the cultivar 9311 (Fig. 4C and D). Given the crucial role of plant hormones in abiotic stress responses, we hypothesize that RH-AZ treatment improves rice growth under salt stress by regulating plant hormone signaling pathways, with a more pronounced effect in the moderately salt-tolerant variety 3931 than in the salt-sensitive 9311. These results indicate that the growth-promoting effect of RH-AZ treatment under salt stress involves the activation of signaling networks and metabolic pathways, yet the response strategies and regulatory emphasis differ between varieties with contrasting salt tolerance.

Modulation of Key Genes Related to Salt-Tolerance by RH-AZ Treatment

Osmotic adjustment and antioxidant mechanisms play crucial roles in mediating salt tolerance in rice (Liu et al. 2022). In our previous study, we demonstrated that the application of RH-AZ treatment significantly increased proline (Pro) content and enhanced the activities of antioxidant enzymes (APX, CAT, SOD, POD) in both rice varieties under salt stress (Gao et al. 2025). To further explore the molecular mechanisms underlying these physiological changes, we analyzed the transcriptional profiles of genes associated with osmotic regulation and antioxidant defense. A total of 50 related DEGs were identified, showing broadly similar expression trends in both varieties. Under salt stress (Treatment B), osmotic-related genes were up-regulated in both cultivars, with the moderately salt-tolerant 3931 exhibiting a greater number of up-regulated genes than the salt-sensitive 9311. Under the salt stress with the intervention of RH-AZ treatment (Treatment C), most of these genes were further significantly up-regulated compared to Treatment B. Notably, the magnitude of up-regulation was generally higher in 3931 than in 9311 (Fig. 5A). Among these, the Catalase gene OsCATA (BGIOSGA007252) showed the highest relative expression abundance in both varieties. Key target genes related to Pro, APX, CAT, SOD, and POD were selected for RT-qPCR validation (Fig. 5B and F), which confirmed the reliability of the transcriptomic data. These results clearly indicate, at the transcriptional level, that RH-AZ treatment enhances osmotic adjustment and antioxidant capacity in both rice varieties under stress, thereby strengthening the seedlings’ innate defense mechanisms.

The regulatory mechanisms of core salt-responsive genes are essential for understanding how rice adapts to saline environments, as these genes govern key physiological processes under salt stress. We analyzed 75 core salt-tolerance DEGs, which were classified into five functional categories: ion transport and homeostasis, signaling and transcription, cell wall remodeling, secondary metabolism and defense, and protein homeostasis. Under salt stress (Treatment B), the majority of these genes were up-regulated in both varieties, with only a small number being down-regulated. The salt-sensitive 9311 showed a markedly greater number of up-regulated genes than the moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931. Notably, transcription factors OsNAC22 (BGIOSGA011514) and OsMYBS3 (BGIOSGA002232), together with the copper chaperone gene OSATX1 (BGIOSGA006337) showed the most pronounced up-regulation in both cultivars. In contrast, the small heat shock protein gene OsHSP17.9 (BGIOSGA012293) was the only core gene which is commonly down-regulated (Fig. 6A and B). Collectively, these findings indicate that the salt-sensitive cultivar 9311 mounts a stronger, albeit potentially less finely tuned, global transcriptional response to salt stress than the moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931.

Compared to Treatment B, RH-AZ treatment substantially altered the salt-induced gene expression profile. DEGs analysis revealed that the number of salt tolerance-related DEGs was markedly higher in the salt-tolerant cultivar 3931 than in the salt-sensitive 9311. Twelve salt-tolerance genes were differentially expressed in both cultivars, with the signal transduction gene OsCDPK21 (BGIOSGA026567) showing the most significant down-regulated transcriptional changes. In the salt-tolerant cultivar 3931, RH-AZ treatment significantly up-regulated several key genes, including the protein phosphatase gene OsPP2A (BGIOSGA020329), the nuclear transcription regulator gene OsNFXL1 (BGIOSGA002351), the metabolic enzyme gene OsPGD1 (BGIOSGA022167), and the mitochondrial RNA helicase gene OsSUV3 (BGIOSGA009822). Furthermore, RH-AZ treatment alleviated the salt-induced suppression of specific stress-response genes, such as the signal transduction gene OsMAPK1 (BGIOSGA021882), the ABC transporter gene OsABCG5 (BGIOSGA012359), and the mitochondrial transcription termination factor gene OsmTERF9 (BGIOSGA026020). Conversely, RH-AZ treatment down-regulated several genes that were induced by salt stress, including the transcription factor gene genes OsMYB2 (BGIOSGA010857), OsWRKY71 (BGIOSGA007670), and OsbZIP23 (BGIOSGA005551), together with the receptor-like kinase gene OsSTRK1 (BGIOSGA014633). Some expression patterns were conserved in the salt-sensitive cultivar 9311 (Fig. 6A and B). However, key differences were observed; for instance, the transcription factor gene OsNAC45 (BGIOSGA034716), the calcineurin B-like protein gene OsCBL24 (BGIOSGA020919), and the cyclin-like protein gene OsCYL4 (BGIOSGA030237) were up-regulated in the salt-sensitive cultivar 9311 but down-regulated in the moderatedly salt-tolerant cultivar 3931 following RH-AZ treatment. Key signature genes, including OsCYL4, OsPGD1, OsSTRK1, and OsWRKY71, were selected for RT-qPCR validation across treatment groups, which confirmed the transcriptomic data (Fig. 6C-F). These findings indicate that RH-AZ exerts a selective effect on key genes related to salt-tolerance, which may optimize resource allocation between stress defense and growth. Furthermore, RH-AZ treatment differentially modulated the expression of major salt-tolerance genes in a cultivar-specific manner.

Hormonal and Transcriptional Changes in IAA and ABA Pathways

RH-AZ treatment significantly alleviated salt stress-induced growth inhibition in both rice cultivars. Given the known role of IAA in mediating plant salt tolerance (Musazade et al. 2025; Yu et al. 2020), IAA levels in both cultivars across the different treatment groups were quantified. Quantitative mass spectrometry revealed that IAA content increased significantly under salt stress in the moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931, and was further elevated following RH-AZ treatment (Fig. 7A). In the salt-sensitive cultivar 9311, IAA content also exhibited an increasing trend under salt stress and after RH-AZ treatment application, although the changes were not statistically significant (Fig. 7A). The expression patterns of IAA pathway-related DEGs were then analyzed using RNA-seq data. RH-AZ treatment markedly altered the transcriptional abundance of key IAA pathway genes in both cultivars treated with salt stress and RH-AZ treatment compared to salt stress alone. In the cultivar 9311, RH-AZ treatment resulted in the upregulation of only one synthesis-related gene, the YUCCA family flavin monooxygenase gene OsYUC1 (BGIOSGA004101; Yamamoto et al. 2007) (Fig. 7B). In contrast, in the cultivar 3931, RH-AZ treatment induced the upregulation of nearly all IAA biosynthesis-related genes, with the exception of OsYUC10 (BGIOSGA003245; Yamamoto et al. 2007), correlating with the significant increase in IAA content (Fig. 7B). Expression of the auxin oxidase gene OsDAO (BGIOSGA014888), involved in IAA catabolism, was suppressed in both cultivars. Analysis of IAA signaling pathway genes revealed that the expression profile in the cultivar 9311 following RH-AZ treatment more closely resembled that of the unstressed control (Fig. 7B). In contrast, RH-AZ treatment reversed the salt stress-induced expression profile of IAA signaling genes in the cultivar 3931, but this profile did not revert to the unstressed state (Group A) (Fig. 7B). To validate these findings, key IAA synthesis genes OsTAA, OsYUC2 (BGIOSGA004401; Han et al. 2020) and OsYUC5 (BGIOSGA004401; Yamamoto et al. 2007), metabolic gene (OsDAO) and the auxin/indole-3-acetic acid protein gene OsIAA24 (BGIOSGA024710; Jain et al. 2006) were validated by RT-qPCR analysis. The concordance between the RT-qPCR results (Fig. 7C) and the RNA-seq data confirmed the reliability of the transcriptomic analysis.

The influence of RH-AZ treatment on the ABA signaling pathway was strikingly divergent between the two varieties. Effects of RH-AZ Treatment on JA and SA Biosynthesis and Signal

Under salt stress, JA and SA mediate key adaptive responses, including antioxidant system activation, ion homeostasis regulation, osmolyte biosynthesis, and photosynthetic protection, which are crucial for crop salt tolerance (Liu et al. 2022). Quantification via LC-MS/MS revealed a significant increase in JA content in both cultivars under salt stress. Following RH-AZ treatment application under salt stress, JA content was significantly further elevated in both cultivars compared to salt stress alone (Fig. 9A). Comparative RNA-seq analysis showed that RH-AZ treatment downregulated most JA biosynthesis-related DEGs in both cultivars (Fig. 9B). However, the observed increase in JA content despite the downregulation of biosynthesis genes suggests potential post-transcriptional regulation or temporal delays in metabolite turnover (Chung et al. 2008; Conrath et al. 2015). Analysis of JA signaling pathway DEGs showed that all were upregulated in the salt-sensitive cultivar 9311 under salt stress (Fig. 9B). However, RH-AZ treatment under salt stress restored their expression patterns to levels closer to the control group (Fig. 9B). In contrast, all JA signaling pathway DEGs were downregulated in the moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931 under salt stress (Fig. 9B). Upon intervention with the RH-AZ treatment under salt stress, the downregulation of these DEGs was further enhanced, with the exception of the jasmonate ZIM-domain protein gene OsJAZ3 (BGIOSGA026952; Ye et al. 2009) (Fig. 9B). Regarding SA, its content was significantly increased under salt stress in both cultivars compared to the control. RH-AZ treatment did not significantly alter SA levels relative to salt stress alone (Fig. 10A). The expression patterns of SA signaling pathway DEGs following RH-AZ treatment under salt stress were nearly opposite to those in the control group in both cultivars (Fig. 10B and C). Key marker genes for JA (Fig. 9C) and SA (Fig. 10C) synthesis and signaling were selected for validation by RT-qPCR assay. The RT-qPCR results confirmed the expression trends observed in the transcriptomic analysis.

Co-expression Network Analysis and Hub Gene Identification

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that RH-AZ treatment enhances salt tolerance in rice via a complex phytohormone regulatory network rather than through single-gene effects. To explore the relationship between phytohormone levels and gene expression, we constructed a weighted gene co-expression network using WGCNA. This analysis identified 16 distinct gene modules from the DEGs, each exhibiting unique expression patterns (Fig. 11A). The correlations between each module and key physiological traits of salt tolerance, including the contents of four phytohormones (ABA, IAA, JA, SA), Pro, MDA, and the activities of antioxidant enzymes (APX, CAT, SOD, POD) were assessed. The MEpink module showed strong positive correlations (r > 0.7, P < 0.05) with ABA, IAA, and SA levels, and a moderate correlation with JA (P < 0.05). Furthermore, this module was highly correlated with Pro content and the activities of APX, CAT, and SOD (P < 0.05), and moderately correlated with POD activity (P < 0.05) (Fig. 11B).

To elucidate the functional roles of the MEpink module genes, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in diterpenoid metabolic processes (GO:0016101) and biosynthesis of phytochemicals (Fig. 11C). KEGG analysis further confirmed enrichment in the diterpenoid biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 11C). Notably, the functional annotation of MEpink module genes aligned with the overall functional trends identified in our analyses aforementioned. This shift from general metabolism to specific defense-related pathways suggests that RH-AZ not only activates broad-spectrum secondary metabolism but also precisely modulates diterpenoid synthesis. In addtition, 14 hub genes within the MEpink module were identified based on high intramodular connectivity (KME > 0.9) (Fig. 10). These hub genes were functionally categorized into seven groups: diterpenoid metabolism, signal transduction/defense, amino acid metabolism, flavonoid metabolism, glycosylation, protein degradation, and lignin synthesis, with one gene of unknown function potentially involved in DNA repair (Table 1). Several hub genes were directly associated with diterpenoid metabolism in rice. Functionally, these genes encompass the complete diterpenoid biosynthetic pathway, from skeleton formation (BGIOSGA015980) to structural modification (CYP450s) and final compound production (BGIOSGA026331, BGIOSGA022303). These hub genes are directly involved in the biosynthesis of defensive diterpenoids. These findings demonstrate that RH-AZ treatment enhances the growth of rice and its salt-tolerance through a dual regulatory mechanism. This mechanism involves not only the activation of classic stress-response (hormonal, osmotic and antioxidant) pathways but also the fine-tuning of secondary metabolic networks.

Discussion

In response to the increasing severity of abiotic stresses, which threaten global food security, the use of beneficial microorganisms to improve crop resilience has emerged as a highly viable sustainable agricultural strategy (He et al. 2024; Vurukonda et al. 2016). Although the beneficial effects of plant–microbe interactions are well documented, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood and represent a major research frontier. In this study, an integrated approach combining RNA-seq, hormone profiling, and WGCNA were employed to elucidate the multi-layered regulatory network through which RH-AZ treatment enhances salt tolerance in both salt-sensitive (9311) and moderately salt-tolerant (3931) rice cultivars. Furthermore, the influence of genetic background on microbe-mediated stress adaptation mechanisms were also explored.

Salt tolerance-related genes play a central role in regulating plant adaptation to salt stress. They precisely perceive external salt stress signals and integrate them with endogenous growth cues. These genes maintain a balance between defense responses and growth processes through coordinated networks involving ion homeostasis, osmotic adjustment, reactive oxygen species metabolism, and transcription factors (Liu et al. 2022; Zhu 2016). The expression profiles of key salt tolerance-related genes in the cultivars 9311 and 3931 across different treatments were analyzed. Aligning with the physiological improvements, RH-AZ treatment significantly upregulated genes governing osmotic adjustment (Proline biosynthesis) and antioxidant defense (e.g., OsCATA) in both cultivars, validating a synchronized molecular and biochemical response to mitigate oxidative and osmotic stress. By promoting Proline accumulation to stabilize cellular water potential (Szabados and Savouré 2010) and concurrently upregulating antioxidant genes, RH-AZ activates a dual protective mechanism that efficiently scavenges ROS and preserves redox homeostasis under salinity (Mittler 2017). Interestingly, RH-AZ treatment did not universally activate stress-response genes but instead fine-tuned the core salt-tolerance network in a targeted manner. Under salt stress, plants typically activate broad-spectrum stress-response genes, which is an effective but energy-intensive strategy (Zhu 2016). RH-AZ treatment optimized this response under salt stress. Specifically, RH-AZ treatment suppressed the expression of key transcription factors (OsMYB2, OsWRKY71, OsbZIP23), which act as hubs in abiotic stress signaling pathways. Their overactivation often leads to growth inhibition (Goyal et al. 2023; Xiang et al. 2008). This suppression may represent a resource-conserving strategy. Conversely, RH-AZ treatment specifically activated other key salt tolerance-related genes, including OsPP2A, OsPGD1 and OsNFXL2. We propose that RH-AZ treatment induces an energy-efficient defense strategy in rice, minimizing the growth penalty under salt stress while maintaining normal development.

The trade-off between growth and stress defense is largely governed by the antagonistic balance between IAA and ABA, typically manifests as stress triggering defense responses at the expense of growth (Emenecker and Strader 2020). Microbial-derived IAA plays a pivotal role in salt tolerance by remodeling root system architecture (RSA) to maximize water uptake and by stimulating plasma membrane H+-ATPases to maintain ion homeostasis (Chen et al. 2016; Egamberdieva et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2025). In our study, RH-AZ treatment effectively elevated IAA levels in the moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931 by coordinating the upregulation of biosynthesis genes and the suppression of catabolic genes. This hormonal boost likely counteracts salt-induced root inhibition, enabling the seedlings to explore a larger soil volume for water and nutrient acquisition, thereby diluting toxic ions. In contrast, RH-AZ treatment did not significantly alter IAA content in the cultivar 9311. However, by precisely modulating the expression of IAA signaling pathway genes toward patterns resembling the control group, RH-AZ treatment may still alleviate salt stress-induced growth inhibition in the salt-sensitive cultivar 9311. The differential effects of RH-AZ treatment were more pronounced in the regulation of ABA biosynthesis and signaling genes between the two cultivars with contrasting salt tolerance. In the cultivar 3931, RH-AZ treatment significantly suppressed key ABA biosynthesis genes and signaling components, reducing ABA content from its stress-induced peak. ABA is essential for initiating immediate stress signaling, its prolonged accumulation under salinity often imposes a severe growth penalty by inducing stomatal closure and inhibiting carbon assimilation (Zhu 2016). Therefore, fine-tuning ABA levels is critical: sufficient ABA is needed for signaling, but excessive accumulation must be curbed to allow recovery (Yang and Guo 2018). Consequently, by curtailing excessive ABA production, RH-AZ likely prevents an overshoot of the stress response, alleviates stomatal limitations, and helps maintain photosynthetic potential. Although the sensitive cultivar 9311 did not show a significant reduction in ABA levels, RH-AZ modulated its signaling sensitivity to alleviate growth inhibition. Ultimately, the RH-AZ-mediated increase in the IAA/ABA ratio, particularly in cultivar 3931, marks a strategic shift from an ABA-dominated survival mode to an IAA-driven growth recovery mode, optimizing the plant’s adaptation to saline environments (Waadt et al. 2022).

JA and SA act as double-edged swords during salt stress. While essential for initiating defense responses that enhance tolerance, their excessive accumulation diverts energy and imposes a significant growth penalty (Yang et al. 2023; Mur et al. 2006). A paradoxical effect was observed with RH-AZ treatment: despite the transcriptional downregulation of JA biosynthesis and signaling genes, JA content remained elevated compared to unstressed controls. This suggests that JA homeostasis is regulated at multiple levels, and a delay between transcriptional changes and metabolite turnover may explain the discrepancy (Fernie and Stitt 2012; Glauser et al. 2008). In parallel, the fermentation broth essentially stabilized SA accumulation and reversed the expression patterns of SA signaling pathway genes relative to the control group (A), thereby preventing its further induction. These fine-tuned regulatory effects suggest that RH-AZ may rebalance trade-offs between salt tolerance and growth of rice. Notably, a well-documented antagonistic interaction exists between SA and JA signaling in plant immunity (Verma et al. 2025). We observed that JA pathway activation suppresses the SA signaling pathway, mediated by the downregulation of NPR1, which recapitulates previously reported antagonistic crosstalk and is consistent with our results (Yuan et al. 2007) .

Furthermore, we observed distinct regulatory effects of RH-AZ treatment between the two rice varieties. In the moderately salt-tolerant cultivar 3931, RH-AZ treatment demonstrated high compatibility with the host’s intrinsic signaling network, precisely activating core salt-tolerance genes and remodeling an efficient multi-hormone regulatory framework. This optimized model sustained growth momentum under salt stress while reducing energy consumption for defense, thereby achieving an improved growth-defense trade-off (Chakraborty et al. 2025; Munné-Bosch and Müller 2013). In contrast, the RH-AZ treatment demonstrated a limited capacity to extensively remodel the hormonal network in the salt-sensitive rice cultivar 9311, exerting its regulatory influence primarily through modulation of hormone signaling pathways rather than by altering hormone levels. Nevertheless, even in the absence of optimized hormonal resource allocation, cultivar 9311 still benefited from the RH-AZ treatment, achieving considerable mitigation of salt stress effects through the enhancement of basal defense mechanisms.

Finally, A pivotal finding from WGCNA is the identification of a core module enriched with genes governing diterpenoid biosynthesis. While diterpenoids have traditionally been regarded as chemical defenses against pathogens in rice(Kato-Noguchi and Peters 2013). Recent evidence indicates that their accumulation is also linked to abiotic stress tolerance, including drought and salinity (Rayee et al. 2024; Xuan et al. 2016; Yamane 2013). Our findings align with emerging evidence supporting their critical role in abiotic stress resilience. Crucially, prior research indicates that severe salinity stress typically compromises diterpenoid biosynthesis. Previous attempts to reverse this suppression have largely relied on abiotic chemical additives, such as Magnesium Sulfate or Fulvic Acid (Jesmin et al. 2023; Xuan et al. 2022). Aligning with these findings, our WGCNA results reveal that RH-AZ treatment effectively mimics this restorative mechanism. Distinct from these chemical approaches, our study positions RH-AZ treatment as a novel biotic elicitor derived from PGPR. We demonstrate that RH-AZ treatment effectively reinvigorates the diterpenoid pathway—targeting central hubs like OsKSL4 (Wilderman et al. 2004)—which is otherwise silenced by salt toxicity. This implies that RH-AZ treatment triggers a sophisticated metabolic rescue strategy. In this context, the accumulated diterpenoids likely serve a dual function: beyond pathogen defense, they may act as non-enzymatic antioxidants and membrane stabilizers, synergizing with phenolics to maintain cellular redox homeostasis (Quan et al. 2016). This confers a robust cross-tolerance phenotype, where the plant’s biotic defense machinery is repurposed to mitigate the physiological damage of salt stress. This provides a theoretical basis for obtaining cross-tolerant germplasm resources.

RH-AZ treatment serves as a potent microbial biostimulant, priming basal resistance mechanisms and orchestrating a functional optimization of the intricate link between hormonal regulation and secondary metabolism under salinity. Our multi-omics data support a hierarchical regulatory model where RH-AZ treatment-mediated hormonal homeostasis acts as the upstream trigger to reactivate the downstream diterpenoid pathway. Existing literature establishes that JA signaling serves as the primary master switch for upregulating diterpenoid biosynthesis genes in rice (Li et al. 2025; Ogawa et al. 2017; Riemann et al. 2013). By elevating endogenous JA levels, RH-AZ effectively engages this signaling network. By restoring diterpenoid biosynthesis, RH-AZ treatment effectively mimics the protective effects of exogenous metabolite application, providing strong physiological evidence that these metabolites are indispensable effectors in this microbe-induced tolerance mechanism. Unlike single-target genetic manipulation, RH-AZ treatment orchestrates a systemic metabolic rescue, creating a permissive physiological environment that allows the plant to invest in these costly protective metabolites without suffering growth penalties. This study presents a novel insight into rice stress adaptation, highlighting how exogenous biostimulants can reactivate latent metabolic modules to confer robust abiotic stress resilience.

Conclusion

In summary, our experimental data demonstrate that RH-AZ treatment mitigates salt stress in both salt-sensitive (9311) and moderately tolerant (3931) rice cultivars by orchestrating a systemic reconfiguration of basal defense, hormonal signaling and diterpenoid metabolism. This microbe-induced regulation optimizes the growth-defense trade-off in a genotype-dependent manner. Beyond these physiological insights, we propose that the repurposing of diterpenoid represents a latent adaptive mechanism for abiotic stress. Consequently, this study establishes a functional blueprint for developing microbial biostimulants to reactivate these intrinsic defense modules for sustainable crop resilience.
