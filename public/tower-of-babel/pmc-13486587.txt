Historical slavery predicts contemporary disparities in mortality between Black and White Americans
Authors: Manuel J. Galvan, B. Keith Payne, Keely A. Muscatell, Julian M. Rucker, Paschal Sheeran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceBlack–White mortality disparities remain a persistent form of inequality in the United States. This study provides evidence that the proportion of enslaved people across U.S. counties in 1860 predicts contemporary Black–White mortality disparities. Counties with greater reliance on slavery in 1860 have higher contemporary mortality rates for Black Americans but lower rates for White Americans. The association persists in analyses designed to address confounding from unobserved historical factors and in tests of alternative explanations. Structural inequalities in segregation, economic conditions, and educational access emerge as key pathways linking historical slavery to contemporary mortality disparities. This work underscores the need for interventions that address not only proximal determinants but also the historical and structural foundations, of racial health disparities.

A majority of Americans believe that there has been substantial progress toward racial equality over the last 60 y (1). Despite this perceived racial progress, large health disparities between White and Black Americans persist. Black–White health disparities can be seen across many chronic conditions (2) and contribute to substantial and enduring differences in all-cause mortality and life-expectancy (3, 4). The scale and cost of these disparities is considerable. Caraballo et al. (5) estimated that in the 21 y between 1999 and 2020, the Black American population had 1.63 million excess deaths, resulting in the loss of 80 My of potential life.

An emerging body of research has linked present-day health disparities to historical systems of racial oppression (6). One can trace such disparities to practices such as redlining (7), or institutions such as the Jim Crow system in the 20th century (8). But these systems were all downstream of the institution of chattel slavery, which fundamentally organized the economic and political systems in the southern United States before the Civil War, and structured resistance to civil rights during Reconstruction and Jim Crow periods thereafter. Research in this area has found that patterns of antebellum enslavement continue to predict a wide array of racial disparities, including residential and educational segregation, disparities in educational achievement and income, and health disparities (9–14). Prior research has found an association between historical patterns of enslavement and disparities in several specific health problems, including stroke, heart disease, and infant and maternal health (15–17). Reece (18) reported an association between the density of historically enslaved populations and racial disparities in life expectancy in Southern states. In county-level analyses across the former slave South, greater reliance on enslaved labor in 1860 predicted wider Black–White gaps in longevity. This is consistent with a pattern of lower life expectancy among Black residents and higher life expectancy among White residents in those places.

The present research extends this initial work in three ways. First, instead of focusing on estimated life expectancy, we analyze age-adjusted, all-cause mortality rates from 2010 to 2020, providing a direct, period-specific measure of death rate that is ideal for county-level disparity analyses between populations with different age distributions, like Black and White Americans (19). Second, we estimate mediational models that test a theorized pathway by which historical slavery engendered contemporary Black–White mortality disparities via structural inequalities between Black and White residents (segregation, economic inequality, and disparities in education and poverty). Third, we evaluate the robustness of these findings and the plausibility of causal inference by examining alternative explanations (e.g., that the link between slavery and mortality is due to demographic composition, historical mortality trends, or agricultural development; see SI Appendix). A key concern for such inferences is endogeneity, which occurs when a predictor is bound up with unmeasured factors that also affect the outcome. In our case, counties with more slavery in 1860 may differ in other ways that shape health today, so an association between slavery and mortality need not be causal. To address this concern, we implement instrumental variable analysis to evaluate the plausibility of a causal interpretation under the assumption that cotton suitability influences contemporary mortality disparities and structural inequalities only through its historical impact on slavery (20, 21). Taken together, the current study aims to strengthen what we know about slavery’s link to contemporary mortality disparities by using a more direct outcome (age-adjusted death rates), theoretically grounded mediators, and designs that address key endogeneity concerns.

Background

A large body of empirical research demonstrates the social origins of Black–White racial health disparities in the United States (22). Social determinants of racial health gaps include the many social, economic, and political factors that shape racial differences in where people live, how educated they are, and whether they experience poverty or economic inequality. Social and economic resources are distributed in a way that, on average, provides health advantages in White communities but disadvantages Black communities (22–24).

Systemic racism theory offers a framework for understanding how historical injustices, such as slavery, produce enduring racial disparities. This perspective posits that racial inequality is maintained partly by individual biases but also by societal institutions that influence the distribution of resources, opportunities, and life outcomes across generations (25). Because such institutions are place-based and slow to change, they can generate durable local patterns in resources and opportunities that persist long after formal policies end. Under this framework, oppressive social, economic, and political systems, like the institution of slavery, are foundational to the creation and maintenance of racial hierarchies in the United States. The structural mechanisms that shaped racial disparities in wealth, education, and healthcare access are often recognized as the social determinants that shape racial health disparities (22). Systemic racism theory thus treats social determinants of health as the consequences of institutional policies that shape cumulative exposure to risk and access to protection over the life course. In this framework, structural inequalities serve as potential mediators when they are well-established social determinants of health and reflect durable, place-based conditions through which slavery may shape contemporary mortality. Here, we consider three kinds of structural inequalities that are plausible candidates as mediators of the association between slavery and mortality disparities: racial segregation, economic disparities, and education disparities.

Racial Segregation.

Several recent empirical investigations have documented robust associations between enslaved populations in 1860 and contemporary levels of racial segregation (10, 11, 13). Following the Civil War, counties that relied on slave labor continued to hinder the development of Black communities with Black Codes and Jim Crow laws that provided a legal means of segregation. From a systemic racism perspective, segregation constitutes not only physical separation but a durable institutional arrangement that concentrates opportunity and harm in different communities. A recent review found that segregation was associated with widening racial health disparities between White and Black Americans in 56 out of 66 relevant studies (26): Segregation predicted disparities in birthweight and preterm birth, self-rated health, sedentary living, and poorer healthcare outcomes.

Economic Disparities.

U.S. counties with more reliance on Antebellum slavery have higher contemporary Black–White disparities in poverty and economic inequality (12, 14). The overall level of poverty in an area is a strong predictor of nearly all aspects of health (27). Yet, racial disparities in poverty may be especially consequential for racial health disparities because they map unequal economic hardship onto racial group membership and thereby structure unequal exposure to health-promoting resources across communities (28). This is partly because Black Americans are disproportionately likely to experience poverty and to spend longer escaping poverty through education (29, 30). Thus, when poverty decreases, it may result in improved health for the general population but disproportionately advantage White communities and reinforce preexisting racial disparities. In other words, when improvements in income and poverty are unequally shared, they may not only fail to reduce racial health disparities but could even exacerbate them (31).

As with poverty, overall income inequality is also associated with greater racial health disparities (32, 33). Although income inequality is not race-specific, it can still be consequential for racial health disparities when Black and White residents are unequally positioned within the local income distribution, such that increases in overall inequality translate into larger racial gaps in material security and related social determinants (33). From a systemic racism perspective, income inequality can amplify relative (dis)advantage by widening gaps in access to material resources and the conditions that protect health. Rising economic inequality may concentrate material difficulties such as poor housing conditions, limited healthcare, and disease exposure within communities that are poor and disproportionately Black (see ref. 34). Additionally, larger economic gaps may reinforce social perceptions of racial subordination, which perpetuate racial discrimination and prejudice for Black Americans of all social classes (35). Economic inequality and racial disparities in poverty, in turn, are both known to shape racial health disparities (31, 32).

Education Disparities.

As with poverty and income inequality, the legacy of slavery predicts contemporary racial disparities in educational outcomes (9). Educational attainment relies on place-based institutional resources that shape who has access to opportunity and protection over the life course. Slavery created an initial gap in educational attainment in the early 1900s, which strongly predicts contemporary educational disparities. Because education is a strong social determinant of health (e.g., shaping stable employment, income security, and access to healthcare), it is an important explanatory factor that has entrenched Black–White racial health disparities (36, 37).

White Advantages and Disadvantages.

There is a longstanding debate in the literature whether slavery and other discriminatory policies created overall advantages or disadvantages for White Americans. Scholars have proposed two broad frameworks for understanding how slavery affected Black and White Americans (see ref. 38). The competition perspective argues that White Americans broadly benefited from racial subordination (e.g., ref. 39) by privileging White Americans with additional resources and opportunities. The class exploitation perspective, on the other hand, proposed that benefits are concentrated among White elites and may either fail to benefit the broader White middle and working class, or actively harm them (e.g., ref. 40).

Recent literature predominantly supports the competition perspective. Most relevant for our analysis, Reece (18) found that slavery was linked to increased White life expectancy but decreased Black life expectancy. Other investigations have similarly found that slavery is linked to White advantages across many social outcomes and stroke mortality (15, 41). We also expect that such advantages and disadvantages are driven by structural inequalities. This is consistent with the results of a recent analysis showing that racial inequalities in poverty in the United States are driven by structural and political factors rather than behavioral factors (42). Some scholars have found that the link between slavery and racial disparities in incarceration rates is mediated by carceral privileges that White populations have in slave-dependent locations (43) whereas others have argued that systems of racialized economic inequality and segregation undermine societal well-being more broadly and may only help the most affluent of White Americans (44, 45).

These frameworks suggest different empirical patterns: Results consistent with the competition perspective would include lower contemporary White mortality in counties with higher historical reliance on slavery, producing a larger Black–White mortality gap; results consistent with the class exploitation perspective would include slavery being weakly or even positively associated with contemporary White mortality, alongside higher Black mortality. Further, based on systemic racism theory, we would expect that the link between slavery and racial mortality disparities is partly attributable to place-based structural inequalities. The present study addresses these frameworks by empirically testing whether slavery’s legacy predicts modern mortality disparities through structural inequalities that advantage White populations and disadvantage Black populations.

The Present Research

This study examines whether the proportion of enslaved people in 1860 predicts contemporary Black–White mortality disparities (2010–2020) across U.S. counties in historical slave-owning states. Building on prior evidence that slavery is linked to enduring racial inequalities in health, wealth, and education, we hypothesize that counties with greater historical enslavement will show higher mortality rates among Black Americans and lower mortality rates among White Americans. This pattern is consistent with the competition perspective, which holds that racialized systems broadly advantaged White populations.

Slavery predated 1860 by centuries, but during the 18th and 19th centuries northern states gradually abolished it, beginning in New England and spreading through the Mid-Atlantic. In contrast, the South’s cotton-based economy became so dependent on enslaved labor that southern states resisted abolition (20). As a result, the geographic distribution of slavery in 1860 reflected a political stalemate that helped precipitate the Civil War and the resistance to change that later defined the Reconstruction and Jim Crow eras. We focus on 1860 because it is the last antebellum census before the slave labor economy was disrupted by the Civil War and, to our knowledge, the only year for which harmonized county-level enslavement data are publicly available (46).

We further hypothesize that the relationship between slavery and mortality disparities is mediated by structural inequalities that connect historical slavery to modern racial health disparities. Specifically, we test whether segregation, economic inequality, and racial disparities in poverty and college attainment account for the association between slavery and Black–White mortality disparities. These factors qualify as plausible mediators because they are historically rooted in slavery, persist across generations, and represent well-established social determinants of health. To ensure appropriate temporal ordering, we use slavery data from 1860, structural inequality data from 1990 to 2000, and mortality data from 2010 to 2020.

A key challenge for interpreting the association between slavery intensity and contemporary mortality disparities is endogeneity: Counties with higher slavery in 1860 may have differed in other ways that plausibly predict long-run health, such as agricultural productivity and land endowments, early economic development and infrastructure, or disease ecology. To evaluate the plausibility of a causal interpretation, we conducted a two-stage least squares (2SLS) analysis using cotton suitability—a county-level measure of how well land could support cotton cultivation—as an instrument (i.e., an exogenous predictor used to isolate the portion of slavery unrelated to confounders) for the prevalence of slavery in 1860. For our instrumental variable analysis to be informative about causal inference, it must meet three assumptions (see ref. 47): First, cotton suitability must be relevant to slavery. This assumption is supported by the association between cotton suitability and slavery found in our data and in many prior studies (20, 48, 49). The logic of this approach is that cotton-suitable land was a precondition that made slavery economically viable and therefore more likely in certain regions. Second, under the exclusion restriction assumption that cotton suitability influences contemporary racial health disparities only through its historical impact on slavery, the 2SLS approach isolates the component of slavery predicted by cotton suitability to estimate its effects on structural mediators and contemporary mortality disparities. Intuitively, there is no obvious reason why soil properties that favor cotton cultivation should be associated with mortality disparities if not through slavery. However, this exclusion restriction assumption cannot be directly tested and may only be inferred through failed attempts to falsify it. Third, our use of instrumental analysis rests on the exchangeability assumption that the link between cotton suitability and mortality is unconfounded. This assumption can be assessed through implementation of appropriate covariates in the instrumental variable analysis.

The instrumental variable analyses presented in the current paper implement extensive historical and contemporary covariates to support the assumption of exchangeability. We further conducted a set of robustness analyses attempting to falsify the exchangeability and exclusion restriction assumptions. The results of these analyses are presented in SI Appendix. Together, these analyses provide converging evidence for the robustness of our findings and the plausibility of a causal interpretation under the identifying assumptions.

Results

Estimating the Association between Enslavement and Racial Mortality Disparities.

We first estimated the unweighted, aggregated magnitude of the racial mortality gap in our dataset using a paired sample t test. This test computed all of the within-county differences in racial disparities, then tested if the average gap across counties was different than zero. There was a large, statistically significant difference (79.5 per 100,000, 95% CI [71, 88]) in the estimated mortality rate between White and Black Americans, t(1168) = 17.66, P < 0.001, dz = 0.52. The mean mortality rate for Black Americans was 974 per 100,000 (SD = 169.1) and for White Americans was 894 per 100,000 (SD = 132.8). Without the use of control variables or nesting the data in a more complex multilevel modeling (MLM) analysis, these results confirm that our data conforms with well-established findings concerning racial health disparities in the United States.

Two separate MLMs estimated the link between historical slavery and race-specific mortality, while accounting for the mortality estimate of the other racial group as well as a host of control variables. Fig. 1 shows the estimated relationship between slavery and Black and White mortality. The trend lines show that as the proportion of enslaved Black Americans in 1860 increases, the 2010–2020 Black mortality rate (dark blue line) increases and the 2010–2020 White mortality rate (red line) decreases. As depicted in Fig. 1, in counties with the lowest proportion of the population enslaved in 1860 (0%), we estimated that there are 11 additional deaths per 100,000 people in the contemporary Black population (898/100,000) compared to the White population (887/100,000). In contrast, counties with the highest proportion enslaved in 1860 (92%) exhibited a dramatically larger mortality disparity—214 additional deaths per 100,000 for Black Americans compared to White Americans (1,023 vs. 809 per 100,000)—a disparity nearly 20 times greater than counties with no history of slavery.

Our model estimated that for every 10% increase in the proportion of enslaved people in 1860, there are 13.6 additional deaths per 100,000 people in the Black population in 2010–2020 (b = 13.6, 95% CI [7.3, 19.9], t = 4.2, ΔR2 = 0.017). In contrast, the model estimated that every 10% increase in the proportion of enslaved people in 1860 is associated with 8.5 fewer deaths per 100,000 people in the White population in 2010–2020 (b = −8.5, 95% CI [−13.5, −3.5], t = −3.3, ΔR2 = 0.007). The change in total variance explained by fixed effects when historical slavery is added to the model [ΔR2; (50)] is necessarily modest because both models already condition on the other group’s mortality rate, which captures much of the county’s shared mortality environment. Of course, these two diverging trend lines need to be considered together to understand how county-level slavery shapes racial disparities. If we combine the decrease in White mortality and the increase in Black mortality, the resulting county-level racial mortality disparity grows by 22.1 additional deaths per 100,000 for every 10% increase in the proportion of enslaved people in 1860. Reported values are estimated using only data from southern slave States, but the pattern of results is similar when estimated using counties from all U.S. States (SI Appendix, Supplemental Analysis S1).

All bold paths presented in Fig. 2 are statistically significant (P < 0.05; correlated residuals and covariates are omitted for clarity). The model indicated full statistical mediation: Once the mediators were included, the direct association between slavery and mortality disparities was no longer statistically significant (β′ = 0.086, P = 0.11). Counties with higher rates of historical slavery have greater Black segregation, economic inequality, and Black–White disparities in poverty and college attainment. The links between slavery and segregation (β = 0.731, P < 0.001) and slavery and poverty disparities (β = 0.367, P < 0.001) were particularly strong. Historical slavery was positively associated with both college attainment disparities (β = 0.295, P < 0.001) and economic inequality (β = 0.287, P < 0.001). Thus, relative to White Americans, Black Americans are less likely to attain a college degree and more likely to experience poverty in areas with a higher proportion of enslaved people in 1860. Our model accounted for 54% of the variance in segregation, 27% of the variance in poverty disparities, 13% of disparities in college attainment, and 17% of disparities in economic inequality across the counties in our sample. These associations indicate that slavery is related to the modern material conditions of Black and White Americans.

These structural inequalities were also associated with mortality. Greater economic inequality, and Black–White poverty and college attainment disparities each uniquely predicted greater racial disparities in mortality. The lack of a significant indirect effect for segregation, despite significant zero-order associations with both slavery and mortality, likely reflects collinearity with economic inequality and Black–White poverty disparities (SI Appendix, Supplemental Analysis S6). As mediators, county-level differences in slavery predict Black–White mortality disparities via economic inequality (β = 0.053, P < 0.001) and racial disparities in poverty (β = 0.042, P = 0.018) and college attainment (β = 0.05, P < 0.001). The model accounted for 20% of the variance in mortality disparities.

Causal Identification Strategy

We next estimated a series of instrumental variable (IV) models using cotton suitability as an instrument for slavery (Table 2). The first-stage regression confirmed the relevance assumption, by revealing that cotton suitability significantly predicted the proportion of the population enslaved in 1860 (b = 0.241, SE = 0.04, P < 0.001), with a first-stage F-statistic of 46.1 (df = 1, 778). The second-stage models revealed robust effects of slavery on mortality and 3 of 4 mediators. Counties with higher rates of slavery as predicted by cotton suitability had significantly larger modern Black–White mortality disparities (b = 547.19, SE = 181.01, P < 0.01), greater racial segregation (b = 0.822, SE = 0.17, P < 0.001), larger Black–White poverty disparities (b = 39.55, SE = 10.15, P < 0.001) and lower relative rates of Black college attainment (b = 0.416, SE = 0.09, P < 0.001). Slavery did not significantly predict general income inequality (Gini coefficient; b = –0.012, SE = 0.04, P = 0.78). These results are consistent with a causal interpretation under the exclusion restriction and exchangeability assumptions. While there is no direct test of these assumptions, we completed several robustness tests to see if these assumptions could be falsified.

Robustness Testing

Drawing causal inference from the instrumental variable analysis rests on the exclusion restriction (i.e., cotton suitability influences contemporary racial health disparities only through its historical impact on slavery) and the exchangeability assumption (i.e., the link between cotton suitability and mortality is unconfounded). These assumptions cannot be tested directly, but they can be falsified if we find that cotton suitability shapes mortality outside of its association with slavery or if confounding variables that are irrelevant to our model can be shown to account for our findings. The Supplemental Materials report several failed attempts to falsify these assumptions.

We did not find evidence that our results are a consequence of county demographic composition (SI Appendix, Supplemental Analysis S2) and results held when county-level Black–White disparities in 1968–1978 were considered (SI Appendix, Supplemental Analysis S3, S4, and S9). We observed that Hispanic-White mortality disparities were uncorrelated with slavery and often had the opposite pattern of associations with the structural inequality variables (SI Appendix, Supplemental Analysis S5). Instrumental variable analysis results remained consistent even including cotton share as a covariate that predicts agricultural development (SI Appendix, Supplemental Analysis S8). We also found that cotton suitability does not predict Black–White mortality disparities in non-slave-state counties (SI Appendix, Supplemental Analysis S10). Finally, the association between historical slavery and contemporary racial mortality disparities was robust to spatial dependence (SI Appendix, Supplemental Analysis S11). Taken together, these tests failed to falsify the assumptions that the instrumental variable analysis relies upon, and thus increase the plausibility of a causal interpretation of our results.

Discussion

The present research used county-level data to investigate how historical slavery is associated with contemporary Black–White mortality disparities. Counties that relied more on slave labor in 1860 have larger racial mortality gaps in the modern era, relative to places that relied less on slavery. Our analysis estimated that the mortality gap between White and Black Americans is nearly 20 times larger in areas of the country with the most historical slavery compared to areas with no history of slavery. Beyond the magnitude of this relationship, our analysis also investigated how this relationship unfolded for Black and White Americans separately.

We found evidence consistent with the competition perspective (41), which suggests that historical racial oppression generated widespread and persistent material advantages for White Americans while disadvantaging Black Americans. Our analysis of the mediating role of structural inequalities in linking slavery to Black–White mortality disparities sheds light on some of the ways that the Black disadvantage and White advantage engendered by slavery shaped mortality disparities. The populations living in counties that relied more on slave labor in 1860 had more exposure to the many correlated social determinants of health (Table 1) that maintained and exacerbated health disparities. The mediation analyses indicate that slavery’s legacy may operate through multiple structures: Economic inequality, disparities in poverty, and college education disparities were the most consequential mediators of the mortality gap. Once these socioeconomic disparities are accounted for, segregation was no longer a significant mediator.

Importantly, these results were observed even after accounting for a host of historical and contemporary county- and state-level factors that might influence the variables. By controlling for many demographic, agricultural, geographic, and economic factors that differ between counties, our results focus on the specific relationships among slavery, structural inequality, and mortality. The instrumental variable analyses provide evidence consistent with a causal interpretation under the exclusion restriction. The results supported our core findings: Slavery was significantly associated with modern Black–White disparities in mortality, as well as key structural mediators including segregation, poverty disparity, and college attainment disparity. In contrast, the instrumental variable estimates for county income inequality were not statistically significant. This divergence is consistent with the idea that the legacy of slavery is expressed more strongly through structural conditions that directly measure racialized access to resources (e.g., poverty and educational disparities) than through class-based income inequality, which can vary for reasons not tightly linked to racial hierarchy. Additionally, our robustness tests consider several possible alternatives to the proposed causal model underlying our tests. Across these robustness checks, we do not find evidence that slavery’s link to mortality disparities is driven by alternative mechanisms or confounding variables.

These results are consistent with the theory of systemic racism (25) which holds that historical systems of oppression, such as slavery, created structural inequities that persist over time and manifest in a range of outcomes, including health disparities (51, 52). Our findings are also consistent with theorizing that Black–White inequality is maintained by processes that disadvantage Black communities and advantage White communities (39). Critically, our analyses quantify specific pathways through which slavery’s local intensity may have shaped racial gaps in mortality, via county-level structural inequalities. By estimating the size of these indirect effects, the present work moves beyond documenting an association between slavery and health and instead isolates measurable structural channels through which systemic racism continues to shape population health.

Strengths and Limitations.

The county-level sample, use of temporally ordered data and extensive control variables are strengths of our approach. Our mediation analyses identified a subset of structural inequality indicators that link slavery to mortality: Segregation, economic inequality, and racial disparities in poverty and college attainment. Taken together, our mediational model accounted for 20% of the variance in Black–White mortality disparities.

There are limitations of our data that should be acknowledged. We focused on disparities in college attainment, but this does not consider known racial inequalities in the overall education pipeline, such as in K-12 education, disciplinary involvement, or college experience (53–55). Similarly, statistics that measure Black and White poverty at a single time point may not capture the entrenchment of poverty in the Black community over time (56). Our analyses relied on observational data, so we incorporated several design features to evaluate the plausibility of a causal pathway. Our models followed the principle of temporal ordering, using historical data on slavery (1860) to predict structural inequalities (1990–2000) and racial mortality disparities (2010–2020). To reduce the risk of confounding, we included a rich set of historical control variables drawn from Acharya et al. (20), including county-level indicators of economic development, agricultural infrastructure, and land inequality. We also adjusted for modern county area and population and used random effects at the state level to account for unobserved heterogeneity across regional political and institutional contexts.

Of course, residual confounding cannot be fully ruled out. However, our instrumental variable analyses were consistent with a plausible causal interpretation under stated identifying assumptions. Our instrumental variable analysis assumed that cotton suitability affected contemporary racial mortality disparities only through its historical effect on slavery and not due to confounds. Although this assumption cannot be tested directly, we address several plausible alternative pathways (as reported in SI Appendix). We therefore interpret the instrumental variable results as strengthening the case for causal interpretation but not proof of causation.

Future Directions and Policy Implications.

Future work could take an even more refined approach and examine the relative impact of specific social determinants of health (e.g., incarceration, K-12 education) in explaining the link between historical slavery and contemporary racial disparities in health. Ultimately, researchers will need to untangle the complex links between slavery and modern racial mortality disparities through time, including time-series analyses which capture unfolding historical events such as the Great Migration, White flight, the rise and fall of the civil rights era, and the era of mass incarceration (e.g., ref. 57).

Our findings support the view that current racial disparities in mortality are the result of long-standing and consequential policy decisions. We focused on the historical policies allowing enslavement, but other policies explicitly created racial disparities through Black Codes, sharecropping, lynching, Jim Crow laws, sundown towns, redlining, employment discrimination, and mass incarceration. Scholars have identified these policies as pivotal in maintaining and deepening structural disadvantages, shaping the social determinants of health that continue to drive racial health disparities today (8, 58). Future research should examine how the timing, emergence, and co-occurrence of these policies contributed to today’s inequities. Our results suggest that these racially discriminatory policies built a society that broadly advantaged White Americans and disadvantaged Black Americans.

Prior investigations (59–61) and the present findings show that racial health disparities began to decline in the 60s and 70s, following the introduction of civil rights legislation. Yet, the large racial disparities in mortality we observe in the 21st century suggest that such remedial efforts have been insufficient. One reason may be that public health interventions have focused on individual-level outcomes and proximate causes, rather than on the upstream social determinants examined in this paper. Efforts to improve access to healthcare and reduce harmful health behaviors are important—but should be complemented by structural interventions that expand access to good incomes and college education for Black Americans (22). Our findings align with calls to address the full spectrum of social determinants of health to close racial mortality gaps (9, 12, 14, 26, 32, 36, 62). In contrast to the discriminatory policies that created racial disparities, robust remediating, egalitarian policies have the potential to improve the health and well-being of all Americans.

Materials and Methods

All data and analysis scripts are publicly available on Open Science Framework [OSF; (63); https://osf.io/xh3sg/?view_only=80bd1c2e4ed44049a0da0b025db34cd6].

Data Sources.

Enslavement rates.

To evaluate the relationship between the proportion of the county that was enslaved and racial mortality disparities, we merged county-level 1860 census data harmonized to modern (year-2000) county boundaries (20, 46). The county-level proportion enslaved ranges from 0 to 92% (e.g., Sharkey County, MS = 92%). To ensure that our findings are consistent with the bulk of research on this topic (see refs. 18 and 20), our primary sample restricts to counties in the sixteen states where slavery was legal in 1860: Alabama, Arkansas, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maryland, Missouri, Mississippi, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, and West Virginia.

Contemporary mortality estimates.

We gathered county-level, race-specific, age-adjusted, all-cause mortality estimates (which we will henceforth describe as “mortality rates” or “mortality”), separately for both non-Hispanic Black Americans and non-Hispanic White Americans from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Wonder dashboard (64). 10 y of mortality estimates were drawn for all counties in the United States (2010–2020). Cases were not included in our analysis when the CDC labeled the estimate as unreliable because there were too few people to robustly estimate race-specific mortality, or the estimate proved an extreme outlier in Mahalanobis distance analyses (P < 0.001).

Structural inequality estimators.

We collected data on county-level racial segregation in the year 2000 (the Black isolation index) from the RAND Center for Population Health and Health Disparities Data Core Series, available as part of the Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research (65). We used two indicators of economic inequality: disparities in poverty rates and overall economic inequality. Estimates for White and Black poverty were taken from the U.S. Census Bureau (66) 2,000 Decennial Census. The census provides estimates of the number of Black and White people experiencing poverty as well as the total number of people within each zip code. These values were used to calculate the percentage of Black and White people experiencing poverty and to create a disparity variable (i.e., Black poverty rate minus the White poverty rate). The level of economic inequality was measured using the Gini coefficient for each U.S. County in 2000, as obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau (67). County-level, race-specific college attainment rates were obtained from the Opportunity Atlas (68) which reflects data from individuals born between 1978 and 1983. Again, the Black and White estimates were used to compute a disparity variable (i.e., Black college attainment rate minus the White college attainment rate). The college attainment variable was then recoded to flip the sign, so that increased numbers represent lower Black college attainment relative to White college attainment.

These data all come from different sources and aggregate data using different geocodes (e.g., FIPS codes, commuting zones, and zip codes). Where data sources provided different geographic identifiers, such as commuting zones or zip codes, we used crosswalk files from the U.S. Census Bureau, Department of Housing and Urban Development, and the U.S. Department of Agriculture Economic Research Service to match the data to corresponding FIPS codes (69–71).

Control variables.

To more precisely estimate the unique contribution of historical slavery to modern racial disparities in mortality, we included a rich set of historical control variables drawn from Acharya et al. (20). Most of these control variables account for county-level differences in geographic, demographic, and economic conditions in 1860. Geographic and demographic context was captured through total population in 1860 and the proportion of free Black residents in 1860. To adjust for historical patterns of agricultural development and economic capacity, we included indicators of rail and waterway access, farm value per capita, total livestock value, and total farm value. We also included two variables describing the historical distribution of land: the Gini coefficient of land inequality and the proportion of farms under 50 acres. Finally, we also controlled for modern county area and population. Together, these variables control for potential confounds related to modern population density and historical infrastructure, agricultural productivity, wealth, and economic inequality that might otherwise obscure the distinct legacy of slavery.

Instrumental variable.

We used county-level cotton suitability as a geographic index of how well a county’s land could support cotton cultivation in our instrumental variable analysis. Cotton suitability was used as an instrument for the proportion of the population enslaved in 1860 in a 2SLS analysis, to estimate the effect of slavery on modern mortality disparities while accounting for potential endogeneity.

Analytic Approach.

To account for the nested structure of the data, we used a MLM approach with counties nested within states. Two separate MLMs were used to estimate the county level association between the proportion of enslaved people in 1860 and White and Black mortality estimates from 2010 to 2020. The models accounted for the mortality estimates of the other racial group (i.e., the analysis of White mortality controls for Black mortality and vice versa), and the many geographic, economic, and social control variables listed in the methods section. The models include random intercepts for states, allowing each state to have its own baseline mortality level and accounting for clustering of counties within states. This adjusts for between-state differences in average mortality that operate as common intercept shifts but does not identify the sources of those differences. These controls allow the model to account for geographic clustering and shared contextual factors that influence mortality rates. By isolating the portion of the racial mortality gap specifically attributable to historical slavery, the MLMs provide a more robust and nuanced estimate of the association.

Prior to the MLM analysis, cleaning procedures were followed to remove counties that were a) identified as outliers (based on Mahalanobis distance, P < 0.001) or b) lacked robust estimates of Black or White mortality according to the CDC. This yielded 1,169 counties with complete mortality and slavery data, which we use for the initial paired t test comparison. The multilevel models additionally require complete data on the historical covariates, giving a final analytic sample of 901 unique counties in all 16 states, covering counties with roughly 27% of the U.S. population in the year 2000, based on Census estimates.

We used path analysis to examine whether patterns of segregation, economic inequality, and racial disparities in college attainment and poverty are consistent with a theoretical model in which these structural inequalities link slavery and contemporary racial disparities in mortality. Again, the models were designed to account for the many geographic, economic, and social control variables listed in the methods section. The disparity variables were computed as the difference between Black and White rates such that higher values indicate worse outcomes for Black Americans relative to White Americans. Thus, while the dependent variables (DVs) in our MLM analyses were Black and White mortality (respectively), the DV in our path analyses is the Black–White mortality disparity itself.

Prior to the path analysis, cleaning procedures were followed to remove counties that were outliers or did not have complete data with respect to the mediational and control variables. Because the analysis required complete data for several new variables, the final analytic sample was smaller than the MLM sample and included 829 unique counties in all 16 states. The path analysis model was estimated using maximum likelihood estimation (ML) with the NLMINB optimization method on a sample of 829 county-level observations. The model included 78 parameters and had 2 degrees of freedom, indicating that model was overidentified and thus subject to statistical evaluation of fit (described in Results section).

The path analysis was used to summarize a theoretically motivated set of relationships among historical slavery, later structural inequalities, and contemporary racial mortality disparities within a single coherent framework. However, mediation in observational data is not causally identified. Thus, we interpret model pathway estimates descriptively. To strengthen causal inference, we used an instrumental-variable design as an identification strategy (see below).

To strengthen causal inference and address the concern that the proportion of the population enslaved in 1860 may be correlated with unmeasured economic or political factors, we conducted a series of instrumental variable (IV) analyses using cotton suitability as an instrument. Cotton suitability is a geographic measure of how well each county’s land could support cotton cultivation, based on climate and soil conditions. Soil suitability reflects natural environmental features and is not plausibly shaped by human decisions, yet it is a strong predictor of where slavery was economically viable in the 19th century (20). This makes cotton suitability a historically grounded and theoretically appropriate instrument for leveraging plausible exogenous variation in slavery intensity, that is, variation driven by natural geography rather than by potentially confounding social or political factors.

A concern with instrumental variable analysis is the exclusion restriction (21): Cotton suitability could independently affect contemporary structural inequality and mortality disparities through pathways other than slavery. To mitigate this concern, all instrumental variable models include state effects and the same set of historical geographic, economic, and social covariates used in the MLM and path analyses, thereby comparing counties with similar baseline development profiles within broader policy and institutional contexts. We implemented a 2SLS regression framework. In the first stage, cotton suitability was used to predict the proportion enslaved in each county. In the second stage, the predicted values of slavery were used to estimate its causal effect on mortality disparities and each of the mediators used in the path analysis.

We conducted several complementary robustness exercises to probe alternatives to our hypothesized pathway from slavery to contemporary mortality disparities. A full description of these robustness tests is provided in SI Appendix.
