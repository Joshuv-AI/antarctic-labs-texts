High-throughput discovery of ultrahigh-temperature multi-principal element alloys by combinatorial additive manufacturing
Authors: Weiqi Wang, Jian Liu, Chenyang Li, Yi-Cheng Lai, Wei Chen, Yunzhuo Lu, Wen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeveloping structural materials with ultrahigh-temperature capabilities is crucial for aerospace and energy applications, yet achieving a balance of strength, heat-softening resistance, and plasticity remains challenging. Here, we report a tungsten-based W-Re-Os alloy with exceptional mechanical properties up to 1400 °C. Utilizing multi-principal element alloy design principles, three refractory metals with melting points above 3000 °C—W, Re, and Os elements are alloyed using combinatorial additive manufacturing. This approach enabled rapid fabrication of ~500 compositions in a single run. High-throughput micro-indentation testing identified W42Re30Os28 as a standout candidate, exhibiting an ultrahigh yield strength of ~1.8 GPa and ~9% compressive plasticity at room temperature, while retaining ~1.4 GPa yield strength with remarkable strain-hardening at 1400 °C, far surpassing other high-temperature alloys reported to date. These properties arise from its dual-phase hypoeutectic microstructure and multiple deformation mechanisms, including basal and non-basal dislocation slip, deformation twinning, and hetero-deformation-induced geometrically necessary dislocations.Subject terms: Metals and alloys, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

The increasing demands of space exploration and energy production require the development of new materials capable of withstanding extremely high-temperature environments1,2. To date, Ni-based superalloys have been the workhorse candidates for use at elevated temperatures3. However, they experience rapid softening above 1000 °C due to their relatively low melting points4. Recently, a paradigm-shifting alloy design strategy known as multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs), also referred to as compositionally complex alloys or high-entropy alloys, has been proposed to advance structural materials5,6. In particular, refractory MPEAs have emerged as a promising class of materials for high-temperature applications, primarily due to their ability to maintain strength at elevated temperatures6–8. For example, a CrMoNbV refractory MPEA can achieve a compressive yield strength of 1 GPa and demonstrate excellent resistance to heat-softening at 1000 °C9.

While refractory MPEAs broaden the search for alloys with enhanced high-temperature performance, the immense compositional space inherent to multi-principal alloys presents a significant challenge in efficiently screening suitable compositions10. The conventional trial-and-error approach in metallurgy is inadequate, as experimentally identifying optimal alloys in such an enormous composition space is costly and time-consuming11,12. Recently, high-throughput experimental approaches have been developed to search for MPEAs13,14. Gradient thin-film deposition, utilizing techniques such as magnetron sputtering or multi-arc ion plating, is the most widely used route to high-throughput preparation of compositional libraries15. These combinatorial methods may be suitable for developing certain functional materials, where composition typically dominates over microstructure in governing the resulting properties, like optoelectronic properties16. For structural materials, however, mechanical properties are highly sensitive to microstructural characteristics, including grain size and orientation, phase constitution, texture, dislocations, and stacking faults (SFs)17. In this regard, the microstructural features and length scales of thin films often differ significantly from those in bulk materials18. As a result, insights gained from combinatorial thin film approaches are often limited or even irrelevant for the development of bulk materials, which are necessary for many structural applications.

Laser additive manufacturing (AM) is a disruptive technology that enables the fabrication of bulk metallic components with complex geometries. There are two main types of laser AM techniques: laser powder-bed fusion (L-PBF) and laser-directed energy deposition (L-DED)19. In L-PBF, a flat bed of powders is spread over a substrate, and a laser beam selectively fuses the exposed regions to form the part layer by layer. In L-DED, powders are fed through multiple nozzles and melted by a coaxial laser beam to deposit materials onto a substrate in a layer-wise fashion. The multiple nozzles in L-DED allow for simultaneous feeding and melting of different powder feedstocks with individual control over the flow rates20. By dynamically varying the flow rate of each powder, a combinatorial library with spatially varied compositions can be generated, enabling the exploration of a vast compositional space within a target alloy system21–23. Compared to thin film deposition, the combinatorial AM (CAM) technique produces compositional libraries at millimeter-to-centimeter scale, allowing for the characterization of bulk mechanical properties24. The high energy provided by the laser during AM process also offers the potential for net-shaping of “hard-to-process” refractory alloys into geometrically complex components, which are difficult to achieve via traditional processing routes25. In recent years, CAM has emerged as a powerful tool for exploring refractory MPEAs11,26. However, most refractory MPEAs reported hitherto suffer from room-temperature brittleness and/or limited thermal stability at ultrahigh temperatures above 1000 °C27, and the advancements have been confined to a limited subset of elements and compositions, leaving the vast potential of MPEAs largely untapped.

In this work, we report a series of W-Re-Os refractory MPEAs that exhibit an exceptional combination of room-temperature plasticity and high-temperature strength up to 1400 °C, achieved through the high-throughput CAM approach. In particular, the identified W42Re30Os28 refractory MPEA demonstrates an ultrahigh yield strength of 1.83 ± 0.03 GPa and a compressive plasticity of 9.0 ± 0.5% at room temperature, along with excellent thermal stability, retaining an ultrahigh yield strength of 1.38 ± 0.02 GPa at 1400 °C. Our work not only introduces a novel refractory alloy family for applications at extreme temperatures but also paves the way for rapid discovery of advanced MPEAs with targeted properties.

Results

Alloy design

The goal of this study is to develop refractory MPEAs with exceptional room-temperature plasticity and high strength across a broad temperature range. The design process for such MPEAs starts with the careful selection of constituent elements. In general, alloys begin to soften significantly when heated above 0.5 ~ 0.6 Tm (where Tm is the melting temperature)27. Thus, we selected refractory elements with high melting temperatures for improved thermal stability of the MPEAs. We also prioritized elements with high intrinsic strength, as this often contributes to elevating the strength of the resulting alloy. Since yield strength typically correlates with Young’s modulus in most metals, we used Young’s modulus, an easily accessible mechanical property, as a key parameter to select metallic elements with high strength. Figure 1a summarizes the melting temperatures of most metallic elements in relation to their Young’s moduli28. Refractory elements W, Re, and Os stand out in the upper right corner, highlighting their potential for ultrahigh-temperature applications. The Tm values for W, Re, and Os all exceed 3000 °C. In addition, W is well-known for its excellent resistance to oxidation and irradiation29,30, while Re and Os are favored for applications requiring high-temperature strength and wear resistance31,32. Hence, we focus on the ternary W-Re-Os MPEA system to explore novel refractory MPEAs suited for structural applications at extreme temperatures.

High-throughput fabrication

The multi-principal element nature of MPEAs greatly expands the number of possible alloys, creating an enormous compositional space for alloy design. However, this vast compositional space also presents a significant challenge for efficiently screening target alloy compositions. To rapidly identify refractory MPEAs with ultrahigh-temperature capabilities within the ternary W-Re-Os alloy system, we synthesized a large compositional library using the CAM technique. Elemental powders of W, Re, and Os were fed independently from three separate powder hoppers. These powders were directed through coaxial nozzles by argon flow and subsequently delivered into the melt pool (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1). The compositional spread of the library was controlled by adjusting the mass flow rate of each elemental powder feedstock during laser printing. Using the CAM approach, we rapidly prepared a W-Re-Os bulk compositional library of 496 patch samples with continuously varied compositions, covering nearly the entire ternary phase diagram in compositional steps of 3 at.% (Fig. 1c). Each patch sample in the library is approximately 5 mm in diameter and 3 mm in height, as shown in the magnified view in Fig. 1d. Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was conducted on randomly selected W-Re-Os samples to verify the accuracy of the chemical compositions within the library (see “Methods”). The compositions of these selected samples closely matched the target compositions (see Supplementary Table 1), confirming that CAM is an effective and accurate high-throughput synthesis approach.

High-throughput characterization and screening

After obtaining the W-Re-Os compositional library, we applied high-throughput characterization techniques to investigate the phase constituents and thermal stability of the samples. The phase constituents of all samples at room temperature were analyzed via X-ray diffraction (XRD) (see “Methods”). Figure 2a presents the phase map of the W-Re-Os library, showing three single-phase regions, i.e., hexagonal close-packed (HCP) solid solution, body-centered tetragonal (BCT) intermetallic compound, and body-centered cubic (BCC) solid solution, as well as two dual-phase regions of HCP + BCT and BCT + BCC. Specifically, chemical composition analyses indicate that the BCT phase in the HCP + BCT dual-phase region corresponds to a ReW-type intermetallic phase, whereas the BCT phase in the BCT + BCC dual-phase region is identified as a W7Os3-type intermetallic phase (Supplementary Table 2). The XRD patterns of four representative samples, selected from the four primary phase regions and labeled as b, c, d, and e in Fig. 2a, are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. To further verify the microstructures and phase constituents, we performed electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) on these four samples (Fig. 2b–e). The EBSD phase maps reveal distinct microstructural features, including single-phase columnar grain structure and dual-phase hypoeutectic structure. Among the synthesized alloys, the alloys with single-phase BCT have cracks, as exemplified by sample d (Fig. 2d), likely due to the high thermal stresses during AM and the brittleness of such an intermetallic structure. Most other alloy compositions in the library are free of cracks. These W-Re-Os alloys demonstrate robust phase stability, as evidenced by high-temperature XRD measurements up to the instrument’s temperature limit of 1000 °C (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Methods). The XRD spectra of these representative samples remain essentially unchanged at 400, 600, 800, and 1000 °C. Notably, these W-Re-Os refractory MPEAs retain their phase even beyond the 1000 °C limit of our XRD instrument. For instance, after annealing at 1400 °C for 6 h, similar XRD patterns were observed (Supplementary Fig. 3). This excellent thermal stability likely stems from the low homologous temperature of the heat treatment (<0.5 Tm) relative to the alloy’s ultrahigh melting temperature (>3000 °C, see “Discussion”), at which phase transformations are largely suppressed.

To identify W-Re-Os MPEAs with high strength over a wide range of temperatures, the Vickers hardness of the W-Re-Os library was measured from room temperature up to 1000 °C using a high-temperature Vickers indentation system (see “Methods”). The Vickers indentation probes a sample size in the range of hundreds of micrometers, providing a reliable measure of a material’s strength33. The room-temperature hardness (H25 °C) map of the W-Re-Os library is shown in Fig. 3a, where H25 °C of the samples ranges from 323 ± 4 to 1608 ± 17 HV. Comparing this map with the phase map in Fig. 2a reveals that the samples containing BCT phase exhibit significantly higher hardness than other samples. Among the samples containing BCT phase, the two MPEAs, W51Re12Os37 and W69Re12Os19, exhibit the highest hardness among the crack-free and cracked alloys, respectively, with hardness values of 1064 HV and 1608 HV. We conducted a comparative analysis of the Vickers hardness values of the crack-free W51Re12Os37 MPEA against those of other crack-free alloys fabricated through AM, as illustrated in Fig. 3b26,34–42. Remarkably, our W51Re12Os37 MPEA demonstrates unprecedentedly high hardness, outperforming state-of-the-art hard materials, including tool steels34–36, Ni-based superalloys37, cemented carbides38, tungsten alloys39, and other refractory MPEAs26,40–42. High-temperature hardness maps of the W-Re-Os library measured at 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 °C are provided in Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 4. The hardness of all samples decreases with increasing temperature, but the rate of decrease varies widely. To quantify the thermal stability of the W-Re-Os alloys, we propose a hardness retention rate (RT), defined as: RT = HT/H25 °C, where HT and H25 °C are the hardness values at high temperature (T) and room temperature, respectively. The RT maps of the W-Re-Os library at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 5. Based on these RT maps and the phase map in Fig. 2a, it is seen that alloys containing BCT phase have higher RT values than other samples, indicating their superior thermomechanical stability among the considered alloys.

Although refractory alloys are often malleable at high temperatures, many suffer from limited plasticity at room temperature9,43,44. Therefore, assessing room-temperature plasticity is a critical criterion for screening W-Re-Os alloys in this work. Compared to the high-throughput characterization of hardness (or strength), high-throughput measurement of room-temperature plasticity is challenging. This difficulty arises because standard plasticity tests require laborious sample preparation and adherence to specific protocols (e.g., ASTM standards). In this study, we use the pile-up morphology around the Vickers hardness indentation as a quantitative measure of room-temperature plasticity. Given that plastic deformation incurred during hardness testing results in a pile-up in the vicinity of the indentation, the magnitude of this pile-up is a reliable metric for assessing a material’s plastic deformability. This method has been widely used in aluminum and cuprum alloys45,46 and is employed in this study for high-throughput assessment of plasticity of the W-Re-Os alloys. Supplementary Fig. 6 illustrates a typical indentation pile-up morphology, while Fig. 3e presents a map of the average pile-up height (h¯) across different alloys in the W-Re-Os library. In the case where a crack is observed around an indentation, h¯ is recorded as 0. Overall, these results suggest that alloys with higher Re contents exhibit better room-temperature plasticity.

To identify W-Re-Os compositions with an optimal combination of high-temperature strength, heat-softening resistance, and room-temperature plasticity, the technique for order preference by similarity to ideal solution (TOPSIS)47, was adopted in this study. TOPSIS provides an intuitive approach for ranking alternatives, enabling the selection of the most favorable composition based on multiple key metrics. Details of the TOPSIS evaluation process are provided in the Supplementary Note 1. Three key factors were considered when searching for target W-Re-Os alloys: normalized high-temperature hardness measured at 1000 °C (H1000∘C/Hmax1000∘C), normalized hardness retention rate measured at 1000°C (R1000∘C/Rmax1000∘C), and normalized average height of the pile-up around Vickers indentation (h¯/h¯max) measured at room temperature. The mappings of these three normalized properties for the W-Re-Os library are presented in Supplementary Fig. 7. By calculating the relative proximity (Ci, where i = 1 ~ 496) for the entire sample library, we successfully narrowed down the target composition region featuring higher Ci values, as delineated by the gray dashed circle in Fig. 3f. Notably, the Ci values within this region all exceed 0.5, suggesting their high potential for achieving exceptional mechanical properties. In particular, alloy composition labeled as “3” distinguishes itself with the highest Ci value of 0.63.

Microstructures of target alloys

After locating the target composition region (Fig. 3f), we selected five representative W-Re-Os alloys in this region, denoted as WRO-1 (W51Re12Os37), WRO-2 (W45Re21Os34), WRO-3 (W42Re30Os28), WRO-4 (W33Re48Os19), and WRO-5 (W27Re63Os10), and further examined their microstructures. The XRD patterns (Supplementary Fig. 8) show that all five alloys consist of an HCP solid solution (lattice parameters a = 0.277 nm, c = 0.4511 nm) and a ReW-type BCT intermetallic phase (a = 0.963 nm, c = 0.501 nm), hereafter denoted BCT-ReW. Interestingly, they exhibit a type of dual-phase hypoeutectic microstructure (Fig. 3g–k). The phase fractions and phase-specific compositions of the five selected alloys are shown in Supplementary Table 3. As the W content continuously decreases from 51 to 27 at% from WRO-1 to WRO-5, the BCT-ReW phase fraction decreases from 34.7 to 16.4%, while the HCP fraction increases from 65.3 to 83.6%. As a representative case, we examined the overall microstructure of the WRO-3 MPEA across three regions with markedly distinct thermal histories: the top and bottom of the melt pool and the heat-affected zone (HAZ) beneath the melt pool (Supplementary Fig. 9). The as-printed WRO-3 MPEA exhibits a hierarchical microstructure comprising globular-rosette and columnar hypoeutectic colonies (Supplementary Fig. 9b1, c1, d1). Both types of hypoeutectic colonies consist of HCP and BCT-ReW phases (Supplementary Fig. 9b2, c2, d2). Higher-magnification SEM and EBSD images reveal morphologically similar dual-phase hypoeutectic structures in all regions (Supplementary Fig. 9b3–b5, c3–c5, d3–d5), with slightly coarser colonies in the HAZ. In the dual-phase region, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy and EDS elemental maps show a characteristic eutectic structure, with alternating HCP and BCT-ReW nanolamellae with an average thickness of 360 nm and 430 nm, respectively (Fig. 3l). The HCP solid solution is rich in Os and Re, while the BCT-ReW intermetallic is enriched by W and Re. High-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) and corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns at the phase interface further verify their crystal structures (Fig. 3m and “Methods”). Similar HCP/BCT-ReW nanolamellar morphology was also observed in other dual-phase eutectic regions (Supplementary Fig. 10). Therefore, for the hypoeutectic WRO-3 alloy, the HCP phase is the primary (proeutectic) phase that crystallizes first from the liquid, followed by a eutectic reaction that generates the HCP + BCT-ReW lamellar eutectic colonies during solidification. We further assessed the thermal stability of the dual-phase hypoeutectic microstructure in WRO-3 alloy. After annealing at 1050 °C for 120 h, both the HCP solid solution and BCT-ReW intermetallic are retained without evident coarsening (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating superior thermal stability.

Mechanical properties of target alloys

We further performed uniaxial compression tests for the five selected alloys at room temperature (see Methods). As shown in Fig. 4a, WRO-1 and WRO-2 alloys exhibit ultrahigh yield strength (σ0.2 > 2 GPa) but have limited plasticity (<5%). In contrast, WRO-4 and WRO-5 alloys present large plasticity (>10%) but lower yield strength (<1.5 GPa). Notably, WRO-3 alloy (W42Re30Os28) achieves a yield strength of 1.83 ± 0.03 GPa and a fracture strength of 2.07 ± 0.02 GPa while maintaining a large plasticity of ~9%. A direct comparison of the mechanical properties of these W-Re-Os alloys with other AM48,49 and cast refractory MPEAs9,43,50–65 is illustrated in Fig. 4b. WRO-3 demonstrates an excellent combination of strength and plasticity, outperforming the existing refractory MPEAs. We note that the WRO-3 alloy fractures at approximately 900 ± 85 MPa in the elastic regime under uniaxial tension with no measurable tensile ductility at room temperature. This is because microstructural defects (e.g., microcracks) and weak links (e.g., grain boundary decohesion) of the microstructure can rapidly propagate in many refractory MPEAs under tensile stresses66,67. Nevertheless, such tensile fracture strength is substantially higher than that of many previously reported refractory MPEAs66,67. Therefore, the WRO-3 alloy was selected as the benchmark for further investigation of high-temperature mechanical properties.

The compressive strain-stress responses of WRO-3 at elevated temperatures up to 1400 °C are shown in Supplementary Fig. 12. Although the yield strength of WRO-3 gradually decreases with increasing temperature, it retains high at 1.38 ± 0.02 GPa even at 1400 °C. Furthermore, the WRO-3 MPEA also exhibits exceptional work-hardening capability at 1400 °C, significantly exceeding other conventional alloys that soften severely at high temperatures67. The yield strength of WRO-3 at various temperatures is summarized in Fig. 4c, in comparison to other AM and cast Ni-based superalloys and refractory alloys27,43,48,63–65,68,69. The WRO-3 MPEA demonstrates substantially higher yield strengths at temperatures above 800 °C. More strikingly, the WRO-3 MPEA exhibits ultrastrong thermal stability or heat-softening resistance.

To further understand the superb heat-softening resistance of WRO-3 MPEA, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed with the exact muffin-tin orbitals coherent potential approximation (EMTO-CPA)70 to predict the yield strengths of HCP and BCT-ReW phases as a function of temperature (Fig. 4d). The details of DFT calculations are provided in Supplementary Note 2. As shown in Fig. 4d, the predicted yield strength of the BCT-ReW phase is higher than that of the HCP phase in the whole temperature range, demonstrating that the BCT-ReW phase plays a key role of high-temperature strengthening in WRO-3 alloy. The experimental yield strengths of WRO-3 tested at elevated temperatures are also shown in this figure. The experimental results fall between the predicted values of HCP and BCT-ReW phases. Moreover, the WRO-3 MPEA exhibits slightly stronger heat-softening resistance compared to the individual HCP and BCT-ReW phases. It is important to note that this DFT-based model describes the intrinsic lattice resistance to dislocation motion in a perfect, defect-free crystal with idealized phase compositions (without considering local chemical modulation)71,72. While the approach has proven predictive under simple edge- or screw-dislocation–dominated conditions, the operative dislocation character in WRO-3 alloy is likely more complex. As such, the DFT-derived yield strengths should be viewed as relative trend indicators under idealized conditions rather than precise absolute values. Nonetheless, a key advantage of this DFT model lies in its ability to capture temperature-dependent trends in intrinsic phase strength, which helps rationalize how each phase contributes to the mechanical behavior over a wide temperature regime. Indeed, our experimental measurements of temperature-dependent strength align qualitatively with these predictions (Fig. 4d).

Discussion

We have successfully identified a series of high-performance W-Re-Os refractory MPEAs by using the CAM approach in combination with high-throughput material characterization. As a representative validation of the introduced high-throughput alloy discovery method, WRO-3 MPEA achieves a high yield strength and plasticity at room temperature, paired with ultrastrong heat-softening resistance at extreme temperatures up to 1400 °C. Understanding the mechanisms underlying these superb mechanical properties is imperative for future design of ultrahigh-temperature refractory MPEAs.

The high room-temperature yield strength of the WRO-3 MPEA stems primarily from the strengthening effects of the dual-phase structure. The WRO-3 hypoeutectic MPEA features HCP primary dendrites and lamellar HCP + BCT-ReW eutectic regions, with overall HCP and BCT-ReW phase fractions of 76% ± 5% and 24% ± 5%, respectively. The BCT-ReW phase forms lamellar structures with HCP in the eutectic colony regions. Notably, the lamellar eutectic regions form a continuous network and interpenetrate with the primary HCP phase throughout the sample. Hence, the BCT-ReW phase effectively strengthens the lamellar eutectic regions and contributes to the alloy’s yield strength. This is corroborated by the accumulation of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) not only within HCP but also in the lamellar eutectic regions at ~1% strain upon yielding, while the lamellar interfaces mostly remain intact even at ~8% strain—indicating good interfacial strength (see Fig. 5 later). For such a hypoeutectic alloy, we employed the rule of mixture as a first-order estimate of the overall yield strength of WRO-373. According to DFT simulations, we calculate the yield strength of the pure HCP and BCT-ReW phase alloys to be 1.55 ± 0.01 GPa and 2.53 ± 0.08 GPa, respectively, giving rise to a predicted yield strength of 1.79 GPa based on the rule of mixture (Fig. 4d). As a result, the estimated room-temperature yield strength from DFT simulations closely matches the experimental measurement (1.83 GPa) (Fig. 4a).

The excellent room-temperature plasticity of the WRO-3 MPEA originates from its sustained work-hardening capability. Post-mortem microstructure analyses were conducted at various strain levels to unravel the deformation mechanism of WRO-3 (Fig. 5). At ~1% strain, the sample starts to yield macroscopically. Deformation slip traces appear in the HCP matrix, while plastic deformation in the BCT-ReW phase is minimal (Fig. 5a–c). This heterogeneous deformation behavior results from the substantial difference in hardness (or yield strength) between the HCP and BCT-ReW phases. The hardness values of the HCP and BCT-ReW phases are measured by nanoindentation to be ~8.6 GPa and ~18.4 GPa, respectively (see “Methods”). The soft HCP phase yields first during deformation. Geometric constraints from the hard BCT-ReW phase induce strong local plastic strain gradients near the HCP/BCT-ReW interfaces, which are accommodated by GNDs74, as evidenced by the Kernel average misorientation map (Fig. 5c) and TEM micrograph (Supplementary Fig. 13). The continued build-up of GNDs near the phase interfaces generates strong back stresses, leading to significant back-stress hardening. The sample-level back stress can be quantitatively assessed through loading-unloading experiments (Supplementary Fig. 14). The back stress reaches ~1.2 GPa at ~1.3% strain and increases to ~1.3 GPa at ~2.6% strain, accounting for 74% of the overall flow stress. The continuous increase in back stress imparts a steady work-hardening ability to the WRO-3 MPEA, resulting in a high plasticity. Additionally, numerous pyramidal <c + a> dislocations are observed at the HCP/BCT-ReW interfaces (Fig. 5d). Supplementary Fig. 15 provides more details of these pyramidal <c + a> dislocations. This observation implies that multiple slip systems, including both basal and non-basal slips, are activated in the WRO-3 MPEA, in contrast to conventional HCP alloys, which typically deforms through basal slip only75. The underlying reasons for activating such non-basal <c + a> dislocations are two-fold: first, the activation of the non-basal <c + a> dislocations is closely related to the crystallographic axial ratio c/a of the hexagonal unit cell, which is directly linked to the lattice spacing. The value of the c/a ratio for ideal HCP lattice is 1.633, corresponding to the close packing of hard spheres in a perfect HCP structure. Reducing the c/a ratio to below 1.633 is an efficient way of lowering the atomic packing density of non-basal planes, thus reducing the critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) of non-basal <c + a> dislocations64,76,77. The lattice parameters of the HCP phase in the WRO-3 MPEA, determined by neutron diffraction spectra (Supplementary Fig. 16), are a = 0.2771 nm and c = 0.4508 nm, giving c/a = 1.627. This reduced c/a ratio effectively decreases the CRSS for <c + a> dislocation slip systems in the HCP matrix, thereby activating a large number of <c + a> dislocations. Second, during plastic deformation, high stress concentration near the HCP/BCT-ReW interfaces makes it easier to reach the CRSS for <c + a> dislocations78. Therefore, <c + a> dislocations can be found to be preferentially activated near the phase boundaries79 (Fig. 5d). The activation of the atypical non-basal slip promotes more uniform deformation, thereby providing a progressive work-hardening mechanism in our WRO-3 alloy77.

As the strain increases to ~4%, abundant slip lines emerge (Fig. 5e). Twinning becomes a pivotal deformation mechanism within the WRO-3 MPEA (Fig. 5f), effectively hinder the movement of dislocation slip (Fig. 5g). The {112¯1}<11¯26> twins are frequently observed and tend to align parallel to each other (Fig. 5h–j). Certain variants of these twins intersect, forming an intricate network (Supplementary Fig. 17). Notably, the {112¯1} twins can traverse grain boundaries, thereby effectively augmenting the twin density and impeding dislocation motion80. This is corroborated by evident dislocation pile-up and/or blockage along the twins (Fig. 5g, k), which results in enhanced work-hardening. Furthermore, the twinning nucleated at the phase boundaries has effectively alleviated the stress concentration at the HCP/BCT-ReW interfaces (Fig. 5k, l). In addition, deformation-induced SFs are activated in the HCP solid solution (Fig. 5m). The intersections between twin-twin and twin-HCP/BCT-ReW phase interfaces (Fig. 5f, k, l) present a unique three-dimensional network, facilitating progressive and steady work hardening in WRO-3 MPEA. As the strain increases to ~8%, dense slip lines are observed in the HCP solid solution (Fig. 5n). Part of the slip bands in the figure is identified as the high-density twins by the IPF map analysis (Fig. 5o). The hard BCT-ReW phase continues to interact with deformation twins, forming a massive three-dimensional network of obstacles to dislocation propagation (Fig. 5p). Additionally, the SEM characterization also reveals that multiple microcracks emerge at the HCP/BCT-ReW interfaces, arrested and deflected by the adjacent softer HCP solid solution (Fig. 5n). These high-density HCP/BCT-ReW interfaces serve as soft crack buffers that act on the tips of microcracks, separating incipient microcracks and effectively postponing the crack propagation and coalescence. This feature promotes uniformly distributed microcracks and reduces local stress concentration, thereby stabilizing the plastic flow81. This can be verified by the microstructure of WRO-3 MPEA near the fracture surface, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 18. A variety of microcrack morphologies are observed in this figure, including tortuous and bifurcated microcracks, which are strong evidence for the deflection of crack propagation. The resistance to crack propagation and coalescence in WRO-3 MPEA during deformation stands in contrast to other brittle materials, which typically fail due to the propagation of a single dominant crack. Moreover, we also performed quasi in situ neutron diffraction experiments to understand the origin of the steady work-hardening ability of WRO-3 MPEA. The neutron diffraction spectra indicate that no phase transition occurs during deformation. The peak broadening further confirms that the dislocations and twins play dominant roles during room-temperature plastic deformation (Supplementary Fig. 16). These post-mortem SEM, EBSD, TEM, and neutron diffraction results indicate that the activation of both basal and non-basal slips, along with deformation twinning in the HCP phase and hetero-deformation-induced GNDs at the HCP/BCT-ReW phase interfaces, collectively contribute to the steady work-hardening at high flow stresses. This collective deformation behavior of the WRO-3 MPEA is schematically illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 19.

Next, we discuss the superb mechanical properties at high temperatures. The excellent heat-softening resistance of WRO-3 MPEA is first attributed to its ultrahigh Tm. Notably, the HCP solid solution and BCT-ReW intermetallic compound show no discernible microstructural coarsening or dynamic recrystallization even at extremely high temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 20), which is closely related to its ultrahigh Tm. Generally, alloys with higher Tm exhibit higher yield strengths at elevated temperatures as diffusion-controlled deformation mechanisms become active to cause softening at temperatures above approximately 0.5–0.6 Tm. Tm of WRO-3 is estimated to be 3244 °C by the rule of mixture43, which is the highest among reported refractory MPEAs to our knowledge9,27,43. In addition, the dual-phase structure of WRO-3 MPEA contributes significantly to its excellent heat-softening resistance, as shown in Fig. 4d. The higher heat-softening resistance of the HCP phase, compared to other single phases like BCC, results from the lower rate of atomic diffusion in the close-packed HCP structure82. For BCT-ReW intermetallic phase, the complex polyhedra crystal structure with a high coordination number form extremely small interstices and a high atomic packing density, thereby stabilizing their strength at high temperatures83. As illustrated in Fig. 4d, the yield strength of the dual-phase WRO-3 MPEA decreases more slowly with increasing temperature than the single HCP and BCT-ReW phases, suggesting that the WRO-3 MPEA exhibits better heat-softening resistance. The dual phases form an interlocked hypoeutectic structure, which may help hinder the high-temperature migration of grain boundaries84. Meanwhile, the interfaces between the hard BCT-ReW intermetallic and relatively soft HCP matrix act as a barrier to slow down atomic diffusion85. The phase interfaces can also suppress grain boundary sliding during high-temperature deformation as compared with single-phase materials to further enhance thermal stability86.

The WRO-3 MPEA also exhibits excellent work-hardening ability at high temperatures, particularly when the temperature exceeds 800 °C. EBSD experiments were conducted to detect changes in the crystallographic orientation relationship of the WRO-3 MPEAs after deformation at different temperatures (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 21). The results reveal enhanced twinning activities with increasing deformation temperature. Of particular interest, unlike the single type of deformation twins of {112¯1} at temperatures <800 °C (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 21), two types of twins with distinct misorientations are observed at temperatures above 800 °C by boundary misorientation analysis (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Fig. 21f). These are {112¯1} twins with a misorientation of ~34° (blue) and {101¯2} twins with a misorientation of 85° (yellow). The deformation structure at 1200 °C shows evident intragranular misorientations, revealing significant dislocation slips and deformation twinning in the HCP phase (Fig. 6a, b). Bright field TEM and precession electron diffraction (PED) were used to further investigate the deformation structures of the sample at a higher resolution (Fig. 6c–g). The high density of tangled dislocations in the vicinity of the HCP/BCT-ReW phase interfaces revealed by TEM (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 22) suggests that hetero-deformation between the dual phases persists. Meanwhile, numerous deformation twins become visible at the phase interfaces (Fig. 6d, e). PED was employed to analyze the twin orientations. Figure 6f, g confirms two types of twins in the HCP phase, identified as {112¯1} and {101¯2} twins, consistent with the EBSD experimental observations. The difference between low-temperature (<800 °C) and high-temperature (>800 °C) twinning behavior originates from the different activation barriers for the respective twinning systems. The {112¯1} twins are more readily activated than {101¯2} twins, in part because pyramidal <c + a> dislocation slip can incorporate a {112¯1} twin embryo87. Furthermore, the {112¯1} twin in the HCP formation phase only requires twinning shear, whereas the activation of {101¯2} twins involves significant lattice shuffling and atomic interchange88. Due to slow atomic diffusion at low temperatures, activation of {101¯2} twins is challenging, with only {112¯1} twins occurring under shear. As temperature rises, enhanced atomic diffusion facilitates activation of the secondary type of {101¯2} twins at temperatures above 800 °C. In addition, the two types of activated twins are inclined in different orientations and intertwined with each other. The formation of twin junctions acts as an effective barrier to twin propagation, contributing to the excellent work-hardening ability of the WRO-3 MPEA at higher temperatures (>800 °C).

In summary, we developed a family of W-Re-Os refractory MPEAs that exhibit excellent combinations of ultrahigh-temperature strength, heat-softening resistance, and room-temperature plasticity. This was achieved through a high-throughput material discovery approach integrating CAM-based high-throughput alloy synthesis and high-throughput material characterization. Among the studied alloys, W42Re30Os28 demonstrates an ultrahigh yield strength of ~1.8 ± 0.03 GPa while maintaining a significant plasticity of 9.0 ± 0.5% at room temperature. Additionally, this alloy exhibits outstanding heat-softening resistance, retaining a yield strength of ~1.4 ± 0.02 GPa and steady work-hardening ability at 1400 °C, far surpassing other high-temperature alloys reported to date. These remarkable mechanical properties are primarily attributed to the alloy’s high melting temperature and the dual-phase hypoeutectic microstructure, where multiple deformation mechanisms, including dislocation slips, twinning, and hetero-deformation-induced GNDs, collectively operate to deliver sustained strengthening. The exceptional mechanical performance of the W42Re30Os28 MPEA makes it a strong candidate for critical ultrahigh-temperature (>1000 °C) components engineered to operate under compressive/contact-dominated stresses, particularly in some aerospace and nuclear energy applications that demand resilience in extreme environments. Looking ahead, we will integrate our high-throughput experimental platform with dislocation-based mechanistic models89–92 to search for high-strength, thermally stable refractory MPEAs with additional tensile ductility for a broader application space. The high-throughput alloy discovery framework described in this study represents a significant advancement in accelerating new alloy development for multi-faceted target properties.

Methods

Materials fabrication

The bulk W-Re-Os alloy library in this study was fabricated by a coaxial powder feeding L-DED-based CAM system, which was equipped with a fiber laser with a maximum power of 6 kW and a focal diameter of 3 mm. Figure 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1 show the schematic illustration of the experimental setup for the CAM system. The laser power was 3000 W, and the travel speed of the laser beam relative to the substrate was kept constant at 600 mm/min. CAM experiments were carried out in a working chamber filled with argon to maintain an oxygen level below 10 ppm. The powder feeding system utilized a rotary feed wheel and carrier gas to transport the powder feedstock, allowing for independently controlled powder flow rates. Prior to fabricating the alloys, the powder feeding system underwent calibration through a series of experiments to measure the powder flow rate for each elemental feedstock. The 45-carbon steel plate was used as the substrate during the CAM experiments. The substrate surface was ground with 400-grit SiC papers and thoroughly cleaned in ethanol prior to CAM in order to aid the laser absorption process. The fabricated alloy library, presented in Fig. 1c, was composed of ~500 discrete patch samples with a diameter of ~5 mm and a height of ~3 mm. The three elemental powders of W, Re, and Os, stored separately in three powder hoppers, were fed through coaxial nozzles by argon flow and delivered simultaneously into the melt pool created by the laser beam. The compositions of the deposited W-Re-Os patch samples were accurately controlled through tuning the feed rates of the three powders.

Microstructure characterization

The phase constituents and thermal stability of the patch samples within the bulk composition library were efficiently identified from 25 to 1000 °C using high-temperature XRD instruments at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The compositions of the patch samples were checked by the EDS. The pile-up around the room-temperature Vickers hardness indentation was characterized using the Olympus OLS-3100 equipment. Five representative W-Re-Os MPEA bulk samples, labeled as WRO-1 to WRO-5, were also prepared by the coaxial powder feeding LAM system described above. The phase composition of the bulk samples was identified via a Cu-Kα XRD (Empyrean). The microstructures were observed by using an optical microscope (4XC20-V) and an SEM (Zeiss-SUPRA55). The SEM is equipped with a NordlysMax2 backscatter detector, enabling EBSD analysis. For EBSD characterization, the accelerating voltage was 20 keV, and the sample was tilted to 70°. Analysis of the orientation maps was pursued using the Oxford HKL Channel 5 software. TEM samples were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB, Helios G4, ThermoFisher) lift-out method. TEM characterizations were performed using a JEM-2100 (JEOL) transmission electron microscope. FEI Tecnai G2 F20 TEM equipped with a NanoMEGAS ASTAR system was operated at 200 keV for the PED experiments, focusing on an area of interest of 3 μm × 1 μm. The precession angle was 0.3°, and the step size was 20 nm when acquiring the map. The acquired PED file was exported as an EBSD-readable file for further twin relationship analysis. The HR-TEM images were obtained using the JEM-2100, and the corresponding FFT images were calculated using Gatan Digital Micrograph software. Neutron diffraction experiments were conducted at the beamline 7 engineering materials diffractometer, VULCAN93, located at the Spallation Neutron Source, Oak Ridge National Laboratory. With the longitudinal axis positioned at 45° to the incident neutron beam, the time-of-flight neutron diffraction data were simultaneously collected along both the longitudinal and transverse directions by two detector banks situated at ±90° to the incident neutron beam. Utilizing a slit size of 3 mm (length) × 3 mm (width), the neutron diffraction patterns were acquired in a high-intensity mode, achieving a lattice spacing resolution (Δd/d) of approximately 0.45%. Single-peak fitting and the determination of phase weight fractions were accomplished through full-pattern Rietveld refinement using the GSAS-II software94.

Mechanical testing

The hardness of the alloy library was measured using a high-temperature Vickers hardness tester (ZD-HVZHT-10) with 10 kg load and a dwell time of 15 s. The entire alloy library consisting of 496 patch samples was lapped and sanded up to 4000-grit paper, followed by polishing using a 1.5 μm polycrystalline diamond suspension before high-throughput Vickers hardness characterization. In addition, the samples for nanoindentation experiments were further etched by a solution of nitric acid and hydrofluoric acid (HF) with a ratio of 1:1 to identify individual HCP and BCT-ReW phases during nanoindentation. Nanoindentation experiments (KLA nano indenter G200) were performed for the individual BCT-ReW and HCP phases to measure their hardnesses at room temperature. A maximum load of 50 mN was applied, and the load was kept for 10 s before unloading.

Cylindrical compression specimens with a diameter of 2.5 mm and a height of 5 mm were cut from the W-Re-Os bulk samples by electrical discharge machining and finally polished to a metallurgical grit of 3000 silicon carbide paper. Quasi-static uniaxial compression tests were performed on a SUNS UTM5105 universal testing machine at room temperature under a strain rate of 3 × 10−4 s−1. The compressive strain was measured by a dual digital micrometer system (Mitutoyo) with a displacement resolution of 0.1 μm as a type of contact extensometer. The back-stress evolution was measured through the loading-unloading tests. Upon loading to each specific strain level at a strain rate of 3 × 10−4 s−1, the specimen was unloaded to 30 MPa under a force-control mode with a rate of 10 MPa s−1, followed by reloading at a strain rate of 3 × 10−4 s−1.The high-temperature compression tests up to 1400 °C were carried out using a dynamic thermal simulation testing machine (Gleeble-3800). Cylindrical specimens with a diameter of 3 mm and a height of 6 mm were cut from the WRO-3 MPEA samples by electrical discharge machining. The heating rate was 300 °C/min and the compressive strain rate was 10−3 s−1. Compressive strains were measured by directly measuring the displacements of indenters. For high-temperature tests, the sample chamber was evacuated to minimize the influence of oxygen on the experimental outcomes. The above mechanical tests were repeated at least three times for each type of specimen.

First-principles calculations

DFT calculations were performed with the EMTO-CPA70 to predict the structural properties of the studied refractory MPEA systems. The EMTO method uses optimized overlapping muffin-tin potential95 spheres to represent the exact one-electron potential. The one-electron equations were solved using scalar relativistic approximation and the soft-core scheme. The CPA method96 is particularly powerful for modeling disordered multicomponent alloys when local relaxation and short-range order effects are not significant. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof generalized gradient approximation97 was used to approximate the exchange-correlation energy. The EMTO Green’s function was self-consistently calculated for 16 exponentially distributed complex energy points. The initial magnetic state was described using the disordered local magnetic moment approach, and each element was regarded as having separate up- and down-spins. The Brillouin zones were sampled using a 25 × 25 × 25 k-point mesh to obtain the required energy accuracy. The elastic constants were determined by analyzing the total energy change with a series of volume-conserving deformations to the lattice98. The results of these calculations were presented in Supplementary Table 5.
