Equity-informed strategies to promote COVID-19 vaccination uptake undertaken by public health units in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Ana Paula Belon, Naomi Schwartz, Stephen Hunter, Candace IJ Nykiforuk, Brendan T Smith, Roman Pabayo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper examined local public health strategies to promote COVID-19 vaccination equity in Ontario, Canada between 2020 and 2023. Our descriptive mixed-methods study gathered data from local public health units (PHUs) in Ontario on public health’s COVID-19 equity-driven vaccination efforts. PHUs are responsible for delivering local public health services in the province and are provincially mandated to address health equity in their geographic boundaries. Data was collected through a seven-item questionnaire. Open-ended questions were analyzed through an inductive content analysis. From all 34 PHUs, 25 agreed to participate. Eighty-percent of the participating PHUs reported provision of information, community engagement, and improved physical accessibility to COVID-19 vaccines were high/very high priorities. Only 39.1% of PHUs indicated social determinants of health-related data collection as a high/very high priority. Overall, 90% of PHUs reported prioritizing groups, including those with lower socioeconomic status, Indigenous populations, and people experiencing homelessness or precarious housing, in their vaccination response. Four emergent themes included: (1) defining priority populations with data-driven and health equity-oriented processes; (2) promoting vaccination through health equity-informed strategies; (3) prioritizing Indigenous health and sovereignty in vaccination efforts; and (4) recommendations to address health equity-related barriers to COVID-19 vaccination. Examples of recommendations included: adopting culturally-sensitive and context-relevant communication strategies; enabling data linkage of data sources; and providing guidance on the development of intersectoral collaborations. Health equity-informed efforts in COVID-19 vaccination decision-making and actions were broadly adopted to better respond to local needs. Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic resulted in drastic changes to public health systems worldwide. As vaccines became available in late 2020, public health officials had to quickly implement strategies to strengthen COVID-19 vaccine delivery systems and ensure equitable access to the vaccines. In Canada, the federal government, alongside the 10 provincial and 3 territorial health departments, worked together to coordinate the mass vaccination efforts [1, 2].

As elsewhere in Canada, in Ontario (the most populous province), the COVID-19 vaccines were prioritized to high-risk populations (e.g., elderly, immunocompromised, healthcare workers) from 2020 to early-2021 due to the supply shortages [2, 3]. Health equity issues were raised with these distribution strategies [3, 4] as socio-economically vulnerable groups were not included, despite shouldering a disproportionate health burden, with higher COVID-19-related incidence, hospitalization, and mortality rates [4, 5]. Strengthening the public health system to better respond to growing health inequities in vaccination and prevent unintended consequences became central in the public health debate across Ontario [3, 6].

In April 2021, Ontario’s Ministry of Health implemented an accelerated vaccination strategy which included targeting most supplies to COVID-19 hotspots, or postal code areas with higher risk of hospitalization and deaths from COVID-19 [5, 7]. The 34 public health units (PHUs) in Ontario−responsible for delivering public health services within their geographic boundaries−were tasked to implement the province’s vaccination program [7].

The Ontario vaccination effort provides an opportunity to study the challenges of integrating health equity-informed vaccination actions and following top-down federal government guidance to provide tailored support to high-risk populations during a public health crisis. The PHUs are required, through the Ontario Health Equity Standard since 2018, to include health equity in their planning and delivery of public health services [8]. The PHUs’ ongoing health equity work was met with the federal government’s vaccination guidance to provinces to address increasing COVID-19 incidence and mortality rate [9–11]. The purpose of our study was to investigate how PHUs addressed health equity in vaccination strategies during their mass COVID-19 vaccination efforts over a 3-year-period (2020–2023) in Ontario. The decentralized public health system in Ontario provided a unique opportunity to investigate the contextual barriers and different regional strategies used to promote equitable access to COVID-19 vaccination.

Methods

This study uses a mixed-methods approach to describe data collected in the COVID-19 health equity programs survey. The survey was administered by the study team to measure impacts of PHU programming during the COVID-19 pandemic. An Advisory Council, including 10 public health leaders in Ontario, supported the recruitment activities and advised on survey methods and questionnaires. Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Board (Panel B)/University of Alberta (Pro00099672), and Public Health Ontario (PHO 2022 − 028.01). Both waived the need to gather informed consent as Public Health Units completed the survey as an organization.

Data collection

All of the 34 PHUs in Ontario were invited to participate through recruitment emails sent to their PHU leadership (e.g., Medical Officers of Health, Chief Executive Officer) in January 2023, with up to four follow-up emails, as needed, through to August 2023. PHUs who agreed to participate received a secured REDCap survey link. PHU leadership was instructed to appoint one or more PHU staff members who were most knowledgeable about health equity in COVID-19 vaccinations to answer the questions on behalf of their organization. Twenty-five PHUs responded to the survey (response rate = 73.5%). These PHUs represented all of the six geographic regions across Ontario (according to the classification from the Association of Local Public Health Agencies (alPHa) [12]): four out of seven were from North region; five out of six were from Central East; four out of seven from Central West; six out of seven from South West Ontario; five out of six from East; and Toronto.

The survey questionnaire was developed by the research team and pilot-tested with an advisory group that was part of the Ontario Public Health Association (who have advocated for integrating health equity into public health programs and policies). This pilot-testing supported further refinement of the survey questions. The survey questionnaire included two parts, with questions regarding: (1) health equity in COVID-19 outcomes (e.g., testing, providing information); (2) health equity in COVID-19 vaccinations. We analyzed the 7-item sub-module on health equity in COVID-19 vaccination efforts during the period December 2020-spring/summer 2023. Combining closed- and open-ended questions, this sub-module (Appendix 1) asked about the prioritization of the following to address health equity: population groups; resources and staff; and specific activities. It also included questions about barriers to promoting health equity, collecting data on the social determinants of health (SDOH), and the use of tools to support decision-making on equitable access and distribution of COVID-19 vaccines. Findings from the first survey module, i.e., concerning health equity in COVID-19 outcomes, are published elsewhere [13].

Data analysis

We conducted descriptive statistics for the close-ended questions for all participating PHUs. Open-ended questions were examined through an inductive content analysis. First, one researcher (APB) performed open coding of survey responses. Reflecting on the research question of the study, two researchers (APB, NS) worked together to create categories by grouping codes. Abstraction followed with all researchers (APB, NS, SH, CIJN, BTS, RP) meeting regularly to define themes. This collective theming process enhanced conformability and credibility of findings [14] and supported addressing researcher bias [15]. The team discussions revealed our own positionality in the interpretation of findings, which was shaped by our lived experiences, expertise, and professional roles. Our team is composed of academics and public servants who bring a wealth of diverse professional knowledge and expertise (e.g., sociology, social epidemiology, geography) and lived experiences as patients of PHUs. Therefore, our analysis reflects a comprehensive interpretation of the interrelationships between structural determinants of health and decision-making processes in public health. We used Stata 17 [16] and Microsoft Word to assist in data analysis.

Results

Descriptives

Findings showed that more than 80% of participating PHUs indicated high/very high levels of prioritization for the provision of information, community engagement, and facilitated physical accessibility to COVID-19 vaccines. Only 39.1% reported high/very high priority of collecting data or monitoring equity related to SDOH. The level of prioritization of staffing and resources to address health inequities were high/very high (> 75.0%) across PHUs from the early vaccination effort (Dec 2020-March 2021) to booster campaigns (Dec 2021-spring/summer 2023) (Table 1). 60% of PHUs reported high/very high levels of priority for health equity in ‘COVID-19 vaccination for children’.

Qualitative findings

Four main themes and respective subthemes emerged from data analysis, including: (1) defining priority populations with data-driven and health equity-oriented processes; (2) promoting vaccination through health equity-informed strategies; (3) prioritizing Indigenous health and sovereignty in vaccination efforts; and, (4) recommendations to address health equity-related barriers to COVID-19 vaccination.

Defining priority populations with data-driven and health equity-oriented processes

This theme refers to the processes PHUs used to define priority populations and promote health equity in vaccination strategies. It included two subthemes: data-driven decision-making processes; and strategies to embed health equity considerations into decision-making.

Data-driven decision-making process

PHUs explained that prioritization of populations was initially based on guidance from Ontario’s Ministry of Health indicating eligibility criteria through the mass immunization response [3, 17]. In response to the growing public debate on health inequities, PHUs also reported adopting a more data-driven process in population prioritization, by relying on local statistics from data monitoring and measurement. Eight PHUs used GIS-based mapping of the material and social deprivation neighbourhood-level data (e.g., census areas, postal codes) and vaccination uptake and COVID-19 cases. Two PHUs reported using the Ontario Marginalization Index (ON-Marg). However, one PHU emphasized the need for more granular geographic data, particularly in rural areas.

Fifteen PHUs reported using individual-level data from COVax (which is an electronic documentation database for recording all COVID-19 vaccinations in Ontario) and their health equity indicators. However, four PHUs indicated the COVax database had limited fields to capture sociodemographics, which were only added after the mass immunization rollout started. According to the PHUs, there were a few critical barriers that contributed to low completion rates and limited usability of sociodemographic data collected through the COVax database (Table 3).

Seven PHUs explained they chose not to collect sociodemographic data due to concerns of data privacy or lower priority. Four PHUs decided to use other locally-developed databases for population prioritization with voluntary participation and commissioned surveys (e.g., parent surveys on vaccine hesitancy). PHUs also reported using existing data repositories (e.g., health administrative data available through ICES (Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences)), including those generated by provincial governing bodies to monitor vaccination rates and to inform mobile clinic location in lower-coverage areas and partnership development to reach priority populations. One PHU used the Occupational Exposure to COVID-19 Risk Tool [18] and three PHUs created dashboards that used data from Case and Contact Management (CCM) and the integrated Public Health Information System (iPHIS). PHUs also referred to national and provincial government resources (e.g., coverage for social assistance beneficiaries) to infer if current trends would apply to their local contexts. With ongoing assessment, the identified gaps and case clusters were also used to support (re)definition of priority populations.

Strategies to embed health equity considerations into decision-making

To make health equity-informed decisions, PHUs reported using: health equity frameworks; health equity indicators; the Health Equity Impact Assessment tool; and the National Collaborating Centre for Determinants of Health (NCCDH)’s resources [19].

Seven PHUs reported seeking advice from ethicists or committees and working groups with representatives of equity-deserving groups (i.e., groups of people who face systemic barriers to access, resources, and opportunities due to discrimination and disadvantages). Community engagement and forums were also cited, with one PHU describing a Community Engagement Team that included staff with similar diverse backgrounds as the communities they were serving. One PHU reported using a vaccine clinic checklist they developed that included equity considerations for clinics to tailor their strategies to meet local needs, such as a venue’s accessibility. However, a reported barrier to including health equity lenses in their decision-making on vaccination strategies was limited staff, which was seen as a factor in PHU leadership giving low priority to considering local and cultural needs.

Promoting vaccination through health equity-informed strategies

This theme includes strategies to equitably promote vaccination across equity-deserving populations with four connected subthemes: (a) cross-sectoral collaboration; (b) targeted communication strategies; (c) tailored vaccination delivery strategies; and (d) advocacy.

Cross-sectoral collaboration

As highlighted by PHUs, key to vaccination rollout and uptake was collaboration with other government departments (e.g., Community Services) and community organizations (e.g., paramedics, shelters). Ongoing efforts were made to create or strengthen such partnerships. Engagement of working groups and committees involving equity-deserving groups, and feedback from public health experts and community service providers (e.g., food programs, shelters) guided the design of COVID-19 vaccination strategies.

A common challenge referred to by five PHUs was building new relationships with community organizations and members during a pandemic. To develop trusting relationships with clients, one PHU, for instance, said their staff made routine visits in specific service provider locations (e.g., soup kitchens, shelters). Funding scarcity also acted as a barrier for substantial collaboration.

Targeted communication strategies

A variety of communication strategies were adopted to reach out to different population groups. Examples include: advertisements for radio stations and town newspapers on COVID-19 vaccine rollout to update people who did not have internet access; targeted landline support in areas with a larger senior population; printed resources to northern/rural communities; and webinars for parents to increase confidence in pediatric COVID-19 vaccination). Social media campaigns (e.g., live Facebook events with priority populations) with use of imagery and WhatsApp and email messaging were used. Another example was adoption of a multi-sectoral health equity-informed, culturally-sensitive approach to address misinformation and vaccine hesitancy. Language and interpretation services, translation of resources, and addition of a google-translate button were also part of the communication strategies to reach populations with low English or French proficiency. However, five PHUs noted that the lack of staff who could speak languages other than English and French deterred spontaneous conversations with clients.

Tailored vaccination delivery strategies

PHUs highlighted there was a deep understanding that priority populations needed tailored support to meet their unique needs. For example, PHUs offered pre-registration options for priority populations in mass immunization clinics. They also provided weekend, evening, and pop-up clinics in lower socioeconomic status neighbourhoods, rural and remote areas (e.g., clinics on farms), and specific settings where priority populations lived or congregated (e.g., long-term care facilities, grocery stores, libraries). Also, maps were designed to indicate locations of community clinics to inform the priority populations about the accessibility and vaccine coverage.

PHUs used mobile clinics to deliver and promote vaccination aligned with health equity goals. These included paramedic-led clinics for homeless populations, hospital-led clinics for immuno-compromised people, primary care-led clinics for children and pregnant women, in-home vaccinations for homebound populations, bus clinics in areas with low vaccine uptake, dedicated smaller clinics within vaccination clinics or drive-thru clinics to serve people with disabilities and young children, and school clinics for students and their families. Additionally, specialized clinics were created to meet the needs of two specific groups: for people experiencing anxiety, this involved a one-to-one clinic approach; and for people with autism, developmental disabilities, or other issues related to light or noise sensitivity, a sensory-friendly clinic with less noise and dimmed lighting was designed. To help organize and promote such specialized clinics, PHUs collaborated, for instance, with faith-based groups (e.g., Muslim community organizations), Black community organizations, shelters, food programs, hospitals, and recreation centres.

Such collaboration also aimed to address misinformation and mistrust in vaccines and to promote vaccination. An example is a Vaccine Ambassador Program. Targeting racialized communities, it was meant to build vaccine confidence, serve as a trusted local leader, and amplify public health messaging across their communities. The trained Ambassadors were from the communities, reflecting their diverse backgrounds.

To reach those with limited transportation options, PHUs partnered with local businesses and organizations to offer cost-free bus or taxi transportation to and from clinics, for migrant workers, families with low income, etc. PHUs identified challenges that may have negatively affected the effectiveness of such tailored strategies, such as difficulties to reach those clients who are not linked to the healthcare system as well as to find appropriately sized and suitable venues to deliver mass immunization in each community. Another potential challenge pointed out by the PHUs was the digital divide. With no or limited digital skills or access to online resources, digital technologies, and the internet, equity-deserving populations might have experienced barriers to accessing provincial vaccination booking systems and online information.

Advocacy

PHUs described their advocacy efforts to improve COVID-19 vaccine access, which included: conversations with the Ministry of Health staff to discuss their local needs; discussions at ethical prioritization tables; setting up primary care advisory committees to advocate for improved access to vaccine through primary care; requests to engage more primary care providers to support vaccine administration to their patients; and calls for further evidence dissemination with community organizations, including in congregate settings and workplaces.

Prioritizing Indigenous health and sovereignty in vaccination efforts

This theme regarding Indigenous health and sovereignty is a reflection of PHUs’ declared acknowledgement of the autonomy of Indigenous peoples (i.e., First Nations, Métis, and Inuit) in designing and delivering COVID-19 vaccination strategies. They all referred to strategies being built from the strengths of the Indigenous communities. From their own perspectives, PHUs worked towards embracing culturally-sensitive practices for vaccination. In descriptions of their work with Indigenous peoples, Indigenous-led or Indigenous-serving organizations, PHUs reported establishing immunization clinics on Indigenous reserves (i.e., lands set aside by the federal government to be used by Indigenous peoples), mass immunization clinics with Indigenous leaders onsite, or dedicated clinics with culturally-sensitive care (e.g., Indigenous leaders in the clinics). Four PHUs worked alongside health practitioners from Indigenous communities in their vaccination efforts or supported Indigenous health centres to build capacity for vaccine administration.

One PHU cited using Statistics Canada First Nations Profiles to identify the population size of the First Nations communities; that information was combined with COVax data on number of people who received the vaccine in order to calculate vaccine coverage. Some PHUs described monitoring vaccination uptake in Indigenous communities living on- and off-reserves (e.g., Indigenous people living in urban areas) through the Ministry of Health’s submission process. One PHU referred to chairing an urban Indigenous vaccination strategy committee for collaborative decision-making. Other strategies implemented by eight PHUs included offering Indigenous Cultural Safety training to staff, engaging an Indigenous facilitator, tailoring communications and campaigns through an Indigenous health lens, and disseminating information in Indigenous Health centres. Through routine communication channels with the Ministry of Health, PHUs brought forward concerns raised by Indigenous health leaders regarding the strict requirements to receive the COVID-19 vaccination: i.e., the restricted eligibility criteria for urban Indigenous populations living off-reserves and the need for proof of identification. The Indigenous health leaders asked for the removal of such barriers to increase vaccination uptake.

Recommendations to address health equity-related barriers to COVID-19 vaccination

When reflecting on specific barriers PHUs faced in their efforts to promote equitable access to and uptake of COVID-19 vaccination in their jurisdictions, they discussed what could be done to better address their local needs in future health emergencies. Table 4 matches those recommendations with the aforementioned themes and subthemes. An example of recommendations included systematically collecting SDOH data via administrative health databases. PHUs suggested that provincial governments should ensure an efficient data system is in place for systematic collection, monitoring, and analysis of data on the social determinants of health to inform universal and tailored vaccination strategies for COVID-19. Another recommendation was to engage community organizations and residents with lived experiences in the design and delivery of COVID-19 vaccination strategies in order to meet local needs. Adoption of a strength-based approach (i.e., valuing and mobilizing individual assets, experience and knowledge) was indicated by PHUs as a way to improve collaborations with partners and successes of health equity-informed vaccination strategies. PHUs also suggested more efforts towards building strong cross-sectoral collaborations routinely - and not only when a health emergency arises - to ensure enough time to develop meaningful relationships and to respond quickly to health inequities. Working alongside Indigenous communities and adopting a culturally-sensitive approach in order to increase COVID-19 vaccination rates (and uptake of other vaccines) among Indigenous populations were further recommendations. Three PHUs pointed out that these recommendations could be applicable to improve uptake of other vaccines as well.

Discussion

Our study presented how PHUs in Ontario promoted health equity through COVID-19 mass vaccination between 2020 and 2023. PHUs indicated high priority for promoting equitable information access, community partnerships, and increased physical accessibility to vaccines. The priority for data collection and monitoring on SDOH was relatively lower, likely due to the more urgent needs PHUs had to address alongside barriers related to gathering patient information.

PHUs used data-driven and other equity-oriented processes to make decisions that prioritized health equity in vaccinations. However, as reported elsewhere [20], the routine collection of SDOH at the individual level was lacking even before the pandemic. This hindered PHUs’ efforts to prioritize health equity in vaccination efforts since high-quality disaggregated data was missing. Other factors that may have amplified the barriers to prioritizing SDOH-related data collection were the lack of data infrastructure for COVID-19 vaccination alongside inability to change data entry templates.

Insufficient staff training and lengthy processes to obtain patients’ consent on the sociodemographic questionnaire may partially explain the hesitancy and refusals of some PHU staff in asking patients sociodemographic questions over privacy concerns. PHUs also reported some patients were not well-informed about the public health and policy-making relevance of such questions. Growing mistrust in authorities in other Canadian studies have similarly been shown to fuel the uneasiness of patients and health staff alike with such SDOH-related questions [21]. PHU recommendations highlight the need for administrative health databases to systematically collect and analyze SDOH data and undertake ongoing capacity building on monitoring and responding to health inequities. Raising public awareness on the importance of collecting sociodemographic data to inform action may address patients’ concerns. Also important is to investigate the existence of data inequities between on-reserve and urban Indigenous populations.

Community engagement through working groups, committees, and forums on different fronts (e.g., designing tailored vaccination delivery strategies, including health equity in decision-making, and developing cross-sectoral collaboration) was seen as a key component in improving vaccination uptake equitably. However, as reported elsewhere [4], PHUs did not have the necessary time to create meaningful partnerships built on mutual trust with new organizations. As our results show, most of their community engagement was formed from pre-existing networks with organizations, which may explain why some priority populations (e.g., racialized communities) were targeted less often in community engagement. Equitable vaccination strategies could have been more effective if PHUs had prior relationships with a broad scope of organizations working with and for specific equity-deserving population groups. Building and nurturing trusting relationships require time and collaborative systems in place to facilitate open communication, identification of synergies and strengths, and bidirectional and mutual benefit to better respond to the unique needs of each community [22–24]. Adoption of a strengths-based approach (i.e., building on the communities’ capacities and capabilities) may enrich the community engagement experiences and collaborations with organizations and Indigenous populations [25].

According to PHUs, working with community members who lived in the same communities and/or shared the same lived experiences as the residents helped accelerate community trust building with the public health sector, knowledge sharing, vaccine confidence, and address health inequities in vaccination locally. While there is convincing evidence suggesting the effectiveness of community member involvement in strategies related to non-communicable diseases and healthy conditions [26–29], our findings corroborate the emerging literature on the successes of partnering with community brokers (also called lay health promoters, health champions) for communicable disease prevention [30].

When working with and for Indigenous organizations and communities, PHUs adopted strategies that seemed to be aligned with Indigenous principles and values concerning information governance and sovereignty as well as culturally sensitivite approaches. They described respect for data handling, which seemed to reflect First Nations’ core principles on ownership, control, access, and possession of Indigenous data [31]. Despite these efforts, previous research showed lower uptake of two-dose vaccine throughout the pandemic among Indigenous peoples in Ontario, and particularly among those living off reserves [32]. Also, studies reporting on Indigenous Peoples’ own perspectives described barriers to vaccination that included lack of culturally-sensitive strategies to address Indigenous racism and distrust in the healthcare system [33, 34]. A way forward is strengthening the relationship between public health and Indigenous communities at the PHU level to support address the root causes of vaccine hesitancy–a product of the historical legacy of medical abuse and experimentation in Canada [35].

Our study has limitations. Of the 34 PHUs recruited for this study, 25 participated. Despite the high response rate and representation of a broad, diverse range of PHUs, some experiences may be missing. PHUs’ limited capacity and time and potential staff turnover may have impeded a detailed report of their activities and experience with vaccination efforts. Questions on the priority populations for COVID-19 vaccination did not provide specific definitions, such as for people with lower socioeconomic status and people experiencing precarious housing. Categories were left broad (rather than providing a strict definition of categories of measures) in order to encompass a range of groups prioritized across local public health delivery in Ontario. PHUs might have characterized those populations differently, resulting in the potential for high variability of groups prioritized.

With the provincial requirement for PHUs to prioritize health equity, desirability bias in reporting actions to reduce health inequities cannot be ruled out. Certain reports may be less accurate given PHUs were asked to describe activities for a three-year period. Nevertheless, assessing this vast timeframe allowed for capturing how health equity was integrated into a range of vaccination strategies, from targeted campaigns for high-risk populations to more universal ones.

Our study provided some insights on the successes and challenges PHUs faced when designing and implementing health equity-informed strategies to improve COVID-19 vaccination uptake in Ontario. Similar mixed-methods studies with a broader geographic scope (like the one proposed by Aggarwal and colleagues [36]) may reveal further recommendations made by other Canadian jurisdictions on how to promote equitable vaccination distribution and access in future health emergencies.

Conclusion

Overall, PHUs in Ontario adopted a diverse set of health equity-informed actions, including through community engagements, and advocacy efforts. The challenges and recommendations PHUs made to promote COVID-19 vaccine equity can inform universal and tailored strategies before future health crises. Findings suggest a need for improved data infrastructure, adoption of a strength-based approach to build on local assets and knowledge (particularly for Indigenous communities), and supports for authentic community engagement (e.g., creation of inclusive spaces for healthy dialogue) to further optimize operations and policy changes.
