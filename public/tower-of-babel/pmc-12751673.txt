Bioengineered ovary reconstructs follicle-like structures in a mouse model of chemotherapy-induced premature ovarian failure
Authors: Fatemeh Abedi Ghehi, Farideh Eivazkhani, Leila Mirzaeian, Naeimeh Sadat Abtahi, Hussein Eimani, Somayeh Tavana, Rouhollah Fathi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe investigated the applicability of human decellularized ovarian scaffold (HDOS) to create an artificial ovary for preserving germ cells and forming follicular-like structures in a mouse chemotherapy-induced premature ovarian failure (chemo-POF) model after seeding stromal cells and transplantation.MethodsThe present study was conducted in three steps. (1) Creation of a mouse POF model, (2) Production of an artificial ovary (AO), (3) Transplantation of AO to ovariectomized mice. For the first step, female NMRI mice received chemotherapy drugs to induce POF. For AO production, mouse ovarian cell suspension (OCS) from the POF model and the control group were isolated separately and seeded into human decellularized ovarian scaffolds (HDOSs). AO was transplanted to ovariectomized intact and POF mice, which were followed up for 2 months (AOII). After transplantation, histological assessments, hormonal evaluation (FSH/E2), and gene expression analysis were conducted (Blimp1).ResultsA follicle-like structure formed two months after the transplantation. No significant increase was observed in the FSH level in both the Intact/AOII and POF/AOII groups. The gene expression of Blimp1 showed a significant increase in the Intact/AOII group compared to the other groups.ConclusionsThe bioengineered ovary is capable of forming a follicle-like structure in the POF model after cell seeding and transplantation. We also demonstrated the efficacy of these extracellular matrices (ECMs) in preserving germ cells.

Background

The ovary, an endocrine organ of the female reproductive system, produces essential steroids and peptide hormones that are required for puberty and the menstrual cycle. Ovarian hormones also influence the health and integrity of bones, breasts, the vascular system, and endocrine tissue [1–4]. Premature ovarian failure (POF) is a common endocrine disorder in which the ovaries lose their normal functions before the age of 40 years. The POF is known to afflict one in a thousand women in the age group of 15–29 years and one in every hundred women aged 30–39 years [5–7]. Occurrence POFs in younger women [7] can be attributed to various conditions, such as autoimmune disorders, infections, toxin exposure, administration of chemotherapy drugs, and genetic predisposition. It seems that chemotherapy drugs, such as cyclophosphamide, used in cancer therapy, are one of the major causes of POF [8–11]. Chemotherapy-induced ovarian failure occurs due to disruption of cellular division, suppression of DNA, and abnormal upregulation of specific genes [12, 13]. These processes trigger apoptosis in pre-granulosa cells, leading to the destruction of ovarian function and ultimately causing irreversible premature defects in dormant primordial follicles [12, 14, 15]. Additionally, chemotherapy may result in the breakdown of DNA, leading to a decrease in follicular reserve and ovarian dysfunctions [16], which are related to the mTOR signaling pathway. This signaling pathway is involved in follicular activation. When Pten, a negative regulator of PI3K, is specifically deleted from oocytes, the functional role of PI3K signaling is increased, leading to excessive follicular activation and depletion of the primordial follicle pool at once [17]. Clinical interventions in female fertility preservation, especially in prepubertal cancer patients, are considered a challenge. Nowadays, one of the most popular options is cryopreservation and transplantation of ovarian tissue. There have been more than 200 live births reported around the world using this technique, which is increasingly being utilized [18–21].

The transplantation of ovarian tissue has been shown to restore hormonal cycle activity [22]. However, the risk of malignancy recurrence in transplanted tissue is a concern [21–23]. Furthermore, this method may not be appropriate for women who have lost their ovarian reserves or who have not previously frozen their ovarian tissue [24–26]. Alternate safe and effective methods of fertility preservation must be developed.

Tissue engineering has the potential to create an artificial ovary capable of producing hormones and supporting follicle growth, while minimizing the risk of cancer recurrence. Cells can be seeded onto the decellularized scaffolds to maintain the three-dimensional (3D) structure of the organ [27, 28]. Decellularization is a process in which all cells are removed from an organ or tissue, leaving the extracellular matrix (ECM) that is mainly composed of Collagen, Fibronectin, Elastin, Laminin, and other extracellular matrix proteins [29–32]. It has been successfully applied in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine to create 3D bio-scaffolds [33, 34]. ECMs are composed of glycoproteins, glycosaminoglycans, proteoglycans, adhesion molecules, growth factors, chemokines, cytokines, and other compounds. They regulate the structural, mechanical, and functional changes of cells during cell development and repair [32, 35]. Interactions between cells and extracellular matrix components are essential factors for several developmental processes, such as cell migration, morphogenesis, and cell fate determination. A decellularized ovarian tissue can be used as an ECM-based scaffold, potentially supporting the survival and growth of seeded cells and restoring endocrine activity [3, 36].

It seems that bioengineering extracellular matrix-based scaffolds derived from natural tissues could potentially activate existing germ cells and result in the resumption of follicle formation in cases of chemotherapy-induced POF (chemo-POF) [37]. In this study, for the first time, we assess the applicability of decellularized human ovarian tissue as a natural 3D scaffold for seeding ovarian cell suspension from a mouse chemo-POF model and investigate the preservation of germ cells and the restoration of ovarian functional structures following transplantation.

Materials and methods

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Both the animal and human aspects of the current study were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Royan Institute, Tehran, Iran (No. IR.ACECR.ROYAN.REC.1395.192).

In the current study, female Naval Medical Research Institute (NMRI) mice, 6–8 weeks old, were housed in individually-ventilated cages under standard laboratory conditions, including a 12-hour light:12-hour dark cycle (LD), at a temperature of 20 to 25 °C, and humidity levels of 40–60%.

The present study was conducted in three steps: creating a POF mouse model, creating and transplantation of artificial ovary (Fig. 1).

Creating the mouse POF model

Animals and experimental groups

For the creation of a POF mouse model, 24 female NMRI mice weighing 23–28 g with a regular estrous cycle were randomly divided into the control and experimental groups (n = 4 per group). We used different dosages of cyclophosphamide (C) and busulfan (B) drugs to induce POF mouse model. Experimental groups were divided based on the type and duration of chemotherapeutic agents administered. Cyclophosphamide (Endoxan™, Germany) and busulfan (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) were administered intraperitoneally (IP), either together or separately, over a 14-day period. The experimental groups were as follows:

75 C group: A single dose of 75 mg/kg cyclophosphamide was administered for 2 weeks [38]. 100 C group: A single dose of 100 mg/kg cyclophosphamide was administered for 2 weeks [39–41]. 100 C/20 C group: A single dose of 100 mg/kg of cyclophosphamide was administered on the first day, followed by a daily dose of 20 mg/kg for 2 weeks. 100 C/50B group: A dose of 100 mg/kg cyclophosphamide was administered on the first day, followed by a daily dose of 50 mg/kg busulfan for 2 weeks [41]. 75 C/30B group: A combination of 75 mg/kg cyclophosphamide and 30 mg/kg busulfan was administered daily for 2 weeks [42].

The control group did not receive any drug treatment. The estrous cycle, body weight, histology, and follicular count assessments were evaluated in all experimental and control groups. According to the evaluation results, the group receiving 100 mg/kg of cyclophosphamide was chosen as the established POF model. Then the hormonal assessments and gene expression of germ cell markers were only considered in the established POF model (100 C group) and the control group. To evaluate FSH and estradiol hormone levels, blood samples were collected from the mice’s hearts (Euthanasia procedure for mice was performed using blood withdrawal by cardiac puncture after ensuring that the animal was under deep anesthesia, as evidenced by lack of response to a painful stimulus (e.g., toe or tail pinch)). The serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at -80 °C until measurement. To assess germ cell markers including Oct4, Blimp1, Gdf-9, and Dazl, ovaries from the POF model and control group were removed from the mice’s bodies and stored in RNA-later at -80 °C until evaluation.

Vaginal cytology

To evaluate the cyclic ovarian activity, the vaginal cytology of the mice was examined daily (8–10 am), as previously described [43, 44]. The mice that had at least two regular estrus cycles were used in this study.

Ovarian follicle counting and morphological analysis

The mice in both the experimental and control groups were anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of a drug combination containing 10% ketamine (100 mg/kg) and 2% xylazine (10 mg/kg) from Alfasan, Netherlands. The ovaries were removed from the body and first fixed in Bouin’s fixative solution, then in natural formalin buffer (NFB) 10% for 24 h. Subsequently, they underwent dehydration using a tissue processing machine and were embedded in liquid paraffin (Sigma Aldrich (Merc), Germany) [33]. Serial sections of paraffin blocks were obtained at a thickness of 5 μm, and the slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany). The ovarian histopathology examination involved counting live follicles based on their morphology using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse 50i, Japan). The follicles were classified as follows: [1] primordial follicles, where an oocyte is surrounded by a layer of flattened granulosa cells; [2] primary follicles, where an oocyte is surrounded by a layer of cuboidal granulosa cells; [3] pre-antral follicles, where an oocyte is surrounded by two or more layers of granulosa cells; [4] antral follicles, where the oocyte is surrounded by two or more layers of granulosa cells with the antrum cavity; and [5] corpus luteum, identified as a large complex of compact and hypertrophic cells.

A follicle count is conducted by observing the nuclei, which prevents double counting [45]. A viable follicle is considered viable if it contains an intact oocyte and granulosa cells. When they had a shrunken pyknotic oocyte nucleus or unorganized granulosa cells, they were classified as dead or degenerated follicles [46].

Measurement of serum level of E2 and FSH

The serum levels of Estradiol (E2) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) in all groups were measured using two different commercial ELISA kits: the Mouse E2 ELISA Kit (E0072Mo, Bioassay Technology, China) and the Mouse FSH ELISA Kit (E0256Mo, Bioassay Technology, China), following the manufacturers’ instructions [47].

Measurement of gene expression levels

Total RNA was extracted from ovarian tissue of each group (n = 5) using TRIzol® reagent (Sigma, St. Louis, MO), and cDNA synthesis was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (RevertAid™ Minus First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, TaKaRa). Transcript expressions of genes Oct4, Blimp1, Gdf-9, and Dazl were evaluated by real-time PCR, as described by Tavana et al. [44]. Also, a housekeeping gene, Gapdh, was used as an internal control.

Creation artificial ovary (AO)

Ovarian decellularization and ovarian cell suspension seeding

To create an artificial ovary, two elements were required: a scaffold as new niche for the cells and a cell suspension for seeding. To prepare the scaffold, we used human decellularized ovarian scaffolds (HDOS) from transsexuals and for the cell source, we utilized ovarian cell suspension from intact and selected POF mouse model (100 C group).

To prepare HDOS, ovarian tissues were taken from 18- to 22-year-old transsexuals at Arash Hospital in Tehran, Iran. The decellularization process (Fig. 2a) was performed as described completely in our previous published data [33].

Providing mouse ovarian cell suspension

In this step, twenty female NMRI mice were divided into intact and POF groups. After establishing the POF model (100 C group), all mice in the intact and POF groups were euthanized, and their ovaries were removed from the body to provide intact and POF ovarian cell suspension, respectively. To prepare the mouse ovarian cell suspension for seeding into human ovarian decellularized ECM, ovarian tissues were dissected using an insulin syringe needle (G30) in a complex of DMEM F12 (Gibco, USA) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). Then, ovarian fragments were digested in collagenase type IV (4 mg/mL). The samples were incubated for 45–60 min and pipetted every ten minutes. The ovarian suspension was passed through a 40 μm filter mesh (Falcon, USA), and the cell suspension was centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 5 min [33] which contained all ovarian cell types and ECM components.

Seeding mouse ovarian cell suspension into human decellularized ovarian scaffolds

First, pieces of the decellularized human ovarian ECM were sterilized using 70% ethanol for 2 h. Then, the ovarian bio-scaffolds were washed in the PBS, exposed to ultraviolet light for 20 min, and placed in the culture medium for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Then, 5 × 10^6 ovarian cells were injected into the sterilized ovarian scaffold using a 30 G insulin syringe. The artificial ovary (0.5 × 0.5 × 1 mm³) was incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 condition with a complex of DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% non-essential amino acids (Gibco), 1% L-Glutamine (Gibco), 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS) (Gibco), and 1% (5000 units/ml) Penicillin/Streptomycin (Thermo, USA) for three days (Fig. 2b) [33].

Transplantation of artificial ovary (AO)

Group design for AO transplantation

In this part, 20 female NMRI mice were used as recipients of the artificial ovaries. The mice were divided equally into 4 groups (n = 5): Intact/AO I. Artificial ovaries constructed (HDOS + intact ovarian cell suspension) were transplanted for 1 month. Intact/AO II: An artificial ovary constructed (HDOS + intact ovarian cell suspension) was transplanted for 2 months. POF/AO I: An artificial ovary constructed (HDOS + POF ovarian cell suspension) was transplanted for 1 month. POF/AO II: An artificial ovary, constructed using HDOS and POF ovarian cell suspension, was transplanted for 2 months.

Transplantation of AO to ovariectomized mice

Three days after culture of the recellularized scaffolds, the artificial ovaries were transplanted into the sub-serosal facia (between peritoneum and skin) of ovariectomized (OVX) mice. For this purpose, animals were anesthetized by an IP injection with combination of 10% ketamine, (100 mg/kg) and 2% xylazine (10 mg/kg), (Alfasan, Netherland). Then, they were ovariectomized through an abdominal incision. First, the anterior wall of the abdomen was disinfected with the 70% ethanol, shaved and incised using a thin scissor. Then, both artificial ovaries were placed in sub-serosal fascia individually at the right and left sides (Fig. 2c-d), internal layers were sutured with a polyglycolic acid synthetic absorbable string (0.6 mm; Teb Keyhan) and skins were sutured with a non-absorbable polyglycolic acid string (0.5 mm; Teb Keyhan). The mice subjected to surgery were kept in individual cages under standard condition for two months.

Post-transplant assessments

Histological evaluations of the grafts: H & E and immunohistochemistry (IHC) of GDF-9 and AMH

After the predetermined transplantation period (one and two months), the animals were anesthetized, as previously described, to remove the bioengineered ovaries. The human bioengineered ovaries were removed from the body and fixed in formalin (Fig. 2e-f). In addition, an immunohistochemistry assay was performed to detect GDF-9 and AMH positive cells in transplanted AO paraffin sections using specific antibodies (SC7407, Santa Cruz Biotechnology/CA, concentration: 1/200).

Immunofluorescence assay

For immunofluorescence of Anti-Mullerian Hormone (AMH), the sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated by immersion in xylene (2 × 5 min) followed by washing in distilled water. When carrying out antigen retrieval with 10 mM sodium citrate at pH 6, slides were heated in a pressure cooker and washed in 0.05% PBS-Tween. Next, the slides are blocked with a solution containing 10% normal goat serum from the species in which the secondary antibodies were raised. The slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a diluted primary antibody (ab24542, Acbam antibody, England). Again, the slides were washed in 0.05% PBS-Tween and then incubated for 60 min at 37 °C with a labeled secondary antibody (orb688922, Biorbyt antibody, England) against the corresponding IgG of the primary antibody. The slides were washed again in 0.05% PBS-Tween, and the fluorophore label can be visualized directly using fluorescent microscopy. Finally, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for fluorescence microscopy.

Measurement of serum level of E2 and FSH

Once more, levels of estradiol and FSH in all human bioengineered ovaries groups (one and two months after transplantation) were compared with the control and the best POF mouse model (100 C group) as described in Sect. 2.1.4.

Measurement of gene expression levels

After transplantation, the Blimp1 gene expression were evaluated by Real-Time PCR in all groups of artificial ovaries (see details, refer to Sect. 2.1.5) compared to the with control and best POF mouse model (100 C group).

Statistical analysis

Using IBM SPSS software version 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), the statistical comparisons were analyzed. Significant differences among our groups were examined by the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test that followed by the Tukey’s post hoc test. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Evaluation of induced POF mouse model

Body weight

The body weights of the mice were measured and compared in all experimental groups before and after chemotherapy. We found a significant decrease (p < 0.05) in body weight in all experimental groups after chemotherapy. Groups 75 C, 100 C and 75 C/30B, showed a more significant reduction in body weight compared with the control group (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 3).

Various stages of vital follicles were counted in all groups. Results of follicular count showed a significant decrease (p < 0.05) in ovarian reserve in all experimental groups, including 75 C, 100 C, 100 C/20 C, 100 C/50B, 75 C/30B, in comparison with the control group (Fig. 6a). A significant decrease in the number of viable primordial follicles was observed across all experimental and control groups (p < 0.001); 75 C, 100 C, 100 C/20 C, 100 C/50B, 75 C/50B, and between the 100 C group and the 100 C/50B group (p < 0.043) (Fig. 6b). The survival rate of primary follicles in some groups, including 75 C, 100 C/20 C, and 100 C/50B, was increased compared to the control group (p < 0.05). However, a reduction was observed in the 100 C group compared to the 75 C, 100 C/20 C, and 100 C/50B groups (p < 0.001). On the other hand, there was a significant increase (p < 0.05) in the viability of primary follicles in the 75 C and 100 C/50B groups compared to the 75 C/30B group (Fig. 6c). The 75 C group exhibited a significant increase (p < 0.01) in the viability percentage of pre-antral follicles compared to the control group. In contrast, the 100 C group demonstrated a significant decrease compared to the other experimental groups, 75 C, 100 C/20 C, 75 C/30B; (p˂0.001) and 100 C/50B groups (p < 0.01) (Fig. 6d). Our findings showed that the viability percentage of antral follicles in 100 C/20 C and 100 C/50B groups significantly increased (p < 0.05) compared to the control and the 100 C group.

There was a clear decrease in the viability of antral follicles in the 75 C/30B group compared to the 100 C/20 C and 100 C/50B groups (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6e). Following follicular growth, the corpus luteum count was the lowest in the 100 C group (Fig. 6f).

Evaluation of the artificial ovary after transplantation

Histological evaluations in transplanted artificial ovary: H&E and IHC for GDF-9 and AMH

Histological evaluations of the artificial ovary after one month of transplantation showed that the cells could survive and proliferate within the human ovarian scaffold (Fig. 8a-b). Additionally, the formation of blood vessels was observed (Fig. 8c, d), indicating a robust occurrence of angiogenesis in this reconstructed structure. The presence of these structures may indicate that the decellularized human ovarian scaffold is successful in recruiting, homing, and reconstructing the cells. Two months after transplantation, our histological assessments showed follicular-like structures closer to maturity (Fig. 8e-h). Also, immunohistochemistry data showed GDF-9 activity of the large centric cells as oocyte like cells within the follicle-like structures (Fig. 8i, j) and AMH-positive cells at the periphery of these structures (Fig. 8k).

Discussion

Today, extensive research is being conducted in reconstructive medicine and tissue engineering to develop biologically compatible implants for regenerating organs and tissues, including the ovary [48–50]. Chemotherapy agents induce programmed cell death in ovarian cells, making it challenging to consistently repair damaged ovarian cells [51]. The new approach involves creating an artificial ovary by tissue engineering [37]. In this biostructure, damaged ovarian cells, including stem cells or germ cells, are implanted into a new and healthy niche to provide a supportive environment for the resumption of follicular activities. For this purpose, in this study, the damaged ovarian cells were isolated from the POF model, and the human ovarian scaffold or HDOS was used as a healthy new niche.

The enhanced growth and proliferation of ovarian cells from the POF model when seeded into HDOS can be attributed to several mechanisms supported by the ECM components. Decellularized scaffolds retain key bioactive molecules such as collagen, laminin, and growth factors (e.g., FGF, VEGF), which promote cell adhesion, migration, and homing through integrin-mediated signaling pathways [52, 53]. In POF models, damaged cells often exhibit impaired PI3K/AKT and mTOR pathways due to chemotherapy-induced apoptosis [12]; however, the healthy niche provided by HDOS may restore these pathways by offering a supportive microenvironment that mitigates oxidative stress and enhances stem cell renewal, as observed in xenogeneic scaffolds for POF. Furthermore, scaffolds facilitate the reorganization of somatic cells (granulosa and theca) around germ-like cells, promoting folliculogenesis via paracrine signaling and improved vascularization. This is consistent with studies showing that decellularized ovarian bioscaffolds optimize cell differentiation and proliferation in vitro by mimicking native ovarian architecture [49].

Consistent with available studies, we used chemotherapy drugs, cyclophosphamide and busulfan, to create POF mouse model [11, 51, 54–56]. In the current study, reduced body weights, a significant depletion in the survival rate of primordial follicles, and a decrease in ovarian reserve were observed in all experimental groups. According to the results (including the reduction of ovarian size, irregular estrous cycles, histological and hormonal findings), the experimental group treated with 100 mg/kg of cyclophosphamide was established as a model for POF. Our findings were consistent with the results of some studies that established the POF model by injecting 100 mg/kg of cyclophosphamide in mice [41, 57] .

Numerous studies have shown that a key indicator of developing a POF model is a decline in ovarian reserve. In the current study, follicular count results showed that chemotherapy-induced POF triggered the activation of a large number of dormant primordial follicles, leading them toward maturation and reducing the ovarian reserve at once. This finding is consistent with other studies [17, 41].

While the plasma levels of estradiol and FSH are important early approval markers of a POF model [58], our results showed that in the group receiving 100 mg/kg of cyclophosphamide, FSH increased and estradiol hormone levels decreased in the blood, establishing the successful creation of the POF model. The increase in the FSH hormone is due to the rapid depletion of the follicular reserve in the ovary, which is consistent with previous studies [11, 41, 51, 55].

In order to check the presence of germ cells after creating the POF model, we examined the gene expression of two germ cell markers, Oct4 and Dazl, in the POF group compared to the control group. Our results showed statistically significant increases in the expression of Oct4 and Dazl genes, indicating the presence of germ line markers in the POF group. Some studies have indicated the presence of very small embryonic-like stem cells (VSELs) in mouse ovaries. These cells express pluripotency markers such as Oct-4, Nanog, and Sox-2, and have been found to survive after chemotherapy [59–61]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the surviving germ cells in the POF model may include VSELs, though further characterization is needed to confirm their identity and functional role. Some studies have shown that ovarian aging is comparable to a POF ovary [62], For example, Niikura et al., reported that Stra8 and Dazl are expressed in aged and atrophic ovaries [63] which was in line with our study demonstrating the expression of the Dazl marker in the POF model.

Some investigators observed a rare population of premeiotic germ cells in the superficial layer of the aged ovarian epithelium that are unable to develop into an oocyte. When transplanted into a young host mouse environment, these cells differentiated into oocytes. They suggested that oocyte formation failure in the elderly ovary may be due to impairment in the niche and the damaged microenvironment [63–65]. Thus, by altering the environment, damaged cells can be transformed into functional cells. For this purpose, we separated the damaged and dysfunctional cells of the POF model from their injured environment and seeded them into a healthy niche (HDOS). This new structure, or bioengineered artificial ovaries, was effective in promoting the growth and proliferation of damaged cells.

In the present study, the histological results (H & E and IHC) demonstrated the formation of follicular-like structures, defined as cellular aggregates with GDF-9 and AMH-positive cells resembling oocyte-like and granulosa-like cells, in the human ovarian scaffold post-transplantation. These structures suggest early folliculogenesis but do not fully recapitulate native follicle function [3, 26, 28]. These findings were consistent with some studies that observed follicle-like structures after transplanting decellularized human ovarian fragments [28, 33]. Our findings also revealed cell gatherings that are consistent with the study by Eivazkhani et al., study [33]. We observed qualitative evidence of blood vessel formation in the bioengineered ovary, consistent with previous studies [26, 28], though the extent and functionality of vascularization require further quantitative analysis. In general, these studies confirmed that biological scaffolds made of human ovarian tissue can support cell growth and proliferation during transplantation [26, 28, 33]. In addition, we believe that these structures are “follicular-like structures,” not real follicles. So, it is not logical to expect to visit normal follicles with their actual oocyte and granulosa cells. On the other hand, these structures involve the primary aggregation of cells to form an active follicular unit in the future. The term “follicular-like structures” refers to cellular aggregates that mimic the early morphology of ovarian follicles. These include oocyte-like cells positive for GDF-9 and Blimp1, surrounded by granulosa-like cells expressing AMH. However, they do not fully recapitulate the complete structure or function of native follicles, such as antral formation or hormonal cyclicity. Their presence reflects partial folliculogenesis and early reconstruction potential within the bioengineered scaffold [3, 26, 28].

The blood plasma level of E2 did not significantly increase in the groups that received artificial ovary transplants. The discrepancy between unchanged E2 levels and decreased FSH after two months may arise from several factors. In POF animal models, elevated FSH results from reduced follicular feedback, and its decrease post-transplantation indicates partial restoration of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis through inhibin or other factors from granulosa-like cells [66]. However, the lack of E2 increase could reflect limited maturation of follicular-like structures to estrogen-producing antral stages, as E2 synthesis requires coordinated granulosa-theca interactions responsive to FSH [67]. Additionally, the absence of estrous phase-specific sampling may mask cyclic E2 fluctuations, and the bilateral ovariectomy model might impair systemic feedback compared to unilateral models, where residual ovarian tissue could amplify E2 production. So, we suggested that if artificial ovaries were transplanted into unilaterally ovariectomized mice, the remaining ovary could help in the increased production of Estradiol. On the other hand, the decrease in FSH levels after transplantation represents one of the successful signs of ovarian tissue transplantation, our results showed that one month after transplantation, FSH levels still could not decrease compared to the non-transplanted groups. However, two months after transplantation, we observed a non-significant decrease in FSH levels in the transplanted groups. This trend suggests partial restoration of ovarian feedback but highlights the need for longer-term studies to assess hormonal recovery.

Gene expression results after the transplantation of an artificial ovary in the present study showed the expression of Blimp1, demonstrating that the bioengineered ovary could effectively maintain the germ cells.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of a mouse model may not fully replicate human ovarian biology, limiting direct clinical applicability. Second, the follicular-like structures observed are preliminary and do not exhibit full follicle functionality, such as antral formation or ovulation, which restricts conclusions about reproductive potential. Third, the follow-up period of two months is insufficient to evaluate long-term outcomes, such as sustained hormonal production or fertility restoration. Future studies should extend the observation to at least 4–6 months to assess complete folliculogenesis and reproductive potential. Since the onset of fibrosis and shrinkage in the transplanted tissue has been observed after approximately one month [28], the use of supportive strategies such as growth factors, hydrogels, or encapsulation in combination with the artificial ovary is recommended [68]. Fourth, variability in scaffold decellularization or cell seeding efficiency may affect reproducibility, which was not quantified in this study. Finally, the lack of estrous phase-specific hormone analysis and the use of bilateral ovariectomized mice may have confounded hormonal outcomes, as unilateral ovariectomy could potentially enhance systemic hormonal feedback [65].

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the human decellularized ovarian scaffold, serving as a healthy niche, shows promise in supporting the growth and proliferation of damaged ovarian cells and facilitating early ovarian tissue reconstruction. Additionally, bioengineered ovary structures were able to support the maintenance of germ cells and their differentiation. Our findings suggest that artificial ovarian structures may be more effective in long-term transplantation scenarios, though this hypothesis requires validation through extended follow-up studies and functional reproductive assessments. Future research should focus on optimizing scaffold design, evaluating long-term hormonal and fertility outcomes, and exploring clinical applications for fertility preservation in chemotherapy-treated patients.
