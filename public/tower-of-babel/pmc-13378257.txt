Prevalence of late presentation of HIV among older adults in Asia: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Xiaoyu Du, Jiajia Liu, Xiaochen Yan, Ruixue Hui, Wendi Guo, Xincen Liu, Yucheng Gao, Wenjiao Li, Jia Chen, Nina Wang, Wenjun Chen

Background

The Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) recently issued “end the AIDS by 2030” and achieve “95-95-95” (95% detection, 95% treatment, 95% viral suppression) testing and treatment target declaration [1]. However, progress toward achieving the first “95” target remains far behind schedule. By the end of 2023, the UNAIDS report had reached only 86–89-93 globally [2]. It is estimated that at least 1.8 million people living with HIV (PLWH) have advanced HIV disease (AHD) and 630,000 people died from HIV-related diseases worldwide [2, 3]. Delayed diagnosis contributes significantly to these AHD and HIV-related deaths [4, 5]. This puts their health at risk, increases the risk of HIV transmission, and adds to the burden on health systems [6]. Timely diagnosis of HIV is the first step and one of the key strategies to access early treatment and reduce the risk of transmission [7, 8].

According to the definition established by the European Late Presenter Consensus working group, late presentation (LP) refers to PLWH with a CD4 cell count below 350 cells/mL, or presenting with an AIDS-defining event regardless of their CD4 cell count at the first diagnosis [9]. Additionally, the recent EuroTEST Working Group on Late Diagnosis has highlighted that some infected individuals may experience a rapid decline in CD4 cell counts to <350 cells/µL during the acute infection phase, presenting a false positive (FP) issue [10]. Therefore, they present a revised consensus definition of late HIV diagnosis, in which people known to be diagnosed during seroconversion are reclassified as “not late.” However, the vast majority of published original studies currently employ the 2010 definition, meaning their data likely overestimate the true prevalence of LP. In addition, the promotion of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) will inevitably pose challenges of false-negative (FN) HIV diagnoses [11]. Among patients receiving injectable PrEP, diagnoses may be missed or delayed, leading to an underestimation of the actual prevalence of LP [12].

LP poses a significant barrier to global HIV prevention and control, with considerable prevalence reported across different regions. In Europe, the prevalence of newly diagnosed PLWH ranges from 47% to 58.5% [13]. Among these, the specific data for certain developed countries are as follows: EU/European Economic Area (55.5%), United Kingdom (40%), France (57.2%), Spain (50.0%), Ireland (58.5%) and so on [14–16]. As for other countries, the prevalence in the United States has been reported to range from 23.33% to 41.88% [17, 18]. Similarly, the prevalence in Australia ranges from 25 to 39% [19, 20]. However, compared to these regions, LP is particularly high and severe in Asia. A recently published meta-analysis targeting Asia confirms that the overall prevalence of LP reaches as high as 56.0%, with most Asian countries reporting rates exceeding 50% [21]. Particularly concerning is the fact that, while LP prevalence is declining in some regions (such as Oman), the trend of LP is actually increasing in populous countries like China and the Philippines.

With the success of antiretroviral therapy (ART) and global aging, the worldwide HIV epidemic is also undergoing an aging process [22]. Both the proportion and absolute number of older people living with HIV (OPLWH, defined as those aged 50 and above) are rapidly increasing [23–25]. Asia, as one of the rapidly aging and most densely populated regions globally, faces significant challenges in addressing OPLWH. As research reports indicate, while the global age-standardized incidence of OPLWH decreased, the rate increased significantly in central Asia and the high-income Asia Pacific [26]. In recent years, the burden of LP in the elderly has been increasing year by year. Most cross-sectional studies agreed that age significantly influences LP. Jeong et al. [27] (OR = 2.03) and Su et al. [28] (OR = 2.23) indicate that individuals aged 50 and older in Asia are more likely to LP. LP has dual devastating consequences for OPLWH. For individuals, it often means more comorbidities, poorer immune treatment outcomes, and even higher mortality. For society, it increases the risk of viral transmission, places greater strain on healthcare systems, and imposes higher economic costs [29, 30].

Asia faces the double burden of HIV aging and population aging, which explains the unique and urgent challenge of addressing LP for OPLWH. Firstly, stigma and discrimination related to HIV and sexual behavior in Asia (particularly South and Southeast Asia) pose significant barriers to HIV testing and treatment among older adults [31]. Secondly, the imbalance in healthcare resources across Asian countries also poses another significant barrier. In remote areas such as Pakistan and Vietnam, where medical resources are scarce, there is a lack of routine HIV screening guidelines and testing capacity for older adults [32]. This contrasts sharply with the standardized screening policies in America and Europe.

Currently, meta-analyses in the LP of HIV focus mostly on developed Western countries and high-risk populations, such as in the European Region [33], Hispanics in the United States [34] and drug-used HIV patients [35]. Due to the unique characteristics of Asian elderly in terms of demographic structure, sociocultural factors, and healthcare systems, research from Western countries cannot directly support public health in Asia. Although two meta-analyses on LP in Asia have been conducted in recent years, they included PLWH across all age groups in Asia. Ghazali et al. analyze the prevalence and the trend of late HIV diagnosis in the Asian region [21]. Wong et al. analyze HIV late presentation and its associated factors in China from 2010 to 2020 [7]. There is a lack of advanced evidence to synthesize the prevalence of LP among OPLWH in Asia. This study aims to summarize the prevalence of LP and its associated factors in the OPLWH in Asia. This study will provide critical data support for developing HIV screening policies and public health interventions targeting older adults in Asia. It will help promote early HIV diagnosis among this population, improve health outcomes, and advance the achievement of the “95-95-95” targets.

Method

We will report the protocol following the 2015 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) [36]. The systematic review will follow PRISMA 2020 checklists and Meta-analyses Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) [37, 38]. The PRISMA-P and MOOSE Checklist is available in additional file 1 and file 2. We have registered this protocol with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024618152).

Information sources and search strategy

We will search six international electronic databases including PubMed (Medline), Web of Science Core Collection (via Clarivate), Cochrane Library database (CENTRAL), Scopus (via Elsevier), CINAHL (via EBSCOhost) and Embase (via Ovid), and three Chinese language databases, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (via CNKI), Wan fang and Chongqing VIP databases. The search strategy will have no restrictions on language or publication date. The search strategy for each database will be constructed using a combination of controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH, Emtree, CINAHL Headings) and free-text keywords, incorporating 3 concepts: HIV/AIDS, LP and older adults. These terms will be systematically combined using Boolean operators (OR or AND). The search strategy will be finalized based on a discussion among team members (XD, WC, and JC) with the consultation of an academic librarian at Central South University. We displayed a full search strategy for each database in additional file 3. We will also screen the reference list of included articles, previous reviews, and published meta-analysis that meets the eligibility criteria.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria will be constructed using PCC framework.

Types of population: We will include studies that report disaggregated data on LP of OLPWH (defined aged 50 years and above). Studies that only report aggregated data for a wider age range will be excluded, unless data for the OPLWH can be requested from the authors.

Types of concept: The core concept is the LP of HIV. Studies that reported the prevalence of LP as main outcome will be included. Study selection

We will import the search results into Covidence [39] for literature screening. First of all, after removing duplicates, two researchers (XD, WG) will examine the title and abstract of each study retrieved against the eligible criteria independently. Those that do not meet the criteria will be eliminated at this step. Second, the full-text of eligible studies at this stage will be retrieved and screened by XD and WG independently. Any discrepancies that arise during the screening process will be discussed and resolved by the other two senior researchers (WC, JC).

Data collection extraction

We developed a preliminary data extraction from that will be used to collect information of eligible studies as follows: (1) Characteristics of the study: author name, publication year, study year, study design, country, country income; (2) Characteristics of the sample: sample size, age, gender; education level; marital status; income level; (3) LP-related data: Prevalence of LP, CD4 cell count, the number of participants who delayed diagnosis; (4) the odds ratios (OR) of associated factors and 95% CIs: Sociodemographic Characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education level, marital status) and Clinical Factors (e.g., ART, current CD4 cell count, comorbidity and polypharmacy). The form will be adjusted following a pilot test will at least three included studies. Two authors (XD, WG) will independently conduct data extraction. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion, with consultation of a third reviewer (JC) if necessary. We will contact the corresponding author by email for missing data. If there is no response within one month, a follow-up email will be sent. Authors who do not respond after two attempts or who decline to provide the data will be excluded from the analysis.

Quality assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for analytical cross-sectional studies and prevalence studies will be used to assess quality and risk of bias of included studies [40]. Each article receives a corresponding score based on the extent to which it meets the following criteria: Yes, No, Unclear, and Not Applicable. Each article will be independently by two authors (XD, WC). Any disagreements that arise will be discussed with the other two senior researchers (JC, WC).

Data analysis

Data analysis will be performed in Stata 18. The random-effects model will be conducted to estimate the pooled prevalence of late presentation among OPLWH, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [41, 42]. The between-study variance will be estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). We will use the Freeman-Tuckey variant of the arcsine square root transformation to stabilize the variance of the proportions [43]. Heterogeneity across included studies will be assessed using Cochran Q test and the I2 statistic, where I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% will be interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Meta-regression and subgroup analyses will be performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses will be conducted based on variables such as the definition of LP, study design, countries, Asian subregions and national income levels. These analyses will also address the secondary objective of identifying risk factors linked to LP among OPLWH. Forest plots will be used to visualize the pooled results. To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analysis will be carried out by serially omitting each study. Publication bias will be evaluated using funnel plots and Egger regression tests.

Certainty of evidence

Two authors (XD, JL) will independently assess the certainty of evidence in terms of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias using the GRADE methodology (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) [44–46]. All discrepancies will be discussed and verified by a third reviewer (JC). Evidence presented in this systematic review and meta-analysis starts as high certainty and is rated down based on the GRADE domains as “high,” “moderate,” “low” or “very low.”

Risk of bias will be assessed by limitations in the study design and execution that may result in overestimates or underestimates of prevalence of LP, including representativeness of sample, objective and unbiased measurement of outcome, and completeness of follow-up. Inconsistency will be assessed by the similarity of point estimates, the overlap of confidence intervals (CIs), and the I2 statistic in forest plots. (Note: Due to the extremely large sample sizes included in certain observational studies, CIs may become very narrow. In such cases, the I2 statistic for heterogeneity in the meta-analysis will be high. Evidence downgrading should be performed cautiously in combination with I2 from subgroup analyses.) Indirectness requires consideration of whether the populations included correspond to OPLWH, and whether the outcomes clearly and unambiguously report LP and AHD. Imprecision will be assessed based on both the width of 95% CIs of the aggregated prevalence estimate and the number of participants included in our studies. Publication bias will be assessed by tests for funnel plot asymmetry (e.g., Egger’s or Begg’s tests).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis will summarize the prevalence of LP and its associated factors among OPLWH in Asia. Older people are often poorly informed about their HIV status due to limited access to HIV prevention resources [47]. OPLWH engages in high-risk sexual behaviors and is often diagnosed at a later stage, which is particularly concerning among married older people [48, 49]. The awareness of HIV counseling and testing in this population remains low and needs greater attention. As of 2023 global statistics, only 78% of PLWH in the Asia–Pacific region are aware of their infection status [2]. Despite efforts across Asia to promote HIV testing through increased relevant national policies and health resources, an estimated 34–72% of people present with LP at their first diagnosis [7, 50–52]. Individual observational studies are difficult to integrate the cultural context, national policies, etc. of the entire region of Asia, and are not sufficient to analyze in depth the current status of LP in Asian OPLWH. There is a lack of high-level evidence-based research on the factors that contribute to LP among OPLWH in Asia. Meanwhile, relevant factors in the literature will be further extracted to better understand the impact of HIV testing routes, sample sources, and marital status on the elderly. This study will promote relevant research on multiple targeted interventions, such as expanding HIV screening, promoting self-testing, and other intervention strategies to ultimately improve HIV prevention and diagnosis in older people. We anticipate that this study will eventually lead to significant effects on HIV prevention for older people.

However, some limitations should be considered. First, some relevant literature may have been missed. We will only search international and Chinese databases. Studies published in other Asian databases (e.g., Japanese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese) may have been missed. Additionally, gray literature will not be systematically retrieved and included in this study. Caution should be exercised when interpreting our results. In addition, the heterogeneity in definitions of LP may pose challenges in pooling data. Subgroup analyses for LP definitions will be conducted to explore the sources of heterogeneity. Furthermore, limited data availability for the ≥50 age group may directly impact the studies and sample sizes included in our study. This limitation could reduce the statistical power.

Ethics and dissemination

This study will involve the analysis of previously published literature and will not include the recruitment of human participants. Therefore, ethical approval is not required. This systematic review will provide comprehensive evidence-based evidence on the late presentation rate among OPLWH in Asia. We anticipate that it will identify high-risk factors for late presentation and provide recommendations for subsequent intervention strategies. We aim to publish the research findings in national and international conference presentations, peer-reviewed publications and the general media channels.
