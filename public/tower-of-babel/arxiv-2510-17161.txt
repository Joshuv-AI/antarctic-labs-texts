1.
Introduction

Positivity of anticanonical divisors imposes strong restrictions on the geometry of algebraic varieties. Over the field of complex numbers,
[
Cal57
,
Kaw85
,
Amb05
]
show that the Albanese morphism of a Calabi-Yau variety is a locally trivial fibration. This phenomenon is also conjectured to hold for the Albanese morphism of a Kähler manifold with nef anticanonical divisor
[
DPS96
]
, and has been affirmatively established for projective manifolds by
[
Cao19
]
and for Kähler manifolds by
[
MWWZ25
]
. For similar results on log pairs with singularities, see
[
Wan22
,
MW23
]
. Additionally, the MRC fibrations of such varieties exhibit analogous properties
[
CH19
,
MW23
,
MWWZ25
]
. These structures are closely related to the (numerically) non-vanishing of anticanonical divisors
[
LMP+23
,
Mül23
]
.

In positive characteristic, similar results have been established assuming that the fibers have good singularities
[
PZ19
,
Eji23
,
EP23
]
. However, wild fibrations in positive characteristic prevent the application of techniques for such generalizations. On the other hand, the wild fibration is a unique phenomenon of algebraic geometry in positive characteristic, setting it apart from algebraic geometry over complex numbers. The authors attempt to understand this phenomenon in
[
CWZ23
,
CWZ24
]
. They first focus on varieties with nef anticanonical divisors and the Albanese morphism of relative dimension
1
. They prove that for such a variety
X
, the Albanese morphism
a_{X}:X\to A
is a fibration, and for their purposes they establish a very precise structure of a threefold
X
with
K_{X}\equiv 0

[
CWZ24
, Theorem 1.1]
: roughly speaking, there exists an isogeny
A^{\prime}\to A
such that the normalization of the reduced fiber product satisfies

(X\times_{A}A^{\prime})_{\mathrm{red}}^{\nu}\cong A^{\prime}\times F,

where
F
is either an elliptic curve or
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Following
[
CWZ24
]
, the present note completes the last piece of this series of works by studying the explicit structures of the Albanese morphism of relative dimension
1
.
To be more precise, we work in the following setting.

(Setting
\dagger
)
Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
p>0
. Assume that

(1)

the resolution of singularities holds in dimension
<\dim X
,

(2)

-K_{X}
is nef but not numerically trivial, and

(3)

the Albanese morphism
a_{X}:X\to A
is of relative dimension one.

Condition (3) implies that the arithmetic genus of the generic fiber
p_{a}(X_{\eta})=0
by the canonical bundle formula in
[
CWZ23
, Theorem 1.2, 1.3]
.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
4.2
& Theorem
4.3
)
.

In
(Setting
\dagger
)
, if
a_{X}
is separable, then there exists an isogeny
B\to A
between abelian varieties of degree at most
2
, such that

X\times_{A}B\simeq\mathbb{P}_{B}(\mathcal{G})

where
\mathcal{G}
is a numerically flat vector bundle of rank
2
.

Besides, we have
h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(-2K_{X}))>0
.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
5.2
& Theorem
5.3
)
.

In
(Setting
\dagger
)
, if
a_{X}
is inseparable, then

(i) there exists an isogeny
A^{\prime}\to A
of abelian varieties, which is purely inseparable of degree 4, such that
X\times_{A}A^{\prime}
is not reduced, and the normalization
X^{\prime}
of

is isomorphic to

;

(ii)

where

is a smooth rank one foliation of

such that

h^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(-2K_{X}))>0
.

We now outline the contents of this paper. Section
2
provides essential background material and sets up the notation used throughout. Section
3
surveys recent results concerning varieties with nef anticanonical divisors in characteristic
p
. In Section
4
, we examine the case where the Albanese morphism is separable, distinguishing three subcases. For the first two, we establish a projective bundle structure on
X
and prove the non-vanishing of
-K_{X}
(Theorem
4.2
). In the third case, we show that, after an isogeny base change of degree
2
, the situation reduces to the preceding cases (Theorem
4.3
). Section
5
is devoted to the study of inseparable Albanese morphisms. We prove that, after an isogeny base change, passage to the reduced scheme structure, and normalization yield a product structure
A^{\prime}\times\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1}
, namely,
X
arises as the quotient of
A^{\prime}\times\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1}
by a rank one foliation
\mathcal{F}
(Theorem
5.2
). Furthermore, when
X
is threefold, we classify all possible such foliations on
A^{\prime}\times\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1}
(Theorem
5.3
).

2.
Preliminary

In this section, we gather relevant concepts and results that will be used later.

2.1.
Notation and convention

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of
\operatorname{char}k=p>0
. A variety
X
over
k
is a separated integral scheme of finite type over
k
. A curve is a
k
-variety of dimension
1
. For a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier divisor
D
on
X
, we write
\kappa(X,D)
(resp.
\nu(X,D)
) for the Kodaira dimension (resp. numerical dimension) of
D
. We also write
D\succcurlyeq 0
if
\kappa(X,D)\geq 0
. A projective morphism
f:X\to S
between normal varieties is called a fibration if
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}=\mathcal{O}_{S}
. For a base change
T\to S
, we set
X_{T}\coloneqq X\times_{S}T
.

A variety
X
is of maximal Albanese dimension if
\dim a_{X}(X)=\dim X
, where
a_{X}:X\to A
is the Albanese morphism.

2.2.
Foliations and purely inseparable morphisms

Let

be a normal variety over
k
.
By a

which is a subsheaf of ring containing

There is a one-to-one correspondence (cf.

\left\{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\text{foliations}}{\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal T_Y}\right\}\leftrightarrow\left\{\vbox{\hbox{finite purely inseparable morphisms $\pi\colon Y\to X$}\hbox{over $k$ of height one with $X$ normal}}\right\},

\mathcal{F}~\mapsto~\pi\colon Y\to\mathrm{Spec}(\mathrm{Ann}(\mathcal{F}))\text{ \ and \ }\pi\colon Y\to X~\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{Y/X}:=\Omega_{X\to Y}^{\perp}

where

As a side note,

.
Let

be a smooth point and

A foliation

is said to be

is a subbundle, namely both

\hbox{$\mathcal{F}$ is smooth at $y$ $\Leftrightarrow$ $Y/\mathcal{F}$ is smooth at $x$ $\Rightarrow$ $\Omega^{1}_{Y/X}$ is locally free at $y$}.

2.4.
Numerical flat vector bundles

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a vector bundle on a projective variety. Then
\mathcal{E}
is said to be numerically flat if
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\vee}
are both nef.

Numerically flat vector bundles on abelian varieties have a particularly simple structure, as described by the following result.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
Eji23
, Theorem 5.4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a vector bundle on an abelian variety
A
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{E}
is numerically flat;

(2)

\mathcal{E}
is obtained as iterated extensions of numerically trivial line bundles;

(3)

\mathcal{E}\simeq\bigoplus_{i}U_{i}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{i}
, where each
U_{i}
is a unipotent vector bundle and each
\mathcal{L}_{i}
is an numerically trivial line bundle.

is nonzero for all
e\geq 1
. Let
[X:Y:Z]
be a homogeneous coordinate of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Set
H=\{Z=0\}
. As
-2K_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}-2C\simeq 2H
, it suffices to show

{\rm Tr}^{e}_{\mathbb{P}^{2},C}(2H):H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2},\omega_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(C+2p^{e}H))\rightarrow H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2},\omega_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(C+2H))

is nonzero (see
[
Tan15
, Lemma 2.4]
).

Let
U_{2}\coloneqq\{[x:y:1]\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\}
be an open set. Consider

\gamma\coloneqq\frac{x^{p^{e}-1}y^{p^{e}-1}}{G(x,y,1)}~dx\wedge dy\in H^{0}(U_{2},\omega_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(C+2p^{e}H)).

Then
\gamma
extends to a section of
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2},\omega_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(C+2p^{e}H))
. In fact, on the open set
U_{0}\coloneqq\{[1:y:z]\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\}
, we have

[1/z:y/z:1]\in U_{0}\cap U_{2}.

Hence,

\gamma=\frac{(1/z)^{p^{e}-1}(y/z)^{p^{e}-1}}{G(1/z,y/z,1)}~d(\frac{1}{z})\wedge d(\frac{y}{z})=\frac{y^{p^{e}-1}z^{1-2p^{e}}}{G(1,y,z)}~dz\wedge dy

extends to a section of
H^{0}(U_{0},\omega_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(C+2p^{e}H))
. A similar calculation shows that
\gamma
also extends to a section of
H^{0}(U_{1},\omega_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(C+2p^{e}H))
with
U_{1}=\{[x:1:z]\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\}
. Now, by the explicit description of the trace map on the smooth affine set (for example, see
[
Tan15
, Remark 2.2 and Remark 2.3]
or
[
BK07
, Lemma 1.3.6]
), we have

{\rm Tr}^{e}_{\mathbb{P}^{2},C}(2H):\frac{x^{p^{e}-1}y^{p^{e}-1}}{G(x,y,1)}~dx\wedge dy\mapsto\frac{1}{G(x,y,1)}~dx\wedge dy.

In particular,
{\rm Tr}^{e}_{\mathbb{P}^{2},C}(2H)
is nonzero, and thus shows the first claim.

When
C\simeq\mathbb{P}^{1}_{K}
, then as it is Frobenius split by
[
SZ14
, Example 2.2]
, there exists an
\mathcal{O}_{C}
-module homomorphism
\phi
such that composition morphism

\mathcal{O}_{C}\xrightarrow{F_{C}^{e\sharp}}F_{C*}^{e}\mathcal{O}_{C}\xrightarrow{\phi}\mathcal{O}_{C}

is the identity. Tensoring with
\mathcal{O}_{C}(-D)
and applying the functor
\operatorname{Hom}(-,\omega_{C})
, we get

{\rm id}:\omega_{C}(D)\rightarrow F_{C*}^{e}\omega_{C}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}(D)\xrightarrow{{\rm Tr}_{C}^{e}(D)}\omega_{C}(D).

Tensoring with
\omega_{C}^{-1}
and taking global sections, we get

{\rm id}:H^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(D))\rightarrow H^{0}(C,F_{C*}^{e}\omega_{C}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}(-K_{C}+D))\xrightarrow{\Phi_{e}}H^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(D)).

Therefore,
S^{0}(C,D)=\bigcap_{e\geq 0}{\rm Im}(\Phi_{e})=H^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(D))\neq\emptyset
.
∎

(II-iii)
:

\deg f_{1}=\deg f_{2}=0

Denote
t_{1}:=f_{1}^{2}\in k,\,t_{2}:=f_{2}^{2}\in k
. By Equation (
5.3.2
) and
\gcd(a,b)=1
, we have
a|(t-t_{2})
and
b|(t-t_{1})
. More precisely,

a=(t-t_{2}),\quad b=(t-t_{1}),\quad c=(t-t_{1})(t-t_{2}).

Case
(II-iv)
:

\deg f_{1}=\deg f_{2}=1

Both polynomials
t+f_{1}^{2}
and
t+f_{2}^{2}
have distinct roots, since they are not perfect squares. Moreover, Equation (
5.3.2
) implies that polynomials
t+f_{1}^{2}
and
t+f_{2}^{2}
have only one common root
t_{0}\in k
. More precisely, there exist distinct
t_{1},\,t_{2}\in k
, such that

t+f_{1}^{2}=\frac{(t-t_{0})(t-t_{1})}{t_{0}+t_{1}}
and
t+f_{2}^{2}=\frac{(t-t_{0})(t-t_{2})}{t_{0}+t_{2}}
.
By Equation (
5.3.2
) and
\gcd(a,b)=1
, we have
a|(t-t_{2})
and
b|(t-t_{1})
. More precisely,

a=(t_{0}+t_{1})(t-t_{2}),\quad b=(t_{0}+t_{2})(t-t_{1}),\quad c=(t+f_{1}^{2})a=(t-t_{0})(t-t_{1})(t-t_{2}).

Case
(III)
:

By (
5.3.1
) Condition (C3) becomes
\frac{a^{2}+ca^{\prime}}{a}=\frac{cb^{\prime}}{b}=\frac{cc^{\prime}}{c}
whenever the denominators are non-zero. The case
c=0
can be ruled out by Conditions (C1) and (C2). In the following, we assume that
c\neq 0
.
After the same calculations as in case (I) and case (II), if
a\neq 0
, then
c=(t+f_{1}^{2})a
. If
b\neq 0
, then
c=f_{2}^{2}b
. Note that
\rm gcd(a,b)=gcd(a,b,c)=1
. If
a=0
, then
b=1,c=f_{2}^{2}b=f_{2}^{2}
, which contradicts Condition (C2). If
b=0
, then
a=1,c=(t+f_{1}^{2})a=t+f_{1}^{2}
, which contradicts Condition (C2).

From now on, we assume that
a,b\neq 0
. The following discussion is based on the Equation

(5.3.3)

(t+f_{1}^{2})a=c=f_{2}^{2}b.

Case
(III-i)
:

\deg a=1,\,\deg b=0

By Equation (
5.3.3
) and
\gcd(a,b)=1
, we have
b=1
,
\deg f_{1}=0
and
\deg f_{2}=1
. Denote
t_{1}:=f_{1}^{2}\in k
, and then Equation (
5.3.3
) becomes
(t-t_{1})a=(t+f_{1}^{2})a=c=f_{2}^{2}
. Therefore, there exists nonzero
s\in k
, such that

a=s(t-t_{1}),\quad b=1,\quad c=(t-t_{1})a=s(t-t_{1})^{2}.

Case
(III-ii)
:

\deg a=0,\,\deg b=1

By Equation (
5.3.3
) and
\gcd(a,b)=1
, we have
a=1
and
\deg f_{1}=\deg f_{2}=0
. Denote
t_{1}:=f_{1}^{2}\in k
and nonzero
s:=f_{2}^{2}\in k
and then Equation (
5.3.3
) implies that

a=1,\quad b=\frac{t-t_{1}}{s},\quad c=t-t_{1}.

Case
(III-iii)
:

\deg a=1,\,\deg b=1

Let
t_{1},\,t_{2}
be root of
b,\,a
respectively. By Equation (
5.3.3
) and
\gcd(a,b)=1
, we have
b|(t+f_{1}^{2})
,
a|f_{2}^{2}
and then
\deg f_{1}=\deg f_{2}=1
, since
\deg c\leq 3
. Moreover, polynomial
t+f_{1}^{2}
has roots
t_{1},t_{2}
and
f_{2}
has root
t_{2}
of multiplicity 2. More precisely,
t+f_{1}^{2}=\frac{(t-t_{1})(t-t_{2})}{t_{1}+t_{2}}
, and there exists nonzero
s\in k
, such that
f_{2}^{2}=s(t-t_{2})^{2}
. Therefore, Equation (
5.3.3
) implies that

a=(t_{1}+t_{2})(t-t_{2}),\quad b=\frac{t-t_{1}}{s},\quad c=(t-t_{1})^{2}(t-t_{2}).

Case
(IV)
:

By (
5.3.1
) Condition (C3) becomes
\frac{ca^{\prime}}{a}=\frac{a^{2}+cb^{\prime}}{b}=\frac{cc^{\prime}}{c}
whenever the denominators are non-zero. If
c=0
, then
a=0
,
b=1
, which contradicts Condition (C2). In the following, we assume that
c\neq 0
, and then the above Equation becomes

(5.3.4)

ca^{\prime}=ac^{\prime},\quad a^{2}+cb^{\prime}=c^{\prime}b.

If
a=0
, then
b\neq 0
. By calculations in case (I) for the second Equation of (
5.3.4
), we have
c=f_{2}^{2}b
. Thus, we cannot find
b
and
c
satisfying both (C1) and (C2) simultaneously.
If
b=0
, then
a\neq 0
. By calculations in case (I) for the first Equation of (
5.3.4
), we have
c=f_{1}^{2}a
. Thus, we cannot find
a
and
c
satisfying both (C1) and (C2) simultaneously.
In the following, we assume that
a,b\neq 0
. By calculations in case (I), the first Equation of (
5.3.4
) can be solved and then the second Equation of (
5.3.4
) can be simplified as

c=f_{1}^{2}a,\quad(\frac{b}{a})^{\prime}=\frac{(ab)^{\prime}}{a^{2}}=\frac{1}{a^{2}}\cdot(bc\cdot\frac{1}{f_{1}^{2}})^{\prime}=\frac{(bc)^{\prime}}{a^{2}f_{1}^{2}}=\frac{1}{f_{1}^{2}}.

Solve the differential Equation above using integration by parts, and then there are coprime polynomials
\tilde{f}_{2},\tilde{g}_{2}
, such that

(5.3.5)

c=f_{1}^{2}a,\quad b=\frac{t}{f_{1}^{2}}a+\frac{\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}}{\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}}a=\frac{t\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}+f_{1}^{2}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}}{f_{1}^{2}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}}a.

The following disscussion is based on the above Equation (
5.3.5
). Note that to make
c
a polynomial of
\deg\leq 3
, it follows that
\deg f_{1}\leq 1
.

Case
(IV-i)
:

\deg f_{1}=0

In this case, note that
t\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}+f_{1}^{2}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}
and
f_{1}^{2}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}
are coprime polynomials. By Equation (
5.3.5
), there is a polynomial
\tilde{h}_{2}
, such that
a=f_{1}^{2}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}\tilde{h}_{2},\,b=(t\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}+f_{1}^{2}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2})\tilde{h}_{2}.

Then
\deg a\leq 1
implies that
\deg\tilde{g}_{2}=0
. Let
f_{2}:=\tilde{f}_{2}/\tilde{g}_{2},\,h_{2}:=f_{1}^{2}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}\tilde{h}_{2}
. We get
a=h_{2},\,b=(t/{f_{1}^{2}}+\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}/\tilde{g}_{2}^{2})f_{1}^{2}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}\tilde{h}_{2}=(t/{f_{1}^{2}}+f_{2}^{2})h_{2},\,c=f_{1}^{2}a=f_{1}^{2}h_{2}.

Note that
h_{2}=\gcd(a,b,c)=1
and
\deg f_{2}=0
since
\deg b\leq 1
. Denote
s_{1}:=f_{1}^{2}\neq 0,\,t_{2}:=f_{2}^{2}\in k
. Therefore,

a=1,\quad b=\frac{t}{s_{1}}+t_{2},\quad c=s_{1}.

Case
(IV-ii)
:

\deg f_{1}=1,\,\deg\tilde{g}_{2}=0
or
\deg f_{1}=1,\,\tilde{f}_{2}=0

By Equation (
5.3.5
), to make
b
a polynomial of
\deg\leq 1
, it follows that
f_{1}^{2}|ta
and
\deg\tilde{f}_{2}=0
. Since
\deg a\leq 1
, we can assume that
a=t
and
f_{1}^{2}=s_{1}t^{2}
for some nonzero

. Denote
t_{2}:=\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}/\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}\in k
. Therefore, Equation (
5.3.5
) becomes

a=t,\quad b=\frac{t}{f_{1}^{2}}a+\frac{\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}}{\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}}a=\frac{1}{s_{1}}+t_{2}t,\quad c=f_{1}^{2}a=s_{1}t^{3}.

Case
(IV-iii&iv)
:

\deg f_{1}=1,\,\tilde{f}_{2}\neq 0

By Equation (
5.3.5
), to make
b
a polynomial of
\deg\leq 1
, it follows that
\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}|(t\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}+f_{1}^{2}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2})a
. Because
\tilde{f}_{2}\neq 0
and
\gcd(\tilde{f}_{2},\tilde{g}_{2})=1
, we have
\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}|f_{1}^{2}a

2\deg\tilde{g}_{2}\leq 2\deg f_{1}+\deg a\leq 3

.
Thus,
f_{1}^{2}=s_{1}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}
for some nonzero
s_{1}\in k
. Then Equation (
5.3.5
) can be simplified as

(5.3.6)

c=f_{1}^{2}a,\quad b=\frac{t+s_{1}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}}{s_{1}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}}a.

By Equation (
5.3.6
), to make
b
a polynomial of
\deg\leq 1
, it follows that
\deg\tilde{f}_{2}\leq 1
.

Case
(IV-iii)
:

\deg\tilde{g}_{2}=1,\,\deg\tilde{f}_{2}=0

In this case, to make
b
a polynomial, Equation (
5.3.6
) implies that
\tilde{g}_{2}|(t+s_{1}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2})
,
\tilde{g}_{2}|a
and hence
b
is nonzero constant. Denote nonzero
r_{2}:=\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}\in k
. We can assume that
a=t+s_{1}r_{2}
and
\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}=s_{2}(t+s_{1}r_{2})^{2}
for some nonzero
s_{2}\in k
. Therefore, Equation (
5.3.6

a=t+s_{1}r_{2},\quad b=\frac{t+s_{1}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}}{s_{1}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}}a=\frac{1}{s_{1}s_{2}},\quad c=f_{1}^{2}a=s_{1}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}a=s_{1}s_{2}(t+s_{1}r_{2})^{3}.

Case
(IV-iv)
:

\deg\tilde{g}_{2}=1,\,\deg\tilde{f}_{2}=1

Note that the polynomial
t+s_{1}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}
has two distinct roots
t_{1},t_{2}\in k
, since it is not a perfect square. More precisely,
t+s_{1}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}=\frac{(t-t_{1})(t-t_{2})}{t_{1}+t_{2}}
. To make
b
a polynomial, Equation (
5.3.6
) implies that
\tilde{g}_{2}|(t+s_{1}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2})
,
\tilde{g}_{2}|a
and hence
b
is a polynomial of degree 1. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\tilde{g}_{2}
has root
t_{1}
. Thus, let
a=t-t_{1}
and then
\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}=s_{2}(t-t_{1})^{2}
for some nonzero
s_{2}\in k
. Therefore,

a=t-t_{1},\quad b=\frac{t+s_{1}\tilde{f}_{2}^{2}}{s_{1}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}}a=\frac{t-t_{2}}{s_{1}s_{2}(t_{1}+t_{2})},\quad c=f_{1}^{2}a=s_{1}\tilde{g}_{2}^{2}a=s_{1}s_{2}(t-t_{1})^{3}.
