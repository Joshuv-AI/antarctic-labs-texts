Do microenvironmental changes in degenerative disc disease reflect in systemic circulation? A proteomic investigation
Authors: Sharon Miracle Nayagam, Narmatha Devi Palraj, Murugesh Eswaran, Ganesh Selvaraj, Sunmathi Rajendran, Karthik Ramachandran, Divya Arunachalam, Chitraa Tangavel, Srivijay Anand KS, Muthurajan Raveendran, Shanmuganathan Rajasekaran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe complement system plays a crucial role in immune regulation and inflammation, contributing to intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) pathogenesis. While tissue-specific complement activation in degenerated discs is well-documented, its systemic expression in degenerative disc disease (DDD) plasma remains unclear.MethodsThis study employed high-throughput mass spectrometry to analyze the plasma and tissue proteomes of 40 DDD patients, comprising of Modic change (MC) and non-Modic change (NMC) patients, alongside 20 healthy volunteers (HV). Only plasma and no plasma-matched tissue samples were collected from HV group.ResultsProteomic analysis identified 707 proteins in DDD plasma and 655 in HV plasma, with 508 common. Complement and coagulation cascades were enriched, with 46 complement proteins identified. The DDD-plasma group exhibited upregulation of most complement proteins, except for C1q complex (1.38-fold) and Complement Factor D (CFD, 0.64-fold), alongside slight downregulation of vitronectin (VTN), clusterin (CLU), and complement C8G. Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were observed in DDD-plasma, indicating systemic inflammation. Correlation analysis revealed weak associations between plasma and tissue complement protein levels, suggesting potential regulatory mechanisms.ConclusionOur study reveals that systemic complement alterations in DDD, supporting their potential as blood-based biomarkers reflecting localized disc pathology. Further validation in larger cohorts across different disease stages is needed to explore their diagnostic and therapeutic implications. Introduction

Degenerative disc disease (DDD) is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder characterized by the deterioration of intervertebral discs, often resulting in chronic back pain and a decreased quality of life [1, 2]. The molecular and cellular changes in DDD involve imbalances in metabolic events, inflammatory responses, and disruptions in the extracellular matrix (ECM) equilibrium, leading to the loss of structural integrity, pain, inflammation, and altered cellular homeostasis [3–6]. Unravelling the molecular associations in disc health and disease is imperative for developing effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Proteomic analysis of plasma is a non-invasive potential avenue to explore molecular signatures indicative of disc conditions.

The complement system, an integral part of the innate immune system, plays diverse roles in immune surveillance, inflammation, and tissue homeostasis [7–9]. While complement system activation is crucial for maintaining tissue integrity, dysregulation may contribute to the inflammatory milieu in various pathological states. Complement cascades can be activated via classical, lectin, and alternative pathways. Activation of these pathways leads to proteolytic cascades eliminating target pathogenic cells. Activation of the classical pathway occurs by the binding of the C1Q, C1R and C1S complex to antibody-antigen complexes or directly to pathogen surfaces. The lectin pathway is activated by carbohydrate structures on pathogen surfaces, and the alternate pathway by microbial surfaces. All the three pathways activate complement C3, a central component of complement system, thereby leading to generating effector molecules C3a and C5a, which are potent inflammatory mediators, and formation of membrane attack complex (MAC) [10, 11].

In the plasma of healthy individuals, complement activation maintains a delicate equilibrium, defending against infections while minimizing tissue damage. However, in the context of DDD, aberrant complement activation may play a role in perpetuating inflammation, tissue damage, and disease progression. Kuhn et al., reported enhanced activation of complement was due to anaphylatoxin generation and deposition of terminal complement complex in human degenerated disc cells [12, 13]. Complement activation products, such as anaphylatoxins (C3a and C5a) and membrane attack complex (MAC), can induce inflammatory responses and contribute to the breakdown of extracellular matrix components [14]. Rajasekaran et al., reported that discs with modic change (MC) exhibited downregulation of extracellular matrix components, such as various collagens and proteoglycans, and upregulation of inflammatory molecules, including plasminogen, angiogenin, and kininogen leading to the activation of pathways related to the immune response, including Fc Fragment of IgG Receptor IIIa-mediated phagocytosis, regulation of Toll-like receptors by endogenous ligands, and neutrophil and platelet degranulation implicating the complement system in the pathogenesis of intervertebral disc degeneration associated with MC [15].

Modic changes (MC) are vertebral bone marrow alterations adjacent to degenerated intervertebral discs, frequently detected on MRI, and classified into distinct subtypes based on signal characteristics. They are considered imaging biomarkers of disc degeneration and are closely associated with low back pain [16]. The biological significance of MC lies in their strong link to disc inflammation, neovascularization, and increased systemic inflammatory mediators, including CRP and ESR [17]. Recent evidence also suggests that Modic changes may influence local immune activity, including complement activation, thereby amplifying inflammatory injury within the disc–endplate complex [15]. In parallel, non-Modic DDD represents disc degeneration without marrow changes, which may progress through alternative degenerative and fibrotic pathways.

In intervertebral disc degeneration, inflammation is widely recognized as central drivers of disease progression. The degradation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components and the progressive deterioration of intervertebral discs (IVDs), loss of hydration, progressive loss of structural demarcation are significantly linked to chronic inflammatory processes [18]. The dysregulation of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and mediators in IVDD pathophysiology defines the importance of immune responses in disease progression [19]. Complement activation represents a crucial component of these inflammatory cascades, with complement proteins serving as key effectors of innate immunity and inflammation [8].

Several reports show crosstalk between macrophages and intervertebral discs contribute to the promotion of inflammation and degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM). These studies suggest immune exposure to avascular structure through a series of events including neovascularization [20–22]. Understanding the correlation between complement proteins in the plasma and tissue of DDD patients is crucial for unravelling the immunological dynamics associated with this musculoskeletal disorder.

This study aims to elucidate the relationship between complement proteins in the plasma and the respective disc tissue of individuals diagnosed with degenerative disc disease (DDD). In this study we report the results of a systematic proteomic profiling of plasma proteins obtained from healthy volunteers (HV), and DDD (modic change (MC), and non-modic change (NMC)) group. The results revealed significant upregulation of classical, lectin, and alternative pathway components, including complement components C2, C3/C5 convertase, C5, C8A, and C8B, was observed. However, significant downregulation of C1q, C1r, C1s, CFD were noticed in DDD-plasma. The association studies showed a correlation pattern between plasma and tissue proteins significant positive correlations for specific complement proteins such as CFI, CFH, C1S, C4A, C4B, while many complement proteins showed significant negative correlations between plasma and tissue levels, indicating potential regulatory mechanisms influencing their expression. Our findings suggest that complement activation may play a crucial role in DDD, with differentially expressed proteins in plasma reflecting the disease state in disc tissue and highlighting the potentiality of plasma complements being explored as promising candidates for disease progression and inflammatory processes in DDD.

Materials and methods

Study participants and experimental design

A cohort of individuals, including both healthy subjects and those diagnosed with DDD, were recruited for the study with proper ethical clearance and written consent from patients adhering to the declaration of Helsinki as guided by ICMR. The control group had 20 plasma samples collected from healthy volunteers (HV) with Pfirmann grading I who were devoid of any history of low back pain or other medical co-morbidity. The degenerative disc disease (DDD) group comprised patients with vertebral end-plate changes called modic change (M, n = 20) and without being called non-modic change (NMC, n-20). Both plasma and their corresponding disc tissue samples were collected from individuals belonging to MC and NMC who were undergoing surgery with Pfirmann grading III, IV and V. However, no tissue specimens were collected from healthy volunteers. Collection of plasma samples, depletion and protein pre-fractionation

About 5 mL of whole blood was systematically collected and centrifuged at 1000–2000×g to separate plasma. The plasma samples were stored at −80 °C before use. High-abundant proteins such as albumin (ALB) and immunoglobulin G (IgG) were depleted from plasma samples using the Sigma ProteoPrep Immunoaffinity Albumin and IgG Depletion Kit (# cat.no. PROTIA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Further, protein concentration was estimated and 100 µg of samples were subjected for in-gel based tryptic digestion as described earlier [23].

Excision of disc tissue samples, protein extraction and digestion

For global protein extraction, 200 mg of human intervertebral disc nucleus pulposus (NP) tissue samples was collected from all diseased subjects undergoing microdiscectomy or fusion surgery. The samples were pulverized using a lysis buffer containing 8 M urea in 50mM TEAB at pH 8.0 [20], followed by pulverization with liquid nitrogen, sonication, and protein estimation using Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit. Around 100 µg of samples were subjected for in-solution based tryptic digestion as described earlier [23–25].

Peptide purification, ESI-LC-MS/MS analysis and protein identification

Trypsin digested peptides were purified using C18 spin columns [Agilent Peptide Clean-up C18 Spin Column (#Cat No. 51882750)]. Around 1000ng (nanogram) of the tryptic peptides from each sample were subjected to tandem ESI-LC MS/MS analysis for this study. Tandem mass spectrometric analysis of the plasma and tissue samples was performed by Q-Exactive™ Plus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap™ Mass Spectrometer based on the mass/charge (m/z) ratio. Proteins with high peptide confidence and peptide rank 1 were considered for the entire analysis [26].

Data normalization, clustering and discriminant analysis

The data was analyzed with metaboanalyst software v6.027 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). NSAF-based normalization (normalized spectral abundance factor) was done for proteins with ≥ 5PSM (Peptide Spectral Matches) [27, 28]. Further, the data were quantile normalized using an R script, log transformed and auto scaled prior to clustering and discrimination. A normalized data matrix was used to calculate the partial least square discriminant analysis (2 components, FDR < 0.05) and to generate a volcano plot for differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Proteins showing significant fold changes (log2FC ± 0.5) were considered for the classification of functional annotations. Illustrative images were generated using SRplot [, 29, 30].

Functional enrichment and statistical analysis

The network analysis and visualization of plasma/tissue proteins, which exhibited significant modulation in health and disease, were performed using Stringdb (https://string-db.org/) and Cytoscape (v3.8.2) (https://cytoscape.org/). Hierarchical clustering of identified proteins was executed following Z-score normalization, and significantly different proteins among groups were calculated using a student’s t-test (two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test (multiple groups). For non-normally distributed data or unequal variances, a non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction was applied. A two-tailed α of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

ROC curve analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the discriminatory performance of selected complement and non-complement proteins between DDD-plasma and HV-plasma groups. Area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated using the Youden index to determine optimal cutoff points. Statistical significance of AUC values was assessed against the null hypothesis (AUC = 0.5) using a non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test. Proteins included in ROC analysis were pre-selected based on their differential abundance (log₂ fold change), prevalence across samples, and biological importance; therefore, raw p-values are reported without additional multiple testing correction.

Results

The demographic data of the study subjects belonging to healthy volunteers (HV) and individuals with degenerative disc disease (DDD), which includes both modic change (MC) and non-modic Change (NMC) samples specified in Table 1 (under materials and methods section). The sex ratio, average BMI, certain blood profiles and dietary patterns were comparable between the groups. Increased expression levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were found with only CRP being statistically significant (p, < 0.05) (Fig. 2) on comparing DDD-plasma with that of HV indicating the inflammatory nature of the DDD-plasma group. Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores were 0 in all HV participants, while in DDD patients they ranged from 17 to 78 (median: 40), indicating significant moderate to severe disability. VAS scores were also 0 in HV and ranged from 4 to 9 (median: 8) in DDD patients, reflecting significant pain levels. Total proteome analysis identified a total of: 655 and 707 proteins in the albumin-globulin depleted plasma of HV and DDD-plasma respectively. In the corresponding DDD-tissue of the MC and NMC patients revealed 1966 total proteins indicating the number of proteins in the DDD-NP tissue were more compared to their plasma.

Box plots illustrate significant variations in clinical and blood parameters between healthy volunteer (HV) and degenerative disc disease (DDD) plasma samples, specifically for erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain scores. Significant differences were observed for CRP, ODI, and VAS between HV and DDD groups (p < 0.05), while ESR differences were not statistically significant. Elevated CRP, ODI, and VAS values in the DDD group are indicative of systemic inflammation, functional impairment, and pain burden, respectively, supporting the presence of active disease processes

Global protein profiling of study groups

The Partial Least Square Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) model demonstrated a variance of 13.7% observed across total proteins between HV-plasma and DDD-plasma groups respectively (Fig. 3a). Comparative proteome profiling between HV-plasma and DDD-plasma groups revealed 508 common proteins. In addition, the unique presence of 147 proteins in HV and 199 in DDD groups were found (Fig. 3b). Gene ontological classification based on biological process and pathway enrichment analysis for common proteins using the KEGG database (Supplementary Table S1) revealed significant pathways as illustrated in Fig. 3c and d. A total of 46 complement proteins were participating in complement and coagulation cascades. Furthermore, a total of 147 and 199 proteins were found to be uniquely present in HV-plasma and DDD-plasma, respectively, as listed in Supplementary Table S2. Functional characterization of HV-plasma–specific proteins revealed GO-enrichment in molecular binding, innate immune response, and transport. Proteins unique to DDD-plasma were associated with significant processes related to the adaptive immune response, extracellular matrix structural constituents conferring tensile strength, apoptosis, and cell death.

Other plasma proteins beyond complement components

To understand the expression levels of other plasma proteins, pathway enrichment analysis was performed excluding complement proteins. Positive log₂FC denotes higher abundance in DDD plasma relative to HV plasma; negative log₂FC denotes lower abundance in DDD-plasma. Significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 5a) were lipoprotein remodeling/transport (chylomicron/HDL/plasma lipoproteins), platelet activation–degranulation and hemostasis, neutrophil degranulation/innate immune system, scavenger-receptor ligand uptake and heme scavenging, retinoid transport, and integrin MAPK signaling. Differential expression analysis having log2Fc ± 0.5 (Fig. 5b) showed upregulated levels of VWF (von Willebrand factor), HABP2 (Hyaluronan Binding Protein 2), LYZ (Lysozyme), EFEMP2 (Fibulin-4), CELSR2 (Cadherin EGF LAG seven-pass G-type receptor 2), DBH (Dopamine β-hydroxylase), LCP1 (L-plastin), and CTTN (cortactin), implicating endothelial/platelet activation, innate immune engagement, and cytoskeletal signaling in DDD-plasma. Downregulated levels of APOA1 (significant q, < 0.05), CAT (catalase), ADAMTS13 (the VWF-cleaving protease), CDH5 (VE-cadherin), DPP4 (Dipeptidyl peptidase-4, serine exopeptidase), GZMK (Granzyme K- tryptase protease), and HSPA9 (Heat shock protein family A (Hsp70) member 9). Expression levels such as ↑VWF with ↓ADAMTS13 and ↓CDH5 remains suggestive of pro-thrombotic, endothelial-activation phenotype, while ↓APOA1 and ↓CAT indicate perturbed HDL-associated lipid handling and diminished antioxidant capacity in DDD.

Expression of complement proteins- classical pathway

Complement proteins participating in classical pathway were identified and their differential expression patterns in DDD-plasma in comparison with HV-plasma. Our study identified classical pathway complements such as Complement component C1q (C1q), along with its interacting proteins Complement component C1r (C1r) and Complement component C1s (C1s); as well as Complement component C2 (C2), Complement component 3 (C3), Complement component C4 (C4) and C3/C5 convertase as shown in Fig. 6a. In the DDD-plasma, differentially expressed complements with a fold change threshold of (± 0.5) revealed significant downregulation of C1q complex (log2fc, −1.38) along with marginal decrement in the C1r, C1s. However, other complements showed marginal increase compared to HV-plasma but these differences were not statistically significant in post hoc analysis (p > 0.05 across all group comparisons). The box plot analysis (Fig. 6b) confirmed that the C1q complex abundance was markedly lower in both DDD-MC and DDD-NMC groups relative to HV, whereas no significant differences were observed between the two DDD subtypes.

Other pattern recognition molecules, including FCN2 and FCN3 showed minimal downregulation. However, none of these expression changes were found to be statistically significant. Although fold change analysis suggested an upregulation of MBL2 and MASP1, these differences were not statistically significant. Similarly, FCN2, FCN3, and MASP2 exhibited minimal variation between groups.

Association of complement expression between DDD-tissue and DDD-plasma

We analyzed the relationship between plasma expression levels of complement proteins and their respective tissue expression in individuals with DDD (Table 3). Heatmap analysis demonstrated a higher magnitude of complement protein expression in plasma compared to tissue, except for classical pathway complements (C1Q, C1R, C1S, C3/C5 convertase), terminal pathway components/regulators (CFD, CFB, CLU, C8G), and coagulation factors (F2, F7, F13A1), which exhibited low expression in plasma (Fig. 9a). Correlation analysis, as shown in Fig. 9b, revealed weak positive and negative correlations (± 0.50) between plasma and tissue expression levels.

ROC curve analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminatory performance of complement and non-complement proteins between DDD-plasma and HV-plasma groups (Fig. 10). Discussion

Among the many inflammatory cascades implicated in IVDD, complement activation is increasingly recognized as a key contributor of disease progression. The complement system can be activated by cell necrosis, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, and bacterial infiltration into the damaged intervertebral disc [26, 31]. Dysregulated complement activation has been linked to excessive inflammation, ECM degradation, and nucleus pulposus (NP) cell apoptosis, suggesting that complement-driven mechanisms may significantly influence IVDD pathology [12]. Various research suggests the evidence of the presence of ECM degradation products, such as collagen type II (COL2A1) and aggrecan (ACAN), detected in the plasma of IVDD patients, suggests that matrix breakdown within the disc contributes to systemic inflammatory responses [32]. Recent proteomic analysis further confirmed that plasma proteins play a role in IVDD progression, with several identified proteins involved in ECM remodeling and inflammatory pathways. The study demonstrated a causal association between specific plasma proteins, such as Myoneurin (MYNN), Intersectin 1 (ITSN1), and EIF4G3, and IVDD, further reinforcing the systemic impact of local disc degeneration [33].

Analyzing plasma in patients with degenerative disc disease (DDD) involves evaluating systemic biomarkers that reflect inflammatory, immune, and metabolic alterations associated with disc degeneration. The study of complement activation, cytokines, metabolic changes, and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation markers in plasma can provide insights into disease progression, systemic inflammation, and potential therapeutic targets. Our study is one of its kind to unravel the inverse relationship between the disc and plasma inflammatory milieu to understand the pathological means of disease progression. We employed ESI-MS/MS to profile plasma and disc tissue proteins across control- HV (Healthy Volunteers) and DDD (MC- Modic change; NMC- Non-modic change) groups.

Our study identified alterations of inflammatory, ECM, immune, metabolic-related proteins in DDD-plasma, with complement family of proteins widely altered and observed as predominant network of dysregulation. Pathway enrichment analysis of the common plasma proteome (Fig. 3c) revealed clustering in immune regulation, coagulation cascades, and inflammatory signaling, converging towards the centrality of host-defense pathways in DDD pathogenesis. The presence of pathways such as platelet activation, lipoprotein remodeling, neutrophil degranulation, and ECM–receptor interaction remains suggestive of vascular, immune, matrix- oriented processes in systemic circulation. Mapping of the identified complement proteins onto the KEGG complement–coagulation cascade (Fig. 4) further represented nearly all pathways, including classical, lectin, alternative, and terminal components reinforcing complement involvement.

Furthermore, our pathway enrichment and differential expression analysis (Fig. 5) revealed upregulation of proteins such as von Willebrand factor (VWF), hyaluronan-binding protein 2 (HABP2), alongside the downregulation of ADAMTS13, the VWF-cleaving protease remaining suggestive of intervertebral disc microvasculature as reported earlier [34, 35]. In addition, elevated levels of lysozyme (LYZ) and L-plastin (LCP1) suggests innate and adaptive immune activation resulting from inflammatory injury in IVDD [36]. Elevated EFEMP2 (Fibulin-4) further implies matrix crosslinking and fibrotic remodeling [32]. The significant downregulation of systemic protective proteins including APOA1, a major component of HDL; APCS, an acute phase pentraxin; and catalase (CAT), a key antioxidant enzyme, processes known to aggravate oxidative stress, disc cell apoptosis and accumulation of uncleared cellular debris [35–37]. Non-complement alterations along with complement dysregulation forms core inflammatory responses in DDD.

In plasma samples. our findings reveal a significant downregulation of the C1q complex (C1q, C1r, and C1s) in individuals with degenerative disc disease (DDD) (Fig. 6). The classical complement pathway, initiated by C1q binding to immune complexes, apoptotic cells, or pathogen-associated molecular patterns, plays a crucial role in immune surveillance and clearance of cellular debris [38]. Complement activation via classical pathway is typically initiated by C1q binding to immune complexes or altered self-components, triggering C1r and C1s activation to cleave C4 and C2, leading to the formation of C3 convertase (C4b2a). The observed downregulation of C1q remains suggestive of compromised classical pathway activation in DDD-plasma, which may lead to inefficient clearance of apoptotic disc cells and circulating extracellular matrix remnants, aggravating chronic inflammation [7, 39, 40].

The diminished levels of C1q in DDD-plasma systemic circulation suggests altered systemic complement regulation, potentially due to local sequestration in inflamed disc tissues, consumption due to chronic complement activation, or diminished hepatic synthesis as a regulatory response. A dysfunctional classical pathway could contribute to the prolonged presence of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) in circulation, further enhancing chronic inflammation and tissue deterioration [41]. In addition, C1q complex is involved in modulation of immune responses and homeostasis, its deficiency may impair anti-inflammatory and reparative mechanisms, potentially favoring a more pro-inflammatory microenvironment in DDD.

Analyzing the biochemical parameters, we found elevated levels of ESR and CRP in DDD-plasma (Fig. 2). C-reactive protein (CRP), an acute-phase protein that binds to the surface of pathogens, was significantly upregulated in DDD-plasma, indicating a systemic inflammatory response. CRP, an acute-phase reactant plasma protein produced by the liver in response to interleukin-6 (IL-6) and other pro-inflammatory cytokines, elevated CRP levels in DDD suggest a chronic state of low-grade systemic inflammation. C1q can also bind to cell surfaces via pattern recognition molecules such as C-reactive protein (CRP), an acute-phase protein that binds to the surface of pathogens or injured cells and activates the C1q-mediated complement activation [42]. As the C1q complex is downregulated, there may be impairment of C1q and CRP interaction leading to inefficient opsonization and clearance of necrotic or apoptotic cells, further sustaining inflammatory processes [43–45].

In addition to dysregulation of the classical pathway, our data indicate a complex regulation of the alternative pathway in DDD-plasma. Complement Factor D (CFD) was significantly downregulated, suggesting reduced initiation of the alternative pathway. However, this was accompanied by the upregulation of regulatory proteins CFH and CFI, which act to suppress excessive alternative pathway activity by inhibiting and degrading C3 convertases [46]. Despite these inhibitory signals, the increase in terminal pathway components (C5, C8A, and C8B) (Fig. 8) indicates that downstream complement activation still occurs, enabled via crosstalk from the classical and lectin pathways. Thus, while the alternative pathway initiation appears dampened due to low CFD, the presence of activated terminal components suggests that the alternative pathway is not fully silenced but rather operates in a regulated, compensatory manner alongside classical and lectin pathway activation.

The upregulation of MBL2 and MASP1 in DDD-plasma indicates that the lectin pathway is activated in response to DAMPs released during disc degeneration (Fig. 7). This activation leads to the formation of C3 convertase (C4b2a), amplifying the inflammatory response. Potential involvement of ficolins that recognize released glycan structures circulating in the plasma may further contribute to lectin pathway activation [47]. Classical pathway components such as C2 and C3/C5 convertase were also upregulated, leading to enhanced C3 and C5 activation and subsequent inflammation. With reduced C1q-mediated clearance of apoptotic cells and debris, DAMPs may accumulate, triggering more robust MBL/MASP activation and enhancing LP-driven inflammation [48, 49]. This could worsen systemic inflammation in DDD due to sustained C3 and C5 activation, leading to MAC formation (C5b-9) and tissue damage. On the other hand, several reports suggest that elevated levels of FCN2 (ficolin 2) (Table 3) mediates enhanced complement activation, leading to inflammatory responses within the intervertebral disc microenvironment [50, 51]. In addition, they are also known to contribute to persistent inflammation in the disc. As standing evidence, we could observe unique proteins of diseased plasma are actively enriched in cell death, the apoptotic signaling pathway (Table S1). However, proteins specific to control plasma were found enriched in humoral response, innate immune response suggesting an established homeostasis (Table S2).

The downregulation of C1q, C1r, and C1s in DDD-plasma indicates suppression of the classical pathway, which plays an important role in opsonization and apoptotic cell clearance. C1q deficiency has been strongly linked to defective clearance of apoptotic debris and the development of autoimmunity and chronic inflammation [52, 53]. In DDD, reduced classical pathway initiation likely promotes secondary necrosis of disc cells and persistence of matrix fragments, sustaining a pro-inflammatory milieu. Similarly, Complement Factor D (CFD), a rate-limiting enzyme of the alternative pathway, was reduced, suggesting diminished amplification capacity. Such reductions may reflect either regulatory exhaustion due to chronic activation or inadequate complement-driven clearance, as reported in other chronic inflammatory disorders [40]. In addition, clusterin (CLU) and vitronectin (VTN), key regulators that inhibit Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) assembly, were also downregulated. Loss of these regulators predisposes to unrestrained MAC formation and cytolysis, processes implicated in tissue injury, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation [54]. The concurrent downregulation of classical and alternative pathway initiators with regulatory inhibitors suggests a state of complement imbalance, where impaired clearance of apoptotic material and weakened regulatory control exists to accelerate inflammation-driven disc degeneration.

In plasma samples, ROC curve analysis highlighted the discriminatory potential of both complement and non-complement proteins in distinguishing DDD patients from healthy controls (Fig. 10). Among complement proteins, C1q and C8B emerged as significant plasma markers, reflecting classical pathway dysregulation and terminal pathway activation, respectively where findings remain consistent with reports linking complement imbalance to chronic inflammation and tissue injury [55, 56]. Although C5 and CFI demonstrated moderate performance, their lack of statistical significance suggests that they may have greater value within a multi-analyte biomarker panel rather than as individual predictors. In contrast, the non-complement proteins APOA1 and APCS showed superior discriminatory capacity in plasma, with AUC values exceeding 0.75. Reduced APOA1 levels reflect impaired lipid metabolism and antioxidant defenses, while decreased APCS points toward defective acute-phase and opsonization responses. These plasma-based findings suggest that while complement alterations reflect ongoing immune and inflammatory dysregulation, systemic proteins such as APOA1 and APCS provide more robust diagnostic utility. Integrating complement-derived markers with systemic plasma proteins may offer a more clinically translatable biomarker panel for degenerative disc disease.

Comparative proteomic analysis of DDD-plasma and DDD-tissue samples elucidated the complex interplay between systemic and local immune responses in IVDD pathogenesis (Fig. 9). While several complement proteins (C4B, CFHR1, F5) display moderate plasma–tissue concordance, others show weak or even inverse relationships (CFD, C1S, C3), suggesting compartment-specific regulation. The lack of correlation for C1q suggests a potential decoupling of systemic versus local complement activity, highlighting the complexity of using plasma proteins as direct markers of disc tissue changes. The upregulation of complement component C1q in IVDD tissue suggests a potential role for local immune modulation and tissue remodeling processes [57, 58]. The dysregulation of complement cascades in DDD may contribute to the perpetuation of inflammation, tissue damage, and altered immune responses within intervertebral discs. Prior investigations have provided promising evidence for the involvement of complement dysregulation in IVDD.

Studies have reported elevated expression levels of complement components, including C1q, C3, and membrane attack complex (MAC), in degenerated human intervertebral discs [15, 26, 59]. These findings suggest a potential role for complement-mediated inflammation and immune responses in driving IVDD pathogenesis. Moreover, animal models of disc degeneration have demonstrated increased complement activation within the disc microenvironment, further implicating complement dysregulation in disease progression [7]. Earlier studies report that intervertebral disc as an immune privileged organ as the annulus fibrosus and cartilaginous end plates are termed as Blood-NP barrier (BNB) which is rich in immunosuppressive molecular factors. During degeneration, as the BNB disrupts, the auto-immune response occurs with various downstream immunological and inflammatory cascades [60].

Our results suggest an imbalance in the immune system, reflecting an inverse relationship between complement activity in the blood and affected disc tissue. While complement activation is weakened in the bloodstream, it remains active in the damaged disc, as indicated by reduced levels of a key immune protein (C1q) in the blood and its increased presence in disc tissue. This shift in immune activity may contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue damage. Therefore, restoring balanced complement activation could help regulate immune responses and control inflammation in DDD.

Conclusions

Our exploratory study reveals comprehensive proteomic analysis of plasma samples procured from healthy and degenerated disc disease subjects revealing significant complement dysregulation in degenerative disc disease (DDD), reflected in both systemic and local environments. Elevated inflammatory markers, including CRP and ESR, confirm the inflammatory nature of DDD-plasma. In DDD-plasma, the classical pathway (C1q, C1r, C1s) was significantly downregulated, while the lectin pathway showed mild upregulation of MBL2 and MASP1. Additionally, the alternative pathway regulator CFD was markedly downregulated, whereas terminal pathway components (C5, C8A, C8B) and regulators (CFI, CFHR4, CFH) were upregulated, indicating enhanced complement activation. The inverse correlation between systemic (DDD-plasma) and DDD-tissue complement expression suggests compensatory immune modulation. Importantly, ROC curve analysis identified C1Q and C8B (complement proteins) together with APOA1 and APCS (non-complement proteins) as the most robust plasma discriminators of DDD-plasma from HV-Plasma. These findings highlight the potential of complement proteins as biomarkers for DDD progression suggesting further investigation into systemic complement-targeted therapies.

Limitation of the study

Age differences between groups may confound CRP elevation, although the exclusion of diabetes and arthritic conditions minimizes alternative inflammatory sources. In future, age-matched cohorts will be analyzed.
