A new era of genome-wide association studies in the field of Alzheimer’s disease and overlapping co-pathologies: lessons learned from a neuropathology-centered approach
Authors: Celeste Laureyssen, Dietmar Rudolf Thal, Kristel Sleegers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) and overlapping pathologies represent a growing worldwide health concern. With the first disease-modifying treatments on the rise, it becomes increasingly important to move research in this area forward. Genetic research is excellent at discovering novel contributors to disease mechanisms, which has been demonstrated by the discovery of over 75 disease loci associated with AD. However, classical large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) use cohorts of individuals that have been assigned a case or a control status based on a clinical diagnosis. For AD, this can result in bias due to the complex nature of the disease profile. More specifically, on the neuropathological level, AD is multifaceted with co-morbid pathological lesions being the norm rather than the exception. Together with the substantial preclinical phase, this can lead to the introduction of type I and type II errors. An alternative to using large-scale clinical cohorts is to shift toward studying individuals where the disease diagnosis has been neuropathologically confirmed, or cohorts where an endophenotype is used which can directly reflect ongoing pathological processes in vivo. Such endophenotypes could entail biofluid or imaging-based biomarkers, but the most undiluted signal is obtained when employing neuropathological data. These data typically represent the presence or absence of a lesion or reflects the semi-quantitative burden of pathological features. Here, we review what this shift toward more detailed phenotypes has already contributed to the field by investigating the genetic background of AD hallmark lesions as well as commonly observed co-pathologies.

Introduction

In the general population, decline in memory and cognitive functions is often regarded as a normal and unavoidable part of getting older. However, there is a clear distinction between some forgetfulness that can be attributed to the aging brain and actual cognitive decline as observed in dementia patients. Dementia is one of today’s leading critical health concerns with estimates showing that up to one-third of people aged 85 or older will clinically present with symptoms. Although there are a lot of underlying diseases which can cause dementia, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is by far the most common one [167]. Cognitive impairment due to underlying AD pathology manifests itself clinically as a variety of neurological and psychiatric symptoms, including short term memory loss, language deficits, confusion, learning difficulties, hallucinations and eventually, global cognitive impairment [54, 105]. Long-term consequences of dementia impose grave repercussions upon the daily lives of patients and their primary care providers. Not only does the disease have devastating consequences for patients and their direct environment, but it also inflicts a tremendous economic burden [185]. Although AD has first been described over a century ago [7] and accounts for 60–70% of all dementia cases, disease-modifying therapies have only recently started to emerge [152, 178].

Clinical diagnosis of AD traditionally relied on cognitive test batteries with diagnostic accuracy being enhanced through the evaluation of positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) and blood-based biomarkers [9, 77, 127]. However, recent revisions have been made to these diagnostic criteria [115]. More specifically, now the assessment of an abnormal “core 1” biomarker, such as amyloid PET, validated CSF measures or accurate plasma biomarkers, is sufficient to warrant the confirmation of an AD diagnosis during life. However, these criteria are still mainly focused on pathological features that are considered the hallmark lesions for AD. These core features comprise the extracellular buildup of long, aggregation-prone β-amyloid (Aβ) fragments and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFT) of hyperphosphorylated tau protein in the brain [7]. It is, however, rare that these are the sole lesions observed in the diseased brain. Several neuropathological subtypes of AD have been described [118] and compiling evidence illustrates that co-morbid neuropathological lesions and overlapping pathologies [81, 150], such as cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), α-synuclein, and TDP-43 inclusions and granulovacuolar degeneration bodies (GVBs) [14], are the standard rather than the exception in the aging brain [125, 147, 159, 173].

Over the past decades, it has become increasingly clear that a strong genetic component underlies the risk of developing AD. Twin studies have estimated the heritability of sporadic AD to be around 60–80% [59, 84], making genetic investigation a pivotal and quickly advancing area of research. With the advent of genome-wide association studies (GWAS), it has become possible to unravel a large range of genetic risk factors underlying complex genetic diseases [89]. In GWAS, millions of common genetic variants (minor allele frequency (MAF) > 1%) referred to as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are investigated for association with disease status and the presence or severity of a phenotypic trait [181]. SNP-based heritability estimates (h2) for AD have been estimated to be around 0.25–0.34 in clinical cohorts and 0.36–0.59 for autopsy or biomarker confirmed cohorts [12]. GWAS has so far discovered over 75 risk loci to be associated with AD diagnosis [19].

Sample size is an important player in the statistical power of GWAS to discover significant associations. Typically, large-scale GWAS relies on very large numbers of cases and controls which are selected based on clinical information [109]. However, the phenotypic heterogeneity of clinical AD and its complex underlying neuropathological profile increase the risk of introducing type I and type II errors in such large-scale studies [48, 157, 186]. Moreover, due to the neuropathological complexity and frequent presence of co-morbidities, interpretation of genetic signals discovered in clinical GWAS can be challenging. Signals can reflect co-pathologies rather than pure AD pathogenesis [85] or can represent genetic diversity coming from subgroups of misdiagnosed individuals. An alternative to ever-increasing sample sizes in clinical case–control study designs is to use smaller cohorts where phenotypic accuracy is improved [48, 108]. This can be achieved by focusing on individuals where a post-mortem neuropathological diagnosis was made. Evidently, these cohorts are much smaller in size due to the limited availability of study samples where individuals have been subjected to a full post-mortem examination, but they yield incredibly valuable information about the genetic risk of underlying pathologies. The same holds true for studies using information directly reflecting neuropathologic burdens, such as fluid or image-based biomarkers, as they offer a glance into ongoing pathological processes in the brain.

A thorough understanding of the complex nature of AD is fundamental for delineating the mechanisms that drive its pathology. Therefore, in this review, the focus is set on summarizing what is known regarding the genetic etiology of specific neuropathological lesions observed in AD. This comprises the hallmark AD lesions and extends to the frequently observed co-morbidities such as Lewy body inclusions, CAA and TDP-43 aggregates.

Genetic etiology of clinical AD

AD can be stratified into different subtypes depending on the age of onset, familial history and genetic predisposition of patients. The most abundant form is multifactorial, sporadic AD which accounts for 90% of the total number of AD cases. Sporadic AD is often referred to as late-onset AD (LOAD) since patients typically have a later onset age than cases with familial AD (60 to 65 years [17]). However, a later onset age does not exclude familial AD, and early-onset AD (EOAD) is not necessarily familial. The familial form of AD is characterized by autosomal dominant mutations in the amyloid precursor protein (APP), presenilin 1 (PSEN1) or PSEN2 genes. Familial AD cases account for less than 1% of the total number of AD cases and patients can already start displaying symptoms in their early thirties [187]. For sporadic AD, the most important genetic risk factor is the APOE ε4 allele. In a European ancestry background, one ε4 allele results in a three-fold increase in risk compared to non-carriers for AD development. When a person is homozygous for the ε4 allele, the odds ratio compared to a non-carrier is as high as twelve [180], severely increasing the risk for AD development throughout life. The APOE effect is modulated across ancestral backgrounds where in East Asian populations, effect sizes are reported to be higher as opposed to African populations where the effect on AD risk is attenuated [20, 114, 137]. Genetic association studies have uncovered a myriad of additional genetic risk factors for sporadic AD, linking various biological pathways to the disease. An important tool in genetic association studies is GWAS, where millions of variant positions throughout the genome are interrogated for association with a certain disease phenotype. For AD, protective as well as disease-promoting rare and common variants have been discovered and have significantly increased our understanding of the underlying pathological mechanisms. Most variants discovered by GWAS are situated in noncoding regions [62] and are common (MAF > 1%), often accompanied by smaller effect sizes [129].

An important observation is that associated variants discovered with genetic studies are not necessarily the causal variants in a certain locus. Due to the experimental design of GWAS, the lead variant can be in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with the actual causal variant or could be tagging a larger structural variation. An example of the latter is a 1 Mb inversion in the MAPT genomic region resulting in two major haplotypes where, due to repressed recombination, SNPs on these haplotypes can reliably distinguish between the two [39, 160]. The H1 haplotype has been associated with an increased risk for various neurodegenerative brain diseases, and this association can be detected through tagging SNPs in GWAS (such as for AD [19] and PD [120]). Another example is the pathogenic C9orf72 repeat expansion which could also be detected through a tagging SNP in a Finnish GWAS for ALS [95]. Therefore, much effort is being put in post-GWAS analysis through the use of fine-mapping strategies, gene prioritization [26] and functional validation studies [28]. Post-GWAS investigation is necessary to determine the causal variants in a risk locus and subsequently prioritize genes that might be involved in pathogenic processes. Over the last decade, GWAS has succeeded in uncovering a large number of risk loci for AD. Sequencing-based approaches have also broadened the comprehension of the genetic architecture by discovering associations with rare genetic variants, such as those observed in ABCA7, SORL1 and TREM2 [18, 41, 131, 161]. The power of sequencing approaches to detect rare variants is significantly higher than that of GWAS, since the focus of GWAS is often kept on unraveling common genetic variations due to methodological restraints. GWAS has allowed the discovery of a large additional number of risk loci implicated in different biological pathways, which has shed new light on the etiology and pathogenesis of AD. Some examples of the earliest associated loci that were discovered are BIN1, CLU, CR1 and PICALM [71, 96, 194]. Now, over 75 genetic loci have been associated with sporadic AD [19]. Pathway enrichment analysis of genes in these loci has highlighted a variety of biological processes. Associated pathways have pointed to intuitive disease mechanisms, like APP and tau processing, but also implicated novel pathways like endocytosis, lipid metabolism and innate immunity [79, 94, 183, 188]. GWAS in the context of AD has mainly been performed using clinical diagnosis as an outcome variable. Alternatively, specific endophenotypes observed in AD have proven to add significant value when used in GWAS. Investigating association with endophenotypes, such as CSF or serum biomarkers and brain imaging data has allowed the discovery of novel risk loci and substantiated various loci discovered with case–control designed studies [74, 78, 102]. In the next paragraphs, we will take a closer look at the specific neuropathological protein aggregates or lesions observed in AD and what is known regarding their genetic etiology. The risk loci and the genetic effects reported in the following paragraphs are derived from studies conducted largely on individuals from European ancestry. Neuropathological lesions and their accompanying genetic risk profile

Amyloid, neuritic plaques, and tau tangles

Amyloid plaques are formed by the accumulation of misfolded Aβ fibrils, typically adopting a β-sheet-rich structure. The Aβ peptide is cleaved from the amyloid precursor protein (APP) by β- and γ-secretases. Different isoforms are generated depending on the γ-secretase cleavage site, with the 40 amino acids long Aβ40 being the most abundant [193]. An imbalance in APP cleavage, production or clearance can result in elevated levels of the more toxic, aggregation-prone Aβ42 isoform. This isoform has elevated amyloidogenic properties and is also the most abundant protein observed in amyloid plaques [66]. Mutations in genes directly involved in the processing of APP, such as PSEN1 and PSEN2, are well known for their causal role in autosomal dominant AD [17] and contribution to amyloid plaque formation. When amyloid plaques are associated with dystrophic neurites, the plaques are referred to as neuritic plaques [163, 174]. Dystrophic neurites are generally positive for tau pathology and correlate well with cognitive deterioration [121]. Neurofibrillary tangles are observed in the neuronal cell bodies and consist of hyperphosphorylated aggregates of tau protein. Tau protein, encoded by the MAPT gene [63], has a biological function in stabilizing microtubules necessary for maintaining the neuronal cell structure [67]. In AD, both 3R and 4R tau isoforms are hyperphosphorylated, causing them to detach from the microtubules and to assemble in paired helical filaments, which then ultimately aggregate into neurofibrillary tangles [65]. Both amyloid and tau pathology can be observed independently, with the occurrence of lone amyloid plaques observed in 20–30% of the cognitively normal aging population [53, 70]. Tau pathology in absence of amyloid plaques is observed more frequently and is often a consequence of a battery of underlying diseases referred to as tauopathies [148]. A wide range of primary tauopathies can be observed, with some examples being progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal degeneration (CBD), Pick’s disease (PiD) and primarily age-related tauopathy (PART) [36, 92]. The presence of these lesions in the brain can be challenging to study in vivo. However, ongoing pathology in the brain is typically well reflected by fluid biomarkers and can, to a certain extent, be visualized using imaging-based approaches.

Protein levels of Aβ42 are typically reduced in the CSF and blood plasma of AD cases [116, 158], and the Aβ42 level as well as the ratio of Aβ42/40 [44] is commonly used as biomarkers to assist in the formulation of an AD diagnosis [77]. Numerous studies have used amyloid biomarker data as an endophenotype in GWAS to detect novel genes that could be involved in the molecular pathways underlying amyloid plaque formation. The large majority of these studies report APOE as a significant modifier of Aβ42 in CSF and PET biomarker studies [6, 38, 43, 88, 139, 166], but additional loci have been implicated as well. Some of these loci are overlapping with known AD risk genes discovered through clinical GWAS, such as CR1 [6, 80, 87, 135], ABCA7 [6, 135], FERMT2 [6, 87, 135], CLU [135], BIN1 [135], and SORL1 [87]. However, novel loci and subsequent genes have been identified as well, such as SERPINB1 [42, 43], IL1RAP [138], GLIS1 [43], RBFOX1 [136], GADL1 [135], SUCLG2 [140], BCHE [139], ZFHX3 [74], and EPC2 [88]. For tau pathology, frequently used CSF biomarkers include total tau (tTau) and phosphorylated tau (pTau) [77]. Additionally, PET imaging using 18F-flortaucipir can be used as an in vivo marker for the assessment of tau pathology [30]. Several GWAS have been performed using tau biomarker levels as an endophenotype to reflect underlying pathology, with the most common genetic driver identified here also being APOE [38, 43, 88, 166]. Known AD risk factor BIN1 was replicated in a CSF pTau181 biomarker GWAS [80], with several studies discovering additional genes associated with tau endophenotypes such as GMNC [38, 42, 43, 74, 80, 166], GLIS3 [38, 43], CYP1B1 [124], C16orf96 [80], NCR2 [38], EPC2 [88], CTDP1 [43], PDCH8 [43], and DNAAF [166].

Although biomarkers are incredibly valuable in offering diagnostic support and generally reflect ongoing brain pathology well, they remain a proxy for the actual ongoing processes and only offer information regarding hallmark AD lesions, without regarding co-pathologies [75]. Moreover, for fluid-based biomarkers, there is still the possibility that their levels are influenced by unrelated mechanisms, complicating the interpretation and translatability of using them as outcome measures in genomic studies [45]. Therefore, the use of directly observed brain pathology upon postmortem investigation offers an interesting outcome measure to reduce phenotypic heterogeneity.

Subsequently, an increasing number of studies have used neuropathological measures as outcomes for discovering genetic factors associated with an increased or decreased risk of developing AD-related lesions. These include hypothesis-driven studies, in which a series of genes implicated in disease risk by clinical studies are tested for their association with neuropathological features. Furthermore, hypothesis-generating GWAS approaches have been used as well (Table 1). An early study investigated the impact of known AD risk genes from clinical case–control GWAS on neuritic plaque burden in the brain, reflecting both tau and amyloid pathology [32]. This study used data generated on the Religious Orders Study (ROS) and Rush Memory and Aging Project (MAP) cohort. ROSMAP prospectively follows individuals without dementia at baseline, includes longitudinal clinical evaluations and participants brain donation at death [22]. Here, a subset was used to investigate commonly known AD risk variants in CLU, PICALM, and CR1 and the authors established an association between an intronic variant in CR1 and an increased quantity of neuritic plaques [32]. With increasing success of GWAS for clinical AD, the number of candidate variants for AD has grown substantially [19, 97]. More recent work investigated if there was an association between 83 AD risk variants implicated in the largest clinical AD case–control GWAS to date [19] and neuropathological phenotypes in a cohort of over 4000 individuals from ROSMAP and ADRC [85]. For AD hallmark lesions, they investigated NFT and neuritic plaque presence or absence. For NFTs, the Braak stage was investigated and there was a significant risk-increasing association between GWAS lead variants mapping to BIN1, CR1 and COX7 and a risk-decreasing effect for MME. A suggestive risk-decreasing signal was observed for the EED/PICALM and INPP5D variants while a risk-increasing suggestive signal was detected for SPI1. For neuritic plaques, the authors observed a risk-increasing effect for GWAS lead variants mapping to BIN1, APH1B, and FERMT2 and a risk-decreasing effect for MME, EED/PICALM and RBCK1. Additionally, a suggestive risk-increasing association was observed between neuritic plaque pathology and the PTK2B GWAS lead variant [85]. A study using a similar approach investigating known AD risk variants in a smaller, novel European cohort discovered a significant association between APOE and a semi-quantitative score for Aβ plaques, Braak staging for NFTs and a score for neuritic plaques [100]. Additionally, this study also reported significant associations between APH1B and a neuritic plaque score and the Braak NFT stage [100]. Nominally significant risk-increasing association signals were observed between the PTK2B GWAS lead variant and the score for neuritic plaques as well as the NFT stage. This study also investigated genetic risk associated with a semi-quantitative score for Aβ plaque pathology and reported a significant risk-increasing effect for APOE. A nominal risk-decreasing signal was observed for the EED lead variant and a risk-increasing signal was reported for the CR1 lead variant [100]. Shulman and colleagues [154] performed the first neuropathological GWAS on the ROSMAP cohort, using the same quantitative measure for neuritic plaques as used by Chibnik and colleagues [32]. They reported a genome-wide significant signal for APOE and nominal signals for other known AD risk variants near CR1, ABCA7 and CD2AP associated with increased neuritic plaque load, but these did not pass the genome-wide suggestive threshold [154].

A GWAS performed on a larger set of individuals from the ROSMAP cohort further corroborated the genome-wide significant association between NFT pathology and APOE and additionally discovered a significant signal from an intronic region of PTPRD, which was replicated in an independent cohort [33]. Later, the Alzheimer’s Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC) performed a larger-scale GWAS of multiple neuropathological phenotypes, including Braak stage for NFT and presence or absence of neuritic plaques on a large cohort of almost 5000 individuals [16]. The main findings here were genome-wide significant associations for APOE with all investigated lesions. Additional significant signals were reported as protective for neuritic plaque development for variants mapping to GALNT7, ABCG1 and IER5I. Moreover, this study investigated 22 known AD risk variants proposed by earlier work [97] and discovered several interesting signals. Among these findings were the earlier reported [154] risk-increasing effect of variants mapping to ABCA7 and CD2AP on neuritic plaque pathology, together with a reported protective effect for those mapping to MS4A4 and CD3392. Additionally, overlapping nominal association signals were reported for NFTs and neuritic plaques with a risk-increasing effect for BIN1 and risk-decreasing effects for CASS4, MEF2C and PICALM. Unique nominal association signals were observed as protective for NFTs for variants mapped to CLU, SORL1, and ZCWPW1. The authors concluded that there was a significant overlap between the molecular drivers underlying the formation of individual lesions (neuritic plaques and NFTs) and the drivers for developing AD [16].

There is also evidence for a sex-specific risk for the development of hallmark AD lesions. Dumitrescu and colleagues performed a sex-stratified analysis for the presence or absence of NFTs and neuritic plaques. This was done on a cohort which was largely overlapping with the earlier reported GWAS performed on the ADGC cohort [16], but with an increased sample size. The authors reported significant associations for APOE with both phenotypes, for males and females [46]. Moreover, a significant signal was observed specifically in males for rs34331204, where the minor allele was protective for the presence of NFTs. The lead SNP was located in the proximity of BZW2 but was reported as an expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex tissue for TSPAN13 [46, 76]. While these results are suggestive for a sex-specific, protective role of TSPAN13 expression for NFT formation, (sex-) stratified analysis may lead to spurious results attributed to a reduced statistical power, thus indicating that this finding still requires independent replication. Moreover, a large GWAS investigating 11 AD-related neuropathological phenotypes was conducted in a dataset including over 7000 autopsied participants from ROSMAP, NACC, and ACT cohorts [153]. Of note, this study population included many individuals that were overlapping with earlier work by Beecham and colleagues [16]. Three phenotypes investigated here were reflective of hallmark AD pathological lesions: a score representing the severity of amyloid pathology (A score), Braak staging for NFT pathology and a score reflecting the burden of neuritic plaques (CERAD score). APOE was significantly associated with all phenotypes, with a risk-increasing effect. An additional genome-wide significant signal was observed for BIN1, where the effect allele was associated with higher Braak NFT stages. Subsequently, AD risk loci [19] were interrogated which resulted in the discovery of various significant associations. Higher Braak NFT stages were associated with CELF/SPI1 and TMEM106B SNPs while lower Braak stages were associated with MME and HLA variants. For neuritic plaques, a higher CERAD score was associated with AD risk SNPs mapping to ABCA7, PTK2B, CR1, SNX1, FERMT2, and CELF/SPI1 and a lower CERAD score was associated with PICALM, INPP5D, ZCWPW1, and SORL1 loci. Earlier reported risk genes [16] for neuritic plaques were not replicated here. This could, in part, be attributed to differences in phenotype assessment and additional heterogeneity that might be introduced when compiling large, multi-center study cohorts.

Farrell and colleagues conducted a GWAS in a unique cohort of neuropathologically confirmed PART cases compiled from multiple study centers [50]. PART encompasses the presence of tau lesions indistinguishable from those observed in AD, in the absence of amyloid plaque pathology with limited cognitive consequences [144]. Farrell et al. reported one genome-wide significant locus on chromosome 4 with rs56405341 as the lead variant. At this position, the effect allele was associated with increased Braak NFT stages. The nearest protein-coding gene to the variant was C4orf33, but two other genes, JADE1 and SCLT1, were also situated within the region. A locus 150 kb downstream of the lead variant was nominally associated with Braak stage in earlier work performed by Beecham et al., 2014 [16] and also nominally associated with decreased risk of NFT pathology in males according to Dumitrescu et al. 2019 [46]. These SNPs, however, represent independent signals as they were not in LD and suggest a complex involvement of this locus with tangle pathology. Farrell and colleagues also performed functional validation experiments further emphasizing JADE1 as the plausible risk gene associated with tau pathology [50]. Subsequently, they investigated associations for known AD risk loci and found a protective effect for SLC24A4 and MS4A6A and a risk-increasing effect for HS3TS1. This study illustrated the importance of investigating novel neuropathologically confirmed sets of individuals for novel risk gene discovery compared to works which have consistently included largely overlapping sets of individuals. An important note to make here is that this cohort is fundamentally different from the previously described cohorts in terms of inclusion criteria. Only individuals with confirmed PART, and thus individuals that are positive for tau pathology, were included here.

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA)

CAA, observed in up to 90% of AD cases alongside hallmark lesions, is a cerebrovascular disorder in which Aβ fragments accumulate inside leptomeningeal and cortical blood vessel walls [182]. These aggregates lead to weakening of the blood vessel walls and impaired blood flow which ultimately contributes to neuronal loss [35], and can result in microbleeds or intracranial hemorrhages. Two distinct types of CAA pathology are described. The main distinction between both is the additional accumulation of Aβ in the capillaries in CAA Type I, whereas pathology is restricted to large- and mid-sized blood vessels in Type II [171]. Type I is further characterized by widespread pathology in the neocortex and hippocampal areas [170]. Specific mutations in APP, such as APP Dutch-type (p.E693Q), are causative for familial CAA. The APOE ε4 allele has been linked specifically to CAA Type I, with its frequency being up to four times higher in Type I compared to Type II cases [171]. Additionally, the ε2 allele is observed more frequently in CAA Type II patients [171]. The strong connection between CAA and APOE has been well documented [16, 100, 141, 142, 153]. Mechanistically, APOE mutations are thought to impair Aβ clearing mechanisms, adding to Aβ buildup in the blood vessel walls [10, 11, 49]. Additional risk genes for clinical CAA besides APOE have emerged as well. A clinical GWAS followed by a hypothesis-based approach for validation in a neuropathological cohort linked known AD risk gene CR1 to an increased burden of CAA pathology [24]. Another hypothesis-based study found an association between the previously identified AD risk gene UNC5C and increased CAA burden using a larger neuropathological cohort, with evidence for a synergistic interaction with APOE ε4, resulting in more severe CAA pathology among ε4 carriers [190].

A more recent GWAS conducted in a cohort of over 800 individuals discovered a novel locus referred to as LINC-PINT [142]. This locus was associated with decreased CAA burden among APOE ε4 negative individuals. LINC-PINT is a long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) which was detected to be upregulated in a hypothesis-free lncRNA screen in patients with a neurodegenerative brain disease (NBD). Moreover, in vitro work suggests a neuroprotective role [155]. Finally, a large GWAS focused on neuropathological phenotypes in a postmortem cohort of over 7000 individuals discovered an association between CAA severity and a protective polymorphism in APOC2, which is located within the larger APOE region, but was independently associated with CAA [153]. Further work reported that the GWAS lead variant, rs7247551, was colocalized with an eQTL which was associated with a reduced APOE expression in brain microglia, suggesting APOE rather than APOC2 as the actual molecular driver of the effect [21]. Of note, hypothesis-based associations, such as those observed for CR1 [24] and UNC5C [190], were not replicated in neuropathological CAA GWAS studies. It is important to consider that there are substantial differences between these studies. Reddy and colleagues [142] used a cohort including only AD individuals, creating a different cohort composition compared to other studies where a variety of NBD patients and healthy aged individuals were included [24, 153, 190]. Additional discrepancies can be attributed to differences in phenotype definitions and potential dilution of certain signals when merging data in larger multicenter studies. TDP-43 pathology

TAR DNA-binding protein (TDP-43) has mainly been studied for its pathogenic role in ALS and FTLD-TDP [107]. Although not considered as a hallmark lesion of AD, TDP-43 inclusions in neuronal cell bodies can be observed as a co-morbidity in up to 57% of AD patients [8, 72, 113]. More recently, the occurrence of TDP-43 inclusions in the brain of AD and non-AD individuals has been formulated as a distinct TDP-43 proteinopathy, referred to as LATE (limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy [122]). LATE neuropathological change (LATE-NC) is commonly observed and often accompanied by mild cognitive impairment, hippocampal sclerosis, and other co-morbid pathologies. LATE-NC is distinct from FTLD-TDP based on the onset age and the distribution of TDP-43 pathology throughout the brain. LATE is generally observed in older patients compared to FTLD and the distribution of TDP-43 pathology throughout the brain is more restricted [122]. TDP-43, encoded by the TARDBP gene, is ubiquitously expressed and is physiologically involved in RNA regulation. Under pathological conditions, post-translational modifications to the protein can cause the translocation from its physiological cellular location, the nucleus, to the cytoplasm [55]. Translocated proteins can then aggregate in the cytoplasm and eventually form pathological inclusions which can interfere with cellular function and promote toxicity [165].

Insights regarding the genetic drivers of TDP-43 pathology mainly come from the fields of ALS and FTLD-TDP research. FTLD-TDP is the most common neuropathological subtype of FTLD, a heterogeneous neurodegenerative dementia with a stronger familial component than AD with 20–25% of FTD cases suspected to be familial [64]. Several genes have been found to harbor mutations that are causal for FTLD-TDP. These include C9orf72, which is also a well-known ALS gene [117], and the progranulin-coding gene GRN [13], both mostly linked to FTLD-TDP subtype A, as well as TBK1 which is linked to FTLD-TDP subtype B [61], and VCP [40] which is associated with FTLD-TDP subtype D. An early GWAS performed on neuropathologically confirmed FTLD-TDP patients proposed TMEM106B as an important genetic risk factor [177]. TMEM106B had not been implicated in previous FTLD association studies that did not consider neuropathological subtypes of FTLD, indicating the added value of a study design where phenotypic heterogeneity is minimized. Subsequent studies revealed that TMEM106B is a modifier of disease risk in carriers of GRN and C9orf72 mutations [37, 52, 57, 99, 176, 177]. Pottier and colleagues performed a GWAS in clinical and pathological samples of GRN mutation carriers and replicated the previously reported association with TMEM106B. This study additionally discovered a genome-wide significant signal in GFRA2 and showed a potential physical interaction of the latter to progranulin, suggesting its importance in GRN mutation carriers [134].

A larger GWAS conducted on individuals with a neuropathologically confirmed FTLD-TDP diagnosis and controls discovered DPP6 as a novel, risk-increasing gene [133]. DPP6 interacts with, and affects the affinity of, the potassium channel Kv4.2. Rare variants in DPP6 have also been implicated in FTLD and familial AD [27]. The FTLD-TDP neuropathology GWAS also detected a risk-increasing effect for UNC13A and HLA-DQA2. The association at UNC13A overlapped with a known association signal for ALS [179]. UNC13A plays a key role in synaptic transmission and neurotransmitter release [82]. Interestingly, nuclear loss of TDP-43 results in cryptic exon inclusion in the UNC13A transcript, which leads to reduced UNC13A expression due to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay [106]. Genetic variation in UNC13A increases this alternative splicing event. While the HLA-DQA locus had previously been reported in a clinical FTLD GWAS [51], the FTLD-TDP signal reported here is independent. The HLA locus plays a key role in the immune system and is a highly polymorphic genomic region with more than 140 coding genes [93]. Furthermore, multiple HLA genes have been implicated in sporadic AD [29, 101, 192].

A larger study, including neuropathologically, confirmed FTLD-TDP cases and control individuals enabled investigation of common genetic risk factors underlying specific FTLD-TDP pathological subtypes [132]. When using the full neuropathological dataset, this study replicated the risk-increasing signal at the UNC13A locus. Moreover, a meta-analysis with a clinical FTLD-TDP cohort substantiated the UNC13A locus with a lead variant that was in LD with that from the neuropathological GWAS and, additionally, discovered a genome-wide significant signal for TNIP1. For FTLD-TDP Type A, there was a significant risk-increasing signal for GRN and an additional risk-increasing signal for three novel genes: TINAG, MZT1, and FARP2. These genes are all enriched in lysosomal functioning pathways [132]. For FTLD-TDP Type B, association at the UNC13A locus was observed. Moreover, three novel loci were reported: TNIP1, RCL1, and PDS5B with the TNIP1 locus being risk-decreasing and the other two loci being risk-increasing. Interestingly, TNIP1 was also reported as a hit in the meta-analysis with the clinical FTLD-TDP cohort and has been described as a risk gene for ALS [23] and AD [19], with the signal identified here overlapping with the AD signal, supporting the idea that, to some extent, these disorders share some underlying pathogenic mechanisms [58]. TNIP1 has a known function in autoimmunity and is a negative regulator of inflammation through inhibition of nuclear factor kappa-β [69, 111], which could explain its general involvement across AD and TDP-43-proteinopathies [128, 132, 143]. For Type C, in this study, a risk-increasing effect for C19orf52 (or TIMM29), which is involved in mitochondrial membrane and notch signaling processes, was discovered. Because many neuropathological FTLD-TDP cases were initially classified as Type C based on historical semantic variant primary progressive aphasia (svPPA) diagnoses, in the absence of neuropathological examination of the actual subtype, the authors performed an additional GWAS restricted to cases where the Type C subtype assignment was neuropathologically confirmed [132]. For this GWAS, the C9orf72 locus was not detected, but four other risk-increasing loci were observed: LRP1B, TMEM135, COL22A1, and TRPC4, again emphasizing the added value of using highly specific, neuropathologically established phenotypes [132].

The genetic risk relating to TDP-43 pathology in AD patients is far less documented. Several studies discovered an association between APOE ε4, the strongest genetic risk factor for AD, and TDP-43 in the context of LATE, revealing a positive correlation between the number of APOE ε4 alleles and the burden of TDP-43 pathology in elderly people [100, 146, 149, 184, 191], but the exact underlying mechanism requires further investigation. A hypothesis-driven approach investigating known AD risk variants from clinical case–control GWAS [19] for association with TDP-43 presence or absence confirmed the association between TDP-43, in the context of LATE, and TMEM106B [85] and reported a suggestive risk-increasing signal for the GRN lead variant [85]. Interestingly, this study reported no association of these variants with AD neuropathological change, implicating them specifically in TDP-related pathological processes. Additional suggestive risk-increasing signals were reported for SORL1 and TPCN1 [85]. A study with a similar setup investigated the association of the same AD risk variants in a novel European cohort and corroborated the relation between TDP-43 and TPCN1 by also reporting a nominally significant association with higher LATE-NC stages [100]. Moreover, this study reported suggestive risk-increasing signals for MAF and risk-decreasing nominally significant signals for ANK3 and SLC2A4RG across multiple TDP-43 phenotypes [100]. Using a genome-wide approach investigating LATE-NC staging in a neuropathological cohort, significant associations were confirmed for APOE, and suggestive associations were observed for TMEM106 and GRN. TMEM106B was reported to be associated with lower LATE-NC stages, whereas GRN and APOE were associated with higher stages [153]. These studies further illustrate substantial overlaps in genetic risk profiles between AD and TDP-43 proteinopathies and emphasize the involvement of potentially shared underlying mechanisms. TMEM106B is a lysosomal transmembrane protein, and recent work has shown that its C-terminal domain can form amyloid fibrils [130]. Together with genetic evidence, this places TMEM106B at the intersection of lysosomal dysfunction, protein aggregation, and neurodegeneration across diseases. Additionally, GRN encodes a lysosomal growth factor (progranulin) with mutations resulting in lysosomal dysfunction [83]. Therefore, both GRN and TMEM106B implicate genetic perturbation of lysosomal mechanisms as a shared mechanism underlying disease pathogenesis. Lewy bodies

Under physiological conditions, α-synuclein is predominantly a presynaptic neuronal protein which has a function in processes, such as synaptic vesicle trafficking [110] and neuronal plasticity [34]. Under pathological conditions, it tends to misfold and accumulate in the neuronal cytoplasm as the major component of Lewy bodies [34]. Lewy bodies are hallmark lesions of Parkinson’s disease (PD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), but they can be observed in over 60% of AD cases as a co-morbidity, where they are linked to an aggravated and accelerated disease process [123]. α-Synuclein is encoded by SNCA and mutations in this gene have been linked to familial forms of PD [68]. Moreover, this gene has also been implicated in the sporadic form of PD through GWAS [47, 86] and has additionally been linked to AD and DLB, together with LRRK2 [103]. Other known risk loci discovered from clinical GWAS and familial studies focused on Lewy Body dementias are BIN1, TMEM175, APOE, GBA, and VPS13C [31, 98]. Interestingly, both APOE and BIN1 are also AD risk genes. Studies using neuropathological cohorts in which the presence or absence or a semi-quantitative score of Lewy body pathology was assessed, confirmed links to APOE [16, 153] on the genome-wide level. A recent GWAS conducted by Valentino and colleagues investigated the relation between AD hallmark phenotypes and LBD features in a cohort of neuropathologically confirmed LBD cases [175]. The authors observed genome-wide significant associations for APOE variants with greater severity of Braak NFT score, amyloid score and LBD subtype. These findings were observed in discovery and replication cohorts. When investigating a subgroup of individuals with lower levels of AD pathology, they observed a strong association between APOE and LBD subtypes which persisted when correcting for hallmark AD pathologies, which is highly suggestive for a connection between APOE and α-synuclein pathology beyond overlapping AD pathology [175]. Moreover, when investigating the impact of known AD risk variants on different neuropathological features, Katsumata and colleagues noted a suggestive association between BIN1 and USP6NL and increased risk of Lewy body presence [85]. Both suggestive signals were replicated in an independent study as described by Laureyssen et al. [100], where a nominal suggestive association with UMAD1 was highlighted as well. Contradicting evidence for a direct link between BIN1 and Lewy body pathology was proposed by Talyansky and colleagues [168] who performed a stratified neuropathology GWAS comparing four groups: AD individuals with and without Lewy bodies and non-AD individuals with and without Lewy bodies. The authors reported significant associations for APOE and BIN1, associated with increased risk of AD in Lewy body-positive as well as Lewy body-negative individuals. However, when investigating the risk of Lewy body development in AD and AD-negative individuals, these associations were not found. However, a significant signal was detected in BLMH associated with an increased risk of Lewy body presence in AD-negative individuals, together with a risk-increasing signal downstream of ZNF365 [168]. BLMH has a role in regulating chemokine release, whereas ZNF365 is involved in DNA damage repair. These results highlight APOE and BIN1 as AD risk genes, but suggest no mechanistic connection with Lewy body pathology [168]. GWAS conducted on neuropathologically confirmed PD cases discovered a protective genome-wide significant signal intronic in TCEANC2, which is situated in the broader PARK10 locus [15]. However, larger clinical studies do not replicate the association of the PARK10 locus with the disease phenotype [156]. The authors also noted nominal association for known PD risk loci SNCA, MAPT, and GAK. Lastly, a neuropathology GWAS in LBD cases by Valentino and colleagues identified an intronic variant in MCTP2, which was associated with lower Lewy body counts in the middle frontal brain regions, an intronic variant in TLE3 with lower Lewy body counts in the cingulate cortex and finally an intergenic variant located in the GRIN2A/ATF7IP2 locus with lower Lewy body counts in the para-hippocampal region [175], suggesting that the integration of spatial information regarding neuropathology can be valuable in genomic discovery studies. Granulovacuolar degeneration

Beyond co-morbid protein aggregates that are observed as hallmark lesions in other NBDs, there are certain lesions which are present across the NBD spectrum and of which little is known regarding their etiology. Especially on a genetic level, these lesions are largely unexplored, but their ubiquitous presence in AD and co-morbid pathologies makes them increasingly interesting targets for investigation. Granulovacuolar degeneration (GVD) is an example of such underexplored lesions. GVD presents itself in the brain as large, intraneuronal vacuoles enclosed by a double membrane layer, with a dense protein core [119]. GVD bodies are enriched in the brain of patients suffering from AD and overlapping pathologies and are especially numerous in diseases where tau pathology is a core neuropathological feature, such as AD [90, 126, 151, 162]. GVD presence is strongly linked to tangle pathology as well as endocytosis [189], autophagy [56], apoptosis [164], and necroptosis [91]. It could be hypothesized that due to the strong link with these processes, respective risk genes might be overlapping, but this has not been shown in the current work. There is, however, an increased presence of GVD lesions in C9orf72 repeat expansion mutation carriers, suggesting mutations in this gene might contribute to GVD development [145], which is substantiated by the presence of TDP-43 fragments in GVD lesions [73]. A hypothesis-based study investigating the association between the neuropathological phenotypes and the 85 lead variants from the largest clinical AD GWAS [19] described a significant association between the GVD stage [169] and APOE where the APOE ε4 allele was associated with higher GVD stages [100], but graphical network modeling suggested that this effect might be indirect and could be modulated through the association with Aβ pathology. A nominally significant risk-increasing association was also observed for APH1B, which was also reported as a likely indirect effect through the strong connection between GVD and NFT pathology [100].

Concluding remarks and future directions

Compiling evidence has highlighted the relevance of using neuropathological cohorts in genetic investigations. Neuropathological data can be used as a refining strategy after conducting a large clinical GWAS to pinpoint which genetic signals are overlapping with certain pathological features. Such fine-tuning of earlier discovered association signals is interesting since larger studies using clinical phenotypes often contain substantial phenotypic heterogeneity. Furthermore, neuropathological cohorts, although generally much smaller in sample size compared to clinical cohorts, can be used as independent discovery datasets. The reduction of phenotypic heterogeneity creates an increase in statistical power to detect novel associations that might have been missed in a large-scale clinical study due to a diluted phenotype. Another benefit of using neuropathological cohorts is the possibility to detect genomic variants that are not only associated with altered odds of receiving a disease diagnosis, but variants that can be linked to an increased or decreased burden of certain neuropathological features. Additional values can be found in investigating neuropathological hallmarks or co-morbid lesions across different disease diagnoses. By not restricting an investigation to a diagnosis, the discovery of overlapping genetic risk profiles is facilitated. This is valuable since neuropathological co-pathologies are frequently observed across neurodegenerative diseases and share some genetic overlap although the extent varies considerably between disease pairs and may be restricted to certain shared loci or underlying biological pathways [58, 104, 132].

Another opportunity that is provided by combining genetic and neuropathological parameters is network analysis. By using techniques such as the graphical LASSO [112] or structural equation modeling, where conditional dependencies between different variables can be modeled, we could already show that these types of analyses lead to a better position of APOE as modulating factor for AD. APOE had strong edges with amyloid pathology as represented by Aβ phases and by the presence/absence of CAA, whereas its impact on tau pathology is mainly indirect through Aβ and CAA [60, 100]. These types of network analysis appear to be quite powerful for understanding pathomechanisms and impact of co-pathologies as well as their modulation by genetic risk factors.

There are however some important caveats to consider when using neuropathological cohorts for genomic discovery or validation studies. Due to the limited availability of donated brain tissue, individual cohorts are often small. Therefore, many of the studies described here are performed on individuals compiled from multiple research centers. Especially for neuropathological data, this can impose difficulties. Neuropathological data collection is highly susceptible to variation. Although automated or semi-automated pipelines for image analysis are increasingly being used in some study centers, traditional neuropathological scoring and staging are performed by pathologists and rely either on semi-quantitative assessment, counts, or visual observations in predefined brain regions. As a result, these measures may be the subject of inter-observer variability across raters, centers, and cohorts. Additionally, differences in brain autopsy and staining protocols can vary between research centers and have an influence on the resulting outcome measures. Neuropathological data acquired over multiple centers often need to be harmonized, complicating the interpretation of downstream findings. Harmonization of neuropathological assessment at different brain banks is an important step and had been initiated by the BrainNetEurope [2–4]. Here, interlaboratory comparison was performed for staining techniques for amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles pointing to the use of immunohistochemistry for tau and Aβ with distinct antibodies (4G8 for Aβ and AT8 for abnormal phosphorylated tau) as the most robust staining methods among the participating labs [1–5]. Likewise, interrater analysis of stained sections was performed and resulted in the superiority of dichotomous diagnostic approaches in different brain regions (e.g., Braak stages for neurofibrillary tangle pathology [1, 25], phases of Aβ deposition in the brain according to Thal et al. [5, 172] Accordingly, it is tempting to speculate that AI tools will also be most accurate when only a dichotomous distinction is needed as results will be less skewed by variability in staining intensities (appears also when using autostainers). Currently, the ISTAART professional interest area “The eye as a biomarker for AD” has a working group that aims for harmonization of amyloid plaque pathology reporting including retinal Aβ deposits.

Subsequently, the differences in the generation of genetic data and subsequent quality control measures between research facilities could also introduce bias and hamper replication or harmonization efforts. Furthermore, due to the limited cohort availability, many results reported here have been generated on cohorts that include largely overlapping sets of individuals. This signifies the need for the generation of novel cohorts where data acquisition is standardized on the neuropathological and the genetic level.

Identifying risk genes associated with specific neuropathological features will also be useful in terms of translatability to a clinical setting. These genes might comprise new therapeutic targets that will facilitate the treatment of the full neuropathological spectrum of a disease rather than just targeting one feature. Additionally, uncovering genetic risk underlying the presence or absence of certain co-morbidities can help with patient stratification, which is especially important in clinical trials and could also be valuable in predicting the disease trajectory. There is, however, an important consideration to make here. The genes that are reported after a GWAS or hypothesis-based genetic association study are often the nearest protein-coding genes to the significant lead variants. Due to underlying LD structures, the lead variants are not necessarily the actual causal variants underlying the signal. Additionally, the nearest protein-coding gene is not necessarily the gene that is affected by the signal. Most GWAS hits are actually in noncoding regions, which implicates that variants here could be in regulatory regions or be quantitative trait loci (QTLs) for more distant genes. To address these issues, gene prioritization and subsequent functional validation studies are necessary to pinpoint the causal genes underlying the signal before interpretations regarding functional relevance can be made. In short, bigger is not always better. Ever-increasing sample sizes in clinically diagnosed cohorts are not the only way forward in GWAS, and genetic research in general. Deeply characterized cohorts of post-mortem individuals are increasingly being recognized for their added value. This is demonstrated by the new insights that have already been gained and will only be expanded upon in future.
