Systematic Identification and Functional Characterisation of Colletotrichum fructicola Effectors During Camellia oleifera Colonisation
Authors: Lingxue Cao, Wentong Dong, Kaile Sun, Xizhe Sun, Chenxue Song, Lisong Ma, Geng Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Camellia oleifera is an important woody oil crop in southern China, but its production is severely threatened by anthracnose caused by Colletotrichum fructicola. C. fructicola deploys secreted effector proteins to establish infection. However, systematic identification and functional characterisation of C. fructicola effector genes upregulated during infection remains largely unexplored. Here, we integrated genome‐wide secretome prediction with RNA‐seq data from C. oleifera leaves inoculated with C. fructicola to identify candidate effectors induced during infection, followed by functional screening, targeted gene deletion, complementation and pathogenicity assays. Five novel effectors required for C. fructicola full virulence were identified, all of which suppressed Bax‐induced cell death in Nicotiana benthamiana. Targeted deletion of the corresponding genes in C. fructicola reduced lesion areas by 47%–78% on C. oleifera leaves and by up to 67% on apple fruits, whereas complementation restored their virulence to wild‐type levels. Transcriptomic profiling of infected postharvest C. oleifera fruits identified differentially expressed genes enriched in GO terms related to copper ion response, as well as in KEGG pathways associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, and plant–pathogen interactions. In addition, superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and peroxidase (POD) activities, as well as malondialdehyde content, were altered in C. oleifera leaves inoculated with the effector‐deletion mutants compared with those infected with the wild‐type strain, suggesting that these effectors may contribute to the modulation of host oxidative stress responses. Taken together, our findings provide genetic and physiological evidence that these effectors contribute to C. fructicola virulence, offering potential targets for anthracnose control.Keywords: anthracnose, Camellia oleifera, Colletotrichum fructicola, effector, pathogenicity

Introduction

Oil‐tea Camellia, a woody oil crop native to China, has been cultivated for more than 2000 years (Chen et al. 2023). The edible oil extracted from its seeds is rich in unsaturated fatty acids and secondary metabolites such as saponins and vitamins, and is also valued for its potential medicinal properties (Cheng et al. 2015; Voronina et al. 2019; Xiao et al. 2017). Owing to its deep taproot system and adaptability to diverse ecological niches, oil‐tea Camellia is widely cultivated in the mountainous regions of southern China, including drought‐prone and nutrient‐poor areas. However, the continued expansion and productivity of oil‐tea Camellia are severely constrained by anthracnose.

Anthracnose is one of the most destructive diseases of oil‐tea Camellia and occurs in all major cultivation regions (Chen et al. 2023). The disease typically causes annual yield losses of 10%–30%, with losses exceeding 50% during severe outbreaks (Li et al. 2021). Several Colletotrichum species have been identified as causal agents of anthracnose in oil‐tea Camellia, including Colletotrichum fructicola, C. siamense , C. gloeosporioides, C. camelliae and C. karstii (Li et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2021). Among these, C. fructicola is recognised as a predominant causal agent because of its wide distribution and strong pathogenicity (Yang et al. 2024). As a hemibiotrophic pathogen, C. fructicola infects a broad spectrum of crops, vegetables and fruits by employing secreted effectors that promote host colonisation (Guo et al. 2022). However, the functional characteristics of these effectors, especially their roles in oil‐tea Camellia–C. fructicola interactions and disease development, remain largely uncharacterised.

Pathogen effectors include proteinaceous and non‐proteinaceous molecules, such as small RNAs, that are delivered to the host apoplast or intercellular spaces or compartments, where they can interfere with host immunity or metabolism (Sun et al. 2023; Zhang, Li, et al. 2022). Effectors primarily fulfil their roles via 10 distinct modes of action: damage, inhibition, hijacking, promotion, subversion, mimicry, reprogramming, evasion, decoying and adaption (Ai et al. 2025; Deng et al. 2022; Shang et al. 2025). For example, the stripe rust effector Pst9653 perturbs the subcellular localisation of the wheat catalase TaCAT3, thereby promoting susceptibility‐associated reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging in wheat (Wei et al. 2025). The Phytophthora parasitica RXLR effector PpE18 inhibits an ascorbate peroxidase activity to suppress plant immunity (Cao, Qiang, et al. 2024). Two C. fructicola effectors CfEC12 and CfEC28 directly target apple signalling components and modulate metabolic flux in chloroplasts, thereby disrupting the salicylic acid signalling and shikimate pathway to suppress host immunity (Shang, Liang, et al. 2024; Shang, Liu, et al. 2024). Nevertheless, genome‐wide effector identification and functional characterisation in Colletotrichum species infecting Camellia oleifera remain limited. Therefore, the mechanisms underlying C. fructicola effector‐mediated virulence, likely involving the regulation of plant physiology, antioxidant systems and defence pathways, remain poorly understood.

In this study, we conducted the genome‐wide identification of C. fructicola candidate effectors secreted through both classical and non‐classical pathways and prioritised infection‐induced candidates based on transcriptome data from infected C. oleifera leaves. In addition, the roles of five cell death‐suppressing candidate effectors during C. fructicola infection were functionally characterised, and their underlying molecular mechanisms in virulence were investigated.

Results

Germination of C. fructicola Conidia and Infection Progression on C. oleifera Leaves

To determine the optimal sampling time points for RNA sequencing, the developmental progression of C. fructicola was examined both in vitro on cellophane and in planta on C. oleifera leaves. On the cellophane surface, conidial germination was observed as early as 3 h post‐inoculation (hpi), with approximately 70% of C. fructicola conidia producing short germ tubes (Figure 1a,b). At 6 hpi, the germ tubes had elongated, and some had begun to differentiate into swollen primary appressoria (Figure 1a,b). By 9 hpi, the appressoria exhibited more distinct morphology and progressively increased pigmentation. At 12 hpi, over 50% of the appressoria were mature, with oval to irregular shapes, brown to dark‐brown pigmentation and firm adhesion to the cellophane (Figure 1a,b). Based on these observations, appressoria were collected at 12 hpi, whereas cellophane‐induced infectious hyphae (CIH) were obtained at 36 hpi for in vitro transcriptome analysis. WGA‐AF488 staining in planta showed that melanised appressoria were observed on the leaf surface at 24 hpi (Figure 1c). By 72 hpi, intracellular biotrophic hyphae were clearly visible within epidermal and mesophyll cells, indicating successful intracellular colonisation of host cells. At 96 hpi, more extensive secondary hyphal growth was observed. The intracellular colonisation detected at 72 hpi provided a cytological basis for selecting this time point for infected‐leaf (IL) RNA sequencing and for screening effectors potentially involved in maintaining the biotrophic interaction.

Genome‐Wide Identification of Candidate Effectors With High Expression During C. fructicola Infection

To predict the candidate effectors of C. fructicola , we modified an effector prediction pipeline adapted from previously established approaches (Haddadi et al. 2016; Neu and Debener 2019; Sperschneider et al. 2018; Sun et al. 2022) (Figure 2a). A total of 17,388 proteins from the genome annotation of C. fructicola were selected as the basis for the prediction of the secreted proteins. Using SignalP v. 5.0 and TMHMM v. 2.0c, we identified 2055 proteins with signal peptide and without transmembrane domains (Figure 2a and Table S4). Additionally, non‐classically secreted proteins were predicted using SecretomeP v. 1.0, leading to the identification of 195 proteins lacking signal peptides and transmembrane domains but predicted to be secreted (Table S5). A total of 2250 proteins were selected for further analysis (Figure 2a, Table S6). We then excluded proteins predicted to be targeted to the endoplasmic reticulum (ScanProsite; de Castro et al. 2006) or mitochondrion (TargetP; Emanuelsson et al. 2000), as well as those predicted with GPI‐anchored (PredGPI; Pierleoni et al. 2008). This filtering process yielded 2034 putative secreted proteins, representing 11.7% of the C. fructicola proteome (Figure 2a, Table S7).

To further refine the effector candidate genes that are highly upregulated during C. fructicola infection, we integrated RNA‐seq data derived from cellophane‐induced infectious hyphae (CIH), representing the in vitro control and C. fructicola ‐infected C. oleifera leaves (IL) collected at 72 hpi. Comparative transcriptome analysis identified 2476 upregulated and 1597 downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CIH and IL (Figure 2b). Next, the predicted candidate effectors with IL transcripts per million (TPM)> 100 and CIH TPM < 1 and a protein length of ≤ 300 amino acids (aa) were selected, yielding 77 high‐confidence CfCEs effector candidates at 72 hpi (Table S8). Among these, the 20 most highly expressed candidates lacking functional annotation were selected for functional characterisation, screening and designated CfCE1 to CfCE20 (Table S9). Hierarchical clustering showed strong infection‐associated expression of these candidates (Figure 2c).

Five Novel CfCEs Suppress Bax‐Induced Cell Death

To validate the expression patterns of CfCEs obtained from RNA‐seq analysis, the top 20 infection‐induced candidates were examined by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) using RNA isolated from CIH and C. fructicola ‐infected C. oleifera . The expression profiles of 17 candidate effector genes were consistent with trends observed in the RNA‐seq data (Figure 3a).

Validation of the expression profiles of the top 20 CfCE genes and suppression of Bax‐induced cell death in Nicotiana benthamiana. (a) Reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR validation of candidate effector genes expression in cellophane‐induced infectious hyphae (CIH) and Colletotrichum fructicola‐infected Camellia oleifera leaves (IL) at 72 h post‐inoculation. (b) Suppression of Bax‐induced cell death in N. benthamiana by CfCEs. Representative leaf images showing cell death symptoms at 3 days post‐infiltration. Co‐infiltration of CfCE1, CfCE2, CfCE12, CfCE13 or CfCE20 with Bax significantly inhibited Bax‐induced cell death compared to the expression of Bax alone.

To assess their functions, all candidate effectors validated by RT‐qPCR were transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana. Infiltration of Bax alone consistently induced strong cell death within 48–72 h, whereas infiltration of individual candidate effectors caused no visible symptoms (Figure 3b). Notably, co‐infiltration of CfCE1, CfCE2, CfCE12, CfCE13 or CfCE20 with Bax markedly reduced the severity and area of Bax‐induced cell death compared with Bax alone (Figure 3b). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that these five effector candidates, CfCE1, CfCE2, CfCE12, CfCE13 and CfCE20, can suppress Bax‐induced cell death in N. benthamiana, suggesting potential roles in suppressing plant immune response.

Five CfCE Effectors Are Required for Full Virulence on C. oleifera

To determine the roles of the five cell‐death suppressing candidate effectors during C. fructicola infection, detached fresh leaves were inoculated with wild‐type (WT) strain, and two independent deletion mutants for each target gene (ΔCfCEs‐1, ΔCfCEs‐2), or the corresponding complemented strains (ΔCfCEs‐C). Lesion areas were quantified at 2 dpi. All CfCE deletion mutants exhibited significantly smaller lesions than the WT and complemented strains (Figure 4a,b), whereas no significant differences were detected between the WT and complemented strains. Specifically, compared with the WT, lesion areas were reduced by 58.7% and 59.2% for the two ΔCfCE1 mutants (Figure 4c), by 78.4% and 76.9% for ΔCfCE2 mutants (Figure 4d), by 58.8% and 64.9% for ΔCfCE12 mutants, by 50.0% and 51.2% for ΔCfCE13 mutants, and by 78.8% and 71.7% for ΔCfCE20 mutants. Collectively, these results demonstrate that all five genes are required for full virulence of C. fructicola on oil‐tea C. oleifera leaves.

Five CfCEs Contributes to the Virulence of C. fructicola on Apple

Because C. fructicola has a broad host range, we tested whether the five CfCEs also contribute to the virulence on apple ( Malus domestica ), a host species belonging to the Rosaceae family. WT, two independent deletion mutants for each CfCE (ΔCfCEs‐1 and ΔCfCEs‐2), and the corresponding complemented strains (ΔCfCEs‐C) were inoculated onto apple fruits, and lesion development was assessed (Figure 5a). ΔCfCE1 and ΔCfCE2 mutants exhibited approximately 20% reductions in lesion area compared to WT (Figure 5b,c). Notably, the ΔCfCE12 mutants displayed the highest reduction by 67.6% and 68.3% (Figure 5d). The ΔCfCE13 and ΔCfCE20 mutants produced lesions approximately 30% smaller than those caused by WT. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that all five CfCEs contribute to the virulence of C. fructicola on apple, indicating that their virulence roles are not restricted to C. oleifera .

Transcriptomic Responses of C. oleifera Fruits Following C. fructicola Infection

To characterise the host response to C. fructicola infection, transcriptome sequencing was performed on healthy and C. fructicola ‐inoculated C. oleifera fruits (Figure 6a). A total of 3577 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 1568 upregulated and 2009 downregulated genes (Figure 6b). GO enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs were mainly associated with hydrolase activity, ion transmembrane transporter activity, plasma membrane components, responses to copper ions, secondary metabolism and chloroplast RNA modification (Figure 6c), suggesting substantial changes in oxidative defence, membrane and cell‐wall processes and secondary metabolism. In addition, KEGG analysis identified eight significantly enriched pathways, including taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, carotenoid biosynthesis, plant–pathogen interaction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, anthocyanin biosynthesis, galactose metabolism, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms and α‐linolenic acid metabolism (Figure 6d). Collectively, these results reveal broad metabolic and defence‐related responses of C. oleifera fruits to C. fructicola infection.

Candidate Effectors Modulate Host Oxidative Stress Responses During C. fructicola Infection of C. oleifera Leaves

To further characterise the functional roles of the five candidate effectors during host infection, we examined whether their deletion affected oxidative stress‐related responses in C. oleifera leaves. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content as well as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) activities were measured in C. oleifera leaves inoculated with the WT strain and the corresponding effector gene deletion mutants at multiple inoculation time points. As shown in Figure 7a, MDA content increased significantly in leaves inoculated with the WT strain, whereas leaves inoculated with each of the five effector deletion mutants showed significantly lower MDA levels than the WT‐inoculated group at the corresponding time points. During the early stage of infection (0–24 hpi), SOD activity was higher in leaves inoculated with the five effector deletion mutants than in those inoculated with the WT strain, except for the ∆CfCE1, ∆CfCE12 and ∆CfCE13 groups at 12 hpi (Figure 7b). However, at 48 and 72 hpi, SOD activity in most mutant‐inoculated groups was significantly lower than that in the WT group, with the exception of ∆CfCE2, ∆CfCE12 and ∆CfCE20 at 72 hpi (Figure 7b). CAT activity was significantly reduced in leaves inoculated with each of the five effector deletion mutants compared with the WT‐inoculated group throughout the infection period (Figure 7c). Similarly, POD activity was significantly lower in leaves inoculated with ∆CfCE1, ∆CfCE2 and ∆CfCE12 mutants than in WT‐inoculated leaves at all corresponding time points (Figure 7d). In contrast, leaves inoculated with the ΔCfCE13 mutant exhibited significantly higher POD activity than WT‐inoculated leaves throughout the infection period. In the ∆CfCE20‐inoculated leaves, POD activity was markedly higher than that in the WT‐inoculated leaves at 12 and 72 hpi, but significantly lower at the remaining time points (Figure 7c). Taken together, these results indicate that some candidate effectors likely modulate C. oleifera oxidative stress‐related responses during C. fructicola infection.

Discussion

Camellia oleifera is one of the most economically important woody oil crops worldwide, and its yield and fruit quality are severely threatened by Colletotrichum species, the causal agent of anthracnose (Tian et al. 2016). Effectors secreted by pathogenic fungi are key weapons that modulate host immunity, facilitate infection and drive disease progression (Cui et al. 2018; Xiang et al. 2024). However, systematic identification and functional verification of Colletotrichum effectors associated with anthracnose development and postharvest decay in C. oleifera remain largely limited (Cao, Shi, et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2022). In this study, we identified high‐confidence effector candidates by combining genome‐wide effector prediction with time‐course transcriptomic analysis during infection. We further verified the expression and immunomodulatory functions of top candidates, generated gene‐deletion mutants and characterised their contributions to virulence and host physiological responses. Our findings represent the first systematic effector mining and functional analysis in the Colletotrichum–C. oleifera pathosystem, providing novel insights into fungal pathogenicity mechanisms and disease control of woody oil crops.

A major limitation in understanding C. oleifera pathogenicity is the lack of small number of effectors supported by functional evidence (Cao, Shi, et al. 2024). We identified 2034 secreted proteins by integrating classical and non‐classical secretion predictions and then used the 72 hpi fungal transcriptomes to prioritise 77 infection‐associated candidates (Figure 2). The stringent thresholds (IL TPM > 100 and CIH TPM < 1) were chosen to generate high‐specificity, experimentally tractable shortlist enriched for strongly infection‐associated genes. This strategy which combining genome‐wide prediction and host‐induced transcriptional activation significantly improves the accuracy of effector identification and avoids false positives from purely in silico analysis (Sonah et al. 2016; Sperschneider et al. 2016). Similar approaches have been successfully applied in other pathogens, highlighting the necessity of combining genomic and transcriptomic data to capture biologically relevant effectors during actual host colonisation (Cui et al. 2018; Karan et al. 2026; Lv and Fan 2026). However, we cannot exclude the possibility that the stringent thresholds applied in our study may have neglected stage‐specific or low‐abundance candidates that are weakly expressed in mycelia but highly induced during infection. Therefore, future studies may benefit from adopting fold‐change‐based filtering, multitemporal transcriptome profiling, or adjusted expression cut‐offs, to enable a more comprehensive identification of stage‐specific effector candidates.

Our results establish a valuable effector resource for elucidating molecular interactions between Colletotrichum and its natural host C. oleifera . The effector prediction of this work was based on the genome of C. fructicola Nara gc5, whereas functional validation was performed in strain CFLH16. While strain‐specific genomic variation cannot be entirely excluded, we consider this bias to be limited. Nara gc5 has been routinely used as the reference genome for functional studies of C. fructicola genes in CFLH16 in recent years. Moreover, cloning and sequencing of multiple genes from CFLH16 have consistently revealed a high degree of nucleotide sequence similarity between the two strains (Chen et al. 2025; Gan et al. 2023; Guo and Zhang 2024; Zhang, Li, et al. 2022; Zhang, Guo, et al. 2022). Together, these observations suggest limited genomic divergence, supporting Nara gc5 as a reliable reference for CFLH16. Importantly, the five candidates identified from this reference‐guided pipeline were functionally characterised in strain CFLH16. Deletion of these genes resulted in reduced virulence, supporting the functional relevance of the identified effector candidates in the CFLH16 genetic background.

Given the complexity of the transcriptomic response, we focused on the 20 most abundantly and dynamically expressed genes to identify functionally relevant candidates. Subsequent RT‐qPCR assays confirmed their consistent upregulation during infection (Figure 3), strongly implicating these genes as central effectors underlying the corresponding biological process. Transient expression in N. benthamiana revealed that five effectors strongly suppressed Bax‐induced programmed cell death (PCD) (Figure 3), a key indicator of effector‐mediated suppression of host innate immunity (Lu et al. 2022; Toruño et al. 2016). Inhibition of host PCD is a well‐documented strategy employed by hemibiotrophic pathogens such as Colletotrichum to maintain biotrophic infection and prevent early host defence activation (Shang, Liu, et al. 2024; Shang et al. 2020). This result indicates that these five candidates act as canonical immunosuppressive effectors, enabling the pathogen to evade basal resistance and establish successful infection.

As a key novelty of this study, we extended in silico and expression‐based predictions to direct functional validation by constructing targeted deletion mutants of the five effectors. Subsequent pathogenicity tests on two distinct host tissues clearly demonstrated their roles as important contributors to virulence during C. fructicola infection. Compared with the WT strain, all mutant strains exhibited significantly reduced lesion expansion, lower disease severity and weakened colonisation ability, demonstrating that these five effectors are indispensable for full virulence of Colletotrichum (Figures 4 and 5). These results provide direct genetic evidence that the identified effectors function as critical pathogenicity determinants during anthracnose development. For C. oleifera and stored fruits, such core effectors represent ideal targets for developing molecular resistance breeding strategies and environmentally friendly control agents to reduce postharvest decay losses. Although transcriptomic profiling at 72 hpi provided an effective basis for identifying candidate effectors associated with C. fructicola colonisation in our study, the use of a single infection time point may not identify the full effector repertoire of C. fructicola . Because Colletotrichum infection is a dynamic and stage‐dependent process, some effectors may be preferentially expressed at earlier stages. Therefore, effectors acting at 24 or 48 hpi cannot be excluded and likely have been under‐represented or missed by our screening strategy. Future transcriptome analyses spanning multiple infection stages, in combination with functional characterisation of temporally regulated candidates, will provide a more comprehensive identification and understanding of the effectors involved in C. oleifera anthracnose.

More importantly, transcriptomic profiling of postharvest C. oleifera fruits infected by wild‐type Colletotrichum further uncovered the host defence responses during fungal infection. DEGs were significantly enriched in secondary metabolism, plant–pathogen interaction, cell wall component synthesis and defence‐related pathways (Figure 6). Our enrichment results are consistent with previous studies; these pathways are central to postharvest fruit resistance, as secondary metabolites and cell wall reinforcement form physical and chemical barriers against fungal invasion, while plant–pathogen interaction pathways initiate immune signalling cascades (Ma, Gao, et al. 2022; Ma, Yang, et al. 2022; Zeng et al. 2026). However, successful Colletotrichum infection relies on effector‐mediated suppression of these host responses, which directly contributes to the rapid onset of anthracnose and postharvest fruit rot.

Of particular significance, to dissect the physiological basis underlying effector‐mediated virulence, we examined the activities of key antioxidant enzymes including SOD, POD and CAT in C. oleifera leaves inoculated with wild‐type and effector‐deletion strains. The fluctuation of SOD activity in early and late infection stages, together with reduced CAT activity in effector mutants, suggests that C. fructicola dynamically manipulates the host's redox homeostasis (Figure 7). This aligns with reports that fungal effectors often target specific antioxidant enzymes; for instance, PstGSRE4 inhibits TaCZSOD2 to suppress H2O2 accumulation (Liu et al. 2022), while effectors such as PsCRN63/115 and PsAvh113 interfere with CAT function or transcription (Zhang et al. 2015; Zhu et al. 2023). The differential regulation of POD by our effectors further reflects functional diversity, consistent with findings that Pep1 inhibits peroxidase activity to dampen host immunity (Hemetsberger et al. 2012). Together, these findings suggest that C. fructicola effectors play a critical role in manipulating host defence mechanisms, particularly through the regulation of oxidative stress and the activation of metabolic pathways that promote pathogen survival. The pathogen's ability to suppress host antioxidant enzymes allows it to manipulate the host's redox environment, redirecting ROS from a defence mechanism into a tool for tissue damage and nutrient acquisition. Although effectors from other pathosystems have been reported to directly target antioxidant enzymes, our current data do not support a direct interaction between the five candidate effectors and the specific enzymes or pathways identified in the transcriptomic analysis. Instead, the global transcriptomic profiling revealed that C. fructicola infection profoundly perturbed host oxidative stress, membrane alterations and defence‐related metabolism. Given that pathogen effectors classically facilitate infection by interfering with host basal immunity, including suppressing the ROS burst and PCD, we hypothesised that these candidate effectors might contribute to virulence by modulating this specific oxidative defence response. Therefore, the altered antioxidant enzyme activities observed in the effector deletion mutants (Figure 7) are interpreted as a downstream physiological consequence of the release of host immunity from effector‐mediated suppression, rather than as evidence of direct effector–enzyme interactions. Identifying the genuine host targets of these effectors and elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms remains a key priority for future studies.

This study presents the first systematic genome‐wide mining and functional characterisation of C. fructicola effectors associated with anthracnose in C. oleifera . By integrating genome‐based secretion predictions with C. fructicola transcriptomic data obtained during infection, we identified 77 highly upregulated candidate effectors from 2034 putative secreted proteins. Five candidates suppressed Bax‐induced cell death and were genetically demonstrated to be required for the full virulence of C. fructicola on both C. oleifera and apple fruits. These effectors are also associated with altered host oxidative stress responses in infected C. oleifera leaves, whereas host transcriptome analysis revealed extensive changes in defence‐ and metabolism‐related pathways following infection. Collectively, these results provide a basis for dissecting the molecular mechanisms underlying C. fructicola effector‐mediated virulence and identify potential targets for RNA‐based silencing strategies against C. fructicola infection.

Experimental Procedures

Fungal Strain, Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Colletotrichum fructicola CFLH16 was used as the wild‐type (WT) strain throughout this study. Routine maintenance and subculture of the isolate were conducted on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco) at 28°C in the absence of light. Tender, fully expanded healthy leaves and fruits were collected from 5‐year‐old C. oleifera ‘Hua Shuo’ trees cultivated in the campus orchard of Central South University of Forestry and Technology. N. benthamiana plants were grown and maintained as described by Yang et al. (2020). Mature, unblemished fruits of apple ( M. domestica ‘Gala’) purchased locally were surface‐sterilised with 75% ethanol and washed thoroughly with sterile distilled water prior to inoculation.

Colletotrichum fructicola Inoculation and Preparation of RNA‐Seq Samples

Conidia were produced by culturing C. fructicola CFLH16 in potato dextrose broth (PDB) at 28°C with shaking at 150 rpm. Conidia were harvested from 5‐day‐old cultures by filtration through three layers of Miracloth (Merck Millipore), followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min. After the supernatant was discarded, the conidial pellet was washed twice by resuspension in sterile water, followed by another same centrifugation step. The purified conidia were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. To induce appressorium formation, 100 μL of conidial suspension (107/mL) was inoculated onto 2% agar overlaid with cellophane. Germ tubes and appressoria were collected from the upper surface of the cellophane at 12 hpi. To produce cellophane‐induced infectious hyphae (CIH), the same concentration of conidial suspension was plated onto cellophane covering 2% agar supplemented with 0.01% yeast extract. At 36 hpi, the cellophane was removed and CIH were collected from its agar‐contacting surface with a cell scraper. Healthy tender C. oleifera leaves were collected from trees, and surface sterilised before inoculation with C. fructicola conidia. For RNA sequencing, the abaxial surfaces of C. oleifera leaves were inoculated with WT C. fructicola conidia suspension (106/mL) by hand spray. C. oleifera fruits were collected from the oil‐tea Camelllia orchard (Tianluhu oil‐tea Camellia park, Zhuzhou, China) and punctuated on the basal side with a sterile needle before inoculation with the same conidial suspension. The inoculated leaves (IL) and fruits were incubated in a humid chamber at 28°C, and five randomly chosen infected leaves or fruits were used for RNA isolation at 72 hpi.

Detached, mature leaves of C. oleifera were wound‐inoculated with WT, gene‐deletion mutants (two independent isolates per target gene), or complemented strains for bioassay. The conidial suspensions were adjusted to 106 conidia/mL for inoculation. Control leaves were mock‐inoculated with sterile water. Disease severity was assessed by measuring lesion areas at 2 dpi, as described previously (Cao et al. 2023). For the inoculation of apple fruits, surface‐sterilised fruits were wounded to a depth of 2 mm using a sterile nail and inoculated with agar plug containing mycelia of WT, mutants, or complemented strains. The inoculated fruits were incubated in humid chambers at 25°C. Lesion area (cm2) was measured at 7 dpi. Ten apple fruits were used for each biological replicate.

Staining and Confocal Microscopic Observation of C. fructicola in Infected Leaves

Colletotrichum fructicola‐inoculated C. oleifera leaves were harvested at the indicated time points and immersed in ethanol for 5 days to remove chlorophyll. The ethanol was replaced with 10% KOH, and the samples were incubated at 85°C for 1 h. The samples were then washed five times in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl and 12 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) and immersed in PBS containing 10 μg/mL wheat germ agglutinin‐Alexa Fluor 488 (WGA‐AF488; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 20 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI; Sigma‐Aldrich). Samples were vacuum‐infiltrated at 250 millibar for four 5‐min cycles, with 5‐min intervals at atmospheric pressure, and then washed twice with PBS. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a TCS SP8 confocal laser‐scanning microscope (Leica). WGA‐AF488 fluorescence was excited at 488 nm and PI at 560 nm. For dual‐label imaging, the fluorescence signals were acquired sequentially, with emission light at 500–540 nm for WGA–AF488 and 580–620 nm for PI.

RNA Isolation and Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA Simple Kit (Tiangen). RNA degradation and contamination were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific), and RNA integrity was evaluated using the RNA 6000 Nano Kit on an Agilent 2100 Bioananlyzer. Library preparation and paired‐end 150‐bp sequencing were performed by Personal Gene Co. Ltd. (Nanjing, China) using an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. Libraries from conidia, appressoria and cellophane‐induced infectious hyphae (CIH) were sequenced to a target depth of 6 Gb of clean data per library, whereas the library derived from infected leaves (IL) and fruits was sequenced to 12 Gb clean data.

RNA‐Seq Data Analysis

HISAT v. 2.2.0 was used to build the index of the C. fructicola reference genome (GCA_000319635.2), and to align the reads of transcriptome data to the reference genome (Kim et al. 2019). Read counts were generated with HTSeq‐count v. 0.12.4. DEGs between cellophane‐induced infectious hyphae (CIH) and infected leaves (IL; fungal reads recovered from C. fructicola ‐infected C. oleifera leaves at 72 hpi) were identified using DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014), with thresholds of |log2FC| > 2 and adjusted p‐value (p adj) < 0.01. To elucidate the biological functions and active pathways of the DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (Yu et al. 2012). GO terms and KEGG pathways with an adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. After normalisation and log2 transformation, the expression profiles of secretome‐related genes were visualised using TBtools (Chen et al. 2020). The sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under NCBI BioProject accession number PRJNA1467209 and PRJNA1497757.

Genome‐Wide Secretome and Candidate Effector Prediction

The previously sequenced and annotated genome of C. fructicola Nara gc5 (GCA_000319635.2; NCBI BioProject PRJNA763717) was used as the reference strain for bioinformatic prediction. To predict signal peptides (SP), the complete set of annotated genome‐encoded proteins was subjected to analysis using SignalP v. 5.0, and a cut‐off score of 0.5 was applied (Almagro Armenteros et al. 2019). The mature proteins lacking SP were analysed using TMHMM v. 2.0c to detect and characterise transmembrane (TM) domains (Krogh et al. 2001). Proteins containing SP and lacking TM domains were retained as classically secreted candidates. Proteins lacking SP and TM were analysed with SecretomeP v. 1.0 for the identification of unclassical secreted proteins. Non‐classically secreted proteins were identified using strigent thresholds: NN score > 0.9 and odds score > 3 (Bendtsen et al. 2004). All predicted secreted proteins were subsequently analysed using Targetp v. 1.1 to remove the mitochondrion target proteins (Emanuelsson et al. 2000). The remaining sequences were screened against the PROSITE database (PS00014 ER_Targeting) using the web serve of ScanProsite to remove the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) target proteins (de Castro et al. 2006; Sigrist et al. 2002). PredGPI was then used to predicted glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)‐anchoring signals, with the false positive (FP) rate threshold set < 0.005 (Pierleoni et al. 2008). Finally, proteins passing these filters and having a mature protein length ≤ 300 amino acids were defined as candidate small secreted proteins (SSPs).

qPCR Analysis

Quantitative real‐time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the Premix Ex Taq (Perfect Real Time) kit (Takara) on an ABI 7500 Real‐Time Cycler (Applied Biosystems), according to the manufacturer's instructions. All relevant primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Bax‐Induced Cell Death Suppression Assay in N. benthamiana

The open reading frames (ORFs) of the candidate effector genes validated by RT‐qPCR, excluding the predicted signal peptide sequences, were amplified from C. fructicola CFLH16 cDNA using Phusion High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB). Primers sequences are listed in Table S2. The resulting PCR products were cloned between the NcoI and SpeI sites of the binary vector pCAMBIA1302, downstream of the CaMV 35S promoter and in‐frame with a C‐terminal mGFP5 tag. The recombinant constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens , and positive colonies were selected on Luria Bertani (LB) agar containing rifampicin and kanamycin. Overnight cultures were centrifuged, and the bacterial pellets were resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, pH 5.6; 10 mM MgCl2, 150 μM acetosyringone) to an OD600 of 0.5 for candidate effectors/controls and 0.2 for Bax. Agrobacterium suspensions carrying individual candidate effectors, Bax, or mixtures of Bax and individual candidate effectors were infiltrated into the abaxial side of fully expanded N. benthamiana leaves using a 1 mL needleless syringe. The empty pCAMBIA1302 vector served as the negative control. Cell death symptoms were photographed and recorded at 3 dpi. Eight leaf discs per treatment were analysed.

Generation of C. fructicola Gene Deletion Mutants and Complemented Strains

Gene deletion constructs were generated using the split‐marker method. Approximately 1.0 kb DNA fragments flanking the 5′ and 3′ regions of each target gene (CfCE1, CfCE2, CfCE12, CfCE13 or CfCE20) were amplified from the genomic DNA of C. fructicola strain CFLH16 using Phusion polymerase and the corresponding primers (Table S3). The hygromycin phosphotransferase (HPH) selectable‐marker cassette was amplified from plasmid pKNTG using the primer pair Hyg‐F/Hyg‐R. The 5′ and the 3′ flanking fragments were fused separately to the corresponding portions of the selectable‐marker cassette using overlap‐extension PCR (Sweigard et al. 1998).

For complementation, the full‐length genomic sequence of each target gene, including its native promoter and terminator, was amplified from WT genomic DNA by corresponding primers and cloned into pYF1. Each complementation construct was transformed into the protoplasts of the corresponding deletion mutant following the protocol described previously (Gao et al. 2022).

Measurements of Antioxidant Enzyme Activity and MDA Content

Camellia oleifera leaf tissue (0.1 g) was homogenised in 1.8 mL of ice‐cold 0.05 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and incubated on ice for 2 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 1000 g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was stored at −20°C for further analysis. The activities of SOD, CAT and POD, and MDA content were quantified using commercial kits (Geruisi‐bio) following the manufacturer's protocols. All assays were performed using three biological replicates per treatment.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software. Depending on the experimental design, differences between groups were evaluated using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test or by Student's t‐test, as specified in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, p < 0.01, or p < 0.001, as indicated in the text and figure legends.
