Patient Preference and Adherence in Mammography Screening: A Narrative Review
Authors: Yina Wang, Haiou Qi, Yanhua Zheng, Wanping Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine how mammography-related pain, anxiety, compression experience, perceived control, and patient-centered interventions influence patient preference and screening adherence.MethodsA structured literature search of PubMed, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library was conducted for peer-reviewed English- and Chinese-language studies published between January 2000 and October 2025. Eligible studies examined mammography-related pain, anxiety, compression experience, perceived control, patient-assisted compression, psychosocial interventions, patient preference, patient experience, or screening adherence.Key FindingsMammography-related pain is multifactorial and is influenced by compression force and pressure, breast morphology, positioning, prior negative experiences, and anxiety-mediated pain sensitization. Pressure-standardized compression may reduce severe pain, particularly among women with smaller breast volumes, while patient-assisted compression may increase patients’ perceived ability to influence the compression process and improve tolerance without compromising diagnostic image quality. Psychosocial interventions, including structured education, patient-centered communication, and music-based interventions, may reduce anxiety and improve satisfaction by interrupting the anxiety–pain feedback loop. Qualitative evidence further suggests that patient-centered communication tailored to diverse ethnic backgrounds may help reduce anxiety and pain during mammography.ConclusionTechnical and psychosocial dimensions of the mammography experience may influence patient preference and screening tolerance; however, evidence directly linking these factors to future screening re-attendance remains limited. A multimodal, patient-centered strategy combining optimized compression, patient participation in the compression process, clear communication, and low-resource psychosocial support may improve screening tolerance, but whether these improvements translate into actual repeat screening participation requires further investigation.Implications for PracticeScreening programs should consider integrating pain assessment, patient education, patient-centered communication tailored to diverse ethnic backgrounds, and patient-assisted or pressure-guided compression where feasible to enhance patient-centered mammography care.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy among women globally, driving a continuously rising disease burden with notable regional disparities in incidence and mortality.1–4 Although its exact causes remain unclear,5 early detection and treatment through routine screening can significantly reduce mortality.6,7 While studies continue to evaluate optimal screening frequencies,8,9 economic constraints and suboptimal healthcare infrastructure often lead to late-stage diagnoses, particularly in resource-poor regions.10,11

During mammography, compression paddles apply force to immobilize breast tissue and reduce thickness, thereby lowering radiation dose and improving image quality.12 However, approximately 75% of women report significant pain or discomfort during the procedure; this negative experience directly diminishes service quality13,14 and serves as a major deterrent to subsequent screening re-attendance.15,16

This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on how mammography-related pain, anxiety, compression experience, and perceived control influence patient preference and screening adherence. Specifically, we aim to: (1) describe the biomechanical and psychosocial mechanisms underlying mammography-associated pain and anxiety; (2) evaluate patient-centered strategies, including pressure-standardized compression, patient-assisted compression (PAC), structured education, communication, and music-based interventions; and (3) provide practical recommendations for improving screening tolerance and supporting repeat screening participation.

Methods

Unlike earlier reviews that primarily addressed general pain impacts or basic pharmacological interventions (eg, Whelehan et al, 2013),16 this narrative review uniquely bridges modern biomechanical advancements (eg, pressure-guided systems) with cross-cultural psychosocial perspectives.

We systematically searched PubMed (MEDLINE), CINAHL (EBSCO), PsycINFO, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library for peer-reviewed literature published in English or Chinese between January 2000 and October 2025. The Boolean search strategy (Table 1) integrated three conceptual clusters: imaging modalities, biomechanical parameters (eg, compression force and contact pressure), and psychosocial interventions and patient-reported outcomes. European screening guidelines were used as contextual background but were not included in the final narrative synthesis. To address the concept of patient involvement more explicitly, a supplementary PubMed search was conducted using the same publication period and additional terms including “perceived control,” “sense of control,” “patient participation,” and “autonomy.” The supplementary search identified one additional eligible study addressing patient-assisted compression, patient experience, and anticipated screening re-attendance. Literature Search Strategy and Search Terms

Eligible studies quantitatively or qualitatively assessed the impact of compression techniques or psychosocial interventions on patient-reported outcomes or screening adherence. Studies were considered eligible if they addressed patient-reported outcomes, patient preference, satisfaction, perceived control, willingness to return, or screening adherence in relation to mammography compression or psychosocial interventions. In this review, “patient preference” refers to patients’ values and choices regarding how mammography is delivered, including the compression method, participation in compression-related decisions, communication, information provision, and supportive care. “Perceived control” refers to the patient’s perceived ability to influence the mammography compression process, including communicating discomfort and participating in compression-related decisions, rather than solely controlling pain management. We excluded studies focused exclusively on breast MRI/ultrasound, breast reconstruction, or abstracts lacking full methodological details. Two independent reviewers (YW, HQ) screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, resolving discrepancies via consultation with a senior reviewer (WZ). Measurement and Evaluation of Pain

Quantifying mammography pain is complex, requiring a clinical distinction between nociceptive pain and pressure-induced discomfort.26 For adherence-oriented mammography care, pain assessment should also be interpreted as a patient-reported outcome that reflects not only physical intensity but also perceived control, emotional distress, satisfaction, and willingness to return for future screening. While the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) remain standard tools,27–29 recent studies increasingly incorporate patient satisfaction and willingness to rescreen as broader evaluative metrics.30 Crucially, anticipatory anxiety acts as a central sensitizer, lowering pain thresholds and explaining why identical compression parameters elicit diverse responses among patients.31 Consequently, pain assessment must integrate psychological variables rather than relying solely on measures of physical intensity.

Biomechanical and Psychosocial Contributors to Pain

Biomechanically, compression is the primary technical determinant of pain. Standardizing contact pressure (kPa) helps achieve more equitable compression across varying breast volumes.32 However, significant variations in compression practices persist across institutions.33,34 Evidence indicates that contact area and breast volume predict pain more accurately than absolute compression force,35 providing a theoretical basis for pain-relieving interventions such as cushions, radiolucent pads, or curved paddles.31,36 Additionally, suboptimal patient positioning increases skin traction, aggravating discomfort during image acquisition.37 Intrinsic factors, such as breast density and menstrual cycle phase, further modulate pain sensitivity.38,39

Beyond biomechanics, negative prior experiences and anticipatory fear may exacerbate perceived pain; failing to account for these psychological reactions explains why examinations performed with lower compression forces are sometimes reported as highly painful.30 Embarrassment and inadequate privacy during mammography may negatively affect subsequent screening re-attendance,40 whereas brief pre-examination education significantly improves the patient experience.21 Finally, cultural contexts and regional screening standards heavily influence pain expression and help-seeking behaviors.38 Therefore, optimizing the screening pathway requires addressing both mechanical pressure and diverse psychosocial needs.

Patient Preference, Perceived Control, and Screening Adherence

Patient preference in mammography may be influenced by anticipated pain and the extent to which women feel able to participate in the compression process. Negative pain experiences may reduce satisfaction and willingness to return for subsequent screening.16 Patient-assisted compression directly addresses this issue by allowing women to participate in the compression process after initial positioning by the radiographer. This patient-participation model may improve comfort and perceived control during an otherwise passive and anxiety-provoking procedure.20,35 Therefore, interventions that increase patient participation, provide clear information, and validate patient concerns may be important components of patient-centered mammography care.24

Classification of Pain Intervention Measures

Contextual Background and Pharmacological Interventions

Early literature established the negative impact of mammography pain on screening adherence.16 Initial interventions primarily explored premedication26 or basic relaxation.39 Pharmacological approaches have shown mixed effects. In a randomized trial, topical 4% lidocaine gel significantly reduced mammography-related discomfort.26 However, medication does not address the procedural and psychosocial contributors to discomfort and may therefore be considered an adjunct rather than a standalone strategy. Consequently, these agents are now considered merely adjunctive, prompting a clinical shift toward optimizing biomechanical parameters and enhancing patient empowerment. Optimal breast positioning also remains an important procedural consideration, as suboptimal positioning may increase skin traction and discomfort and compromise breast tissue coverage.41

Optimization of Equipment and Compression Strategies

Cushions and Paddle Designs: Various modifications, including radiolucent cushions and altered paddle shapes (rigid, flexible, or curved), aim to optimize compression geometry. Broeders et al18 found no significant difference in pain between flexible and rigid compression paddles. Although the flexible paddle was associated with a slightly lower radiation dose, it provided poorer depiction of clinically relevant retroglandular tissue and reduced contrast compared with the rigid paddle. Conversely, findings regarding curved paddles are mixed. Although designed to fit breast anatomy, their modest comfort improvements are often offset by technical disadvantages, including negative deviations in subjective image quality and inadequate tissue coverage.18,30

Patient-Assisted Compression (PAC): Shifting compression control to the patient effectively addresses both biomechanical and psychological components of pain. Beyond its technical non-inferiority, PAC is particularly relevant to patient preference because it transforms compression from a provider-controlled procedure into a process involving active patient participation. As demonstrated by Ulus et al35 allowing patients to actively participate in the compression process significantly improves satisfaction and their sense of control. Balleyguier et al19 reported equivalent image quality in 85% of paired examinations and found that 74% of participants believed that the self-compression device would facilitate their re-attendance. These findings suggest that participation in compression may strengthen patients’ perceived ability to influence the procedure and their willingness to return, although actual re-attendance was not measured. Crucially, large-scale randomized clinical trials (N = 548) confirm that PAC significantly improves patient comfort (p < 0.001) and achieves imaging performance equivalent to standard compression, without adversely affecting breast thickness, contrast, clarity, or overall diagnostic acceptability.20,34

Pressure-Targeted Individualized Compression: Pain correlates more strongly with actual contact pressure than with applied force. In a prospective comparative study (N = 433), De Groot et al17 demonstrated that pressure-guided compression significantly reduced the proportion of women experiencing severe pain (from 23% to 11%) while preserving image quality and average glandular doses. However, broad implementation faces practical barriers. The European guidelines mandate sufficient breast tissue spread,12 yet the inherent variability in proprietary algorithms and the inability of legacy systems to calculate real-time contact pressure often necessitate substantial financial investments for software upgrades.17,28

Pre-Examination Education and Psychological Support

Pain and anxiety during mammography are strongly shaped by patient expectations, prior experiences, perceived vulnerability, and the quality of communication before and during the examination. From a patient-preference perspective, structured information and emotional support may improve tolerance by reducing uncertainty and increasing trust in the screening process. Fernández-Feito et al21 found that face-to-face information and emotional support from trained nurses significantly reduced pain during screening mammography, with greater benefit among women with higher baseline anxiety.

Beyond education, auditory interventions have emerged as potentially valuable non-invasive adjuncts. Earlier work by Domar et al39 found that relaxation or music audiotapes did not significantly reduce pain or anxiety. Zavotsky et al22 similarly reported that music reduced anxiety but did not significantly reduce physical pain. More recent small randomized studies have reported reductions in pain and/or anxiety with specific auditory interventions. Nelson et al23 found that a 5-minute exposure to binaural beats improved patient tolerance and reduced mammographic pain perception. Similarly, Köse et al25 reported that music playback reduced pain and state anxiety scores and improved overall examination satisfaction. These findings suggest that auditory interventions may be useful low-resource adjuncts, although the available evidence remains limited.

Quality of Evidence and Methodological Limitations

Several methodological limitations in the current literature must be acknowledged. Crucially, as a narrative review, we did not perform a formal quality or risk-of-bias assessment of the included studies, which inherently restricts our ability to draw definitive practice recommendations. In addition, evidence directly linking specific pain-reduction or psychosocial interventions to long-term screening re-attendance remains limited; therefore, adherence-related conclusions should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Furthermore, many trials investigating novel paddle designs or psychosocial interventions feature small sample sizes and single-center or context-specific designs, limiting generalizability. The nature of these interventions also makes double-blinding unfeasible, introducing potential biases for subjective outcomes like pain. Finally, heterogeneity in pain measurement scales complicates direct comparisons across studies.

Conclusion

Mammography-related pain and anxiety remain modifiable barriers to patient preference, screening tolerance, and future re-attendance. Current evidence suggests that pressure-standardized compression and patient-assisted compression may improve tolerance by reducing excessive pressure and enhancing perceived control without compromising diagnostic image quality. Psychosocial interventions, including structured education, patient-centered communication, and music-based approaches, provide low-resource strategies to reduce uncertainty, mitigate anxiety, and improve satisfaction.

Optimizing patient preference and adherence in mammography screening may therefore require a multimodal strategy that combines mechanical optimization with shared control and culturally sensitive psychosocial support. However, direct evidence linking these interventions to sustained improvements in long-term screening re-attendance remains limited. Future research should prioritize: (1) identifying populations most likely to benefit from patient-assisted or pressure-guided compression; (2) evaluating the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of patient-centered compression protocols in different screening settings; and (3) conducting large-scale longitudinal studies to determine whether improved tolerance and satisfaction translate into actual repeat screening participation.
