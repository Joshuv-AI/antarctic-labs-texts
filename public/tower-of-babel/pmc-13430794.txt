Household food insecurity status in Gulina District, zone 4 of conflict affected Afar Regional State, Northeast Ethiopia
Authors: Fiseha Tsegaye, Goitom Sisay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestigating the Household Food insecurity situation of conflict affected communities has increasingly become a widespread issue around the world. Gulina District, situated within Zone 4 of the Afar region, faced significant challenges related to the impacts of conflict, which severely affect household food security. The study aimed to assess the household food security status in the conflict-affected Gulina District. The study used a cross-sectional study design using a combination of quantitative and qualitative data collection techniques. Data was collected from 366 randomly selected household heads. Food Consumption Score (FCS), Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS), and Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) were among the metrics utilized in the survey questionnaire to evaluate the level of household food security status. Employing HFIAS, 82.8% of households are food insecure; of these, 61.5% and 21.3% are classified as mildly and moderately food insecure, respectively. This demonstrated the problem of household food insecurity’s seeming prevalence. Moreover, the HDDS and FCS survey result show that the study area is mainly characterized by the high consumption of milk and relatively moderate intake of staples and animal protein, but the low consumption of fruits and vegetables due to the pastoral nature of the community.Keywords: Conflict, Dietary diversity, Food consumption score, Household food insecurity, Household food insecurity access scale

Introduction

Food security is defined by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) as a state in which "all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life". This definition was established during the World Food Summit in 1996. The FAO also identifies four key dimensions of food security: Availability (Ensuring a sufficient supply of food), Access (Economic and physical ability to obtain food), Utilization (Proper consumption and nutrition and Stability (Consistency in food availability, access, and utilization over time) [13]. On the other hand, food insecurity refers to the limited or uncertain access to nutritious food, which can also include restrictions on obtaining food in socially acceptable ways [14].

Food insecurity has long been a global development challenge and continues to be a major concern today [16]. Efforts to achieve the Zero Hunger goal by 2030 are increasingly hindered by the rising number of people experiencing food insecurity each year [8]. The latest report on global food insecurity by Global Report on Food Crises (GRFC) 2024, published by the Food Security Information Network (FSIN) in collaboration with 16 partner organizations provides a comprehensive analysis of acute food insecurity worldwide, highlighting that 282 million people across 59 countries faced high levels of food insecurity in 2023 with the majority concentrated in developing regions [19].

The ongoing global issue of food insecurity stems from a variety of causes. Key contributing factors include armed conflicts, unpredictable and extreme climate conditions, and economic recessions [8, 21, 41–43].

Food insecurity is widespread in Ethiopia’s agro-pastoral regions and areas with limited moisture. Even when rainfall is sufficient and harvests are good, communities in lowland agro-pastoral areas continue to face food insecurity [11]. Despite livestock’s vast potential, the sector remains underdeveloped, facing challenges such as low production and productivity, recurrent droughts, and persistent food shortages [24]. Additionally, food insecurity driven by conflict has increasingly become a widespread threat to the well-being and sustainable livelihoods of populations around the world [8, 20, 41, 43].

The Afar communities, who primarily rely on agro-pastoralism in Ethiopia, have been deeply impacted by severe climate-related events such as droughts, floods, and other natural disasters. These events have intensified existing issues related to food insecurity, sanitation, and public health [18, 24, 25, 31, 38, 42]. Furthermore, the civil war that erupted in northern Ethiopia in November 2020 has had a profound impact on agriculture and livelihoods across Tigray, Amhara, and nearby regions such as Afar. The conflict has resulted in widespread abandonment of farmland, disruption of planting and harvesting activities, theft of seeds, looting of agricultural tools, and the slaughter of livestock. Even crops that were successfully planted have often been looted or burned before harvest, severely exacerbating the status food insecurity FEWS [3, 8, 17, 20].

Gulina District, which is one of the districts in Afar Regional State, experiences harsh climatic conditions, limited access to water resources, and land degradation. These factors pose significant challenges to agricultural productivity and food production in the region. Additionally, the district has been affected by conflicts, which disrupt livelihoods, cause displacement, and exacerbate food insecurity among households [42].

While the link between armed conflict and food insecurity has been a central theme in global development and humanitarian discussions, there is still a lack of detailed understanding specific to rural areas [20]. Moreover, empirical studies examining the extent of armed conflict and its connection to household food insecurity in the conflict-affected areas of northeastern Ethiopia is limited and largely absent from the existing literatures [8, 30]. Therefore, understanding the nutritional status and living conditions of affected populations is crucial for the effective design and evaluation of intervention strategies for post conflict recovery (Barrett, 2010; [4, 20]). However, identifying reliable indicators of food security remains a significant challenge due to the complexity and multidimensional nature of the issue [15]. As a result, developing comprehensive and accurate metrics that capture all aspects of food security continues to be difficult [6]. To accurately capture the complexity of food security in any context, a combination of measures and indicators is required [9, 32]. Recognizing this, our study employed multiple food security measurement scales,Food Consumption Score (FCS, Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS, and Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS to deepen understanding of how food insecurity is experienced and interpreted within a conflict-affected context in Gulina District, Zone 4 of Conflict Affected Afar Regional State, Northeast Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Afar National Regional State, located in the northeastern part of Ethiopia, covers an area of approximately 94,760 square kilometers, representing about 8.4% of the country’s total land area. Positioned within the northern section of the Great Rift Valley, it shares borders with Eritrea to the northeast, Tigray to the northwest, Oromia to the south, Somali to the southeast, Amhara to the west, and the Republic of Djibouti to the east [12].

The Afar population primarily practices pastoralism, relying on livestock as their main source of food and income. The region is largely inhabited by Muslim pastoralists and agro-pastoralists. Afar is classified as one of Ethiopia’s Developing Regional States (DRS), with social indicators well below the national average. Communities in the region face recurring challenges such as drought, disease outbreaks, locust infestations, and conflict. Chronic water scarcity further compels families and their herds to migrate frequently, disrupting children’s education, health, and overall well-being [12] Fig. 1.

Study design

This study employed a mixed-methods study design, incorporating both quantitative and qualitative components. The quantitative component consisted of a cross-sectional survey using a structured questionnaire. This method is widely recognized and commonly used in social science research as an effective observational approach. In a cross-sectional survey, data is collected from a sample at a single point in time. According to Babbie [5], surveys are well-suited for descriptive, explanatory, and exploratory research, particularly when individuals are the primary units of analysis. Since this study examines both individuals and families, the survey method is appropriate. The survey questionnaire was primarily directed at heads of households, who served as the main respondents.

The qualitative component was designed to complement and enrich the quantitative findings by providing an in-depth understanding of participants’ experiences, perceptions, and contextual factors related to food insecurity and conflict. This component involved semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs) with purposively selected participants who had relevant experience with the subject under investigation.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings was undertaken during the interpretation phase to provide a comprehensive understanding of the research problem.

Data sources

Data was collected from primary and secondary data source. Primary data was collected through questionnaire, interview and Focus Group discussion. Key informants and focus group participants provided detailed insights on critical issues that were not covered by other respondents.

Sample size

According to the 2019 needs assessment conducted by the Afar Disaster and Food Security Office, the estimated number of households in Gulina District is 7,549. This figure was used to determine the sample size for the study. Accordingly, using a sample size determination developed by Krejcie and Morgan [33], the researchers selected 366 household heads from the total population. For the quantitative component, a convenience sampling technique was used. Sample respondents were recruited based on their availability and willingness to participate during the data collection period. Recruitment took place after obtaining permission from the relevant authorities, and all individuals who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate until the desired sample size was reached. The sample was proportionally distributed among the eight kebeles (the smallest administrative units in Ethiopia) based on their population sizes.

For the qualitative component, purposive sampling was employed to select Key informants and focus group participants with relevant experience and knowledge related to food insecurity and conflict. Accordingly, eight household heads, eight local government representatives, and eight community leaders from the study area were identified, ensuring that diverse perspectives were represented. Selection continued until data saturation was achieved, at which point no new themes were emerging from the data.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected using a structured questionnaire. Qualitative data were collected through semi‑structured interviews and focus group discussions. The questionnaire was administered to participants in person at their respective houses through trained enumerators. Each questionnaire took approximately 30–40 min to complete and was conducted between July–August, 2025.

Semi‑structured interviews were conducted with twenty seven individuals composed of old aged household heads, local government representatives, and eight community leaders to gain deeper insights into the study topic. Each interview lasted approximately 45–60 min and was conducted in person in their respective houses and offices. All interviews followed an interview guide and were audio‑recorded with participants’ consent.

In addition, after preparing discussion checklists, three focus group discussions were conducted, each consisting of eight participants. Each focus group session lasted approximately 90–120 min and was held in the study area. A trained facilitator moderated the discussions using a predefined guide, while a note‑taker recorded key observations. The discussions were also audio‑recorded with participants’ permission.

To complement the data and reduce bias, personal observations were also carried out. This helped link the collected data to current realities and reduced the need for direct respondent involvement. Observations focused on household living conditions, eating habits, and food sources.

Assessing food security is challenging due to the absence of a universally accepted measurement method. Although progress has been made in developing appropriate metrics, most commonly used indicators tend to capture only one dimension of food security at a time. Since no single indicator can comprehensively represent food security, researchers recommend using a combination of measures [27, 32, 34–36]. Accordingly, this study employed a survey questionnaire incorporating elements from the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS), Food Consumption Score (FCS), and Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) to evaluate the food security status of sampled households. These tools collectively address key dimensions of food security, including access, availability, stability, and utilization.

Secondary sources including books, academic journals, and relevant unpublished materials were consulted to provide background information and serve as benchmarks for validating the study’s findings.

Extensive field validation research has repeatedly confirmed that the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) is a dependable and useful metric for use in developing country settings. To calculate the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS), household heads were asked whether their family members had consumed any of the specified food groups within the previous 24 h. Each food group that was consumed was assigned a score of 1, while those not consumed were given a score of 0. The eight primary food groups reported as being consumed by the sampled households included: main staples; pulses, legumes, and nuts; animal protein (such as meat and poultry); milk and dairy products; vegetables (excluding potatoes); fruits; oils; and sugar or honey. The values assigned to each food group consumed were summed to calculate the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS), resulting in a score ranging from 0 to a maximum of 8. The computed Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) was categorized into three levels: High dietary diversity (7 or more food groups consumed), Medium dietary diversity (4 to 6 food groups consumed) and Low dietary diversity (Fewer than 4 food groups consumed). The HDDS serves as a household-level indicator of dietary variety, based on the number of distinct food groups consumed in the previous 24 h. Generally, higher HDDS scores reflect more diverse and nutritionally sufficient diets [2, 24, 29, 37, 39].

Numerous field validation studies have consistently shown that the Food Consumption Score (FCS) is a reliable and practical tool for application in developing countries [24, 30, 32]. The Food Consumption Score (FCS) is a composite indicator used to assess household-level food security by measuring the frequency of food consumption, dietary diversity, and the nutritional value of the various food groups consumed [44]. It is calculated by grouping foods into eight categories: main staples, pulses, fruits, vegetables, meat, fish, dairy, sugar and honey, and oils and fats. Each group is assigned a weight based on its nutritional value specifically: 2, 3, 1, 1, 4, 4, 0.5, and 0.5, respectively. To calculate the Food Consumption Score (FCS), the frequency of consumption for all food items within the same group is summed. If the total exceeds 8, it is recoded to a maximum value of 8. This total frequency is then multiplied by the assigned weight for that food group to produce a weighted score. Finally, the FCS is obtained by summing the weighted scores of all food groups.

According to the FCS classification, household food consumption is grouped into three categories: Poor (scores between 0 and 28), Borderline (scores from 28.5 to 42) and Acceptable (scores above 42) [44].

The Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS), developed by Coates, Swindale and Bilinsky [10], is a tool used to assess the extent of food insecurity within households. It evaluates food insecurity based on individuals’ experiences and perceptions of limited access to food. The HFIAS includes a series of questions that address various aspects of food insecurity, such as concerns about food availability, inadequate food quality and quantity, and the impacts of restricted access. Based on their responses, households are classified into four categories: food secure, mildly food insecure, moderately food insecure, and severely food insecure. Field validation studies have consistently demonstrated that the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) is both practical and effective for use in developing country contexts [8, 10, 20, 21, 26, 32, 43].

The Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) was developed to detect household behaviors that reflect insufficient food access, both in terms of quality and quantity, as well as anxiety and uncertainty about securing food. The HFIAS includes two sets of related questions designed to capture these aspects.

Occurrence Questions: These nine standardized questions are designed to assess varying levels of food insecurity across three key dimensions: food quantity, perceived food quality, and anxiety or uncertainty about food access. Respondents are asked whether specific situations occurred within the past 30 days (or four weeks), answering with either "Yes" (scored as 1) or "No" (scored as 0).

These follow-up questions ask how often the situations mentioned in the occurrence questions took place over the past 30 days. Respondents choose from four response options: "Never," "Rarely" (once or twice), "Sometimes" (three to ten times), or "Often" (more than ten times) [10].

To calculate the household food security score, responses to the nine HFIAS items were totaled, producing a score ranging from 0 to 27. A higher score indicates a greater level of vulnerability to food insecurity. Specifically, households with an HFIAS score between 0 and 1 are considered food secure. A score of 2 or higher indicates food insecurity, which is further categorized into three levels of severity: Mild food insecurity (Scores from 2 to 7), Moderate food insecurity (Scores from 8 to 14) and Severe food insecurity (Scores from 15 to 27). The HFIAS indicator classifies households into four levels of food insecurity severity: food secure, mildly food insecure, moderately food insecure, and severely food insecure. As households report increasingly severe conditions and/or experience those more frequently, their level of food insecurity rises accordingly [10].

According to the HFIAS classification, a household is considered food secure only if it responds “Never” to all nine food insecurity conditions and “Rarely” (1–2 times) to the first condition. Any other combination of responses indicates some degree of food insecurity, reflecting increasing severity as the frequency and seriousness of the conditions reported rise [10].

Reliability and validity

Before the main data collection began, a pilot test was conducted to identify and resolve potential issues related to the clarity, language, translation, structure, and ambiguity of the research instruments. The questionnaire was first reviewed by the academic advisor and subject matter experts, who assessed its validity and offered suggestions regarding item types and wording. The pilot test served not only to refine and improve understanding of the tools but also to validate their effectiveness.

To evaluate the Amharic version of the questionnaire, a field test was conducted with 50 household heads that shared similar characteristics with the actual study participants but were not included in the main sample. Feedback from this pretest was systematically gathered and reviewed. Based on the insights obtained, several revisions were made to improve the questionnaire’s validity. These adjustments included rephrasing sentences, adding new items, and modifying the format of certain questions.

To evaluate the reliability and internal consistency of the scale-based items, a Cronbach’s alpha test was conducted. The decision to retain or discard items followed the guidelines established by George and Mallery [22], which interpret Cronbach’s alpha values as follows: above 0.9 indicates excellent reliability, above 0.8 is good, above 0.7 is acceptable, above 0.6 is questionable, above 0.5 is poor, and below 0.5 is considered unacceptable. The analysis of the nine items produced a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93, signifying excellent reliability.

Data analysis

The collected qualitative and quantitative data were analyzed following convergent mixed-method data analysis techniques, allowing both data types to be collected and analyzed simultaneously before being integrated during interpretation. Descriptive statistical tools such as arithmetic mean, percentages were used to summarize household responses and Pearson correlation was used to assess the association between Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS), Food Consumption Score (FCS), and Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS).

Ethical considerations

Ethical considerations were central to the conduct of this study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Department of Geography and Environmental Studies at Kotebe University of Education (Approval No. GeES/148/2024; 08 January 2024), and all procedures were carried out in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the Kotebe University of Education Scientific Ethical Review Committee (SERC). Informed oral consent was obtained from all participants after they were clearly informed of the study’s objectives. Participants’ privacy and confidentiality were strictly protected throughout data collection, analysis, and reporting to safeguard identities and sensitive information. Particular care was taken to avoid harm or exploitation, especially among conflict-affected and vulnerable groups. Researchers engaged participants with cultural sensitivity and empathy to minimize distress or re-traumatization. The research process emphasized transparency and open communication, including sharing findings and providing feedback to community members and relevant stakeholders. In addition, ethical standards related to data storage, security, and sharing were rigorously maintained to preserve data integrity and confidentiality.

Results and discussion

A thorough examination of data obtained from sample households, key informants, focus group discussion participants and personal observations is presented below. All the surveyed households, key informants, and focus group discussion participants agreed that, in addition to the prevailing environmental and socio-economic challenges, the armed conflict has severely undermined their household food security. This is attributed to reduced agricultural production, land abandonment, destruction or loss of productive assets, market and food assistance disruptions, shortages of agricultural inputs, and the collapse of small businesses and off-farm income generating activities.

Inline to our findings, literatures reveled that armed conflict severely disrupts food systems by destroying agricultural assets, forcing families to flee, and undermining livelihoods. Since November 2020, these impacts such as the loss of crops and livestock, displacement of farming communities, and market blockades have been intensified by existing structural weaknesses like dependence on subsistence agriculture and inadequate infrastructure, leading to increased hunger and malnutrition [8, 20, 21, 23, 43]. Beyond these direct disruptions, conflict also fuels inflation and weakens national currencies, heightening exposure to environmental shocks and further restricting household access to food [43].

The war time household food security status of sample households

In order to analyze the household food security status of the sampled households, Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS), Food Consumption Score (FCS), and Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) were employed. The various facets of household food security, including food availability and usage, food access, and household behavior, were evaluated using these indicators. Moreover the link between HDDS, FCS, and HFIAS, were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficient.

Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS)

The HDDS is a measure of the number of different food groups consumed by the household, with a higher score indicating greater dietary diversity. In many parts of the world where resources are scarce, people often rely on repetitive and nutritionally poor diets [37]. Moreover, limited dietary diversity is a major public health concern in developing countries, as it affects overall nutrition and increases the risk of deficiencies in essential vitamins and minerals [2]. Accordingly, attempts were made to measure the diversity of food types consumed by households during the war time employing Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS).

High food prices in conflict-affected areas lead to decreased dietary diversity and heightened food insecurity [30]. The data shown in Table 1 publicized that only 5.5% of households have a high dietary diversity (7 or more food groups), 44% have a medium dietary diversity (4–6 food groups), and 50.5% have a low dietary diversity (less than 4 food groups). Furthermore, the results displayed in Fig. 2 show that the study area is mainly characterized by the high consumption of milk due to the pastoral nature of the community. This suggests that the diet in the study area is relatively concentrated on these specific food groups, rather than being highly diverse across a wider range of food groups. The emphasis on milk food group in the study area may indicate a need to promote the consumption of a broader range of food items to improve the overall dietary diversity and nutritional status of the households in the region. Participants in the Key Informant Interviews and Focus Group Discussions reported that dietary patterns were repetitive and lacked variety, with limited and poor food choices mainly resulted from the heavy dependence of livestock herding and cultivation of a limited variety of crops. They also emphasized that, beyond providing food and income, livestock serve as valuable assets acting as a store of wealth, a source of credit collateral, and a crucial safety net during times of hardship.

The Key Informant Interviews and Focus Group Discussions indicated that pastoralist communities face heightened vulnerability during periods of conflict because their livelihoods depend on mobility and climate‑sensitive resources, both of which are easily disrupted by insecurity, restricted access to grazing areas, and the loss of livestock. Participants further explained that conflict affects them not only through human and animal casualties, but also by limiting their ability to move freely, which in turn hampers effective use of available pasture and water sources. These disruptions frequently intensify food shortages and increase reliance on external food aid.

Studies conducted in diverse settings reinforce the validity of our findings presented in Table 1; Kafando and Sakurai [30] demonstrate that armed conflict in Burkina Faso reduces dietary variety and severely disrupts agricultural supply chains. A study by WFP and CSA [46] also exposed that about 41% of households in the region consumed three or fewer food groups. An empirical study by Aliyo, Golicha, and Fikrie [2] found that only 19% of households had adequate dietary diversity (consuming seven or more food groups),while 81% experienced inadequate dietary diversity 20% falling in the low category (three or fewer food groups) and 61% in the medium range (four to six food groups). Among those with adequate dietary diversity, 69.5% belonged to the rich wealth index, 17.1% to the middle, and 13.4% to the poor category. The study further revealed that the most commonly consumed food groups in the area were cereals and grains (96.4%), condiments such as salt and beverages like tea (84.3%), and milk and dairy products (80%). Fish and other seafood are among the food groups that are not consumed in the study area [2]. This may lead to a decrease in nutrient intake, as no single food group provides all the essential nutrients needed for the body to function and perform optimally [37]. Dependence on a few number of food groups can significantly heighten vulnerability to conflict-related disruptions. When diets are dominated by few food categories, any interruption in supply chains due to armed conflict can lead to severe food insecurity. This underscores the importance of promoting crop diversification and supporting local production systems that offer a range of food options.

Food Consumption Score (FCS)

The Food Consumption Score (FCS) is a composite statistic that assesses the relative nutritional value of the various food types consumed, the frequency of food consumption, and dietary diversity at the household level. Employing FCS, as displayed in Table 2 it is found that 85.5% of households have an acceptable FCS, while 14.5% have a borderline FCS. This suggests that the majority of households have access to a diverse and nutritious diet, corroborating the earlier finding that severe food insecurity is uncommon. For households reliant on pastoral livelihoods, qualitative data further indicate that acceptable FCS scores may reflect short‑term coping rather than sustained food security. Several participants described selling livestock or drawing on social networks to maintain food consumption during conflict, strategies that help preserve dietary frequency in the short term but may undermine resilience over time. One pastoralist noted, “When the conflict started, we could not move with our animals. Some were stolen, and some died. Selling livestock to buy food became very difficult.” Similarly, focus group discussions highlighted how conflict constrained dietary choices despite regular food intake. One participant noted, “We manage to eat, but we eat what is available, not what is healthy. The markets are far, and during fighting, you cannot travel.” This highlights how pastoral communities may appear nutritionally secure according to FCS indicators while experiencing heightened long‑term vulnerability during conflict.

Similarly, oils and sugar/honey also show high mean values (6.52), indicating substantial consumption of calorie-dense foods. Qualitative accounts suggest that these foods are often prioritized because they are relatively shelf-stable, affordable, and perceived as providing immediate energy during times of stress. One focus group participant explained, “We buy oil and sugar because they last longer, and when there is little food, they help everyone eat something.” While these foods may support short-term energy needs, reliance on them may also reflect constrained dietary choices during conflict.

In contrast, vegetables (excluding potatoes) and fruits have very low mean values (0.24 and 0.26, respectively), indicating minimal consumption of these nutrient-dense food groups. Qualitative narratives highlight that limited market access, insecurity, and high prices restrict households’ ability to obtain fresh produce. As one participant described, “Vegetables are only found in the town, and during fighting, we cannot go there. Even if they are available, they are too expensive.” These findings suggest that conflict exacerbates existing barriers to dietary diversity, particularly for foods that require frequent market access.

Pulses and legumes/nuts also exhibit relatively low mean consumption (1.50), suggesting that they play a limited role in household diets despite their nutritional value. Interview data indicate that these foods are often deprioritized due to cost, availability, or unfamiliarity within pastoral food traditions. One respondent stated, “We know beans are good, but we do not eat them often because they are not common here, and buying them is difficult.” Together, these quantitative and qualitative findings suggest that while pastoral households may maintain caloric intake through dairy and energy-dense foods, conflict significantly constrains access to fruits, vegetables, and plant-based protein sources, raising concerns about dietary quality and long-term nutritional adequacy.

Overall, the data highlights both positive and concerning aspects of the households’ dietary patterns. The high consumption of dairy products and the relatively moderate intake of staples and animal protein suggest some level of food security, but the low consumption of fruits and vegetables, as well as the high intake of oils and sugars, may indicate potential nutritional imbalances and the need for interventions to promote a more diverse and balanced diet.

Consistent to our findings, an empirical study by Getaneh et al. [24] found that only 20.74% of food insecure respondents and 24.17% of food secure respondents fell within the acceptable food consumption score range. This suggests that most surveyed households were in the moderate or borderline categories based on their food consumption score. A study conducted by Bezabih, Belay, and Bantigegn [8] revealed that during the conflict, only 28.1% of households were classified as borderline food secure, while just 5.4% were considered to have acceptable food security. Similarly, the World Food Programme’s (2022) assessment of food security during the Tigray conflict reported that 83% of households in the region were experiencing food insecurity. Kafando and Sakurai [30] found that escalating armed conflict in Burkina Faso negatively affected the Food Consumption Score.

Household food insecurity access scale

For the purpose of creating suitable policy and program alternatives, food security must be measured at the national, regional, community, household, and individual levels [6]. In this study, household were divided into four severity categories of household food insecurity: food secure, mild, moderately, and severely food insecure. This categorization was based on the responses to the nine HFIAS questions and their frequency of occurrence over the previous 30 days.

According to the findings shown in Table 4, the HFIAS score ranges from 0 to 27, with a higher score indicating greater vulnerability to food insecurity. The data shows that 17.2% of households are food secure (score 0–1), 61.5% are mildly food insecure (score 2–7), 21.3% are moderately food insecure (score 8–14), and there are no severely food insecure households (score 15–27).

Qualitative data provide important context for this pattern. Key informant interviews and focus group discussions suggested that households often adopt coping strategies that allow them to avoid outright hunger, even when food options are limited. As one focus group participant explained, “We may not eat the foods we prefer, but we make sure there is something for the family, even if it is only milk or staples.” This suggests that while dietary quality and diversity may be compromised, households are able to buffer against severe food shortages through adaptive practices rooted in pastoral livelihoods and social support mechanisms.

Participants further emphasized that food insecurity in the study area is shaped not only by armed conflict but also by a combination of environmental and socio-economic factors. Key factors identified included large household size, low or unstable monthly income, limited engagement in non-farm income-generating activities, and variation in total livestock holdings. A key informant noted, “Families with more animals can manage better during difficult times, but those with fewer livestock suffer first when conflict affects movement and markets.” Together, these qualitative insights help explain why moderate food insecurity is prevalent while extreme hunger remains uncommon, highlighting the role of livelihood assets and coping strategies in shaping household food security outcomes.

Consistent with our findings, Weldegiargis et al. [43], Araya and Lee [3] identified that 84.6% and 77.38% of households in the conflict affected Tigray region of Ethiopia were food insecure respectively. Moreover, empirical studies by [1, 4, 8, 20, 21, 24, 26, 28, 40, 45] also assured the significant level of food insecurity among households in conflict affected, dry land and pastoral regions of Ethiopia.

The relationship between conflict and food insecurity has been widely documented in various conflict affected regions. Numerous studies have highlighted how armed conflict disrupts livelihoods, damages infrastructure, and limits access to essential services, thereby exacerbating food insecurity. Barakat, Milton, and Elkahlout [7] reported that during the Gaza siege, civilians endured extreme hardship characterized by food shortages, disease outbreaks, and general insecurity. Similarly, Swesi, El-Anis and Islam [41] observed that in conflict-affected Libya, prolonged warfare and road closures caused significant job and income losses among the population.

These empirical findings are consistent with the results of the present study, which also supports the inverse relationship between food insecurity and conflict. The evidence suggests that as conflict intensifies; food security deteriorates, reinforcing the cyclical nature of violence and deprivation in fragile contexts.

A composite household food insecurity indicator

As clearly displayed in Table 6, the findings from the three different household food insecurity measures paint a complex picture of the household food security situation. For the HFIAS measure, the results indicate that the vast majority (82.8%) of the sample households are classified as "Food insecure", while only 17.2% are considered as "Food Secure". This suggests that a significant proportion of the population is experiencing challenges in accessing and acquiring adequate food, which could have serious implications for their overall well-being and nutritional status.

In contrast, the FCS measure shows a very different distribution, with only 14.5% of the samples classified as "Food Insecure" and the majorities (85.5%) are considered as "Food Secure". This discrepancy highlights the fact that the FCS measures capture a different aspect of food security, potentially focusing more on the diversity and quality of the food consumed, rather than just access to food. Similar to our findings a study by Maxwell, Coates and Vaiatla [35] and Kerbo, Degaga and Beyene [32] also revealed that compared to FCS, the more stringent HFIAS indicator identified a higher proportion of households as food insecure.

The HHDD measure presents a more balanced picture, with roughly half of the sample population (50.3%) classified as "Food Insecure" and the other half (49.7%) as "Food Secure". This metric likely provides insights into the diversity of the household’s dietary intake, which can be an important indicator of nutritional adequacy. As displayed in Table 6 the differences observed across these three household food security measures underscore the importance of using a multifaceted approach to assess the food security situation within a population. Each measure captures a different aspect of food security, and by considering the results from all three, researchers and policymakers can gain a more comprehensive understanding of the challenges faced by the community.

In summary, the findings from the three household food security measures highlight the complexities involved in assessing food security and the importance of adopting a multidimensional approach to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the issue. This information can serve as a valuable foundation for designing and implementing effective strategies such as promoting small scale irrigation practices, water harvesting schemes and livelihood diversification strategies to address food insecurity in the target population.

Following the work of Mengesha [39] and Kerbo, Degaga and Beyene [32] Pearson correlation coefficient was employed to assess the link between different household food security measures.

The Pearson correlation coefficient between HDDS and FCS shown in Table 7 is computed to be 0.037, with a p-value of 0.485. This indicates that there is a weak, positive, and statistically non-significant correlation between HDDS and FCS. In other words, the household’s dietary diversity is not strongly associated with its food consumption score. The Pearson correlation coefficient between HDDS and HFIAS is −0.092, with a p-value of 0.080. This suggests a weak, negative, and statistically non-significant correlation between HDDS and HFIAS. This implies that as the household’s dietary diversity increases, the household’s food insecurity level (measured by HFIAS) tends to decrease, but the relationship is not statistically significant. Moreover the same table further reveals that the Pearson correlation coefficient between FCS and HFIAS is 0.044, with a p-value of 0.405. This indicates a weak, positive, and statistically non-significant correlation between FCS and HFIAS. In other words, the household’s food consumption score is not strongly associated with its food insecurity level. In summary, as displayed in Table 7 the correlation analysis suggests that the three indicators (HDDS, FCS, and HFIAS) are moderately correlated with each other. The relationships, though in the expected directions, are moderate and statistically non-significant. This implies that these indicators may be capturing different dimensions of food security and that they should be considered as complementary measures rather than interchangeable. It is discovered that these correlation results on the effectiveness of particular indicators are in line with the earlier empirical discoveries. Mengesha [39] found a strong and positive association (0.828) between HDDS and FCS using the Pearson correlation matrix. The same study’s additional findings showed that there was a moderate connection (0.402) between FCS and HDDS and Net Available Food (NAF).

It is important to note that lack of strong correlations does not necessarily mean that the indicators are unrelated or unimportant. The complex and multifaceted nature of food security may require a comprehensive assessment using multiple indicators to capture its different dimensions. Further research and analysis may be needed to understand the relationships between these indicators and their respective contributions to the overall assessment of food security within the study population.

The cross-sectional nature of this study, along with the lack of pre-conflict baseline data, restricted the application of more advanced statistical techniques that could have yielded clearer causal estimates. Consequently, the study cannot establish definitive cause-and-effect relationships, and the possibility of interview-related bias cannot be ruled out.

Conclusion

This study assessed the food insecurity status of conflict-affected households in the Gulina district using a mixed-methods approach and multiple measurement tools, including the HFIAS, FCS, and HDDS. The findings revealed a severe level of household food insecurity, with 82.79% of surveyed households classified as food insecure. The dietary assessment further indicated that households rely heavily on milk and, to a moderate extent, on staple foods and animal protein, while consumption of fruits and vegetables remains low largely reflecting the pastoral livelihood patterns of the community.

The results underscore the profound effect of the conflict on household food security, as disrupted livelihoods, constrained market access, and weakened social support systems have collectively eroded the community’s resilience. Addressing these challenges requires both immediate and long-term interventions. In particular, promoting conflict resolution and peace-building efforts is essential for restoring stability and improving household food security conditions in the region.

In addition, the study recommends future longitudinal research to capture the complex and context-specific factors that shape household food security over time, including the influence of gender, age, and conflict-related dynamics. Such research will better inform targeted policy and humanitarian responses aimed at strengthening food security in conflict-affected pastoral communities like Gulina.
