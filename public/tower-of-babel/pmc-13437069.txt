The landscape of academic global surgery in Canada: a national environmental scan
Authors: Omar El Sewify, Prachikumari Patel, Shaishav Datta, Eviatar Fields, Karolina Mastalerz-Sanchez, Barbara Jemec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Global surgery has emerged as a critical domain within global health, but despite growing academic interest, the structure, scope, and organization of global surgery initiatives within Canadian academic institutions remain incompletely characterized. We sought to conduct a national environmental scan of academic global surgery initiatives in Canada, examining participant characteristics, the structure and focus of initiatives, community motivations, and future priorities.Methods:We conducted a cross-sectional, survey-based study between July 2024 and April 2025, targeting Canadian physicians, trainees, researchers, and health professionals involved in global surgery. We invited participants via professional networks, academic institutions, and the trainee network of the Canadian Global Surgery Trainees’ Alliance. The survey included both quantitative and qualitative items across 4 domains. We used descriptive statistics and thematic analysis to analyze responses.Results:Forty respondents from 10 Canadian medical schools completed the survey, representing diverse surgical specialties and career stages. Most were affiliated with university-based global surgery offices (62%). Engagement was highest in research (65%), teaching (55%), and capacity building (55%), with trainees playing a central role. Key barriers included limited funding (95%), infrastructure gaps (70%), and lack of training opportunities (54%). Motivations for involvement included commitment to health equity, education, and sustainable partnerships. Respondents also identified future priorities, including training and education, improving access to surgical resources, and enhancing mentorship and evaluation frameworks.Conclusion:This study offers an overview of Canadian global surgery efforts, revealing enthusiastic but fragmented engagement. Findings highlight opportunities to strengthen coordination, formalize training structures, and improve sustainability within global surgery programs in Canada.

Surgical care is a fundamental element of health systems worldwide, yet profound inequities in access continue to affect billions of people. More than 5 billion people lack timely access to safe and affordable surgical, obstetric, and anesthesia services, contributing to an estimated 18 million preventable deaths every year.1 This burden exceeds that of HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria combined, which depicts how essential surgical care is for global health.1,2 Accordingly, strengthening surgical care has become a critical global health priority and a key step toward advancing universal access to essential health services.

In recent decades, global surgery has moved away from short-term humanitarian missions toward long-term partnerships built on equity, sustainability, and mutual benefit. In this context, capacity building refers to efforts aimed at strengthening local systems to independently deliver safe and equitable surgical care. This may involve workforce training, mentorship, curriculum development, research collaboration, infrastructure enhancement, and quality improvement initiatives. Contemporary global surgery initiatives are increasingly structured as partnerships between higher- and lower-resourced settings, whether internationally or within underserved regions of high-income countries, through activities such as education, training, and infrastructure support. 2,3 In the United States and United Kingdom, academic institutions have established formal global surgery centres, structured training pathways, and national collaborative networks. Organizations such as the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) Global Health Research Unit on Global Surgery and the Global Surgery Student Alliance have supported coordinated research, trainee engagement, and partnership development. These structures emphasize capacity building, shared decision-making, and measurable outcomes, helping initiatives move beyond episodic engagement toward sustained impact.2 Although comparable formal national-level coordination has not developed at the same scale in Canada, trainee-led networks, such as the Canadian Global Surgery Trainees’ Alliance (CGSTA), reflect growing grassroots interests and engagement in global surgery among Canadian trainees.

A 2017 environmental scan provided an initial overview of Canadian academic global surgery efforts, identifying early growth but also substantial fragmentation.4 Since then, academic global surgery in Canada has expanded considerably, with 9 of the country’s 17 universities now housing dedicated global surgery offices, 3 of which offer formal curricula.5 Despite these developments, comprehensive data describing the scope, structure, and level of engagement across Canadian academic institutions are limited. Many initiatives have emerged organically, reflecting strong enthusiasm among trainees and faculty but also resulting in variability in visibility, coordination, and long-term sustainability.5 For medical students and residents, opportunities remain limited, with persistent gaps in mentorship, structured training, and defined paths for career development in global surgery.6

Although several academic programs and networks are active, there has been no consolidated characterization of global surgery initiatives within Canadian academic institutions. Without a clearer understanding of the current landscape, opportunities for collaboration, structured training, and program development may remain under-realized. We sought to conduct a national environmental scan of academic global surgery initiatives in Canada, examining participant characteristics, the focus of global surgery initiatives, community motivations, and future priorities.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted this cross-sectional, survey-based study as a collaboration between researchers at the University of Toronto and the CGSTA, a national, trainee-led organization committed to advancing global surgery initiatives in Canada. The research team included members with expertise in surgical education, global surgery, and qualitative research methodologies.

Participant recruitment

Eligible participants included surgeons and anesthesiologists, trainees (medical students, undergraduate and graduate students, residents, fellows), researchers, and other health professionals based in Canada who have engaged in any form of global surgery activity. We employed a purposive sampling strategy to ensure representation across geographic regions, surgical specialties, and career stages.7 We identified potential participants through local CGSTA chapters affiliated with medical universities across Canada, professional networks, Canadian surgical and global health societies, and known academic and institutional global surgery initiatives.

To enhance reach and response rates, we disseminated surveys through several complementary approaches. We distributed targeted email invitations through CGSTA leadership and affiliated university chapters, as well as through publicly available contact information for university-affiliated global surgery offices and programs. The survey was also shared via relevant social media platforms to broaden visibility. In addition, we encouraged participants to forward the survey within their professional networks (snowball sampling) to facilitate wider distribution, including to individuals and groups not directly connected to formal trainee or academic networks. Invitation emails emphasized the national scope and relevance of the study to the Canadian global surgery community. We sent 2 reminder emails throughout the study period to encourage participation. The survey was designed to be concise and completed anonymously to reduce respondent burden. Survey invitations were distributed between July 2024 and April 2025. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before survey completion.

Survey instrument

The survey consisted of 20 questions, organized into 4 thematic domains: participant demographics and training background; global surgery initiatives, capacity building, and characteristics of global surgery projects; building a community of practice and motivation and goals for global surgery; future directions, priorities, and challenges for advancing the global surgery ecosystem. The instrument included a combination of closed-ended questions (multiple choice and Likert scale) and open-ended prompts to capture both quantitative data and qualitative insights. To ensure clarity, relevance, and face validity, we pilot tested the survey internally with members of the research team (B.J., S.D.) with global surgery experience. We made revisions made on this feedback before dissemination. The final survey was administered electronically using Google Forms, allowing for anonymous participation and broad distribution across Canada (Appendix 1A, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.019425/tab-related-content).

Data analysis

We analyzed quantitative data using descriptive statistics, including frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. For open-ended responses, we conducted a thematic analysis. Two researchers (O.E.S. and P.P.) independently reviewed responses, generated initial codes, and collaboratively refined themes through iterative discussion. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. We selected representative quotations to illustrate key themes, with identifying information removed to ensure participant confidentiality. We conducted the analysis with Microsoft Excel (version 16.0).

To ensure clarity and consistency when interpreting survey responses, we applied the following operational definitions. We defined “capacity building” as activities aimed at strengthening the ability of a health system or partner organization to deliver safe, equitable, and sustainable surgical care. These include workforce training, mentorship, curriculum development, research collaboration, infrastructure enhancement, and quality improvement initiatives. “Training and education” referred to structured educational activities — such as curriculum development, trainee exchanges, workshops, and formal teaching sessions — directed toward either Canadian trainees or lower-resourced partner sites. We defined “access to surgical resources” as efforts to improve the availability of essential surgical equipment, supplies, perioperative services, or facility-related infrastructure required to deliver safe operative care. “Workforce expansion” referred to initiatives designed to increase the number, distribution, or retention of surgical, anesthesia, or perioperative personnel in any setting (Canadian or international). Finally, we defined “insufficient infrastructure” as limitations in clinical, educational, research, digital, or health system infrastructure that impeded the effectiveness or sustainability of global surgery initiatives.

Ethics approval

This study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Board at University of Toronto (no. 45856).

Results

Respondent demographics

A total of 40 individuals completed the national survey, including 13 faculty staff (32%), 4 fellows (10%), 9 residents (22%), 9 medical students (22%), and 5 participants (12%) with other roles, such as graduate students (doctoral or master’s students), and individuals involved in education or administrative roles. Respondents represented a wide range of surgical disciplines, including general surgery (n = 16, 40%), plastic and reconstructive surgery (n = 4, 10%), obstetrics and gynecology (n = 4, 10%), cardiac surgery (n = 2, 5%), neurosurgery (n = 2, 5%), orthopedic surgery (n = 1, 2%), urology (n = 1, 2%), and anesthesiology (n = 1, 2%). Nine respondents (22%) were medical students affiliated with the faculty of medicine at their respective universities. Respondents were from 10 Canadian medical schools, with the highest representation from the University of Toronto (22%), the University of British Columbia (20%), and McGill University (15%). Most respondents (62%) were affiliated with a university global surgery office (Appendix 1B).

Scope and nature of global surgery involvement

Most respondents reported engaging in global surgery through research (65%), as well as teaching and capacity-building activities (55% each). Advocacy and leadership activities were reported by 42% of participants, while 32% were involved in clinical care or mentoring roles. The duration of involvement ranged from less than 1 year to more than 20 years, with the largest proportion (35%) reporting 1 to 4 years of engagement. Respondents described collaborations with diverse types of organizations, most commonly not-for-profit organizations (42%), trainee-led global surgery groups (33%), academic institutions (31%), and professional societies (19%) (Table 1). Reported geographic engagement spanned multiple regions. North America (69%) and Africa (47%) were the most frequently cited continents. Countries of engagement included Canada (56%), the US (14%), and Rwanda (8.3%), among others (Figure 1). Multiple countries were frequently reported per respondent, reflecting the networked and multinational nature of global surgery initiatives.

Although 65% of participants reported engaging in research activities, only 32% had established a performance metric to assess the effectiveness of their global surgery initiative. Of the respondents who reported using evaluation metrics, 4 (31%) reported evaluating participant performance and learning outcomes using frameworks such as Kirkpatrick’s Four Levels. Three (23%) participants assessed program sustainability and strategic impact, often referencing published guidelines such as the sustainability checklist for global surgery partnerships.8 Cost-effectiveness and academic output (e.g., publications, alternative metrics) were reported by 2 respondents (15%). Among faculty respondents, 8 (62%) of 13 reported involving trainees in their global surgery activities. According to faculty responses, the Canadian trainees most commonly engaged in their global surgery activities were residents and fellows (54%), followed by medical students (39%). A smaller proportion of initiatives engaged trainees from lower-resourced partner settings, including residents or fellows (34%) and medical students (22%). Trainees contributed in various capacities, including education and teaching local staff or students (20%), research and scholarship (e.g., data collection, manuscript writing, project evaluation) (15%), clinical participation (15%), and program implementation or logistic coordination (15%). Participation occurred both remotely and in person.

In terms of funding support for global surgery projects, 15% of respondents indicated that their initiatives were fully funded, while 45% reported partial funding, and 40% stated that they received no funding at all. Sources of financial support varied, with private donations and philanthropic contributions being the most cited (18%), including support from organizations such as the Red Cross and Doctors Without Borders, and ad hoc donations (e.g., surgical supply shipments to Ukraine). Institutional funding, primarily through university-affiliated departments or academic centres, was also reported by 18% of participants, whereas government funding was less frequently mentioned (8%), sometimes identified indirectly through affiliated institutions.

Motivations and future goals

Participants reported diverse motivations for involvement in global surgery (Table 2), the most common being a commitment to health equity and improving access to surgical care (70%). As a respondent shared, “Everyone deserves safe surgery, no matter where they live.” Education and capacity building were also frequently cited (38%), with participants emphasizing the importance of long-term impact: “Training local teams creates sustainable change.” Others were motivated by humanitarian values (20%) and the opportunity to mentor and collaborate (15%): “Mentorship allows us to lift others as we climb.” In terms of current goals, most respondents expressed interest in capacity building in global surgery (76%), with 42% specifically highlighting mentoring the next generation of trainees. Additionally, 65% of respondents indicated a desire to engage in research collaboration, while 9 (22%) trainees reported seeking mentorship opportunities.

Future directions

When asked which areas within global surgery require greater focus and effort, respondents most frequently identified training and education (72%) and improving access to surgical resources (72%). This was followed by policy and advocacy (68%), research development (62%), and technology and innovation (61%). Additional areas highlighted included patient care guidelines (42%) and workforce expansion (35%). Respondents identified multiple barriers to effective engagement in future global surgery initiatives. Financial constraints emerged as the most common challenge, reported by 95% of participants. Insufficient infrastructure (e.g., availability of surgical instruments, supplies and essential services) was noted by 71% of participants, while 54% identified a lack of training opportunities. Additionally, 37% cited limited research support and cultural or language barriers, and 32% reported regulatory hurdles.

Discussion

This national survey provides a structured overview of Canadian global surgery initiatives, capturing engagement across a diverse group of faculty and trainees from multiple surgical specialties and institutions. Responses highlighted involvement in education, research, advocacy, and capacity building, demonstrating a dynamic landscape of Canadian global surgery activities. Although engagement spanned many specialties, certain specialties, such as orthopedics and anesthesiology, were underrepresented. This highlights opportunities for broader outreach and inclusion in future Canadian global surgery initiatives, ensuring that all disciplines and career stages are adequately represented. These findings build on an initial environmental scan published in abstract form in 2017,4 which provided the first snapshot of academic global surgery offices in Canada and highlighted considerable fragmentation and limited coordination. Although that early scan identified 7 academic institutions with emerging initiatives, it also underscored the absence of national structures and the need for collective approaches. Our current survey expands on this early work by capturing a broader group of faculty, trainees, and professionals, providing a more comprehensive and updated picture of the Canadian landscape.

Participants reported engaging in a wide array of activities, with research (65%), teaching (55%), and capacity building (55%) being the most common. Initiatives involved both domestic and international collaborations, including underserved regions within Canada and partner sites in North America and Africa. This engagement reflects a multilevel, cross-disciplinary commitment to global surgery. Trainees contributed meaningfully to education, research, clinical work, and program implementation, often participating both remotely and in person. Notably, only 32% of respondents reported that the initiatives in which they were involved used formal evaluation metrics, which primarily focused on participant performance, program sustainability, and cost-effectiveness. This gap highlights an opportunity to strengthen structured monitoring and evaluation across initiatives to support long-term impact.

Trainees are often central to global surgery initiatives, frequently driving interest and engagement in global health and surgical activities,9 a pattern reflected in our survey findings. In Canada, CGSTA illustrates this leadership. As a national, trainee-led organization with more than 200 members and 15 active chapters affiliated with medical universities across the country, the CGSTA has created a coordinated platform that connects trainees and facilitates collaborative projects. Among trainees, motivations for engagement were largely driven by health equity (70%), education and capacity building (38%), humanitarian values (20%), and mentorship or collaboration (15%). These findings align with international trends, showing that trainees often drive enthusiasm and innovation in global surgery initiatives. However, structured training opportunities remain a substantial unmet need, as reported by more than half of respondents in our survey. In the Canadian context, opportunities for medical learners are generally restricted to a few short electives, select graduate programs, or didactic lectures in school curricula. 5 Gosselin-Tardif and colleagues10 reported that nearly half of medical students surveyed were already involved in global health research, volunteering, or initiatives, yet many were dissatisfied with existing curriculum offerings and desired greater integration of global health into their education.

Current Canadian initiatives provide some solutions. For instance, the CGSTA hosts virtual mentorship events annually to connect learners with faculty and residents, while the Canadian Association of General Surgeons offers quarterly mentorship sessions for general surgery residents and early-career surgeons. National conferences, including the Bethune Round Table and the University of Toronto’s and McGill’s global surgery conferences, also feature mentorship opportunities. Despite these efforts, access remains limited in scope and frequency, leaving many trainees without ongoing, structured guidance. In the US, 62% of residency programs report some form of global surgery training, but program directors consistently cite lack of mentorship capacity as a challenge.9 In the UK, mentorship has been embedded within multicentre collaborations such as GlobalSurg, where trainees work under senior supervision in research studies across multiple countries.11 A focus on training and education, directed toward either Canadian trainees or lower-resourced partner sites, was reported by respondents as the most important area for the future. Canada could benefit from a coordinated, longitudinal mentorship framework embedded within surgical programs or supported by national surgical institutions to fill this gap and strengthen existing efforts.

Funding was identified as the most consistent barrier. Nearly all survey participants cited financial constraints, and only 15% of initiatives were fully funded. This reliance on partial or volunteer funding has been described in global surgery more broadly.12 Even in other countries where philanthropic and governmental support is more established, funding streams remain uncertain and subject to political or economic changes. The UK NIHR Global Surgery Unit, which built partnerships across more than 120 countries, has faced instability following reductions in aid budgets.13 In Canada, where philanthropic or government commitments to global surgery are limited, the risk of projects becoming short-lived is considerable. Stable funding streams that prioritize local partner capacity and consistent exchanges are essential to avoid perpetuating dependency.

Respondents also identified other priority areas in global surgery, including improving access to surgical resources through workforce expansion and infrastructure support (72%), policy and advocacy (68%), research development (62%), and leveraging technology and innovation (61%) to advance global surgery initiatives. Compared with the early activity documented in the 2017 environmental scan,4 our results suggest meaningful growth in the diversity of institutions represented and the breadth of activities undertaken. This expansion reflects a maturing academic community and underscores the need for coordinated national strategies to support this evolving landscape. Targeting these priorities through collective, structured approaches could expand the reach and impact of Canadian global surgery initiatives, create more consistent opportunities for trainee involvement, strengthen mentorship networks, and support the development of robust evaluation frameworks to ensure sustainable and effective programs.

Limitations

The survey achieved a relatively low number of responses despite dissemination through official global surgery program administration channels, professional networks, and repeated reminders. Because there is no centralized national registry of individuals engaged in global surgery in Canada, the total number of eligible participants is unknown, and a precise response rate could not be calculated. This limitation raises the possibility of nonresponse bias, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Certain specialties were underrepresented in survey responses, despite known involvement in Canadian global surgery initiatives. This absence likely reflects sampling and engagement limitations rather than a lack of national activity. This limitation restricts the representativeness of specialty-specific perspectives and underscores that our findings may not fully reflect the breadth of global surgery engagement across disciplines. In the absence of a national database, we relied on targeted dissemination through trainee and academic networks. Although this strategy enabled outreach to individuals known to be engaged in global surgery, it may have preferentially reached highly connected or academically affiliated participants. As such, the study may overrepresent those already engaged in formal programs while underrepresenting community-based efforts, certain geographic regions, or less formally structured initiatives. Establishing a national repository of ongoing global surgery projects could further enhance coordination, reduce duplication, and foster collaboration across institutions. Additionally, all data were self-reported, introducing the potential for recall bias and social desirability bias. The cross-sectional design precluded assessment of changes in engagement over time or evaluation of causal relationships. Finally, only a subset of initiatives reported using formal performance metrics, limiting our ability to objectively assess the effectiveness and sustainability of reported activities.

Conclusion

This survey provides a structured overview of Canadian engagement in global surgery, highlighting both the diversity of initiatives and areas for growth. Although Canadian academic global surgery has seen substantial growth and increasing trainee engagement across education, research, and capacity-building activities, coordinated national efforts are needed to maximize impact. More formalized pathways for trainee involvement, structured mentorship, and strategic coordination can help consolidate these initiatives, ensuring sustainable development and enhancing both domestic and international contributions to global surgery.
