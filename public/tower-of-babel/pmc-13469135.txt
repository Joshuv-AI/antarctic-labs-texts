A novel hybrid SEPIC–Landsman bidirectional converter for renewable-energy-based EV charging
Authors: Hema S, Ramya Rajendran, M A Inayathullaah, Baswaraju Swathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Charging stations powered by renewable energy are foundational for fostering sustainable transportation by reducing the strain on fossil fuel reserves amid rising energy needs. Thereby, incorporating Renewable Energy Sources (RESs) like Photovoltaic (PV) systems and Wind Energy Conversion Systems (WECS) into Electric Vehicle (EV) charging stations ensure sustained powering facilities, reduced environmental fragilities, and enhanced grid utilities. However, the efficient functioning of these systems depends heavily on the DC-DC converter, which regulates power flow, stabilizes voltage, and adapts to dynamic energy sources. Hence, a novel Hybrid SEPIC–Landsman Bidirectional Converter (HSLBC), which combines the high boosting capability of the SEPIC configuration with the continuous current output characteristics of the Landsman converter, is presented in this work. To ensure effective operation, the converter is equipped with an advanced closed-loop control system utilizing Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) optimized by Chicken Swarm Algorithm (CSA). The Doubly Fed Induction Generator (DFIG) based WECS is interfaced to the DC-link via a PWM rectifier. The intermittency of both these variable power sources is addressed through grid integration in the proposed system. Experimental and simulation results confirm the converter’s functionality, showcasing reduced THD, high energy efficiency, and reliable handling of power flow.

Introduction

In today’s emerging world, transportation is an essential thing which consumes more fossil fuel resulting in immense pollution with massive discharge of greenhouse gases. Hence to preserve the environment, government has taken several steps and one among them is the usage of Electric Vehicles (EV). As the utilization of EV increases, there is a necessity that additional charging stations are to be built with an uninterruptible supply in consideration with environmental preservation. If the existing grid connected charging stations are extended with alternate energy generation sources, it is possible for delivering energy uninterruptedly. There exist many alternate energy resources like solar, hydro and wind; these resources are only region specific, in which the hydro is beneficial for hilly terrains whereas wind is beneficial for coastal terrains. But PV is available throughout the year over any terrain, either coastal or hilly1–4. Hence integration of PV with charging stations has gained more attention now a days. While using PV, DC-DC converters are mandatory for boosting the PV voltage and while using battery, bidirectional converters are mandatory for charging/discharging. The utilization of two converters in the system makes the system complex as well as it requires separate control techniques5. In this paper, a novel converter is designed which performs two functions like boosting the PV voltage and also the charging/discharging of battery.

There are several converters utilized for boosting the oscillating PV voltage, which are discussed as follows: Boost converter is the conventionally employed converter in which the construction is simpler with a less boosting factor. Converters like buck-boost, Cuk and SEPIC are also employed for PV systems, in which the buck-boost converter has discontinuous output current requiring complex sensing circuits, whereas SEPIC converter has continuous input current delivering a discontinuous output current6–8. The performance of the SEPIC converter is further improvised by incorporating a converter which has continuous output current9, and in this work, Landsman converter is incorporated with SEPIC converter as it has continuous output current. Moreover, the proposed hybrid converter features bidirectional functionality. Recent advancements in bidirectional converter topologies are thoroughly reviewed in Table 1. The proposed HSLBC ensures high boosting capability, continuous current flow, smooth bidirectional energy transfer, and enhanced scalability, addressing limitations like current stress, ripple, and parasitic sensitivity. The output of the PV array varies whenever there occur climatic variations, hence it is necessary to use proper algorithms to extract the maximum power. Conventional Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) techniques like P&O, Incremental Conductance along with intelligent based Fuzzy are the commonly employed methods which results in inefficient tracking under quick changes in irradiance; hence some other technologies have to be added with these algorithms for better steady state behavior16,17. Though the MPPT techniques performs well and tracks the maximum PV power, it is possible for controlling the converter’s output voltage by implementing closed loop control also. Generally, a PI controller is used for closed loop control as it eliminates peak overshoot problem providing a faster settling time18 along with appropriate control algorithms. The control algorithms are chosen in the concern of easy implementation with smooth execution over the non-linearity that occurs in the system. Better performance of the system is guaranteed by utilizing optimization algorithms for tuning the PI controller parameters. In many EV applications, Fuzzy PI control, neural network control and sliding mode control have been used which provides suitable results but in some cases these controls fail to provide optimum results19,20. Hence for accurate and faster response, in this research, a hybrid algorithm is used for controlling link-voltage. Though PV is accessible throughout the year, during night time there is a possibility that the energy from PV becomes insufficient and so there is a need to integrate some other energy resources with PV. Wind is one of the promising energy resources which is used in addition with PV26–28. In all the grid connected inverter systems, grid synchronization plays a crucial role because the efficient control is guaranteed only when the grid and the inverter voltages are synchronized. Hence a control algorithm is necessary for grid synchronization. Conventionally, Kalman filters, phase locked loops (PLL) are employed for synchronizing the voltage in both single and three phase systems29–31. Due to the simplicity and easy implementation capability, PLL along with d-q theory is utilized in this work.

Proposed system description

As the utilization of EV has increased, there is a necessity to increase the count of EV charging stations. While designing the charging stations care must be taken that the EVs are to be charged any time irrespective of the available resources. Hence a grid tied charging station with PV and WECS setup is framed as highlighted in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Proposed system description.

Proposed system description.

In this work a novel converter which works in bidirectional mode with high boosting ability is introduced. SEPIC converter is integrated with Landsman converter which has high boosting ability. The charging station’s battery gets charged from the PV whenever PV is available and when there is no PV the battery gets charged from the grid. When both PV and grid connectivity is unavailable, the WECS is utilized for charging the EVs in the charging station. In accordance to PV output, there occurs variations in the dc-link voltage and ANFIS-CS algorithm is utilized for retaining the link voltage without any peak overshoot problems. The PV excess power is given to grid through inverter and it is necessary to synchronize the inverter voltage with the grid voltage. Hence d-q theory based grid synchronization is implemented and the synchronized voltage is fed to the grid. It is also possible to run a grid connected electric train by this proposed work.

The renewable sources are assigned with the highest priority under normal conditions. In this case, PV and WECS supply the EV charging demand through the DC-link whereas the battery acts as a buffer for absorbing short-term power mismatches. When sudden drop occurs in renewable generation, the HSLBC switches between SEPIC and Landsman operating modes for stabilizing the voltage. When the battery reaches predefined SoC limits and if the generation of renewable source is insufficient, the grid is engaged smoothly through controlled power exchange for maintaining charging continuity. In contrast, depending on the grid availability and SoC, excess renewable energy is stored in battery or exported to the grid.

Hybrid SEPIC–Landsman bidirectional converter

A novel converter is framed for the charging/discharging purposes, in which battery charging from grid is done through the Landsman configuration (buck mode) and the excess power from PV is fed to grid through SEPIC configuration (boost mode). The power circuit of HSLBC is displayed in Fig. 3. There are two switches \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{2}$$\end{document} with anti-parallel diodes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{2}$$\end{document} respectively; in case of forward power flow, the current flowing through the inductors is positive and it is vice versa for reverse power flow. Unlike traditional SEPIC based bidirectional converters suffering from discontinuous output current and high ripple, the HSLBC assures buck operation with continuous output current and minimized ripple. Also, unlike conventional Landsman converters with restricted voltage gain, HSLBC offers improved step-up ability and regulation of voltage. Added to this, continuous inductor current is maintained in both directions of power flow resulting in reduced THD and improved efficiency.Fig. 3SEPIC–Landsman converter.

SEPIC–Landsman converter.

Generally, a single bidirectional topology with high voltage gain compromises output current continuity, charging suitability or ripple performance specifically under low-voltage, high-current battery charging conditions. In HSLBC, the SEPIC section offers high boost ability and wide input voltage variation whereas the Landsman section provides continuous output current and reduced current ripple. With this functional partitioning, each power flow direction is allowed to operate under its most suitable electrical characteristics avoiding the exposure of a single topology towards incompatible demands. However, more sophisticated control strategy and slightly increased number of passive components are regarded as the trade-offs. These factors are tackled by reduced magnetic complexity, improved dynamic performance and moderate duty-cycle operation along with enhanced reliability. This converter operates in two modes as in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Operating modes of SEPIC–Landsman converter.

The inductors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{2}$$\end{document} are given as,14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{1} = \frac{{V_{{in\left( {min} \right)}} }}{{\Delta I_{L1} \times f_{sw} }}$$\end{document}15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{2} = \frac{{\left( {1 - D} \right)V_{dc} }}{{\Delta I_{L2} \times f_{sw} }}$$\end{document}

Here, the inductor ripple current in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{2}$$\end{document} are denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta I}_{L1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta I}_{L2}$$\end{document} respectively; the minimum value of the input current is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${V}_{in\left(min\right)}$$\end{document}; the switching frequency is symbolized as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{sw}$$\end{document}. The capacitor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{2}$$\end{document} depends on the RMS current which is given as,16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{{C_{{1\left( {RMS} \right)}} }} = I_{dc} \times \sqrt {\frac{{V_{dc} + V_{D} }}{{V_{{in\left( {min} \right)}} }}}$$\end{document}

ANFIS-chicken swarm algorithm

Hybrid algorithms have gained more attention and these algorithms provide excellent characteristics like quick convergence, flexibility. Fuzzy and Neural network has been integrated as ANFIS. The reliability and speed of the algorithm relies on the training process and the selection of proper algorithm is essential. In this work, ANFIS is trained by using Chicken Swarm (CS) optimization algorithm and the algorithm is termed as ANFIS-CS algorithm.

First layer: This layer represents the fuzzification process and the membership function (MF) is given as,18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O_{1,i} = \left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {\mu A_{i} \left( x \right), i = 1,2} \\ {\mu B_{i - 2} \left( y \right), i = 3,4} \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}

Here the inputs are denoted as x and y with the linguistic labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${A}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{i-2}$$\end{document}. The MF used here is the Gaussian type which is given as,19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu A_{i} \left( x \right) = exp\left\{ { - \left[ {\left( {\frac{{x - c_{i} }}{{a_{i} }}} \right)^{2} } \right]^{{b_{i} }} } \right\}$$\end{document}

Here, the parameters of the membership functions are denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{{a}_{i},{b}_{i},{c}_{i}\right\}$$\end{document} which depends on the shape of the MFs. The variance and the center of the MF is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${a}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{i}$$\end{document} respectively and the trainable parameter is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{i}$$\end{document}.

Second layer: This layer is represented as the product layer as this layer multiplies all the incoming signals and send the product to the next layer. This layer’s output is taken as the weighting function which is given as,20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O_{2,i} = \mu A_{i} \left( x \right)*\mu B_{i} \left( y \right) = W_{i}$$\end{document}

Third layer: This layer is the normalization layer in which the product that is attained from the previous layer is normalized in each note of the layer. The normalized output is given as,21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O_{3,i} = \frac{{W_{i} }}{{w_{1} + w_{2} }}$$\end{document}

Fourth layer: This layer performs defuzzification and the nodes of this layer is adaptive, this layer’s output is expressed as,22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O_{4,i} = O_{3,i} \left( {\alpha_{i} x + \beta_{i} y + \gamma_{i} } \right)$$\end{document}

Here the linear parameters are denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha}_{i},{\beta}_{i} and {\gamma}_{i}$$\end{document}.

Fifth layer: The overall output is attained in this layer which is based on the information of the previous (fourth) layer.23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O_{5,i} = \frac{{\sum w_{i} y_{i} }}{{\sum w_{i} }}$$\end{document}

Figure 5 illustrates the structure of ANFIS. In ANFIS, EBP (Error back propagation) is performed in first layer, the learning process in this layer is used for updating the EBP parameters; and LSE (Least Square Estimator) is performed in the fourth layer for updating the parameters of this layer.Fig. 5ANFIS structure.

Theory of chicken swarm algorithm

This algorithm is framed based on the swarming behavior of chickens and each swarm has a leader rooster with hens and chicks. The leader is chosen based on the fitness value of the individual. The chicken with the best fitness is termed as the rooster whereas the chicken with the least fitness is termed as the chick and the chicken with intermediate fitness values are termed as the hens. Based on the fitness values, the chicken group is selected randomly with random hens-chick, mother–child relationship and this relationship changes for every time step. The chickens not only follow the food but also averts others from eating their food; also, the chickens have the tendency to take the good food which is already found by some other chickens whereas the chicks follow the hen (mother) which has dominant competition in food searching.

The roosters having the better fitness value has the ability to access good food source and the rooster steps is expressed as,24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{i,j}^{t + 1} = x_{i,j}^{t} *\left( {1 + rands\left( {0,\sigma^{2} } \right)} \right)$$\end{document}25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma^{2} = \left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} 1 & {f_{i} \le f_{k} } \\ {exp\left( {\frac{{f_{k} - f_{i} }}{{\left| {f_{i} } \right| + \varepsilon }}} \right)} & {otherwise} \\ \end{array} k \in \left[ {1,N} \right],k \ne i} \right.$$\end{document}

Here the index k of the rooster is chosen randomly from the rooster group, zero mean is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$rands(0,{\sigma }^{2})$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }^{2}$$\end{document} as the standard deviation and epsilon (\varepsilon ) is the small positive integer.

The rooster is followed by the hens group which is searching for food while the hens randomly scramble, compete each other for food. The dominant hen is competitive and the hen’s group is expressed as,26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{i,j}^{t + 1} = x_{i,j}^{t} + S_{1} *Rand*\left( {x_{{r_{1} ,j}}^{t} - x_{i,j}^{t} } \right) + \ldots + S_{2} *Rand*\left( {x_{{r_{2} ,j}}^{t} - x_{i,j}^{t} } \right)$$\end{document}

Here Rand is the random number which is taken from the uniformly distributed interval \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\mathrm{0,1}]$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{1}=exp\left(\frac{{f}_{i}-{f}_{{r}_{1}}}{\left|{f}_{i}\right|+\varepsilon }\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{2}=\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}({f}_{{r}_{2}}-{f}_{i})$$\end{document}; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{2}$$\end{document} are the index of the rooster and chicken respectively which is chosen randomly from the swarm; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{1}\epsilon \left[1\dots N\right], {r}_{2}\epsilon [1\dots N]$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{1}\ne {r}_{2}$$\end{document}.

The movement of chicks in seeking food is given as,27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{i,j}^{t + 1} = x_{i,j}^{t} + FL*\left( {x_{m,j}^{t} - x_{i,j}^{t} } \right)$$\end{document}

As the movement of chick is based on the mother’s position and the mother’s position of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${j}^{th}$$\end{document} chick is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{m,j}^{t}$$\end{document}; the chick’s movement is denoted as FL which is chosen randomly from 0 to 2 and it is noted that each chick has different characteristic movement.

ANFIS trained by using chicken swarm algorithm

The accuracy of ANFIS is improvised by proper training of ANFIS parameters and there are various techniques for training the ANFIS parameters like Gradient descent, Least Square estimation etc. But these techniques result in slow convergence and so optimization techniques are preferred and in this work Chicken Swarm (CS) algorithm is utilized for training the ANFIS parameters. By using this ANFIS-CS algorithm, the ANFIS parameters are trained faster and also in an easier way. The overview of ANFIS-CS is displayed in Fig. 6.Fig. 6ANFIS-CS algorithm.

ANFIS-CS algorithm.

In ANFIS, there are two types of parameters which need to be trained, one is the antecedent part parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{{a}_{i},{b}_{i},{c}_{i}\right\}$$\end{document} and the other one is the conclusion part parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{{\alpha}_{i},{\beta}_{i},{\gamma}_{i}\right\}$$\end{document}. In the first step, the parameter initialization is done randomly and then by using CS algorithm, the parameters are updated. Only one parameter set is updated for every iteration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i.e.,{a}_{i}s$$\end{document} gets updated in the first iteration, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{i}$$\end{document} gets updated in the second iteration. Once all the parameters are updated, again the first parameter is updated and so on.

Similar operating conditions and system model are considered for the generation of training dataset involving a wide range of input–output scenarios like variations in PV irradiance, fluctuations in wind speed, transitions in load and bidirectional power flow changes. The objective function is defined uniformly for all methods as the minimization of composite error index formulated using Integral of Squared Error (ISE) which is given by,28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ISE = \mathop \smallint \limits_{0}^{T} e^{2} \left( t \right)dt$$\end{document}29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Where}},\;e\left( t \right) = x_{ref} \left( t \right) - x\left( t \right)$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{ref}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}-reference signal, x(t)-actual system response.

The convergence criterion is defined by reaching the maximum number of iterations with negligible improvement in the fitness value.

Charging station

The charging station is designed for charging a load of five EVs and the charging necessity for each EV varies time to time. Hence the State of Charge (SoC) limit has to be specified by the user is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${SoC}_{limit}$$\end{document} (time needed for charging in hours h), the current SoC as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${SoC}_{c}$$\end{document} and the SoC required by the vehicle is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${SoC}_{r}$$\end{document}.28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SoC_{r} = SoC_{limit} - SoC_{c}$$\end{document}

By knowing the EV’s battery voltage with its ampere-hour rating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Ah}_{rating}$$\end{document}, the necessary energy for charging the EV (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{EV}$$\end{document}) is expressed as,29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{EV} = \frac{{SoC_{r} V_{EV} Ah_{rating} }}{100}$$\end{document}

To charge an EV in h hours, the necessary dynamic power \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{EV}$$\end{document} is given by,30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{EV} = \frac{{E_{EV} }}{h - t}$$\end{document}

Here t refers the time for which the EV has been charged already. The total power necessary for charging the EV is given as the total power which is given as,31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{tot} = \sum P_{EV}$$\end{document}

A PV array is also incorporated in the charging station (Fig. 7) from which the battery gets charged, meanwhile the surplus energy is channeled to grid through bidirectional converter32. During night time, in the unavailability of solar, the battery replenishes its energy using grid power using bidirectional SEPIC–Landsman converter. The retention of link voltage is assured using ANFIS-CS algorithm. In case, when both the solar and grid connectivity is nil, the charging station runs with the assistance of the energy produced by the WECS. The minimum SoC for the battery is taken as 20%.Fig. 7Charging station-layout.

Charging station-layout.

First mode: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{PV}>{P}_{tot}$$\end{document} and battery’s SoC is less than its limit:When the PV power exceeds the energy demand of EVs, at this instant the EVs gets charged by the PV to its maximum limit (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${SoC}_{limit}$$\end{document}) and the excess energy is directed for battery charging.

Second Mode: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{PV}>{P}_{tot}$$\end{document} and battery’s SoC is maximum:When the PV power exceeds the energy demand of EVs and the Battery’s SoC is also maximum, then excess energy is channeled to grid through bidirectional converter (SEPIC mode).

Third Mode: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{PV}=0$$\end{document} and the battery’s SoC exceeds lower threshold: If there is no PV power (during night), the EVs gets charged from the energy already stored in the battery until the SoC becomes minimum.

Fourth Mode: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{PV}=0$$\end{document} and the battery’s SoC is minimum: If there is no PV power (during night time) and when the battery’s SoC goes beyond the limit, the battery gets charged by the grid through the bidirectional converter (Landsman mode).

Fifth Mode: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{PV}<{P}_{tot}$$\end{document}: If the PV power is insufficient and grid connectivity is nil, the EVs gets charged through WECS.

The proposed system demonstrates robust and stable operation under unbalanced EV charging demand and nonlinear load conditions due to its advanced control strategy and coordinated architecture. A regulated DC-link voltage is maintained by HSLBC amidst varying load currents with the dynamic adjustment of duty cycle. With PWM based interfacing and continuous inductor current characteristics, the nonlinear load-induced harmonics are mitigated effectively. Added to this, fast correction of voltage deviations and limiting overshoot are attained with the help of ANFIS-CS controller owing to its adaptive capability factor. Therefore, the system assures reliable sharing of power and stable voltage regulation under nonlinear and highly dynamic real-world charging scenarios.

Good scalability and practical feasibility are achieved by the proposed system when applied to high power levels and fast charging applications. The HSLBC exhibits modular converter architecture and control flexibility and is highly appropriate for scaling by paralleling converter modules or increasing device ratings required for fast and ultra-fast EV chargers. Subsequently, the ANFIS-CS control can be retrained or adapted for larger systems without the need for structural variations which ensures robust performance under increased load dynamics. Thus the system is scalable and technically viable for real-world fast-charging EV infrastructure.

Conclusion

A PV based charging station with WECS as well as grid connected mode is discussed in this paper. The battery of the charging station gets charged from PV and whenever there is no PV, it is designed to get charged through the grid also. For this bidirectional operation, a novel HSLBC is utilized, in which the charging of battery from grid is done through the landsman mode; whereas the excess energy from PV is supplied to the grid through SEPIC mode. The dc-link voltage control is achieved through a PI controller and its parameters are tuned by using a hybrid ANFIS-CS algorithm. An efficient operation of the charging station and the dc-link voltage control is accomplished in this work. While nourishing this link-voltage to the grid, it is necessary that both the inverter and grid voltage are to be synchronized, which is accomplished by grid synchronization. The complete setup is implemented in MATLAB and validated through experimental setup also. The HSLBC achieves a peak converter efficiency of 97% in step-up mode and 96.8% in step-down mode. Furthermore, grid synchronization is achieved with a THD of 1.85%, ensuring high power quality.
