The preclinical pharmacokinetics of Tolinapant—A dual cIAP1/XIAP antagonist with in vivo efficacy
Authors: Vanessa Martins, George Ward, Tomoko Smyth, Mike Reader, Gianni Chessari, Chris Johnson, Nicola Wilsher
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAT‐IAP (1‐{6‐[(4‐fluorophenyl)methyl]‐3,3‐dimethyl‐1H,2H,3H‐pyrrolo[3,2‐b]pyridin‐1‐yl}‐2‐[(2R,5R)‐5‐methyl‐2‐{[(3R)‐3‐methylmorpholin‐4‐yl]methyl}piperazin‐1‐yl]ethan‐1‐one) was identified as a novel potent non‐alanine small molecule dual inhibitor of cIAP1/XIAP protein. AT‐IAP was assessed in preclinical species, demonstrating favorable bioavailability in rodent species and oral efficacy at 30 mg/kg in MDA‐MB‐231 mouse xenograft models. The major metabolic route of AT‐IAP was identified to be CYP3A driven, resulting in low oral exposure in non‐human primate (NHP) studies, given the comparatively high expression of equivalent CYP3A. AT‐IAP metabolite identification studies determined ring opening of the morpholino or piperazine moiety. An extensive campaign of optimisation resulted in increased polarity by the addition of a hydroxymethyl, which led to the identification of tolinapant (1‐(6‐[(4‐Fluorophenyl)methyl]‐5‐(hydroxymethyl)‐3,3‐dimethyl‐1 H,2 H,3 H‐pyrrolo[3,2‐ b]pyridin‐1‐yl)‐2‐[(2 R,5 R)‐5‐methyl‐2‐([(3R)‐3‐methylmorpholin‐4‐yl]methyl)piperazin‐1‐yl]ethan‐1‐one) with reduced CYP3A metabolism. Tolinapant showed oral bioavailability in rodents and NHP in the range 12–34% at 5 mg/kg. Non‐linear pharmacokinetics in NHP were observed at oral doses in the range 5–75 mg/kg. Pharmacodynamic modulation and efficacy were demonstrated in A375 mouse xenograft models at dose ranges between 5 and 20 mg/kg. On‐target engagement, as measured by reduction of cIAP 1 protein levels, was confirmed in NHP surrogate tissues and applied to target activity assessments in tolinapant phase1/2 clinical trials.

INTRODUCTION

A distinctive feature in human cancer is the evasion of programmed cell death or apoptosis, 1 which is considered to be the leading cause of resistance to current therapies. 2 The up‐regulation of inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (IAPs) is one mechanism by which cancer cells evade apoptosis. The IAP family of proteins are important regulators of cell survival in cancer and are therefore attractive targets for cancer therapies due to their role in the suppression of apoptosis and their up‐regulation in many cancers. 3 Several peptidomimetic IAP antagonists derived from the natural ligand of the N‐terminal peptide sequence AVPI (SMAC‐mimetic) have been evaluated in clinical development. 4 It has been suggested that potent and dual cIAP/XIAP antagonists are more efficient in activating apoptosis than unbalanced cIAP1 antagonists. 5 Novel potent non‐alanine small molecule inhibitors of IAP proteins have been developed using Astex's fragment‐based drug discovery (FBDD) platform Pyramid™ 6 with balanced dual cIAP1/XIAP profiles. 7 The objectives of these studies were to characterize the pharmacokinetics (PK) and drug metabolism of two IAP antagonists in vitro and in vivo, namely AT‐IAP and tolinapant. In this paper, we demonstrate the impact of the introduction of a hydroxymethyl into the core of the structure on the predominant route of metabolism and the change in PK properties and target engagement in vivo.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals and biological reagents

AT‐IAP and tolinapant were synthesized in‐house by the medicinal chemistry group at Astex Pharmaceuticals (Cambridge, UK) using published methods. 8 , 9 Liver microsomes and human Supersomes™ expressing CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP1A2, CYP2B6 and CYP2C19 were purchased from Corning Incorporated (Ewloe, UK). All substrates, inhibitors, reference compounds and NADPH were supplied by Sigma‐Aldrich (Dorset, UK). Rodent plasma was obtained from Harlan Laboratories Inc. (Shardlow, UK) and cynomolgus NHP plasma from Bioculture Ltd. (Senneville, Mauritius).

Intrinsic clearance determinations

AT‐IAP and tolinapant were incubated in duplicate at 1 μM in male CD‐1 mouse, male Sprague Dawley (SD) rat, male cynomolgus NHP and mixed gender human liver microsomes (0.25 mg/mL) or human Supersomes™ expressing CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP1A2, CYP2B6 or CYP2C19 (20 pmol/mL, except CYP2B6 incubated at 40 pmol/mL) at +37°C for 40 min in the presence of NADPH. The positive control, verapamil (microsomes), and appropriate CYP reference compounds (midazolam, bufuralol, diclofenac, ethoxyresorufin, efavirenz, omeprazole) were incubated under the same conditions. Negative control incubations, in the absence of NADPH, were performed. Incubations were sampled (50 μL) at 0, 5, 10, 20 and 40 min, except for the negative controls which were sampled at 0 and 40 min.

Metabolite identification

Tolinapant microsome samples were full scanned over a range from m/z 50–1000 using an Agilent QTOF LC/MS system. Analytes were separated by reverse‐phase chromatography using a gradient mobile phase and detected by MS in positive ionization mode. Potential metabolite peaks were identified by comparison with negative control samples and confirmed by accurate mass analysis and MS/MS fragmentation pattern scans compared to that of parent compound.

Animal husbandry and welfare

Rodent studies were performed according to the Animal (Scientific Procedures) Act (1986) law. Mouse studies were performed in‐house with mice allowed access to food and water ad libitum. Rat studies were performed by Quotient Bioresearch (Rushden, UK). NHP studies were performed at WuXi AppTec (Suzhou, China) in accordance with the Wuxi IACUC standard animal procedures along with the IACUC guidelines which comply with the Animal Welfare Act, the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, the Office of Laboratory Animal Welfare (OLAW) and local ethical reviews. NHPs were housed individually in stainless steel mesh cages in a controlled environment (temperature + 18 to +29°C, relative humidity 30%–70%) and fed twice daily. Astex is committed to the ethical use of animals and adheres to the prinicples of 3Rs.

In vivo PK studies

For intravenous administrations, AT‐IAP or tolinapant was formulated in 100% saline for all species. For oral administrations AT‐IAP or tolinapant was formulated either in 100% water for mice, 0.5% methocel (aqueous) or 100% water for rats and 0.5% methocel (aqueous) or 100% saline for NHPs. For intravenous dosing the compound was administered via the lateral tail vein at dose volumes of 1–5 mL/kg (rodent) or by a slow injection via the cephalic vein at a dose volume of 1 mL/kg (NHP). Oral administrations were by nasogastric gavage at dose volumes of 2–10 mL/kg. All doses were calculated as freebase equivalent per kg of bodyweight. PK studies were performed in male Balb/c mice (0.025 kg, 7–9 weeks old), obtained from Harlan Laboratories Inc. (Shardlow, UK), male SD rats (0.25–0.35 kg, 12–16 weeks old) (Quotient Bioresearch, Rushden, UK) and male cynomolgus NHPs (2.5–7 kg, 2–5 years old) (WuXi AppTec, Suzhou, China). Blood samples were drawn serially via the saphenous vein (mouse), lateral tail vein (rat), cardiac puncture (mouse and rat terminal samples), or the cephalic vein (NHP) and collected into tubes containing potassium EDTA. Additional blood samples were taken from NHP at 24 h following oral administration for the preparation of PBMC. Plasma was separated by centrifugation and stored at ≤ −20°C prior to bioanalysis. Non‐compartmental PK analyses were performed using Phoenix 6.3.0.395® (Certara USA, Inc.) software. Non‐compartmental PK fitting to sparse sampling data allowed the calculation of standard errors on AUClast and Cmax for mouse PK studies. For all other studies non‐compartmental PK fitting was applied to each individual animal (rat and NHP).

Bile duct cannulated study

Tolinapant, formulated in 100% saline, was administered IV to three bile‐duct cannulated male sprague dawley rats, at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Following dosing, each rat was placed in an individual metabowl for bile and urine sample collection at various time points over 24 h. All samples were collected over solid CO2 and stored at −80°C. Tolinapant was extracted from diluted samples by addition of acetonitrile containing IS followed by bioanalysis using liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LCMS).

Bioanalysis

All in vitro and in vivo samples were extracted by protein precipitation with acetonitrile containing a close structural analogue as internal standard (1:3 v/v). For quantitative studies, calibration standards and quality controls were prepared in blank matrix and extracted under the same conditions. All samples were centrifuged at 3700 rpm at +4°C for 20 min. Bioanalysis of all samples was performed using high performance LCMS with either a Quattro Ultima (Waters, UK) tandem mass spectrometer coupled to a CTC HTS PAL autosampler (CTC Analytics AG, Switzerland) and Acquity UHPLC pump (Waters, UK) or a Qtof 6150 time of flight mass spectrometer coupled to a 1200 UHPLC pump (Agilent, UK). Compounds were ionized using positive mode (ESI+) electrospray ionization. Analytes were detected using full scan or multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). The characteristic ion dissociation MRM transitions for AT‐IAP were m/z 510.3 ➔ 257.2 and 510.3 ➔ 125.1 with collision energies of −36 and ‐32 eV respectively. For tolinapant, characteristic ion dissociation MRM transitions were 540.2 ➔ 268.9 and 540.2 ➔ 125.1 with collision energies of −42 and – 36 eV respectively. Chromatographic separation was typically performed using a gradient LC method over 5 min with a 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Separation was typically achieved using an Acquity HSS T3 1.8 μM 50 × 2.1 mm column maintained at +40°C.

PBMC cIAP1 analysis

PBMC pellets were prepared for pharmacodynamic analysis by transferring approximately 1 mL of blood into a 15 mL centrifugation tube and diluted with five volumes of PBS and gently mixed by pipetting. Two volumes of Histopaque solution (2 × original blood sample) were added to a separate 15 mL tube and the diluted blood sample carefully layered on top of the Histopaque by allowing the sample to slowly run down the inside of the tube. The tubes were centrifuged at 400 × g for 30 min at room temperature and the centrifuge allowed to slow down without application of the brake. The PBMC interface was collected and transferred into a fresh 15 mL centrifugation tube, washed with 5 mL RPMI‐1640 medium and the cells pelleted by centrifugation (800 × g for 5 min). The cell pellet was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until analysis.

PBMC cell pellets were lysed by adding 200 μL ice‐cold 1 × complete Tris lysis buffer (1% Triton X‐100, 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris.HCl pH 7.5, plus protease inhibitors (Roche, complete mini, one tablet/10 mL), 50 mM NaF and 1 mM Na3CO4). After mixing and standing on ice for 30 min, lysates were centrifuged (14 000 rpm, 15 min, +4°C) and an aliquot of the supernatant removed for protein determination (BCA assay – Pierce, Thermo Fisher; cat. no. 23225). Measurement of cIAP1 levels in the PBMC lysates was performed using streptavidin‐coated Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) plates which had been pre‐coated with biotinylated goat anti‐cIAP1 polyclonal antibody (R&D Systems, cat. no. AF8181). Equivalent amounts of total protein (100 μg) were incubated overnight at +4°C with shaking on the MSD plates. After washing, detection of levels of cIAP1 bound was achieved by incubating the plates with sulfo‐tagged anti‐cIAP1 polyclonal antibody (R&D Systems, cat no. AF8181) for 2 h at room temperature with shaking, followed by washing and reading the plates in the presence of MSD read buffer.

RESULTS

In vitro intrinsic clearance

AT‐IAP and tolinapant demonstrated species differences in intrinsic clearance, with rodents having the lowest turnover (Table 1). The intrinsic clearance of tolinapant in NHP and human liver microsomes was significantly lower than that of AT‐IAP (p < .001). Incubations in human Supersomes™ expressing CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP1A2, CYP2B6 or CYP2C19 identified AT‐IAP and tolinapant as substrates of CYP3A4 with intrinsic clearances of 2.8 ± 0.6 and 1.1 ± 0.16 μL/min/pmol, respectively. Intrinsic clearances on incubation with other CYPs were not measurable (<0.5 μL/min/pmol) for either AT‐IAP or tolinapant (data not shown).

Metabolite identification

Several oxidative metabolites of tolinapant were detected following the incubation with hepatic microsomes (Figure 1). Based on MS response, the main metabolite(s) resulted from addition of a hydroxy group or oxidation of the hydroxymethyl group. Structural assignment was based on accurate mass MS and co‐elution with an authentic standard. Accurate mass analysis indicated that these metabolites underwent further oxidation to form the subsequent carboxylic acid or combinations of hydroxylation's. Further structural elucidation of other detected metabolites was not undertaken. Based on accurate mass analysis of detected metabolites, there was no evidence of opening of the morpholine ring.

PKs in preclinical species

There was no significant difference in the total clearance of AT‐IAP and tolinapant following IV administration to NHPs (Table 2). However, tolinapant achieved significant (p < .05) 7‐fold increase in exposure (AUClast) in NHPs following a single 5 mg/kg oral dose compared to AT‐IAP (Table 3; Figure 2). Whilst a concomitant increase in Cmax was observed, the 6‐fold change was not statistically significant due to inter‐individual variability. Following increasing oral doses to NHPs, the exposure of AT‐IAP and tolinapant showed a greater than dose proportional linear increase (Figure 3). Low oral AT‐IAP bioavailability was observed up to 30 mg/kg, whilst tolinapant demonstrated increasing bioavailability with increasing dose (Table 4).

Nonclinical PD modulation

Pharmacological activity was evaluated by semi‐quantitation of cIAP1 degradation in PBMCs isolated from NHP blood following once daily oral administration of tolinapant for 7 or 14 days. The cIAP1 protein levels were readily detected in PBMC lysates using a cIAP1 immunoassay set up on the Mesoscale Discovery (MSD) platform. Tolinapant binding to cIAP1 leads to auto‐ubiquitination of the target protein followed by degradation via the proteasome. Following daily administration of 30, 45 or 100 mg/kg there was a complete reduction in cIAP1 levels to background levels measurable in the PBMC lysates via the MSD cIAP1 assay, at either day 8 or day 15 (Figure 4). PD modulation of cIAP1 target engagement was therefore successfully achieved using NHP PBMC lysates using this assay.

Measurement of relative cIAP1 levels by MSD assay in PBMC lysates prepared at the indicated time after the indicated dose of tolinapant. PBMC pellets were prepared for pharmacodynamic analysis from whole blood by density gradient centrifugation (Histopaque). PBMC lysates were prepared in Triton‐X100 lysis buffer and normalized by total protein assay (BCA assay). Equivalent amounts of total protein (100 μg) were analyzed by immunoassay (MSD platform) using biotinylated and sulfo‐tagged anti‐cIAP1 capture and detection antibodies.

DISCUSSION

AT‐IAP was identified using fragment‐based drug design as a first in class non‐peptidomimetic potent dual cIAP1/XIAP antagonist. 7 , 9 AT‐IAP was a potent antagonist of cIAP1 (0.32 nM) and XIAP (5.1 nM), with a GI50 of 4.4 nM in MDA‐MB‐231 cells. 9 The piperazine group was critical to optimisation of the dual potency against cIAP and XIAP. 7 AT‐IAP was orally bioavailable in mouse, showing PD modulation following a single dose and efficacy following repeat once daily oral administration at 30 mg/kg in an MDA‐MB‐231 xenograft models. 9

Dogs are known to be sensitive to the downstream effects of IAP antagonists causing a so‐called ‘cytokine storm’. 11 NHP was therefore selected as the non‐rodent preclinical toxicology species. The bioavailability of AT‐IAP following oral administration to NHP indicated that achieving appropriate oral exposure to explore the toxicological profile would be challenging. In vitro CYP phenotyping studies indicated AT‐IAP was predominantly metabolized by CYP3A4. It has previously been established that NHPs have high expression of their equivalent CYP, namely CYP3A8, in the small intestines. 12 Furthermore, the turnover in NHP hepatic microsomes indicated that AT‐IAP would undergo significant hepatic first pass effect. The bioavailabilities of CYP3A4/CYP3A8 substrates in this preclinical model were therefore likely to be limited by an intestinal first pass effect. 12

AT‐IAP represented a structurally novel, potent, and in vivo active advanced lead compound for further optimisation focusing on reduction of metabolism in CYP3A4 phenotyping studies by the introduction of polarity. 8 Putative structural assignment of metabolites of AT‐IAP detected by LCMSMS indicated that ring opening of the morpholino group was the major metabolite formed in vitro, 8 compound design therefore focused on modification of the morpholine moiety and introduction of polarity. The introduction of a hydroxymethyl group in the azaindoline heterobicycle led to the identification of tolinapant with increased metabolic stability in the CYP3A4 phenotyping assay. Furthermore, the intrinsic clearance in NHP hepatic microsomes was reduced compared to AT‐IAP. The excretion and metabolism of tolinapant was investigated. In rat, 34% of the dose was eliminated as unchanged parent compound, indicating that metabolism was also an important route of clearance. Tolinapant underwent in vitro metabolism by hepatic microsomes and the metabolites detected indicated that metabolism was predominantly oxidation of the hydroxymethyl. In contrast to AT‐IAP, ring opening of the morpholino or piperazine groups was not detected.

Preclinically in mouse, tolinapant demonstrated both PD modulation and efficacy in A375 xenograft models at doses levels between 5 and 20 mg/kg. 13 Tolinapant was orally bioavailable in mouse, rat and NHP ranging between 12% and 34% and IV clearances of less than 50% liver blood flow. 8 Further evaluation of the exposures in NHP following oral administration showed that the exposure increased on dose escalation in a greater than dose proportional manner. The oral exposures of tolinapant achieved were sufficient to allow further exploration of the pharmacology and toxicology in this species. Tolinapant was shown to cause a reduction in cIAP1 protein levels in PBMCs following oral dosing. This assay gave a sensitive determination of on target cIAP1 engagement by tolinapant leading to rapid auto‐ubiquitination of cIAP1 and subsequent degradation by the proteasome in a surrogate tissue (PBMCs) and has subsequently been used as the clinical determination of on‐target activity by tolinapant in phase 1/2 clinical trials. 14

SIGNIFICANCE STATEMENT

This work demonstrates the importance of utilizing metabolite identification early in drug discovery to inform drug design and overcome metabolic liabilities. Identifying the site of metabolism of AT‐IAP allowed a campaign of further compound optimisation, focusing on the reduction of CYP3A4 metabolism, to identify the clinical candidate tolinapant.
