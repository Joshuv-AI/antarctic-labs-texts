A Qualitative Needs Assessment for Pediatric Care Partnerships in Community Emergency Departments
Authors: Rajesh K Daftary, Ashley A Foster, Anneka Hooft, Chris Baker, Ryman Crone, Mimi Lu, Sara Malakzay, Inder Narula, Sonny Tat, Shruti Kant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMost pediatric emergency care in the United States occurs within community emergency departments (CEDs). CEDs often rely on pediatric academic medical centers (PAMCs) for specialty consultation and care continuation. These collaborations may be conduits for CEDs to enhance delivery of pediatric care. We sought to explore the experiences of CEDs with the provision of pediatric care and their perspectives on ways a PAMC can provide support.MethodsWe used phenomenological qualitative techniques to explore lived experiences of health care leaders of CEDs. We purposefully sampled nurse and physician leaders from CEDs with varying pediatric volumes, rural and nonrural designations, and proximity to a PAMC. In semistructured interviews, we explored their experiences of caring for children and views on current and desired PAMC support. We used reflexive thematic analysis to identify themes.ResultsWe interviewed 15 nurse and 13 physician leaders representing 13 CEDs. Four major themes were identified: (1) existing pediatric infrastructure impacts care, (2) caring for pediatric patients has a unique emotional and psychological impact, (3) increased PAMC support for CEDs is desired, and (4) expedient interfacility transfer is valued, but challenges in execution exist. Participants cited loss of inhouse pediatric-specific support as challenging and desired partnerships with PAMCs to improve care via linkage to subspecialty care, facilitation of timely transfer, and educational support.ConclusionED leaders at CEDs value the ability to deliver high-quality pediatric care in their settings but often encounter barriers. PAMCs can enhance partnerships with CEDs and help advance optimal pediatric emergency care delivery in these settings.Keywords: pediatric emergency medicine, rural hospitals, emergency medical services, telemedicine, pediatric hospitals, child, quality improvement, ethical dilemmas, patient transfer

The Bottom Line

Although community emergency departments provide 85% of pediatric emergency visits in the United States, their access to pediatric resources varies. Their experiences of providing pediatric care and optimal approaches for pediatric academic medical centers to support their efforts remain poorly understood. Using interviews with leaders in community emergency departments, we identified key areas where these departments face challenges in caring for children and how pediatric academic medical centers can help overcome them.

Introduction

Background

In the United States (US), over 80% of children receive initial care in community emergency departments (CEDs), not in pediatric academic medical centers (PAMCs).1 CEDs are, therefore, critical to frontline pediatric emergency care, despite having variable access to pediatric-specific resources and specialist support.2, 3, 4, 5 Efforts, such as the National Pediatric Readiness Project (NPRP), have linked advanced preparation to improved survival outcomes for CEDs caring for critically ill and injured children.6, 7, 8, 9 These elements of preparation, or “Pediatric Readiness,” include the presence of a pediatric emergency care coordinator (PECC) and pediatric-appropriate equipment, medications, and policies.2,3,10,11 Although such guidance outlines a path toward optimizing pediatric care, CEDs have had varying success in implementation, as evidenced by the range of readiness identified in recent national surveys.3,11,12

Importance

Recent pediatric inpatient closures (19.1%) and loss of skilled personnel since the COVID-19 pandemic illustrate mounting challenges encountered by CEDs caring for children.13, 14, 15 Loss of pediatric resources correlates with lower Pediatric Readiness, furthering concerns about a loss of care capability in community hospital settings.16 When children cannot access care in their communities, it results in delayed care,17 worse outcomes,18 and increased strain on families, required to seek care elsewhere, often in the urban centers where most PAMCs reside.19,20 When pediatric resources are not available locally, CEDs may interface with PAMCs for support, including educational interactions and/or specialty consultation related to clinical decision-making, transfer, admission, or follow-up care.

Goals of This Investigation

Currently, limited data are available on the perspectives of physician and nurse leaders in CEDs regarding pediatric care and their perceptions of partnerships with PAMCs. Therefore, we sought to understand the experience of physician and nurse leadership overseeing pediatric emergency care in CED settings, their perspectives on current support from PAMCs, and what barriers and opportunities exist in improving care delivery.

Methods

Study Design and Participant Recruitment

We conducted a qualitative descriptive study using semistructured interviews and inductive thematic analysis. We identified potential CEDs by reviewing 12-month (Fiscal Year 2023) interfacility transfer data from CEDs to an ED at either of the 2 children’s hospitals within the study PAMC in Northern California. Cross-referencing this list with a state-wide database containing ED encounter data for 2022,21 enabled purposive sampling of CEDs with rural and nonrural designations, those in the same or contiguous counties to the PAMC (near) vs those more than one county away from the PAMC (far), and those representing differing annual pediatric ED volume categories, previously defined in Pediatric Readiness literature (low < 1800 patients, medium = 1800-4999 patients, medium-high = 5000-9999 patients, high ≥ 10,000 patients).3,11 All contacted sites operated as independent systems, not affiliated with the PAMC. We defined physician and nurse leaders as those with a role that oversaw the operations or education of a CED (eg, nurse director, educator, medical director), or as someone whom another leader from that hospital identified as influential in decision-making around pediatric care delivery in the CED (chair of pediatrics, medical officer). No experience threshold was required. We contacted potential participants by email and phone and asked them to schedule an interview via an online calendar (Calendly.com). Participants scheduled individual or combined group interviews with other members of their CED according to their preferences. During group interviews, each participant was asked to answer each question. We attempted contact with a CED up to 4 times before removing them from the potential participant list. Participants received a $50 gift card for participation in this study.

Setting and Participants

To ensure comfort and privacy, participants chose their preferred setting before connecting via Zoom (version 6.5.7). Interviews were performed by pediatric emergency medicine physicians (R.D., A.F., S.K., M.L.) or a pediatric emergency nurse (I.N.), based on availability. All had either prior interview experience, including formal coursework in qualitative research, or received targeted peer training in qualitative research methodology and interviewing before initiating the study. We ensured that participants were not already personally familiar with their interviewer before data collection.

Data Collection

Authors with expertise in Pediatric Readiness and/or qualitative methodology developed a semistructured interview guide (Supplementary Material) based on existing literature and the NPRP's known recommendations.2,3,11 The guide included open-ended questions designed to elicit indepth narratives. Two general emergency physicians, who had a particular interest in pediatric emergency care, pilot-tested the interview guide and provided feedback. Interviewers used the guide to prompt initial discussion, then asked follow-up questions based on participants’ responses. Interviews were conducted between April 04, 2024 and January 01, 2025, with each session lasting approximately 30 to 60 minutes. All interviews were audio-recorded with participant’s consent. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, meaning additional interviews did not reveal new codes or insights. Three additional interviews were conducted after saturation was reached to confirm that no new themes emerged. Transcripts were returned to participants for comment and/or correction.

The study team used an online transcription service (Rev.com) to transcribe audio recordings and reviewed the transcripts for accuracy. Two team members (R.D., S.K.) used the qualitative software Dedoose Version 10.0.35 (Los Angeles, CA) to assign codes to passages in transcripts. They generated codes inductively and resolved discrepancies through adjudication with a third team member (A.F.). A coding framework was developed iteratively and refined as new themes emerged. To enhance analytic rigor, the study team engaged in reflexive practices throughout data collection and analysis, including ongoing discussions regarding how individual backgrounds, experiences, and assumptions might influence data interpretation, to ensure findings were grounded in the participants’ perspectives.22 Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the lead author’s Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided consent before participation.

Themes and Subthemes

Our interviews with these leaders identified 4 major themes and 13 subthemes. Major themes included: (1) existing infrastructure in CEDs impacts pediatric care, (2) caring for pediatric patients has a unique psychological impact, (3) ED leaders in CEDs value sustained partnerships with PAMCs, and (4) expedient interfacility transfer, when indicated, is desired, but challenges in execution exist.

Theme One: Existing infrastructure in CEDs impacts pediatric care

Many participants reported barriers to pediatric care delivery stemming from infrastructure gaps (Table 2). Diminishing inpatient pediatric resources have compounded challenges in some already resource-strained EDs (eg, loss of pediatric personnel, PECC turnover), with one participant noting that in the post-COVID-19 period, “We have no peds floor…we didn’t have … volume enough to keep nursing credentialed” (physician leader, site #7). Such closures were cited in limiting access to onsite pediatric consultation in the CED and support from pediatric nurses previously available on inpatient units. Gaps extended to a lack of pediatric expertise in community settings, which resulted in significant challenges for patients and families: “We don’t have any services or [access to] a pediatric specialist, so we have to send them out no matter what…” (nurse leader, site #12).

Many participants shared that a shortage of skilled staff and the presence of inexperienced staff have limited efforts to improve pediatric care, with one noting, “Even if I tried to [appoint a PECC in] my quality department, currently my quality department is an employee of one” (nurse leader, site #5). Furthermore, most participants perceived that pediatric-specific knowledge, skills, and resources were insufficient to provide high-quality care to children.

Theme 2: Caring for pediatric patients has a unique psychological impact

Caring for pediatric patients was reported to have a unique psychological impact on ED staff, influencing real-time care and having a long-lasting impact (Table 3). They perceived moral distress in instances where they could not meet the needs of children due to gaps in available resources, “…[there is] moral injury because you can’t get the care that you want to give the patient” (nurse leader, site #12). Several participants also expressed that the delivery of pediatric emergency care carries significant weight and may have a lasting impact on staff psyche, depending on outcome, “…many of these cases stick with us as a team, as we see pediatric care as a real opportunity to impact someone’s - essentially the rest of their life” (physician leader, site #13).

Theme 3: CEDs value sustained partnerships with PAMCs

ED leaders identified value in sustained partnerships with a PAMC (Table 4), and expressed a desire for continuous collaboration, quality improvement, and knowledge- and resource-sharing, “…I’m always interested in getting feedback about how we did and what we did wrong, what we did right, [including] unnecessary transfers” (physician leader, site #2). Resource-sharing requests also extended to in situ simulations, “It’s better to have the instruction come to you” and shadowing opportunities, “…I’m going to spend 12 hours and see what you as the expert, what you do…” (nurse leader, site #2). Participants identified that PAMCs could also potentially assist with follow-up care coordination, “this patient, they had a first-time seizure, now they’ve recovered, when can we get em in [to neurology clinic]?…It’s just so helpful for family to know they have something” (physician leader, site #2).

When asked about telemedicine support, participants had mixed reactions and shared both negative (“I’m not sure if it’s a good idea to create an environment or culture where docs…are so nervous in managing a pediatric patient that they reach for that crutch right away…” [physician leader, site #9]) and positive responses (“If families could talk to an expert [via telemedicine] and know that…they had the support of those resources, I think it would be amazing…” [nurse leader, site #12]).

Participants also shared the importance of thoughtful and informed PAMC support and that PAMCs need to have better awareness of the context of CEDs, “One of my clinicians…was [discussing a case with a] tertiary referral center and they said, ‘call pediatric anesthesia’. And he said, ‘you don’t understand that it’s me and there’s no one else.’” (physician leader, site #13).

Theme Four: Expedited interfacility transfer, when indicated, is valued, but challenges in execution exist

Many participants placed a high value on seamless hospital acceptance and transfer. They expressed appreciation when such assistance was provided by PAMCs (Table 5), “We’re not great at taking care of those kids. So [having a pediatric patient easily accepted for transfer] is something that we need, and if we didn’t have that resource, I mean we’d be in trouble” (physician leader, site #7). Participants shared that there are significant barriers to expedited interfacility transfer, which impacts patients, families, and staff, “Oftentimes, the parents would rather take them via their vehicle than wait for an ambulance to come take them because sometimes we could be [waiting] anywhere from two or three hours. That gives a lot of moral distress to staff as well, right?” (nurse leader, site #9). Identified barriers included ambulance shortages, lack of pediatric-specific transport teams, and weather-related challenges for long-distance transports.

Participants noted that such delays led to ED boarding, which compounded negative impacts, “Once we have an accepting [hospital], it's either waiting for transport to come or finding a Critical Care Transport nurse to go … it can be an eight-hour delay and we're sitting on these intubated, dripped kids and I think the long-term management is very daunting” (nurse leader, site #6). Limitations

This study has notable limitations. First, the participants were from one US region and a single PAMC that had pre-existing contact with participating hospitals, which may limit generalizability. As leaders within their CEDs, their perspectives may differ from those of frontline clinicians and stakeholders. Further, CEDs that do not normally interact with PAMCs may have had different perspectives on pediatric care and the benefits of partnerships with PAMCs, potentially introducing selection bias. Although purposive sampling was used to capture a range of CED characteristics, rural and low- to medium-volume hospitals were underrepresented, potentially limiting the transferability of findings to these settings. Furthermore, analyses were conducted across participants and were not designed to compare experiences by hospital characteristics, such as rurality, pediatric volume, or geographic location. Despite these limitations, the diversity and richness of participant narratives provided valuable insights that may be relevant to other CEDs facing similar challenges. Group interviews may have introduced power dynamics that influenced participants’ willingness to respond candidly. Additionally, subjectivity and researcher bias may influence interpretation of the results. The study team attempted to mitigate this by using multiple coders and regular debriefings to enhance reflexivity and consistency.

Discussion

This qualitative study examined the experiences and perceptions of CED nurse and physician leaders in providing pediatric emergency care, as well as their perspectives on the partnership between CED and PAMC. CEDs leaders perceived a link between limited pediatric-specific resources and challenges to consistently meet the needs of children and families seeking emergency care. These challenges were perceived to compound the distinct psychological demands of treating ill and injured children.

Another prominent theme that emerged was the unique psychological impact of caring for pediatric patients on participants and their CED colleagues. Participants shared the enduring nature of memorable pediatric cases, both with positive and negative outcomes, as well as moral distress when care delivery felt insufficient or inadequate.23, 24, 25 Among ED nurses, moral distress has been well described in the literature and may contribute to staff turnover,26 however, there is limited detail connecting the specific influence of pediatric ED care experiences on moral distress. Participants expressed interest in support that might enable more consistent care delivery through quality improvement (feedback about transferred children), knowledge sharing and skill development (didactics, simulation, shadowing), care delivery support (telemedicine), and linkage to subspecialty care (timely transfers and referrals). Future research is needed to identify implementation approaches that might support the varying needs of ED staff caring for critically ill and injured children, including exploring the potential role of strengthened partnerships with PAMCs.

Addressing system-level contributors to both patient outcomes and clinician well-being requires coordinated, national efforts. The NPRP has worked over the past 2 decades to support CEDs in advancing the care of ill and injured children. Meeting these recommendations has been challenging, especially as this model centers on a CED largely self-directing these efforts, often with little reported hospital-level support. For example, staffing and funding constraints impair CEDs' ability to appoint and retain PECCs,13,27 whose absence in a CED may result in lower readiness scores and worse pediatric outcomes.9,28,29 Further, loss of experienced staff in CED settings, decreased access to pediatric inpatient capabilities, and loss of pediatric-specific expertise over the past few years, particularly in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, compound the challenges ahead.14,30, 31, 32 Participants recounted how these challenges were being experienced in their CEDs; they linked them to subsequent moral distress when unable to deliver the level of care they believed their pediatric patients required. They expressed interest in developing partnerships with PAMCs to address persistent challenges within their CEDs.

Some participants expressed interest in a “hub-and-spoke” framework to enhance collaboration with PAMCs.33,34 In such a model, a PAMC serves not only to assist with clinical care but also as a central resource for robust education and training among a group of several CEDs in their region. In turn, these CEDs provide feedback on lessons learned, solutions developed, and ongoing needs to the PAMC and to other CEDs, thus enabling knowledge exchange. This model has already been shown to improve CED Pediatric Readiness scores.33, 34, 35 It has also been used to scale pediatric education and empower local champions to deliver simulation-based learning and readiness drills that are contextually relevant and sustainable.35,36 Participants saw potential value in PAMCs working to meet specific local needs by providing onsite assessments of CEDs and incorporating those findings into educational offerings. They expressed high value in a regular cadence of didactics, simulation, case review, and sharing of current evidence-supported practice via sustained partnerships with PAMCs.

Participants had mixed reception to the concept of telehealth assistance from PAMCs. Some CEDs sought support for performing pediatric assessments, procedures, and decision-making during resuscitations, whereas a few felt it may hinder comfort, learning, and development of pediatric expertise within CEDs. Previous literature suggests that PAMC-provided real-time consultation with pediatric subspecialists can assist in the care of complex pediatric patients37,38 and reduce the impact of limited access to local pediatric expertise in communities, potentially averting the need for some transfers.39,40 Given differing participant perspectives, future studies should focus on how telehealth can be tailored to the needs of CEDs in the provision of emergency care for children.

Even with telehealth as a support mechanism, some patients may still require inperson care at a PAMC. Participants shared that timely access to pediatric expertise can be challenging, creating difficulties for patients who need subspecialty care after discharge. They perceived benefit in facilitated appointment scheduling before the patient is discharged from the CED. For patients needing immediate transfer to a PAMC for ongoing care, participants sought interventions to reduce delays. Studies have shown that delays in transfer for children with select time-sensitive presentations can negatively impact outcomes.41,42 Although not available for many communities, children transported by specialized pediatric teams experience fewer adverse events compared with those transported by general teams.43,44 Additionally, the creation of centralized transfer coordination may enhance the efficiency of transfer and transport.45 Although PAMCs may themselves experience workforce, financial, and infrastructure constraints, engaging with CEDs may enable the identification of shared priorities and optimal use of finite resources to meet the needs of children in their region.

In conclusion, this study examined how CEDs experience pediatric care delivery and their relationships with PAMCs. Despite resource limitations and shifting health care system capabilities, health care staff at CEDs are committed to high-quality pediatric care and seek to enhance existing care. Moving forward, targeted support for integrated, scalable PAMC partnership models, such as robust hub-and-spoke networks and centralized transfer coordination, holds promise in advancing equitable, high-quality emergency care for all children, regardless of where they enter the health care system.
