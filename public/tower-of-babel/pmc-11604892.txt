Lithium: current state of the art and future directions
Authors: Michael Gitlin, Michael Bauer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLithium is our oldest continuously prescribed medication in psychopharmacology, with its history as an agent for treating mood disorders extending from the 19th century. Although clinicians prescribe it less frequently than in the past, its utility in treating bipolar disorder is unquestionable. Novel potential indications for its use in psychiatry have created excitement about broader roles for lithium in treating and preventing other disorders.ContentLithium is effective both in treating acute mania, as an adjunctive antidepressant, and as a maintenance treatment in bipolar disorder. Lithium has also shown some efficacy in treating and preventing unipolar depression, but less clearly than for bipolar maintenance treatment and acute mania. Common side effects include nausea, polyuria, tremor, weight gain and cognitive dulling. These side effects are typically manageable with reasonable clinical strategies. Lithium affects renal, thyroid and parathyroid function. With clinical monitoring, these effects are easily managed although infrequent cases of severe renal insufficiency may occur with long term use. Although not all studies are positive, a consistent database suggests the efficacy of lithium in decreasing suicide attempts and suicides, likely due to its effect on impulsivity and aggression as well as its prophylaxis against depressive and manic recurrences. Recent data have suggested lithium’s potential efficacy for a number of new clinical indications. Lithium’s neuroprotective effects suggest potential efficacy in preventing mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia as well as in aiding recovery from strokes. Higher (but still trace) lithium levels in drinking water are associated with lower rates of dementia. It is still not clear how much lithium-and what serum lithium levels- are required for either of these effects. Other preliminary research suggests that lithium may also have antiviral effects and may decrease cancer risk.ConclusionsLithium continues to be the mainstay treatment of mood disorders in general and in bipolar disorder specifically. Other potential clinical uses for lithium in psychiatry have re-invigorated excitement for research in other areas such as suicide, preventing cognitive impairment and possibly preventing viral infections and diminishing cancer risk.Keywords: Lithium, Bipolar disorder, Depression, Cognitive impairment, Suicide

History/background

For over half a century, lithium has been the gold standard in the treatment of bipolar disorder]. The use of lithium as a psychopharmacological agent is commonly believed to have originated with Cade’s 1949 paper detailing lithium’s antimanic effects (Cade 1949). However, there is evidence dating back to the early 19th century suggesting that lithium may have positive effects in treating psychiatric disorders (Shorter 2009). The history of lithium in psychiatry reached its first peak in the late 19th century with Lange’s monograph entitled ‘On Periodical Depressions and their Pathogenesis’ (Lange, 1895). In this book, Lange describes his positive experiences in treating approximately 2000 patients with recurrent depression using lithium at doses that would be considered appropriate by today’s standards. Although originally written in Danish, which limited its accessibility to a larger international community, Lange’s book was translated into German in 1896 which made the book more widely available in German-speaking countries. However, Lange’s experiences with lithium treatment were disregarded by the psychiatric community, which was likely due to the dismissal of Lange’s theory of depression caused by an excess of uric acid in the body by one of the most influential psychiatrists of the time, Emil Kraepelin (Kraepelin, 1889; Schioldann 2011).

Likely aware of the 19th-century literature on lithium, Cade sparked the modern revival of lithium (Cade 1949). Acceptance of lithium’s efficacy still took some time, due partly to the prior experience of its use as a salt substitute for hypertensive patients leading to many cases of lithium toxicity (Johnson 1984). Schou and colleagues published his first systematic report on the antimanic effects of lithium in 1954 (Schou et al. 1954). During the 1960s, Hartigan (1963) and Baastrup (1964) made sporadic observations, while Baastrup and Schou conducted systematic but open investigations in 1967 (Baastrup and Schou 1967), suggesting that lithium had prophylactic properties against recurrent mood disorders, both bipolar and unipolar. Eventually, spurred by the studies in Denmark (Baastrup and Schou 1967; Schou 1968), lithium achieved a new reputation as a remarkably effective medication and was approved by the FDA in 1970. However, new anxieties surrounding lithium arose in 1977 after the publication of the first paper describing renal biopsy changes in lithium-treated patients (Hestbech et al. 1977). Starting in the 1980s, other medications became commonly used as alternative mood stabilizers, especially anticonvulsants. Partly due to pharmaceutical marketing of these other medications, prescription rates of lithium decreased dramatically, both in Europe and in the US (Kessing et al. 2016; Rhee et al. 2020).

Yet, even now, over seventy years after Cade’s initial report, lithium’s efficacy in disorders other than bipolar disorder are being studied resulting in exciting preliminary data. This paper will review optimal standards of prescribing lithium for bipolar disorder, summarize the relevant data on its organ toxicity and present recent data on new potential indications for lithium.

Search strategy and selection criteria

References for this review were identified through searches of PubMed for articles published from January, 1971, to June, 2024, by use of the terms “lithium” and “bipolar disorder” or “major depressive disorder”, or “mania” or “maintenance treatment” or “acute treatment” or “neuroprotection” or “suicidal behavior” or “suicide”. Relevant articles published between the late nineteenth century and 1971 were identified through searches in the authors’ personal files and textbooks on lithium. Articles resulting from these searches and relevant references cited in those articles were reviewed. Articles published in English and German were included. However, this paper should be considered a narrative review.

Efficacy and predictors of response- acute mania treatment

Mania

Controlled studies have confirmed what Cade (1949) and Schou and colleagues (1954) independently described more than seven decades ago in their pioneering work: lithium is an effective antimanic agent that does not rely on sedation for its efficacy. Two meta-analyses compared the efficacy of all antimanic agents tested in controlled trials for at least three weeks (Cipriani et al. 2011; Yildiz et al. 2011). Lithium was more effective than placebo and was in the middle of the group of antimanic agents, as measured by effect size (0.37 and 0.39 in the two meta-analyses, respectively) or odds ratio.

Several predictors of response to lithium in mania have been identified: lower levels of agitation/hyperactivity, euphoric, grandiose features (absence of depressive or mixed features), < 10 lifetime manic episodes, past history of response to lithium in acute mania, and absence of a history of rapid cycling (Bauer and Gitlin 2016). Not surprisingly, all recent treatment guidelines consider lithium as a first-line treatment for mild to moderate euphoric mania as opposed to mixed or dysphoric mania (Nivoli et al. 2012). This non-universal recommendation is mainly due to its relatively slow onset of action, lack of sedation and narrow therapeutic window. Severe manic episodes treated with lithium usually require a combination with benzodiazepines and/or – a second generation antipsychotic due to lack of sedation and the need for control of psychomotor hyperactivity.

Lithium doses in treating acute mania vary widely but are generally higher than in maintenance treatment. Target serum levels range between 0.8 and 1.0 mEq/l, although younger individuals often require even higher levels. Conversely, geriatric patients are known to be sensitive to high lithium levels. Despite a consistent data base examining the issue, consensus recommendations are to target lower lithium levels in older patients since many elderly manic patients respond to serum levels in the range of 0.4–0.7 mEq/L and will exhibit lithium toxic symptoms at levels above this (Dols et al. 2016; Shulman et al. 2019). Overall, however, lithium can be safely prescribed in older adults with bipolar disorder (Forlenza et al. 2022).

Efficacy and predictors of response- acute depression treatment

Depression

In contrast to the consistent positive results seen with lithium in the treatment of acute mania, the efficacy of lithium in acute depression has been controversial from the earliest clinical observations (Schou 1968). Now, many decades later, the place of lithium in the treatment of acute depression remains uncertain. Although the data supporting its use as a viable initial treatment for bipolar depression remain relatively weak, with the only modern study finding no difference in efficacy between lithium and placebo (Young et al. 2010), international guidelines list lithium as a first-line treatment (Yatham et al. 2018).

Far more common than the use of lithium as monotherapy for unipolar or bipolar depression is the addition of lithium to an ongoing antidepressant that has failed to adequately treat a major depressive episode (commonly referred to as augmentation therapy). In a meta-analysis of the ten placebo-controlled trials with a relatively small total number of participants (n = 269), lithium was more effective than placebo when prescribed as an adjunct, with odds ratios of 3.11 and 2.89 in the two meta-analyses (Crossley and Bauer 2007; Nelson et al. 2014). The vast majority of subjects in these studies had unipolar depression, with a smaller number having bipolar depression (Crossley and Bauer 2007). The number needed to treat (NNT) for adjunctive lithium was 5, indicating a substantial clinical effect (Crossley and Bauer 2007). Adjunctive lithium is effective with tricyclic antidepressants, but also with second-generation antidepressants (Nelson et al. 2014).

Efficacy and predictors of response-maintenance treatment

Maintenance treatment of bipolar disorder

Because of the high lifelong risk of relapse, pharmacologic maintenance treatment is essential for patients with bipolar disorder. The main goals of long-term treatment are to prevent mood episodes, prevent suicidal acts, reduce subthreshold symptoms, and improve social and occupational functioning. Two independently published meta-analyses including all available placebo RCTs demonstrated that lithium was statistically significantly superior to placebo for the prevention of mood episodes of any polarity, manic episodes, and, depending on the methodology used, depressive episodes (Miura et al. 2014; Severus et al. 2014).

A systematic review of nonrandomized controlled observational studies by an International Society for Bipolar Disorders (ISBD) Task Force found that in eight of nine studies identified, with a total of > 14 000 patients, maintenance lithium therapy was associated with improved outcomes compared with another mood stabilizer including valproate, lamotrigine, olanzapine, quetiapine, carbamazepine/lamotrigine, and unspecified anticonvulsants and antipsychotics as monotherapy (Kessing et al. 2018). Of course, observational studies are subject to selection bias but the results of these studies are consistent with the RCTs. Today, the major international treatment guidelines worldwide recommend lithium as the first-line maintenance treatment for bipolar disorder (Yatham et al. 2018; Grunze et al. 2013; Goodwin et al. 2016; Fountoulakis et al. 2017).

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 71 studies examining clinical predictors of lithium response in bipolar disorder, including over 12,000 patients identified six predictors of good response, shown in Table 1 (Hui et al. 2019). There was weak evidence for number of episodes before lithium treatment, number of hospitalizations before lithium, and family history of lithium response as response predictors (Hui et al. 2019). Because of the limitations of the data, particularly the limited number of RCTs, it is difficult to separate predictors of lithium response from predictors of a benign course of illness.

Maintenance treatment of recurrent unipolar depressive disorder

In recent decades, lithium has been mainly considered as a preventive treatment for bipolar disorder and for acute mania. Less attention has been paid to lithium’s potential value in preventing depressive episodes in patients with recurrent unipolar depression. Lithium has shown consistent evidence in preventing depressive episodes in unipolar depression (Souza and Goodwin 1991; Davis et al. 1999). A meta-analysis (Davis 2006) that included nine randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials in recurrent unipolar depression found lithium to be highly effective: 75% of patients experienced a relapse on placebo compared with 36% on maintenance lithium. Notably, this beneficial effect in unipolar recurrent depression is similar to the prophylactic efficacy of lithium in bipolar disorder (Davis 2006). A meta-analysis based on “mirror-image” studies – in which relapse rates on lithium are compared with those before lithium during the same periods of time – reported a 69 per cent reduction in depressive recurrence rate (Davis 2006).

Managing lithium treatment

Pre-treatment work-up

Medical evaluation before lithium treatment is initiated varies somewhat between countries, practice guidelines and texts. As an example, the International Group for Studies of Lithium (IGSLI) recommends measuring renal function via serum creatinine and urea-nitrogen (BUN) as well as plasma levels of sodium, potassium, calcium, thyroid stimulating hormone, parathormone, and electrocardiogram (Tondo et al. 2019). A number of other tests can be requested but should be considered optional, including thyroid ultrasound, serum T3 and serum T4, complete blood count (CBC) and fasting glucose. Overall, the minimal requirements should be a measure of renal function-preferably an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR or serum creatinine), thyroid function test-specifically thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and serum calcium.

Lithium levels during maintenance treatment

More than any other medication in psychiatry, lithium doses are regulated and monitored by serum lithium levels. Lithium levels should be drawn approximately 12 h after the last dose. Lithium levels should be drawn five days after a consistent daily dose (longer in older patients) when a steady state has been achieved. Even though the renal excretion patterns between once daily and twice daily lithium regimens differ resulting in higher 12-hour levels with once daily dosing (Amdisen 1975), the consensus recommendation is to ignore the dosing regimen when interpreting serum lithium levels (Nolen et al. 2019).

In treating nongeriatric patients with acute mania, optimal lithium levels range between 0.8-1.0mEq/l. For older patients, lower levels (e.g. 0.5-0.7mEq/l) are more typical. In maintenance treatment, consensus recommendations have changed over the last decade with current recommendations of optimal levels ranging between 0.6 and 0.8 mEq/L for most patients. In patients aged 65 or older, target serum lithium levels should be between 0.4-0.6mEq/L (Bauer and Gitlin 2016; Nolen et al. 2019) and is the same for prevention of mania, depression, or mixed states (Nolen et al. 2019).

Maintenance treatment monitoring

During maintenance treatment, lithium levels should be checked between every three to six months (Tondo et al. 2019; Nolen et al. 2019) and typically after each dosage change.

Since lithium may affect thyroid, renal and parathyroid function (see below), serum thyroid stimulating hormone [TSH], eGFR (or serum creatinine) and serum calcium levels should be monitored regularly during maintenance lithium treatment with the frequency of monitoring ranging from every three months to a year (Bauer and Gitlin 2016; Nolen et al. 2019). Of course, should changes in any of these levels – e.g. a rising TSH, an increase in serum calcium or a decrease in the eGFR, test frequency should increase.

Lithium side effects and their management

Most lithium treated patients experience one or more side effects (Gitlin 2016), the most frequent of which are nausea, thirst and polyuria, tremor, weight gain and cognitive dulling. In evaluating lithium side effects, clinicians must be aware of potential misattribution e.g., attributing fatigue or cognitive dullness to lithium when it may be a manifestation of a depressive state. Additionally, since most bipolar patients are taking more than one psychotropic agent, any side effect-e.g., increased appetite resulting in weight gain – could be the result of the additive effects of multiple medications, not just lithium.

Excessive urination and thirst (polyuria/polydipsia) are consistently found to be among the most common side effects (Bone et al. 1980; Vestergaard et al. 1980). Polyuria is more frequent and more severe with longer exposure to lithium and probably higher lithium levels. Initially, the polyuria is functional and reversible upon lithium discontinuation. There is also evidence that once daily lithium regimens are associated with lower 24-hour urine volumes (= less subjective polyuria and therefore thirst) compared to twice daily administration (Bauer and Gitlin 2016; Gitlin 1999, 2016). This may reflect that low lithium levels during some part of the day-as would be seen in a once daily regimen 23 h after the whole daily dose- may be needed for renal regenerative processes (Plenge et al. 1982). However, hydrochlorothiazide and amiloride have been recommended for the treatment of lithium-induced polyuria (Kosten and Forrest 1986; Gitlin 2016).

Weight gain – following increased appetite – from lithium has traditionally been considered to be a common side effect (Vestergaard et al., 1981; Chengappa et al. 2002) and is typically rated as being among the most distressing side effects and one of the most frequent reasons for rejecting or discontinuing the treatment (Gitlin et al., 1989). In contrast, a recent meta-analysis of 20 studies did not demonstrate significant weight gain with lithium compared to placebo (Gomes-da-Costa et al. 2022). Metformin has been suggested as a possible treatment to counterbalance the effect on lithium on increased appetite (Mcintyre et al., 2024). Future studies on GLP-1 agonists to treat lithium induced weight gain will also be helpful.

When patients taking lithium complain of cognitive dulling, other potential etiologies such as depression, missing the unusual cognitive sharpness of hypomania/mania, hypothyroidism and the effect of other psychotropic medications should be considered. Neurocognitive testing results inconsistently document lithium’s negative effect on cognition (Wingo et al. 2009). However, there is some evidence that creativity may be negatively affected by lithium (Shaw et al. 1986).

Lithium and long-term effects on organ systems

The three organ systems that lithium may affect during long term treatment are the kidneys, thyroid gland and parathyroid glands.

Lithium and the kidneys

Lithium’s effects on renal function have been known since the 19th century (Johnson 1980). To summarize our current knowledge base on lithium and renal function (Gitlin and Bauer 2023): The risk of renal damage, defined by the incidence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), Stage 3 = eGFR < 60 ml/minute) is 30% higher in lithium treated patients compared to a control population (Tondo et al. 2017; Van Alphen et al. 2021). In a recent study, 26% of lithium treated patients met criteria for CKD 3 (Schorestsanitis et al. 2021). Of note, bipolar disorder itself (Kessing et al. 2015) and age (Wetzels et al. 2007; Hill et al. 2016) are associated with decreased eGFR. Additionally, there is no clear relationship between polyuria and the development of CKD. Other risk factors for lithium-induced nephropathy are length of treatment and prior episodes of lithium toxicity (Aiff et al. 2015; Bocchetta et al. 2015; Fransson et al. 2022; Damba et al. 2022).

The more critical question is whether lithium is associated with higher rates of end stage renal disease (ESRD), defined by an eGFR < 15 and inevitably leading to dialysis or renal transplantation. Although not all studies agree (e.g. Kessing et al. 2015; Nielsen et al. 2018), it is likely that the risk for ESRD is higher in a lithium treated population compared to a control population (Aiff et al., 2015). Even though the relative risk may be substantial, estimated as 7.8% in one study (Aiff et al. 2014), the absolute risk is relatively small. In the same study, lithium treated patients progressing to ESRD had been on lithium for a mean of 27 years, suggesting the very slow and prolonged nature of renal deterioration (Aiff et al., 2015).

The course of renal function after lithium discontinuation is variable. The best predictor of the trajectory of renal function after lithium discontinuation is probably the eGFR at the time of stopping the lithium with lower levels predicting worse outcome. One recent study found that further deterioration was more likely in those whose eGFR was < 32 ml/minute (Hoekstra et al. 2022).

Lithium and thyroid function

Lithium’s effect on impairing the thyroid function has been known since the 1960s. Lithium interferes with a number of thyroid-mediated functions including inhibition the synthesis of thyroid hormones, as well as possibly diminishing iodine trapping by the gland (Lazarus 2009; Kibrige et al. 2013). The prevalence of lithium-induced hypothyroidism varies across studies from 3 to 40% (Lazarus 2009) due primarily to the differing definitions of hypothyroidism which range from overt disease with associated biological markers (e.g. high TSH, low T and T3) to subclinical hypothyroidism (high TSH, normal T3 and T4 without symptoms of hypothyroidism.) Predictors for lithium-induced hypothyroidism are older age, female sex, and presence of antithyroid antibodies (Gitlin 2016).

Consensus for optimal management of emerging hypothyroidism in lithium-treated patients remains unclear. Certainly, patients with clear hypothyroidism – both biologically and clinically – should be treated with L-thyroxine. What is less clear is how best to manage a rising TSH with normal T3 and T4 levels in asymptomatic patients. An earlier review suggested thyroid replacement therapy in patients with TSH > 10 mU/L even if asymptomatic (Kleiner et al. 1999). The same review recommended that patients with TSH levels above the normal range (usually 4-4.5 mU/L) but below 10 should be treated if the patient has lassitude or refractory depression. Others have recommended thyroid replacement therapy even in asymptomatic patients with mildly high TSH levels (e.g., 4–10 mU/L). However, one recent study found that half of lithium-treated patients with subclinical hypothyroidism showed spontaneously normalized thyroid function tests without clinical intervention (Duce et al. 2023), implying that continued monitoring without early intervention might be the wisest course.

Because hypothyroidism is so easy to treat, patients who are lithium responders who develop hypothyroidism should not discontinue lithium but be given thyroid replacement therapy by either the treating psychiatrist or by an endocrinologist.

Lithium and hyperparathyroidism

As recently reviewed (Kovacs et al. 2022) in contrast to its effect on renal and thyroid function, awareness of lithium’s association with hypercalcemia and hyperparathyroidism is relatively recent. Rates of hypercalcemia and hyperparathyroidism in lithium treated patients range between 8 and 24% (Albert et al. 2013; Meehan et al. 2015), far higher than in a control population. Women are at higher risk for lithium-induced hyperparathyroidism (Meehan et al. 2020). Multiple pathways explain the mechanism of lithium induced hyperparathyroidism including direct stimulation of parathyroid cell growth (Misfud et al. 2020), and antagonizing the calcium sensing receptor (CaSR), thereby stimulating PTH secretion (Nemeth 2002). Most – but not all – cases of lithium induced hyperparathyroidism reflect hyperplasia of all four parathyroid glands. In contrast, most cases of primary hyperparathyroidism are caused by a parathyroid adenoma of a single gland (Vestergaard et al. 2000).

Thus, regular monitoring of serum calcium-along with TSH and eGFR- remains the only reliable method of monitoring and detecting lithium-induced hyperparathyroidism and hypercalcemia.

When hyperparathyroidism emerges during the course of lithium treatment, consultation with an endocrinologist is in order. Calcimimetic drugs such as cinacalcet should be considered as a reasonable therapeutic option Whether lithium should be discontinued in the context of hyperparathyroidism depends on a risk/benefit ratio discussion with the patient focusing on factors such as the robustness of lithium’s efficacy, the calcium level (and potential secondary complications on bone and renal function) and the efficacy of relatively benign therapies such as cinacalcet.

Lithium intoxication

Given its narrow therapeutic index with toxicity symptoms emerging at only slightly higher than therapeutic levels, lithium intoxication is relatively common. Although earlier studies suggested high mortality from severe lithium intoxications, improvements in medical management have decreased mortality rates to a fraction of 1% (Baird-Gunning e t al, 2016) with the best study showing a mortality rate of 0.16% (11 deaths out of 6815 toxic exposures [Mowry et al. 2013]).

Signs of lithium toxicity

Other than acute overdose, the causes of lithium intoxication are all linked to alterations in salt and water balance-as might be seen from dehydration or fever or a sudden decrease in sodium intake- and/or changes in renal function, whether due to new medications that decrease lithium excretion-e.g. NSAIDs or acute kidney injury (Hansen et al., 1978; Haussmann et al. 2015; Ott et al. 2016).

Episodes of lithium intoxication are associated with later development of renal damage. However, the most important potential long-term irreversible effects of lithium intoxication are neurological, especially cerebellar damage, resulting in ataxia, dysarthria and dysmetria (Adityanjee et al., 2005).

Management of lithium intoxication depends on the severity of the toxicity as measured by both serum lithium level and symptoms. If toxic symptoms are mild, simply discontinuing the lithium and monitoring may suffice. In cases of severe acute toxicity, management focuses on enhancing gastrointestinal excretion through saline diuresis, gastric lavage and whole bowel irrigation (Perrone and Mudan 2022). With chronic toxicity, hemodialysis may be indicated. More detailed information on treatment of lithium intoxication can be found elsewhere (Baird-Gunning et al. 2016; Decker et al. 2015; Spatola et al. 2023.)

Lithium during pregnancy and the postpartum period

Management of bipolar disorder in young women of childbearing age poses the most complex risk/benefit decision making among all psychiatric treatments. What is clear is that the risk of mood recurrences in bipolar women during pregnancy is substantial (Viguera et al. 2000; Cohen e t al, 2006; Viguera et al. 2007; Viguera et al. 2011) and even higher among those who discontinue any mood stabilizers (Viguera et al. 2007) and lithium specifically (Viguera et al. 2000). This risk is even enhanced during the postpartum period (Viguera et al. 2011) but, in the limited number of randomized trials, lithium was more effective than no lithium in preventing postpartum relapse (OR = 0.16, NNT = 3) (Fornaro et al. 2020).

Domains of concern for lithium during pregnancy

In the best recent meta-analysis, lithium exposure during the first trimester was associated with higher odds of spontaneous abortion (OR = 3.77, NNH = 15) compared to an unexposed group but similar to unexposed patients with mood disorders (Fornaro et al. 2020). Neither pre-term birth nor low birth weight were associated with lithium exposure. The same meta-analysis showed a small but higher risk of congenital anomalies, especially cardiac in neonates exposed to lithium in utero compared to both any unexposed group and with the general population (OR = 4.0) but the difference was not significant when compared to unexposed patients with mood disorders (OR = 1.59) (Fornaro et al. 2020). A dose-response relationship for malformations may exist with progressively higher risk ratios with higher daily doses: RR = 1.1 for dose of 600 mg or less; RR = 1.60 for 601–900 mg, and RR = 3.22 for > 900 mg (Patorno et al. 2017).

Management of lithium during pregnancy and in the neonatal period birth requires increased attention to serum levels. Renal lithium clearance increases during pregnancy due to a greater volume of distribution and increased renal blood flow. Therefore, regular monitoring of lithium levels and usually increased doses are required to maintain a constant lithium level. At delivery, however, these effects reverse themselves requiring a rapid dose decrease in order to avoid lithium toxicity. Recommendations vary from close monitoring at the time of childbirth to lowering the dose just before delivery to pre-pregnancy doses (Bauer and Gitlin 2013; Molenaar et al. 2021).

Lithium is secreted into breast milk, with levels averaging just under half of the maternal serum levels. Thus, breastfed neonates are exposed to substantial amounts of lithium. Therefore, the advantages of breastfeeding must be balanced against the risk of postpartum mood episodes in bipolar women and the evidence for lithium in preventing these episodes. This difficult balance has given rise to contradictory recommendations with some authors recommending against breast feeding in lithium-treated mothers while others recommend cautious consideration.

Surprisingly, few studies have examined the developmental outcome of children exposed to lithium in utero. In the most recent and largest study, no abnormalities were seen in 56 children examined up to age 14 exposed to lithium in utero compared to a control group after controlling for confounding variables (Poels et al. 2022). Maternal lithium levels during pregnancy were not predictive of outcome.

Suicide prevention

Patients with mood disorders have an increased standardized mortality ratio (SMR) for all-cause mortality compared to the general population, with higher rates of suicide at younger ages and of fatal medical illness in later life (Staudt-Hansen et al. 2019).

Lithium has been reported to reduce the risk of life-threatening suicide attempts and death by 60–80%. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have concluded that maintenance lithium treatment significantly reduces the rates of suicide and suicide attempts in patients diagnosed with bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and schizoaffective disorder Tondo et al. 2001; Cipriani et al. 2005; Guzzetta et al. 2007; Cipriani et al. 2013; Tondo et al., 2024). As one example, a meta-analysis of 31 clinical reports, including 5 randomized controlled trials, found a 4.91-fold lower risk of suicide and suicide attempts in patients with recurrent major mood disorders, not just bipolar disorder (Baldessarini et al. 2006). This finding suggests that the antisuicidal effects of lithium treatment may not be related only to its antidepressant or mood-stabilizing effects, but also to a reduction in impulsivity and aggression (Müller-Oerlinghausen et al., 2010).

One randomized, controlled study reported a substantial, but not statistically significant difference in rates of suicidal acts between major affective disorder patients treated for 12 months with lithium versus placebo (Lauterbach et al. 2008). A post-hoc analysis revealed that all three suicides observed in the study occurred in the placebo-treated group (Lauterbach et al. 2008). A recently published placebo-randomized trial was interrupted for futility because suicide-related events in 519 subjects from a US Veterans Administration population remained similar in lithium and placebo-treated cases (Katz et al. 2022). Methodological concerns, which may explain these negative results, included lower serum lithium levels than those typically used in clinical practice (half of the subjects had a serum lithium level < 0.5mEq/L), allowance of additional uncontrolled treatments, and a brief 6-month lithium treatment period.

Epidemiological studies provide compelling evidence that lithium has anti-suicidal effects. A Danish national registry of 13,186 patients comparing those who had received one or more prescriptions for lithium to the general population who had never been prescribed lithium found that patients who purchased lithium more than once had a lower suicide rate than those who did not purchase lithium (Kessing et al. 2005). A recent Danish registry analysis compared psychotropic medications for bipolar disorder and found that lithium was associated with lower rates of suicide, self-harm, and psychiatric hospital readmission (Fitzgerald et al. 2022). Additionally, lithium was superior to no treatment in suicide prevention (Fitzgerald et al. 2022). Of course, observational studies may be due to selection bias. Nonetheless, lower suicide rates in these studies is consistently found.

Aggression and disruptive behaviour

The potential antisuicidal effects of lithium may be linked to its ability to decrease aggression and impulsivity, two factors knowingly associated with suicidal behavior. This has been demonstrated in various species, populations, and settings, including animals, elderly nursing home residents, mentally handicapped individuals, children and adolescents with hyperactive, hostile, and aggressive behavior, and particularly in hyperaggressive inmates of correctional facilities and prisons (Müller- Oerlinghausen et al., 2010).

Naturally occurring lithium in drinking water and suicide rates

Several ecological studies have examined the hypothesis that trace doses of naturally occurring lithium in drinking water may have a protective effect against suicide in the general population. A random-effects meta-analysis of 15 ecological studies revealed a consistent inverse association between lithium levels or concentration in publicly available drinking water and total suicide mortality rates (Memon et al. 2020). However, the most recent study did not find an association between lithium and suicide rates (Pichler et al. 2024) it is important to note that the concentration of lithium in drinking water is dramatically lower than the concentration used to treat bipolar disorder and that ecological studies do not indicate if the treatment was taken or not by the individuals who die of suicide. Nevertheless, there is no evidence that levels of trace lithium influence the risk of developing bipolar disorder (Kessing et al. 2017a).

New indications

Neuroprotective effects of lithium

Lithium has been shown to have neuroprotective and neurotrophic effects both in vivo and in vitro. These effects may be responsible in preventing apoptosis and promote cellular longevity (Malhi et al. 2013; Alda 2015).

Data from basic science

Consistently replicated evidence from cross-sectional and prospective studies demonstrates a positive association between lithium treatment and hippocampal and cortical gray matter volume. review of 9 imaging studies out of 13 found increased total hippocampal volume or hippocampal subfield volumes (Lucini-Paoini et al., 2021). The relationship between lithium treatment and hippocampal volume appears to be independent of long-term treatment response, even in subjects experiencing bipolar disorder episodes on lithium (Hajek et al. 2014). In a recent multicenter study, patients with bipolar disorder who were taking lithium had greater cortical gray matter volume than those who were not (Hozer et al. 2021).

A systematic review of studies that evaluated diffusion tensor imaging measures to assess white matter integrity found that lithium appears to have a positive influence on the evolution of white matter abnormalities in individuals with bipolar disorder (Espanhol et al., 2022). Furthermore, studies using magnetic resonance spectroscopy have found increased levels of N-acetyl aspartate (NAA), which is considered a putative marker for neuronal health, during lithium administration at therapeutic doses (Moore et al. 2000). Patients with bipolar disorder with no or limited lifetime lithium exposure exhibited significantly lower prefrontal NAA levels than healthy controls, whereas those with a similar illness burden but treated with lithium showed prefrontal NAA levels comparable to those of healthy controls (Hajek et al. 2012).

Data from population and clinical studies

Lithium may have pleiotropic effects in preventing or delaying cognitive decline in the general population (Kessing et al. 2017a, b), in older adults with bipolar disorder (Kessing et al. 2017a, b; Nunes et al. 2007) and with mild cognitive impairment (Forlenza et al. 2019) and extend health and lifespan in different animal models and humans (Araldi et al. 2023).

Epidemiological studies have shown that long-term lithium treatment in bipolar disorder is associated with lower rates of dementia (Kessing et al. 2017a, b; Nunes et al. 2007; Chen et al. 2022). A meta-analysis of three randomized, placebo-controlled trials testing lithium as a treatment for patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) suggests that lithium treatment may have beneficial effects on cognitive performance in individuals with MCI and AD dementia (Matsunaga et al. 2015).

A nationwide, population-based study conducted in Denmark reported on a negative correlation between trace lithium in groundwater and the incidence of dementia across different geographical regions (Kessing et al. 2017b). Also a negative association was found between trace lithium concentrations in drinking water and rates of age-adjusted Alzheimer’s disease mortality in Texas (Fajardo et al. 2018). The most recent systematic review of the topic concluded that trace lithium levels in drinking water are sufficient to lower the incidence and/or mortality from dementia (Fraiha-Pegado, 2024). Moreover, another systematic review investigated the neuropsychiatric health effects of low-dose lithium interventions and found significant benefits compared to placebo in attenuating cognitive decline in individuals with bipolar disorder (Strawbridge et al. 2023).

Overall, the available evidence suggests that lithium has the potential to attenuate cognitive decline in individuals with MCI. Additionally, lithium has disease-modifying properties in AD and positive effects on lifespan. The molecular mechanisms of these neuronal and cognitive effects in humans remains elusive and require further investigation.

Antiviral activity and influence on cancer risk

Preclinical and clinical studies suggest that lithium can inhibit viral replication including SARS-CoV-2 virus (Murru et al. 2020). Analysis of lithium use in over 300,000 patients from three US-based health systems who underwent PCR testing for SARS-CoV-2 demonstrated a 50% reduced risk of COVID-19 in patients taking lithium (Liu et al. 2021).

Lithium inhibits GSK-3, an enzyme that is also involved in the pathogenesis of cancer. A retrospective cohort study using the National Health Insurance Research Database in Taiwan showed that lithium exposure was associated with significantly lower cancer risk in patients with bipolar disorder (Huang et al. 2016). In addition, a meta-analysis of population-based studies provided evidence that cancer risk is increased in individuals with BD and that lithium may have a potential protective effect on cancer (Anmella et al. 2021). Nevertheless, a recent population-based study did not find that the use of lithium compared to lamotrigine or valproate was associated with decreased rates of indent cancer overall or of any subtype (Kessing et al. 2024).

Future directions/conclusions

Despite the many studies demonstrating the relative infrequent prescription of lithium for bipolar patients (Kessing et al. 2016; Rhee et al. 2020; Malhi et al. 2023), from both a data viewpoint as well as from Practice and Clinical Guidelines, lithium’s place in treating acute mania, as a maintenance treatment and as an adjunctive treatment for unipolar disorder have been well demonstrated. More data would be very helpful in further evaluating lithium’s efficacy in treating acute unipolar and bipolar depression. Unfortunately, lack of financial support for clinical research make large scale studies in this area unlikely.

Evaluating side effects and chronic effects on organ function, lithium’s effects on parathyroid function and calcium levels need more study. Should serum PTH and calcium levels be monitored regularly in lithium treated patients? If so, with what frequency? Additionally, very long term studies (e.g. decades) are critical to clarify the risk of CKD in lithium treated patients.

However, the most important and exciting new data that are needed in the near future reflect the potential clinical uses of lithium reviewed just above. It would be important to establish whether lithium is sufficiently neuroprotective to warrant its use in general populations, whether it prevents MCI and dementia, and which lithium levels are required for this effect. If future data suggest that lithium levels lower than those needed to prevent bipolar episodes are neuroprotective and with limited side effects, the risk/benefit ratio for taking lithium for this purpose would shift markedly. Finally, the preliminary hints that lithium may prevent viral infections and lower cancer risk need to be explored further, both as a matter of scientific interest and clinical use.
