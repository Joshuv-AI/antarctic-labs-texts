Dynamic and Geometric Shifts in Wave Scattering
Konstantin Y. Bliokh, Zeyu Kuang, Stefan Rotter

Abstract
Since Berry’s pioneering 1984 work, the separation of geometric and dynamic contributions in the phase of an evolving wave has become fundamental in wave physics, underpinning diverse phenomena in quantum mechanics, optics, and condensed matter. Here we extend this geometric-dynamic decomposition from the wave-evolution phase to a distinct class of wave scattering problems, where observables (such as frequency, momentum, or position) experience shifts in their expectation values between the input and output wave sates. We describe this class of problems using a unitary scattering matrix and the associated generalized Wigner-Smith operator (GWSO), which involves gradients of the scattering matrix with respect to conjugate variables (time, position, or momentum, respectively). We show that both the GWSO and the resulting expectation-values shifts admit gauge-invariant decompositions into dynamic and geometric parts, related respectively to gradients of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the scattering matrix. We illustrate this general theory through a series of examples, including frequency shifts in polarized-light transmission through a time-varying waveplate (linked to the Pancharatnam-Berry phase), momentum shifts at spatially varying metasurfaces, optical forces, beam shifts upon reflection at a dielectric interface, and Wigner time delays in 1D scattering. This unifying framework illuminates the interplay between geometry and dynamics in wave scattering and can be readily applied to a broad range of physical systems.



I
Introduction

In 1984, Michael Berry introduced the concept of
geometric phase
: a fundamental addition to the usual
dynamic phase
in quantum (wave) evolution, which exhibits remarkable geometric properties
Berry 1984
. It was soon recognized that this universal concept has an extraordinary broad range of applications: from mechanical systems and polarization optics to Hall effects, topological insulators, and quantum field theory
Wilczek and Shapere 1989
;
Vinitskii et al. 1990
;
Zwanziger et al. 1990
;
Bhandari 1997
;
Xiao et al. 2010
;
Bliokh et al. 2015
;
Baggio et al. 2017
;
Cohen et al. 2019
;
Cisowski et al. 2022
.

Initially, Berry considered the adiabatic evolution of an
n
-level quantum system characterized by a time-varying Hermitian Hamiltonian
Berry 1984
. In this case, the dynamic phase accumulated by an adiabatically-evolving instantaneous eigenstate is determined by an integral of the corresponding
eigenvalue
of the Hamiltonian. In turn, the geometric phase is described by an integral of the
Berry connection
, which is proportional to the derivative of the corresponding unit
eigenvector
. (The Berry connection describes local rotations and parallel transport of the eigenvectors forming a fiber bundle over the corresponding base space.) Due to adiabaticity and the time derivative involved, the geometric phase has the next order of smallness compared to the dynamic phase.
Importantly, geometric phases and Berry connections appear not only in time-varying systems but also in those with space- and momentum-varying Hamiltonians
Wilczek and Shapere 1989
;
Vinitskii et al. 1990
;
Zwanziger et al. 1990
;
Bhandari 1997
;
Xiao et al. 2010
;
Bliokh et al. 2015
;
Baggio et al. 2017
;
Cohen et al. 2019
;
Cisowski et al. 2022
.

In this work, we present a related decomposition of dynamic and geometric contributions, but in a principally different setting. Instead of the continuous quantum evolution of a wave state over the base space, we consider a generic
wave scattering
problem with varying parameters. In this context, an input wave state is immediately transformed into an output state, and the unitary
scattering matrix
describing this transformation depends on certain basic variables (time, coordinates, momentum, etc.), as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Furthermore, instead of examining the phase of the output wavefunction, we analyze the shifts in the expectation values of the variables conjugate to these basic variables. In particular, the time/coordinates/frequency/momentum-dependent scattering produces shifts in the expectation values of frequency/momentum/time/coordinates, respectively.

Well-known examples of such shifts in wave-scattering problems include the Goos-Hänchen shift of an optical beam reflected from an interface characterized by a momentum-dependent reflection matrix
Goos and Hänchen 1947
;
Artmann 1948
;
Bliokh and Aiello 2013
, as well as the Wigner time delay experienced by a wavepacket scattered by a frequency-dependent system
Wigner 1955
;
Smith 1960
;
Winful 2006
;
Chiao and Steinberg 1997
.
Following earlier work in mesoscopic physics
Brouwer et al. 1997
;
Brouwer et al. 1999
,
these phenomena have recently been generalized and extended to a broader class of wave systems and conjugate variables using the
generalized Wigner-Smith operator (GWSO)
framework
Ambichl et al. 2017
;
Horodynski et al. 2020
;
Hüpfl et al. 2023
;
Orazbayev et al. 2024
(see also
Cao et al. 2022
;
Rachbauer et al. 2024
for recent reviews on this subject).

Here we show that both the GWSOs and the corresponding expectation-value shifts in scattered waves can be unambiguously decomposed into dynamic and geometric parts. Similarly to Berry’s phase decomposition, these dynamic and geometric contributions are associated with the
eigenvalues
and
eigenvectors
of the scattering matrix, respectively (see Fig.
1
). Furthermore, the geometric part is expressed via the corresponding Berry connection. However, unlike the adiabatic-evolution scenario, now the dynamic and geometric contributions have the same order of magnitude. This is because the dynamic part involves
derivatives
of the eigenvalues. (This is explained by the fact that we are dealing with the eigenvalues of a unitary scattering matrix, which is analogous to the evolution operator involving an
integral
of the Hamiltonian in the evolution problem.) Moreover, while the geometric phase in the adiabatic-evolution problem is gauge-invariant only for
cyclic
evolution in the base space, we show that the geometric and dynamic shifts in the scattering problem are gauge-invariant
at each point
of the base space.

We explore applications of this general framework to various observable phenomena, mostly in optical systems. In particular, we find geometric and dynamic frequency shifts in light transmission through a time-varying waveplate, which offer a more fundamental description of the scattered light state than the conventional Pancharatnam-Berry (PB) phase
Pancharatnam 1956
;
Berry 1987
(for ambiguities in the PB phase description, see
Vinitskii et al. 1990
;
Malykin and Pozdnyakova 2004
;
Simon et al. 1988
;
Bretenaker and Le Floch 1990
).
In another example, the mutually-orthogonal Goos-Hänchen and Imbert-Fedorov (spin-Hall effect) shifts of an optical beam reflected from a dielectric interface
Bliokh and Aiello 2013
;
Bliokh et al. 2015
are identified as the dynamic and geometric parts, respectively, of the total beam shift.
We also describe examples of momentum shifts in light transmission through space-varying anisotropic plates (metasurfaces) and Wigner time delays in 1D wave-scattering systems.

Thus, our work introduces a new fundamental concept, offers fresh insights and a unified perspective on a range of observable phenomena, and can have useful implications in a variety of wave systems, both classical and quantum.

II
General formalism

We consider a wave system described by an
n
-component wavefunction
|\psi\rangle
in a flux-normalized basis,
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1
.
The scattering experienced by this wave is described by a unitary matrix operator
\hat{S}
depending on some parameters (‘coordinates’)
{\bm{\rho}}
:
|\psi_{\rm out}\rangle=\hat{S}({\bm{\rho}})\,|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle
, see Fig.
1
. Assuming that the canonically-conjugate ‘momentum’ operator is
{\bm{\pi}}=-i\partial/\partial{\bm{\rho}}
, the shift in the expectation value of
{\bm{\pi}}
due to the scattering is given by
Ambichl et al. 2017

\Delta{\bm{\pi}}=\langle{\psi}_{\rm out}|{\bm{\pi}}|{\psi}_{\rm out}\rangle-\langle{\psi}_{\rm in}|{\bm{\pi}}|{\psi}_{\rm in}\rangle=\langle{\psi}_{\rm in}|\hat{\bf Q}|{\psi}_{\rm in}\rangle\,.

(1)

Here
\hat{\bf Q}=-i\hat{S}^{\dagger}\dfrac{\partial\hat{S}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}
is the Hermitian GWSO.
Similarly, if the scattering matrix depends on the ‘momentum’
{\bm{\pi}}
, the shift in the expectation value of the conjugate ‘coordinates’
{\bm{\rho}}=i\partial/\partial{\bm{\pi}}
is given by Eq. (
1
), with the substitution
{\bm{\rho}}\leftrightarrow{\bm{\pi}}
,
\partial/\partial{\bm{\rho}}\to-\partial/\partial{\bm{\pi}}
.

The Wigner-Smith operator was originally introduced to quantify the Wigner time delay, involving a derivative with respect to frequency
Wigner 1955
;
Smith 1960
;
Winful 2006
;
Chiao and Steinberg 1997
, but later it was generalized to other conjugate variables
Brouwer et al. 1997
;
Brouwer et al. 1999
;
Ambichl et al. 2017
;
Horodynski et al. 2020
;
Hüpfl et al. 2023
;
Orazbayev et al. 2024
. It is useful to note that the maximum shift in, e.g., the
x
-component of the ‘momentum’ is achieved when the input wave is an eigenvector of the corresponding GWSO with the largest eigenvalue:
\hat{Q}_{x}|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle=q_{x,{\rm max}}|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle
and
\Delta\pi_{x,{\rm max}}=q_{x,{\rm max}}
.

In geometric-phase problems, the parameter-dependent diagonalization of the Hamiltonian provides an efficient method for separating the dynamic and geometric phases. Here we adopt a similar approach but apply it to the scattering matrix
\hat{S}({\bm{\rho}})
, which we assume to be diagonalizable via a unitary transformation

\hat{U}^{\dagger}({\bm{\rho}})\hat{S}({\bm{\rho}})\hat{U}({\bm{\rho}})=\hat{S}^{\prime}({\bm{\rho}})={\rm diag}\!\left(s_{1}({\bm{\rho}}),...,s_{n}({\bm{\rho}})\right)\,.

(2)

Here
s_{i}({\bm{\rho}})=e^{i\alpha_{i}({\bm{\rho}})}
,
i=1,...,n
, are the unimodular eigenvalues of
\hat{S}
, with real-valued phases
\alpha_{i}
, whereas the columns of the unitary matrix
\hat{U}
are the normalized eigenvectors of
\hat{S}
.
Making the corresponding substitution in the wavefunction,
|\psi\rangle=\hat{U}|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
and performing straightforward algebra, we re-write Eq. (
1
) as

\Delta{\bm{\pi}}=\underbracket{\langle{\psi}^{\prime}_{\rm in}|\hat{\bf Q}^{\prime}|{\psi}_{\rm in}^{\prime}\rangle}_{\text{dynamic}}+\underbracket{\langle{\psi}^{\prime}_{\rm out}|\hat{\bf A}|{\psi}_{\rm out}^{\prime}\rangle-\langle{\psi}^{\prime}_{\rm in}|\hat{\bf A}|{\psi}_{\rm in}^{\prime}\rangle}_{\text{geometric}}.

(3)

Here
\hat{\bf Q}^{\prime}=-i\hat{S}^{\prime{\dagger}}\dfrac{\partial\hat{S}^{\prime}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}={\rm diag}\!\left(\dfrac{\partial\alpha_{1}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}},...,\dfrac{\partial\alpha_{n}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}\right)
is the diagonalized GWSO, while
\hat{\bf A}=-i\hat{U}^{\dagger}\dfrac{\partial\hat{U}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}
is the Hermitian
Berry connection
over the
{\bm{\rho}}
-space.

Equation (
3
) consists of two contributions: (i) the expectation value of the diagonal GWSO
\hat{\bf Q}^{\prime}
, which involves derivatives of the
eigenvalues
of the scattering matrix; and (ii) the shift in the expectation value of the Berry connection, which is associated with derivatives of the
eigenvectors
of the scattering matrix. These two contributions determine, respectively, the
dynamic
(
\Delta{\bm{\pi}}_{d}
) and
geometric
(
\Delta{\bm{\pi}}_{g}
) parts of the total momentum shift (
1
), see Fig.
1
.

Transforming Eq. (
3
) back to the original basis,
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle=\hat{U}^{\dagger}|\psi\rangle
, and comparing it with Eq. (
1
), we find that the GWSO
\hat{\bf Q}
can also be decomposed into dynamic and geometric (both Hermitian) parts,
\hat{\bf Q}=\hat{\bf Q}_{d}+\hat{\bf Q}_{g}
:

\hat{\bf Q}_{d}=\hat{U}\hat{\bf Q}^{\prime}\hat{U}^{\dagger},\quad\hat{\bf Q}_{g}=\hat{U}\!\left(\hat{S}^{\prime{\dagger}}\hat{\bf A}\hat{S}^{\prime}-\hat{\bf A}\right)\!\hat{U}^{\dagger}.

(4)

Equations (
3
) and (
4
) are the key general results of this work.
They establish a geometric-dynamic decomposition of the shift in the conjugate variable (‘momentum’), as well as of the associated GWSO.
Note that the geometric part of the GWSO is traceless:
{\rm tr}(\hat{\bf Q}_{g})=0
. Considering, e.g., the
x
-components of the shifts, it is easy to show that if the input wave is an eigenvector of the dynamic GWSO,
\hat{Q}_{d,x}|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle=q_{d,x,i}|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle
, then the geometric shift vanishes:
\Delta{\pi}_{d,x}=\langle\psi_{\rm in}|\hat{Q}_{d,x}|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle=0
.
In particular, the maximum dynamic shift
\Delta{\pi}_{d,x}=q_{d,x,{\rm max}}
is necessarily accompanied by the vanishing geometric shift.
In turn, the maximum total shift cannot exceed the sum of the maximum dynamic and geometric shifts:
\Delta{\pi}_{x,{\rm max}}\leq\Delta{\pi}_{d,x,{\rm max}}+\Delta{\pi}_{g,x,{\rm max}}
. This follows from Weyl’s inequality for eigenvalues of a sum of Hermitian matrices.

Importantly, the geometric-dynamic decomposition (
3
) and (
4
) is
gauge-invariant
. The diagonalizing transformation (
2
) is defined up to a diagonal gauge transformation:
\hat{U}({\bm{\rho}})\to\hat{U}({\bm{\rho}})\hat{G}({\bm{\rho}})
, where
\hat{G}({\bm{\rho}})={\rm diag}\!\left(e^{i\gamma_{1}({\bm{\rho}})},...,e^{i\gamma_{n}({\bm{\rho}})}\right)
. This transformation leaves the diagonalized scattering matrix and the GWSO invariant,
\hat{S}^{\prime}\to\hat{S}^{\prime}
,
\hat{\bf Q}^{\prime}\to\hat{\bf Q}^{\prime}
, but modifies the Berry connection:
\hat{\bf A}\to\hat{G}^{\dagger}\hat{\bf A}\hat{G}-i\hat{G}^{\dagger}\dfrac{\partial\hat{G}}{\partial{\bm{\rho}}}
.
Nonetheless, straightforward calculation shows that the dynamic and geometric ‘momentum’ shifts,
\Delta{\bm{\pi}}_{d}
and
\Delta{\bm{\pi}}_{g}
, as well as the corresponding GWSOs, remain gauge-invariant:
\hat{\bf Q}_{d}\to\hat{\bf Q}_{d}
,
\hat{\bf Q}_{g}\to\hat{\bf Q}_{g}
.
This indicates that the geometric-dynamic decomposition (
3
) and (
4
) is physically meaningful and observable.

III
Examples

We now apply the general framework to several examples of wave systems that exhibit dynamic and geometric shifts in different conjugate variables.

III.1
Frequency shifts at time-varying waveplate

We start with examining the transmission of polarized light through a uniaxial anisotropic plate (waveplate), as shown in Fig.
2
. This problem represents reflection-less two-channel scattering, corresponding to the transmission of two polarization modes. These modes can be described by the wavefunction (normalized polarization Jones vector) in the circular-polarizations basis
|\psi\rangle=\left(\begin{matrix}e^{+}\\
e^{-}\end{matrix}\right)
,
|e^{+}|^{2}+|e^{-}|^{2}=1
, with the corresponding scattering (Jones) matrix
Bomzon et al. 2002
;
Hasman et al. 2005
;
Bliokh et al. 2015
:

\hat{S}=\left(\begin{matrix}\cos\!\frac{\delta}{2}&ie^{-2i\varphi}\sin\!\frac{\delta}{2}\\
ie^{2i\varphi}\sin\!\frac{\delta}{2}&\cos\!\frac{\delta}{2}\end{matrix}\right).

(5)

Here
\varphi
is the angle of the slow anisotropy axis of the plate in the
(x,y)
-plane (assuming light propagates along the
z
-axis), and
\delta
is the retardation phase between the orthogonal linear polarizations aligned with the anisotropy axes, which is determined by the thickness of the anisotropic plate.

This generates a shift in frequency (associated with the operator

(6)

The corresponding Berry connection is then given by

\hat{A}=i\hat{U}^{\dagger}\dfrac{\partial\hat{U}}{\partial t}=\!\left(\begin{matrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{matrix}\right)\!\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\,.

(7)

Substituting Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
) into general Eqs. (
4

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{d}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\begin{matrix}0&e^{-2i\varphi}\\
e^{2i\varphi}&0\end{matrix}\right)\!\frac{\partial\delta}{\partial t}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{g}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}-1+\cos\delta&ie^{-2i\varphi}\sin\delta\\
-ie^{2i\varphi}\sin\delta&1-\cos\delta\end{matrix}\right)\!\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\,.

(8)

One can see that the eigenvalues and eigenvectors (forming the diagonalization matrix
\hat{U}
) of the scattering matrix
\hat{S}
are determined by the parameters
\delta
and
\varphi
, respectively. Correspondingly, the dynamic and geometric parts of the GWSO are proportional to the time derivatives of these parameters

.

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\tau\cos 2\varphi+\chi\sin 2\varphi\right)\!\frac{\partial\delta}{\partial t}\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{g}

\displaystyle=-\left[\sigma(1-\cos\delta)+\chi\cos 2\varphi\sin\delta-\tau\sin 2\varphi\sin\delta\right]\!\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\,.

Here

,

The time integral of the frequency shift yields the total phase shift accumulated in the transmitted light (with respect to the incident light) over a given time interval:
\Delta\Phi=-\int\Delta\omega\,dt

It also determines the total energy exchange, i.e., the

\Delta\omega
and the integral phase shift

First, the parameter

of the waveplate. Increasing it by

This is the standard form of the

(assuming the absence of inhomogeneities or other bodies in the surrounding space). Indeed, rotations of the scattering object do not change the eigenvalues of the scattering matrix, and can only affect its eigenvectors. Therefore, any rotations can produce only

III.2
Relation to the Pancharatnam-Berry phase

The geometric and dynamic frequency shifts (
9
) at a time-varying waveplate exhibit rather nontrivial interconnections with the PB phase, which is widely recognized as the geometric phase associated with polarization evolution
Pancharatnam 1956
;
Berry 1987
;
Vinitskii et al. 1990
;
Bhandari 1997
. We recall that for a cyclic polarization evolution, the PB phase is given by minus half the solid angle
\Omega
embraced by the evolving Stokes vector
\vec{\Sigma}=(\tau,\chi,\sigma)
on the unit Poincaré sphere:
\Phi_{PB}=-\Omega/2
.

Consider first the case of circularly-polarized incident light:
\sigma=\pm 1
,
\tau=\chi=0
. Then, the dynamic frequency shift (
9
) vanishes,
\Delta\omega_{d}=0
, whereas the geometric frequency shift is directly related to the PB phase [see Appendix A]:

\Delta\omega\,dt=\Delta\omega_{g}dt=-d\Phi_{PB}=\frac{1}{2}d\Omega\,.

(11)

Here the infinitesimal solid angle
d\Omega
is formed by: (i) the Stokes vector of the incident light,
\vec{\Sigma}
, (ii) the Stokes vector of the transmitted light
\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime}(t)
, (iii)
\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime}(t+dt)
, and geodesic lines on the Poincaré sphere connecting these points, see Fig.
3
(a).
Equation (
11
) also holds for linearly-polarized incident light, circularly-polarized transmitted light, and
\varphi(t)
-rotations of the waveplate with
\delta={\rm const}
. These configurations, involving incident circular or linear polarizations and rotating quarter-wave or half-wave plates, were employed in most of the key experimental demonstrations of the PB phase and its associated frequency shifts
Garetz and Arnold 1979
;
Bhandari and Samuel 1988
;
Chyba et al. 1988
;
Simon et al. 1988
;
Bretenaker and Le Floch 1990
;
Mashhoon et al. 1998
.

Remarkably, our approach is free of these peculiarities and ambiguities of the PB phase, and provides a gauge-invariant decomposition of dynamic and geometric effects, Eqs. (

Two observations can explain the limitations of the PB-phase approach in the system under consideration. First, light transmission through a time-varying optical element is not a problem with continuous evolution of time-varying instantaneous eigenmodes, where the geometric-phase approach was originally introduced

scattering

(See also

Second, the PB formalism treats the Poincaré sphere as an

Finally, we note that the total phase shift

\sigma=\pm 1

III.4
Beam shifts at an interface

We now consider a scenario involving a momentum-dependent scattering matrix. A simple example is provided by the reflection of a paraxial Gaussian-like optical beam from a planar dielectric interface. The Fresnel reflection coefficients for plane waves in the incident beam depend on the direction of the wavevector
{\bf k}
,
and, hence, the scattering martix is
{\bf k}

The geometry of the problem is shown in Fig.

\theta

(Z,X)

p

|\psi\rangle=\left(\begin{matrix}e_{p}\\
e_{s}\end{matrix}\right)

R_{p,s}(\theta)=e^{i\alpha_{p,s}(\theta)}

III.5
Wigner time delays in 1D scattering

Finally, we consider the Wigner time delays (shifts), which originally motivated the introduction of the Wigner-Smith operator
Wigner 1955
;
Smith 1960
;
Winful 2006
;
Chiao and Steinberg 1997
.
A simple example is provided by the 1D scattering of a wavepacket (sufficiently narrow in frequency bandwidth, i.e., sufficiently extended in time) at a scatterer, such as a quantum-mechanical potential barrier or an optical resonator, see Fig.
6
.
There is a pair of incoming waves (from two sides of the scatterer) and a pair of outgoing waves, so that the wavefunction can be written as
|\psi\rangle=\left(\begin{matrix}\psi^{L}\\
\psi^{R}\end{matrix}\right)
, where
\psi^{L}
and
\psi^{R}
are the waves on the left and right of the scatterer, respectively. The corresponding unitary scattering matrix (assuming time-reversal symmetry) has the form
Tannor 2007

\hat{S}=e^{i\varphi}\left(\begin{matrix}e^{i\delta}r&\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-r^{2}}&-e^{-i\delta}r\end{matrix}\right),

(19)

where
r\in[0,1]
is an effective reflection coefficient, whereas
\varphi
and
\delta
are phase parameters. We assume that all parameters depend on the wave frequency
\omega
, so that the corresponding dispersion-induced time shifts of the output wavepackets are described by the GWSO
\hat{Q}=-i\hat{S}^{\dagger}\dfrac{\partial\hat{S}}{\partial\omega}
.

Note that the time shift in this context,
\Delta t
, represents appropriately averaged shifts of the intensity centroids of the two outgoing wavepackets,
\Delta t_{L,R}
. These shifts are defined with respect to the expected temporal positions of the outgoing wavepackets for a nondispersive scattering matrix
\hat{S}(\omega_{c})
, where
\omega_{c}
is the central frequency of the incoming wavepackets, see Fig.
6
.

Figure 6:

\hat{S}(\omega)
, resulting in the Wigner time delays
\Delta t
for the scattered wavepackets. The gray profiles show the expected output wavepackets assuming a non-dispersive scattering matrix
\hat{S}(\omega_{c})
, evaluated at the central frequency
\omega=\omega_{c}
of the incident wavepackets.

If the scatterer is mirror-symmetric, then
S_{11}=S_{22}
, resulting in
\delta\equiv\pi/2
, and the matrix (
19
) reduces to:

\hat{S}_{\rm sym}=e^{i\varphi}\left(\begin{matrix}ir&\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-r^{2}}&ir\end{matrix}\right).

(20)

This matrix can be diagonalized by a constant unitary transformation:

\hat{U}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{matrix}\right),\quad\hat{S}^{\prime}={\rm diag}\!\left(e^{i(\varphi+\vartheta)},-e^{i(\varphi-\vartheta)}\right),

(21)

where
\vartheta={\rm arcsin}(r)
. The eigenvectors of
\hat{S}_{\rm sym}
, which form the columns of
\hat{U}
, are constant. Therefore, all geometric contributions vanish:
\hat{Q}_{g}=\hat{A}=0
. In this case, the dynamic GWSO coincides with the total GWSO and reads:

\hat{Q}=\hat{Q}_{d}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\omega}+\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right)\frac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial\omega}\,.

(22)

The corresponding averaged time shift for the scattered wavepackets is given by the expectation value of
\hat{Q}
with the incident wavefunction
|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle=\left(\begin{matrix}\psi^{L}_{\rm in}\\
\psi^{R}_{\rm in}\end{matrix}\right)
:

\Delta t=\Delta t_{d}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\omega}+\tau\frac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial\omega}\,.

(23)

Here we defined
\tau=2\,{\rm Re}(\psi^{L*}_{\rm in}\psi^{R}_{\rm in})
, analogous to the Stokes parameter in polarization optics, and the frequency derivatives are evaluated at the central frequency
\omega=\omega_{c}
. For a wavepacket incident from one side only,
\tau=0
, the time shift reduces to the derivative of the total phase
\varphi
, which corresponds to the original Wigner formula.

Consider now a specific scatterer without mirror-symmetry, described by the scattering matrix (
19
) with
\varphi\equiv 0
and
\delta\equiv 0
:

\hat{S}_{\rm asym}=\left(\begin{matrix}r&\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-r^{2}}&-r\end{matrix}\right).

(24)

We chose this matrix as a toy model that allows for a simple analytic solution. It has constant eigenvalues
s_{1,2}=\pm 1
, and therefore the dynamic contribution to the time shift vanishes:
\hat{Q}_{d}=0
. In turn, the eigenvectors of
\hat{S}_{\rm asym}
depend on
r
, which results in the following diagonalization matrix:

\hat{U}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{matrix}\sqrt{1+r}&-\sqrt{1-r}\\
\sqrt{1-r}&\sqrt{1+r}\end{matrix}\right),\penalty\ \penalty\ \hat{S}^{\prime}={\rm diag}\!\left(1,-1\right).

(25)

The corresponding Berry connection is

\hat{A}=-i\hat{U}^{\dagger}\dfrac{\partial\hat{U}}{\partial\omega}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{matrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{matrix}\right)\!\dfrac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial\omega}\,.

(26)

Substituting Eqs. (
25
) and (
26
) into Eqs. (
4
), we obtain a purely geometric GWSO:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}=\hat{Q}_{g}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{matrix}\right)\!\dfrac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial\omega}\,.

(27)

This results in a purely geometric time shift:

\Delta t=\Delta t_{g}=\sigma\frac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial\omega}\,,

(28)

where
\sigma=2\,{\rm Im}(\psi^{L*}_{\rm in}\psi^{R}_{\rm in})
. Thus, to observe the geometric time shift (
28
), one has to consider incoming wavepackets from the left and right that are phase-shifted by
\pi/2
.

These examples demonstrate that even the simplest 1D systems can exhibit both dynamic and geometric shifts of conjugate variables. Remarkably, the signs of dynamic and geometric time shifts (
23
) and (
28
), proportional to the reflection-coefficient derivative
\partial\vartheta/\partial\omega
, can be controlled by the relative phase of wavepackets incident from the two sides:
\psi_{R}=\pm\psi_{L}
(
\tau=\pm 1
) or
\psi_{R}=\pm i\psi_{L}
(
\sigma=\pm 1
).

IV
Conclusions

In this work, we have presented a unified theoretical framework that extends the geometric-dynamic decomposition (originally developed in the wave-evolution context) to general wave-scattering problems. Moreover, instead of analyzing the wave phase, we consider shifts in the expectation values of observables between the input and output wave states. Central to our analysis is the generalized Wigner-Smith operator (GWSO) involving gradients of the unitary scattering matrix with respect to physical parameters, such as time, position, frequency, or wavevector. This operator captures the expectation-values shifts of the conjugate variables: frequency, wavevector, time, or position.

Our key contribution is a gauge-invariant decomposition of these shifts into dynamic and geometric components: the former arising from gradients of the scattering matrix’s eigenvalues, and the latter from gradients of its eigenvectors, via the corresponding Berry connection.
We demonstrated the broad applicability of this decomposition through diverse examples, including time-varying waveplates, spatially varying metasurfaces, beam shifts at interfaces, and Wigner time delays in 1D scattering.

Our approach not only recovers known phenomena (such as the Goos-Hänchen and Imbert-Fedorov shifts or the Pancharatnam-Berry phase) within a unified formalism, but also reveals new physical features, provides new insights, and uncovers previously unnoticed interconnections. For instance, our geometric-dynamic decomposition of frequency shifts in polarized-light transmission through a time-varying waveplate provides a more fundamental description than the conventional Pancharatnam–Berry phase framework: it does not require cyclic evolution and remains gauge-invariant at every point of the base parameter space.
Importantly, identifying the geometric and dynamic origins of various scattering-induced shifts offers new ways to control wave-scattering phenomena by tailoring the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the scattering matrix.

Looking ahead, the presented decomposition offers several promising avenues for further exploration. One important direction is the extension of this formalism to non-Hermitian and open systems
Moiseyev 2011
;
El-Ganainy et al. 2018
;
Özdemir et al. 2019
, where the scattering matrix becomes non-unitary and complex shifts, as well as singularities associated with exceptional points, may arise
Asano et al. 2016
;
Giovannelli and Anlage 2024
;
Byrnes and Foreman 2024
.
Another important extension involves anomalously large shifts in the context of ‘quantum weak measurements’
Kofman et al. 2012
;
Dressel et al. 2014
;
Dennis and Götte 2012
;
Asano et al. 2016
where the output wave undergoes ‘post-selection’, i.e., projection on a specifically chosen final state.
Furthermore, integrating the GWSO-based decomposition into inverse design and machine-learning strategies could enable intelligent control of scattering in disordered or structured media
Horodynski et al. 2022
, optimizing wavefronts for applications ranging from imaging
Pai et al. 2021
;
Matthès et al. 2021
;
Ber et al. 2025
to micromanipulation
Hüpfl et al. 2023
.

In summary, the decomposition of scattering-induced shifts into geometric and dynamic components provides a powerful and unifying perspective through which a wide array of wave phenomena can be analyzed and engineered.

\varphi
.
Finding the general relation between the PB phase and frequency shifts, Eqs. (
9
) in the main text, is challenging; therefore we consider specific simplified cases.

First, for a circularly-polarized incident light,
\vec{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{matrix}0\\
0\\
\sigma\end{matrix}\right)
,
calculations of Eqs. (
30
) and (
A
) yield:

\displaystyle d\Phi_{PB}=\sigma(1-\cos\delta)\,d\varphi=-\Delta\omega_{g}\,dt\,.

(31)

In this case, the PB phase (
31
) is determined by the geometric frequency shift related to variations of the parameter
\varphi(t)
, whereas the dynamic contribution vanishes:
\Delta\omega_{d}=0
. See Fig.
3
(a) in the main text.

Second, for a vertically or horizontally polarized incident light,
\vec{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{matrix}\tau\\
0\\
0\end{matrix}\right)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle d\Phi_{PB}=-D_{\tau}\,\tau\left(-\frac{1}{2}\cos 2\varphi\,d\delta+\sin 2\varphi\sin\delta\,d\varphi\right)=-D_{\tau}(\Delta\omega_{d}+\Delta\omega_{g})\,dt\,,

(32)

where
D_{\tau}=\dfrac{1-\tau^{\prime}\tau}{1+\tau^{\prime}\tau}
. In this case, the PB phase (
32
) is proportional to the sum of the geometric and dynamic frequency shifts (
9
), i.e., to the total frequency shift.

Finally, for a diagonally-polarized incident light,
\vec{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{matrix}0\\
\chi\\
0\end{matrix}\right)
, we derive:

\displaystyle d\Phi_{PB}=-D_{\chi}\,\chi\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\varphi\,d\delta+\cos 2\varphi\sin\delta\,d\varphi\right)=-D_{\chi}(\Delta\omega_{d}+\Delta\omega_{g})\,dt\,,

(33)

where
D_{\chi}=\dfrac{1-\chi^{\prime}\chi}{1+\chi^{\prime}\chi}
. The PB phase (
33
) is also proportional to the sum of the geometric and dynamic frequency shifts (
9
).

Thus, Eqs. (
31
)–(
33
) show that the PB phase is related to the
total
frequency shift induced by variations of the parameters
\delta(t)
and
\varphi(t)
. This phase is ‘geometric’ only in the sense of its relation to the solid angle on the Poincaré sphere. Moreover, the coefficients
D_{\tau}
and
D_{\chi}
in Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) show that there is no universal relation between the observable frequency shift and the PB phase (i.e., solid angles on the Poincaré sphere).

For the incident
\tau
-linear or
\chi
-linear polarizations,
1\pm\vec{\Sigma}\cdot\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime}=1\pm\tau^{\prime}\tau
or
1\pm\vec{\Sigma}\cdot\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime}=1\pm\chi^{\prime}\chi

D_{\tau}
and
D_{\chi}

d\tilde{\Phi}_{PB}=-\frac{(\vec{\Sigma}\times\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime})\cdot d\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime}}{2(1-\vec{\Sigma}\cdot\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime})}\,,

(34)

which corresponds to plus half of the solid angle formed by the Stokes vectors
-\vec{\Sigma}
,
\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime}
, and
\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime}+d\vec{\Sigma}^{\prime}

, Fig. 3(b)
For this definition, Eqs. (
32

33

\displaystyle d\tilde{\Phi}_{PB}=-(\Delta\omega_{d}+\Delta\omega_{g})\,dt\,.

(35)

However, in this case Eq. (
31

\displaystyle d\tilde{\Phi}_{PB}=-\tilde{D}_{\sigma}\Delta\omega_{g}\,dt\,,

(36)

where
\tilde{D}_{\sigma}=\dfrac{1+\sigma^{\prime}/\sigma}{1-\sigma^{\prime}/\sigma}

Appendix B
Another spherical representation for time-varying waveplate

Since the total frequency shift
\Delta\omega
consists of the two contributions proportional to
\partial\delta/\partial t
and
\partial\varphi/\partial t
, Eqs. (
9
), the phase shift accumulated during evolution of the waveplate can be written in the form
\Delta\Phi=-\int_{0}^{t}\Delta\omega\,dt=\int({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}d\delta+{\mathcal{A}}_{\varphi}d\varphi)
, i.e., as a contour integral of the corresponding vector field
({\mathcal{A}}_{\delta},{\mathcal{A}}_{\varphi})
connection in the
(\delta,\varphi)
space. This representation acquires an interesting form if we assume that this space is a
sphere
with spherical angles
(\theta,\phi)=(\delta,2\varphi)
. In this case, we obtain:

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi

\displaystyle=\int({\mathcal{A}}_{\theta}\,d\theta+{\mathcal{A}}_{\phi}\sin\theta\,d\phi)\,,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\tau\cos\phi+\chi\sin\phi)\,,\quad{\mathcal{A}}_{\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\sigma\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}-\tau\sin\phi+\chi\cos\phi\right).

(37)

Here the
\sigma
-dependent term in
{\mathcal{A}}_{\phi}
corresponds to half of the standard connection on the sphere.

For a cyclic evolution on the
(\theta,\phi)
-sphere, the contour integral can be transformed into the corresponding surface integral:

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi

\displaystyle=\iint{\mathcal{F}}\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\,,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}({\mathcal{A}}_{\phi}\sin\theta)-\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}_{\theta}}{\partial\phi}=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\sigma+\tau\frac{\sin\phi}{\sin\theta}-\chi\frac{\cos\phi}{\sin\theta}\right).

(38)

Here the
\sigma
-term corresponds to half of the solid angle enclosed by the contour on the
(\theta,\phi)
-sphere. Thus, for a circularly-polarized incident light with
\tau=\chi=0
, the total phase acquires the form similar to the PB phase, but now on the
(\theta,\phi)
-sphere instead of the Poincaré sphere. For a non-circular incident polarization, there is no such simple geometric interpretation. This agrees with the main conclusions from the consideration of the PB phase on the Poincaré sphere.

Note the difference between the two representations. The Poincaré sphere represents the polarization
(\tau,\chi,\sigma)
-states of the incident and transmitted light, which depend on the waveplate parameters
(\delta,\varphi)
. Calculations of the PB phase on this sphere use a fixed standard connection and curvature, corresponding to the solid angle. In contrast, the
(\theta,\phi)
-sphere represents the
(\delta,\varphi)
-state of the plate. Calculations of the observable phase
\Delta\Phi
on this sphere involve an effective connection
({\mathcal{A}}_{\theta},{\mathcal{A}}_{\phi})
and curvature
{\mathcal{F}}
depending on the polarization
(\tau,\chi,\sigma)
-state of the incident light. In both representations, only the case of circular incoming polarization allows clear geometric interpretations.
