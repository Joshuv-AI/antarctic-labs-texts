Depression, diabetes, and digitally governed integrated public mental health services
Authors: Deby F. Eprilianto, Yuyun E. K. Sari
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Narra and colleagues’ systematic review and meta-analysis[1] on depression and glycemic control in type 2 diabetes mellitus provides an important reminder that chronic disease outcomes are shaped by mental health, service organization, and patient behavior. Even though the reported HbA1c difference was modest and the certainty of evidence was limited, the policy message remains significant: diabetes care cannot be treated as a purely metabolic service.

The findings support a digitally governed integrated care pathway in which depression screening becomes a routine component of non-communicable disease services.[2] In many health systems, patients with diabetes repeatedly interact with primary care, pharmacies, laboratories, and specialist clinics, yet mental health screening is often dependent on individual clinician initiative. Digital public service workflows can reduce this gap by embedding validated tools, such as PHQ-2 followed by PHQ-9,[3] into diabetes visits, electronic medical records, and follow-up systems.[4]

However, digitalization should not be understood as simple data capture. Screening must be tied to clear responsibility for action. A positive PHQ-9 result should trigger a stepped response: brief counselling, medication adherence review, family education when appropriate, tele-mental health referral, and follow-up reminders. Without such protocols, digital screening risks becomes administrative documentation rather than integrated care.

Indonesia offers a relevant policy context. The national SATUSEHAT (Indonesia’s national digital health services platform)[5] aims to improve health-data interoperability across facilities, while the broader electronic-based government agenda emphasizes integrated, accountable, and user-oriented public services. These reforms create an opportunity to connect diabetes management, mental health screening, and community follow-up in a way that empowers patients rather than merely monitoring them. For example, assisted digital workflows through community health workers could help patients who lack smartphones, have low digital literacy, or fear stigma in disclosing psychological symptoms.

Ethical governance is equally important. Mental health data are sensitive and may expose patients to stigma or discrimination. Therefore, integrated digital services should include consent, privacy safeguards, role-based access, and clear limits on secondary data use. Patient empowerment should be measured not only by symptom scores but also by improved understanding, shared decision-making, adherence support, and confidence in self-management.

The article by Narra et al. should therefore stimulate not only further prospective research but also service redesign. Depression screening in diabetes care can become a quality indicator for public health systems when it is linked to interoperable records, trained providers, referral capacity, and accountability for follow-up. Such a model would translate psychiatric evidence into better chronic disease governance.
