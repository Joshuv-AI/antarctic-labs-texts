Rapid weight loss practices and methods among Mongolian judo athletes
Authors: Enkhzaya Battsengel, Jaesin Sa, Jounghee Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose] Rapid weight loss is prevalent and potentially hazardous in combat sports; however, evidence among Mongolian athletes remains limited. This study characterized rapid weight loss practices among Mongolian judo athletes and compared behavioral practices and social influences between Limited-Method-Use and Combined-Method-Use profiles.[Methods] This cross-sectional study included 100 competitive Mongolian judo athletes. Of the 81 athletes who reported rapid weight loss, hierarchical cluster analysis classified 57 as Limited-Method-Use and 24 as Combined-Method-Use based on 12 weight-cutting modalities.[Results] Pre-competition rapid weight loss was reported by 81.0% of participants. Compared with the Limited-Method-Use group, the Combined-Method-Use group had greater body mass reduction (6.86% vs. 5.25%, p = 0.001) and post-competition body mass regain (6.62% vs. 5.04%, p = 0.001). The Combined-Method-Use group relied heavily on multifaceted dietary restrictions and thermal dehydration methods [sauna, fluid restriction, and plastic suit training (all p < 0.05)]. Regression analysis identified total body mass reduction percentage as the sole significant predictor of weight regain (β = 0.647, p < 0.001). Coaches and teammates were the primary social influences, whereas healthcare professionals had limited influence.[Conclusion] Rapid weight loss is deeply embedded as a normative practice within the Mongolian judo community, with many competitors adopting aggressive, dehydration-driven methodologies. The strong association between weight cuts and severe weight regain highlights the hazardous nature of these practices. These insights underscore the critical need for evidence-based sports nutrition education and preventative screenings targeting athletes and coaching staff.

INTRODUCTION

Rapid weight loss (RWL) refers to a rapid, short-term reduction in total body mass, typically achieved within 2-7 days before competition through severe caloric restriction, voluntary fluid deprivation, and active thermal dehydration. Although scholarly interest in weight cutting in combat sports expanded substantially during the 1970s [1], weight-making practices had been documented considerably earlier, including reports in combat athletes as early as 1930 [2,3]. RWL is highly prevalent in weight-classified sports, such as judo, wrestling, and boxing, with international studies reporting prevalence rates of 60-90% [4-9]. To gain a competitive advantage, athletes often intentionally reduce a substantial proportion of body mass, typically exceeding 5%, within a few days before competition through aggressive methods such as severe energy and fluid restriction, induced sweating, and increased training volume [10]. However, these practices are associated with severe adverse health consequences, including acute dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, impaired physical performance, and hormonal disturbances [7,11-13].

Evidence regarding the effects of RWL on athletic performance and physiological parameters remains equivocal. Some studies suggest that RWL compromises strength, endurance, and overall performance [14,15], whereas others indicate that performance may be preserved through proactive fluid and energy replenishment during the recovery period following official weigh-ins [16-18]. Current International Judo Federation regulations require athletes selected for a random weigh-in on the morning of competition to weigh no more than 5% above the official maximum limit for their weight category. This rule may influence post-weigh-in body mass regain strategies among judo athletes [19].

Despite growing international concern regarding the safety and ethics of RWL [20,21], empirical data on these practices among Mongolian judo athletes remain scarce. Mongolian athletes train within a distinct dietary and sociocultural context characterized by traditional diets rich in animal proteins and fats [22], long-standing perceptions of body mass and physical strength associated with traditional Mongolian wrestling (Bokh), in which weight categories are absent and greater body mass is traditionally associated with physical power, and variable access to specialized sports nutrition support compared with Western or East Asian cohorts. National dietary patterns in Mongolia are further shaped by long-standing food preferences and the limited seasonal availability of diverse food groups [23]. These factors may create contextual challenges for adherence to standardized sports nutrition guidelines. Rather than assuming distinct behavioral outcomes, understanding how these specific socio-cultural contexts potentially intersect with RWL practices and recovery strategies represents an important area of empirical inquiry. Studying Mongolian judo athletes therefore extends the global combat sports literature by describing weight-cutting practices within a previously underrepresented regional context. Establishing baseline evidence on RWL in this population may inform future health risk assessments, tailored sports nutrition education, and evidence-based interventions. Accordingly, this study aimed to (1) investigate the prevalence, frequency, magnitude, and methods of RWL among Mongolian judo athletes, and (2) compare RWL practices and sources of social influence between the Limited-Method-Use and Combined-Method-Use groups.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

Data were collected between January and February 2026 during the competitive season using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. A non-probability convenience sampling strategy was employed to recruit 100 competitive judo athletes from the Mongolian national team and five prominent judo training facilities affiliated with the Mongolian Judo Association. Of the 100 participants, 81 (81%) reported engaging in RWL practices, based on the screening question, “Did you cut weight to compete last season?” (Figure 1); their data were subsequently utilized for detailed analysis of RWL behaviors and social influences. Head coaches and team administrators at designated national training sites in Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia, were contacted to facilitate recruitment. Potential participants present during the scheduled group training sessions were verbally briefed on the study objectives and invited to participate voluntarily.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a minimum of 3 years of judo training experience, consistent with previous RWL research in combat-sport athletes using the same minimum training duration [24]; (3) current participation in regular, structured training; and (4) participation in at least one official competition during the preceding 12 months. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kunsan National University (IRB No. 1040117-202512-HR-034). Before data collection, all participants were informed of the study purpose and provided written informed consent.

Measurements and Instrumentation

Anthropometric measurements were obtained to assess body composition and basic physical characteristics. Body mass and body composition were measured using a bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody 970, InBody Co., Seoul, Korea) while participants wore light clothing and no shoes. Height was measured using a stadiometer to the nearest 0.1 cm. All measurements were performed by the lead investigators during morning sessions at the team training centers under standardized conditions.

In addition to anthropometric assessment, RWL behaviors, self-reported post-competition body mass regain, weight fluctuation history, and specific weight-loss methods were assessed using the Mongolian version of the Rapid Weight Loss Questionnaire (RWLQ), a validated instrument designed to assess the prevalence, frequency, magnitude, and methods of weight loss among weight-category athletes [25]. The RWLQ is widely utilized in combat sports, including judo and wrestling, and includes items on fluid and food restriction, sauna/sweat suit use, increased training load, and pharmacological methods (e.g., diuretics and laxatives). The Mongolian version was developed using standard forward-backward translation procedures. To assess linguistic clarity and conceptual equivalence, the translated instrument was reviewed by an expert panel consisting of 10 specialists (including sports scientists, nutritionists, and experienced coaches). The content validity of the translated questionnaire was robust, with scale-level content validity indices (S-CVI/Ave) of 0.96 for relevance and 0.98 for clarity, supported by significant agreement among the experts (Kendall’s W, p < 0.05). A pilot study involving 30 active combat athletes was conducted to assess comprehensibility and readability; these participants were excluded from the main analytical sample. The RWLQ-MGL yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.61, indicating modest internal consistency. Accordingly, findings based on this instrument should be interpreted cautiously.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Athletes were classified according to their RWL profiles using hierarchical cluster analysis with Ward’s linkage method and squared Euclidean distance applied to binary indicators (yes/no) of 12 RWL methods: food restriction, fluid restriction, sauna utilization, rubber/plastic suit training, room temperature manipulation, increased training volume, starvation, spitting, laxatives, diet pills, diuretics, and vomiting. Based on the rate of body mass loss, athletes losing ≥ 2.0% of body mass/day were classified as high risk because rapid weight loss has been consistently associated with dehydration, impaired physical performance, endocrine disturbances, and other adverse physiological outcomes [10,11,13].

Differences between the identified RWL groups were examined using independent-samples t tests for normally distributed continuous variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed continuous variables (e.g., weight loss frequency). Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square tests. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of post-competition weight regain percentage. The four predictors—body mass loss percentage, weight loss duration, sex, and weight loss group—were selected based on previous research examining RWL behaviors and post-competition weight dynamics in combat-sport athletes [1], as they represent key characteristics of the weight-loss process and individual athlete profile. These variables were entered into the model as independent variables. Unstandardized and standardized regression coefficients were reported. Before model interpretation, multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values, with thresholds of VIF < 5.0 and tolerance > 0.10. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05; p values from 0.05 to 0.10 were considered suggestive without reaching statistical significance.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

The Limited-Method-Use (n = 57) and Combined-Method-Use (n = 24) groups did not differ significantly in age, height, body mass, sex distribution, training experience, age at RWL onset, or typical RWL duration (all p > 0.05; Table 1).

Rapid Weight Loss Characteristics

Weight loss frequency did not differ significantly between the groups (5.0 vs. 4.5 times/year, p = 0.504). However, the Combined-Method-Use group lost significantly more weight than the Limited-Method-Use group, both in absolute terms (4.75 vs. 3.68 kg, p = 0.008) and as a percentage of body mass (6.86% vs. 5.25%, p = 0.001). A significantly higher proportion of athletes in the Combined-Method-Use group reported losing ≥ 5% of their body mass compared with that in the Limited-Method-Use group (79.2% vs. 52.6%, p = 0.026). The proportion meeting the high-risk weight loss rate (≥ 2.0% body mass/day) did not differ significantly between groups (25.0% vs. 21.1%, p = 0.696). Post-competition weight regain was significantly greater in the Combined-Method-Use group than in the Limited-Method-Use group, both in absolute terms (4.71 vs. 3.54 kg, p = 0.005) and as a percentage of body mass (6.62% vs. 5.04%, p = 0.001; Table 2).

Sources of Social Influence

No significant between-group differences were observed in the reported influence of coach, teammate, family, internet, dietitian, doctor or medical staff, or other sports athletes (all p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Comparison of RWL Methods

The Combined-Method-Use group reported significantly higher prevalence of food restriction (87.5% vs. 61.4%, p = 0.001), fluid restriction (75.0% vs. 49.1%, p = 0.032), room temperature manipulation (70.8% vs. 32.1%, p = 0.001), sauna use (95.8% vs. 7.0%, p < 0.001), and training in rubber/plastic suits (79.2% vs. 36.8%, p = 0.001) than the Limited-Method-Use group (Table 4). No significant differences were observed in increased training volume, starvation, spitting, all-day plastic suit use, or laxative use (all p > 0.05). Self-induced vomiting was reported by one athlete in the Combined-Method-Use group; no participant in either group reported using diet pills or diuretics.

Multiple Linear Regression Predicting Post-Competition Weight Regain Percentage

Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of post-competition weight regain percentage. The overall model was statistically significant (F(4, 76) = 20.029, p < 0.001) and explained 51.3% of the variance in weight regain percentage (R² = 0.513; adjusted R² = 0.488). Body mass loss percentage was the only significant predictor of post-competition weight regain percentage (B = 0.646, β = 0.647, p < 0.001), indicating that greater percentage weight loss during the RWL period was associated with a greater percentage of post-competition weight regain. Weight loss duration, sex, and weight loss group were not significant predictors (all p > 0.05; Table 5). VIFs ranged from 1.02 to 1.42 and tolerance values from 0.70 to 0.98, indicating no problematic multicollinearity and no violation of the prespecified thresholds (VIF < 5.0; tolerance > 0.10).

DISCUSSION

This study provides one of the few comprehensive assessments of RWL practices among Mongolian judo athletes. A substantial majority of the sampled athletes reported pre-competition weight reduction, a prevalence comparable to the high rates reported in international cohorts across combat sports [4-9,24,26]. These findings indicate that RWL is common within the Mongolian judo community and occurs at a frequency similar to that reported elsewhere. The present study extends previous work by examining behavioral patterns rather than considering individual weightloss methods in isolation [1,5,8,9,27]. Classifying athletes into Limited-Method-Use and Combined-Method-Use profiles based on their reported methods provides a more integrated description of weight-cutting practices in this population.

Athletes predominantly reported traditional weight-loss strategies, including caloric restriction, fluid restriction, and increased training volume, consistent with previous studies [4,9,27]. Although these methods are commonly used for acute weight reduction [1], they may impose substantial physiological strain, including acute fluid imbalance, metabolic fatigue, and impaired athletic performance [13-15], alongside other adverse health effects [28,29]. The groups differed primarily in the extent to which multiple strategies were combined: the Combined-Method-Use group more frequently reported food restriction, fluid restriction, sauna use, room temperature manipulation, and training in rubber/plastic suits. By contrast, extreme or pharmacological methods, including laxatives, diet pills, diuretics, and self-induced vomiting, were rarely reported overall and did not differ significantly between groups.

Although these extreme behaviors were infrequent overall, their concentrated presence within the Combined-Method-Use group remains concerning because of potential adverse health effects, highlighting the need to address these practices at both the individual and broader sporting environment levels [20,30]. Crucially, from a psychopathological standpoint, even a single report of a purging behavior such as self-induced vomiting warrants clinical attention; such practices serve as critical subclinical triggers that can rapidly transition into clinical eating disorders, such as bulimia nervosa or anorexia athletica, in weight-sensitive sports environments [31]. Furthermore, empirical evidence in combat sports indicates that reliance on these extreme, highly distressing compensatory methods significantly exacerbates cognitive anxiety, impairs self-esteem, and ultimately drives burnout, occasionally leading to premature withdrawal from the sport [32,33]. Therefore, the isolated case of vomiting should not diminish the urgency of preventative screening.

The cumulative magnitude of weight loss further illustrates the potential health risk associated with these practices, as the Combined-Method-Use group lost a significantly greater percentage of body mass than the Limited-Method-Use group. Across the total sample, a substantial proportion of athletes exceeded the 5% body mass threshold, which has been associated with increased health risk [11,12,15]. A notable proportion of Mongolian judo athletes met the high-risk daily weight loss threshold of ≥ 2% body mass/day. This pattern is concerning because current sports nutrition guidance recommends gradual weight reduction of no more than 1-1.5% of body mass per week [12,34]. A recent review similarly reported that RWL is highly prevalent among judo competitors, who commonly lose approximately 5% of body mass before competition, and highlighted the potential health risks of these practices. Although RWL may not necessarily impair sports performance when sufficient recovery time is available between weigh-in and competition, the absence of short-term performance impairment does not establish physiological safety [35].

Although the proportion of athletes meeting the highrisk daily weight loss threshold did not differ significantly between groups, the Combined-Method-Use group achieved a significantly greater overall weight cut and greater post-competition weight regain. This pattern suggests that the greater severity of the combined-method profile may reflect the cumulative magnitude of weight loss and the simultaneous use of multiple thermal and dietary stressors rather than the daily rate of weight loss alone [21,34]. The regression analysis supported this pattern, with body mass loss percentage emerging as the only significant predictor of post-competition weight regain. Greater weight loss was associated with greater subsequent regain, reinforcing concerns regarding cyclical weight fluctuation in combat sports. Previous recommendations for judo emphasize structured weight-management programs that establish appropriate minimum competitive weights, monitor body mass and hydration, and prevent excessive short-term weight reduction [36]. These approaches are particularly relevant to Mongolian judo, given the high prevalence and magnitude of RWL observed in this study, and support a shift from acute pre-competition weight cutting toward longer-term, supervised weight management.

The pronounced use of saunas, rubber/plastic suits, and room temperature manipulation in the Combined-Method-Use group suggests a predominance of dehydration-based strategies within this profile. Consequently, the greater weight loss and post-competition regain observed in this group likely reflect acute shifts in total body water rather than substantial changes in tissue mass. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that recovery after rapid weight loss involves intensive refueling and rehydration. However, complete restoration of body water and cellular glycogen stores may require more than 24-48 hours, indicating that rapid post-competition weight regain should not be equated with complete physiological recovery [9,16].

These behavioral patterns may be influenced by the social environment of training. Coaches and teammates were the primary reported sources of influence on RWL practices, whereas healthcare professionals had limited influence. This finding is consistent with qualitative research suggesting that weight regulation among combat-sport athletes may extend beyond pursuit of a physical advantage and become integrated into sporting identity, pre-competition preparation, and perceptions of discipline and psychological readiness37. Predominant reliance on non-professional and traditional mentorship is consistent with historical patterns in combat sports [9,27,33] and may facilitate the transmission of potentially harmful weight-cutting practices. Without evidence-based interventions, such practices may continue to be modeled by senior athletes and reinforced within coaching environments. These findings support structured educational initiatives targeting athletes and the coaches who help shape sport-specific norms [20,34,38,39]. Integrating qualified sports nutrition professionals into the training environment may help reconcile competitive weight management with long-term athlete health [9,12,20,39].

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the observed relationships. Second, the reliance on self-administered questionnaires introduces potential recall and social desirability biases, particularly for sensitive or prohibited weight-loss methods. Third, participants were recruited from the national team and major clubs affiliated with the Mongolian Judo Association, resulting in a relatively homogeneous sample of highly trained competitors exposed to structured, high-intensity training environments. Consequently, the findings may not be generalizable to recreational judo athletes or combat-sport athletes in different sociocultural settings. Nevertheless, this homogeneity strengthens the internal validity of the findings for elite Mongolian judo. Future longitudinal studies should examine the long-term effects of cyclical RWL on body composition, athletic longevity, and health outcomes. Future research should further evaluate whether targeted nutrition education improves weight-management behaviors, nutritional literacy, and hydration practices among Mongolian judo athletes.
