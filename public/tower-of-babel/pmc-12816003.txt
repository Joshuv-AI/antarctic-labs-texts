Low-carbon supply chain logistics risk prediction using meta-learning-based graph convolutional network on prototype space
Authors: Yueqi Wang, Yonghe Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting rare yet high-impact risks in low-carbon supply chains (LCSCs) is critical for sustainable operations but challenged by data imbalance and complex interdependencies. This study proposes a Meta-Learning-based Graph Convolutional Network on Prototype Space (ML-GCNPS) to address these issues. Using a real-world, multi-modal dataset of firm-level carbon emissions and supply-chain relationships, we model the LCSC as a graph. The model first extracts node features and embeds them into a prototype space for enhanced class discrimination. An adaptive Vertex-to-Edge (V2E) network then constructs the graph topology, enabling risk propagation via GCN. Extensive experiments demonstrate that ML-GCNPS significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, achieving an AUPRC of 0.850 and reducing the False Negative Rate (FNR) to 0.080. These results, coupled with a low Weighted Average Cost (WAC) of 45, confirm its practical value for accurate and cost-effective risk early-warning.Keywords: Meta-learning, Graph convolutional network, Vertex-to-Edge, Prototype spaceSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Amid global efforts to promote the “carbon peak and carbon neutrality” strategy, low-carbon supply chains have become a crucial pathway toward achieving green transformation1. However, due to their complex structures, numerous interconnections, and vulnerability to policy and environmental fluctuations, these systems face multiple uncertainties and risks during logistics operations—such as supplier environmental violations, cross-border transport disruptions, and carbon-quota fluctuations2. These risks, though infrequent, can have severe consequences, leading to limited historical samples and highly imbalanced data distributions3. Therefore, developing an intelligent risk-prediction model that integrates graph-structure modeling and few-shot learning (FSL) while balancing prediction accuracy, generalization, and interpretability has become a key research challenge in low-carbon supply-chain management.

Most existing approaches rely on statistical or shallow machine-learning models. For example, Niu et al.4 proposed an integrated analytical framework combining the Slack-Based Measure model with machine-learning techniques to predict regional low-carbon economic development trends through an efficiency-evaluation system. Wang et al.5 developed an intelligent renewable-energy output prediction model by integrating a gated recurrent unit network with Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME), providing a transparent and reliable framework for sustainable-energy management and decision-making in low-carbon city development. However, these methods struggle to capture the complex topological structures, dynamic risk-propagation mechanisms, and multi-source heterogeneous data inherent in supply chains. They also lack rapid generalization in small-sample scenarios, which limits proactive risk warning and decision support.

To address these challenges, this study proposes a meta-learning (ML)-based graph convolutional network (GCN) on prototype space model (ML-GCNPS), designed to tackle the construction of high-dimensional heterogeneous relationships, mitigate weak generalization caused by scarce high-risk samples, and balance prediction accuracy with business cost in low-carbon supply-chain logistics risk prediction. Feature extraction module: We design a convolutional network that extracts discriminative features from multi-modal firm data, serving as a robust foundation for subsequent graph learning and few-shot classification.

Furthermore, compared to cost-sensitive learning, focal loss, and calibrated thresholds, this paper utilizes machine learning to train the model on a large number of heterogeneous prediction tasks, enabling it to acquire the capability to capture general patterns of risk prediction. Consequently, when faced with new risk categories containing only minimal samples, the model can achieve rapid generalization.

Related work

Amid global efforts toward carbon neutrality and green transformation, low-carbon supply chains have become a key pillar of sustainable development6. Yet, their complex structure, dynamic operations, and reliance on external factors introduce major risks. Carbon emissions within these supply chains are easily affected by policy changes, energy-price fluctuations, and extreme weather events. Traditional operational risks combine with those from the low-carbon transition, forming a new risk system marked by multiple sources, interlinked pathways, and hidden vulnerabilities. Accurate forecasting and effective management of such risks help enterprises reduce disruptions and compliance losses. More importantly, they support national carbon-neutrality goals and strengthen the resilience and green competitiveness of industrial supply chains7.

Low-carbon projections based on traditional methods

Traditional approaches to predicting risks in low-carbon supply chains mainly rely on statistical analysis, expert systems, and conventional machine-learning models. These methods usually construct deterministic or probabilistic frameworks based on historical data, identifying linear relationships, rule-based patterns, or shallow dependencies to assess risks. Dixit et al.8 developed a hybrid predictive model that combines decision trees with Bayesian networks to improve forecasting accuracy and capture probabilistic dependencies in data-driven decision-making across multiple industries. Zhu et al.9 proposed an integrated data-driven framework that merges predictive analytics, anomaly detection, and multi-objective optimization. By incorporating attention-based long short-term memory (LSTM) models, their approach significantly enhances supply-chain forecasting accuracy and resilience to disruptions, achieving measurable operational and sustainability benefits. Zhou et al.10 introduced a hybrid framework combining the analytic hierarchy process with fuzzy comprehensive evaluation to systematically assess multidimensional sustainability risks in the coal supply chain.

However, these traditional methods face limitations when addressing high-dimensional, nonlinear, and dynamically evolving supply-chain systems. They often struggle to integrate multi-source heterogeneous data effectively and depend heavily on manual feature engineering and domain-specific prior knowledge, resulting in restricted generalization and limited adaptability.

Low-carbon projections based on deep learning methods

Deep learning enables the extraction of complex risk patterns from multi-source and heterogeneous data, effectively addressing challenges such as data complexity and sparse labeling often encountered during the green transformation of high-carbon industries11. Wang et al.12 developed a forecasting model based on singular spectrum analysis and LSTM networks to more accurately quantify financial risks in the context of carbon neutrality. Wang et al.13 proposed a deep neural network–based investment decision framework for low-carbon grid transformation. By combining a multi-branch architecture with a spatiotemporal recurrent neural network, their method significantly improves the predictive accuracy of multi-energy installed capacity, sustainability metrics, and investment returns, achieving accuracy levels above 90%. Luo et al.14 introduced a composite forecasting model that integrates a multiverse quantum harmony search algorithm with a dynamic fuzzy system. When coupled with data envelopment analysis, this approach enables precise carbon-emission forecasting and effective evaluation of low-carbon economic development efficiency.

Low-carbon projections based on ML methods

ML enables models to adapt rapidly to sparsely labeled data in target domains by transferring knowledge from multiple related tasks. This approach greatly enhances generalization in predicting emerging risks such as supply-chain carbon exposure and energy-transition uncertainty15. Li et al.16 proposed a carbon-emission forecasting method that combines ML with differential LSTM, transferring multi-source task knowledge through a ML framework while reducing dependence on large datasets. Enamoto et al.17 developed a ML-based multivariate single-step fusion model for greenhouse gas emission forecasting. By incorporating task-specific feature extraction and meta-knowledge transfer, their model achieved high prediction accuracy on 60 years of Brazilian emission data, demonstrating strong generalization and computational efficiency in low-carbon applications. Mohammed et al.18 introduced a forest-carbon assessment framework that integrates multi-source remote-sensing data with ML. Using ensemble learning and statistical testing, their method significantly improved the prediction accuracy of forest above-ground carbon stocks in data-scarce regions and revealed notable differences.

ML-GCNPS

The structure of the ML-GCNPS model is illustrated in Fig. 1. It primarily comprises two components: a graph vertex set generation network and an information transmission network. The vertex set generation network includes a feature extraction module and a prototype space embedding module, which jointly generate the representations of each vertex in the graph, forming the foundation for leveraging the propagation capability of the GCN. The information transmission network consists of the V2E mapping module and the GCN module, which construct the edge-set structure and facilitate information transmission between nodes. Together, these modules enable the model to perform label prediction for the query set samples.

ML-GCNPS model description.

This paper constructs a risk prediction task under the situational meta learning paradigm. Each task by randomly sampling a 5-way 1-shot 15 query set from the meta training data set. That is, each task contains 5 different risk classes, each class has 1 tagged support example and 15 unlabeled query examples, resulting in a support set size of 5 and a query set size of 75. The goal of training model is to minimize the cross entropy loss of query set prediction. The task specific adaptive inner loop learning rate is set to 0.01, with 5 gradient steps, while the outer loop learning rate of Adam optimizer is 0.001.

The V2E network is a four-layer MLP with ReLU activation and batch normalization after each hidden layer, culminating in a sigmoid output to constrain edge weights between 0 and 1. A sparsification strategy integrating domain knowledge is applied, where an edge is activated and weighted only if the prior material-flow correlation between firms exceeds a threshold of 0.3, ensuring learned edges are grounded in real supply-chain relationships. The undirected graph assumption is justified by the mutual nature of risk propagation in supply chains, and while the V2E process has O(N2) complexity, it remains feasible for the typical graph sizes in this study.

Experimental results and analysis

Experimental dataset and hardware devices

Experimental dataset

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed model, this study employs a real-world supply-chain dataset jointly developed by Carbon Monitor (carbonmonitor.org.cn) and the International Energy Agency (IEA) (World Energy Outlook 2021 dataset (https://www.iea.org/data-and-statistics/data-product/world-energy-outlook-2021-free-dataset)). It covers over 500 global companies from 2015 to 2022. For node features, we extracted corporate carbon-emission intensity, financial stability Z-scores, geographic coordinates, and a binary environmental-sensitivity indicator; missing financial and emission values were handled via multiple imputation, and continuous variables were Z-score normalized. Undirected, weighted edges were constructed based on the Pearson correlation of material flow between firms, thresholded at 0.3 to sparsify the graph; this construction was validated against S&P Capital IQ records for a 100-firm subset, achieving 92% consistency. Risk labels (Low, Medium, High) were extracted from public financial reports and verified news sources, with a labeling window of one fiscal year. Three annotators independently labeled events, achieving a Fleiss’ Kappa of 0.81, resulting in a final class distribution of 78.0% Low-Risk, 16.3% Medium-Risk, and 5.7% High-Risk.

Event classification system and annotation procedure: To ensure the accuracy and consistency of risk labels, we established a detailed event classification and annotation protocol. (1) Event Classification System: A three-tier risk classification method was adopted to explicitly categorize supply chain logistics risk events into “Low Risk,” “Medium Risk,” and “High Risk.” (2) Annotation Window: The annotation window for risk events was defined as one full fiscal year following the occurrence of the event, ensuring comprehensive capture of its financial and operational impact on the enterprise. (3) Deduplication Rule: For multiple reports within the same fiscal year triggered by the same root cause, consolidation was performed based on unique event IDs or the date of the initial report, ensuring each independent risk event was recorded only once in the dataset. (4) Annotator Workflow: Three trained annotators worked independently, extracting risk events from public financial reports and verified news sources and assigning labels according to the established classification system, followed by a consensus phase. (5) Inter-Annotator Consistency: We evaluated the annotation results of the first 200 independent events, calculating a Fleiss’ Kappa coefficient of 0.81, indicating an “almost perfect” level of agreement among annotators, thereby effectively ensuring label quality and the external validity of the study.

Hardware devices

The experiments were conducted on a high-performance workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB VRAM), enabling efficient parallel computation for large-scale graph neural network (GNN) training and memory-intensive task processing. The hardware platform also included an Intel Xeon Gold 6226R CPU and 256 GB DDR4 memory, ensuring stable data preprocessing and seamless graph-structure loading for the large-scale supply-chain dataset. All experiments were implemented using the PyTorch 1.12 framework with CUDA 11.6 support, and model training was performed under single-precision floating-point (FP32) computation. The complete training cycle of the ML-GCNPS model required approximately 17 h on average.

All experiments were carried out within the PyTorch framework, and the models were optimized using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, dynamically adjusted via a cosine annealing learning-rate schedule. In ML tasks, the number of inner-loop update steps was set to 5, with an inner learning rate of 0.01 and a task batch size of 4. The hidden-layer dimension of all model components was fixed at 128, and the GCN module adopted a two-layer architecture. Each layer was followed by a ReLU activation function and a Dropout mechanism with a rate of 0.5 to prevent overfitting.

The class prototypes in the prototype space were computed as the mean feature representations of samples within each support set category. For feature extraction, a pretrained ResNet-18 network was employed to enhance convergence speed and stability. An early-stopping strategy was applied with a patience value of 20 epochs, and the maximum training epoch was limited to 500. All hyperparameters were tuned via grid search using the validation task set to ensure optimal performance and generalization.

Baseline models and performance indicators

Baseline models

To verify the effectiveness of the ML-GCNPS model proposed in this article, we selected 5 state-of-the-art baseline models for comparison. (1) Graph ML via Local Subgraphs (G-MLLS)19: This model extracts local subgraphs around the central node for ML, which can effectively handle heterogeneous graph structures and alleviate the distribution shift problem caused by new nodes. It is a representative method in the field of small sample learning in graphs. (2) Model Agnostic ML with Graph Neural Networks (MAML-GNN)20: This baseline replaces the underlying models in the classic MAML framework with GNN, enabling the models to obtain good GCN initialization parameters through ML and quickly adapt to new tasks. It is commonly used to evaluate the fundamental performance of combining ML with graph structures. (3) Prototypal Networks on Graph (ProtoNG) 21: This method is a direct extension of the prototype network on graph data, which performs small sample classification by computing the prototypes of nodes in the graph. It is suitable for evaluating the performance of metric based ML methods on graphs. (4) ML -based Relational GNN (ML-RGCN)22: RGCN explicitly models different types of relationships, and this baseline embeds it into a meta learning framework to handle diverse edge relationships in the supply chain, which is used to verify the importance of explicit relationship modeling for risk prediction. (5) Graph Attention Networks for FSL (GAT-FSL)23: This model introduces graph attention mechanism into small sample learning and aggregates information by assigning different weights to neighboring nodes. It can be used to test the effectiveness of attention mechanism in supply chain risk transmission modeling.

F1 score (Macro F1-Score): This metric computes the F1 value—defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall—for each class individually and then takes the macro average. By assigning equal weight to all classes, it ensures that minority (high-risk) categories are not overshadowed by majority (low-risk) ones.

Overall, all three performance metrics exhibit a monotonic increasing trend as K grows, indicating that providing more support samples for each category consistently enhances the model’s discriminative ability and classification reliability. Specifically, when K increases from 1 to 5, all performance curves display the steepest growth slope, suggesting the most significant marginal benefits of adding samples within this interval. The results of the ablation experiments demonstrate that the full ML-GCNPS model achieves optimal performance across all five evaluation metrics, particularly excelling in the safety indicator (FNR = 0.080) and the economic indicator (WAC = 45). These outcomes confirm the model’s strong ability to accurately identify high-risk events while simultaneously minimizing economic losses associated with misclassification. When the ML framework is removed (w/o ML), all metrics decline noticeably, with Macro-F1 decreasing by 8.5% and FNR increasing to 0.162. This validates the crucial role of ML in small-sample learning: by simulating multi-task training, it enables the model to obtain well-generalized initialization parameters that facilitate rapid adaptation to previously unseen risk patterns.

Eliminating the GCN structure (w/o GCN) results in the most severe degradation, with MCC dropping to 0.722 and WAC rising to 92. This underscores the indispensable role of GCN in modeling supply-chain topologies—prototype-based networks alone are insufficient to capture the complex mechanisms of risk propagation among interconnected entities. Replacing the adaptive V2E network with a fixed fully connected graph (w/o V2E Network) leads to a decline in AUPRC to 0.815, indicating that the V2E module’s adaptive edge-weight construction—based on inter-node feature differences—more accurately reflects real-world risk dependencies within supply-chain networks. This adaptive approach clearly outperforms the simplistic assumption of uniform connectivity. Finally, removing the prototype space embedding module (w/o Prototype Space) results in a 6.5% decrease in Macro-F1 and a 7.8% decrease in MCC, confirming that prototype embedding enhances the model’s capacity to extract essential class-level representations through prototype-based feature aggregation and feature–label concatenation.

Conclusions

This study proposed the ML-GCNPS model to address the challenges of limited samples, high-dimensional relational data, and class imbalance in low-carbon supply-chain logistics risk prediction. By representing supply-chain entities and their interconnections as attribute graphs, and incorporating ML task paradigms, prototype space embedding, and an adaptive V2E edge construction mechanism, the model effectively captures complex risk propagation pathways and the intrinsic class characteristics within supply-chain systems. Experimental results demonstrate that ML-GCNPS achieves outstanding overall performance, with AUPRC = 0.850 and MCC = 0.851, substantially surpassing existing baseline methods. Meanwhile, its FNR of 0.080 and WAC of 45 highlight both superior rare-risk detection capability and enhanced economic efficiency in real-world decision-making. Furthermore, the ablation experiments validate the indispensable roles and synergistic contributions of the four core modules—namely, the ML framework, GCN structure, V2E network, and prototype space embedding—in achieving robust, interpretable, and cost-effective risk prediction.

When a large number of new entities flood in simultaneously or their feature distribution significantly differs from the training set, performance still faces challenges. Therefore, exploring the integration of inductive graph learning and zero-shot learning mechanisms to more systematically address the cold-start problem is an important direction for our future research.
