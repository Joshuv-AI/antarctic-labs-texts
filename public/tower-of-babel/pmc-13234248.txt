Immediate and sustained resolution of chronic post-traumatic diplopia following lower eyelid adhesiolysis using plasma energy (Plexr): An 8-month follow-up case report
Authors: Sotiris G. Tsioumas, Getu Gamo Sagaro, Giovanna Ricci, Vincenzo Bellitto, Giulio Nittari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA plasma adhesiolysis procedure is a minimally invasive technique that uses plasma energy generated by the Plexr device to separate fibrous adhesions between tissues. Although this technology has been used in periocular procedures, its application in treating chronic restrictive diplopia is largely undocumented. This case aimed to illustrate the potential alternative to surgery in patients with long-term, post-traumatic restricted diplopia via non-surgical intervention.A 35-year-old woman presented with chronic post-traumatic diplopia persisting for eleven years after a left orbital floor blow-out fracture. She had previously undergone two orbital reconstructions using porous polyethylene (Medpor) implants, performed in 2014 and 2019, without achieving functional improvement. In October 2025, she underwent a minimally invasive procedure involving plasma-assisted adhesiolysis of the lower eyelid using the plasma energy (Plexr) device. The intervention led to immediate improvement: ocular motility was restored intraoperatively, and the patient's diplopia resolved completely within approximately 5 min.Importantly, at long-term follow-up on May 12, 2026 (approximately eight months later), the patient remained completely free of diplopia in all gaze positions, with full ocular motility bilaterally and sustained aesthetic improvement of the left lower eyelid, including reduction of edema and tissue asymmetry.This case demonstrates the potential of non-surgical plasma adhesiolysis to achieve both immediate and durable restoration of ocular motility in chronic restrictive diplopia secondary to trauma and multiple orbital surgeries.The fast improvement seen in this case suggests that plasma-based adhesion release may serve as an alternative to traditional surgery for managing long-standing post-traumatic motility restrictions.

Background

An orbital floor fracture, also known as a blowout fracture, is an isolated break in the thin orbital walls without disruption of the orbital rim [1]. These injuries are often caused by blunt trauma mechanisms such as falls, sports-related impact, traffic accidents, or interpersonal violence. Diplopia, enophthalmos, and sensory disturbances are the common complications of orbital floor fractures [1], [2], [3], [4]. Diplopia is the principal complication associated with orbital floor fractures caused by mechanical entrapment of the inferior rectus muscle or secondary fibrosis and scarring after surgical intervention [5], [6]. As a result of adhesions between the muscle sheath, the orbital fat, the periosteum, and the implant material, restrictive diplopia may persist in chronic cases. Revision surgery often provides limited improvement and carries the risk of additional scarring. The Plexr plasma device has been used in oculoplastic and aesthetic medicine for non-ablative blepharoplasty, skin tightening, and scar revision [7]. Histological studies have demonstrated that plasma energy induces controlled epidermal sublimation without damaging the basement membrane, while simultaneously inducing dermal contraction and the reorganization of elastic fibres [8]. Within 3–4 weeks, type III collagen neosynthesis occurs, enhancing tissue elasticity and texture. These biological effects suggest that Plexr may function as a precise, minimally invasive method for releasing periocular fibrotic adhesions contributing to restrictive ocular motility disorders. In this case report, we discuss the case of a female patient who experienced chronic post-traumatic diplopia that persisted for eleven years following a left orbital floor blowout fracture and was significantly improved through the administration of non-surgical plasma Adhesiolysis (Plexr).

Case presentation

Initial trauma (2014)

A 35-year-old woman presented with chronic diplopia of the left eye, ongoing since a boxing-related blow-out fracture sustained in 2014 (Fig. 1). Initial trauma assessment with computed tomography (CT) on 26 June 2014 demonstrated a left orbital floor fracture with a 4-mm bony defect and herniation of orbital fat (measuring 1.4 × 1.5 × 1.6 cm) into the maxillary sinus. The inferior rectus muscle was noted to be edematous but not entrapped. The orbital floor was surgically repaired shortly after the injury, resulting in partial improvement of ocular motility; however, residual diplopia persisted in upward gaze.Fig. 1Clinical photography of patient with a chronic diplopia of the left eye before treatment.Fig. 1

Early postoperative period (2014–2016)

After surgery in 2014, the patient reported persistent diplopia and incomplete convergence of the left eye. The visual acuity in both eyes remained at 10/10 by 2016, but ocular motility limitations remained, consistent with chronic mechanical restrictions.

Revision surgery (2019)

In April 2019, the patient underwent a revision orbital floor reconstruction at the University Hospital of Patras. To treat chronic sinusitis associated with this procedure, a porous polyethylene implant (Medpor, 0.85 mm) was placed via periocular approach in conjunction with functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS). Despite an uneventful postoperative healing, persistent diplopia and limited mobility remained with functional discomfort during gaze movements.

Ophthalmologic evaluation (2024)

As a result, the patient was reevaluated on October 24, 2024. According to the examination, both eyes had visual acuity of 10/10 (OU), and stereopsis was normal (Frisby 3/3). In the left eye, there was 3 to 4° hypotropia and 10 to 12° esotropia, mild left ptosis which improved with a right head turn and tilt. The patient also reported persistent lower eyelid edema and nausea during left gaze, strongly suggestive of restrictive mechanical pathology.

Intervention

In October 2025, Plexr was used on the left lower eyelid to release superficial and mid-depth postoperative scar adhesions from previous incisions (Fig. 2). The procedure took about 5 min and required no surgical incision. Immediately after the procedure ocular movement improved significantly afterward. Full abduction of the left eye was restored, and diplopia disappeared completely. The patient reported a sensation that “the eyes felt released” and experienced immediate relief from nausea. Following treatment, the patient showed complete restoration of abduction in the left eye and no longer had diplopia in any gaze position with symmetrical binocular eye movements. The procedure was well-tolerated, with an uneventful recovery and only mild, temporary lower eyelid swelling that resolved on its own.Fig. 2Clinical photography of Plexr plasma technology during the treatment on the left lower eyelid to release post-surgical scar adhesions at both superficial and mid-depth levels.Fig. 2

Short-term follow-up

Follow-up visits at one week and one month confirmed continued normal eye movement, with no recurrence of diplopia or complications, indicating lasting functional gains (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Clinical photography of post-treatment demonstrated sustained normal ocular motility without recurrence of diplopia or adverse events, confirming stable functional improvements.Fig. 3

Long-term follow-up (May 12, 2026)

At approximately eight months after treatment the patient reported a no recurrence of diplopia, with full ocular motility persisted bilaterally. Absence of diplopia in primary, lateral, upward, and downward gaze positions, stable binocular fusion in all gaze directions. Sustained aesthetic improvement of the left lower eyelid, and a significant reduction of lower eyelid edema and tissue asymmetry. Clinical photographs obtained at follow-up (Fig. 4) demonstrate a complete restoration of left ocular motility, symmetric eye alignment, full movement of both eyes in all gaze positions without diplopia. Discussion and conclusion

The immediate restoration of ocular motility in a patient with an eleven-year history of diplopia strongly suggests a purely mechanical aetiology, most likely due to superficial or mid-depth adhesions involving the lower lid retractors, subcutaneous periocular tissues, and peri-implant fibrosis surrounding the Medpor implant. Treatment with plasma adhesiolysis achieved precise thermal micro-disruption of these fibrotic tissue bridges, providing instantaneous mechanical release without the need for surgical incision. The persistence of normal ocular motility and absence of recurrent diplopia after eight months further suggest that plasma-induced tissue remodelling contributed to stable long-term functional recovery.

Histopathologic studies of Plexr's plasma arc provide insight into its mechanism of action [8], [9], [10]. The plasma energy induces epidermal sublimation while preserving the integrity of the basement membrane, promotes dermal contraction through reorganization of elastic fibres, and stimulates type III collagen neogenesis within 3–4 weeks, thereby enhancing tissue strength and elasticity. This controlled dermal remodelling likely accounts for the sustained clinical improvement and progressive aesthetic enhancement in the lower eyelid over time, as well as the preservation of tissue flexibility in this patient.

This case underscores the potential of plasma-based Adhesiolysis as a minimally invasive therapeutic option or adjunctive therapy for chronic restrictive diplopia, particularly in situations where conventional surgical approaches are contraindicated or have failed. The results highlight that targeted, non-surgical disruption of fibrotic adhesions can produce rapid functional recovery even after prolonged post-traumatic diplopia.

In conclusion, Plexr is effective in immediate and long-lasting restoring ocular motility in cases of chronic post-traumatic restrictive diplopia. The procedure is minimally invasive, bloodless, and painless, incision-free and so does not require anesthesia, making it an attractive minimally invasive alternative to revision orbital surgery in selected cases. By targeting fibrotic adhesions precisely, it may reduce the risks associated with open surgery while achieving immediate functional improvement. In this patient, complete resolution of diplopia occurred within minutes after treatment and remained stable for approximately eight months, accompanied by significant aesthetic improvement of the lower eyelid. However, further studies involving larger patient cohorts are necessary to evaluate long-term reproducibility, safety, and clinical indications.
