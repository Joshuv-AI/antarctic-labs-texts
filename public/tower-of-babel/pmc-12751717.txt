Effect of radial bowing on pronation and supination in pediatric forearm diaphyseal fractures: comparison of dominant and nondominant arms
Authors: Koksal Gundogdu, Ozcan Alaca, Ahmet Soyucok
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric forearm diaphyseal fractures are common and may require surgery to restore function. Radial bowing loss affects pronation-supination, but its impact on recovery and arm dominance remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of radial bowing on pronation and supination limitations in the surgical treatment of pediatric forearm fractures and to investigate differences in outcomes between fractures of the dominant and nondominant arms. MethodsForty pediatric patients with diaphyseal forearm fractures underwent surgical treatment. Pronation-supination functions and radial bowing were assessed through maximal radial bowing (MRB) and maximal radial bowing localization (MRBL) values. Patients were divided into dominant and nondominant arm fracture groups, and functional outcomes were evaluated using the Price criteria.ResultsThe MRB and MRBL values in the fractured arms were significantly lower than those in the intact arms (p < 0.001), highlighting reduced radial bowing postfracture. While no significant relationship was found between MRB-MRBL and functional outcomes in children ≤ 9 years of age, this relationship became significant in children > 9 years of age. Pronation and supination limitations were more pronounced in nondominant arm fractures than in dominant arm fractures, where functional adaptation appeared to be better.ConclusionsRadial bowing plays a critical role in preserving pronation and supination functions following pediatric forearm fractures. Maintaining radial bowing should be a primary surgical goal, particularly in patients over 9 years of age. Dominant arm fractures exhibit less functional restriction due to better adaptive capabilities, emphasizing the need for targeted rehabilitation, especially in nondominant arm fractures.Keywords: Pediatric forearm fractures, Radial bowing, Pronation, Supination

Introduction

Childhood forearm diaphyseal fractures account for 30% of pediatric fractures [1]. The treatment of these fractures is crucial because of potential functional impairments later in life. They typically occur from an indirect force transmitted to the forearm bones after a fall on an open hand [2]. The diagnosis of forearm diaphyseal fractures is generally straightforward through radiographic evaluation in addition to clinical findings such as pain, swelling, crepitus, and deformity after trauma. However, despite successful initial diagnosis, subtle deformities—particularly loss of radial bowing—may be overlooked on routine imaging and remain clinically silent until rotational limitations develop. This highlights the need for meticulous radiographic assessment to ensure complete restoration of forearm function. These fractures are classified as plastic deformation, greenstick , or complete fractures, and the displacement direction can be volar, dorsal, radial, or ulnar. The elastic nature of bones and their remodeling potential in this age group generally make conservative treatment an effective option [3]. Most radial and ulnar shaft fractures in childhood can be treated nonsurgically [4]. Surgical treatment is considered in cases where conservative methods are insufficient, when reduction cannot be achieved, or in the presence of complications such as open fractures or neurovascular injuries. Intramedullary nailing (IMN), plate-screw fixation, and external fixation are among the treatment options [5]. Recently, IMN has gained popularity due to advantages such as minimal scarring, short surgical duration, high union rates, and the absence of the need for secondary surgeries to remove implants. This method is particularly preferred for fractures of the middle third of the forearm, where remodeling challenges are more pronounced [5–7].

Restrictions in pronation and supination movements of the forearm, ranging between 3% and 30%, have been reported. These limitations can significantly affect children’s daily lives, particularly their basic functions, such as school activities and hygiene. For example, pronation is essential for writing, whereas supination is required for perineal hygiene [8, 9]. Measuring the limitations in pronation and supination is crucial for clinical decision-making and outcome evaluation in these fractures [10].

Studies on human cadavers have shown that axial deformities less than 10 degrees minimize movement restrictions, whereas deformities of 20 degrees or more result in significant functional losses [11, 12]. Owing to the complex anatomy and movements of the forearm, understanding the causes of these limitations remains challenging [13]. In general, these restrictions can result from bone malunion or pathology in the surrounding soft tissues. In particular, malunion of the radius and/or ulna is a well-known cause of supination and pronation limitations [14]. While axial deformities are easily recognized and treated, bowing deformities of the radius can often be overlooked. Deviation from normal radial bowing following a fracture is a common injury in children [15]. The bowing of the radius contributes significantly to forearm rotation around the fixed ulna. Therefore, restoring anatomical radial bowing is critical for preserving full forearm function. Previous studies have shown that a good functional outcome is possible by regaining at least 80% regular forearm rotation, which is directly related to restoring normal radial bowing [16].

However, studies examining the relationship between radial bowing and movement limitations are limited. Some studies report pronation and supination limitations without malunion [12, 17], whereas others describe cases with malunion but no movement restrictions [18]. Additionally, in some malunion cases in which anatomical alignment is corrected by osteotomy, permanent movement limitations are observed [19, 20].

There is limited research in the literature comprehensively investigating the effects of radial bowing on supination and pronation movements. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of radial bowing on pronation and supination limitations in pediatric forearm diaphyseal fractures. Furthermore, by comparing both the intact and fractured arms of the same patient, we investigated the effect of the dominant arm on functional outcomes.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study followed the principles of the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its subsequent amendments. The study was conducted with approval from the Pamukkale University Noninterventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (dated 25.07.2023; number: E-60116787-020-397020/12). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all the participants before this study commenced.

Patient selection

Patients aged 5–15 years who had both-bone forearm fractures and were treated with intramedullary osteosynthesis at the Orthopedics and Traumatology Clinic of Denizli State Hospital between July 2019 and July 2023 were included in the study. All patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and agreed to participate were evaluated. The demographic data of the patients, mechanism of injury, fracture type, time between the fracture and surgery, type of implants used, and removal times, as well as any complications that developed, were recorded.

A total of 65 pediatric patients with diaphyseal forearm fractures were initially screened between July 2019 and July 2023. Of these, 25 were excluded: 7 patients were outside the 5–15 age range, 4 had fractures close to the epiphyseal plate, 3 had associated vascular or nerve injuries, 5 presented with isolated radial or ulnar fractures, 2 had head trauma or multiple traumatic events, and 4 were lost to follow-up. Finally, 40 patients who met all the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate were included in the study. The exclusion criteria used in the study are summarized in Table 1.

Determination of the dominant arm

The dominant arm was identified on the basis of the patient and/or parent report regarding habitual hand use in daily activities such as writing, eating, and throwing a ball. When needed, investigators also observed functional use to confirm dominance.

Surgical procedure standardization and blind analysis

All surgeries were performed by experienced orthopedic trauma surgeons following a standardized surgical protocol to ensure the consistent application of fixation principles. The decision to use titanium elastic nails (TENs) or K-wire fixation was based on fracture characteristics and standard treatment guidelines. Intraoperative fluoroscopic control was used to confirm proper reduction and implant positioning. Postoperative radiographic assessments were performed to ensure alignment accuracy and correct fixation techniques. To minimize observer bias, radiographic measurements and functional assessments were conducted in a blinded manner.

Postoperative management and immobilization

All patients were immobilized in a neutral forearm position with a long-arm splint for 5‒6 weeks, depending on fracture stability and early signs of healing. This approach aims to preserve the interosseous space and prevent rotational deformities. Splint removal was performed approximately 5‒6 weeks post-operatively on the basis of the clinical and radiographic confirmation of adequate callus formation. Radiographic evaluations were conducted regularly throughout the immobilization period to ensure proper fracture alignment and maintenance of the interosseous space.

Follow-up and timing of measurements

All radiographic and functional assessments (maximal radial bowing (MRB), maximal radial bowing localization (MRBL), and pronation-supination measurements) were conducted at the final follow-up visit, which was scheduled on the basis of clinical and radiographic confirmation of complete fracture healing. On average, patients were evaluated at least 6 months post-operatively, ensuring sufficient time for functional recovery and bone remodeling. The criteria for the final follow-up included the absence of pain, full range of motion, and no tenderness on palpation, along with radiographic evidence of bridging callus formation and the absence of fracture lines. Additionally, patients who required rehabilitation were assessed only after completing their therapy and regaining age-appropriate functional capacity. This standardized approach ensured that all measurements reflected long-term functional and radiographic outcomes, minimizing variations due to early posttreatment changes.

Radiological evaluation

To assess radial bowing, we used the measurement criteria defined by Schemitsch and Richards and later modified by Firl et al. [16], as illustrated in Fig. 1.

The modified Schemitsch and Richards technique for determining the MRB and MRBL

The measurement parameters used in this study were defined on the basis of standardized anatomical landmarks. The radial length (‘y’) represents the longitudinal axis of the radius, which is defined as the straight line connecting the bicipital tuberosity (proximal landmark) to the radial styloid process (distal landmark). This reference line, shown in red, serves as the basis for measuring radial bowing. The maximum radial bowing distance (‘r’), shown in black, is determined as the perpendicular distance from the point of maximal radial bowing to the longitudinal axis (‘y’). Additionally, the distance to the maximum radial bowing site (‘x’), represented in yellow, is measured as the distance from the bicipital tuberosity to the point of the MRB along the radial shaft. These measurements allow for standardized assessment of radial bowing, ensuring accurate comparisons across individuals with different radial lengths.

The MRB is defined as the perpendicular distance (‘r’) from the point of maximal radial bowing to the longitudinal axis (‘y’). An experienced radiologist, using digital imaging software, conducted all the radiographic measurements, ensuring high precision and minimizing potential measurement bias.

To eliminate variability due to forearm rotation, all radiographs were obtained with the forearm in a neutral position, with the elbow at 90° flexion, and the wrist in a standardized anteroposterior (AP) projection. This standardized positioning prevented excessive supination or pronation, which could otherwise affect the apparent radial bowing. A single radiographic protocol was used for all patients to ensure consistency, and an experienced radiologist conducted the measurements using digital imaging software. Additionally, to minimize the effects of individual bone length differences and positioning variations, MRB (r/y × 100) and MRBL (x/y × 100) were expressed as percentages, allowing for reliable and standardized comparisons across patients.

Goniometric measurements

Patients were called for follow-up, and the range of motion (ROM) for both the treated and intact forearms was measured via a goniometer. The measurements were performed by a physical medicine and rehabilitation specialist with the patient seated, shoulders in neutral abduction, the forearm and wrist in neutral, and the elbow at 90° flexion [10]. All pronation and supination measurements were measured as active range of motion (AROM), with patients performing the movements voluntarily without external assistance. This method ensures that the results reflect functional forearm mobility in daily activities, as passive range of motion (PROM) can sometimes overestimate mobility by not accounting for muscle strength, pain, or neuromuscular control.

Functional evaluation

The functional outcomes were assessed according to the criteria established by Price et al. [21].

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed via the SPSS 21.0 software package (IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Continuous variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. To assess differences between groups, if parametric test assumptions were met, Student’s t test was performed, and if parametric test assumptions were not met, Kruskal‒Wallis analysis of variance was used, following which comparisons between independent groups were made via the Mann‒Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that radial bowing (MRB and MRBL) plays a crucial role in maintaining pronation and supination functions in pediatric forearm diaphyseal fractures treated surgically. Compared with the intact arm, the fractured arm presented significantly lower MRB and MRBL values, indicating that the loss of radial bowing directly impacts functional outcomes. Additionally, functional differences were observed on the basis of arm dominance, with less pronation and supination loss in cases where the dominant arm was fractured. This finding supports the hypothesis that the dominant arm develops better functional adaptation due to more frequent use.

Forearm diaphyseal fractures constitute approximately 30% of all childhood fractures [1]. While conservative treatment remains the primary approach, deformities in the middle third of the forearm may not always remodel adequately, necessitating surgical intervention ​ [22, 23]. Intramedullary nailing has gained popularity in recent years because of its minimal invasiveness and effectiveness in maintaining fracture stability. However, even after surgical treatment, pronation and supination limitations exceeding 20% have been reported in 3–30% of cases [8, 9]. Our findings suggest that preserving radial bowing is a key factor in minimizing these functional deficits.

Forearm rotation is not only a simple twisting motion but also a complex process involving both rotation and translation. While malunion of the radius is commonly cited as the primary cause of restricted motion, loss of radial bowing—together with axial deformities—may also lead to significant functional limitations [11, 21, 23, 24]. Pronation and supination movements are essential for daily activities and generally require an approximate functional range of 100°. When the arc of motion falls below 50° in either direction, considerable disability may occur, emphasizing the importance of meticulous surgical planning and execution in pediatric forearm fractures [24].

Axial deformities are usually more easily recognized and treated, whereas loss of radial bowing may be missed. Therefore, maintaining radial bowing is essential, particularly for preserving the rotational capacity of the forearm​ [22]. Although age, dominant extremity, fixation method, and postoperative rehabilitation may influence function, radial bow is the only surgically modifiable parameter directly related to forearm rotational mechanics. Cadaveric biomechanical data show that reduced radial bow alone leads to a 19–20% decrease in pronation and supination compared with normal bow models [25]. Research has indicated that restoring at least 80% of forearm rotational function is critical for good functional outcomes, reinforcing the importance of preserving radial bowing during treatment [26].

Our results align with those of previous studies. According to Firl et al., the normal MRB and MRBL values in the pediatric population are 7.21 ± 1.03 and 60.39 ± 3.74, respectively [22]. In our study, these normative values were observed in the intact arms, confirming preserved physiological bowing on the uninjured side. However, in the fractured arms particularly in children older than 9 years both MRB and MRBL were significantly lower than normal ranges, indicating insufficient restoration of radial bow following intramedullary fixation. Weber et al. analyzed 211 human cadaveric specimens and reported that the mean location of maximal bowing was 45% of the radial length, with a maximum bowing of 3.8% [27]. Additionally, Wongcharoenwatana et al. reported that an MRB value below 6.84% may be associated with pronation below 70°, whereas an MRB below 5.75% could result in supination below 80° [26]. In our study, the MRB value in the treated arm was 6.22 ± 0.82, supporting the relationship between reduced radial bowing and limited rotational function. Recent large-cohort normative data (Kass et al., 2025) reported a mean MRB of 7.7% and MRBL of 61.7%, closely aligning with both Firl’s values and our intact-arm measurements, further supporting our reference standard [28].

Another significant factor influencing the preservation of radial bowing is age-dependent remodeling capacity. Studies indicate that remodeling potential progressively decreases as skeletal maturity approaches, occurring earlier in girls (approximately 8 years) and later in boys (approximately 10 years) [3, 29]. Wongcharoenwatana et al. reported that MRB had no significant effect on pronation-supination in children aged 1–9 years, but this relationship became significant in older children [26]. As skeletal maturity progresses, radial cortical thickness and intrinsic curvature increase, while bone elasticity and remodeling capacity decline. Thus, small postoperative changes in bowing that would spontaneously remodel in younger patients become less likely to correct in children aged > 9 years. Additionally, the relatively straight configuration of intramedullary implants may biomechanically flatten the naturally curved radius, particularly in more rigid bone [3, 22, 26, 28, 29]. In accordance with the literature, our results showed that in children ≤ 9 years, differences in MRB and MRBL between fractured and intact arms were not associated with clinically meaningful rotational restriction, whereas in children > 9 years, greater loss of radial bowing corresponded to significantly impaired pronation-supination. Therefore, the more pronounced bowing loss observed in the older subgroup of our study likely reflects a combined effect of age-related biomechanical constraints and reduced remodeling capacity rather than merely technical inadequacy. These findings highlight that restoring and maintaining the radial bow should be a primary surgical objective, especially in children over 9 years old, because their limited remodeling ability increases the risk of ongoing functional limitations affecting daily activities.

Another notable finding in our study was the impact of arm dominance on postoperative functional limitations. Our functional assessment showed that the fractured arm had not fully regained its physiological rotational capacity, indicating postsurgical movement restrictions. Importantly, supination and pronation deficits were statistically significant only in patients with nondominant arm fractures, suggesting that these movements are more severely limited in the nondominant extremity and may require targeted rehabilitation during the healing process. In contrast, patients with dominant-arm fractures exhibited no significant loss of rotation, which supports the notion that the dominant arm may have a greater capacity for functional adaptation. Although limited data exist in the literature, the more frequent use and better preservation of motor patterns in the dominant arm likely contribute to this adaptive advantage.

Although both TENs and K-wire fixation are widely used for pediatric forearm fractures, directly comparing these techniques is difficult due to differences in implant biomechanics, stability, and typical fracture patterns that influence technique selection. Therefore, functional outcomes depend not only on the fixation method but also on fracture characteristics and the child’s capacity for age-related remodeling. The choice of fixation method may also influence the ability to maintain radial bowing postoperatively. In our study, both TENS and K-wire fixation were included to reflect real-world clinical practice, acknowledging that TENS provides greater stability and flexibility, which may better preserve radial bowing than K-wire fixation. Although a more homogeneous comparison focusing on a single fixation type could yield clearer conclusions, our findings remain clinically relevant, as they highlight the importance of maintaining radial bowing, regardless of fixation choice. Future studies focusing exclusively on one fixation method may further clarify the impact of fixation techniques on radial bowing and functional recovery.

In addition, Price et al.‘s criteria were used to evaluate functional outcomes in our study [21]. In a study by Kang et al., 84% of 76 patients achieved good and excellent outcomes [30]. Similarly, in our study, 80% of patients had excellent results, 17.5% had good results, and 2.5% had acceptable results, supporting the effectiveness of intramedullary nailing in pediatric forearm fractures.

Finally, this study demonstrated that intramedullary osteosynthesis is a suitable treatment option for pediatric forearm diaphyseal fractures, with acceptable complication rates and successful functional outcomes [7]. IMN is widely preferred for diaphyseal radius and ulna fractures in younger age groups, allowing for minimally invasive, stable fixation with high success rates. However, complications such as implant irritation, infection, osteomyelitis, loss of reduction, delayed union, nonunion, extensor tendon damage, radial nerve injury, refracture, and compartment syndrome have been reported [7, 31]. In our study, the overall complication rate was 20%, which is consistent with the reported range of 14.6% to 42% in the literature [32]. The most common complication observed was skin irritation, further supporting the safety and efficacy of IMN in this patient population.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, a significant limitation is the relatively small sample size (n = 40). Although we demonstrated statistically significant differences in some parameters, the limited number of participants may have lowered the statistical power to detect subtle yet clinically important differences, especially in subgroup analyses such as age and arm dominance. Future research with larger, more homogeneous cohorts is necessary to confirm our results and further explore how age and dominance might affect radial bowing and functional outcomes. Second, although TEN and K-wire fixation methods were included, the sample size did not allow for a detailed comparative analysis of these methods regarding radial bowing and functional outcomes. Future studies focusing on a single fixation method or directly comparing the two methods could yield more definitive conclusions. Finally, an important limitation of our study is the lack of preinjury MRB and MRBL measurements. Since these values could not be obtained prior to fracture, our comparisons were necessarily made via postoperative data and reference values from the literature. This limitation may restrict the precision of our conclusions regarding the extent to which surgical interventions restore native radial bowing. Future studies designed with baseline imaging and prospective follow-up could provide stronger evidence for evaluating true anatomical restoration.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of preserving radial bowing (MRB and MRBL) in maintaining pronation and supination functions during surgical treatment of childhood forearm diaphyseal fractures. The MRB and MRBL values were significantly lower in the fractured arm than in the intact arm, and this reduction was directly associated with functional loss. Functional deficits were more pronounced in nondominant arm fractures. In contrast, dominant-arm fractures exhibited better adaptation and less motion loss, supporting the hypothesis that greater use of the dominant arm enhances functional recovery. Our findings emphasize the importance of preserving radial bowing, particularly in children over 9 years of age, who have reduced remodeling capacity, and underscore the need for structured rehabilitation planning to optimize functional outcomes following surgical treatment.
