Information Processing in Quantum Thermodynamic Systems: an Autonomous Hamiltonian Approach
Shou-I Tang, Emery Doucet, Akram Touil, Sebastian Deffner, Akira Sone

Abstract
Extending the quantum formulation of [Phys. Rev. X 3 , 041003 (2013)] to a more general setting for studying the thermodynamics of information processing including initial correlations, we generalize the second law of thermodynamics to account for information processing in such autonomous systems. We consider a composite quantum system consisting of a principal system, heat bath, memory, and work source, and adopt an autonomous Hamiltonian framework. We derive constraints on the total Hamiltonian that ensure the work source to act as a catalyst preserving its original randomness, namely that the total unitary evolution must have a unitary partial transpose. We show that this requirement is equivalent to the commutativity of operators acting on the joint system of the principal system, bath, and memory, which underlies the Hamiltonian structure. Next, we generalize the quantum speed limit for the joint dynamics of system and memory to the quantum thermodynamic speed limit , from which we obtain a dynamical version of Landauer’s bound. More importantly, we also interpret this quantum thermodynamic speed limit in the context of quantum hypothesis testing.

Reference
30

Specifically, the work source is assumed to maintain its initial randomness throughout the evolution, and the energy of the memory reservoir is considered to stay constant during the process. Key results are generalizations of the Kelvin-Planck, Clausius, and Carnot statements in the presence of information gathering, establishing that Shannon entropy should be regarded on equal footing with Clausius entropy in formulations of the second law. This perspective elucidates, for instance, that extracting work from a single heat bath or efficiencies beyond Carnot are perfectly thermodynamically sound, provided that the associated entropy reduction is balanced by information storage in the memory. The distinguishing feature of this framework is an autonomous, fully inclusive treatment, in which the memory, also called
information reservoir

In the present paper, we extend the framework of Ref.
30

In our quantum setting, Liouville dynamics is replaced by unitary evolution under the autonomous (time-independent) total Hamiltonian, Shannon entropy by von Neumann entropy, and classical ensemble averages by quantum expectation values with respect to density operators. We emphasize autonomous Hamiltonians since they provide a closed, self-contained description of all subsystems, ensuring that energy and entropy exchanges among the subsystems arise solely from internal interactions, without the need for externally imposed controls. Consequently, thermodynamic laws emerge naturally and self-consistently from the underlying unitary dynamics. Within this framework, we determine the conditions that the total system evolution and Hamiltonian must satisfy to be thermodynamically consistent, derive a quantum version of Landauer’s principle to capture energetic constraints, and investigate the effective QSL for the composite of the principal system and memory. We further examine the relationship between the Landauer bound and the effective QSL, elucidating their operational significance in the context of quantum hypothesis testing.

IV
Laws of Thermodynamics

Now that we have constructed the Hamiltonian from its corresponding dynamics, we formulate the statements of the laws of thermodynamics.

IV.1
First Law of Thermodynamics

To state the first law of thermodynamics, we must have expressions for heat, work, and internal energy. As always, we take heat
Q
to be the energy dissipated from the heat bath,

\displaystyle Q\equiv\text{tr}\left\{\left(\rho_{b}^{\text{eq}}-\mathcal{E}_{b}\left(\rho_{b}^{\text{eq}}\right)\right)H_{b}\right\}\,,

(29)

work
W
to be the energy dissipated from the work source,

\displaystyle W\equiv\text{tr}\left\{\left(\rho_{w}-\mathcal{E}_{w}\left(\rho_{w}\right)\right)H_{w}\right\}\,.

(30)

The internal energy change
\Delta E
as the system’s energy change during evolution simply is,

\displaystyle\Delta E\equiv\text{tr}\left\{\left(\mathcal{E}_{s}\left(\rho_{s}\right)-\rho_{s}\right)H_{s}\right\}\,.

(31)

Following Refs.
30
;
102
, and using Eq. (
10
), we immediately have for the memory

\displaystyle 0=\text{tr}\left\{\left(\rho_{m}-\mathcal{E}_{m}\left(\rho_{m}\right)\right)H_{m}\right\}\,.

(32)

Combining Eqs. (
3
), (
29
), (
30
), (
31
), and (
32
), the first law of thermodynamics then naturally emerges,

\displaystyle\Delta E=Q+W\,.

(33)

We emphasize that there is no energetic contribution from the memory, since its reduced Hamiltonian is fully degenerate and, hence, the memory cannot exchange energy with the other subsystems directly. However, as we will see in the formulation of the second law, its change in entropy does lead to an exchange of energy among the other subsystems.

IV.2
Second Law of Thermodynamics

To formulate the second law of thermodynamics, we note that the heat bath can be assumed to be in a Gibbs state (
4
), and we have

\displaystyle\ln\rho_{b}^{\text{eq}}=-\beta H_{b}-\ln Z_{b}\,.

(34)

Using Eq. (
29
), this gives

\displaystyle\beta Q=S(\rho_{b}^{\text{eq}})+\text{tr}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{b}\left(\rho_{b}^{\text{eq}}\right)\ln\rho_{b}^{\text{eq}}\right\}\,.

(35)

We now define

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)

\displaystyle\equiv U(\tau)\left(\rho_{\overline{w}}\otimes\rho_{w}\right)U^{\dagger}(\tau),

(36)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{tot}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\rho_{b}^{\text{eq}}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{s}(\rho_{s})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{m}(\rho_{m})\otimes\mathcal{E}_{w}(\rho_{w})\,,

(37)

where Eq. (
37
) represents the weak-coupling limit for the total system and assumes that the Born-Oppenheimer approximation is valid for the heat bath
16
;
51
. Further, we write
S(\rho\,||\,\sigma)\equiv-S(\rho)-\text{tr}\{\rho\ln\sigma\}
for the quantum relative entropy of
\rho
with respect to
\sigma
. Then, defining

\displaystyle\Delta S(\rho_{s})

\displaystyle\equiv S(\mathcal{E}_{s}(\rho_{s}))-S(\rho_{s})

(38)

\displaystyle\Delta S(\rho_{m})

\displaystyle\equiv S(\mathcal{E}_{m}(\rho_{m}))-S(\rho_{m})\,,

(39)

and the quantum relative entropy difference,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\delta_{\text{rel}}(\tau)\equiv S(\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)\,||\,\sigma_{\text{tot}}(\tau))-S(\rho_{\text{tot}}(0)\,||\,\sigma_{\text{tot}}(0))\,,

(40)

we obtain the second law of thermodynamics as

\displaystyle\Delta S(\rho_{s})+\Delta S(\rho_{m})-\beta Q=\delta_{\text{rel}}(\tau)\,.

(41)

See Appendix
B
for a more detailed derivation.

Observe that Eq. (
41
) asserts that the irreversible entropy production, that is the difference between change in entropy and heat exchanged with the heat bath, is governed by the change of entropy in the memory. Thus, Eq. (
41
) can be read a as quantum generalization of Landauer’s principle for autonomous quantum information processing. This can be made even more precise by defining the effective heat as

\displaystyle Q_{\text{eff}}=Q-\beta^{-1}S(\rho_{\text{tot}}(0)\,||\,\sigma_{\text{tot}}(0))\,,

(42)

from which we obtaion

\displaystyle\Delta S(\rho_{s})+\Delta S(\rho_{m})-\beta Q_{\text{eff}}=S(\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)\,||\,\sigma_{\text{tot}}(\tau))\,.

(43)

Due to the non-negativity of the quantum relative entropy,
S(\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)\,||\,\sigma_{\text{tot}}(\tau))\geqslant 0
, we then have the quantum Landauer bound,

\displaystyle\Delta S(\rho_{s})+\Delta S(\rho_{m})\geqslant\beta Q_{\text{eff}}\,.

(44)

Here, note that
S(\rho_{\text{tot}}(0)\,||\,\sigma_{\text{tot}}(0))=S(\rho_{b}^{\text{eq}})+S(\rho_{s})+S(\rho_{m})-S(\rho_{\overline{w}})
is equivalent to the tripartite quantum mutual information contained in the initial state
\rho_{\overline{w}}

114
, meaning that the Landauer bound depends on the initial correlations.

V
Quantum Thermodynamic Speed Limit

The autonomous Hamiltonian approach to quantum information processing has the added benefit that we also have full access to the dynamics. In particular, we can now also formulate statements for the rate of entropy change, the entropy production, which is upper bound by the QSL
31
.

While there are many formulations for open quantum systems, the version of the QSL from Ref.
35
is particularly convenient for our present purposes. This version of the QSL is expressed in terms of the Schatten
p
-norm, which enables flexible analyses of numerical stability and spectral sensitivity. For instance, when

Given a operator

-dimensional Hilbert space, its Schatten

-norm

\displaystyle|\!|A|\!|_{p}\equiv\left(\text{tr}\left\{\left(A^{\dagger}A\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right\}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

(45)

satisfies the following inequality
63

\displaystyle|\!|A|\!|_{p}\leqslant|\!|A|\!|_{q}\leqslant d^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}|\!|A|\!|_{p}~~(0<q\leqslant p)\,.

(46)

Following Refs.
35
;
32
;
34
, we define the time-averaged Schatten
p
-norm as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{s,m}^{(p)}(\tau)\equiv\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}|\!|\partial_{t}\rho_{s,m}(t)|\!|_{p}dt\,,

(47)

where
\partial_{t}\equiv\partial/\partial t
, and the QSL time as

\displaystyle T_{s,m}^{(p)}\equiv\frac{\ell_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{s,m}(\rho_{s,m}),\rho_{s,m})}{\Lambda_{s,m}^{(p)}(\tau)}\,,

(48)

where

\displaystyle\ell_{p}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\equiv|\!|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}|\!|_{p}

(49)

denotes the Schatten
p
-distance between two quantum states
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}

27
.

From Fannes inequality
47
;
4
;
76
;
35
and Eq. (
46
), we then have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\!\!\!\!|\Delta S_{s}+\Delta S_{m}|&\leqslant d_{s}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d_{s})\ell_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{s}(\rho_{s}),\rho_{s})\\
&+d_{m}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d_{m})\ell_{p}(\mathcal{E}_{m}(\rho_{m}),\rho_{m})+\frac{2}{e}\,.\end{split}

(50)

By using the dimension relation

\displaystyle d\equiv\dim(\mathcal{H}_{s}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m})=d_{s}d_{m}\,,

(51)

we define

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p}^{\star}\equiv\frac{d_{s}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d_{s})\Lambda_{s}^{(p)}+d_{m}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d_{m})\Lambda_{m}^{(p)}}{d^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d)},

(52)

and the effective QSL time

\displaystyle T^{\star}_{p}\equiv\frac{d_{s}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d_{s})\Lambda_{s}^{(p)}T_{s}^{(p)}+d_{m}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d_{m})\Lambda_{m}^{(p)}T_{m}^{(p)}}{d_{s}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d_{s})\Lambda_{s}^{(p)}+d_{m}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d_{m})\Lambda_{m}^{(p)}}\,.

Here, we refer to

as the
Quantum Thermodynamic Speed Limit
(QTSL) time (of order
p
); cf. Appendix
C
for two examples illustrating the quantity
T_{1}^{\star}
.

This thermodynamic version of the quantum speed limit determines the average rate with which thermodynamic resources can be exchanged between the different subsystems. In particular, it bounds the rate with which information
about
the principal system can be encoded in the memory. This interpretation becomes more stringent by reinspecting the quantum Landauer bound (
44
). In particular, we can write

\displaystyle|\Delta S_{s}+\Delta S_{m}|\leqslant d^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\ln(d)\Lambda^{\star}_{p}T^{\star}_{p}+\frac{2}{e}\,.

(54)

Following Ref.
35
, using the quantum information theoretic version of Bremermann-Bekenstein bound of order
p
defined as

\displaystyle B^{\star}_{p}(\tau)\equiv\ln(d)d^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\Lambda^{\star}_{p}(\tau)\,,

(55)

from Eq. (
43
), we obtain

\displaystyle S(\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)\,||\,\sigma_{\text{tot}}(\tau))+\beta Q_{\text{eff}}\leqslant T^{\star}_{p}B^{\star}_{p}(\tau)+\frac{2}{e}\,.

(56)

Equation (
56
) can be interpreted as a dynamical version of the Landauer bound for the communication of information between a thermodynamic system and a quantum memory. Importantly, the two bounds Eq. (
44
) and Eq. (
56
) are complementary: the Landauer bound (
44
) provides a lower bound on the change in entropy of the system and memory, while the QTSL bound Eq. (
56
) provides an upper bound.

V.1
Quantum hypothesis testing

Finally we discuss the operational meaning of
T^{\star}_{p}
in the context of quantum hypothesis testing, as described by the quantum Stein lemma
78
;
15
.
Let
p_{n}(\tau)
be the minimum type-I​I error probability of a testing protocol,i.e., the probability that the positive operator-valued measure (POVM) measurement indicates
\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)
when the true state is
\sigma_{\text{tot}}(\tau)
, given
n
copies to test. Then

\displaystyle S(\rho_{\text{tot}}(\tau)\,||\,\sigma_{\text{tot}}(\tau))=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln p_{n}(\tau)\,.

(57)

From Eq. (
56
), we obtain

\displaystyle-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln p_{n}(\tau)\leqslant T^{\star}_{p}B^{\star}_{p}(\tau)-\beta Q_{\text{eff}}+\frac{2}{e}\,.

(58)

Thus, QTSL time provides an upper bound on the scaling behavior in quantum hypothesis testing.

VI
Concluding remarks

In conclusion, we have extended the classical framework of information processing in thermodynamic systems from Ref.
30
to the quantum domain. Focusing on a composite system comprising a principal system, heat bath, memory, and work source within an autonomous Hamiltonian setting, we derived constraints on the total Hamiltonian required for thermodynamically consistent description of quantum information processing. By generalizing to scenarios where the system, bath, and memory may initially be correlated, we extended the second law of thermodynamics to account for information-processing effects in autonomous quantum systems. We proposed that the total unitary evolution should admit a unitary partial transpose and be compatible with the state of the work source, ensuring that the work source acts catalytically and preserves its von Neumann entropy. This condition enforces commutativity among operators acting on the joint system and establishes the Hamiltonian structure. Moreover, by analyzing the quantum speed limit of the system and memory, we introduced the concept of a quantum thermodynamic speed limit characterized by the Schatten norm and demonstrated its connection to the quantum Landauer bound. Finally, considering quantum Stein’s lemma, we provided an operational interpretation of this speed limit in the context of quantum hypothesis testing. Together, these results establish a unified framework for understanding the interplay between energetic and temporal constraints in quantum information processing within general quantum thermodynamic systems.

Appendix A
Hermiticity of Partially Transposed Hamiltonians

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian acting on a composite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{w}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{w}
. In the computational basis, we can express

\displaystyle H=\sum_{ijk\ell}h_{ijk\ell}|\hskip 1.0pti\rangle\langle j\hskip 1.0pt|\otimes|\hskip 1.0ptk\rangle\langle\ell\hskip 1.0pt|\,.

(59)

Then, we can write

\displaystyle H^{\dagger}=\sum_{ijk\ell}h_{ijk\ell}^{*}|\hskip 1.0ptj\rangle\langle i\hskip 1.0pt|\otimes|\hskip 1.0pt\ell\rangle\langle k\hskip 1.0pt|=\sum_{ijk\ell}h_{ji\ell k}^{*}|\hskip 1.0pti\rangle\langle j\hskip 1.0pt|\otimes|\hskip 1.0ptk\rangle\langle\ell\hskip 1.0pt|\,,

(60)

where
*
represents the complex conjugate. Since
H
is Hermitian, i.e.,
H=H^{\dagger}
, it follows that

\displaystyle h_{ijk\ell}=h_{ji\ell k}^{*}\,.

(61)

Applying the partial transpose
\top_{\overline{w}}
over subsystem
\mathcal{H}_{\overline{w}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle H^{\top_{\overline{w}}}=\sum_{ijk\ell}h_{ijk\ell}|\hskip 1.0ptj\rangle\langle i\hskip 1.0pt|\otimes|\hskip 1.0ptk\rangle\langle\ell\hskip 1.0pt|\,.

(62)

Its conjugate transpose is

\displaystyle\left(H^{\top_{\overline{w}}}\right)^{\dagger}=\sum_{ijk\ell}h_{ijk\ell}^{*}|\hskip 1.0pti\rangle\langle j\hskip 1.0pt|\otimes|\hskip 1.0pt\ell\rangle\langle k\hskip 1.0pt|=\sum_{ijk\ell}h_{ji\ell k}^{*}|\hskip 1.0ptj\rangle\langle i\hskip 1.0pt|\otimes|\hskip 1.0ptk\rangle\langle\ell\hskip 1.0pt|\,.

(63)

From Eq. (
61
), we can finally find that the partial transpose preserves Hermiticity

\displaystyle H^{\top_{\overline{w}}}=\left(H^{\top_{\overline{w}}}\right)^{\dagger}\,.

(64)

&=\lambda\frac{\cos^{2}(\varphi)+e^{2}\sin^{2}(\varphi)}{\tau(1+e^{2})}\mathfrak{E}\left[4\tau\,\Bigg|\,\left(\frac{e\sin(2\varphi)}{\cos^{2}(\varphi)+e^{2}\sin^{2}(\varphi)}\right)^{2}\right]\,.\end{split}

(109)

Therefore, we can obtain the QTSL

\displaystyle T_{1}^{\star}=\frac{L(\tau,\varphi)}{\Lambda(\tau,\varphi)}\,,

(110)

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}L(\tau,\varphi)&\equiv|\sin(2\tau)|\left(|\sin(2\varphi)|+\frac{2}{1+e^{2}}\sqrt{\cos^{4}(\varphi)+e^{4}\sin^{4}(\varphi)+\frac{e^{2}\sin^{2}(2\varphi)}{2}\cos(4\tau)}\right)\\
\Lambda(\tau,\varphi)&\equiv\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\left(2N+(-1)^{N}\sin(2\tau)\right)|\sin(2\varphi)|+\frac{\cos^{2}(\varphi)+e^{2}\sin^{2}(\varphi)}{1+e^{2}}\mathfrak{E}\left[4\tau\,\Bigg|\,\left(\frac{e\sin(2\varphi)}{\cos^{2}(\varphi)+e^{2}\sin^{2}(\varphi)}\right)^{2}\right]\right)\,.\end{split}

(111)

T_{1}^{\star}
,
\varphi
and
\tau
is illustrated in Fig.

.
Note that these results share many features of the results obtained in Fig.

.

It is evident from the final expressions for
L
and
\lambda
that
T_{1}^{\star}
does not depend on either
\lambda
.
This demonstrates that the presence or absence of entanglement between the system and memory does not necessarily have any impact on the QTSL.

C.2.2
When QTSL is independent of

Other bases show different behavior, depending on the relationship between the initial state and the preferred bases of the the Hamiltonian. When
\{|a_{1}\rangle\otimes|b_{1}\rangle,|a_{2}\rangle\otimes|b_{2}\rangle\}
are the
X_{s}\otimes X_{m}

\varphi
.
In this case,
|\psi(\varphi)\rangle
is given by

\displaystyle|\psi(\varphi)\rangle=\cos(\varphi)|+\rangle_{s}\otimes|+\rangle_{m}+\sin(\varphi)|-\rangle_{s}\otimes|-\rangle_{m}\,.

(112)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{s}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)&0\\
0&\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(113)

\displaystyle\rho_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1+\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)&\cos^{2}(\theta)\\
\cos^{2}(\theta)&1-\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)\end{pmatrix},

(114)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{s}(\rho_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}4\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)&i\sin(2\theta)\sin(2\tau)\\
-i\sin(2\theta)\sin(2\tau)&4\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(115)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{m}(\rho_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1+\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)&\frac{e^{-4i\tau}(1+e^{2+4i\tau})\cos(\theta)\left(1+e^{4i\tau}(-1+\cos(\theta))+\cos(\theta)\right)}{2(1+e^{2})}\\

(116)

which leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ell_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{s}(\rho_{s}),\rho_{s})&=2\,\lambda\,|\cos(2\varphi)|\sin^{2}(\tau)\propto\lambda\\
\ell_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{m}(\rho_{m}),\rho_{m})&=\lambda\,|\cos(2\varphi)\sin(2\tau)|\frac{\sqrt{1+e^{4}-2e^{2}\cos(4\tau)}}{e^{2}+1}\propto\lambda\,.\end{split}

(117)

Also, for
\Lambda_{s}^{(1)}(\tau)
, we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{s}^{(1)}(\tau)=\frac{2\,\lambda\,|\sin(\varphi)+\cos(\varphi)|\sqrt{1-\sin(2\varphi)}}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}|\sin(2t)|dt\,.

\displaystyle\Lambda_{s}^{(1)}(\tau)=\lambda\,|\sin(\varphi)+\cos(\varphi)|\,\frac{\sqrt{1-\sin(2\varphi)}}{\tau}\left(2N+1-(-1)^{N}\cos(2\tau)\right)\propto\lambda\,,

(120)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Lambda_{m}^{(1)}(\tau)&=\frac{2\,\lambda\,|\sin(\varphi)+\cos(\varphi)|\sqrt{1-\sin(2\varphi)}}{e^{2}+1}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sqrt{1+e^{4}-2e^{2}\cos(8t)}dt\,,\\
&=\lambda\,|\sin(\varphi)+\cos(\varphi)|\,\frac{\sqrt{1-\sin(2\varphi)}}{\tau}\,\frac{e^{2}-1}{2(e^{2}+1)}\,\,\mathfrak{E}\left[4\tau\,\,\Bigg|\,\,-\left(\frac{2e}{e^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\right]\propto\lambda\,\end{split}

C.2.3
General statement

As we have seen, the QTSL with Werner-like initial states may or may not depend on the angle
\varphi
, depending on the specific bases selected to define the superposition.
The independence of the QTSL on the mixing parameter
\lambda
, however, does not.
Further, this independence is not unique to the example Hamiltonian we have considered thus far but is in fact generally true.

To show this, consider generic initial states for the work reservoir and bath combined with a Werner-like state for the system and memory,

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{tot}}(0)=\left(\frac{1-\lambda}{4}\openone+\lambda|\psi(\varphi)\rangle\!\langle\psi(\varphi)|\right)\otimes\rho_{w}\otimes\rho_{b}^{th}.

(126)

The first component of this state will remain stationary under the dynamics generated by
H_{\text{tot}}
if,

\displaystyle\left[H_{s}+H_{m}+H_{w}+H_{b}+H_{sm}+H_{sw}+H_{sb}+H_{mb},\openone_{s}\otimes\openone_{m}\otimes\rho_{w}\otimes\rho_{b}^{th}\right]=0.

(127)

Since the initial system and memory states are maximally mixed, it is clear that

\displaystyle\left[H_{s}+H_{m}+H_{sm},\openone_{s}\otimes\openone_{m}\otimes\rho_{w}\otimes\rho_{b}^{th}\right]=0.

(128)

By the definition of a thermal state, we also have that

\displaystyle\left[H_{b},\openone_{s}\otimes\openone_{m}\otimes\rho_{w}\otimes\rho_{b}^{th}\right]=0.

(129)

For the two Hamiltonians involving the work reservoir
H_{w},H_{sw}
, we may use the requirements listed in Eq. (
22
) to show that their contribution to Eq. (
127
) also vanishes,

\displaystyle\left[H_{w}+H_{sw},\openone_{s}\otimes\openone_{m}\otimes\rho_{w}\otimes\rho_{b}^{th}\right]=0.

(130)

What remains is to show that
H_{sb}
and
H_{mb}
commute with this state.
Take
H_{sb}
(the same arguments work for
H_{mb}
), and consider decomposing it into bi-partite operators

\displaystyle H_{sb}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}\otimes B_{j},

(131)

with
\{A_{j}\}
a collection of distinct operators acting on the system and
\{B_{j}\}
a collection of not necessarily distinct operators acting on the bath.
Then the commutator,

\displaystyle\left[H_{sb},\openone_{s}\otimes\rho_{b}^{th}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}\otimes\left[B_{j},\rho_{b}^{th}\right],

(132)

vanishes only if

\displaystyle\left[B_{j},\rho_{b}^{th}\right]=0~(\forall j)\,.

(133)

That is, we must require that the bath remains stationary in its equilibrium thermal state under the action of
H_{sb}
. Similar arguments produce the same result for
H_{mb}
.

All together, we have that

\displaystyle\left[H_{\text{tot}},\openone_{s}\otimes\openone_{m}\otimes\rho_{w}\otimes\rho_{b}^{th}\right]=0,

(134)

whenever we require that the bath remains quasistatic in its equilibrium thermal state and that the dynamics generated by
H_{\text{tot}}
ensure that the work source acts as a catalyst with initial state
\rho_{w}
as discussed in Sec.
III
.
Therefore, it will always be the case that the QTSL for Werner-like states depends only on the identity of the projector involved and not on the mixing parameter.
