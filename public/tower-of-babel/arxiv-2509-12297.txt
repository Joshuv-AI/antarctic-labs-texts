1.
Introduction

Connecting the discrete world of prime numbers with the continuous framework of analysis is considered a central challenge in analytic number theory. Such a connection should not only allow for the identification of integers with specific arithmetic properties but also provide structural insight through its analytic behavior. This paper introduces the ’Fejér–Dirichlet Lift’, that offers a new perspective on this challenge. It transforms discrete divisor information into a smooth, entire function, analogous to how a Fourier series turns a sequence of coefficients into a periodic function.

Based on this lift, two specific entire indicators are developed that interpolate the prime/composite pattern at integer arguments and admit explicit spectral factorizations. The original indicator,
\mathfrak{F}(z,q)
, is a normalized superposition of Fejér filters with a geometric weight. A refined, tangent–matched variant,
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(z,q)
, augments this construction with a small periodic term. This adjustment is central to the analysis and allows for precise control over the function’s real-zero geometry: by enforcing a vanishing slope at every integer, it structurally controls the function’s real-zero geometry near primes and eliminates additional "companion zeros" that appear in simpler models.

Minimal notation and parameter regime

The Fejér kernel is

F(z,i)\;=\;\left(\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\sin(\pi z/i)}\right)^{\!2}\;=\;i\;+\;2\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}(i-k)\cos\!\bigl(2\pi kz/i\bigr),\hskip 20.00003pt\phi_{i}(z):=\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}.

At integers,
\phi_{i}(n)=\mathbf{1}_{\,i\mid n}
, so the Fejér kernels act as divisor filters.
For an arithmetic weight
a:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
with Dirichlet series
A(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a(n)n^{-s}
, the Fejér–Dirichlet lift is

\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)\;=\;\sum_{i\geq 1}a(i)\,\phi_{i}(z),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{which interpolates}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)=(a*1)(n).

Here
\zeta(s)
denotes the Riemann zeta function and Dirichlet convolution is written
*
.
The parameter regime is
|q|>1
unless stated otherwise; for
q>1
the branch
q^{-z}=e^{-z\log q}
(principal real
\log q
) is used; for
q=-1
all Dirichlet–series identities are taken in the Abel sense; for
q=-Q<-1
with
Q>1
the weighted alternating Dirichlet series
\eta_{Q}
appears.
Complete conventions and branch details are collected in the appendix.

Integer anchors and two indicators.

The core of the construction is a weighted superposition of Fejér divisor filters,
\phi_{i}(z)=F(z,i)/i^{2}
. Geometric weights
q^{-i}
are used to ensure convergence for a parameter
q>1
, leading to the following definition for the sum term:

S_{q}(x):=\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\,\phi_{i}(x),\hskip 20.00003ptq>1.

The original indicator is then defined by subtracting a simple corrector:

\mathfrak{F}(x,q)=(q-1)q\Big(S_{q}(x)-q^{-x}\Big),

so that
\mathfrak{F}(p,q)=0
for primes and
\mathfrak{F}(m,q)>0
for composites
m\geq 4
.
The tangent–matched variant, which is the main focus for real-axis analysis, incorporates a periodic normalizer:

\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(x,q)=(q-1)q\Big(S_{q}(x)-q^{-x}\big(1+(\log q)\,S_{1}(x)\big)\Big),\hskip 10.00002ptS_{1}(x):=\tfrac{\sin(2\pi x)}{2\pi},

which preserves the same integer values and additionally satisfies
\partial_{x}\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(n,q)=0
for all integers
n
.

The Core Mechanism: A Regularized Divisibility Test

2.1.
A trigonometric representation of trial division

The foundational idea, detailed in
[
6
]
, is to express the divisibility of a number
z
by an integer
i
using the quotient
Q(z,i)=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi z)}{\sin^{2}(\pi z/i)}
. This form becomes indeterminate (
0/0
) precisely when
i
is a divisor of an integer
z
.

2.2.
Regularization via the Fejér kernel

The indeterminacy is resolved by invoking the identity for the Fejér kernel, a classical construction from Fourier analysis. This identity transforms the indeterminate quotient into a finite, and therefore everywhere-defined, cosine sum. This leads to the definition of the essential component of the construction presented herein, a function
F(z,i)
which is analytic for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

Definition 2.1

(The Fejér Kernel Term)
.

For any
z\in\mathbb{C}
and integer
i\geq 2
, the function
F(z,i)
is defined as

F(z,i)=i+2\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}(i-k)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi kz}{i}\right).

This function forms the fundamental component of the construction presented herein. For an integer
n
, it has the property that
F(n,i)=i^{2}
if
i
is a divisor of
n
, and
F(n,i)=0
otherwise. This property follows directly from the evaluation of finite trigonometric sums.
Case 1 (
i
divides
n
): Let
n=im
for some integer
m
. The argument of the cosine becomes
\frac{2\pi k(im)}{i}=2\pi km
, which is an integer multiple of
2\pi
. Thus,
\cos(\dots)=1
for all
k
. The sum becomes
F(n,i)=i+2\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}(i-k)=i+2\left(i(i-1)-\frac{i(i-1)}{2}\right)=i+i(i-1)=i^{2}
.
Case 2 (
i
does not divide
n
): The sum is a classical identity related to the Dirichlet kernel. For any integer
n
not divisible by
i
,
\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\cos\!\bigl(\frac{2\pi kn}{i}\bigr)=-1
and
\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}k\cos\!\bigl(\frac{2\pi kn}{i}\bigr)=-\frac{i}{2}
hold. Substituting these into the definition yields
F(n,i)=i+2\left(i\sum\cos(\dots)-\sum k\cos(\dots)\right)=i+2(i(-1)-(-\frac{i}{2}))=i-2i+i=0
. A derivation follows from geometric series identities and taking real parts at
x=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n/i}\neq 1
; see e.g.
[
4
, Ch. 3]
,
[
22
, Ch. I]
,
[
15
, Ch. I]
. For completeness, proofs are recorded in the Appendix.
This discrete arithmetic behavior of an analytic function represents the central mechanism of this work. The Fejér identity establishes a connection, transforming the analytic concept of a ’relative divisibility remainder’, encapsulated by the quotient
Q(z,i)
, into a purely arithmetic output for integer arguments: either zero or the square of the divisor
i
. This direct equivalence motivates its use as the core component for the subsequent constructions.

Proposition 2.2

(Closed form of the Fejér term)
.

For
i\geq 2
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

F(z,i)=\sum_{k=-(i-1)}^{i-1}(i-|k|)\,e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}kz/i}=\left(\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\sin(\pi z/i)}\right)^{2},

where the apparent poles on the right-hand side are removable; the value at
z\in i\mathbb{Z}
equals
i^{2}
.

Sketch.

Standard Fejér kernel identity with
N=i-1
; removability at
z\in i\mathbb{Z}
via first-order expansions.
Full proof: Appendix
B
.
∎

Remark 2.3

(Relation to the classical Fejér kernel)
.

With
N=i-1
and
\theta=2\pi z/i
, the normalized Fejér kernel is

K_{N}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N+1}\left(\frac{\sin\big((N+1)\theta/2\big)}{\sin(\theta/2)}\right)^{\!2}=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\Bigl(1-\frac{|k|}{N+1}\Bigr)e^{\mathrm{i}k\theta}.

Accordingly,

F(z,i)=\left(\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\sin(\pi z/i)}\right)^{\!2}=(N+1)\,K_{N}(2\pi z/i).

This identification explains the evenness around integers and the quadratic contact at
z\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark (Divisor-filter).
The identity
F(n,i)/i^{2}=\mathbf{1}_{i\mid n}
shows that
F
plays the role of a
divisor filter
at integer inputs.
This role underlies the Fejér–Dirichlet Lift developed in Section
3
, where divisor filtering is combined with Dirichlet convolution and Möbius inversion.

Convention.
It is convenient to set
F(z,1):=1
so that the divisor-filter identity extends to
i=1
.

3.
The General Fejér–Dirichlet Lift (FD-Lift)

In this section the FD-lift is formulated, the divisor-sum anchor at integers is established, and the Dirichlet-series factorization
\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)n^{-s}=\zeta(s)A(s)
is recorded, together with the polylog–zeta specialization for
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{F}
.

Standing assumptions and scope

Unless stated otherwise, the following assumptions and conventions are used in this section:

•

If
\sum_{i\geq 1}|a(i)|<\infty
, then
\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)=\sum_{i\geq 1}a(i)\,F(z,i)/i^{2}
converges locally uniformly on
\mathbb{C}
and defines an entire function of order
\leq 1
and exponential type
\leq 2\pi
.

•

If only
\sum_{i\geq 1}|a(i)|\,i^{-\sigma}<\infty
holds for some
\sigma>1
, then Abel-regularized or renormalized versions of the lift are used when entire-ness is required away from the integers (see the renormalized constructions later in the paper).

•

The Dirichlet-series identity
\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)n^{-s}=\zeta(s)A(s)
is asserted on the half-plane of absolute convergence of
A(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a(n)n^{-s}
(and extended by analytic continuation when available).

Bridge at a glance.
For weights
a:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
,

\text{local:}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)=(a*1)(n)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{corresponds to}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{spectral:}\hskip 9.24994pt\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)\,A(s),

where
A(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a(n)n^{-s}
. The kernel
F(z,i)/i^{2}
acts as a divisor filter at integers, while Dirichlet convolution transfers to multiplication in the Dirichlet–series domain.

This section introduces the general framework of the Fejér–Dirichlet Lift (FD-Lift). The method constructs an entire function
\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)
from a given arithmetic sequence of weights
a(n)
. This function serves as an analytic interpolant for the Dirichlet convolution
(a*1)(n)
and possesses a Dirichlet series that factors in terms of the Riemann Zeta function.
This section abstracts the mechanism of Section
4
into a linear operator
\mathcal{T}_{a}
.

3.1.
Definition and Core Properties

The construction of the lift is a linear superposition of the fundamental Fejér kernel terms, weighted by the arithmetic information encoded in a sequence
a:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
. The logic follows from the observation that the term
F(z,i)/i^{2}
serves as an analytic continuation of the divisor-indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{i|n}
.

Definition 3.1

(The General Fejér–Dirichlet Lift)
.

For a complex sequence of weights
a:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
, the FD-Lift
\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)
is defined as

\mathcal{T}_{a}(z):=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a(i)}{i^{2}}F(z,i),\hskip 20.00003ptF(z,1):=1.

If the series of weights converges absolutely,
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a(i)|<\infty
, the series defining
\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)
converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
. Since each term in the sum is an entire function, the limit function
\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)
is, by the Weierstrass theorem on uniformly convergent series of analytic functions, an entire function of order at most
1
and exponential type at most
2\pi
.

The constructed function
\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)
possesses two foundational properties that establish its role as a bridge between the local space of integers and the spectral space of Dirichlet series. These are summarized in the following theorem.

Lemma 3.2

(Growth bound and exponential type)
.

For
z=x+\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{C}
and integer
i\geq 2
the Fejér–Dirichlet kernel satisfies

\bigl|F(z,i)\bigr|\;\leq\;i^{2}\,\cosh(2\pi|y|).

Consequently, if
\sum_{i\geq 1}|a(i)|<\infty
, then the FD-Lift

\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)\;=\;\sum_{i\geq 1}a(i)\,\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}

obeys the global bound

\bigl|\mathcal{T}_{a}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\bigr|\;\leq\;\Bigl(\sum_{i\geq 1}|a(i)|\Bigr)\,\cosh(2\pi|y|),

and in particular
\mathcal{T}_{a}
is an entire function of order at most
1
and exponential type at most
2\pi
, since
\cosh(2\pi|y|)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(e^{2\pi|y|}+e^{-2\pi|y|})
provides a global
e^{2\pi|y|}
–bound along vertical lines.

Remark 3.3

(Order
\leq 1
and exponential type
\leq 2\pi
)
.

An entire function
H
has exponential type at most
T
if for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
C_{\epsilon}
with
|H(x+\mathrm{i}y)|\leq C_{\epsilon}\,e^{(T+\epsilon)|y|}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
(see, e.g., Levin
[
13
]
). Lemma
3.2
yields

|\mathcal{T}_{a}(x+\mathrm{i}y)|\leq\Bigl(\sum|a(i)|\Bigr)\cosh(2\pi|y|)\leq C\,e^{2\pi|y|}\hskip 20.00003pt(x,y\in\mathbb{R}).

Since
|y|\leq|z|
for
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
, this immediately gives the global bound

|\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)|\leq C\,e^{2\pi|z|}\hskip 20.00003pt(z\in\mathbb{C}),

so
\mathcal{T}_{a}
is of order at most
1
and exponential type at most
2\pi
in the usual radial sense. The geometric corrector
z\mapsto q^{-z}=e^{-z\log q}
(used elsewhere in the paper) has modulus
|q^{-x-\mathrm{i}y}|=q^{-x}
along vertical lines and therefore exponential type
0
; it does not affect the type when combined linearly with Fejér superpositions.

Proof.

Using the cosine series
F(z,i)=i+2\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}(i-k)\cos\!\bigl(2\pi kz/i\bigr)
and
|\cos(x+\mathrm{i}y)|\leq\cosh(y)
gives

|F(z,i)|\;\leq\;i\cdot\cosh(0)\;+\;2\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}(i-k)\cosh\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi k}{i}|y|\Bigr)\;\leq\;i\;+\;2\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}(i-k)\cosh(2\pi|y|).

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}(i-k)=\tfrac{i(i-1)}{2}
, it follows that
|F(z,i)|\leq i^{2}\cosh(2\pi|y|)
. Summation with weights
|a(i)|/i^{2}
yields the claimed bound for
\mathcal{T}_{a}
. The growth estimate implies order
\leq 1
and exponential type
\leq 2\pi
.
∎

Theorem 3.4

(Fundamental Properties of the FD-Lift)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)
be the FD-Lift for a sequence of weights
a(n)
whose associated Dirichlet series is
A(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a(n)n^{-s}
. The following properties hold:

(1)

(Local Property: Integer Values)
For any integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the value of the function is equal to the Dirichlet convolution of
a
and the unit function
1(n)=1
:

\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)=\sum_{d|n}a(d)=(a*1)(n).

(2)

(Spectral Property: Dirichlet Series)
In the half-plane of absolute convergence, the Dirichlet series of the sequence
\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)
is the product of the Riemann Zeta function and the Dirichlet series of the weights:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)A(s).

This identity holds for all
s
with
\Re s>\max\{1,\sigma_{0}\}
, where
A(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a(n)n^{-s}
converges absolutely on
\Re s>\sigma_{0}
; extension to other
s
follows by analytic continuation, when available.

Sketch.

(1) For integers
n
, the projector property yields
F(n,i)/i^{2}=\mathbf{1}_{i\mid n}
, hence
\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)=(a*1)(n)
.
(2) For the Dirichlet series, swap sums (absolute convergence) and regroup:
\sum_{n}\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)n^{-s}=\sum_{d}a(d)d^{-s}\sum_{m}m^{-s}=A(s)\zeta(s)
.
Details: Appendix
C
.

(Order
1
and exponential type
\leq 2\pi
)
.

Assume
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a(i)|<\infty
. Then the FD-Lift
\mathcal{T}_{a}

. In particular, there exists a constant

3.2.
Functional and Algebraic Properties

The FD-Lift framework respects the fundamental algebraic structures of arithmetic functions and their associated Dirichlet series. The lift operator
\mathcal{T}:a\mapsto\mathcal{T}_{a}
exhibits the following key properties.

•

Linearity.
The lift is a linear operator. For any two sequences
a_{1},a_{2}
and complex constants
c_{1},c_{2}
, it holds that

\mathcal{T}_{c_{1}a_{1}+c_{2}a_{2}}(z)=c_{1}\mathcal{T}_{a_{1}}(z)+c_{2}\mathcal{T}_{a_{2}}(z).

•

Möbius Inversion.
Since
\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)=(a*1)(n)
, the original weight sequence
a(n)
can be recovered from the integer values of the lift by Dirichlet convolution with the Möbius function
\mu(n)
:

a(n)=(\mu*\mathcal{T}_{a})(n).

In the spectral space, this corresponds to the division of the resulting Dirichlet series by
\zeta(s)
.

•

Logarithmic Derivative Identity.
A relationship holds between the logarithmic derivatives of the involved Dirichlet series. Let
M(s)=\zeta(s)A(s)
denote the spectrum of the lift. Then

-\frac{M^{\prime}(s)}{M(s)}\;=\;-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}\;-\;\frac{A^{\prime}(s)}{A(s)}.

This is a purely analytic identity. An arithmetical “von Mangoldt” interpretation of
-A^{\prime}(s)/A(s)
requires that
A(s)
admit an Euler product (e.g., when the weights are multiplicative and satisfy standard convergence hypotheses); without such a product,
-A^{\prime}(s)/A(s)
is only a formal logarithmic derivative and does not carry canonical positivity or prime-power counting properties.

•

Divisor lattice algebra.
The basis functions
F(z,i)/i^{2}
encode divisibility at integer arguments. For integers
n,i,j
one has the pointwise identity

\frac{F(n,i)}{i^{2}}\,\frac{F(n,j)}{j^{2}}=\mathbf{1}_{i\mid n}\,\mathbf{1}_{j\mid n}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{lcm}(i,j)\mid n}=\frac{F(n,\mathrm{lcm}(i,j))}{\mathrm{lcm}(i,j)^{2}},

which highlights compatibility with Dirichlet convolution. No such identity is claimed for non-integer arguments.

3.3.
Connection to Prime Number Theory: Two Complementary Approaches

A key application of the FD-Lift is its ability to establish a direct connection to prime number theory and the zeros of the Riemann Zeta function. Two fundamentally different but complementary approaches to leverage this connection are presented.

(1)

The Direct Adapter Method (Analysis).
This is a targeted approach where the weights
a(n)
are precisely tailored to isolate a specific arithmetic property—primality, as represented by the von Mangoldt function
\Lambda(n)
—and thereby directly generate its spectrum,
-\zeta^{\prime}(s)/\zeta(s)
.

(2)

The Dynamic Method (Synthesis).
This is a synthetic approach where, instead of isolating one property, a general, parameter-dependent family of functions (related to
\sigma_{-s}(n)
) is constructed. A new, complex property related to prime numbers is then derived through an analytic operation (differentiation) within this family.

Both tracks illuminate the same underlying structures from different perspectives and demonstrate the generality and flexibility of the FD-Lift framework. The following sections detail each approach.

Takeaways (FD-lift).

Integer anchors satisfy
\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}a(d)
.
The Dirichlet-series factorization
\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)n^{-s}=\zeta(s)A(s)
is obtained.
For
a(i)=(q-1)q\,q^{-i}
the specializations for
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{F}
follow via
\operatorname{Li}_{s}(1/q)
.

3.4.
Track 1: The Direct Adapter Method for Isolating

In this subsection a direct adapter is set up to isolate the von Mangoldt function at integers via the FD-lift. A renormalized kernel is employed to ensure absolute and locally uniform convergence; the integer anchor

Proposition 3.6

(Explicit formula via the adapter method)

Remark 3.7

(Explicit formula: form used)

Proof.

Fix

[

3.4.1.
Strategy: From Zeros to Poles

A central goal in analytic number theory is to understand the distribution of the non-trivial zeros
\rho
of the Zeta function. In complex analysis, poles are often more tractable than zeros. The strategy is therefore to construct a function whose poles coincide exactly with the zeros of
\zeta(s)
. The canonical function with this property is the logarithmic derivative,
-\zeta^{\prime}(s)/\zeta(s)
. On the
z
-side, the renormalized entire interpolant from Theorem
3.8
is used, thereby avoiding unrenormalized Abel-limit issues away from integers.
The FD-Lift is "tuned" by choosing weights
a(n)
such that the resulting spectrum matches this target:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)A(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}.

3.4.2.
The Adapter Mechanism

To achieve the target spectrum, the Dirichlet series of the weights must be
A(s)=-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)^{2}}
. From the theory of Dirichlet series, the arithmetic function corresponding to this spectrum is the Dirichlet convolution of the Möbius function
\mu
and the von Mangoldt function
\Lambda
. The mechanism proceeds in three steps:

(1)

Define the weights.
The weights are set to
a(n):=b(n)=(\mu*\Lambda)(n)
. The associated Dirichlet series is then, as required:

B(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{b(n)}{n^{s}}=\left(\sum\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{s}}\right)\left(\sum\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}\right)=\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}\cdot\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}\right)=-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)^{2}}.

(2)

Evaluate the lift (spectral side).
The spectrum of the lift
\mathcal{T}_{b}(z)
is, by Theorem
3.4
,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{b}(n)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)B(s)=\zeta(s)\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)^{2}}\right)=-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)}.

(3)

Evaluate the lift (local side).
The value at integer arguments is
\mathcal{T}_{b}(n)=(b*1)(n)=(\mu*\Lambda*1)(n)
. Using the standard identities
(\Lambda*1)(n)=\log n
and
(\mu*\log)(n)=\Lambda(n)
(see, e.g., Apostol
[
2
, Ch. 2]
), it follows that:

\mathcal{T}_{(\mu*\Lambda)}(n)=\Lambda(n).

Theorem 3.8

(Renormalized FD-lift for
a=\mu*\Lambda

.

Let
\phi_{i}(z):=F(z,i)/i^{2}
and
\phi_{\infty}(z):=(\sin(\pi z)/(\pi z))^{2}

\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{ren})}_{\mu*\Lambda}(z)\ :=\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\mu*\Lambda)(i)\,\bigl(\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z)\bigr)

and hence defines an entire function. Moreover, for every integer

\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{ren})}_{\mu*\Lambda}(n)=\Lambda(n).

Sketch.

On
|z|\leq R

\sin(\pi z/i)=\frac{\pi z}{i}(1+E_{i}(z))

.
With
(\mu*\Lambda)(i)\ll\log i

At integers, the renormalization removes the

\Lambda(n)
.
Full proof: Appendix

∎

Remark 3.9

(Abel regularisation away from integers)

For

3.5.
Track 2: The Dynamic Method via the Two-Variable Lift

In this subsection a two-variable FD-lift
\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{ren})}(z,s)
is introduced; holomorphy on
\Re s>-1
is documented, the integer identity
\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{ren})}(n,s)=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{-s}

Variables.
Throughout this section,

denotes the interpolation variable,
s
the weight parameter in the two–variable lift, and

the Dirichlet–series variable. This convention prevents clashes between the interpolation and spectral sides.

This second approach explores a dynamic structure by introducing a new complex parameter into the weights themselves. Instead of constructing a single function, an entire family of functions is generated, and a new arithmetic relationship is revealed by analyzing the behavior of this family.

3.5.1.
A Parameter-Dependent Family of Functions

The lift is generalized by choosing weights that are themselves a function of a complex variable
s
, namely
a(i,s)=i^{-s}
. This elevates the FD-Lift to a function of two complex variables,
\mathcal{T}(z,s)
.

Definition 3.11

(The Two-Variable Lift)
.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the two-variable lift is defined as

\mathcal{T}(z,s):=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{i^{-s}}{i^{2}}F(z,i)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{s+2}}.

Convergence and regularity.

For each fixed
s
with
\Re s>1
, the series defining
\mathcal{T}(z,s)
converges absolutely and uniformly on compact subsets of
z\in\mathbb{C}
, hence
z\mapsto\mathcal{T}(z,s)
is entire.
For each fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the series converges absolutely for
\Re s>1
and defines a function holomorphic in
s
on
\Re s>1
.
Moreover, gliding differentiation with respect to
s
is justified on every half-plane
\Re s\geq 1+\delta
with
\delta>0
by the Weierstrass
M
-test applied to the majorant
\sum_{i\geq 1}(\log i)/i^{1+\delta}
.
At integer points
n\in\mathbb{N}
one has the interpolation identity

\mathcal{T}(n,s)=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{-s}=\sigma_{-s}(n).

•

In the local space
, at an integer argument
n
, each function in the family interpolates the sum-of-powers-of-divisors function
\sigma_{-s}(n)
:

\mathcal{T}(n,s)=\sum_{d|n}d^{-s}=\sigma_{-s}(n).

•

In the spectral space
, the Dirichlet series of this sequence (over the variable
u
) is the product of two Zeta functions, a central object in number theory:

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{-s}(n)}{n^{u}}=\zeta(u)\zeta(u+s),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }\re(u)>1,\re(u+s)>1.

The parameter
s
acts as a "dial" that continuously adjusts the arithmetic properties of the system.

3.5.2.
The Differentiation Operator as an Arithmetic Transformer

The potential of this dynamic approach is demonstrated by applying an analytic operation to the parameter
s
: differentiation at the origin
s=0
. The **differentiation operator** is defined as
\mathcal{D}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\big|_{s=0}
. This operator acts as a operator that transforms the arithmetic properties of the function on all levels of the lift.

Proposition 3.12

(Renormalized spectral differentiation at
s=0
)
.

Let
\phi_{i}(z):=F(z,i)/i^{2}
and
\phi_{\infty}(z):=\bigl(\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z}\bigr)^{2}
with
\phi_{\infty}(0):=1
.
For
\Re s>-1
define the renormalized two-variable lift

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{ren}}(z,s)\ :=\ \sum_{i\geq 1}\bigl(\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z)\bigr)\,i^{-s}\;+\;\phi_{\infty}(z)\,\zeta(s+2).

Then:

(1)

For each fixed
s
with
\Re s>-1
, the map
z\mapsto\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{ren}}(z,s)
is entire; for each fixed

, the map
s\mapsto\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{ren}}(z,s)
is holomorphic on
\Re s>-1
.

(2)

The derivative at
s=0
exists and equals

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{ren}}(z,s)\Big|_{s=0}\ =\ -\sum_{i\geq 1}\bigl(\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z)\bigr)\,\log i\;+\;\phi_{\infty}(z)\,\zeta^{\prime}(2),

where the series converges absolutely and locally uniformly in

.

(3)

For every integer

and all

with
\Re s>-1

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{ren}}(n,s)=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{-s}=\sigma_{-s}(n).

Consequently,

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\sigma_{-s}(n)\Big|_{s=0}\ =\ -\sum_{d\mid n}\log d\ =\ -(\tau*\Lambda)(n).

(4)

On the spectral side,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_{-s}(n)}{n^{u}}=\zeta(u)\,\zeta(u+s)\hskip 20.00003pt(\Re u>1,\ \Re(u+s)>1).

Differentiating at

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{-\sum_{d\mid n}\log d}{n^{u}}\;=\;\zeta(u)\,\zeta^{\prime}(u)\hskip 20.00003pt(\Re u>1),

and, equivalently,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(\tau*\Lambda)(n)}{n^{u}}\;=\;-\,\zeta(u)\,\zeta^{\prime}(u)\hskip 20.00003pt(\Re u>1).

Hence, in the Dirichlet–series variable
u

-\;(\tau*\Lambda)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \zeta(u)\,\zeta^{\prime}(u).

Lemma 3.13

(Divisor–log identity)
.

For every

\sum_{d\mid n}\log d\;=\;(\tau*\Lambda)(n).

Proof.

Two proofs are recorded.

(i) Dirichlet–series proof.
The Dirichlet series of

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\sum_{d\mid n}\log d}{n^{u}}=\sum_{d\geq 1}\frac{\log d}{d^{u}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{1}{m^{u}}=\zeta(u)\sum_{d\geq 1}\frac{\log d}{d^{u}}=\zeta(u)\,\bigl(-\zeta^{\prime}(u)\bigr)\hskip 20.00003pt(\Re u>1).

Since
\sum_{n\geq 1}(\tau*\Lambda)(n)n^{-u}=\zeta(u)^{2}\cdot(-\zeta^{\prime}(u)/\zeta(u))=-\zeta(u)\zeta^{\prime}(u)
on
\Re u>1

(ii) Prime–power check.
It suffices to verify the identity on

and use multiplicativity of both sides. For

\sum_{d\mid p^{k}}\log d=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\log(p^{j})=(0+1+\cdots+k)\log p=\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\log p.

On the other hand,
(\tau*\Lambda)(p^{k})=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\tau(p^{j})\Lambda(p^{k-j})=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(j+1)\log p=\frac{k(k+1)}{2}\log p
, using
\Lambda(p^{m})=\log p
for
m\geq 1
and
0
otherwise. This matches the divisor–log sum.
∎

Proof.

Fix
R>0

The construction uses the general FD–lift

\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)=\sum_{i\geq 1}a(i)\,\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}},

with the

Definition 4.1

(The Normalized Analytic Prime Indicator)
.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
|q|>1

\mathfrak{F}(z,q)
is defined as

\mathfrak{F}(z,q)=\underbrace{\left((q-1)q\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)^{i}\right)}_{\mathfrak{S}(z,q)}-\underbrace{(q-1)q\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)^{z}}_{C(z,q)}.

Theorem 4.2

(Analyticity and radial growth of
\mathfrak{F}(\cdot,q)
)
.

For any fixed
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
|q|>1
, the map
z\mapsto\mathfrak{F}(z,q)
is entire of order at most
1
and exponential type at most
2\pi
.

Proof.

Uniform convergence on compact sets follows from the Weierstrass M-test (see Appendix
B
). The cosine expansion of
F
and
|\cos(x+\mathrm{i}y)|\leq\cosh(y)
imply

|F(x+\mathrm{i}y,i)|\leq i^{2}\cosh(2\pi|y|)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}i^{2}\big(e^{2\pi|y|}+e^{-2\pi|y|}\big).

Consequently, for some
C=C(q)
and all

,

|\mathfrak{F}(z,q)|\;\leq\;C\,e^{2\pi|y|}\;\leq\;C\,e^{2\pi|z|},

because
|y|\leq|z|
. The corrector
z\mapsto q^{-z}
has exponential type
0
. Hence the function is entire of order
\leq 1
and exponential type
\leq 2\pi
in the radial sense.
∎

4.3.
Properties of the Prime Indicator

Theorem 4.3

(Integer Prime-Zero Property)
.

For any integer
n\geq 2
and any real number
q>1
,

\mathfrak{F}(n,q)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptn\text{ is a prime number}.

Proof.

By construction,

\mathfrak{F}(z,q)=(q-1)q\Big(\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\,\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}\;-\;q^{-z}\Big),\hskip 20.00003pt\frac{F(n,i)}{i^{2}}=\mathbf{1}_{i\mid n}\ \ (n\in\mathbb{N}).

For a prime
p
, only
i=p
contributes among
i\geq 2
, hence
\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\tfrac{F(p,i)}{i^{2}}=q^{-p}
and
\mathfrak{F}(p,q)=0
.

For a composite
n\geq 4
(the only composites in range), the divisor
i=n
contributes
q^{-n}
, which is exactly removed by the correction
q^{-n}
. Thus

\mathfrak{F}(n,q)=(q-1)q\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
2\leq d<n\end{subarray}}\!q^{-d}.

Since
n
is composite, the set of proper divisors is nonempty; for real
q>1
each term satisfies
q^{-d}>0
, hence the sum is strictly positive. This proves the equivalence.
∎

Remark.
For complex
q
, cancellations can occur, meaning the reverse implication (zero
\Rightarrow
prime) does not hold in general for composite integers.

4.6.
An intrinsic periodic normalizer and elimination of companion zeros

The companion zeros observed for the original indicator are caused by a
tangent mismatch
at integers: the Fejér sum

S_{q}(z):=\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\,\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}

is even around each integer (hence
S^{\prime}(n)=0
), while the exponential corrector
q^{-z}
has nonzero slope at every integer. A structural remedy is obtained by enforcing the same local geometry (value
and
vanishing first derivative) for the corrector at all integers via a
1
-periodic entire modulation.

Definition 4.10

(Periodic normalizer and optimized indicator)
.

Let
q>1
and define the
1
-periodic entire function

S_{1}(z):=\frac{\sin(2\pi z)}{2\pi}.

Set

C_{\sin}(z,q):=(q-1)q\,q^{-z}\,\bigl(1+(\log q)\,S_{1}(z)\bigr),\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(z,q):=(q-1)q\,S_{q}(z)\;-\;C_{\sin}(z,q).

Lemma 4.11

(Analyticity and vertical growth of
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}
)
.

Fix
q>1
. For
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
one has

\bigl|\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(x+\mathrm{i}y,q)\bigr|\;\leq\;(q-1)q\Bigl(\Sigma(q)\cosh(2\pi|y|)\;+\;q^{-x}\Bigl(1+(\log q)\,\frac{\cosh(2\pi|y|)}{2\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr),

where
\Sigma(q):=\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}/i^{2}
. In particular,
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(\cdot,q)
is entire of order
\leq 1
and exponential type
\leq 2\pi
.

Proof.

Write
S_{q}(z)=\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\,\phi_{i}(z)
with
\phi_{i}(z)=F(z,i)/i^{2}
. Using

F(z,i)=i+2\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}(i-k)\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi k}{i}z\Bigr),\hskip 20.00003pt|\cos(x+\mathrm{i}y)|\leq\cosh(y),

gives
|F(x+\mathrm{i}y,i)|\leq i^{2}\cosh(2\pi|y|)
and hence
|S_{q}(x+\mathrm{i}y)|\leq\Sigma(q)\cosh(2\pi|y|)
.
For the corrector note

S_{1}(z)=\frac{\sin(2\pi z)}{2\pi},\hskip 20.00003pt|\sin(2\pi(x+\mathrm{i}y))|\leq\cosh(2\pi|y|),

so
|S_{1}(x+\mathrm{i}y)|\leq\cosh(2\pi|y|)/(2\pi)
. Therefore

\bigl|q^{-x-\mathrm{i}y}\bigl(1+(\log q)S_{1}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\bigr)\bigr|\;\leq\;q^{-x}\Bigl(1+(\log q)\,\tfrac{\cosh(2\pi|y|)}{2\pi}\Bigr).

Combining both estimates yields the claim. The growth in
|y|
is
\ll e^{2\pi|y|}
, hence order
\leq 1
and exponential type
\leq 2\pi
.
∎

Lemma 4.12

(Integer values and tangent matching)
.

For every integer
n\geq 2
and real
q>1
,

\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(n,q)=\mathfrak{F}(n,q),\hskip 20.00003pt\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\,\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(z,q)\Big|_{z=n}=0.

Proof.

Since
\sin(2\pi n)=0
, one has
C_{\sin}(n,q)=(q-1)q\,q^{-n}
, hence
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(n,q)=\mathfrak{F}(n,q)
. Differentiating
q^{-z}\bigl(1+(\log q)S_{1}(z)\bigr)
gives

\frac{d}{dz}\Big(q^{-z}\bigl(1+(\log q)S_{1}(z)\bigr)\Big)=q^{-z}\Big(-(\log q)\bigl(1+(\log q)S_{1}(z)\bigr)+(\log q)S_{1}^{\prime}(z)\Big).

At
z=n
,
S_{1}(n)=0
and
S_{1}^{\prime}(n)=\cos(2\pi n)=1
, so the bracket vanishes and the derivative equals
0
. As the Fejér sum satisfies
S^{\prime}(n)=0
, the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 4.13

(Uniform positivity with explicit bounds)
.

Fix
q>1
. For every odd prime
p\geq 5
,

\frac{1}{2}\Big(\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{2}\,q^{-2}+\tfrac{8\pi^{2}}{27}\,q^{-3}+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{4}\,q^{-4}-\big(\tfrac{2\pi^{2}}{3}+(\log q)^{2}\big)\,q^{-5}\Big)\ \leq\ K(q,p)

and

where

K(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\,\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(p)\;-\;q^{-p}(\log q)^{2}\right).

In particular,
K(q,p)\geq c_{1}(q)>0
for all odd primes
p\geq 5
, with
c_{1}(q)
given by the lower bound, and
K(q,p)\leq c_{2}(q)
with
c_{2}(q)
given by the upper bound (both independent of

).

Proposition 4.14

(Local quadratic nonnegativity at primes)

Let
q>1
and let

such that for all

Moreover, the bounds in Lemma

Subtract the

6.
Extended Regimes: Alternating and Negative Parameters

6.1.
The alternating limit
q=-1
and the
\eta
-bridge

In this subsection, structural facts are recorded for the alternating regime
q=-1
; all analytic series are to be understood in the Abel sense throughout.
Concretely, a damping parameter
0<r<1
is inserted and the locally uniform limit
r\uparrow 1
is taken.
Integer anchors at integer arguments remain exact without Abel summation.

Definition 6.1

(Alternating Abel normalisation)
.

For
0<r<1
set

\mathfrak{S}_{r}(z):=2\sum_{i\geq 2}(-r)^{i}\,\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}},\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{F}_{r}(z):=\mathfrak{S}_{r}(z)-2\,e^{-\,\mathrm{i}\pi z}.

The alternating limit is defined by the locally uniform limit

\mathfrak{S}(z,-1):=\lim_{r\uparrow 1}\mathfrak{S}_{r}(z),\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{F}(z,-1):=\lim_{r\uparrow 1}\mathfrak{F}_{r}(z).

Proposition 6.2

(Existence and symmetry)
.

The Abel limits
\mathfrak{S}(\cdot,-1):=\lim_{r\uparrow 1}\mathfrak{S}_{r}(\cdot)
and
\mathfrak{F}(\cdot,-1):=\lim_{r\uparrow 1}\mathfrak{F}_{r}(\cdot)
exist
locally uniformly
in
z
and define entire functions.
Moreover,

\overline{\mathfrak{F}(-\overline{z},-1)}=\mathfrak{F}(z,-1)\hskip 20.00003pt(z\in\mathbb{C}),

hence the complex zeros are symmetric with respect to the imaginary axis.

Remark 6.3

(Abel summation—reader’s guide)
.

For alternating weights at
q=-1
, the series are interpreted in the Abel sense: the sum
\sum_{i\geq 2}(-r)^{i}(\cdots)
is first considered for
0<r<1
, and then the limit
r\uparrow 1
is taken. In this way, analyticity is preserved and the continuous extension of the weights is matched. Intuitively, oscillations are gently damped and the damping is removed afterwards.

Proposition 6.4

(Dirichlet series identities (Abel sense))
.

For
\Re s>1
and
q=-1
, the following identities hold
in the Abel sense
:

\sum_{n\geq 2}\frac{\mathfrak{S}(n,-1)}{n^{s}}\;=\;2\,\zeta(s)\,\bigl(1-\eta(s)\bigr),\hskip 20.00003pt\sum_{n\geq 2}\frac{\mathfrak{F}(n,-1)}{n^{s}}\;=\;2\,\bigl(\zeta(s)-1\bigr)\,\bigl(1-\eta(s)\bigr),

where
\eta(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n-1}n^{-s}=(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)
is the Dirichlet eta function.

Corollary 6.5

(Behaviour at
s=1
)
.

The factor
1-\eta(s)
is entire and nonzero at
s=1
(indeed
1-\eta(1)=1-\log 2\neq 0
).
Hence the pole at
s=1
in the Dirichlet series above is entirely due to
\zeta(s)
(or
\zeta(s)-1
).

Proposition 6.6

(Alternation operator and the 2-adic split)
.

Let
a:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
with Dirichlet series
A(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a(n)n^{-s}
. Define
(\mathcal{A}a)(n):=(-1)^{n}a(n)
and
E(s):=\sum_{m\geq 1}a(2m)m^{-s}
.
Then

A_{\mathcal{A}}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n}a(n)}{n^{s}}=2^{\,1-s}\,E(s)\ -\ A(s),

and therefore the FD-lift satisfies

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}a}(n)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)\,A_{\mathcal{A}}(s)\ =\ 2^{\,1-s}\,\zeta(s)\,E(s)\ -\ \zeta(s)\,A(s).

Proof.

Split the sum into even and odd indices; for even indices write
n=2m
. The identities are immediate.
∎

Lemma 6.7

(Integer prime-zero property, sign caution)
.

For any prime

For composites, cancellations of alternating contributions may occur.
∎

6.2.
Structural Analysis of the
q=-1
Regime: 2-adic Geometry and Phase-Locking

This subsection complements Section
6.1
by recording a dyadic identity for Fejér filters, which reveals the underlying 2-adic geometry of the alternating regime. This structure is then used to establish a
real-axis rigidity
for the tangent-matched indicator at
q=-1
in the Abel sense. A complete classification of the integer zeros is also given.

Setup and Abel interpretation

For
0<r<1
define

\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}_{r}(z,-1)\;:=\;2\sum_{i\geq 2}(-r)^{i}\,\phi_{i}(z)\;-\;2\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\pi z}\,\bigl(1+\mathrm{i}\pi\,S_{1}(z)\bigr),\hskip 20.00003ptS_{1}(z)=\tfrac{\sin(2\pi z)}{2\pi},

and set
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(z,-1):=\lim_{r\uparrow 1}\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}_{r}(z,-1)
, where the limit exists locally uniformly in
z
by the same argument as in Proposition
6.2
. On the real axis, the Fejér sum is real, so the imaginary part of
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}_{r}(x,-1)
equals the negative of the imaginary part of the corrector.

A dyadic cosine cascade

Lemma 6.10

(Dyadic cosine cascade)
.

For
m\geq 1
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\phi_{2m}(z)\;=\;\phi_{m}(z)\,\cos^{2}\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi z}{2m}\Bigr).

Proof.

Since
F(z,i)=\bigl(\frac{\sin\pi z}{\sin(\pi z/i)}\bigr)^{2}
and
\sin(\pi z/m)=2\sin(\pi z/(2m))\cos(\pi z/(2m))
,

\frac{F(z,2m)}{(2m)^{2}}=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi z)}{4m^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi z/(2m))}=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi z)}{m^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi z/m)}\;\cos^{2}\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi z}{2m}\Bigr)=\frac{F(z,m)}{m^{2}}\,\cos^{2}\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi z}{2m}\Bigr).

∎

Real-axis rigidity (phase locking)

Theorem 6.11

(Real-axis rigidity at
q=-1
(Abel sense))
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(x,-1)=0
, then
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. In particular, there are no real zeros off the integers.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
S_{r}(x):=2\sum_{i\geq 2}(-r)^{i}\phi_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
.
With
e^{-\mathrm{i}\pi x}=\cos(\pi x)-\mathrm{i}\sin(\pi x)
and
S_{1}(x)=\sin(2\pi x)/(2\pi)
,

\mathrm{Im}\Bigl(e^{-\mathrm{i}\pi x}\bigl(1+\mathrm{i}\pi S_{1}(x)\bigr)\Bigr)=\cos(\pi x)\cdot\frac{\sin(2\pi x)}{2}\;-\;\sin(\pi x)=\sin(\pi x)\bigl(\cos^{2}(\pi x)-1\bigr)=-\,\sin^{3}(\pi x).

Hence
\mathrm{Im}\,\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}_{r}(x,-1)=2\,\sin^{3}(\pi x)
for all
0<r<1
; letting
r\uparrow 1
gives

\mathrm{Im}\,\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(x,-1)=2\,\sin^{3}(\pi x)
. If
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(x,-1)=0
, then
\sin(\pi x)=0
, so
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Remark 6.12

(Original indicator)
.

The same argument without the tangent-matching factor shows

\mathrm{Im}\,\mathfrak{F}_{r}(x,-1)=2\,\sin(\pi x)
, so any real zero of
\mathfrak{F}(\cdot,-1)
is also constrained to the integers.

Integer zeros: complete classification

At any integer
n
, the corrector of the sharp variant simplifies as
S_{1}(n)=0
. Thus,
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(n,q)=\mathfrak{F}(n,q)
for any complex
q
, and their integer zeros coincide. The next lemma gives the exact integer zero set for the special case
q=-1
.

Lemma 6.13

(Integer zeros at
q=-1
)
.

For every integer
n\geq 2
,

\mathfrak{F}(n,-1)\;=\;2\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\geq 2\end{subarray}}(-1)^{d}\;-\;(-1)^{n}\Bigr).

Consequently,

\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(n,-1)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\text{$n$ is prime, \;or\; $v_{2}(n)=1$ (i.e.\ $n=2\cdot m$ with $m$ odd).}

Proof.

At integers,
\phi_{i}(n)=\mathbf{1}_{i\mid n}
, so the displayed formula follows directly from the definition of

\mathfrak{F}(\cdot,-1)
in Section
6.1
; the Abel interpretation is not needed at integers.
If
n
is odd, then all divisors
d\geq 2
are odd and contribute
-1
, so

\sum_{d\mid n,\,d\geq 2}(-1)^{d}=-(\tau(n)-1)
, which equals
-1
iff
\tau(n)=2
, i.e.
n
is prime.
If
n
is even with
v_{2}(n)=1
, write
n=2m
with
m
odd. The even divisors are exactly
2
times the divisors of
m
,
and the odd divisors
\geq 3
are the nontrivial divisors of
m
; hence

\sum_{d\mid n,\,d\geq 2}(-1)^{d}=\#\{\text{even divisors}\}-\#\{\text{odd divisors}\geq 3\}=\tau(m)-(\tau(m)-1)=1
,
which matches
(-1)^{n}=+1
and yields zero. If
v_{2}(n)\geq 2
, a direct count shows

\sum_{d\mid n,\,d\geq 2}(-1)^{d}-(-1)^{n}\neq 0
.
∎

Remark 6.14

(Scope)
.

On the real axis and in the Abel sense, Theorem
6.11
forces any real zero to be an integer.
Lemma
6.13
classifies exactly which integers are zeros: all primes and, in addition, all even integers with
v_{2}(n)=1
.
No positivity statement for composites is claimed in the alternating regime.

Remark 6.15

(Outlook: From dyadic to cyclotomic twists)
.

The dyadic identity in Lemma
6.10
is the first step in a larger hierarchy. A similar analysis can be performed by replacing the alternating weights
(-1)^{i}
with other periodic sequences, such as Dirichlet characters
\chi(i)
. This is expected to yield analogous "twist" identities and entire functions whose spectral properties are governed by Dirichlet L-functions,
L(s,\chi)
. A systematic treatment of these "cyclotomic twists" is left for future work.

6.3.
Lerch–
L
Bridge and Residue-Weighted Divisor Balance for General Complex
q

This subsection generalizes the findings for
q=-1
by investigating the structural principles that govern the framework for any complex parameter
q=R\,e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
. Two central questions are addressed: (1) How does the spectral signature, governed by the polylogarithm
\operatorname{Li}_{s}(q^{-1})
, relate to classical number-theoretic objects for arbitrary
q
? A
Lerch–
L
bridge
is established to answer this. (2) How can the appearance of additional integer zeros for certain
q
be explained and predicted? A
residue-weighted divisor balance
provides a concrete arithmetic criterion. Finally, the phase-locking mechanism observed at
q=-1
is generalized to a compact real-axis condition for the tangent-matched variant.

Notation.

Write
q=Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
with
R>0
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
For a positive integer
m
and
a\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
put
\theta=2\pi a/m
(rational angle).
The Lerch transcendent is
\Phi(z,s,a)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{z^{k}}{(k+a)^{s}}
for
\Re s>1
and

|z|<1
. The Hurwitz zeta is
\zeta(s,\alpha)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(k+\alpha)^{-s}
(
\Re s>1
,
\alpha\notin-\mathbb{N}_{0}
).

Proposition 6.16

(Lerch–
L
bridge on the unit circle)
.

Let
\theta=2\pi a/m
with integers
m\geq 1
and
a\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
. Then, for
\Re s>1
,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta n}}{n^{s}}\;=\;m^{-s}\sum_{r=1}^{m}e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\mathrm{i}ar}{m}}\,\zeta\!\left(s,\tfrac{r}{m}\right)\;=\;m^{-s}\sum_{\chi\!\!\!\pmod{m}}\tau(\overline{\chi},a)\,L(s,\chi),

where
\tau(\overline{\chi},a)=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\overline{\chi}(r)\,e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\mathrm{i}ar}{m}}
is a Gauss sum.

In particular
, for
|q|=1
with rational angle, the polylog factor in the FD-lift satisfies

\operatorname{Li}_{s}(q^{-1})=\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta n}n^{-s}
and admits a finite
L
–decomposition.

Proof sketch.

Finite Fourier expansion on residues modulo
m
yields

\sum_{n\geq 1}\!e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta n}n^{-s}=m^{-s}\sum_{r=1}^{m}e^{-\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}ar}{m}}\zeta(s,r/m)
.
Expanding the basis
\{e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}ar/m}\}_{r}
into Dirichlet characters gives the stated
L
–combination.
∎

Proposition 6.17

(Lerch deformation for
R>1
, rational angle)
.

Let
q=Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
with
R>1
and
\theta=2\pi a/m
as above. Then, for
\Re s>1
,

\operatorname{Li}_{s}\!\big(q^{-1}\big)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(R^{-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta})^{n}}{n^{s}}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\mathrm{i}ar}{m}}\,R^{-r}\,\Phi\!\left(R^{-m},\,s,\,\tfrac{r}{m}\right).

Consequently, the spectral factor in the FD-lift deforms continuously from a finite
L
–combination
(
R\downarrow 1
) to a finite sum of Lerch transcendent terms (
R>1
).

Proof sketch.

Split
n=mk+r
with
1\leq r\leq m
:

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(R^{-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta})^{n}}{n^{s}}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta r}R^{-r}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(R^{-m})^{k}}{(mk+r)^{s}}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta r}R^{-r}m^{-s}\Phi\!\left(R^{-m},s,\tfrac{r}{m}\right)
.
The stated form follows since
e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta r}=e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\mathrm{i}ar}{m}}
.
∎

Lemma 6.18

(Residue-weighted divisor balance at integers)
.

Let
q=Re^{\tfrac{2\pi\mathrm{i}a}{m}}
with
R>0
and integers
m\geq 1
,
a\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
.
For
n\geq 2
define the weighted residue sums

S_{r}(n;R)\;:=\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
2\leq d<n\\
d\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)\end{subarray}}\!R^{-d}\hskip 20.00003pt(r=0,1,\dots,m-1).

Then the integer values of the (non-sharp) indicator admit the decomposition

\mathfrak{F}(n,q)\;=\;(q-1)q\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\mathrm{i}ar}{m}}\,S_{r}(n;R).

In particular,

\mathfrak{F}(n,q)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\mathrm{i}ar}{m}}\,S_{r}(n;R)=0,

i.e., the residue-weighted divisor vector
S(n;R)=(S_{0},\dots,S_{m-1})
is orthogonal to the phase vector

(e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\mathrm{i}ar}{m}})_{r}
.

Proof.

At integers,
\phi_{i}(n)=\mathbf{1}_{i\mid n}
and by definition

\mathfrak{F}(n,q)=(q-1)q\sum_{2\leq d<n,\,d\mid n}q^{-d}=(q-1)q\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}e^{-\tfrac{2\pi\mathrm{i}ar}{m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n,\,2\leq d<n\\
d\equiv r(m)\end{subarray}}R^{-d}
.
∎

Remark 6.19

(The geometry of integer zeros)
.

Lemma
6.18
provides a geometric interpretation for the integer zeros. For each integer
n
, one can associate a vector of its divisor sums, weighted by
R^{-d}
and partitioned by residue classes modulo
m
. A zero occurs if and only if this "divisor vector" is perfectly orthogonal to the "phase vector" determined by
q
’s angle. This explains why zeros at composites are rare for real
q>1
(where all components are positive, preventing orthogonality) but become possible for complex
q
where cancellations can occur.

Corollary 6.20

(Special cases and suppression by
R>1
)
.

(i) For real
q>1
(
m=1
) all terms are positive and
\mathfrak{F}(n,q)=0
if and only if
n
is prime (Theorem
4.3
).

(ii) For
q=-1
(
m=2
,
R=1
) Lemma
6.18
recovers the exact integer-zero classification in Lemma
6.13
.

(iii) For
q=\pm\mathrm{i}
(
m=4
,
R=1
) additional integer zeros may occur via residue cancellations (e.g.
n=8
at
q=\mathrm{i}
).

(iv) For fixed rational angle and
R>1
, the weights
R^{-d}
favour small divisors and generically suppress accidental cancellations; in particular, extra composite integer zeros become sparse (heuristically rare) as
R
increases.

Proposition 6.21

(Phase-locking condition for real zeros of the sharp variant)
.

Let
q=Re^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
with
R>0
, and let
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
For the tangent-matched indicator
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(\cdot,q)
any real zero
x
must satisfy the
phase condition

\tan(\theta x)\;=\;\frac{\theta\,S_{1}(x)}{1+(\ln R)\,S_{1}(x)},\hskip 20.00003ptS_{1}(x)=\frac{\sin(2\pi x)}{2\pi},

in addition to the real-part matching. In particular, for
q=-1
(
R=1
,
\theta=\pi
) this reduces to

\tan(\pi x)=\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi x)
and hence
\sin^{3}(\pi x)=0
, implying that no off-integer real zeros exist
(Theorem
6.11
).

Proof sketch.

For real
x
, the Fejér sum is real. Writing
q^{-x}=R^{-x}e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta x}
and

\log q=\ln R+\mathrm{i}\theta
, the imaginary part of the corrector

e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta x}\bigl(1+(\ln R+\mathrm{i}\theta)S_{1}(x)\bigr)
equals

\cos(\theta x)\,\theta S_{1}(x)-\sin(\theta x)\bigl(1+\ln R\,S_{1}(x)\bigr)
.
The vanishing of the imaginary part yields the stated phase relation; the
q=-1
reduction is immediate.
∎

Remark 6.22

(Scope and use)
.

Propositions
6.16
–
6.17
identify when the spectral factor
collapses to a finite
L
–combination (unit circle, rational angle) and how it deforms for
R>1
.
Lemma
6.18
provides a concrete criterion for integer zeros via residue classes,
recovering the alternating regime and predicting additional cases at roots of unity.
Proposition
6.21
gives a compact real-axis constraint for the sharp variant,
with strict locking at
q=-1
.
No claim is made here regarding the density or distribution of extra composite integer zeros for general
q
;
empirically they are rare for
R>1
, and a quantitative study would require additional divisor-sum bounds.

6.4.
Negative real parameters
q<-1
: a weighted-
\eta
bridge and basic morphology

Robust properties for negative real parameters
q<-1
are recorded. Write
q=-Q
with
Q>1
.

Analyticity and integer values.

For fixed
Q>1
the series

\mathfrak{F}(z,-Q)=Q(Q+1)\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}\,(-Q)^{-i}\;-\;Q(Q+1)\,(-Q)^{-z}

converges absolutely and defines an entire function of
z
. At integers,

\mathfrak{F}(n,-Q)=Q(Q+1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
2\leq d<n\end{subarray}}(-Q)^{-d},

hence
\mathfrak{F}(p,-Q)=0
for every prime
p
, whereas no positivity statement for composites is asserted.

Definition 6.23

(Weighted alternating eta)
.

For
Q>1
define the entire function

\eta_{Q}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{Q^{\,n}\,n^{s}}\hskip 20.00003pt(\Re s>1\ \text{for absolute convergence, analytic continuation elsewhere}).

Note that
\operatorname{Li}_{s}(-1/Q)=-\eta_{Q}(s)
.

Proposition 6.24

(Dirichlet series for negative parameters
q=-Q<-1
)
.

Fix
Q>1
and set
q=-Q

Discussion and Future Work

The Fejér–Dirichlet lift provides a general and constructive framework for creating entire functions that interpolate arithmetic convolutions of the form
(a*1)
. This approach moves beyond specific constructions to offer a systematic method with several noteworthy conceptual aspects, which are discussed below alongside avenues for further research.

A Constructive Viewpoint: Mechanism versus Data

Heuristic only; no formal equivalence is claimed.

The framework presented in this paper can be seen as offering a perspective complementary to classical methods in analytic number theory. Classical explicit formulas, for example, rely fundamentally on spectral data—namely, the non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function. In that view, the analytical complexity is concentrated in these input data, whose deep structure remains largely conjectural.

The FD-Lift, in contrast, can be viewed as relocating this complexity from the data to an explicit, constructive process. The properties of the resulting entire functions are derived from the well-understood structure of the Fejér kernel and the chosen weights. Two complementary ways to encode prime information are contrasted below.

Local/filtering view (this paper).
Simple, structured input data (divisors at integers) are processed by an explicit but nontrivial analytic superposition (Fejér-based lift with normalization). The outcome is an entire function whose
integer-argument
behavior (prime zeros, composite positivity, tangent matching) is quantitatively controlled.

Spectral/global view (classical).
A simple analytic object (the Riemann zeta function via Dirichlet series and Euler product) is coupled to highly structured spectral input (its nontrivial zeros), yielding the explicit formula that governs fluctuations in prime counting.

Takeaway.
The FD–Lift transports Dirichlet convolution into spectral multiplication (
\zeta\cdot A
) and thus isolates a local, verifiable mechanism at integers. This picture is heuristic and does
not
assert any new equivalence between local filtering and the spectral theory of
\zeta
beyond the proved statements in this paper.

The Polylog–Zeta Connection as a Central Identity

Beyond a change of perspective, the framework reveals a structural identity that appears to govern a broad class of constructions. For any weight sequence
a:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
with Dirichlet series

A(s)\;=\;\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{a(i)}{i^{s}},

the Fejér–Dirichlet lift obeys

(1)

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)}{n^{s}}\;=\;\zeta(s)\,A(s)\hskip 20.00003pt(\text{on the joint half-plane of absolute convergence}),

cf. Theorem
3.4
. In this identity the zeta factor is structural, reflecting the divisor filter at the integers, while the companion factor
A(s)
is dictated by the chosen
i

thus appears as the geometric instance of (
1

i

\zeta(s)

The picture suggested by these examples is semantic as well as analytic. The divisor filter furnishes a universal "global carrier" in the form of
\zeta(s)
, while the chosen summability method determines how the local divisor data manifest on the spectral side through the companion factor

a(i)

Different damping or regularisation schemes select different spectral signatures (polylogarithms, Lerch transcendent, shifted or inverted zeta factors), providing a systematic way to generate new analytic objects with prescribed arithmetic properties. A comprehensive catalogue of admissible summation procedures and their corresponding companion factors appears to be a natural and promising direction for further work.

Motivation and Derivation of the Framework

This constructive viewpoint is rooted in the foundational observation that motivated the general lift presented herein. The quotient of squared sines, encapsulated in
F(z,i)
, can be viewed as an analytic proxy for divisibility. At integer arguments, where this proxy becomes indeterminate, the Fejér identity provides a crucial bridge to the arithmetic domain. A key insight from antecedent work was that this transformation does not merely yield arbitrary non-zero values for composite integers. Instead, a superposition of these terms produces highly structured arithmetic information—specifically, functions such as the sum of the squares of the divisors (
\sigma_{2}(n)
). The fact that a canonical arithmetic function emerges directly from an analytic process based on ’divisibility remainders’ was the principal motivation for abstracting this mechanism into the general FD-Lift framework.

The FD-Lift as a Modular Design Space

The framework presented can be understood as a modular toolkit, wherein different functions are constructed by combining specific components to achieve distinct analytical and arithmetical goals. A central distinction arises between properties holding within the open domain of convergence (e.g., for real

Summary of Contributions

The main contributions of this work can be summarized in three areas. First, the General Fejér-Dirichlet Lift provides a unified and constructive framework for creating entire functions that interpolate arithmetical convolutions, starting from the classical identity
(a*1)\ \leftrightarrow\ \zeta A
. Second, two complementary approaches to prime number theory are demonstrated within this framework: a direct "adapter" method that interpolates the von Mangoldt function
\Lambda(n)
, and a dynamic method that reveals a structural connection between divisor functions and
\Lambda(n)
via a differentiation operator. Third, a key technical innovation is the use of a periodic normalizer for "tangent matching," which is shown to provide precise control over the real-zero geometry of the prime indicator
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}
in the vicinity of integers.

Future Research Directions

The results presented open several avenues for future investigation:

(1)

Analysis of the Differentiation Operator.
The properties of the new entire functions generated by higher-order operators,
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}(z):=\mathcal{D}^{k}[\mathcal{T}(z,s)]
, are completely unexplored. An analysis of their zero distributions could provide new insights into the arithmetic functions they represent, such as the convolutions involving higher derivatives of the Zeta function.

(2)

The Inverse Spectral Problem.
A natural question concerns the
z
–
s
duality suggested by the two-variable lift. For a fixed
z_{0}
, the set of complex numbers
s
for which
\mathcal{T}(z_{0},s)=0
constitutes a new type of spectrum associated with the number
z_{0}
. Investigating whether this "s-spectrum" encodes the arithmetic properties of
z_{0}
is a promising research direction.

(3)

The Product Form of
\mathfrak{F}(z,q)
.
While the Dirichlet series of the FD-Lift possesses an Euler product if the weights are multiplicative, the entire function
\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)
itself possesses a Weierstrass product over its zeros. An explicit determination of the Weierstrass product for
\mathfrak{F}(z,q)
could provide a representation of an entire function in which the prime numbers appear as fundamental factors at the level of zeros, as a conceptual parallel to the Euler product in the spectral space. At present, a canonical product is not expected without substantially sharper control of zero locations and growth on vertical lines.

In conclusion, the framework offers a set of constructive tools for exploring the interface between discrete number theory and continuous analysis, suggesting that the explicit design of analytic mechanisms can be a fruitful complement to the study of given spectral data.

–test yields local uniform convergence of
\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{F(z,i)}{i}q^{-i}
. Consequently
\mathfrak{F}_{\sigma}(\cdot,q)
is entire.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

At integers
n
, the Fejér filter satisfies
F(n,i)/i^{2}=\mathbf{1}_{\,i\mid n}
, hence
\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}a(d)=(a*1)(n)
. For
\Re s>1
, absolute convergence of
\sum_{n\geq 1}|a(n)|n^{-\Re s}
and of
\zeta(s)
justifies Tonelli/Fubini. Therefore

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{a}(n)}{n^{s}}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{s}}\sum_{d\mid n}a(d)=\sum_{d\geq 1}\frac{a(d)}{d^{s}}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{1}{m^{s}}=A(s)\,\zeta(s).

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

With
a(i)=(q-1)q\,q^{-i}
for
i\geq 2
and
a(1)=0
,

A(s)=(q-1)q\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{q^{-i}}{i^{s}}=(q-1)q\bigl(\operatorname{Li}_{s}(1/q)-q^{-1}\bigr).

By Theorem
3.4
,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathfrak{S}(n,q)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)A(s).

Since
\mathfrak{F}(n,q)=\mathfrak{S}(n,q)-(q-1)q\,q^{-n}
, subtraction of
\sum_{n\geq 1}(q-1)q\,q^{-n}n^{-s}
gives the stated identities.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.12
.

Uniformly on compact
z
-sets,

\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z)=\left(\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\sin(\pi z/i)}\right)^{\!2}\frac{1}{i^{2}}-\left(\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z}\right)^{\!2}=O(i^{-2}),

since
\sin(\pi z/i)=(\pi z/i)\bigl(1+O(i^{-2})\bigr)
. Hence, for
\sigma=\Re s>-1
,

\sum_{i\geq 1}\bigl|\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z)\bigr|\,i^{-\sigma}\ll\sum_{i\geq 1}i^{-2-\sigma}<\infty,

with normal convergence on compact
z
-sets and vertical strips
\sigma\geq\sigma_{0}>-1
. Therefore
z\mapsto\sum_{i\geq 1}(\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z))i^{-s}
is entire and
s\mapsto\sum_{i\geq 1}(\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z))i^{-s}
is holomorphic on
\Re s>-1
. Termwise differentiation in
s
is justified by
\sum_{i\geq 1}(\log i)\,i^{-2-\sigma}<\infty
.

At integers
n
,
\phi_{\infty}(n)=0
and
\phi_{i}(n)=\mathbf{1}_{\,i\mid n}
, so
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{ren}}(n,s)=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{-s}
. The Dirichlet identity
\sum_{n\geq 1}\big(\sum_{d\mid n}d^{-s}\big)n^{-u}=\zeta(u)\,\zeta(u+s)
on
\Re u>1
follows by absolute convergence. Differentiation at
s=0
is permitted on
\Re u>1
, which yields the stated logarithmic weights and, with Apostol’s identity
\mu*\log=\Lambda
, the claims of the proposition.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

Fix
R>0
. Set
\phi_{\infty}(z):=\left(\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z}\right)^{2}
with
\phi_{\infty}(0):=1
. For
|z|\leq R
,

\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi z}{i}\right)=\frac{\pi z}{i}+\Big(\frac{\pi z}{i}\Big)^{3}\rho_{i}(z),

where the remainder satisfies
|\rho_{i}(z)|\leq\tfrac{1}{6}
by the standard bound
|\sin w-w|\leq|w|^{3}/6
; hence

\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi z}{i}\right)=\frac{\pi z}{i}\,\bigl(1+E_{i}(z)\bigr),\hskip 20.00003pt|E_{i}(z)|\ \leq\ \frac{\pi^{2}R^{2}}{6\,i^{2}}.

Consequently,

\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}=\phi_{\infty}(z)\,\frac{1}{\bigl(1+E_{i}(z)\bigr)^{2}}=\phi_{\infty}(z)\,\bigl(1+O_{R}(i^{-2})\bigr),

so
\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z)=O_{R}(i^{-2})
uniformly on
|z|\leq R
. Therefore

\sum_{i\geq 1}\bigl|\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z)\bigr|\ \ll_{R}\ \sum_{i\geq 1}i^{-2}<\infty.

Moreover,

(\mu*\Lambda)(i)=\sum_{d\mid i}\mu(d)\,\Lambda(i/d)\ \ll\ \log i\hskip 20.00003pt(i\geq 2),

see Apostol
[
2
, Ch. 2]
. Consequently,

\sum_{i\geq 1}\bigl|(\mu*\Lambda)(i)\bigr|\,\bigl|\phi_{i}(z)-\phi_{\infty}(z)\bigr|\ \ll_{R}\ \sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\log i}{i^{2}}<\infty,

uniformly on
|z|\leq R
. This proves absolute and locally uniform convergence on
\mathbb{C}
and entireness.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
one has
\phi_{\infty}(n)=0
and
\phi_{i}(n)=\mathbf{1}_{i\mid n}
. Hence

\mathcal{T}^{(\mathrm{ren})}_{\mu*\Lambda}(n)=\sum_{i\mid n}(\mu*\Lambda)(i)=(\mu*\Lambda*1)(n).

Since
(\Lambda*1)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\Lambda(d)=\log n
and Möbius inversion yields
\mu*\log=\Lambda
(Apostol
[
2
, Ch. 2]
), it follows that

(\mu*\Lambda*1)(n)=\mu*(\Lambda*1)(n)=\mu*\log(n)=\Lambda(n).

This proves Theorem
3.8
.
∎

Lemma C.1

(Trigonometric sums)
.

Let
m\geq 2
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
with
m\nmid n
. Then

\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\cos\!\Big(\frac{2\pi kn}{m}\Big)=-1,\hskip 20.00003pt\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k\,\cos\!\Big(\frac{2\pi kn}{m}\Big)=-\frac{m}{2}.

Proof.

Let
\omega=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n/m}\neq 1
. Since
\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\omega^{k}=0
, taking real parts gives the first identity. For the second, use the standard formula

\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}k\,\omega^{k}=-\,\frac{m}{1-\omega},

and take real parts; with
\omega=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
and
\theta=2\pi n/m
,
\Re\big((1-\omega)^{-1}\big)=\frac{1}{2}
, hence the value
-\frac{m}{2}
.
∎

Appendix D
Standard tools

Absolute convergence
\Rightarrow
reordering.

Whenever a double series
\sum_{n}\sum_{m}b_{n,m}
is absolutely convergent, reordering is legitimate (Fubini for series). Throughout, all rearrangements are justified by absolute convergence on the stated half-planes.

Weierstrass
M
–test (local uniformity).

If
\sum_{i}M_{i}<\infty
and
|f_{i}(z)|\leq M_{i}
on compact sets, then
\sum_{i}f_{i}(z)
converges uniformly on compacta; sums and derivatives can be exchanged termwise within the indicated ranges.

Dirichlet convolution conventions.

Dirichlet series of convolutions satisfy
D_{f*g}(s)=D_{f}(s)\,D_{g}(s)
on
\Re s>1
, provided both sides converge absolutely. The Möbius and von Mangoldt identities used here follow Apostol
[
2
, Ch. 2]
.

Fejér kernel facts.

The Fejér kernel
F(z,i)
satisfies
F(n,i)/i^{2}=\mathbf{1}_{i\mid n}
at integers
n
(divisor filter). For the functions
\phi_{i}(x)=F(x,i)/i^{2}
one has a local quadratic behavior at integers: writing
x=n+\eta
,

\phi_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{i^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi n/i)}\,\eta^{2}+O(\eta^{4}),&i\nmid n,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle 1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\!\left(1-\frac{1}{i^{2}}\right)\eta^{2}+O(\eta^{4}),&i\mid n,\end{cases}

whence
\phi_{i}^{\prime}(n)=0
for all integers
n
. This local structure, rather than a global evenness property, is used in the symmetry arguments; see Zygmund
[
22
]
.

Real-zero window analysis I (Original Indicator)

Supports:

.

Transition (back to the original indicator
\mathfrak{F}

The next two subsections pertain to the original indicator
\mathfrak{F}(\cdot,q)
without the periodic normalizer.
For comparison, the behaviour of
F
without tangent matching is recorded; the companion-zero phenomenon is quantified, whereas it is structurally eliminated for
\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}
by tangent matching at integers.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
q>1
and
p
be prime. Then

\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(p,q)\;=\;(q-1)q\,(\log q)\,q^{-p}\;>\;0.

In particular, the linear term of the correction factor determines the sign in a left neighbourhood of
p
.

Proof.

Write
\mathfrak{F}(x,q)=(q-1)q\big(S(x)-q^{-x}\big)
with
S(x)=\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\phi_{i}(x)
and
\phi_{i}(x)=F(x,i)/i^{2}
. For any integer
n
and
x=n+\eta
,

\sin(\pi x)=\pi\eta+O(\eta^{3})\hskip 10.00002pt\Rightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\sin^{2}(\pi x)=\pi^{2}\eta^{2}+O(\eta^{4}).

If
i\nmid n
, then
\sin(\pi n/i)\neq 0
, so
\csc^{2}(\pi x/i)
is real-analytic and bounded near
x=n
. Hence

\phi_{i}(x)=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi x)}{i^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi x/i)}=A_{i}\,\eta^{2}+O(\eta^{3})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptA_{i}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{i^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi n/i)},

and therefore
\phi_{i}^{\prime}(n)=0
. If
i\mid n
, then
n=i\ell
and

\sin\!\Big(\frac{\pi x}{i}\Big)=(-1)^{\ell}\Big(\frac{\pi\eta}{i}-\frac{\pi^{3}\eta^{3}}{6i^{3}}+O(\eta^{5}/i^{5})\Big),

so a direct expansion yields

\phi_{i}(x)=1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\Big(1-\frac{1}{i^{2}}\Big)\eta^{2}+O(\eta^{4}),

and again
\phi_{i}^{\prime}(n)=0
. By Lemma
F.9
with
k=1
, the derivative series converges uniformly on a neighborhood of
p
; hence termwise differentiation is justified and
S^{\prime}(p)=\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\phi_{i}^{\prime}(p)=0
. Since
\frac{d}{dx}(-q^{-x})=(\log q)\,q^{-x}
, it follows that

\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(p,q)=(q-1)q\Big(S^{\prime}(p)+(\log q)\,q^{-p}\Big)=(q-1)q\,(\log q)\,q^{-p}>0.

For the tangent-matched variant, Lemma
4.12
implies
(\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp})^{\prime}(p,q)=0
.
∎

E.1.
Normalization identity: details and auxiliary bounds

Three modifications are applied to the general form:

(1)

Weighting via FD-Lift:
Use the standard FD-Lift form
\mathcal{T}_{a}(z)=\sum_{i\geq 1}a(i)\,F(z,i)/i^{2}
with
a(i)=(q-1)q\,q^{-i}
.

(2)

Convergence and normalization:
This choice ensures absolute convergence for
|q|>1
and yields bounded contributions at integers; see Lemma
E.2
.

(3)

Correction term:
Subtract the term
(q-1)q\,q^{-z}
to remove the contribution of the improper divisor
d=n
in the evaluation at integers.

The role of the prefactor is a normalization: the upper envelope of the geometric contribution equals
\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}q^{-i}=\frac{1}{q(q-1)}
. Multiplication by
(q-1)q
therefore scales the summation part to the unit range, which is convenient for comparisons across
q
.

E.2.
Correction term and final definition of
\mathfrak{F}(z,q)

Let
\mathfrak{S}(z,q)
denote the normalized sum:
\mathfrak{S}(z,q)=(q-1)q\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}(\frac{1}{q})^{i}
. For a prime
p
, this function evaluates to the non-zero value
(q-1)q(1/q)^{p}
. To ensure primes are mapped to zero, a correction term is subtracted. This term is formulated with the complex variable
z
in the exponent to preserve the analyticity of the overall function across the complex plane.

In this subsection,
\mathfrak{F}(z,q)
refers to the function defined in Section
4.2
.

E.3.
Analyticity

Analyticity of
z\mapsto\mathfrak{F}(z,q)
for fixed
|q|>1
follows from uniform convergence on compacta via the Weierstrass M-test; details are provided in Appendix
B
.

The Normalization Property

Lemma E.2

(Normalization identity)
.

For

,

(q-1)q\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}q^{-i}=1.

Proof is immediate from the geometric series

.

.

Numerically it is observed that if

E.5.
Quantitative displacement of companion zeros

Let
q>1
be real and let
p\geq 5
be an odd prime. There exists at least one real zero

x_{p}(q)\in(p-1,p)

of
\mathfrak{F}(\cdot,q)
, referred to as a
companion zero
to
p
(i.e. a zero located in
(p-1,p)
). Set

\Delta_{p}(q):=p-x_{p}(q)\in(0,1].

The following result quantifies
\Delta_{p}(q)
and explains its exponential decay in

.

Definition E.4

(Companion zero)
.

Let
p
be a prime. A
companion zero
to
p
is a real zero of the indicator located in the window
(p-1,p)
. When the position matters, the term “left companion zero” is used interchangeably.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

At
x=p
,
\mathfrak{F}(p,q)=0
and by Lemma
E.1
,

\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(p,q)=(q-1)q(\log q)q^{-p}>0
. Hence
\mathfrak{F}(x,q)<0
for all
x\in(p-\varepsilon,p)
with
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

At the left endpoint,
x=p-1
, Lemma
F.6
gives

S(p-1)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid p-1\\
d\geq 2\end{subarray}}q^{-d}\ \geq\ q^{-2}.

Therefore,

\mathfrak{F}(p-1,q)=(q-1)q\big(S(p-1)-q^{-(p-1)}\big)\ \geq\ (q-1)q\big(q^{-2}-q^{-(p-1)}\big)\ >\ 0,

since
p-1\geq 4
. A sign change between
x=p-1
and a point
x\in(p-\varepsilon,p)
yields a zero in
(p-1,p)
by the intermediate value theorem.
∎

Lemma E.5

(Master Taylor bound)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3}]
. There exist
C_{\varepsilon}>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,\varepsilon]
such that for all
i\geq 2
and all
x
with
|x-m|\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,

\bigl|\phi_{i}^{(k)}(x)\bigr|\leq C_{\varepsilon}\hskip 20.00003pt(k=0,1,2,3),

where
\phi_{i}(x)=i^{-2}\left(\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\sin(\pi x/i)}\right)^{2}
.

Proof.

Write
x=m+\eta
. If
i\nmid m
, then
\sin(\pi m/i)\neq 0
and
\sin(\pi x/i)=\sin(\pi m/i)+O(\eta/i)
, so
\csc(\pi x/i)
and
\cot(\pi x/i)
stay
O(i)
on
|\eta|\leq\varepsilon_{0}
. Repeated differentiation of
\sin^{-2}(\pi x/i)
yields
O(i^{2})
factors, while
i^{-2}
in front cancels them; combined with boundedness of derivatives of
\sin^{2}(\pi x)
, Leibniz gives
|\phi_{i}^{(k)}(x)|\ll 1
for
k\leq 3
. If
i\mid m
, then
m=i\ell
and the Taylor expansion at
x=m
gives
\phi_{i}(x)=1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}(1-\frac{1}{i^{2}})\eta^{2}+O(\eta^{4})
, implying bounded derivatives up to order
3
. Uniform constants follow by shrinking
\varepsilon_{0}
if necessary.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

Set
S_{q}(x):=\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\phi_{i}(x)
and
H(\varepsilon):=S_{q}(p+\varepsilon)-q^{-(p+\varepsilon)}
. Lemma
F.6
gives
S_{q}(p)=q^{-p}
and
S_{q}^{\prime}(p)=0
. By Lemma
F.9
(with
k\leq 3
), the series for
S_{q}^{(k)}
converge uniformly on a neighbourhood of
p
, so
S_{q}
is
C^{3}
there and Taylor’s theorem with remainder applies:

H(\varepsilon)=a\,\varepsilon+b\,\varepsilon^{2}+R_{3}(\varepsilon),

with

a=(\log q)\,q^{-p},\hskip 20.00003ptb=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(S_{q}^{\prime\prime}(p)-(\log q)^{2}\,q^{-p}\Big)=K(q,p),

and
|R_{3}(\varepsilon)|\leq C(q)\,|\varepsilon|^{3}
for
|\varepsilon|\leq\varepsilon_{0}(q)
. Lemma
4.13
shows
b
is bounded above and below by positive constants depending only on
q>1
.

At
x=p-1
,
\mathfrak{F}(p-1,q)>0
(Theorem
4.4
); for
0<-\varepsilon\ll 1
,
\mathfrak{F}(p+\varepsilon,q)<0
by Lemma
E.1
. Hence a zero
\varepsilon_{\star}\in(-1,0)
exists. The quadratic approximation
a\varepsilon+b\varepsilon^{2}=0
has root
\varepsilon_{0}=-a/b
, and
|\varepsilon_{0}|=O(q^{-p})
. A standard perturbation estimate using
|R_{3}(\varepsilon_{0})|\leq C(q)|\varepsilon_{0}|^{3}
yields

\varepsilon_{\star}=\varepsilon_{0}\Big(1+O(|\varepsilon_{0}|)\Big)\ =\ -\frac{(\log q)\,q^{-p}}{K(q,p)}\Big(1+O(q^{-p})\Big).

Therefore
\Delta_{p}(q)=-\varepsilon_{\star}=\frac{\log q}{K(q,p)}\,q^{-p}\big(1+O(q^{-p})\big)
, uniformly in

, and
x_{p}(q)=p-\Delta_{p}(q)\in(p-1,p)
.
This proves Proposition
4.5
.
∎

Remark E.6

(Standing assumption)
.

In what follows, bounds for
K(q,p)
use the fact that, for a prime
p
, the only divisor
i\geq 2
with
i\mid p
is
i=p
. Consequently, among the terms in
\sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(p)
at most a single negative contribution arises, namely the

term.

Proof of Lemma
4.13
.

From Lemma
F.8
, for
i\nmid p
,

\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(p)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{i^{2}\sin^{2}(\pi p/i)}\ \leq\ \frac{\pi^{2}}{2},

using
\sin(\pi/i)\geq 2/i
. If
i\mid p
and
i\geq 2
, then

\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(p)=-\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}\Big(1-\frac{1}{i^{2}}\Big)\geq-\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}.

For
p
prime, the only divisor
i\geq 2
with
i\mid p
is
i=p
; hence among the terms with
i\geq 2
there is exactly one possible negative contribution, namely the
i=p
term, weighted by
q^{-p}
. All other terms (
i\nmid p
) are nonnegative and
\leq\pi^{2}/2
. The explicit contributions at

equal
\pi^{2}/2
and
8\pi^{2}/27
. For

the generic bound

yields the displayed terms. The tail

Since

contains the factor

outside the sum, the tail contributes at most

\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{q^{-7}}{1-1/q}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\,\frac{q^{-7}}{1-1/q}.

Finally, subtract

and note

for

to obtain the lower bound. This shows that

is bounded below by a positive
c_{1}(q)
and above by
c_{2}(q)
, both independent of

.
∎

Theorem E.9

(Uniqueness of the companion zero for the original indicator)
.

Let
q>1
and
p\geq 5
be an odd prime. Assume there exists
\alpha\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
such that

(L0)

\displaystyle\cos^{2}\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi\alpha}{2}\Big)\

\displaystyle\geq\ q^{-2},

(M0)

\displaystyle\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha)\!\!\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{q^{-i}}{i^{2}}\

\displaystyle\geq\ q^{-4-\alpha}.

Define

T_{3}(q,\alpha)\;:=\;\sup_{|x-p|\leq\alpha}\ \sum_{i\geq 2}q^{-i}\,\big|\phi_{i}^{(3)}(x)\big|\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptK(q,p)\;:=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(S^{\prime\prime}(p)-(\log q)^{2}q^{-p}\Big).

Assume in addition the explicit convexity condition

(C0)

\alpha\ \leq\ \frac{K(q,p)}{\,T_{3}(q,\alpha)\;+\;(\log q)^{3}\,q^{-p}\,}.

Then
\mathfrak{F}(\cdot,q)
has exactly one real zero in
(p-1,p)
.

Proof.

Write
\mathfrak{F}(x,q)=(q-1)q\big(S(x)-q^{-x}\big)
and split
(p-1,p)
into
I_{\mathrm{L}},I_{\mathrm{M}},I_{\mathrm{R}}
with the same
\alpha
as above. Under (
L0
) and (
M0
), Lemmas
F.14
–
F.15
give
\mathfrak{F}(x,q)>0
on
I_{\mathrm{L}}\cup I_{\mathrm{M}}
. By Lemma
E.1
,
\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(p,q)>0
, hence
\mathfrak{F}(x,q)<0
for
x
sufficiently close to
p
on the left.

To exclude more than one zero, it suffices to prove strict convexity on
I_{\mathrm{R}}=[p-\alpha,p)
. Differentiating twice yields

\mathfrak{F}^{\prime\prime}(x,q)=(q-1)q\Big(S^{\prime\prime}(x)-(\log q)^{2}q^{-x}\Big).

A first-order Taylor bound around
x=p
gives

S^{\prime\prime}(x)\ \geq\ S^{\prime\prime}(p)\ -\ \sup_{|t-p|\leq\alpha}\,|S^{(3)}(t)|\cdot|x-p|\ \geq\ S^{\prime\prime}(p)-T_{3}(q,\alpha)\,\alpha,

using

and the locally uniform summability of

Lemma F.15

(Left and middle lower bounds)

Fix an odd prime

Proof of Theorem
4.8
.

Partition
(p-1,p)

I_{\mathrm{L}}

I_{\mathrm{M}}
, and

Right window.
By Lemma

and
\alpha\leq\delta_{\sin}(q)

\mathfrak{F}^{\sharp}(x,q)\geq(q-1)q\,K_{0}(q)\,(p-x)^{2}>0

Left window.

S(x)\geq q^{-2}\cos^{2}(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2})

Proof of Proposition
6.2
.

For
0<r<1
set

\mathfrak{S}_{r}(z)=2\sum_{i\geq 2}(-r)^{i}\,\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}},\hskip 20.00003pt\mathfrak{F}_{r}(z)=\mathfrak{S}_{r}(z)-2\,e^{-\,\mathrm{i}\pi z}.

Let
\phi_{\infty}(z):=(\sin(\pi z)/(\pi z))^{2}
with
\phi_{\infty}(0)=1
. On compact
z
-sets,

\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}-\phi_{\infty}(z)=O(i^{-2})\hskip 20.00003pt(i\to\infty),

since
\sin(\pi z/i)=(\pi z/i)+O(i^{-3})
uniformly in
z
, hence

\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}=\left(\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z}\right)^{2}+O(i^{-2})=\phi_{\infty}(z)+O(i^{-2}).

Therefore,

2\sum_{i\geq 2}(-r)^{i}\Big(\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}-\phi_{\infty}(z)\Big)

converges absolutely and locally uniformly up to
r\uparrow 1
by comparison with
\sum i^{-2}
. Moreover,

2\sum_{i\geq 2}(-r)^{i}\,\phi_{\infty}(z)=2\,\phi_{\infty}(z)\,\frac{r^{2}}{1+r}\ \xrightarrow{\,r\uparrow 1\,}\ \phi_{\infty}(z).

Thus the Abel limits exist locally uniformly and

\mathfrak{S}(z,-1)=\sum_{i\geq 2}(-1)^{i}\Big(\frac{F(z,i)}{i^{2}}-\phi_{\infty}(z)\Big)+\phi_{\infty}(z),

is entire; hence so is

\mathfrak{F}(z,-1)=\mathfrak{S}(z,-1)-2e^{-\,\mathrm{i}\pi z}.

The conjugation symmetry follows from
F(-z,i)=F(z,i)
and real weights
(-1)^{i}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
6.24
.

Write
q=-Q
with
Q>1
. For
\Re s>1
,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathfrak{S}(n,-Q)}{n^{s}}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{s}}\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{(q-1)q}{i^{2}}q^{-i}F(n,i)=(q-1)q\Big(\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{q^{-i}}{i^{s}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{s}}\Big),

where absolute convergence allows Fubini and the Fejér filter identity
F(n,i)/i^{2}=\mathbf{1}_{i\mid n}
has been used. With
q=-Q

(q-1)q=Q(Q+1)

\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{q^{-i}}{i^{s}}=\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{(-1)^{i}Q^{-i}}{i^{s}}=\operatorname{Li}_{s}(-1/Q)-(-1)Q^{-1}=-\eta_{Q}(s)+Q^{-1}.
