Alpha-lipoic acid pretreatment attenuates TNF-α-triggered auditory hair cell damage via Nrf2 signaling-mediated antioxidant activity
Authors: Junyeong Yi, Myungjoo Shim, Jhang Ho Pak, Jong Woo Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExcessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and proinflammatory cytokines is a pivotal mechanism causing sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) through damage to auditory hair cells. In particular, high expression levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were observed in SNHL. Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) has demonstrated protective effects against cochlear hair cell damage and hearing deterioration. This study investigated the protective effect of ALA against TNF-α-triggered ototoxicity in auditory hair cells and in a noise-induced hearing loss mouse model. Cells were pretreated with ALA prior to TNF-α treatment, after which cell viability, ROS generation, and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)-mediated antioxidant activity were assessed. In addition, we observed the hearing levels, the number of outer hair cells (OHCs), and Nrf2-related protein expression in ALA-pretreated mice before noise exposure. ALA pretreatment significantly enhanced cell viability, suppressed ROS production, and activated Nrf2-mediated antioxidant activity compared with TNF-α treatment alone. In the animal model, noise-exposed mice exhibited elevated TNF-α expression in cochlear tissues. ALA pretreatment decreased the auditory brainstem response threshold and reduced OHCs damage. Furthermore, ALA administration upregulated Nrf2-related antioxidant protein expression in cochlear tissues. Taken together, these findings suggest that ALA pretreatment confers cytoprotective effects against TNF-α-triggered auditory hair cell damage through activation of the Nrf2 signaling-mediated antioxidant activity.Keywords: Alpha-lipoic acid, Auditory hair cells, Noise-induced hearing loss, Nrf2 signaling, Tumor necrosis factor-alpha

INTRODUCTION

Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) is caused by damaged auditory nerves or hair cells through various factors such as viral infection, ototoxicity, and noise exposure. Dysregulation of oxidative stress and expression of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6) are critical cellular mechanisms causing SNHL (1, 2). In particular, excessive levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) trigger the release of proinflammatory cytokines, which can further damage auditory hair cells. Another common feature observed in SNHL is increased expression of TNF-α (3, 4), which reduces blood flow in the cochlea and induces apoptosis by activating the ROS-mediated signaling pathway (5, 6). In animal models, blockage of TNF-α signaling using inhibitors has been shown to restore cochlear blood flow and protect hearing from noise exposure (7, 8).

Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) is an antioxidant that plays a vital role in mitochondrial activities and regulates oxidative stress through reactions with ROS (9). ALA can react with fat- and water-soluble substances and neutralize intracellular and extracellular ROS. Regarding its ROS-scavenging role, several studies have reported that pretreatment with ALA reduces cisplatin-induced ROS generation in auditory cells (10, 11). In response to oxidative stress, ALA activates nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling (12). ALA also regulates the secretion of inflammatory cytokines in numerous cell types by inhibiting nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling. Some studies have shown that ALA plays a role in anti-inflammation and antioxidants by Nrf2 signaling activation (13, 14).

Nrf2 signaling is a crucial defense mechanism in response to oxidative damage by regulating the expression of antioxidant genes. Under unstressed conditions, the repressor protein Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) blocks the transcriptional activity of Nrf2 through binding with Nrf2, and proteasomes degrade ubiquitinated Nrf2 in the cytoplasm. However, under oxidative stress conditions, Keap1-dependent Nrf2 ubiquitination decreases. Therefore, these accumulated Nrf2 translocate to the nucleus, bind to antioxidant-responsive elements, and activates antioxidant-related transcription factors, such as glutamate-cysteine ligase (GCLC), heme oxygenase, and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen generation (15). Under oxidative stress conditions, Nrf2 also increases the level of intracellular antioxidant, glutathione (GSH), by synthesizing endogenous antioxidant enzymes (16). GSH is a reduced form, whereas its oxidized form is glutathione disulfide (GSSG) (17). The GSSG/GSH ratio is an indicator of the redox state of cells and is known to increase under oxidative stress conditions.

Most previous studies have focused on the protective effect of ALA against cisplatin-induced ototoxicity. However, the protective effect of ALA against TNF-α, which is generally highly expressed in SNHL, and the associated mechanisms are not completely elucidated. Therefore, we conducted this study to investigate the effects of ALA pretreatment in TNF-α-treated mouse auditory hair cells (HEI-OC1) and a noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) mouse model. We also examined whether the protective effect of ALA was associated with the Nrf2 signaling-mediated antioxidant activity.

RESULTS

Effects of ALA on cell viability and ROS generation in HEI-OC1 cells

We previously reported that 10 ng/ml TNF-α triggered cytotoxicity and ROS generation in auditory hair cells (18). To investigate the cytotoxicity of ALA in auditory hair cells (HEI-OC1), the cells were treated with 0.1-4 mM ALA for 24 h. As shown in Fig. 1A, ALA treatment markedly decreased cell viability in a dose-dependent manner; however, no significant differences were observed at relatively lower ALA concentrations (0.1-1 mM) compared with the untreated control. Next, the effects of ALA on intracellular ROS generation were measured using a fluorescence dye, CM-H2DCFDA. Compared with that in the untreated control, exposure to 2 or 4 mM ALA increased ROS generation by ∼1.56- or ∼1.70-fold, respectively (Fig. 1B). These results suggest that treatment with less than 2 mM ALA does not affect either cell viability or ROS generation. Most previous studies used 1 mM ALA to demonstrate the protective effect of ALA in HEI-OC1 cells (11, 19). Considering these results, we used 1 mM ALA and 10 ng/ml TNF-α in our subsequent experiments in the present study.

Cell viability (A) and ROS production (B) were measured in ALA-treated cells. All data are expressed as mean ± SEM of three independent experiments. **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, *: compared with untreated control.

Pretreatment effects of ALA on cell viability, ROS generation, and Nrf2 signaling in TNF-α-treated HEI-OC1 cells

To examine the protective effect of ALA against TNF-α-induced cytotoxicity, cells were pretreated with 1 mM ALA for 1 h and then incubated for 24 h in 10 ng/ml TNF-α. ALA pretreatment markedly increased the cell viability by ∼70.2% compared with that of TNF-α-treated cells, which showed 52.3% cell viability (Fig. 2A). Moreover, the increased ROS levels in TNF-α-treated cells significantly decreased by ∼1.28-fold in ALA-pretreated cells (Fig. 2B). These results indicated that ALA has a protective effect against TNF-α-induced damage to auditory hair cells.

(C) Immunoblot images and quantification graphs of Nrf2 signaling-related proteins in cells fractionated into the cytoplasm and nucleus. We next investigated whether the protective effect of ALA is associated with Nrf2 signaling. Cells were fractionated into cytosol and nucleus fractions, and Nrf2 signaling-related protein expression levels were evaluated by western blotting. As shown in Fig. 2C, ALA treatment alone reduced cytosolic Nrf2 and Keap1 expression levels while increasing nuclear Nrf2 expression. In contrast, TNF-α-treated cells exhibited no significant differences in cytosolic or nuclear Nrf2 expression compared with that in the untreated control. The Keap1 expression levels in TNF-α-treated cells significantly increased by ∼1.80-fold compared with that in the untreated control. Cells pretreated with ALA showed markedly decreased Keap1 expression levels compared with TNF-α-treated cells. The ALA-pretreated cells also showed decreased cytosolic Nrf2 expression levels and increased nuclear Nrf2 expression levels compared with the untreated control and TNF-α-treated cells, indicating that ALA pretreatment triggered Nrf2 signaling. These findings suggested that ALA attenuated the TNF-α-induced cytotoxicity through the activation of Nrf2 signaling.

Pretreatment effect of ALA on Nrf2-related antioxidant activity in TNF-α-treated HEI-OC1 cells

Next, mRNA expression levels were measured by RT-qPCR to investigate whether ALA pretreatment activates Nrf2-target antioxidant genes. As shown in Fig. 3A, the mRNA expression levels of Sod1, Gclc, Nqo1, and Hmox1 were significantly increased in cells treated with ALA alone, whereas no changes were observed in TNF-α-treated cells compared with those in untreated control. ALA pretreatment significantly elevated these mRNA expression levels compared with those in untreated or TNF-α-only treated cells. Similarly, protein expression analysis revealed increased levels of SOD1, γ-GCSc, NQO1, and HO-1 in cells treated with ALA alone. In contrast, TNF-α-treated cells exhibited expression levels comparable to those of the untreated control. These protein expression levels were markedly elevated in ALA-pretreated cells compared with those in TNF-α-treated cells (Fig. 3B). Nrf2 also increases GSH expression by increasing the activity of GSH-synthesizing enzymes. The GSSG/GSH ratio decreased by 2.25-fold in ALA-treated cells, whereas it increased by 5.56-fold in TNF-α-treated cells compared with the untreated control. Pretreatment with ALA significantly decreased the GSSG/GSH ratio compared with that in TNF-α-treated cells (Fig. 3C). Taken together, these results indicated that ALA pretreatment triggered the Nrf2-related antioxidant activities to protect against TNF-α-induced cytotoxicity.

ALA pretreatment protects against noise-induced hearing loss in mice

The expression levels of TNF-α in mice exposed to noise were examined, for which the cochlea was stained with an anti-TNF-α antibody. Compared with that in control mice, the expression levels of TNF-α significantly increased in the cochlea of noise-exposed mice (Fig. 4A). We next measured the hearing levels using auditory brainstem response (ABR) tests to examine the effects of ALA treatment on hearing. No significant differences in the ABR threshold were observed between the control and ALA-treated groups at any frequency, indicating that the ALA injection concentration used in this study did not affect hearing levels. However, the ABR threshold significantly increased in the noise-exposed groups compared with that in the control group at all frequencies. The ALA plus noise group showed a markedly decreased ABR threshold compared with that in the noise groups (Fig. 4B). Finally, we stained the hair cells to investigate whether ALA protects against the hair cell damage caused by noise exposure. Fig. 4C shows the disrupted arrangement of OHCs at all turns of the cochlea in the noise groups. Moreover, the number of OHCs significantly decreased in the noise groups compared with that in the control group at all turns of the cochlea. However, the loss of OHCs significantly decreased in the ALA plus noise group, suggesting that ALA pretreatment protects hearing by reducing noise-induced hair cell damage.

ALA pretreatment increased Nrf2-related antioxidant protein expression in the cochlea of mice

We next performed immunohistochemical analysis to investigate Nrf2-related antioxidant protein expression in the cochlear tissues of mice. The noise groups showed similar expression levels of Nrf2, SOD1, and HO-1 to those in the control group. However, the ALA plus noise group showed significantly elevated expression levels of these proteins in the spiral ganglion (SG), organ of Corti (OC), and stria vascularis (SV) compared with those in the noise groups (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, high-magnification images of OC showed a high expression of these proteins in the inner hair cells (IHCs) and OHCs (Fig. 5B). These results showed that pretreatment with ALA triggered the Nrf2-related antioxidant protein expression in the cochlea of mice to protect against noise-induced damage.

DISCUSSION

Oxidative stress has been postulated to be a potent pathogenesis of SNHL, and various antioxidants (e.g., N-acetylcysteine, vitamin E, and sodium thiosulfate) have been suggested as treatment strategies (1, 20). Furthermore, the crosstalk between ROS and proinflammatory cytokines is known to cause further damage to auditory hair cells. We previously demonstrated that TNF-α triggers apoptosis signaling and disrupts the auditory hair cells in cochlear explants (18). ALA has been reported to decrease the TNF-α-induced NF-κB activation or apoptosis in various types of cells (21, 22). However, the effects of ALA on TNF-α-mediated hearing loss and the associated molecular mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. Therefore, this study investigated the pretreatment effects of ALA against TNF-α-triggered hair cell damage in HEI-OC1 cells and the NIHL mouse model.

Several studies have shown that noise exposure elevated the ABR threshold and TNF-α mRNA levels in the cochlea of rats (4, 23). In addition, increased TNF-α expression was detected in the cochlear tissues of vibration-stimulated guinea pigs (3). Similar to these results, we observed elevated TNF-α expression in the cochlear tissues of noise-exposed mice (Fig. 4A). Disrupted stereocilium bundles and loss of hair cells were observed in TNF-α-treated cochlear explants (18, 24). However, the inhibition of TNF-α protected hearing levels and cochlear structures (7, 25). For instance, the administration of TNF-α inhibitors to loud noise-exposed guinea pigs resulted in a decreased ABR threshold compared with that in the control group (7). These results indicate that TNF-α plays a critical role in causing damage to hair cells, subsequently resulting in hearing loss.

ALA, known as an antioxidant, plays a critical role in scavenging ROS. We previously reported that ALA-fed mice preserved their hearing in an age-related hearing impairment mouse model (26). Many studies have demonstrated that treatment with 1 mM ALA does not affect cell viability and ROS production in HEI-OC1 cells (11, 19). Consistent with these results, we observed that cell viability and the level of ROS generation were not changed with exposure to 1 mM ALA (Fig. 1). Pretreatment of 1 mM ALA increased cell viability and decreased ROS generation in cisplatin-treated HEI-OC1 cells (11, 19). Moreover, there was a decrease in the mRNA expression levels of cisplatin-induced proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and interferon-γ) (10). Similar to these results, we showed that 1 mM ALA pretreatment restored cell viability and reduced ROS generation in TNF-α-treated cells (Fig. 2A, B). However, treatment with 2 mM ALA increased ROS generation, and pretreatment with 2 mM ALA had no protective effects in TNF-α-treated cells (e.g., under 10 ng/ml) (data not shown). These findings suggest that the protective effects of ALA against TNF-α-induced damage may be influenced by its cytotoxicity at higher concentrations. In animal models, the injection of ALA preserved OHCs, SV, and spiral ganglion neurons in the cochlea of aging mice (27). Han et al. demonstrated that ALA injection after noise exposure decreased the ABR threshold in mice (28). ALA pretreatment protected IHCs and OHCs from cisplatin-induced hair cell loss in OC explants (10, 19). Consistent with these results, pretreatment with ALA in mice decreased the ABR threshold and the number of damaged OHCs by noise exposure (Fig. 4B, C). Taken together, these findings suggest that pretreatment of ALA has a protective effect on the TNF-α-triggered hair cell damage.

Nrf2 signaling is a critical defense mechanism for protecting cells against oxidative stress by inducing the expression of antioxidant genes such as NQO1, GCLC, and SOD1. Hoshino et al. reported that aged Nrf2-knockout mice showed a higher ABR threshold and faster loss rate of hair cells and SG cells than wild-type mice (29). In the present study, we found that Nrf2 signaling was activated in ALA-pretreated cells (Fig. 2C). ALA pretreatment increased the mRNA and protein expression levels of HO-1 and SOD1 and activated Nrf2 signaling in cisplatin-treated HEI-OC1 cells (10). Furthermore, intraperitoneal administration of CDDO-lm, an activator of NRF2, increased the mRNA expression levels of Nqo1, Ho-1, and Gclc in the mice cochlea (30). Consistent with these results, we observed that Nrf2-target antioxidant gene and protein expression levels increased in ALA-pretreated cells and mice (Figs. 3A, B and 5). Nrf2-mediated antioxidant enzymes increase GSH synthesis. GSH plays a vital role in protecting cells against toxicity or oxidative stress. Excessive oxidative stress results in depleted cellular GSH and increased GSSG levels, leading to an increased intracellular GSSG/GSH ratio (31). Kim et al. reported that pretreatment or posttreatment with ALA decreased the GSSG/GSH ratio in cisplatin-treated cells (11). In animal models, injection of GSH after noise exposure decreased the ABR threshold and loss of hair cells in GSH-deficient guinea pigs (32). Similar to these results, we showed that ALA pretreatment decreased the GSSG/GSH ratio compared with that in TNF-α-treated cells (Fig. 3C). Taken together, these findings suggested that ALA pretreatment protected the auditory hair cells against TNF-α-triggered damage through Nrf2-mediated antioxidant activity.

In conclusion, ALA pretreatment plays a role in attenuating TNF-α-triggered hair cell damage by upregulating Nrf2 signaling-mediated antioxidant activities in both auditory hair cells and the NIHL mouse model. These findings contribute toward understanding the protective effect of ALA with associated molecular mechanisms in TNF-α-triggered ototoxicity and provide a basis for designing therapeutics to prevent NIHL.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Recombinant TNF-α was purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA) and ALA was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The following polyclonal antibodies were used in this study: TNF-α (ab6671; Abcam, Cambridge, UK); Nrf2 (NBP1-32822; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA); Keap1 (sc-514914), Lamin B (sc-374015), Superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1; sc-101523), Gamma-GCSc (γ-GCSc; sc-390811), NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1; sc-32793), HO-1 (sc-390991), and Myosin 7a (sc-74516; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA); and GAPDH (A300-641A) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Bethyl Laboratories, Inc., Montgomery, TX, USA).

Cell culture and treatment

The immortalized mouse auditory hair cell line (HEI-OC1) was kindly provided by Dr. Federico Kalinec (House Ear Institute, Los Angeles, CA, USA). HEI-OC1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium containing 10% FBS at 33°C in a 10% CO2 atmosphere. For TNF-α or ALA treatment, the cultured cells were incubated in a serum-free medium for 3 h and treated with TNF-α or ALA. In ALA pretreatment experiments, serum-starved cells were treated with 1 mM ALA for 1 h before treatment with 10 ng/ml TNF-α and then incubated for 24 h.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using a D-PlusTM CCK kit purchased from Dongin LS (Seoul, Korea). Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. After serum starvation for 3 h, the cells were treated with ALA or ALA plus TNF-α as described above. Absorbance values were read at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices Corp., Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

ROS measurement

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using a fluorescent dye obtained from Invitrogen (CM-H2DCFDA, Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were washed twice with Hank’s balanced salt solution and incubated for 20 min with 5 μM CM-H2DCFDA. Then, the fluorescence values were detected using a Perkin-Elmer VICTOR3 spectrometer (Perkin-Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Cytosol and nuclear fractionation

Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were obtained using the NE-PERTM Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents kits obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were lysed with CER I and CER II lysis buffer and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C to separate the cytoplasm (supernatant) and nucleus (pellet). Nuclear pellets were incubated with ice-cold NER buffer for 40 min and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C to obtain a soluble nuclear extract.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) containing a phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktail. Proteins were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and then separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate−polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and blocked with 5% skim milk in TBS-T (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween 20). The membranes were incubated overnight with appropriate primary antibodies at 4°C, washed three times with TBS-T, and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h. After washing three times with TBS-T, the membranes were treated with enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagents (Dongin LS), and protein bands were detected using an Image-Quant LAS 500 biomolecular imager (GE Healthcare Biosciences).

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNeasy Mini Kits purchased from QIAGEN (Hilden, Germany). cDNA was synthesized from 100 ng of RNA using an AMPIGENE cDNA Synthesis kit (ENZO, Farmingdale, NY, USA). Next, qPCR was performed using a TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (TaKaRa Bio, Inc., Kusatsu, Japan) with specific primers. The PCR conditions were as follows: 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 15 s. Gapdh was used as the reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. The primers used for quantitative PCR were as follows: Sod1 (forward, GGTGAACCAGTTGTGTTGTCAG; reverse, ATGAGGTCCTGCACTGGTACAG), Gclc (forward, ACACCTGGATGATGCCAACGAG; reverse, CCTCCATTGGTCGGAACTCTAC), Nqo1 (forward, GCCGAACACAAGAAGCTGGAAG; reverse, GGCAAATCCTGCTACGAGCACT), Hmox1 (forward, CACTCTGGAGATGACACCTGAG; reverse, GTGTTCCTCTGTCAGCATCACC), and Gapdh (forward, TGCACCACCAACTGCTTAGC; reverse, GGATGCAGGGATGATGTTCT).

Measurement of GSH and GSSG levels

The levels of GSH and GSSG were measured using a GSH/GSSG Ratio Detection Assay Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were lysed in a 1× mammalian lysis buffer (0.5% NP-40/phosphate-buffered saline [PBS]) and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min. Trichloroacetic acid was added to supernatant, incubated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min. Sodium bicarbonate was added to neutralize the collected supernatant to pH 4-6. The supernatant was incubated with the agent (GSH assay mixture or total glutathione assay mixture) for 15 min in the dark, after which fluorescence was measured using a Perkin-Elmer VICTOR 3 spectrometer (Perkin-Elmer).

Animals and experimental design

All animal experiments were approved by the Committee on Use and Care of Animals of the University of Ulsan (IACUC No. 2023-20-099; approval date, April 19, 2023). The animal experiments and care were performed according to the National Institutes of Health guidelines under the supervision of the Laboratory Animal Unit of the Asan Institute for Life Sciences. Male CBA/N mice (4 weeks old, 15-20 g) were purchased from Central Laboratory Animal Inc. (Seoul, Korea). Mice were divided into the following four groups: control, noise, ALA, and ALA plus noise. The control and noise groups were injected with 0.9% saline, and all ALA groups were injected with ALA (100 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal (IP) administration. The ALA plus noise group was injected with ALA, followed by exposure to noise the next day.

Noise exposure and hearing test

Mice were placed in individual cages and located below a loudspeaker inside the chamber. White noise was amplified by an INTER-M R300 plus power amplifier (Inter-M, Seoul, Korea) and delivered to the speakers. Mice were exposed to the white noise for 2 h at 110-dB SPL, and hearing levels were measured before and the next day after noise exposure.

An ABR workstation (Tucker-Davis Technologies, Alachua, FL, USA) was used for measuring the hearing levels as described previously (33). Briefly, mice were anesthetized with alfaxalone (60 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). To measure the ABR, the tone-burst stimulus was generated with 1-ms rise/fall times and 2-ms plateaus at frequencies of 4, 8, 16, and 32 kHz. After measuring the ABR threshold, the mice were heavily anesthetized, and the cochlea was isolated and dissected.

Hair cell staining

The dissected cochlea was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% normal goat serum. It was then incubated overnight with the primary antibody (anti-myosin 7a, 1:100), followed by incubation with the Alexa Fluor 564 goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:200, Invitrogen). Finally, the cochlea was mounted using a mounting solution and visualized with a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The number of outer hair cells (OHCs) was counted per 160 μμ in the apical, middle, and basal turn of the cochlea, respectively.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed using a Vectastain Elite ABC-HRP (rabbit IgG) kit (Vector Laboratories, Inc., Burlingame, CA) or Mouse on Mouse polymer IHC kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 5-μm-thick cochlear paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and heated in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The sections were treated with a normal blocking solution, incubated overnight with primary antibodies (1:100 dilution), and subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies (mouse or rabbit). After three washes in PBS, the sections were treated with the VECTASTAIN Elite ABC reagent. Finally, the sections were treated with DAB staining solution using ImmPACTTM DAB Peroxidase Substrate kit (Vector Laboratories, Inc.), counterstained with hematoxylin solution, and mounted using the mounting solution. Stained images were observed under an upright microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are represented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was conducted using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance with post hoc Tukey’s test in the SigmaPlot 12.0 program (Jandel Scientific., San Rafael, CA, USA). Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05.
