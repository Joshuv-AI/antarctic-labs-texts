Causal effects of gut microbiota on periodontitis risk: a meta-analysis of Mendelian randomization studies
Authors: Jianfeng Yu, Yuling Zuo, Lina Yang, Qianrong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the causal relationship between gut microbiota and periodontitis (PD) by synthesizing Mendelian randomization (MR) studies across taxonomic levels.MethodsWe systematically searched five databases to November 2024 for MR studies linking gut microbiota (Order/Family/Genus/Species) to PD. Study-level odds ratios (ORs) were pooled with random-effects models; small-study effects were explored; and multiplicity was controlled with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)—within each taxonomic level (primary) and globally across all taxa (sensitivity). Reporting and certainty were appraised using STROBE-MR and a GRADE framework adapted for MR. Meta-analyses used Stata 15.1.ResultsSix MR studies were included. Enterobacterales (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.17–1.44) and Pasteurellales (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.04–1.21) remained significant after within-level FDR. At the genus/species levels, Granulicatella and Prevotella salivae were risk-increasing, whereas Butyricicoccus and Ruminiclostridium 6 were protective (significant within-level FDR; suggestive under global FDR). Across taxa combined, the pooled association indicated increased PD risk (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.11–1.26).ConclusionsThis meta-analysis supports a causal contribution of specific gut taxa to PD risk. Risk-increasing Orders (Enterobacterales, Pasteurellales) and risk-modifying genera/species (Granulicatella, Prevotella salivae; Butyricicoccus, Ruminiclostridium 6) may inform hypothesis-driven prevention and mechanistic studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06992-2.Keywords: Periodontitis, Gut microbiota, Mendelian randomization, Meta-Analysis, Causality, Microbial markers

Introduction

Periodontitis (PD) is one of the most common oral diseases worldwide. It is characterized by bleeding gums, clinical attachment loss, alveolar bone resorption, and periodontal pocket formation, which may ultimately lead to tooth mobility or even loss [1, 2]. As a chronic, irreversible, and destructive inflammatory disease, periodontitis severely affects chewing function, aesthetics, and mental health, significantly reducing quality of life. It is also closely linked to various systemic diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic lower respiratory diseases, and Alzheimer’s disease [1–4]. According to statistics, approximately 50% of adults worldwide are affected by mild periodontitis, with severe periodontitis becoming the sixth-largest epidemic. Its prevalence increases significantly with age [5]. The economic burden of periodontitis constitutes a significant portion of the global economic burden of oral diseases, highlighting its substantial socio-economic and medical impact [5].

Although existing studies have identified various risk factors associated with periodontitis, including smoking, alcohol consumption, poor oral hygiene, and genetic susceptibility, controlling these factors has not been enough to reduce the high incidence of periodontitis [6]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify and validate additional modifiable risk factors to develop more effective prevention and intervention strategies. In this context, the gut microbiota, as the largest microbial habitat in the human body, has attracted widespread attention in recent years due to its crucial role in metabolic and immune functions [7]. The gut microbiota influences several physiological processes, including bone metabolism, by regulating intestinal metabolites and immune responses, suggesting that it may play an important role in the onset and progression of periodontitis [8, 9].

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have focused on the bidirectional communication pathway of the “gut-oral axis,” which refers to the interaction between the gut and oral microbial communities and may play a key role in periodontitis and related systemic diseases [10, 11]. Specifically, gut dysbiosis may indirectly promote the development of periodontitis by affecting systemic inflammation and immune regulation. Conversely, the circulation of oral pathogens and inflammatory mediators caused by periodontitis may also impact overall health through changes in the gut microbiome [10, 11]. However, most current studies are observational, making it difficult to establish a causal relationship between gut microbiota and periodontitis, with potential confounding factors and reverse causality interference [11].

Mendelian Randomization (MR) is a method that uses genetic variation as an instrumental variable to assess the causal relationship between exposure factors and disease outcomes. It effectively avoids confounding bias and reverse causality issues common in traditional observational studies, and has been widely applied in epidemiological research in recent years [12]. In particular, MR analyses based on large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data provide a powerful tool for exploring the potential causal mechanisms of complex diseases [12]. Regarding the relationship between gut microbiota and periodontitis, although preliminary evidence suggests an association, there is a lack of systematic studies evaluating the causal effects between them.

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of Mendelian randomization (MR) studies to evaluate the causal effect of genetically proxied gut microbiota on periodontitis (PD) risk across taxonomic levels (Order, Family, Genus, Species). Our primary objective was to estimate pooled effects using random-effects models. Prespecified secondary objectives were to: (i) control multiplicity with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)—within-level as the primary analysis and global as a sensitivity analysis; (ii) appraise reporting quality and certainty using MR-specific tools (the STROBE-MR checklist and a GRADE framework adapted for MR); and (iii) summarize pleiotropy and instrument-strength diagnostics reported in the source MR studies (e.g., MR-Egger intercepts, MR-PRESSO, instrument counts/F statistics). This aggregation aimed to provide more precise estimates and to inform prevention and management strategies.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (registration ID: CRD420251128261). The protocol prespecified the research question (PICO), eligibility criteria, data items, primary and secondary outcomes, and the synthesis plan (random-effects pooling, heterogeneity assessment, and multiple-testing control). Reporting followed PRISMA and STROBE-MR guidance. A completed PRISMA 2020 checklist, indicating the page number where each item is addressed in the manuscript, is provided as Additional file 1.

Retrieval strategy

This study strictly followed the guidelines for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) to systematically assess the causal effects of gut microbiota on periodontitis risk by aggregating existing Mendelian Randomization (MR) studies [13, 14]. To ensure the comprehensiveness and scientific rigor of the study, we conducted extensive literature searches across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang Database. The search covered the period from the establishment of each database to November 2024, with no language restrictions. The search strategy combined keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), including terms like “gut microbiome”, “periodontitis”, “Mendelian randomization”, “causality”, and “GWAS”. Boolean operators “AND”, “OR”, and “NOT” were used to combine these keywords, ensuring that the search results included relevant topics. Additionally, we supplemented the search with other sources such as reference screening, conference papers, and dissertations to maximize the inclusion of all relevant studies and minimize search omissions.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

This study included all studies using Mendelian randomization, comprising both two-sample MR and multi-sample MR studies. The exposure factor was the composition of the gut microbiome, including different taxonomic levels (e.g., phylum, class, order, family, genus). The outcome measure was the diagnosis of periodontitis, defined by clinical criteria (such as CDC/AAP criteria, Community Periodontal Index (CPI)) or genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Study data should provide statistical information on the causal relationship between gut microbiota and periodontitis, such as odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI). The subjects included in the studies should cover individuals of all ages and ethnic backgrounds to minimize the effect of genetic heterogeneity.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded reviews, meta-analyses, animal studies, conference abstracts, and literature without human studies. Studies not involving gut microbiota or periodontitis were also excluded, as well as studies for which the full text could not be obtained or valid data could not be extracted. Republished literature was excluded, retaining only the most recent or complete studies.

Literature screening, data extraction and quality evaluation

The literature screening process was carried out independently by two trained researchers, using Endnote 21 software to manage and de-duplicate the search results. Preliminary screening was based on the title and abstract, excluding studies that clearly did not meet the inclusion criteria. Subsequently, the full text of the remaining studies was read to further select those meeting the inclusion criteria. In case of disagreement, a third-party researcher was invited to arbitrate until an agreement was reached.

Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers, using a pre-designed standardized data extraction form to ensure accuracy and consistency. The extracted information included: study characteristics (such as author name, publication year, and study design type); participant characteristics (e.g., sample size, average age, sex ratio, ethnic background); exposure and outcomes (e.g., specific classification of gut microbiota, diagnostic criteria and definition of periodontitis); estimation of causal effects (e.g., odds ratio (OR), 95% confidence interval (CI), p-value); and instrumental variables (e.g., number of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) used and their screening criteria). The two researchers independently extracted the data and cross-checked it to resolve any inconsistencies. We explicitly considered core MR assumptions—relevance, independence, and exclusion restriction—when extracting and synthesizing estimates. Where reported by the original studies, we recorded instrument strength (e.g., F-statistics), applied pleiotropy-robust estimators (MR-Egger, weighted median), and summarized MR-PRESSO global and outlier tests. Directionality checks (e.g., Steiger filtering) and locus-level colocalization, when available, were noted in data extraction to contextualize instrument validity.

Recognizing that generic observational checklists (e.g., NOS) may not capture biases unique to Mendelian randomization (MR), we evaluated study quality using two MR-focused frameworks. First, we assessed reporting completeness against the STROBE-MR checklist across core domains (pre-specification of instruments and assumptions; data sources and harmonization; handling of sample overlap; instrument strength/F-statistics; LD clumping thresholds; pleiotropy diagnostics including MR-Egger, weighted median, and MR-PRESSO; directionality tests/colocalization; ancestry control; and multiple-testing control). Each domain was rated as Yes/Partial/No (Supplemental Table MR-1). Second, we graded the certainty of evidence using a GRADE framework adapted for MR (GRADE-MR), considering MR-specific risk of bias related to instrument validity (relevance, independence, exclusion restriction; weak instruments; horizontal pleiotropy; population stratification; sample overlap), inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias (Supplemental Table MR-2). Two reviewers assessed all items independently; discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in Stata 15.1 [15, 16]. We pooled study-level odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using random-effects models. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified with I² and the Cochrane Q test (I² >50% or P < 0.10 indicating substantial heterogeneity).Small-study effects were explored with Egger’s test [17] and trim-and-fill as a sensitivity analysis [18]. Because multiple taxa were tested across several taxonomic levels, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) to the two-sided p values from the pooled effects—within each taxonomic level (primary) and globally across all taxa (sensitivity). Associations with q < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant; 0.05 ≤ q < 0.10 were considered suggestive. Forest plots display unadjusted pooled ORs and 95% CIs; FDR control informs inference but does not alter effect estimates. FDR-adjusted q values are reported in Supplemental Table 2.

Results

Literature search results and basic features of included studies

According to the predetermined search strategy, a total of 320 articles related to gut microbiota and periodontitis were retrieved from multiple electronic databases, including PubMed (137 articles), Web of Science (97 articles), Cochrane Library (2 articles), China National Knowledge Network (CNKI) (57 articles), and Wanfang Database (27 articles). After de-duplication, 295 records remained. During the initial screening, 283 articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, including studies not using Mendelian randomization, those not involving gut microbiota or periodontitis, animal studies, and reviews. Subsequently, the full texts of the remaining 12 records were read, and 6 studies were further excluded due to incomplete data or lack of necessary statistical information. Finally, 6 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this meta-analysis (Fig. 1) [19–24].

Flowchart of the study screening process for the meta-analysis, showing the steps of literature identification, exclusion, and final inclusion of studies

This meta-analysis included six studies using two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), covering different populations and geographic regions. All studies were based on genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets assessing the causal relationship between gut microbiota and periodontitis. The sample sizes ranged from 2,002 Chinese individuals to 18,340 individuals of mixed ancestry. The number of periodontitis cases ranged from 4,434 to 17,353. The study populations included individuals of European, North American, East Asian, and other ancestries, with a focus on European ancestry to minimize the impact of genetic heterogeneity. The age range of the studies focused on adults, although some studies did not specify exact ages. Gender ratios were not detailed, but all studies included both male and female participants. Periodontitis was diagnosed based on the CDC/AAP criteria, Community Periodontal Index (CPI), or ICD-10 codes, while gut microbiota was assessed using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing or whole-genome sequencing techniques. All studies excluded individuals without genotype or gut microbiota data, as well as SNPs that did not meet the genome-wide significance threshold (p < 1 × 10⁻⁵). The composition of gut microbiota was assessed using genomic methods covering 196 known microbial genera. Genetic instrumental variables (IVs) selected SNPs significantly associated with gut microbiota, ranging from hundreds to thousands, and all IVs satisfied linkage disequilibrium (LD) conditions (r² < 0.001, distance >10,000 kb). The diagnosis of periodontitis was based on clinical evaluation and periodontal index, in accordance with ICD-10 criteria. Causal inference was primarily made using inverse variance weighting (IVW), with sensitivity analyses such as weighted median (WM), MR-Egger, and MR-PRESSO. The GWAS data for gut microbiota mainly came from the MiBioGen consortium, while the periodontitis data came from the GLIDE and FinnGen consortia (Table 1) [19–24].

STROBE-MR compliance was generally high for instrument specification, LD clumping, data sources/harmonization, ancestry control, and the use of pleiotropy-robust estimators (MR-Egger, weighted median, MR-PRESSO). Reporting of instrument strength (F-statistics) and directionality/colocalization was incomplete in several studies, and sample overlap was variably addressed (Supplemental Table MR-1). Meta-analysis results

This meta-analysis aimed to assess the causal effects of gut microbiota on periodontitis risk at different taxonomic levels (Order, Family, Genus, Species). Below are the main outcome indicators and meta-analysis results for each taxonomic level.

At the Order level, the meta-analysis included two orders, both of which showed a significant causal association with periodontitis risk. Enterobacterales significantly increased the risk of periodontitis (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.17–1.44, P < 0.001). Pasteurellales also significantly increased the risk of periodontitis (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.04–1.21) (Fig. 2). Both associations remained significant after within-level FDR adjustment.

Forest plot summarizing the causal effects of gut microbiota at the order level on periodontitis risk, including Enterobacterales and Pasteurellales

At the Family level, the meta-analysis included four families, three of which had significant causal associations with periodontitis risk. Enterobacteriaceae significantly increased the risk of periodontitis (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.12–1.44). Pasteuricoccaceae also significantly increased the risk of periodontitis (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.04–1.21). Muribaculaceae significantly increased the risk of periodontitis (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.12–1.29). However, the Oxalobacteraceae family showed no significant association (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.76–1.38) (Fig. 3). Enterobacteriaceae, Pasteuricoccaceae, and Muribaculaceae remained significant after within-level FDR correction, whereas Oxalobacteraceae was not significant (q ≥ 0.10).

Forest plot displaying the causal effect of gut microbiota at the Family level, highlighting the impact of Enterobacteriaceae, Pasteuricoccaceae, and Muribaculaceae on periodontitis risk

At the Genus level, seven genera remained significant after within-level FDR correction: Granulicatella (risk-increasing), Oxalobacter (risk-increasing), Butyricicoccus (protective), Ruminiclostridium 6 (protective), Alistipes (protective), Lachnospiraceae UCG008 (risk-increasing), and Ruminococcaceae UCG013 (risk-increasing). Leuconostoc and Eisenbergiella showed suggestive associations (0.05 ≤ q < 0.10), and Cronobacter was not significant after FDR adjustment. (Fig. 4; Supplemental Table 2.)

Forest plot for the Genus level, illustrating the significant associations between Granulicatella, Prevotella salivae, Butyricicoccus, and Ruminiclostridium 6 and periodontitis risk

At the Species level, Prevotella salivae remained significantly associated with increased periodontitis risk after within-level FDR correction. Bifidobacterium angulatum and Streptococcus constellatus were suggestive (0.05 ≤ q < 0.10), and Escherichia albertii was not significant. (Fig. 5; Supplemental Table 2.)

Forest plot for the Species level, showing the causal relationship of Bifidobacterium angulatum and Prevotella salivae with periodontitis risk

Combining the results at the Order, Family, Genus, and Species levels, this meta-analysis found that gut microbiota, as a whole, significantly increased the risk of periodontitis (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.11–1.26). The overall analysis exhibited low heterogeneity (I² = 11.9%, P = 0.339), but the inter-group heterogeneity test (P = 0.022) suggested possible sources of variation among different taxonomic levels.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias detection

The Egger regression test was used to assess publication bias. The results indicated that the Order level showed statistical significance in the Egger regression (Intercept = 2.091, P = 0.040), while no significant publication bias was detected at the Family, Genus, or Species levels. Given the small number of included studies (k = 6), the power and precision of diagnostics are limited; therefore, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, the effect sizes adjusted using Duval and Tweedie’s Trim and Fill method were consistent with the original effect sizes, further supporting the robustness of the results. Although some taxonomic levels showed statistical significance for the intercept term in the Egger regression, the Trim and Fill method adjustments revealed that the overall effect size did not change significantly due to publication bias. Thus, the findings of this study remain robust (Table 2).

Multiple-testing control

Consistent with the prespecified approach, within-level Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control (Order/Family/Genus/Species) retained the principal signals (q < 0.05), namely Enterobacterales and Pasteurellales at the Order level and Granulicatella and Prevotella salivae at the genus/species levels. Butyricicoccus and Ruminiclostridium 6 also met the within-level FDR threshold (protective). Several additional family- and genus-level associations that were nominally significant (p < 0.05) did not survive FDR adjustment and are therefore considered exploratory; their effect directions were generally concordant with the main analysis. In the sensitivity analysis using a global FDR across all taxa, Enterobacterales, Pasteurellales, Granulicatella, and Prevotella salivae remained significant, whereas Butyricicoccus and Ruminiclostridium 6 were suggestive (0.05 ≤ q < 0.10). Detailed q values for each taxon are reported in Supplemental Table 2.

Summary of meta-analysis results

After within-level FDR control, signals at the Order level (Enterobacterales, Pasteurellales) and select genus/species (Granulicatella, Prevotella salivae; Butyricicoccus, Ruminiclostridium 6) remained robust, whereas several nominal family/genus associations did not survive FDR and are considered exploratory.

Discussion

This meta-analysis pooled six high-quality Mendelian randomization (MR) studies to systematically assess the causal effect of gut microbiota at different taxonomic levels (such as Order, Family, Genus, and Species) on the risk of periodontitis (PD). By synthesizing the results of these independent studies, we found that specific gut microbiome genera were significantly associated with the risk of developing periodontitis. In particular, five microbial genera were associated with an increased risk, while one genus was associated with a reduced risk.

The main contribution of this study is to further confirm the causal relationship between gut microbiota and periodontitis by using Mendelian randomization (MR) with genetic variation as instrumental variables. By integrating multiple MR studies, we were able to identify microbiota, such as Enterobacterales and Pasteurellales, which significantly increase the risk of periodontitis. These findings align with previous studies in inflammatory settings, suggesting that these microbiota may exacerbate the inflammatory response through metabolic changes and pro-inflammatory properties [22]. At the same time, increased abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, Pasteuricoccaceae, and Muribaculaceae was associated with a higher risk of periodontitis, potentially influencing oral health by enhancing the systemic inflammatory response and disrupting intestinal microecological balance [19, 21, 22]. Furthermore, the increase in specific genera, such as Granulicatella and Prevotella salivae, further supports the crucial role of the gut microbiota in the pathogenesis of periodontitis [9]. In particular, Prevotella has shown a significant increase in severe cases of periodontitis, indicating its important role in the onset and progression of the disease [9].

The findings of this study help fill the knowledge gap regarding the causal relationship between gut microbiota and periodontitis, providing potential targets for future intervention and treatment strategies. The gut microbiota participates in the development of periodontitis through several mechanisms, including regulation of the host immune system, modulation of the inflammatory response, production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and maintenance of intestinal barrier function [25, 26]. For instance, Butyricicoccus and Ruminiclostridium 6 have anti-inflammatory effects through butyrate production, which may reduce the risk of periodontitis. Some pro-inflammatory bacteria, such as Lachnospiraceae UCG008, may worsen the inflammatory response by promoting the release of inflammatory factors (such as IL-6 and TNF-α), thereby increasing the host’s sensitivity to inflammation [19, 21, 23]. These results further confirm the multifaceted role of the gut microbiota in periodontitis, highlighting the potential for preventing and treating periodontitis by modulating specific microbiota.

Additionally, the role of the bidirectional communication mechanism between the gut and the mouth — the “gut-oral axis” — in periodontitis and related systemic diseases has been further supported [10, 27, 28]. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota not only affects systemic inflammation but may also exacerbate intestinal inflammation by introducing oral pathogens into the gut via the bloodstream and digestive pathways [10, 28]. This interaction underscores the strong connection between oral health and overall health, suggesting that regulating the gut microbiota could offer new strategies for the prevention and management of periodontitis. For example, probiotics, especially those containing lactic acid bacteria, have been shown to inhibit the growth of Lachnospiraceae and Bacteroidales S24.7 bacteria, which are associated with an increased risk of periodontitis, providing a theoretical basis for the use of probiotics in managing periodontitis [21, 27].

To make the mechanistic rationale more explicit, we note that the risk-increasing Orders (Enterobacterales, Pasteurellales) include LPS-rich taxa capable of TLR4–NF-κB activation, favoring Th17 skewing and RANKL-mediated osteoclastogenesis, thereby linking systemic inflammatory tone to alveolar bone resorption. Prevotella salivae has been associated with pro-inflammatory cytokine induction (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), biofilm remodeling, and proteolysis that can potentiate periodontal tissue breakdown. In contrast, SCFA-producing genera (e.g., Butyricicoccus, Ruminiclostridium 6) generate butyrate, which can reinforce epithelial barrier integrity and promote immune tolerance via GPR41/43 signaling, HDAC inhibition, and Treg/IL-10 pathways. These taxon-to-pathway links provide a coherent biological scaffold for the observed MR signals; nonetheless, we emphasize that such pathways are hypothesis-generating at the taxon level and warrant functional and interventional confirmation.

An important strength of this meta-analysis is that all included investigations used Mendelian randomization, which reduces confounding and reverse causation by leveraging germline instruments. Beyond this design-level protection, we appraised study quality with MR-specific frameworks rather than generic observational tools: STROBE-MR to evaluate reporting across core MR domains and a GRADE framework adapted for MR (GRADE-MR) to rate certainty of evidence (see Supplemental Tables MR-1 and MR-2). Reporting was generally robust for instrument specification, LD clumping, ancestry control, and pleiotropy diagnostics, with gaps noted for F-statistics, sample-overlap handling, and colocalization—points we explicitly acknowledge in the limitations and that informed our cautious interpretation of borderline associations. In addition, our synthesis pooled large GWAS-based samples across multiple taxonomic levels and implemented within-level FDR control, which together enhanced statistical power while limiting false discoveries, supporting the robustness of the principal findings.

However, there are several limitations in this study. First, although the overall heterogeneity was low, the inter-group heterogeneity test revealed some heterogeneity in certain microbial groups (e.g., the Oxalobacteraceae family and specific genera and species levels), which may be attributed to differences in study design, sample characteristics, or microbiota measurement methods. Second, the study sample primarily consisted of individuals of European descent, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other ethnic groups. Additionally, since the GWAS database provides mainly genus-level microbial information, we were unable to explore the causal effects of lower taxonomic levels, such as species and subspecies, on periodontitis. The inherent limitations of the MR method, particularly the pleiotropy of instrumental variables, could also affect the accuracy of causal inference. Although the trim-and-fill analysis suggested minimal impact of publication bias and only the Order level showed Egger’s intercept asymmetry, we cannot exclude residual bias. Subgroup analyses (e.g., by ancestry or GWAS source) would be informative but were not feasible with currently available data: most included MR studies drew exposures from overlapping discovery resources (e.g., MiBioGen) and reported single pooled effect estimates rather than stratum-specific estimates; for several taxon-level syntheses, the number of independent estimates per stratum was < 2, precluding meaningful stratification or meta-regression without violating independence and precision assumptions. We therefore prioritized consistent random-effects pooling, MR-specific risk-of-bias appraisal, and FDR control, and we highlight the need for future MR reports to provide per-ancestry/per-dataset estimates to enable robust subgroup synthesis.

Clinical applicability and translational outlook

Emerging clinical pathways can be inferred from our results. First, risk stratification: taxa associated with higher periodontitis (PD) risk (e.g., Enterobacterales, Pasteurellales, Granulicatella, Prevotella salivae) and potentially protective taxa (e.g., Butyricicoccus, Ruminiclostridium 6) could be incorporated into multivariable, microbiome-informed risk models to prioritize early periodontal assessment, individualized recall intervals, and proactive maintenance—particularly in patients with comorbid diabetes or cardiovascular disease. Second, adjunctive prevention: strategies that enrich short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)–producing communities (dietary fiber optimization, targeted pre-/probiotics, or postbiotics) align with the protective signal we observed for butyrate-linked taxa; these approaches should be tested in pragmatic RCTs as add-ons to standard mechanical debridement and oral hygiene instruction. Third, biomarker development: stool or saliva-based panels incorporating risk-increasing genera/species may aid early identification of individuals at heightened susceptibility, although current MR-estimated effects are modest and require validation in prospective cohorts before clinical deployment. Finally, care integration: our findings support a gut–oral axis care model in which periodontists collaborate with nutrition and primary-care teams to address systemic inflammatory tone while maintaining emphasis on core periodontal therapies. Importantly, these applications are adjunctive—they do not replace established PD prevention and treatment.

Methodological considerations and potential confounding in MR

While Mendelian randomization (MR) mitigates classical confounding and reverse causation, several issues could still bias causal estimates in microbiome MR [1]. Instrument relevance and weak-instrument bias: microbiome-associated SNPs often explain limited variance in taxon abundance (especially with 16 S-based GWAS), which can attenuate effects toward the null and, under sample overlap, bias estimates toward observational associations [2]. Exclusion-restriction violations (horizontal pleiotropy): microbiome-associated variants may influence PD risk via immune, metabolic, or inflammatory pathways independent of the targeted taxa. We summarized pleiotropy-robust estimators (MR-Egger, weighted median) and MR-PRESSO outlier detection across included studies, but residual pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded [3]. Population structure and assortative mating/dynastic effects: incomplete control for ancestry and family-level effects can induce associations between instruments and confounders [4]. Directionality and colocalization: without consistent Steiger filtering and locus-level colocalization across data sources, reverse causation and linkage confounding remain possible [5]. Phenotype definition and compositionality: heterogeneity in PD case definitions (CDC/AAP, CPI, ICD-10) and the compositional nature of microbiome data (taxa inter-correlation, multiple testing) may lead to misclassification and unstable instruments [6]. Batch effects and taxonomic resolution: cross-study differences in sequencing platforms and taxonomic pipelines (16 S vs. WGS) can affect instrument portability. Collectively, these considerations argue for cautious interpretation, replication in multi-ancestry datasets, and triangulation with interventional evidence to confirm clinical utility. Notably, several nominal associations did not withstand FDR control and are interpreted as exploratory (Supplemental Table 2), whereas the principal signals—particularly Enterobacterales, Pasteurellales, Granulicatella, and Prevotella salivae—were robust to multiple-testing adjustment. To address potential subjectivity of generic observational tools, we replaced NOS with STROBE-MR and GRADE-MR for quality appraisal. This triangulated assessment supports good transparency for instrument selection and pleiotropy diagnostics while identifying gaps in F-statistics reporting, sample-overlap handling, and colocalization, which informed our cautious interpretation of borderline associations.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis integrated six high-quality Mendelian randomization studies, revealing significant causal effects of gut microbiota on periodontitis risk across different taxonomic levels. The results emphasize the critical role of gut microbiota in the pathogenesis of periodontitis, suggesting that regulating its composition may offer new strategies for the prevention and management of the disease. However, given the limitations of this study, future research should further validate these causal relationships and investigate the functional mechanisms of specific microbiota in the development of periodontitis. This will help advance personalized and precise strategies for the prevention and treatment of periodontitis.
