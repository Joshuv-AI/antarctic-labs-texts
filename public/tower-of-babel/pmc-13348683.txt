The digital pulse of disaster: tracking public health needs via X during 2023 Türkiye earthquakes
Authors: M Fevzi Esen, Fatma Kantaş Yılmaz, Saadet Karakuş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial media plays an important role in disseminating information and identifying real-time needs during disasters. Its importance was shown on February 6, 2023, when Türkiye was struck by two destructive earthquakes. This resulted in tens of thousands of fatalities and economic devastation. In the early aftermath, social media was utilized as an important instrument for public coordination, information dissemination and social mobilization.MethodsThe study analyzed 697,313 tweets shared between February 6 and 14, 2023. Data were acquired via the X API v2 and processed to assess the engagement metrics of digitally expressed disaster needs using Python (version 3.12.7). Named Entity Recognition approach was applied to a combined rescue-request corpus to extract and geocode spatial data for heatmap visualization.ResultsMost frequent hashtags, types of requests, and patterns of interaction were investigated to examine how individuals communicated and expressed their needs during the earthquake. Findings showed that X contained time-sensitive, publicly generated signals related to rescue appeals, shelter needs, hygiene concerns and humanitarian coordination. During the first 12 hours of the earthquake, communication was primarily characterized by urgent requests for emergency rescue and heavy machinery. By the second day, shelter-related posts became more prominent with blanket and tent related requests. After the fourth day, there was a consistent rise in the posts about hygiene items and mobile toilets. The requests reached a peak between days five and seven.ConclusionThe study highlights the potential value of social media data as a supplementary source for understanding publicly expressed needs during disaster response. X posts reflected a temporary shift from urgent rescue appeals to shelter, hygiene, and recovery-related concerns. However, because this study was descriptive and did not assess post accuracy, operational impact, or predictive performance, the findings should be interpreted as digital indicators of public concern rather than direct evidence of response effectiveness.Keywords: Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, Social media and disaster response, Digital health communication, Crisis communication, Citizen generated data

Introduction

On February 6, 2023, two devastating earthquakes (magnitudes 7.8 and 7.6) struck southeast Türkiye, resulting in over 53,000 fatalities, the collapse of 9,432 buildings and the displacement of more than 2 million people [1]. The cost of the damage is expected to reach $100–155 billion with uncertain long-term socio-economic effects from disaster-induced domestic migration [2]. During the crisis, unique features of social media such as accessibility, mobility, and immediacy caused an increase in social media activity with numerous posts containing firsthand information. This allowed individuals to act as an information node, transforming an event into a globally visible incident almost instantly [3, 4]. As previous studies have shown, those affected used these platforms to request immediate help, supply delivery, debris removal, or search and rescue operations [5–7].

Among social media platforms, X is a practical tool for crisis response due to its real-time nature [8, 9]. With over 415.3 million active users and 400 million daily tweets, X serves as a crowdsourcing platform for information dissemination during disasters. It enhances situational awareness for decision makers [10, 11]. An expanding body of literature examines X data to explain public behaviour during or after disasters [12, 13]. These studies describe crisis-related information propagation and analyze the varied range of information regarding the features of the event. It also allows people to spread information instantly and coordinate resources by improving situational awareness. X was utilized by government and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) as a real-time monitoring system in events such as the Indonesian tsunamis, California wildfires, and Australian floods [14, 15].

By enabling users to categorize content and engage in rapid information exchange beyond their networks, hashtags have become important features of digital interaction [16]. While this functionality extends audience reach and facilitates real-time communication, it introduces challenges such as unverified information, rumors, and imprecise data [17, 18]. Despite this, X is the most investigated platform in social media analysis due to its data volume, speed, and simplicity [19]. It is conceptualized as a “social sensor network”, where the collective input of distributed human sensors provides a real-time, crowdsourced data stream essential for evaluating and directing field operations.

Effective public health communication in such contexts requires immediate and focused communication anchored in the “3 Rs” framework: “Review, Recognize and Respond”. This principle ensures that health communication is data-driven, goal-oriented, and meaning-driven [20]. The examination of social media data provides a view into the psychological states and urgent public concerns. However, dissemination of unverified information or emotional content such as the significant shock and prayer tweets following the Kahramanmaraş event, poses risks and can escalate collective anxiety [21, 22]. Consequently, disaster policies should integrate mechanisms to monitor psychological sensitivity and combat misinformation on social media platforms [23].

Disaster preparedness should be expanded to encompass digital resilience. Unlike traditional models that focus on physical infrastructure and logistics, digital preparedness emphasizes the reliability of communication systems [24, 25]. This framework requires clear strategies for pre-planned messaging and the management of misinformation. During the pre-crisis stage, social media is instrumental in identifying early warning signs and building public trust. However, during emergencies, the focus shifts: alerts must be brief and actionable. Effective crisis communication should consider the significant cognitive pressure imposed on the public by prioritising practical, verified information rather than secondary details [26, 27]. The contribution of this study is therefore context-specific and empirical. First, it describes the temporal sequence of publicly expressed needs during the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, including the shift from urgent rescue appeals to shelter, hygiene, sanitation, and recovery-related concerns. Second, it compares tweet volume and engagement patterns to distinguish frequently mentioned needs from highly amplified needs. Third, it applies and manually validates a Turkish language usable location detection workflow for non-geotagged rescue-related posts.

Previous studies in crisis informatics have shown that social media facilitates communication and enhances situational awareness during disasters. However, the evolution of public requirements within Turkish-language social media during the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes is not well understood. In particular, the transition from urgent rescue appeals to longer-term concerns regarding shelter, hygiene, and recovery has not been sufficiently documented. Additionally, the process of extracting location data from non-geotagged Turkish posts remains a challenge that requires empirical attention. This study investigates X usage during Kahramanmaraş earthquakes by analyzing message volume, content, and engagement patterns. It also traces the timing and geographic distribution of disaster-related posts to understand how affected communities expressed their needs, rather than to claim novelty for the general concept of social media situational awareness or to measure operational effectiveness or predictive accuracy of social media monitoring.

Material and method

This study was designed as a descriptive analysis of open public X posts. It did not assess the accuracy of individual posts, compare social media signals with official field records, or perform predictive modeling. The analytical framework focused on identifying patterns of digitally expressed needs and analyzing observed engagement within the sample to better understand public communication during the disaster.

Tweets were collected between February 6 and 14, 2023, through X API v2 using Python’s Tweepy library. Data retrieval was based on predefined earthquake-related hashtags and phrases listed below. A post was eligible for inclusion if it was publicly available, posted during the study period, and contained at least one of the predefined search terms. The search query was constructed using boolean logic to capture any public tweet containing at least one of the predefined list. The data collection resulted in 721,783 tweets. To isolate original posts, retweets and replies were excluded directly during the data retrieval process using the API filtering. Quote tweets, which include user commentary alongside shared content, were retained as original posts. Retweets were excluded as separate observations because they reproduced the content of the original posts; however, the retweet and like counts of each retained original tweet were preserved as engagement variables. Because some search terms were province names (e.g., #Hatay, #Gaziantep), initial retrieval also contained content unrelated to the earthquake response, such as advertisements, city-promotion campaigns, photographs, and other noisy X streams. To address this issue, the dataset was processed using the Python Pandas library. We applied a custom exclusion dictionary to identify and remove irrelevant posts based on text content. Duplicate records were removed based on the tweet’s unique ID number. Consequently, a total of 24,470 non-usable records were identified and removed. Although the total number of excluded records was retained, the specific exclusion counts for each category were not preserved during preprocessing. The final sample consisted of 697,313 publicly available and usable tweets. For each original tweet, the dataset included tweet’s unique ID, user ID, message, post date, number of retweets, and number of likes. Location data were not available in the X dataset.

To identify the messages about the earthquake, the following hashtags/phrases were used: #deprem (earthquake), #depremoldu (earthquake occurred), #Gaziantep, #Hatay, #AFAD (Disaster and Emergency Management Presidency), #Malatya, #Adıyaman, #yardım (help), #enkaz (rubble), #kızılay (Red Crescent), #enkazaltındayım (I’m under the rubble), #iskenderun, #Kahramanmaraş, #Ahbap (The Ahbap Association), #seferberlik (campaign), #AKUT (Search and Rescue Association), #hatayyardimbekliyor (hatay is waiting for help), #ohal (State of Emergency Rule), çok acil (very urgent), #depremsondakikadeprem (earthquake breaking news), teyitli bilgi (confirmed information), and #acildeprem (earthquake emergency).

Trending topics data was provided from X API’s GET trends/place function that returns the top 50 trending topics at the country level. The trend lists were created based on tweet volumes which are array data of trend objects in the X API’s function. The most up-to-date information available is returned on request. The X API v2 also gave access to volume streams, which can identify and track trends in a given time period.

We used publicly available rescue requests to create a heatmap visualizing the concentration of collapsed buildings. Data were retrieved from the Deprem platform (deprem.io) and the posts from X. Tweets were selected to reflect emergency situations such as “I’m under rubble”, “collapsed building”, “help”, or “address”. These combined data sources were merged into a single corpus. The initial corpus was raw and unstructured, filled with stop words, URLs, and symbols. We used the Python NLTK library to achieve a clean and consistent format. This involved converting all text to lowercase, removing punctuation and non-textual elements, and applying tokenization and lemmatization. A hybrid approach was utilized to identify addresses, combining a Named Entity Recognition model with custom rule-based patterns. Multiple posts could originate from a single collapse. For this reason, we implemented a deduplication strategy. Reports that geocoded to within a 50-meter radius were clustered and treated as a single incident to prevent over-representing any one location. The final list of unique addresses was then converted into longitude and latitude coordinates using the Google Maps Geocoding API. Finally, these coordinates were visualized using the ggmap library in R. The resulting heatmaps highlight the main hotspots within the earthquake zones, where the density of rescue requests was highest.

Manual validation of usable location detection

To examine the reliability of the location extraction process, two human reviewers independently evaluated a validation sample of 673 posts, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. The validation sample was not stratified according to province, date, or time period. Reviewers evaluated whether each post contained usable location data, defined as spatial information more specific than a general provincial reference that permitted geocoding. Posts that contained unclear, general, or unverifiable location references were coded as missing location data. Algorithm outputs were compared with the human-reviewed classifications, and precision, recall, F1-score, accuracy, and specificity were calculated.

Results

The study reports three quantitative sources: (1) counts, classifications, and engagement metrics from the sample of 697,313 original tweets; (2) platform-level trend indicators obtained from X API trend functions; and (3) the combined rescue-request corpus used for spatial mapping. These measures were employed for different analytic purposes and were not interpreted interchangeably.

Two hundred twenty nine point one thousand tweets containing “I am under rubble” began to emerge and rose to the top of the list of most frequent tweets, between 8 and 9 am. Requests for foreign aid started to increase from 8 am onwards. Between 9 and 10 am, users started to send tweets requesting help for their friends trapped under the rubble, using the phrases starting with “my friend’s address/name….” and province, district, street and building names. Additionally, the tweets containing prayers, moral support, and motivation that began with “Oh my God” were among the top 10 hashtags. These tweets expressed shock and prayers regarding the severity of the earthquakes. After 11 am, tweets requesting help, including building and street addresses, began to feature in the top 10 tweets. According to platform-level volume streams, the 12 − 1 pm interval had the highest platform-level trend volume for earthquake-related terms, totaling approximately 7 million across the listed terms globally. The second earthquake with a magnitude of 7.6 that occurred in Kahramanmaraş at 01:24 pm was reflected in the tweets posted between 1 and 2 pm. The tweets starting with phrases such as “my friend’s address/name…” showed an increasing trend. As aftershocks occured, tweets with phrases like “aftershock” rose to the top. Place names such as Odabaşı (in Hatay province), Nurdağı (in Gaziantep province), and Onikişubat (in Kahramanmaraş province) appeared frequently among the top terms in several hourly intervals. These findings indicate high mention frequency within the sampled X posts and should not be interpreted as independent evidence of damage severity, rescue demand, or structural collapse in these locations. At 2:00–3:00 pm, hashtags such as “let’s donate blood” did not rank high, whereas “spread, please” took the lead. In addition, the tweets tagged with the names of cities affected by the earthquake were observed in the top trending hashtags within every hour.

The most popular hashtags in the hours following the first 12 hours after the earthquake were given in Table 2. The tweets reporting that people’s relatives or friends were under the rubble (I’m under rubble, still under rubble, my friend’s address…) had the highest number of retweets at almost every time interval until 4 am. From 4 to 5 pm, tweets related to “government and people working together”, “Turkish Red Crescent”, and “blankets” became prominent. While Odabaşı tweets continued to appear as top trending tweets at many hours, tweets about “Nurdağı” district (one of the epicenters of the earthquakes) took the lead at 7–8 pm. The tweets calling for “the cancellation of transactions in the stock market” and “the stock market’s closure until February 20th” gained significant attention and ranked high in popularity. The hashtag “Haluk Levent”, the founder of Ahbap foundation and famous rock musician, was among the top 10 between 8 and 9 pm. After this hour, the Turkish Red Crescent consistently ranked high in popularity.

Discussion

Our analysis indicates that social media engagement after the earthquake progressed through certain phases, reflecting the evolving needs of society.

Initial rescue-appeal period (first 12 hours)

The posts sent in the first hours of the earthquake (starting at 04:17 TRT) were characterized by shock and urgent survival appeals. These messages included statements such as “earthquake occurred” and expressions of shock such as “Oh my God”, “get well soon”, which indicate an immediate cognitive response to the event. This content provides insight into the mental health of the affected population [9]. Between 8 and 9 a.m., “I am under rubble”, “still under rubble” (n = 229.1 thousand) emerged as the highest-ranked expressions, excluding the general “earthquake” hashtag. This indicated a shift to an intense period of crowdsourced rescue appeals. Highly specific location data including building names (e.g., Arzu apartment) and district names (e.g., Odabaşı) were noticed in the following hours. Frequent use of regional district names during early critical hours may indicate decentralized and highly localized information bottlenecks that formal reporting systems can ignore. This level of detail may be useful for search-and-rescue coordination if the information can be quickly verified, geocoded, and integrated with official response systems. The increase in requests for external aid after 8:00 a.m. demonstrated an early public awareness of the destruction and lack of local capacity.

Logistics and shelter period (days 2–4)

Following the first 24 hours, the attention shifted to critical humanitarian logistics (e.g., take water to region, need for shrouds). Despite the ongoing requests for rescue during the second and third days, new hashtags appeared. This represents emerging priorities such as shelter and housing, drinkable water, medications, cranes, and mobile toilets.

A key finding within the “Requests and Needs” category was the difference between volume and engagement. Requests for blankets had the highest volume (n = 43,582). However, despite its lower volume (n = 2,257), tent-related posts received more engagement (180.01 retweets per post). The high level of engagement with tent-related posts suggests that shelter was highly salient to users and was perceived as an urgent concern within the online discourse [28]. It also indicates that tents were viewed as both harder to access and more urgently needed. These findings are consistent with the post-disaster assessment of 2023 Türkiye-Syria earthquakes, which found providing shelter more challenging than delivering other aid [29]. Similarly, the media’s depiction of public outrage concerning the insufficient number of tents and difficulties in their distribution, further corroborates this issue. High retweet rates for cranes and tents suggest that posts about specific, actionable resources received greater public amplification than more general humanitarian messages.

Socio-economic and recovery period (first week)

In the following days, X functioned as a platform for citizen engagement, policy advocacy, and socio-economic recovery. The topics expanded from immediate survival to encompass demands for the cancellation of stock market transactions, as well as solutions for distance education, and temporary accommodation. This reflects how disasters trigger the restructuring of public priorities such as economic continuity, social cohesion, and stability [30]. The delayed appearance of specific public health requests, such as for “sanitary pads” and “baby diapers/rash cream”, indicates a gap in addressing certain specialized needs. This pattern suggests that X may provide supplementary information about emerging public concerns that could inform policy discussions when interpreted alongside verified field assessments. These findings are relevant both to emergency response and to understanding the longer-term socioeconomic impacts of the disaster [7].

Digital signals of public health needs during disaster response

The analysis of early content highlights four prominent types of disaster-related health priorities visible in the dataset. The first priority was search and rescue, reflected in location-specific posts such as “I’m under rubble” and “voice from the rubble”, as well as messages including addresses and street numbers. Several studies have indicated that X can function as an improvised tool for crowd-sourced mapping of incident sites [10]. In the dataset, phrases like “I’m under rubble,” “help,” or specific building addresses contain direct, real-time rescue related information.

Following the earthquake, the “deprem.io” platform was launched as a community-driven project to assess rubble location data acquired from X. However, the analysis of non-geotagged posts uncovered a significant limitation: spatial information supplied by victims on X cannot be automatically retrieved and converted into accurate and machine-readable structures. Enhancing the clarity and structure of such information could improve the usability of crowdsourced rescue-related information with current damage assessment technologies [31].

A second priority emerged for emotional support. Tweets expressing shock such as “Oh my God”, and prayer messages “get well soon” indicated an urgent need for psychological aid. Emotional intensity conveyed through this content reflects a collective anxiety following the disaster. This demonstrates the necessity of deploying pre-established mental health communication strategies not only to provide assistance but also to mitigate the spread of distress and confusion on digital platforms [32].

The third priority involved the requests for essential resources. Requests for items like “blankets”, “take water to region”, and “pharmacies” highlighted acute logistic challenges, particularly the importance of pre-positioning high-demand non-food items near high-risk areas. Our results align with existing research on humanitarian logistics, which shows the value and efficiency of forward-locating resources during peak demand [33]. In this context, real-time social media signals may provide supplementary signals about localized needs when combined with verified field assessments. Finally, a fourth and later emerging priority focused on hygiene and sanitation needs. Posts calling for “mobile toilets”, “sanitary pads”, and “masks” revealed a gap in how specific health and hygiene needs were addressed during early response. These signals reflect more than logistical delays; they indicate structural oversights in preparedness planning [31, 33]. These findings extend previous crisis informatics research by showing how specific public health issues, especially hygiene, sanitation, and dignity-related requirements, were increasingly prominent over time in this earthquake dataset. Because the present analysis is descriptive, the observed patterns should be interpreted as indicators of online discourse and public amplification, rather than as validated measures of field-level need, response effectiveness, or resource scarcity.

Engagement metrics and public amplification of disaster needs

Virality, measured by average retweets and likes, was used as an indicator of public amplification and engagement during crises [4]. Under high stress and urgency, users tend to share messages that offer clear, practical value [21]. However, these indicators are prone to artificial inflation by automated bot networks and coordinated amplification efforts, and should not be treated as a direct measure of need severity, logistical scarcity, or operational priority without external validation. According to the results, tent aid requests were retweeted nearly 15 times more often (average retweets of 180.01 per tweet) than the high-volume requests for blankets (average retweets of 12.10 per tweet). These engagement patterns may help generate hypotheses about which needs were most visible or urgent within the online public sphere. Tents represent complex, scalable shelter solutions that are critically scarce, while blankets are easily sourced items. The high amplification of tent requests suggests that shelter-related needs were especially visible and widely circulated among X users, although this visibility cannot be assumed to reflect purely organic prioritization by users. High virality may reflect moments when users collectively amplified specific needs [3]. These findings suggest that preparedness policies could consider monitoring engagement metrics as one supplementary input, provided that such signals are verified and combined with official needs assessments [5].

Public trust appears to be an important determinant of message spread [6]. Hashtags related to trusted organizations and figures (e.g., Turkish Red Crescent, Ahbap Association, Haluk Levent) received significant engagement (89.62 average retweets for the category), contrasting with low engagement for symbolic, propaganda-oriented hashtags such as “85 million one heart” and “state and nation together” with average retweets of only 8.79 and 22.51, respectively. This underscores that during a crisis, the public pays greater attention to credible information from the actors over symbolic campaigns [11]. For government officials, these findings provide feedback: messages should focus on verified aid delivery and transparent logistics, not symbolic campaigns. Managing misinformation and digital resilience

While virality facilitates the rapid spread of urgent help requests, it also amplifies the risk of spreading misinformation. Evidence suggests that misinformation spreads significantly faster than true news, often via human actors rather than bots [34]. Policies on managing information must therefore address the challenge of optimizing the dissemination of requests while implementing enhanced verification for highly shared content. A practical starting point would involve training citizens and first responders to provide verifiable, machine-readable data from the outset, allowing for swifter authentication of high-priority requests.

This highlights a critical limitation: the inability to automatically extract reliable and machine-readable spatial data from tweets, which necessitated a reliance on manual validation. Because the success of rescue operations depends on the speed of location data [29, 35], there is a clear need for increased investment in digital infrastructure and automated tools [12]. Technology that can immediately translate a non-geotagged report into a GIS coordinate would transform social media from a general “signal” into a precise operational tool. Moreover, the real-time monitoring capability of X, shown by the correlation between seismic events and posting frequency, could be explored as a supplementary communication input within broader early warning and response systems [36]. The visibility of trusted scientific figures in trending topics also shows a public demand for technical information. Policies should establish direct communication channels for these experts to utilize their credibility in crisis management.

Beyond the immediate response, social media intelligence remains a vital tool during the recovery phase for tracking how public priorities change over time. In the later stages of the disaster, public discourse shifted noticeably toward issues of governance and financial stability. This result is in line with research reflecting public anxiety regarding economic losses and long-term uncertainty [37]. By monitoring policy-related hashtags, authorities can gain a more responsive understanding of public sentiment as it unfolds [38]. Consequently, preparedness plans should include economic intervention strategies designed to address financial panic and support social stability throughout the extended recovery period [39].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the study. First, the technical constraints of the X API v2 search endpoint and keyword-based retrieval strategy set restrictions on data collection. The API retrieves only posts that were publicly available at the exact moment of data extraction, as it functions as a temporal cross-sectional sample. Any tweets that were deleted by users or removed by moderators prior to extraction are systematically absent. Furthermore, reliance on predefined Boolean queries means the dataset may exclude relevant discourse that utilized alternative phrasing, typographical errors, or implicit references. In addition, the category-level engagement analysis was limited to selected high-frequency hashtags and phrases. To ensure categorical clarity and prevent double-counting, we excluded posts that either lacked specific keywords or contained overlapping hashtags. While this approach likely underrepresents less frequent, mixed-topic, or more complex posts, it maintains the integrity of the individual categories. Consequently, the temporal peaks and engagement metrics observed in the study should be interpreted as patterns within the analyzed dataset rather than exact estimates of total X activity.

Second, by focusing specifically on original posts, our methodology excludes the broader spectrum of engagement involving NGOs and government organizations on X. This gap is further widened by the relative inactivity of these organizations on X, which likely skewed the representation of their institutional efforts within the final dataset. Beyond these organizational limits, the reliance on social media data introduces inherent demographic and geographic imbalances. Because individuals in rural regions or those with lower digital literacy are often marginalized in digital datasets, the findings primarily reflect the perspectives of users with consistent internet access. Consequently, while the data captures a specific segment of the discourse, it may overlook some of the most severely affected and hard-to-reach communities.

Third, individual posts were not cross-referenced with official records regarding rescue, damage, or logistics. While engagement metrics like retweets and likes provide a clear picture of what the public amplified, they do not necessarily correlate with the actual severity of a crisis or the effectiveness of a response. This distinction is important because engagement counts on X can be skewed by automated bots or coordinated campaigns rather than reflecting organic public concern. Consequently, the metrics in this study should be viewed as measures of platform visibility rather than absolute indicators of public priority. Similarly, the timing of these posts shows how needs were expressed online, but this does not imply that social media data can reliably forecast future resource needs. To build on this work, future research should integrate social media analysis with bot detection tools and official field reports to better assess data accuracy and operational value.

Finally, the heatmaps are subject to spatial uncertainty. The precision of these visualizations depends on a combination of factors, ranging from the reliability of user-provided details to the technical performance of our extraction and geocoding workflows. For example, incomplete or inaccurate data can compromise spatial accuracy, while the deduplication of reports might inadvertently mask the true density of distinct collapse sites located in close proximity. Although manual validation showed satisfactory performance performance for usable location detection, the validation sample was not stratified by province, date, or time period. Moreover, the study did not independently verify the field accuracy of every geocoded coordinate. These heatmaps should be viewed as approximate representations of digital rescue requests rather than definitive records of structural damage or operational field activity. Future research should also investigate how cultural, regional, and technological factors shape digital engagement during disasters.

Conclusion

This research contributes to established crisis-informatics literature by providing a detailed analysis of Turkish-language disaster communication during the 2023 earthquakes. A key finding is the documentation of how public priorities evolved on social media; while early discourse was dominated by urgent rescue requests, the focus steadily shifted toward longer-term recovery needs such as shelter, hygiene, and sanitation. Findings confirm that X was actively used in both disseminating urgent calls for assistance and in the monitoring of public health and logistics issues.

The analysis supported three conclusions. First, X served as a decentralized channel through which users shared urgent, location-specific rescue appeals during the initial hours of the disaster. Second, the findings show that tweet volume and engagement metrics captured different dimensions of online attention. While high-volume terms reflected widely expressed needs, highly retweeted posts indicated which needs received stronger public amplification. These metrics should be interpreted as descriptive indicators of online salience rather than validated measures of logistical bottlenecks. This was evidenced by the significantly higher engagement for tents compared to more general requests for blankets. Third, public trust is paramount in crisis communication. The data consistently showed higher engagement with messages from trusted civil organizations and those offering direct aid. In contrast, symbolic or non-actionable campaigns received significantly less attention.

These findings suggest several considerations for disaster communication and public health preparedness. Recommendations include investing in digital infrastructure, especially automated geoparsing tools to generate usable GIS coordinates. Humanitarian and government organizations could explore the use of engagement metrics as supplementary monitoring signals, provided that they are combined with verification procedures, official field reports, and safeguards against misinformation. To address these challenges effectively, official communication must prioritize both clarity and practical utility. Beyond the messaging itself, public health teams should incorporate sentiment monitoring into their workflows to identify and mitigate the rapid escalation of public distress. Strengthening disaster preparedness further requires the strategic integration of scientific experts and civil society organizations into official networks, thereby fostering a more transparent and reciprocal dialogue between institutions and the public.
