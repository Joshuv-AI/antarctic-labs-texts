Association between baseline BMI and in-hospital mortality in critically ill cardiac surgery patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Gang-gang Peng, Pan Zhou, Wei Liu, Jing-heng Lei, Yue-jie Zhou, Zhi-hui Cao, Shu-ya Li, Zhe Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBMI–mortality associations in critically ill cardiac surgery lack precise threshold characterization. We investigated the baseline BMI and in hospital mortality in ICU cardiac surgery patients.MethodsWe included 8032 cardiac surgery patients from eICU (208 US hospitals, 2014–2015). Focus exposure: BMI; key outcome: 30-day mortality. Adjustments encompassed demographics, laboratory values, APACHE-IV scores, comorbidities, and interventions. Nonlinear patterns were examined via penalized spline-fitted generalized additive modeling.ResultsAmong 8,032 patients (BMI: 29.6 ± 6.8 kg/m2), in-hospital mortality occurred in 289 cases (3.6%). Standard linear modeling detected no BMI–mortality relationship (OR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.97–1.01, P = 0.210). Conversely, spline analysis identified a U-shaped pattern with inflection at 25.0 kg/m2. BMI increases below this point conferred 10% mortality reduction per unit (OR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.83–0.96, P = 0.003), while higher values showed null association (OR = 1.01, 95% CI 0.98–1.03, P = 0.567). Log-likelihood ratio testing favored the nonlinear approach (P = 0.010).ConclusionsOur analysis identified a U-shaped BMI–mortality relationship in critically ill cardiac surgery patients, revealing an optimal risk threshold at 25.0 kg/m2. These findings may provide valuable guidance for perioperative BMI management in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03389-3.Keywords: BMI, Cardiac surgery, In-hospital mortality, Nonlinear relationship, Multicenter study

Introduction

Cardiac surgery represents essential treatment for advanced cardiovascular disease yet carries substantial perioperative mortality[1, 2]. Contemporary registry analyses indicate 30-day mortality varies considerably, from ∼1% in low-risk operations to > 10% in high-complexity cases, escalating further among critically ill patients[3]. Such heterogeneity underscores the intricate perioperative challenges in cardiac surgery cohorts, especially within intensive care settings, where multi-organ dysfunction and hemodynamic instability substantially contribute to in-hospital mortality, necessitating precise risk stratification.

Body mass index (BMI) is endorsed by the WHO as the primary metric for assessing nutritional condition and classifying obesity[4]. It incorporates aspects of fat distribution and nutritional reserves, and has multifactorial associations with disease initiation and progression. Epidemiological evidence has identified complex relationships between BMI and chronic non-communicable diseases, including cardiovascular disease[5], diabetes[6], and malignancies[7]. In the context of critical illness, BMI displays unique associations with patient outcomes, wherein moderate overweight may sometimes be linked to better prognosis—a phenomenon termed the “obesity paradox”[8, 9], as observed in acute myocardial infarction[10] and sepsis[11]. However, investigations specifically addressing the link between baseline BMI and in-hospital mortality following major cardiac surgery remain scarce. Moreover, the majority of prior studies have adopted linear models, which may fail to accurately depict potential non-linear relationships or threshold effects between BMI and mortality in these patients.

In response to existing research limitations and pressing clinical demands, this study utilizes the American eICU Collaborative Research Database v2.0 (eICU–CRD v2.0) to retrospectively assess the relationship between baseline BMI and in-hospital mortality among cardiac surgery patients admitted to intensive care units.

Methods

Data source

Data for this multicenter retrospective cohort analysis were extracted from eICU–CRD v2.0, a large-scale clinical repository created through partnership between Philips Healthcare and MIT's Laboratory for Computational Physiology. This repository encompasses detailed records of > 200,000 ICU encounters from 208 U.S. medical centers spanning 2014–2015, incorporating patient characteristics, physiological parameters, laboratory values, therapeutic interventions, diagnostic codes, and clinical endpoints. Patient anonymity was preserved through comprehensive de-identification protocols.

Ethics statement

This study utilized the publicly available eICU–CRD v2.0 database containing de-identified patient information. Original data collection received Institutional Review Board approval from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. This secondary analysis of anonymized data without patient contact was exempt from additional ethical review per federal human subjects research regulations, waiving informed consent requirements. The research team completed mandatory training and registration protocols, strictly adhering to database usage agreements to ensure patient privacy protection.

Study population

Cardiac surgery types included coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valve procedures, and combined operations as documented in the eICU database. Critically ill adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent cardiac surgery and were subsequently admitted to the ICU were included in this analysis. After screening, 8032 individuals met the criteria: ICU stay of 24 h to 30 days, available baseline BMI, and in-hospital mortality data. Exclusion criteria comprised age < 18 years, ICU stay outside the specified range, incomplete outcome or BMI information, and extreme BMI values (> 3 standard deviations from the mean) to reduce bias from possible inaccuracies. Variables

Exposure variable and outcome variable

The primary exposure was BMI, calculated from weight and height measured within 24 h of ICU admission (kg/m2). BMI was analyzed both continuously and categorically: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), obesity (30.0–39.9 kg/m2), and morbid obesity (≥ 40.0 kg/m2)[8, 12]. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death occurring during the index hospitalization among patients with ICU stays of 24 h to 30 days[13]. In-hospital mortality data were obtained from electronic medical records documenting death status at hospital discharge and analyzed as a binary variable (0: alive at discharge, 1: died during hospitalization).

Covariates

Potential confounders were chosen based on clinical importance, prior evidence, and established associations with both BMI and mortality in critically ill cardiac surgery patients[14–16]. Covariates were selected according to epidemiologic best practices to reduce residual confounding and ensure model simplicity. Demographic factors included sex, ethnicity, and age. Laboratory assessments—hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count, serum creatinine (SCr), and blood urea nitrogen(BUN)—were collected within 24 h of ICU admission using standardized protocols. Disease severity was measured by the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation IV (APACHE-IV) score, a validated index for ICU mortality risk. Comorbidities were extracted from electronic medical records and included acute respiratory failure (ARF), atrial fibrillation (AF), acute coronary syndrome (ACS), congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, based on their known impact on cardiac surgery outcomes and potential to confound the BMI–mortality relationship. Treatment data comprised the administration of antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, lipid-lowering drugs, cephalosporins, vancomycin, and mechanical ventilation, all documented as binary variables for usage within the first 24 h post-ICU admission.

Missing data handling

Missing data assessment was performed systematically across all study variables. Five variables exhibited missing values: Hb (43 cases, 0.5%), BUN (44 cases, 0.5%), SCr (37 cases, 0.5%), platelet count (121 cases, 1.5%), and APACHE-IV score (1,070 cases, 13.3%). Missing at random (MAR) assumptions were evaluated through pattern analysis[17–19], and multiple imputation was conducted using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE)[18, 19]. MICE was chosen for its capacity to manage mixed variable types while preserving inter-variable relationships, yielding superior accuracy vs. single imputation approaches. This methodology aligns with established guidelines for observational research and has been widely validated in ICU database studies[18, 19].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.0.0, The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, non-normally distributed data as median (interquartile range), and categorical data as frequency (percentage). Between-group comparisons employed chi-square tests for categorical variables, t tests for normal continuous variables, and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normal continuous variables. Sample size and statistical power: as a retrospective analysis of the eICU database, sample size (n = 8032) was determined by available data meeting inclusion criteria rather than prospective power calculation.

Collinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis prior to multivariate modeling. All variables demonstrated VIF values < 5 (see Supplementary Table 1), supporting their inclusion in subsequent models. A systematic three-stage analytical approach was implemented to evaluate the BMI–mortality association, advancing from conventional to sophisticated statistical methods:

Stage 1 employed sequential logistic regression models to assess association robustness under progressive adjustment strategies. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (crude association), Model 2 (adjusted for age, gender, ethnicity), and Model 3 (fully adjusted for all baseline covariates). This approach examined BMI effect variations across adjustment levels and evaluated result stability. Covariates were retained when their inclusion altered odds ratios by ≥ 10%, following epidemiological conventions[20].

Stage 2 employed generalized additive models (GAM) with penalized splines to evaluate non-linear BMI–mortality relationships. When non-linearity was detected, inflection points were calculated using recursive algorithms, and two-piecewise linear regression models were constructed around these knot points. Model performance was compared using log-likelihood ratio tests to determine optimal fit for the BMI–mortality association.

Stage 3 conducted subgroup analyses using stratified linear regression or generalized additive models to evaluate effect modification by clinical characteristics. Effect modification was assessed via likelihood ratio tests. All analyses were pre-specified based on clinical relevance and literature evidence.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents comprehensive baseline characteristics for 8,032 cardiac surgery patients stratified by BMI. The majority (70.3%) were overweight or obese. Three key patterns emerged: (1) progressive age decrease with increasing BMI (69.07 years in underweight vs. 62.37 years in morbidly obese, P < 0.001); (2) Rising diabetes prevalence across BMI categories (6.03–24.90%, P < 0.001); and (3) Increasing disease severity (APACHE-IV scores from 41.00 to 46.00, P = 0.010). Laboratory parameters (Hb, platelet, BUN, and SCr) and select comorbidities (AF, ACS, COPD) showed significant between-group differences (all P < 0.05), while acute renal failure, congestive heart failure, hypertension, and medication use patterns did not differ significantly. Notably, in-hospital mortality showed no significant difference across BMI groups (3.22–5.17%, P = 0.092), motivating subsequent non-linear analysis.

Linear correlation between BMI and in-hospital mortality

Table 2 demonstrates the linear association between BMI and in-hospital mortality through three sequential regression models with progressive confounder adjustment. BMI showed consistent non-significant associations across crude analysis (OR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.97–1.01, P = 0.3179), demographic-adjusted model controlling for gender, age, and ethnicity (OR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–1.01, P = 0.6046), and fully adjusted model incorporating laboratory parameters, disease severity (APACHE-IV score), comorbidities (ARF, AF, ACS, CHF, COPD, hypertension, diabetes mellitus), and interventions (OR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.97–1.01, P = 0.2101). The uniform OR of 0.99 suggests each 1 kg/m2 BMI increase was associated with a 1% decreased mortality odds; however, this minimal effect was not statistically significant as confidence intervals consistently crossed 1.0. This retrospective analysis suggests no significant linear correlation between BMI and in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients.

Nonlinear association between BMI and in-hospital mortality

Figure 3 demonstrates the nonlinear BMI–mortality relationship across patient subgroups using smooth curve fitting analysis, with red curves representing fitted splines and blue curves indicating 95% confidence intervals. In the entire cohort (Fig. 3a, n = 8032), standard linear regression showed no significant BMI–mortality association (OR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.97–1.01, P = 0.2101) as detailed in Table 3 (Model I). However, two-piecewise regression analysis revealed significant nonlinearity with a turning point at 25.0 kg/m2. Below this threshold, each BMI unit increase significantly reduced mortality risk by 10% (OR = 0.90, 95%CI 0.83–0.96, P = 0.003), while above this point, no significant association was observed (OR = 1.01, 95%CI 0.98–1.03, P = 0.5668). Likelihood ratio testing confirmed nonlinear model superiority (P = 0.010).

This U-shaped pattern was consistently observed across all analyzed subgroups, with similar threshold effects clustering around 25.0 kg/m2. These consistent threshold effects support the generalizability of the obesity paradox phenomenon, highlighting the clinical importance of maintaining adequate BMI levels while suggesting that excessive weight gain beyond these saturation points confers no additional survival benefits.

Stability assessment through stratified analysis

To evaluate the consistency of the BMI–mortality association across diverse patient populations, we conducted stratified analyses of 8,032 patients by seven clinically relevant variables: gender, acute coronary syndrome, congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, anticoagulation therapy, and mechanical ventilation (Table 4). Interaction testing revealed no significant effect modification across any examined variables (gender: P = 0.6371; ACS: P = 0.3075; CHF: P = 0.6331; diabetes mellitus: P = 0.5214; hypertension: P = 0.6189; anticoagulation therapy: P = 0.7922; mechanical ventilation: P = 0.1679). These non-significant interactions demonstrate that the BMI–mortality relationship remains stable across diverse patient characteristics, supporting universal applicability in critically ill cardiac surgery patients regardless of comorbidities or treatment modalities and providing clinicians confidence in BMI-based risk stratification strategies across the broad spectrum of cardiac surgery practice.

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study of 8,032 cardiac surgery patients from the eICU database, we systematically evaluated the association between baseline BMI and in-hospital mortality. Methodological strengths include the large sample size across 208 hospitals, comprehensive confounder adjustment, and advanced statistical approaches utilizing generalized additive models with spline curve fitting to detect non-linear relationships. Our analysis revealed a significant U-shaped BMI–mortality association with an inflection point at approximately 25.0 kg/m2, demonstrating protective effects below this threshold and no association above it. Likelihood ratio testing confirmed non-linear model superiority over traditional linear approaches.

This study demonstrates a U-shaped BMI–mortality association with an optimal threshold at 25.0 kg/m2 in critically ill cardiac surgery patients. While large-scale studies have observed non-linear relationships, our precise threshold identification provides quantitative evidence supporting the “obesity paradox” in this vulnerable population. Mariscalco et al. analyzed 401,227 cardiac surgery patients and found reduced mortality in overweight (adjusted OR 0.79, 95% CI 0.76–0.83) and mildly obese patients (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.76–0.86) compared to normal-weight individuals, with increased mortality in underweight patients[21]. However, they did not quantify the precise BMI threshold for mortality risk changes. Similarly, Stamou et al. reported elevated mortality risk in underweight patients and survival benefits in overweight patients among 2,440 patients with long-term follow-up[22], but their categorical approach using traditional BMI classifications failed to identify exact transition thresholds. In addition, two large-scale studies have reported a curvilinear relationship between BMI and mortality in cardiac surgery patients, both focused on general cardiac surgery populations[23, 24]. While sharing methodological similarities with these investigations regarding sample size and covariate adjustment, our analysis of critically ill cardiac surgery patients represents a significant advancement by employing GAM with spline curve fitting to precisely identify threshold effects at 25.0 kg/m2. Moreover, we performed comprehensive subgroup analyses and interaction tests, demonstrating the consistency and robustness of our findings across different clinical strata. This precise threshold identification provides observational insights that may contribute to perioperative risk assessment and could potentially inform future prospective research exploring nutritional optimization strategies in critically ill cardiac surgery patients.

The differential BMI–mortality associations observed may be explained by the interplay of metabolic reserves, inflammatory response, and tolerance to surgical stress[25, 26]. Low BMI often indicates malnutrition[27], limiting nutrient supply and energy for critical illness recovery. In the ICU, heightened metabolic demand in malnourished patients can accelerate fat and muscle depletion, increasing risk. In contrast, moderate overweight may provide protective energy reserves during catabolic stress, whereas severe obesity can compromise outcomes due to inflammatory burden and technical challenges[28]. Our data (Table 1) show underweight patients were older, with lower BUN and SCr, reflecting higher malnutrition risk[29, 30]. Selection bias may also contribute[8], as obese patients are sometimes admitted for close monitoring despite being physiologically less compromised, leading to an association between higher BMI and younger age (Table 1). In addition, obese patients often receive lower weight-based dosages of fluids and vasopressors, possibly reducing adverse therapy effects[8]. Furthermore, obesity independently heightens chronic disease risk[31], which may explain the diminishing protective effects at higher BMI levels. This finding merits consideration in perioperative risk assessment, though retrospective analyses cannot establish causality.

This investigation demonstrates several methodological strengths that enhance clinical applicability. Our multicenter database encompasses 8032 cardiac surgery patients across 208 hospitals. Post-hoc power analysis confirmed > 99% power to detect observed effect sizes, supporting robust generalizability of our findings [32]. We systematically adjusted for demographic characteristics, laboratory parameters, disease severity scores, comorbidities, and therapeutic interventions to minimize confounding. Importantly, our analytical framework employs generalized additive models with penalized spline curve fitting to detect non-linear relationships and threshold effects. Missing data were handled through multiple imputation to ensure analytical integrity. Subgroup analyses across clinically relevant variables showed consistent non-significant interactions (all P > 0.16), supporting broad applicability. The precise quantification of an optimal BMI threshold at 25.0 kg/m2 may inform perioperative risk assessment, though prospective validation is needed to establish clinical utility.

This study has several limitations. First, unmeasured factors beyond BMI could confound the associations. While BMI is the WHO-endorsed standard, the eICU–CRD database lacks nutritional biomarkers, sarcopenia indices, and frailty assessments—an inherent limitation of retrospective research. However, BMI remains the most feasible metric, providing foundational evidence for future studies. Second, our outcome captured in-hospital mortality only, as the eICU–CRD v2.0 database lacks linkage to external death registries, likely underestimating true 30-day mortality. However, in-hospital mortality remains clinically important in cardiac surgery. Third, inclusion criteria restricted to ICU stays of 24 h to 30 days and exclusion of extreme BMI values may limit generalizability to patients with very brief or prolonged ICU stays or extreme BMI. Fourth, this study's reliance on U.S. data (2014–2015) represents an important temporal limitation. While advances in minimally invasive techniques and ICU care have occurred, the BMI–mortality relationship may reflects fundamental biological mechanisms that remain physiologically relevant, and traditional surgeries remain standard for high-risk patients. Nevertheless, geographic and temporal variations may limit generalizability. Prospective validation in contemporary cohorts is warranted. Fifth, the observational design establishes association but not causality.

Conclusion

In summary, this multicenter study of 8,032 cardiac surgery patients found a U-shaped relationship between BMI and in-hospital mortality, with an optimal threshold identified at 25.0 kg/m2—a level not previously quantified. These results suggest evidence-based BMI targets for clinical practice. Further research is needed to confirm these thresholds in other populations and to explore interventions for optimizing BMI-related outcomes.

The eICU-CRD v2.0 database, collaboratively developed by Philips Healthcare and the MIT Laboratory for Computational Physiology, was used in this study. This publicly accessible resource contains de-identified clinical data from 200,859 ICU admissions across 208 U.S. hospitals between 2014 and 2015. Researchers may obtain access after completing the required registration and training. Database access: https://physionet.org/content/eicu-crd/2.0/.
