A deep learning–based digital biopsy for predicting early recurrence in gastric cancer
Authors: Ping’an Ding, Sheng Chen, Honghai Guo, Sen Yang, Xiyue Wang, Xiao Han, Jiaxuan Yang, Haotian Wu, Jiaxiang Wu, Yuan Tian, Wenqian Ma, Xiaolong Li, Zhenjiang Guo, Renjun Gu, Lilong Zhang, Ning Meng, Yueping Liu, Lingjiao Meng, Qun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly postoperative recurrence is a major cause of treatment failure in patients with locally advanced gastric cancer (LAGC), yet current staging systems inadequately capture the biological heterogeneity that underlies recurrence risk. Here, we introduce a clinically interpretable multimodal prediction model, Recurrence Stratification and Assessment (RSA), which integrates deep learning–derived histopathological features from routine hematoxylin and eosin slides with conventional clinical variables. The model was developed using a retrospective multicenter cohort (n = 1,763) and rigorously validated across two internal cohorts, two geographically distinct external cohorts, and an exploratory post-hoc analysis of a prospective clinical trial population (NCT01516944), demonstrating robust and generalizable performance (area under the curves ranging from 0.843 to 0.887). Shapley Additive Explanations-based interpretation identifies key histological features contributing to recurrence risk. To explore biological underpinnings, we perform transcriptomic sequencing and immune profiling on tumor specimens, revealing immune-enriched microenvironments and elevated checkpoint gene expression in the RSA-defined low-risk group. These findings suggest differential immunological activity may influence recurrence dynamics. This study demonstrates the application of digital pathology–based artificial intelligence for recurrence risk prediction in LAGC, offering not only a high-performance and biologically informed tool, but also a transparent framework for clinical deployment. The RSA model may support risk-adapted postoperative surveillance and provides a biologically informed framework for exploring the potential utility of immune checkpoint inhibitors.Subject terms: Gastric cancer, Cancer models, Machine learning, Cancer imaging, Cancer genomics

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a global health challenge, particularly in East Asia, where it ranks among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality1,2. In China, approximately 50% of the world’s new gastric cancer cases are diagnosed, and about 80% of these patients present with locally advanced or late-stage disease at diagnosis3,4. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, perioperative management, and adjuvant therapies, early postoperative recurrence remains common in patients with locally advanced gastric cancer (LAGC), with approximately 40–60% of patients experiencing recurrence after treatment, which severely impacts their prognosis and overall survival5,6. Accurate prediction of early recurrence remains difficult, as current staging systems primarily rely on pathological TNM classifications, which are insufficient to account for the considerable biological heterogeneity and clinical variability observed in gastric cancer7–9. Consequently, many patients who are deemed low-risk by traditional staging systems still experience rapid recurrence, highlighting an urgent clinical need for improved predictive methodologies.

Previous efforts to enhance recurrence prediction in LAGC have predominantly focused on traditional clinical and pathological parameters such as tumor stage, histological subtype, and lymph node metastasis10–12. The risk stratification methods derived from these parameters remain suboptimal, with concordance index or area under the ROC curve (AUC) values typically ranging from 0.740 to 0.780; moreover, these representative models are often developed on relatively small, single-center cohorts (sample sizes generally 200–500 patients) and lack robust external or prospective validation13–15. Their limited performance is further attributable to methodological shortcomings: they rely on human-defined “hand-crafted” pathological features and limited computational power, making them incapable of quantifying the vast, subtle information critical to tumor prognosis embedded within digital histopathology slides. This failure to adequately capture the intricate features of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and histopathological heterogeneity, which are increasingly recognized as critical determinants of tumor aggressiveness and recurrence patterns, greatly limits their generalizability and clinical applicability in real-world settings16,17.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, has shown great promise in extracting high-dimensional morphological features from histopathological slides18–25. These techniques enable the objective, reproducible quantification of tumor architecture and microenvironmental patterns using routine H&E-stained images26–28. While several studies have explored AI-driven pathology for outcome prediction in gastrointestinal cancers, most have focused on early-stage gastric cancer or other tumor types, with limited applicability to the more aggressive and heterogeneous LAGC29–33. Moreover, few studies have integrated deep learning-derived histopathological features with clinical data in a multimodal framework, and even fewer have conducted large-scale multicenter or prospective validation specific to LAGC.

Here, we show the Recurrence Stratification and Assessment (RSA) model, a multimodal deep learning tool for predicting early postoperative recurrence in LAGC. Methodologically, RSA is superior to prior work as it integrates digital pathology–derived features from routine H&E slides with conventional clinical variables. We rigorously validated this high-performance model across a large, multicenter cohort from six Chinese hospitals and further confirmed its clinical applicability using data from a prospective clinical trial (NCT 01516944). Our primary objective was to establish this accurate, multimodal prediction tool. Our secondary objective involved leveraging transcriptomic and immune profiling to reveal the biological underpinnings of recurrence risk, thereby providing actionable insights for potential immunotherapeutic strategies. This study applies digital pathology-driven AI to LAGC recurrence risk prediction, delivering a clinically interpretable and biologically grounded framework that bridges surgical oncology, pathology, and precision medicine.

Results

Patient characteristics

The final study cohort comprised 1763 eligible patients with LAGC. These individuals were systematically apportioned across the three main study arms: the training cohort constituted 855 patients (48.5% of the total), the internal validation cohort included 410 patients (23.3%), and the external validation cohort contained 498 patients (28.2%) (Fig. 1D). Complete clinicopathological profiles for the six contributing medical centers are presented in Supplementary Table 7. Initial demographic and pathological metrics demonstrated a general equilibrium across all cohorts, with no statistically significant discrepancies observed (all P > 0.05).

Predictive models were constructed using several machine learning algorithms, including support vector machine, decision tree, random forest, and logistic regression. These individual models were subsequently integrated into an ensemble framework to improve overall predictive performance. Model robustness and accuracy were evaluated using ROC curves and radar plots. D Model Validation: The final ensemble model was validated across multiple independent cohorts, including two internal validation cohorts (n = 410), two external validation cohorts (n = 498), a prospective cohort (n = 456), and the TCGA cohort (n = 251). Of the total patient population, 1154 individuals (65.5%) were male, contrasting with 609 females (34.5%). The sex of participants was determined based on self-report. Sex and gender were considered in the study design, and the baseline characteristics (including sex and age) were balanced across cohorts as detailed in Supplementary Table 7. The overall median age for the cohort was 58.0 years (IQR: 45.0–66.0). A highly consistent incidence of early postoperative recurrence was documented across the groups: 451 patients (52.7%) in the training cohort, 222 patients (54.1%) in the internal validation cohort, and 281 patients (56.4%) in the external validation cohort experienced this outcome. Detailed comparisons of clinicopathological factors stratified by recurrence status are summarized in Supplementary Table 8.

Construction and validation of the RSA predictive model

The development of the predictive system began with a pathology-driven ensemble model for early recurrence, achieving an AUC of 0.853 (95% CI: 0.828–0.878), sensitivity of 0.816, and specificity of 0.740 in the training cohort (Supplementary Fig. 2C–G and Supplementary Table 9). The model maintained high-performance across internal (AUCs: 0.843–0.847) and external cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 3), with negative predictive value (NPV) > 0.740 and positive predictive value (PPV) > 0.770. The final RSA model (Fig. 2A, B) was formalized by integrating coefficients of independent predictors (pathological type, pT stage, pN stage, and selected pathology features) identified via multivariate logistic regression (Supplementary Table 10). This RSA model registered an AUC of 0.887 (95% CI: 0.864–0.909) in the training cohort (Fig. 2C and E, Supplementary Fig. 4A, and Supplementary Table 11). The performance gain over the clinical-only model (AUC = 0.746, 95% CI: 0.713–0.779) was confirmed by DeLong’s test (P < 0.001), along with significant quantitative improvements: net reclassification improvement (NRI) = 0.531 (95% CI: 0.397–0.668; P < 0.001) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) = 0.273 (95% CI: 0.244–0.304; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 12). Calibration curves confirmed strong predicted-to-observed concordance (Supplementary Fig. 4B). To confirm the robustness of the 24-month early recurrence cutoff, a comprehensive sensitivity analysis using 12-month and 18-month cutoffs was performed across the training cohorts (Supplementary Table 13). The RSA model maintained consistently strong predictive performance (AUCs: 0.859–0.887), confirming that its discriminative ability is robust regardless of the specific time-point used for defining early recurrence.

Survival analysis validated this stratification based on the RSA model’s optimal cutoff value: high-risk patients had markedly poorer 5-year OS compared to low-risk patients (42.8% vs. 59.4%; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Multivariate Cox regression confirmed the RSA model as an independent prognostic factor for 5-year OS (Supplementary Table 14). Subgroup analyses [peripheral blood tumor markers (CEA/CA72-4/CA199)/HER2 expression] consistently showed the RSA model surpassed single-feature models (Fig. 2G, Supplementary Figs. 4D and 5, and Supplementary Table 15), with prognostic distinction persisting across all subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 6). The model’s superior robustness was established through tenfold cross-validation (Fig. 2H) and 1000-iteration bootstrap resampling (Mean AUC = 0.8864, 95% CI: 0.8857–0.8871) (Fig. 2F). Clinically, concentric circle plots demonstrated that the RSA model better identified low-risk individuals (46.7% vs. 42.1%) (Supplementary Fig. 4E), and decision curve analysis affirmed the superior clinical net benefit (Fig. 2D).

Internal validation of the RSA model

To rigorously assess the model’s performance beyond the training dataset, we retrospectively analyzed an internal cohort of 410 LAGC patients from the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (FHHMU). This internal validation cohort was longitudinally separated based on diagnosis time: Internal Validation Set I (Jan 2012–Jan 2014, n = 215) and Internal Validation Set II (Jan 2017–Jan 2019, n = 195) (Fig. 3A). Both subgroups exhibited balanced clinical characteristics, demonstrating no significant baseline differences (all P > 0.05, Supplementary Table 16).

In Set I, the RSA model proved superior to both the clinical-only and pathology-only models, achieving a discriminative accuracy of AUC = 0.881 (95%CI: 0.833–0.929) (Fig. 3B, D, and E, Supplementary Fig. 7A, and Supplementary Table 17). The significance of this enhanced prediction was confirmed by DeLong’s test (P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 12). Furthermore, the RSA model provided quantifiable superiority over the clinical model alone, marked by a NRI of 0.531 (95% CI: 0.396–0.675; P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.273 (95% CI: 0.244–0.303; P < 0.001). Model calibration also indicated strong concordance (Brier score: 0.128; Supplementary Fig. 7B). Comparable results were secured in Set II (Fig. 3C, F, and G, Supplementary Fig. 7C, D, and Supplementary Table 17). Visualization via concentric circle plots verified that the RSA model efficiently classified more early recurrence cases into the high-risk group than the clinical model in both Set I (41.9% vs. 37.7%, Supplementary Fig. 7E) and Set II (50.3% vs. 39.5%, Supplementary Fig. 7F). The practical clinical utility was further evidenced by decision curve analysis across both cohorts (Fig. 3H, I). Prognostic evaluation via survival analysis stratified patients effectively, showing that low-risk individuals possessed significantly greater 5-year OS rates than high-risk individuals in Set I (61.4% vs. 44.6%, P = 0.006) and Set II (60.3% vs. 35.0%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3J, K). Lastly, multivariate Cox regression confirmed that the RSA classification retained its status as an independent prognostic indicator for 5-year OS across both validation cohorts (Set I: HR = 2.581, 95%CI: 1.672–3.985, P < 0.001; Set II: HR = 2.646, 95%CI: 1.728–4.052, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables 18 and 19).

The design incorporated a geographically stratified approach to address regional variability: External Validation Set I comprised 286 patients from three medical centers in Northern China (Shijiazhuang People’s Hospital (SJZPH), Baoding Central Hospital (BDCH), and HSCPH), while External Validation Set II consisted of 212 patients from two medical centers in Southern China (Wuhan University (WHPH) and Nanjing Jinling Hospital (NJJLH)) (Fig. 4A). In Set I, the RSA model demonstrated superior prediction capability for early postoperative recurrence compared to single-feature models, registering an AUC of 0.882 (95% CI: 0.84–0.923, Fig. 4B and F, Supplementary Fig. 8A, B, and Supplementary Table 21). The improvement over the clinical-only model was notable, quantified by an NRI of 0.634 (P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.263 (P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 12). Furthermore, calibration curves supported the accuracy of its predictions (Brier score = 0.129; Supplementary Fig. 8C). Comparable outcomes were obtained in Set II (Fig. 4C and G, Supplementary Fig. 8D–F, and Supplementary Table 21). The model’s clinical benefit was consistently affirmed by decision curve analysis across both external cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 8G, H). Examination of concentric circle plots indicated that the RSA model successfully captured a higher proportion of early recurrence instances within the designated high-risk category when contrasted with the clinical-only model (Set I: 49.7% vs. 40.9%; Set II: 44.8% vs. 39.6%) (Fig. 4D, E). Analysis of long-term outcomes showed that high-risk patients had markedly poorer 5-year OS rates compared to low-risk patients in both external validation sets (Set I: 38.7% vs. 59.3%, P < 0.001; Set II: 40.4% vs. 61.2%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4H, I). This prognostic capability was finalized by Multivariate Cox regression, which maintained the RSA model’s standing as an independent prognostic predictor for 5-year OS within both external cohorts (Set I: HR = 2.677, 95%CI: 1.847–3.881, P < 0.001; Set II: HR = 2.701, 95%CI: 1.759–4.150, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables 22 and 23).

Evaluate the real-world applicability of the RSA model

To ascertain the practical utility of the RSA model in a clinical environment, an exploratory post-hoc analysis was executed using records from the multicenter prospective clinical trial NCT01516944. Following stringent inclusion/exclusion protocols, 456 patients were identified for inclusion. The cohort breakdown included: 106 individuals undergoing primary surgical resection, 168 receiving XELOX (oxaliplatin plus capecitabine) neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and 182 receiving SOX (oxaliplatin plus S-1) neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 9A). The comprehensive clinicopathological profiles are detailed in Supplementary Table 24, with clinical outcome reports referenced in prior publications34.

In the cohort of patients undergoing primary surgery, the RSA model demonstrated excellent discriminative power for predicting early postoperative recurrence, exhibiting an AUC of 0.873 (95% CI: 0.806–0.940), outperforming various single-feature prediction tools (Fig. 5A and C, Supplementary Fig. 9B–D and Supplementary Table 25). When compared against the baseline clinical model, the RSA instrument achieved meaningful enhancements in prediction accuracy, evidenced by an NRI of 0.708 (95% CI: 0.360–1.075; P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.264 (95% CI: 0.179–0.352; P < 0.001), with similar gains noted versus the pathology-only model (Supplementary Table 12). Graphical analysis using concentric circles showed that, compared to clinical methods, the RSA method was able to correctly identify more low-risk individuals without early recurrence (38.7% vs. 22.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 9F). The model’s superior clinical net benefit compared to alternative single-feature models was validated by decision curve analysis (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, survival curve assessment demonstrated that individuals categorized as low-risk experienced significantly enhanced 5-year OS rates versus high-risk patients (63.5% vs. 38.9%, P = 0.007) (Fig. 5G).

Conversely, for the cohort receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the RSA model yielded a reduced discriminative capacity for early recurrence, achieving an AUC of 0.741 (95% CI: 0.689–0.793) (Fig. 5B, E, F, and I, Supplementary Fig. 9E, G, and I, and Supplementary Tables 12 and 25). No noteworthy correlation was identified between the model’s risk stratification and chemotherapy efficacy outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 9J). Nonetheless, the prognostic value held true, as high-risk patients designated by the RSA tool still showed significantly worse 5-year OS outcomes compared to their low-risk counterparts (41.8% vs. 58.3%, P = 0.002) (Fig. 5H). To directly quantify the added clinical value of the RSA model in clinical decision-making, we performed a head-to-head comparison against the 8th AJCC TNM staging system, stratified by treatment regimen. In the direct surgery subgroup, RSA (AUC = 0.873, 95% CI: 0.806–0.940) significantly outperformed TNM staging (AUC = 0.672, 95% CI: 0.567–0.778; DeLong P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). Furthermore, in the neoadjuvant chemotherapy subgroup, RSA (AUC = 0.741, 95% CI: 0.689–0.793) remained superior to TNM staging (AUC = 0.611, 95% CI: 0.552–0.669; DeLong P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 10C, D).

Validation of the RSA model using TCGA dataset

The RSA model’s clinical generalizability was assessed using the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. After applying selection criteria, 251 gastric cancer patients were included (Fig. 6A). In this independent population, the RSA model provided significantly superior predictive performance for postoperative recurrence (AUC = 0.782, 95% CI: 0.711–0.852) compared to the standalone clinical model (AUC = 0.619, 95% CI: 0.548–0.689) in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 6C and E, Supplementary Fig. 11A, and Supplementary Table 26). Specifically, the RSA model improved risk stratification by identifying a higher proportion of low-risk patients who did not recur (61.4% vs. 39.4%) and more accurately predicting recurrence within the high-risk group (23.5% vs. 19.9%) (Supplementary Fig. 11B). This clinical utility was further evidenced by decision curve analysis (Fig. 6D). The model’s robust diagnostic ability persisted across the four primary TCGA molecular subtypes (CIN, EBV, GS, and MSI) (Fig. 6F, Supplementary Fig. 12, and Supplementary Table 27). Prognostic evaluation was also confirmed: the high-risk group defined by RSA showed significantly worse 5-year OS (log-rank P = 0.003) compared to the low-risk group (Supplementary Fig. 11C).

To further evaluate its ability to refine prognosis across molecular landscapes, the RSA model was applied to the Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG) classification35. 217 patients from the training cohort were randomly selected for immunohistochemical analysis of eight relevant proteins (E-cadherin, Vimentin, N-cadherin, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and P53) to define four ACRG subtypes: MSI (45 patients, 20.7%), MSS/EMT (38 patients, 17.5%), MSS/TP53− (80 patients, 36.9%), and MSS/TP53+ (54 patients, 24.9%) (Fig. 6B). Within each of these defined ACRG categories, the RSA model maintained superior predictive accuracy compared to single-feature alternatives (Fig. 6G, H, Supplementary Fig. 11D, and Supplementary Table 28). Crucially, survival analysis within every ACRG subtype confirmed that RSA-defined high-risk patients suffered significantly poorer 5-year OS (log-rank P values: MSI P = 0.004; MSS/EMT P = 0.017; MSS/TP53− P < 0.001; MSS/TP53+ P = 0.007) (Fig. 6I). These findings collectively demonstrate that the RSA model offers comprehensive prognostic insights that extend beyond established molecular classifications.

Prognostic enhancement and model interpretability

Next, we evaluated the clinical utility of the RSA model to determine whether it enhances the ability to predict survival when applied within the context of the traditional TNM staging system. Stratified Kaplan–Meier analyses across patients with LAGC demonstrated that the RSA model successfully classified patients into high- and low-risk groups exhibiting significantly different OS outcomes across all individual TNM stages (I, II, and III) (all log-rank P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 13). To establish the robustness of this finding and minimize potential confounding from clinical variables, a 1:1 propensity ccore matching analysis was subsequently performed, with baseline characteristics detailed in Supplementary Table 29. Survival evaluations consistently showed a worse prognosis for the RSA-defined high-risk groups after PSM: Stage I (pre-PSM: 47.5% vs. 81.4%, P < 0.001, Fig. 7A; post-PSM: 44.8% vs. 86.2%, P < 0.001, Fig. 7D); Stage II (pre-PSM: 48.8% vs. 68.9%, P < 0.001, Fig. 7B; post-PSM: 49.6% vs. 66.4%, P = 0.003, Fig. 7E); and Stage III (pre-PSM: 34.5% vs. 51.7%, P < 0.001, Fig. 7C; post-PSM: 38.0% vs. 53.0%, P < 0.001, Fig. 7F). These compelling results demonstrate that the RSA model offers significant additional prognostic value to the current TNM staging system. Furthermore, multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed the RSA classification as an independent prognostic predictor for 5-year OS within each TNM stage stratum (Supplementary Tables 30–32).

Given the inherent complexity of ensemble models such as RSA, we adopted the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework to provide a simplified, clarified interpretation of the model’s predictions. SHAP values were utilized to quantify the additive contribution of each feature to the model’s output, where higher values reflect greater feature importance. Utilizing this framework, we successfully identified eight key histopathological features with substantial impacts on recurrence prediction. The model’s predictive insights were comprehensively presented across datasets using SHAP summary bee swarm plots (Supplementary Fig. 14) and feature importance plots (Fig. 7G). To illustrate the critical role of these microscopic features, Fig. 7H presents a clinical comparison of two representative patients who, despite sharing identical clinical characteristics and treatments, exhibited divergent clinical outcomes based solely on differences in their underlying histopathological features.

Immune status and immunotherapy potential in gastric cancer patients stratified by the RSA model

To investigate molecular biological differences between high-risk and low-risk groups classified by our model, we compared samples from the TCGA and FHHMU gastric cancer cohorts (Fig. 8A). Differential gene expression was identified using edgeR (Supplementary Fig. 15A), followed by GSEA and GSVA analyses, which revealed significant enrichment of immune and inflammatory pathways, including IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, inflammatory response, and interferon gamma response, in the low-risk group (Fig. 8B, C and Supplementary Fig. 15B). ESTIMATE analyses demonstrated higher stromal, immune, and comprehensive ESTIMATE scores in the low-risk group, while the high-risk group showed higher tumor purity, indicating distinct immune microenvironments (Supplementary Fig. 15C).

We further characterized immune infiltration heterogeneity using MCPcounter, observing increased immune cell infiltration in the low-risk group (Supplementary Fig. 15D). CIBERSORT analysis revealed a notable abundance of M0 macrophages in high-risk tumors (Fig. 8D and Supplementary Fig. 15E). Additionally, the expression of immune checkpoint genes, particularly those related to T cell co-stimulation such as TNFRSF4, PDCD1LG2, and PDCD1, as well as those associated with immune checkpoint blockade therapy including LAG3, TIGIT, and CTLA4, was significantly elevated in the low-risk group (Supplementary Fig. 15F, G). A multicellular community analysis identified higher abundance of cell cluster CE6 in low-risk patients (Fig. 8E and Supplementary Fig. 15H). This combined profile of high immune activation and high checkpoint expression is characteristic of a T-cell-inflamed TME and suggests a state of adaptive immune resistance36,37. IFNγ and T-cell signature scoring further supported this finding, demonstrating higher scores for IFNγ-related and effector T-cell signatures in the low-risk group (Fig. 8F).

Consistent results were obtained in the FHHMU cohort. Transcriptome sequencing indicated global upregulation of immune (interferon-γ, interferon-α responses) and inflammatory (IL6/JAK/STAT3, TNFα, IL2/STAT5 signaling, inflammatory responses) pathways in low-risk patients (Fig. 8G–I and Supplementary Fig. 16A, B). Similar to TCGA findings, ESTIMATE and immune infiltration analyses showed significantly elevated immune-related scores and increased infiltration of immune cells, particularly T cells and neutrophils, in the low-risk group (Fig. 8J and Supplementary Fig. 16C–E). Additionally, immune checkpoint gene expression related to T-cell activation was significantly higher in low-risk patients (Supplementary Fig. 16F, G), corroborating the adaptive immune resistance profile observed in the TCGA cohort, and multicellular community mapping highlighted increased abundance of CE3 and CE9 in low-risk samples (Fig. 8K and Supplementary Fig. 16H). IFNγ and T-cell signature scoring in the FHHMU cohort corroborated these findings, with significantly higher scores observed in the low-risk group (Fig. 8L).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and rigorously validated a deep learning-integrated multimodal predictive model, the RSA model, to stratify early postoperative recurrence risk in patients with LAGC. By leveraging quantitative histopathological features derived from digital biopsy approaches and integrating conventional clinical-pathological parameters, our model demonstrated significantly enhanced predictive accuracy compared to traditional clinical staging systems alone. Notably, the RSA model consistently achieved robust predictive performance across diverse internal and external cohorts, as well as a prospective clinical trial cohort. Comprehensive bioinformatics analyses further revealed marked differences in immune and inflammatory signatures between high- and low-risk groups, with prominent immune activation and elevated expression of immune checkpoint genes observed predominantly in low-risk patients. These findings suggest the RSA model not only provides superior prognostic stratification but also highlights subgroups that might particularly benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies, underscoring its potential clinical utility.

Several studies have previously explored predictive models for postoperative recurrence in gastric cancer, predominantly utilizing conventional clinical-pathological variables such as tumor size, histological subtype, lymph node metastasis, and TNM staging10–12. For instance, Ma et al. reported a nomogram incorporating clinicopathological variables, achieving moderate accuracy (AUC approximately 0.780) in predicting gastric cancer recurrence11. Similarly, Wang et al. developed a prognostic model using basic clinical data and achieved acceptable predictive performance; however, this model was limited by a lack of multicenter validation10. Our RSA model substantially improves upon these limitations, demonstrating higher predictive accuracy (AUC consistently above 0.85) across multicenter external validations, including geographically diverse populations. This improved performance likely stems from the integration of deep learning-derived digital pathology features, effectively capturing subtle morphological cues reflecting tumor aggressiveness that traditional clinical models fail to detect. Moreover, unlike previous studies, our model underwent rigorous multicenter and prospective trial validation, thereby significantly enhancing its robustness, reproducibility, and potential clinical translation13,33.

Recent advances in AI have prompted increasing interest in digital pathology-based predictive modeling. Xu et al. reported a deep learning-based model utilizing histological images to predict prognosis in gastric cancer, yielding an AUC of approximately 0.809 in validation cohorts38. Similarly, Jiang et al. employed convolutional neural networks for recurrence prediction in colorectal cancer, highlighting digital pathology’s capability to capture prognostic information beyond routine clinical-pathological parameters39. Relative to these and other published deep learning models, the RSA model’s robust performance (AUCs 0.843–0.887) demonstrates superior predictive capability, particularly due to its multimodal integration and rigorous multi-center and prospective validation40–44. Our results demonstrate that deep learning-driven digital biopsy features significantly enhance traditional predictive indicators, providing additional independent prognostic value. The comprehensive comparison in our study between RSA and conventional clinical models consistently demonstrated superior discriminatory power and clinical utility, validated by NRI and IDI analyses. Collectively, these comparisons underscore the added value of integrating deep learning approaches into standard prognostic frameworks. To bridge the gap between abstract AI outputs and clinical insights, we anchored our final feature set to recognizable histopathological concepts. Specifically, some of the manually extracted high-contribution features, such as Granularity_6_Eosin, Granularity_7_Hematoxylin, and Granularity_12_Hematoxylin, essentially reflect the textural complexity and granularity of the images under different staining conditions. These quantifiable features are known to correspond to pathological patterns such as heterogeneity of nuclear chromatin distribution and cytoplasmic texture variations, which are closely related to cellular proliferative activity and malignant progression38.

At a molecular level, our transcriptomic and immune profiling analyses revealed striking biological distinctions between RSA-defined high-risk and low-risk patients. Specifically, the low-risk group showed significant enrichment in immune- and inflammation-related pathways, including IL6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, interferon-γ response, and various inflammatory signaling cascades. Prior literature has established the dual role of inflammation and immune activation in tumor progression and immunotherapy response45,46. For example, the interferon-γ pathway is well-recognized for its critical role in enhancing anti-tumor immunity, primarily by activating effector T cells and promoting antigen presentation47. Our results further confirm these observations, showing elevated immune checkpoint gene expression and increased infiltration of immune-effector cells in low-risk patients, consistent with a more immunologically “hot” TME. The co-existence of activation and checkpoint upregulation may reflect an active but complex adaptive immune response attempting to control the tumor, thus conferring a lower likelihood of recurrence. This phenotype is frequently associated with responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade, suggesting a potential therapeutic vulnerability. However, we acknowledge that this translational suggestion remains purely hypothesis-driven and is not supported by clinical efficacy data within our study; therefore, it requires rigorous clinical validation in future prospective trials.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. First, despite extensive validation across multiple cohorts, the retrospective nature of the training cohorts remains a potential source of selection and information biases. Future studies involving larger-scale prospective, multi-regional validations are warranted to further establish clinical applicability and global generalizability. Second, although our model included comprehensive clinical-pathological features and deep learning-derived histopathological characteristics, additional biological information, such as genomic mutations, circulating tumor DNA, and metabolomic data were not integrated, potentially missing other clinically relevant predictors. Future research incorporating a comprehensive multi-omics approach (including the integration of genomic, circulating tumor DNA, and metabolomic data) is indispensable to fully capture the spectrum of tumor heterogeneity and further enhance predictive performance and biological interpretability. Furthermore, large-scale, prospective, multi-regional validation studies across diverse patient populations are essential to confirm the robustness of our model and ensure its clinical generalizability globally. Third, we acknowledge that the predictive performance of the RSA model was substantially reduced in the neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC subgroup (AUC = 0.741). This limitation is likely driven by therapy-induced histological alterations (e.g., fibrosis and necrosis) which disrupt the correlation of pathomics features learned from chemo-naïve tissues. This suggests the model’s generalizability is limited in the complex multimodal treatment setting. Future work will prioritize larger, dedicated NAC cohorts and the integration of treatment-related features (e.g., tumor regression grade) to improve robustness in this critical subgroup. Furthermore, our deep learning model, though highly predictive, remains partially a “black box,” limiting immediate clinical interpretability. Despite our utilization of SHAP values to improve feature interpretability, further methodological advancements in explainable AI are necessary to enhance clinical adoption and trust among clinicians. The SHAP analysis presented in this study, while focusing on the pathohistological features, was applied to the full multimodal RSA model to accurately quantify the contribution of all integrated variables (pathological and clinical) to the final risk prediction. Furthermore, it is important to note that the SHAP values quantify the features’ predictive contribution (correlation) to the model output rather than establishing strict biological or clinical causal relationships. The case-level interpretability is key to demonstrating the independent and non-redundant prognostic power provided by the digital biopsy features.

In conclusion, we successfully developed and validated a deep learning-integrated multimodal predictive model that effectively stratifies early postoperative recurrence risk in patients with LAGC. The RSA model significantly outperformed traditional clinical staging systems across multiple internal, external, and prospective validation cohorts, providing robust and generalizable prognostic stratification. Additionally, immune profiling and transcriptomic analyses uncovered distinct immunological differences between RSA-defined high- and low-risk groups, highlighting significant immune activation and elevated immune checkpoint gene expression in the low-risk subset. These findings strongly support the RSA model’s potential for clinical integration, enabling personalized postoperative surveillance strategies and generating hypotheses for future immunotherapy decisions in gastric cancer management. Our findings contribute to the development of explainable and generalizable AI tools in oncology, tools that not only predict outcomes but also guide future therapeutic strategies, bringing digital pathology to the forefront of clinical decision-making.

Methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and all relevant ethical regulations. The study protocol and all procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the FHHMU (Approval Number: 2024KY127). Specific informed consent for this project was waived by the respective ethics commissions because this study only involves a retrospective, anonymized analysis of archival pathology samples. For the prospective transcriptomic analysis, written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. No compensation was provided to participants in the retrospective cohorts, while participants in the prospective sequencing cohort received no financial compensation beyond standard clinical care. This study was designed and reported in compliance with the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-Artificial Intelligence (STARD-AI) guidelines48.

Study cohort

This study retrospectively analyzed patients with LAGC treated between January 2012 and December 2019. This multicenter study included patients with LAGC from six tertiary hospitals in China: the FHHMU, SJZPH, BDCH, and Hengshui People’s Hospital (HSPH) in northern China, as well as Renmin Hospital of WHPH and NJJLH in southern China.

A total of 1763 patients were retrospectively enrolled, comprising 855 in the training cohort, 410 in the internal validation cohort, and 498 in the external validation cohort. Eligible patients were ≥18 years old, underwent curative gastrectomy confirmed by postoperative pathology, and received preoperative contrast-enhanced CT within 14 days before surgery. Patients who had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy before surgery, died within 90 days postoperatively, were lost to follow-up, or had incomplete CT or hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) data were excluded.

This study additionally retrospectively analyzed data from an independent prospective validation cohort (NCT 01516944, n = 456), which was divided into two subgroups: 106 patients who underwent primary surgery and 350 patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy (XELOX regimen: oxaliplatin + capecitabine, n = 168; SOX regimen: oxaliplatin + S-1, n = 182). Importantly, this validation cohort was used exclusively to evaluate the model’s generalizability across various treatment regimens and real-world clinical settings, with no data from it utilized for model training, parameter adjustment, or fine-tuning. In this multicenter randomized phase III trial34, perioperative chemotherapy was shown to significantly improve R0 resection rates and overall survival compared to adjuvant chemotherapy alone, while the SOX regimen demonstrated non-inferiority to XELOX in terms of disease-free survival.

To explore the biological significance of the identified features, we prospectively collected fresh tumor specimens from 120 LAGC patients for transcriptomic sequencing, followed by functional enrichment analysis. Detailed protocols for RNA processing, sequencing, and analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

All procedures in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating institutions. Written informed consent was waived for the retrospective component, while informed consent was obtained from all patients included in the transcriptomic analysis.

Clinical outcomes

All patients received postoperative management in accordance with the guidelines of the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology49 and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network7. Fluorouracil-based adjuvant chemotherapy was recommended for those with adequate physical condition, and all patients underwent standardized follow-up, including clinical assessments and appropriate laboratory, imaging, or endoscopic examinations. Follow-up was conducted every 3–6 months during the first two years after surgery, every 6 months during years three to five, and annually thereafter. Early postoperative recurrence was defined as recurrence within 24 months after curative resection and was confirmed by imaging, clinical symptoms, or reoperation. This 24-month cutoff aligns with established clinical practice in gastric cancer, as recurrence within this period is strongly associated with distinct tumor biology and poorer long-term prognosis, a criterion supported by prior prognostic studies43,50,51. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause. The primary endpoint was early recurrence, and the secondary endpoint was OS. Follow-up was completed in June 2024, with a median duration of 67 months (range, 57–163 months).

Feature extraction using CellProfiler

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded H&E-stained sections were prepared for all eligible patients. The most representative section for tumor invasion depth was selected by the Director of Pathology at the FHHMU (YPL, 25 years of experience in gastric cancer pathology). This section was defined as the one containing the deepest point of tumor invasion (i.e., the determinant for the most advanced pT stage). All slides were scanned using the KF-SCAN-PL automated digital slide scanner (KFBIO, China) and managed with iViewer software. Under YPL’s supervision, tumor regions were identified, and for each case, a pathologist selected ten non-overlapping regions of interest (ROI) (1000 × 1000 pixels, 0.504 μm/pixel) enriched with tumor cells, defined as areas with dense tumor cell infiltration (evidenced by high nuclear density and characteristic adenocarcinoma architecture) and no obvious non-tumor components (e.g., normal gastric mucosa, adipose tissue (ADI)) or imaging artifacts (Fig. 1A). These ROI were reviewed and confirmed by two additional pathologists (FL and HYD, with 15 and 10 years of experience in gastric cancer pathology, respectively).

Tumor regions were identified under YPL’s supervision. For each case, ten non-overlapping regions of interest (ROI) were selected by YPL and subsequently reviewed by two additional pathologists. These ROIs were strictly defined to ensure consistency: tumor cell density ≥ 70%, and ≤ 5% non-tumor tissue content. To quantify inter-observer agreement, a validation experiment was conducted with 50 randomly sampled cases from the training cohort, stratified by tumor grade (Well-differentiated: n = 10; Moderately differentiated: n = 20; Poorly differentiated: n = 20). Cohen’s Kappa and Intersection over Union (IoU) were used to assess consistency. Detailed results for 50 patients are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Cohen’s Kappa values were 0.893 (95% CI: 0.825–0.961, YPL vs. FL), 0.887 (95% CI: 0.817–0.957, YPL vs. HYD), and 0.884 (95% CI:0.820–0.956, FL vs. HYD), indicating almost perfect agreement (Supplementary Table 2). The IoU values were 0.811 (IQR: 0.778–0.867) for YPL vs. FL, 0.809 (IQR: 0.773–0.838) for YPL vs. HYD, and 0.804 (IQR: 0.768–0.841) for FL vs. HYD, reflecting excellent spatial agreement (Supplementary Fig. 1). Discrepancies, predefined as Cohen’s Kappa <0.7 or IoU <0.7, were resolved via consensus discussion, and no discrepancies were identified in either the validation cases or the full study cohort. These results validate the consistency and reproducibility of the ROI selection process, with final ROIs saved in.tif format to preserve image quality for further histopathological analysis.

Quantitative histopathological features were extracted from the selected ROIs using CellProfiler (version 4.2.6), an open-source image analysis platform developed by the Broad Institute (Cambridge, MA, USA). The “UnmixColors” module was used to separate H&E images into individual grayscale channels for hematoxylin and eosin. The “ColorToGray” module, applying the “Combine” method, further converted the images into grayscale to enable subsequent analysis. Image quality was evaluated using the “MeasureImageQuality” and “MeasureImageIntensity” modules, which assessed characteristics such as blur, intensity, and threshold values. Threshold features were derived via automated Otsu thresholding to distinguish tissue foreground from unstained background38. Pixel-wise co-localization and correlation between hematoxylin and eosin channels were calculated using the “MeasureColocalization” module. Additionally, the “MeasureGranularity” module was used to assess image texture, outputting granularity spectra with a range of 16 scales.

Deep learning-based histopathological feature extraction

We employed deep learning to extract critical histopathological features from 50 annotated whole-slide images (WSI). Each WSI was tiled into non-overlapping 224 × 224-pixel patches and labeled according to the predominant tissue type within the corresponding annotation map. Eight tissue categories associated with the TME were annotated: ADI, debris (DEB), mucus (MUC), muscle (MUS), lymphocyte aggregates (LYM), stroma (STR), normal mucosa (NOR), and tumor epithelium (TUM) (Fig. 1B).

Given the large dataset size (>5 million patches), full-scale training was computationally prohibitive. To ensure representative sampling and prevent bias and data leakage, we used a two-step sampling strategy. First, we performed stratified random sampling by tissue type, ensuring the final subset of 400,000 patches maintained the original distribution: ADI 12.71%, DEB 13.15%, LYM 11.82%, MUC 8.73%, MUS 15.58%, NOR 8.99%, STR 10.57%, TUM 18.45%. Second, we enforced patient-level separation between training (60% of patients) and validation (40% of patients) sets, ensuring no data leakage between sets. Overlapping and non-square edge patches were excluded, and all images underwent preprocessing with normalization techniques, including style transfer, to account for staining variations. Overlapping patches and non-square edge patches (mostly blank) were excluded to remove redundancy and uninformative features. All images underwent normalization, including style transfer to standardize staining variations. The independent HMU-GC-HE-30K dataset with WSIs from patients outside our original 50-WSI cohort was used as an external test set with no training or validation involvement to verify the model’s generalizability.

Model training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU for 50 epochs using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.001) and cross-entropy loss. Among the ResNet18, ResNet34, ResNet50, UNI23, and CONCH24 architectures, ResNet18 achieved the highest accuracy (97.79%) on the external test set (Supplementary Table 3). Using the ResNet18 architecture, the final fully connected layer was removed to create a feature extractor. For feature extraction, all WSIs were tiled using the same patch size, and each patch was passed through the feature extractor. Patch-level features were then averaged to generate 512-dimensional WSI-level deep learning feature vectors.

Histopathological feature selection

A total of 794 features were extracted using both deep learning and CellProfiler-based methods. To identify recurrence-related features, we implemented a three-step selection strategy using the training cohort. First, redundant features were removed using the maximum relevance minimum redundancy algorithm52. Second, a Pearson correlation matrix was applied to eliminate highly correlated features with low relevance to recurrence. Finally, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression with tenfold cross-validation was used for further dimensionality reduction (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B), resulting in eight selected features (Supplementary Table 4).

Development and evaluation of a multimodal model for predicting early postoperative recurrence in LAGC

We constructed a histopathological feature-based prediction model for early postoperative recurrence in LAGC using an ensemble learning strategy (Fig. 1C). This approach incorporated multiple algorithms, including logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, and support vector machine. To ensure the optimal performance of each base learner, we conducted tenfold cross-validation (CV) based hyperparameter tuning (Supplementary Table 5). The base learners were then integrated using a voting regressor (soft voting). This ensemble strategy, which combines predictions by averaging the predicted probabilities of the individual learners, was chosen over alternatives like stacking because it offered superior performance generalizability, particularly higher sensitivity in external cohorts, and better computational efficiency, as validated by CV results across all cohorts (Supplementary Table 6). The voting regressor was ultimately selected as the ensemble meta-estimator.

To complement the pathology-based model, univariate logistic regression was used to assess associations between clinical variables and early recurrence in the training cohort. Significant factors were incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model to build a clinical prediction model. By integrating independent clinical predictors and histopathological features, we developed a multimodal nomogram, referred to as the RSA model.

The recurrence risk score predicted by the RSA model was dichotomized into low- and high-risk categories using the Youden index to maximize the sum of sensitivity and specificity. Model performance was evaluated using several metrics: AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, and F1-score. To quantify improvements in predictive accuracy, we calculated the NRI and IDI. AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test. Calibration curves were plotted to assess agreement between predicted and observed recurrence rates, and overall model performance was evaluated using prediction error curves and the integrated Brier score.

Biological characteristics and immune infiltration

A total of 120 tissue samples were prospectively collected for RNA sequencing and exploratory analysis. Functional enrichment analysis was performed to investigate the biological roles of identified features. Immune cell subtype composition was estimated using CIBERSORTx based on RNA transcriptomic data. Additionally, the EcoTyper framework was applied to infer cell types, cell states, and multicellular ecosystems. Detailed procedures for RNA processing, sequencing, and data analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM) and R version 4.3.3 (http://www.r-project.org). The baseline clinicopathological data (pathological type, pT stage, and pN stage) for the included patients were complete, thereby obviating the need for missing data imputation in model development and validation. Continuous variables were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, while categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the prognostic significance of variables on survival. Detailed statistical procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
