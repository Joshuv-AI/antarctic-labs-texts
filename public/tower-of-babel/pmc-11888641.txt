A Rare Case of Lupus Nephritis with Predominant Gastrointestinal Manifestation: A Case Study
Authors: Jasmine Podimon, Deepa Susan Arons, Adarsh J. Janard, Sunil Mathew, Maria Davis, Anees Ahammed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLupus nephritis (LN) is one of the most dreaded complications of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Gastrointestinal involvement is relatively uncommon and usually manifests as moderate and nonspecific indications. The present case report draws attention to the unusual presentation of SLE as LN with predominant gastrointestinal manifestations. Early detection and treatment of this condition improves the outcome of a potentially fatal ailment.

INTRODUCTION

A patient may develop lupus nephritis (LN) at any time, but it is more common in the early stages of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).[1] SLE is a multisystem autoimmune disease that can affect almost any organ and manifest in many ways.[2] It primarily affects the skin, muscles, joints, kidneys, hematological, and neurological systems, but the damage goes beyond these areas. SLE often causes moderate, nonspecific gastrointestinal dysfunction. This spectrum includes lupus enteritis, a severe form of SLE.[3] Individuals with SLE who have renal disease have higher risks of premature death and morbidity, with corrected death rates ranging from 6 to 26 times higher for those with LN or kidney damage. Age, race, ethnicity, and gender affect disease prognosis. Renal biopsy is the standard method for finding and assessing LN inflammatory responses and chronic impairment and determining the etiology and tailored treatment.[4] Despite the rising corpus of knowledge concerning SLE, extensive studies of LE and cases of it as the main indicator of SLE involvement are rare.[35]

CASE PRESENTATION

A 25-year-old married woman with no medical history complained of painful mouth ulcers, vomiting, skin rash, fatigue, and weight loss. The patient has had painful oral ulcers and swallowing problems for a month and a half, initially treated as pharyngitis at a local clinic. She had incessant vomiting, which was nonbilious, nonprojectile, or nonblood stained. She reported oral ayurvedic treatment caused hyperpigmented lesions on her face, hands, and soles. The nonpruritic, nonscaling lesion started on the forehead and cheeks (not the nasolabial fold) and extended to the palms and soles. Sunlight worsens skin lesions. She was admitted to the dermatology unit after numerous hospital reviews for dermatitis medicamentosa. The prescribed medicines were targeted in the management of anemia (not required). She then reported to our institution in view of the persistence of her symptoms like decreased food intake, fatigue, and significant weight loss (10 kg weight reduction over three months). No chest pain, palpitations, difficulty breathing, fever, headache, joint pain, early morning stiffness, limb claudication (upper or lower limb), hematuria, frothy urine, bone pain, dark-colored stool, loose stool, genital ulcer, epistaxis, hemoptysis, nasal crusting, eye pain or redness, recurrent upper or lower respiratory tract infection, altered sensorium, or she had normal urine, bowel, eating, and sleep routines. She reported pedal edema four months earlier, which cleared spontaneously. She had no familial history of cancer or hematologic diseases.

She appeared to be aware of time, place, and person. Despite her 17.2 kg/m2 BMI, she was poorly built and emaciated. She had dry, lustreless hair, extensive alopecia, and pedal edema. Cyanosis, clubbing, and lymphadenopathy were absent. The cheeks (excluding the nasolabial fold), ears, palms, soles, and upper back had several scabbed and nonpruritic maculopapular hyperpigmented lesions [Figure 1]. Oral candidiasis was also seen. Afebrile, she had a pulse rate of 80/min, stable rhythm, normal volume, and no radio-radial or radio-femoral delay. Every peripheral pulse was palpable. The patient had 110/80 mmHg blood pressure, 20/minute respiration, and 95% SpO2. Her abdomen was normal, with all quadrants moving uniformly with respiration. The abdominal skin was healthy, without dilated veins, lumps, pulsations, or peristalsis. There was no local temperature rise on palpation. However, there was no abdominal tenderness or hepatosplenomegaly. The top margin liver dullness was shown by percussion in the fifth right ICS in MCL, with no evidence of any free fluid. There were no renal or aortic bruits and bowel sounds were positive. No abnormalities were found in the respiratory, cardiovascular, or central nervous systems assessments.

Blood tests revealed that her total count was 6400 cells/cubic millimeter, hemoglobin was 7 g/dl, and ESR was 105 mm/hour. Laboratory tests revealed elevated CRP levels of 22.8 mg/dl (<6 mg/dl), LDH of 519 IU/L (normal: 230-460 IU/L), serum creatinine of 1.7 mg/dl (normal: 0.59–1.04 mg/dl), uric acid of 8.2 mg/dl (normal: 3.5–7.2 mg/dl), urea of 135 mg/dl (normal: 5–20 mg/dl), low A/G ratio of 0.7, and low folic acid level of 2 ng/ml (normal range: 3.1–17.5 ng/ml). The antinuclear antibody (ANA) test was positive (1:80 titer, homogeneous). Complements C 3 and C 4 levels were lowered to 44.3 mg/dL (normal range: 90-180 mg/dL) and 9 mg/dL (normal range: 10-40 mg/dL), respectively. The serum iron level was 37 μg/dl (37–145 μg/dl), total iron binding capacity was 178 ug/dl (normal value: 250–400 ug/dl), transferrin saturation was 21% (normal value: 15–50%), and ferritin was 570.2 ng/ml (normal value: 10–291 ng/ml), peripheral blood smear indicating moderate anisopoikilocytosis, normocytic normochromic anemia, and mild neutrophilia. The HIV, HBsAg, HCV, and VDRL tests were all negative. The Direct Coombs test was positive. Urine examination indicated 20–25 pus cells and albuminuria, with a 24 hour urine protein level of 670 mg/day. The urine protein creatinine ratio was 1.6:1. There was no growth in urine or blood culture. Serum protein electrophoresis, antiphospholipid antibodies, and antiparietal cell antibodies all yielded negative results. The ultrasound abdomen was normal, with no ascites or pleural effusion. Endoscopic examinations, including esophagogastroduodenoscopy, revealed mild pangastritis.

The renal biopsy sections revealed a linear core of cortical renal tissue, 15 glomeruli with intercapillary matrix enlargement, and areas of mild-to-moderate mesangial hypercellularity. Para-mesangial capillary walls thickened, capillary lumina narrowed, and capillary loops became congested. Two glomeruli demonstrate circumferential thickening of capillary walls, resulting in wire loop lesions [Figure 2a] with hyaline thrombi [Figure 2b]. One glomerulus has segmental matrix sclerosis [Figure 2c] and accompanying capsular synechiae. The extraglomerular compartment has a back-to-back configuration of tubules and tiny patchy foci of interstitial fibrosis (<5%) [C.I.-1+]. Tubules are moderately dilated, with frayed edges. The vascular component observed in the parts studied was unremarkable. Direct immunofluorescence revealed full-house staining along mesangial capillary wall deposits, including IgG (1+/2+), IgA (1+), IgM (focal 1+), C3 (1+/2+), C1q (minimum), kappa (1+), and lambda (1+/2+). The kidney biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of localized LN, International Society of Nephrology/kidney Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) Class III (A/C lesions), NIH-modified activity index: 1/24, and NIH-modified chronicity index: 2/12.

One packed red blood cell unit was transfused after cross-matching. After infusing 500 mg of methylprednisolone IV for three days, the initial symptoms improved quickly and completely. After that, the patient was administered on 40 mg oral prednisolone daily, 500 mg mycophenolate mofetil, 100 mg hydroxychloroquine, 40 mg telmisartan, and nutritional support. After one week of treatment, symptoms and lab results improved. The patient had been on tapering doses of Mycophenolate mofetil, prednisolone, and hydroxychloroquine with regular monitoring of blood routine, urine protein creatinine ratio, and fundoscopic examination. The patient had no relapses or worsening of symptoms during the 3 month follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Lupus nephritis (LN) is one of the most severe forms of SLE. It is frequently the presenting sign that leads to the diagnosis of SLE. LN is a leading cause of death and disability due to its wide spectrum of clinical features and severity.[6] It can affect the oral mucosa and rectum, as well as the gastrointestinal system. LE develops in 0.2–5.8% of SLE cases, but 40–50% of people have gastrointestinal tract problems.[78]

Preliminary renal biopsy can diagnose and classify LN. It measures renal system inflammation and chronic damage, which may help tailor treatment and prognosis. Renal involvement in LN has many histologic features. Immune complex accumulation causes most LN glomerular impairment. Immune deposit position and afflicted glomeruli proportion determine ISN/RPS classification. Nonimmune complex-dependent illnesses including lupus podocytopathy, collapsing nephropathy, thrombotic microangiopathy, and interstitial nephritis can develop but are not classified under ISN/RPS.[1]

Clinical manifestations of LN range from asymptomatic immune complex accumulation (“silent LN”) to rapidly worsening nephritic conditions. Histopathological renal results have been connected to clinical manifestations. However, several investigations have reported moderate to poor agreement between renal biopsy clinical symptoms and histological results. Low proteinuria and inactive urine sediment may indicate class III, IV, or V LN on kidney biopsy. In kidney biopsies, nephrotic syndrome predicts class V LN, whereas class IV LN is more prevalent.[9] A low complement C3 level often indicates SLE.[2]

Skepticism is needed to distinguish disease progression from infections and other factors. The preferred treatment is oral or intravenous corticosteroids. Despite proven outcomes from cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine, azathioprine, mycophenolate, methotrexate, rituximab, and anti-TNFα, randomized controlled trials are lacking to define the most effective immunosuppressant therapy strategy.[10]

CONCLUSION

The present case report draws attention to the presence of an uncommon presentation of SLE as LN with predominant gastrointestinal manifestations. Early detection and treatment of this condition improves the outcome of a potentially fatal ailment.
