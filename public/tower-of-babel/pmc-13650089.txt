Dermatologists’ Perspectives and Real-World Assessment of Alopecia Areata Severity, Burden, and Healthcare Resource Utilization in Adults in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Mohammed Abdulaziz F Al Ajlan, Khalidah A Alenzi, Mohammed A Al-Haddab, Ibtisam AlHarbi, Abdulrahman Alturaiki, Mohammad Saeed Arsalan, Ayman Behiry, Samantha K Kurosky, Gerardo A Encinas, Peter Anderson, Ashley S Cha-Silva, Shailja Vaghela, Juliana Machado Canosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlopecia areata (AA), an autoimmune disease, causes hair loss on the scalp, face, and body, with a prevalence of approximately 2% in the Middle East. AA is associated with significant impacts to patient quality of life (QoL). This study described dermatologist perceptions and experiences treating AA in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) while new treatments were introduced, and examined patient disease burden and unmet treatment need.MethodsData were analyzed from the Adelphi Real World AA Disease Specific Programme™, conducted September 2022–March 2023. Dermatologists rated their agreement with statements on diagnosing/managing AA and provided clinical and treatment information for four patients (mild/moderate AA: one each; severe/very severe AA: two). A subset of these patients completed the SKINDEX-16, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment—AA (WPAI-AA) questionnaires. Analyses were stratified by percent scalp hair loss (SHL).ResultsA total of 50 dermatologists and 185 patients were included. Most dermatologists reported severe AA was difficult to treat owing to lack of effective therapies. A median of 51.5% SHL was reported as typical of severe AA. Impact of AA on patient QoL was the most frequently reported factor informing disease severity. Overall, 52.4% of patients had severe or very severe AA (as assessed by their physician). Approximately one-third of patients had abnormal anxiety and depression scores as measured by HADS. Patients with ≤ 20% SHL generally had lower mean Skindex-16 scores (impact of AA on QoL). Patients with ≥ 50% SHL reported greater work and activity impacts on the WPAI-AA. Most patients were receiving their first AA treatment, with the most common treatments differing by SHL category. Healthcare resource utilization and costs were greater among patients with ≥ 50% SHL.ConclusionsIn the KSA, the burden of AA extends beyond SHL to include both mental health and economic burdens, highlighting the need for effective treatments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-026-01873-0.Keywords: Alopecia areata, Cross-sectional survey, Dermatologists, Disease burden, Disease severity, Healthcare resource utilization, Saudi Arabia

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Alopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by patchy or complete nonscarring hair loss on the scalp, with or without additional loss of facial and/or body hair, and is associated with a significant impact on quality of life (QoL).In 2023 and 2024, the first targeted therapies for the treatment of severe AA were approved in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA).This study aimed to describe perceptions and experiences of dermatologists treating AA in the KSA at the time new treatments were introduced, and to examine patient disease burden (including patient QoL, healthcare resource utilization, and associated economic burden) and unmet treatment needs. What was learned from this study? Dermatologists in the KSA considered the impact of AA on patient QoL to be an important factor informing disease severity and reported that there was a lack of effective treatments for patients with severe disease; patients with AA in the KSA reported mental health and economic burdens, with those with severe disease reporting greater burdenThe burden of AA in the KSA extended beyond scalp hair loss to include mental health and economic burdens, and a need for effective treatments was highlighted.

Introduction

The autoimmune disease alopecia areata (AA) is characterized by nonscarring scalp hair loss ranging from patchy to complete scalp hair loss; AA can also result in additional loss of facial and/or body hair [1]. The prevalence of AA within the Middle East is approximately 2% [2]. The hair loss associated with AA is unpredictable, and many patients report experiencing anxiety, depression, and significant impacts to their health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [3, 4]. There is a scarcity of evidence on the specific burden of disease that AA confers in the Middle East; however, one cross-sectional survey in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) of 30 patients with AA found moderate quality of life (QoL) impacts overall on the basis of the results of four questionnaires: the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire, the Dermatology Life Quality Index, the Patient Depression Questionnaire, and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale [5].

In the KSA, standard-of-care AA treatments such as intralesional and topical corticosteroids are associated with limited effectiveness, adverse events, and restricted duration of use [6]. In 2023, the Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor baricitinib gained regulatory approval in the KSA for the treatment of severe AA, followed by the JAK3/tyrosine kinase expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (TEC) family kinase inhibitor ritlecitinib, which was approved in 2024. These treatments represent the first targeted therapies for the treatment of AA. Understanding dermatologists’ views on how they treat AA with traditional therapies will be essential for aligning clinical practices and overcoming barriers to integrating these new therapies into regional healthcare. Additionally, understanding the mental health and economic burden of AA as well as unmet needs with conventional treatments can ensure that patients who may benefit have access to newer treatment options.

This study had two aims: to describe perceptions and experiences of dermatologists treating AA in the KSA at the time new treatments were introduced; and to examine disease burden—including patient QoL, healthcare resource utilization, and associated economic burden—and unmet treatment need in patients with AA in the KSA.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

Data were analyzed from the Adelphi Real World AA Disease Specific ProgrammeTM (DSP) [7, 8], a cross-sectional survey of dermatologists and their consulting adult patients with AA that includes retrospective data from medical records. The AA DSP was conducted in the KSA from September 2022 to March 2023. The DSP’s study design was previously described [9].

Dermatologist inclusion criteria included active involvement in AA drug management and a minimum monthly workload of four adults with AA (including at least one patient with mild AA, one with moderate AA, and two with severe/very severe AA, to provide information on their next four consulting patients following dermatologist agreement to participate in the study). Patient inclusion criteria included age ≥ 18 years, a physician-confirmed diagnosis of AA, and no current involvement in a clinical trial.

Dermatologists’ perspectives on assessing disease severity and available treatments in AA were captured in a physician survey. Dermatologists rated their agreement with statements about diagnosing and managing AA on a 5-point scale: completely disagree (1), disagree (2), neither agree nor disagree (3), agree (4), or completely agree (5). Dermatologists also reported patient demographics, clinical characteristics and treatment information for their next four consulting patients (one each with currently mild and moderate AA, and two with currently severe or very severe AA).

The same patients were invited to complete a voluntary questionnaire that included the Skindex-16, hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS), and work productivity and activity impairment (WPAI) [10–12]. The Skindex-16 is an assessment designed to measure the effect of a skin disease on QoL [10]. Patients indicated on a 5-point scale (from never to all the time) how often they experienced the effect described in each item. Answers were then calculated on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater negative impact on QoL. It includes subscores for three domains: Emotions, Symptoms, and Functioning. The HADS questionnaire measures the presence of symptoms of anxiety and depression within the past week using seven items each [11]. The WPAI questionnaire evaluates impairment in workplace productivity and daily activities owing to the respondent’s AA [12]. Rates of absenteeism, presenteeism (defined as reduced productivity while working), and overall impairment on work productivity and daily activities were measured. A subset of these patients also completed a survey independently of their physicians on associated out-of-pocket costs for prescribed medication for AA.

Ethical Approval

Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association guidelines, and as such this study does not require ethics committee approval. The study protocol was reviewed by the Western Institutional Review Board and deemed to be exempt. The study was also conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization Guideline for Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Analysis

Analyses were stratified by percent scalp hair loss (SHL). Statistical analysis was conducted using Stata version 19 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Statistical analyses across severity groups were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for numeric variables and Fisher exact test for categorical variables, with χ2 tests used when Fisher exact test could not be calculated. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for ordered categorical variables. As all subgroups contained > 30 patients, the central limit theorem implies that the sampling distribution of the group means will be approximately normal, even if the underlying data are not normally distributed.

Results

Dermatologists’ Perceptions and Experiences

A total of 50 dermatologists who practiced in the KSA were included in the analysis (Table 1). Seven dermatologists (14.0%) identified themselves as hair specialists, and overall, dermatologists spent a mean 5.3% of their time fulfilling academic or teaching duties. The majority of dermatologists agreed that AA is well understood (90.0%), they were confident in diagnosing AA (92.0%), and they had the tools to diagnose AA (92.0%) (Fig. 1A).

Most dermatologists found managing severe AA was challenging owing to a lack of effective treatments (Fig. 1B). Conventional treatments were recognized as having safety risks, prohibitive costs, and monitoring and compliance issues. The majority of dermatologists (80.0%) agreed that AA should be treated with a long-term strategy.

Dermatologists reported that, on average, 13.4% of their patients with AA experienced 50–94% SHL, while 9.6% had 95–100% SHL (Table 1). A median of 51.5% SHL was reported as a typical severe AA case and a median of 81.0% SHL was reported as a typical very severe AA case (Fig. 2).

Impact of AA on patient QoL was the most frequent factor reported by dermatologists as informing patients’ disease severity (Table 1).

Overall, 86.0% of dermatologists reported that the primary measure of treatment success was hair regrowth. For patients with ≥ 50% SHL, dermatologists considered a median regrowth of approximately 60% from baseline to be a success (Fig. 3). The most reported reason for considering a treatment switch (indicated by 68.0% of dermatologists) was a patient showing a reduced QoL while receiving therapy.

Patients

A total of 185 patients with AA from the KSA were analyzed (Table 2). The mean (SD) patient age was 28.3 (6.7) years, with 58.4% of patients being male. Most patients were diagnosed with AA by a dermatologist (95.6%). Overall, 56 patients had ≤ 20% SHL, 41 had 21–49% SHL, and 88 had ≥ 50% SHL. In total, 97 patients (52.4%) had severe to very severe AA (as assessed by the physician). Most (98.9%) patients with ≥ 50% SHL were assessed by their physician to have severe or very severe disease. Hair loss on areas other than the scalp varied, with 51.9% and 32.4% of patients experiencing eyebrow and facial hair loss, respectively. The majority of patients (93.4%) were adults at the onset of AA signs and symptoms; 6.6% were ≤ 17 years of age. Age, race and ethnicity, time since initial diagnosis, facial hair loss, eyebrow hair loss, eyelash hair loss, insurance type, and current treatment duration were significantly different between percent SHL groups. In general, as percent SHL increased, age and clinical characteristics also increased in time or frequency.

Patient Health-Related Quality of Life

Of all patients, 32.7% had abnormal anxiety scores as measured by HADS, a percentage that increased to 54.8% among patients with ≥ 50% SHL (Fig. 4a). Overall, 32.7% of all patients had abnormal depression scores; the number of patients with depression varied between those with ≤ 20% SHL and those with 21% to ≥ 50% SHL (Fig. 4b).

Skindex-16 scores of 32.7, 32.8, and 27.5 were reported across the emotions, symptoms, and functioning domains (Fig. 4c). Patients with ≤ 20% SHL tended to have lower mean Skindex-16 scores (lower impact of AA on HRQoL) than patients with 21% to 49% or ≥ 50% SHL.

Based on the results of the WPAI-AA, patients reported a mean of 42.8% activity impairment, 38.5% work impairment, and 25.4% absenteeism, with greater impact among those with ≥ 50% SHL (Fig. 4d).

Patient Treatment Goals and Patterns

The most common patient treatment goals were reducing SHL (98.4%), improving HRQoL (83.8%), and achieving long-term control (81.6%). Most patients (83.1%) were receiving their first AA treatment (Table 2). Overall, 10.2% of all patients were on their second treatment, and 6.8% were on their third or subsequent treatment. The most common current treatments were oral corticosteroids (33.5%), oral or topical minoxidil (30.8%), topical corticosteroids (29.2%), and topical calcineurin inhibitors (23.8%, Fig. 5). Common treatments differed between patients with ≥ 50% SHL and those with < 50% SHL, with oral or topical JAK inhibitor and systemic immunosuppressant usage higher in those with greater hair loss.

Among those who had received prior AA treatment, the most common reasons for switching from their first line of prior therapy in KSA were loss of response (62.9%), worsening AA (48.6%), and lack of initial efficacy (42.9%).

Healthcare Resource Utilization and Out-of-Pocket Costs

Patients had a mean (SD) of 4.9 (2.8) and median (IQR) of 4.0 (3.0–6.0) dermatologist visits in the 12 months prior to the survey (Supplementary Fig. S1). Among patients with ≤ 20% SHL, the mean and median number of dermatologists visits were 3.6 (2.9) and 3.0 (2.0–4.0), respectively, while for those with ≥ 50% SHL, they were 5.9 (2.7) and 6.0 (4.0–7.0).

The total expected mean (SD) and median (IQR) consultations over the next 12 months were 5.6 (2.9) and 5.0 (3.0–7.0), respectively, and ranged from 4.0 (2.3) and 3.0 (2.0–5.0) for patients with ≤ 20% SHL to 7.0 (3.0) and 6.0 (5.0–9.0) among those with ≥ 50% SHL (Supplementary Fig. S2).

The mean (SD) and median (IQR) number of medications that patients were currently receiving for AA were 2.3 (1.8) and 2.0 (1.0–3.0), respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3).

The median (IQR) monthly out-of-pocket costs per patient for prescription medication was 240 (135–450) Saudi Arabian riyal (SAR) for patients with ≤ 20% SHL and 300 (120–600) SAR for those with ≥ 50% SHL (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional survey conducted to assess dermatologist perceptions and the burden of disease in patients in the KSA, the burden of AA extended beyond SHL to include mental health and economic burdens, and a lack of effective treatments was highlighted.

Dermatologists in the KSA felt confident in diagnosing AA. However, managing severe disease remained challenging owing to the limited effectiveness and safety concerns with long-term use of conventional treatments. Additionally, the most common reason for switching from a first-line therapy in patients was a loss of response, highlighting the limited long-term efficacy of conventional treatments and the need for new treatments that provide more-effective, sustainable solutions—particularly for patients with more extensive hair loss [6]. At the time of the survey, no targeted treatments were approved; however, newer therapies targeting JAK1/2 and JAK3/TEC family kinases (baricitinib and ritlecitinib) have since been approved for use in the KSA. These therapies, unlike conventional treatments, act directly on pathways associated with AA disease pathogenesis, and they may help address a gap in treating patients with severe disease [13, 14].

Overall, dermatologists considered a median of 51.5% SHL to be typical of severe AA and 81.0% SHL to be typical of very severe AA. This contrasts with the AA investigator global assessment scale, which defines severe AA as 50% to 94%, and very severe AA as 95% to 100% [15]. This may, in part, be attributed to multiple factors underlying assessment of disease severity. In our study, dermatologists considered a wide range of SHL percentages as typical of moderate, severe, and very severe AA and also reported that the impact of AA on patient HRQoL was the most important factor in determining AA severity.

Patients with AA reported experiencing significant anxiety, depression, reduced productivity, and emotional, symptomatic, and functional impairments. Such burdens were especially pronounced among those with ≥ 50% SHL. Yet those with 21% to 49% SHL reported similar or greater burden as measured by HADS-D and Skindex-16 than those with ≥ 50% SHL. Patients living with AA for longer periods of time and without access to effective treatments may be more likely to have a more pronounced burden over time. When looking at patient demographics, those with greater percent SHL tended to be older, with more time since their initial diagnosis and a greater treatment duration. In addition, patients with 21% to 49% SHL and ≥ 50% SHL also had higher proportions of patients with eyebrow and facial hair loss than those with ≤ 20% SHL, characteristics that are often seen in patients with more severe disease [16, 17]. These losses can potentially be impactful in countries such as the KSA, in which there may be a greater cultural emphasis on having these features. Altogether these findings suggest that extent of SHL alone does not capture the full burden of AA, and that integrating individualized assessment of AA burden and treatment plans into clinical practice is an important step towards achieving meaningful disease control. While hair regrowth is typically defined as the primary criterion for determining treatment success, a decline in patient HRQoL often leads to changes in treatment. These findings highlight the importance of prioritizing the patient’s experience as a key measure of treatment success, and show the value of a patient-centered approach in managing AA.

Adults with AA also had substantial healthcare resource utilization, with frequent dermatologist visits and out-of-pocket costs for medication. This economic burden was more pronounced in patients with extensive hair loss of ≥ 50% SHL than patients with ≤ 20% SHL. Dermatologists also noted that conventional treatments had cost and compliance issues, which may prevent patients with severe disease from receiving optimal care for their individual case.

This study had some limitations. Patients included in the AA DSP are representative of the consulting AA population and may overrepresent those with healthcare-seeking behavior. Owing to soft sampling severity quotas, this may not reflect natural distribution of severity. The cross-sectional design of this study enabled the inclusion of patients at various stages of their treatment course. As such, some patients may be experiencing better treatment effects than others in the sample. Dermatologists eligible to participate in the AA DSP were required to see a minimum number of patients with AA. This may have eliminated providers who fell under the monthly workload quota in either disease area. Data were self-reported by patients and dermatologists and may be subject to recall bias. Reported data may also be limited owing to missing fields or limited access to a patient’s medical record forms. Findings of this study may not be generalizable to other age groups, disease conditions, or geographic regions.

Conclusions

Collectively, the findings in this study indicate that a comprehensive approach to assessing disease severity that incorporates impacts on patient HRQoL and economic burdens should be considered and may aid in treatment selection and goal setting.
