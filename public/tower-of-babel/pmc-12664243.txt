Skeletal deformities and surgical procedures in orthognathic surgery patients: a 10-Year retrospective analysis of 1095 cases
Authors: Merve Ece Erdem, Gökhan Çoban, Taner Öztürk, Ahmet Yağcı, İbrahim Yavuz, Celal Irgın, Nisa Gül Amuk, Gökhan Türker, Ahmet Emin Demirbaş, Emrah Soylu, Yusuf Nuri Kaba, Suheyb Bilge, Canay Yılmaz Asan, Cihan Topan, Alper Alkan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthognathic surgery is a well-established method for correcting skeletal dentofacial deformities. Despite its widespread application, large-scale data describing deformity prevalence and the distribution of surgical approaches remain limited. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and patterns of skeletal deformities and orthognathic surgical procedures in patients who underwent orthognathic surgery.MethodsIn this retrospective study, the radiographic records of 1095 patients (663 females, 432 males; aged 16–65 years; mean age: 23.07 ± 5.6 years) treated between 2014 and 2024 were analyzed. Sagittal deformities were classified using the ANB angle as Class I (0° ≤ ANB ≤ 4°), Class II (ANB > 4°), and Class III (ANB < 0°), supported by Wits appraisal, overjet, and soft tissue profile evaluation. Maxillary transverse deficiency was diagnosed clinically or radiographically, with surgically assisted rapid palatal expansion (SARPE) recorded as the treatment modality. Surgical procedures included Le Fort I, bilateral sagittal split osteotomy (BSSO), segmental osteotomy, distraction osteogenesis, condylectomy, and genioplasty. Patients were further classified by sequencing (maxilla-first vs. mandible-first) and protocol (surgery-first vs. orthodontics-first).ResultsClass III deformity was most frequent (71.3%), followed by Class II (17.6%) and Class I (2.1%). Maxillary transverse deficiency was observed in 8.9% of patients (34.7% Class I, 29.6% Class II, 35.7% Class III), for whom surgically assisted rapid palatal expansion (SARPE) was performed as the treatment modality. Asymmetry was found in 10.8% of patients (1.6% with condylectomy), anterior open bite in 11.5%, vertical maxillary excess in 1.3%, and cleft lip/palate in 1.9%. Genioplasty was performed in 7.9%. Rarely, segmental osteotomy (0.7%) or mandibular distraction (0.3%) was applied. The most frequent procedure was bimaxillary surgery involving Le Fort I and BSSO (69.1%). Maxilla-first sequencing was preferred in 91.4% of bimaxillary cases, while 1.2% underwent surgery-first treatment.ConclusionIn this large cohort, the maxilla-first approach was predominantly preferred in bimaxillary surgery, with most patients being young adult females with skeletal Class III deformities. Maxillary transverse deficiency (8.9%) was managed with SARPE, while the rarity of surgery-first protocols reflected case complexity. These findings underscore the importance of individualized planning, patient counseling, and the integration of digital technologies to optimize outcomes in diverse dentofacial deformities.Trial registrationNot applicable. This was a retrospective observational study and did not involve any prospective health care intervention.Keywords: Orthognathic surgery, Skeletal malocclusion, Dentofacial deformity, Surgical sequencing, Prevalence

Introduction

Dentofacial deformity is defined as abnormalities in the development of the teeth, jawbones, and facial structures that affect chewing, speech, respiration, and facial aesthetics, resulting in significant disharmony and deviation from normal proportions between these structures [1]. Such deformities are influenced by genetic factors, growth and developmental disorders, harmful habits such as thumb sucking and prolonged pacifier or bottle use, nutritional and environmental factors, as well as ethnic background [2].

The prevalence of skeletal anomalies worldwide has been determined through epidemiological studies conducted in various populations. The global prevalence of Class II anomalies is reported to be 19.56%. However, this rate varies significantly among different populations, ranging from 1.6% in Nigeria to 63% in Belgium. The global prevalence of Class III anomalies is 5.93%, with reported rates of 3.8% in Africans, 5.92% in Whites, and up to 19.9% in the Chinese population [3]. According to a study conducted on the Turkish population, the prevalence of skeletal Class II and Class III anomalies is 35.7% and 17.7%, respectively [4].

The treatment of individuals with dentofacial deformities varies depending on the patient’s growth stage and the severity of the anomaly. In individuals who have not completed growth, growth modification treatments are employed to influence the position of the maxilla and mandible both dentally and skeletally. In adult patients with completed growth, treatment options include orthognathic surgery or camouflage therapy, depending on the severity of the anomaly. In adult patients with severe dental and facial disharmony, orthognathic surgery is the only treatment option to improve the maxillomandibular relationship, function, and facial profile [5]. Currently, the most commonly used procedures for the correction of midfacial deformities include Le Fort I osteotomy, bilateral sagittal split osteotomy (BSSO) for the mandible, and genioplasty for chin corrections. Depending on the severity and etiology of the deformity, osteotomies may be performed on the maxilla only, the mandible only, or on both jaws simultaneously [6]. In cases where large skeletal movements (>10 mm) are required—particularly in syndromic, congenital, or traumatic deformities—gradual bone elongation through distraction osteogenesis is considered more effective [7]. In addition to sagittal and vertical discrepancies, maxillary transverse deficiency also represents a clinically significant deformity, often associated with posterior crossbite and transverse disharmony between the maxilla and mandible. In skeletally mature patients, surgically assisted rapid palatal expansion (SARPE) is frequently utilized to manage this condition [8].

The type and frequency of skeletal deformities in patients undergoing orthognathic surgery are crucial for refining surgical procedures and treatment strategies. However, there is a lack of comprehensive reports describing the distribution of orthognathic surgical procedures and deformity patterns across large patient populations. Previous studies have generally been limited by relatively small sample sizes and have often focused on a single deformity type or surgical approach, which restricts their ability to provide reliable prevalence estimates or to capture less frequent deformities such as anterior open bite, vertical maxillary excess, or cleft-related discrepancies. As a result, large-scale and comprehensive analyses that simultaneously evaluate the prevalence of multiple skeletal deformities and the full spectrum of surgical procedures remain scarce. Such analyses are essential not only to improve diagnostic accuracy and refine individualized treatment planning, but also to inform interdisciplinary collaboration and optimize healthcare resource allocation. Addressing this gap requires sufficiently large cohorts to enable meaningful subgroup comparisons and to identify clinically relevant trends over time. To this end, the present study retrospectively analyzed 1095 consecutively treated patients, providing one of the largest single-center clinical datasets reported in the literature to date.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This retrospective study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Erciyes University (Decision No: 2025/198). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Patient selection

Records of 1116 patients who underwent orthognathic surgery at the Faculty of Dentistry, Erciyes University, between 2014 and 2024 were evaluated. Twenty-one patients were excluded due to incomplete records. The study was conducted on the records of 1095 patients (663 females and 432 males, aged between 16 and 65 years; mean age: 23.07 ± 5.6 years) (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Radiographic evaluation and skeletal classification

Panoramic, lateral cephalometric, and anteroposterior radiographs of the patients were examined by the same investigator (first author). Patients were classified according to sagittal skeletal deformity based on ANB angle measurements as Class I (0° ≤ ANB ≤ 4°), Class II (ANB > 4°), and Class III (ANB < 0°). In addition to the ANB angle, supporting diagnostic criteria such as Wits appraisal, overjet measurement, and soft tissue profile evaluation were also considered to enhance the accuracy of sagittal skeletal classification and to minimize misdiagnosis due to individual anatomical variations. In cases where discrepancies were observed between ANB angle and supporting diagnostic criteria, the ANB angle was prioritized as the primary reference, while the final classification was confirmed by assessing the overall diagnostic consistency of all parameters. Maxillary transverse deficiency was diagnosed based on clinical findings (e.g., posterior crossbite, narrow maxillary arch) and/or radiographic indicators of transverse maxillomandibular discrepancy observed on posterior-anterior cephalometric radiographs. Regardless of sagittal classification, individuals who underwent surgically assisted rapid palatal expansion (SARPE) were categorized into a separate group. This procedure was performed exclusively in patients with isolated skeletal maxillary transverse deficiency and was carried out without separation of the pterygomaxillary junction.

Anterior open bite was defined in patients showing an overbite of less than 0 mm on lateral cephalometric radiographs, as previously described by Alawy et al. [9]. Vertical maxillary excess was identified in patients who underwent surgical maxillary impaction of 7 mm or more [10]. On anteroposterior radiographs, patients with a deviation of the gnathion point from the midsagittal plane (Gn-MSP) greater than 4 mm were classified as having facial asymmetry [11], and were further subcategorized based on whether they underwent condylectomy. Condylectomy was performed in patients diagnosed with condylar hyperplasia based on clinical findings and confirmed by bone scintigraphy. Patients with cleft lip and/or palate (CLP) were also categorized as a separate group. The cephalometric criteria used for classification are summarized in Table 2.

Classification of surgical procedures

Based on the procedures performed, patients were classified as having undergone Le Fort I osteotomy, bilateral sagittal split osteotomy (BSSO), segmental surgery, or distraction osteogenesis. Additionally, patients were grouped according to whether or not they received condylectomy and genioplasty.

Among those who underwent bimaxillary surgery, further categorization was made based on surgical sequencing as either maxilla-first or mandible-first approaches. Patients were also classified according to treatment protocol as either surgery-first or orthodontics-first.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). In addition to descriptive statistics, the chi-square test was used to evaluate differences among the skeletal deformity groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Method error

To ensure intraobserver consistency, 10% of the radiographs were randomly selected and re-measured by the same investigator after a two-week interval. The results were found to be consistent on visual inspection.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The obtained data are presented in detail using tables and figures. Of the individuals in the sample, 60.5% (n = 663) were female, and 39.5% (n = 432) were male. The mean age of the patients was 23.07 ± 5.6 years (range: 16 to 65 years), with the most common age range being 16 to 24 years, which constituted 75.8% of the patient group (Table 1; Fig. 1). When the distribution over the years was examined, an increase in the number of patients undergoing orthognathic surgery over time was observed (Fig. 2).

Distribution of skeletal deformities

The distribution and demographic characteristics of the patients according to their dentofacial deformities are presented in Table 1. The highest proportion of patients had skeletal Class III deformity (71.3%), followed by Class II (17.6%) and Class I (2.1%). Maxillary transverse deficiency was also observed in 8.9% of patients (34.7% Class I, 29.6% Class II, 35.7% Class III), for whom surgically assisted rapid palatal expansion (SARPE) was performed as the treatment modality. No statistically significant difference was found among the skeletal deformity groups in terms of age at surgery (P = 0.539). However, a significant difference was observed among the groups in terms of gender distribution (P < 0.001).

Additional dentofacial characteristics

Asymmetry was identified in 10.8% of the cases (condylectomy was performed in 1.6%), vertical maxillary excess in 1.3%, anterior open bite in 11.5%, and cleft lip and/or palate (CLP) in 1.9%. Statistically significant differences were found among the skeletal groups in terms of the incidence of asymmetry, vertical maxillary excess, and anterior open bite (P < 0.001). In the skeletal Class I group, 26.1% had asymmetry (condylectomy was performed in 8.7%) and 47.8% had anterior open bite. In the Class II group, 3.1% had asymmetry, 5.2% had vertical maxillary excess, and 8.8% had anterior open bite. In the Class III group, asymmetry was observed in 12.3% (condylectomy performed in 1.8%), vertical maxillary excess in 0.4%, anterior open bite in 11.1%, and CLP in 2.4% of patients (0.1% with asymmetry and 0.4% with anterior open bite) (Table 3).

Discussion

Orthognathic surgery is a treatment modality applied in combination with orthodontic therapy in adult patients with severe skeletal discrepancies. Although the prevalence of dentofacial deformities requiring orthognathic surgery varies across different populations and studies, it is generally reported to be around 5% [12]. Since all 1095 patients analyzed in this study presented with dentofacial deformities, it is not possible to make a definitive assessment of the prevalence of these deformities in the general population.

In a similar study examining the demographic characteristics of orthognathic surgery patients, the mean age was reported as 24.0 ± 6.4 years, and a higher proportion of female patients was observed, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1 [13]. In another study conducted by Castro et al., the mean age was reported as 28.5 years, and females comprised 63.4% of the patient population. Moreover, the most common age range was found to be between 15 and 24 years [14]. Consistent with these findings, the present study revealed that the most prevalent age range was between 16 and 24 years, with a female majority (60.5%) and a mean age of 23.07 ± 5.6 years. The literature indicates that aesthetic concerns are more prominent among women and younger individuals, whereas men tend to seek orthognathic surgery primarily for functional problems. Additionally, older individuals are often more reluctant to undergo surgery due to concerns regarding surgical risks [14–16]. Younger patients are also reported to adapt more easily to aesthetic changes, and soft tissue changes are more predictable and reflective of skeletal modifications in this group. Therefore, it is recommended that preoperative consultations with patients over the age of 30 be more comprehensive and explanatory [17].

Both conventional and digital methods are used in orthognathic surgery planning. Although traditional and digital model surgery techniques yield similar surgical outcomes, three-dimensional digital planning enables more precise, predictable, and efficient treatment results. Additionally, digital methods require less time compared to conventional techniques, which stands out as a significant advantage [18]. In the clinic from which the sample in this study was obtained, a notable increase in the number of patients undergoing orthognathic treatment was observed after the implementation of digital model surgery in 2020 (Fig. 2). While the adoption of digital planning appears to have facilitated a notable increase in case acceptance and surgical efficiency, it is unlikely to be the sole driver of the rising surgical volume. Improved interdisciplinary training and collaboration between orthodontists and maxillofacial surgeons may have contributed to more predictable treatment outcomes. Moreover, broader insurance coverage, including partial reimbursement of combined orthodontic–surgical treatment by public health systems, has increased accessibility. At the same time, growing patient awareness of facial esthetics and functional benefits—fueled by social media and cultural emphasis on appearance—likely influenced treatment demand, as aesthetic concern has been shown to be a powerful predictor of patients’ decision to undergo orthognathic surgery [19, 20]. Thus, the observed increase in surgical volume can be interpreted as the cumulative outcome of technological advances, enhanced professional collaboration, expanded healthcare accessibility, and evolving cultural–esthetic expectations. Among patients seeking orthognathic surgery treatment, skeletal Class III deformity was found to have the highest prevalence. In a study conducted by Sato et al. involving 251 individuals who underwent orthognathic surgery between 2003 and 2011, Class III deformity was the most common at 55%, followed by Class II at 29.5% and Class I at 15.5% [21]. Similarly, in a study by Olkun et al. on 200 individuals who underwent orthognathic surgery between 2014 and 2018, the prevalence of Class III, Class II, and Class I deformities was reported as 69%, 19%, and 12%, respectively [22]. Specific data on the prevalence of SARPE, which is an effective method in the treatment of severe maxillary transverse deficiency in adults, are limited. However, the prevalence of maxillary transverse deficiency has been reported to range between 8.5% and 22% [23]. The skeletal deformity prevalence data obtained in this study are consistent with previously published findings in the literature.

The etiology of facial asymmetry is multifactorial, shaped by the interaction of genetic and environmental factors. In the literature, the prevalence of facial asymmetry has been reported to range between 11% and 37% [24]. Marked deviations exceeding 4 mm in the soft tissue menton region can be perceived even by lay observers [25]. One of the pathological causes of severe asymmetry is condylar hyperplasia, a unilateral or bilateral overgrowth of the mandibular condyle, which can result in both skeletal discrepancy and occlusal disturbances. In such cases, condylectomy—performed alone or in combination with orthognathic surgery—may be required to arrest abnormal growth and restore symmetry [26]. In our cohort, asymmetry was observed in 10.8% of patients, with 1.6% requiring condylectomy. This rate is within the wide range reported in the literature and underlines the importance of systematic evaluation for underlying condylar pathology. Recognition of asymmetry is essential, since mild cases may be managed orthodontically, while severe skeletal deviations frequently necessitate surgical correction.

Anterior open bite is another complex malocclusion with a multifactorial etiology, involving skeletal, dentoalveolar, and functional components. Its prevalence has been reported to range from 2.9% to 17% [26]. In our series, anterior open bite was diagnosed in 11.5% of patients, consistent with the upper range of previous reports. Beyond esthetic concerns, anterior open bite is associated with impaired mastication, swallowing difficulties, altered speech articulation, and an increased risk of relapse, particularly when functional habits such as tongue thrust persist [27]. Mild open bite cases may be managed with orthodontic interventions, while severe skeletal forms often require orthognathic surgery for stable correction. Thus, reporting its prevalence is clinically meaningful, as it informs treatment planning, highlights the functional consequences of the deformity, and underlines the need for long-term stability strategies.

In addition to asymmetry and open bite, vertical maxillary excess (VME) is a clinically relevant deformity characterized by excessive gingival display (EGD) on smile. EGD is commonly defined as >2–3 mm of gingival exposure and is often graded as mild (2–4 mm), moderate (4–8 mm), or severe (>8 mm), with severity guiding the choice between orthodontic camouflage and surgical correction. The perception of gingival exposure as excessive generally begins when more than 3 mm of gingiva is visible during smiling, although this threshold may differ depending on individual and cultural factors. An attractive smile is often characterized by a gingival display of about 1–2 mm, while exposure exceeding 3 mm is frequently regarded as less esthetic. Epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of excessive gingival display in the general population varies widely, reported between 10% and 30% [10]. VME represents not only a vertical skeletal discrepancy but also a dentoalveolar and soft tissue imbalance, arising from disproportionate downward growth of the maxilla. This condition is typically associated with lip incompetence and excessive incisor exposure at rest, often accompanied by a gummy smile. Beyond esthetics, VME can compromise oral function, contributing to difficulties in mastication, altered speech articulation, and in some cases airway-related issues due to lip incompetence and altered oral seal [28]. However, despite its relatively high occurrence as an esthetic concern, there is no clear evidence in the literature regarding the true prevalence of VME requiring surgical correction. Although the prevalence of VME in our cohort was relatively low (1.3%), documenting such frequencies remains clinically valuable. This low rate likely reflects the stringent radiographic criteria we applied for vertical excess and the predominance of sagittal discrepancies among patients referred to our center. Understanding the epidemiology of vertical maxillary excess not only aids in anticipating diagnostic challenges but also refines treatment planning by helping to distinguish cases suitable for orthodontic camouflage from those requiring surgical intervention, such as Le Fort I impaction alone or in combination with mandibular procedures. Furthermore, awareness of its prevalence supports resource allocation in maxillofacial centers and highlights the potential influence of cultural and esthetic expectations on treatment demand, ultimately contributing to more effective and individualized patient care.

Studies have shown that the primary motivation for individuals with skeletal Class III deformity to seek treatment is the improvement of facial esthetics, and that most of these individuals experience psychological and functional problems related to facial appearance [29]. On the other hand, individuals with skeletal Class II deformity are reported to be more likely to prefer orthodontic treatment over orthognathic surgery [13]. The relatively lower frequency of Class II patients undergoing surgery may be influenced by multiple factors including patient preference, severity of the skeletal discrepancy, and orthodontic treatment planning. It is possible that some patients were managed orthodontically due to borderline discrepancies or personal reluctance toward surgical intervention. Moreover, the presentation style and treatment preferences of individual orthodontists might have influenced patient decisions; however, this was beyond the scope of our retrospective radiographic analysis and could not be evaluated in this study. Prospective studies incorporating patient interviews and treatment planning records would be necessary to explore this aspect. In this study, it was observed that patients in the skeletal Class I group sought treatment due to skeletal anomalies such as anterior open bite or asymmetry—either severe (>4 mm) or mild-to-moderate (≤ 4 mm). In addition, some individuals in this group were diagnosed with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and underwent bimaxillary advancement surgery.

It was also found that patients with cleft lip and/or palate (CLP) accounted for 1.9% of all individuals who underwent orthognathic treatment. A meta-analysis on the global prevalence of cleft lip and/or palate reported a prevalence rate ranging from 0.3 to 0.45 per 1000 live births [30]. Furthermore, previous studies have indicated that 51% of individuals with clefts require orthognathic surgery, and these patients are generally characterized by skeletal Class III malocclusion [31, 32]. Although CLP patients were analyzed as a separate subgroup, their inclusion was intentional in order to determine their proportion within the overall orthognathic surgery population over the 10-year period. Reporting this distribution was considered important to reflect the full clinical spectrum encountered in daily surgical practice.

The prevalence of bimaxillary surgery (75.8%) was found to be higher compared to other surgical procedures and was more frequently performed in individuals with skeletal Class III deformity (79.5%). This finding suggests that as the severity of the deformity increases, the need for bimaxillary surgery also rises. Moreover, the growing preference for combined surgical approaches may be attributed to their proven ability to provide better esthetic outcomes and greater postoperative stability [13]. Segmental osteotomies performed for alveolar protrusion and distraction osteogenesis procedures required for large advancements (>10 mm) constituted only a small portion of the sample analyzed in this study. The limited indications for such treatments may explain their less frequent utilization [33].

In the majority of patients who underwent bimaxillary surgery, a maxilla-first approach was preferred (91.4%). Although the literature suggests no significant difference in postoperative stability between maxilla-first and mandible-first approaches, surgeons generally favor the maxilla-first technique. However, in cases where the maxilla requires downward repositioning or when counterclockwise rotation of the jaws is planned, a mandible-first approach is reported to yield more predictable outcomes [34]. The surgery-first protocol, in which orthognathic surgery is performed prior to orthodontic treatment, aims to shorten the overall treatment time and achieve early esthetic improvement. This approach is typically selected for individuals with minimal dental compensation, favorable soft tissue balance, mild-to-moderate dental crowding, and incisor positions that are compatible with postoperative occlusion [35]. In the present study, only 1.2% of patients underwent surgery-first treatment. This low prevalence may be attributed to the fact that most patients seeking orthognathic treatment presented with severe skeletal and dental discrepancies, necessitating comprehensive pre-surgical orthodontic preparation.

Interestingly, our findings suggest an increasing trend in the combined use of orthodontic treatment and orthognathic surgery over the 10-year period studied. While this shift may partially reflect evolving treatment philosophies and improved interdisciplinary collaboration, several other contributing factors could be considered. These may include increased patient awareness and demand for facial esthetics, greater availability of trained professionals in orthognathic treatment planning, or improved access to care. Additionally, regional socioeconomic dynamics, such as the growing role of private and public insurance coverage, the expansion of university-based or hospital-based surgical services, or the out-of-pocket affordability of treatment, may also influence patient choices and treatment uptake. In Türkiye, for instance, the Social Security Institution (SGK) covers a substantial portion of the cost for combined orthodontic and surgical treatment in patients whose orthodontic treatment was initiated before the age of 18, even if the surgical intervention itself is performed at a later age. The estimated coverage provided by SGK amounts to approximately $4.000. Such public funding likely contributes to increased accessibility and acceptance of orthognathic procedures among eligible patients.

A limitation of this study is its retrospective, single-center design, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, this large-scale clinical dataset provides a unique contribution to the literature by consolidating orthognathic surgery trends in a sizeable, consecutive patient population. Our findings not only corroborate global trends regarding the predominance of Class III deformities and the need for bimaxillary procedures, but also highlight the evolving surgical preferences such as maxilla-first sequencing. These insights may serve as a valuable reference point for comparative studies and international treatment planning strategies. These findings may provide valuable data for all professionals involved in interdisciplinary orthognathic treatment planning, supporting more informed and individualized approaches for patients with skeletal deformities.

Conclusions

The majority of patients undergoing orthognathic surgery in this large retrospective cohort were young adults, with a predominance of female patients. Skeletal Class III deformity was the most frequent diagnosis, often requiring bimaxillary surgical correction, underscoring the complexity of treatment in this group. Maxillary transverse deficiency requiring surgically assisted rapid palatal expansion (SARPE) was also relatively common (8.9%), highlighting the importance of recognizing transverse discrepancies in adult patients. Although less prevalent than major sagittal discrepancies, asymmetry (10.8%), anterior open bite (11.5%), and vertical maxillary excess (1.3%) represent clinically significant deformities that demand careful diagnostic assessment for condylar pathology, vertical stability, and functional rehabilitation. Cleft lip/palate cases (1.9%) further underline the need for tailored multidisciplinary management strategies in this unique subgroup. Segmental osteotomies and distraction osteogenesis were rarely indicated, reflecting their role in highly specific clinical scenarios such as severe alveolar protrusion or large skeletal advancements.

Over the 10-year study period, the number of patients undergoing orthognathic surgery increased, a trend likely influenced by the adoption of digital planning technologies, expanded insurance coverage, and rising patient awareness of esthetic and functional benefits. From a surgical perspective, the predominance of maxilla-first sequencing (91.4%) over mandible-first reflects current clinical preference, offering predictable outcomes in most cases, while the low use of surgery-first protocols (1.2%) underscores the complexity of this cohort, where comprehensive orthodontic preparation remains essential.

Taken together, these findings emphasize the importance of individualized treatment planning, resource prioritization, and interdisciplinary collaboration in addressing the diverse spectrum of dentofacial deformities. Documenting the prevalence and distribution of skeletal deformities and surgical procedures in a large patient population not only provides valuable insights for clinical decision-making but also facilitates anticipation of case complexity and supports the optimization of surgical strategies in contemporary craniofacial practice.
