Antibiotic cocktail-induced changes in gut microbiota drive alteration of bile acid metabolism to restrain Th17 differentiation through the FXR–NLRP3 axis
Authors: Yue Shao-Yu, Di Niu, Jia Chen, Wei-Yi Li, Xu Wang, Qing-Wei Meng, Wei-Jie Song, Yi-Ge Yang, Hui Wang, Rong-Rong Li, Bo-Yang Li, Li-Gang Zhang, Chao-Jie Hu, Ling-Fan Xu, Hui-Hui Wang, Li Zhang, Chao-Zhao Liang, He-Xi Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotics influence both gut microbial composition and immune regulation, but the detailed mechanisms are still undefined. Shifts in the microbiome caused by antibiotic exposure can modulate immune activity through various pathways. Therefore, we aimed to explore how antibiotics affect immune-inflammation by regulating Th17 cells through the gut microbiota of mice with experimental autoimmune prostatitis (EAP). Antibiotic-driven shifts in gut microbial communities and metabolite profiling in EAP mice were performed by integrating 16S rRNA sequencing with mass spectrometry–driven metabolomic analysis. Antibiotic cocktail (ABX) therapy mitigated EAP, modified the gut microbiome composition, and influenced bile acid metabolism. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) using microbiota from ABX-treated feces into EAP mice effectively altered gut microbiome composition and alleviated disease symptoms, indicating that microbiome intervention reduces autoimmune inflammation and decreases deoxycholic acid (DCA) in mice. Subsequent experiments demonstrated that DCA suppresses farnesol X receptor (FXR) expression which can inhibit the NLRP3‒ IL17A axis, thus promoting Th17 cell development and exacerbating inflammatory cell infiltration of the prostate. Our initial clinical examination of patients with prostatitis and antibiotic treatment indicated that bile acid metabolism and Th17 cell development are affected by antibiotic therapy. This work revealed that antibiotic-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis decreases the bile acid metabolite DCA, further restraining Th17 cell differentiation via the FXR‒NLRP3 axis to alleviate autoimmune prostatitis. Our results reveal new perspectives regarding the interconnected dynamics of antibiotics, gut microbiota, bile acid metabolism, and immune regulation, with potential relevance for therapies targeting immune-mediated diseases.Keywords: Gut microbiota, antibiotic, Th17 cell, prostatitis, FXR, bile acid

Introduction

The gut microbiome is crucial for sustaining a dynamic and stable equilibrium in the host's internal environment. Multiple factors, such as nutrition and pharmaceutical interventions, can modify the dynamic interplay between humans and the microbiota.1,2 Antibiotics, as commonly used drugs, can reshape gut microbial composition and disturb its functions. Growing research demonstrates that such modulation of the microbiota influences immune cell activity and may contribute to immune-related diseases.3 As an example, administration of an antibiotic cocktail (ABX) markedly attenuates experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis through selective targeting of the CD5+ B-cell population.4 Curiously, the potential impact of antibiotics on chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS), a frequent urinary tract disease, has yet to be determined. Evidence has shown marked variations in the gut microbiota between men in the CP/CPPS group and those in the control group.5 Considering the ability of antibiotics to alter gut microbiota and consequently modulate immune function, we speculate that their therapeutic potential in CP/CPPS may be mediated through microbiome regulation. However, this hypothesis needs to be verified through actual experiments.

The gut microbiome clearly is essential for the inflammatory process of autoimmune illnesses across different phases of their development6. The metabolism of host-derived compounds, including bile acids, shapes immune regulation and other physiological processes.7-9 In particular, the gut microbiome profoundly affects bile acid metabolism. Apart from their established function in lipid digestion, bile acids serve as critical signaling mediators. By activating host receptors such as Takeda-G-protein-coupled-receptor−5 (TGR5) and the nuclear receptor FXR (NR1H4), they orchestrate diverse metabolic and immune pathways.10 A wide array of microbial metabolites, including secondary bile acids, profoundly affects both homeostatic and physiological functions, such as those related to cancer, inflammation, and metabolism.11-13 Bile acids, produced from hepatic cholesterol, are released into the duodenum postprandially to aid lipid emulsification and absorption.14 When primary bile acids reach the duodenum, local bacteria begin to promote the dissociation and biological conversion of primary to secondary bile acids15. Activated by bile acids, this nuclear receptor is broadly expressed on various cells, including immune cells. Studies have demonstrated that FXR expressed by macrophages is implicated in the regulation of metabolism and modulating immune responses, particularly under inflammatory conditions.16,17 However, it remains unclear how antibiotic-induced alterations of the gut microbiome affect FXR-mediated immune regulation and whether bile acids are involved in this process.

There is a strong association between bile acids and the processes governing Th17 cell differentiation and maturation.18 In inflammatory bowel disease, alterations in the abundance of colonic microbiota and bile acids concentrations can modify Th17 differentiation.19 A recent study reported that oral delivery of bile acids, including DCA, inhibits Th17 cell differentiation and alleviates rheumatoid arthritis.20 Importantly, the Th17 cell-mediated immunological response was also identified as a key pathogenic factor in patients with CP/CPPS.21 In animal models, IL-17A levels and Th17 cell frequencies are consistently elevated in EAP mice, with hyperactivated Th17 cells contributing critically to EAP onset and progression.22,23 Our previous research revealed that the gut microbiome metabolite propionate could suppress the differentiation of Th17 cells.24 Furthermore, we have also demonstrated that meals rich in alcohol and sweets can modify the Th17 ratio in EAP.25,26 These results indicate that the gut microbiota contributes significantly to immune modulation in EAP mice by regulating Th17 cell development. Nonetheless, the influence of gut microbiome–mediated bile acid metabolism on Th17 cells in EAP requires further research.

In this study, we explored the influence of antibiotic therapy on Th17 cell responses in EAP, focusing on its modulation of bile acid metabolism mediated via the gut microbiota. ABX treatment in EAP mice induces gut microbiota disruption and thus drives alterations in bile acid metabolism. Moreover, bile acid metabolism associated with EAP mice remains modifiable with the transplantation of feces from antibiotic-treated mice. Further investigation revealed that the bile acid metabolite DCA facilitates Th17 development and intensifies EAP by suppressing FXR function. Importantly, ABX modulates DCA metabolism via alterations in the gut microbial community, increasing FXR expression, suppressing NLRP3 expression, blocking Th17 cell differentiation, and ultimately mitigating the progression of EAP. Moreover, for the first time, we performed a comparative examination of the variations in patient feces, serum, 16S rRNA, metabolomics, and proteomics before and after antibiotic therapy. Consequently, our results suggest that antibiotic therapy and FXR targeting may be efficacious in the treatment of CP/CPPS.

Results

Antibiotic therapy modifies gut microbes and mitigates EAP

To explore the role of the gut microbiome in EAP, we initiated antibiotic treatment 14 days post–model establishment to achieve microbial depletion (Figure 1A). Gut microbiota alterations were evaluated by 16S rRNA-based sequencing of cecal contents from EAP and ABX mice. Diversity analyses (Chao1, Simpson, ACE) revealed that ABX therapy led to a marked decline in microbial α-diversity (Figure 1B and Supplementary Figure 1A). Bray–Curtis principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed a clear separation in β-diversity, demonstrating substantial variations in the composition of the gut microbiota and structure between EAP and ABX-treated mice. (Figure 1C). Following ABX administration, the gut microbial profile of EAP mice was significantly altered, with notable enrichment of Bacteroides at the genus level (Figure 1D). Detailed shifts in bacterial genera and species for both are shown in Supplementary Figures 1B and 1C. To further characterize microbial differences, we performed LEfSe analysis, combining LDA with effect size measurement, to identify taxa enriched in the two groups (Figure 1E). The relative abundance of bacterial species, including Bacteroides, Enterobacteriaceae, Gammaproteobacteria, and Hungatella, was elevated in EAP mice treated with ABX. In EAP mice, the microbial communities whose expression was elevated included Lachnospiraceae, Oscillospira, and Alistipes, among others. Notably, functional prediction via the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) revealed enrichment in Th17 cell differentiation and IL−17 signaling pathways (Figure 1F).

Thereafter, we performed in vivo experiments in these mice. In ABX-fed EAP mice, we noted a decrease in tactile allodynia (Figure 1G). EAP mice presented no substantial weight alterations following the administration of ABX (Figure 1H and Supplementary 1D). The accumulation of immune cells into the prostate tissue interstitium of EAP mice treated with ABX was reduced, suggesting that prostate inflammation in these mice was mitigated (Figure 1I). Consequently, IL-17A expression at the protein level in prostate tissue of EAP mice was elevated (Figure 1J). To further assess Th17 responses in vivo, we labeled CD4+ lymphocytes in the spleen and quantified the cytokine IL-17A released by Th17 cells, along with the principal transcription factor RORγt for IL−17. Our findings demonstrated that the fraction of CD4+ IL-17A + cells in the spleen and prostate decreased following ABX administration (Figure 1K and Supplementary Figure 8A). The expression of IL-17A mRNA in mouse prostate tissue corresponded with the detection results in the prostate (Figure 1L). Considering that RORγt, a critical regulator of Th17 cell development, is another important indicator of Th17 cells,27 we labeled CD4+ RORγt + cells to validate the changes in the fraction of Th17 cells. The proportion of CD4+ RORγt + cells in EAP mice also exceeded that in ABX-treated EAP mice (Figure 1M). ABX-treated EAP mice exhibited lower RORγt mRNA levels in prostate tissue (Figure 1N). Additionally, we investigated the expression of GM-CSF, IL-17A, and IFN-γ in the serum (Figure 1O) and found that ABX decreased the concentrations of inflammatory markers in EAP mice. In summary, antibiotic treatment reshaped the intestinal microbiota in EAP mice, which was associated showing a lower proportion of Th17 cells and diminished inflammatory infiltration in the prostate. The present findings reveal that the gut microbiome is essential for modulating the immunological response in EAP mice.

Bile acid metabolism is associated with ABX therapy status in EAP mice

To investigate the mechanism by which ABX reduces prostate inflammation in EAP, both untargeted and targeted metabolomics were used to analyze feces, prostate, and serum samples from the two groups. We initially conducted a PCA on the fecal metabolomic data and discovered that the fecal metabolomics of EAP animals diverged from that of ABX-treated mice (Figure 2A). Utilizing criteria for screening were defined as VIP > 1 and P < 0.05, we discovered substantial differences in fecal metabolites between EAP and ABX-treated mice (Figure 2B). Enrichment analysis indicated that these metabolites were enriched in histidine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, and bile acid metabolism (Figure 2C). Due to the well-established association of the gut microbiome with bile acid metabolism28, we examined the alterations in bile acid metabolism induced by ABX. We employed targeted metabolomics to identify bile acids in mouse serum, detecting a total of 25 bile acids (Figure 2D). Furthermore, we performed targeted bile acid profiling in prostate tissue from the mice. Notably, the concentrations of six bile acids, such as DCA, chenodeoxycholic acid, and taurohyodeoxycholic acid, were substantially reduced in prostate tissue (Figure 2E). The patterns of DCA in mouse feces, serum, and prostate were congruent (Supplementary Figure 2A). We further examined the correlations between metabolite levels and gut microbiome abundances by integrating 16S rRNA gene sequencing with bile acid metabolites in feces. At the genus level of the microbiome and bile acid metabolites, we found a negative correlation between DCA, chenodeoxycholic acid, and the LDA scores of the prominent Bacteroides and Hungatella, whereas the Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group and Alistipes demonstrated a positive correlation (Figure 2F). We subsequently illustrated the important differentially abundant metabolites and the gut microbiota to construct a correlation network diagram. Simultaneously, we assessed the associations between each omics variable and the model components to identify essential traits. The feature point is larger when the correlation is higher, and the feature is more significant the further it is from the center.

Our investigation revealed that DCA and glycochenodeoxycholate−3-sulfate are significant in terms of network correlation and feature‒component connections (Figure 2G,H). Research indicates that DCA is significantly associated with in vivo inflammation. Consequently, we generated a scatter plot of the sample distribution and discovered that DCA exhibited a substantial association with the majority of the microbial communities (Figure 2I and Supplementary Figure 2B). To further elucidate the involvement of bile acid metabolism in the effects of antibiotic therapy on EAP mice, we quantified expression levels of bile acid–associated genes synthesis, in the liver of the mice. Bile acid biosynthetic genes in the liver of ABX-treated mice were modified (Figure 2J). In conclusion, ABX treatment influences bile acid metabolism in EAP mice, particularly in relation to DCA, which is intricately linked to the gut microbiome.

FMT from EAP + ABX mice changes the gut microbiome and alleviates prostatitis in recipient EAP mice

Importantly, ABX therapy alters the gut microbiome and modifies the profile of microbiota-dependent bile acids, such as DCA, which may result in the mitigation of EAP-related phenotypes in mice. Therefore, we investigated how the gut microbiome functions in EAP following ABX treatment. We administered FMT from EAP or ABX group mice to EAP mice for two weeks and observed changes in the gut microbiome and inflammatory status in vivo (Figure 3A). Our investigation into the α-diversity of the gut microbiome (Chao1, Simpson, ACE, etc.) revealed that only Chao1, ACE, and observed species exhibited significant changes in microbial α-diversity (Figure 3B and Supplementary Figure 3A). Fecal transplantation in EAP mice led to significant alterations in gut microbial β-diversity, as revealed by Bray–Curtis PCoA. (Figure 3C). At the genus level of the gut microbiome, we observed that following antibiotic microbiota transplantation in EAP mice, the expression of Bacteroides, Muribaculum, and others increased, whereas Lactobacillus and other genera presented increased expression levels in the mice that underwent EAP-mouse microbiota transplantation (Figure 3D). The detailed alterations in genus and species between the two groups are illustrated in Supplementary Figure 3B and 3C. LEfSe analysis revealed that Bacteroides, Muribaculum, DSSF69, and others presented relatively high expression levels in FMT from EAP + ABX mice. Mice that received EAP-mouse fecal transplantation presented elevated expression levels of Lactobacillus, Rikenella, and Odoribacter, among others (Figure 3E). ABX induces dysbiosis, which is characterized by enrichment of Bacteroidetes (e.g., Bacteroides) and Proteobacteria (e.g., Enterobacteriaceae), and depletion of Firmicutes such as Lachnospiraceae. Notably, many Lachnospiraceae and related clostridial taxa are responsible for the 7α-dehydroxylation of cholic acid into DCA. This bile acid 7α-dehydroxylation pathway is confined to a narrow group of anaerobic commensals (exemplified by Clostridium scindens and C. hylemonae) that possess the bai operon and convert cholic acid into the secondary bile acid DCA29,30. Accordingly, ABX-driven loss of these DCA-producing bacteria caused a significant decline in DCA levels in treated mice. We performed KEGG functional prediction, which revealed enrichment of the IL−17 signaling pathway (Figure 3F). We subsequently assessed the impact of FMT on the pathological symptoms and immune context of EAP mice. In the EAP mice that received ABX FMT, we noted a reduction in chronic pelvic discomfort (Figure 3G). The presence of inflammatory infiltrates in prostatic tissue that received FMT from EAP + ABX mice was greatly reduced, and the inflammation score decreased (Figure 3H). To further analyze alterations in Th17 cells, we assessed the fraction of CD4+ IL-17A + cells in the spleen and prostate, as well as CD4+ RORγt + cells in the spleen, and found a similar decrease in the proportion of Th17 cells following FMT from EAP + ABX mice. (Figure 3I,J and Supplementary Figure 8B). Moreover, to assess the alteration of Th17 cells in prostate tissue, we measured the mRNA expression levels of IL-17A and RORγt, which were reduced in prostate tissue following FMT of EAP + ABX (Figure 3K,L). We conducted IHC staining for IL-17A in prostate tissue to determine its expression location and quantity. We noted a substantial increase in the IL-17A expression level inside the prostatic interstitium of the mice subjected to FMT from the EAP mice (Figure 3M). We subsequently examined the levels of key inflammatory cytokines in vivo and found that the concentrations of IL-17A, GM-CSF, and IFN-γ in the serum and prostate tissue decreased following FMT from EAP + ABX mice (Figure 3N,O). We propose that FMT from EAP + ABX mice markedly alleviates prostate tissue inflammation and refines the gut microbiome structure in recipient EAP mice.

FMT from EAP + ABX mice alters bile acid metabolism in recipient EAP mice

Initially, we performed PCA of fecal metabolites and determined that the fecal metabolites of recipient EAP mice treated with FMT from EAP or EAP + ABX mice were markedly different (Figure 4A). With VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 as screening criteria, we discovered substantially distinct metabolites in fecal samples of FMTs from the EAP and EAP + ABX groups (Figure 4B). We conducted KEGG enrichment analysis, focusing on efferocytosis, glycerophospholipid metabolism, the sulfur relay system, and bile secretion. Unfortunately, no significant differences were detected in primary bile acid biosynthesis (Figure 4C). Since we had already targeted bile acid metabolism in mice, we focused on alterations in bile acid metabolism following FMT from EAP + ABX mice. After FMT, we employed targeted metabolomics to identify bile acids in the serum of the mice and identified 20 bile acids (Figure 4D). Furthermore, we sequenced mouse prostate tissue to analyze targeted bile acids. Interestingly, we discovered that DCA levels remained much lower in prostate tissue from the FMT group than in that from the EAP + ABX group (Figure 4E). As expected, the expression trends of DCA in feces, serum, and prostate tissue were consistent (Supplementary Figure 4). We further investigated the correlations between metabolite levels and gut microbiota abundances by integrating 16S rRNA gene sequencing with bile acid metabolites in mouse feces. At the bacterial genus level, we observed that the DCA and LDA scores were inversely associated with Bacteroides and Muribaculum but positively correlated with Lactobacillus and Rikenella (Figure 4F). We subsequently delineated notable disparities in metabolites and the gut microbiota for correlation networks. Only the log2-fold change in the DCA results was shown as a negative number and exhibited the greatest association (Figure 4G). We subsequently constructed an association between DCA and a scatter plot of the intestinal microbial distribution. Using a significance threshold of p < 0.05, we identified a link between DCA and UCG−009, Novosphingobium, and DSSF69, among others (Figure 4H). Next, we investigated the connection between genus-level abundance and the expression levels regulated through DCA (Figure 4I). To further determine whether bile acid metabolism functions in FMT, we analyzed the expression of genes regulating bile acid metabolism in liver (Figure 4J). These findings indicate that the bile acid metabolite DCA may be important for the treatment of EAP mice.

DCA promotes Th17 cell differentiation by inhibiting FXR expression

Our findings demonstrate that DCA plays an important role in ABX treatment and gut microbiome transplantation, influencing inflammatory responses. Researchers have also demonstrated that bile acid metabolism can modulate the differentiation of Th17 cells.18,31 However, these investigations did not explore the consequence of DCA on Th17 cell differentiation. This study further assessed the impact of DCA on inflammation and Th17 cells within prostate tissue, identifying possible therapeutic targets for prostatitis. After the EAP model was constructed, one cohort of EAP mice were fed a routine diet (EAP group) or an ABX diet (EAP + ABX group), whereas another cohort was fed an ABX diet supplemented with DCA (EAP + ABX + DCA group) (Figure 5A). We subsequently assessed the sensitivity of the mice to abdominal pain and revealed that the mice in the ABX + DCA group exhibited increased sensitivity to abdominal stimulation (Figure 5B). We noted inflammatory cell infiltration in the prostate, with a particularly pronounced presence of immune cells in the EAP + ABX + DCA group, whereas the mice treated with ABX had only serous fluid and minimal immune cell infiltration (Figure 5C). The mRNA expression of RORγt in prostate tissue was elevated in the EAP + ABX + DCA group (Figure 5D). To evaluate changes in Th17 cells, we quantified the proportion of CD4+ IL-17A + cells in the spleen (Figure 5E) and prostate (Supplementary Figure 8C). In parallel, the percentages of CD4+ RORγt + cells was measured in the spleen (Figure 5F). These observations demonstrated that Th17 cell levels were significantly increased in the ABX + DCA group. To further examine the expression of Th17 cells in the prostate, we conducted IHC labeling for IL-17A, and the findings indicated that the expression level of IL-17A in the prostate tissue of EAP + ABX + DCA mice increased (Figure 5H). We subsequently assessed the characteristic inflammatory cytokines in the serum and discovered that the levels of inflammatory factors in the EAP + ABX + DCA group were highly increased (Figure 5I). We then extracted naïve CD4+ T cells from the mouse spleen to examine the impact of DCA on Th17 cell differentiation. DCA increased the fraction of CD4+ IL−17 + cells and CD4+ RORγt + cells derived from naïve CD4+ T cells (Figure 5K,N). The DCA group presented increased expression of RORγt mRNA (Figure 5O). Next, we obtained the supernatant from the cell culture medium and observed elevated concentrations of IL-17A, GM-CSF, and IFN-γ in the medium from the DCA group (Figure 5L).

FXR functions mainly as a bile acid receptor. Research has indicated that FXR activation can mitigate or enhance colonic inflammation32. We then evaluated protein expression and mRNA level of FXR in prostate. Our findings indicated that DCA may markedly suppress FXR expression in the prostate (Figure 5G,J). The expression level of FXR in T cells was low. Nonetheless, our results showed that FXR mRNA expression was reduced after DCA treatment of CD4-differentiated Th17 cells (Figure 5P). We subsequently conducted proteomic analyses of the isolated cells. PCA revealed notable differences between the two proteomic groups (Supplementary Figure 5A). We subsequently identified 405 upregulated proteins and 430 downregulated proteins (Supplementary Figure 5B). Supplementary Figure 5C shows the relevant protein expression data. Notably, we observed an increase in the expression of IL-17A, MAPK6, and NLRP3, although other T-cell markers, such as FoxP3 and TBX21, were not significantly different. We conducted KEGG enrichment analysis and identified the IL-17 signaling pathway as important (Figure 5M). Our gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that the IL-17 signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation, and cholesterol metabolism were activated in the DCA group (Supplementary Figure 5D). These findings further illustrate that DCA can promote Th17 cell differentiation via FXR.

DCA initiates the NLRP3‒IL17A pathway to promote Th17 differentiation

FXR has been proposed to be crucial in multiple diseases through the regulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.33-35 Our previous proteomic analysis revealed high NLRP3 expression, and NLRP3 activation can influence Th17 differentiation.36 We investigated whether DCA could activate the NLRP3 inflammasome to influence Th17 cell differentiation by using MCC950, an NLRP3 inhibitor.37 We divided the EAP mice into three groups, as shown in Figure 6A. Upon model completion, behavioral assessments were performed, revealing that the abdominal pain sensitivity in the ABX + DCA + MCC950 group was diminished compared with that in the ABX + DCA group (Figure 6B). Notably, the administration of MCC950 significantly reduced immune cell infiltration in prostate tissue (Figure 6C). mRNA analyses subsequently revealed that MCC950 could decreased the expression of RORγt mRNA in prostate tissue (Figure 6D). To further validate the alteration in the Th17 cell ratio in the spleen and prostate, we analyzed CD4+ IL-17A + and CD4+ RORγt + T cells. The results revealed that the percentage of Th17 cells in mice treated with MCC950 was reduced (Figure 6E,F and Supplementary Figure 8D). Relative to those in the ABX + DCA group, the serum levels of inflammatory factors in the ABX + DCA + MCC950 group were lower (Supplementary Figure 6A). Simultaneously, we used IHC to assess the expression of NLRP3-associated proteins in prostate tissue. The abundance of NLRP3, IL-17A, and associated proteins were elevated in the ABX + DCA group but decreased in the ABX + DCA + MCC950 group. Notably, FXR expression was elevated in the ABX group but diminished in the ABX + DCA and ABX + DCA + MCC950 groups (Figure 6J). We subsequently isolated naïve CD4+ T cells from the mouse spleen; one group consisted solely of Th17 cells, in the other group, cells were supplemented with DCA, and in the final group, cell were supplemented with both DCA and MCC950. We assessed the role of DCA on Th17 cell differentiation via NLRP3 in vitro and observed that a greater percentage of CD4+ T cells supplemented with DCA developed into CD4+ IL-17A + and CD4+ RORγt + cells. Conversely, the fraction of CD4 + IL-17A + and CD4 + RORγt + cells decreased with the addition of MCC950 (Figure 6G,H). We then isolated proteins from the incubated cells and examined the expression of proteins associated with the NLRP3-IL17A signaling pathway.

We applied the FXR agonist GW4064 and the NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor MCC950 to further examine the regulatory function of FXR in the NLRP3-IL17A pathway. After antibiotic treatment, the EAP mice were divided into four groups (Figure 7A). H&E staining of prostate issue exhibited enhanced inflammatory infiltration in the ABX + DCA group, whereas inflammation was reduced in the prostate of the mice treated with GW4064 or MCC950 (Figure 7C). Moreover, the expression of RORγt mRNA in prostate tissue was assessed, revealing a reduction in mice treated with MCC950 or GW4064 (Figure 7D). We subsequently investigated the expression of FXR in the prostate. The expression of FXR mRNA of mice treated with GW4064 was elevated, whereas that in mice treated with MCC950 was decreased (Figure 7E). Spleen and prostate from the mice exhibited considerable reductions in the proportions of CD4+ IL-17A + and CD4+ RORγt + T cells subsequent to treatment with MCC950 and GW4064 (Figure 7F,G and Supplementary Figure 8E). The levels of GM-CSF, IFN-γ, and IL-17A in mouse serum decreased following the administration of MCC950 and GW4064 (Figure 7H). Then, we conducted in vitro sorting of naïve CD4+ T cells and divided them into four groups. Among them, one group underwent standard in vitro differentiation, another group was treated with AAV-FXR, the third group received both AAV-FXR and GW4064, and the last group was administered AAV-FXR and MCC950. We subsequently observed NLRP3-IL17A-related mRNA expression and found that cells treated with AAV-FXR presented elevated levels of NLRP3 and other proteins, whereas the introduction of GW4064 activated FXR and decreased NLRP3 expression (Figure 7L). To assess the extent of inflammatory alterations, we obtained supernatants from the cell culture medium. The findings indicated that the levels of GM-CSF, IFN-γ, and IL-17A in the supernatants of the medium increased upon FXR inhibition (Figure 7I). To further examine the alterations linked to NLRP3-IL17A, we extracted proteins from the cells and found that the expression levels of NLRP3, ASC, and IL-17A increased upon FXR inhibition, whereas inflammation decreased following treatment with MCC950 and GW4064 (Figure 7M). Next, we conducted IHC analysis of prostate tissue and found that the expression of the inflammatory protein NLRP3 was elevated, whereas the expression of FXR was reduced in the prostate tissue of mice treated with only DCA (Figure 7N). To clarify whether antibiotics also alleviate the symptoms of patients with CP/CPPS through the gut microbiome and bile acid metabolism, we performed clinical research to obtain feces and serum from nine CP/CPPS patients who had not previously taken antibiotics. After 14 days of fluoroquinolone treatment 40 samples were taken.38 Thereafter, we conducted metabolomic and 16S rRNA gene analyses on the samples. PCA revealed substantial differences between fecal metabolomic samples before and during antibiotic treatment (Figure 8A). Additionally, we discovered compounds that exhibited substantial changes in the feces of CPPS patients (Figure 8B). Discussion

The significance of the gut microbiome during antibiotic administration and the molecular processes that affect autoimmune illnesses and metabolic regulation are poorly understood. This work highlights two distinct but fundamentally connected effects of alterations in the host microbiome on mitigating immune inflammation in EAP mice and modulating bile acid metabolism following antibiotic therapy. Initially, alterations in the gut microbial community composition and metabolism induced by antibiotics were assumed to be essential for the complete manifestation of their effects on Th17 cell differentiation and bile acid metabolism. Additionally, we present evidence that ABX-treated intestinal microbiota transfer significantly diminishes prostatic inflammatory infiltration in EAP mice, which inherently influences the amelioration of the intestinal microbiota and bile acid metabolism. Collectively, our results indicate that metabolic alterations in the gut microbiome of ABX-treated prostatitis patients are not only essential but also adequate to facilitate the advantageous metabolism of ABX intervention through FMT as the principal function. The degree of Th17 cell differentiation determines the infiltration and inflammatory response of prostate tissue.21,39,40 Our findings demonstrate a significantly diminished differentiation capacity of Th17 cells in FMT from antibiotic-treated mice. Consequently, the microbiome following antibiotic therapy possesses significant qualities for reducing prostate inflammation, potentially paving the way for innovative microbiome-based therapeutics for CPPS/CP patients. Importantly, our research also reinforced our previously proposed concept of the “gut– prostate axis”, which involves immunometabolism connections between the intestine and extraintestinal organs, such as the prostate.24

After ABX treatment, Bacteroides and Enterobacteriaceae were the dominant bacterial communities in the gut of the mice. There is no evidence in early reports that Bacteroides can convert CA to DCA.41 However, recent studies have shown that Bacteroides may convert CA into DCA.42 Therefore, the function of Bacteroides needs to be further explored. With respect to Escherichia coli, a species of Enterobacteriaceae, recent reports have shown that the autotransporter of Escherichia coli, can induce mucin degradation when it is incubated with DCA.43 In patients with cirrhosis, the abundance of 7α-dehydroxylating bacteria, such as Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae, is low, and patients have low secondary bile acid levels.44 Clinical studies have shown that DCA, Ruminococcaceae, and Lachnospiraceae levels are proportional.45,46 In EAP mice, the abundance of 7α-dehydroxylating bacteria was significantly elevated, supporting the high abundance of DCA in the gut of EAP mice from another point of view. Thus, ABX treatment reduced the abundance of these DCA-producing 7α-dehydroxylating bacteria, leading to a significant reduction in DCA levels in ABX mice. Steroid compounds undoubtedly play a role in the management of metabolic and immunological balance following ABX therapy-induced changes.47 In this work, we discovered a crucial function in bile acid metabolism that can be addressed by FMT from ABX-treated EAP mice to EAP mice and may be caused via certain bacterial metabolic activities. In particular, we detected reduced DCA abundance in the feces, serum, and prostate of mice following ABX and FMT. Our metabolomics and microbial composition results indicate that bacteria have essential functions in the immune response and DCA metabolism. Interestingly, on the basis of the literature, we also found that some specific changes in the microbial communities in this study may be related to inflammation. For example, at the genus level, Bacteroides serve as useful bacteria that alleviate inflammation, whereas Alistipes are strongly associated with microbiome dysregulation and inflammation in mice.48,49 Network correlation and feature‒component association analyses revealed a significant connection between DCA and gut microbiome metabolism. Recent findings also indicate that DCA-induced ferroptosis exacerbates inflammation.50 These observations illustrate the complex interactions among the gut microbiome, immune cells, and host homeostasis, whereby bile acid metabolism serves as a significant conduit for mutual interaction. An increase in DCA metabolism and its products may radically modulate the signaling activation of cholesterol and bile acid-related steroid genes and FXR receptors. We demonstrated that alterations in bile acid content and metabolism in mice following ABX therapy and FMT influence inflammatory infiltration of the prostate via FXR–NLRP3 signaling. In mice administered ABX, the gut microbiome was eliminated, and endogenous bile acid levels were altered in DCA-treated mice.

Integrating the in vivo Th17 ratio, prostate inflammation, and in vitro Th17 cell differentiation data indicated that DCA can directly influence CD4+ T-cell differentiation. Our findings are consistent with previous research conducted by Li et al., which indicates that DCA combines CYP51 to upregulate RORγt to regulate the differentiation of Th17 in the context of a high-fat diet.51 On the other hand, the current studies only indicate that LCA and their bacterial metabolites do not have direct evidence suggesting that DCA can inhibit RORγt.18,52,53 In this study, RORγt is only used as a detection index of Th17, while the direct relationship between DCA and RORγt requires further study.

Proteomic sequencing revealed activation of the Th17 differentiation signaling pathway, the IL−17 signaling pathway, and the cholesterol signaling pathway, confirming the findings of our previous investigations regarding the relationship between Th17 cell differentiation and bile acids. Moreover, NLRP3 is crucial for Th17 cell differentiation.54,55 We observed activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in EAP, and administration of the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 alleviated DCA-induced inflammation and diminished Th17 differentiation. These findings indicate that MCC950-mediated suppression of NLRP3 results in a reduction in IL-1β, subsequently causing a decrease in the number of Th17 cells.56-58 Our research revealed that the NLRP3 pathway is involved in the process by which antibiotics improve EAP through the gut microbiome.

Interestingly, FXR reportedly suppresses hepatocyte endoplasmic reticulum kinase activity and NLRP3 expression and ameliorates liver damage.34 In colitis, Bacteroides fragilis mitigates necrotizing enterocolitis by reestablishing bile acid metabolic equilibrium and suppressing the FXR–NLRP3 signaling cascade through bile brine hydrolysis enzymes.35 FXR, a nuclear receptor that modulates ligand activation and governs bile acid production, transport, and secretion, is implicated in several metabolic disorders.59,60 In this study, FXR expression in T cells and prostate tissue is lower; however, its importance should not be overlooked. We employed Chip-qPCR and luciferase assays to verify that FXR interacts with NLRP3 primarily during CD4+ T-cell differentiation into Th17 cells. Dysregulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is linked to bile acid-related disorders.61,62 The recent findings that FXR is a negative regulator and that DCA is an activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome may elucidate the causative links among FXR signaling, bile acids, the NLRP3 inflammasome, and the pathological progression of these disorders.51,63 In conclusion, our findings indicate that FXR expression suppresses NLRP3 expression in Th17 cells, thereby influencing Th17 cell differentiation. Certainly, better clarification of the mechanism by which FXR modulates the expression of the pivotal transcription factor RORγt in Th17 cells, as well as the protective impact of FXR activation on prostate tissue, is essential for prospective research.

In the present study, in the clinical cohort, fluoroquinolones, which were more targeted antimicrobial agents, were administered to patients with CP/CPPS. Fluoroquinolones primarily target gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli, and are clinically empirical treatment agents.38 ABX comprehensively affects the composition and function of the gut microbiome for EAP mice. Ampicillin belongs to the penicillin group of beta-lactam antibiotics and can penetrate both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. Previous studies have shown that the administration of ampicillin alone suppressed most mouse gut bacteria but did not eliminate all of them.64,65 Neomycin is an aminoglycoside antibiotic that mainly targets gram-negative bacteria, causing messenger RNA decoding errors, blocking mRNA, transferring RNA translocations, and inhibiting ribosome circulation.66 Metronidazole is commonly used to treat infections caused by anaerobic bacteria accounting for most of the bacterial components of the gut microbiome.67 Vancomycin is used to treat gram-positive infections. Mice treated with vancomycin showed reduced numbers of gram-positive bacteria and increased compensation for gram-negative bacteria.68. The combination of antibiotics minimized the abundance and diversity of the gut microbiome in mice.69 Compared with antibiotic therapy in patients, ABX therapy in mice affected a wider range of the gut microbiome and eliminated the flora more thoroughly, which could improve the exploration of the role of the gut microbiome in prostatitis.

The discrepancies between our findings and those of previous studies suggest that the proinflammatory role of antibiotics may stem from differences in disease models, microbial contexts, and immune pathways involved.70,71 Prior studies have focused on infectious disease models, where antibiotics induce acute immune activation via endotoxin release from bacterial lysis.72,73 In contrast, our study employs a noninfectious autoimmune model in which the immune response is driven by aberrant T-cell activation rather than direct pathogen-induced inflammation. Additionally, while previous studies have often emphasized the overgrowth of gram-negative bacteria following antibiotic treatment, our findings highlight an alternative consequence of antibiotic-mediated dysbiosis: the loss of proinflammatory microbial metabolites (such as DCA) that sustain chronic inflammation via adaptive immune pathways.74 These findings suggest that the impact of antibiotics on immune function is highly context-dependent and influenced by microbiota-derived metabolic changes rather than solely by bacterial burden.

In conclusion, our study challenges the conventional paradigm that antibiotics universally promote inflammation and instead highlights their potential immunomodulatory role through gut microbiota-derived metabolic pathways. By elucidating the gut microbiota–bile acid–FXR– NLRP3 axis, we provide a novel mechanistic framework for understanding the broader impact of antibiotic therapy beyond its direct antimicrobial effects. Future research should explore whether targeted microbiota modulation or bile acid-based therapies could offer safer and more precise alternatives to broad-spectrum antibiotics in the treatment of immune-mediated diseases.

Materials and methods

Animals

Nonobese diabetic (NOD) male mice (5–7 weeks old, 18 ± 2 g) were obtained from the Nanjing Model Animal Center (Nanjing, China) and housed in the specific pathogen-free facility of Anhui Medical University. Every animal experiment was carried out in compliance with the rules set out by Anhui Medical University's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (LLSC20221264). NOD mice are commonly used to develop EAP models for investigating the pathophysiology of chronic prostatitis.75,76 EAP was generated in mice according to a method reported in our earlier work.77 In the EAP group, the mice were injected subcutaneously with prostate antigens (PAg), which were made up of equal volumes of rat male accessory gland (MAG) extracts and complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA, Sigma‒Aldrich, F5881), at three different locations: the base of the tail (0.050 mL), bilateral foot pads (0.025 mL per side), and the shoulder (0.050 mL). The mice were injected with MAG extracts emulsified in an equivalent amount of incomplete Freund's adjuvant (IFA; Sigma‒Aldrich, F5506) two weeks after the previous vaccination. Blood glucose and insulin levels were assessed in EAP mice to exclude potential confounding effects of diabetes (Supplementary Figure 1F-I).

Clinical sample collection

In accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, all specimens were obtained and analyzed with the patients' informed consent. Forty fecal samples and forty serum samples were obtained from CP/CPPS patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University authorized our work (Grant No: PJ 2024−11−58). Supplementary Table 1 presents the patients’ age, sex, height, and weight.

Behavioral test

Von Frey filaments were used to assess the tactile allergic and referred hyperalgesia of NOD mice to replicate the pelvic pain responses of EAP mice.78 Forces of 0.04 g, 0.16 g, 0.4 g, 1.0 g, and 4.0 g were applied to the lower abdomen of each mouse ten times, as was done in our earlier research on day 28.79 On the basis of the three responses listed below, response specificity was verified: i) leaping; ii) instantly licking or clawing the testing region; or iii) pulling back the lower abdomen.

Animal antibiotics and drug treatment

After the second immunization, EAP mice assigned to the ABX group were given a chronic antibiotic-containing diet according to established protocols.80 Ampicillin (1 g/l, Sigma), neomycin sulfate (1 g/l, Sigma), metronidazole (1 g/l, Sigma), and vancomycin (500 mg/l, Sigma) were added to the drinking water for two weeks. The drinking water was changed every three days until the mice were sacrificed.81 Conversely, the EAP control group received a normal diet. The mice were randomly divided into 5 groups: the antibiotic mixture treatment group (ABX), the antibiotic treatment with diet group (ABX + DCA, 0.2% DCA, MCE, HY-N0593), the antibiotic treatment with DCA and the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 group (ABX + DCA + MCC950, MCE, HY−12815), the antibiotic treatment with DCA and FXR agonist GW4064 group (ABX + DCA + GW4064, MCE, HY−50108), and the ABX + DCA + MCC950 + GW4064 group.63,82,83 The therapeutic dosage of the medication was administered according to the manufacturer's product instructions. After continuous treatment for 14 days, the mice were euthanized, and the tissues were collected for subsequent analysis.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

To decrease the bacterial load in the recipient EAP mice, they were treated with 1 ml of antibiotic cocktail consisting of ampicillin (1 g/l, Sigma), neomycin sulfate (1 g/l, Sigma), metronidazole (1 g/l, Sigma), and vancomycin (500 mg/l, Sigma) once daily by oral gavage for 7 consecutive days.84 To rule out the effects of residual antibiotics, ELISA kits (Jining Bio, ampicillin JN39139, neomycin sulfate JN39092, metronidazole JN39120, vancomycin JN39109) were used to detect antibiotic residues in the feces of EAP mice after 48 hours (washout period) of antibiotic treatment (Supplementary Figure 1E).85,86 For two weeks, fecal samples from EAP and EAP + ABX mice were collected, diluted at 1 g per 10 mL of sterile PBS, and administered daily by gavage to recipient EAP mice.

Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining

The prostate was fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde solution. To ensure anatomical consistency across all groups, the prostates were placed on a cooled platform with the bladder neck used as the proximal landmark. A series of coronal sections was made perpendicular to the urethra at a distance of 3 mm distal to the bladder neck. Sections were categorized into lobes (ventral, dorsolateral, and anterior) based on identifiable histological landmarks (Supplementary Table 2).87 H&E staining was subsequently used for histopathological examination of the prostate tissue. The inflammatory level of prostate tissue was assessed according to a previously reported grading principle (0–4, where 0 represents a noninflammatory status and 4 represents severe infiltration of immune cells).88,89 The mean score for each section was determined by two experienced pathologists through independent evaluation.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

The slides were prepared, fixed and permeated according to the instructions of the IHC assay. The sections were incubated with mouse anti-IL-17A antibodies (Proteintech, 66148−1-lg), rabbit anti-FXR antibodies (Santa Cruz, sc−25309), rabbit anti-caspase-1β antibodies (Affinity, AF5418), rabbit anti-ASC antibodies (Affinity, DF6304), rabbit anti-NLRP3 antibodies (Santa Cruz, sc−134306), rabbit anti-cleaved caspase−1 antibodies (Affinity, AF4005), mouse anti-IL-1β antibodies (Affinity, BF8021) and cleaved-IL-1β antibodies (Affinity, AF4006) overnight at 4 °C, and appropriate biotinylated goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG (Servicebio, China). Digital images of tissue sections were acquired with a Pannoramic MIDI scanner (3DHistech, Hungary). Immunohistochemical quantification was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Tissue images were imported, DAB and hematoxylin channels were separated via Color Deconvolution, and a threshold was applied to the DAB channel to identify positive areas. The 'Measure' function was then used to quantify the stained area.

Flow cytometry (FCM)

Lymphocytes were extracted from mouse spleen and prostate and placed in flow cytometry tubes. Cells were labeled with FITC-anti-CD4 (rat anti-mouse, BD, 553047) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) containing PMA (MultiSciences, CS0001), monensin (MultiSciences, CS0004), and ionomycin (MultiSciences, CS0002−500) at 37 °C for 4 h. After fixation and permeabilization (Invitrogen, 00−5523−00), cells were stained with PE-IL-17A (rat anti-mouse, BD, 559502) or RORγt (mouse anti-mouse, BD, 562607) for 60 min, washed, and subjected to flow cytometric analysis (Beckman Coulter).

Quantification of fecal bacterial density by flow cytometry

Mouse fecal samples (0.2 g) were resuspended in PBS, and debris was removed by centrifugation at 300g for 20 seconds at room temperature.90 The clarified supernatant was diluted 1:200 in PBS and stained with Syto™ BC (ThermoFisher, B7277) for 15 minutes at room temperature. Calibration beads were then added to the sample. Flow cytometry was performed, and bacterial counts were quantified based on the number of bacterial events acquired after recording 10,000 calibration bead events (Supplementary Figure 1J).

Naïve CD4+ T-cell isolation and in vitro differentiation

Naïve CD4+ T cells were isolated from the spleen of the mice and differentiated into Th17 cells in vitro according to our previously established protocol.24 Magnetic purification of CD4⁺ T cells from splenic lymphocytes was carried out using the CD4⁺ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi, 130−104−454) and LS Columns (Miltenyi, 130−042−401). Naïve CD4⁺ T cells were grown in RPMI 1640 supplemented with anti-CD3 (Bio X Cell, BE0001−1) and anti-CD28 (Bio X Cell, BE0015−1). Th17 cells were stimulated with IL−6 (Novoprotein, CG39), TGF-β1 (Novoprotein, CA59), anti-IL−4 (Bio X Cell, BE0045), anti-IFN-γ (Bio X Cell, BE0055), and 100 μM DCA (MCE, HY-N0593).51

FXR Suppression

Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) was used to inhibit the gene encoding FXR in Th17 cells. FXR shRNA (shFXR) and the empty vector (shControl) were purchased from Shanghai Han Heng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (HH20230721WY-LV02). The target sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 3. To produce stable gene knockdown cells, AAV-shFXR was transfected strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of the cytokines IL‒17A, interferon gamma (IFN‒γ), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM‒CSF),and insulin were measured with an Elabscience ELISA kit in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions (Elabscience, IL‒17A E‒EL‒M0047c, GM‒CSF E‒EL‒M0032c, IFN‒γ E‒EL‒M0048c and insulin E-EL-M1382c).

16S rDNA gene sequencing analysis

Stool samples were obtained and promptly frozen upon collection. A QIAamp 96 PowerFecal QIAcube HT Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) was used for the extraction of bacterial genomic DNA from the fecal samples. Data were generated on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Paired reads were processed with Trimmomatic, assembled by FLASH, and denoised using QIIME (v1.8.0). The Vsearch program was subsequently employed to exclude primer sequences and perform clustering to produce operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with a similarity criterion of 97%. The QIIME software application assigned a representative sequence for each OTU. All remaining readings were annotated and processed with the RDP Classifier in the Silva database (version 138).

Metabolome sequencing

The blood samples were centrifuged at 1500 × g at 4 °C for 10 minutes to collect the serum. The tissue and fecal samples were stored at -80 °C. The serum, fecal, and prostate tissue samples were sent to OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for metabolite extraction and liquid chromatography‒tandem mass (LC‒MS) spectrometry analysis. Bioinformatics analysis was subsequently conducted. Target metabolites were identified and quantified using UPLC-ESI-MS/MS. Chromatographic separation was performed with a 5 μL injection volume and a flow rate of 0.45 mL/min. The mobile phases were: A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (methanol:acetonitrile:isopropanol = 1:1:1, 0.1% formic acid), applied under a gradient elution program.MS parameters were: curtain gas, 35 psi; CAD, medium; spray voltage, −4500 V (negative mode) and 5500 V (positive mode); source temperature, 450 °C; column temperature, 45 °C; Both nebulizer gas (Gas1) and auxiliary gas (Gas2) at 55 psi. Quantification was performed in MRM mode using a triple quadrupole. TICs from QC samples were overlaid to assess technical reproducibility. Peaks were manually aligned based on retention time and shape. Peak areas represented relative metabolite abundance. Calibration curves were established using serial dilutions of mixed standards. The concentration-to-internal standard ratio (x-axis) was plotted against peak area ratio (y-axis). Metabolite concentrations were calculated by fitting sample peak areas into the corresponding regression equations. A t test was used to calculate the statistical significance of the observed differences in the study variables (P value). Metabolites with VIP > 1, P < 0.05, and a fold change > 2 were considered differentially abundant.

Proteomics

CD4+ T cells that had differentiated and been cultured in vitro were collected. The total protein from each sample was extracted, one portion was removed for protein concentration determination and SDS‒PAGE analysis, and another portion was used for trypsin digestion. After the digested peptides were desalted, LC‒MS/MS identification was performed on the samples. LC–MS/MS data were acquired using DIA technology. Spectronaut Pulsar 18.7 (Biognosys) was employed to process raw DIA files, including spectrum matching, quantitative extraction, and subsequent bioinformatics analysis.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP detection was performed according to the kit instructions. For immunoprecipitation, an anti-FXR (NR1H4) antibody (Santa Cruz, sc−25309) was used. In addition, the purified DNA was analyzed via qPCR. The sequences of the primers used were as follows: Nlrp3 site 1 (F: TGGTGTTTGCTCATCCTCCT, R: CCAAGACCAATCAATCGAACT), Nlrp3 site 2 (F: TGAGCCCTTCATACATTCCA, R: CGATGAGCAAACACCACTCA), and Nlrp3 site 3 (F: CCGCCTGTTCACAATGTTT, R: CTGTCTGTGCCTCCTGAGC). q‒PCR and agarose gel electrophoresis were used to analyze the results of the ChIP.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT‒PCR)

Total RNA was obtained from cultured cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026) as recommended by the manufacturer. cDNA was generated using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, RR047A), and qPCR was performed with the TB Green Premix Ex Taq kit (Takara, RR820A) on an ABI 7500 instrument (Applied Biosystems). The primers are provided in Supplementary Table 4. All reactions were conducted in a 20 µL tube, with mouse GAPDH as the internal standard. The data were processed using the formula 2−ΔΔCt for each sample.

Western blot analysis

CD4⁺ T cells under Th17-inducing conditions were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013) plus protease inhibitors (Beyotime, ST506−2). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against IL-17A (Proteintech, 66148−1-lg), FXR (Santa Cruz, sc−25309), caspase−1 (Affinity, AF5418), ASC (Affinity, DF6304), cleaved-caspase−1 (Affinity, AF4005), NLRP3 (Santa Cruz, sc−134306), IL-1β (Affinity, BF8021), and cleaved-IL-1β (Affinity, AF4006). Protein bands were detected with ECL reagents (Advansta, K−12043), recorded on ChemiScope 5600 film (Hengmei Technology), and quantified using ImageJ software.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT)

Mice in the EAP and EAP + ABX groups were fasted for 6 hours before receiving an intraperitoneal injection of glucose (2 g/kg). Blood samples were obtained from the tail tip at 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 minutes, and glucose levels were determined with an Accu-Chek Guide device (Accu-Chek, Germany).25

Intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (IPITT)

Following a 12-h fast, EAP and EAP + ABX mice were injected intraperitoneally with insulin (0.5 U/kg), and tail-vein glucose was measured at 0, 15, 30, 45, and 60 minutes.

Statistical analysis

All the data are presented as the means ± SEMs. The statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Two-tailed Student's t tests were performed to compare two groups. The results were considered statistically significant when P values were < 0.05.
