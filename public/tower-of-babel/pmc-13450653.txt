Neuroimaging of the ictal–interictal continuum: Where do we stand? A systematic review and meta‐analysis of current evidence
Authors: Panagiota‐Eleni Tsalouchidou, Kateriine Orav, Tiago Lerda‐Casaccia, Leona Möller, Georgios Tsivgoulis, Anastasios Bonakis, Lukas Machegger, Giorgi Kuchukhidze, Susanne Knake, Eugen Trinka, Pilar Bosque‐Varela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePatterns within the ictal–interictal continuum (IIC) are frequently encountered in critically ill patients undergoing continuous electroencephalographic studies and may reflect ongoing ictal activity or secondary brain injury. Peri‐ictal neuroimaging abnormalities (PNA) have been described in association with status epilepticus (SE), but their occurrence in patients with IIC across different neuroimaging modalities remains incompletely characterized. We aimed to assess the occurrence of PNA in adults with IIC across structural and functional neuroimaging techniques.MethodsWe performed a systematic review and meta‐analysis following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Embase, and Scopus were searched up to January 2026 for studies reporting PNA in adults with IIC. Eligible modalities included magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), CT perfusion (CTP), and positron emission tomography (PET). Pooled detection rates were estimated using random‐effects generalized linear mixed models.ResultsOf 8400 records identified, seven studies met the inclusion criteria. Of these, four contributed MRI data (98 patients), two—CTP data (38 patients), and two—PET data (30 patients); no SPECT study was identified. One study contributed data to two modalities. Pooled proportion of PNA was 0.41 (95% CI: 0.30–0.52; I 2 = 30.4%) for MRI, 0.34 (95% CI: 0.21–0.50; I 2 = 0%) for CTP, and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.73–0.97; I 2 = 0%) for PET.SignificanceThere are currently a limited number of studies evaluating PNA in IIC. PET showed the highest pooled proportion of PNA, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further studies are required to better define the neuroimaging spectrum within the IIC and to clarify its clinical and diagnostic implications.Keywords: hyperperfusion, perfusion imaging, peri‐ictal MRI abnormalities, possible nonconvulsive status epilepticus, status epilepticus

INTRODUCTION

The ictal–interictal continuum (IIC) defines a spectrum of electroencephalographic patterns situated between clearly interictal activity and unequivocal seizures. This spectrum is frequently encountered in critically ill patients, 1 particularly in the setting of acute brain injury, subtle or nonconvulsive manifestations, and often a severely impaired level of consciousness. 2 Its clinical significance remains uncertain and continues to fuel debate regarding optimal management, specifically whether IIC patterns warrant aggressive antiseizure treatment or may be safely observed without intervention. 3 , 4 According to the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society (ACNS) terminology, 5 this spectrum includes periodic discharges (PDs) and spike‐and‐wave patterns occurring at frequencies >1–2.5 Hz, or at 0.5–1 Hz when accompanied by a plus modifier or fluctuation, as well as lateralized rhythmic delta activity (LRDA) averaging >1 Hz with a plus modifier or fluctuation, provided that these patterns do not meet criteria for definite seizures or status epilepticus.

Electroencephalography (EEG) is essential for defining IIC. 5 , 6 However, EEG alone is insufficient to characterize the underlying biological activity across this spectrum of different EEG patterns. In particular, the underlying etiology may substantially shape the observed EEG signatures, which may often be an epiphenomenal activity of severe brain damage and therefore may not necessarily reflect ongoing ictal activity. 7 Consequently, ancillary diagnostic tools may be required.

In this context, neuroimaging has gained increasing importance in recent years, providing meaningful insights into structural, 8 , 9 perfusion, 10 and metabolic alterations 11 associated with status epilepticus (SE). In patients with SE, peri‐ictal neuroimaging abnormalities (PNA) have been described across multiple imaging modalities, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), 12 computed tomography perfusion (CTP), 13 positron emission tomography (PET), 11 and single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT).

Rather than representing static findings, PNA constitute a dynamic continuum that evolves over time in accordance with the underlying peri‐ictal pathophysiological cascade. Depending on the timing of image acquisition and the stage along the ongoing ictal activity, 14 these abnormalities may manifest as focal signal changes, hyperperfusion, or hypermetabolism, reflecting varying degrees of neuronal hyperexcitability, altered neurovascular coupling, and metabolic demand. Furthermore, in patients with SE, PNA have been associated with certain EEG patterns, such as lateralized periodic discharges (LPDs). 15 , 16

While PNA have been well documented in patients with definitive SE, the occurrence of PNA across different neuroimaging modalities in IIC—and their relationship to EEG patterns—remains largely unexplored. Therefore, we aimed to systematically assess the proportion of PNA across multiple imaging modalities in adults with IIC. In a secondary analysis, we examined whether the study‐level proportion of LPDs was associated with the proportion of PNA reported across studies.

METHODS

Study selection

A systematic search of PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase, and Scopus was conducted to identify studies reporting peri‐ictal imaging abnormalities in adults with IIC. Searches encompassed MRI, MR perfusion, CT perfusion, PET, and SPECT and were last updated on January 15, 2026. Full search strategies are provided in Supplementary Material S1.

All records were imported into Mendeley Desktop (version 1.19.8; Elsevier), and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (PT and PBV) independently screened titles and abstracts and assessed full texts for eligibility, resolving discrepancies by consensus. The review followed a predefined protocol aligned with PRISMA 2020 17 guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261298231). 18

Study inclusion criteria

Eligible studies enrolled adult participants (≥18 years) with IIC identified through clinical evaluation or electroencephalographic evidence and reported PNA, including MRI, CT perfusion, PET, and SPECT. Observational studies, including cohort studies, cross‐sectional analyses, and case series comprising at least five patients, were included. Publications focusing exclusively on children, single‐patient reports, abstracts without full manuscripts, narrative or systematic reviews, and studies lacking extractable data specific to IIC were excluded. Only articles published in English were considered.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (PT and PBV) independently extracted data using a predefined standardized form. Extracted variables included study characteristics, patient demographics, imaging modality, and the number and proportion of patients exhibiting PNA.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers (KO and TLC) using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies. Given the objective of the study, the NOS domain “comparability of cohorts” was deemed not applicable. The risk of bias was assessed regarding the selection of study participants and outcome ascertainment. Any inconsistencies in scoring were addressed through discussion and, when required, adjudicated by a third reviewer (PBV).

Data synthesis and statistical methods

PNA were defined as peri‐ictal MRI abnormalities (PMA) or regional hyperperfusion or hypermetabolism on functional imaging that were considered attributable to IIC. The prevalence of these findings was synthesized using generalized linear mixed effect models with a binomial distribution and random study‐level effects. Variability across studies was quantified using τ2 and I 2. Funnel plots and formal tests for publication bias were not performed because fewer than 10 studies were available for each meta‐analysis. An exploratory random effects meta‐regression using a generalized linear mixed model was performed to assess the association between the study‐level proportion of LPDs and PNA. For the purposes of this analysis, LPDs were defined as a binary variable (present vs. absent). All statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.4.2) with the meta and metafor packages. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22

RESULTS

Literature search results and study characteristics

The comprehensive database search identified a total of 8400 records across PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Embase. Following the removal of 1680 duplicate records, 6720 unique citations remained and were screened at the title and abstract level. Of these, 331 articles underwent full‐text review for eligibility. Full‐text exclusions occurred due to publication type (n = 89), inappropriate study design (n = 25), ineligible population (n = 120), outcomes not relevant to peri‐ictal imaging in the IIC (n = 80), and non‐English language (n = 10). Ultimately, seven unique studies met the inclusion criteria and were included. 10 , 11 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 One study reported both MRI and CTP findings from the same cohort 10 ; therefore, this cohort contributed to both modality‐specific analyses, comprising a total of four MRI studies, two CTP studies, and two PET studies. No SPECT study was identified. Neuroimaging abnormalities in the ictal–interictal continuum

Magnetic resonance imaging

Four studies reporting PMA in patients with IIC were included, comprising a total of 98 patients. Using a random effects generalized linear mixed model, the pooled proportion of patients with PMA was 0.41 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.30–0.52). Between‐study heterogeneity was low to moderate (τ2 = 0.0036; χ2 = 4.31, degrees of freedom [df] = 3, p = 0.23; I 2 = 30.4%). The corresponding forest plot is shown in Figure 2A.

Squares indicate study estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs); the diamond represents the pooled estimate. (B) Forest plot of pooled peri‐ictal CT perfusion (CTP) abnormalities in ictal–interictal continuum (IIC) using a random effects generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). A sensitivity analysis excluding Tada et al. showed a pooled proportion of PMA of 0.39 (95% CI: 0.27–0.52; I 2 = 44.6%), compared with 0.41 (95% CI: 0.30–0.52; I 2 = 30.4%) in the primary analysis, indicating that the overall findings were unchanged.

Computed tomography perfusion

Two studies evaluating peri‐ictal CTP abnormalities were included, with a total of 38 patients. The pooled proportion of patients demonstrating peri‐ictal hyperperfusion on CTP was 0.34 (95% CI: 0.21–0.50). No between‐study heterogeneity was observed (τ2 = 0; χ2 = 0.10, df = 1, p = 0.75; I 2 = 0%). Forest plots for CTP are presented in Figure 2B.

Positron emission tomography

Two studies reporting peri‐ictal PET hypermetabolism were included, comprising 30 patients. The pooled proportion of patients with peri‐ictal PET hypermetabolism was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.73–0.97). No heterogeneity was detected across studies (τ2 = 0; χ2 = 0.06, df = 1, p = 0.8; I 2 = 0%). Forest plots for PET findings are shown in Figure 2C.

Meta‐regression of LPDs on EEG and PNA

Meta‐regression, including five studies, showed a nonsignificant trend between the proportion of LPDs and the proportion of peri‐ictal imaging abnormalities (β = 4.07, 95% CI: −0.29 to 8.43; p = 0.067), with moderate residual heterogeneity (τ2 = 0.62; I 2 = 57.8%). Given the small number of included studies, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Risk of bias assessment

The included studies exhibited moderate methodological quality, with the majority performing well in participant selection and outcome assessment. Comparability of cohorts was deemed nonapplicable. Studies utilizing advanced imaging modalities such as PET are likely subject to referral bias to include more severe patient groups, especially if retrospective. A detailed summary of individual NOS scores and domain‐specific evaluation is provided in Supplementary Material S4.

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review and meta‐analysis, PNA were frequently detected across different imaging modalities in adults with IIC. Specifically, pooled proportions were 41% for MRI, 34% for CTP, and 90% for PET abnormalities, indicating that PNA are common even when formal electrographic SE criteria are not met. While acknowledging differences in imaging timing and EEG monitoring across the included studies, the observed findings suggest that IIC may share pathophysiological mechanisms with definitive SE.

In patients with definitive SE, PMA are well characterized. Diffusion restriction and FLAIR hyperintensity occur in approximately 25% of patients with definitive SE. 8 , 27 These changes are considered to reflect a mismatch between increased metabolic demand and insufficient energy supply. 14 In nonconvulsive status epilepticus (NCSE), higher proportions of PMA were reported in a recent meta‐analysis, with DWI and FLAIR alterations observed in 36% and 51% of patients, respectively. The prevalence of PMA in IIC observed in our analysis lies between these previously reported estimates for definitive SE. Moreover, a recent large prospective MRI study directly compared PMA in patients with definitive SE and those with IIC, finding comparable rates of PMA between groups (47% vs. 43%, p = 0.37). 27

Hypermetabolism and hyperperfusion are more commonly observed than DWI and FLAIR changes in SE, likely reflecting early regional responses that compensate for increased metabolic demand. 14 Focal hypermetabolism rates as high as 92% have been reported in patients with suspected NCSE. 11 In a recent meta‐analysis, the pooled proportion of hyperperfusion on CTP and arterial spin labeling (ASL) was 79% and 89%, respectively, in patients with NCSE. 28 Furthermore, the direct comparison between IIC and definitive SE revealed no significant difference in ASL hyperperfusion, which was present in 41% and 38% of patients, respectively. 27 The comparable occurrence of PNA across imaging modalities in our meta‐analysis, relative to previously reported findings in SE, may suggest shared pathophysiological features with SE. Beyond this, the overlap in neuroimaging findings between IIC and SE strengthens the rationale for incorporating neuroimaging as a diagnostic biomarker in patients with IIC. Moreover, we propose neuroimaging as an add‐on in the diagnosis of SE in combination with the clinical picture (etiology) and EEG findings. 29 , 30

The IIC gray zone likely consists of EEG patterns with different levels of ictality. 24 LPDs have been most frequently associated with PNA across all imaging modalities in SE. 15 , 23 , 31 , 32 In our exploratory meta‐regression, we observed a nonsignificant trend toward higher proportions of PNA with increasing prevalence of LPDs across studies. In a PET study, focal hypermetabolism was observed across a range of LPDs frequencies without a consistent pattern, suggesting that discharge frequency alone does not reliably reflect metabolic burden. 11 The same study also reported focal hypermetabolism in four patients whose EEG patterns (periodic discharges 0.5–1 Hz and lateralized rhythmic delta activity 0.5 Hz) did not even meet current IIC criteria. Similarly, a recent study reported higher rates of hyperperfusion in IIC patterns with lower frequency (0.5–1 Hz) periodic discharges, spike‐and‐wave and sharp‐and‐wave patterns compared to frequencies above 1 Hz (63% vs. 24%, respectively). 27 Figure 3 shows a case of a patient with IIC with a low frequency of LPDs and PMA. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because LPDs have been consistently linked to a higher risk of seizures, 33 and particularly nonconvulsive seizures could go unnoticed in studies not utilizing continuous EEG monitoring.

LIMITATIONS

This meta‐analysis has some limitations. The available literature remains scarce, with only a small number of heterogeneous studies meeting inclusion criteria, modest sample sizes across the different imaging modalities, and inconsistent reporting of sequence‐specific MRI findings, while studies for some modalities, like SPECT, were absent. Moreover, the available evidence may be influenced by publication and reporting bias of positive findings. A further limitation is the inconsistent reporting of time to EEG and time to neuroimaging across studies, with these data frequently unavailable. In this review, LPDs were analyzed as a dichotomous variable (presence versus absence), without the possibility of accounting for more detailed EEG characteristics. These limitations restrict generalizability, suggesting that larger prospective studies with standardized imaging protocols and integrated EEG correlation are needed to better define PNA and their clinical utility within the SE gray zone.

CONCLUSIONS

This meta‐analysis demonstrates that PNA are frequently observed in patients with IIC across multiple imaging modalities with similar occurrence rates comparable to patients with SE and NCSE. Among modalities evaluated, PET showed the highest pooled detection rate, followed by CTP and MRI, although these findings should be interpreted in light of differences in imaging timing across the included studies. These findings may suggest that IIC shares biological features with definitive SE and highlight the potential role of advanced neuroimaging as an adjunctive biomarker in this diagnostically challenging population. Overall, the comparable rates of PNA across the broader ictal spectrum reinforce the notion that IIC may reflect ongoing ictal physiology rather than a purely interictal state.
