Optimized nanofluid coolants enhance thermal performance in ruffled fin automotive radiators
Authors: Ferdinard Dika Oshionebo, Doga Kavaz, Dilber Uzun Ozsahin, Michael Adedeji, Berna Uzun, Mustafa Dagbasi, Humphrey Adun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanofluids offer superior heat transfer compared to conventional automotive coolants, with further improvements achievable through particle hybridization and particle shape effects. This study numerically investigates the thermal performance of a ruffled-fin radiator using ternary hybrid nanofluids composed of ZnO, Al2O3, TiO2, MWCNTs, graphene, Fe, Cu, and Ag nanoparticles at varying volume fractions. Combinations such as Fe/Cu/Ag, Fe/Cu/ZnO, and Ag/Al2O3/TiO2 enhanced convective heat transfer, as reflected in increased Nusselt numbers and overall heat transfer coefficients. Increasing the volume fraction reduced outlet coolant temperature, with an optimal nanofluid achieving a 44.6% temperature drop at the engine outlet. The performance index, defined as a composite measure of heat transfer and cooling efficiency, decreased by 5.8–11.7% across the eight ternary nanofluids, with non-metallic oxides exhibiting the smallest reduction. These results demonstrate the role of ternary hybrid nanofluids in improving radiator cooling while indicating efficiency trade-offs at higher volume fractions.Keywords: Radiator, Specific heat capacity, Ternary, CoolantsSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

The automotive industry continues to advance thermal-management technologies to reduce fuel consumption and CO2 emissions. The engine cooling system is central to this effort, as it typically rejects nearly 30% of the fuel energy to the coolant in the form of waste heat. Insufficient heat rejection elevates engine temperature, leading to higher fuel consumption, knock, and accelerated wear, whereas effective cooling reduces fuel use, prolongs engine life, and limits emissions. In addition, efficient cooling shortens start-up and warm-up times, allowing the engine to reach optimal operating temperature more quickly1.

The radiator is the primary heat exchanger in the automotive cooling system, transferring heat from the engine coolant, typically a water–glycol mixture, to the surrounding air. Its design and size strongly influence cooling effectiveness, overall vehicle weight, and fuel efficiency. To enhance air-side heat transfer, fins are commonly incorporated to increase surface area and convective coefficients. However, conventional fin configurations face limitations due to size constraints, pressure drop, and restricted heat transfer enhancement, motivating research into advanced designs and alternative coolants2.

Other strategies to enhance radiator performance include the selection of construction materials with higher thermal conductivity and lower weight, as well as alternative flow configurations such as cross-flow and counter-flow designs. These approaches influence both heat transfer effectiveness and system efficiency, but each presents trade-offs in terms of cost, complexity, and pressure drop3. Although these radiator configurations provide certain advantages, they are limited by size constraints, material cost, and durability issues. Enhancing heat transfer through the use of advanced high-performance fluids offers an effective solution to excessive heat generation in the engine block, particularly in modern high-performance vehicles4. Historically, water was used as the primary engine coolant due to its high specific heat capacity and availability. However, its boiling point near 100 °C limits heat removal under high engine loads, while freezing at low ambient temperatures poses reliability concerns. To address these issues, ethylene glycol-based antifreeze has been employed, either alone or in mixtures with water, to raise the boiling point and lower the freezing point of the coolant. Although water–glycol mixtures remain the standard in modern vehicles, their thermal conductivity and heat transfer performance are still limited, motivating the search for advanced alternatives5. The efficiency of a coolant in a radiator system is associated with its thermophysical properties, primarily thermal conductivity, specific heat capacity, viscosity, and density. Conventional water–glycol mixtures are limited in these properties, motivating the development of nanofluids to achieve superior heat transfer performance6. Efforts to develop advanced heat transfer fluids with superior thermophysical properties have led to the emergence of nanofluids—suspensions of nanoparticles in base fluids such as water or ethylene glycol. Nanofluids have been widely investigated for enhancing thermal performance in different heat exchangers, including shell-and-tube7, double-tube8, and plate heat exchangers9. However, comparatively few studies have examined their application in automotive radiators, despite the potential for significant improvements in cooling efficiency. Addressing this gap requires further investigation of nanofluids as radiator coolants10.

Nanofluids have been extensively studied for more than two decades because of their wide range of applications and superior thermophysical properties compared to conventional coolants. They are typically prepared by dispersing nanoparticles into a base fluid at controlled volume fractions, which increases the effective thermal conductivity and enhances convective heat transfer through the high conductivity of solid particles, increased surface area, and nanoscale effects11–13. These properties position nanofluids as potential substitutes for conventional water–glycol coolants in engine cooling systems and have motivated investigations across various heat exchanger configurations, including automobile radiators. Building on these findings, recent studies have explored hybrid nanofluids that combine multiple nanoparticle types to further improve thermal performance.

Ali et al.14 investigated the thermal–hydraulic performance of automotive radiators using a ZnO–water nanofluid and found that nanofluid application significantly improved the heat transfer coefficient and cooling effectiveness. Ellahi et al.15 developed a mathematical model to evaluate entropy generation in nanofluid flows, employing correlations for Nusselt number and skin friction. Other studies16,17 have examined the influence of nanoparticle structure on energy and exergy performance in radiators and heat exchangers, demonstrating that particle geometry significantly affects heat transfer characteristics. Yu et al.18 further reported that the use of nanofluids can enhance heat transfer by 15–40%, underscoring their potential to surpass conventional coolants. Such improvements make it possible to design smaller and lighter automotive radiators without compromising thermal performance. The resulting reduction in radiator size lowers frontal area and drag coefficient, thereby improving vehicle fuel efficiency.

Numerous studies have examined the effectiveness of nanofluids as radiator coolants through both theoretical and experimental approaches19–21. The thermophysical properties of nanofluids, including specific heat capacity, viscosity, and thermal conductivity are strongly influenced by factors such as the choice of base fluid, nanoparticle volume fraction, particle size, shape, and material. Commonly studied nanoparticle types include Cu, Ag, CuO, Al2O3, MgO, TiO2, ZnO, and SiO222. However, prior research has also shown that the addition of nanoparticles alone does not necessarily ensure improvements in heat transfer performance or overall system efficiency. Nasajpour et al.23 conducted an experimental investigation to explore the turbulent heat transfer characteristics of water-based ZnO–Ag nanoparticles with a volume proportion of up to 4%. They discovered that increasing the volume percentage of nanoparticles by up to 2% improved the heat transfer coefficient. The mixture’s higher density and viscosity, however, produced a decrease in heat transfer efficiency and an increase in pressure drop as the nanoparticle concentration was above 2%. Soylua et al.11 conducted a theoretical analysis to determine the maximum potential of radiators using nanofluids consisting of four different types of nanoparticles. Their findings showed that incorporating Ag as a dopant improves the heat transmission properties of nanoparticles, resulting in a 26.15% increase in convective heat transfer coefficient at a volume concentration of 1%. Moreover, the shape of the nanoparticle was found to affect the performance of the heat exchanger. Choi24 conducted a study on fuel saving in automobiles using nanofluid and found that by adding 0.5 vol% of 2 nm gold nanoparticles, heat transfer was enhanced by over 10%. In a separate investigation, Esfe et al.25 discovered that adding less than 1% vol. of MgO nanoparticles to a base fluid improved the heat transfer capability of the fluid. Although the pressure drop was higher in the nanofluid than in the base fluid, the use of nanofluid increased heat transfer without a significant increase in consumed power. It was also found that increasing the volumetric concentration of nanoparticles increases the thermal conductivity of nanofluid; however, this also increases viscosity, resulting in an increase in the boundary layer thickness and potentially leading to a decrease in convective heat transfer. In their study, Peyghambarzadeh et al.26 examined the performance of a car radiator by utilizing a nanofluid based on Al2O3/water. They experimented with different volumetric concentrations, ranging from 0.1% to 1%. Their findings indicated that the maximum improvement in heat transfer was 45%, achieved at a volumetric concentration of 1%.

Hybrid nanofluids, which combine two types of nanoparticles in a base fluid, offer synergistic effects that can improve thermal conductivity and convective heat transfer, making them attractive for applications such as automotive radiators. Abbas et al.27 employed a novel hybrid nanofluid containing Fe2O3-TiO2 (50:50) nanoparticles suspended in a water base fluid to enhance convective heat transfer in an aluminum tube automotive radiator. The experiment consisted of testing three hybrid nanoparticle concentrations (0.005 vol.%, 0.007 vol.%, and 0.009 vol.%), with the maximum heat transfer rate enhancement of 26.7% achieved at an inlet temperature of 56 °C, flow rate of 15 LPM, and a 0.009 vol. % nanoparticle concentration. Sahoo28 investigated the use of a water-based ternary hybrid nanofluid containing Al2O3 (spherical), CNT (cylindrical), and graphene (platelet) nanoparticles as a radiator coolant, evaluating its thermohydraulic performance, exergetic efficiency, and morphology. Their study examined the effects of numerous variables on thermal performance, including the concentration of the ternary hybrid nanofluid, coolant flow rate, and frontal air velocity. The findings highlight that particle shape is a critical factor influencing the thermohydraulic behavior of ternary hybrid nanofluids in automotive radiators. According to the study, with a volume fraction range of 1% to 3%, the efficiency and the rate of heat transfer both increase by 18.45% and 6.3%, respectively. When ternary hybrid nanofluids were applied under varying coolant flow rates and frontal air velocities, system irreversibility increased. The entropy generation rose by 27.27%, primarily due to a greater entropy change in the air stream compared to the coolant.

Beyond conventional radiator studies in internal combustion engines, researchers have also extended nanofluid investigations to advanced cooling systems such as proton-exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs). Tao et al.29 experimentally investigated hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) nanofluids in a simulated PEMFC cooling radiator, reporting a marked increase in heat transfer rates compared to conventional water/ethylene glycol mixtures, particularly at higher nanoparticle concentrations. Their findings underscore the promise of two-dimensional nanomaterials for next-generation cooling systems. Complementing these nanofluid studies, research on conventional water/ethylene glycol mixtures remains important for benchmarking radiator performance. Bargal et al.30 conducted a comprehensive parametric and sensitivity analysis of automotive radiators using different water/ethylene glycol mixture ratios. Their thermo-hydraulic model identified a 40:60 mixture as optimal, balancing improved heat transfer with reduced pumping power requirements. Such studies highlight the critical role of coolant composition and system operating parameters in radiator performance optimization.

Numerical approaches have further expanded understanding of thermal management challenges in fuel cell systems. Tao et al.31 simulated the temperature distribution within a fuel cell vehicle engine compartment when nanofluids were employed as coolants. Their results showed performance gains with ZnO and BN nanofluids, with maximum heat transfer rate improvements of 7.6% and 2.0% respectively, providing practical guidance for coolant selection in fuel cell vehicle applications.

Beyond conventional radiator geometries, attention has also shifted to microchannel heat sink designs. Souby et al.32 numerically assessed graphene quantum dot (GQD) nanofluids in a ribbed microchannel with secondary channels. At 0.5% volume fraction, they reported significant improvements in heat transfer (24.9%) and entropy generation reduction (14.7%), though at the cost of increased pressure drop. These findings illustrate the inherent trade-offs between enhanced heat transfer and flow resistance in micro-scale cooling systems.

Together, these studies demonstrate a clear trajectory in the literature: experimental validation of novel nanofluids for radiator and PEMFC cooling, coupled with advanced numerical studies that probe performance-efficiency trade-offs in emerging designs. However, gaps remain in systematically linking synthesis methods, stability considerations, and multi-component formulations (e.g., ternary nanofluids) to practical thermal performance.

Extensive research has established the effectiveness of hybrid nanofluids in improving thermal performance; however, the role of ternary hybrid nanofluids, particularly in terms of stability and heat transfer characteristics, remains largely unexplored. Radiator performance with ternary nanofluids has been scarcely investigated. It is also important to mention that the complexity of ternary nanofluids could be a challenge in their practical applications as coolants due to the possibility of coagulation33. Therefore, the hybridization effect of different nanoparticles and their application in radiators constitute an important subject matter34.

Bargal et al.35 provided a comprehensive overview of nano-coolants in car radiators, reporting performance improvements of 6.3–124% for hybrid nanofluids such as Al2O3/TiO2, SiC/MWCNTs, and graphene/Al2O3/CNT. Their findings confirm that nanoparticle concentration (0.05–2 vol%), size, and flowrate are key determinants of radiator heat transfer performance. This review emphasizes that experimental and numerical investigations of ternary nanofluids remain scarce, underscoring the novelty of our work in numerically assessing the performance of ruffled fin radiators operating with ternary nanofluids. The objective of this work is therefore to analyze the heat transfer properties of an automotive car radiator using ternary nanofluids as coolants and to compare the thermal performance with that of a radiator utilizing traditional coolants. Furthermore, the investigation will explore the influence of nanoparticle volume fraction and temperature on the thermal performance of the ruffled fin-type radiator. In addition, we investigate the properties of different ternary hybridized nanofluids across different particle sizes and shapes. This work gains novelty as it builds on the previous works on the development of nano-synthesis and practical applications of nanofluids as automobile coolants.

In this work, we limited nanoparticle volume fraction to a maximum of 2%. Previous experimental studies have shown that hybrid nanofluids with CNT/oxide combinations retain Newtonian behavior at similar or even higher concentrations. For example, Esfe et al.45 observed Newtonian rheology in an MWCNT + Al2O3 nanolubricant up to 2% loading over a range of shear rates. Their linear stress–shear rate curves confirm that, under such loadings, non-Newtonian effects were not significant. The broader literature also indicates that although non-Newtonian or shear-thinning behavior can emerge at higher concentrations or very low shear rates (e.g. in Al2O3–water systems), at moderate shear rates typical of radiator flow, many nanofluids revert to Newtonian response. Taken together, these observations support the validity of assuming Newtonian behavior for our ternary nanofluids in the concentration range studied.

Nanofluid analysis

Ternary nanofluids (also referred to as ternary hybrid or trihybrid nanofluids) represent a new generation of engineered coolants that combine three distinct nanoparticles in a base fluid to harness synergistic effects beyond those attainable with mono- or binary hybrid nanofluids. Experimental studies reviewed by Adun et al.46 have shown that these fluids can achieve significant improvements in thermal conductivity and heat transfer performance, often ranging from ~ 10% up to 70% depending on particle composition, concentration, and operating temperature. Reported synthesis methods are primarily the two-step technique, in which pre-synthesized nanoparticles are ultrasonically dispersed into water, ethylene glycol, or oil with surfactants to prevent agglomeration, although one-step methods such as sol–gel and sonochemical synthesis are also used. Mixture ratios vary from equal (1:1:1) to skewed proportions (e.g. 60:30:10), and the choice of nanoparticle composition, size, morphology (spherical, tubular, platelet), volume fraction (typically 0.05–2%), and stability strategies (e.g., surfactants, pH control, surface functionalization) strongly influence thermal and rheological behavior. These variables govern the trade-offs between thermal conductivity enhancement and viscosity increase, making them critical in the design of effective ternary nanofluids.

Several experimental studies illustrate these principles. Ahmed et al.47,48 reported that an Al2O3/TiO2/ZnO ternary nanofluid at 0.1 wt% enhanced the average heat transfer coefficient by ~ 71% relative to water, with local improvements up to 3200 W/m2K. Mousavi et al.49 synthesized CuO/MgO/TiO2 nanofluids (0.1–0.5 vol%) and found Newtonian rheology with viscosity decreasing at higher temperatures (15–60 °C). Sahoo50 studied Al2O3/SiC/TiO2 ternary nanofluids prepared with Span-80 surfactant, reporting stable dispersions and viscosity increases with concentration. Dezfulizadeh et al.51 investigated Cu/SiO2/MWCNT nanofluids (1–3 vol%), demonstrating significant thermal conductivity improvements and Newtonian behavior across temperatures of 15–65 °C. Adun et al.52 synthesized Fe3O4/Al2O3/ZnO nanofluids and observed enhanced specific heat capacity compared with binary hybrids. Collectively, these findings show that composition and particle type (metallic, oxide, carbon-based), along with synthesis approach and mixture ratio, determine both the magnitude of heat transfer improvement and the stability of the suspension.

Informed by this experimental evidence, the present study evaluates eight ternary nanofluids spanning combinations of metallic, oxide, and carbon-based nanoparticles, in a ruffled fin radiator configuration. This design enables systematic comparison of distinct particle classes and mixture types, while extending analysis to compositions not yet widely reported in the literature, thereby addressing the research gap in ternary nanofluid applications to automotive cooling systems.

Comprehensive work has been carried out by the authors on nanofluids, with a portfolio consisting of conventional single nanofluids53,54, two-particle nanofluids, ternary nanofluids52, and their applications in renewable energy technologies55,56. The heat transfer characteristics of conventional fluids have been shown in diverse research to be significantly augmented by nanoparticles57,58. Interestingly, the composition of three nanoparticles does not directly mean better thermophysical properties of nanofluids, relative to the two-particle nanocomposites. This has been attributed majorly to the ratio of the individual particles in the mixture59. Some other factors which also affect the heat transfer improvements of nanofluids include their size and shape. The literature on the latter is rather few, as more emphasis has been placed on volume fraction and temperature used in their synthesis. More information on the discourse of ternary nanofluids and the effects of these factors can be retrieved from the authors work49,60.

In this study, we have emphasized the factors which have received less look-into in literature regarding the synthesis and ultimately the performance of nanofluids when used as cooling agents. To the best knowledge of the authors at the time of this study, no research has considered altering the chemical composition of nanoparticles in their mixture and the influence of particle size and shape. First, it is important to mention that nanoparticles used in synthesis have unique qualities, which affect the characteristics of nanofluids. Metallic nanoparticles like iron (Fe), silver (Ag), and gold (Au) have higher thermal conductivity compared to non-metallic particles. However, the latter is known to be more stable than the former. This creates a situation where it becomes imperative to consider the types of particles in the synthesis of nanocomposite, even much more in ternary nanocomposites. The thermophysical properties of the ternary nanofluids are presented. (a) Density (b) Specific heat capacity (c) Thermal conductivity (d) Viscosity.

Figure 3b illustrates the effect of increasing the volume fraction of nanofluids on the specific heat capacity of each ternary nanofluid. The results indicate a reduction in the specific heat capacity of the nanofluids as the volume fraction increases. This trend can be attributed to several factors related to the presence of nanoparticles within the nanofluids. As the volume fraction of nanoparticles increases, the concentration of nanoparticles within the nanofluid becomes higher. Nanoparticles typically have lower specific heat capacities than base fluid74. Therefore, when the nanoparticle concentration increases, the average specific heat capacity of the nanofluid decreases. This effect becomes more pronounced with larger volume fractions, resulting in a reduction in the specific heat capacity. Regarding the specific case of ternary fluid 2, the nanofluid experiences the most significant reduction in specific heat capacity, from 4150 J/kgK to 3600 J/kgK. This is attributed to the nanocomposite of metallic particles in the fluid, which generally have lower specific heat capacity75.

Figure 3c presents the effect of increasing the volume fraction of nanofluids on the thermal conductivity of each ternary nanofluid. The results indicate that the thermal conductivity of the nanofluids generally increases as the volume fraction of nanoparticles increases. This can be attributed to the packing effect of the nanoparticles, which makes them tend to come into closer proximity to each other, as the volume fraction increases76–78. This particle-packing effect leads to improved thermal conductivity, as the heat transfer pathways become more efficient due to the increased contact between nanoparticles. The nanoparticle shapes can also influence the thermal conductivity of nanofluids. Certain nanoparticle shapes, such as platelets, tend to exhibit higher aspect ratios. This increased aspect ratio enhances the thermal conductive pathways within the nanofluid, leading to higher thermal conductivity. Thus, the specific shapes of the nanoparticles in the ternary nanofluids can contribute to the observed increase in thermal conductivity, particularly in nanofluids such as ternary fluid 8. The biggest increase in thermal conductivity is found in ternary fluid 8, where the thermal conductivity increases from 0.68 W/mK to 0.725 W/mK. In CNT-based ternary nanofluids, the enhancement of thermal conductivity is closely associated with the unique geometry of multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs). MWCNTs typically exhibit diameters of 10–20 nm and lengths on the order of 5–15 µm, corresponding to aspect ratios of approximately 250–1500. Such high aspect ratios promote the formation of extended percolation networks even at low particle volume fractions. These networks act as continuous thermal bridges, enabling phonon transport across the base fluid and lowering the interfacial (Kapitza) resistance. This geometry-driven mechanism explains the anomalously high thermal conductivity improvements of CNT-based nanofluids compared to those containing spherical nanoparticles, as widely documented in the literature79,80.

Figure 3d demonstrates the effect of increasing the volume fraction of nanofluids on the viscosity of each ternary nanofluid. The results indicate that the viscosity of the nanofluids generally increases as the volume fraction of nanoparticles increases81–83. The biggest increase in viscosity is again from ternary fluid 8, whose viscosity increases from 0.00032 to 0.00043 Pa.s. This can be attributed to the increase in the interactions of the nanoparticles as the volume fraction of the nanofluids increases.

Nanoparticles have larger surface areas compared to the base fluid (water), which can lead to increased interactions between particles. These interactions, known as particle–particle interactions or interparticle forces, can cause an increase in the viscosity of the nanofluid84,85. The presence of nanoparticles and their clustering or agglomeration at higher concentrations can impede the flow of the fluid, resulting in higher viscosity.

Figure 4a presents the effect of increasing the volume fraction of the nanofluids on the Nusselt number of each ternary nanofluid. The figure illustrates that the behavior of the Nusselt number varies among the different nanofluids, with some showing an increase and others exhibiting a decrease as the volume fraction increases. The Nusselt number (Nu) is a dimensionless parameter used to evaluate the convective heat transfer performance of fluid86–88. It quantifies the ratio of convective heat transfer to conductive heat transfer across a surface and indicates the efficiency of heat dissipation. From Fig. 4a, ternary fluid 2, ternary fluid 3, and ternary fluid 5 all experience an increase in Nusselt number as the volume fraction increases. The biggest increase is in ternary fluid 2, whose Nusselt number increases from 11.3 to 11.5. This behaviour can be attributed to the enhanced heat transfer characteristics introduced by the nanoparticles present in these nanofluids. The inclusion of nanoparticles, such as ZnO, Al2O3, SiO2, Fe, Cu, and Ag, can lead to several beneficial effects on convective heat transfer. Firstly, the nanoparticles tend to increase the thermal conductivity of the nanofluid, enabling more efficient heat dissipation. However, the Nusselt numbers of ternary fluid 4, ternary fluid 6, ternary fluid 7, and ternary fluid 8 decrease as the volume fraction increases. This behaviour may be attributed to the interplay between the nanoparticle concentration and its effect on fluid dynamics. Higher viscosity reduces the fluid flow rate, hindering convective heat transfer. The reduction in Nusselt number observed in these nanofluids, particularly in ternary fluid 8 with the largest decrease, suggests it can be a result of the higher viscosity observed in these fluids. It is worth stating that the maximum Nusselt number of the ternary fluid is 3.5% higher than that of water. This is comparably lower than that obtained in the study by Hatami et al.89 which also examined nanofluid`s effect in radiators. The margin of difference in both studies is tied to the base fluid of ethylene glycol used in their study. This further solidifies the advantage of using oils as base fluids for nanofluid application in thermal systems. However, the basis of this study is concentrated on the effective nanocomposites; therefore, it is conclusive that using a ternary nanocomposite of metals or a combination of metals and nanotubes results in high convective heat transfer in radiator systems.

Effect of volume fraction on the (a) Nusselt Number and (b) heat transfer coefficient on ternary nanofluids.

The results can be seen to follow the same trend as the results in Fig. 4b; the behaviour of the overall heat transfer coefficient varies among the different nanofluids as the volume fraction increases. From the figure, ternary fluid 2, ternary fluid 3, and ternary fluid 5 all experience an increase in overall heat transfer coefficient as the volume fraction increases. The higher concentration of nanoparticles, as volume fraction increases, leads to improved thermal conductivity, enabling more efficient heat transfer. Additionally, the presence of nanoparticles can enhance convective heat transfer through various mechanisms, such as increased surface area and enhanced fluid turbulence. These effects collectively contribute to the observed increase in the overall heat transfer coefficient.

Figure 7 displays the effect of increasing the inlet temperature of the coolants on the NTU at a fixed volume fraction of 0.02. The results show that increasing the inlet temperature of the ternary nanofluids causes the NTU to also increase. The increase in NTU that occurs as the coolant inlet temperature increases can be attributed to the enhancement of thermophysical properties. The kinetic energy of the nanoparticles in the ternary nanofluid increases as the temperature of the inlet increases. This accelerates Brownian motion and micro-convection, which facilitates the transfer of heat between the radiator walls and the fluid. The effective thermal conductivity and convective heat transfer coefficient are both enhanced as a result of this increased microscopic activity, which in turn increases the NTU value. Additionally, the heat exchange process is more efficient due to the greater temperature disparity between the coolant and the surrounding air, which facilitates heat transfer.

Figure 8 shows the effect of increasing the mass flow rate of the coolants on the NTU at a volume fraction of 0.02. According to the results, the NTU drops as the mass flow rate increases. The observed drop in NTU with an increasing mass flow rate is due to the coolant spending less time in the radiator core. The coolant moves through the tubes faster when the mass flow rate is higher, which means that there is less time for the fluid and the fin surfaces to exchange heat effectively. A further in-depth look at the findings also shows that Ternary fluid 6 had the highest decline in NTU values of all the ternary nanofluids tested. This can be attributed to its specific combination of thermophysical properties that lead to a higher effective heat capacity rate as its mass flow increases.

The performance index is typically designed to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the nanofluid’s ability to transfer heat and cool the system. The irreversibility, entropy generation, exergy loss, and exergy efficiency of the radiation for each nanofluid are presented in Fig. 12a–d. Irreversibility refers to the loss or dissipation of useful energy in a thermodynamic process. Irreversibility arises from factors such as friction, heat transfer across finite temperature differences, irreversibly mixing different substances, and irreversibilities in fluid flow. Entropy generation is a quantitative measure of irreversibilities and represents the extent of disorder or randomness produced in a system during a process. It is related to the dissipation of energy and the degradation of available energy. Exergy loss is the irrecoverable part of the exergy that cannot be converted into useful work and is associated with the generation of entropy. Exergy efficiency indicates how effectively a system converts available energy into useful work or useful outputs while minimizing exergy losses and entropy generation. A higher exergy efficiency implies a more efficient utilization of available energy and a lower amount of exergy loss and entropy generation. These concepts are interconnected and closely related. The irreversibilities result in the dissipation of energy in the form of heat or other less useful forms, which increases entropy and reduces the amount of available energy (exergy) in the system. The irreversible processes contribute to exergy loss, entropy generation, and a decrease in the overall system efficiency. The results from this study for irreversibility (Fig. 12a), entropy generation (Fig. 12b), and exergy loss (Fig. 12c) all exhibit the same trends. As the volume fraction increases, all 3 factors also increase for each nanofluid. Figures 12a,b,c also show that the biggest increase in irreversibility, entropy generation, and exergy loss is from ternary fluid 2, and the smallest increase is in ternary fluid 6. As the definitions and relations of the concepts have shown, an increase in irreversibility indicates increased entropy generation and exergy loss, which will lead to a reduction in exergy efficiency. This is illustrated in Fig. 12d, where ternary fluid 2, which had the highest increase in irreversibility (Fig. 12a), now shows the biggest drop in exergy efficiency. The exergy efficiency of ternary fluid 2 drops from about 33.8% to about 31%.

The results indicate that the pressure drop across the radiator increases as the volume fraction of nanoparticles increases for all nanofluids. The ternary nanofluids all show a higher-pressure drop compared to water, and the smallest increase in pressure drop is observed in ternary fluid 1. The increase in pressure drop can be attributed to the increase in the viscosity of the coolants as the volume fraction increases (shown in Fig. 3d). The presence of nanoparticles will lead to an increase in the viscosity of the nanofluid, making it more resistant to flow. Consequently, the increased concentration of nanoparticles results in greater fluid friction and subsequently leads to an increased pressure drop across the radiator. A similar result was retrieved from a study by Sakar et al.21, where the pressure drop increased from 370 to 420 Pa as volume concentration increased from 0.01 to 0.04.

A practical consideration when employing nanofluids with a high fraction of metallic nanoparticles is the potential for corrosion within the metallic components of radiators. Metallic nanoparticles such as Fe, Cu, and Ag can accelerate electrochemical reactions and promote localized corrosion when present in elevated concentrations. Although corrosion effects were not explicitly modelled in this study, previous experimental reports highlight that mitigation strategies are available, including the use of protective surface coatings, incorporation of corrosion inhibitors in coolant formulations, and optimization of nanoparticle volume fractions. Hybrid nanofluids that combine metals with oxides or carbon-based nanostructures, as examined in this work, also reduce the relative fraction of reactive metallic particles, thereby lowering corrosion susceptibility. These factors suggest that the thermal performance benefits observed here can be achieved in practice without compromising material durability, provided that established coolant engineering practices are followed.

Conclusion

The numerical examination on the performance of undulating fin-type radiators utilizing different ternary nanofluids revealed important information about their heat transmission properties and effectiveness. Comparing the numerical model’s outputs to experimental data showed a low average error of 3%, confirming the model’s reliability and correctness. Significant patterns emerged during the examination of nanofluid characteristics.

The coolant temperature decreased by up to 78% for the optimal ternary nanofluid formulation (ternary fluid 6), as it goes through the radiator, demonstrating significantly enhanced cooling performance.

The coolant temperature decreased by up to 78% for the optimal ternary nanofluid formulation (ternary fluid 6), as it goes through the radiator, demonstrating significantly enhanced cooling performance.

Ternary fluid 8 demonstrated the most significant improvement in thermophysical parameters. The thermal conductivity rose by 6.62% (from 0.68 W/mK to 0.725 W/mK) at a volume fraction of 0.02, directly corresponding with elevated heat transfer coefficients and Nusselt numbers.

Ternary fluid 8 demonstrated the most significant improvement in thermophysical parameters. The thermal conductivity rose by 6.62% (from 0.68 W/mK to 0.725 W/mK) at a volume fraction of 0.02, directly corresponding with elevated heat transfer coefficients and Nusselt numbers.

The pressure drop and pumping power of the ternary nanofluids rose by approximately 6–15%, compared to water, indicating a moderate hydraulic penalty for the improved thermal performance.

The performance index dropped by 4.5–11.7% throughout the eight ternary formulations, whereas exergy efficiency reduced by 2.6–9%, with an increase in volume fraction, underscoring the need to balance thermodynamic efficiency with hydraulic losses.

The present numerical study lays a solid groundwork by determining the best particle-shaped ternary hybrid nanofluids for ruffled-fin radiators in terms of heat transmission and pressure drop management. The obvious next step in translating this study into an industrial application is to validate the expected thermophysical characteristics and heat transfer performance experimentally in a full-scale radiator test rig under all operating situations. Importantly, an accompanying study needs to find a commercially viable, non-destructive coolant by tackling the real-world problems of corrosion potential and long-term stability, especially with metallic-based formulations. Lastly, by extending the numerical methodology, a thorough techno-economic analysis can be conducted to measure the life-cycle cost–benefit ratio of using these nanofluids. Additionally, their performance in other complex heat exchanger geometries, like wavy-fin or louvered structures, can be investigated.

A key limitation of this work is that colloidal stability of the ternary nanofluids was not investigated experimentally. In our numerical model, the nanofluids were assumed to behave as homogeneous and stable suspensions, consistent with previous numerical studies at low nanoparticle concentrations (≤ 2 vol%)45. In practice, the long-term stability of ternary nanofluids remains a challenge due to agglomeration and sedimentation, particularly for metallic and carbon-based nanoparticles91. Ensuring stability may require surfactants, pH control, or surface functionalization, as demonstrated in prior experimental work. Future studies should therefore validate the stability of these ternary systems under radiator operating conditions to assess their practical viability.
