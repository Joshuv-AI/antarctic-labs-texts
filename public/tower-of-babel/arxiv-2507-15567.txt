Well-Posed Geometric Boundary data in General Relativity, III: Conformal-volume data
Zhongshan An and Michael T. Anderson

Abstract
. In this third work in a series, we prove the local-in-time well-posedness of the IBVP for the vacuum Einstein equations in general relativity with twisted DIrichlet boundary conditions on a finite timelike boundary. The boundary conditions consist of specification of the pointwise conformal class of the boundary metric, together with a scalar density involving a combination of the volume form of the bulk metric restricted to the boundary together with the volume form of the boundary metric itself.

The main result of this paper is the following (local-in-time) well-posedness result for the IBVP with boundary data given by (
1.3

Let
\mathbb{V}_{S}^{\prime}
be the space of smooth vector fields along

be the subset consisting of

Theorem 1.1
.

The spaces
\mathbb{E}^{H}
and
[\mathcal{I}_{0}\times\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{S}\times{\mathcal{B}}]_{c}
are smooth tame Frechet manifolds and the map

(1.7)

\begin{split}&\Phi^{H}:\mathbb{E}^{H}\rightarrow[\mathcal{I}_{0}\times\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{S}\times{\mathcal{B}}]_{c},\\
&\Phi^{H}(g)=(g_{S},K,\nu_{S},[g_{{\mathcal{C}}}],\mu_{g}),\\
\end{split}

is (locally-in-time) a smooth tame diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{E}^{H}
onto
[\mathcal{I}_{0}\times\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{S}\times{\mathcal{B}}]_{c}
with smooth, tame inverse. Thus the IBVP with
boundary data (
1.3
) is well-posed in harmonic gauge.

Briefly, vacuum Einstein metrics on
M
, with arbitrarily given compatible initial and boundary data, exist for a short time, and are uniquely
determined in harmonic gauge by their initial data in
\mathcal{I}_{0}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}^{\prime}
and boundary data in
{\mathcal{B}}
. Further, such solutions depend continuously
and in fact smoothly on the target data in
[\mathcal{I}_{0}\times\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{S}\times{\mathcal{B}}]_{c}
; the IBVP is stable with respect to variation of the target data. A version of
Theorem
1.1
holds also off-shell (for non-vacuum Einstein metrics), cf. Theorem
6.1
. We refer to
[
7
]
for details regarding
Frechet spaces and tame maps.

We note here a simple consequence of the proof of Theorem
1.1

Corollary 1.2

The marked moduli space
{\mathcal{E}}_{*}
and the moduli space
{\mathcal{E}}
of vacuum Einstein metrics on
M
as above are smooth tame Frechet manifolds.

Note that the boundary data

is invariant under a restricted group of unimodular or volume-preserving diffeomorphisms. Thus, given

In general Theorem

implies two vacuum Einstein metrics

and

\varphi_{1}^{*}dv_{g_{1}}(dv_{(g_{1})_{{\mathcal{C}}}})^{-2/n}=\varphi_{2}^{*}dv_{g_{2}}(dv_{(g_{2})_{{\mathcal{C}}}})^{-2/n}.

in (

Hence the validity of (

g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
and scalar
\mu_{g}=dv_{g}(dv_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}})^{-2/n}
with

([g_{{\mathcal{C}}}],\mu_{g})\in{\mathcal{B}}^{s-1/2}.

Here
\mu_{g}
is identified with a scalar function on
{\mathcal{C}}
by the choice of a fixed but otherwise arbitrary smooth volume form on
M
and an
induced volume form on the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
.

We consider the map

(2.2)

\Phi:Met(M)\rightarrow[S^{2}(M)\times\mathcal{I}\times{\mathcal{B}}]_{c}:={\mathcal{T}},

\Phi(g)=(\mathrm{Ric}_{g},g_{S},K,[g_{{\mathcal{C}}}],\mu_{g}),

where
(g_{S},K)
and
([g_{{\mathcal{C}}}],\mu_{g})
are the initial and
{\mathcal{B}}
-valued boundary data induced by
g
. The subscript
c
denotes the compatibility
conditions between the initial and boundary data at the corner
\Sigma
; these are discussed in detail in §2.3-2.4. For simplicity, we have dropped
here the notation for the topologies, indexed by
s
.

This is the extension of the map
\Phi
in (
1.5
) to the space of all globally hyperbolic Lorentz metrics on
M
.

2.2.
Localization

As usual with hyperbolic PDE problems, the main existence results will first be proved locally, in regions where the constant coefficient
approximation is effective. Such local solutions are then patched together, by a standard and well-known process, via rescaling and a
partition of unity,

Given a metric
g
on
M
as in §1, the localization at a point
p\in\Sigma
is a (small) neighborhood
U\subset M
of
p
, diffeomorphic via
a local chart to a Minkowski corner

\mathbf{R}=\{(t=x^{0},x^{1},\dots,x^{n}):t\geq 0,x^{1}\leq 0\},

with
S\cap U\subset\{t=0\}
,
{\mathcal{C}}\cap U\subset\{x^{1}=0\}
and
x^{\alpha}(p)=0
.

In such a metrically small neighborhood
U
, the metric and coordinates are renormalized simultaneously by rescaling; thus
for
\lambda
small, set

\widetilde{g}=\lambda^{-2}g,\ \ \widetilde{x}^{\alpha}=\lambda^{-1}x^{\alpha}

so that
\partial_{\widetilde{x}^{\alpha}}=\lambda\partial_{x^{\alpha}}
and

(2.3)

\widetilde{g}(\partial_{\widetilde{x}^{\alpha}},\partial_{\widetilde{x}^{\beta}})|_{\widetilde{x}}=g(\partial_{x^{\alpha}},\partial_{x^{\beta}})|_{\lambda x},

and similarly for variations
h
,
\widetilde{h}
of
g
and
\widetilde{g}
. While the components
g_{\alpha\beta}
of
g
are invariant under such a rescaling,
all higher derivatives become small:

(2.4)

\partial_{\widetilde{x}^{\mu}}^{k}\widetilde{g}_{\alpha\beta}=\lambda^{k}\partial_{x^{\mu}}^{k}g_{\alpha\beta}=O(\lambda^{k}).

Thus (for smooth metrics
g
) the coefficients are smoothly close to constant functions in the rescaled chart,

(2.5)

||\widetilde{g}-g_{\alpha_{0}}||_{C^{\infty}(U)}\leq\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\lambda,g),

where
g_{\alpha_{0}}
is a flat (constant coefficent) Minkowski metric. This is the frozen coefficient approximation.

The
\lambda\rightarrow 0
limit blow-up metric
g_{\alpha_{0}}
is the Minkowski metric, given in adapted coordinates as above by

(2.6)

g_{\alpha_{0}}=-dt^{2}-\alpha_{0}dtdx^{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(dx^{i})^{2}.

Here
\alpha_{0}=\alpha(p)=\langle\nu_{S},\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}\rangle_{p}
is the value of the (scale-invariant) corner angle at
p
.
Note that for
g_{\alpha_{0}}
,

(2.7)

\nu_{S}=-\nabla t=(1+\alpha_{0}^{2})^{-1/2}(\partial_{0}+\alpha_{0}\partial_{1}),\ \ \nu_{\mathcal{C}}=\nabla x^{1}=(1+\alpha_{0}^{2})^{-1/2}(\partial_{1}-\alpha_{0}\partial_{0}).

Note also that the leading order Sobolev norms scale as follows: for
D\subset S
or
D\subset{\mathcal{C}}
of dimension
n
,

||f||_{H_{\widetilde{g}}^{s}(D)}^{2}=\lambda^{2s-n}||f||_{H_{g}^{s}(D)}^{2},

||f||_{{\mathcal{N}}_{\widetilde{g}}^{s}(M)}^{2}=\lambda^{2s-n}||f||_{{\mathcal{N}}_{g}^{s}(M)}^{2}.

Also as in
[
2
]
,
U
is always assumed to be smoothly embedded in a larger region
\widetilde{U}
,

U\subset\widetilde{U},

with
\widetilde{U}
still covered by the adapted coordinates
(t,x^{i})
, with
t\geq 0
and
x^{1}\leq 0
in
\widetilde{U}
so that the initial surface
S
, boundary

{\mathcal{C}}
and corner
\Sigma
in
\widetilde{U}
are an extension of the corresponding domains in
U
. We also assume (
2.5
) still holds in
\widetilde{U}
.
All target data in
{\mathcal{T}}
as in (
2.2
) and later in
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
which is given in
U
is extended off
U
to be of compact support in
\widetilde{U}
away
from
S\cap U
and
{\mathcal{C}}\cap U
. Thus in such localizations, all target data vanishes in a neighborhood of the full timelike boundary of
\widetilde{U}
and in
a neighborhood of the initial slice
\{t=0\}
away from
{\mathcal{C}}\cap U
and
S\cap U
respectively. The same statements hold for variations of the
target data, i.e. in the tangent spaces
T({\mathcal{T}})
or
T({\mathcal{T}}^{H})
.

For later reference, we note that solutions
h
of linear systems of wave equations on
\widetilde{U}
appearing below and throughout §3 - §4 enjoy
the finite propagation speed property; if the initial and boundary data of
h
are compactly supported in
\widetilde{U}
, then
h
is also compactly
supported in
\widetilde{U}
, for some definite (possibly small) time
t>0
.

2.3.
Gauge analysis.

Due to gauge issues, the map
\Phi
in (
2.2
) is well-known to be neither surjective nor injective, or given by non-linear hyperbolic evolution equations.
A choice of gauge is used to deal with these issues. This will be described here by a choice of vector field
V
on
M
and replacing the vacuum Einstein equations
(
1.1
) by the reduced Einstein equations

(2.8)

\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta^{*}V=0,

where
\delta^{*}V=\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{V}g
. As in
[
2
,
3
]
we choose here the (generalized) harmonic or wave coordinate gauge, given by

(2.9)

V_{g}=\Box_{g}x^{\alpha}\partial_{x^{\alpha}},

where
\Box_{g}
is the d’Alembertian or wave operator with respect to
g
. It is well-known that the gauge (
2.9
) can be naturally globalized
to all of
M
by choice of a wave map to a fixed target space, cf.
[
2
]
for instance.

The gauged version of the map
\Phi
in (
2.2
) is then given by

(2.10)

\Phi^{H}:Met(M)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}^{H},

\Phi^{H}(g)=\big((\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta_{g}^{*}V_{g}),(g_{S},K,\nu_{S},V|_{S}),([g_{{\mathcal{C}}}],\mu_{g},V|_{{\mathcal{C}}})\big),

where
V|_{S}
and
V|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
are the restrictions of
V_{g}
to
S
and
{\mathcal{C}}
respectively and
\nu_{S}
is the future unit normal to
S
in
(M,g)
. In

4
-dimensions, the initial data
(g_{S},K,\nu_{S},V|_{S})
consists of the (required) 20 degrees of freedom, while the boundary conditions

([g_{{\mathcal{C}}}],\mu_{g},V|_{{\mathcal{C}}})
consist of the (required) 10 degrees of freedom. Similar consistency holds in all dimensions.

The target space
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
in (
2.10
) is given as follows. Let
{\mathcal{U}}
denote the uncoupled space of target data, so that

{\mathcal{U}}=S^{2}(M)\times\mathcal{I}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}\times{\mathcal{B}}\times\mathbb{V}_{{\mathcal{C}}}.

This is a product space, extending the product space in (
2.2
);
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{S}
is the space of
H^{s}
vector fields along
S
nowhere
tangent to
S
,
\mathbb{V}_{S}
is the space of
H^{s-1}
vector fields
V_{S}
along
S
and
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is the space of
H^{s-3/2}
vector
fields
V_{{\mathcal{C}}}
along
{\mathcal{C}}
. By construction,
{\mathcal{U}}
is a separable Hilbert space, and Frechet space when
s=\infty
, i.e. for
C^{\infty}

data.

The metric
g
induces data in
{\mathcal{U}}
through the map
\Phi^{H}
. The two equations for
g
, namely the bulk equation
\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta^{*}V_{g}=Q

and the definition of the gauge field
V_{g}
in (
2.9
), as well as definition of the normal vector
\nu_{S}
, determine compatibility
conditions for the data in
{\mathcal{U}}
at the corner
\Sigma
. These compatibility conditions are expressed in terms of equations on the data in
{\mathcal{U}}
.
The subspace of compatible target data, given by

{\mathcal{U}}_{c}=[S^{2}(M)\times\mathcal{I}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}\times{\mathcal{B}}\times\mathbb{V}_{{\mathcal{C}}}]_{c}\subset{\mathcal{U}},

is then the subspace satisfying these compatibility conditions. The compatibility conditions are expressed as the zero-locus of a (large) collection of
functions on
{\mathcal{U}}
, ordered according to the degree of differentiability.

Note that the initial gauge velocity
\partial_{t}V|_{S}
is not prescribed by the data in
{\mathcal{U}}
or
{\mathcal{U}}_{c}
.
In fact, the gauge velocity at
S
is only prescribed by imposing the constraint equations, i.e. the Gauss and Gauss-Codazzi equations,
on
S
:

(2.11)

|K|^{2}-(\mathrm{tr}_{g_{S}}K)^{2}-R_{g_{S}}=R_{g}-2\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(\nu_{S},\nu_{S})=\mathrm{tr}_{g_{S}}\mathrm{Ric}_{g}-3\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(\nu_{S},\nu_{S}),

(2.12)

{\rm div}_{g_{S}}(K-Hg_{S})=\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(\nu_{S},\cdot).

To express this in terms of target data in
{\mathcal{U}}_{c}
, denote

Q=\mathrm{Ric}+\delta^{*}V,

so that (
2.11
)-(
2.12
) becomes

(2.13)

|K|^{2}-(\mathrm{tr}_{g_{S}}K)^{2}-R_{g_{S}}=\mathrm{tr}_{g_{S}}(Q-\delta^{*}V)-3(Q(\nu_{S},\nu_{S})-\delta^{*}V(\nu_{S},\nu_{S})),

(2.14)

{\rm div}_{g_{S}}(K-Hg_{S})=Q(\nu_{S},\cdot)-\delta^{*}V(\nu_{S},\cdot).

It is straightforward to check that
\delta^{*}V
is determined along
S
by the initial data
(V|_{S},\partial_{t}V|_{S})
.
Let then
\mathbb{V}_{S}
, the space of vector fields along
S
, representing the target space for
\partial_{t}V|_{S}
and consider the constraint operator

C:{\mathcal{U}}_{c}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}_{S},

(2.15)

\begin{split}&C(Q,\gamma_{S},\kappa,V_{S},\partial_{t}V_{S},\nu_{S},\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}},V_{{\mathcal{C}}})=\begin{cases}|\kappa|^{2}-(\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma_{S}}\kappa)^{2}-R_{\gamma_{S}}-\mathrm{tr}_{g_{S}}(Q-\delta^{*}V)+3(Q(\nu_{S},\nu_{S})-\delta^{*}V(\nu_{S},\nu_{S})),\\
{\rm div_{\gamma_{S}}}\kappa-d_{S}(\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma_{S}}\kappa)-Q(\nu_{S},\cdot)+\delta^{*}V(\nu_{S},\cdot).\\
\end{cases}\end{split}

We then define

(2.16)

{\mathcal{T}}^{H}=({\mathcal{U}}_{c}\times\mathbb{V}_{S})_{0},

to be the zero-set of the constraint operator
C
. By inspection in (
2.15
), on
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
,
\partial_{t}V_{S}
is uniquely determined by the remaining
data in
{\mathcal{U}}_{c}
, and so
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
is naturally identified with
{\mathcal{U}}_{c}
.

The overall method of proof of well-posedness is to prove that
\Phi^{H}
in (
2.10
) is a tame Fredholm map with a tame inverse at the
linearized level and to then apply the Nash inverse function theorem in Frechet spaces. Thus, most of the analysis to follow deals with the
study of the linearization

(2.17)

D\Phi_{g}^{H}:T_{g}Met(M)\rightarrow T_{\tau}({\mathcal{T}}^{H}),

where
\tau=\Phi^{H}(g)
.

The bulk term in the linearization
D\Phi_{g}^{H}
is given by

(2.18)

L(h)=\mathrm{Ric}^{\prime}_{h}+\delta^{*}V^{\prime}_{h}+(\delta^{*})^{\prime}_{h}V.

The linearization of the gauge field
V=V_{g}
is given by

(2.19)

V^{\prime}_{h}=\beta_{g}h-\langle D^{2}x^{\alpha},h\rangle\partial_{x^{\alpha}},

where
\beta_{g}=\delta_{g}+\frac{1}{2}d\mathrm{tr}_{g}h
is the Bianchi operator. The bulk linearized equation
L(h)=F
then has the form

(2.20)

L(h)=\tfrac{1}{2}D^{*}Dh+P(h)=F,

where
P
is a first order linear differential operator on
h
and
D^{*}D=-\nabla_{e_{\alpha}}\nabla_{e_{\alpha}}+\nabla_{\nabla_{e_{\alpha}}e_{\alpha}}
is the tensorial
wave operator on symmetric bilinear forms. Of course all the coefficients of the derivatives in (
2.20
) depend on the background metric
g
at
which the linearization is formed.

The equation (
2.20
) may be written in local coordinates in the form

(2.21)

(L(h))_{\alpha\beta}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\Box_{g}(h_{\alpha\beta})+P_{\alpha\beta}(h)=F_{\alpha\beta}.

where
P(h)
involves
h
and its first derivatives. In the localized context discussed in §2.2, the coefficients of
P
are
all of order
\varepsilon
,
P(h)=\varepsilon(\partial h,h)
. Here
\Box_{g}f=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\alpha}(g^{\alpha\beta}\sqrt{-g}\partial_{\beta}f)
is the D’Alembertian or
wave operator with respect to
g
.

In later sections, we will repeatedly need to solve linear wave equations of the form (
2.21
), i.e.

(2.22)

-\tfrac{1}{2}\Box_{g}v+p(v)=\varphi,

for given
\varphi
. Here
v
may be either scalar valued or vector-valued and again
p(v)
is first order in
v
. Although solvability of the linear
equation or system (
2.22
) holds more generally, we will only need to actually solve such systems in
C^{\infty}
, so with
g
,
p
and

\varphi
in
C^{\infty}
.

The next Lemma is well-known and will be used repeatedly in §3 and §4.

Lemma 2.1
.

For Dirichlet boundary data, the
C^{\infty}
system (
2.22
) is solvable for any smooth
\varphi
, with standard energy estimates.

Proof.

This is a standard and well-known result, cf.
[
5
]
,
[
11
]
for further details. Of course it is assumed here implicitly that the initial data
and boundary data for
v
satisfy the necessary smooth compatibility conditions (as above) at the corner
\Sigma
, cf. also §2.4. Energy estimates for such
equations are also standard; for later reference, we provide the main result here. Thus, the strong or boundary stable energy estimate with
Dirichlet boundary data states that any (smooth) solution of (
2.22
) satisfies the bound

(2.23)

||v||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}^{2}\leq C[||v||_{H^{s}(S_{0})}^{2}+||\partial_{t}v||_{H^{s-1}(S_{0})}^{2}+||v||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}+||\varphi||_{H^{s-1}(M_{t})}^{2}],

where
t\in[0,1]
and
C
is a constant depending only on
g
and the coefficients of
p
. Note the difference in the stronger and weaker
norms on the left and right of (
2.23
). We recall that
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}=\{p\in{\mathcal{C}}:t(p)\leq t\}
and similarly for
M_{t}
.

∎

Remark 2.2
.

One also has existence and uniqueness for smooth solutions of the equation (
2.22
) with given Neumann boundary data
b(v)=\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}(v)
.
However, in this case, there is no effective energy estimate as in (
2.23
); instead the energy estimate only holds with a loss of a (fractional) derivative.
The optimal result in general is:

(2.24)

|v||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}^{2}\leq C[||v||_{H^{s}(S_{0})}^{2}+||\partial_{t}v||_{H^{s-1}(S_{0})}^{2}+||\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}(v)||_{H^{s-1/3}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}+||\varphi||_{H^{s-1}(M_{t})}^{2}],

so there is a ’loss’ of
2/3
of a derivative. We refer to
[
9
, Ch.8]
, and in particular to
[
12
, Thm.9]
for the proof and further details.

We next discuss the gauge term
V
and its linearization
V^{\prime}_{h}
. Consider the bulk equation

(2.25)

\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta_{g}^{*}V=Q,

where
Q
is given data in the target data space
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
. We will need the following results on the gauge field from
[
2
]
.
For completeness we state the full result but refer to
[

Lemma 2.3
.

The gauge field
V
satisfies the equation

(2.26)

-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Box V+\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(V))=\beta(Q).

on
M
, where
\Box
denotes the vector wave operator
\Box=-D^{*}D
acting on vector fields. The initial data
V|_{S}
,
\partial_{t}V|_{S}
and
boundary data
V|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
are determined by the target data in
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
. Thus such data uniquely determine
V
.

Similarly, for the linearization
L(h)=F
as in (
2.20
), one has

(2.27)

-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Box_{g}V^{\prime}_{h}+\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(V^{\prime}_{h}))+\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{V}V^{\prime}_{h}=\beta(F)+\beta^{\prime}_{h}\mathrm{Ric}+O_{2,1}(V,h),

where
O_{2,1}(V,h)
is second order in
V
, first order in

and vanishes if
V=0
.

The initial data
V^{\prime}_{h}|_{S}
,
\partial_{t}V^{\prime}_{h}|_{S}
and boundary data
V^{\prime}_{h}|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
are determined by the target space data
in
T({\mathcal{T}}^{H})
.

As in
[

]
, decompose the gauge variation

as a sum

(2.28)

V^{\prime}_{h}=V^{\prime}_{F}+W^{\prime}_{h},

where
V^{\prime}_{F}=V^{\prime}_{F}({\mathcal{T}})
is the vector field satisfying

(2.29)

-\tfrac{1}{2}[\Box V^{\prime}_{F}+\mathrm{Ric}(V^{\prime}_{F})]+\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{V}V^{\prime}_{F}=\beta(F),

with
(V^{\prime}_{F})|_{S}=(V^{\prime}_{h})|_{S}

(so is independent of

(2.30)

-\tfrac{1}{2}[\Box W^{\prime}_{h}+\mathrm{Ric}(W^{\prime}_{h})]+\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{V}W^{\prime}_{h}=\beta^{\prime}_{h}\mathrm{Ric}+O_{2,1}(V,h),

2.4.
Geometry at the corner

As noted in
[
2

, the conformal factor
\varphi
of the given boundary data

by the initial data

at
\Sigma

(2.31)

\alpha=\langle\nu_{S},\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}\rangle.

In fact for the boundary data

\alpha
is not determined by initial and boundary data, cf. again
[
2
]
.
On the other hand, it was shown in

Lemma 2.5
.

In local coordinates
\{x^{\mu}\}
near
\Sigma
with
g_{11}=f^{2}
and
g_{1a}=0
,
a=2,\dots,n
(zero shift), one has

(2.32)

dv_{g}=\sqrt{f^{2}det(-g_{{\mathcal{C}}})+\alpha^{2}det(g_{\Sigma})}dx^{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge x^{n},

where
\alpha=-g_{01}
. Consequently,

(2.33)

\mu_{g}=\frac{\sqrt{f^{2}det(-g_{{\mathcal{C}}})+\alpha^{2}det(g_{\Sigma})}}{[det(-g_{{\mathcal{C}}})]^{1/n}},

and so at the corner
\Sigma
,

is uniquely determined, up to sign, by the initial and boundary data in

The formula (
2.32
) is a simple calculation in local coordinates, as is the relation
\alpha=-g_{01}
. As noted above, at
\Sigma
, both

and the conformal factor

are determined by initial data. Hence,

and

are determined at

by the target data in

. Thus the second statement follows from the formula (

).
∎

Remark 2.6

A change

\begin{split}||h^{\mathsf{T}}||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}&\leq C[||h^{\mathsf{T}}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}+\varepsilon||h||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{U}_{t})}+||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}]\\
&\leq C[\int_{0}^{t}[||\ring{h}||_{H^{s+1}({{\mathcal{C}}_{t^{\prime}})}}+||V^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{C}}_{t^{\prime}})}.+\varepsilon||h||_{\bar{H}^{s}({\mathcal{C}}_{t^{\prime}})}]dt^{\prime}+\varepsilon||h||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{U}_{t})}+||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}],\\
\end{split}

where the last inequality comes from integration of (
4.6
)-(
4.7
) over
t^{\prime}\in[0,t]
.
Taking the maximum over
t\in I
then gives

(4.9)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h^{\mathsf{T}}||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq C[||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}+\varepsilon||h||_{\widehat{H}^{s}(\widetilde{U})}],

where
||h||_{\widehat{H}^{s}(\widetilde{U})}:=||h||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{U})}+||h||_{\bar{H}^{s}({\mathcal{C}})}
.

Similarly, the Dirichlet energy estimate for
h_{\nu\nu}
gives

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h_{\nu\nu}||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq C[||h_{\nu\nu}||_{H^{s}({{\mathcal{C}})}}+\varepsilon||h||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{U})}+||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}],

and so using the definition of
\mu^{\prime}_{h}
,
h_{\nu\nu}=\mu^{\prime}_{h}-\frac{n-2}{n}u
, together with (
4.9
) gives

(4.10)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h_{\nu\nu}||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq C[||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}+\varepsilon||h||_{\widehat{H}^{s}(\widetilde{U})}],

Finally for
h(\nu)^{\mathsf{T}}
, from the Neumann boundary estimate (
2.24
), we have

||h(\nu)^{\mathsf{T}}||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq C[||\nabla_{\nu}h(\nu)^{\mathsf{T}}||_{H^{s-1/3}({{\mathcal{C}}_{t})}}+\varepsilon||h||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{U}_{t})}+||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}].

Examination of the form of the Neumann boundary data in (
3.3
) gives

(4.11)

\begin{split}||h(\nu)^{\mathsf{T}}||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq&C[||\ring{h}||_{H^{s+2/3}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}+||\mu^{\prime}_{h}||_{H^{s+2/3}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}\\
&+||V^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}}||_{H^{s-1/3}({\mathcal{C}})}+\varepsilon(||h||_{H^{s-1/3}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}+||h||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{U}_{t})})+||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}].\end{split}

Summing up the estimates (
4.6
)-(
4.11
), by choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently small depending on
g
, one may absorb
the
\varepsilon||h||_{\bar{H}^{s}(\widetilde{U})}
terms on the right side of these equations into the total sum on the left, using the fact that

|h||_{\bar{H}^{s}(\widetilde{U})}\leq\max_{t\in[0,1]}|h||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}
, giving then

(4.12)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq C||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}.

We note that the estimate (
4.11
) is the key ingredient in establishing the bound (
4.12
) in comparison with the work in
[
2
,
3
]
.

The bound on the left in (
4.12
) gives a bound on

(4.13)

h\in L^{\infty}(I,\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S)).

However, from the regularity theory for wave equations with Dirichlet boundary data as in Lemma
2.1
, by performing the computations
in the proof of Theorem
4.1
over arbitrarily small time intervals
I^{\prime}=[t_{0},t_{1}]\subset I
, it is easy to verify that this can be improved
to a bound on

h\in C^{0}(I,\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S)),

so that (
4.12
) gives in fact

(4.14)

||h||_{{\mathcal{N}}^{s}}^{2}\leq C||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

∎

5.
Linear Analysis II

In this section, we prove the global version of the results of §3 and §4. This is done by patching together local solutions and estimates to obtain
global in space solutions and estimates. The following result proves the well-posedness of the IBVP for the linearized problem, allowing for loss-of-derivatives.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth globally hyperbolic Lorentzian manifold,
M\simeq I\times S
, with compact Cauchy surface
S
having compact boundary

\partial S=\Sigma
and with timelike boundary
({\mathcal{C}},g_{{\mathcal{C}}})
. Then for any smooth

\tau^{\prime}\in T_{\tau}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})
, the equation

(5.1)

D\Phi_{g}^{H}(h)=\tau^{\prime}

has a unique
C^{\infty}
solution
h
. The constructed solution
h
depends smoothly on the data
\tau^{\prime}
.

Moreover, any
h
satisfying (
5.1
) satisfies the tame energy estimate

(5.2)

||h||_{{\mathcal{N}}^{s}}\leq C||D\Phi_{g}^{H}(h)||_{H^{s}(T^{H})},

where
C
depends only on
g
.

Proof.

Given the previous local results, the proof of Theorem
5.1
is essentially standard. We provide the details below,
following the proof of the analogous result in
[
2
]
.

Given
(M,g)
as in Theorem
5.1
, first choose an open cover
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of the corner
\Sigma
, where each
U_{i}
is sufficiently
small so that the local existence result Theorem
3.2
holds for each
U_{i}
. Also, choose an open set
U_{0}
in the interior
M\setminus{\mathcal{C}}

such that
(\cup_{i=1}^{N}U_{i})\cup U_{0}
also covers a tubular neighborhood of the initial surface, i.e.
S\times[0,t_{0}]
for some time
t_{0}>0
.

Let
\{\widetilde{U}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
be a thickening of the cover
\{U_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
, so that
U_{i}\subset\widetilde{U}_{i}
, as described in §2.2. Let

\rho_{i}
be a partition of unity subordinate to the cover
\{\widetilde{U}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
, so that
{\rm supp}\rho_{i}\subset\widetilde{U}_{i}
,
\rho_{i}=1
on

U_{i}
and
\sum_{i}\rho_{i}=1
. Let

\tau^{\prime}=\big(F,(\gamma^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},V_{S}^{\prime}),(\ring{\gamma^{\prime}},\eta^{\prime},V_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime})\big),

be arbitrary
C^{\infty}
data in
T({\mathcal{T}}^{H})
on
M
. Then the data
\rho_{i}\tau^{\prime}
has compact support in the sense of §2.2 in
\widetilde{U}_{i}

and by Theorem
3.2
there exists a solution
h_{i}
in
\widetilde{U}_{i}
satisfying

(5.3)

D\Phi^{H}_{g}(h_{i})=\rho_{i}\tau^{\prime}.

(Here
\tau^{\prime}
is rescaled by the relevant powers of
\lambda
as in §2.2, but the notation will not reflect this). Moreover, as noted at the end of §2.2, by the finite propagation
speed property, the solution
h_{i}
has compact support in the sense of §2.2 for
t\in[0,t_{i}]
for some
t_{i}>0
. Thus,
h_{i}
extends smoothly as the
zero solution on
M_{t_{i}}\setminus\widetilde{U}_{i}
.

Similarly, in the interior region
U_{0}\subset\widetilde{U}_{0}
, let
h_{0}
be the solution to the Cauchy problem

L(h_{0})=\rho_{0}F\ \mbox{ in }\widetilde{U}_{0},\ \ (g_{S},K,\nu_{S},V_{g})^{\prime}_{h}=\rho_{0}(\gamma^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},V_{S}^{\prime})\ \mbox{ on }S_{0}.

Then again
h_{0}
has compact support in
\widetilde{U}_{0}
for some time interval
[0,t_{0}]
and as above extends to
M_{t_{0}}
. We relabel so that

t_{0}
is a common time interval for all
h_{i}
,
i\geq 0
.

The sum

(5.4)

h=\sum_{i=0}^{N}h_{i},

is thus well-defined on
M_{t_{0}}
and by linearity

(5.5)

D\Phi_{g}^{H}(h)=\tau^{\prime}.

(For this global solution, the rescaling above is of course removed). By the local energy estimate in Theorem
4.2
, the solution
h_{i}
of (
5.3
) is
unique. It follows that the global solution
h
is the unique smooth solution (
5.5
), since if
h^{\prime}
is any other solution of (
5.5
), one may apply the
local decomposition (
5.4
) in the same way to
h^{\prime}
. Moreover, the partition of unity property shows that the local energy estimates from (
4.2
) sum
to give the global energy estimate (
5.2
).

The smoothness statement follows from that in Theorem
3.2
.

∎

6.
Nash-Moser theory

The analysis in §5 shows that the linearized IBVP with conformal-volume (or twisted Dirichlet) boundary data is well-posed and has tame energy
estimates if one allows for a loss of derivatives for the initial and boundary data. This is the setting of the Nash-Moser implicit function theorem, which we now
apply to prove Theorem
1.1
.

First we prove a general off-shell result.

Theorem 6.1
.

The mapping

(6.1)

\Phi^{H}:Met(M)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}^{H}

\Phi^{H}(g)=\big(\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta^{*}V,(\gamma_{S},K,\nu_{S},V|_{S}),([g_{{\mathcal{C}}}],\mu_{g},V|_{{\mathcal{C}}})\big),

is a smooth tame diffeomorphism between tame Frechet manifolds.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.8
, the target space
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
is a smooth tame Frechet manifold, as is the domain
Met(M)
. By construction, it is easy to see that the
map
\Phi^{H}
is then a smooth, tame map between Frechet manifolds, cf.
[
7
]
for the basic definitions regarding tame maps and Frechet manifolds here.

By Theorem
5.1
, the derivative
D\Phi_{g}^{H}
is a tame isomorphism at any
g\in Met(M)
and by (
5.2
), the inverse
(D\Phi_{g}^{H})^{-1}

exists and is a tame isomorphism. The Nash-Moser inverse function theorem, cf.
[
7
]
,
[
14
]
, then implies that
\Phi^{H}
is everywhere a smooth local
diffeomorphism.

Next we claim that
\Phi^{H}
in (
6.1
) is not only locally one-to-one but in fact globally one-to-one. To see this, suppose
g_{1},g_{2}
are two metrics
with the same gauged target data, i.e.
\Phi^{H}(g_{1})=\Phi^{H}(g_{2})
. If
g_{1}\neq g_{2}
, then there exists an arbitrarily small neighborhood

U
of some
p\in\Sigma
such that
g_{1}\neq g_{2}
in
U
. One may then perform a localization or rescaling of each
g_{i}
as in §2.2 so that the almost
constant coefficient metrics
\widetilde{g}_{i}
satisfy
\widetilde{g}_{1}\neq\widetilde{g}_{2}
in
U
. However, by (
2.5
), the metrics
\widetilde{g}_{i}
satisfy

||\widetilde{g}_{2}-\widetilde{g}_{1}||_{C^{\infty}}\leq\varepsilon,

in
U
, for a prescribed
\varepsilon>0
. It then follows from the local uniqueness in the Nash-Moser theorem above that

\widetilde{g}_{2}=\widetilde{g}_{1}

in some
U^{\prime}\subset U
. Hence
g_{2}=g_{1}
in
U^{\prime}
, which proves the global uniqueness.

To see that
\Phi^{H}
is globally surjective, let
\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
be arbitrary. Consider a small corner neighborhood
U
of
p\in\Sigma
and rescale the data

\tau
to
\widetilde{\tau}
. Then
Q\sim 0
,
\gamma_{S}
is close to a flat half-space
\{x^{1}\leq 0\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\kappa\sim 0
,
\nu_{S}
is close to the value in (
2.7
)
and
V\sim 0
. Thus
\widetilde{\tau}
is close to the value
\widetilde{\tau}_{0}=\Phi(\widetilde{g}_{\alpha_{0}})
, where
\widetilde{g}_{\alpha_{0}}
is the Minkowski corner metric in (
2.6
).
The Nash-Moser theorem above applies and gives the existence of
\widetilde{g}
in
U
such that
\Phi(\widetilde{g})=\widetilde{\tau}
. Rescaling back then gives the existence
of
g
such that
\Phi(g)=\tau
in
U
. Since
p\in\Sigma
is arbitrary, such local solutions exist everywhere near
\Sigma
and the uniqueness above implies
the local solutions agree on overlaps. Together with existence and uniqueness of solutions to the Cauchy problem on
S
, this gives the existence of
g

on
M
(for small time) with
\Phi(g)=\tau
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
6.1
.

∎

Next we turn to the proof of Theorem
1.1

Proof.

Let

{\mathcal{T}}^{H}_{0}=\{\big(0,(\gamma,\kappa,\nu_{S},0),(\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}},\mu,0)\big)\}\subset{\mathcal{T}}^{H},

so
Q
and the initial and boundary data of the gauge field in
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
are set to zero. It is proved in
[
3
]
that
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}_{0}
is a smooth submanifold
of
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
. (The fact that the boundary data here is twisted Dirichlet boundary data as opposed to Dirichlet boundary data plays no role in the proof).

Observe that

(\Phi^{H})^{-1}({\mathcal{T}}^{H}_{0})=\mathbb{E}^{H}\simeq{\mathcal{E}}_{*}.

Namely,
\Phi^{-1}({\mathcal{T}}^{H}_{0})
is the space of Lorentz metrics
g
on
M
with
Q=0
on
M
and
V=0
on
S\cup{\mathcal{C}}
. Since then

\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta^{*}V=0
, it follows from Remark
2.4
that
V=0
on
M
. Thus the metric
g
is in harmonic gauge and
\mathrm{Ric}_{g}=0
,
with given unit timelike normal
\nu_{S}
at
S
.
It follows then from Theorem
6.1
that the map

\Phi^{H}:\mathbb{E}^{H}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}^{H}_{0},

is a smooth, tame diffeomorphism between Frechet manifolds, with smooth, tame inverse
(\Phi^{H})^{-1}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

∎

Next we give the proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Proof.

We have shown in the proof of Theorem
1.1
above that
\mathbb{E}^{H}
is a smooth, tame Frechet manifold. The group
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)/\mathrm{Diff}_{1}(M)
is a
smooth tame Frechet Lie group (cf.
[
7
, I.4.6]
) which acts smoothly and freely on
\mathbb{E}^{H}
. It then follows from standard theory,
cf.
[
7
, Cor.III.2.5.2]
that the quotient
\mathbb{E}^{H}/(\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)/\mathrm{Diff}_{1}(M))
is a smooth, tame Frechet manifold. As noted in §1,

{\mathcal{E}}\simeq\mathbb{E}^{H}/(\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)/\mathrm{Diff}_{1}(M)),

and hence
{\mathcal{E}}
is a smooth, tame Frechet manifold.

∎

Remark 6.2
.

All of the discussion above has been phrased in the
C^{\infty}
context. However, all the results may be extended to Sobolev spaces
H^{s}
of finite
differentiability. Thus, as discussed in §2, the maps
\Phi
and
\Phi^{H}
are well-defined smooth maps
between a scale of Banach manifolds,

\Phi^{H}:Met^{s}(M)\rightarrow({\mathcal{T}}^{H})^{s},

indexed by
s
. The energy estimates in (
5.2
) hold on the
H^{s}
completions of the domain and target spaces for suitable values of
s
, giving the
existence of a tame inverse for the derivative
D\Phi^{H}
. One may then apply the finite derivative version of the Nash-Moser inverse
function theorem due to Zehnder
[
13
]
, or
[
14
, Thm.6.3.3]
; in the notation used there, the loss of derivative is
\gamma=3
.
However, we will not carry out here the details that the hypotheses of
[
14
, Thm.6.3.3]
do hold.

Remark 6.3
.

As in
[
2
,
3
]
, the results in this work hold for any value of the cosmological constant
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. The results also hold for the Einstein equations
coupled to Kaluza-Klein matter fields, which derive from the vacuum Einstein equations in higher dimensions by symmetry reduction. The boundary data
of the KK fields are then induced from the higher dimensional vacuum boundary data in
{\mathcal{B}}
.
