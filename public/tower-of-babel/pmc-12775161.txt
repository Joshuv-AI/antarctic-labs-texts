A narrative review of imaging misallocation in peripheral artery disease: a value-based medicine perspective
Authors: Daniel Raskin, Sasan Partovi, Abraham Levitin, Sean P Lyden, Levester Kirksey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveImaging for peripheral artery disease (PAD) is frequently misallocated: advanced cross-sectional studies are over-ordered for low-risk claudication, while high-risk chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI) patients often receive no timely anatomic study. This narrative review summarizes current guideline pathways, quantifies real-world deviations, and identifies value-based remedies that better align modality and timing with clinical need.MethodsData sources were PubMed, professional-society websites [American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA), European Society for Vascular Surgery/European Society of Cardiology (ESVS/ESC), American College of Radiology (ACR)], and gray literature in a timeframe of January 2015–February 2025. Eligible items were English-language PAD imaging guidelines/consensus statements, registry/claims analyses, cohort/comparative studies, and cost/equity evaluations; single-case reports and non-vascular imaging were excluded. We extracted guideline-recommended diagnostic pathways, compared them with contemporary utilization and cost data, categorized misallocation and operational drivers.Key Content and FindingsAcross four contemporary guidelines, the benchmark diagnostic sequence is physiologic testing with the ankle-brachial index or toe-brachial index (ABI/TBI), followed by duplex ultrasonography (DUS); when results would change management, computed tomography angiography (CTA) or magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) should be performed, with catheter-based digital subtraction angiography (DSA) reserved for intervention. Cross-sectional imaging increased three-fold in Medicare from 2011–2021, while first-line physiologic testing declined. Only 54% of CLTI patients receive CTA/MRA within 30 days, and each month of delay raises major amputation risk. Imaging access is poorest among minoritized, socio-economically disadvantaged, and rural groups, whereas supplier-induced demand amplifies scans in affluent settings. Misallocation exposes patients to avoidable radiation and contrast, strains radiology capacity, and contributes >US $4 billion in annual CLTI costs. Evidence shows guideline-aware clinical decision support can cut rarely-appropriate imaging by 10–40%, limb-salvage fast-track pathways reduce major amputations by ~30% and expanding sonographer staffing shifts after-hours demand away from CTA.ConclusionsPAD imaging is misaligned with value-based medicine: over-applied where benefit is marginal and under-applied where it is limb-saving. Implementing sequencing guardrails, decision-support tools, expedited CLTI workflows, and workforce remedies can rebalance utilization, enhance equity, and improve clinical and economic outcomes.Keywords: Peripheral artery disease (PAD), imaging, value-based health care

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, particularly among aging populations and those with cardiometabolic comorbidities (1,2). The burden is not evenly distributed: PAD disproportionately affects minoritized groups, rural communities, and people with lower socioeconomic status, with documented disparities in diagnosis, access to specialty evaluation, revascularization, and limb outcomes (3,4). Clinical presentation spans a continuum from asymptomatic disease to exertional claudication and chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI). Diagnosis rests on history, symptomatology, and physical examination, supported by physiologic testing (1). Prior reviews have summarized PAD epidemiology and imaging foundations (1,2), but few explicitly contrast guideline-endorsed sequencing with real-world utilization drift or frame the consequences through a value-based lens. Accordingly, we integrate guideline pathways (3) with recent utilization analyses and operational realities, yielding actionable, value-focused guidance (4-6).

Cross-sectional imaging—computed tomography angiography (CTA) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA)—provide high-fidelity anatomic roadmaps and pathophysiologic insight that can guide endovascular or surgical planning. Recent advances, including ultra-low-contrast CTA protocols, dual-energy and photon-counting detectors, and non-contrast MRA techniques such as quiescent-interval slice-selective (QISS), have expanded capability while mitigating risks from radiation and iodinated or gadolinium contrast (2,7-9). At the same time, substantial variability in advanced imaging use has emerged. Real-world practice shows over-utilization of cross-sectional angiography in lower-risk claudication and under-utilization or delay in high-risk CLTI, particularly in underserved populations, raising concerns about patient safety, equity, and unnecessary expenditures (5,6,10,11). Viewed through a value-based medicine (VBM) lens in vascular care (12), this bidirectional misallocation undermines the “Triple Aim” (13): it inflates cost without improving outcomes in low-risk patients and, conversely, compromises population health and equity when high-risk patients are imaged too late or not at all.

In this narrative review, we synthesize contemporary guideline pathways that reserve cross-sectional imaging for scenarios in which results will change management; delineate where practice diverges; and outline pragmatic, value-based actions to realign imaging with outcomes, equity, and stewardship. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://cdt.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/cdt-2025-426/rc).

Methods

We conducted a literature search and guideline review to inform this narrative analysis. In May 2025 we searched PubMed and professional society websites for English-language publications from January 2015 through February 2025 related to PAD imaging guidelines, utilization patterns, and outcomes. Key search terms included combinations of “peripheral artery disease”, “PAD imaging”, “CTA”, “MRA”, “duplex ultrasound”, “guidelines”, “appropriateness”, and “utilization”. We identified and reviewed the major recent PAD imaging guidelines or consensus documents, including the 2024 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) Lower-Extremity PAD guideline, the 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guideline on peripheral arterial diseases, the 2024 European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS) clinical practice guidelines (for asymptomatic PAD and intermittent claudication), and the 2022 American College of Radiology (ACR) Appropriateness Criteria for PAD imaging. From these, we extracted recommended diagnostic algorithms and indications for each imaging modality.

We also included relevant population-level studies of imaging utilization, prospective cohort studies comparing imaging strategies, and published cost-effectiveness analyses of PAD imaging modalities. Inclusion criteria were broad, focusing on studies that provided data or insights on imaging guideline adherence, regional or demographic variation in imaging use, patient-centered outcomes, and/or healthcare costs associated with imaging. Imaging in PAD

Physiological tests [ankle-brachial index (ABI) and related studies] are the first line for PAD diagnosis. The ABI is a simple bedside ratio of ankle to arm systolic pressure; an ABI ≤0.90 confirms PAD, whereas ABI >1.40 suggests non-compressible arteries requiring alternative measures (toe-brachial index, TBI). These tests objectively document perfusion impairment and are class I recommendations for initial PAD evaluation in all guidelines. If PAD is confirmed and symptoms are significant, duplex ultrasound (DUS) is often the next step. DUS combines real-time B-mode imaging with Doppler flow assessment, providing a non-invasive map of arterial lesions and hemodynamic significance. It is operator-dependent but highly useful for localization and grading of stenoses, and for surveillance of bypass grafts or stents. Given its safety (no radiation or nephrotoxic contrast) and low cost, DUS is frequently the preferred initial imaging modality if an anatomic study is needed (2,3,14).

CTA offers high-resolution, three-dimensional visualization of the arterial tree, which is crucial for identifying occlusions, evaluating stenoses, and for procedure planning purposes. Low-dose protocols, dual-energy imaging and photon-counting CT angiography have improved diagnostic accuracy and minimized radiation exposure as well as nephrotoxic contrast administration while maintaining a high diagnostic accuracy. However, CTA is associated with a reduced reliability in the presence of severely calcified atherosclerotic disease leading to blooming artefacts (7,15,16).

MRA provides a radiation free cross-sectional imaging alternative, with excellent contrast-to-noise ratios. Its ability to utilize non-contrast techniques, such as time-of-flight, QISS sequence and phase-contrast imaging, makes it particularly valuable for patients with renal insufficiency or contraindications to iodinated contrast agents. However, MRA utilization is limited due to limited availability, increased costs, longer acquisition times, and susceptibility to motion artefacts (9,17).

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) continues to be utilized for procedural planning in PAD, particularly when simultaneous diagnostic assessment and therapeutic intervention are anticipated (3,18).

Additional techniques

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS)

Utilizes injection of microbubble contrast agents to improve ultrasound imaging, enabling real-time assessment of tissue perfusion and vascularity and ability to detect subtle perfusion abnormalities. This technique is beneficial for evaluating the microcirculation without the risks associated with ionizing radiation or administration of nephrotoxic contrast agents, but its use in PAD remains investigational and its value has been considered limited at this point and is not yet guideline-endorsed (19).

Perfusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques

Dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) and dynamic susceptibility contrast (DSC) imaging are capable of assessing blood flow within tissues and evaluate the microvascular function. Blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) imaging complements these approaches by assessing tissue oxygenation dynamics. In PAD these techniques help gauge ischemic burden, collateral development, and response to therapy, yet they are limited to research settings due to lack of clear practical value in the clinical arena (20,21).

Radionuclide imaging

Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) and positron emission tomography (PET), is an experimental tool in the evaluation of PAD. SPECT allows for the assessment of regional perfusion. PET provides a more detailed evaluation of blood flow, vascular inflammation, and tissue metabolism in the lower extremities. The development of tracers such as 15O-water and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) may advance the ability to quantify microvascular perfusion and assess tissue viability. Early studies show some interesting findings with regard to detection of micro-vascular dysfunction and monitoring of angiogenesis, but routine clinical use of this experimental modality in PAD is not recommended (22).

Guideline recommendations

The 2024 ACC/AHA, 2024 ESC, 2024 ESVS, and 2022 ACR Appropriateness Criteria all advocate a stepwise diagnostic strategy for PAD. Non-invasive, cost-effective assessments with ABI, TBI, and DUS should be pursued for initial evaluation. CTA and MRA are recommended for PAD patients requiring detailed anatomical visualization, especially for procedural planning purposes. While both modalities could be applied for the assessment of complex or multilevel lesions, CTA is preferred due to its wide availability, rapid acquisition and high spatial resolution and MRA is reserved for patients with contraindications to iodinated contrast agents such as those with chronic kidney disease or severe allergic reaction to iodine-based contrast agents or when avoiding radiation exposure is desirable, such as in younger patients affected by PAD (3,18,23-25). The ACR notes that DSA should be reserved for therapeutic use or for cases in which non-invasive imaging is inconclusive (25). The ESVS guidelines also incorporate broader diagnostic tools, including biomarkers and treadmill testing for complementing imaging findings, thereby providing a more comprehensive approach to PAD management (18). Variation in imaging utilization and resource allocation

Real-world PAD imaging shows wide regional and provider-level variability. In Medicare, cross-sectional lower-extremity CTA use rose, while vascular ultrasound use fell, and MRA use declined markedly over the last decade, signaling a system-level drift toward advanced anatomic imaging even as guidelines continue to emphasize a stepwise, physiology-first approach (5). Across specialty vascular clinics in the international PORTRAIT registry, just over half of symptomatic patients underwent any vascular imaging, with site-level imaging rates ranging from single digits to nearly universal use, variation driven more by country and provider than patient factors (6).

Underuse clusters in the highest-risk population. In the CLIPPER Medicare cohort, delays from CLTI diagnosis to revascularization were common, and a delay beyond 30 days was associated with progressively higher major amputation risk among those eventually treated endovascularly, quantifying the cost of slow pathways (10). Medicare data illustrate this aspect: lower-extremity CTA claims climbed from 6.4 per 10,000 beneficiaries in 2011 to almost 18.2 per 10,000 in 2021, and hospital use ranged from 2% to 23% of PAD encounters (5). Yet only 54% of patients presenting with CLTI received any cross-sectional study within 30 days, with imaging rates 12 percentage points lower in African Americans compared to non-Hispanic White beneficiaries and 46% among dually eligible (Medicare + Medicaid) patients (6,10,11,26,27).

Rural hospitals, in which imaging resources are limited, performed roughly three-fold fewer CTA/MRA studies per PAD visit than urban teaching centers (5,28). Such wide variation spans countries, institutions, and individual providers. Additionally, supplier-induced demand, where financial incentives nudge physicians toward more services, has been linked to excess imaging (29-32), whereas fiscal constraints in safety-net settings appear to suppress indicated studies (6).

Consequences of misallocation

Economic burden

Unnecessary cross-sectional imaging in PAD represents a measurable, avoidable drain on public-payer budgets. Overuse of advanced imaging in low-risk claudication adds unnecessary cost and can trigger downstream cascades of further tests or unwarranted interventions without improving outcomes (33). Medicare data show a 14% rise in CTA utilization for PAD from 2011 to 2021 even as cheaper DUS use fell by 17%, reflecting a shift toward higher-cost imaging (5). Such over-imaging not only incurs the direct expense of each scan [e.g., an MRI averages ~$4,000 in charges (34)] but also fuels an “intervention cascade”, incidental findings and invasive procedures that inflate spending (33). Indeed, the cost of performing peripheral interventions for claudication has been deemed unsustainable, creating financial burdens for patients and inefficiencies in the health system (35). Conversely, underuse of imaging in high-risk PAD, particularly CLTI, delays critical treatment and ultimately raises costs. CLTI care already consumes over $4 billion annually in the U.S. (36) (with most costs borne by Medicare/Medicaid), and major amputations rank among the costliest surgical procedures (37). In a value-based imaging approach, the aim is to avoid waste in claudication while ensuring timely imaging for CLTI, which prevents higher downstream costs (35,38).

Patient harm

Overuse of imaging, especially CT scans, exposes patients to ionizing radiation that has been linked to heightened cancer risk. A meta-analysis including >111 million adults found that cumulative CT exposure was associated with significantly higher odds of cancer (combined OR: 5.9, 95% CI: 3.5–10.4) (39). Another modeling study estimated that CT scans in the U.S. in 2023 could lead to over 100,000 future cancer cases, accounting for up to 5% of new cancers annually (40). On the other hand, underuse or delayed imaging in PAD, especially CLTI, is associated with delays in treatment and linked to major amputation rates up and mortality (41-44). Contrast-associated kidney injury is uncommon in the general population; however, patients with chronic kidney disease with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <45 mL/min/1.73 m2, or those administered with iso-osmolar contrast media, warrant caution (45).

Access and equity

Patients with CLTI, racial and ethnic minorities and those who are uninsured or enrolled in Medicaid frequently face delayed or absent vascular imaging, resulting in significantly poorer outcomes. Black patients, for instance, disproportionately present with advanced stages of PAD, including CLTI, yet are substantially less likely to receive timely subspecialty vascular evaluation or appropriate diagnostic imaging. This disparity contributes to major amputation rates that are approximately four times higher compared to non-Hispanic White patients (6,10,11,26,27). Similarly, rural residents with PAD experience markedly lower rates of pre-amputation arterial imaging compared to their urban counterparts, further reflecting geographic disparities in care delivery and resource availability (6,46-50). In well-resourced health systems, patients with mild claudication or stable PAD often undergo frequent repeat vascular imaging (CTA, MRA, DUS) despite no change in symptoms or management (30). In a commercial-claims cohort of >110,000 newly diagnosed claudication patients, only 38% received an ABI before their first cross-sectional imaging, and privately insured enrollees were twice as likely as Medicaid patients to undergo cross-sectional imaging without prior physiologic testing (6). A separate Medicare study found 61% of lower-extremity CTAs were not preceded by any ABI or treadmill test, yet most scans were performed in beneficiaries from higher-income ZIP codes (30). This pattern of excess use among well-insured populations starkly contrasts with the under-imaging documented in vulnerable PAD groups, who often miss timely ABI screening and consequently present with more advanced disease and higher amputation risk (51,52).

Operational strain

Misallocation of imaging in PAD imposes significant operational burdens. Widespread sonographer shortages [ultrasound exam volumes grew ~55% from 2011–2021 while the sonographer workforce rose only ~44% (53)]. Medicare data reflect this shift: CTA utilization for PAD climbed ~14% in the past decade as vascular ultrasound use decreased ~17% (5). This trend adds to radiology workload, since advanced vascular studies are more complex: one center found CTA exams prompted additional physician communication in 25% of cases (up from 9%) (54), indicating greater interpretative effort and cognitive burden on radiologists. Consequently, in an already staff-constrained radiology sector, such volume and complexity can slow turnaround times for results.

Site-of-service patterns and value implications

Across the United States, peripheral vascular interventions (PVIs) for claudication have increasingly migrated to office-based laboratory (OBL)/ambulatory surgery center (ASC) settings, with a disproportionate rise in atherectomy volume and allowed charges over the past decade. Recent Medicare analyses demonstrate marked growth of tibial PVI and atherectomy for claudication in ambulatory settings along with higher spending, emphasizing the need to align imaging with indication and treatment intent rather than serving as a gateway to discretionary intervention (35,55). Moreover, practice-pattern studies comparing OBLs with hospital outpatient departments report systematic differences in case selection and workflow that can influence downstream imaging and therapy choices (56,57). For example, a Medicare-matched analysis found that claudicant patients who underwent infrapopliteal intervention had significantly worse 3-year outcomes than those treated with femoropopliteal PVI alone: higher rates of repeat intervention (41.0% vs. 38.2%, P<0.01), conversion to CLTI (33.3% vs. 23.8%, P<0.001), and major amputation (8.1% vs. 2.8%, P<0.001) (58). Likewise, patients requiring multiple reinterventions after an initial claudication PVI have been shown to be at significantly increased risk of progressing to CLTI and eventual limb loss (59). From a VBM standpoint, aligning indication, modality, and site of service is central to curbing low-value imaging and downstream discretionary interventions (57).

Strategies to mitigate misallocation

Clinically driven sequencing

All major guidelines (3,18,24,25) endorse a stepwise pathway: confirm PAD with ABI ± TBI (or exercise ABI when resting ABI is normal yet symptoms persist), localize with duplex when anatomic detail is needed, and reserve CTA/MRA (or catheter angiography) for cases in which imaging will directly guide revascularization. Implementing this sequence reduces low-value cross-sectional scans in stable claudication and shortens time-to-definitive therapy in CLTI by channeling urgent studies to those with limb threat (10,60,61). Guideline-aware decision support

Electronic health record (EHR) “soft-stop” alerts that ask clinicians to supply a clinical justification, before ordering non-urgent cross-sectional studies can cut rarely-appropriate imaging by 10–40% (62,63). Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted free-text indications can boost the share of orders receiving an appropriateness score from 30% to 52%, showing how machine learning can amplify clinical decision support effectiveness while keeping clinician burden low (64). Controlled deployments improved order appropriateness and reduced targeted high-cost imaging (e.g., 6% in a large randomized evaluation), with effects maintained after rollout; pairing clinical decision support with monthly peer-comparison dashboards (65,66).

Rapid triage for limb threat

Multidisciplinary limb-salvage programs that guarantee next-business-day DUS, reserve after-hours CTA for true emergencies, and target revascularization within 30 days have halved imaging wait times and reduced six-month major amputation rate by ~30% (67). Multidisciplinary limb-salvage programs are associated with higher revascularization before major amputation and lower amputation-related failure, with several centers also reporting shorter imaging-to-revascularization intervals, supporting the safety and efficiency of rapid-access DUS and judicious after-hours CTA triggers (68,69). Published reports show dedicated programs that dramatically cut amputation risk and shorten hospital stays compared to standard care (69,70).

Workforce pinch point

Shared technologist pools and expanded on-call vascular-lab staffing shift initial work-up toward DUS-first, trimming off-hours CTAs by roughly one-fifth in early pilots (53,71). Sites that piloted shared technologist pools and evening DUS blocks describe budget-neutral redeployment and measurable reductions in off-hours CTAs, while tracking simple guardrails (DUS access time, off-hours CTA proportion, return-visit rates) to ensure no unintended delays (72). Early pilots reported that doubling on-call vascular lab coverage (rather than CTA techs) cut unscheduled CTA volumes by roughly one-fifth, without harming timeliness or outcomes. The key is measuring guardrails: track DUS wait times, urgent CTA proportions, and 30-day revisit rates to ensure expanded DUS staffing truly shifts imaging rather than delaying it (69).

Implementing these stewardship tactics creates value across health systems: fewer rarely appropriate advanced scans by anchoring orders to guideline-concordant indications; faster, more equitable access for limb-threatened patients; stable or improved outcomes when care is coordinated through multidisciplinary limb-salvage pathways.

Discussion

This review confirms a bidirectional misallocation of imaging in PAD. Advanced cross-sectional studies are ordered with increasing frequency for low-risk claudication, Medicare CTA claims rose from 6.4 to 18.2 per 10,000 beneficiaries in just ten years (5), whereas first-line ultrasound fell, despite ABI-first algorithms in the 2024 ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines and their European counterparts (3,18,24,25). At the opposite extreme, only 54% of patients with CLTI receive any cross-sectional study within 30 days of diagnosis, and each additional month of delay is associated with a progressively higher risk of major amputation (10).

Viewed through a VBM lens, over-imaging violates the Triple Aim (13) by adding cost without benefit and it exposes patients to potentially avoidable harm of increased subsequent cancer (39), while modeling predicts that current CT use could generate more than 100,000 future cancers annually in the United States (40). Under-imaging of CLTI, conversely, perpetuates inequity; Black patients and socio-economically disadvantaged groups are less likely to receive timely vascular imaging and bear amputation rates up to four times higher than their non-Hispanic White counterparts (4,11,50).

Evidence-based system interventions can realign imaging with value. Reinforcing the ABI/TBI → DUS → advanced imaging sequence endorsed by ACC/AHA, ESC, ESVS, and ACR (3,18,24,25) curbs low-value scans, while electronic “soft-stop” alerts integrated into clinical-decision-support systems can reduce rarely appropriate cross-sectional orders when coupled with individualized audit-and-feedback reports (62,63) and natural-language-processing modules can have additional role orders appropriateness (64). Strengths and limitations

This review takes a practice-oriented approach consistent with day-to-day vascular care: physiologic testing and DUS first, with CTA/MRA for problem-solving and DSA where management depends on it. We place four contemporary guidelines side-by-side with real-world utilization, registry/claims data and cost analyses, highlighting where pathways diverge in routine practice and what that means for value-based and equity-aware care.

Limitations reflect the narrative design. Formal risk-of-bias tools were not applied, and heterogeneity in reporting (e.g., ABI/TBI cut-offs, duplex velocity criteria, imaging protocols and definitions of “inappropriate” use) precluded pooled estimates. Observational and administrative datasets are vulnerable to coding drift, selection and survivorship bias, and residual confounding; many cost figures rely on fee schedules or list charges rather than net prices. Evidence is predominantly U.S.-centric and English-language, with sparse representation from rural or low-resource settings and limited quantification of operational constraints (scanner availability, turnaround times, prior-authorization denials). Perspectives of patients, payers and vascular technologists were not systematically incorporated, and emerging AI-based appropriateness tools were not appraised in a formal framework. These factors temper generalizability and argue for local adaptation when implementing the recommendations.

Conclusions

Imaging for PAD today is pulled in opposite directions: overused in low-risk claudication and under-deployed or delayed in CLTI, contrary to the stepwise, physiology-first algorithms laid out by contemporary guidance. Correcting this misallocation is central to VBM PAD imaging as it reduces unwarranted imaging, contains escalating costs, and, most critically, shortens the path to limb-saving revascularization for high-risk patients. Evidence reviewed here shows that adherence to clinically oriented guidelines supported sequencing improves equitable access to timely studies where they matter most. Sustained implementation of these system-level levers offers a route to deliver the right modality to the right patient at the right moment, securing better outcomes while safeguarding healthcare resources.
