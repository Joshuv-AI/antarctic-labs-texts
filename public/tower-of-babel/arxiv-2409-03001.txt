Quantum theory at the macroscopic scale
Miguel Gallego, Borivoje Dakić

Abstract
The quantum description of the microscopic world is incompatible with the classical description of the macroscopic world, both mathematically and conceptually. Nevertheless, it is generally accepted that classical mechanics emerges from quantum mechanics in the macroscopic limit. In this paper, we challenge this perspective and demonstrate that the behaviour of a macroscopic system can retain all aspects of the quantum formalism, in a way that is robust against decoherence, particle losses and coarse-grained (imprecise) measurements. This departure from the expected classical description of macroscopic systems is not merely mathematical but also conceptual, as we show by the explicit violation of a Bell inequality and a Leggett-Garg inequality.



I
Introduction

Quantum mechanics is one of the most successful scientific theories, and it is generally accepted as more fundamental than classical mechanics. However, quantum behaviour is not observed at larger scales, where classical physics provides a better description. To explain the macroscopic world we perceive in our everyday life, it is believed that there must exist a quantum-to-classical transition or, in other words, that classical mechanics must somehow emerge from quantum mechanics in the macroscopic limit (somewhat in the spirit of Bohr’s correspondence principle
[
1
]
). The questions of when and how exactly this transition occurs, in spite of being active for almost a hundred years now
[
2
]
, are still debated today
[
3
]
.

One way to explain the emergence of classicality is to introduce genuine non-quantum effects (thus modifying quantum theory), such as the dynamical
[
4
]
or gravitationally induced collapse
[
5
]
of the wave function. Another standard way is to investigate the quantum-to-classical transition from within quantum theory (which is also the subject here). One of the most famous approaches in this direction is the decoherence mechanism
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, which shows that macroscopic systems, being hard to isolate, lose coherence in their interaction with the environment. Consequently their description becomes effectively classical. Complementary to this approach is the coarse-graining mechanism
[
9
,
10
]
, which shows that outcomes of macroscopic measurements admit a classical description when their resolution is limited even for perfectly isolated systems. While the main focus of the decoherence mechanism is on the dynamics, the coarse-graining approach focuses on the kinematic aspect of the transition to classicality. One way or another, the common conclusion is that quantum effects disappear in the macroscopic limit. Here, we want to challenge this view and ask
how much decoherence or coarse-graining is needed to observe classicality?
Typically, one takes a “transition” parameter, such as the size of the system: for example, the number of microscopic constituents
N
of a large system. Then, the standard statement is rather qualitative, positing that for a measurement resolution much greater than
\sqrt{N}
, one obtains an effective classical description
[
11
]
. Nevertheless, the precise mathematical meaning of what “much greater than” means is vague in a concrete experimental situation. Our goal here is to make such statements mathematically precise and to show a well-defined macroscopic scale at which large quantum systems can fully preserve a quantum description, in the sense of the typical ingredients of quantum theory such as the notion of
Hilbert space
, the
Born rule
and the
superposition principle
. Furthermore, we will show that these are not merely mathematical artefacts but genuine quantum phenomena by explicitly showing the violation of
Bell

[
12
]
and
Leggett-Garg

[
13
]
inequalities for such systems.

II
Coarse-grained measurements and the quantum-to-classical transition

The concept of coarse-grained measurement in quantum mechanics appeared already long ago as an attempt to address Born’s rule using the relative frequency operator (fuzzy or coarse-grained observables
[
14
]
). Continuing this line of inquiry, many subsequent works have followed, including discussions related to the weak and strong laws of large numbers
[
14
,
15
]
, as well as the quantum-to-classical correspondence (see
[
6
,
7
]
and references therein). A significant breakthrough in the operational understanding of the emergence of classicality through the coarse-graining mechanism came due to Kofler and Brukner
[
9
,
10
]
. In their works, the authors focus on classicality via the notion of macroscopic realism
[
13
]
or Bell’s local realism
[
12
]
. Such a framework opens an operational route to study the observability of genuine quantum effects, signalled by the violation of Leggett-Garg
[
13
]
or Bell
[
12
]
inequalities. Their main result is that with a sufficient level of coarse-graining (much greater than
\sqrt{N}
) the outcomes of the experiment can be modelled by a classical distribution (at least for finite dimensional systems)
[
9
,
10
]
. In particular, outcomes of successive measurements on a single system satisfy macroscopic realism (i.e. satisfy all Leggett-Garg inequalities)
[
9
]
, and local measurements on a bipartite system satisfy local realism (i.e. satisfy all Bell inequalities)
[
10
]
. Similar results can be found in the context of more general (post-quantum) theories in
[
16
,
17
]
. On the other hand, if the level of coarse-graining is just right, namely of the order
\sqrt{N}
, then we have the following facts: for
independent and identically distributed
(IID) pairs of (finite-dimensional) quantum systems, the coarse-grained quantum correlations satisfy Bell locality
[
18
]
(an analogous result can be shown for quantum contextuality
[
19
]
), while non-IID quantum states can exhibit nonlocal correlations as shown in our earlier works
[
20
,
21
]
. Furthermore, these works show that an entire family of (quantum) non-central limit theorems arises in such non-IID scenarios, raising an interesting situation in which, although being coarse-grained, macroscopic quantum systems exhibit quantum phenomena. We formalized this via the idea of
macroscopic quantum behaviour

[
20
,
21
]
, a property of a system in the macroscopic limit that retains the mathematical structure of quantum theory under the action of decoherence, particle losses, and coarse-graining. We have shown that it is possible to preserve the typical ingredients of the quantum formalism in the macroscopic limit, such as the Born rule, the superposition principle, and the incompatibility of the measurements.

In this paper, we build on our previous work and develop a
unified framework for quantum theory at the macroscopic scale
, which describes the theory of successive concatenation of measurements in the macroscopic limit. We use the formalism of Kraus operators in the limit Hilbert space, which allows us to show a violation of a Leggett-Garg inequality in the macroscopic limit, which in turn shows the incompatibility of coarse-grained measurements (at the
\sqrt{N}
level) with a macrorealistic description of the large quantum system.

Decoherence vs. coarse-graining

Before proceeding further, we make some remarks on decoherence and its relation to coarse-graining. The decoherence mechanism stresses the role of dynamical loss of quantum coherence due to the (instantaneous) interaction with the environment followed by the einselection process of the pointer basis
[
6
,
7
]
. On the other hand, the coarse-graining mechanism focuses on a kinematical aspect of the transition to classicality by measuring collective coarse-grained observables. Nevertheless, if decoherence is understood broadly as a mechanism of “classicalization” due to interaction with the environment, then coarse-graining can be seen as an instance of such a mechanism. Namely, suppose such a process is described by an interaction Hamiltonian of the type
H=H_{S}\otimes H_{E}
, with
H_{S}=\sum_{i}h_{i}
describing the collective Hamiltonian of the large quantum system (here
h_{i}
refers to the operators associated with local, microscopic constituents). In that case, the decoherence model effectively reduces to the measurement model of collective coarse-grained observables. This will be precisely our model of measurement, which we will introduce in the next sections. Therefore, essential aspects of the standard decoherence mechanism are incorporated in our study through coarse-graining of the measurements, and this is the standard argument to draw the parallel between the two approaches to the quantum-to-classical transition
[
22
]
. Notice that the system interacts collectively with the environment in that case. On the other hand, microscopic constituents can as well be independently subjected to a decoherence channel (such as the dephasing or depolarizing channel)
[
23
]
. We will also include this mechanism in our study and refer to it as
local decoherence
to distinguish it from the standard decoherence mechanism (a precise definition will be provided to avoid misunderstandings). Our aim is to show robustness of quantum phenomena in the macroscopic limit against both mechanisms.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
III
, we define the setting under consideration and specify all relevant assumptions. The notion of
macroscopic quantum behaviour
is introduced in Section
IV
, encompassing the quantum properties of systems in the macroscopic limit, with concrete examples provided. In Section
V
, we further analyse the properties of the system, demonstrating the genuineness of macroscopic quantum behaviour through device-independent tests such as the explicit violations of Bell inequalities as well as Leggett-Garg inequalities. Finally, we conclude with final remarks and open questions in Section
VI
.

III
Setup

We consider a macroscopic quantum measurement scenario analogous to the one presented in
[
18
,
20
,
21
]
(see Figure
1

\mathsf{S}
and a quantum measurement apparatus
\mathsf{M}
. To model a realistic situation in the absence of perfect control, we assume these satisfy certain assumptions.

Macroscopic system

First, we assume the system
\mathsf{S}
satisfies the following conditions:

(
i
)

Large
N
.
The system is composed of a large number
N
of identical particles or subsystems with associated Hilbert space
\mathfrak{h}
. We describe the state of the system with a density matrix
\rho_{N}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{h}^{\otimes N})
, i.e. a positive, self-adjoint bounded linear operator on
\mathfrak{h}^{\otimes N}
satisfying
\text{tr}\,\rho_{N}=1
.

(
ii
)

Local decoherence.
The system is subject to independent, single-particle decoherence channels
\Gamma
, such as the depolarizing or the dephasing channel. The effective state thus becomes
\Gamma^{\otimes N}(\rho_{N})
.

(
iii
)

Particle losses.
Each individual particle has a probability
p\in(0,1]
of reaching the measurement apparatus, while
1-p
is the probability of being lost (we assume
p>0
to avoid the trivial case where no particles reach the apparatus). Therefore, in each run of the experiment, only a number
M\leq N
of particles reach the measurement apparatus with a probability
f_{N}(M)={N\choose M}p^{M}(1-p)^{N-M}
. Consequently, the state received by the measurement apparatus is of the form
\text{tr}_{1\dots N-M}\rho_{N}
(where
\text{tr}_{i}
is understood as the partial trace over the Hilbert space
\mathfrak{h}
of the
i
-th particle).

Given these assumptions, for an initial state of the system
\rho_{N}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{h}^{\otimes N})
, the effective state of the system can be written in Fock-like space
\mathcal{F}_{N}=\oplus_{M=0}^{N}\mathfrak{h}^{\otimes M}
as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N}(\rho_{N})=\bigoplus_{M=0}^{N}f_{N}(M)\,\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\frac{\text{tr}_{\pi(1)\dots\pi(N-M)}\Big[\Gamma^{\otimes N}(\rho_{N})\Big]}{N!}\,,

(1)

where
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
is the symmetric (permutation) group of
N

Coarse-grained measurements

To model the macroscopic measurements, we assume the measurement apparatus
\mathsf{M}
satisfies the following conditions:

(
iv
)

Collective measurement
. The measurement setting of the apparatus is given by a single-particle observable, i.e. a Hermitian bounded operator
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{h})
. We denote by
A|{a}\rangle_{A}=a|{a}\rangle_{A}
the diagonalization of
A
, where
a\in\mathbb{R}
are its eigenvalues and
|{a}\rangle_{A}\in\mathfrak{h}
are its eigenstates. We denote by
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{h})
the set of (experimentally) accessible single-particle observables.

(
v
)

Intensity measurement.
Given a measurement setting
A\in\mathcal{A}
, the measurement apparatus measures the intensity
\sum_{i}a_{i}
, i.e. the sum of individual outcomes. The corresponding observable in Fock-like space is therefore the intensity observable
I_{N}(A)=\oplus_{M=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{M}A_{i}
, where
A_{i}=\mathbb{I}\otimes\dots\otimes A\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{I}

A

i
-th particle and with the identity on the rest.

(
vi
)

Coarse-graining.
The measuring scale for the intensity

has a limited resolution of the order of
\sqrt{N}
(the square-root of the total number of particles), meaning that it cannot distinguish between values that differ by approximately less than

Measurement model

In order to implement these assumptions explicitly, we follow von Neumann’s model of quantum measurements

24

\mathsf{M}

\mathsf{S}

\sigma\sqrt{N}

\displaystyle\Phi_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi N\sigma^{2})^{1/4}}e^{-x^{2}/(4N\sigma^{2})}\,.

(2)

The coupling between the system and the pointer is described by the Hamiltonian
H(t)=\gamma(t)\oplus_{M=0}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{M}A_{i}\otimes P

\displaystyle P_{N}(x|A)=\text{tr}\Big[K_{N}(x|A)\,\Lambda(\rho_{N})\,K_{N}^{\dagger}(x|A)\Big].

(3)

Here, the Kraus operators

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{M=0}^{N}\sum_{a_{1}\dots a_{M}}\Phi_{N}\left(x-\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}\right)\,\Pi_{a_{1}|A}\otimes\dots\otimes\Pi_{a_{M}|A}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{K_{N}(x|A)\,\Lambda(\rho_{N})\,K_{N}^{\dagger}(x|A)}{\text{tr}\big[K_{N}(x|A)\,\Lambda(\rho_{N})\,K_{N}^{\dagger}(x|A)\big]}\,,

Macroscopic limit

The macroscopic limit corresponds to the limit of an infinite number of particles, i.e.,
N\rightarrow\infty

and

\displaystyle K_{N}(x|A)

\displaystyle\mapsto\lambda_{N}^{-1/2}\,K_{N}\big(\lambda_{N}^{-1}(x-\mu_{N})|A\big)\,,

respectively.

IV
Quantum theory at the macroscopic scale

We now argue that, in the context of the above scenario, it is possible to define a joint notion of convergence for states and measurements that
preserves the complete mathematical structure of quantum theory in the macroscopic limit
. Furthermore, we will show how this formalism specifically applies to device-independent quantities such as
correlations
(both spatial, as in Bell experiments, and temporal, as in Leggett-Garg experiments). In other words, we will show that, in the limit
N\to\infty
, states
\rho_{N}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{h}^{\otimes N})
can be mapped to states
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{H})
in some “limit” Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}
and Kraus operators
K_{N}(x|A)
can be mapped to Kraus operators
K(x|A)
acting on
\mathfrak{H}
in a way such that the essential ingredients of quantum mechanics, including the Born rule, the superposition principle and the incompatibility of measurements, are retained. To do this, let us introduce the concepts of
macroscopic quantum representation
and
(robust) macroscopic quantum behaviour of order

n
(MQB
n
).

Definition 1
.

Given a closed subspace
\mathfrak{H}_{N}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\otimes N}
and a subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{h})
, a
macroscopic quantum representation
is a limit of the form

\displaystyle\Big(\mathfrak{H}_{N},\rho_{N},K_{N}(x|A)\Big)\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\Big(\mathfrak{H},\rho,K(x|A)\Big)

for all
\rho_{N}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{H}_{N})
and for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
, where

•

\mathfrak{H}
is a Hilbert space;

•

the limit state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}
is a “linear” function of
\rho_{N}
, in the sense that if the pure states
|{\psi}\rangle_{N}
and
|{\phi}\rangle_{N}
are mapped, respectively, to
|{\psi}\rangle
and
|{\phi}\rangle
, then the linear combination
\alpha|{\psi}\rangle_{N}+\beta|{\phi}\rangle_{N}
is mapped to the linear combination
\alpha|{\psi}\rangle+\beta|{\phi}\rangle
;

•

the limit Kraus operators
K(x|A)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{H})
form a non-compatible set of measurements.

Definition 2
.

A closed subspace
\mathfrak{H}_{N}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\otimes N}
and a subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{h})
possess
MQB
n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
if there exists a macroscopic quantum representation

\displaystyle\Big(\mathfrak{H}_{N},\rho_{N},K_{N}(x|A)\Big)\to\Big(\mathfrak{H},\rho,K(x|A)\Big)

such that for every state
\rho_{N}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{H}_{N})
and for every sequence of measurement settings
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})\in\mathcal{A}^{\otimes n}
, the random vector

\displaystyle\vec{X}_{N}\sim\text{tr}\bigg[K_{N}^{(n)}\dots K_{N}^{(1)}\,\rho_{N}\,K_{N}^{(1)\dagger}\dots K_{N}^{(n)\dagger}\bigg]

(or an affine transformation thereof, i.e.
\vec{\lambda}_{N}\odot\vec{X}_{N}+\vec{\mu}_{N}
for some suitably chosen
\vec{\lambda}_{N},\vec{\mu}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
), where
K_{N}^{(j)}=K_{N}(x_{j}|A_{j})
, converges in distribution as
N\to\infty
to some random vector

\displaystyle\vec{X}\sim\text{tr}\big[K^{(n)}\dots K^{(1)}\,\rho\,K^{(1)\dagger}\dots K^{(n)\dagger}\big]\,,

where
K^{(j)}=K(x_{j}|A_{j})
(as given by the macroscopic quantum representation).

In references
[
20
,
21
]
, only the case of a single measurement was considered (

), and the definition of “MQB” given there corresponds to MQB
1
as defined here. Now we define a stronger notion, where the macroscopic quantum representation is robust against decoherence and losses as introduced in assumptions

and

respectively.

Definition 3
.

A closed subspace
\mathfrak{H}_{N}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\otimes N}
and a subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{h})
possess

MQB
n

if there exist a macroscopic quantum representation

\displaystyle\Big(\mathfrak{H}_{N},\rho_{N},K_{N}(x|A)\Big)\to\Big(\mathfrak{H},\rho,K(x|A)\Big)

and

such that for every state
\rho_{N}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{H}_{N})
, for every sequence of measurement settings

of the form (

\displaystyle\vec{X}_{N}\sim\text{tr}\bigg[K_{N}^{(n)}\Lambda^{(n)}\Big(\dots K_{N}^{(1)}\,\Lambda^{(1)}(\rho_{N})\,K_{N}^{(1)\dagger}\dots\Big)K_{N}^{(n)\dagger}\bigg]\,,

(or an affine transformation
\vec{\lambda}_{N}\odot\vec{X}_{N}+\vec{\mu}_{N}
for some suitably chosen
\vec{\lambda}_{N},\vec{\mu}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
), where

where
K^{(j)}=K(x_{j}|A_{j})
(as given by the macroscopic quantum representation).

We have shown that the above system, consisting of Dicke states

VI
Outlook and open questions

Our results shed new light on the question of the quantum-to-classical transition, suggesting that genuine quantum phenomena might be more robust than previously thought. There are several interesting questions to be addressed in the future:

•

Question of scale
. Our results show that genuine quantum behaviour can be visible through measurements with a precision of the order of
\sqrt{N}
, even in the presence of (single-particle) decoherence and losses. The relevant parameter that defines the scale of these quantum effects is, therefore, the resolution of the measurements as a function of the system’s size
N
. An open question is whether genuine quantum effects exist at a scale larger than
\sqrt{N}
, thus surviving even more coarse-graining than the system we consider. The results of Kofler and Brukner
[
9
,
10
]
indicate that this is not possible, showing classicality for a resolution much larger than
\sqrt{N}
. But their result only applies to finite-dimensional systems, and the case of infinite dimensional systems (in our language above, the case where the single-particle Hilbert space
\mathfrak{h}
is infinite-dimensional) is still to be investigated.

•

Macroscopicity measures
. The question of macroscopic quantum states dates back to Schrödinger
[
2
]
, and still today work is done to characterize the “macroscopicity” of quantum states (see
[
28
]
and references therein). An example of a quantum state that is typically thought to be macroscopically quantum is the Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ)
[
29
]
state
(|{0}\rangle^{\otimes N}+|{1}\rangle^{\otimes N})/2
(also called cat-like state). However, such a state is extremely fragile, since the loss of coherence of a single particle destroys the coherence of the global state, collapsing it into a classical mixture. For this reason, the GHZ state is not useful for our purposes. Another state typically associated with macroscopic quantumness is the
W
state
[
30
]
, which in our bipartite setting corresponds to the Dicke state
|{2N,1}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|{N,1}\rangle\otimes|{N,0}\rangle+|{N,0}\rangle\otimes|{N,1}\rangle\big)
. We have however not found any Bell violation for such a state. These two examples show that quantum macroscopicity does not necessarily result in non-classicality in the macroscopic limit as defined in our sense. More precise relations are to be left for future considerations.

•

Classical limit and infinite tensor products
. There is a recent proposal that suggests that focusing on type I operator algebras might be the source of the problem and considering instead quantum mechanics on type II operator algebras might provide a framework that encompasses the classical macroscopic limit in a natural way (see
[
31
]
). This seems to provide another formalism to arrive at the macroscopic limit, and an interesting question to be investigated is how these findings relate to our result.

•

Experimental implementations
. Of particular interest are experimental considerations to test our findings. Some potential experimental settings that seem to provide the appropriate characteristics are atomic memories and Bose-Einstein condensates (see e.g.
[
32
,
33
]
). While our results are derived in the explicit limit
N\rightarrow\infty
, one has to derive concrete bounds for finite
N
or at least provide numerical simulations.

Proof.

\displaystyle P_{N}(x)=\text{tr}\Big[K_{N}(x|A)\Lambda(\rho_{N})K_{N}^{\dagger}(x|A)\Big]\,,

By Lévy’s continuity theorem, in order to show that

converges in distribution it is sufficient to show that its characteristic function
\chi_{N}(t)

converges pointwise to some function

continuous at

. We have

\displaystyle\chi_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,e^{itx}\,P_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,e^{itx}\,\text{tr}\Big[K(x|A)\Lambda(\rho_{N})\,K_{N}^{\dagger}(x|A)\Big]

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left[\Lambda(\rho_{N})\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,e^{itx}K_{N}^{\dagger}(x|A)K_{N}(x|A)\right]

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,e^{itx}\,\Phi^{2}\Big(x-\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,e^{itx}\,\frac{e^{-(x-\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i})^{2}/(2N\sigma^{2})}}{\sqrt{2\pi N\sigma^{2}}}

\displaystyle=e^{it\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}-N\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2}\,,

(40)

we have

\displaystyle\chi_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-N\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2}\,\text{tr}\left[\Lambda(\rho_{N})\bigoplus_{M=0}^{N}\sum_{a_{1}\dots a_{M}}e^{it\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}}\,\Pi_{a_{1}|A}\otimes\dots\otimes\Pi_{a_{M}|A}\right]

Appendix D
Leggett-Garg CHSH inequality violation

Here we compute the maximal violation of the Leggett-Garg CHSH inequality (
10
) using states and measurements as obtained in the macroscopic limit (see main text). For convenience, we will work with phase-space representation of operators. In particular, we define

\displaystyle W_{\rho}(x,p)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\,e^{2ips}\langle{x-s}\vphantom{\rho}|{\rho}\vphantom{x+s}|{x+s}\rangle\,,

\displaystyle E(x,p)

\displaystyle:=2\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\,e^{-2ips}|{x-s}\rangle\langle{x+s}|\,,

(89)

so that
\rho=\int dx\,dp\,W_{\rho}(x,p)E(x,p)
. Defining
K_{\varphi}(\xi):=K(\xi|\cos\varphi\,\sigma_{x}+\sin\varphi\,\sigma_{y})
corresponding to the case where Alice measures spin in the
(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,0)
direction, we have

\displaystyle K_{0}(\xi)\rho K_{0}(\xi)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,W_{\rho}(x,p)K_{0}(\xi)E(x,p)K_{0}(\xi)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\cdot 2\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\,e^{-2ips}K_{0}(\xi)|{x-s}\rangle\langle{x+s}|K_{0}(\xi)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=2\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,ds\,W_{\rho}(x,p)e^{-2ips}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy\,\frac{e^{-(y-\xi)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}}{(\pi\sigma^{2})^{1/4}}\,|{y}\rangle\langle{y}\vphantom{x-s}|{x-s}\vphantom{y}\rangle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy^{\prime}\frac{e^{-(y^{\prime}-\xi)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}}{(\pi\sigma^{2})^{1/4}}\langle{x+s}\vphantom{y^{\prime}}|{y^{\prime}}\vphantom{x+s}\rangle\langle{y^{\prime}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi\sigma^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,ds\,W_{\rho}(x,p)e^{-2ips}\,e^{-(x-s-\xi)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}\,e^{-(x+s-\xi)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}\,|{x-s}\rangle\langle{x+s}|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi\sigma^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,ds\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,e^{-2ips}\,e^{-(x-\xi)^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\,e^{-s^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\,|{x-s}\rangle\langle{x+s}|\,.

(90)

Therefore

\displaystyle P_{\varphi}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\Big[K_{\varphi}(\eta)K_{0}(\xi)\rho K_{0}(\xi)^{\dagger}K_{\varphi}(\eta)^{\dagger}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi\sigma^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,ds\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,e^{-2ips}\,e^{-(x-\xi)^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\,e^{-s^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\,\text{tr}\Big[K_{\varphi}(\eta)|{x-s}\rangle\langle{x+s}|K_{\varphi}(\eta)^{\dagger}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi\sigma^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,ds\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,e^{-2ips}\,e^{-(x-\xi)^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\,e^{-s^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\,\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy\frac{e^{-(y-\eta)^{2}/\sigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi\sigma^{2}}}\langle{x+s}\vphantom{y}|{y}\vphantom{x+s}\rangle_{\varphi}\,{}_{\varphi}\langle{y}\vphantom{x-s}|{x-s}\vphantom{y}\rangle\,.

(91)

Now, using that
[
35
]

\displaystyle\langle{x}\vphantom{y}|{y}\vphantom{x}\rangle_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi|\sin\varphi|}}\exp\left\{-\frac{i\cos\varphi}{2\sin\varphi}\left(x-\frac{y}{\cos\varphi}\right)^{2}\right\}\,,

(92)

the above expression reads

\displaystyle P_{\varphi}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}|\sin\varphi|}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,ds\,dy\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,e^{\alpha(x,p,s,y)}\,,

(93)

where

\displaystyle\alpha(x,p,s,y)

\displaystyle=-2ips-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{(y-\eta)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{i\cos\varphi}{2\sin\varphi}\left(x+s-\frac{y}{\cos\varphi}\right)^{2}+\frac{i\cos\varphi}{2\sin\varphi}\left(x-s-\frac{y}{\cos\varphi}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-2ips-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{(y-\eta)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{2i\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}\left(x-\frac{y}{\cos\varphi}\right)s\,.

(94)

Integrating in
s
gives

\displaystyle P_{\varphi}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi^{3}\sigma^{2}}|\sin\varphi|}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,dy\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,e^{\beta(x,p,y)}\,,

(95)

where

\displaystyle\beta(x,p,y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{(y-\eta)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}\left[2p+\frac{2\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}\left(x-\frac{y}{\cos\varphi}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{y^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{2\eta y}{\sigma^{2}}-\sigma^{2}p^{2}-\sigma^{2}\frac{\cos^{2}\varphi}{\sin^{2}\varphi}\left(x-\frac{y}{\cos\varphi}\right)^{2}-2\sigma^{2}p\frac{\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}\left(x-\frac{y}{\cos\varphi}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{y^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{2\eta y}{\sigma^{2}}-\sigma^{2}p^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}x^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi}{\sin^{2}\varphi}-\frac{\sigma^{2}y^{2}}{\sin^{2}\varphi}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}xy\cos\varphi}{\sin^{2}\varphi}-\frac{2\sigma^{2}px\cos\varphi}{\sin\varphi}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}py}{\sin\varphi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sin^{2}\varphi}p^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sin^{2}\varphi}x^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\sin^{2}\varphi}x\cos\varphi\,p\sin\varphi

\displaystyle\quad\,-\frac{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}{\sigma^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}y^{2}+\left(\frac{2\eta}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}x\cos\varphi}{\sin^{2}\varphi}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}p}{\sin\varphi}\right)y\,.

(96)

Integrating in
y
gives

\displaystyle P_{\varphi}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,e^{\gamma(x,p)}\,,

(97)

where

\displaystyle\gamma(x,p)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sin^{2}\varphi}(x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi)^{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{\sin^{2}\varphi}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}\left(\eta+\frac{\sigma^{4}}{\sin^{2}\varphi}(x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\sin^{2}\varphi}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}\right)\eta^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sin^{2}\varphi}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{4}}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}\right)(x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\,+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}\eta(x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}\eta^{2}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}(x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi)^{2}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}\eta(x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}\big(x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi-\eta\big)^{2}\,.

(98)

So we have

\displaystyle P_{\varphi}(\xi,\eta)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,\exp\left\{-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}\big(x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi-\eta\big)^{2}\right\}\,.

(99)

Then, the correlator of the random variables
\sgn(\xi)
and
\sgn(\eta)
is

\displaystyle\langle{\sgn(\xi)\sgn(\eta)}\rangle_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\xi\,d\eta\,\sgn(\xi)\sgn(\eta)P_{\varphi}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\xi\,\sgn(\xi)e^{-\frac{(x-\xi)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\eta\,\sgn(\eta)e^{-\frac{\sigma^{2}(\eta-x\cos\varphi-p\sin\varphi)^{2}}{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,\sqrt{\pi\sigma^{2}}\,\text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)\sqrt{\pi\frac{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}{\sigma^{2}}}\,\text{erf}\left(\frac{x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,dp\,W_{\rho}(x,p)\,\text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)\text{erf}\left(\frac{x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)\,.

(100)

Now let us compute the Wigner function of the state
\rho=\sum_{k,l}c_{kl}|{l}\rangle\langle{k}|
:

\displaystyle W_{\rho}(x,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k,l}c_{kl}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\,e^{2ips}\langle{x-s}\vphantom{l}|{l}\vphantom{x-s}\rangle\langle{k}\vphantom{x+s}|{x+s}\vphantom{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{k,l}\frac{c_{kl}}{\sqrt{2^{k}\,k!\,2^{l}\,l!}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\,e^{2ips}e^{-(x-s)^{2}/2}H_{l}(x-s)e^{-(x+s)^{2}/2}H_{k}(x+s)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{\pi\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{k,l}\frac{c_{kl}}{\sqrt{2^{k}\,k!\,2^{l}\,l!}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\,e^{-s^{2}+2ips}\,H_{l}(x-s)H_{k}(x+s)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{\pi\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{k,l}\frac{c_{kl}}{\sqrt{2^{k}\,k!\,2^{l}\,l!}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}ds\,e^{-(s-ip)^{2}-p^{2}}\,H_{l}(x-s)\,H_{k}(x+s)\,.

(101)

Defining
\zeta:=s-ip
we have

\displaystyle W_{\rho}(x,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-x^{2}-p^{2}}}{\pi\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{k,l}\frac{c_{kl}}{\sqrt{2^{k}\,k!\,2^{l}\,l!}}\int_{-\infty-ip}^{+\infty-ip}d\zeta\,e^{-\zeta^{2}}\,H_{l}\big(x-\zeta-ip\big)\,H_{k}\big(x+\zeta+ip\big)\,.

(102)

Since the integrand is holomorphic, we can set to zero the imaginary shift in the integration contour. Then, using the property
[
36
]

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,e^{-x^{2}}\,H_{k}(x+y)\,H_{l}(x+z)=2^{\max(k,l)}\sqrt{\pi}\,\min(k,l)!\,y^{k-\min(k,l)}z^{l-\min(k,l)}L_{\min(k,l)}^{|k-l|}(-2yz)\,,

(103)

we can write

\displaystyle W_{\rho}(x,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-x^{2}-p^{2}}}{\pi}\sum_{k,l}c_{kl}\,(-1)^{l}\sqrt{\frac{2^{\max(k,l)}\min(k,l)!}{2^{\min(k,l)}\max(k,l)!}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\big(-x+ip\big)^{l-\min(k,l)}\big(x+ip)^{k-\min(k,l)}L_{\min(k,l)}^{|k-l|}\big(-2(-x+ip)(x+ip)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-x^{2}-p^{2}}}{\pi}\Bigg\{\sum_{k\geq l}c_{kl}(-1)^{l}\sqrt{\frac{2^{k}\,l!}{2^{l}\,k!}}(x+ip)^{k-l}\,L_{l}^{k-l}\big(2x^{2}+2p^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{k<l}c_{kl}(-1)^{l}\sqrt{\frac{2^{l}\,k!}{2^{k}\,l!}}(-x+ip)^{l-k}\,L_{k}^{l-k}\big(2x^{2}+2p^{2}\big)\Bigg\}\,.

(104)

Plugging this back into the correlator (
100
) yields

\displaystyle\langle\sgn(\xi)\sgn(\eta)\rangle_{\varphi}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k\geq l}c_{kl}(-1)^{l}\sqrt{\frac{2^{k}\,l!}{2^{l}\,k!}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dxdp\,e^{-x^{2}-p^{2}}(x+ip)^{k-l}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\cdot L_{l}^{k-l}\big(2x^{2}+2p^{2}\big)\text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)\text{erf}\left(\frac{x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k<l}c_{kl}(-1)^{l}\sqrt{\frac{2^{l}\,k!}{2^{k}\,l!}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dxdp\,e^{-x^{2}-p^{2}}(-x+ip)^{l-k}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\cdot L_{k}^{l-k}\big(2x^{2}+2p^{2}\big)\text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)\text{erf}\left(\frac{x\cos\varphi+p\sin\varphi}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{4}+\sin^{2}\varphi}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)\,.

(105)

Let us restrict to the 3-dimensional subspace spanned by the first three excitations in the Hilbert space so that the sums over
k
and
l
only run until 2. In this case we can analytically perform the integrals using the identities
[
37
]

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,e^{-x^{2}}\,\text{erf}(ax+b)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}\,\text{erf}\left(\frac{b}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,x\,e^{-x^{2}}\,\text{erf}(ax+b)

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{b^{2}}{1+a^{2}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,x^{2m}e^{-ax^{2}}\,\text{erf}(bx)\text{erf}(cx)

\displaystyle=2\frac{(-1)^{m}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial a^{m}}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\arctan\left(\frac{bc}{\sqrt{a^{2}+a(b^{2}+c^{2})}}\right)\right]\,,\quad(a>0\,,\,\,m=0,1,\dots)\,,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,x^{2}\,e^{-x^{2}}\,\text{erf}(ax+b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\text{erf}\left(\frac{b}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}\right)-\frac{a^{2}b}{(1+a^{2})^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{b^{2}}{1+a^{2}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,x^{3}\,e^{-x^{2}}\,\text{erf}(ax)

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{1+a^{2}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,x^{4}\,e^{-x^{2}}\,\text{erf}(ax+b)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\text{erf}\left(\frac{b}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}\right)-\frac{3}{2}\frac{a^{2}b}{(1+a^{2})^{3/2}}\left[1+\frac{2a^{2}b^{2}}{3(1+a^{2})^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+a^{2}}\right]\exp\left(-\frac{b^{2}}{1+a^{2}}\right)\,.

(106)

The results are compact expressions, but too long to show here. Finally, the Leggett-Garg CHSH parameter is

\displaystyle C=\langle\sgn(\xi)\sgn(\eta)\rangle_{\varphi_{A}^{(1)}-\varphi_{B}^{(1)}}+\langle\sgn(\xi)\sgn(\eta)\rangle_{\varphi_{A}^{(1)}-\varphi_{B}^{(2)}}+\langle\sgn(\xi)\sgn(\eta)\rangle_{\varphi_{A}^{(2)}-\varphi_{B}^{(1)}}-\langle\sgn(\xi)\sgn(\eta)\rangle_{\varphi_{A}^{(2)}-\varphi_{B}^{(2)}}\,.

(107)

Maximizing
C
over the four angles and the coefficients
c_{kl}
we have the following violation of the inequality
C\geq 2
:

\displaystyle C=\frac{2}{675\pi}\Big(577+\sqrt{1244179}+2700\arctan(1/3)\Big)\simeq 2.416\,,

(108)

obtained for the angles

\displaystyle\varphi_{A}^{(1)}=\frac{\pi}{4}\,,\quad\varphi_{A}^{(2)}=\frac{3\pi}{4}\,,\quad\varphi_{B}^{(1)}=\frac{\pi}{2}\,,\quad\varphi_{B}^{(2)}=0\,,

(109)

and the state

\displaystyle|{\psi}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{577}{2\sqrt{1244179}}}\,|{0}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{577}{2\sqrt{1244179}}}\,|{2}\rangle\,.

(110)
