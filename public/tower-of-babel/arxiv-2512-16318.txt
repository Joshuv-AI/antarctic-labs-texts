Learning Recursive Attenuation Filters Under Noisy Conditions
Gloria Dal Santo, Karolina Prawda, Sebastian J. Schlecht, Vesa Välimäki

Abstract
Recursion is a fundamental concept in the design of filters and audio systems. In particular, artificial reverberation systems that use delay networks depend on recursive paths to control both echo density and the decay rate of modal components. The differentiable digital signal processing framework has shown promise in automatically tuning both recursive and non-recursive elements given a target room impulse response. This is done by applying gradient descent to loss functions based on energy-decay or spectrogram differences. However, these representations are highly sensitive to background noise, which is ubiquitous in real measurements, producing spurious loss minima and leading to incorrect attenuation. This paper addresses the problem of tuning recursive attenuation filters of a feedback delay network when targets are noisy. We examine the loss landscape associated with different optimization objectives and propose a method that ensures correct minima under low signal-to-noise conditions. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach through statistical analysis on 80 individual optimization examples. The results reveal that explicitly modeling the noise restores correct minima. Furthermore, we identify the sensitivity of attenuation filter parameters tuning to perturbations in frequency-independent parameters. These findings provide practical guidelines for more robust and reproducible gradient-based optimization of feedback delay networks.

INTRODUCTION

Differentiable digital signal processing (DDSP)
[
10
,
14
]
for filter design is an approach that applies automatic differentiation to digital signal processing operations. By implementing filters in a differentiable manner, it becomes possible to backpropagate loss gradients through their parameters, enabling data-driven optimization without losing domain knowledge. DDSP has gained popularity due to these domain-appropriate inductive biases, yet it still presents several challenges
[
14
]
. The parameters of differentiable filters are often constrained by stability conditions
[
24
]
, may be subject to the responsibility problem
[
15
]
, and may span different domains and distributions, making optimization nontrivial.

In artificial reverberation, the feedback delay network (FDN) became a particularly popular choice for late reverberation modeling due to its versatility and computational efficiency
[
20
,
51
]
. More recently, the use of FDN has also gained traction within DDSP frameworks for blind reverb estimation
[
25
,
13
]
and effects matching
[
56
]
. The application of differentiable FDNs is also supported by research focused on gradient-based optimization without the use of neural networks, aiming instead to optimize FDN parameters for a given target room impulse response (RIR) in a more traditional fashion
[
6
,
8
,
31
,
29
]
.

The design of an FDN typically follows a two-step process. First, a lossless FDN
[
40
]
is constructed such that the input energy circulates through the feedback loop without attenuation, resulting in no decay over time. In the second step, attenuation filters are added to the feedback paths to control the rate of energy decay, thereby determining the system’s reverberation time (
T_{\textrm{60}}
). While the latter is more crucial when matching a target RIR, given the perceptual role of
T_{60}
, poor lossless FDN designs can lead to coloration and sparseness issues that degrade the plausibility of the system’s output. Our previous work
[
6
,
8
]
addressed this by optimizing the frequency-independent parameters using gradient descent to produce a perceptually colorless and temporally dense response.

The design of the attenuation filter depends on several factors, including the desired control flexibility, computational complexity constraints, and required accuracy. One common requirement is to ensure that the filter strength is proportional to the corresponding delay length, thereby ensuring a correct decay rate across modes. Early implementations primarily relied on low-order lowpass filters
[
32
,
20
,
19
,
21
]
, which provided only limited control over a few frequency bands. Higher-order filters were later used to simulate the
T_{60}
in octave bands, among which proportional graphic equalizers (GEQ) became widely popular due to their simplicity
[
22
]
, and availability of accurate designs
[
42
,
38
,
52
]
.
These filter designs, however, assume that the target
T_{60}
values are known.

While standard methods exist for estimating
T_{60}
directly from the RIR, typically based on the Schroeder backward integration via energy decay curves (EDCs)
[
45
]
, these approaches often provide wrong estimations. Such errors generally arise from mismatches with the assumed single-decay slope model, the presence of high background noise, or non-stationary noise
[
33
,
23
,
54
,
12
]
. It is therefore highly desirable to estimate the filter parameters directly from the measured RIR, or from the reverberated material itself. The latter poses a significantly more challenging problem and could benefit from recent advances in techniques such as DDSP and other deep learning-based methods.

In this context, Lee et al.
[
25
]
directly learn the parameters of differentiable parametric equalizers (PEQs) implemented with state-variable filters. However, their design was not proportional, which limits its applicability. Ibnyahya et al. introduced a scalable, proportional, and differentiable PEQ that maintains accuracy and stability comparable to state-of-the-art informed methods
[
18
]
. Nonetheless, this design has not yet been integrated into a complete FDN architecture
[
18
]
. Mezza et al.
[
29
]
implemented the filter bank as a single one-dimensional convolutional layer, assigning a dedicated kernel to each frequency channel. Nevertheless, neither of these works incorporates coloration or sparsity into their loss functions, requiring additional components to suppress coloration and increase echo density
[
25
]
.

The first works to incorporate a sparsity penalty term within a DDSP framework are those on blind estimation using acoustic embeddings
[
13
]
, and on grouped FDNs for coupled rooms
[
9
]
. The former employed a non-proportional GEQ design, while the latter used a subband structure requiring only scalar attenuations. Both methods jointly optimized frequency-independent and frequency-dependent parameters through composite loss functions. Specifically, besides the sparsity penalty term,
[
13
]
uses a multiscale spectral (MSS) loss, while
[
9
]
includes a distance metric on EDCs. While both approaches produced promising results, many of the design choices regarding the loss functions were somewhat arbitrary. As noted by the authors in
[
7
]
, common time–frequency representations such as the MSS, although effective for many synthesis tasks, do not fully capture the statistical properties characteristic of RIRs. EDC-based losses, while they are more perceptually valid
[
7
]
, present asymmetries around the minimum and are highly sensitive to background noise and spectral discrepancies. Moreover, although attenuation is fully determined by the strength of the attenuating elements in the feedback loop, MSS and EDC representations are influenced by variations in the frequency-independent elements, whose effects have not yet been examined.

Measured RIRs naturally contain background and measurement noise. Such noise can be reduced either by RIR truncation, where the noise-only portion of the signal is discarded
[
23
,
28
,
1
,
36
]
, or by averaging or median filtering
[
37
]
, which rely on multiple recordings of the same space. However, in the context of blind reverberation estimation based on DDSP, noise estimation and suppression, or explicit noise modeling, have received comparatively little attention. In
[
48
]
, RIRs are represented using a trained filterbank and temporal masks that are applied to shape a noise sequence, whereas in
[
25
,
13
]
, the impact of measurement noise is not addressed, and the learnable system does not model its presence. Although publicly available RIR datasets generally exhibit a high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), mixed-reality applications, particularly those using microphones embedded in head-worn devices, often involve higher levels of measurement noise and background noise that can impair the learnability of energy decay rates. However, existing research on this topic has primarily focused on enhancing spatial properties rather than addressing the noise robustness of energy decay estimators
[
53
,
11
]
.

This paper investigates the performance of different loss-function configurations for attenuation filters in FDNs. We evaluate the accuracy of the loss function’s minimum in identifying the ground-truth filter parameters and its robustness to background noise and changes in frequency-independent variables. Our work focuses explicitly on loss functions that use the EDC and MSS representations of the RIR, and we demonstrate how their accuracy varies with different noise levels. We show that accuracy in capturing ground-truth parameter values is significantly improved by matching the SNR of the target RIRs by adding synthetic noise to the estimated FDN response during loss computation. To enable visualization of the loss landscape, our study is restricted to a low-order filter design parameterized by only two variables: the reverberation time at dc and the crossover frequency. Although methods for projecting higher-dimensional loss functions onto a 2D plane exist (e.g.,
[
27
]
), we demonstrate that valuable insights can be derived from this simpler configuration. The investigation of higher-order filters is reserved for future work.

The paper is structured as follows. Sec.
2
provides background information on the FDN, and presents common loss functions used to learn its parameters. Sec.
3
describes the system configuration and methodology used to analyze the effects of parameter perturbations on the loss landscape and on performance under gradient-descent optimization. The details of these tests and their results are presented in Sec.
4
. The results are further discussed in Sec.
5
. Finally, Sec.
6
offers concluding remarks.

BACKGROUND

In this section, we present the FDN in its standard configuration, depicted in Fig.
1
, which uses proportional filters in the recursive path to model the desired energy decay. In our study, we adopt the frequency-sampling method to implement a differentiable FDN and its associated attenuation filters, and we explain the main principles of this approach here. We then discuss a limitation inherent to the FDN response, namely its coloration and sparsity, and emphasize that mitigating these issues should not conflict with the optimization of the attenuation filters. We conclude this section by outlining a straightforward approach to visualizing the loss landscape and by describing the loss functions under investigation.

2.1
Feedback Delay Network

An FDN is a recursive system consisting of delay lines, a set of gains, and a feedback matrix through which the delay-line outputs are coupled with the delay-line inputs, as shown in Fig.
1
.
The transfer function of a single-input, single-output (SISO) FDN can be written as

\displaystyle H_{\text{FDN}}(z)=\frac{Y(z)}{X(z)}=\bm{c}^{\top}\big[\bm{D_{m}}(z)^{-1}-\bm{A}(z)\big]^{-1}\bm{b}\,,

(1)

where
\bm{A}(z)
is a filter feedback matrix (FFM) consisting of an
N\times N
mixing matrix and
N
parallel filtering stages, where
N
is the number of delay lines,
\bm{D_{m}}(z)
is an
N\times N
delay matrix, vectors
\bm{b}
and
\bm{c}
are
N\times 1
column vectors of input and output gains, respectively, and the operator
(\cdot)^{\top}
denotes the transpose. The FFM is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{A}(z)=\bm{U}\bm{\Gamma}(z),

(2)

where
\bm{U}
is an
N\times N
orthogonal matrix and
\bm{\Gamma}(z)
is a diagonal attenuation matrix, whose diagonal entries are the delay-line specific attenuation filters
\Gamma_{i}(z)
.

\Gamma_{i}(z)

2.2
Modal Interpretation of the FDN Parameters

To better understand the contribution of each parameter in Eq. (
1
), Schlecht et al.
[
43
]
examined the FDN through its modal decomposition. For orthogonal
\bm{U}
, the decay of the modes is governed entirely by the attenuation filters
\bm{\Gamma}(z)
. The modal frequencies are determined by both
\bm{U}
and
\bm{A}(z)
, and statistical analyses have shown that, for randomly generated FDNs, these frequencies are nearly equidistributed
[
43
]
. Although the initial phase of the modes is generally regarded as less perceptually relevant, the distribution of their initial magnitudes, referred to as modal excitation, has been shown to correlate with perceived coloration
[
17
]
. The modal excitation is influenced by all FDN parameters except the attenuation filters
\bm{\Gamma}(z)
.
These observations imply that adjusting the attenuating elements is sufficient to control the modal decay. Yet, the available modal decomposition algorithms are prohibitively expensive, preventing their use within iterative, loss-based optimization. Instead, we can optimize modal decay using the signal’s energy decay, which depends on both modal excitation and frequency distribution, and therefore constitutes a noisier objective.

2.3
Proportional Filter Design

To ensure that all system modes decay at the same rate, corresponding to a target frequency-dependent reverberation time
T_{60}(\omega)
, the strength of the attenuation filters must be proportional to the corresponding delay lengths, that is, longer delays require stronger attenuation
[
20
]
. This proportionality allows the definition of a global per-sample attenuation that is valid regardless of the signal’s path through the feedback loop.

More specifically, for a target
T_{60}(\omega)
the prototype magnitude response of the attenuation filter is

\displaystyle|{\Gamma}_{\textrm{prot}}(z)|=10^{\frac{\gamma_{\textrm{dB}(\omega)}}{20}}=10^{-\frac{3}{f_{\textrm{s}}T_{60}(\omega)}},

(3)

where
\gamma_{\textrm{dB}}(\omega)=-60/f_{\textrm{s}}T_{60}(\omega)
denotes the per-sample attenuation in dB and
f_{\textrm{s}}
is the sampling rate.
The prototype response is then scaled according to the delay-line length
m_{i}
, resulting in a target attenuation filter for each delay line:

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{i}(z)=\bigg|10^{-\frac{\gamma_{\textrm{dB}}(\omega)}{20}}\bigg|^{m_{i}}=|{\Gamma}_{\textrm{prot}}(z)|^{m_{i}}.

(4)

This construction requires the filters to be
proportional
, i.e., filters
\Gamma_{i}(z)
must have a magnitude response in dB that can be obtained by scaling the prototype response by a frequency-independent factor. For self-similar filters, which satisfy this proportionality condition, each attenuation filter
{\Gamma}_{i}(z)
can be derived from the filter associated with the longest delay line,
m_{\textrm{max}}
, which is the most challenging filter to design in stable systems. Given
{\Gamma}_{\textrm{max}}(z)
, the filter gains can be adjusted based on the ratio
\alpha=m_{i}/m_{\textrm{max}}

[
52
]
.

2.4
Frequency Sampling for Differentiable Filters

Several techniques are available for implementing differentiable linear and time-invariant (LTI) filters. Finite impulse response (FIR) filters lend themselves naturally to convolutional-layer implementations
[
14
]
, whereas infinite impulse response (IIR) filters are more challenging because of their recursive structure and inherent stability requirements. A common approach is to implement IIR filters in the time domain using recurrent neural networks and backpropagation through time, although this strategy often results in high memory consumption. To alleviate the limitations of time-domain optimization
[
14
,
24
,
49
]
, the frequency sampling method provides an effective alternative. In this approach, an FIR approximation of the desired filter is obtained by sampling its frequency response at selected complex frequencies
[
35
,
34
]
. The sampling resolution is chosen to minimize time-aliasing distortion
[
25
,
34
]
.

The response of a real-valued LTI filter is sampled on a vector of linearly-spaced frequencies from 0 to
\pi
rad/s:

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{M}=[e^{\imath\pi\frac{0}{M-1}},e^{\imath\pi\frac{1}{M-1}},\dots,e^{\imath\pi\frac{M-2}{M-1}},e^{\imath\pi}],

(5)

where
M
is the number of frequency bins and
\imath=\sqrt{-1}
. The frequency response of a SISO IIR filter
H(z)
of order
\mathcal{{M}}
can be evaluated over
\bm{z}_{M}
as follows

\displaystyle H(\bm{z}_{M})=\frac{\text{DFT}(\mathbf{b})}{\text{DFT}(\mathbf{a})}=\frac{{b}_{H,0}+\textup{b}_{H,1}\bm{z}_{M}^{-1}+\dots+\textup{b}_{H,\mathcal{{M}}}\bm{z}_{M}^{-\mathcal{{M}}+1}}{\textup{a}_{H,0}+\textup{a}_{H,1}\bm{z}_{M}^{-1}+\dots+\textup{a}_{H,\mathcal{{M}}}\bm{z}_{M}^{-\mathcal{{M}}+1}},

(6)

where coefficients
\mathbf{b}=[b_{H,0},b_{H,1},\dots,b_{H,\mathcal{{M}}}]
and
\mathbf{a}\penalty\ =\penalty\ [a_{H,0},a_{H,1},\dots,a_{H,\mathcal{{M}}}]
are the feedforward and feedback gains of a general filter
H
, respectively.

A drawback of the frequency sampling methods is time-aliasing, which becomes relevant when the effective duration
L
of the filter impulse response (IR) exceeds the Fourier transform length
2(M-1)

[
47
,
25
,
14
]
. This issue is especially significant for lossless systems, where there are frequency points
z_{m}=e^{\imath\pi\frac{m}{M-1}}
such that
H(z_{m})=\infty
.
For lossy IIR filters, the time-aliasing error asymptotically decreases as
M
increases
[
25
]
, at the cost of higher computational cost. In
[
5
]
, the authors presented a method to mitigate distortions by sampling the transfer function outside the unit circle.

2.5
Loss Landscape

Whether the filter parameters are learned via simple gradient-based optimization or via a neural network, the underlying problem typically involves minimizing a high-dimensional, non-convex loss function by adjusting a set of parameters
\theta
, which may represent either the neural network weights or the DDSP system parameters. The convergence of such methods depends on the quality of the loss landscape, which in turn is influenced by various design choices and modeling considerations
[
27
]
.
A straightforward approach to visualize a loss function is to select two parameter sets,
\theta^{\prime}
and
\theta^{\prime\prime}
, that intersect the target parameters
\theta^{*}
, and then plot the loss values along the line connecting these points via linear interpolation
[
27
]
. This technique provides insight into the sharpness of different minima and, at higher resolutions, the smoothness of the overall loss landscape.
However, visualization is feasible only when
\theta
is at most two-dimensional, which limits its applicability to higher-dimensional loss functions and prevents the direct observation of non-convexities. Despite this constraint, in DDSP applications, this method has proven valuable for evaluating the quality of loss functions, particularly for sinusoidal frequency estimation tasks
[
46
,
16
]
. For more advanced visualization techniques, the reader is referred to the work of Li et al.
[
27
]
.

2.6
Loss Functions

The MSS loss is a widely used loss function in audio machine learning, with applications across numerous tasks, including those related to reverberation
[
48
,
25
,
3
,
50
,
13
]
. MSS addresses the inherent trade-off between time-frequency resolution present in magnitude spectrograms by incorporating multiple short-time Fourier transforms (STFTs) with varying time-frequency resolutions into a unified loss function
[
55
]
.
The MSS is composed of a spectral convergence term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SC}}
and a spectral log-magnitude term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SC}}(h,\hat{h})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lVert|{H}(t,f)|-|{\hat{H}(t,f)}|\rVert_{F}}{\lVert|{H}(t,f)|\rVert_{F}}

(7)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}(h,\hat{h})=\frac{1}{S}\lVert

\displaystyle\log(|{H}(t,f)|)-\log(|{\hat{H}(t,f)}|)\rVert_{1}\,,

(8)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\textrm{1}}
is the
\ell_{1}
norm,
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{F}
is the Frobenius norm,
|\cdot|
is the absolute value, and
S
is the number of STFT frames.
The MSS loss is defined as the average error across each of the
M_{r}
resolutions, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSS}}(h,\hat{h})=\frac{1}{M_{r}}\sum_{r=1}^{M_{r}}(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SC}}^{(r)}(h,\hat{h})+\mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}}^{(r)}(h,\hat{h}))\,.

(9)

To achieve optimal performance, it is necessary to select appropriate values for the frame size, window type, and hop size, as systematic analyses have shown that different hyperparameter configurations can significantly influence the loss
[
46
]
. However, this aspect was not further investigated in the present work.

A more common representation in artificial reverberation is the EDC, which is used to calculate
T_{60}

[
45
]
. Given
h(t)
of length
L
samples, the EDC is computed through Schroeder backward integration:

\displaystyle\varepsilon(t;f_{\textrm{bn}})=\sum_{\tau=t}^{L}h_{f_{\textrm{bn}}}^{2}(\tau)\,,

(10)

where
h_{f_{\textrm{bn}}}
is the input RIR at a frequency band with center frequency
f_{\textrm{bn}}
. It is usually reported on a decibel scale, here denoted by
\varepsilon_{\textrm{dB}}
. The EDC has been previously presented as loss function in
[
30
,
39
,
7
]
, with general form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{EDC, dB}}=\frac{\sum_{f_{\textrm{bn}}\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{t=0}^{L}(\varepsilon_{\textrm{dB}}(t;f_{\textrm{bn}})-\hat{\varepsilon}_{\textrm{dB}}(t;f_{\textrm{bn}}))^{2}}{\sum_{f_{\textrm{bn}}\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{t=0}^{L}\varepsilon_{\textrm{dB}}(t;f_{\textrm{bn}})^{2}}\,,

(11)

where
\mathcal{B}
indicates the set of frequency bands. The term in the denominator normalizes the loss with respect to the target EDC energy, stabilizing its dynamic range and making it more suitable for use in composite loss functions. This normalization rescales the gradient magnitude without altering its direction.

Delay network architectures inherently introduce coloration artifacts due to recirculating delays. To address these artifacts, the authors in
[
6
,
8
]
proposed two loss functions designed to iteratively minimize coloration while simultaneously increasing temporal density for a given set of delay lengths. The latter, referred to as sparsity loss
\mathcal{L}_{U}
, encourages a fast build-up of temporal reflection, resulting in a perceptually smooth reverberation tail
[
4
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{U}=\frac{N\sqrt{N}-\sum_{i,j}\lvert U_{i,j}\rvert}{N\left(\sqrt{N}-1\right)},

(12)

where
U_{i,j}
is the entry of matrix
\bm{U}
in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column.

3
METHOD

For the study presented in this paper, it is important to note that the system described in Eq. (
1
) does not model any sources of noise, including background or measurement noise. As a result, when FDNs are optimized on measured RIRs, a model mismatch occurs. Analyzing the effect of this mismatch on the loss landscape is therefore crucial to assess the errors it introduces.

In this section, we introduce a method for visualizing loss landscapes and assessing their variability under noisy targets, as well as when the output is subject to additional optimization objectives, such as minimizing coloration and controlling temporal density.
The loss functions used in this work are based on similarity measures between representations of the system’s IR,
\hat{h}
, and the target RIR,
h
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{J}\Bigl(g\bigl({h}(t)\bigr),g\bigl(\hat{h}(t)\bigr)\Bigr)\penalty\ ,

(13)

where
\mathcal{J}(\cdot)
denotes a chosen similarity measure, and
g(\cdot)
represents a transformation applied to the IRs, such as the STFT or the EDC, as introduced in Sec.
2.6
.
This approach corresponds to a learning paradigm that does not rely on knowledge of either the ground-truth
T_{60}
or the attenuation filter parameters.

3.2
Simulating Parameter Perturbation

When minimizing loss functions of the form in Eq. (
13
), the effect of the attenuation filters on the output cannot be fully isolated from the contribution of the frequency-independent parameters
\theta_{\text{FI}}=\{\bm{U}
,
\bm{b}
,
\bm{c}
}. Although the modal decay rates are determined uniquely by
\bm{\Gamma}(z)
, these parameters still influence modal characteristics
[
41
,
43
]
, as discussed in Sec.
2.2
. One way to stabilize the system’s response during optimization would be to fix the frequency-independent parameters. However, this approach precludes joint minimization with losses such as Eq. (
12
) and may, intuitively, lead to convergence to undesirable local minima.

To verify the intuition described above and to examine how auxiliary losses affect Eq. (
13
) through perturbations of the parameters in
\theta_{\text{FI}}
, we compute the loss landscape at different instances of
\theta_{\text{FI}}
. Fig.
3
illustrates the proposed approach, using
\bm{U}
as an example. We use the method introduced in Sec.
2.5
to compute the loss landscape between two states,
\theta_{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\prime}
and
\theta_{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\prime\prime}
, such that the target values
\theta^{*}_{\bm{\Gamma}}
fall within the interval
[\theta_{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\prime},\theta_{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\prime\prime}]
. This interval is divided into linearly or logarithmically spaced points, denoted
\theta_{\bm{\Gamma},i}
, where
i
indicates the step index. At each step
i
, the FDN IR is computed for
K
different instances of a chosen parameter, such as
\bm{U}
, whose value is drawn from a normal distribution with a constant mean and standard deviation across instances. Each instance produces an IR given by

\displaystyle h_{i,k}=\text{iDFT}\left({H_{i,k}}\right)=\text{iDFT}\left(\frac{Y_{i,k}(z)}{X(z)}\right)\,,

(17)

where iDFT indicates the inverse discrete Fourier transform, and

\displaystyle H_{i,k}(z)=\bm{c}^{\top}\big[\bm{D_{m}}(z)^{-1}-\bm{U}_{k}\bm{\Gamma}(z;\theta_{\Gamma,i})\big]^{-1}\bm{b}\,,

(18)

which is then used to compute the loss values.

3.3
Explorative Analysis

DISCUSSION

The results reported above show that explicitly modeling background noise in the reverb model (here, the FDN) improves the accuracy of the estimated attenuation filter. Moreover, in our experiments,
noise-aware
configurations (tests 3 and 4) converged faster than the noise-unaware ones (tests 1 and 2): on average, tests 3 and 4 required respectively 17.51 and 30.94 epochs to reach the early-stopping criterion, compared with 32.38 and 35.86 epochs for tests 1 and 2. We interpret this as noise acting to regularize the optimization landscape: modeling the noise reduces systematic mismatch between model and measurements, which yields faster, more stable parameter updates. When both parameter sets are tuned jointly, however, the increased dimensionality of the optimization problem causes the loss to require more iterations to settle.

Because measured RIRs typically exhibit lowpass behavior, background noise can cover large portions of the STFT (see Fig.
4
), increasing the density of errors in frequency–time bins that are less perceptually relevant. Allowing frequency-independent parameters to vary during optimization (test 3) mitigates this effect by giving the model additional freedom to balance the spectral energy.

The EDC-based representation benefits strongly from incorporating noise into the model: tests 3 and 4 show consistent improvements in EDC estimation with little difference between linear and logarithmic scales. Conversely, in Tests 1 and 2, the linear scale performed better, a result due to the logarithmic transform disproportionately amplifying differences near the noise floor, which consequently yielded greater errors when the target reached the noise floor. Overall, the linear EDC scale down-weights differences in low-amplitude regions (such as the noise floor), which makes it more robust when background noise is present.

In this study, we used a low-order low-pass shelving filter, which yields smooth transitions between octave bands and motivates the use of one-octave resolution in the EDC. For more complex filter designs, such as GEQ and PEQ, a higher frequency resolution in the loss function is advisable to capture spectral nuances. For even higher frequency resolutions, the energy decay relief (EDR)
[
19
]
can be used. The EDR is based on the Schroeder backward integration (Eq.
10
) of the STFT rather than the impulse response. We expect the results to be similar to those obtained with EDC-based losses, given the commutative nature of the operations.

In these experiments, we deliberately design the noise
w_{2}
based on the SNR level used when synthesizing the target response. In a practical scenario, one would need to employ a technique to estimate the noise floor from a measured RIR
[
1
]
. Alternatively, a noise sequence with learnable spectral energy could be incorporated into the reverberation model. A detailed study of the training framework required for such an approach is left for future work.

Taken together, these findings indicate that (1) including a background-noise model is important both for accuracy and for faster convergence, (2) in
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{EDC}}
the choice of the scale (linear vs. logarithmic) affects robustness to the noise floor, and (3) in
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{MSS}}
allowing some additional degrees of freedom in the model might reduce errors introduced by noise.

6
CONCLUSION

The present study analyzes the automatic tuning of FDN elements to match the decay of a target RIR, with a focus on the parameters of a low-order attenuation filter, namely the decay time at dc and the crossover frequency, and investigates their sensitivity to background noise. Two losses that are commonly used in reverberation synthesis and FDN optimization are examined: the energy decay curve (EDC) loss and the multi-scale spectral (MSS) loss.

Evaluation of loss profiles reveals the differences between the EDC and MSS when used on signals on linear and decibel scale. In the noiseless case, the losses’ global minima point to the correct parameter values, but when the target RIR contains a background noise term, the global minima are significantly shifted, leading to erroneous results in both optimized parameters. Computing loss values on the dB scale emphasizes the error compared to the linear scale. The study shows that adding a noise term to the FDN IR to match the target SNR alleviates this problem in both EDC and MSS losses.

The manuscript reveals that perturbations in the non-recursive elements of the FDN also affect the parameter optimization. The input gains
\bm{b}
have the largest impact on the differences between target and obtained parameters, and MSS loss is generally more affected by such perturbations than the EDC loss.

The results presented in this reveal potential sources of error in gradient-based FDN parameter tuning. This will lead to more robust and accurate reverb matching and synthesis, and will contribute to increased reproducibility of FDN optimization.
