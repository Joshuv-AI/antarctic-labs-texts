Dual-opioid spinal anaesthesia in enhanced recovery after caesarean section: effects on postoperative opioid requirement and maternal recovery
Authors: Giulia Fierro, Luisa Patanè, Giorgio Pagani, Barbara Milan, Elena Buelli, Laura Ongaro, Santa Maria Barresi, Marco Carnelli, Ferdinando Luca Lorini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimisation of post-caesarean delivery and perioperative outcomes have emerged as a pivotal concern in obstetrics. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of implementing Enhanced Recovery After Caesarean Section (ERAC) combined with a pre-existing dual-opioid subarachnoid anaesthesia technique on the decrease in rescue opioid doses needed for acute pain management after caesarean delivery. The secondary aim was to compare the postoperative outcomes before and after the implementation of the ERAC pathway.MethodsWe conducted an observational, retrospective single-centre study involving 322 pregnant women, 156 pre-ERAC, and 166 post-ERAC. The eligible women were > 18 years old, without invasive placental disorders, no prior abdominal surgery with longitudinal laparotomy, and no disease. All women received subarachnoid anaesthesia with sufentanil and morphine, in addition to local anaesthetic and postoperative analgesia with paracetamol and ketorolac. Acute pain assessment was performed with a numeric pain rating scale (NRS); if the NRS was ≥ 4, a rescue dose of tramadol was administered in both groups.ResultsSignificant reductions in the need for rescue opioid analgesia were observed in the ERAC group, with logistic regression confirming an 84.5% reduction on postoperative day one. Additionally, outcomes, including early mobilisation, spontaneous diuresis, and return of gastrointestinal function, improved. The ERAC group also demonstrated a higher rate of discharge on the second postoperative day.ConclusionsThe findings of our study suggest that implementing the ERAC strategy with a pre-existing dual-opioid subarachnoid anaesthesia technique may be associated with reduced opioid requirements following caesarean delivery and to improved maternal outcomes.Keywords: Caesarean delivery, ERAC, Multimodal analgesia, Opioid-sparing, Pain relief, Spinal anaesthesia

Introduction

Caesarean delivery, the most common surgical procedure worldwide, is performed more than a million times annually in the USA alone [1]. Despite advancements in the safety of caesarean deliveries, major surgical interventions pose inherent risks to mothers and neonates, including extended hospital stays, delayed recuperation, and increased healthcare costs. Consequently, optimisation of post-caesarean delivery and perioperative outcomes have emerged as a pivotal concern in obstetrics.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) is a comprehensive perioperative care strategy aimed at mitigating surgical stress and opioid use, improving outcomes, and shortening hospital stays [2]. Although ERAS protocols have transformed recovery across various surgical domains, their extension into obstetrics, particularly caesarean delivery, marks a pioneering step towards better maternal outcomes [3].

The central objectives of Enhanced Recovery After Caesarean Section (ERAC) include effective pain management, early mobilisation, and reduced duration of hospitalisation [4–9].

As evidenced by previous research, the transformation of acute pain into chronic pain represents an increasing public health issue. Chronic pain at three months after caesarean delivery is especially relevant to obstetrics because it affects as many as one in four mothers [10].

Acute postsurgical pain is one of the primary causes of chronic postsurgical pain after caesarean delivery. This connection has been substantiated by studies that have identified associations between chronic pain and elevated pain scores after delivery and increased analgesic consumption during the first 48 h [11].

The ERAC protocols provide improved management of maternal postoperative pain using a multimodal approach [6, 12]. Current ERAC guidelines and the PROSPECT guideline recommend the use of intrathecal opioids along with regional blocks, wound infusions of local anaesthetics, and multiple non-opioid analgesics [6, 12, 13]. It has been shown that the use of ERAC effectively reduces the morphine equivalent in the postoperative period [14].

Nonetheless, it is important to note that oral or intravenous postoperative opioids still play a significant role in the overall postoperative analgesic strategy within the ERAC framework [14–17], and as many as 41% of mothers still require opioids within 24 h of discharge [14]. Therefore, there is a considerable scope for improvement.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of implementing ERAC with a pre-existing dual-opioid subarachnoid anaesthesia technique on reducing the requirement for rescue opioid doses for acute pain management after caesarean delivery.

The secondary aim was to compare the postoperative outcomes between pre- and post-implementation of the ERAC pathway.

Materials and methods

This observational, retrospective single-centre study was conducted on pregnant women undergoing caesarean delivery at the Obstetric Department of ASST Papa Giovanni XXIII (Bergamo, Italy), a tertiary care referral centre in northern Italy. Between 1 January 2022 and 31 December 2022, we assessed the effects of ERAC on maternal outcomes before and after its implementation, following the STROBE guidelines [18]. Eligible women were those aged > 18 years without invasive placental disorders, who had never undergone abdominal surgery with longitudinal laparotomy, including caesarean delivery, and had no history of maternal disease. The pre-ERAC group consisted of patients enrolled immediately before the ERAC pathway introduction, from 1 January to 31 July 2022. The ERAC protocol was initiated for all patients admitted between 1 August and 31 December 2022 and was aligned with recommendations from the Society for Obstetric Anesthesia and Perinatology [12]. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committee of ASST Papa Giovanni XXIII (reg. 140/24). All patients provided informed consent for both anaesthesia and surgery.

All enrolled women received subarachnoid anaesthesia. Lumbar puncture was performed in asepsis with the patient in a sitting position, and a 25-G spinal needle was introduced into the lumbar subarachnoid space. Each patient received local anaesthetic (either hyperbaric bupivacaine 10 mg or levobupivacaine 12.5 mg in pre-ERAC group and levobupivacaine 12.5 mg in ERAC group) with sufentanil 2.5 μg and morphine 100 μg and postoperative analgesia with paracetamol and ketorolac. Postoperative respiratory monitoring and sedation assessment were conducted for 12 h in the delivery room, during the 2 h after caesarean delivery by a dedicated midwife, and afterward in the obstetrics ward [19].

Acute pain assessment was performed every 4 h until discharge of the patient using a numeric pain rating scale (NRS; an 11-point scale ranging from 0 = no pain to 10 = worst pain imaginable). If the NRS score was ≥ 4, a rescue dose of 100 mg tramadol was administered intravenously or orally in both the pre-ERAC and ERAC groups. Caesarean delivery was performed using either uterine exteriorisation or in situ repair, according to the surgeon’s preference and intraoperative conditions. Both techniques were employed in the pre-ERAC and ERAC groups. All procedures were performed by the same surgical team, whose operative practices remained consistent over time.

The main aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of implementing ERAC with a pre-existing dual-opioid subarachnoid anaesthesia technique on decreasing the need for rescue doses required for acute pain management after caesarean delivery.

Secondary objectives included the assessment of various postoperative outcomes such as nausea, vomiting, mobilisation, bladder catheter removal within 6 h after caesarean delivery, spontaneous diuresis, return of gastrointestinal function, total blood loss, rate of readmission, rate of opioid prescription at discharge, and rate of patient discharge on day two.

Statistical analysis

A power analysis was conducted prior to data collection to determine the minimum sample size required to detect a 50% reduction in the rescue dose, starting from 25%, with a power of 80% and an alpha of 0.05. The analysis revealed that each group required a minimum of 152 participants. Data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test of normality. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), whereas categorical variables are presented as n (%). Pearson’s coefficient or Spearman’s rho was used to determine correlations between continuous variables. Categorical variables were analysed using Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test, whereas continuous variables were compared using the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test.

Multivariable adjustment was pre-specified exclusively for the primary outcome. BMI was the only variable differing significantly between groups at baseline and was therefore included as the sole covariate in the logistic regression model.

Secondary outcomes related to protocol implementation were analysed using univariate methods, as they primarily reflect adherence to protocol components rather than independent clinical effects and therefore were not considered appropriate for confounder adjustment. The measure of association used is the odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals, as reported in Table 3. Formal checks of model assumptions, including assessment of multicollinearity among covariates, were performed. Given that only one covariate (BMI) was included in the model, multicollinearity was not a concern. Model fit was considered adequate based on the observed sample size relative to the number of events.

We employed rigorous eligibility criteria and standardised interventions to minimise potential biases inherent in observational studies. Furthermore, our analysis included statistical adjustments for baseline differences and power analysis to ensure an adequate sample size and enhance the reliability of the study. Additionally, we employed a post-hoc power analysis to offer further context and exploratory insights into the reliability of our findings for secondary outcomes. This approach was intended to augment our understanding of the study's power based on observed effect sizes, rather than serving as a definitive tool for validating these outcomes. The post-hoc power analysis was conducted independently of the a priori sample size calculation, with the specific purpose of verifying whether the observed sample provided adequate power to detect the effect sizes actually observed for secondary outcomes; it was not intended as a validation tool for the primary endpoint but as an exploratory assessment of precision for secondary analyses.

Results

In total, 322 women were included in the analysis: 156 in the pre-ERAC cohort and 166 in the ERAC cohort (Fig. 2). The proportion of missing data was below 2.5% for all variables included in the analysis; therefore, complete-case analysis was considered appropriate.

The need for a rescue opioid dose was significantly lower in the ERAC group on day zero (2.45% vs. 7.74%, p = 0.039), day one (2.47% vs. 13.50%, p < 0.001), and day two (0.62% vs. 5.19%, p = 0.016).

Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that ERAC was significantly associated with an 84.5% reduction in the need for a rescue dose on day one; no significant correlation was observed on day zero or day two (Table 3).

No differences were noted in terms of nausea, vomiting, or total blood loss, whereas the ERAC group displayed significantly higher rates of mobilisation within 6 h after caesarean delivery, spontaneous diuresis, and return of gastrointestinal function. On day two following caesarean delivery, 60.1% of the women in the ERAC group were discharged, with a statistically significant difference compared with the pre-ERAC group (p < 0.001; Table 4).

Discussion

Our investigation into the ERAC protocol revealed a significant improvement in post-caesarean pain management. The demand for rescue opioid doses in the ERAC group was less than 2.5%.

This reduction suggests a clinically relevant benefit in the obstetric setting. If pain is well controlled, maternal mobilisation is facilitated, and exposure to opioids is minimised, which is particularly important for reducing potential neonatal exposure during the early postpartum period.

The ERAC pathway had a significant impact on reducing the need for rescue doses on day one. Statistical analyses, including logistic regression adjusted for BMI, revealed significant correlations. Implementing early oral intake, which accelerates the return of normal gastrointestinal function, early mobilisation, and removal of the bladder catheter helped to improve the effectiveness of multimodal analgesia in accordance with ERAS Society guideline and the recommendations of the Society for Obstetric Anesthesia and Perinatology [4–6, 12]. Effective pain control on day one improved mothers’ ability to care for their newborns, thereby improving neonatal safety.

Logistic regression analysis did not establish a link between need for rescue doses on days zero and two and ERAC. While day one showed a robust significant reduction, the study was likely underpowered to reach the same level of precision for day zero and day two, requiring caution in interpreting the magnitude of the effect for those days.

Although the analysis suggests a potential effect on day zero, the imprecision of the estimate warrants cautious interpretation of its magnitude. For day two, the confidence interval crossed unity, indicating that the study may not have been sufficiently powered to detect a clinically meaningful difference at this time point.

Rescue dose requirements were low in both pre-ERAC and ERAC groups compared to the data reported in a recent study by Cojocaru et al. [1]. Likewise, no significant differences between pre-ERAC and ERAC groups regarding immediate postoperative opioid use were reported in another study in which all patients received neuraxial anaesthesia with morphine and the same intraoperative analgesics [21].

Adequate intraoperative pain management can prevent the development of pain sensitisation caused by surgical trauma, which is one of the main determinants of postoperative pain [22]. Intrathecal morphine at doses of 100 µg is highly recommended for pain management in patients undergoing caesarean delivery [13]. This hydrophilic molecule has a slow onset, typically between 30 and 60 min, and caesarean delivery is performed before morphine reaches peak effectiveness, often relying exclusively on local anaesthetics for spinal anaesthesia. This practice may account for the considerable incidence of intraoperative pain. Braga et al. reported that 29% of patients required lidocaine via an epidural catheter for pain control when spinal morphine alone was added to hyperbaric bupivacaine [23]. Intrathecal morphine instead provides analgesia for over 24 h [24, 25].

Our procedure involved the combination of local anaesthetics, intrathecal morphine, and sufentanil. We also used i.v. dexamethasone after delivery as an adjuvant, which has demonstrated improvement in pain scores and reduced opioid consumption [13].

From a pharmacodynamic perspective, sufentanil has a very high binding affinity to the mu opioid receptor; the Ki value of sufentanil is 0.138 nM compared with 1.168 nM for morphine, 1.346 nM for fentanyl, and 0.216 nM for buprenorphine [26]. Intrathecal sufentanil combined with local anaesthetics allows for rapid pain relief, enabling surgical incision within minutes even before total muscle block, and sustained pain relief for 4 h in the postoperative phase [27, 28]. Additionally, it reduces the required local anaesthetic dose, thereby minimising the risk of hypotension commonly associated with spinal anaesthesia, and effectively addresses visceral pain, ensuring patient comfort during procedures such as uterine manipulation or foetal extraction [29]. An important intraoperative item for improved mother and newborn outcomes is minimising hypotension during subarachnoid anaesthesia [12]. The available data support the use of vasopressors. In our ERAC pathway, we used phenylephrine bolus or ephedrine to prevent excessive bradycardia [30], which can be induced by mu receptor agonists.

The secondary objective of this study was to compare postoperative outcomes before and after the implementation of the ERAC pathway. Less than 10% of the women in both groups experienced nausea and vomiting, and we did not find a statistically significant difference in the postoperative period between the two groups. The absence of preoperative fasting and early feeding in the immediate puerperium did not seem to increase the incidence of intra- or postoperative nausea and vomiting.

The occurrence of spontaneous diuresis within 6 h following the bladder catheter removal, together with early mobilisation and return of gastrointestinal function, showed a statistically significant difference in the ERAC group. These variables should be considered protocol-driven process measures and interpreted as indicators of adherence to the ERAC pathway rather than independent clinical outcomes. Their improvement in the ERAC group reflects successful implementation of protocol components aimed at enhancing recovery. The active role of midwives on the ward likely contributed to the consistent implementation of early mobilisation within the ERAC pathway. However, successful implementation of early mobilisation depends on adequate pain control.

The final outcome measure was hospital discharge. In the pre-ERAC group, 98.7% of the patients were discharged on the third day. Patients in the ERAC group were allowed to go home once their clinical condition was considered favourable and were subject to the mother’s consent. Our data revealed that 60.1% of the mothers were discharged as early as on the second day. Voluntary discharge emphasises good health and minimal pain, as none of the patients were discharged with opioid prescriptions at home [31], and no patients were readmitted for complications.

We cannot exclude that, even in pre-ERAC, some women could have been discharged within 48 h after caesarean delivery. However, given the absence of early mobilisation, the lack of a structured recovery pathway, and the institutional norm of day-three discharge, it is unlikely that a substantial proportion would have been discharged within 48 h.

In our hospital, patients in ERAC group were always discharged without an opioid prescription at home if they had not requested an opioid rescue dose in the 24 h prior to discharge [21].

Undoubtedly, Italian society has a propensity to use anti-inflammatory drugs over opioids; however, the fact that only one patient has needed second-day opioid rescue doses since the ERAC protocol was introduced underlines how effective our protocol is in significantly reducing the demand for opioids.

ERAS studies include compliance metrics to demonstrate that the protocol has been followed [32]. The interdisciplinary team at our hospital selected a limited number of indicators for detection and analysis as part of the ERAC implementation (Fig. 1B). We had high observance (> 75% compliance) in intraoperative indicators because many of these indicators existed before the ERAC implementation. In contrast, compliance was moderate (25–75%) for preoperative indicators and moderate/high for the postoperative indicators analysed. Moderate compliance might have been observed because the data were collected within the first six months of protocol implementation [30].

Our study has some limitations. First, it was a retrospective study, meaning that the analysis relied on historical data and medical records. This approach could introduce selection bias, as the available data may not accurately represent a completely random sample of the larger population. Although the proportion of missing data was low (< 2.5% for all variables), the retrospective design remains inherently susceptible to information bias. Second, the study was monocentric, which may limit the generalisability of our findings.

In addition, the pre/post study design is inherently susceptible to temporal bias. Changes over time, such as organisational modifications, seasonal variation in case mix, and staff learning curve effects, might have influenced the observed outcomes independently of the ERAC implementation. However, several aspects of our study may have mitigated this risk, including a consistent surgical team, stable operative practices throughout the study period, and the inclusion of both groups within the same calendar year, thereby limiting exposure to major temporal confounders.

We did not investigate maternal satisfaction or potential long-term effects such as the development of chronic pain, which may have resulted from the implementation of our protocol. In addition, we now clarify that although the study detected significant differences in the primary outcome, the precision of the estimates—especially for postoperative day zero—remains limited. Based on our findings, a larger sample size (e.g. > 300 patients per group) would likely be required to more definitively assess the magnitude of the effect at this time point.

Despite these limitations, the consecutive inclusion of all eligible patients and the use of multivariate regression modelling were intended to mitigate, although not eliminate, potential sources of bias inherent to the observational design.

In conclusion, in this cohort study, we describe how the ERAC strategy after caesarean delivery under subarachnoid anaesthesia with morphine and sufentanil in addition to local anaesthetics may be associated with reduced postoperative opioid requirements and enhances maternal recovery, representing a critical advancement in obstetric care. The implications of our findings extend beyond immediate postoperative care, potentially leading to a shift in pain management after caesarean delivery.

Future prospective, multi-centre studies are warranted to confirm our findings and improve their generalisability.
