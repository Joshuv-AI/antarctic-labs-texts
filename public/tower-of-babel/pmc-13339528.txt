Strengthening Pharmacovigilance Reporting for Withdrawal Reactions to Support Deprescribing
Authors: Zakir Khan, Frank Moriarty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPharmacovigilance plays a crucial role in post‐marketing surveillance of medicines and relies on the spontaneous reporting of adverse drug reactions (ADRs). Discontinuation of a drug can cause clinically relevant signs and symptoms, including withdrawal reactions; however, this subset of ADRs is not fully accommodated in current pharmacovigilance practice. We discuss proposed revisions to current ADR forms that could enhance the capture of withdrawal reaction evidence.

PHARMACOVIGILANCE SYSTEMS AND GUIDANCE

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines pharmacovigilance as “the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug‐related problem.” 1 Pharmacovigilance plays an important role in post‐marketing drug safety surveillance and relies on spontaneous reporting of adverse drug reactions (ADRs, noxious, unintended, and undesirable effects of a drug at a normal therapeutic dose) to inform regulatory actions and clinical practice. 1 , 2

According to the World Health Organization‐Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‐UMC) guidelines, relevant ADRs, that is, any suspected reaction that may affect patient safety or require clinical management, should be reported. 3 This includes all suspected reactions for new drugs, including minor reactions, as well as all serious or unexpected reactions for established drugs. Relevant ADRs also include drug–drug, drug‐food, or drug–food supplement interactions, including those due to herbal and complementary products, and reactions related to drug abuse or drug use during pregnancy and lactation. Additionally, ADRs resulting from overdose or medication errors, those associated with drug withdrawal, reactions due to lack of efficacy, and suspected pharmaceutical defects are considered relevant. 3 The WHO‐UMC guidelines specify core sections of ADR forms, including patient demographics, details of the suspected medicinal product(s), a description of the suspected reaction, clinical outcomes, and reporter details. 3 Member countries adapt this reporting guidance to their national context, and generally follow the same WHO‐UMC specification of core data elements and documentation standards for ADR reporting. Additional details on the global pharmacovigilance system and guidance are provided in Supplemental Material Document .

In addition to WHO‐UMC guidance, pharmacovigilance practices are also strongly influenced by regulatory authorities involved in the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) process, which play a central role in the development and interpretation of regulatory standards. 4 These include regional regulatory bodies (i.e., European Medicines Agency, the Pan American Health Organization, the Gulf Health Council) as well as national agencies such as the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency, the United states Food and Drug Administration, Japan's Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency, Health Canada, Singapore's Health Sciences Authority, and Australia's Therapeutic Goods Administration. 4

WITHDRAWAL REACTIONS AFTER STOPPING MEDICATIONS

Discontinuation of a drug can lead to clinically relevant signs and symptoms, which can be referred to as adverse drug withdrawal events (ADWEs). 5 , 6 , 7 They can be categorized into withdrawal reactions/physiological withdrawal reactions (occurring due to physiological adaptation following prolonged exposure to a drug which typically have an immediate onset and are often short term) including rebound phenomena (short‐term return of original symptoms with a greater intensity due to drug‐induced physiological changes). Withdrawal reactions are one form of ADWE, which also includes the return of the condition that was being treated (often a sustained response, due to reemergence of the original illness or disease). 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Withdrawal reactions can range from mild (e.g., mild dyspepsia and heartburn after withdrawal of proton pump inhibitors) 12 to severe (e.g., seizure recurrence or status epilepticus after withdrawal of antiepileptic drugs or suicidal ideation after discontinuation of antidepressants), 13 , 14 sometimes requiring the need for hospitalization 9 , 15 and may also be fatal. 16 , 17

Withdrawal reactions, including rebound phenomena, fit within the definition of ADRs (classified by the WHO's adverse reaction terminology as type E reactions—withdrawal/end of use), 18 as they represent harmful, unintended, and undesirable effects caused by prior drug exposure at normal doses after discontinuation. In contrast, the return of the condition reflects the absence of the drug's effect, and thus is not strictly an ADR. These types are often discussed together in clinical practice but have different implications for pharmacovigilance signal detection and causality assessment.

Withdrawal reactions may occur in multiple clinical contexts, including abrupt discontinuation of a medication, switching therapies, medication nonadherence, and planned deprescribing. Deprescribing (the process of reducing or stopping unnecessary or harmful medications) is one of several scenarios in which withdrawal reactions may occur. 8 , 19 The pharmacovigilance system has developed to support safe prescribing of medication; however, deprescribing is an increasing area of focus which has not been explicitly considered in the pharmacovigilance system. 19 Deprescribing fits within good prescribing practice, as it involves reviewing the ongoing need for medicines on each occasion it is being prescribed again, and reducing or stopping where appropriate. 20 , 21 Implementing deprescribing, where appropriate and aligned with patient goals, can reduce inappropriate polypharmacy to address medication harm. 19 , 22 However, its implementation into routine practice is impeded by barriers, including clinician and patient fear of adverse consequences, and a perceived lack of evidence on the effects of stopping individual medications. 19 One key aspect of this is withdrawal reactions.

GAPS IN PHARMACOVIGILANCE GUIDANCE FOR WITHDRAWAL REACTIONS REPORTING

However, despite some withdrawal reactions being a form of ADR, explicit guidance that these should be reported is lacking, which may in particular lead to underreporting by clinicians and patients in routine clinical practice. The WHO‐UMC guidance on detecting and reporting ADRs briefly mentions in the “What should be reported” section that suspected ADRs associated with drug withdrawals should be reported; however, it does not specify which types of withdrawal effects or reactions are included. 3 Moreover, ADR reporting forms have no designated section for capturing ADWEs‐related information. This may unintentionally exclude ADWEs from the scope of reporting and creates ambiguity as to whether both categories of ADWEs, or only physiological withdrawal reactions should be reported within pharmacovigilance systems.

Proposed revisions to current ADR forms would be beneficial to more effectively accommodate and capture reliable withdrawal reaction data, which would strengthen the evidence base to support deprescribing. The proposed modifications are outlined below:

Potential modifications to structured reporting fields

ADR forms also include fields for suspect and other medication details, include drug name, dose and frequency, and the start and end dates of treatment. The medication start and end date fields remain applicable for withdrawal reactions but require clarification: as withdrawal reactions happens after the drug has been stopped (the treatment end date is expected to occur before the start date of the reaction), which differs from typical ADRs where reactions often start during treatment. However, withdrawal reactions may also occur during tapering of a medication (i.e., gradual reduction with the intention of stopping). As it is currently not possible to indicate whether a medication was being tapered, the existing drug dose and frequency fields in ADR forms could be adapted to capture withdrawal strategies (e.g., tapering vs. abrupt cessation) for withdrawal reactions.

ADR reporting forms currently include section on “action taken with the drug” (e.g., dose decreased, dose increased, drug withdrawn, not applicable), which are useful for documenting the clinical outcome of reactions and the actions taken with respect to the suspected drug. However, this information alone is insufficient to fully characterize the management of withdrawal reactions. Specifically, information on whether the drug was restarted to resolve symptoms is not currently captured. To address this gap, the existing “action taken with the drug” field could be modified to include an additional option: “drug restarted after withdrawal.” Modifications to structured reporting fields should be implemented by regulatory authorities and national pharmacovigilance centers responsible for individual case safety report form design.

MedDRA terminology and coding refinements

Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities Terminology (MedDRA) preferred terms (PTs) are widely used in pharmacovigilance databases to classify and report the type and signs/symptoms of reactions in ADR forms. MedDRA includes a limited set of terms related to drug withdrawal, which may contribute to under‐recognition and misclassification of withdrawal reaction. Many of the PTs relating to withdrawal events indicate only that the reaction occurred after drug discontinuation, without providing information on the specific signs/symptoms, or nature of the reaction (i.e., whether it is a physiological withdrawal reaction or a return of the condition). This differs from typical ADR reporting, where PTs usually describe the specific signs and symptoms (e.g., insomnia, hypertension, dizziness, anxiety). Clearer guidance and potentially modification of current ADR forms could more effectively capture this information. One approach could be to expand the number of MedDRA PTs for withdrawal events to specify those that relate to specific signs/symptoms. Alternatively, withdrawal reaction could be captured independently of PTs, for example, through a dedicated field specifying whether a reaction was related to prescribing, or due to deprescribing or drug withdrawal. The specific signs and symptoms of such reactions, recorded as PTs, could then be described. These modifications would allow more precise characterization and detailed documentation of the clinical characteristics of withdrawal reaction. Therefore, the MedDRA Maintenance and Support Services Organization (MSSO) under the coordination of the ICH should consider potential refinements to MedDRA terminology.

Guidance on how withdrawal reactions should be interpreted in pharmacovigilance practice

Current ADR reporting forms include fields to document “Dechallenge” (whether a reaction to the suspect medication abated after drug discontinuation or dose reduction) and “Rechallange” (whether a reaction reappeared after reintroduction of the suspect medication). While these processes are used to support causality assessment for ADRs resulting from prescribing, these fields may be ambiguous when applied to withdrawal reactions occurring after drug discontinuation. To assess causality for withdrawal reactions, the equivalent actions would be reintroducing the suspect medication that was discontinued to see if the reaction resolves, and then reattempting discontinuation to assess if the same reaction occurs. To address the limited applicability of these fields to reactions due to discontinuations, the existing dechallenge and rechallenge fields can be adapted to explicitly describe how these terms apply to withdrawal reaction reporting. For example, that dechallenge be considered as attempting to deprescribe the medication again following a withdrawal reaction, with rechallenge being reintroduction of the medication following a withdrawal reaction. Guidance on how to interpret withdrawal reactions in pharmacovigilance practice should be developed and disseminated by regulatory authorities, pharmacovigilance centers, with input from marketing authorization holders (who are legally required to establish and operate pharmacovigilance systems to ensure continuous monitoring of medicinal safety throughout the product lifecycle).

CONCLUSION

Withdrawal reactions are a clinically important subset of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) that are unintentionally under‐recognized within current pharmacovigilance systems. Some regulators have taken action relating to drug‐class‐specific withdrawal reactions, indicating increased focus on these events; however, further system change to generate actionable evidence on withdrawal reactions is needed. Although existing ADR reporting forms can capture reliable data on typical ADRs, they have several deficiencies which limit the potential to document withdrawal reactions‐specific information, including the symptoms/signs involved, types, actions taken for management, and attempted dechallenge or rechallenge. Therefore, more explicit guidance on the reporting of withdrawal reactions via pharmacovigilance systems should be developed, accompanied by appropriate modifications to ADR reporting forms, to ultimately enhance evidence relating to deprescribing medications and support enhanced patient care. These modifications would support accurate withdrawal reaction reporting, build enhanced evidence on the effects of deprescribing, and ultimately strengthen pharmacovigilance systems, inform clinical decision making, and support deprescribing practices and patient safety. However, it is important to consider potential implementation challenges. Expanded reporting requirements could increase the burden on healthcare professionals, potentially leading to longer submission times or incomplete data entry. Therefore, any modifications should balance the goal of improved data quality with feasibility in routine clinical practice.
