RANKL inhibits macrophage proinflammatory Toll-like receptor 2 and 4 signaling and impairs killing of intracellular bacteria
Authors: Clara D Si, Christopher T Peek, Sana R Fatah, Andrew J Beaudoin, Anton R Zhelonkin, Kara R Eichelberger, Stacy L Hahn, Robert O Watson, Denis A Mogilenko, James E Cassat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonocyte-macrophage lineage cells, crucial components of the innate immune system, can uniquely form bone-resorbing osteoclasts upon exposure to the cytokine receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) in the bone microenvironment. Recent studies have also begun to uncover extensive extraskeletal roles of RANKL. However, how monocyte-macrophage lineage cells respond to RANKL outside of the bone, and the impact that this signaling pathway exerts on the host immune response, is not fully understood. In this study, we sought to define how RANKL exposure shapes the macrophage inflammatory response to pathogens by using the model intracellular bacterium Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium, which coopts macrophages to cause life-threatening infections. We found that exposing both mouse and human macrophages to subosteoclastogenic levels of RANKL increased intracellular Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium burdens and decreased proinflammatory cytokine production. RNA sequencing revealed downregulation of pattern recognition receptor signaling pathways in RANKL-treated macrophages during the early stages of infection. Therefore, we hypothesized that RANKL impairs pattern recognition receptor–dependent signaling pathways that are important for proinflammatory cytokine production. We discovered that RANKL-treated macrophages exhibit reduced nuclear factor κB and interferon regulatory factor 3 activation, specifically in response to Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) and TLR4 stimulation. We determined that prior RANKL exposure decreases abundance of the TLR2 and TLR4 adaptor proteins TRAM (TRIF-related adaptor molecule) and TIRAP (TIR domain–containing adaptor protein). Together, these data suggest that RANKL exposure negatively impacts the macrophage TLR-mediated inflammatory response to bacteria.Keywords: innate immunity, macrophages, RANKL, Salmonella Typhimurium, TLR signaling

Introduction

Monocytes and macrophages play vital roles as innate immune sentinels and regulators of organ homeostasis. In the skeleton, monocyte-macrophage lineage precursors are central to homeostatic bone remodeling through differentiation into bone-resorbing osteoclasts. The key regulator of osteoclastogenesis is receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL), a tumor necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily cytokine.1 Binding of RANKL to the RANK receptor on monocyte-macrophage lineage cells triggers extensive transcriptional reprogramming and epigenetic changes that support expression of genes required for osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption.2 Dysregulated RANK/RANKL signaling results in aberrant bone remodeling; therefore, the RANKL signaling network is tightly regulated. Clinically, therapeutic interventions against RANKL are effective for treating skeletal conditions such as osteoporosis and cancer-related bone diseases.2

Although RANKL is best known for its role in bone remodeling, accumulating evidence suggests that the RANK/RANKL pathway also plays pivotal roles in immune tolerance, secondary lymphoid organogenesis, intestinal microfold cell differentiation, and other extraskeletal physiology.3,4 RANKL is widely distributed throughout the body, with high messenger RNA (mRNA) expression in the peripheral lymph nodes, thymus, and lung and low expression in the bone marrow, spleen, skeletal muscle, thyroid, peripheral blood, and heart.5–7 The RANKL protein is abundant in the lymph nodes, and can be moderately detected in the bone marrow, spleen, and other lymphoid tissues.6–8 However, the level of RANKL detection does not linearly correlate with its biological function, as its activity is greatly influenced by membrane-bound versus soluble isoforms, the presence of costimulatory signals, and the abundance and localization of its decoy receptor, osteoprotegerin.4,9,10 Given the profound impact of RANKL signaling on monocyte-macrophage lineage cells in the skeleton, it is possible that macrophages encountering RANKL in other organs might also undergo cellular reprogramming in response to this cytokine. RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis is known to downregulate transcription factors that are critical immune regulators, such as interferon regulatory factor 8 (IRF8).11,12 Yet, how RANKL shapes the coordinated antimicrobial and inflammatory programs mounted by macrophages against invading microorganisms has not been defined. Previous studies have observed protection from endotoxic shock in mice following RANKL administration, establishing a potential role for RANKL as an innate immune modulator.13,14 The effects of RANKL signaling on macrophages may be especially consequential for pathogens that have adopted intracellular lifestyles within macrophages, such as Salmonella spp., Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Listeria monocytogenes, as their fate depends heavily on sustained macrophage programs for both replication and control.

During infection, recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns by a repertoire of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) is a key event that initiates the macrophage innate immune response. Among the PRRs, the Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are some of the best characterized, comprising 10 members (TLR1–TLR10) in humans and 12 (TLR1–TLR9, TLR11–TLR13) in mice.15 Each TLR contains a leucine-rich repeat domain that identifies PAMPs, a transmembrane region, and a Toll/IL-1 receptor (TIR) domain that facilitates signal transduction. Successful signal transduction also relies on the recruitment of TIR domain–containing adaptors. Broadly, depending on adaptor recruitment, TLR signaling is divided into the myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88)–dependent and MyD88-independent pathways. TLR adaptor proteins include MyD88, TIR domain–containing adaptor protein (TIRAP) (also known as MyD88-adaptor-like), TIR domain-containing adaptor-inducing interferon β (TRIF) (also known as TICAM1 [TIR-containing adaptor molecule-1]), and TRIF-related adaptor molecule (TRAM) (also known as TICAM2).15,16 All TLRs, except TLR3, utilize MyD88, with TLR2 and TLR4 also using TIRAP as a sorting adaptor to recruit MyD88 to the cell surface. TRIF associates with TLR3 and TLR4, while TRAM is selectively recruited to endosomal TLR4 and TRIF. Recently, the TRIF/TRAM pathway has also been shown to be active in TLR2 signaling.17,18 The MyD88-dependent pathway converges on the activation of nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), while the MyD88-independent pathway activates IRFs, NF-κB, and MAPKs, collectively driving the expression of inflammatory cytokines and type I interferons.15

In this study, we investigate the effects of RANKL exposure on the macrophage response to intracellular pathogens. To model intracellular infection, we used the Gram-negative bacterium Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (STm), as macrophages are an important niche for STm replication and dissemination.19–21 Furthermore, STm colonizes and replicates within organs where RANKL is detected, such as the intestinal tract, spleen, lymph nodes, and bone marrow.19 We found that prior RANKL exposure impairs TLR2 and TLR4 signaling through deficient transcriptional induction of the MyD88-independent response adaptor TRAM and decreased protein abundance of the MyD88-dependent response adaptor TIRAP, corresponding with decreased NF-κB and IRF3 activation. RANKL-exposed macrophages produce fewer proinflammatory cytokines and have significantly increased intracellular bacteria when infected with STm, suggesting reduced proinflammatory effector functions. Overall, findings from this study implicate the RANK/RANKL axis as an important regulator of macrophage responses to infection.

Methods

Ethics statement

All experiments involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Vanderbilt University Medical Center. All experiments were conducted in accordance with National Institutes of Health guidelines, the Animal Welfare Act, and U.S. federal law.

Animal use

Male and female 8- to 12-wk-old C57BL/6J mice (Jackson stock # 000664) were used for all experiments that involved isolation of bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs). Mice were acclimatized for at least 1 wk in our colony prior to BMDM isolation.

Cell culture

Primary mouse BMDMs were isolated as previously described.22 Briefly, whole bone marrow was flushed from the femurs and tibias of wild-type (WT) mice 8 to 12 wk old using unsupplemented MEM-α medium (Gibco) and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm × 5 min. Red blood cells were lysed with ammonium-chloride-potassium buffer for 5 min and then neutralized with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were passed through a 70 μM filter, centrifuged again, resuspended in MEM-α medium + 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) + 1× penicillin-streptomycin (complete MEM-α), and counted. To enrich for BMDMs, whole bone marrow cells were seeded at a density of 10 million cells in 10 cm tissue culture–treated dishes in complete MEM-α supplemented with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF). Supernatant from the CMG 14-12 cell line served as the M-CSF source and was added at 20% (v/v) to provide a sufficiently high dose of M-CSF for the positive selection of BMDMs. After 4 d of differentiation, BMDMs were harvested by scraping. BMDMs were either replated in tissue culture plates for 3 d in complete MEM-α with 5% CMG supernatant for further differentiation, or cryopreserved in FBS with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide until future use. Following 7 total days of differentiation in complete MEM-α with CMG supernatant, recombinant mouse RANKL (R&D Systems #462-TR-010/CF or Invitrogen #RP-8601) was added at a concentration of 0, 10, or 100 ng/mL in complete MEM-α with 5% CMG supernatant for an additional 1, 2, or 5 d in culture. To enumerate BMDMs, cells were stained with 8 μg/mL fluorescein diacetate and 20 μg/mL propidium iodide diluted in unsupplemented MEM-α for 5 min. BMDMs were then imaged in PBS on a BioTek Cytation 5 (Agilent), and live/dead cells were counted using the BioTek Gen5 software. To assay cell viability, the LDH-Glo Cytotoxicity assay (Promega Corporation) was used. Cell supernatants were diluted in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) Storage Buffer and mixed with LDH Detection Reagent, then assayed on a plate reader. Relative luminescence between samples was normalized to purified LDH as a positive control per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mouse splenic macrophages were isolated from WT mice 8 to 12 wk old using the EasySep Mouse Monocyte Isolation Kit (StemCell Technologies) following manufacturer protocols. Briefly, spleens were manually disrupted in PBS containing 2% FBS before being passed through a 70 μm mesh nylon strainer to remove aggregates and debris. Cells were centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min, and then resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 108 nucleated cells/mL in EasySep buffer. FcR blocker and Selection Cocktail were each added at a concentration of 100 μL per 1 mL of sample, and cells were incubated for 3 min at 4 °C. Cells were then mixed and placed under a magnetic field for 5 min. Magnetic selection was performed on the enriched monocyte population an additional time before cells were centrifuged again. Splenic monocytes were seeded in tissue culture plates in complete MEM-α supplemented with CMG supernatant and differentiated into macrophages over 7 d with a media change at day 3. Splenic macrophages were then exposed to either 0 or 100 ng/mL recombinant mouse RANKL for an additional 2 d in complete MEM-α with 5% CMG supernatant.

Human CD14+ monocytes were negatively selected from commercially sourced peripheral blood mononuclear cells (ZenBio) from healthy donors using the Classical Monocyte Isolation Kit (Miltenyi) following manufacturer instructions. Human monocytes were seeded in tissue culture plates in complete MEM-α with 50 ng/mL recombinant human M-CSF (R&D systems) and differentiated into macrophages over 7 d with a media change at day 3. Human macrophages were then exposed to recombinant human RANKL (R&D systems) at a concentration of 0, 10, or 100 ng/mL in complete MEM-α with recombinant human M-CSF for an additional 1, 2, or 5 d in culture.

RAW264.7 macrophages were maintained in high-glucose (4.5 g/L) Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with L-glutamine (Gibco) + 10% FBS + 1× penicillin-streptomycin (complete DMEM). RAW-Dual and RAW-Blue reporter macrophages (InvivoGen) were maintained in complete DMEM and 100 μg/mL normocin. 200 μg/mL Zeocin was added every other passage to maintain selective pressure per the manufacturer’s recommendations. Cells were seeded in tissue culture plates in complete DMEM without an M-CSF source and exposed to 0 or 100 ng/mL recombinant mouse RANKL for 2 d. For RAW-Dual and RAW-Blue reporter macrophages, supernatant secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase and Lucia luciferase were detected using QUANTI-Blue (InvivoGen) and QUANTI-Luc (InvivoGen) solutions according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All cell cultures were incubated at 37 °C with 5.0% CO2.

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

All infections were conducted with WT STm strains SL1344 or IR715 (ATCC 14028 Nalr). Two days prior to infection, strains were struck out onto LB plates and grown overnight at 37 °C. One day prior to infection, a single colony was inoculated from the LB plate into 5 mL LB and grown for 16 h, shaking, at 37 °C in loosely capped 15 mL conical tubes. Subcultures were prepared by diluting overnights 1:100 into fresh LB and grown until optical density at 600 nm (OD600) = 0.8 to 0.9 (approximately 4 h) to reach the late exponential growth phase, then pelleted by centrifuging at 4,000 g for 5 min. Bacterial inocula were adjusted to the desired multiplicity of infection (MOI) by dilution in the appropriate media and measurement of OD600, where OD600 of 0.7 yielded approximately 8 × 108 CFU/mL.

Infection of macrophages with STm

STm infections were performed in a similar fashion to previously published literature.23 Briefly, following exposure to 0, 10, or 100 ng/mL RANKL for 1, 2, or 5 d, cell monolayers were washed once with 1× PBS and the media replaced with STm diluted to the appropriate MOI in unsupplemented MEM-α. Where indicated, unsupplemented MEM-α was used as mock infection. Plates were centrifuged at 1,000 g for 10 min to facilitate bacterial adherence to cells and then cultured at 37 °C for an additional 20 min. The inoculum was then removed and the cells were gently washed with 1× PBS. After removal of the inoculum, cells were treated with 100 μg/mL gentamicin for 1 h to kill extracellular bacteria and then maintained in 10 μg/mL gentamicin in unsupplemented MEM-α with 5% CMG supernatant for mouse BMDMs and splenic macrophages, 50 ng/mL human M-CSF for human macrophages, or no M-CSF source for RAW264.7 cells until the endpoint of the assay. For quantification of intracellular bacteria, macrophages were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in 96-well tissue culture–treated plates. At the experimental endpoint, cells were lysed in 0.1% Triton X-100, serially diluted, and plated on LB agar for enumeration of bacterial CFUs.

PRR and cytokine receptor stimulation

BMDMs or RAW264.7 macrophages were plated at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well or 3 × 105 cells/well, respectively, in 6-well tissue culture–treated plates and cultured with or without RANKL. Cells were then stimulated with 1 × 107 cells/mL heat-killed S. Typhimurium (HKST) (InvivoGen), 1 µg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-EB Ultrapure (InvivoGen), 1 ng/mL FSL-1 (InvivoGen), 500 µg/mL flagellin from STm Ultrapure (InvivoGen), 5 µM ODN1826 (InvivoGen), 100 ng/mL recombinant mouse TNF-α (R&D systems), 100 µg/mL recombinant mouse interleukin (IL)-1β (R&D systems), 100 µg/mL Pam3CSK4 (InvivoGen), or 10 × 1010 cells/mL heat-killed L. monocytogenes (InvivoGen) in unsupplemented MEM-α.

RNA sequencing

BMDMs were plated at a density of 2 × 106 cells/well in 6-well tissue culture–treated plates and cultured with or without 100 ng/mL RANKL for 2 d. 3 replicates were cultured per sample condition. Cells were infected with STm at an MOI of 1 for 4 or 24 h. Total RNA was harvested using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and quality were assessed using Qubit fluorometric assay and Agilent BioAnalyzer. cDNA libraries were prepared from 300 ng of total RNA and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with 150 bp paired-end reads, targeting an average of 50 million reads per sample. Read preprocessing was streamlined using snakemake v8.16.0.24 Raw paired-end reads were cleaned up using fastp v0.23.42 and then mapped to the Mus musculus reference genome GRCm39 (GCF_00001635.27, released February 2024) using the splice-aware aligner STAR v2.73 in 2-pass mode (STAR—twopassMode Basic).25,26 To decrease the likelihood of false positive splice junction calls, the minimum overlaps for annotated and unannotated splice junctions in STAR were increased from defaults of 3 and 5 nucleotides to 4 and 8 nucleotides, respectively. Postalignment quality control was performed using MultiQC.27 Aligned read counts were calculated using featureCounts.28 All downstream processing was performed in R 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).29 Differential expression analysis for volcano plots was performed using DESeq2 v1.46.030 using significance cutoffs of ≥3× fold change (FC) and ≤0.001 false discovery rate (FDR). Principal component analysis (PCA) plots were generated from these data using PCATools v2.18.0. For interaction contrasts, differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR 4.2.231 and limma-voom 3.62.2.32,33 The DGEList object contained sample-level metadata based on time point (4 h or 24 h), infection treatment (mock or STm), and RANKL dose (0 or 100 ng/mL). To interrogate specific contrasts of interest, factors were concatenated into a single combined group factor with 8 levels (4 h mock 0R, 4 h mock 100R, 4 h STm 0R, 4 h STm 100R, 24 h mock 0R, 24 h mock 100R, 24 h STm 0R, 24 h STm 100R). To model how RANKL modulates the infection response, the interaction contrast (STm 100R – mock 100R) – (STm 0R – mock 0R) at each time point was tested via a linear model framework.34–36 For each contrast, moderated statistics and FDR were computed with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)37 for the interaction contrast was performed using clusterProfiler 4.14.638,39 with fgsea40 as the backend. GSEA was performed with 100,000 permutations and FDR-adjusted P value cutoff (q value) of 0.05. For pathway analysis, pathways with q < 0.05 were considered significant. In a complementary analysis, GSEA was run across all within-time point pairwise contrasts per database with an adjusted P value cut off of 0.01. For each pathway passing this threshold in any contrast, the core enrichment genes (as reported by fgsea) were identified for downstream pathway scoring and heatmaps.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

BMDMs were plated at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in 96-well tissue culture–treated plates and cultured with or without 100 ng/mL RANKL for 2 d. Cells were then infected with STm SL1344, stimulated with 500 ng/mL LPS-EB Ultrapure (InvivoGen), or mock infected. To activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, 30 min prior to supernatant collection, BMDMs primed with LPS for 23.5 h were additionally treated with either 5 mM ATP (InvivoGen) or 10 µM nigericin sodium salt (InvivoGen). To stimulate the NLRC4 inflammasome, BMDMs were treated with 2 µg/mL LFn-Rod (InvivoGen) and 1 µg/mL protective antigen (InvivoGen) in unsupplemented MEM-α with 1% GlutaMAX.41 At 4 and 24 h, culture supernatant was collected. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was performed for IL-1β (Abcam #ab197742), TNF-α (R&D Systems #DY410), and IL-6 (R&D Systems #DY406) according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Western blot

Following PRR stimulation, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS then lysed with RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with added protease (Roche #04693159001) and phosphatase (Roche #PHOSS-RO) inhibitor cocktails. Total protein was quantified by bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were prepared in Laemmli buffer with β-mercaptoethanol and normalized to total protein. For primary BMDMs, following bicinchoninic acid, equal amounts of protein for each sample were concentrated by trichloroacetic acid precipitation. Briefly, 1 volume of trichloroacetic acid was added to 4 volumes of sample and incubated for 30 min on ice. The sample was spun for 16,500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The precipitated protein was then washed 3 times with ice-cold acetone. The final protein pellet was lightly dried, before being resuspended in a solution of 0.25 M Tris pH 8, 1× Laemmli, 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 0.5% 1M dithiothreitol, and 2.5% β-mercaptoethanol. Antibodies were used for NF-κB p65 (L8F6) (Cell Signaling Technology #6956), Phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536) (93H1) (Cell Signaling Technology #3033), IRF-3 (D6I4C) (Cell Signaling Technology #11904), Phospho-IRF-3 (Ser396) (4D4G) (Cell Signaling Technology #4947), TLR2 (Abcam #ab209217), TLR4 (Proteintech #66350-Ig), MyD88 (Proteintech #67969-1-Ig), TRIF/TICAM1 (Proteintech #23288-1-AP), TIRAP (Invitrogen #PA5-88657), TRAM/TICAM2 (Fabgennix #TRAM-101AP), and GAPDH (GA1R) (Invitrogen #MA5-15738). When applicable, Western blot quantification was performed in ImageJ 1.53c (National Institutes of Health) by subtracting background intensity from band intensity, and then normalizing to loading control.42

Results

RANKL-exposed macrophages have increased intracellular STm burdens

We first investigated the effects of RANKL exposure on the capacity of mouse BMDMs to control infection with STm in vitro. As osteoclast differentiation takes between 5 to 9 d of continuous culture with M-CSF and at least 30 ng/mL RANKL, we tested 1, 2, and 5 d of RANKL treatment at 0, 10, and 100 ng/mL (Fig. 1A, B).43,44 We found that 1 d of RANKL exposure at 100 ng/mL of RANKL and 2 d of RANKL exposure at 10 or 100 ng/mL of RANKL resulted in significantly increased intracellular STm 24 h postinfection (Fig. 1A). When using human CD14+ monocyte-derived macrophages, or testing another strain of STm, 2 d of RANKL exposure at 10 or 100 ng/mL also resulted in significantly increased intracellular burdens (Fig. 1B; Fig. S1A, B). We therefore chose 2 d (48 h) of RANKL treatment at 100 ng/mL to model subosteoclastogenic RANKL exposure in all subsequent studies. We have previously demonstrated that this treatment scheme does not induce mature osteoclasts as measured by lack of multinucleated, TRAP (tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase)-positive cells.22 With 2 d of RANKL exposure at 100 ng/mL, splenic macrophages and RAW264.7 macrophages similarly demonstrated significantly increased intracellular STm burdens 24 h postinfection (Fig. 1C, D). Importantly, the 1- to 2-log increase in intracellular bacteria was not associated with increased host cell proliferation, increased cell death as measured by LDH release, or increased bacterial phagocytosis upon RANKL treatment (Fig. 1E; Fig. S1C, D). Together, these data indicate that RANKL-exposed macrophages have a decreased capacity to control intracellular bacterial replication.

RANKL exposure downregulates PRR signaling and defense response pathways in BMDMs

To characterize how RANKL exposure regulates subsequent transcriptional responses to infection, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on 2-d RANKL-treated and -untreated mouse BMDMs at 4 and 24 h postinfection with STm. As a control for the effects of RANKL only, we also harvested RNA from mock-infected time-matched samples. PCA demonstrated close proximity of biological replicates at both time points (Fig. 2A; Fig. S2A). Samples clustered based on RANKL treatment, delineated along the PC1 axis, which accounted for 82.62% of variance between samples at 4 h, while infection status, delineated along the PC2 axis, had a smaller impact on clustering (11.08%). This suggests that RANKL-treated and -untreated BMDMs are significantly different in their transcriptional responses to infection. Notably, samples that were exposed to RANKL clustered very closely together regardless of infection status, demonstrating the dominant impact of RANKL signaling.

Pairwise contrasts were used to explore the effects of RANKL treatment in mock- and STm-infected cells. First, we performed differential gene expression (DGE) analysis of mock-infected cells with or without prior RANKL exposure (mock 100R – mock 0R), which represents the transcriptional effect of RANKL on macrophages without infection. DGE analysis of mock 100R – mock 0R at 4 and 24 h identified several upregulated genes by RANKL with known roles in osteoclast differentiation or function, consistent with known effects of RANKL signaling (Fig. S2B, C). These genes included ACP5 (encoding TRAP type 5), CTSK (cathepsin K), MMP9 (matrix metalloproteinase 9), and OSCAR (osteoclast-associated receptor). The RANKL-treated group also demonstrated downregulation of proinflammatory genes like TNF and IL1B compared with the RANKL-untreated group. Next, we examined STm 100R – STm 0R, which represents the transcriptional effect of RANKL on macrophages with STm infection (Fig. S2D). Notably, in the STm-infected BMDMs, RANKL exposure revealed transcriptional changes that largely mirrored that of the mock-infected cells, emphasizing that RANKL-driven gene regulation dominates during STm infection at both time points (Fig. 2B; Fig. S2D). Compared with the RANKL-naïve samples, the RANKL-exposed samples also had downregulation of several key proinflammatory genes upon infection, including TNF and IL1B. We also conducted DGE analysis of the pairwise contrast STm 0R – mock 0R, representing the transcriptional response to STm infection in RANKL-naïve cells. In the absence of RANKL exposure, BMDMs demonstrated an expected inflammatory response to STm, with TNF, IL1B, and IL6 all transcriptionally upregulated by 4 h postinfection (Fig. S2E).

Next, to identify genes whose transcriptional response to STm infection was altered by RANKL treatment, we tested the interaction contrast (STm 100R – mock 100R) – (STm 0R – mock 0R). Here, STm 100R – mock 100R represents the transcriptional response to STm infection in RANKL-exposed cells, and STm 0R – mock 0R represents the transcriptional response to STm infection in RANKL-naïve cells. The interaction contrast therefore captures infection responses that are modulated in a RANKL-dependent manner. We performed GSEA of the interaction contrast and found that at 4 h postinfection nearly half of the top 20 downregulated pathways by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes and Gene Ontology analyses were related to PRR and cytokine receptor signal transduction pathways, as well as general response to bacteria (Fig. 2C, D). Notably, many of these negatively enriched terms contained overlapping or related gene sets, including the TLR signaling pathway, LPS-mediated signaling pathway, and cell surface PRR signaling pathway. Given that PRR signaling, especially via TLRs, is crucial for mediating the macrophage response to STm, we further explored the TLR pathway in RANKL-treated and -untreated BMDMs. While infection with STm transcriptionally upregulated genes involved in both the MyD88-dependent and -independent TLR pathways, prior treatment with RANKL significantly dampened expression of these genes and prevented their induction during infection (Fig. 2E). At 24 h postinfection, the interaction contrast revealed decreased activation of multiple pathways related to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response and the unfolded protein response (UPR) in RANKL-exposed cells (Fig. S3A, B). Collectively, the transcriptome analysis suggests that exposure of macrophages to RANKL blunts numerous pathways involved in the host response to bacterial pathogens, including PRR signaling pathways.

RANKL-exposed macrophages produce fewer proinflammatory cytokines

To examine the phenotypic relevance of the RANKL-induced transcriptional changes, we examined inflammatory responses in RANKL-treated BMDMs by measuring production of key proinflammatory cytokines induced during STm infection. We quantified TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 after infection with STm as well as following stimulation with LPS at 4 and 24 h. Consistent with the RNA-seq results, BMDMs exposed to RANKL before STm infection produced significantly less TNF-α and IL-1β (Fig. 3A, B), despite having higher intracellular bacterial burdens. When stimulated with LPS, BMDMs exposed to RANKL had decreased TNF-α only, given that LPS alone is not sufficient to trigger mature IL-1β release via inflammasome activation and subsequent IL-1β processing.45 Therefore, we further examined the effects of RANKL on IL-1β production in response to purified inflammasome agonists: LPS with either nigericin or ATP, which activates the NLRP3 (NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3) inflammasome, and Rod-Tox, which activates the NLRC4 (NLR family CARD domain-containing 4) inflammasome.41 All 3 inflammasome agonists yielded decreased IL-1β release in RANKL-treated cells at 24 h postinfection (Fig. 3C), similar to cells infected with live STm (Fig. 3B). While RANKL-treated BMDMs did not show differential expression of IL6 at the transcriptional level after PRR stimulation (Fig. 2B), they did demonstrate decreased IL-6 protein production by 24 h (Fig. 3D). However, IL-6 release was only observed in LPS-stimulated BMDMs, and the relative difference in IL-6 between RANKL-treated and -untreated BMDMs was not as large as compared with other cytokines. Overall, these data support that RANKL exposure decreases macrophage proinflammatory cytokine production.

RANKL exposure dampens canonical NF-κB activation in macrophages upon subsequent TLR2 and TLR4 stimulation

A common signaling event downstream of PRR activation is the activation of canonical NF-κB signaling, which is a central regulator of proinflammatory responses. Given that RANKL-treated BMDMs showed downregulation of multiple PRR signaling pathways during STm infection, along with the observation that these cells produced fewer proinflammatory cytokines, we hypothesized that RANKL exposure reduces canonical NF-κB signaling. BMDMs exposed to RANKL prior to stimulation with HKST showed a significant reduction in NF-κB activation, as measured by phosphorylation of the NF-κB p65 subunit (Fig. 4A). Because HKST is primarily a coagonist of TLR2, TLR4, and TLR5, we examined whether other receptor pathways that activate canonical NF-κB signaling also demonstrated decreased NF-κB activation in RANKL-exposed BMDMs, or if this finding was specific to TLRs. TNF receptor 1, a membrane receptor that binds TNF-α, is expressed on macrophages and also signals through the canonical NF-κB pathway. While TLRs and TNF receptor 1 converge upon canonical NF-κB signaling at the IκB kinase complex, upstream they utilize different adaptor proteins. Unlike when stimulated with the TLR4 agonist ultrapure LPS, BMDMs stimulated with TNF-α did not demonstrate a decrease in NF-κB activation with prior RANKL exposure as measured by NF-κB reporter macrophages (RAW-Blue cells) (Fig. S4A). These findings suggest that BMDMs previously exposed to RANKL exhibit decreased capacity to activate the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway upon subsequent TLR engagement, and that this effect is mediated by changes upstream of the common IκB kinase complex.

Next, we investigated which of the TLRs known to be engaged during Salmonella infection are affected by RANKL exposure. After treating BMDMs with or without RANKL for 2 d, we stimulated the cells with 4 TLR agonists that differ in both their cellular locations and use of adaptor proteins: ultrapure LPS, a TLR4 agonist; FSL-1, a synthetic TLR2/6 activator; ultrapure flagellin from STm, a TLR5 agonist; and CpG ODN 1826, a synthetic mouse TLR9 ligand. Stimulation of TLR4 and TLR2/6 resulted in significantly decreased NF-κB activation in RANKL-exposed cells (Fig. 4B, C). Testing of additional TLR1/2 agonists also demonstrated a reduction in NF-κB activation in RANKL-exposed NF-κB reporter macrophages (Fig. S4B). Conversely, stimulation of TLR5 and TLR9 did not show any significant differences in NF-κB activity between RANKL-treated and -untreated BMDMs (Fig. 4B, C). Collectively, these data indicate that macrophages previously exposed to RANKL demonstrate decreased canonical NF-κB activation upon secondary TLR stimulation, and that this phenotype is specific to TLRs 2 and 4.

IRF3 activation is impaired in RANKL-exposed macrophages

Because TLR2 and TLR4 utilize both the MyD88-dependent and -independent pathways to activate NF-κB, we next examined whether RANKL exposure altered activation of the MyD88-independent, TRIF/TRAM-dependent signaling pathway. We measured TRIF/TRAM pathway activation through phosphorylation of the downstream transcription factor IRF3. Following TLR4 stimulation, RANKL-treated BMDMs showed markedly less IRF3 activation as measured by immunoblot for phospho-IRF3 (Fig. 4D, E). Similarly, TLR2 stimulation also resulted in less IRF3 activation in BMDMs previously exposed to RANKL (Fig. 4D, E). Notably, in the absence of RANKL, signaling through TLR2 led to less IRF3 phosphorylation compared with TLR4, which may reflect the fact that TLR2 predominantly signals through IRF7 in the TRIF/TRAM pathway.18 As a complementary method, we also used dual NF-κB and IRF-3/9 reporter RAW264.7 macrophages (RAW-Dual cells) to simultaneously measure NF-κB and IRF3 activation via secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase and Lucia luciferase production, respectively. When using RAW-Dual cells, both TLR2 and TLR4 stimulation resulted in significantly decreased NF-κB activity in RANKL-treated cells (Fig. 4F). TLR4 stimulation also resulted in significantly decreased IRF-3/9 activity in RANKL-treated cells (Fig. 4G). As previously observed, TLR2 stimulation triggered low levels of IRF activation, and prior RANKL treatment further decreased levels to that of the negative control. As a positive control for Lucia luciferase production and as a measurement of canonical JAK-STAT signaling, we also tested interferon β (IFN-β) stimulation. We observed that RANKL-exposed cells demonstrated a slight, but statistically significant, decrease in IRF activity upon IFN-β stimulation (Fig. 4G). Together, these findings reveal that both NF-κB and IRF3 activation are impaired in RANKL-exposed macrophages upon subsequent stimulation of TLR2 and TLR4.

RANKL-exposed macrophages fail to induce TRAM and demonstrate a reduction in TIRAP

To determine which components of the TLR signaling pathway were contributing to the downstream inhibition of NF-κB and IRF3 activity in RANKL-exposed macrophages, we examined the impact of RANKL on TLR and TLR adaptor protein expression. Because the RNA-seq data identified transcriptional downregulation of TLR2 (Fig. 2B), we first tested whether TLR2 and TLR4 abundance differed in RANKL-exposed BMDMs, which would potentially make the cells less responsive to STm and TLR agonists. We found that protein levels of TLR2 and TLR4 were not reduced in RANKL-treated macrophages (Fig. 5A). Next, corresponding to transcriptional level data, we found no differences in protein expression of the adaptor MyD88 in RANKL-treated versus untreated cells following stimulation of TLR2 or TLR4 (Figs. 2B and 5B, C). Similarly, we found no differences at either the mRNA or protein levels of the adaptor protein TRIF (Figs. 2B and 5B, C). Therefore, because we previously observed that RANKL-exposed cells demonstrated dampened NF-κB activation upon TLR2 and TLR4 stimulation, but not with TLR5 or TLR9, we considered adaptor proteins that were unique to TLR2 and TLR4 signaling. We first examined TRAM (TICAM2), the sorting adaptor that recruits TRIF to endosomal TLR4. TRAM was the only TLR adaptor protein identified by RNA-seq as being significantly downregulated (Fig. 2B). RANKL-exposed cells induced minimal TRAM protein in response to TLR2 or TLR4 agonists (Fig. 5D, E; Fig. S4C, D). We also examined the MyD88-associated bridging adaptor protein TIRAP, which is essential for TLR2 and TLR4 signaling. There were no significant RANKL-driven transcript-level differences in TIRAP expression during STm infection (Fig. 2B). However, while untreated BMDMs significantly increased TIRAP protein expression in response to TLR4 stimulation, RANKL-exposed cells exhibited significantly decreased TIRAP protein abundance (Fig. 5F, G). This decrease in TIRAP protein content was consistently observed when using LPS but not FSL-1, and the quantification of multiple independent experiments is provided in Fig. S4E, F. These data demonstrate that prior RANKL exposure transcriptionally impairs the TRAM-dependent TLR2 and TLR4 pathways. RANKL exposure also dampens the TIRAP-dependent TLR4 pathway, either by decreasing protein production or increasing degradation of TIRAP. Overall, the data from this study reveal that RANKL signaling is associated with decreased macrophage TLR2 and TLR4-mediated signaling, which negatively impacts downstream NF-κB and IRF3 activation and concurrently causes increased intracellular bacterial burdens.

Discussion

Cytokines are key players in orchestrating the innate immune response to pathogens and injury. Understanding how cytokines work in concert to shape complex and specific cellular responses remains an important endeavor in immunobiology research. RANKL, a cytokine best known for its ability to reprogram monocyte-macrophage lineage cells into osteoclasts in bone, can be found widely across the human body. Yet, the contribution of RANKL to the cytokine milieu in determining macrophage functional state and how this shapes the response to bacterial infection is not known. Our study reveals that prior exposure of macrophages to RANKL leads to deficient TRAM induction upon subsequent TLR2 and TLR4 stimulation at both the transcriptional and protein levels, as well as decreased TIRAP protein abundance upon subsequent TLR4 stimulation. As a result, these cells have decreased activation of the NF-κB and IRF3 pathways, indicating impaired TLR2 and TLR4 signaling. Functionally, RANKL-exposed macrophages produce lower levels of proinflammatory cytokines, consistent with reduced NF-κB and IRF3 activity, and are less capable of restricting infection with live STm, resulting in higher intracellular bacterial burdens. This study demonstrates that although RANKL, aptly named, is known to positively regulate osteoclast development in monocyte-macrophage lineage cells through the activation of NF-κB, it limits NF-κB in other contexts. Therefore, macrophages exposed to RANKL beyond the bone microenvironment may have an altered response to inflammatory stimuli.

Macrophages possess an arsenal of effector mechanisms that work in concert to eliminate pathogens. While cytokine production is a cell-extrinsic function and acts to recruit other immune cells, amplify inflammation, and activate cells, macrophages also utilize many cell-intrinsic effector functions to clear pathogens, including phagocytosis, reactive oxygen species production, inflammasome activation, autophagy, and phagolysosomal acidification.46,47 Notably, TLR signaling is intricately tied to these macrophage antibacterial effector functions. Not only do TLRs recognize PAMPs and trigger signaling cascades, but also many of these effector mechanisms also require TLRs for downstream assembly and function. For example, TLRs 2 and 4 are critical for LC3-associated phagocytosis, a major mechanism targeting STm in macrophages, in which they promote the rapid recruitment of autophagy proteins to the SCV.48–50 Furthermore, the inflammasome, which critically restricts cytosolic STm in macrophages, requires TLR signaling to initiate the transcriptional upregulation of essential components like pro-IL-1β and NLRP3.45,51,52 We hypothesize that the RANKL-mediated impairment of TLR2 and TLR4 signaling inhibits one or multiple of these host defense mechanisms downstream of TLR signaling, enabling increased bacterial replication. Rather than inducing specific inhibitor proteins or anti-inflammatory molecules, we hypothesize that RANKL exposure creates a state of reduced priming in macrophages and subsequently weakens antibacterial effector responses during infection.

Our finding that prior RANKL exposure results in downregulation of ER stress response– and UPR-related pathways during late infection may reflect the fact that TLR4 and TLR2 have been shown to specifically activate ER stress response proteins.53 Accordingly, RNA-seq 24 h postinfection demonstrated that RANKL exposure reduced transcriptional signatures of both positive and negative regulatory ER stress and UPR pathways during infection, indicating low ER stress activation rather than specific inhibition of these pathways. This finding supports our hypothesis that RANKL-exposed macrophages are suppressed in mounting TLR-dependent inflammatory responses, and demonstrates that this deficiency is sustained through late infection. Importantly, activation of the ER stress response leads to proinflammatory cytokine production, and impairment of these pathways results in increased bacterial burdens in vivo.53 Furthermore, while TLR signaling activates ER stress, ER stress also amplifies the cellular response to TLR ligation via multiple mechanisms, including NF-κB and RIPK1 (receptor-interacting protein kinase 1) activation.54–57 The role of ER stress/UPR as an innate immune modulator is a newly emerging field of research, and the role of these pathways during RANKL signaling should be studied with longer PRR stimulation. Overall, RANKL exposure causes priming deficits during early infection, which facilitates a permissive niche for Salmonella replication.

Macrophages with decreased priming and reduced proinflammatory outputs may be phenotypically related to alternatively activated macrophages, which are anti-inflammatory.58–60 Alternatively activated macrophages, also known as M2 macrophages, are characterized by reduced killing capacity and reduced proinflammatory cytokine production.59–61 Salmonella is known to exploit M2 macrophages for intracellular replication and systemic spread.62,63 Therefore, we queried our RNA-seq dataset to determine the FC in uninfected RANKL-exposed cells relative to RANKL-naïve cells for characteristic markers associated with M2 polarization, such as Arg1 (log2FC = 2.0, adjusted P value = 0.012), Mrc1 (log2FC −7.6, adjusted P value = 3.15 × 10−249), Cd163 (log2FC = −5.64, adjusted P value = 4.15 × 10−83), Il10 (log2FC = 0.49, adjusted P value = 0.023), Tgfb1 (log2FC = −1.62, adjusted P value = 1.9 × 10−97), and Irf4 (log2FC = −2.23, adjusted P value = 4.03 × 10−9). Because M2-associated genes demonstrated significant, bidirectional expression changes, RANKL signaling likely does not dampen macrophage inflammatory function through the induction of an M2-like transcriptional program.

We observed decreased TLR2 and TLR4 signaling, as measured by NF-κB and IRF3 activation, after 2 d of RANKL exposure in macrophages. Studies have shown that murine osteoclast precursors express TLR1-9, while mature osteoclasts only express TLR2 and TLR4.64 Many of these studies, which utilized LPS to study TLR signaling, have reported both positive and negative roles of TLRs in osteoclastogenesis. LPS has a dual effect on osteoclastogenesis in vitro, stimulating osteoclast differentiation when precursors have been primed with RANKL, and inhibiting RANKL-dependent differentiation when they have not.65 This dual modulation phenotype has been linked to IFN-β, which is a negative regulator of osteoclastogenesis through a negative feedback loop whereby IFN-β produced by osteoclast precursors and osteocytes decreases c-Fos expression and inhibits osteoclast differentiation.66 Our finding that RANKL exposure decreases TRAM expression may represent a mechanism to reduce downstream IFN-β production in order to promote osteoclastogenesis. However, this phenotype may be highly dependent on the timing of RANKL exposure, and it will be important in future studies to measure IRF3 activation and its consequences on gene expression in the first 48 h following RANKL exposure as well as upon subsequent TLR stimulation.

TLR signaling is tightly regulated, as excessive signaling disrupts the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory responses during bacterial infection. These juxtaposed phenotypes represent the balance between host-mediated pathogen recognition and bacterial immune evasion. For example, TRAM plays a central role in generating an immune-suppressive monocyte exhaustion state in response to prolonged LPS exposure, and TRAM deletion has been shown to attenuate monocyte exhaustion and alleviate sepsis severity.67 Furthermore, although TRAM and TIRAP are primarily known for mediating TLR signaling, it is possible that they have non-TLR roles with immune consequences as a result of adaptor deficiency. Overall, TLR4 signaling during bacterial infection is highly nuanced, and further studies are needed to investigate the relative contribution of altered TLR signaling to increased bacterial burdens in RANKL-exposed cells.

A study found that RANKL and LPS costimulation in macrophages caused preferential binding of TRAF6 to the RANK receptor over TLR4, resulting in diminished TLR4 activation.68 Association of TRAF6 to TLR4 requires assembly of signaling platforms mediated by TIRAP in the MyD88-dependent pathway and TRAM in the MyD88-independent pathway.69 Our observations suggest a mechanism whereby these oligomeric TLR signaling complexes may be defective, but further experiments are needed to evaluate their assembly and function. Given that we observed a considerable reduction of TRAM and a modest reduction in TIRAP, an additional major focus of future studies will be defining the relative contributions of the MyD88-dependent and -independent pathways to the RANKL-driven decrease in NF-κB and IRF3 activation.

Our data demonstrate that RANKL-exposed macrophages exhibit decreased TRAM expression upon both TLR2 and TLR4 stimulation and reduced TIRAP protein abundance upon TLR4 stimulation. There are many known post-translational negative regulatory mechanisms of TIRAP, most of which involve targeted ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation.70 Additional studies will address the role of these negative regulators during RANKL signaling and how the potential turnover of TIRAP affects TLR signaling. Regarding TRAM, regulation of TICAM2 transcription during LPS stimulation has been linked to the master myeloid transcription factor IRF8. IRF8 occupies the promoter region of TICAM2 upon LPS stimulation.71 Notably, IRF8 is a major negative regulator of osteoclastogenesis, and RANKL signaling transcriptionally and epigenetically silences IRF8 expression.72 We hypothesize that RANKL-induced silencing of IRF8 prevents TICAM2 induction.

Prior in vivo studies using murine sepsis models have shown that high doses of RANKL are associated with reduced sepsis severity and decreased proinflammatory serum cytokines.14,68,73 In vitro, we observed decreased proinflammatory cytokines and increased bacterial burdens in RANKL-treated, STm-infected macrophages. In vivo, it is possible that RANKL administration would similarly decrease cytokine levels and create an immunosuppressed state that impairs bacterial clearance. Alternatively, the overall reduction in inflammation and disease morbidity might allow for a more balanced immune response that enables disease tolerance. These outcomes highlight RANKL’s contribution to the critical balance between pathogen clearance and excessive inflammation in macrophages. Future studies should examine the outcomes of RANKL administration and blockade on infection outcomes in vivo, as well as its in vivo effect on TLR signaling.

One limitation of this study stems from the challenge of quantifying physiological RANKL concentrations, as RANKL can be found in both soluble and membrane-bound forms.1 We observed differences in intracellular STm burdens and cytokine production with as little as 10 ng/mL RANKL for 48 h, which is not sufficient to induce osteoclastogenesis, but the precise amounts of RANKL that recapitulate extraskeletal RANKL-mediated physiology have not been determined. Another limitation is that when infected with live STm, mouse BMDM IL-6 mRNA production was uncoupled from protein production. STm SL1344 has several type 3 secretion system effectors that can interfere with IL-6 output, such as SteA and AvrA.74–76 Further work is warranted with additional STm strains, MOIs, and timing of sample collection to investigate the effects of RANKL on IL-6.

Additional outstanding questions remain that will drive future studies. First, while the deficiency of TRAM in RANKL-exposed macrophages is stark, the resulting increased bacterial proliferation likely extends beyond TLR signaling, as evidenced by the additional downregulated host defense signaling pathways and genes identified by RNA-seq. For example, JAK-STAT signaling was found to be one of the top twenty downregulated pathways by GSEA, and when we stimulated RAW-Dual Cells with IFN-β, RANKL-exposed cells demonstrated less IRF activity. IFN signaling plays an important role in restricting intracellular pathogens, such as for the induction of antimicrobial guanylate-binding proteins that both directly target cytosolic STm and promote inflammasome responses.77–79 Moreover, there are additional pathways downstream of TLR signaling that contribute to proinflammatory cytokine production, such as the AP-1 (activator protein-1) and MAPK cascades, and whether RANKL signaling impairs activation of these pathways has yet to be determined. Additionally, it is possible that RANKL alters host cell permissiveness to infection through mechanisms beyond blunted cytokine production. In our RNA-seq data, we found that RANKL exposure led to significant differences in expression levels for genes involved in inflammasome signaling, respiratory burst, and iron handling, all of which could contribute to changes in intracellular bacterial burdens. Finally, while this study focused on the effects of RANKL signaling in macrophages, many other cells express the RANK receptor, including intestinal epithelial cells which are the first line of defense against Salmonella infection.4,80,81 Notably, RANKL is essential for microfold cell differentiation, and Salmonella is capable of transforming intestinal follicle-associated epithelial cells into microfold cells through a mechanism involving RANK/RANKL to promote host colonization.81 Future studies should explore how RANKL signaling impacts STm pathogenicity in non–monocyte lineage cells. Overall, this study demonstrates that the RANK/RANKL axis dampens TLR2 and TLR4 signaling in macrophages and plays an important role in shaping the macrophage response to intracellular bacteria.
