A multistage transfer learning framework for intelligent fault diagnosis of rotating machinery under variable operating conditions
Authors: Muhammad Farooq Siddique, Wasim Zaman, Muhammad Khalid, Bayan Hamdan, Jong-Myon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The transition of industrial maintenance toward intelligent and autonomous operation requires non-destructive evaluation frameworks that remain reliable under variable operating conditions and limited labeled data. In practical inspection scenarios, data driven diagnostic models often suffer from performance degradation due to changes in operating speed and insufficient fault samples, which restrict their applicability in real machinery monitoring. To address this challenge, this study presents a multistage transfer learning framework designed to enhance vibration-based fault evaluation of rotating machinery under varying rotational speeds. The proposed framework progressively adapts a pretrained convolutional network through generic feature learning, vibration specific representation refinement, and task focused fine tuning, enabling stable feature reuse while preserving inspection relevant signal characteristics. A layer selective adaptation strategy is employed to retain general low-level representations while refining higher level features that capture speed dependent fault signatures. Experimental investigations conducted on vibration signals acquired from a milling machine operating at five spindle speeds demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves consistent and reliable fault discrimination across operating conditions, with classification accuracy ranging from 97.70% to 100%. The results indicate improved separation of fault induced time frequency patterns compared with conventional single stage transfer strategies. By enhancing robustness to operating variability and reducing dependence on extensive labeled datasets, the proposed framework contributes to reliable nondestructive evaluation and supports practical deployment of vibration-based condition monitoring systems in industrial environments.

Introduction

In the Industry 4.0 revolution, ensuring safety, reliability, and effective nondestructive evaluation of complex industrial machinery has become increasingly critical. Rotating machines, including electric motors, wind turbines, milling machines, and aircraft engines, are fundamental components in modern industrial systems due to their durability, high efficiency, and continuous operation under demanding conditions1–3. The progressive increase in operating speeds, loading variability, and structural complexity has intensified the need for reliable condition monitoring and fault evaluation strategies. Effective detection and identification of incipient faults allow engineers and operators to implement timely corrective actions, such as predictive maintenance or adaptive control reconfiguration, thereby reducing the risk of catastrophic failure and unplanned downtime4. Consequently, significant research efforts over the past decade have focused on advancing vibration based and sensor driven diagnostic methodologies that enable accurate, rapid, and reliable assessment of machine health while minimizing interruption to normal operation5–9.

Different machine faults generate distinct vibration patterns due to their underlying mechanical mechanisms. Bearing defects produce periodic impulsive excitations at characteristic defect frequencies such as BPFO and BPFI, leading to repetitive high-energy ridges in the time–frequency representation. Gear faults typically introduce amplitude modulation around the gear meshing frequency, resulting in sideband structures that appear as periodic energy variations in the scalogram. Tool faults, such as edge chipping or wear, generate irregular transient bursts and broadband energy fluctuations due to unstable cutting interactions, producing more dispersed time–frequency patterns. Similar observations regarding the characteristic vibration signatures of rotating machinery faults have been widely reported in the literature10,11.

Machine learning algorithms, such as nearest neighbor12, random forest13, artificial neural networks (ANNs)14, and support vector machines (SVMs)15, perform well when supplied with sufficient labeled data and well-crafted features. However, the reliance on manually engineered signal features requires substantial human effort and extensive domain specific expertise, which limits scalability in practical inspection scenarios16. This challenge has been partially addressed by deep learning models, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and transformers, which enable automated extraction of fault sensitive representations from sensor-based signals, reducing dependence on manual intervention17–19. Nevertheless, the availability of labeled data remains a fundamental limitation in nondestructive evaluation, as acquiring fault data from operating rotating machinery can be costly, disruptive, or unsafe. Data augmentation techniques, such as synthetic sample generation using generative adversarial networks (GANs), as well as random flipping, translation, and rotation, have been explored to mitigate data scarcity. However, augmented data may not always preserve physically meaningful fault characteristics, and techniques such as GANs may suffer from mode collapse, ultimately limiting generalization to unseen operating conditions20.

Recent advancements in transfer learning (TL) have emerged as an effective strategy to address the limitations of deep learning in nondestructive evaluation scenarios characterized by limited labeled inspection data. In TL, the knowledge (i.e., learned parameters) of a deep network trained on a large-scale source dataset is reused to enhance performance on a related target task with limited labeled data21,22. By employing pretrained models such as those trained on ImageNet, TL facilitates stable model initialization, accelerates convergence, and reduces training cost, while enabling the transfer of low and mid-level representations to the target domain. In vibration-based inspection tasks, this transferability supports consistent extraction of fault-related signal patterns under variable operating conditions. These developments have motivated layer selective fine-tuning strategies, domain adaptation mechanisms, and multisource learning frameworks that improve inspection robustness and fault discriminability across varying speeds, loads, and fault categories in rotating machinery. As a result, TL has become a practically attractive approach for vibration-based condition monitoring and nondestructive fault evaluation, supporting reliable predictive maintenance in industrial environments. Furthermore, recent studies also have explored hybrid frameworks combining advanced signal processing and machine learning models for intelligent fault diagnosis under varying operating conditions while emerging works on physics-guided degradation modeling and trustworthy remaining useful life prediction emphasize cross-condition transfer, degradation consistency, and reliable multistep prognostics under varying operating regimes23,24. These developments further motivate the proposed approach, which integrates time–frequency scalogram representations with a multistage transfer learning strategy for robust fault diagnosis across different operating speeds25–29.

However, when the target domain differs substantially from the source domain in terms of signal characteristics and inspection physics (e.g., ImageNet to vibration signal scalograms), fine tuning only a few upper layers often result in insufficient adaptation of fault sensitive features, leading to poor generalization under unseen operating conditions30. In such cases, fine tuning a greater number of higher-level layers can enhance representation of speed dependent and transient fault signatures, thereby improving diagnostic reliability, particularly for complex fault patterns. This deep adaptation, however, increases reliance on labeled inspection data, which is often difficult, expensive, or impractical to acquire in real industrial nondestructive evaluation environments31,32. Although individual techniques such as wavelet-based signal representations and transfer learning have been widely explored in machine condition monitoring, their integration within a structured multistage transfer learning framework, particularly with explicit intermediate RPM-based staging and selective layer-wise adaptation for progressive domain alignment, has received limited attention33,34. Unlike conventional progressive fine-tuning, the proposed MSTL framework explicitly integrates physically meaningful intermediate RPM domains with selective layer-wise adaptation, enabling gradual feature alignment between heterogeneous domains. This strategy allows the model to progressively adapt feature representations between different rotational speeds, improving the robustness of fault diagnosis models under variable working conditions.

In this study, a multistage transfer learning (MSTL) framework is proposed to enhance vibration based on non-destructive fault evaluation under variable operating conditions, addressing the challenges discussed above. The pretrained ResNet-50 architecture, initially trained on a large-scale visual dataset, serves as the baseline for generic feature representation relevant to signal texture and energy distribution. The proposed framework then proceeds through three progressive stages of adaptation designed to bridge inspection domain discrepancies. In the first stage, vibration specific representations are learned using intermediate operating conditions, such as multiple rotational speed datasets, to reduce the domain gap between generic image features and time–frequency vibration scalograms. In the second stage, data from closely related operating domains are utilized to refine fault sensitive representations, improving separability of mechanically induced signal patterns. Finally, in the third stage, the model is selectively fine-tuned on a completely unseen target dataset to capture task specific inspection characteristics, enabling accurate and robust fault discrimination under realistic operating variability. This hierarchical adaptation strategy preserves transferable low-level features while progressively refining higher level fault representations, resulting in improved generalization even when only limited labeled inspection data are available in the target domain. Related literature

TL has emerged as a powerful tool in fault diagnosis, addressing challenges associated with limited labeled data and domain discrepancies. By leveraging pre-trained models or knowledge from source domains, TL enables accurate fault detection and diagnosis even under varying operational conditions35,36. Recent studies have explored diverse approaches to optimize TL for fault diagnosis, focusing on domain adaptation, feature alignment, and effective use of sparse or multisource data. Below, we summarize significant contributions to TL methodologies in the field of fault diagnosis37,38. Shao et al.39 proposed a TL-based framework that converts sensor data into time-frequency images using Wavelet transformation, followed by fine-tuning a pretrained network for fast and accurate results. Wen et al. introduced a TL method utilizing a three-layer sparse auto-encoder combined with a maximum mean discrepancy term to align features between training and testing data40. Wang et al. developed a hierarchical deep domain adaptation (HDDA) approach to transfer a classifier trained on labeled data under one loading condition to identify faults with unlabeled data from a different loading condition41. Zhu et al.1 proposed a TL method leveraging multisource domain adaptation, employing a multi-adversarial strategy to obtain domain-invariant and goal-discriminative features. Han et al.42 presented a framework for TL with sparse target data, pairing source and target data under similar conditions to reduce discrepancies and prevent negative transfer. A dual-domain alignment approach (DDA-PADA) was introduced by Wang, combining domain-adversarial neural network (DANN) modules, source-domain alignment (SDA), and cross-domain alignment (CDA) to enhance feature alignment across and between domains43. Tang et al.44 proposed Adaptive Independent Layer Transfer Learning Network (AILTLN) for Intelligent Health State Identification of Bearings (IHSIB), incorporating an adaptive Independent Layer (IL) module, a feature extractor with group and transposed convolutions, and loss functions for health identification and domain alignment. Lu et al.45 developed a dynamic sparse transfer learning network with adaptive kurtosis-based alignment and sparse regularization for greater stability.

Recent studies have further advanced this field. Xing et al.46 introduced TL Co-CNN, which embeds the ConvNeXt architecture for rotating machine diagnosis. Vibration signals are transformed into RGB images by combining time, frequency, and time frequency information, and then fine-tuned with an ImageNet pretrained ConvNeXt to enhance fault classification performance. Yang et al.47 presented a joint distribution alignment method enhanced by Squeeze and Excitation attention, combining Joint Adaptation Network (JAN) and conjunction with Correlation Alignment (CORAL) modules with an I-Softmax loss function, thereby improving separability and robustness under domain shifts. Jeong et al.48 advanced transfer learning by proposing a source free domain adaptation framework that incorporates mechanical prior with self-supervised learning, reducing dependence on source data and improving generalization across operating domains. Alam et al.21 developed a physics informed multimodal CNN that integrates vibration and current signals with a physics-based loss term reflecting characteristic bearing frequencies. They compared three transfer strategies, showing that layer wise adaptation provided strong robustness under varying conditions. Shen et al.49 extended this line of research into privacy preserving learning by proposing FedLED, a federated transfer learning framework that enables unsupervised diagnosis across distributed agents while maintaining data confidentiality.

Together, these contributions highlight the breadth of TL applications in fault diagnosis. From domain adaptation and multisource alignment to physics informed multimodal learning and federated approaches, the literature demonstrates a clear progression toward robust, interpretable, and generalizable framework that can operate effectively under the noisy, variable, and data scarce conditions characteristic of industrial environments.

Proposed solution

To overcome the challenges of data scarcity and domain discrepancies in fault diagnosis of rotating machines, this study introduces a MSTL framework designed to achieve robust performance under unseen operating conditions. The framework progressively adapts a pretrained ResNet-50 model from generic to task specific feature learning through three sequential stages: pretraining on ImageNet, vibration specific training using intermediate domains, and task specific fine tuning on the target domain. The overall workflow is illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2, where vibration signals collected under multiple spindle speeds are transformed into CWT scalograms and used as model inputs. The pretrained ResNet-50 first extracts generic features from the ImageNet source domain, which are then progressively refined through intermediate RPM datasets before being fine tuned on the final target domain to achieve accurate and stable fault classification. Furthermore, the architectural details of the proposed MSTL framework. The lower convolutional layers of ResNet-50 remain frozen to retain generic visual representations, while progressively unfreezing higher layers enables adaptation across RPM domains. Intermediate data iteratively guide feature alignment, and only the final two layers are fine tuned on the target domain to establish precise decision boundaries among the fault classes (BF, GF, TF, and N).

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Vibration signals are collected from the experimental milling cutting tool setup under multiple spindle speeds. These raw signals are segmented and converted into Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) scalograms, which provide a two-dimensional representation of localized time–frequency energy distributions. This transformation ensures that transient fault signatures, often missed in conventional analysis, are effectively captured and made suitable for deep learning models. This step contributes by constructing a consistent, discriminative dataset that forms the foundation for progressive transfer learning.

General feature extraction (pretraining)

In the pretraining stage, a deep convolutional network such as ResNet-50 is employed to establish a strong foundation for feature learning. For a model to generalize effectively, it must be trained on a large and diverse dataset; therefore, ResNet-50 is first pretrained on the ImageNet dataset, which contains over a million labeled images across 1,000 categories. This large-scale pretraining enables the network to learn generic visual features such as edges, gradients, and textures that are broadly transferable across different domains.

The lower layers of the pretrained model capture these universal patterns, providing a stable initialization that ensures smooth gradient propagation, mitigates vanishing gradient issues, and accelerates convergence in subsequent adaptation stages. As illustrated in the figure, this stage corresponds to generic feature learning, where the network learns domain-independent representations that later serve as the foundation for vibration-specific and task-specific adaptation. Overall, this pretraining phase eliminates the need to train the network from scratch, thereby reducing computational cost and dependence on large volumes of labeled vibration data.

Domain adaptation-vibration-specific training

In the vibration specific training stage, the pretrained ResNet-50 model adapts generic ImageNet features to the vibration domain using an intermediate RPM dataset. This stage enables the network to learn vibration specific representations that capture the unique patterns of mechanical systems. The number of upper layers to be fine-tuned is determined empirically based on dataset size and feature sensitivity and may vary across configurations. Through progressive unfreezing and fine tuning, the model learns modulation structures, harmonic peaks, and transient bursts that characterize fault conditions, forming a bridge between generic image features and vibration signal characteristics.

The intermediate domain plays an essential role in progressively aligning the feature distributions of the source and target domains, thereby minimizing the domain gap. This process is conceptually illustrated in Fig. 3, which shows how the model incrementally adapts features from the source and intermediate domains to build a robust domain adapted classifier. By learning both RPM invariant and RPM dependent features, this stage ensures that the extracted representations remain discriminative, noise resistant, and robust across varying operating speeds.

In practice, the intermediate RPM domains are selected based on the similarity of vibration characteristics across operating speeds. Rotational speeds that are closer to the target RPM tend to exhibit similar harmonic structures and frequency spectrum distributions. Therefore, intermediate domains are chosen from RPM conditions that lie between the source and target speeds, enabling gradual feature adaptation and reducing abrupt distribution shifts during multistage transfer learning.

Task-specific fine-tuning

In the task specific fine-tuning stage, the model is adapted to completely unseen domains (e.g., 1440 RPM) using a small target dataset containing around 300 labeled samples. This dataset was entirely separate from the training and validation data, ensuring that model performance was evaluated on truly unseen conditions. In this stage, only the last two layers and the classification dense layers are fine tuned to establish precise decision boundaries for fault classification. Although the target data are limited, the hierarchical training process ensures that vibration specific features learned in earlier stages are effectively reused, allowing the model to achieve high accuracy with minimal retraining. Experimental setup and dataset acquisition

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed fault diagnosis framework, vibration signals were collected from a milling machine under controlled operating conditions. The primary objective was to detect and classify faults in multiple machine components, with the proposed method benchmarked against contemporary approaches to demonstrate its advantages in fault detection and classification. The experiments were performed on an INTER-SIEG X1 Micro Mill Drill, a compact cast iron unit with functionality like a small pillar drill (Fig. 4). Data were generated during straight parallel milling of hardened steel workpieces, a widely used process for shaping hard materials. Five steel blocks of dimensions 20 × 35 × 35 mm were used, with Fig. 5a showing unprocessed blocks and Fig. 5b displaying a machined workpiece.

The experimental platform is equipped with vibration sensing, with provision for additional sensing modalities. Signals were recorded using an NI-9223 module (National Instruments, USA) with custom Python software developed at the Ulsan Industrial Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. Vibration signals were acquired at a sampling frequency of 25.6 kHz. A dual-sensor configuration was used, where the primary sensor was mounted near the spindle to capture machine dynamics, while an auxiliary sensor on the motor supported stable data acquisition Fig. 6.

In this study, only vibration signals acquired from a single accelerometer (Channel 1) were used for all analysis, model training, and evaluation. Although the experimental setup supports additional sensing modalities, these signals were not utilized in the proposed framework and do not contribute to the reported results. This ensures that the experimental pipeline is clearly defined and fully based on vibration data.

The accelerometer was mounted near the spindle assembly to capture vibration signatures generated by rotating machine components. These signals were subsequently transformed into Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) scalograms for fault diagnosis.

Milling cutting tool dataset

The milling cutting tool dataset was collected from a testbed consisting of a motor-driven shaft with a 16-tooth gear, a second shaft carrying 32 and 30 tooth gears, and a spindle powered by the 30-tooth gear with a two-flute end mill mounted for machining. Faults were introduced using laser cutting and impact methods, resulting in three conditions: bearing faults with a depth of 3 mm, gear faults caused by tooth impacts, and tool faults from blade breakage. Data were recorded under both idle and cutting states for healthy and faulty cases. Idle signals were captured 15 times for 2 s at a motor speed of 1320 RPM (22 Hz) and a spindle speed of 660 RPM (11 Hz), while cutting operations were carried out at the same speeds with a feed rate of 0.4 mm/s. Fault datasets included 20 idle and 15 cutting sessions for gears, 15 cutting sessions for tool faults, and 20 idle plus 15 cutting sessions for bearings.

In this study, vibration signals sampled at 25.6 kHz were used for dataset construction and subsequent analysis. These signals were acquired using accelerometers mounted on the testbed and recorded through an NI 9234 data acquisition module with four IEPE channels. The resulting dataset represents multiple fault categories, including bearing faults, gear faults, tool faults, and normal conditions, and is used to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed fault diagnosis framework under varying operating conditions.

Continuous wavelet transform scalograms

CWT is an effective tool for analyzing non-stationary vibration signals, as it provides simultaneous time–frequency localization. By projecting the signal onto scaled and shifted versions of a mother wavelet, CWT captures transient fault signatures that conventional Fourier analysis may overlook. In this study, raw signals acquired from vibration sensors are first examined in the time domain (Fig. 8) and then transformed into CWT scalograms (Fig. 9) for all fault classes. While the raw vibration data provide limited visual cues, the scalograms clearly highlight class-specific time–frequency energy distributions, making them highly suitable for deep learning–based feature extraction and fault classification.

Results and performance evaluation

The proposed multistage transfer learning framework demonstrates strong adaptability and robustness in fault diagnosis by progressively aligning domain features across varying operating conditions. Its strength lies in preserving transferable knowledge while selectively refining domain-specific representations, enabling high accuracy with limited labeled data and ensuring reliable performance in real-world rotating machinery applications. In this framework, the pretrained ResNet-50 model is adapted through a controlled training strategy that balances knowledge preservation and domain adaptation. Early convolutional layers retain general visual features learned from ImageNet, while deeper layers are allowed to adjust to vibration scalogram characteristics. Similar observations regarding the effectiveness of selective layer-wise adaptation for cross-domain learning have also been reported in recent studies50.

The proposed MSTL framework differs from conventional transfer learning strategies. Standard fine-tuning typically adapts a pretrained model directly from the source domain to the target domain in a single stage, which can result in unstable feature learning when the domain gap is large. In contrast, the proposed approach introduces hierarchical adaptation through intermediate RPM domains that represent physically meaningful operating conditions of the machine. By progressively transferring knowledge across these intermediate domains, the model gradually reduces domain discrepancy and learns both RPM-invariant and RPM-dependent fault features. This staged adaptation enables more stable feature refinement and ultimately improves the robustness of fault diagnosis under variable operating conditions.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, standard classification metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score are used, as defined in Eq. (8) ˗ (11). These metrics are important for assessing the model’s proficiency in correctly classifying the various fault conditions observed in the milling machine. This comprehensive approach ensures that the proposed model is well-suited for real-world industrial fault detection and diagnosis.

Comparison with state of the art models

To compare the proposed model with recent models, the Dictionary Domain Adaptation Transformer (DDAT) proposed by Zhang et al.53 is a recent advancement in domain adaptation for fault diagnosis, specifically designed to overcome the limitations of batch-wise discrepancy estimation that often reduce the effectiveness of existing DA models. By constructing a feature dictionary from multi-batch data, DDAT provides a more accurate estimation of the distribution gap between source and target domains, thereby reducing the sensitivity to random sampling in individual batches. Coupled with a domain-shared transformer backbone, the framework can capture long-range dependencies and extract domain-invariant features, which enhances its ability to transfer knowledge across machines and conditions. In our experiments, DDAT consistently achieved strong classification performance across all spindle speeds, with accuracies ranging from approximately 94.10% to 96.30% and F1-scores exceeding 93%, demonstrating its effectiveness in mitigating cross-RPM domain shifts as shown in Table 3. Furthermore, in our evaluation on the totally unseen milling cutting tool dataset, the quantitative results presented in Table 3 and the visual evidence from the confusion matrices (Fig. 11f–j) and t-SNE plots (Fig. 12f–j) confirm that DDAT delivers strong baseline performance, successfully reducing cross-RPM variability and providing stable domain alignment. However, some shortcomings are evident: occasional misclassifications occur between spectrally similar fault types (e.g., GF and TF), and the t-SNE feature distributions, while generally well separated, reveal overlaps and less compact clustering across classes. These observations suggest that while DDAT improves adaptation robustness compared with conventional methods, its discriminative capacity is somewhat limited under challenging cross-speed conditions. In contrast, the proposed MSTL framework addresses these challenges by progressively adapting features across hierarchical stages, which allows the model to preserve transferable low-level features while refining high-level representations to the target domain. As shown in the same table and figures, the MSTL approach produces clearer inter-class separability, more compact clusters, and a significant reduction in cross-class misclassifications, thereby delivering higher diagnostic reliability across all RPM settings. Importantly, these improvements are achieved on completely unseen test data, underscoring the strong generalization ability of the proposed model. Taken together, the results highlight DDAT as a valuable and competitive baseline for cross-machine fault diagnosis, while also demonstrating that the multistage adaptation strategy in the proposed framework effectively builds upon DDAT’s strengths and addresses its weaknesses, resulting in superior stability, robustness, and fault classification performance under varying spindle speeds.

Secondly, the Interpretable Domain Adaptation Transformer (IDAT) introduced by Liu et al.54 was designed to enhance fault diagnosis by coupling domain adaptation with an interpretable attention mechanism. Unlike many conventional transfer learning frameworks, IDAT emphasizes transparency in its decision-making process by averaging multi-head attention maps to reveal the contribution of different features, while at the same time reducing domain discrepancies across operating conditions. When evaluated on the unseen milling cutting tool dataset, the results in Table 3 show that IDAT delivers consistently strong performance, with accuracies ranging from 91.48% to 97.97% and macro F1-scores between 90.64% and 97.90% across RPMs. At lower spindle speeds (600 and 660 RPM), the model maintains balanced precision and recall, while at 720 RPM its performance dips due to greater domain shifts, as reflected in increased cross-class confusion. However, the accuracy recovers at higher speeds (1320 and 1440 RPM), where IDAT achieves close to 98% accuracy and F1-score. The visual analyses provide further insight. The confusion matrices (Fig. 11k–o) reveal that IDAT generally classifies fault categories correctly but struggles in certain cases, particularly when distinguishing between spectrally similar faults such as GF and TF. The t-SNE plots (Fig. 12k–o) demonstrate that while IDAT learns domain-invariant representations and forms distinguishable class clusters, the feature groups are not always compact and some overlap persists, particularly under mid-range speeds. These outcomes illustrate that IDAT successfully combines interpretability with domain adaptation, offering a meaningful advance over earlier black-box approaches. At the same time, the comparative analysis with the proposed MSTL framework highlights opportunities for improvement: the latter achieves clearer class separation, minimizes residual misclassifications, and provides more stable performance across all RPM conditions.

Finally, the Multilevel Domain Alignment Adaptation-Based Transfer Fault Diagnosis Method (MDAAT) proposed by Wang et al.55 addresses the challenge of large distribution discrepancies that occur when source and target domains originate from different machines. By combining multilevel alignment with intraclass spatial reconstitution, MDAAT enhances robustness under significant domain shifts and achieves competitive diagnostic performance. On the totally unseen milling cutting tool dataset, the quantitative results in Table 3 and the visual evidence from the confusion matrices (Fig. 11p–t) and t-SNE plots (Fig. 12p–t) confirm that MDAAT adapts reasonably well across RPMs, often exceeding 95% accuracy at lower and mid speeds. However, performance drops at 1440 RPM (91.26% accuracy, 90.70% F1-score), where misclassifications between spectrally similar classes become more evident. The confusion matrices highlight residual errors at higher speeds, while the t-SNE representations show clusters that, although well separated, still exhibit partial overlaps and reduced compactness under challenging conditions. In contrast, the proposed MSTL framework demonstrates clear advantages under the same conditions. The MSTL confusion matrices show substantially fewer cross-class misclassifications across all RPMs, while the t-SNE visualizations reveal compact, non-overlapping clusters that remain stable even at 1440 RPM. These improvements are consistent with the quantitative metrics, where the proposed model maintains accuracies above 97% and achieves perfect or near-perfect class-wise precision, recall, and F1-scores under most speeds. Importantly, these gains are achieved on completely unseen test data, confirming not only the robustness of the progressive adaptation strategy but also its superior generalization capability compared with MDAAT. This comparative evidence underscores that while MDAAT enhances cross-machine transferability beyond single-level alignment methods, the proposed MSTL framework further improves diagnostic reliability by systematically refining features across stages and maintaining consistent stability across all RPM conditions.

The MMD results indicate a significant distribution discrepancy between ImageNet features and vibration scalograms, as reflected by the high MMD value between the ImageNet pretrained model and the domain-pretrained model (0.766). After fine-tuning on the target domain, the discrepancy with respect to ImageNet decreases (0.699), suggesting that the learned representation becomes more adapted to the vibration scalogram characteristics. Meanwhile, the moderate MMD value between the pretrained and fine-tuned models (0.273) indicates that the fine-tuning stage adjusts the feature representation while preserving the general structure learned during the initial domain pretraining.

In summary, across all evaluations, the proposed MSTL framework consistently outperformed state of the art methods, including DDAT, IDAT, and MDAAT, achieving more reliable inspection performance as reflected by higher accuracy, precision, and F1 scores across all rotational speed domains. Unlike these approaches, which exhibit residual misclassification and degraded inspection stability under cross speed conditions, the proposed MSTL framework delivers clearer fault separability in the learned feature space, more compact and physically consistent signal representations, and stable fault discrimination even on completely unseen operating conditions. Furthermore, the performance of the proposed framework depends on the appropriate selection of intermediate operating domains that are physically relevant to the target inspection condition, as poorly matched intermediate domains may limit effective feature transfer. These findings confirm that the progressive multistage adaptation strategy enhances inspection robustness, generalization capability, and diagnostic reliability, making the proposed approach well suited for vibration based non-destructive evaluation of rotating machinery under variable operating speeds. Although the present study evaluates the proposed framework across multiple machine components, the methodology can also be applied to component-specific monitoring scenarios. For example, in tool condition monitoring applications, vibration signals associated with cutting tool degradation can be converted into CWT scalograms and analyzed using the same multistage transfer learning strategy across different cutting speeds or machining conditions. In such cases, the intermediate domains would represent variations in operating parameters relevant to the specific component being monitored56.

Conclusions

This study presented a multistage transfer learning (MSTL) framework for vibration-based non-destructive fault diagnosis under variable operating conditions. The proposed approach addresses domain discrepancies through progressive adaptation across intermediate rotational speeds, enabling effective alignment between generic source representations and target-specific fault characteristics. By combining pretrained deep features with selective layer-wise fine-tuning, the framework captures both invariant and speed-dependent fault signatures, resulting in improved generalization across unseen operating conditions. Experimental results confirm that the MSTL framework provides stable convergence and reliable fault discrimination, consistently outperforming conventional single-stage transfer learning approaches. The model achieves classification accuracies ranging from 97.70% to 100.00% across multiple rotational speed domains, demonstrating strong robustness under limited labeled data scenarios. These results validate the effectiveness of progressive domain adaptation for enhancing diagnostic reliability in practical industrial environments.

Despite these advantages, the framework has certain limitations. Its performance depends on the availability of physically relevant intermediate operating conditions to enable effective feature transfer, and the multistage adaptation process introduces additional computational complexity compared to conventional approaches. Furthermore, the selection of trainable layers remains dependent on domain similarity, which may vary across different machines and operating conditions. Future work will focus on developing systematic strategies for automatic intermediate domain selection and adaptive layer optimization based on domain similarity measures. In addition, the framework can be extended toward multimodal sensing, online adaptive diagnosis, and integration with digital twin systems. These directions will support scalable, real-time, and more reliable condition monitoring, while enabling future extensions toward prognostic tasks such as degradation modeling and remaining useful life prediction.
