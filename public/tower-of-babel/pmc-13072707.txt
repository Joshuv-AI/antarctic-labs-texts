Oxidative stress in neurodegeneration: from a simple insult to a dynamic regulator
Authors: Lan Zhang, Xinyue Zhai, Yalong Yan, Lihong Xiang, Yue Zhang, Xinying Zhou, Lianmei Cai, Zixi Tang, Yiyuan Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the central role of oxidative stress in neurodegenerative diseases, and to explore its dynamic regulatory features, underlying signaling pathways, and molecular mechanisms, as well as advanced technological strategies for antioxidant intervention.MethodsThis review comprehensively evaluated existing literature on oxidative stress in neurodegenerative diseases. It analyzed key regulatory pathways (Nrf2, Keap1, AMPK, mTOR) and molecular processes, including ferroptosis, NETosis, and mitochondrial quality control systems, along with oxidative damage to DNA, lipids, and proteins. The review also assessed advanced technological approaches such as subcellular organelle targeting, nanocarrier delivery systems (e.g. gold nanoparticles, liposomes for glutathione delivery), and single-cell/spatial omics technologies (e.g. single-cell redoxomics, spatial transcriptomics).ResultsOxidative stress exhibits dynamic features, generating protective signals in early stages but transitioning into destructive factors later. A major obstacle for current antioxidant therapies is the blood–brain barrier. Breakthrough strategies identified include precision targeting at the subcellular level, functionalized nanoparticles for efficient antioxidant delivery, and the integration of single-cell redoxomics with spatial transcriptomics to identify specific biomarkers and enable personalized treatments.DiscussionBy integrating novel molecular mechanisms and advanced technological resources, this review redefines oxidative stress not as a singular event but as a complex, dynamically regulated system in neurodegenerative diseases. This reconceptualization provides new perspectives for developing targeted and personalized therapeutic interventions.Keywords: Oxidative stress, neurodegenerative diseases, cellular signaling pathways, mitochondrial oxidative stress, antioxidants, nanocarrier delivery, regulation of oxidative stress, macromolecular oxidative stress

Introduction

Chronic conditions that affect the central nervous system are referred to as neurodegenerative diseases [1] and are characterized pathologically by a loss of neurons or malfunctioning neurons. Some of these are Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), Huntington's disease (HD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [2,3]. Around the world's incidence is gradually rising, there are not enough early diagnosis and treatment means for them, so this situation has an increasing influence on people's lives and society, and these are the most important public health problems.

Oxidative stress is a form of neurodegeneration. When many of these different disorders are observed, and many more reactive oxygen species are produced [4]. A large number of reactive nitrogen species are produced, which throws everything off on what oxidative things are going on and what antioxidant defense your body has too. Endogenous antioxidants, such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx) [5], superoxide dismutase (SOD) [6] and catalase (CAT), safeguard cell redox homeostasis by deactivating ROS and RNS [7] (Figure 1). When serving as signaling molecules at normal levels, ROS and RNS in the presence of oxidation can have adverse reactions with proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, leading to cellular and organelle damage and various oxidative stress-related pathologies [8]. However, oxidative stress is related not only to imbalances in ROS production vs. clearance, but also to fluctuations of certain ROS subtypes (such as the mitochondrial superoxide anion and extracellular hydrogen peroxide H2O2) [9], and to connections between oxidative stress and neuroinflammation and cell death to form a complex network.

When superoxide anions, hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and other substances are overloaded, while antioxidants such as nitric oxide, nitrogen dioxide, and peroxynitrite decrease, an imbalance occurs, leading to an increase in ROS in the body.

In AD, ROS alter the amount of lipids, DNA, RNA and proteins in the brain [10]. Oxidative stress-linked DNA harm indicators are connected to overall tau and amyloid-β1-42 (Aβ42) levels [11]. Regression analysis showed that PC4 (decreased oxidative stress caused by DNA damage and increased oxidative stress caused by antioxidants) was the most related to the tau protein level in midlife. Oxidative-stress biomarkers include F2-isoprostanes of lipid peroxidation [12], malondialdehyde, and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), which covalently modify proteins through specific amino acid residues, and the nucleic acid oxidation products 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and 8-hydroxyguanosine (8-OHG) [13,14]. Inducible heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) is elevated in the brains of AD patients, indicating a close relationship with oxidative stress. There is a regulatory interaction between lncRNAs [15] and oxidative stress in PD pathogenesis [16], where oxidative stress alters lncRNA expression patterns, and then lncRNAs regulate genes involved in oxidative stress pathways. This reciprocal interaction worsens damage to the mitochondria and the neuroinflammatory response, which together lead to the death of neurons [17]. Therefore, fine-tuning the equilibrium between certain lncRNAs and oxidative stress reactions may become a feasible therapy for PD. In addition, oxidative stress can independently regulate the abnormal phosphorylation and accumulation of alpha-synuclein (α-syn), leading to increased neurotoxicity [18]. Moreover, oxidative stress can cause neuroinflammation by stimulating microglia to produce and secrete interleukins that increase α-syn expression. In HD, disease-related oxidative stress with mHtt pathology is characterized by protein oxidation, metabolic energy failure, disturbed metal ion homeostasis, DNA injury, and mitochondrial malfunction [19]. Abnormal mHtt with a lengthy polyglutamine extension at the start of the protein increases intracellular oxidative stress. Oxidative stress also mediates inflammation in HD, which may occur before the appearance of typical symptoms [20]. In ALS, where TDP-43 is a primary biomarker, it has been reported that the abnormal aggregation of TDP-43 in mouse and human-derived neuronal cells causes an increase in oxidative stress [21,22]. Aggregated TDP-43 sequesters particular miRNAs and proteins, producing abnormal protein levels for some and functionally depleting others. This dysfunction disturbs the regulation of mitochondrial proteins that are controlled by the nuclear genome, which leads to an increase in oxidative stress. Oxidative stress can worsen the severity of the motor cortex and has been linked to psychiatric disorders.

In addition, emerging mechanisms, such as mitophagy impairment, are associated with neuronal deficits in neurodegenerative diseases. The brain heavily depends on energy metabolism during normal operations. Mitochondria are the primary source of ROS in the body [23], as they are organelles that produce energy through oxidative phosphorylation and participate in various redox reactions. Both HD and ALS show considerable increases in oxidative stress markers and mitochondrial flaws in neural tissue. In AD, affected brain regions exhibit increased oxidative damage and mitochondrial dysfunction. For example, under normal physiological conditions, PINK1/Parkin mitophagy eliminates aberrant aggregated pathologic proteins in the brain [24], and mitochondrial dysfunction results in abnormal beclin1, LC3II, PINK1, and Parkin expression, leading to ROS accumulation. PINK1 is upstream of Parkin in PD and regulates mitochondrial function [25]. Additionally, the mitochondrial production of ROS is linked to synaptic pruning, PINK1 can work together with the NRF2 signaling pathway to control calcium levels, which affects cognitive dysfunction, and the downregulation of ROS-scavenging proteins such as glutathione S-transferase in the synaptic cleft is also a cause of synaptic dysfunction [26].

Systematic deconstruction of novel molecular bridges formed by oxidative stress (e.g. lipid oxidation, protein oxidation, nucleic acid oxidation, ferroptosis, and NETosis) in neurodegenerative diseases [27], as well as the latest oxidative stress mechanisms in these diseases. It specifically focuses on breakthrough interventions (CRISPR-edited antioxidant circuits and stem cell-derived exosome delivery), as well as defenses that are activated as a result of these emerging mechanisms, rather than repeating the same classic antioxidants.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed and Google Scholar from their inception to November 2025. Preprint data or ‘ahead-of-print’ articles were not included. The search queries employed Boolean operators (AND, OR) to combine keywords from three conceptual groups: (1) ‘Oxidation’ and ‘neurodegenerative in AD, affected regions diseases’ (e.g. ‘Neurodegenerative disease,’ ‘Alzheimer's disease,’ ‘Parkinson's disease,’ ‘Multiple sclerosis,’ ‘Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis’). The search was limited to English-language original research and review articles. After screening, 178 documents were included for analysis, comprising both foundational studies and highly-cited papers from 2021 to 2025.

Molecular bridges linking oxidative stress to neurodegeneration

Oxidative damage to macromolecules

Lipid peroxidation

The cell membranes of neurons in the brain are primarily composed of lipids, which are essential for maintaining cellular integrity and supporting functions such as signal transmission and synaptic plasticity. [28]. However, owing to their chemical structure, these lipid membranes are highly susceptible to oxidation by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and other free radicals. ROS, including reactive nitrogen species (RNS) and reactive lipid species (RLS), participate in various cellular processes; however, their increased concentrations shift the balance between signaling and damage, contributing to neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, PD, and other age-related pathologies [29,30]. In early stages, ROS can play a beneficial role in cellular signaling through a phenomenon known as hormesis—where low to moderate levels of ROS activate adaptive responses that promote cell survival and function. Prolonged or excessive ROS exposure leads to oxidative damage, especially lipid oxidation, which produces secondary products such as lipid peroxides (e.g. MDA, 4-HNE) and alkoxyl radicals. These molecules trigger a cascade of destructive events, including protein dysfunction, DNA damage, and inflammatory responses [31]. In this context, ROS levels act as a dynamic regulator, wherein low levels might trigger protective signaling pathways, while excessive ROS levels lead to irreversible damage. Specifically, ROS can promote the phosphorylation of protein kinases such as Akt, which plays a key role in cellular growth and survival. The Akt2 isoform, when activated by ROS-induced lipid peroxides such as 4-HNE [32], translocates to the membrane, attaching to phosphatidylinositol (3, 4, 5)-trisphosphate, and is phosphorylated at critical residues (e.g. Thr-309 and Ser-474). This activation initiates downstream signaling, including the phosphorylation of GSK3β, a kinase that phosphorylates the tau protein—a hallmark event in neurodegeneration. The interaction between 4-HNE and the Akt2-GSK3β pathway thus indirectly modulates tau protein levels [33,34], potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of tauopathies (Figure 2).

The lipid pathway increases 4-HNE-Akt2-Tau protein phosphorylation, ultimately triggering pathological and inflammatory responses; the protein pathway primarily acts through modifications affecting signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK; the nucleic acid pathway mainly involves ROS-induced mtDNA damage, which, through activating the downstream cGAS-STING signaling pathway, triggers inflammasome activation and an inflammatory cascade, ultimately mediating the onset of neurodegenerative diseases.

Importantly, the role of ROS in lipid peroxidation is not merely destructive. At certain thresholds, ROS-mediated lipid oxidation can enhance the immune response and cell survival via adaptive oxidative signaling. For example, the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in membrane phospholipids leads to the generation of oxidized phospholipids (OxPCs) [35], which have been shown to act as biomarkers of oxidative stress in neurodegenerative diseases. Interestingly, OxPCs may also promote inflammatory responses through the activation of the cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) pathway and the release of cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and GM-CSF [36], further driving disease progression [37,38]. However, as oxidative stress escalates, the protective benefits of ROS are outweighed by the detrimental effects of overabundant lipid peroxidation. The accumulation of lipid peroxides not only damages cellular structures but also disrupts calcium homeostasis [39], particularly within synaptic membranes. This disruption, often observed in neurodegenerative diseases, impairs neurotransmission and contributes to the loss of neuronal function. Moreover, ROS-induced damage contributes to neurodegenerative diseases, and microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, play a crucial role in counteracting this oxidative stress. By clearing oxidized products and modulating inflammatory responses, microglia can help limit the pathological effects of ROS. Therefore, the interplay among ROS, lipid peroxidation, and microglial activity underlines the dynamic nature of oxidative stress, balancing between its protective and damaging roles depending on its concentration and context.

Protein oxidation

The cellular response to oxidative stress is a complex and dynamic process [40,41], in which reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a dual role depending on their concentration and the cellular context. At low concentrations, ROS act as signaling molecules, triggering adaptive responses crucial for cell survival and function. The proadaptive effects of ROS have been extensively studied, with numerous studies demonstrating that low to moderate levels of ROS can activate multiple signaling pathways, including those associated with metabolism, proliferation, and immune responses. For example, H₂O₂ [42], one of the most stable and diffusible ROS, has been shown to activate redox-sensitive transcription factors such as NF-κB [43], which are essential for immune responses and cellular adaptation to stress. However, when ROS levels exceed a certain threshold, they shift from adaptive signaling to causing damage. Proteins, as one of the primary targets of oxidative modification, undergo various modifications, such as carbonylation and disulfide bond formation, which can disrupt protein folding, induce misfolding, and lead to functional loss. For instance, in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, excessive ROS promote the aggregation of Aβ plaques, exacerbating neuronal damage. Similarly, in diabetic retinopathy [44], accumulated ROS due to mitochondrial dysfunction trigger apoptosis and endothelial cell dysfunction through proteins such as DRP1 and Bax.

Moreover, ROS-induced protein modifications may form positive feedback loops, further amplifying oxidative stress [45] and leading to further cellular damage. Therefore, the delicate balance between ROS-mediated signaling and damage is critical in determining cell fate. Under low oxidative stress, ROS act as beneficial regulators of cellular processes, whereas excessive oxidative stress leads to cellular dysfunction and the onset of disease. In conclusion, ROS function as dynamic modulators of protein function, switching between signaling and damage based on their concentration and the cellular environment. Understanding this balance is crucial for elucidating how oxidative stress contributes to normal cellular regulation and pathological processes in cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and metabolic disorders.

Nucleic acid damage

DNA continuously encounters diverse challenges from both the external environment and intracellular processes, leading to various forms of damage, such as alkylation, oxidation, base mismatches, and strand breaks [46–48]. It is estimated that each cell suffers tens of thousands of DNA lesions daily. Although multiple DNA repair pathways exist to maintain genomic stability, accumulated damage can alter DNA sequences, trigger cell death, and contribute to aging and tumorigenesis [49]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is closely associated with hepatitis B or C virus infection [50], aflatoxin B1, and nitrosamines, all of which induce hepatocyte DNA damage and impair repair systems. Cells are constantly exposed to an oxygen-rich environment, and molecular oxygen, due to its chemical reactivity, frequently generates oxidative DNA lesions [51]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anions and hydroxyl radicals, play a central role in this process. ROS production is further exacerbated by disturbances in the pro-oxidant/antioxidant balance or external stressors such as cigarette smoke [52] and ultraviolet radiation [53]. Among oxidative stress biomarkers, 8-OHdG is widely used to quantify DNA oxidative damage [54].

Importantly, ROS exhibit dose-dependent effects on nucleic acids. Low ROS levels can activate DNA repair pathways, such as OGG1-mediated base excision repair, thereby maintaining genomic integrity. In contrast, excessive ROS induces mitochondrial DNA oxidation (Ox-mtDNA), which is released into the cytoplasm, promoting NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and cGAS-STING-mediated inflammation [55]. Within mitochondria, Ox-mtDNA may either undergo repair by OGG1 or be cleaved by endonucleases such as FEN1, with the resulting fragments exported via mPTP-VDAC channels to trigger inflammatory signaling [56]. The balance between DNA repair and Ox-mtDNA-mediated inflammation reflects a dynamic ‘protective–damaging’ switch, emphasizing the role of ROS as a dynamic regulator in cellular homeostasis. Notably, impaired OGG1 function is associated with increased NLRP3 activity, elevated 8-oxoG-DNA accumulation, and heightened susceptibility to chronic diseases such as atherosclerosis and AD [57].

Dysregulation of cell signaling pathways

New perspectives on the canonical pathway: the Nrf2 antioxidant axis

Cellular signaling pathways are essential molecular systems that allow cells to perceive external signals and adjust their activities accordingly [58]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are key signaling intermediates that regulate various cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and metabolism. ROS accumulation has been implicated in the activation of numerous stress-related signaling pathways, including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway (involving ERK, JNK, and p38), which coordinates cellular responses to inflammation and cell death [59–61]. Additionally, ROS influence the NF-κB pathway, driving inflammatory and immune responses, and regulate the PI3K/Akt pathway [62], which determines cell fate between proliferation and apoptosis under oxidative stress conditions. The Nrf2/Keap1 pathway plays a central role in the antioxidant defense system [63,64]. Under normal conditions, Nrf2 is kept inactive in the cytoplasm through its interaction with Keap1 [65]. However, increased ROS levels disrupt this complex, leading to Nrf2 dissociation and its translocation to the nucleus. In the nucleus, Nrf2 activates the antioxidant response element (ARE) located in the promoters of specific genes [66], leading to the transcription of various cytoprotective enzymes, such as glutathione peroxidase (GPX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1) [67]. These enzymes work together to mitigate oxidative damage caused by ROS.

Importantly, the effects of ROS are concentration-dependent, and they can exhibit hormetic effects, where low to moderate ROS levels can act as signaling molecules to activate adaptive responses, while high levels lead to cellular damage. At low concentrations, ROS serve as signaling intermediates that activate pathways such as Nrf2 to promote cell survival and adaptation. However, at higher concentrations, ROS accumulate and overwhelm antioxidant defenses, leading to oxidative damage, inflammation, and apoptosis. This biphasic response underscores the dynamic regulatory role of ROS in maintaining cellular homeostasis and activating survival mechanisms. In neurodegenerative disorders, oxidative stress can lead to neuroinflammation and cell death, often due to disruptions in the NF-κB and Nrf2/Keap1 pathways. These disruptions can be counteracted by additional defense mechanisms, such as heat shock proteins (HSPs) and FOXO signaling networks [68,69]. Recent studies have also revealed that post-translational modifications such as acetylation and deacetylation of Nrf2 can modulate its activity in contradictory ways. Acetylation enhances Nrf2's transcriptional activity, while deacetylation reduces it, creating a fine-tuned regulation of Nrf2 activity. The interplay between Nrf2 and SIRT1, a deacetylase, is particularly crucial, as both directly or indirectly regulate cellular defense mechanisms via complex feedback loops and signaling intermediates such as p62 and PGC-1α [70] (Figure 3).

Thus, the dynamic regulation of ROS and their hormetic effects on Nrf2 activation underscore the importance of maintaining an optimal balance between ROS-mediated signaling and oxidative damage. Both Nrf2 and SIRT1 play essential roles in this process, coordinating a variety of cellular responses to ensure cell survival and function under oxidative stress conditions.

Crosstalk of emerging pathways: the AMPK-mTOR hub

As we are coming to understand how pathogenic communication takes shape in a more concrete way, it shows the importance of the processes of redox reactions that help control cell balance or homeostasis. The central AMPK-mTOR signal hub has emerged as a key intersection between having enough energy and keeping an eye on oxidative pressures [71,72]. At rest, normal amounts of ROS may act as signaling molecules; however, high levels of ROS can result in oxidative stress, which prevents the activation of AMPK. This inhibition occurs in several ways, including having an immediate effect on things like LKB1 [73], causing changes to the cysteine parts of AMPK, possibly adjusting to phosphatase action, and lowering the level of phosphorylation at the Thr172 spot, thus making AMPK work less well [74]. mTOR, this is another kinase that's important in lots of different functions, such as growing cells, killing them, and recycling old ones, and its job can change. mTOR phosphorylation activates the mTOR signaling pathway and suppresses autophagy [75]. AMPK is an upstream regulator that acts as a negative regulator of mTOR, and indirectly activates autophagy.

Autophagy inhibition is harmful for the clearance of toxic substrates such as dysfunctional and misfolded proteins. These undegraded pathogenic proteins, such as Aβ in AD and a-syn in PD, produce more ROS to generate more of these proteins [76]. A vicious positive feedback loop was formed. Low ROS levels induce LC3-II accumulation and autophagy by inhibiting ATG4 via Cys77/81 oxidation [77]. High ROS levels inhibit autophagosome formation by degrading ATG3, ATG7, and ATG10 and activating mTORC1 to suppress autophagy. Studies have shown that environmental contaminants, such as arsenic, abnormally activate autophagy through the ROS-ERK pathway [78], and that endocrine disruptors impair mitophagy. Finally, oxidative stress-triggered pathways, through AMPK inhibition and overactivation of mTOR, actively induce the generation and accumulation of neurotoxic proteins and the occurrence of neurodegenerative pathological changes. This system provides big knowledge concerning how neurodegenerative conditions work and points to this signaling center as a hopeful point for intervention.

In the context of oxidative stress, there exists an intricate regulatory network and interactions among Nrf2, AMPK, and mTOR. AMPK can alleviate oxidative stress by inhibiting mTOR activity, while its activation also enhances Nrf2 activity, promoting antioxidant responses [79]. Nrf2, in turn, protects cells from oxidative damage by initiating the expression of antioxidant enzymes. mTOR plays a balancing role in this network, as its excessive activation may exacerbate oxidative stress [80]. Therefore, the interplay between the Nrf2, AMPK, and mTOR pathways determines cell survival or death under oxidative stress conditions.

Regulatory roles of non-coding RNAs

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a group of non-coding RNA molecules that can perform some functions in the regulation of genes and diseases at the post-transcriptional level, and miR-210 is particularly well known as a key regulator in many diseases. Studies have shown that in colorectal cancer cells, miR-210 expression not only greatly reduces clonogenic and proliferation capabilities [81], but also halts the cell cycle at the G2/M phase. At the same time, overexpression of this miRNA also increases the intracellular ROS content.

Additionally, it triggers apoptosis by increasing the pro-apoptotic protein Bim and boosting the activity of Caspase-2. Pharmacological inhibition of ROS generation also markedly attenuates miR-210-mediated apoptosis [82]. In contrast, miR-210 inhibitors decrease cell survival [83], inhibit angiogenesis, and increase ROS levels; miR-210 and ROS have a positive feedback loop, and miR-210 upregulates ROS generation by downregulating ISCU2 expression [84]. Previously, it was suggested that ROS could serve as an upstream modulator of miR-210 expression. In vascular dementia, exosomes secreted by neural stem cells in a hypoxic state can transfer miR-210 to OGD neurons, enhancing neuronal viability and neurite outgrowth and branching. This process also promotes neuronal survival, improves cognitive function, increases synaptic plasticity and dendritic spine density, and reduces ROS generation and neuronal apoptosis. In addition, miR-210 can promote the transformation of microglia into the M2 phenotype and inhibit M1 polarization [84], thereby reducing neuroinflammation and promoting the growth of neurites.

Emerging molecular mediators

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a newly identified form of regulated cell death that occurs through an iron-dependent mechanism and depletion of GPX4 activity. This leads to the accumulation of lipid peroxides that, when catalyzed by iron ions, undergo harmful peroxidative reactions, ultimately leading to cell death. This process is particularly significant in diseases associated with oxidative stress, where ferroptosis replaces other forms of cell death, such as apoptosis. For instance, in cardiomyocytes [85], oxidative stress triggers ferroptosis instead of apoptosis, revealing a link between oxidative stress and ferroptosis in heart cells [86].

The underlying mechanism involves organic peroxides (such as tBHP and CHP but not H2O2), which induce lipid peroxidation by depleting GSH and degrading GPX4 (Figure 4). This is accompanied by the buildup of labile iron, which may be caused by increased levels of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and decreased activity of the transcriptional repressor Bach1. This process accelerates iron release by breaking down heme. Interestingly, oxidative stress directly induces the transfer of HO-1 into mitochondria, resulting in mitochondrial iron overload and the accumulation of lipophilic ROS. Experimental results show that silencing mitochondrial iron and ROS by overexpressing mitochondrial ferritin (FTMT) and mitochondrial catalase (mCAT) can successfully block ferroptosis [87].

In neurological diseases, neurons are highly susceptible to hypoxia-induced oxidative stress, which can lead to ferroptosis [88,89]. This type of injury can be alleviated by supplementing copper, which acts through the superoxide dismutase (SOD1)-GPX4 axis [90]. Under conditions such as PD, ferroptosis is thought to be triggered by GSH deficiency, excess calcium influx, mitochondrial ROS (mROS), and cytosolic ROS (cROS). These factors contribute to aberrant iron metabolism, leading to neurodegeneration in diseases such as PD [91]. Additionally, the sevoflurane anesthesia used in surgery is associated with perioperative neurocognitive disorders, which are closely linked to the overexpression of T-box transcription factor 2 (Tbx2) in the hippocampus. Tbx2 inhibits the BDNF-induced Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway, leading to the accumulation of ROS, lipid peroxidation, and neuronal loss in the hippocampus [92].

NETosis

NETosis, a form of cell death specific to neutrophils, plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including AD [93]. During NETosis, neutrophils expel their DNA into the extracellular space [94], forming structures known as NETs (neutrophil extracellular traps), which are essential for fighting infections. These NETs are composed of histones, myeloperoxidase, and neutrophil elastase, all of which help combat pathogens. In the context of diseases such as AD, NETosis is triggered by a combination of exogenous and endogenous factors, leading to the generation of ROS and the activation of PAD4, which causes rupture of the nuclear membrane and the release of chromatin. This process is critical for the formation of NETs.

In AD, oxidative stress and DNA damage are closely linked to the regulation of NETosis. ROS, especially those generated by NADPH oxidase (NOX) and mitochondria, are key to activating signaling pathways such as MAPK kinases (including ERK, p38, and JNK) [95]. The activation of these kinases is crucial for NET formation. Studies have shown that oxidative DNA damage repair mechanisms play a regulatory role in NETosis, with the inhibition of DNA repair proteins such as PARP and APE1 potentially enhancing NET formation. Interestingly, basal ROS levels are essential for spontaneous NETosis in AD [96–98]. DNA repair mechanisms, particularly the base excision repair (BER) pathway, have a dual role: while DNA repair proteins inhibit spontaneous NET formation under normal conditions, their inhibition may promote spontaneous NET formation, thus contributing to neuroinflammatory processes in AD.

Furthermore, NETosis is closely tied to neuroinflammation in AD. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in neutrophils triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, further amplifying neuroinflammation [99,100]. Neutrophils infiltrate the blood‒brain barrier (BBB), where they are concentrated in regions such as the cortex and hippocampus near Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles [101]. In these areas, neutrophils release ROS, form NETs, and secrete inflammatory mediators, contributing to classic AD pathology, including BBB disruption, increased amyloid load, activated microglia, chronic inflammation, neurodegeneration, and cognitive decline.

Mechanisms of oxidative stress in neurodegenerative diseases

Alzheimer's disease: temporal ROS dynamics

Alzheimer's disease is currently a very prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, and its major pathological features can be roughly classified into two categories: the first is the pathological aggregation of Aβ peptides or hyperphosphorylation of tau proteins [102], and the second is the loss of neurons and synapses. Taken together, these pathological changes result in general brain atrophy and dysfunction. ROS can also be generated from plaques where Aβ deposits, and Aβ itself has been shown to generate ROS in a metal-dependent manner, such as Fe²⁺ or Cu²⁺ [103]. Mitochondrial dysfunction causes an increase in ROS production, and an increase in ROS production leads to mitochondrial damage. This in turn promotes the accumulation of Aβ peptides. Thus, a vicious cycle is formed. These findings were corroborated in AD mice [104–106], and in post mortem brains from humans. Aβ together with high levels of ROS, leads to progressive oxidative damage and finally cell death (Table 1).

Increased levels of biomarkers of oxidative stress appear before Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), suggesting that oxidative stress is one of the first events in AD. NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) is tightly linked to vascular and inflammatory processes in all tissues, including the brain, and plays opposing roles in the early and late stages of AD. In the early phase, ROS produced by NOX2-activated microglia is involved in Aβ clearance. In the late phase, chronic NOX2 overactivation produces excessive ROS, resulting in neuronal death [108]. Specifically, it is advanced aging, Aβ aggregation and inflammatory cytokines work on NOX2 to activate inflammatory microglia via two pathways: the P38-ERK-IL-1β pathway and the TNF-α pathway to cause neuronal damage [109]. In neurons, NOX2 can be induced by IL-6 and LPS to activate downstream ERK-PLA2 to release ARA or induce ER stress, thereby causing neuronal injury. In summary, oxidative stress promotes AD pathology through multiple targets and pathways, and emerging technologies, such as new nanomaterials that exert antioxidant effects, are promising new antioxidants for AD.

Parkinson's disease: α-synuclein oxidation

Parkinson's disease is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disease, and occurs mainly in populations over 65 years of age [110]. In terms of clinical description, the presence of this condition is defined as the occurrence of accompanying motor and non-motor diseases due to deterioration of nerve cells that secrete dopamine as well as decreased dopamine secretion. Given that it is a disorder of multifactorial etiology involving genetic susceptibility as well as environmental exposures, recessive mutations in α-synuclein and PARK1 have particularly pathophysiological relevance [111]. The accumulation of reactive oxygen species and neuroinflammation has been identified as fundamental pathogenic elements in PD development.

In eukaryotic cells, ROS are involved in selective signaling processes that target sulfhydryl residues in particular proteins. During the pathological progression of PD [112,113], substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons have significantly lower glutathione levels and thus lose their ability to counteract oxidative stress. Simultaneously, excessive iron accumulation promotes the conversion of H2O2 to highly toxic hydroxyl radicals through the ferrous ion-catalyzed Fenton reaction, causing more oxidative damage. Functional impairments in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I not only reduce ATP synthesis but also increase the amount of oxygen radicals generated during dopamine metabolism (Table 1).

Mitochondrial dysfunction is linked to ubiquitin‒proteasome system abnormalities, and patients with sporadic PD have substantially reduced ubiquitin-activating (E1), conjugating (E2), and ligase (E3) enzyme activities in the substantia nigra, which are related to reduced complex I activity. ROS with oxidative stress produce a cycle of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta by two paths [114]: one is through a declining endogenous antioxidant protection system, so neurons can be more vulnerable to oxidative stress by residues of ROS-oxidizing dopamine derivative catechols into reactive dopamine quinones, which are precursors of endogenous neurotoxins and participate in neurotransmission and oxidation processes; another is by triggering the process of lipid peroxidation which will impair the integrity of the electron transport chain, cause DNA damage and thus finally result in mitochondrial/lysosomal deficiency as well as disruption of synaptic integrity. Significantly, ROS-induced mutations in genes such as LRRK2, DNAJC6, SYNJ1, SH3GL2, PRKN and VPS35 [115,116], have been recognized as critical elements for synaptic malfunction and Parkinson's disease.

Other diseases (ALS or HD)

Aside from AD and PD, which are the most frequently occurring neurodegenerative diseases, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and HD belong to other major categories of neurodegenerative conditions. ALS is defined as a chronic, worsening neurological condition characterized by the simultaneous deterioration of both upper and lower motor neurons and eventually leads to the progressive loss of function of the trunk, limbs, and craniofacial muscles innervated by these neurons [117]. Als is not known. RNP-binding proteins (RBPs) are more than 1000 different types of proteins that take a bit of DNA and make it an RNA, take an RNA and slice it apart [118], take an RNA and carry it somewhere else, or get it to do useful things such as building new things. They also know which RNA bits to chop and destroy. These well-orchestrated interactions trigger changes in the expression of downstream target genes. Dysfunction of RBPs is closely associated with ALS pathogenesis, and the mislocalization and aggregation of RBPs from the nucleus to the cytoplasm has been regarded as a core pathological process [119]. Oxidative stress was an age-related risk factor for ALS (Table 1).

Under experimentally induced oxidative stress, TDP-43 is often phosphorylated, mislocalized, or aggregated [120], and the ROS is increased in the TIA1 cysteine, which causes cysteine to be oxidized, stopping the stress granules from happening and resulting in apoptosis. It can also help recruit TDP-43, SFPQ, hnRNPA1, TIA1, and ATXN2 proteins to stress granules [121]. Therefore, protein aggregation may occur. Familial ALS involves a variant of the SOD1 gene, which results in free radical toxicity and motor neuron degeneration. SOD1, which is highly valued owing to its antioxidant capacity in the aerobic metabolism of motor neurons [122], catalyzes the transformation of toxic superoxide radicals (O2·-) into oxygen (O2) and H2O2.

HD is a common illness; it comes with these collections of a twisted kind of Huntington's protein called mHtt [123]. Both experimental studies using cell models and living organisms have shown that there are always specific increases in ROS as well as increased neuronal death in HD models [124]. Mitochondrial dysfunction worsens and causes the release of more free radicals. This makes it more difficult for neurons to survive. In the earlier stages, it is mostly limited to the striatum, as striatal neurons are lost at later stages, so the level of oxidative damage markers decreases. Second, from direct observation, there is clear oxidative damage in HD, as evidenced by massive depletion of succinate dehydrogenase (an enzyme within the TCA cycle) (Table 1) [124–126]. Post-mortem studies of HD patients brain showed a lack of TCA cycle enzymes; less of the mitochondrial complex II (succinate-ubiquinone reductase) was found, as well as a decrease in the activity of mitochondrial complexes II/III in the striatum, a problem only in that area and not in the cerebral cortex or cerebellum. Genes mutated in HD could directly augment mitochondrial ROS generation and lead to lasting DNA damage, highlighting the major role of oxidative stress in HD formation.

Antioxidant defense and therapeutic strategies in neurodegenerative diseases

Endogenous antioxidant systems

The mitochondrial antioxidant system

The mitochondrial antioxidant system plays a crucial role in the endogenous defense mechanisms against oxidative stress in neurodegenerative diseases [127], with SIRT3 being the primary regulator of this system. SIRT3 is essential for ATP production, metabolic regulation, ROS defense, and controlling cell fate, including death and division. As a mitochondrial deacetylase, SIRT3 directly targets and activates the mitochondrial matrix enzyme SOD2 through its deacetylase activity [128]. SOD2 is responsible for removing superoxide anions within the mitochondria. Increased SOD2 activity facilitates the conversion of superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxides, thereby reducing excessive ROS levels, which are central to oxidative stress in neurons. Thus, activating SIRT3 serves as a cellular defense mechanism, particularly in response to oxidative stress.

Under oxidative stress, SIRT3 selectively deacetylates the K271 and K290 residues of FOXO3a [129,130], while the K259 residue remains acetylated, regulating the downstream antioxidant targets of FOXO3a. This regulation enhances cellular antioxidant enzyme activity and lowers ROS levels.

Studies have demonstrated that the deletion of SIRT3 in neurons of the cerebral cortex and hippocampus in female mice expressing NEX induces a series of pathological changes. These include mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation, modifications to SOD2, increased superoxide production, metabolic reprogramming, altered neuronal excitability, and working memory dysfunction. Importantly, inducible deletion of SIRT3 in neurons leads to spatial working memory deficits in females. During Parkinson's disease (PD) progression, SIRT3 regulates mitophagy by deacetylating both PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin. A disrupted function of SIRT3 has been linked to the LRRK2-G2019S mutation commonly found in PD, further highlighting the importance of SIRT3 in redox signaling pathways that affect neuronal susceptibility [131–133]. These findings underscore the critical role of mitochondrial antioxidants in protecting neurons against oxidative damage, which contributes to the onset and progression of neurodegenerative diseases.

Autophagy regulation

Modulation of autophagic flux and its effects on cellular redox homeostasis and the control of ROS production have now become very relevant fields of study under different physiological or pathological conditions [134,135]. On the other hand, oxidative stress also triggers mitophagy, which promotes the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria, thus maintaining homeostasis in cells. Mechanistically, the generation of mitochondrial ROS with specific organelle photosensitizers can effectively activate mitophagy, and the Parkin/PINK1-mediated mitophagy pathway requires O2− production [136]; peroxiredoxin 6 (Prx6) localizes to depolarized mitochondria, regulates local redox balance, and promotes PINK1-dependent mitophagy. In contrast, mitochondrial ROS are not always required for mitophagy [137].

For example, in cytochrome c-depleted cells or in mitochondrial DNA-depleted ρ⁰ cells [138], the induction of mitophagy by staurosporine is independent of ROS production and is insensitive to antioxidants. Additionally, mild oxidative stress can selectively cause mitophagy in the absence of Drp1-dependent mitochondrial fission indicating alternative mechanisms [139]. Remarkably, various autophagy-deficient cell models display more ROS when starved for nutrients but in different ways. In cells defective in bulk autophagy, the accumulation of ROS under starvation conditions is accompanied by low levels of amino acids, low amounts of mitochondria during respiration, and fewer special proteins that deal with ROS. Under glucose deprivation, AMPK directly phosphorylates Ulk1 at Ser317 and Ser777 to activate autophagy-dependent ROS clearance [140]. In contrast, dysfunctional mitochondria accumulate and age and generate a large amount of ROS spontaneously in cells defective specifically in mitophagy. These results provide a basic distinction between the drivers of ROS accumulation in different backgrounds with autophagic deficiency.

Exogenous antioxidant interventions

Antioxidant development

Besides the body's own antioxidant system, we should also consider the exogenous antioxidant system [141]. Currently, many new antioxidants that can precisely control the redox state to eliminate oxidative stress are being studied to combat a common pathological foundation for most major diseases [142]. It is clear how necessary it was to produce antioxidants that can counteract the extra ROS both quickly and properly. In terms of developing new-generation antioxidants, the focus has shifted from ‘broad-spectrum scavenging’ to ‘targeted scavenging.’ Compounds 22–27, which are based on dietary caffeic acid, are mitochondria-targeted antioxidants. The triphenylphosphonium cation is well known for its ability to deliver active molecules into the mitochondria and thus generate antioxidant effects there [143]. SkQR1 [10-(6′-plastoquinonyl) decylrhodamine], which targets mitochondria, delays the accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau protein (Aβ) and its precursor protein APP accumulation [144].

Mitochondrial quinones, such as MitoQ and the antioxidant idebenone, have been shown success in reducing mitochondrial dysfunction in corneal endothelial cells due to accurate mitochondrial targeting [145]. In addition, strategies such as the addition of triphenylphosphonium cations, mitochondria-targeting peptides, and nanocarriers can restore mitochondrial function and increase ATP levels. Thymoquinone (2-isopropyl-5-methylbenzoquinone) [146], a 1963 discovered novel and promising antioxidant, has led researchers to produce a mitochondria-targeted compound, SkQThy [147]. It has a strong antioxidant activity in rat liver mitochondria and yeast and can reduce the production of mitochondrial H₂O₂, protect against pro-oxidant-induced oxidative damage and mitochondrial fragmentation in yeast, and increase the survival rate of the cell. MTAXs, another mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, display neuroprotective effects in Huntington's disease by regulating mitochondrial signaling pathways such as PPARs, PGC-1α, Drp1, Fis1, and SIRT-1.

In AD and PD, it has been studied the delivery of the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant apocynin with biodegradable polyimide nanoparticles into neuronal cells and the ability of the nanoformulations to protect cells from oxidative stress [148]; the nanoscale mitochondrial-targeted apocynin can improve the efficacy of mitochondria-targeted antioxidant therapy for neurodegenerative diseases [149–151]. Among the natural antioxidant candidates, supramolecular derivatives such as calixarenes, resorcinarenes, calixnaphthalenes, calixpyrroles, cucurbiturils, and porphyrins, with their free radical scavenging capabilities [152,153], have drawn attention. Among these, supramolecular chemistry is a very good scaffold for the development of new antioxidants that can modulate radical reactions and improve oxidative stress-related diseases.

Breakthroughs in nanocarriers

Because of their short half-life, instability in systemic circulation, and poor biosolubility, vulnerability to enzymatic breakdown, sensitivity to pH- and excessive lipophilicity or hydrophilicity, as well as inherent chemical instability, antioxidants frequently do not reach their maximum potential for antioxidant activity when used in vivo. Therefore, the use of antioxidants in therapy needs to be improved. Nanocarrier-mediated delivery of antioxidants is an optimized strategy for safe and effective movement of antioxidants through the BBB and into the brain to exert antioxidant effects [154,155]. Several nanocarriers, including nanoemulsions, liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) [156], and polymeric nanoparticles, have been developed to deliver antioxidants both to the skin and through it for therapeutic purposes. Pluronic-based particles have been suggested to stabilize natural antioxidants through the encapsulation of antioxidants in copolymer nanoparticles. Heteroatom-doped carbon dots were also synthesized from chitosan and citrus lemon extract (CLCD) via hydrothermal coupling. As a result, CLCD has great colloidal stability [157], antioxidant capacity, biocompatibility, and drug molecule delivery ability, with good prospects for pharmaceuticals and antioxidants. In addition, some nanomaterials, such as porous silica nanoparticles, have been further improved to form hybrid nano-antioxidant carriers, which are surface-functionalized with the antioxidant rosmarinic acid [158]; thus, they are promising antioxidant nanocarriers for future use in drug delivery.

In order to directly link antioxidant strategies with therapeutic approaches, it is essential to design nanocarriers based on the dynamic characteristics of oxidative stress. For instance, delivery systems should be designed with differential release behaviors targeting early or late oxidative stress events in diseases. Moreover, the application of nanocarriers crossing the blood–brain barrier must consider their potential safety risks, including immune responses, neurotoxicity, and the impact of carrier degradation products [159,160]. For example, gold nanoparticles may accumulate in brain tissue over time, liposomes may activate the complement system, and polymeric nanocarriers are often limited in intracellular antioxidant delivery efficiency because of insufficient lysosomal escape [161].

Challenges and future directions

Current challenges

In the field of research on the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases, we recognize that oxidative stress is the main focus; however, owing to the changes caused by oxidative stress and because they live in the brain microenvironment, they are difficult to treat. However, oxidative stress is a protective physiological factor at an early stage of the disease, but at a later stage of the disease, the increase in ROS makes it a harmful factor, thus making it very difficult to establish the optimal time for intervention. On the other hand, most antioxidants cannot overcome the BBB and are generally non-specific to cell subtypes [162]. This dilemma has prompted researchers to break out of many dimensions. The role of oxidative stress throughout a neurodegenerative disease is not fixed. In the early stages of disease, reactive oxygen species at the physiological level are important signaling molecules that participate in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival pathways. An example might be that something like nitric oxide, which functions as a signal in the nerves, but you have got a get balance. Studies have shown that for artificial neural progenitor cells to maintain normal [NO]/[ONOO−] ratios of around 0.25 is required for cell function and that a 94% reduction in the [NO]/[ONOO−] ratio corresponds to severe cellular dysfunction [163]. Once this balance is disrupted, oxidative stress changes from an adaptive response to a driver of disease progression. In AD, Aβ treatment causes a sharp drop in the [NO]/[ONOO−] ratio of artificial neural progenitors, resulting in nitrification stress, which leads to protein nitrification, lipid peroxidation, and calcium homeostasis imbalance [164]. The transition from protecting to injuring is an ongoing process, so deciding the best time to intervene feels like trying to hit a target: not only do you need to be able to tell if someone is switching from protecting to injuring, but you need to be able to change your strategy fast.

Another major challenge is the: delivery of antioxidant drugs [165]; Getting therapeutic molecules from the right brain cells is very difficult. The BBB functions like a very smart and picky construction that only allows certain things to enter the brain, mostly keeping away from the stuff. Systemic glutathione administration is difficult because it is difficult to obtain sufficient concentrations in the brain owing to its poor stability and difficulty crossing the BBB. This delivery bottle-neck limits the success of treatment and can result in peripheral side effects. With the newness of nanotechnology, an opportunity now exists. Researchers have developed galactose-functionalized AuNPs that encapsulate glutathione as a carrier and cleverly utilize the high expression of glucose transporters in cerebral capillary endothelial cells to promote nanoparticle trans-barrier transport [166]. Glutathione-functionalized gold nanoparticles have been shown to reduce in free radicals in SH-SY5Y and C6 cells [167]. This design is a key part of how this delivery method aims to deliver goods by modifying the surface of the particles to be recognized and taken up by cells.

Future research directions

An increasing number of researchers have started to be able to perform precise analysis of the oxidative stress state of a neuron, as opposed to looking at it in bulk, because single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics techniques have gotten so good. For example, in a study of the monkey cerebral cortex, more than 25 million single-cell spatial transcriptomes were analyzed systematically, and it was found that neurons are distributed in a highly non-random manner and there are many neuron clusters with different cell types [168]. These spatial clusters showed typical spatially coupled interactions between inhibitory and excitatory neurons, and such spatial distributions might be conserved in the mouse cortex. In research on the human brain, scientists built an atlas of adult cortical cells that included 14 cortical regions, which was obtained by combining single-nucleus RNA sequencing and stereo-seq spatial transcriptomics, containing 1,121,772 nuclei of transcriptomic data and the spatial information of 1,888,306 cells [169]. In this atlas, we obtained a refined classification of glutamatergic neurons (9), GABAergic neurons (7), and their locations in the layers of the cortex. For example, RORB-positive excitatory neurons were mostly located around the granular layer, and there were many in the primary visual cortex, but CGE-derived GABAergic inhibitory neurons (such as LAMP5 and NDNF) tended to be in the superficial layer. These patterns provide a physical reason that helps us understand why some kinds of nerve cells might get hurt more by oxidative stress than others. In a recent study, single-cell datasets and oxidative stress gene sets were utilized to identify immune cell types with the highest oxidative activity under different septic conditions. By screening for differentially expressed genes between ‘activated’ and ‘inactivated’ cells, LILRA5 was identified [170]. This gene is predominantly expressed in early-stage macrophages and mediates the significant upregulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within these cells. When applied to AD, single-cell redoxomics has revealed that neurons in the hippocampal CA1 region exhibit significant downregulation of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD2 and GPX4, whereas microglia show upregulation of LILRA5 [171]. This uncovers cellular heterogeneity in oxidative stress responses and suggests distinct redox roles for neurons and immune cells during disease progression. Similarly, spatial transcriptomics in PD has enabled the construction of an oxidative stress map of the substantia nigra, showing spatial colocalization between mitochondrial oxidative stress genes (e.g. NOX4, GPX4) in dopaminergic neurons and inflammatory genes in surrounding astrocytes [172]. Such spatial organization points to neuron–glia interactions as key contributors to oxidative stress-driven neurodegeneration and offers new targets for cell type- or region-specific interventions. Based on these data, future work will be able to further develop single-cell redoxomics to characterize the oxidative and reductive profiles of distinct brain areas and cell types at various disease stages by studying the expression of oxidative stress-related genes in single-cell (e.g. antioxidant enzymes SOD2, GPX1, and CAT) in combination with the analysis of lipid peroxidation products and DNA oxidative damage biomarkers.

In recent years, the gut microbiota has shown great potential in neuroscience research [173–175]. Changes or imbalances in gut bacteria may cause or result in certain neurologies, including AD and PD. The gut microbiota plays an important role in many different functions, such as maintaining a steady condition and regulating the immune system, CNS, and ENS. We have seen with the studies of mice that we have a lot going on between what we carry around in our bellies in our microbiota and things such as neuro-inflammation, which is an inflammation of the brain, how our brain was to work, and even how happy we might be or how a certain type of person we feel like we are. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota is associated with many diseases of the central nervous system. Whether the gut microbiota is involved in oxidative stress and the promotion of disease is not clear, which may be an important issue for us to focus on in the future.

Discussion

Faced with the complex causes of neurodegenerative diseases, a single entity is targeted by interventions, resulting in limited success. Research on anti-aging agents has recently led to the possibility of screening for senolytic substances at a fast and high level. This is a phenotype-driven strategy, which is independent of previous knowledge about the targets, has an intrinsic advantage for identifying multi-target molecules, and has already identified some molecules that can extend the health span of model organisms from natural compound libraries. For example, parallel small molecule library screening approaches have identified inhibitors of three prototypical amyloids in neurodegeneration and related proteinopathies (amylin, Aβ, and tau) [176]. A class of inhibitors that are prominent in various amyloid screens includes catechol-containing compounds as well as redox-related quinones/anthraquinones. Similar strategies could be used to develop antioxidants. Because of each person's level of variation and difference in each individual's oxidized stress levels and antioxidant capacity, it will be an ongoing progress to customize personalized plans to achieve better outcomes. Biomarker detection technology has produced analytical procedures that use UPLC-MS/MS to simultaneously measure four oxidative stress indicators in human urine: 8-OHdG, which is a sign of DNA oxidation; 8-OHG, which represents RNA oxidation; 4-HNE, which indicates lipid peroxidation; and dityrosine, which indicates protein oxidative damage [177]. It has a very good sensitivity and accuracy to back up, which makes it a reliable way of checking how oxidized people are in their bodies on the whole.

In summary, this review summarizes the important role of oxidative stress in neurodegenerative diseases, including signaling pathways, targets, emerging targets such as ferroptosis and NETosis, the context of oxidative stress in different types of neurodegenerative diseases and related antioxidant strategies. It has also combined the newest antioxidant strategies, especially nanocarrier-delivered strategies.
