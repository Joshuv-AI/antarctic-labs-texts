Thumb Ulnar Collateral Ligament Repair Using Suture Anchors and Suture Tape Augmentation
Authors: Dustin J Kress, Lucas C Voyvodic, Nicholas Pettinelli, Patrick Waldron, Ethan Vallellanes, Sydney Solis, Ravleen Kang, Arina Caliman, Chloe Kung, James L Chen, Ajith Malige
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInjury to the ulnar collateral ligament of the thumb metacarpophalangeal joint is commonly encountered in clinical practice, particularly among athletes. Although repair using suture anchors is currently the gold standard, recent evidence suggests suture tape augmentation offers superior initial construct strength, allowing for earlier mobilization. This technical note describes a reproducible technique for thumb ulnar collateral ligament repair using suture tape augmentation. The procedure involves primary repair with a 1.3-mm Suturetape (Arthrex, Naples, FL) followed by internal bracing using FiberTape (Arthrex) secured with dual 3.5-mm SwiveLock anchors (Arthrex) spanning the joint. The metacarpophalangeal joint is positioned at 30° of flexion during repair to achieve optimal tension. Suture tape augmentation provides significantly higher maximum load and load at clinical failure than repair alone, with improved resistance to gap formation under cyclic loading. This enhanced construct allows for accelerated rehabilitation protocols with potentially faster return to sport and work activities in 5 to 6 weeks. The technique preserves native ligament proprioception while providing additional stability during early healing phases.

Injury to the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) of the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint of the thumb is a condition frequently encountered in clinical practice.1 This ligamentous injury generally occurs due to a valgus force on an abducted thumb. It is colloquially known as “skier’s thumb,” in which acute injury occurs after a skier falls on an outstretched hand and abducted thumb.2 A similar injury is described by the term “gamekeeper’s thumb,” in which attritional rupture of the UCL occurs due to repetitive and chronic valgus stresses.2

When healthy, the UCL contributes to static stabilization of the MCP joint and comprises the volar plate, proper, and accessory collateral ligaments.3 The stability of the UCL varies with joint flexion. At 0°, the volar portion of the accessory collateral ligament becomes taut, providing stability in neutral position.3 In contrast, at 30°, the dorsal and middle portions of the proper collateral ligament tighten and stabilize the joint as they stretch around the metacarpal head.3,4 This differential contribution forms the basis for clinical testing and surgical planning.

Patients typically present with pain, edema, and ecchymosis around the thumb MCP joint, in addition to weakness with grasp or pinch movements.5 Diagnosis begins with standard radiographs to identify bony abnormalities, although they have limited sensitivity for UCL injuries. Stress radiographs can help identify joint instability.1,2 Ultrasonography offers high sensitivity (96%) and specificity (91%) for detecting UCL tears and Stener lesions.5,6 Magnetic resonance imaging provides superior diagnostic accuracy and can differentiate between nondisplaced and displaced tears.2,5,6

Treatment options for UCL injuries depend on the degree of ligamentous injury, the patient’s physical demands, and the timing of the injury.6 Partial tears can be treated nonoperatively initially, whereas complete tears often require surgical intervention.6,7

Among surgical options, several techniques have been described, including UCL repair, UCL reconstruction, adductor advancement, and MCP fusion.8 Additionally, several techniques are used for UCL repair, including transosseous sutures, suture anchors with or without suture augmentation, and direct ligament repair to the periosteum.9 Although return-to-play rates approach 100% after surgical UCL repair,10,11 there is a lack of evidence to suggest the superiority of a single repair technique.5,12,13 The current literature lacks comparative studies evaluating the biomechanical advantages of different repair constructs, particularly regarding stability during early rehabilitation phases.14,15 Moreover, there is wide variation in how quickly athletes and non-athletes achieve return to play after surgical repair. However, suture augmentation techniques may potentially allow earlier initiation of rehabilitation protocols owing to enhanced repair stability.15,16

The surgical management of thumb UCL injuries has evolved to include repair with suture tape augmentation, which enhances the biomechanical strength of the repair by providing additional stability.8,14 Biomechanical studies show that this augmented repair technique restores varus-valgus kinematics and provides higher angular stiffness than repair alone,14,15 facilitating accelerated rehabilitation protocols. With combinations of newer techniques and aggressive rehabilitation protocols, return to sport, work, and daily activities occurs in 5 to 6 weeks.10,11,16,17 The purpose of this article and Video 1 is to describe a reproducible technique for repairing the UCL with suture augmentation.

Surgical Technique

Preoperative Evaluation

In addition to a full musculoskeletal and neurovascular examination around the thumb, testing of UCL integrity should be performed in both full extension and 30° of flexion of the MCP joint. Valgus instability of more than 30°—or more than 10° compared with the contralateral thumb in both flexion and extension—indicates complete rupture of the proper and accessory UCL, whereas instability of the MCP joint in flexion indicates rupture of the proper UCL only. A magnetic resonance imaging scan is recommended for additional preoperative planning because it provides a detailed assessment of tear location, injury grade, and ligament quality.

Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned supine on a hand table, with the upper extremity extended. A peripheral nerve block is administered, and general anesthesia is induced along with intravenous antibiotics. A tourniquet is placed on the upper arm and inflated to 250 mm Hg.

Approach

An incision is made centered over the thumb MCP joint on the ulnar side (Fig 1). Soft-tissue dissection is carried down to the level of the adductor aponeurosis (Fig 2). This is incised adjacent to the extensor tendon. The thumb MCP joint UCL is identified.

Repair With Suture Augmentation

A 1.3-mm Suturetape (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed through the distal ligament for the primary repair of the UCL (Fig 3A). An Arthrex 3.5-mm DX SwiveLock is placed in the proximal phalanx (Fig 3B). The Suturetape from the ligament and an extra FiberTape (Arthrex) are placed into this anchor (Fig 3C). This FiberTape is placed into an additional 3.5-mm DX SwiveLock. This is placed into the metacarpal bone while the MCP joint is in 30° of flexion (Fig 3D). Final fluoroscopic examination shows joint stability without valgus laxity and satisfactory tension of the internal brace (Fig 4). The completed repair reveals the internal-brace configuration with suture augmentation construct (Fig 5). The incision is closed and dressed, and the patient is placed into a 3-in plaster thumb spica splint.

Postoperative Protocol

Postoperatively, the patient is non-weight-bearing to the upper extremity in a thumb spica splint and sling. The patient undergoes follow-up at 10 to 14 days for wound evaluation and suture removal. After the first visit, the patient is transitioned into a removable spica splint, and physical therapy is started at 2 weeks.

Discussion

This article provides a technical description of thumb UCL repair using suture augmentation. The technique involves a primary repair using a 1.3-mm Suturetape and 3.5-mm SwiveLock anchor augmented with a FiberTape spanning the joint and secured with a second 3.5-mm SwiveLock anchor.

Successful rehabilitation after tendon repair is primarily influenced by early mobilization to prevent adhesion formation and residual stiffness. However, early mobilization requires a strong repair construct to withstand rehabilitation forces. Increasing the number of core sutures and anchor points enhances repair strength and gapping resistance, whereas internal bracing provides additional stability.16,17 When suture tape is used in primary repair, there is an additional increase in failure load.10 This enhanced fixation allows for early mobilization and rehabilitation without risking rerupture.

When thumb UCL repair with suture tape augmentation is performed, the MCP joint should be positioned at 30° of flexion to achieve optimal tension.18,19 Patel et al.18 showed that properly tensioned thumb UCL repair with suture tape augmentation restores varus-valgus kinematics without over-constraining the joint. Surgeons should pay additional attention to avoid over-tightening the suture tape because this can cause excessive stiffness and reduced range of motion that can potentially limit thumb mobility and function.20 Proper positioning and appropriate tape tension during surgery are important to prevent these complications. A cadaveric biomechanical study by Shin et al.14 compared specimens with either primary repair alone or suture tape–augmented repair of the UCL, finding that specimens with suture tape augmentation showed significantly higher maximum load and load at clinical failure than repair-only specimens. When compared with traditional techniques such as transosseous suture repair or direct ligament repair to the periosteum, suture anchor fixation with tape augmentation has shown superior load-to-failure strength and resistance to gap formation.15,16 Additionally, Lee et al.15 reported that suture tape–augmented repairs showed less displacement under cyclic loading than anchor-only repairs, suggesting improved stability during early rehabilitation phases. This allows more confidence in the repair and potentially earlier progression of rehabilitation. Another surgical option is UCL reconstruction, primarily used in chronic injury when the native ligament is of poor quality or when there is significant ligament retraction preventing direct repair.16,21 Repair with or without suture tape augmentation, by contrast, is preferred in acute settings when ligament tissue retains adequate quality, and tear patterns allow for direct re-approximation, offering advantages of preserved proprioception and native tissue healing.14,15 Recent biomechanical studies show that suture-augmented repairs provide additional stability with less gap formation and equivalent load-to-failure strength compared with traditional reconstruction techniques.22, 23, 24, 25 However, a cadaveric study by Sircar et al.26 found differences in joint stability and mobility depending on joint morphology and reconstruction method, suggesting that patient-specific factors should guide the selection between augmented repair and reconstruction techniques.

Suture augmentation has increased in popularity across multiple orthopaedic applications beyond thumb UCL repair. This technique has shown promising results in medial UCL repair of the elbow, particularly in overhead athletes, where it potentially allows for a faster return to sport than traditional reconstruction.20,25,27 Similarly, suture augmentation has been used in other surgical procedures because of its enhanced biomechanical strength properties, potentially improving clinical outcomes and reducing recovery times in anterior cruciate ligament repairs, Achilles tendon repairs, and deltoid ligament reconstructions.20

Comprehensive surgical technique for thumb ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) repair with internal brace. The patient is positioned supine on a hand table with the upper extremity extended. A peripheral nerve block is administered with general anesthesia and intravenous antibiotics. A tourniquet is placed on the upper arm and inflated to 250 mm Hg. A longitudinal incision is made centered over the thumb metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint on the ulnar side. Soft-tissue dissection is carried down to the level of the adductor aponeurosis, which is incised adjacent to the extensor tendon in line with the underlying UCL fibers. The UCL is then identified and assessed for injury pattern. After debridement of damaged tissue back to the healthy ligament substance, a 1.3-mm Suturetape is placed through the distal UCL using either a Krackow or running-locking baseball-stitch configuration to secure the ligament tissue. This suture tape, along with an additional FiberTape for internal bracing, is loaded into a 3.5-mm DX SwiveLock anchor. The first anchor is placed in the proximal phalanx at the anatomic site of the native UCL insertion. Fluoroscopic guidance confirms proper anchor placement and avoids joint penetration or placement too close to the joint line, which is particularly important in younger patients to ensure placement distal to the physis. The FiberTape from the proximal anchor is tensioned and secured with a second 3.5-mm DX SwiveLock anchor placed in the metacarpal bone at the native UCL insertion site. Critical to the technique is positioning the MCP joint at 30° of flexion during final anchor placement to achieve optimal tension of the proper collateral ligament component and restore normal varus-valgus kinematics without over-constraining the joint. Preoperative and postoperative fluoroscopic valgus stress testing shows restoration of joint stability, confirming adequate repair tension and elimination of pathologic valgus laxity. Final examination shows satisfactory tension of the internal-brace construct without restricting normal joint motion.
