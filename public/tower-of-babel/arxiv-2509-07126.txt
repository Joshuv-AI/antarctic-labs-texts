Short-Term Gaze Prediction: Analysis of Individual Differences, Typical and Extreme-Case Errors
Kateryna Melnyk, Lee Friedman, Oleg Komogortsev

Abstract
Gaze prediction is a diverse field of study with multiple research focuses and practical applications. This article investigates how recurrent neural networks and transformers perform short-term gaze prediction. We used three models: a three-layer long-short-term memory (LSTM) network, a simple transformer-encoder model (TF), and a classification-predictor network (ClPr), which simultaneously classifies the signal into eye movement events and predicts the positions of gaze. The performance of the models was evaluated for ocular fixations and saccades of various amplitudes and as a function of individual differences in both typical and extreme cases. On average, LSTM performed better on fixations and saccades, whereas TF and ClPr demonstrated more precise results for post-saccadic periods. In extreme cases, the best-performing models vary depending on the type of eye movement. We reviewed the difference between the median P 50 P_{50} and high-percentile P 95 P_{95} error profiles across subjects. The subjects for which the models perform the best overall do not necessarily exhibit the lowest P 95 P_{95} values, which supports the idea of analyzing extreme cases separately in future work. We explore the trade-offs between the proposed solutions and provide practical insights into model selection for gaze prediction.

1
Introduction

Eye tracking (ET) is a sensor technology with a wide range of applications in various fields
[
1
]
. The prediction of future gaze position can be used for multiple purposes: to improve user experience in virtual/augmented reality (VR/AR) devices
[
2
]
, to enhance driver assistance systems
[
3
]
(ADAS) by monitoring the driver’s gaze to prevent road accidents, or to create more responsive assistive technologies that can be used by individuals with physical or cognitive disabilities
[
4
,
5
]
. Despite the many advantages of this technology, its integration can contribute to latency in real-time applications, so engineers must optimize resource usage to minimize processing delays and meet all hardware and system requirements.

Foveated rendering (FR), gaze-contingent displays, and eye-controlled interfaces are all sensitive to the latency coming from ET. The observed latency can originate from the eye tracker or the gaze estimation algorithm. Any delay, even within a couple of milliseconds (ms), can be very noticeable for users, leading to their visual discomfort
[
6
]
. Gaze prediction is a technique used to minimize and address this issue in system response. The primary goal of any gaze prediction algorithm is to anticipate where a person will look just before doing so. The design of such an algorithm is highly influenced by the prediction interval (PI), the interval between the current position and the future position we are trying to predict, and the data modalities used as inputs to the model. The task can be classified as a short-term or long-term (
>100
ms) prediction problem based on the PI values. Based on the input data, a task can be seen more as a computer vision or time-series forecasting (TSF) problem.

In this study, we approached gaze prediction as a short-term TSF task predicting gaze within 44 ms PI and using only gaze tracking data. We did not fit any graphical content information or inertial measurement unit (IMU) data into the prediction pipeline. Short-term gaze prediction can be applied to optimize FR. The FR renders the foveal region of human vision at full resolution. The resolution is then gradually reduced across the interfoveal and peripheral areas without sacrificing the quality of the user experience. Most modern head-mounted displays (HMDs) have an ET running at 90 Hz
[
7
]
, which means that the devices collect a new eye position every 11 ms. Using a gaze prediction model, the system can pre-adjust rendering and reduce perceptual lag in VR/AR environments
[
8
]
.

Evaluating real-time algorithms or subject-dependent systems is a complex and knowledge-intensive task. Although the median (
P_{50}
) accuracy is a robust estimator of model performance, assessing performance in extreme-case scenarios is also essential.
P_{95}
error can help quantify when the model struggles with specific edge cases. Depending on the consumer device, systems that utilize FR and gaze prediction may tolerate minor prediction errors. However, a high magnitude of errors at the 95th percentile can lead to incorrect rendering, user confusion, overload, or even disruption of the immersive experience
[
9
]
.

In the current study, we did not use the well-established classical prediction approaches such as the Oculomotor Plant Mathematical Model in Kalman Filter Form (OPKF) since in our previous work we showed that other models outperformed it
[
10
]
. This study explores how transformers and recurrent neural networks perform short-term gaze prediction because both approaches are designed to handle sequential data and have their strengths
[
11
]
. We used three models: a three-layer long-short-term memory (LSTM) network, a simple transformer-encoder model (TF), and a classification-predictor network (ClPr) that simultaneously classifies the signal into eye movement events and predicts the positions of gaze. We evaluated model performance, under typical and tail-end cases, across eye movement types (fixations, saccades, and others). We also studied individual differences in gaze prediction. We explore the trade-offs between the proposed approaches and provide practical insights into model selection for gaze prediction.

Literature Review

Before deep learning (DL) models gained popularity as practical solutions, data- or model-driven approaches were more widely adopted in gaze prediction research. Depending on how gaze prediction is framed as a research problem, multiple strategies exist to address it. Each of them presents unique challenges and methodologies. One of the most commonly used recurrent neural networks (RNNs) for gaze prediction is the LSTM. Different research groups have adopted and used it as a baseline model. In recent years, more attention has been shifted towards Transformer-based architectures and their variations
[
12
]
. Transformers demonstrated their effectiveness in capturing patterns for short-term TSF when the architectures are well-optimized. However, simple models can outperform more complex ones when the dataset or task better suits their strengths
[
13
]
.

People’s gaze behavior can vary over time. Various factors, such as task, system design, environmental changes, and fatigue, can influence gaze patterns, making eye tracking signals non-stationary. RNNs are effective for sequence modeling and processing input one step at a time, making them very relevant for real-time gaze prediction
[
14
,
15
]
. However, they usually struggle to adapt to changing patterns in the data. In that case, Transformers are more adaptive to changes, as they do not process the input strictly in sequence. Therefore, more advanced architectures such as Transformers or attention-augmented RNNs are more relevant for gaze prediction
[
16
]
.

Xu et.al
[
17
]
proposed a hybrid model that predicts human gaze in
360{{}^{o}}
immersive videos. They utilize saliency maps and images for feature extraction using a convolutional neural network (CNN) model, and employ the LSTM model to encode gaze paths for predicting gaze displacement over time. The reported average time for the combined CNN + LSTM architecture per one image frame was equal to 47 ms. Mazzeo et al.
[
18
]
proposed a Transformer-based model for long sequence time-series forecasting (
LSTF
) and an
LSTM
model that uses the estimated gaze vectors as input from their gaze estimation pipeline. Although the Informer architecture was more advanced than the LSTM model, the recurrent network outperformed it.

Hu et al.
[
19
]
proposed an eye-head coordination model
SGaze
, which uses head poses to predict gaze in real time for immersive applications. Their work was motivated by studies investigating the relationship between user gaze positions on the screen and head rotations. The results demonstrated that the relationship between head and eye movement data was sufficiently strong to predict gaze positions without relying on past gaze data. Building on their earlier findings, they continue to develop a new gaze prediction model:
DGaze

[
20
]
. It is a CNN solution that combines object position sequences, head velocity, and saliency features to make predictions. In addition, they proposed an alternative model,
DGaze\_ET
, which incorporates eye-tracking data. They explored PIs from 50 to 1000 ms. They found that gaze data as an input modality was ineffective for forecasting gaze positions in long-term forecasting scenarios of more than 1 second. In their next work
[
21
]
, the authors focused only on forecasting users’ eye fixations. The "FixationNet" network combines the extracted features and the precomputed cluster centers of the fixations to make the predictions. They found that forecasting long-term eye fixations (> 400 ms) was much more difficult than short-term fixations.

At that time, transformers began to gain attention and showed effectiveness in different tasks
[
12
]
. Rolf et al.
[
22
]
proposed a transformer network for gaze prediction. The
GazeTransformer
used gaze, head, task, and image data. They used a PI value of 150 ms and discovered that utilizing image data does not improve the model’s predictive performance. This finding implies that IMU and eye movement data alone may be rich enough to capture gaze dynamics in VR, and lighter versions of the
GazeTransformer
can be applied for gaze forecasting. In the same year, Illahi et al.
[
14
]
presented a lightweight LSTM solution and noted that gaze predictions must occur with low latency for real-time usage, specifically for cloud-rendered VR applications. They presented a two-branch LSTM network that used gaze coordinates, gaze and head rotational velocity, and gaze and head rotational acceleration sequences 200 ms into the past to make predictions over PIs from 22 ms to 150 ms. Each branch predicts one gaze coordinate, and during inference, the outputs of the two branches are concatenated for the final prediction. They found that gaze data alone is a strong predictor, but model performance improves when more features are added, such as gaze velocity or HMD rotations. The results of the model generalization analysis suggest that the input sequence length and prediction windows are dataset-dependent and need to be adjusted accordingly. The inference time analysis revealed that the proposed LSTM approach has a sufficiently low inference latency for VR applications.

Although machine learning (ML) based solutions are still dominant in gaze forecasting studies, model-driven approaches based on mathematical models of the human eye
[
23
]
continue progressing and are being refined to achieve competitive performance in this field. Katrychuk et al.
[
24
]
presented the Oculomotor Plant Mathematical Model in Kalman Filter Form (
OPKF
) with estimation of neural pulse based on peak velocity for continuous gaze prediction. One of the essential modifications was that the OPMM parameters were estimated at a per-subject level
[
25
]
, which means that they were derived for each subject separately when optimizing for the best parameter fit. Although the improved
OPKF
did not outperform the DL models, it delivered competitive results, suggesting that alternative methods can be applied when the model inference time matters. In one of our earlier studies
[
10
]
, the
OPKF
prediction accuracy was compared to the
LSTM
and transformer-based network for multivariate time-series representation learning (
TST
). It was shown that while analyzing gaze prediction as a function of eye movement type and individual differences, the DL models delivered better results than
OPKF
.

Gaze prediction is a diverse field with different research objectives and applications. Key et al.
[
26
]
published a study on the Deeper Clustered EEG Vision Transformer (
EEG-DCViT
) that uses EEG-based data to predict gaze positions on the screen. Their results suggest that gaze prediction is possible using only brain activity signals without relying on eye tracking data from gaze-tracking hardware. Xiao et al.
[
27
]
used gaze prediction as a visual attention prediction task, where the proposed solution
LSTM-CVFAF
(convolution based on video frame correlation features) generates gaze maps for each video frame. The solution demonstrated the effectiveness of the learnable central prior layer, which helps adapt to human bias in gaze behavior that emerges from task-specific visual behavior. Driver gaze allocation is another highly relevant gaze prediction problem in the automotive industry & human-machine interface (HMI) research. Kotseruba et al.
[
28
]
introduced the
SCOUT+
model, a task- and context-aware model for driver gaze prediction. They demonstrated that the success of a driver’s gaze prediction model was highly dependent on the task. Combining scene understanding and task context allows the gaze model to be generalized to real-world scenarios.

3
Methodology

3.1
Dataset

We used a data set recorded with a Meta Quest Pro VR headset. The ET data were captured at a sampling rate of 90 Hz. For a detailed description of the dataset and experiment, refer to the original study
[
29
]
. All recruited participants were college-aged individuals from Texas State University. In total, 78 individuals participated in the experiment. For this analysis, we used two random saccade (RAN) tasks. During these tasks, the target was moved to random positions on the screen along a grid that spanned
\pm 25^{o}
horizontally and
\pm 20^{o}
vertically.

The two task designs are identical. However, for the RAN 127 task, the target was presented against a high-luminance background whose color corresponds to a light gray-intensity value of 127 on a scale from 0 (black) to 255 (white). For the second task, RAN 63, a darker shade of gray (intensity value of 63) was used as the background color. To understand what the visualization task looks like, refer to Figure 1 of the study
[
29
]
.

3.2
Prediction Models

3.2.1
Long-Short-Term Memory Network - LSTM

As mentioned in the Literature review, one of the most widely adopted models for gaze prediction was the LSTM network. LSTM is a type of RNN model specifically designed to model sequences while remembering long-term dependencies. One of its most significant advantages for real-time gaze prediction is that it processes the input sequence step by step, making it a good selection for real-time applications. Since it is already a solid baseline presented in multiple works, we also decided to include it in the current analysis. We used a three-layer LSTM-based network with a custom output head with a dropout rate of 0.2 to prevent overfitting. We selected the Tsai library for its implementation
[
30
]
because it was created specifically for time-series tasks. The library offers significant flexibility for experimentation, allowing for easy adjustment and testing of different variations of the selected DL model.

3.2.2
Transformer Encoder Forecaster Network - TF

Transformers are widely used for forecasting complex, non-linear, and multi-feature data. Unlike RNN models, which process data step-by-step, transformers examine the entire input window at once. This processing mechanism allows them to prioritize which parts of the input data are most relevant for predicting future time stamps. The original Transformer model has an encoder-decoder architecture. The Transformer Encoder Forecaster (TF) model referred to in this work is a light implementation of the regular transformer. It is just a transformer encoder model with a position-wise feedforward network for sequence modeling. First, using a linear layer, the model projects the input data into a high-dimensional space. It then processes the sequence using a stack of transformer encoder layers with multihead self-attention. Finally, a custom prediction head is applied, which projects the encoded sequence into the desired output length.

3.2.3
Classification-Predictor Network - ClPr

The intuition behind the predictor and classification model architecture was to approach gaze prediction as a multitask learning (MTL) problem because MTL can enhance model performance in time-series forecasting. The model learns the shareable data representations used for different objectives. Of course, the success of this approach depends on the quality of the classification. Since we have event-type classification labels, representation learning via additional supervision might help the model to understand eye movement patterns better.

The MTL model simultaneously predicts continuous outputs and categorical labels over time. It contains a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN), LSTM, attention mechanism, prediction, and classification heads built on top of PyTorch Lightning. The TCN Encoder encodes raw eye-tracking signals into temporal features. We used an LSTM module over these encoded features to capture long-range dependencies and temporal contexts. Then, we apply the attention and pooling layer. As a final step, we have the two heads. One outputs continuous values such as positional differences, and the other predicts categorical labels.

3.3
Data Pre-processing

We followed the evaluation strategy based on the analysis of gaze prediction error as a function of eye movement type and individual differences
[
10
]
. We classified eye-tracking signals into eye movement events to implement the ClPr model and perform evaluations.

We started with an improved version of our MNH event classification algorithm
[
31
]
that was designed for the classification of gaze data from an EyeLink 1000 eye tracker with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. A concise description of the updates made to MNH can be found in the Appendix section of
[
10
]
. To work on the Meta Quest Pro dataset (collected at a sampling rate of 90 Hz), extensive modifications were required. These modifications are summarized in the supplemental material for the current study.

We performed a data ablation study to examine the data modalities that worked best as inputs for the models. We explored gaze positions, velocities, accelerations (the magnitude of velocity and acceleration), jerks (rate of change of acceleration), and heading angles, which describe the orientation of the motion. The best-performing input combination was [velocities, heading angle]. In the TF model example, we improved the average test error by
7\%
compared with when we used only [velocities] as the input time series. Furthermore, we found that forecasting changes in positions
\Delta x_{t+PI}=x_{t+PI}-x_{t}
rather than absolute gaze positions was more effective for the models presented in this study. In this study, we used the length of the input and output windows as hyperparameters. We aimed to determine the optimal values for different models. We tested input window sizes ranging from 6 to 20 samples, corresponding to time durations between 66 and 220 ms. We test whether forecasting a sequence of the next
k
steps (e.g.
x_{t-k+PI}
to
x_{t+PI}
, where
k\in[1,PI]
) improves the performance over predicting only the final step
x_{t+PI}
.

Based on our results, the LSTM and TF models used [velocities, heading angle] as input signals to predict [positional differences]. ClPR model utilized [velocities, heading angle, classification labels] to predict [positional differences, classification labels]. The input size for the LSTM, TF, and CIPr models was 16, 18, and 12 samples, respectively. All models predicted the sequence of the next PI steps.

3.4
Training

We randomly split the subjects between the train and test datasets. It reduces the risk of overfitting and allows for a more reliable investigation of individual differences. The results are based on the QuestPro dataset. Each subject has two recordings (RAN 127 and RAN 63 tasks). The training dataset consisted of 112 recordings (66 subjects). The test dataset consisted of 24 recordings (12 subjects). We used the sliding-window approach to train the deep learning models. Each model was assigned its corresponding optimized window length. The order of windows was shuffled before splitting them into batches of 32. To facilitate a smoother training process, the PyTorch Lightning framework
[
32
]
was applied. The loss function was defined as the mean Euclidean distance between the ground truth and predicted gaze data, and we utilized the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
0.0003
. All results employed a 44 ms PI. We included additional plots for the PIs equal to 22 ms and 66 ms as supplemental materials.

The full details regarding the model implementation are outlined in the supplemental materials. The code and data required to replicate the results are publicly available at the following link: (a link to all supplemental materials will be provided in the future.)

3.5
Evaluation

In this study, we examine the average performance and
P_{95}
error plots of the model’s prediction performance.
P_{95}
error plots help us understand edge cases, which are often critical in software production for real-time use. Error-tail performance analysis may reveal how models behave under stress conditions and help identify a model that performs more robustly under unusual inputs or outliers.

We used a well-established set of evaluation metrics in the literature: CDFs of prediction errors across all signal and eye movement types, the median
P_{50}
error subject profiles, the
P_{95}
analysis included the corresponding
P_{95}
cdfs across all eye movement types and
P_{95}
subject profiles. Since we used RAN tasks as a test/train dataset, the expected fixation durations would be longer than the typical fixation length, approximately 200-400 ms for scene viewing
[
33
]
. To analyze the average model performance, we divide the fixation events into short-term (
<400
ms) and long-term (
>400
ms) fixations. To analyze the performance of the model between saccadic periods, we divided them into small-amplitude saccades (
<2^{o}
) and large-amplitude saccades (
>2^{o}
). We analyzed the model accuracy separately across the post-saccadic periods equal to 110 ms, which is referred to in the literature
[
15
]
as the critical evaluation periods (ceps).

5
Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that the average accuracy of the model does not necessarily reflect optimal performance and that analyzing the 95th percentile of the prediction error (
P_{95}
) can provide additional valuable insights. We showed that model selection for short-term gaze prediction is a complex task that requires tuning and evaluation across various eye movement types. The RNN-based solution was generally better on average. However, in the edge cases, the better-performing solution was the transformer-based one. This makes sense since the transformers are more robust to unexpected eye-movement signal changes. TF models do not process the input strictly in sequence. They can better handle unexpected shifts in gaze behavior, while LSTMs are more efficient when working with short- to medium-length sequences and when the dataset size is small.

Saccadic suppression usually starts approximately 50 ms before saccadic onset and ends approximately 50 - 100 ms after saccade offset
[
34
]
. The point of vision recovery is a function of saccade amplitude: vision recovers later after large saccades. Artifacts or delayed updates caused by FR during this period can be very noticeable and cause user discomfort. We refer to this period as a critical evaluation period (cep)
[
29
]
, equal to 110 ms after the saccade. TF was the most accurate model during the ceps based on the average and
P_{95}
performance metrics.

In our view, individual differences in gaze prediction errors are important and are not typically studied in the literature. There are several reports on individual differences in eye movements
[
35
,
36
]
. Furthermore, there are noted individual differences in noise
[
37
]
. For gaze prediction models to be practically useful and more accurate, they must deal with this subject variability
[
10
]
.

Our
P_{95}
analyses further reveal the performance of models in outlying cases, which are sensitive to individual differences in gaze data. The LSTM model had the lowest
P_{95}
errors for fixations and small-amplitude saccades, but the TF model had lower errors for the CEP and large-amplitude saccades. Subject patterns across
P_{95}
and
P_{50}
errors were similar for fixations and large saccades but not for CEPs or small saccades, suggesting that the models prioritized different features for their predictions.

Our analysis points to two critical goals that require attention in future gaze prediction studies: (1) to enhance accuracy per subject by reducing the performance gap between the highest error subjects and those performing best, and (2) minimize the difference between
P_{50}
and
P_{95}
errors across subjects. One future direction would be to train a model on a full subject dataset and then do fine-tuning or light adaptation using only 5 minutes of recent ET data to make it more specialized for a specific subject. For more discussion of subject-specific models, see
[
10
,
24
]
.

5.1
Limitations

One limitation of the current study is that we only used gaze-tracking data. For the present report, we evaluated the physiologically significant derivatives of gaze positional signals. Other research groups
[

,
14

Any additional source of available data should at least be evaluated. In the

On the one hand, the correlation can help to make predictions more reliable, but since two time series are correlated, deep learning models may not benefit much from the extra data. In such a case, the solutions based solely on gaze vectors can be faster in inference time than those that utilize multiple sources of information or saliency maps. In

Another limitation pertains to the eye-tracking task employed. The present report is based on a random saccade task, a controlled task that is independent of scene content or user interest. Participants are typically instructed to look at targets that appear at random positions on a display (or in VR). One of the advantages of this task selection is that we can observe a higher and more consistent saccade count, suggesting that models trained on this dataset better capture some aspects of saccadic dynamics, such as velocity-amplitude relationships and acceleration profiles.

We are interested in gaze prediction as a function of eye-movement types, and our results are particularly relevant to tasks that do not include smooth pursuit. Free-viewing tasks lead to unpredictable gaze behavior, mixed events, and noise, making automatic classification difficult. It is possible to create a task with both clear saccades and smooth pursuit targets. The evaluation of gaze prediction during smooth pursuit is an interesting future question. We will also be interested in training and testing the models on different tasks, such as reading, horizontal saccades, and video viewing.

In this paper, we compared three different classes of DL models, and one of these solutions utilizes classification labels to forecast signals. Although this approach is valid, it is essential to note that the classification step will require more computational resources and introduce more latency into the system, making this model harder to adopt and integrate for real-time use. In the future, it would be interesting to explore more transformer variants and experiment with different learning techniques to better adapt the model to the specifics of eye-tracking signals.

There are particular trade-offs between classical approaches, such as Kalman Filter Frameworks and DL-based solutions. When the Kalman filter solution is well optimized and calibrated, it runs quickly, making real-time predictions easy and fast to compute. However, recent reports have shown that ML models outperform OPKF across all eye-movement events
[
24
]
. In that study, the authors suggest that to improve the effectiveness of the OPKF model, it would be better to use the model in some hybrid ML-OPMM version instead of utilizing it in its original form. Another disadvantage of the OPKF model is that its training and optimization can be time-consuming when it comes to selecting parameters that lead to accurate predictions.

In contrast, DL solutions, such as RNNs or Transformers, are data-driven, they directly learn patterns from raw data. They can be easier to adopt for the prediction of gaze sequences, compared to optimizing a Kalman filter with OPMM. However, since they usually require more computational resources, they need to be carefully tuned to meet real-time performance requirements.

5.2
Future Work

In the current study, the models presented were not optimized to their full potential for real-time applications. Since we use transformer-based approaches, they will require more investigation, and for longer PIs quantization and pruning to meet real-time performance requirements. One critical part of the development of a real-time gaze prediction algorithm is inference time analysis, which might include the time it takes for the model to predict a single input in milliseconds, CPU/GPU usage, and memory consumption. Although we did not report these numbers for the full gaze prediction pipeline in the current study, it will be essential to do so in the future.

It is worth exploring other hybrid models that combine transformers and RNNs. Solutions such as a Transformer with RNN-based positional encoding or an RNN encoder with a Transformer decoder can be jointly effective and enhance the overall prediction performance under average and extreme-case conditions. Future studies should also consider training the model to adjust to each subject’s unique eye movement patterns. This could include incorporating subject-specific embeddings into the input or utilizing meta-learning to enable the model to adapt quickly to user-specific patterns.

6
Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrated that evaluating the models’ median performance is insufficient to fully characterize the models’ effectiveness under varying conditions. High
P_{95}
gaze prediction error can lead to frustration for difficult subjects. Prediction performance is obviously influenced by the model applied. A model with strong average performance may still perform poorly in edge cases. A model with strong prediction performance across fixations can be less successful when it comes to post-saccadic periods. Therefore, the particular needs and constraints of the application must guide the selection of the gaze prediction algorithm for real-time use.
