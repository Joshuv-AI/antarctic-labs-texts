Improving cardiometabolic multimorbidity prediction with a composite obesity–TyG index: a study of middle-aged and older adults in CHARLS
Authors: Yan Wang, Haonan Wang, Xinyao Liu, Junhui Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have demonstrated the importance of obesity and insulin resistance in increasing the risk of cardiovascular and metabolic diseases. However, few studies have demonstrated the association between visceral fat and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM). In addition, the interaction of visceral fat and insulin resistance on CMM and the predictive value of the combination of the two remain incompletely understood.ObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the relationships among novel obesity indices, the TyG index, and the CMM and to evaluate the predictive value of composite indices that combine obesity indices with the TyG index for CMM risk, thereby providing a basis for developing refined and multidimensional intervention strategies.MethodsWe used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), including baseline information collected in 2011 and follow-up data gathered in 2015 and 2018, which included 9162 participants. The associations between the obesity indices, TyG and CMM were investigated via Cox regression models, Kaplan–Meier curves, the MacKinnon product distribution method, interaction effect analyses, ROC analysis and related indicators (NRI, IDI).ResultsAmong the study population, 1530 participants (16.70%) developed CMM. Cox regression analysis indicated significant associations of obesity indices and the TyG index with CMM (P < 0.001). TyG mediated over 20% of the associations between obesity indices and CMM. The interaction effects between obesity indices and TyG on CMM were the most significant. The CVAI showed the best predictive performance among the obesity indices in this study, and the combination of obesity indices and the TyG index showed enhanced prediction performance for CMM (P < 0.001).ConclusionOur study demonstrated that the composite index constructed by combining novel obesity indices with TyG significantly improved the accuracy of predicting CMM risk in middle-aged and elderly individuals, underscoring the critical role of maintaining a healthy weight and enhancing insulin sensitivity in the prevention of CMM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01762-6.

Introduction

As the world’s population ages, 4.7% of people aged 60 years and older have two or more cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) [1]. A population survey of 500,000 people aged 30–79 years in China revealed that the prevalence of cardiometabolic disease was 6.0% [2]. A cohort study based on British Regional Heart data revealed a 31.0% incidence of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) in men aged 60–79 years in the UK [3]. The presence of cardiometabolic disease greatly increases the risk of developing other diseases. In addition, the prevalence of CMM is expected to rise globally, not only increasing the risk of death for patients and reducing life expectancy but also creating significant challenges for socioeconomic and healthcare systems [4, 5].

CMM, defined as the coexistence of two or more CMDs, mainly includes type 2 diabetes, heart disease and stroke [6]. Obesity, as a reliable predictor, has been shown to be strongly associated with many individual CMDs, such as hypertension, coronary heart disease, stroke [7], insulin resistance, and metabolic disorders [8]. Similarly, obesity has become a recognized risk factor for many cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, including stroke, type 2 diabetes and heart disease, and it affects approximately 36.9% of men and 38.0% of women globally [9]. In recent years, many studies have found that some newly established obesity indicators have important predictive value for cardiovascular and metabolic diseases in the population, such as Chinese Visceral Adiposity Index (CVAI) [10], Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI) [11], Lipid Accumulation Product (LAP) [12] and Body Roundness Index (BRI) [13]. Insulin resistance is a key factor in the pathogenesis of a range of cardiovascular [14] and metabolic diseases, including diabetes [15] and stroke [16]. Insulin resistance refers to the body’s reduced sensitivity to insulin, resulting in insulin being unable to effectively transport glucose into cells, resulting in metabolic abnormalities such as hyperglycemia [17]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a measure of an individual’s insulin sensitivity and has shown significant utility in epidemiological studies [18, 19]. A CHARLS-based study revealed that a high TyG index was associated with a greater risk of CMM events [20].

In conclusion, obesity and the TyG index are independent risk factors for CMM. Previous studies have confirmed that the composite indices combined with the TyG index and obesity indices has a better predictive effect than a single index does [21]. For example, Rong‑Rui Huo et al. combined body mass index (BMI) with TyG and reported that the TyG-BMI performed better than the TyG and BMI in predicting stroke in elderly individuals [22, 23]. However, current studies have focused on the influence of traditional obesity indicators and the TyG index on certain cardiovascular diseases or metabolic diseases, and few studies have considered the associations of new obesity indices, such as the CVAI, VAI, LAP, BRI and TyG composite index, with CMM. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of new obesity indices, such as the CVAI, VAI, BRI and LAP, as well as the two traditional indicators of BMI, body fat percentage (BFP) and TyG, on CMM in a cohort of middle-aged and elderly Chinese people. In addition, we explored the interaction effects between these obesity indices and TyG on CMM, as well as the mediating role of TyG. Moreover, we further evaluated the predictive value of composite indicators for CMM.

Methods

Study sample

We used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), and the data can be accessed at http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en. The CHARLS is a longitudinal, nationwide cohort that targets adults aged 45 years and older. It is China’s first nationally representative survey covering both rural and urban regions, launched in 2011 [24]. To ensure a representative sample, a multistage probability sampling method was employed during the baseline survey [25]. We collected data at baseline in 2011 and conducted follow-up surveys in 2015 and 2018. The Peking University Biomedical Ethics Review Committee agreed to collect CHARLS data (IRB0000105211015), and all participants signed informed consent forms. We initially screened 17,551 participants from the CHARLS 2011, 2015, and 2018 waves. After applying exclusion criteria, we included 9162 participants and followed them up until 2018. The participant selection flow chart is shown in Fig. 1, and the exclusion criteria included the following: (1) the participants were younger than 45 years old (n = 365); (2) missing data for any component of the obesity indices (n = 7579) and CMM (n = 316); (3) participants with deficient outcome variables and lacking covariates (n = 129).

Studies have shown that LAP plays an important role in identifying diabetes and cardiovascular disease risk and is more reflective of visceral fat accumulation [28].

Outcome assessment

The outcome measured in this study was the first occurrence of cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) in participants from 2011, 2015 and 2018 defined as suffering from two or more comorbid conditions, such as hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, stroke, cardiac events, and diabetes mellitus, in a single individual [30]. The identification of participants with coronary heart disease or stroke was mainly based on self-reports collected during baseline and follow‐up surveys (‘Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with hypertension/hyperlipemia?’; ‘Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with diabetes or high blood sugar?’; ‘Have you been diagnosed with coronary artery disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems by a doctor?’ and ‘Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a stroke?’).

Other covariates

According to prior knowledge, we considered several variables in our analysis, including age, sex, marital status (unmarried, married, divorced/widowed), education level (illiterate/primary school, junior high school, High school or equivalent and university/college junior college or above), cigarette smoking status, current alcohol consumption status and number of chronic diseases at baseline (0, 1, 2 and ≥ 3). Furthermore, a comprehensive set of anthropometric and clinical biomarkers was considered. These comprised adiposity indices, including the Chinese Visceral Adiposity Index (CVAI), Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI), Body Roundness Index (BRI), Lipid Accumulation Product (LAP), Body Mass Index (BMI), and Body Fat Percentage (BFP); the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index; and blood biomarkers, including glucose, cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglyceride, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, uric acid, C-reactive protein (CRP), and glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c).

Statistical methods

All the statistical analyses were performed via R software (version 4.3.3). To account for the complex sampling design of CHARLS, the data cleaning and merging procedures followed the CHARLS analytical guidelines.

The participants were divided into two groups on the basis of their CMM status (non-CMM and incident-CMM) to describe their characteristics. Categorical variables and continuous variables are presented as numbers (percentages) and means (standard deviations), respectively. In addition, the participants were divided into four quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4) on the basis of obesity indices and the TyG index. Demographics were compared among the four groups via ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\chi\:}^{2}$$\end{document} test for categorical variables. Multivariate-adjusted Cox regression models based on four quartiles were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between obesity indices and the TyG index and subsequent CMM incidence. Hazard ratios (HRs) quantify the relative risk of developing CMM per unit increase in an obesity index. For example, HR = 1.5 indicates a 50% higher risk. Confidence intervals (95% CIs) reflect the precision of this estimate – if the interval excludes 1 (e.g. 95% CI 1.2–1.8), the association is statistically significant (P < 0.05). Model 1 was the net model with unadjusted covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 was further adjusted for education level and marital status at baseline, and Model 4 was further adjusted for current cigarette smoking status, current alcohol consumption status, number of other chronic diseases and other blood test indices at baseline on the basis of Model 1. All covariates included in the above models were measured at baseline in 2011 and were treated as time‑fixed variables in the Cox regression analyses. The cumulative rates of CMM for each obesity index and TyG index were compared among groups categorized by quartile via Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test. We performed the cox.zph() function with R software to confirm the proportional hazards assumption. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analyses were conducted with four knots at the 5th, 25th, 75th and 95th percentiles of obesity indices and TyG distributions to explore the potential dose‒response relationships. The linear portion reflects the direct effect of a unit change in exposure on the outcome in the region below the first knot (or across the entire range if no knots are present). The nonlinear component is revealed by the shape of the overall curve, indicating whether there are increases, decreases, or inflection points in risk/effect when exposure exceeds a specific knot. This approach is particularly valuable for interpreting complex relationships that cannot easily be adequately captured by linear models alone. To explore the influence of different obesity indices on CMM in elderly individuals, a separate intermediary effect analysis was conducted with 6 obesity indices as independent variables X, the dependent variable Y as CMM, the intermediary variable M as TyG, and the control variables as other variables with statistical significance in the univariate analysis [31]. The first step is the Cox regression analysis of X to Y, and the total effect c of X to Y is obtained. The second step is the Cox regression analysis of X to M, and the effect a of X to M is obtained. The third step is the Cox regression analysis of X and M on Y, and the effect b of M on Y and the direct effect c’ of X on Y are obtained. Finally, the R-Mediation package is used to calculate the 95% confidence interval (CI) of Za×Zb via the MacKinnon product distribution method. If the interval does not contain 0, the mediation effect is significant (where Za and Zb are the Z test statistics of a and b, respectively, defined as Za = a/SE(a) and Zb = b/SE(b). We further assessed the additive and multiplicative interactions of TyG with obesity indices [32]. Following the methods of Huo et al. [33], three indicators were used to assess additive interaction effects: relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the proportion attributable to interaction (AP), and the synergy index (SI). Among them, RERI = 0, AP = 0, and SI = 1 indicate that the independent variable and TyG had no interaction effect on the influence of CMM. Conversely, when RERI >0, AP >0, and SI >1, the combined effect of the independent variables and TyG on CMM is greater than the sum of their individual effects, suggesting a synergistic effect. In contrast, if RERI < 0, AP < 0, and SI < 1, the combined effect is less than the sum of individual effects, suggesting antagonism. In addition, the discriminative ability of obesity indices and the composite index of obesity indices with TyG models was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with the area under the ROC curve (AUC) calculated for each model. The net reclassification index (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement index (IDI) were used to further compare the incremental predictive value of the combined and individual effects of obesity indices and the composite index. NRI = 0 means that the new model has no improvement, and >0 means that the new model is better than the old model. When the IDI >0, the larger the value is, the better the prediction effect of the new model. To test the robustness of this study, the following sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) the above statistical analysis process was repeated according to different subgroups by means of stratified analysis; (2) the E value was calculated to test the sensitivity of our analysis [34]. (3) We selected two waves of data from CHARLS in 2015 and 2018 to further verify the predictive value of the results of single and composite indicators in this study. All P values were two tailed, and a significance level of 0.05 was used.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants according to CMM status

The baseline characteristics of the participants stratified by incident CMM status during follow-up are shown in Table 1. A total of 9162 participants were included at baseline (2011), and 1530 participants (16.70%) newly developed CMM during follow‑up (2015 and 2018). Compared with participants in the non-CMM group, participants who subsequently developed CMM during follow-up tended to be female, have a higher education level, be smokers, be current drinkers, and to have a higher burden of other chronic diseases at baseline (all P < 0.05). Significant differences in baseline obesity indices (including CVAI, VAI, BRI, LAP, BMI and BFP) and TyG were observed between the two groups defined by incident CMM during follow-up (all P < 0.001), and participants in the incident-CMM group presented higher baseline levels of these indicators than did those in the non-CMM group. Additionally, participants in the incident-CMM group had higher baseline levels of relevant blood test indicators, including glucose, cholesterol, LDL-C, triglyceride, creatinine, uric acid, CRP and HbA1c; however, their baseline HDL-C was lower (all P < 0.001). Associations between obesity indices and CMM incidence

The associations between obesity indices and CMM incidence are shown in Supplementary Table S1 and Table 2 via Cox regression analyses. Table 2 presents the Cox regression analysis results for Model 4 after adjusting for all covariates significant in the univariate analyses. The corresponding HRs (95% CIs) per SD increase in CVAI, VAI, BRI, LAP, BMI, BFP and TyG were 3.200 (2.814, 3.640), 1.618 (1.519, 1.722), 4.076 (3.525, 4.715), 6.892 (5.732, 8.287), 1.375 (1.049, 1.803), 1.674 (1.469, 1.845) and 2.387 (1.121, 4.706), respectively, all of which were statistically significant (P<0.001). In addition, compared with the lowest quartile groups (Q1) at baseline, the fully adjusted HRs (95% CIs) for CMM in the highest quartile group (Q4) for CVAI, BRI, LAP, BMI, BFP and TyG were 1.039 (1.019, 1.058), 1.189 (1.111, 1.273), 1.037 (1.022, 1.052), 1.039 (1.019, 1.058), 1.039 (1.019, 1.058) and 1.673 (1.389, 2.016), respectively. By contrast, the association between VAI and CMM incidence was not statistically significant in the quartile-based analysis. Fig 2 shows the cumulative hazard of CMM increased across successive quartiles, and log-rank tests confirmed significantly higher risks for Q4, Q3, and Q2 versus Q1 across most indices, with VAI as the notable exception. In addition, multivariable-adjusted RCS revealed a significant dose‒response relationship between all seven indices and CMM (P for overall trend < 0.001; P for nonlinear trend < 0.001), as shown in Supplementary Fig S2.

Assessment of the predictive performance of obesity indices and TyG for incident CMM and the additional predictive value

The ROC curves and the AUC values of single obesity indices and the composite indices combining these indices with TyG demonstrated their predictive ability for incident CMM, as shown in Fig. 3A. Among the single indices, CVAI achieved the largest AUC (all P < 0.001). In addition, the performance of each composite index was superior to that of its corresponding single obesity index for predicting CMM incidence. The AUCs (95% CIs) of CVAI-TyG/VAI-TyG/BRI-TyG/LAP-TyG/BMI-TyG/BFP-TyG were 0.689 (0.672, 0.701), 0.627 (0.604, 0.636), 0.690 (0.675, 0.705), 0.679 (0.664, 0.693), 0.682 (0.667, 0.697) and 0.642 (0.628, 0.658), respectively.

Figure 3B and Supplementary Table S5 show the additional predictive performance for CMM achieved by adding the composite indices to Model 4, the baseline model described in the Methods. We compared a baseline model plus a composite index against the same baseline model plus the corresponding single index, using NRI and IDI. The composite indices demonstrated significantly enhanced incremental predictive ability according to the IDI and NRI estimates (all P < 0.001). When added to the baseline model (which included age, sex, marital status, education, number of NCDs, smoking status, drinking status, and blood test indices), the continuous NRIs and 95% CIs comparing the composite versus single indices were 0.003 (0.002, 0.004), 0.014 (0.011, 0.017), 0.015 (0.012, 0.018), 0.012 (0.010, 0.015), 0.015 (0.012, 0.018) and 0.025 (0.022, 0.029), respectively (all P< 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses for the relationships between obesity indices, TyG, and CMM

As displayed in Supplementary Table S3, subgroup analyses stratified by the baseline characteristics of participants revealed that the associations of the six obesity indices and the TyG index with CMM remained statistically significant in most subgroups, indicating that all were risk factors (P for association < 0.01). In the sensitivity analyses, we further explored whether the number of chronic diseases modified the associations between obesity indices and CMM. Significant interactions were observed between the number of chronic diseases and several obesity indices (CVAI, VAI, BRI, LAP, BMI), indicating that obesity may have stronger effects on CMM among participants with more pre-existing conditions. In addition, to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, we calculated the E-values for the HRs via the Cox regression models (Supplementary Table S4). A comparison of the observed HRs with their corresponding E-values indicated that unmeasured confounding was unlikely to have substantially altered the results. Notably, as a further sensitivity analysis, we repeated the prediction assessment using data from the 2015 and 2018 survey waves. These results were consistent with our primary findings, confirming that CVAI had the largest AUC and that the composite indices performed better than the single indices. The detailed results are shown in Supplementary Figure S4 and Table S5.

Discussion

In this study, drawing on data from the CHARLS cohort, we systematically evaluated multiple obesity indices (CVAI, BRI, LAP, BMI, BFP, VAI) and the TyG index for their associations with cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM). Our findings indicated that CVAI, BRI, LAP, BMI, and BFP were significantly associated with CMM risk, whereas the VAI was not. In addition, TyG was strongly linked to CMM incidence. Mediation analyses revealed that TyG partially mediated the associations between CVAI, BRI, LAP, BMI, BFP and CMM, but fully mediated the relationship between VAI and CMM. Furthermore, combining obesity indices with TyG moderately improved predictive performance, with CVAI displaying particularly robust predictive value both alone and in conjunction with TyG. These observations underscore the importance of jointly considering obesity status and insulin resistance when assessing the CMM risk among middle-aged and older adults.

Notably, our Cox regression analysis results are consistent with those of previous Chinese cohort studies, which reported strong associations between certain obesity metrics (e.g., CVAI, LAP, BMI) and TyG with individual cardiometabolic conditions such as stroke, diabetes, and heart disease. This pattern aligns with the capacity‒load model, suggesting that obesity increases the metabolic load and disrupts the body’s metabolic capacity, thereby increasing the risk of cardiometabolic diseases [35–37]. For unconventional new indicators such as CVAI, VAI, BRI, and LAP, which do not yet have uniform clinical thresholds, we calculated both per-unit and per-SD increases, which could guide patients in assessing whether their measurements fall within the population distribution, and then to evaluate their corresponding risk changes. However, our study did not find a significant relationship between the VAI and CMM. Indeed, previous evidence has shown that VAI was inferior to CVAI, BMI, and WC in evaluating visceral adipose accumulation among Chinese adults [38, 39].The reason for this may stem from the fact that VAI was originally developed and calibrated using non-Asian populations. Significant variations in visceral fat content across different ethnic groups are closely linked to a complex interplay of biological and environmental factors. Consequently, VAI might not fully capture the specific adiposity characteristics prevalent in Chinese individuals. In contrast to VAI, although both VAI and CVAI incorporate triglycerides (TG) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) into their calculations, the CVAI goes a step further by explicitly accounting for the influences of age and BMI within the Chinese population. CVAI attempts to adjust for certain biological factors to a greater extent, thereby offering a potentially more accurate reflection of visceral adiposity in the Chinese demographic. Li Liu et al.‘s findings in postmenopausal women, in whom the VAI was also unrelated to CVD, could partially support the results of the present study [40]. Moreover, the log-rank tests revealed that the cumulative incidence of CMM increased from Q1 to Q3 for some obesity indices but decreased in Q4, which could be explained by increased mortality in the severe obesity group. Several studies support this phenomenon: one clinical trial reported that one-year mortality in stroke patients increases significantly with increasing BMI [41], whereas a British cohort study reported that central obesity was positively associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [42], and similar patterns were also documented for CVAI in a Chinese cohort [43]. Additionally, RCS analyses in our study hinted at nonlinearity in the impacts of obesity and TyG on CMM, possibly reflecting the multietiological nature of CMM as well as specific RCS parameter settings.

Furthermore, we demonstrated that TyG acts as a mediator in the relationship between obesity and CMM. The existing studies may provide some basis for explaining the mechanism of this mediating effect. Obesity is considered an important and modifiable risk factor for metabolic diseases such as stroke and diabetes [44]. Lipid accumulation, insulin resistance, apoptosis and inflammation caused by obesity are common pathways involved in CMM development [45, 46]. More specifically, the hypoxic milieu caused by mismatched adipose tissue expansion triggers oxidative stress and promotes adipocyte fibrosis and necrosis [47]. In addition, it upregulates the expression of proinflammatory genes in tissue-resident immune cells, which promotes insulin resistance in fat cells and leads to elevated blood glucose [48]. In particular, individuals with abdominal or visceral obesity are more likely to develop insulin resistance, which indicates early impaired glucose metabolism [49]. As an indicator of insulin resistance and metabolic health, TyG provides a window into the complex metabolic processes by which elevated obesity markers may increase the risk of CMM. The role of the TyG index as a mediator of the association highlights the importance of obesity indicators and the TyG index in metabolic health assessment. Our study also explored the potential interactions between obesity indices and TyG on CMM, and only the interaction between VAI and TyG showed a statistically significant synergistic effect, whereas no significant interactions were found for TyG with CVAI, BRI, LAP, BMI, or BFP, which is consistent with previous findings on the predominant role of visceral adiposity [50]. The development of both visceral fat accumulation and insulin resistance is often driven by shared lifestyle or genetic factors, such as physical inactivity and poor diet. Consequently, their biological mechanisms are likely to overlap significantly in the pathogenesis of CMM. This mechanistic overlap may obscure the detection of measurable synergistic or antagonistic interactions in statistical models. Therefore, further investigation is warranted into the specific biological pathways through which these factors influence CMM. As a multifactorial condition, CMM is also affected by smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and diet, in addition to obesity and insulin resistance [51]. These additional factors may mask or neutralize the observed interaction between obesity and TyG. Moreover, the pathways linking obesity indices and TyG to CMM risk may differ, and the specific mechanisms by which they contribute to CMM require further exploration. Even if this study fails to account for the presence of an interaction effect, the results of the mediating and interacting effects still underscore the importance of these obesity indices and the TyG index in assessing CMM risk.

Regarding predictive performance, our ROC analysis indicated that CVAI emerged as the strongest single obesity indicator for CMM among the six measures tested, and composite indices incorporating TyG did improve risk discrimination, albeit modestly. The conclusion that CVAI performs better than other measures of obesity has been reported; for example, the results of a cohort study from rural China revealed that CVAI is the most suitable predictor of stroke among multiple measures of insulin resistance. A study from China also revealed that CVAI is better than LAP, AIP and other similar indicators in predicting CMM risk [52]. Moreover, Rong‑Rui Huo et al. reported that the TyG-BMI composite index predicted 5-year mortality and chronic heart failure risk better than either index alone did, which is similar to the results of this study [53]. A NHANES-based study also revealed that TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR were more effective than TyG, WC and WHtR in diagnosing cardiovascular disease and cause-specific mortality [23, 54]. Furthermore, integrating TyG with obesity indices presents a more accurate insulin resistance evaluation than the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) or the TyG index alone [29, 55]. However, in this study, even though it can be used as a simple and easily accessible clinical assessment tool to predict the risk of CMM, it is important to note that the AUC of all the measures we measured was less than 0.7. Given the complexity of the pathogenesis of CMM, finding a perfect indicator for risk prediction is difficult. Obesity, especially abdominal obesity, can only partially explain the incidence of CMM. In summary, the results of our study revealed that the composite index is better than the single obesity index in the risk prediction of CMM and still has potential utility in the diagnosis, prevention and treatment of clinical and public health conditions.

Our findings suggest that a comprehensive assessment of obesity indices and TyG can be used to identify at-risk populations for more targeted and tailored prevention strategies. In addition, our study highlights the importance of addressing the obesity epidemic and promoting metabolic health to significantly reduce the overall burden of CMM and improve the health of the population at the public health level.

Despite these promising findings, our study has several limitations. First, this study has an observational design, which precludes the inference of causality. Moreover, the disease outcome (CMM) investigated was derived from self-reporting by the study participants. This methodology is a significant limitation, as recall bias, omission, and misreporting could lead to an underestimation of CMM incidence and potentially attenuate the observed associations between visceral adiposity and CMM. Even if we have identified a mediating role of TyG in the association between obesity indices and CMM, further experimental verification remains needed. Second, the reliance on self-reported diagnoses and health-related behaviors introduces measurement bias. In addition, since the CHARLS database does not provide specific information on the dietary composition of the study population, and the frequency of physical activity in the last week or month was obtained via the participants’ recall, resulting in a substantial amount of non-imputable missing data. As a result, we were unable to adjust for these potential confounders in our analysis. However, visceral obesity in the present study was associated with both physical activity and diet, and this primary variable may also compensate somewhat for the bias introduced by this issue. Third, since the CHARLS database does not collect information on participants’ use of lipid-lowering medications (such as statins), it is impossible to control for the confounding effects of drug interventions when constructing predictive models. Future datasets containing more detailed medication usage information will help further refine the accuracy and generalisability of these models. Finally, considering the contextual differences in culture and healthcare in other countries, and the Chinese-specific indicator of CVAI, the application of the results of this study should be cautiously generalised to other regions.

In conclusion, this work highlights the significant role of novel obesity measures—particularly CVAI, which reflects visceral adipose deposition—and the insulin resistance marker TyG in predicting CMM among middle-aged and older adults. While TyG partly or wholly mediates obesity–CMM associations, its integration with obesity measures can further aid in the early detection and risk stratification of individuals vulnerable to cardiometabolic multimorbidity. Although additional research is necessary to enhance predictive models and address various confounders, our findings underscore the importance of maintaining healthy weight and improving insulin sensitivity in the prevention and management of multiple cardiometabolic conditions. Ultimately, more refined and multidimensional assessment strategies are warranted to identify and intervene in high-risk populations to mitigate the overall burden of CMM.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that the novel obesity indices and the insulin resistance marker TyG have potential value in the assessment of CMM in middle-aged and older adults. We found that not only are individual indices associated with CMM, but TyG also partially mediated the relationship between obesity indices and CMM. This suggests that insulin resistance could be an important mechanism by which obesity contributes to CMM. Furthermore, the composite index constructed by combining obesity indicators with the TyG index shows promise for the early identification of high-risk individuals. Overall, these findings emphasise the importance of maintaining a healthy body weight and improving insulin sensitivity for the prevention and intervention of CMM and indicate that a more comprehensive assessment approach should be considered for patients with established CMM to support the development of an integrated obesity management strategy.
