Candida glabrata vertebral osteomyelitis following L3–4 transforaminal lumbar fusion: illustrative case
Authors: Bryce R. Michael, Adam Youssef, Paige Grafton, Saleh Mostafa, Jonathan Sayegh, Luis A. Robles, Abduljabbar Alhammoud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCandida vertebral osteomyelitis is rare, particularly following instrumented spine surgery. Candida glabrata presents additional management challenges due to its frequent resistance to azole antifungal treatment and its association with immunocompromised hosts.OBSERVATIONSA 74-year-old immunosuppressed man underwent L3–4 transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion with cyst excision. Seven weeks postoperatively, he presented with constitutional symptoms and elevated inflammatory markers but was discharged with presumed viral illness. Nine months after surgery, imaging revealed cage displacement. Intraoperative cultures during hardware removal identified C. glabrata. The patient underwent repeat irrigation and debridement with hardware exchange and was treated with intravenous micafungin and oral fluconazole and later transitioned to posaconazole. Despite ongoing antifungal therapy, inflammatory markers remained elevated, and follow-up imaging showed progressive vertebral destruction without hardware failure. The patient continues on chronic antifungal suppression with multidisciplinary monitoring.LESSONSThis case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of postoperative C. glabrata vertebral osteomyelitis. Early suspicion, culture confirmation, and organism-specific antifungal therapy, alongside timely surgical management, are critical to achieving infection control and preserving spinal stability in immunosuppressed patients. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25555

Candida glabrata is a rare, opportunistic yeast that can present significant challenges in postoperative infections, especially among immunosuppressed patients.1 Although bacterial pathogens are the most common cause of postoperative spinal hardware infections, fungal pathogens, such as those caused by C. glabrata, are often underrecognized in their potential to contribute to diagnostic delays, increased morbidity, suboptimal surgical outcomes, and significant postoperative complications. More specifically, C. glabrata exhibits unique features that may complicate treatment in the setting of postoperative spinal infection, including intrinsic resistance to common azole antifungals,2,3 which limit therapeutic options and often require higher doses and prolonged courses of treatment and recovery.

This report presents the case of a 74-year old male who developed a rare C. glabrata infection following a transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) procedure secondary to severe spinal stenosis. The patient’s persistent postoperative infection required multiple surgical interventions and prolonged antifungal therapy, complicating the patient’s recovery and emphasizing the importance of early recognition and targeted management of fungal postoperative infections.

Illustrative Case

In February 2024, a 74-year-old man with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, benign prostatic hyperplasia, anxiety, insomnia, and alopecia on chronic immunosuppressive therapy with baricitinib (September 2023–March 2024) and with recent exposure to high-dose corticosteroids (prednisone 50 mg daily for 20 days in January 2024 during neurological evaluation for suspected amyotrophic lateral sclerosis or Guillain-Barré syndrome) underwent an L3–4 TLIF combined with excision of an intracanal synovial cyst causing severe canal stenosis. His baricitinib therapy was not held prior to surgery due to the urgency of the procedure. Workup for chronic fatigue after the index procedure included colonoscopy and HIV testing, of which both were negative. Preoperatively, he reported low back pain with bilateral L4, L5, and S1 radiculopathies, and MRI confirmed degenerative facet disease with a synovial cyst at L3–4 (Figs. 1 and 2). His initial postoperative course was uncomplicated, with resolution of his radicular symptoms. Seven weeks postoperatively (April 2024), he presented to the emergency department with 3 days of fever up to 38.5°C, night sweats, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and an unintentional 25-pound weight loss after an orthopedic spine consultation. Laboratory evaluation revealed a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 15.4 mg/dL, an absolute neutrophil count of 8.87 × 103/µL (81.3% neutrophils), and immature granulocyte count of 0.17 × 103/µL (1.3%); an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was not obtained. Lumbar CT imaging demonstrated expected postoperative changes limited by metal artifact, and his neurological examination was unremarkable. With no definitive source of deep infection identified, he was discharged with a presumptive diagnosis of viral gastroenteritis.

Nine months after his fusion (November 2024), he followed up with his primary care provider for abdominal pain and concurrently requested evaluation of worsening back pain. Radiographs and lumbar CT images unexpectedly revealed displacement of the L3–4 interbody cage (Figs. 3 and 4). This delay in imaging and diagnosis reflected a lapse in routine follow-up, as the patient had been seen at 3-week and 3-month postoperative visits but missed his 6-month follow-up appointment. The eventual return was prompted by abdominal/pelvic CT imaging ordered for unrelated symptoms rather than scheduled spine surveillance. He denied radicular pain, bowel or bladder dysfunction, and signs of cauda equina syndrome, but described intermittent thigh numbness. He was admitted and taken to the operating room for incision and drainage (I&D), removal of the displaced cage, and placement of a wound VAC. The patient was no longer taking baricitinib or any other immunosuppressive medications at this time. Intraoperative cultures grew C. glabrata within 24 hours. No rapid fungal assays, such as Fungitell, were performed. There had been no preoperative suspicion for fungal infection, as the revision was undertaken for cage displacement and nonunion repair. Intraoperatively, an abnormal fluid collection with atypical color was noted, prompting cultures that revealed fungal growth. Laboratory workup at that time showed an ESR of 56 mm/hr, CRP level of 17.1 mg/dL, and mild anemia. Intravenous micafungin was initiated pending susceptibilities. Five days later, he returned for a second I&D with hardware exchange and application of a Prevena negative-pressure wound therapy device; a lumbar drain was removed on postoperative day 2. Oral fluconazole 800 mg daily was added to micafungin, with a treatment plan of 6–12 months followed by lifelong suppressive therapy given the retained hardware.

At his 2-week postoperative visit, he reported significant improvement in back pain and paresthesia. Examination revealed only paraspinal tenderness without focal neurological deficits; he continued to deny radicular pain, bowel or bladder dysfunction, or cauda equina signs. Inflammatory markers remained elevated (CRP level 22.8 mg/dL, ESR 70 mm/hr), and his hemoglobin level was 11 g/dL. Lumbar radiographs showed stable alignment without hardware loosening.

At an infectious disease follow-up in January 2025 (11 months postoperatively), he remained compliant but reported fatigue, nausea, insomnia, and exercise-related weakness. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated an improving CRP level of 8.4 mg/dL but persistently elevated ESR of 65 mm/hr. Given these results, posaconazole 300 mg daily was continued indefinitely after insurance delays postponed its start from December 2024 to January 2025.

In early February 2025, per patient request, a posaconazole drug level was drawn; he required Norco 5/325 mg four times daily for breakthrough back pain. Follow-up CT imaging in early March 2025 (13 months postfusion) demonstrated progressive erosive and sclerotic changes at L3–4, soft tissue extension into the spinal canal, and an ossific fragment within the right L3–4 foramen, without new hardware failure (Fig. 5). He remains on chronic posaconazole with ongoing infectious disease and spine clinic monitoring for persistent C. glabrata vertebral osteomyelitis and retained hardware. Surgical Technique

Initial Surgery

Following standard posterior midline exposure, subperiosteal dissection was carried down to the level of L3 and L4. Pedicle screw instrumentation was placed bilaterally at L3 and L4 under navigation guidance. Intraoperative findings revealed a large, bilateral synovial cyst extending from the right to the left facet complex, causing significant thecal sac compression. A bilateral total facetectomy was performed at the L3–4 level to fully expose the cyst.

The synovial cyst was carefully dissected off the dura mater and neural elements using a microsurgical technique and subsequently excised in total. Once adequate decompression was confirmed, a standard TLIF was performed using a titanium interbody cage filled with autograft and bone morphogenetic protein/allograft material. Final fluoroscopic imaging confirmed appropriate cage and screw placement. Vancomycin powder was applied to the wound prior to a layered closure.

First Revision Surgery

The patient returned to the operating room for revision of the displaced interbody cage. On reopening the prior incision, abnormal serous fluid was encountered in the surgical field, surrounding the dura and interbody space. Fluid and soft tissue cultures were obtained. The wound was thoroughly irrigated with hydrogen peroxide, betadine solution, and normal saline.

The thecal sac was carefully retracted and protected. The previously placed interbody cage was found to be displaced and was removed without complication. Additional irrigation of the disc space was performed. Given concern for ongoing infection, the wound was partially closed, and a vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) dressing was applied to allow for continued drainage and wound management.

Second Revision (third surgery)

A second-look procedure was performed 48 hours later. On exploration, no purulence or active drainage was noted. The wound and disc space were irrigated again. The prior pedicle screws were removed and exchanged due to concerns for colonization, and only autograft and morselized allograft were used in the disc space to achieve interbody fusion.

The wound was closed in a standard layered fashion following final irrigation. No intraoperative complications occurred. Postoperative imaging confirmed hardware position and alignment.

Discussion

Observations

This case presents a rare instance of postoperative osteomyelitis due to C. glabrata, an opportunistic fungal pathogen with inherent resistance to standard azole therapies. The primary observation was that timely identification of C. glabrata (after an initial delay) enabled the initiation of a targeted antifungal regimen consisting of intravenous micafungin and high-dose oral fluconazole. This, in combination with repeated I&D, hardware exchange, and negative pressure wound therapy (wound VAC), led to successful infection control and clinical improvement.

This observation is significant because it adds to the growing clinical awareness of non–Candida albicans species as emerging pathogens in postoperative infections. The importance lies in the pathogen’s resistance profile and the need for alternative antifungal strategies. Similar cases have been reported in the literature, including those requiring combination antifungal therapy4,5 or, in resistant cases, treatment escalation to amphotericin B and voriconazole.6 A systematic review of 211 cases supports the effectiveness of echinocandins like micafungin in Candida osteomyelitis,7 reinforcing the clinical relevance of our therapeutic approach.

Lessons

The most important lesson from this case is that C. glabrata must be considered in the differential diagnosis of persistent postoperative infections, especially when patients do not respond to broad-spectrum antibacterial agents. Early identification through appropriate cultures and fungus-specific diagnostics is essential to prevent delayed treatment and prolonged morbidity. This case also emphasizes that combination antifungal therapy—tailored to susceptibility profiles—can be both effective and well tolerated, even over extended durations.

A key limitation of this case is that it represents a single patient experience and, therefore, cannot be generalized. Moreover, the prolonged treatment course may not be suitable for all patients due to potential toxicity, cost, and patient adherence challenges. Nevertheless, the relevance of this case lies in highlighting that individualized, pathogen-specific treatment strategies are critical when dealing with similar resistant organisms.

In conclusion, C. glabrata is a rare but important cause of postoperative fungal infection, especially in immunocompromised patients. Its treatment requires early recognition, prolonged therapy, and often combination antifungal strategies. Failure to identify C. glabrata early may result in chronic, difficult-to-treat infections. Clinicians should maintain a high level of suspicion for fungal pathogens in nonhealing postoperative infections and initiate appropriate diagnostic testing promptly to improve outcomes and avoid long-term complications.
