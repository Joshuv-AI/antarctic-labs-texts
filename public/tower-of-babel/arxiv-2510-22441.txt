Metric Entropy and Minimax Risk of Ellipsoids with an Application to Pinsker’s Theorem
Thomas Allard

Abstract
We study how large an ℓ 2 \ell^{2} ellipsoid is by introducing type- τ \tau integrals that capture the average decay of its semi-axes. These integrals turn out to be closely related to standard complexity measures: we show that the metric entropy of the ellipsoid is asymptotically equivalent to the type- 1 1 integral, and that the minimax risk in non-parametric estimation is asymptotically determined by the type- 2 2 and type- 3 3 integrals. This allows us to retrieve and sharpen classical results about metric entropy and minimax risk of ellipsoids through a systematic analysis of the type- τ \tau integrals, and yields an explicit formula linking the two. As an application, we improve on the best-known characterization of the metric entropy of the Sobolev ellipsoid, and extend Pinsker’s Sobolev theorem in two ways: (i) to any bounded open domain in arbitrary finite dimension, and (ii) by providing the second-order term in the asymptotic expansion of the minimax risk.

Formally, for a sequence of non-negative real numbers

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}\coloneqq\Bigg\{\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})\,\Bigm|\,x_{n}=0,\text{ if }\mu_{n}=0,\text{ and }\sum_{n\colon\mu_{n}>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert{x_{n}}/{\mu_{n}}}}\right\rvert^{2}\leq 1\Bigg\}.

Ellipsoids have a rich structure,

M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\mid\mu_{n}\geq\varepsilon}}\right\}}}\right\rvert,\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0,

(2)

\varepsilon

The compactness of the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}

(i)

as

(ii)

Our claim is that compactness is more naturally measured by the asymptotics of the type-
\tau

We justify this by relating the type-

2
Metric Entropy and Type-1 Integral

Metric entropy is the most standard quantitative measure of compactness.
Its definition, provided in Definition
1
,
is motivated as follows.
In a metric space, a set is compact if and only if it is complete and totally bounded—that is, it can be covered by finitely many balls of same arbitrary radius.
Metric entropy goes one step further by measuring the minimal cardinality of such coverings:
the smaller this cardinality, the more compact the set.
In this sense, metric entropy indeed provides a quantitative measure of compactness.

Definition 1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
\mathcal{K}
be a compact subset of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
.
An
\varepsilon
-covering of
\mathcal{K}
is a set
\{x_{(1)},\dots,x_{(N)}\}\subset\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
such that for each
x\in\mathcal{K}
, there exists an
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
satisfying
\|x-x_{(i)}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
.
The
\varepsilon
-covering number
N(\varepsilon;\mathcal{K})
is the smallest cardinality of an
\varepsilon
-covering of
\mathcal{K}
.
The metric entropy of
\mathcal{K}
is obtained by taking the natural logarithm of the
\varepsilon
-covering number, that is
H(\varepsilon;\mathcal{K})\coloneqq\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N(\varepsilon;\mathcal{K})}}\right)
.

Some authors prefer defining metric entropy as the logarithm in base two of the covering number.
This is common in information theory when interpreting
H(\varepsilon;\mathcal{K})
as the bit length required to uniformly encode elements of
\mathcal{K}
to accuracy
\varepsilon

[
34
,
21
,
22
,
75
]
.
In that case, the corresponding results differ from our results by a multiplicative factor
\ln(2)
.

We consider the case when the compact set
\mathcal{K}
is an ellipsoid.
Before stating the main result,
we need to introduce an additional assumption on the semi-axis-counting function.

Regularity Condition (RC) on
M_{\mu}
.

There exists
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})
non-increasing such that
M_{\mu}(x)\sim f(x)
, as
x\to 0
, and whose elasticity

\rho(t)\coloneqq h^{\prime}(t),\quad\text{where }\quad h(t)\coloneqq\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-t}}}\right)}}\right),\quad\text{for all }t>0,

(4)

satisfies either
(i)
\rho(t)
has a finite limit as
t\to\infty
, or
(ii)
\rho(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, with
\ln(\rho(t))=O_{t\to\infty}(\rho(t/2))
, and
\rho
non-decreasing on
(t^{*},\infty)
for some
t^{*}>0
.

We refer to this regularity condition as (RC), or
\text{(RC)}_{b}

when we need to specify the limit
\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho(t)\eqcolon b\in[0,\infty]
.
It is set to avoid pathological cases, notably to prevent an accumulation of jumps of
M_{\mu}
.
It is not clear whether (RC) is necessary for our result or whether it can be relaxed.
In any case, it is not restrictive;
see Appendix
A
for a list of examples satisfying it.
We note that the finite-
b
case (item (i) in (RC)) is equivalent to
M_{\mu}
being regularly varying at zero (in the sense of Karamata) with index
b
; see Appendix
C.1
.

Theorem
2

establishes that, under (RC), the metric entropy of ellipsoids is asymptotically equivalent to the type-1 integral
I_{1}
, and provides a precise characterization of the error term.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mu=\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be a sequence such that
\mu_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
,
with corresponding semi-axis-counting function

satisfying (RC).
Then, the metric entropy of
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}

H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)=I_{1}(\varepsilon)+O_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{M_{\mu}(\varepsilon),\sqrt{M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)}}\right)\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)}}}\right\}}}\right).

(5)

In particular, we have

H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\sim I_{1}(\varepsilon),\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(6)

Proof sketch.

The relation (
5
) is established by proving matching upper and lower bounds.
The lower bound comes from a standard volume argument.
The upper bound relies on a block decomposition technique as introduced in
[
5
]

Finally, (
6
) is obtained from (

-term is always asymptotically negligible compared to

The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
B.1

∎

In many situations, one has access to the asymptotic expansion of

The asymptotics are preserved through integration and therefore yield corresponding asymptotics for

First, for finite-dimensional ellipsoids, i.e., when

(7)

Another fundamental case consists of polynomially-decaying semi-axes, say
\mu_{n}\sim(c/n)^{1/\alpha}

, or, equivalently,

.
Such ellipsoids typically arise as unit balls in smoothness classes (e.g., Sobolev spaces; see Section

, where

is the smoothness index and

Their metric entropy has been characterized in

,

,
4
,
5

Once again, we recover standard results using (

H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\sim\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{M_{\mu}(u)}{u}\mathop{}\!du\sim\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{c\,u^{-\alpha}}{u}\mathop{}\!du=\frac{c\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}{\alpha},\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(8)

Let us emphasize that there is much more to Theorem

than just the asymptotic equivalence (

For instance, given
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}>0
, and
\alpha_{1}>\dots>\alpha_{N}>0

-term generalization of (

M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{i}}+o_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{N}}}}\right),\quad\text{implies}\quad H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{c_{i}\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{i}}}{\alpha_{i}}+o_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{N}}}}\right),

provided
\alpha_{1}<2\alpha_{N}
(which ensures that the error term in (

-term).
The case

appears already in

By (
38
) in the proof of Theorem
2
,
the type-1 integral can equivalently be expressed as

I_{1}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\ln_{+}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\varepsilon}}}\right),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

(9)

With

of the form (

In that sense, the effective dimension of the problem is the number of semi-axes greater than

First, in his study of nuclear spaces,

which, with our notation, reads

I_{1}(2\varepsilon)\leq H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\leq I_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right)+2\ln(2)M_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

(10)

Theorem
2
and (

) are complementary:
the former is sharper but only asymptotic,
while the latter holds non-asymptotically but fails to capture even the leading term precisely.
Moreover, using (

)–(

, one can improve on the lower bound in (

) according to

Second, for finite-dimensional ellipsoids,
the asymptotic equivalence

was already obtained by Dumer, Pinsker, and Prelov in
[

]
,
[

[

Their setting differs slightly from ours as they allow the dimension

3
Minimax Risk, Type-2, and Type-3 Integrals

We leave metric entropy for a moment and turn to the study of the minimax risk for non-parametric estimation in the Gaussian sequence model.
Informally, the task is to denoise an unknown element known to lie in a compact set
\mathcal{K}
, observed with Gaussian noise of level
\sigma
.
When
\sigma
scales as
1/\sqrt{m}
, the problem is equivalent to non-parametric regression from
m
noisy samples;
we focus on the small-noise—equivalently, large-sample—regime.
In practice,
\mathcal{K}
is often a coefficient body relative to a basis—e.g., Besov classes in a wavelet basis, see the work of Donoho and Johnstone
[
26
,
24
,
20
,
23
,
25
]
.
We refer to
[
81
,
53
,
66
]
for a general treatment of non-parametric estimation, with a strong emphasis on the minimax risk for the Gaussian sequence model.

Specifically, for
\sigma>0
and
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
,
the Gaussian sequence model
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
aims to recover a sequence
x\in\mathcal{K}
from noisy observations
y=x+\sigma\xi
,
or, in components

y_{n}=x_{n}+\sigma\xi_{n},\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},

(12)

where
\xi=\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
has independent standard normal coordinates.
A statistical estimator is a measurable map
\hat{x}_{\sigma}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
that takes the noisy observation
y
to an estimate
\hat{x}_{\sigma}(y)
of the original parameter
x
.
Its performance is assessed by the mean squared error

\textnormal{MSE}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,\hat{x}_{\sigma};\mathcal{K}}}\right)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{x}_{\sigma}(y)-x}}\right\|^{2}_{2}}}\right],

where
y\sim x
indicates that the expectation is taken over
y
distributed according to (
12
).
The minimax risk is then defined as the best achievable performance in the worst-case scenario:
take the supremum of
\textnormal{MSE}_{\sigma}(x,\hat{x}_{\sigma};\mathcal{K})
over
x\in\mathcal{K}
, and then the infimum over
\hat{x}_{\sigma}
.

Definition 3
.

Let
\sigma>0

and
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
.
The minimax risk associated with the Gaussian sequence model
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
is

R_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{K}}}\right)\coloneqq\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\hat{x}_{\sigma}}\,\sup_{x\in\mathcal{K}}\,\textnormal{MSE}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,\hat{x}_{\sigma};\mathcal{K}}}\right).

(13)

The linear minimax risk associated with the model
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
is obtained from the minimax risk by restricting the infimum to linear estimators, that is,

R^{L}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{K}}}\right)\coloneqq\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\hat{x}_{\sigma}\text{ linear}}\,\sup_{x\in\mathcal{K}}\,\textnormal{MSE}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,\hat{x}_{\sigma};\mathcal{K}}}\right),

(14)

where we say that an estimator is linear if there exists a sequence of coefficients
\{c_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
such that

\hat{x}_{\sigma}(y)=\{c_{n}y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
.

We call the model
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
consistent if
R_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{K}}}\right)\to 0
as
\sigma\to 0
.
For a closed and bounded
\mathcal{K}\subset\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
,
consistency of

is equivalent to compactness of
\mathcal{K}

(see
[
50

48
]
and
[
53

).
This motivates using the decay of the minimax risk as a measure of compactness:
the faster
R_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{K}}}\right)
tends to zero, the more compact
\mathcal{K}
is.
Specializing to ellipsoids
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{E}_{\mu}
,
we thus expect a direct link between
R_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)
(or
R_{\sigma}^{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)
)
and other measures of compactness previously considered, such as the type-
\tau

for
\tau\geq 1
.
The next result makes this link precise by relating the linear minimax risk to the type-2 and type-3 integrals.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\sigma>0

\sigma^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2I_{3}(\varepsilon)-\frac{I_{2}(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}}}\right)=1.

(16)

Proof sketch.

The relation (

The existence and uniqueness of the solution of (

\varepsilon

We refer to Appendix

for the detailed proof.
∎

Theorem
4

for reasons that will become clear in Section

Using (

), and (

R_{\sigma}^{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\varepsilon>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\sigma^{2}\varepsilon\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(I_{2}(\varepsilon)-I_{3}(\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon}}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\}=2\sigma^{2}\varepsilon_{\sigma}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(I_{2}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})-I_{3}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})\,\varepsilon_{\sigma}}}\right)+\varepsilon_{\sigma}^{2},

To illustrate the use of Theorem

Corresponding results for the non-linear minimax risk follow by asymptotically relating it to the linear minimax risk.

.
∎

Using the asymptotics of the Lambert W function—namely,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}+2}{\alpha_{1}}}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}+2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c_{1}\sigma^{2}}{\alpha_{1}+1}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{\alpha_{1}+2}}

4
Metric Entropy and Minimax Risk

Section
2
shows that the metric entropy of
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}
is asymptotically determined by the type-
1
integral
I_{1}
.
Section
3
characterizes the linear minimax risk—and therefore the asymptotics of the (non-linear) minimax risk—in terms of the type-
2
and type-
3
integrals
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
.
To obtain an explicit relation between metric entropy and minimax risk,
we thus need to relate the asymptotics of the various integrals
I_{\tau}
as
\tau
varies.

First, the asymptotics of
M_{\mu}
always determine those of
I_{\tau}
, for every
\tau\geq 1
;
this is a consequence of the preservation of asymptotics under integration (see Lemma
11
in Appendix
C.2
and the subsequent discussion).
The converse fails in general,
but it does hold for regularly varying
I_{\tau}
(see Lemma
12
in Appendix
C.2
).
However, even in the regularly varying case, second-order asymptotics for
I_{\tau}
do not in general transfer to
M_{\mu}
;
this limitation will play a concrete role in Section
5
.

We just saw that
M_{\mu}
asymptotically determines
I_{\tau}
,
but how do the integrals
I_{\tau}
compare for different values of
\tau
?
The next lemma provides a formula to transfer properties from a type-
\tau_{1}
integral to a type-
\tau_{2}
integral.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\geq 1

and let
\mu=\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be a sequence of semi-axes such that
\mu_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Then, the corresponding
integrals of types
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
satisfy

I_{\tau_{1}}(\varepsilon)=I_{\tau_{2}}(\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon^{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}+(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}I_{\tau_{2}}(u)\,u^{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}-1}\mathop{}\!du,\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

(24)

Proof sketch.

The result follows directly via integration by parts;
the details are provided in Appendix
C.3
.
∎

Lemma
6
is non-asymptotic in nature.
However, when
\text{(RC)}_{b}
holds with
b\in[0,\infty]
,
it can be turned into an asymptotic statement,
except in the degenerate case
b=0
and
\tau_{2}=1
.
(The later condition prevents the asymptotics of the two terms on the right-hand side of (
24
) from cancelling out, see Appendix
C.2
.)
In all other cases, the leading term of
I_{\tau_{2}}
determines the leading term of
I_{\tau_{1}}
, for
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\geq 1
.

Particularizing to metric entropy,
we conclude that its leading term is determined by that of
M_{\mu}
and by that of
I_{\tau}
, for any
\tau\geq 1
;
the converse however does not hold.
The situation for the minimax risk is slightly more involved and requires to split the analysis into three regimes, depending on the value of
b
.
This is the content of the next theorem.

Theorem 7
.

Let
b\in[0,\infty]
and
let
\mu=\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be a sequence such that
\mu_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
,
with corresponding semi-axis-counting function
M_{\mu}
satisfying
\text{(RC)}_{b}
.
Then, the minimax risk associated with the model
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E}_{\mu},\sigma)
satisfies

R_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\sim\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\varepsilon>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma^{2}\,V_{b}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\}\sim\sigma^{2}\,V_{b}(\varepsilon_{\sigma})+\varepsilon_{\sigma}^{2},\quad\text{as }\sigma\to 0,

(25)

where
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
is the critical radius introduced in Theorem
4
and

V_{b}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\\[-9.95863pt]
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon),&\quad\text{if }b=0,\\[14.22636pt]
\frac{2\,b\,H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)}{(b+1)(b+2)},&\quad\text{if }b\in(0,\infty),\\[6.40204pt]
\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{2H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)}{u}\mathop{}\!du,&\quad\text{if }b=\infty,\\[4.97931pt]
\end{cases}\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

Proof sketch.

Upon application of Pinsker’s asymptotic minimaxity theorem,
we get that the linear minimax risk associated with the model
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{E}_{\mu},\sigma)
is asymptotically optimal, that is,
R_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\sim R_{\sigma}^{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)
, as
\sigma\to 0
.
It is therefore sufficient to prove (
25
) for the linear minimax risk.
This is done by first applying Lemma
6
to establish a relation between the integrals
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
appearing in (
17
), and then applying Theorem
2
.
The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
B.4
.
∎

Theorem
7
splits into three regimes.
When
b=0
, the leading term of the minimax risk is determined by the asymptotics of the semi-axis-counting function; see Lemma
13
in Appendix
C.2
.
When
b\in(0,\infty)
, knowledge of the leading term of the minimax risk is equivalent to knowledge of the leading term of the metric entropy (and hence of
I_{\tau}
, for all
\tau\geq 1
); see Lemmas
13
and
14
in Appendix
C.2
.
When
b=\infty
, the leading term of the minimax risk is determined by that of the metric entropy (and thus of
I_{\tau}
, for all
\tau\geq 1
), but the converse generally fails.
These dependencies are made explicit for the typical examples presented in Appendix
A
.

To interpret (
25
),
we focus on
b\in(0,\infty)
(the discussion extends to the other regimes).
By Theorem
7
,
the minimax risk admits the asymptotic decomposition

R_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\sim\frac{2\,b\,\sigma^{2}}{(b+1)(b+2)}H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon_{\sigma};\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}_{\sigma},\quad\text{as }\sigma\to 0.

(26)

This decomposition is an incarnation of the bias–variance tradeoff from statistical learning theory (see, e.g.,
[
74
, Chapter 5]
):
smaller values of
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
reduce the bias term
\varepsilon^{2}_{\sigma}
, but at the expense of increasing the variance—or complexity—term captured by the metric entropy. Conversely, larger values of
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
increase the bias while reducing the variance.
Theorem
7
shows that the leading term of the minimax risk is obtained by optimizing this tradeoff;
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
achieves the optimum, hence the terminology ‘critical radius’.

We conclude with a short discussion on related work.
Characterizing the performance of statistical estimators via metric-entropy methods is a long-standing idea,
dating back at least to the
works of LeCam and Birgé
[
9
,
58
]
;
see also
[
83
,
82
,
41
]
.
For minimax risk over ellipsoids, Donoho
[
22
]
highlighted a strong parallel between minimax techniques and metric-entropy methods. More recently,
[
90
]
studied the minimax risk as an optimization problem under storage or communication constraints captured by metric entropy.
A bias-variance tradeoff akin to (
26
)—i.e., where an
\varepsilon^{2}
-term competes against a metric-entropy term—already appears in
[
89
]
to derive lower bounds on the minimax risk in a broader setting than ellipsoids.

5
Sobolev Ellipsoids and Pinsker’s Theorem

Sections
2
–
4

establish that the type-
\tau
integrals
I_{\tau}
(for
\tau\geq 1
)
quantify the compactness of ellipsoids
as they directly relate to metric entropy and minimax risk.
Section
4
and Appendix
C.2
further show that the asymptotics of
I_{\tau}
are determined by those of
M_{\mu}
,
which might suggest that
M_{\mu}
is an equally suitable measure of compactness.
We claim otherwise: the semi-axis-counting function carries, in a sense, too much information.
To support our claim, we particularize our analysis to the Sobolev ellipsoid and show that, in this case, working directly with type-
\tau
integrals yields stronger results than methods based on the asymptotics of
M_{\mu}
.
Additionally, the Sobolev ellipsoid provides a natural test-case for the results of the paper
given the extensive literature on its metric entropy
[
33
,
22
,
63
,
64
,
4
]

and its minimax risk (mainly via Pinsker’s Sobolev theorem
[
68
,
66
]
).
The theory laid out in Sections
2
–
4
improves on all these classical characterizations.

Specifically, for
k,d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we consider the Sobolev space of order
k
on a bounded open domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see
[
1
,
10
]
).
Informally, this space corresponds to the class of square-integrable functions on
\Omega
—i.e., elements of
L^{2}(\Omega)
—whose
k
-th (weak) derivatives also lie in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
The Sobolev ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}^{\text{Sob}}_{d,k}
is the unit ball of this space.
Equivalently,
\mathcal{E}^{\text{Sob}}_{d,k}

is the image of the unit ball of
L^{2}(\Omega)
under the compact linear operator

T=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{Id}+(-\Delta)^{k}}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(27)

where
-\Delta
denotes the Dirichlet Laplacian on
\Omega
;
see the discussion in
[
4
, Section 3.2]
.
This makes the terminology ‘Sobolev ellipsoid’ transparent:

\mathcal{E}^{\text{Sob}}_{d,k}
is isometric to the ellipsoid in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})
with semi-axes
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
given by the eigenvalues of the operator
T
.

To apply the results of the previous sections,
we need information on the asymptotic properties of
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
.
By (
27
),
this is equivalent to an asymptotic characterization of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
of
-\Delta
.
On the template of (
2
), we thus introduce the eigenvalue-counting function of the Laplacian

M_{\lambda}(s)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{n\mid\lambda_{n}\leq s}}\right\}}}\right|,\quad\text{for all }s>0.

Note the fundamental difference with
M_{\mu}
:

M_{\lambda}(s)
counts the eigenvalues of
-\Delta
below a threshold
s
,
while
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)
counts the semi-axes of
\mathcal{E}^{\text{Sob}}_{d,k}
above a threshold
\varepsilon
.
This difference reflects that the eigenvalues of the Laplacian tend to infinity
[
76
, Chapter 9.5]
,
while the semi-axes of the ellipsoid tend to zero.
Accordingly, we are interested in the asymptotics of
M_{\lambda}(s)
in the limit
s\to\infty
.
The leading term was first characterized by Weyl in
[
86
,
87
,
88
]
(see also
[
76
, Chapter 9.5]
) according to

M_{\lambda}(s)\sim\frac{\omega_{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Omega)}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,s^{\frac{d}{2}},\quad\text{as }s\to\infty,

(28)

where
\omega_{d}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{H}^{d}(\cdot)
denotes the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

A second-order term can also be obtained, typically under the additional assumptions

\Omega
has smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
and the measure of its periodic billiards is zero.

(29)

The domain
\Omega
has smooth boundary if it is locally the graph of a smooth—i.e., infinitely differentiable—function; see
[
1
, Chapter 4]
and
[
35
, Appendix C]
.
Informally, a billiard
is
the data of an initial point and an initial direction, generating a trajectory in
\Omega
which is said to be periodic if it loops back onto itself
; we refer to
[
72
, Chapter 1.3]
for a formal introduction to billiards.
Under (
29
), V. Ivrii established in
[
51
]
(see also
[
46
, Corollary 29.3.4]
and the survey
[
52
]
) the two-term expansion

M_{\lambda}(s)=d\,\chi_{d}(\Omega)\,s^{\frac{d}{2}}-\frac{(d-1)\chi_{d-1}(\partial\Omega)}{4}\,s^{\frac{d-1}{2}}+o_{s\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}}\right),

(30)

where
\chi_{r}(S)\coloneqq\omega_{r}\mathcal{H}^{r}(S)/(r\,(2\pi)^{r})
, for all
r\in\{1,\dots,d\}
and measurable
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In
[
39
, p. 1001]
,
the authors report that Bronstein–Ivrii claim (
30
) holds under a
C^{1}
-Dini smoothness assumption on
\partial\Omega
, slightly weaker than (
29
),
still under the periodic-billiard condition.
Combining (
27
) and (
30
),
one can show

M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)=d\,\chi_{d}(\Omega)\,{\varepsilon}^{-\frac{d}{k}}-\frac{(d-1)\,\chi_{d-1}(\partial\Omega)}{4}\,\varepsilon^{-\frac{d-1}{k}}+o_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-\frac{d-1}{k}}}}\right),

(31)

see
[
4
, Proof of Theorem 6]
for an explicit derivation.

There exists a corresponding literature that characterizes two-term asymptotics of the Laplacian eigenvalues ‘on average’;
see, e.g.,
[
36
,
40
,
38
,
56
,
37
,
39
]
.
In particular,
[
39

\displaystyle H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\sim\frac{c\ln^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)}{2\ln^{(2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)},

\displaystyle R_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\sim\frac{c\sigma^{2}\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{-1}}}\right)}{\ln^{(2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{-1}}}\right)};

\displaystyle H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\sim\frac{c\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}{\alpha},

Item (i) concerns finite-dimensional ellipsoids.

Ellipsoids with semi-axes as in items (ii) and (iii) (and, more generally,

Appendix B
Proofs of Main Results

B.1
Proof of Theorem
2

A series formula for
I_{1}
.

We first prove that

I_{1}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\ln_{+}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\varepsilon}}}\right),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0,

(38)

where we recall the notation
\ln_{+}(x)=\max\{0,\ln(x)\}
, for all
x\geq 0
.
To this end, let us fix
A>0
large enough to have
M_{\mu}(A)=0
.
Such an
A
is guaranteed to exist thanks to the assumption
\mu_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
The integral
I_{1}
can then be rewritten as the Stieltjes integral

I_{1}(\varepsilon)=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{M_{\mu}(u)}{u}\mathop{}\!du=\int_{\varepsilon}^{A}\frac{M_{\mu}(u)}{u}\mathop{}\!du=\int_{\varepsilon}^{A}M_{\mu}(u)\mathop{}\!d\ln(u),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

(39)

Upon application of the integration-by-parts formula for the Stieltjes integral (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Theorem 7.6]
), we obtain

\int_{\varepsilon}^{A}M_{\mu}(u)\mathop{}\!d\ln(u)=M_{\mu}(A)\ln(A)-M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\ln(\varepsilon)-\int_{\varepsilon}^{A}\ln(u)\mathop{}\!dM_{\mu}(u),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

(40)

Now, recall that
A
has been chosen such that
M_{\mu}(A)=0
, so that the first term in the right-hand side of (
40
) vanishes.
Furthermore, since
M_{\mu}
is a step function with negative jumps located at each
\mu_{n}
,
we can reduce the Stieltjes integral appearing in the right-hand side of (
40
) to a finite sum (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Theorem 7.11]
) according to

-\int_{\varepsilon}^{A}\ln(u)\mathop{}\!dM_{\mu}(u)=\sum_{\mu_{n}\in[\varepsilon,A)}\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{n}}}\right),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

(41)

Note that, with our choice of
A
, the condition
\mu_{n}<A
is automatically satisfied for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Therefore, combining (
39
), (
40
), and (
41
), and using that there are, by definition of
M_{\mu}
, precisely
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)
semi-axes satisfying
\mu_{n}\geq\varepsilon
,
we get

I_{1}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{\mu_{n}\geq\varepsilon}\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{n}}}\right)-M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\ln(\varepsilon)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\ln_{+}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\varepsilon}}}\right),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0,

which proves (
38
).

In what follows, we may assume that at least two semi-axes are non-zero.
If all semi-axes are zero,
H(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu})=I_{1}(\varepsilon)=0
, for all
\varepsilon>0
.
If there is a single non-zero semi-axis
\mu_{*}
,
(
38
) reduces to
I_{1}(\varepsilon)=\ln_{+}(\mu_{*}/\varepsilon)

and one easily verifies that
H(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu})=\ln(\lceil\mu_{*}/\varepsilon\rceil)
, for all
\varepsilon>0
.
In both cases (
5
) and (
6
) hold trivially.

A lower bound on metric entropy.

We next prove the following lower bound on metric entropy

H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\ln_{+}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\varepsilon}}}\right),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

(42)

To this end, let us fix
\varepsilon>0
and
introduce the ellipsoid
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}
obtained from
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}
by retaining only the semi-axes greater than or equal to
\varepsilon
and setting the remaining ones to zero.

\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}
is therefore a finite-dimensional ellipsoid
of dimension
\mathfrak{d}\coloneqq M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)
.
By construction, we have the inclusion
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{\mu}
, which, in terms of metric entropy, translates into

H(\varepsilon;\mathcal{E}_{\mu})\geq H(\varepsilon;\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}).

(43)

We have reduced the problem of lower-bounding the metric entropy of
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}
to that of lower-bounding the metric entropy of a finite-dimensional ellipsoid.
We are thus in position to use a volume argument—namely
[
85
, Lemma 5.7]
—to get

N(\varepsilon;\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu})\geq\varepsilon^{-\mathfrak{d}}\frac{\text{vol}(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu})}{\omega_{\mathfrak{d}}}=\varepsilon^{-M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)}\prod_{\mu_{n}\geq\varepsilon}\mu_{n},

(44)

where we recall that
\omega_{\mathfrak{d}}
denotes the volume of the
\mathfrak{d}
-dimensional Euclidean ball, and we have used the standard formula for the volume of a finite-dimensional ellipsoid (for more details see, e.g., the proof of
[
3
, Theorem 4]
).
Taking logarithm on both sides of (
44
) and using (
43
) concludes the proof of (
42
).

Preparation.

Before turning to the upper bound,
we fix
\varepsilon>0
small enough for
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\geq 2
to hold (which is possible since we assumed that at least two semi-axes are non-zero), and introduce

\varepsilon^{\prime}\coloneqq\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x>0\mid x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+M_{\mu}(x)^{-1}}}\right)<\varepsilon}}\right\}.

(45)

We readily check from its definition in (
2
) that
M_{\mu}
is a left-continuous function, that is,

\lim_{\eta\downarrow 0}M_{\mu}(x-\eta)=M_{\mu}(x)
, for all
x>0
.
In particular, using the left-continuity of
M_{\mu}
in (
45
) guarantees that

\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+M_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1}}}\right)\leq\varepsilon,\quad\text{and, therefore, that}\quad\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1+M_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1}}\eqcolon\tilde{\varepsilon}.

(46)

We further set
d\coloneqq M_{\mu}(\tilde{\varepsilon})
,
fix an integer
k\leq d
,
and define

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\gamma}\coloneqq\tilde{\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{k+1}}{d^{\gamma}}}}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad\gamma\coloneqq\frac{\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})}}\right)}{\ln(d)}+1.

(47)

Using that
M_{\mu}
is non-increasing, we get
d\geq M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\geq 2
and
\gamma\geq 2
.
Additionally, using the definition (
47
) of
\gamma
, we have

\frac{\sqrt{k+1}}{d^{\gamma}}=\frac{\sqrt{k+1}}{dM_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})}<\frac{1}{M_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})},

(48)

where we used both

and

Plugging the definition (

) of
\tilde{\varepsilon}
in the definition (
47

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\gamma}

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\gamma}=\varepsilon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1+d^{-\gamma}\sqrt{k+1}}{1+M_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1}}}}\right)<\varepsilon.

(49)

Combining (
46
), (
47
), and (

),
we get
\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}<\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\gamma}<\varepsilon
,
which, using that the semi-axis-counting function
M_{\mu}
is non-increasing, turns into
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\geq M_{\mu}(\tilde{\varepsilon})\geq M_{\mu}(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\gamma})\geq M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)

In fact, it will be useful to additionally prove that
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})
and
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)
are asymptotically equivalent, in the sense that

M_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\sim M_{\mu}(\tilde{\varepsilon})\sim M_{\mu}(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\gamma})\sim M_{\mu}(\varepsilon),\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,

(50)

Note that it is sufficient to establish
M_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right)\sim M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)

\varepsilon\to 0
.
To this end,

\varepsilon^{\prime}
in (

\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\eta}\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\eta}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+M_{\mu}(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\eta)^{-1}}}\right)\geq\varepsilon,\quad\text{for all }\eta>0.

We next take
f

(see Appendix

), to get

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\eta(\varepsilon^{\prime})}}}\right)\sim f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\eta(\varepsilon^{\prime})}}\right),\quad\text{as }\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0,

for any map

\eta(\varepsilon^{\prime})\to 0
, as
\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0
.
By continuity of

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\bar{\eta}(\varepsilon^{\prime})}}\right)\sim f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right),\quad\text{and, therefore,}\quad f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\bar{\eta}(\varepsilon^{\prime})}}}\right)\sim f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right),\quad\text{as }\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0.

M_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\bar{\eta}(\varepsilon^{\prime})}}}\right)\sim M_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right),\quad\text{as }\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0.

Combining the asymptotic equivalence in (

Finally, we relate the integrals

.

where the last step uses that

is non-increasing.
Recalling the definition of
\tilde{\varepsilon}
in (

we see that the right-hand side in (

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{C_{1}\kappa^{\prime}\,k\,d(d+2)}{2\kappa^{2}(d+k)(d+2k)}-\frac{C_{2}k(d+1)}{2\kappa(d+k-1)}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa\sigma^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{2k+1}{d+2k}}+o_{\sigma\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{\frac{4k+2}{d+2k}}}}\right).

From the definitions of
\kappa
and
\kappa^{\prime}
in (
201
),
we verify that

\frac{C_{1}k(d+2)}{2\kappa(d+k)}=\frac{d+2k}{d},

(213)

and

\frac{C_{1}\kappa^{\prime}\,k\,d(d+2)}{2\kappa^{2}(d+k)(d+2k)}-\frac{C_{2}k(d+1)}{2\kappa(d+k-1)}=\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{d+2k-1}{d-1}}}\right)=-\frac{2\kappa^{\prime}k}{\kappa(d-1)}.

(214)

The ratio
\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa
can be further developed, using (
179
) and (
201
), according to

\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}=\frac{C_{2}(d-1)(d+1)(d+k)(d+2k)}{C_{1}d(d+2)(d+k-1)(d+2k-1)}=\frac{(d-1)^{2}(d+k)(d+2k)\chi_{d-1}(\partial\Omega)}{4d^{2}(d+k-1)(d+2k-1)\chi_{d}(\Omega)}.

(215)

Putting (
211
)–(
212
), (
213
), (
214
), and (
215
) together, we get

R_{\sigma}^{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}^{\text{Sob}}_{d,k}}}\right)=K_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa\sigma^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{2k}{d+2k}}+K_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa\sigma^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{2k+1}{d+2k}}+o_{\sigma\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{\frac{4k+2}{d+2k}}}}\right),

(216)

where

\kappa=\frac{C_{1}k\,d(d+2)}{2(d+k)(d+2k)}=\frac{k\,d^{2}\,\chi_{d}(\Omega)}{(d+k)(d+2k)}

and

K_{1}=\frac{C_{1}k(d+2)}{2\kappa(d+k)}=\frac{d+2k}{d}\quad\text{and}\quad K_{2}=-\frac{k(d-1)(d+k)(d+2k)\chi_{d-1}(\partial\Omega)}{2d^{2}(d+k-1)(d+2k-1)\chi_{d}(\Omega)}.

The asymptotic characterization (
216
) holds for the linear minimax risk.
In order to deduce a corresponding characterization for the non-linear minimax risk using (
23
),
it is sufficient to show that

\frac{\sqrt{\ln(v_{\sigma})}}{v_{\sigma}}=o_{\sigma\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{\frac{4k+2}{d+2k}}}}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad v_{\sigma}\coloneqq\frac{\varepsilon_{\sigma}}{\sigma R^{L}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)}.

(217)

To this end, we use (
204
)–(
205
) and (
216
) to obtain the respective asymptotics

\varepsilon_{\sigma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle K}}{{\sim}}\sigma^{\frac{2k}{d+2k}}\quad\text{and}\quad R^{L}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle K}}{{\sim}}\sigma^{\frac{4k}{d+2k}},\quad\text{as }\sigma\to 0.

They imply

v_{\sigma}=\frac{\varepsilon_{\sigma}}{\sigma R^{L}_{\sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}}}\right)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle K}}{{\sim}}\sigma^{-\frac{d+4k}{d+2k}},\quad\text{as }\sigma\to 0.

(218)

Together, the assumption
d\geq 3
and the asymptotic scaling (
218
) yield

\frac{\sqrt{\ln(v_{\sigma})}}{v_{\sigma}}=o_{\sigma\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{\frac{4k+2}{d+2k}}}}\right),

which establishes the desired result (
217
), thereby finishing the proof of (
37
).

Appendix C
Auxiliary Results

C.1
Regular Variation at Zero and Karamata’s Theorem

In many places in the paper we use tools from the theory of regular variation
(see, e.g., Theorem
7
and the end of Appendix
B.1
).
The classical theory is formulated for functions whose argument tends to infinity
[
8
,
54
]
,
whereas we are mostly interested in variables tending to zero.
Fortunately, statements ‘at infinity’ translate easily to statements ‘at zero’
via the change of variables
s=1/\varepsilon
;
the present appendix illustrates this claim on Karamata’s theorem.

Specifically, given
b\in\mathbb{R}
, we say that a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is regularly varying at zero with index
b
if

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{f(\lambda\varepsilon)}{f(\varepsilon)}=\lambda^{-b},\quad\text{for all }\lambda>0.

Equivalently, the map
s\mapsto f(s^{-1})
is regularly varying (at infinity) with index
b
in the sense of
[
8
, Definition 1.4.2]
.
Additionally,
f
is slowly varying at zero if it is regularly varying at zero with index
b=0
.
We can then reformulate Karamata’s theorem at zero as follows (see
[
8
, Theorems 1.5.11 and 1.6.1]
for the statement at infinity).

Lemma 10
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\tau>1-b
, and let
f\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be bounded on
(\varepsilon,\infty)
, for all
\varepsilon>0
.
Then,
f
is regularly varying at zero with index
b
if and only if

\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{f(u)}{u^{\tau}}\mathop{}\!du\sim\frac{\varepsilon^{1-\tau}}{b+\tau-1}f(\varepsilon),\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(219)

Proof.

Let us first perform the change of variables
v=u^{-1}
in the left-hand side of (
219
) to get

\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{f(u)}{u^{\tau}}\mathop{}\!du=\int_{0}^{s(\varepsilon)}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v^{-1}}}\right)\,v^{\tau-2}\mathop{}\!dv,\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0,

(220)

where
s(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\varepsilon^{-1}
tends to
\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The function
f
being regularly varying at zero with index
b
is equivalent to the map
s\mapsto f(s^{-1})
being regularly varying at infinity, also with index
b
.
Additionally, one gets, from the corresponding assumption on
f
, that
s\mapsto f(s^{-1})
is locally bounded—therefore also locally integrable—on
[0,\infty)
, so that the conditions of the classical version of Karamata’s theorem
[
8
, Theorems 1.5.11 (i) and 1.6.1 (i)]
are satisfied.
Upon its application, one gets that
s\mapsto f(s^{-1})
is regularly varying at infinity with index
b
if and only if

\int_{0}^{s(\varepsilon)}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v^{-1}}}\right)\,v^{\tau-2}\mathop{}\!dv\sim\frac{s(\varepsilon)^{\tau-1}}{b+\tau-1}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s(\varepsilon)^{-1}}}\right),\quad\text{as }s(\varepsilon)\to\infty.

(221)

Replacing
s(\varepsilon)
by
\varepsilon^{-1}
in (
221
) and combining with (
220
) yields the desired result.
∎

Regularly varying functions appear throughout the paper because item (i) in (RC) is equivalent to
M_{\mu}
being regularly varying at zero.
Indeed, if
M_{\mu}
is regularly varying at zero with index
b\in[0,\infty)
,
the smooth variation theorem applied to the regularly varying function
M_{\mu}
(see
[
8
, Definition 1.8 and Theorem 1.8.2]
) guarantees the existence of
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})
satisfying
M_{\mu}(x)\sim f(x)
as
x\to 0
and whose elasticity
\rho
has the limit
\rho(t)\to b
as
t\to\infty
.
This is precisely
\text{(RC)}_{b}
.
Conversely, arguments as in (
81
)–(
82
) yield

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{f(\varepsilon)}{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f(u)\,u^{-1}\mathop{}\!du}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)}}\right)\eqqcolon b,

from which we conclude—using Lemma
10
with
\tau=1
—that
M_{\mu}
is indeed regularly varying at zero with index
b
.

C.2
Asymptotic Equivalence and Integration

Throughout the paper, we have repeatedly integrated asymptotic equivalences.
This is justified by the following lemma.

Lemma 11
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be integrable on
(\varepsilon,\infty)
,
for all
\varepsilon>0
.
If
f_{1}(\varepsilon)\sim f_{2}(\varepsilon)
and
\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du\to\infty
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
then

\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du\sim\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du,\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

Proof.

Let us fix
\delta>0
.
The asymptotic equivalence
f_{1}(\varepsilon)\sim f_{2}(\varepsilon)
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, guarantees the existence of
\varepsilon_{\delta}>0
such that

(1-\delta)f_{1}(\varepsilon)\leq f_{2}(\varepsilon)\leq(1+\delta)f_{1}(\varepsilon),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{\delta}).

(222)

Integrating (
222
) yields

(1-\delta)\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon_{\delta}}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du\leq\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon_{\delta}}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du\leq(1+\delta)\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon_{\delta}}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du,\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{\delta}).

(223)

Moreover, under the assumptions

\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du\to\infty,\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\varepsilon_{\delta}}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du,\int_{\varepsilon_{\delta}}^{\infty}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du<\infty,

we get

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\cfrac{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du}{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du}=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\cfrac{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon_{\delta}}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du+\int_{\varepsilon_{\delta}}^{\infty}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du}{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon_{\delta}}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du+\int_{\varepsilon_{\delta}}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du}=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\cfrac{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon_{\delta}}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du}{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon_{\delta}}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du}.

(224)

With a similar line of arguments, we also come to the conclusion that

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\cfrac{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du}{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du}=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\cfrac{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon_{\delta}}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du}{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon_{\delta}}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du}.

(225)

Combining (
224
) and (
225
) with (
223
), we have proven that

1-\delta\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\cfrac{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du}{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du}\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\cfrac{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du}{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du}\leq 1+\delta.

(226)

The parameter
\delta
can be taken arbitrarily small in (
226
), implying

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\cfrac{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{2}(u)\mathop{}\!du}{\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f_{1}(u)\mathop{}\!du}=1,

and thus establishing the desired result.
∎

Lemma
11
is particularly useful for us because it transfers asymptotics of
M_{\mu}
to asymptotics of
I_{\tau}
, for every
\tau\geq 1
.
Specifically, suppose the semi-axis-counting function satisfies
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\sim\psi(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
for a given function
\psi
.
Applying Lemma
11
with
f_{1}\colon u\mapsto M_{\mu}(u)/u^{\tau}
and
f_{2}\colon u\mapsto\psi(u)/u^{\tau}
yields
I_{\tau}(\varepsilon)\sim\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\psi(u)/u^{\tau}\mathop{}\!du
.
For example, if
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\sim c\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha}
, for
c,\alpha>0
, then

I_{\tau}(\varepsilon)=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{M_{\mu}(u)}{u^{\tau}}\mathop{}\!du\sim\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{c\,u^{-\alpha}}{u^{\tau}}\mathop{}\!du=\frac{c\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha-\tau+1}}{\alpha+\tau-1},\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(227)

Note that the infinite-limit condition of Lemma
11
,
namely
\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}M_{\mu}(u)/u^{\tau}\mathop{}\!du\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
always comes for free.
Moreover, we emphasize that only the local properties around zero matter.
For instance, when deriving the metric entropy of
d
-dimensional ellipsoids,
i.e., when
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\sim d
and
\tau=1
,
one wishes to integrate the map
u\mapsto d/u
, which is not integrable at infinity.
However, it may be replaced by the truncated function
u\mapsto d\mathbbm{1}_{(0,1)}(u)/u
, which is integrable at infinity and has the same asymptotics at zero.
Applying Lemma
11
then yields the standard result (
7
) according to

I_{1}(\varepsilon)=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{M_{\mu}(u)}{u}\mathop{}\!du\sim\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{d}{u}\mathop{}\!du=d\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right),\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

We next ask whether one can deduce the asymptotics of
I_{\tau_{1}}
from those of
I_{\tau_{2}}
when
\tau_{1}\neq\tau_{2}
.
From Lemma
6
, this is possible provided the leading terms on the right-hand side of (
24
) do not cancel out,
i.e., unless we have

\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}I_{\tau_{2}}(u)\,u^{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}-1}\mathop{}\!du\sim\frac{\varepsilon^{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}}{\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}I_{\tau_{2}}(\varepsilon),\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(228)

Such a cancelation can only happen when both sides of (
228
) are non-negative,
which forces
\tau_{1}>\tau_{2}
.
Moreover, applying Lemma
10
with
b=0
and
\tau=1+\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}>1
shows that the scenario (
228
) can occur only if
I_{\tau_{2}}
is slowly varying at zero.
In this case, l’Hôpital’s rule (see
[
71
, Theorem 5.13]
) applied to the functions
\varepsilon\mapsto I_{\tau_{2}}(\lambda\varepsilon)
and
\varepsilon\mapsto I_{\tau_{2}}(\varepsilon)
, for
\lambda>0
, yields the limits

1=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{I_{\tau_{2}}(\lambda\varepsilon)}{I_{\tau_{2}}(\varepsilon)}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{-\lambda M_{\mu}(\lambda\varepsilon)/(\lambda\varepsilon)^{\tau_{2}}}{M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon^{\tau_{2}}}=\lambda^{1-\tau_{2}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{M_{\mu}(\lambda\varepsilon)}{M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)}.

(229)

In particular, (
229
) implies that
if
I_{\tau_{2}}
is slowly varying at zero then
M_{\mu}
is regularly varying at zero with index
1-\tau_{2}
.
If we had
\tau_{2}>1
, this index would be negative so that
M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
—a contradiction.
Hence, we must have
\tau_{2}=1
;
then, by (
229
),
M_{\mu}
is slowly varying.
In summary, the asymptotics of
I_{\tau_{2}}
determine those of
I_{\tau_{1}}
whenever either
\tau_{2}>1
or
M_{\mu}
is not slowly varying.
Returning to example (
227
), for all
c,\alpha>0
and
\tau_{1}\neq\tau_{2}
,
M_{\mu}
is not slowly varying, and we obtain

I_{\tau_{1}}(\varepsilon)\sim\frac{c\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha-\tau_{1}+1}}{\alpha+\tau_{1}-1},\quad\text{if and only if}\quad I_{\tau_{2}}(\varepsilon)\sim\frac{c\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha-\tau_{2}+1}}{\alpha+\tau_{2}-1},\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

In general, the converse to Lemma
11
fails:
we cannot recover the asymptotics of a function given that of its integral.
In particular, the asymptotic behavior of
I_{\tau}
need not determine that of
M_{\mu}
.
However, the converse does hold when the integral is regularly varying at zero and the integrand is eventually monotone.
This is the content of the next lemma.

Lemma 12
.

Let
b>0
, let
f\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
be integrable and eventually monotone near zero, and let
g\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{*}
be regularly varying at zero with index
b
.
If

\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f(u)\mathop{}\!du\sim g(\varepsilon),\quad\text{then}\quad f(\varepsilon)\sim b\,\varepsilon^{-1}\,g(\varepsilon)\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

(230)

Proof.

We adapt the proof of the classical monotone density theorem to regular variations at zero; see
[
8
, Theorem 1.7.2]
.
Let us first introduce the integral

J(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f(u)\mathop{}\!du,\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

For
c_{2}>c_{1}>0
, one can express the difference between two evaluations of these integrals according to

J(c_{1}\,\varepsilon)-J(c_{2}\,\varepsilon)=\int_{c_{1}\,\varepsilon}^{c_{2}\,\varepsilon}f(u)\mathop{}\!du,\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

This difference can be upper- and lower-bounded for
\varepsilon
small enough using the assumption that
f
is eventually monotone near zero.
(We assume that
f
is eventually non-increasing, the non-decreasing case follows analogously.)
Specifically, there exists
\varepsilon_{*}>0
such that

(c_{2}-c_{1})\,\varepsilon\,f(c_{2}\,\varepsilon)\leq J(c_{1}\,\varepsilon)-J(c_{2}\,\varepsilon)\leq(c_{2}-c_{1})\,\varepsilon\,f(c_{1}\varepsilon),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{*}).

(231)

Moreover, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{J(c_{1}\,\varepsilon)}{g(\varepsilon)}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{J(c_{1}\,\varepsilon)}{g(c_{1}\,\varepsilon)}\cdot\frac{g(c_{1}\,\varepsilon)}{g(\varepsilon)}=c_{1}^{-b},

(232)

where we used the assumption
J(\varepsilon)\sim g(\varepsilon)
from (
230
) and the fact that
g
is regularly varying at zero with index
b
.
Similarly, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{J(c_{2}\,\varepsilon)}{g(\varepsilon)}=c_{2}^{-b}.

(233)

Combining (
231
) with the limits (
232
) and (
233
) yields

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon f(c_{2}\,\varepsilon)}{g(\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{c_{1}^{-b}-c_{2}^{-b}}{c_{2}-c_{1}}\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon f(c_{1}\,\varepsilon)}{g(\varepsilon)}.

(234)

Choosing
c_{2}=1
and taking the limit
c_{1}\to 1
, the lower bound in (
234
) yields

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon f(\varepsilon)}{g(\varepsilon)}\leq-\lim_{c_{1}\to 1}\frac{c_{1}^{-b}-1}{c_{1}-1}=b.

(235)

Likewise, choosing
c_{1}=1
and taking the limit
c_{2}\to 1
, the upper bound in (
234
) yields

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon f(\varepsilon)}{g(\varepsilon)}\geq b.

(236)

Combining (
235
) and (
236
) gives

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon f(\varepsilon)}{g(\varepsilon)}=b,

which is the desired result.
∎

The hypothesis in Lemma
12
that
f
is eventually monotone near zero is not restrictive in our setting since we are mostly interested in the case
f=M_{\mu}
.
The important assumption is regular variation of the integral.
It is for instance satisfied in the example (
227
), so that

I_{\tau}(\varepsilon)\sim\frac{c\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha-\tau+1}}{\alpha+\tau-1},\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,\quad\text{implies}\quad M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)\sim c\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha},

for
c,\alpha>0
and
\tau\geq 1
.
In principle, the result can be extended to higher orders.
For instance, suppose

I_{\tau}(\varepsilon)=\frac{c_{1}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{1}-\tau+1}}{\alpha_{1}+\tau-1}+\frac{c_{2}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{2}-\tau+1}}{\alpha_{2}+\tau-1}+o_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{2}-\tau+1}}}\right),

for
c_{1},c_{2}>0
,
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>0
, and
\tau\geq 1
.
Equivalently,

\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{M_{\mu}(u)-c_{1}\,u^{-\alpha_{1}}}{u^{\tau}}\mathop{}\!du=\frac{c_{2}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{2}-\tau+1}}{\alpha_{2}+\tau-1}+o_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{2}-\tau+1}}}\right).

Therefore, assuming the second-order term of
M_{\mu}
is eventually monotone near zero, Lemma
12
yields

M_{\mu}(\varepsilon)=c_{1}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{1}}+c_{2}\,\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{2}}+o_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{-\alpha_{2}}}}\right).

In practice, however, verifying the eventual monotonicity of the second-order term is rarely feasible.

In view of Theorem
7
,
we also need to understand how asymptotics behave under an infimum.
The next lemma shows that one can take the infimum without losing asymptotic equivalence.

Lemma 13
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
If
f_{1}(\varepsilon)\sim f_{2}(\varepsilon)
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
then

\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\varepsilon>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma^{2}f_{1}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\}\sim\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\varepsilon>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\},\quad\text{as }\sigma\to 0.

Proof.

Let us fix
\delta>0
.
The asymptotic equivalence
f_{1}(\varepsilon)\sim f_{2}(\varepsilon)
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, guarantees the existence of
\varepsilon_{\delta}>0
such that

(1-\delta)f_{1}(\varepsilon)\leq f_{2}(\varepsilon)\leq(1+\delta)f_{1}(\varepsilon),\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{\delta}).

Consequently, we get

(1-\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{2}f_{1}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right)\leq\sigma^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{2}f_{1}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right),

(237)

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{\delta})
and
\sigma>0
.
We next observe that the map
\sigma\mapsto\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\{\sigma^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}\}
is non-decreasing.
(Indeed, let us choose
\sigma_{+}>\sigma_{-}>0
arbitrarily, and let
\{\varepsilon_{n,+}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be such that
\sigma_{+}^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon_{n,+})+\varepsilon_{n,+}^{2}\to\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\{\sigma_{+}^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}\}
, as
n\to\infty
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\varepsilon>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma_{+}^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{+}^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon_{n,+})+\varepsilon_{n,+}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{-}^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon_{n,+})+\varepsilon_{n,+}^{2}\geq\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\varepsilon>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma_{-}^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\},

from which the desired non-decreasing property follows.)
Consequently, we can find
\sigma_{1}>0
small enough to have

\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\varepsilon>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{\delta}^{2}}{2},\quad\text{for all }\sigma\in(0,\sigma_{1});

(238)

take, for instance,
\sigma_{1}=\varepsilon_{\delta}/(2\sqrt{f_{2}(\varepsilon_{\delta}/2)})
.
This implies the existence of a sequence
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
attaining the infimum in the left-hand side of (
238
) such that—using the assumption that
f_{2}
is non-negative—we have
\varepsilon_{n}<\varepsilon_{\delta}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Combining this observation with (
237
) yields

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\varepsilon>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma^{2}f_{1}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{2}f_{1}(\varepsilon_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}}\right)

(239)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{inf}\vphantom{\mathrm{sup}}}_{\varepsilon>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sigma^{2}f_{2}(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\},

(240)

for all
\sigma\in(0,\sigma_{1})
.
Likewise, there exists
\sigma_{2}>0
such that
