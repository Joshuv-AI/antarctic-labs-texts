Illite Dissolution under Sodium Hydroxide Solution: Insights from Reactive Molecular Dynamics
Authors: Wenguo Ma, Wenhang Yuan, Peng Wang, Xuan Liu, Yueqi Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In alkali/surfactant/polymer (ASP) flooding systems, alkalis react with clay minerals such as Illite, montmorillonite, and kaolinite, leading to reservoir damage and impacting oil recovery rates. Therefore, studying the dissolution effects of strong alkalis on clay minerals is crucial for improving oil recovery. This study uses Illite as a representative clay mineral and employs the ReaxFF reactive force field and molecular dynamics simulations to model its dissolution in NaOH solution. We investigated the diffusion coefficients of metal cations in Illite and their interactions with hydroxide ions at various NaOH concentrations. The study also explores the evolution of dissolution products and protonation characteristics during the dissolution of Illite. By calculating the changes in ionic energy throughout the dissolution process, we analyzed variations in ionic reactivity within the system. Simulation results show that as the NaOH concentration increases, metal cations in Illite form stable chemical bonds with hydroxide ions, creating highly aggregated clusters with strong ionic interactions that hinder migration. Consequently, the diffusion coefficients of metal cations gradually decrease. During the reaction, water dissociates to produce hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions. Ion exchange occurs between the solution cations and Illite cations. Illite cations gradually precipitate and form metal hydroxides by combining with hydroxide ions under electrostatic forces. Protonation propagates from the surface to the internal structure during the reaction. Moreover, the degree of protonation increases with higher NaOH concentrations. Changes in the average ionic energy before and after the reaction indicate that K+ exhibits the highest reactivity. Intermediate silicate products are unstable in NaOH solution, with some Si4+ ions showing higher energy and stronger reactivity.

Introduction

Alkali/surfactant/polymer (ASP) flooding significantly enhances oil recovery postpolymer flooding, achieving a 20% increase compared to water flooding in field trials.1 ASP flooding has ensured stable and high production rates in oilfields. However, chemical reactions between the alkali and minerals in the reservoir postinjection lead to dissolution and detachment of reservoir minerals.2 This results in reduced permeability, increased injection pressures, and potential near-wellbore formation damage, significantly decreasing liquid production capacity and reducing the liquid yield index by 50% to 95.5%.3 These challenges have constrained the widespread adoption and advancement of ASP flooding, posing a bottleneck to its further development. Hongtao’s static leaching experiments with strong alkalis and natural core and clay minerals revealed that the rate and extent of leaching are related to the chemical composition and crystal type of the core samples. Qinghe’s ASP floodingexperiments at a temperature of 45 °C and a pressure of 10.99 MPa indicated that alkalis primarily leach the cementing materials between sand grains, such as Illite, montmorillonite, and kaolinite.4,5 Köhler et al. measured the dissolution rate of natural Illite by monitoring Si release rates under conditions of pH 1.4 to 12.4 and temperatures ranging from 5 to 50 °C. Their results showed that the dissolution rate increased with increasing pH under alkaline conditions. However, due to the lack of independent control over Al and Si during the experiments, the sequence of dissolution reactions for Al and Si could not be clearly determined.6 Werling et al. assessed the solubility of three different clay minerals (kaolinite, montmorillonite, and Illite) in a pH 12 to 14.73 NaOH solution after calcination. The solubility of all clay minerals was found to be directly proportional to the concentration of the alkaline solution.7 Research on the dissolution of alkali on clay minerals primarily relies on laboratory experiments, but the mechanisms remain unclear due to limitations in research methods and tools (see Figure 1).

Molecular Dynamics (MD) methods serve as a bridge connecting microscopic phenomena to macroscopic properties, providing insights into molecular-level mechanisms. MD has been extensively applied in the study of clay minerals. In 2004, the empirical force field CLAYFF was developed for hydration and multicomponent mineral systems, along with their interfaces with aqueous solutions. CLAYFF has significantly advanced the characterization of clay mineral properties, contributing to research areas such as CO2 geological sequestration and clay mineral adsorption characteristics.8−11 Empirical force fields rely on static bonds and fixed charges associated with atoms, limiting their applicability to nonreactive systems. The ReaxFF reactive force field, pioneered by Van Duin et al., bridges the gap between quantum-scale and classical MD methods by explicitly simulating bond dissociations and charge equilibrations. Initially employed in studying chemical reactions during hydrocarbon combustion,12 ReaxFF has since been extended to various metal oxide systems including calcium and copper by Manzano, Jeon et al.13−17 Rahaman et al. developed ReaxFF for proton transfers in glycine and for simulating peptides and proteins, providing a valuable tool for studying proton transfer reactions in organic molecules within aqueous environments.18,19 Fedkin et al. expanded ReaxFF to study water-electrolyte systems involving cations (Li+, Na+, K+, Cs+) and anions (Cl–), offering opportunities for broad applications encompassing electrolyte ions and solutions.20 Pitman employed ReaxFF to analyze large-scale atomic models of multilayer montmorillonite particles under low hydration conditions. His investigation into the diffusion processes revealed that the diffusion of water, when complexed with Ca hydrates, is significantly slower compared to the diffusion of free water across the clay surface.21 Hahn et al. utilized ReaxFF to investigate the structure of sodium silicate crystals and glasses, as well as the transport properties of sodium ions and protons within amorphous structures, thereby elucidating detailed chemical dissolution mechanisms. They observed that interactions with water progressively replace sodium ions associated with surface nonbridging oxygen. Additionally, they found that in the presence of these modifier cations, molecular water dissociates into H+ and OH–.22,23 Li calculated the energy and charge change to investigate the formation mechanism of [SiOsOw] trigonal individual atoms in the hydration system.24 MD simulations based on ReaxFF facilitate the study of mineral dissolution processes, elucidating crucial elemental reaction pathways and analyzing their mechanistic roles.

In this study, we utilize molecular dynamics simulations and the ReaxFF method to model the dissolution process of Illite in NaOH solution. We investigate how the concentration of NaOH solution affects the diffusion coefficients of metal cations within Illite and the changes in their bonding with hydroxide ions. Additionally, we analyze variations in the protonation levels during the dissolution process. By monitoring changes in key reaction species and ion energies, we aim to elucidate the dissolution mechanism of Illite.

Materials and Methods

Model Construction

This study focuses on Illite, a common clay mineral in sandstone reservoirs. Illite belongs to the 2:1 layer-type clay minerals, where its basic structural unit consists of two tetrahedral sheets of silicon and one octahedral sheet of aluminum. The oxygen atoms at the apexes of the tetrahedral sheets are oriented toward the octahedral sheet, interconnected by shared oxygen atoms. Illite predominantly contains exchangeable potassium ions. The diameter of potassium ions is comparable to the diameter of the inscribed circle within the hexagonal lattice structure of the tetrahedral sheet, facilitating their entry but hindering their release from this structure. This results in tightly bonded layers that are resistant to penetration by water. The initial structure of the Illite model was adopted from research conducted by Drits in 2010.25 The simulated Illite model has dimensions of 36.92 Å × 32.21 Å in its basal area and a height of 18.26 Å.

Based on the geometric parameters of O–H bond length = 1 Å and H–O–H bond angle = 104.50°, individual water molecules were generated. The charge distribution of sodium ions and hydroxide ions was referenced from the OPLS (Optimized Potentials for Liquid Simulations) force field,26 which provides optimized parameters for accurately describing molecular interactions in liquids and solutions, including both nonbonded and bonded interactions. The charge of Na+ is 1.0e, the charge of oxygen in OH– is −1.3e, and the charge of hydrogen in OH– is 0.3e. To simulate sodium hydroxide solutions at low, medium, and high concentrations, 10, 30, and 50 sodium and hydroxide ions were combined with 500 water molecules, respectively. The concentration of the sodium hydroxide solution and the pH increase with the number of added sodium and hydroxide ions. The molar concentration and pH of the sodium hydroxide solutions were as follows: low concentration, 46.38 mol/L and pH 15.67; medium concentration, 128.24 mol/L and pH 16.11; high concentration, 198.18 mol/L and pH 16.3. First, we calculate the number of moles of the molecules using their density, molar mass, and quantity. Next, we determine the molar concentration using Avogadro’s constant, and subsequently convert this concentration to pH.27 Energy minimization of the sodium hydroxide solution was then performed using the conjugate gradient (CG) method before adding it to the Illite system.

Simulation Details

We employed LAMMPS (Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator)28,29 to simulate the dissolution process of Illite in a sodium hydroxide solution. The simulation utilized periodic boundary conditions to enhance realism. Initially, the system underwent energy minimization using the conjugate gradient method to optimize the model and alleviate potential configuration overlaps, thereby stabilizing its structure. Subsequently, relaxation was conducted under NVT ensemble conditions, maintaining a temperature of 323.65 K and pressure of 10 MPa.

The dissolution chemical reaction process was simulated using the ReaxFF force field, necessitating control over the system’s atom count, temperature, and volume to maintain NVT ensemble conditions. Initially, the system was set to a temperature of 323.65 K, with initial velocities randomly assigned to all atoms using a Gaussian random number generator to match this temperature. Temperature and pressure were regulated using the Berendsen thermostat30 and Berenden barostat algorithms to maintain fluctuations near the predetermined values.

A time step of 0.25 fs was employed, and the velocity-Verlet integration scheme31 was used to integrate the equations of motion. To enhance computational efficiency, a simulated annealing method was applied by briefly increasing the temperature to 3000 K to accelerate reaction kinetics,32 allowing chemical reactions to occur within the time scale of the molecular dynamics simulation. The simulation was conducted using a time step of 0.25 fs over a duration of 200 ps.

Result Analysis and Discussion

We conducted an analysis by monitoring and statistically analyzing several key parameters to elucidate the differences and mechanisms underlying the dissolution of Illite. This included tracking the mean square displacement (MSD) of cations within Illite, diffusion coefficients, radial distribution functions, number distributions, changes in ion energies, and the temporal distribution of various reaction species. This approach allowed us to comprehensively understand how Illite dissolves under sodium hydroxide solution, providing insights into the dynamics and transformations of ions involved in the dissolution process.

Diffusion Capacity

Due to the ion exchange between electrolyte solutions and clay minerals occurring through mutual diffusion, the diffusion capability of ions can to some extent reflect the strength of ion exchange. Therefore, by analyzing the Mean Square Displacement (MSD) curves of cations within Illite, we can fit the diffusion coefficients of cations in sodium hydroxide solution. This process involves the diffusion of cations, including potassium (K), aluminum (Al), and silicon (Si), throughout the entire dissolution process of Illite. It encompasses the diffusion of these cations within the Illite structure, their leaching from the Illite, and their subsequent diffusion in the NaOH solution. The diffusion process includes both reactive and nonreactive diffusion, with reactive diffusion occurring through proton exchange mechanisms and nonreactive diffusion occurring via density gradients. Under ReaxFF, the connectivity between all atom pairs is computed in real-time based on the local atomic environment and updated at each simulation time step. This feature enables ReaxFF to systematically capture chemical reaction processes, accommodating the breaking and formation of arbitrary bonds.

The Mean Square Displacement (MSD) is a measure that describes the average square of the deviation of a group of particles from their initial positions during continuous movement. The radial distribution function (RDF) is a function describing the density of particles as a function of distance from a reference atom. It is a crucial method for studying how atomic density varies with distance from a reference atom, providing insights into interatomic distances and atomic number densities within a system. RDF analysis is particularly suitable for evaluating bond lengths, and in this section, it was used to study the bonding between cations and hydroxide ions in Illite under varying concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution. The RDF results for K-Ooh, Al-Ooh, and Si-Ooh at different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution are illustrated in Figure 3. The peak intensity and position respectively represent the strength of interactions and distances between ions. Generally, peaks within 3.5 Å primarily consist of chemical and hydrogen bonds, while peaks beyond 3.5 Å mainly involve Coulombic and van der Waals interactions. In the initial system, At low concentrations, the radial distribution function (RDF) curves for each ion exhibit distant peak distances: K-Ooh shows no peak within 10 Å, Al-Ooh’s peak extends beyond 6 Å, and Si-Ooh’s peak is beyond 4 Å, indicating weak interactions and the absence of stable chemical bonds. As the simulation progresses to 200 ps, the RDF curve for K-Ooh gradually shifts forward, indicating increased interaction with hydroxide ions as sodium hydroxide concentration rises: at low concentrations, the short-range peak appears around 9 Å, decreasing to approximately 7.5 Å at medium concentrations, and approaching 6 Å at high concentrations, with minimal overall peak value changes. The RDF curves for Al-Ooh and Si-Ooh show increasing intensities of short-range peaks with little change in peak position, suggesting stronger interactions with the central atom. At higher concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution, the short-range peak for Si is around 1.5 Å with a significantly high peak value, indicating the formation of stable chemical bonds between Si and hydroxide ions, characterized by strong interactions.

The initial peak values of the RDF curves for K-Ooh, Al-Ooh, and Si-Ooh increase with concentration, reflecting increased particle aggregation and stronger interaction strengths. Within highly aggregated clusters, these strong interactions hinder the movement of metal cations, resulting in reduced diffusion coefficients for K+, Al3+, and Si4+.

Species and Proton Number Analysis

In the simulation of Illite dissolution reaction, the key species observed in the process mainly include K+, Na+, OH, H2O, Al3+, Si4+ and other metal hydroxides only appear in the simulation within an instant, for example, Al(OH)3 will undergo isomerization in alkaline solution, while the formation of silicic acid is unstable in solution and easy to polymerize into hydrated silica. Temporal changes in these species are difficult to track and therefore cannot be used as an indicator of reaction processes. The dissolution reaction process of Illite was analyzed by tracking the quantity changes of key substances such as K+, Na+, OH– and H2O during the simulation.

In this study, a bond order cutoff value of 0.3 was used to identify molecules. If at some time t the bond order between two atoms is greater than 0.3, the two atoms are considered to be covalent at the time interval (t, t + Δt), and the two atoms are composed of one molecule. The bond sequence and species at different time of the simulation results were counted, and the curve of the number of target molecules changing with time was drawn. In the simulation process, because OH– does not exist in isolation, OH– will be complexed with H2O to form H3O2–, In turn, H3O2– will also be complexed with H2O to form H5O3–, and so on.21 Therefore, in the statistics of the number of OH–, each H3O2– is one water molecule and one OH–, and each H5O3– is two water molecules and one OH–. NH2Ot is defined as the number of H2O molecules when the simulation time is tps, NH3O2t is the number of H3O2– molecules when the simulation time is tps, and NH5O3t is the number of H5O3– molecules when the simulation time is tps, then NH2Ot = NH2Ot + NH3O2t + NH5O3t. Similarly, NOHt = NH3O2t + NH5O3t. The quantity changes of K+, Na+, OH– and H2O were postprocessed in the above way, and the curve of species changes over time was drawn, as shown in Figure 4.

Quantity of ion species in Illite with time.

During the simulation, a trend of initial increase followed by decrease in hydroxide ion concentration was observed. Simultaneously, the number of water molecules decreased, indicating hydroxide ions as intermediate species in the reaction, with significant consumption of both water molecules and hydroxide ions. In contrast, the quantities of potassium and sodium ions remained relatively stable, suggesting a dynamic equilibrium of these ions throughout the reaction. In the initial simulation stages, water dissociated into hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions, resulting in decreased water molecule count and increased hydroxide ion count. Concurrently, cations in the solution, such as sodium and hydrogen, exchanged with cations in the clay minerals, particularly potassium ions, leading to an overall decrease in water molecules and hydroxide ions. During this phase, cations in Illite precipitated gradually, bonding with hydroxide ions generated from water dissociation and already present in the system to form metal hydroxides, thus reducing the number of hydroxide ions.

Analysis of Illite species revealed significant variations in hydroxide ion and water molecule quantities during the simulation. To investigate dissolution mechanisms further, the distribution of hydrogen and sodium in the Illite simulation solution, termed the proton number distribution, was statistically examined. From Figure 5, it is evident that by 50 ps of simulation time, there is a notable increase in the proton count at the interface of Illite, indicating initial protonation36 occurring predominantly at the mineral surface. Protonation can increase the positive charge of minerals, which may lead to a reduction in their stability. The binding of protons to negatively charged functional groups on the mineral surface can result in the disruption of the mineral’s layered structure. In other words, the protonation density of Illite provides an indication of its solubility to some extent. As the simulation progresses, protonation gradually penetrates deeper into the internal structure. By 200 ps of simulation time, the highest proton count is observed within the Illite structure.

Changes in Ion Energy

Hongtao discovered in laboratory studies on the dissolution of Illite that under closed conditions, Illite dissolves in sodium hydroxide solution. Significant physical changes in the Illite structure were observed, including thinning, reduction in size, and the loss of angular features. As dissolution progressed, the transparency of the leachate decreased while the silicon ion concentration increased. The dissolution process occurred from the outer surface to the inner surface. During the simulation process, it was observed that when sodium hydroxide solution contacts Illite, sodium cations and hydrogen ions generated from water dissociation first bind to nonbridging oxygens (Al–O–Si bonds) on the mineral surface, causing the cleavage of Al–O bonds and resulting in the formation of Si–OH and Si–O–Na species on the surface. Potassium ions (K+) as interlayer cations, bonded via weak K–O bridges, tend to diffuse freely within the crystal lattice. They readily undergo exchange reactions with hydroxyl ions and water, forming potassium hydroxide. As the reaction progresses, more Al–O and Si–O bonds break, leading to the formation of Al(OH)3 and Si(OH)4, gradually disintegrating the mineral structure. During the dissolution of Illite, metal cations such as K, Al, and Si, along with bridging and nonbridging oxygens within the Illite structure, play crucial roles. To investigate the dissolution mechanism of Illite, we computed the variation in energy of key ions within the Illite-solution system. Negative ion energy indicates that electrons are bound around the atomic nucleus, forming stable ions. In this state, the interaction between electrons and atomic nuclei reduces the system’s total energy below what is required to separate electrons and atomic nuclei. When ion energy is zero or positive, electrons are no longer bound around the atomic nucleus, indicating the separation of electrons and atomic nuclei, forming ions or free electrons. The more negative the energy value, the more stable the ion is.

Figure 7a,b depict the atomic energy cloud maps of the Illite system (xz direction) before and after reaction with high concentration sodium hydroxide solution. Significant changes in atomic energy are observed before and after molecular dynamics simulation. In the initial structure, the octahedral atoms of Illite exhibit relatively low energy, whereas the interlayer cation K+ shows higher energy, indicating greater reactivity. Figure 7c–g illustrate the atomic energy cloud maps (xy direction) of K+, Al3+, Si4+, Nonbridging oxygen (NBO), and Bridging oxygen (BO) at 200 ps simulation time. From Figure 7, it is evident that the energy of K+ is consistently above zero, indicating the highest reactivity. Al3+ maintain a relatively low energy level overall, suggesting the formation of stable molecules. Some Si4+ exhibit higher energies, indicating they have not yet stabilized. Compared to NBO, BO has lower atomic energy, suggesting that the structure and surrounding environment of BO are more stable compared to NBO, although both energies are below zero, indicating lower reactivity overall.

Figure 8 illustrates the average energy change of individual ions in the Illite system before and after reaction. The energy of K+ shows a significant decrease, while Ow, Ooh, BO, NBO, and Al3+ also exhibit varying degrees of decrease. Conversely, Si4+ experiences a moderate increase in energy. Before and after the reaction, the energy of K+, Al3+, Ooh, BO, and NBO atoms in the Illite system decreases, while the energy of Si atoms increases. Specifically, the average energies of K+, Al3+, Ooh, BO, and NBO decrease by 9, 1, 60, 19, and 11 kcal/mol, respectively, while the average energy of Si4+ increases by 11 kcal/mol. This is attributed to the fact that the ions in the Illite crystal have a complete crystal structure initially, with long-range ordered arrangements. However, as the simulation progresses, the atoms form new bonds with solvent ions through dissolution and hydration processes. Furthermore, at 98 ps into the simulation, the generation of silicic acid was observed, and at 138 ps, the formation of silicic hydroxide was detected. Silicic acid is an intermediate product of the reaction and is unstable in solution, readily polymerizing to form hydrated silica dioxide. Hence, some Si4+ exhibit higher energy levels and higher reactivity, leading to the observed increase in Si4+ energy.

Conclusions

Based on reactive molecular dynamics simulations, this study investigates the dissolution mechanism of Illite in strong alkaline environments. It analyzes how different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solutions affect the dissolution process of Illite, focusing on diffusion coefficients and radial distribution functions (RDF). Discussions encompass the proton distribution, species, and ion energies during Illite dissolution under high-concentration sodium hydroxide solutions. Ultimately, the dissolution mechanism of clay minerals in strong alkaline environments is elucidated at the molecular scale, leading to the following conclusions and insights:1.As the concentration of NaOH solution increases, the diffusion coefficients of cations gradually decrease. The first peak of the RDF curves for K-Ooh, Al-Ooh, and Si-Ooh increases with higher NaOH concentrations, indicating an increase in ion aggregation and interaction strength. With higher NaOH concentrations, metal cations in Illite more readily form stable chemical bonds with hydroxide ions, leading to the formation of highly aggregated clusters. The strong interactions between ions hinder the movement of cations, resulting in a gradual decrease in their diffusion coefficients.2.During the dissolution of Illite in sodium hydroxide solution, water dissociates into hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions. Hydroxide ions act as intermediate species, accompanied by significant water molecule dissociation. Protonation initially occurs at the mineral surface and progresses inward into the structure as the reaction advances. Furthermore, the degree of protonation increases with the rising concentration of sodium hydroxide solution.3.Analysis of the average energy change of ions in the Illite system before and after the reaction reveals that K+ exhibits the highest reactivity. Highly reactive K+ ions can more effectively engage in exchange reactions with other ions in the solution, such as Na+ and H+, thereby disrupting the mineral’s crystal structure.
