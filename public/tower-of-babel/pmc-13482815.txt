Predicting visual mental imagery: structural and transcriptomic signatures in the human brain
Authors: Ruxin Xu, Dai Zhang, Ke Zhou, Liqin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual mental imagery (VMI) vividness varies substantially across individuals, yet the structural and molecular bases of this variability remain elusive. A central debate concerns whether VMI ability depends primarily on early visual cortex (V1) or arises from distributed higher-order cortical systems. Here, in a cohort of 229 adults, we apply parcel-wise cortical morphometric modeling with 20-fold cross-validation with random splits to predict individual differences in VMI ability. We show that the morphometric signature spans ventral temporal, parietal, and prefrontal cortices, with no dominant contribution from early visual regions. This distributed profile is selectively associated with individual performance in tasks requiring high versus low imagery demand. Transcriptomic alignment using the Allen Human Brain Atlas reveals gene expression enrichment in neuronal system development, synaptic signaling, and inorganic ion transmembrane transport. Together, these findings support a distributed cortical architecture underlying VMI vividness and provide a molecular context for its neurobiological processes.

Introduction

Visual mental imagery (VMI), the capacity to generate percept-like visual experiences without external input, shows pronounced inter-individual variability, ranging from aphantasia (near absence of imagery) to hyperphantasia (exceptionally vivid imagery)1. This variation has important implications for spatial reasoning, memory, creativity, and mental health, yet its neural bases remain debated. The classic “imagery debate”, contrasting depictive (sensory) and propositional (symbolic) representational accounts, has largely converged on the view that imagery relies on depictive, perception-like representations2,3. However, the neural implementation of these representations and whether they depend critically on early visual cortex (EVC) remained unresolved3–6.

Neuroimaging evidence consistently showed that VMI engaged a large-scale cortical network spanning frontoparietal and ventral-temporal regions that supported top-down control, semantic access, and working memory3,6,7. Within this network, EVC activation appeared variable, influenced by stimulus feature, task demands, and imagery vividness8,9. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies revealed that imagery could elicit retinotopically organized activation in EVC, supporting a depictive representational format10,11, and that V1 encoded low-level visual features and detailed spatial information12. Structural variation in V1 further correlated with individual imagery strength and precision—smaller V1 surface area predicts stronger but less precise imagery, whereas larger V1 supports higher spatial precision13. Similarly, reducing occipital excitability via transcranial direct current stimulation enhanced imagery vividness14.

Nevertheless, lesion studies demonstrated that vivid imagery could persist even with extensive occipital damage15,16, and that EVC received nonretinal, top-down inputs conveying abstract content17. Together, these findings supported a distributed, feedback-driven framework in which VMI arose from coordinated interactions among perceptual, semantic, and control processes. Within this framework, EVC contributed to sensory fidelity and spatial resolution but was not the sole generator of VMI.

Despite substantial functional evidence, the stable anatomical substrates giving rise to individual differences in VMI vividness remained insufficiently characterized. Here, we asked whether VMI vividness was better predicted by a distributed cortical morphometric pattern encompassing ventral-temporal, parietal, and frontal cortices, or by local anatomical variability within EVC. We further evaluated the functional generalizability of this morphometric signature by examining its association with performance on behavioral tasks with high-imagery demands, following the method of ref. 18. A separate but equally important gap concerned the molecular architecture that supported imagery-related cortical morphology. Imaging-transcriptomic methods enable spatial alignment of functional and structural networks with cortical gene-expression profiles, bridging macroscale anatomy and function with cellular and metabolic processes19–22. By mapping the cortical morphometric signature of VMI vividness and situating it within the transcriptomic landscape, we aimed to provide a reference-based transcriptomic annotation and identify molecular pathways that might be relevant to imagery-related processing. This multiscale framework provided a bridge between anatomical variability, molecular architecture, and the quality of VMI.

Results

A distributed cortical morphometric signature predicted VMI ability

To delineate the spatial profile of imagery-related morphology, we aggregated parcels meeting the partial-correlation selection criterion across folds. This procedure identified 24 of the 148 Destrieux parcels as ROIs; see Supplementary Table 1 for detailed information. Positive associations were observed in ventral-temporal cortex (right inferior and bilateral middle temporal gyri), parieto-occipital regions (right parieto-occipital sulcus), and precentral regions (inferior part of the right precentral sulcus). Negative associations were concentrated in bilateral anterior cingulate gyri, insular regions (including the inferior circular sulcus of the insula), and prefrontal areas (left superior frontal gyrus, bilateral middle frontal sulcus, left orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus). Notably, no parcel within EVC was selected, indicating that VMI-related structural variability was distributed across high-order cortical systems rather than confined to EVC (Fig. 2B). Convergent and discriminant validity: MDS is selectively related to high-imagery-demand behavior

To evaluate convergent and discriminant validity, we tested whether the MDS generalized to behavioral tasks differing in imagery demands. Tasks requiring explicit visuospatial construction (scene recognition, mental rotation, and VSTM) were contrasted with tasks involving lower imagery reliance (old/new recognition, attentional blink, and visual search).

As shown in Fig. 3, higher MDS were associated with better performance on scene recognition (Spearman’s ρ = 0.224, p < 0.001), mental rotation (ρ = 0.161, p = 0.030) and VSTM (ρ = 0.162, p = 0.014). In contrast, no significant associations were observed between MDS and accuracy of old/new recognition (ρ = 0.109, p = 0.104), AB magnitude (ρ = -0.093, p = 0.231) or distractor suppression effect of VS (ρ = 0.061, p = 0.377). Imaging-transcriptomic alignment and functional enrichment: transcriptomic context of the VMI morphometric signature

To situate the morphometric signature of VMI within a molecular context, we aligned the 23 predictive ROIs to postmortem cortical gene-expression profiles from the AHBA and subjected positively aligned transcripts to functional enrichment. Spatial correlations identified 580 genes whose regional expression in the AHBA reference data covaried with the imagery-related morphometric pattern (Supplementary Data 1).

ORA analysis in Metascape revealed convergent enrichment across neuronal system development, synaptic signaling, and inorganic ion transmembrane transport. Key signaling pathways regulating cellular architecture (Rho GTPases) and development (Wnt signaling) were also significantly enriched, alongside processes critical for vesicle-mediated transport and neuron projection development. Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that individual differences in VMI vividness could be predicted by a distributed morphometric signature including temporal, parieto-occipital, prefrontal, precentral, and cingulo-insular regions, rather than localized anatomy within EVC. This distributed configuration selectively generalized to tasks with explicit visuospatial construction, such as scene recognition, mental rotation and VSTM, but not to low-demand tasks (e.g., object recognition, AB, and VS), supporting its convergent and discriminant validity as a neural index of imagery capacity. Finally, Imaging-transcriptomic alignment further indicated that imagery-related morphometric signature is spatially associated with gene-expression profiles primarily enriched for neuronal system development, synaptic signaling, and inorganic ion transmembrane transport across distributed cortical circuits.

The spatial configuration of the morphometric signature aligns with hierarchical, feedback-driven models of imagery generation. Positive associations in temporal, parieto-occipital, and premotor cortices converge with evidence that vivid imagery involves reactivation of perceptual, spatial, and motor representations under top-down control. Temporal areas, particularly the inferior and middle temporal gyri, likely mediated the reinstatement of semantic–visual integration and category-level representations12,23–27, whereas parieto-occipital and precuneus regions facilitated visuospatial construction and viewpoint transformation8,28–31. The involvement of premotor cortex suggested partial recruitment of embodied motor simulation mechanisms, consistent with evidence that mental rotation and dynamic scene construction rely on shared visuomotor networks32,33. Together, these findings point to a distributed cortical architecture in which perceptual, mnemonic, and motor components were jointly recruited to generate vivid mental imagery.

Conversely, negative associations within anterior cingulate, insular, and prefrontal regions suggested that enhanced imagery vividness may be associated with reduced dependence on executive control or salience monitoring. This pattern supported a regulation–representation trade-off: individuals with more vivid imagery might exhibit more automatic perceptual reinstatement with less top-down inhibitory control34–37. This interpretation aligned with findings that individuals with aphantasia exhibited increased frontal engagement, possibly reflecting compensatory reliance on semantic or verbal strategies in the absence of visual reactivation37. Similarly, creative visual thinking has been associated with reduced cortical thickness and GMV in control-related regions, such as the inferior frontal gyrus and anterior cingulate cortex, consistent with greater cognitive flexibility and reduced inhibition facilitating imagery-based recombination38,39. Together, these convergent patterns suggested that vivid imagery arises from efficient representational reinstatement across perceptual and mnemonic systems, balanced against reduced executive regulation.

Beyond the temporal, parieto-occipital, premotor, and cingulo–insular regions highlighted above, several additional association parcels also entered the predictive signature with relatively low weights. The morphometric signature is not restricted to imagery-specific regions, but instead engages a broader higher-order association network that subserves multiple complex cognitive operations, which is consistent with the view of imagery as a multi-component process drawing on distributed neural resources.

Importantly, the lack of a dominant morphometric predictor in EVC should not be interpreted as evidence against its involvement in VMI. Converging evidence indicated that EVC involvement in imagery was conditional on excitability state, task demands, and individual vividness. High-resolution fMRI studies demonstrated that imagery could elicit retinotopically organized activation in EVC, though at lower amplitude and signal reliability than during perception10,40,41. Furthermore, EVC exhibited top-down modulation by frontoparietal feedback42–44. Reducing occipital excitability enhanced imagery vividness14, and individual differences in V1 surface area correlated inversely with imagery strength13. On the other hand, imagery could persist after occipital lesions15,16, underscoring that V1 was modulatory rather than essential. These findings collectively suggested that EVC contributes as a state-dependent amplifier rather than a generative source—its influence governed by transient excitability and feedback precision rather than stable macrostructural traits. In contrast, the distributed morphometric predictors identified here reflected trait-like architecture supporting the generative and manipulative components of imagery across ventral–temporal, parietal, and prefrontal systems. Integrating these perspectives yielded a hierarchical model in which EVC modulated perceptual fidelity, while distributed associative networks determined imagery construction and vividness.

At the behavioral level, the MDS specifically predicted performance on tasks requiring the construction or transformation of internal visuospatial representations—such as scene recognition, mental rotation and VSTM—rather than tasks dominated by distractor suppression or attentional filtering. This pattern indicated that visual imagery was not a pervasive cognitive operation but a flexible process selectively engaged when internal simulation conferred cognitive advantage. Scene recognition involved reconstruction of spatial relations and viewpoint shifts45,46. Mental rotation relied on both visuospatial representation and motor simulation, depended critically on the same parieto-premotor systems identified in our morphometric signature33. And VSTM involved maintaining structured visual scene representations47. In contrast, object recognition, AB, and VS could be solved through verbal labeling or rehearsal, reducing their reliance on imagery mechanisms3. The observed dissociation thus delineated the conditions under which imagery contributed to behavioral performance, and emphasized that vivid imagery reflected the efficiency of distributed cortical networks supporting reconstructive visuospatial processes. In addition, the original VVIQ score did not show significant associations with performance on the high-imagery-demand tasks, suggesting that the morphometric signature may reflect a neurobiological substrate of VMI closely aligned with objective task performance, which cannot fully be captured by subjective report.

At the transcriptomic level, regions predicting higher VMI vividness showed enriched expression of genes involved in neuronal system development, synaptic signaling, inorganic ion transmembrane transport, as well as key signaling pathways regulating cellular architecture and neurodevelopment (e.g., Rho GTPase signaling and Wnt signaling). This pattern aligns with prior evidence that large-scale functional networks were mirrored by correlated expression of synaptic and ion-channel genes22. Population genetics and family studies further indicated a diffuse, polygenic architecture for imagery: genome-wide analyses identified no single large-effect locus but identified subthreshold variants within SYT1, a key regulator of Ca²⁺-dependent vesicle exocytosis48, and familial data placed aphantasia and hyperphantasia on a heritable continuum1. Together, these findings point to a heritable, polygenic architecture for VMI vividness, and our hypothesis-driven results offer a foundation for future investigations into the specific genes and pathways—such as those involved in synaptic transmission and ion-channel function—that may shape individual differences in VMI.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, limitations regarding sample size and reproducibility. Although our sample (N = 229) is comparatively large relative to previous neuroimaging studies of visual imagery individual differences, it remains modest for establishing highly stable brain–behavior associations49. Future replication in larger, independent cohorts is thus essential to confirm the generalizability of the reported morphometric signature. Second, the VVIQ used here is a self-report questionnaire. While it is widely adopted in imagery research and demonstrates good reliability and validity, its outcomes may be influenced by individual differences in metacognitive ability, linguistic expression tendencies, and introspective habits. It would therefore be valuable to combine self-report with more objective, behavior-based indices of imagery strength (e.g., binocular rivalry priming, imagery acuity tasks) or psychophysiological measures to better triangulate individual differences in imagery. While our findings point to structural substrates of VMI, they do not capture the dynamic neural processes underlying real-time imagery generation. Future work should therefore integrate task-based fMRI and functional connectivity measures to examine how distributed cortical networks dynamically support VMI. Moreover, interventional approaches—such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to modulate network excitability, and pharmacological or genetic manipulations targeting the molecular pathways suggested by our imaging-transcriptomic analyses—could establish causal links between network physiology and individual differences in VMI.

Methods

Participants

A total of 229 healthy undergraduate students from Beijing Normal University (age 17–24 years; M = 22.58, SD = 1.02; 105 female) participated in this study. All participants completed a structural T1-weighted MRI scan and the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ). Subsamples additionally completed behavioral tasks used to assess the convergent and discriminant validity of the morphometry-derived prediction: scene recognition (n = 227), mental rotation (n = 183), visual short-term memory (VSTM) (n = 228), old/new recognition (n = 224), attentional blink (AB) (n = 166) and visual search (VS) (n = 209). Variations in sample size were due primarily to scheduling conflicts or voluntary withdrawal; behavioral analyses were performed on available data for each task without imputation.

The study was conducted within a larger multimodal project examining relationships among brain structure, cognitive function, and genetic factors50–53. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Normal University (approval number: BNU202503030070). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Behavior measures

Assessment of VMI ability

VMI ability was assessed using the VVIQ54,55. The scale includes 16 items grouped into four familiar scenarios: (1) a frequently seen relative or friend, (2) a rising sun, (3) a familiar shop, and (4) a rural landscape with trees, mountains, and a lake. For each item, participants were instructed to close their eyes and vividly imagine the described scene, then rate its clarity on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = no image at all; 5 = perfectly clear). Total scores range from 16 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater imagery vividness. A validated Chinese version of the VVIQ was administrated, and the total score served as the measure of individual VMI ability.

Assessment of general intelligence

To account for variability related to general intelligence, participants completed Raven’s Advanced Progressive Matrices (RAPM) under standard procedures56.

Scene recognition task

Participants performed a two-alternative forced-choice scene recognition task adapted from ref. 57. Stimuli were computer-generated 3D landscapes consisting of four distinct hills arranged around a central point58. Each trial comprised a fixation, a 2-s study image, a 2-s retention interval, and two probe images shown simultaneously. One probe matched the studied scene from a novel viewpoint, whereas the other served as a distractor. Three conditions were administered: shadow (changes in low-level shading; control), configural (swapping the positions of two hills), and spatial (radially shifting one hill without altering pairwise relations). Participants were instructed to select the studied scene as quickly and accurately as possible. Accuracies from the configural and spatial conditions were averaged to index individual scene recognition ability.

Mental rotation task

Mental rotation ability was assessed using a 40-trial version of the classic Shepard–Metzler paradigm59,60. Each trial presented a fixation, a 700-ms cube figure, a 500-ms inter-stimulus interval (ISI), and a second 700-ms cube figure. Participants indicated whether the second figure could be obtained by rotating the first, responding as quickly and accurately as possible. Accuracy served as the measure of individual mental rotation ability.

Visual short-term memory (VSTM) task

Participants completed a dual-task paradigm, combining a color–location memory task with a concurrent digit-sequence comparison task, detail referred to ref. 61. Each trial began with a digit sequence, followed by an array of five colored dots presented simultaneously at distinct locations. After a brief retention interval marked by placeholders, a single probe appeared, and participants judged whether its color–location pairing matched the studied array. The digit comparison was performed immediately afterward. VSTM capacity was indexed using Cowan’s K computed from the color–location judgments at set size 5.

Old/new recognition task

Object recognition ability was assessed using an old/new recognition paradigm for two stimulus categories—faces and flowers—presented in counterbalanced blocks51,62,63. Stimuli were grayscale photographs; face images depicted front-view Chinese adults with external contours removed, and flower images were background-cropped to match faces in low-level properties.

In each block, participants studied ten items twice (1 s per item; 0.5 s ISI), followed by a 40-trial yes/no recognition test containing the ten studied items (each repeated twice) intermixed with 20 novel items. Participants judged whether each test image had appeared during the study phase. Recognition accuracy was averaged across the face and flower blocks to provide an index of object recognition ability.

Attentional blink (AB) task

AB effect was assessed using a rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) paradigm61. Stimuli consisted of black uppercase letters (A–Z) and digits (1–9) presented on a gray background. Each trial started when the participant pressed the space key. Two numerical targets were embedded within the stream: the first target (T1) appeared randomly between the 8th and 16th positions, and the second target (T2) appeared either four or seven items after T1 (Lag 4 and Lag 7). Participants were asked to report both targets as accurately as possible. For each lag, T2 | T1 accuracy was computed as the proportion of trials in which T2 was correctly identified given correct identification of T1. The magnitude of attentional blink (ABM) was calculated as the relative drop in T2 | T1 performance at Lag 4 compared with Lag 7: (T2|T1lag7 − T2|T1lag4)/T2|T1lag7.

Visual search (VS) task

In the VS task, each trial began with a fixation cross presented for 1000–1500 ms, followed by an array of white circular placeholders (set size: 5 or 9) arranged around fixation. After 1000 ms, arrows appeared within all placeholders. Participants were instructed to indicate the direction of the horizontal target arrow (left or right) as quickly and accurately as possible. In half of the trials, a color singleton distractor was introduced: one placeholder turned black while the target never appeared at that location64. The distractor suppression effect was defined as the normalized response time (RT) difference between singleton-present and singleton-absent conditions across both set sizes: [(RTsingleton_setsize5 − RTnonsingleton_setsize5)/(RTsingleton_setsize5 + RTnonsingleton_setsize5) + (RTsingleton_setsize9 − RTnonsingleton_setsize9)/(RTsingleton_setsize9 + RTnonsingleton_setsize9)]/2.

MRI acquisition and pre-processing

High-resolution structural T1-weighted images were acquired on a Siemens 3T Trio scanner using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence (TR = 2530 ms; TE = 3.39 ms; TI = 1100 ms; flip angle = 7°; FOV = 256 × 256 mm²; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1.33 mm³).

Preprocessing of the structural MRI data were conducted using voxel-based morphometry (VBM) implemented in SPM8 (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). Each T1-weighted image was first segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using the unified segmentation algorithm65. GM segments were then rigidly aligned, resampled to 2 × 2 × 2 mm³, and normalized to MNI152 space using DARTEL66. Normalized GM maps were modulated to preserve local tissue volume, smoothed with an 8-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, and then masked using an absolute threshold of 0.2. Further analysis was conducted using these masked-modulated GM maps.

Predictive modeling of VVIQ (Morphometry-derived score)

Feature extraction

Parcel-wise GM volume (GMV) was extracted using a downsampled Destrieux atlas comprising 148 cortical parcels67. For each participant, mean GMV was computed within each parcel, yielding a 229 × 148 feature matrix for predictive modeling.

Predictive model construction and training

Individual-level prediction was performed using 20-fold cross-validation with random splits (20-fold RS-CV) with out-of-fold evaluation. In each iteration, participants were randomly partitioned into 20 folds; onefold was held out as the test set and the remaining 19 folds comprised the training set. Within the training set, we calculated partial Spearman correlations between parcel-wise GMV and VVIQ score while controlling for age, gender, RAPM score, and total intracranial volume (TIV). Parcels showing significant correlations (p < 0.05) were retained as predictors.

A multiple linear regression model was then fitted to the training set, with VVIQ score as the dependent variable, selected parcels’ GMVs as predictors, and the same covariates included. The trained model was applied to the held-out fold to generate an out-of-fold predicted VVIQ score, termed the morphometry-derived score (MDS). This procedure was repeated across all folds, yielding one MDS per participant.

Model evaluation and permutation testing

Model predictive accuracy was quantified as the Spearman correlation between MDS and observed VVIQ score. Statistical significance was evaluated using 10,000 permutation tests, in which observed VVIQ scores were randomly shuffled and the prediction pipeline was repeated. The empirical p value was defined as the proportion of permutations producing correlations equal to or greater than the observed value.

Visualization of the morphometric signature

Using the 20-fold RS-CV pipeline, we first quantified selection times for each parcel, defined as the number of cross-validation folds in which the parcel met the within-fold partial Spearman significance threshold (i.e., p < 0.05). Parcels with non-zero selection times (n = 24) were defined as regions of interest (ROIs). We then computed partial Spearman coefficients (ρ) between each ROI’s mean GMV and observed VVIQ score (controlling age, gender, RAPM score, and TIV) across the full sample (229 participants) and applied Fisher’s z transformation to the resulting coefficients. The ROIs were visualized on the Destrieux cortical surface, with color representing both the sign and magnitude of the Fisher z–transformed coefficients (z). The surface representation was referred to as the morphometric signature of VMI. Convergent and discriminant validity of MDS

We administered six behavioral measures with varying imagery demands: scene recognition, mental rotation, VSTM, old/new recognition, AB, and VS. A priori, we classified tasks as having high-imagery-demand based on a single criterion: whether task success explicitly depends on constructing/maintaining a spatial representation or performing a visuospatial transformation. Under this criterion, high-imagery-demand tasks comprised: scene recognition (novel-view matching with sensitivity to configural/spatial changes), mental rotation (visuospatial rotation), and VSTM (encoding and maintaining the spatial configuration of several colored items); low imagery-demand tasks included old/new recognition, AB, and VS, which primarily reflect VSTM for objects, temporal attentional selection, or distractor suppression. This predefined classification was used to evaluate the convergent and discriminant validity of MDS across cognitive domains.

To assess convergent and discriminant validity across cognitive domains, we related the MDS to performance on each of the six tasks using Spearman’s correlation (ρ) (uncorrected). For each task, we defined a specific outcome measure: accuracy for scene recognition, mental rotation, and old/new recognition; Cowan’s K for VSTM; AB magnitude for AB; and the distractor suppression effect for VS. For comparison, we also conducted the same set of correlations between the observed VVIQ score and each behavioral outcome.

Imaging-transcriptomic alignment

AHBA preprocessing and mapping

We used postmortem gene-expression maps from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA; http://human.brain-map.org/), derived from a small independent donor cohort rather than our imaging participants, to test parcel-level spatial correspondence between the morphometric signature and reference gene-expression profiles. Because only two donors (H0351.2001, H0351.2002) provided bilateral cortical sampling, analyses were restricted to these donors to avoid hemisphere mirroring artifacts. Following established preprocessing procedures69, we excluded samples within the brainstem and cerebellum, re-annotated the gene names of probes, removed low-intensity probes, and averaged expression values across all probes detecting the same gene. Within each donor, expression values were normalized using a scaled robust sigmoid transform and then gene-wise centered to reduce inter-donor variability.

Each sample’s MNI coordinate was assigned to the nearest Destrieux parcel via a nearest-point search algorithm. Parcel-level gene expression was then calculated by averaging samples within donors and across donors, yielding a 148 × 15,745 parcel-by-gene matrix aligned to the morphometric signature of VMI.

Morphometric vector construction

The morphometric vector used for the imaging-transcriptomic analysis was identical to the vector used to visualize the morphometric signature in Fig. 2B. Specifically, for the ROIs identified by the predictive model, we computed partial Spearman coefficients between mean GMV and observed VVIQ score (controlling age, gender, RAPM score, and TIV) and applied Fisher z–transformed coefficients. One ROI lacked AHBA coverage and was excluded, yielding a final set of 23 ROIs for subsequent correlation.

Gene-wise spatial correlation

For each gene, expression values across the 23 ROIs were assembled into a spatial vector and correlated with the morphometric vector using Spearman’s correlation. We focused on positively aligned genes (ρ > 0, p < 0.05), reflecting transcripts whose cortical expression covaried with the strength of the imagery-related morphometric pattern.

Functional enrichment analysis

Positively aligned genes (n = 580) were entered into Metascape (https://metascape.org) for over-representation analysis (ORA) across GO (BP/MF/CC), KEGG, Reactome, and MSigDB Hallmark libraries. Enrichment statistics were computed using hypergeometric tests with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (default criteria: ≥3 overlapping genes, enrichment factor ≥1.5, nominal p < 0.01). Term redundancy was reduced via Kappa-based clustering (threshold = 0.3).

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were conducted at the participant level unless otherwise specified. The sample size (N = 229) was determined by data availability and is comparable to prior individual-differences studies using structural MRI. Each participant contributed a single independent observation and was treated as one biological replicate. No technical replicates were acquired. Behavioral measures were obtained in single sessions for each task, and analyses were performed within available subsamples without imputation. Associations between variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation, with two-tailed p values reported.

For predictive modeling, a 20-fold RS-CV framework was implemented to estimate out-of-fold performance, and statistical significance was evaluated using permutation testing (10,000 iterations). Cross-validation folds do not constitute independent replicates but were used to estimate generalization performance. Feature selection and model fitting were performed strictly within training sets to avoid information leakage.

Imaging–transcriptomic analyses were based on the Allen Human Brain Atlas, an independent postmortem dataset. These analyses are correlational and exploratory, and no biological replication within the current sample was performed.

All statistical procedures were implemented using standard MATLAB toolboxes. Exact p values, effect sizes, and analysis details are reported in the main text and supplementary materials.
