Memento: Augmenting Personalized Memory via Practical Multimodal Wearable Sensing in Visual Search and Wayfinding Navigation
Indrajeet Ghosh 1 , Kasthuri Jayarajah 2 , Nicholas Waytowich 3 , Nirmalya Roy 1 1 Information Systems, UMBC, 2 Computer Science, NJIT, 3 DEVCOM, Army Research Lab, USA

Abstract
. Working memory involves the temporary retention of information over short periods. It is a critical cognitive function that enables humans to perform various online processing tasks, such as dialing a phone number, recalling misplaced items’ locations, or navigating through a store. However, inherent limitations in an individual’s capacity to retain information often result in forgetting important details during such tasks. Although previous research has successfully utilized wearable and assistive technologies to enhance long-term memory functions (e.g., episodic memory), their application to supporting short-term recall in daily activities remains underexplored. To address this gap, we present Memento , a framework that uses multimodal wearable sensor data to detect significant changes in cognitive state and provide intelligent in situ cues to enhance recall. Through two user studies involving 15 and 25 participants in visual search navigation tasks, we demonstrate that participants receiving visual cues from Memento achieved significantly better route recall, improving approximately 20-23% compared to free recall. Furthermore, Memento reduced cognitive load and review time by 46% while also achieving a substantial reduction in computation time ( 3.86 secs vs. 15.35 secs ), offering an average 75% effective compared to computer vision-based cues selection approaches.

1.
Introduction

Working Memory (WM)
refers to the temporary retention of information for short periods, enabling humans to process information
online

(
Ma et al., 2014
;
Barrouillet et al., 2012
;
Barrouillet and Camos, 2021
)
. It supports tasks such as dialing a phone number, recalling where keys were placed, or deciding which turn to take in a mall. Unlike long-term or episodic memory, which involves storing and retrieving information over extended periods (e.g., years) and is shaped by life experiences, WM focuses on maintaining and manipulating information momentarily. Individual differences in WM capacity—how much one can hold—affect performance and contribute to
forgetting

(
Lewis-Peacock and Norman, 2014
;
Aslan and Bäuml, 2011
)
.

The rise of pervasive sensing technologies (e.g., smartphone, wearable cameras, social media logs) has spurred research on augmenting human memory
(
Allé et al., 2017
;
Davies et al., 2015
;
Dingler et al., 2021
;
Kandappu et al., 2021
;
Gonçalves and Jorge, 2004
;
Blanc-Brude and Scapin, 2007
)
. While studies have explored assistive technologies like lifelogging for episodic memory
(
Kandappu et al., 2021
)
and marker-based tracking for older adults
(
Li et al., 2019
)
, they primarily enhance long-term memory (e.g., autobiographical, episodic) via cameras, raising privacy concerns. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has specifically aimed at enhancing working memory for short-term retention. Through this work, we address the
two
fundamental research questions:

•

RQ1
:
Can non-invasive multi-modal wearables effectively capture key high-attentional
event-related potential
(ERP) episodes during working memory tasks?

•

RQ2
:
Can capturing and highlighting the high-attentional ERP episodes and intelligently cueing them can improve memory recall of significant personalized moments ("
mementos")?

Key Challenges:
Although memory augmentation aids effectively enhance recall across various age groups
(
Le et al., 2016
;
Hong, 2017
)
, most techniques rely on lifelogging
(
Elagroudy et al., 2023
)
or continuous ego-centric vision processing, posing significant privacy concerns. While wearable-based physiological sensing—especially EEG—has been explored
(
Pierce and Mann, 2021
;
Sun et al., 2016
)
, limited spatial and temporal resolution, along with susceptibility to artifacts (e.g., eye blinks, random noise), reduces practical utility
(
Cao et al., 2021
)
. Moreover, artifacts in galvanic skin response (GSR) and photoplethysmography (PPG) signals
(
Cao et al., 2021
;
Patel et al., 2018
;
Rihet et al., 2024
)
further compromise these modalities in real-world applications.

To this end, we propose
Memento
, a multimodal wearable sensing system that enhances human
recall
by integrating functional connectivity-based reconstruction with proactive identification of
moments of significance
.
Memento
improves signal quality across wearable devices and generates
mementos
of working memory, aiding later retrieval of task-relevant information.
Our focus is on
visuospatial working memory
, which plays a crucial role in visual search and wayfinding
(
Ye et al., 2022
;
Shi et al., 2021
)
. We employ multimodal sensors (EEG, GSR, and PPG) to detect significant moments without obtrusiveness during the task. Through two user studies (
15
and
25
participants), we show that
Memento
outperform
six
baseline methods in detecting these moments and improving recall. This work lays the foundation for smart assistive interfaces that augment personalized moment-of-significance for memory augmentation.

In this work, we make the following
Key Research Contributions
:

•

Physiological Sensing with commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) Devices:
We propose
Memento
, which integrates (a) functional connectivity-based EEG signal reconstruction and (b)
incremental feature-level fusion
with GSR and PPG sensors to enhance the accuracy of COTS EEG devices. This enables effective detection of high-attentional ERP episodes during visuospatial working memory tasks.

•

Multimodal Wearable Sensing for ERPs Extraction:
Through a user study with
15
participants across four environments, we demonstrate that combining EEG reconstruction with multimodal sensing effectively detects high-attentional moments during navigation.
Memento
successfully extracts up to
72.6%
of episodes correlating with subjects’ visual attention to object instances—a
35%
improvement over raw EEG signals alone.

•

Enhancing Navigation Recall with Wearable-based Visual Cues:
In a separate study with
25
participants, we quantitatively show that
Memento
surpasses
six
baselines in both navigation retrace recall and cognitive load reduction. Specifically,
Memento
improves recall accuracy by
\approx{(8-12)\%}
compared to a computer vision-based approach and
\approx{(20-23)\%}
over no memory aid. Moreover,
Memento
yields the lowest PAAS and NASA TLX scores for perceived cognitive load and reduces review time by
46%
compared to the CV-based approach. Additionally, the proposed physiological-driven approach achieves a significant reduction in processing complexity, requiring
3.86 seconds
compared to
15.35 seconds
per user session, resulting in
\approx

75%
reduction.

Related Work

This section summarizes the relevant literature in contrast to
Memento

(i)
memory augmentation using pervasive sensing technologies and physiological sensing, memory and cognition in ubiquitous computing. We mainly focus on the aspect of our
Memento
approach that differentiates from the state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods.

Memory Augmentation using Pervasive Sensing Technologies.
The rise of pervasive sensing (e.g., smartphones, wearable cameras) has spurred research on memory augmentation
(
Allé et al., 2017
;
Davies et al., 2015
;
Dingler et al., 2021
;
Silva et al., 2013
;
Li et al., 2019
;
Kandappu et al., 2021
)
. Davies et al.
(
Davies et al., 2015
)
highlight privacy risks of “total recall” and potential memory manipulation. Dingler et al.
(
Dingler et al., 2021
)
provide design guidelines for camera-based lifelogging, emphasizing positioning, logging, and summarization. While focused on episodic memory, they underscore cueing’s role in reinforcement, central to our approach. Silva et al.
(
Silva et al., 2013
)
show that reviewing lifelogged data (e.g., SenseCam) enhances neurophysiological assessments beyond episodic memory, surpassing journaling. Kandappu et al.
(
Kandappu et al., 2021
)
explore marker-based tracking for memory support in older adults, addressing privacy-preserving lifelogging. Existing studies predominantly target
long-term memory
(e.g., autobiographical, episodic) via camera-based methods, raising privacy concerns. To our knowledge, this work is among the first to (a) enhance short-term memory, specifically working memory, and (b) leverage physiological sensing to capture ERP cues for short-term memory augmentation.

Physiological Sensing, Memory, and Cognition in Ubiquitous Computing.
Wearable-based physiological sensing has been extensively studied in ubiquitous computing, particularly for applications such as emotion recognition in lifelogging
(
Jiang et al., 2019
)
, assessing emotional climate in classrooms
(
Gashi et al., 2018
;
Di Lascio et al., 2018
)
, and promoting well-being
(
Di Lascio, 2018
)
. Chwalek et al.
(
Chwalek et al., 2021
)
have developed sensor integration frameworks to support large-scale user studies. While recent studies have explored cognitive processes
(
Niforatos et al., 2017
;
Gashi et al., 2019
)
, cognition-based interventions remain underexplored. Similar to our work in identifying high/low cognitive load through physiological sensing, Prompto
(
Chan et al., 2022
)
opportunistically delivers prompts when users exhibit lower cognitive load, optimizing learning and memory retention. Bulling and Roggen
(
Bulling and Roggen, 2011
)
demonstrated the feasibility of recognizing visual memory recall using Electrooculography (EOG) features to distinguish between familiar and unfamiliar images. Our work is the first to extract visual cues from physiological signals to enhance visuospatial working memory recall during navigation tasks.

3.1.
Signal Pre-processing

Electroencephalogram (EEG,
\mathcal{E}^{R,C}
).
EEG is a widely used non-invasive neuroimaging modality in cognitive neuroscience
(
Luo et al., 2018
)
. However, COTS EEG devices with sparse electrodes often suffer from degraded sensing fidelity and connectivity
(
Niso et al., 2023
)
. Recent studies
(
Ramakrishnan and Satyanarayana, 2016
)
indicate that EEG channels exhibit high cross-correlation, known as
functional connectivity
. Leveraging this insight and motivated by
(
Antar et al., 2023
;
Price and Bidelman, 2021
)
, we employ a channel-wise functional connectivity-based approach to reconstruct
enhanced
EEG signals from low-density devices, improving their ability to capture high-attention episodes related to cognitive and ERP events. To synchronize EEG data with other modalities, we downsample the sampling frequency from 128 Hz to 32 Hz using a decimation approach. The decimation factor is computed as:
\frac{Org_{fs}}{Tar_{fs}}
, where
Org_{fs}
and
Tar_{fs}
denote the original (128 Hz) and target (32 Hz) sampling frequencies, respectively. To mitigate aliasing, a Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of
0.5\times Tar_{fs}
Hz (14 Hz) is applied before decimation, preventing temporal shifts and signal distortion. The cutoff frequency is normalized using
\frac{Tar_{fs}}{Org_{fs}}
, and a filter order of 4 is employed, assuming the EEG signal is band-limited below the Nyquist frequency.

Reconstruction:
We perform Independent Component Analysis (ICA)
(
Stone, 2002
)
, commonly used for Blind Source Separation (BSS), on the filtered signals, assuming high-density electrode availability during training. ICA separates incoming signals into their respective sources by assuming statistical independence and non-Gaussianity. To evaluate the reconstructed signals, we compute the cosine similarity
(
Birvinskas et al., 2012
;
Niknazar et al., 2018
)
(Eq.
1
) between the transformed ICA matrix (
t
) and the subset of EEG signals (
e
) from the low-density device. The reconstruction effectiveness is demonstrated in Figure
3
, highlighting
Memento
’s capability to separate high-variability noise and preserve the underlying temporal representations of EEG signals.

Galvanic Skin Response (GSR,
\mathcal{G}^{R,C}
).
GSR measures changes in skin conductance, providing insights into autonomic nervous system activity and emotional arousal, which correlate with cognitive performance deterioration
(
Posada-Quintero et al., 2017
;
Posada-Quintero and Chon, 2019
)
. Motivated by this, we extract the
phasic
component of the Skin Conductance (SC) signal for downstream event detection. First, we remove noise by applying a
Butterworth band-pass filter
(0.1–5 Hz). Then, we employ the CvxEDA algorithm
(
Greco et al., 2015
)
, a convex optimization method, to decompose the SC signal into
phasic
,
tonic
, and
white noise
components. The phasic component captures fluctuations linked to cognitive and emotional states (e.g., arousal, cognitive workload)
(
Bradley and Lang, 2000
;
Gashi et al., 2020
)
. As illustrated in Fig.
2
,
Skin Conductance Response (SCR) peaks
mark heightened activation phases, while
SCR onsets
indicate the initial rise from baseline. The
tonic component
represents the baseline skin conductance level, whereas the
white noise component
accounts for measurement artifacts. Additionally,
SCR half-recovery
marks the point where skin conductance has decreased halfway between the peak and baseline, reflecting the recovery phase post-arousal.

Photoplethysmograph (PPG,
\mathcal{P}^{R,C}
).
PPG enables the extraction of key physiological parameters, including heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate
(
Lei et al., 2020
)
. The PPG signal reflects pulsatile blood volume changes in response to incident light, with
peaks
and
valleys
corresponding to distinct cardiovascular events. The waveform consists of the
systolic peak
, representing maximal blood volume during heart contraction, and the
diastolic notch
, indicating minimal blood volume during relaxation. Prior studies have shown that PPG can effectively diagnose acute stress in high-stress environments
(
Wright et al., 2014
)
. Motivated by this, we adopt the approach in
(
Elgendi et al., 2013
)
to extract time-series features such as heart rate and PPG peaks/valleys. The goal is to estimate
systolic blood pressure (SBP)
, which is known to be influenced by visuospatial cognitive tasks
(
Swan et al., 1998
)
. To extract peaks, we apply a
Chebyshev-II 4
th
order band-pass filter
(0.1–5 Hz) to remove noise, following
(
Liang et al., 2018
)
. Figure
3
compares raw and filtered PPG signals, where detected peaks reflect blood pressure variations during stimuli, providing a refined representation of pulse waveforms.

3.3.
Event-related Potentials (ERPs) Episodes Extraction

Next, we apply change point detection (CPD)
(
Aminikhanghahi and Cook, 2017
)
to identify abrupt changes in the time series data when a property of the signal shifts. We adopt a sliding window-based CPD approach for
Memento
due to its lower complexity and competitive performance on similar time series tasks
(
Silva et al., 2021
)
(see Section
5.2.1
for runtime performance). Mathematically, sliding window CPD is defined using a cost function
C(y)_{i}=\sum_{t=i}||y_{i}-\overline{y_{i}}||_{1}
, where
({y_{t}})_{t}
represents the input signal, and the discrepancy function

d(y_{u,v},y_{v,w})=C(y_{u,w})-C(y_{u,v})-C(y_{v,w})
measures the cost gain when splitting the subsignal
y_{u...w}
at the index
v
. If the statistical properties of the sliding windows
u...v
and
v...w
are similar, the discrepancy is low; otherwise, a significant discrepancy indicates a change point. Sliding window CPD is widely used for non-stationary data to detect abrupt changes
(
Naqvi et al., 2020
;
Chu, 1995
)
. However, EEG signals are prone to artifacts such as muscle movements, blinks of the eyes, electrode interference, and environmental noise, which can distort the signal and affect the reliability of the analysis
(
Sadiya et al., 2021
)
.

To mitigate artifacts and effectively capture ERP episodes, we employ Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT), which decomposes EEG signals into wavelet functions for joint time-frequency analysis. CWT is particularly effective in detecting transient events such as ERPs and identifying artifacts localized within specific time segments
(
Kumar et al., 2021
;
Qassim et al., 2013
)
. We utilize the Morlet wavelet for its optimal time-frequency localization, ability to capture oscillatory components, superior frequency selectivity, adaptability to nonstationary signals, and proven utility in ERP detection, with the CWT defined as:
\frac{1}{\sqrt{|a|}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{Fus}_{\theta}^{R,C}(t)\psi^{*}\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right)dt
.

The ERP extraction approach integrates continuous wavelet transform (CWT) with sliding window CPD, as defined in Eq.
3
. Here,
t_{i}=i\cdot\Delta t
represents the start time,
\Delta t
is the step size,
\text{CWT}_{i}(a,b)
denotes the CWT of the
i
-th window,
w
is the window length, and
x(t)
is the signal. This method leverages sliding window’s localized analysis and wavelet transformation’s multi-resolution capabilities for EEG signal reconstruction. A grid search optimized the window size for ERP extraction, exploring durations from 0.1 to 1.0 seconds, with an optimal window size of
0.75
seconds. Due to varying response delays across physiological modalities, resulting in change points or peaks occurring at different times, the sliding window approach effectively addressed this challenge.

Instead, we evaluate recall accuracy by comparing first-person views captured during the experiments, following prior image-based localization approaches
(
Kosecka et al., 2003
)

(a) Case 1
—Pixel intensity similarity between image views at identical time instants across original and re-traced routes, and
(b) Case 2

Case 1
, we use the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)
(
Wang et al., 2004
)
and Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD)
(
Rubner et al., 2000
)
to robustly assess visual similarity and
Case 2
, we apply the FastDTW algorithm
(
Salvador and Chan, 2007
)
, a DTW-based method that accounts for temporal misalignment while preserving spatial accuracy.

Assessment of Reviewing Load.

While our results indicate that the physiological sensing-based approach performs as well as, or better than, the computer vision-based approach in the route recall task, we further assess the
efficiency
of the cueing process by analyzing the reviewing load. Given the strict 1-minute review limit, we compare the number of frames selected for cueing by both the CV-based and
Memento
-EGP approaches. At a 67.7% memorability threshold, the CV-based approach selects
71–76%
of the total frames from the original explorations, amounting to approximately
435–478
frames per episode. In contrast, the
Memento
framework, leveraging pre-processed multimodal sensors (
Memento-EGP
) and EEG sensors only (
Memento-EEG
), selects significantly fewer frames—approximately
228–257
and
295–324
frames, respectively. This reduction corresponds to a
16–46%
decrease compared to the CV-based approach. As discussed in Section
5.2.1
, these findings align with participant ratings in the post-study questionnaire.

Furthermore, employing the CV-based approach (AmNet) for memory recall reveals a notable disparity in the retrieval of relevant information, as shown in Fig.
11
(b) and (c). The comparison indicates that humans recall fine-grained, specific details, whereas the CV-based AmNet system, despite its efficiency, integrates both relevant and irrelevant information, potentially leading to cognitive overload misaligned with human memory priorities. Our model
personalizes
memory recall by prioritizing salient features and cue objects tailored to individual user preferences. In contrast, the CV-based AmNet system follows a generalized approach, presenting broad, non-personalized information that lacks human-centric relevance. This fundamental difference aligns
Memento
with users’ selective attention and memory patterns—focusing on meaningful events rather than overwhelming them with non-personalized, generic semantic cues, as shown in Fig.
11
(b). As a result, AmNet tends to
increase cognitive load
, i.e., perceived load during memory retrieval, whereas
Memento
effectively reduces review effort and cognitive load by capturing and leveraging user-specific cues.

While our primary focus has been on establishing the feasibility of using COTS physiological sensors for memory augmentation, the choice of sensing modalities significantly impacts system performance. To evaluate computational efficiency, we compared the runtime of our

Memento

Memento

75%

Key Takeaways:

The findings from Study 2 demonstrate that visual cueing significantly enhances navigation task recall. While EEG-based and CV-based approaches showed comparable performance, integrating additional physiological modalities (GSR and PPG) further improved cueing effectiveness. Notably, participants rated the physiological sensing-based approach more favorably, indicating a lower perceived cognitive load, as it enabled the capture of more
personalized cues
compared to other methods.

6.
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed and evaluated a first-of-its-kind system which extracts moments or highlights from a user’s short-term episodes to cue those important moments during visuospatial working memory tasks, intending to improve the memory recall of those moments. Through a system implementation and two separate user-studies, we establish that improving one’s recall with intelligent cueing is possible during visual search and wayfinding navigation. We show that participants receiving visual cues from
Memento
improved route recall by
20–23%
, reduced cognitive load and review time by
46%
, and achieved a
75%
faster computation time (
3.86 secs
vs.
15.35 secs
) compared to vision-based cue selection.

7.
Limitation and Future Directions

In this work, we primarily employed time- and frequency-domain features, however, one limitation of our current approach is the lack of integration of non-linear physiological indices. As part of future work, we plan to incorporate additional non-linear measures
(
Laborde et al., 2017
)
to enable more comprehensive physiological interpretations. Furthermore,
Memento’s
strong empirical results align with retrieval practice
(
Roediger III and Karpicke, 2006
)
, which emphasizes personalized memory augmentation through active recall, and dual-coding
(
Paivio, 1991
)
, which underscores the benefits of combining visual and verbal cues. In future work, we aim to enhance human-computer interaction through immersive environments
(
Han et al., 2021
;
Saeghe et al., 2022
;
Hallgarten et al., 2024
)
, extending beyond navigation to applications in memory recall student learning
(
Laporte et al., 2023
)
and older adults
(
Lindgren et al., 2024
)
.
