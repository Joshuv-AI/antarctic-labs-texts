Efficacy of Liposuction of the Posterior Arm Fat Pad in Axillobrachioplasty after Massive Weigh Loss: A Pilot Study
Authors: Gudjon Leifur Gunnarsson, Linda Regina Gudjonsdottir, Amelia Koidil, Lars Haukeland, Erik Berg, Jorn Bo Thomsen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:The topographic anatomical changes of the upper arms and axilla after massive weight loss (MWL) seem ill-described in the literature. The importance of the axilla in MWL brachioplasty and how the procedure differs from the postmenopausal “batwing” deformity of the upper arms is also unclear. Consideration of the underlying pathology seems imperative for a successful reconstruction. We aim to test a procedure specifically designed to mend the underlying pathology of obesity and MWL, liposuction-assisted axillobrachioplasty (LAAB).Methods:A prospective pilot study was designed to test the effect of LAAB on the axilla and evaluate the results comparative to patient’s preoperative complaints and well-being and allow for adjustments for future trials. We measured the diameter of the arm and axilla at three specific points, A, B and C, and applied the arm module of BODY-Q regarding body perception and psychosocial well-being.Results:Fifteen women, for a total of 30 arms, were included. Age average was 48 years (29–59) and showed a significant improvement in all measurements A–C, most significantly at point A in the axilla, where the reduction was associated with relief of preoperative ailments and great improvement on BODY-Q ratings. The procedure was uncomplicated, with high patient satisfaction.Conclusions:LAAB significantly improves the axilla and upper arm contour after MWL and correlates strongly with improvement of preoperative ailments. The study furthermore supports the clinical relevance of a posterior arm fat pad. The presented procedure seems to be uncomplicated and reproducible with a high patient satisfaction warranting a long-term clinical trial

Takeaways

Question: Does liposuction of the posterior arm fat pad (PAFP) and axillobrachioplasty restore the axilla and improve preoperative complaints after massive weigh loss (MWL)?

Findings: Reduction of the PAFP with liposuction ensures a successful axillobrachioplasty with significant reduction of the circumference of the axilla and upper arm, as well as improvement of preoperative ailments measured with BODY-Q.

Meaning: The PAFP contributes to the descent of the axilla after MWL, and its removal with liposuction together with axillobrachioplasty reconstructs the axilla and improves patients’ quality of life.

INTRODUCTION

There is a well-known variation in the size and shape of patients’ upper arms after massive weight loss (MWL). The changes of brachioptosis, according to Lockwood, are the result of a combined pull of heavy upper arm fat on the skin and weakening of the connective tissue.1 The demand for brachioplasty increased dramatically (4473%) in the past 2 decades in the United States, coinciding with a surge in obesity and MWL.2 We have noticed that brachioptosis in our patients with MWL differs from our non-MWL patients with greater affection of the axilla, posterior axillary fold in particular and greater physical burden. It seems logical how a constant load of heavy fat of the posterior arm and shoulder pulls down the skin of the upper arms and posterior axillary fold. Together with decreasing connective tissue quality, the continuum creates an irreversibly sagging axilla and ptotic upper arm skin (Fig. 1). The extent of each contributor is unknown, and limited research supports the underlying etiology of the various topographic changes of the axilla and upper arms observed after MWL.

An underlying hormonal sensitivity and genetic predisposition seems to be evident despite lacking systematic reference; a majority (98%) of patients seeking operative treatment are women.2,3 The female predominance coheres with a frequent occurrence of the deep fat pad of the posterior upper arms, the posterior arm fat pad (PAFP), a condition rarely seen in men, unless previously severely obese or with the condition of benign symmetric lipomatosis or Madelung disease.4 Surgeons who deal with liposuction and upper arm contouring are aware of the clinical presence of a PAFP (Figs. 2, 3).5,6 The recurrent heavy PAFP is often responsible for residual ptosis of the arms and is one of the leading cause of complaints of insufficient results after brachioplasty, shortly after unsightly scarring.3

Removal of the PAFP may be essential to avoid recurrence of arm ptosis and complaints of insufficient results after brachioplasty.7 Body contouring surgery of any region is linked to increased quality of life and health benefits including aid of sustainable weight loss, although the specific impact of separate body regions remains to be investigated.8,9

The purpose of this study is to test if liposuction of the PAFP in combination with a medial liposuction/avulsion-based brachioplasty is feasible and effective in treating ptosis of the upper arm and posterior axillary fold after MWL, and second, to evaluate the impact of the treatment on the patients’ quality of life.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We constructed a feasibility study to test the efficacy and safety of specific liposuction of the PAFP together with correction of ptosis of the lax tissue of the upper arm and axilla and measure the physical burden of topographic anatomical changes before and after the operation, using patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs).

Patients were included consecutively after oral and written approval by all participants from June 2021 until July 2023 at 2 affiliated hospitals in Norway. Patients who were already awaiting treatment for symptomatic burden of loose skin and residual fat of their upper arms after MWL were invited to participate according to the inclusion criteria set by Norwegian national guidelines for treatment after MWL.10 MWL refers to a significant weight loss of a morbidly obese individual causing fat and skin redundancy of clinical significance.11 Exclusion criteria were patients with non-MWL, psychiatric illness, or ongoing pregnancy. The sample size was set around 10%–20% of a full-scale study. Participants had preoperative evaluation and received information about the procedure, possible complications, and expected surgical outcome. Those who agreed to participate signed a consent for their participation and publication including clinical photographs and video documentation that were used to make pre- and postoperative comparisons. Medical history and clinical examination were registered in the patient medical chart. Measurements of the diameters of the upper arm were assessed in three different locations starting at the axilla and outward. The diameter of the most proximal location, closest to the axilla, is referred to as point A. The diameter of the central upper arm is referred to as point B. The diameter of the distal location, at the level of the elbow crease is referred to as point C (Fig. 4A).

All patients filled out a standardized body perception and psychosocial well-being questionnaire, BODY-Q, standardized Danish version.12 The patients were followed up for an average of 6 months postoperative (range 4–12 months). Repetition of the standardized questionnaire along with measurements/ABC and information on any complications were gathered. The study was conducted according to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Operative Procedure: Liposuction-assisted Axillobrachioplasty

The arms were marked with a permanent marker with the arms abducted and flexed at the elbow before surgery and measurements at points A, B, and C were registered in a standing posture. We estimated and outlined the medial scar and amount of safe medial resection, axillary dome, and PAFP. Our medial scar is placed at the lower border of the bicipital groove. A single dose of cefuroxime 1.5 g was administered intravenously preoperatively. In general anesthesia, the arms and axilla were infiltrated with a solution of normal saline with 1 mg adrenaline and 40 mg lidocaine one arm at a time. Approximately 500–1000 mL was used for each arm but varied according to the volume of the arm. The PAFP was removed by liposuction performed through an incision of the posterior arm with the arm flexed and extended for accessibility (Fig. 5). We used a 5-mm three-hole “Mercedes-type” cannula connected to a wall mount suction, and care was taken to leave the subdermal fat intact. The liposuction then commenced subcutaneously in the medial aspect of the arm for removal of the skin, taking care not to undermine or liposuction the skin outside of intended resection. The axilla and posterior shoulder were also liposuctioned through an incision inferior to the axilla, and additional skin was removed in the axilla as needed. The skin was removed by scalpel and diathermy and is easily dissected due to the plane created by liposuction, sparing underlying lymphatics and nerves. Hemostasis was secured, and tranexamic acid 500 mg diluted with 10 mL 0.9% saline solution and Marcaine-adrenaline 2.5 mg/mL/5 µg/mL was applied topically on the wound before its immediate closure. The skin was adapted with towel clamps and sutured in 2 layers with resorbable 2.0 braided polyglactin and a 3.0 running poliglecaprone monofilament. Tape, resorbable dressing, and compression bandage Coban2 lite were applied from wrist up and left for 1–3 days and then replaced by elastic sleeves, worn for 4 weeks day and night followed by 4 weeks daytime thereafter. RESULTS

We performed the axillobrachioplasty using liposuction of the PAFP in combination with medial skin/tissue removal using an avulsion technique in 15 patients with an average age of 48 years (29–59 years). The median follow-up was 6 months (range: 4–12 months).

Patient parameters are presented in Table 1 and results in Table 2: the mean height is 167 cm (152–181 cm); weight, 71.9 kg (44–94 kg); median body mass index (BMI), 24 kg/m2 (19–32.9 kg/m2); and pre–weight loss BMI, 42 kg/m2 (39–52 kg/m2). Four patients were overweight at the time of operation (27%). All weights were stable over a period of 2 years after MWL. Eleven (67%) patients had gastric bypass surgery, 5 (33%) had a gastric sleeve operation, and 1 patient had both gastric bypass and gastric sleeve surgery. Ten (67%) patients were free of comorbidity and risks. Three patients had diabetes mellitus and 3 had hypertension. Eight (53%) patients were postmenopausal at the date of surgery, and none had hormone replacement therapy. Three patients had a history of pregnancy and normal delivery. Three patients smoked cigarettes, and 1 patient had connective tissue disorder.

DISCUSSION

All 15 women showed clinical improvement from their preoperative condition after the operation with high rated satisfaction. The procedure results in a significant reduction of the axilla, point A (P = 0.000002194) and median arm, point B (P = 0.001541886). However, the most distal point C does not change significantly (P = 0.300769968).

Patients with the lowest amount of aspirated fat had lesser reduction in axillary and mid arm circumference, points A and B; they included the patients with lowest postoperative weight and the youngest patients with better elasticity. There was otherwise no significant correlation with BMI, maximal weight, or amount of weight loss. Although not statistically significant, this might be indicative of minimal residual fat coinciding with the Lockwood theory that ptosis of the arms is exaggerated by age and overweight based on underlying degeneration of the superficial fascia structure.

MWL Progeria

The topographical changes of MWL include weakened connective tissue attachments of the skin and fat, resembling premature aging (Fig. 1). The mean age in our series is 48 years (29–59 years) and concurs with the median of published studies [48 (20–75)].3 Fifty-three percent of the enrolled patients were postmenopausal, reflecting that the burden of loose skin of the arms increases with age and deterioration of connective tissue. A deteriorating soft tissue support is a major reason for descending skin and development of deep skinfolds that are associated with aging. We postulate that MWL escalates the age-related changes of skin and subcutaneous tissue due to the apparent visual resemblance.

Posterior Arm Fat Pad

The results furthermore suggest a correlation of aspirated fat from the PAFP and measured improvement of the axilla. This emphasizes the importance of residual fat in the topographic appearance of the axilla and upper arm ptosis. In 1975, Testut and Jacob5 made a distinction between fibrous areolar superficial fat and laminal deep subfascial fat separated by the superficial fascia. Avelar6 reported that the superficial fibrotic areolar layer is less forgiving to injury by liposuction compared with the deep laminar layer and pointed out clinically relevant examples of overdeveloped deep laminar fat-pads in certain areas, among these the deep fat of the posterior arm (PAFP).

This study accentuates the importance of addressing the axilla and posterior axillary fold along with any residual fat of the posterior arm of patients with MWL. We hold the posterior axillary fold and upper arm to be an inseparable anatomical unit that requires extensive deconstruction with removal of underlying pathology before reconstruction. This marks a clear shift from non-MWL treatment that trends toward minimally invasive brachial contouring procedures, which aim to minimize visible scarring and are indicative of different etiologies. The choice of procedure must therefore consider the presence or absence of a descendent posterior axillary fold and residual PAFP.

Patient-reported Outcome Measure

The PROM did not reflect the magnitude of ailments; every patient had preoperative complaints, ranging from psychosocial to physical issues such as intertriginous eczema, embarrassing sounds from their armpits and challenges to find fitting clothes. The results seemed unrelated to the magnitude of tissue removed or improvement of the measurements.

The BODY-Q results were unanimous and showed a clear contrast of preoperative and postoperative satisfaction, from a median preoperative score of 9 (7 is a minimum) to a general satisfaction with the operative results (26 of 28). All 15 patients felt that they had accomplished their goals with the operation and rated their overall satisfaction 9.93 on a scale of 1–10.

Weakness and Strength

Despite the limited power of a small number of patients and short-term follow-up, the results seem supportive of the clinical relevance of the PAFP and warrant future investigations. Addition of a control group and randomization would strengthen its value.

We applied a Danish version of the BODY-Q for our Norwegian patients; a Norwegian module has not been calibrated. This did not cause any confusion with any of the participants. Written Norwegian (Bokmål) is essentially the same as written Danish with minor spelling differences. The measured values of points A, B, and C indicate a standard error of measurement of ±1 cm.

Where Do Our Results Fit in the Context of Current Literature?

Brachioplasty was designed in the 1950s to lift the sagging upper arm skin of “respectable women,” hence the stigmatizing term “bingo wings.”13 The surge of MWL procedures contributed to a rise in the demand for brachioplasty, and still only 2% of the patients are men.2 Our patients were included on a “first come, first served” basis to avoid selection bias, and were all women, reflecting the gender predisposition to seek treatment for clinically significant axillobrachial ptosis after MWL.

The LAAB approach has been adapted over the years, initially as a secondary procedure for recurrent ptosis. It is inspired by the Hurwitz L-shaped brachioplasty without the Lockwood deltopectoral fascia attachment, and Hunstad skin-avulsion with the addition of systematic debulking of the posterior arm to lighten the heavy skin flap.14,15 Our findings of improvement of the posterior axillary fold concur with those of Bianchi et al,16 who measured the effect postoperatively using Photoshop.

The PROM results resonate with the satisfactory findings of Symbas and Losken17 despite different approaches. We used a medial scar, whereas the posterior approach is largely favored in the United States and has the advantage of resecting the heavy PAFP directly while avoiding the medial neurovascular structures and lymphatics altogether. We postulate that a similarly high PROM despite different approaches reflects the burden of the underlying problem and importance of treatment after MWL.

Complications were modest in this series: skin breakdown due to suture spitting, observed and treated conservatively in 1 patient, and no seroma or hematoma. The literature is mainly concerned with scarring, fibrous bands, and sensory disturbances.3,7,9,18

The safety of liposuction of the arms along with brachioplasty has been debated in the past. Although Bossert et al18 asserted the safety to perform liposuction along with brachioplasty skin resection in 2013, they had a high overall rate of complications, 20% in both groups indicating technical challenges and/or challenging patients. Additionally, they found slightly higher rates of scar dehiscence and lymphedema in the liposuction group, although not statistically significant. In a recent meta-analysis by Aljerian et al,7 brachioplasty complications were 32.9% overall, and liposuction-assisted complications were somewhat lower than 29.5% and found to reduce the incidence of seroma, lymphedema, and neural injury significantly. BMI greater than 30 kg/m2 had the highest complication rate at 54.5%. There seems to be room for improvement if this is indicative of the current practice. Superficial liposuction has been shown to create a dissection plane that spares the medial brachial cutaneous nerve, along with most of the lymphatics, and this concurs with our experience.16

The results of BODY-Q were unanimous in terms of satisfaction with the treatment. Each aspect of the questionnaire improved after the procedure, and none felt worse afterward. The lowest overall score was how patients think about the appearance of their arms when they are not covered and appearance when arms are raised, indicative of the stigmatizing scar.

Patients who rated their arms higher preoperatively tended to have lower ratings after the procedure, indicating a more modest change. Vigen et al19 presented a similar trend in a retrospective cohort of patients with MWL after thighplasty, the lesser the patient’s preoperative ailments, the lesser the postoperative satisfaction and, in particular, the dissatisfaction with scarring and uncovered appearance of their thighs.

SUMMARY

This article has addressed the physical burden of topographic anatomical changes of the axilla and upper arms in a small group of patients with MWL. We tested and confirmed the safety of the LAAB and measured the effect of the procedure on general well-being and improved appearance after the operation with BODY-Q. The operative findings seem indicative of the existence of PAFP, and the results support its potential clinical impact. A possible correlation with PAFP and sagging of the axilla links the patient’s clinical burden to visible topographic anatomical changes of the upper arms and axilla after MWL and the absence of relation to BMI or weight loss indicates that the extent of topographical changes cannot be explained by obesity alone.

Despite the improvement of a preoperative ailment shown by the operation, we have yet to define a clear criterion for what is considered abnormal or pathologic (L98.7 excessive skin/tissue; especially after MWL). We believe that future classifications should consider the volume of the PAFP to be of similar impact as the significance of the central abdominal pannus and breast hypertrophy for reduction and abdominoplasty. Based on the results of this pilot study, an anatomical dissection and a prospective clinical trial with a non-MWL cohort of patients seem feasible.

CONCLUSIONS

Thorough liposuction of the PAFP in combination with a medial liposuction/avulsion-based brachioplasty is feasible and effective in treating ptosis of the upper arm and posterior axillary fold after MWL. Removal of the pathologic tissue facilitates a satisfying reconstruction that improves body perception measured with BODY-Q.
