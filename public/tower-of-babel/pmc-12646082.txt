Civic Education as Community Capacity Building for Primary Care
Authors: Rike Erlande, Bayu Prasetio
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Primary care increasingly confronts problems that extend beyond the clinical encounter and into the civic life of communities. Schools and other community institutions now act as arenas where public knowledge, social norms, and health behaviours are negotiated, and they therefore constitute an under used lever for strengthening primary care outcomes. Empirical reviews indicate that community engagement initiatives and community oriented primary care models improve service relevance and uptake, while supportive educational settings enhance mental wellbeing among marginalised youth. These findings imply that integrating civic education with primary care strategy could strengthen health literacy, community agency, and trust in primary services [1,2].

Civic education, understood as the cultivation of deliberative skills, civic responsibility, and respect for pluralism, offers practical competencies that can be transferred to health contexts. Democratic school climates foster skills such as civic discussion, procedural fairness, and collective problem-solving, all of which underpin community capacity to interpret health information, participate in local decision-making, and sustain public health actions [3,4]. Embedding these competencies within community-oriented primary care frameworks enables primary care teams to partner with schools and civic groups in ways that amplify health literacy and collective action [5–7].

Several pragmatic entry points exist for primary care. First, primary care curricula and continuing professional development should include modules on community facilitation, participatory communication, and methods for co-producing health information with citizens. Second, primary care quality frameworks should recognise civic engagement capacities such as convening local dialogues or supporting school based civic health literacy as legitimate service outcomes alongside clinical metrics. Third, primary care teams could pilot structured collaborations with schools for targeted problems such as vaccine uptake, mental health promotion, or combating misinformation, using iterative evaluation to measure both clinical and civic outcomes. Reviews of community oriented primary care and community engagement show that locally tailored and evaluated interventions yield measurable benefits when health services work with community structures rather than around them [8–10].

There are legitimate concerns about role boundaries. Schools cannot and should not substitute for clinical judgement or family authority on sensitive health matters. Procedural neutrality, defined as facilitating informed discussion without imposing medical decisions, is therefore essential, as is shared jurisdiction between educators, clinicians, and families. This precaution protects institutional legitimacy and ensures appropriate referral to clinical or social services when needs exceed educational remit. Evidence on community health literacy indicates that interventions are most effective when they respect local norms while progressively strengthening individuals’ ability to act on health information [11,12].

For Education for Primary Care readers, the practical implication is clear: primary care leaders should treat civic education and school partnerships as components of population health strategy rather than desirable extras. Short term projects that embed civic facilitation into routine practice can be evaluated rapidly and scaled when effective. In particular, evaluating outcomes that combine health indicators (for example, vaccination coverage and service utilisation) with civic indicators (for example, community participation in health planning and measures of health literacy) will help build the evidence base necessary to mainstream this approach. Policy makers and educators should consider pilot funding for joint health education initiatives that explicitly target both clinical and civic outcomes.

In summary, applying a civic education lens reframes primary care engagement with communities from transactional service delivery to capacity building for collective health action. By investing in school health partnerships that develop deliberative skills, procedural fairness, and civic health literacy, primary care can enhance resilience, improve health equity, and secure broader community support for evidence based interventions. Educators, clinicians, and service leaders are encouraged to initiate pragmatic pilots and share findings through platforms such as Education for Primary Care to inform wider implementation.
