Proteome of Bronchial Alveolar Lavage fluid and Serum from Tuberculosis patients during drug treatment
Authors: Luisa Maria Nieto Ramirez, Karen M. Dobos, Annie Moradian, Anandi Bierman, Rao Shruti, Eric Osei, Marisa Harton, Dylan Kain, David Lewinsohn, John T. Belisle, Jennifer Van Eyk, Gerhard Walzl, Nelita Du Plessis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
People with tuberculosis (TB) undergo months of drug treatment before cure. Recent studies suggest that, even after successful treatment, TB patients may not fully clear the bacterium and be at risk for disease recurrence due to reactivation of resident bacilli. In this study, we conducted an analysis of the proteome of ten TB patients at week 1 after initiation of therapy and after twenty-five weeks of therapy, and also in 10 healthy community controls to identify dynamic and stable protein patterns in the lung and blood. We used bronchial alveolar lavage fluid (BALf) and serum from each TB patient at both time points for our analyses and identified 2,060 and 416 proteins in the BALf and serum samples, respectively, from at least 60% of the samples from each group. Six proteins shared significant trends between the two sample types. Data are available via ProteomeXchange PXD073337 for comparative studies and include a unique, paired BALf proteome data set in addition to a paired serum sample set from the same study subjects.

Background & Summary

Tuberculosis (TB), a pulmonary disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) is the deadliest infectious disease of humans and remains one of the top ten causes of death1. Effective drug regimens for the control and cure of TB have been available and successfully used for over 70 years. However, despite improved drug regimens, global TB transmission rates have shown little decline over the past 25 years. Comorbidities, such as HIV infection, malnutrition, and diabetes, may explain transmission resilience; however, delayed diagnosis with ongoing transmission is considered the predominant reason TB transmission rates have not declined. The major challenge presented for early diagnosis of TB is that most people infected with Mtb do not continue to active disease at onset, are asymptomatic, and represent over a quarter of the global population. The disease life cycle is not a linear progression from infection to asymptomatic disease to active disease, further complicating treatment decisions2–4. In the study “Lung Resident, MR1-restricted T cells: Association with differential outcomes following exposure to M. tuberculosis”, subjects were recruited to study specific immunological phenotypes and the collection of multiple sample types at onset of active disease and the end of treatment was included as part of the study plan. These samples were collected for multi-omics studies and for the identification of mechanisms and/or biomarkers associated with non-sterilizing cure or risk of disease recurrence. This descriptor provides the data set obtained from proteomic analyses of the serum and BALf samples from this study. An examination of additional data repositories and publications was performed to inform whether similar data sets were available for comparative analysis. We chose to query proteome analysis of BALf from TB patients (Table 1), as serum proteome datasets are abundant. One proteomic data set is available and includes the BALf proteome from five TB patients, four of which were at end-of-treatment, and one was a TB relapse5. No similar dataset for paired timepoints from TB patients’ BALf nor paired serum and BALf proteomic analyses was found. Methods

Study design

Subjects were recruited as part of a study to define the MR1 Restricted T cell phenotype in the lower airways and periphery. This proteomics dataset was generated from samples collected from n = 12 females and n = 8 males between the ages 18–47years (average: 34 years) who were living without HIV infection in a high TB endemic setting.

A multi-omics sub-study was included along with the primary study aim. Peripheral blood and bronchial alveolar lavage fluid (BALf) samples were collected from individuals with active TB (pre- and post-treatment) as well as healthy community controls, as previously described6. All controls were positive for the interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA+), indicating T cell responses consistent with prior exposure to Mtb antigens. Ethics approval

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee (N18/10/118) and Biological and Environmental Safety Committee (BES-2023-23871) at Stellenbosch University. Written informed consent for participation and data sharing was obtained from all participants.

Obtaining BALf

Bronchoscopies with BAL were performed according to current recommendations7,8 in a research bronchoscopy facility (SU-IRG Biomedical Research Unit, Stellenbosch University). In brief, all participants were pre-screened for fitness for bronchoscopy according to predefined criteria by a study clinician with knowledge of the procedure. Controls were excluded from this study if they presented with a cough of any duration, fever or used antibiotics in the past 4 weeks before the bronchoscopy. Prior to the bronchoscopy, chest X-ray and TB sputum testing were performed. No lung parenchymal abnormalities were observed in controls, and all control participants tested negative for TB by sputum GeneXpert Ultra and liquid culture. The bronchoscope was targeted to lung regions affording ease of accessibility and the lavage was performed by instilling sterile saline solution at 37 °C up to a maximum volume of 240 mL in aliquots of 60 mL at a time, with aspiration between aliquots. Aspirated fluid was collected in sterile 50 mL polypropylene tubes and transported on ice to the laboratory.

Sample preparation

BALf were centrifuged at 400 × g to exclude the cell fraction and peripheral blood in serum separator tubes centrifuged at 1500 × g to collect serum. Samples were frozen at −80 °C until use. Upon thawing, samples were treated with a chloroform:methanol solution (2:1, v/v) to inactivate any residual Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) prior to mass spectrometry processing. BALf and serum samples were mixed with the inactivation solution at sample-to-solution ratios of 1:5 and 1:31.25, respectively, and incubated overnight. Pilot optimization experiments determined that input volumes of 0.600 mL for BALF and 0.032 mL for serum were optimal for downstream proteomic analysis. Following inactivation, samples were dried under nitrogen and resuspended in a solvent mixture of acetonitrile:methanol (65:35, v/v) with 0.5% formic acid to extract soluble peptides and polar metabolites while precipitating proteins, supporting a unified proteomics–metabolomics processing approach. Samples were incubated in this extracting solution in agitation at room temperature for 10 minutes. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g, the protein pellet was recovered (Fig. 1B).

Protein digestion

Serum protein pellets were suspended in 2% sodium deoxycholate (SDC) and Bicinchoninic Acid assay (BCA) for determining protein concentration in the sample was performed. Based on the results 20 µg of each sample was loaded into a 96-well plate according to a randomized plate layout. Protein digestion was performed on a Beckman i7 automated workstation (Beckman Coulter) using a protocol adapted from Ardle et al., substituting SDC for trifluoroethanol (TFE)9.

BALf protein pellets were similarly suspended in a buffer containing 2% SDC and 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and BCA assay was performed. Due to the low total protein content, 2 µg of each sample was aliquoted into a randomized 96-well plate. Digestion of BALf samples was conducted using the Single-Pot, Solid-Phase-Enhanced Sample Preparation (SP3)10 method on the same automated platform.

Mass spectrometry

LC-MS/MS was performed on a Thermo UltiMate 3000 LC system coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved using reverse phase chromatography and a linear gradient of water with 0.1% formic acid as buffer A and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid as buffer B. Samples were injected and loaded on a C18 column (Phenomenex 15 cm × 300 μm, 100 Å C18 beads), and for both sample types 2 µg of digested peptides were separated over a 60-minute gradient at a flow rate of 9.5 μL/min.

MS/MS data on the Orbitrap were collected with a data-independent acquisition (DIA) method with the following parameters: full MS scans were acquired at 60k orbitrap resolution from 400–1000 Da. Data-independent fragmentation in MS2 was performed over a mass range of 400–1000 Da using 12 Da windows with an overlap of 1 Da. Peptides were fragmented with a normalized collision energy of 28%. Fragmentation spectra were collected at 15k resolution in the orbitrap from 200–2000 Da. All data were acquired with positive polarity in profile mode.

Proteomics data analysis

DIA analysis was conducted using SpectronautTM version 19 (19.0.240606.62635 for serum and 19.1.240724.62635 for BALf samples, Biognosys), with the directDIA + library-free workflow. DIA data from five samples from healthy controls were included to improve precursor and protein identifications in the same directDIA + algorithm. Experiment thresholds were set at ≤ 1% FDR for PSM, peptide and protein identification. Peptide identification was restricted to sequences between 7 and 52 amino acids in length, allowing a maximum of two missed cleavages by trypsin. Five maximum variable modifications included oxidation of methionine and acetylation of protein N-termini, and carbamidomethylation of Cysteine as a fixed modification. Searches were performed against Uniprot reference proteomes for: Homo sapiens (UP000005640_9606, retrieved May 22, 2024) and Mtb H37Rv (UP000001584, retrieved July 22, 2024). Quantification was done using proteotypic peptides only using the MaxLFQ algorithm with cross-run normalization based on RT dependent local regression model described by Callister et al.11.

Statistical analysis

After the directDIA + analysis in SpectronautTM, proteins were filtered to retain only those that were identified with proteotypic peptides and in at least 60% of all samples. A two-step imputation strategy was applied, distinguishing between Missing Not At Random (MNAR) and Missing At Random (MAR) patterns. If a protein was missing in ≥ 66% of the replicates within a group were assumed to be MNAR, likely due to biological absence or expression below detection limits in that condition. MNAR imputation was therefore performed using a left-shifted truncated normal distribution, reflecting the expected low intensity of such proteins in these samples. The imputation was centered at μ - 1.8σ with a standard deviation of 0.3σ, where μ and σ represent the group-wise mean and standard deviation of observed intensities, respectively. MAR proteins (those not classified as MNAR) were subsequently imputed using the random forest method with the “missForest” R package12. After this, data were log2-transformed to perform quantitative analysis using limma differential comparison with multiple contrasts across the three groups or paired t-test to compare samples from Week 1 vs Week 25 from the same patient. For both analyses, Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction for FDR was applied.

Serum samples demonstrated a robust and consistent pattern of proteins and peptides across all samples, even at the pre-filtering stage, as opposed to BALf that showed a more heterogeneous distribution (Fig. 2). After applying the filtering criteria and imputation strategies (Fig. 3), 416 proteins and 2,060 proteins were quantified in serum and BALf, respectively. Further, of the 28 serum and 104 BALf proteins demonstrating significantly different abundance patterns (Supplementary Tables 1, 2), 6 were shared between the two sample types, all of which belonged to the immunoglobulin superfamily (Fig. 4). Principal component analysis of sample data demonstrates a more diverse proteome in TB-infected individuals at the start of treatment compared to controls or the same patients at the end of treatment (Fig. 5). During data quality assessment of the BALf samples, one control sample (BALF_MR144) was found to contain only about 23% of the 2,331 identified proteins across the samples (Fig. 2). This substantial reduction in identified proteins strongly suggested a technical failure or severe sample loss, and the sample was therefore excluded from downstream analyses. This observation also highlights the inherent heterogeneity of BALf as a biological matrix, underscoring the need for standardized collection and normalization protocols. Beyond normalization to total protein content, additional measures such as using urea concentration, an endogenous marker of dilution, in both BALf and matched serum may improve consistency and comparability across studies13.

Technical Validation

Instrument is calibrated weekly according to manufacturer protocol, and it passed the mass accuracy requirements for this instrument. Commercially prepared HeLa cell digest was employed as a system suitability control to evaluate the performance and stability of the LC-MS DIA platform throughout the analytical workflow. HeLa samples were injected at the beginning, middle, and end of each batch to monitor quantitative consistency over time. A panel of 12 HeLa proteins, selected to span the full dynamic range of expression levels—from high to low abundance—was used to assess reproducibility. The intensities of these proteins were tracked across all runs and plotted to visualize platform stability. This approach provides a robust measure of analytical reproducibility across the dynamic range and throughout the duration of the experiment.

Pooled human serum purchased commercially was utilized as a quality control material to evaluate the consistency and reproducibility of the enzymatic digestion process. Each experimental plate included four aliquots of the same pooled serum, distributed across different wells in separate regions of the plate. Because these aliquots were identical, comparing their proteomic profiles enabled assessment of intra-plate variability in digestion efficiency. Protein quantification in these quality control samples was analyzed using the percent coefficient of variation (%CV), a standard metric for reproducibility. In discovery proteomics workflows, it is common practice to evaluate the proportion of identified proteins with a %CV below 30%, which serves as a benchmark for acceptable technical variability. In this study, the %CV values for the two plates were 9% and 17%, indicating high reproducibility of the digestion process across the plates.
