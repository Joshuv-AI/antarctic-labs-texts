Moderate moisture evaporation strengthens the network structure and practical features of konjac glucomannan/soy protein isolate system during drying process
Authors: Wenyao Tu, Xizhong Liu, Kao Wu, Binjia Zhang, Guohua Zhao, Amjad Ali, Fatang Jiang, Dongling Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The effect of thermal radiation drying (TR) and thermal convection drying (TC) methods on drying kinetics and physicochemical properties of konjac glucomannan/soybean protein isolate (KGM/SPI) gels was comparatively evaluated. Upon heating, the shortened molecular distance and the restricted molecular movement ability promoted hydrogen bonding between components and buried hydrophobic groups on SPI, increasing hydrogen bonds and reducing hydrophobic interactions. Compared with TC drying process, the relative moderate drying rates (reflected by smaller drying rate and effective moisture diffusivity Deff) during TR drying favored stronger hydrogen bonding and occurrence of smaller SPI aggregates with concentrated size distribution. These contributed to the formation of a denser and more compact network structure, thus improved mechanical properties and increased their water barrier properties (decreased water vapor permeability and increased water contact angle). They help understanding the drying process of polysaccharide/protein assembly and provide a reference for the rational choosing of drying methods.

Introduction

Assemblies formed by polysaccharides and proteins have a wide range of applications in food systems, such as emulsion (Lopes-da-Silva & Monteiro, 2019), capsules (Xie et al., 2023), gels (Yang et al., 2021), core-shell particles (Yang et al., 2020), and films (Mohamed et al., 2020). In many cases, drying the assembly structure of polysaccharide/protein is required for allocation. For example, zein/Tremella fuciformis polysaccharides gels are dried to prepare the capsule shells (Tian et al., 2021), or pectin/whey protein assembly is spray-dried to fabricate the shell materials of folic acid-loaded core–shell encapsulation system (Assadpour et al., 2017), or solutions of faba bean protein/cellulose nanocrystal are casting-dried into films as edible food packaging material (Rojas-Lema et al., 2021).

There are lots of drying methods including hot-air oven drying, contact drying, microwave convective drying, super-heated steam drying, and so on. These drying methods are classified into thermal conduction drying, thermal convection drying, and thermal radiation drying according to the heat transfer ways (Mohammadi-Moghaddam et al., 2024). Conduction drying usually uses sand, salt, or a hot metal surface to directly transfer heat to food through their interface (Bhattacharya, 2014). Convection drying transfers heat to food by hot air, causing water diffusion from the surface of food to the surrounding air by convection and inside food by diffusion, convection, or capillarity (Castro et al., 2018). Radiation drying transfers heat by electromagnetic waves or heat radiation from electric conventional coils throughout the food, and provides heat uniformly throughout the food (Sarkar et al., 2020). Nowadays, thermal convection (TC) and thermal radiation (TR) drying are widely employed, as thermal conduction drying often suffers from drawbacks such as overheating, quality deterioration, and poor temperature control.

Thermal drying methods affect the moisture vaporization and drying rate during thermal treatments. For example, compared with infrared power, the hot air heating imposed the highest drying rate curve and the lowest moisture content at 200 °C (Bagheri & Kashaninejad, 2018). Besides, heat treatment causes changes in the conformation and size of protein (Setiowati et al., 2020), molecular interaction (Setiowati et al., 2020), and thereby alters the structure and properties of polysaccharide/protein assemblies. TR (such as microwave) heated konjac glucomannan (KGM)-Alaska pollock surimi protein gel had a greater gel strength and water-holding capacity than that of thermal conduction (water bath) heating because polysaccharide chains stretched in proteins and interacted into a network via microwave treatment instead of polysaccharide aggregates inserted in proteins via water bath treatment (Ji et al., 2017).

Soybean protein isolate (SPI), consisting of 2S, 5S, 7S (β-conglycinin), and 11S (glycinin), has been applied to fabricate numerous plant-based protein products due to its low cost and rich functional properties (e.g., water-solubility, emulsifying property, and gelling ability) (Zheng et al., 2022). KGM is a soluble plant polysaccharide with good biocompatibility and nutritional functions (e.g., hypoglycemic and cholesterol-lowering function, and reducing fat absorption, as well as anti-cancer and anti-swelling) as well as functional properties [water-solubility, thickening capacity, film-forming property and mechanical properties (Tu et al., 2025). The KGM/SPI composite gel system integrates these attributes, showing great promise as wall materials for bioactive delivery (e.g., fish oil) or as biodegradable food packaging (Cui et al., 2021; Tu et al., 2025).

However, the industrial application of KGM/SPI systems faces significant performance bottlenecks related to drying processes. Traditional methods, such as spray drying or freeze-drying, often yield inconsistent microstructures. For instance, high-temperature convective air can induce rapid, non-uniform moisture migration, leading to structural collapse or excessive shrinkage, which subsequently compromises encapsulation efficiency and film barrier properties (Cui et al., 2021).

While existing research has explored various drying parameters (e.g., temperature, humidity), a critical knowledge gap persists regarding how distinct heat transfer mechanisms—specifically thermal radiation (TR) versus thermal convection (TC)—influence the intermediate structural evolution and the final functional quality of KGM/SPI assemblies. This study aims to bridge this gap by systematically evaluating the effects of TR (roast oven) and TC (hot-air oven) drying on the molecular interactions, secondary structures, and macroscopic performance of KGM/SPI films. By establishing the correlation between moisture mass transfer and structural hierarchy, this work provides a theoretical basis for selecting optimal thermal processing strategies to achieve desired functional properties in polysaccharide/protein assemblies.

Materials and methods

Materials

SPI was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). KGM with a molecular weight of 9.67 × 105 Da was purchased from Hubei Konson Konjac Technology Co., Ltd. (Ezhou, China). Glycerol was bought from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Thermal drying treatment and characterization of gel sample

Sample preparation and thermal drying treatment

Referring to the method, the KGM/SPI gels were prepared (Tu et al., 2025). About 3.2 g KGM and 16.8 g SPI were dispersed into 200 mL of pre-heated reverse osmosis (RO) water (around pH 6.7) at 90 °C. The mixture was then subjected to continuous magnetic stirring (2200 rpm) for 1.5 h while being maintained at a constant temperature of 90 °C in a water bath to ensure complete dissolution and molecular assembly. The resulting solution was poured into a series of circular glass plates (diameter 60 mm glass Petri dish) with 10 ± 0.1 g of sample per plate. After being cooled to room temperature (about 25 °C) and stored overnight at 4 °C, the KGM/SPI gels (about diameter 60 mm, thickness 5 mm) were obtained.

The KGM/SPI gels were dried at 80 °C for 4 h. TC drying was performed in a circulating hot-air oven (UFE600, Memmert, Germany) with maximum air velocity (100% fan speed), while TR drying was conducted in a specialized roast oven (K35FK602, Supor, China). To ensure comparability, all samples were positioned at a fixed orientation and an equal distance from the heat source. The surface temperature of the samples was monitored using a thermometer to confirm that the thermal exposure was consistent. Furthermore, the small thickness-to-diameter ratio (≤5 mm thickness vs. 60 mm diameter) was specifically designed to minimize internal thermal gradients, ensuring that the structural evolution was dominated by the drying kinetics rather than temperature variations within the matrix. To explore the drying process, the sample was withdrawn with a time interval of 1 h. In the following, TC-1, TC-2, TC-3, and TC-4 represented samples dried with TC for 1, 2, 3, and 4 h, respectively; TR-1, TR-2, TR-3, and TR-4 represented samples dried with TR for 1, 2, 3 and 4 h, respectively. K16S84 indicates the sample without thermal treatment. All samples that are not completely dry via TC or TR were freeze-dried at approximately −40 °C and 40 Pa (as a rough and non-rigorous reference only) to maintain the original physical characteristics at the time of drying collection for subsequent storage and measurement.

The weight of the KGM/SPI sample during the drying process under thermal treatment with different methods was measured with a time interval of 1 h. Molecular interaction forces

According to the reported methods (Qiao et al., 2025) with some modifications, the molecular interaction forces maintaining the structure of KGM/SPI were measured using different types of molecular interaction force elimination reagents. About 0.03 g small pieces of freeze-dried samples were mixed with 5 mL the following five different reagents: solution A, distilled water; solution B, Tris-glycine buffer (0.086 M Tris, 0.09 M glycine, 4 mM Na2EDTA, pH 8.0); solution C, solvent B and 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS); solvent D, solvent C and 8 M urea; solvent E, solvent D and 2% β-mercaptoethanol. To ensure comprehensive extraction and minimize the influence of sample heterogeneity, the mixture was incubated in a shaker (25 °C, 120 rpm) for a standardized period of 5 days. This extended duration was maintained until the final extraction (Solvent E) achieved a clear, transparent, and homogeneous state, indicating the complete solubilization of the KGM/SPI matrix. To ensure high reproducibility, all experiments were performed in triplicate. After centrifugation at 10,000×g for 15 min and dilution to the appropriate test concentrations, the protein content in the diluted supernatant was determined by the Detergent Compatible Bradford Protein Assay Kit method (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at the absorbance of 595 nm by a Thermo Fisher Scientific MULTISKAN Sky microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

The solubility of protein in different solvents was measured to indicate molecular interaction forces as the total samples could be totally resolved after adding all reagents. The molecular interaction forces were calculated by the difference in protein solubility in the supernatant between two adjacent solvents. The difference between protein solubility in the supernatant SA and SB, SB and SC, SC and SD, SD and SE represents electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and disulfide bonds, respectively.

Raman spectroscopy

Protein secondary structures were calculated using a method of Wang (Wang et al., 2022). Raman spectra of pressed sheet samples after freeze-drying were recorded by a XploRA PLUS Raman spectrometer (HORIBA FRANCE SAS, Montpellier, France) equipped with a 785 nm laser source with a 50% filter in the range from 600 to 2000 cm−1. The acquisition time and accumulation were 10 s and 6, respectively. After scanning, baseline correction, normalization against the phenylalanine band of around 1003 cm−1. The detail (positions and areas of peaks) of protein secondary structures (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil) was obtained by deconvolution at the range of 1600–1700 cm−1 (amide I region in protein) using Peakfit version 4.12 software (Systat Software, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and their percentages were calculated by their peak area proportion.

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

Following a documented method, the SAXS data of samples was collected in a q range of 0.03–0.2 Å−1 by the BL19U2 SAXS beamline at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (Shanghai, China) (Lin et al., 2025). The powders of about 0.1–0.4 g freeze-dried samples were blended with distilled water at 1:10 (w/w) ratio and then returned to a gel-like state after complete hydration for ≥4 h at room temperature (about 25 °C). Samples in a gel-like state filled in sample cell (inner diameter 1 cm and thickness 2 mm) and then were fixed on sample test stub with tape. After each sample scattering subtracted the background of distilled water, final data was obtained for analysis and diagram.

To quantitatively analyze the nanostructure of the KGM/SPI assemblies, the SAXS data was fitted using a hybrid model consisting of a Power-law function and a Cauchy (Lorentzian) function. The Power-law term accounts for the scattering from the large-scale disordered fractal network formed by KGM and SPI. The Cauchy function was introduced to describe the inter-particle correlations and the ordered arrangement within the protein-rich domains, as indicated by the presence of a characteristic scattering peak. This approach allows for the decoupling of the global network topology from the specific structural features (such as average size and spatial distribution) of the SPI particles on an intermediate scale. This modeling strategy is consistent with established methodologies for characterizing similar multi-component biopolymer systems (Tu et al., 2025).(7)Iq=A/qn+I0/1+4∗q−q1/Δq2+Bwhere n and A are the power-law prefactor and the power-law component, respectively; q1 donates the peak center position, which is related to the average size of SPI particles; I(0) represents the maximum scattering intensity at the peak center position q1 (Å−1); Δq (Å−1) indicates the full width at half maximum of the peak, reflecting size distribution of SPI particles; and B suggests the scattering background.

Preparation and characterization of KGM/SPI film sample

Preparation of film by thermal drying treatments

KGM (3.2 g) and SPI (16.8 g) were dissolved in 200 mL of RO water containing 8 g of glycerol (equivalent to 40% of the total dry matter mass). This concentration was determined to be optimal based on preliminary trials, providing sufficient flexibility without compromising the film's mechanical strength. The mixture was stirred magnetically at 90 °C for 1.5 h to ensure complete dissolution and molecular assembly. The solution was then poured into circular glass plates (15 cm in diameter). A precise amount of 60 ± 0.5 g of the blend solution was used for each plate to ensure that the resulting gels had a nearly identical thickness to those prepared in the 60-mm-diameter glass Petri dishes described in section 2.2.1. This controlled the surface area available for moisture evaporation per unit mass of the sample. After being cooled to room temperature (about 25 °C) and stored overnight at 4 °C, gel samples (about diameter 150 mm, thickness 5 mm) were dried at 80 °C for 4 h via TC in a hot-air oven or TR in a roast oven described in section 2.2.1. After this, the KGM/SPI film samples were obtained, and equilibrated at room temperature (about 25 °C) and 57.6% RH for 7 d to reach consistent moisture content (about 14%) before the following mechanical properties and water vapor permeability as well as water contact angle measurements.

Mechanical properties of KGM/SPI film

The mechanical properties of the films were measured according to the methods of Zhou (Zhou et al., 2022) by a TA-XT plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, Surrey, UK). The films were cut into strip shape (length 50 mm, width 10 mm), and then the two ends (10 mm/the end) of the long strip-shaped film sample were clamped tightly by the grip on the sample stage. The initial grip distance was 30 mm, and the cross-head speed was 1.0 mm/s at room temperature. The film thickness (T, cm) was tested using a micrometer (Shanghai Liuling Instrument Company, Shanghai, China). The tensile strength (σt, kPa) and elongation at break (εb, %) of the films were calculated by Eqs. (8), (9):(8)σt=F/T×W(9)εb=L−L0/L0×100%where F, T and W represent the stress for breaking films (N), the cross-sectional thickness (cm) and width (cm) of the film, respectively; L and L0 are the final length at break and the initial length of the film, respectively.

Water vapor permeability (WVP) of KGM/SPI film

WVP of films was tested according to the method of Ghanbarzadeh (Ghanbarzadeh et al., 2011) with slight modifications. The mouth of a glass cup (diameter = 1.8 cm) containing 3 g of anhydrous calcium chloride inside was covered and sealed with the KGM/SPI films. Then, these cups were placed in the desiccators with a relative humidity of 57% at 25 °C. The weight of each cup was measured before and after 3 days. The WVP (g⋅mm−1⋅d−1⋅kPa−1) was calculated by Eq. (10).(10)WVP=Δm⋅h/Δt⋅s⋅Rwhere ∆m and ∆t are the additional weight of the glass cup after 3 days (3 d) and time, respectively, h is the film thickness, s is the effective exposed area of the film, R (= 3169.2 × 10−3 kPa) is the saturated vapor pressure of water at 25 °C.

Water contact angle (WCA) of KGM/SPI film

WCA on the smooth surface of films was examined using an OCA15EC contact angle goniometer (Dataphysics, Filderstadt, Germany) according to the sessile-drop method (Qiao et al., 2019). The contact angle on the surface of the film changed gradually with time due to its hydrophilic nature or microstructure. The wettability and compactness of the film were reflected by WCA at 30th s after 6.0 μL deionized water was dropped on the smooth surface of the film in this study. The WCA value was measured on both sides of water drops and averaged.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted three times at least, and results were expressed as means ± standard deviation. The statistical analysis for the data was subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using Walter-Duncan's test for molecular interaction forces and secondary structure composition as well as SAXS or t-test for water vapor permeability and water contact angel as well as mechanical properties with IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM software, Chicago, IL, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Drying rate, moisture ratio and effective moisture diffusivity (Deff)

Drying rate and moisture ratio of KGM/SPI gels during TR or TC process are shown in Fig. 1a and b, respectively. Drying rate of samples during TR remained stable (〈3h) and then decreased slightly (≥ 3 h) in Fig. 1a, reflecting a first quick and stable, and then a slow diffusion/elimination process of moisture. This corresponds to a higher decrease and then a lower one in moisture ratio during TR process (Fig. 1b). The drying rate of samples with TC treatment gradually became smaller in Fig. 1a, which echoes that the downward trend of moisture ratio slowed down in Fig. 1b. As is observed in Fig. 1a, drying rate in the early drying stage (< 3 h) during TR process was lower than TC, which is due to the help of additional air flow supported by TC. In the late stage (≥ 3 h) of thermal drying treatment, both drying rates were comparable with each other. This donates the minimal promotion effect of air flow for the moisture diffusion due to the very low moisture ratio (approximately 0.05–0.1), which is also reported in the lemon slices drying process (Torki-Harchegani et al., 2016).Fig. 1Drying rate (a) and moisture ratio (b) of the KGM/SPI sample during TR and TC drying processes.Fig. 1

Drying rate (a) and moisture ratio (b) of the KGM/SPI sample during TR and TC drying processes.

The effective moisture diffusivity (Deff) was introduced to describe the inherent moisture diffusivity property in KGM/SPI samples (Bagheri & Kashaninejad, 2018). The Deff of TR (2.8541 × 10−7 m2/s) was smaller than that of TC (3.8977 × 10−7 m2/s), which suggests the slower moisture reduction under TR than TC. A higher Deff (2.074 × 10−9 m2/s) value also occurred in soybean system when dried with a TC method (hot air drying at 160 °C), compared with the lower one 1.727 × 10−9 m2/s dried with a TR method (infrared radiation drying at 200 W) (Bagheri & Kashaninejad, 2018).

The smaller Deff was consistent with the slower drying rate and a higher moisture ratio during TR under two thermal treatments in Fig. 1. Presumably, the flowing air produced by the air-circulating equipment under TC promoted moisture diffusion at the same drying temperature, which was similar to the hot-air convective drying of garlic slices (Tao et al., 2018). The flowing air sped up moisture diffusion, improving Deff during TC.

Molecular interaction forces analysis

The molecular interaction forces in the sample were tested by gradually eliminating these forces with different types of reagents, using the dissolved SPI as an indicator to quantify these forces. The results of samples undergoing the TR and TC drying are displayed in Fig. 2a and b, respectively. The intensity of molecular interaction forces in K16S84 was in the order of hydrophobic interactions > hydrogen bonds > electrostatic interactions or disulfide bonds, showing the dominant role of hydrophobic interactions in constructing the network structures in samples.Fig. 2Molecular interaction forces in the sample during TR (a) and TC (b) drying processes. Hydrophobic interaction weakened and the other molecular interaction forces enhanced with prolonged drying time during the two thermal treatments. As the moisture eliminated during drying, the molecular distance decreased and their movement ability restricted as a consequence. In this scenario, the presence of abundant -OH on KGM chains widely dispersing in the matrix of KGM/SPI sample favored the formation of hydrogen bonds, contributing to the gradual increase of hydrogen bonds. Simultaneously, the shortened distance between KGM and SPI promoted the reorientation of the protein chains. Due to the superior hydrophilicity of KGM, the hydrophobic moieties of SPI were preferentially buried within the interior of the self-assembled aggregates, while hydrophilic and ionizable groups were exposed at the surface (Isom et al., 2010). This structural transition effectively counteracted the intrinsic tendency of high temperatures to enhance hydrophobic interactions, leading to a net reduction in total hydrophobic interaction strength. Consequently, the interaction profile of the TR-dried films was dominated by enhanced hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, rather than hydrophobic-driven aggregation. Thermal treatment might promote the oxidation of sulfhydryl groups and the formation of disulfide bonds (Wen et al., 2023).

It should be noted that hydrophobic interactions during the drying process of TR (Fig. 2a) were lower than those of TC (Fig. 2b), while the hydrogen bonds showed higher values after heating for 2 h, 3 h, and 4 h. It is seen from Fig. 1b that the moisture ratio decreased to 0.15 and 0.35 when drying extended to 2 h for TC and TR, respectively. The relatively faster moisture evaporation (drying rate and Deff) and lower moisture residue for TC probably caused the local aggregation of SPI, leading to the uneven distribution of KGM chains in the matrix and thus the decreased heating related secondary bond variation (showing reduced increment of hydrogen bond and reduction of hydrophobic interaction).

Secondary structure composition analysis

Protein secondary structures (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil conformations) of SPI in samples during drying process of TC and TR are revealed in Fig. 3 by analyzing Raman spectra of the amide I band (around 1665 cm−1). It is seen that the content of random coil and α-helix gradually decreased, while the content of β-sheet increased with drying time during both thermal drying processes. The reduction of random coil was presumably attributed to the enhanced SPI assembly during drying. As mentioned, the newly formed hydrogen bonds promoted the burying of hydrophobic groups of SPI, which are usually distributed on the outside of α-helix. This destroyed the α-helix to some extent and was responsible for its reduction. At the same time, the polypeptide chains self-assembled in parallel to β-sheet via hydrogen bonds (Aggeli et al., 1997; Bowerman & Nilsson, 2012). It is also noticed that the total content of α-helix, β-sheet, and β-turn for samples treated with TR was higher than that of TC. This is aligned with the more presented hydrogen bonds in the system (see Fig. 2), confirming that the mild moisture evaporation condition (see Fig. 1) benefits for the molecular assembly in the KGM/SPI system.Fig. 3Secondary structure compositions of samples during TR (a) and TC (b) drying processes.Fig. 3

The related fitting parameters are shown in Fig. 4c and d. The average aggregation size d, obtained through Woolf-Bragg's equation d = 2π/q1 based on the peak position q1, decreased accordingly with longer thermal treatment time. The shortened molecular distance enhanced the interactions between KGM and SPI (see Fig. 2), which might inhibit the aggregation of SPI and reduce the size of nanoscale scatters (d) based on the previous research on SPI aggregates' size in KGM/SPI gel system (Tu et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 4c, d value of KGM/SPI sample treated by TR was slightly lower than that by TC. Perhaps, the relatively even distribution of KGM in the system was conducive to the occurrence of intermolecular interaction between KGM and SPI, which further inhibited the SPI aggregation and generated smaller d values for TR samples. The Δq value reflecting size distribution of the SPI aggregate (Tu et al., 2025) of the KGM/SPI sample treated by TR and TC increased with the prolonging of drying time in Fig. 4d. This signifies that the re-assembly of components during drying widened the size distribution of nanoscale scatters in the system, leading to more SPI aggregates of different sizes and poorer size uniformity of aggregates. The Δq value of KGM/SPI treated with TR for 1 h and 2 h was smaller than that of TC. The local aggregation of SPI, caused by relatively severe moisture evaporation, was presumably responsible for this wider distribution of nanoscale scatters in the KGM/SPI system.

Mechanical properties analysis

Different drying methods affect application properties of samples by generating different drying rates, Deff, and altering their assembly structures. The mechanical properties of KGM/SPI films (final state of drying for KGM/SPI gel) treated by TR and TC processes are presented in Fig. 5. Both the tensile strength (σt) and elongation at break (εb) of F-TR (KGM/SPI film dried with TR) were higher than those of F-TC (KGM/SPI film dried with TC). The smaller (d value) and more homogeneous aggregates inside KGM/SPI system dried with TR (see Fig. 4), along with the stronger hydrogen bonds (see Fig. 2), contributed to the formation of the denser, more compact network structure in F-TR and thus the higher tensile strength. The higher content of ordered secondary structure consisting of α-helix, β-fold, and β-sheet in KGM/SPI dried with TR (see Fig. 3) also enhanced the tensile strength of films. The potential increase of intermolecular crosslinking between KGM and SPI supported by more hydrogen bonds during the TR drying process might be responsible for the improved elongation at break of F-TR (Li et al., 2021; Ortiz et al., 2017).Fig. 5Mechanical properties of films prepared by TR and TC. WVP and WCA analysis

WVP and WCA values of KGM/SPI films treated with TR and TC drying are presented in Fig. 6. F-TR showed the lower WVP value but higher WCA value, indicating higher water resistance and lower hydrophilic surface than F-TC. These were not consistent with the less exposed hydrophobic groups on the surface of SPI in F-TR film (in Fig. 2). Presumably, more hydrogen bonds strengthened cross-linking between KGM and SPI aggregates with smaller d and Δq, contributing to the formation of the tight surface of film and denser network structure (proved by higher mechanical properties of F-TR in Fig. 5), further lowering WVP and enlarging WCA value of F-TR. Relationship between drying kinetics and structural-functional properties

The distinct drying kinetics of TR and TC methods fundamentally governed the multi-scale structural evolution and the resulting functional properties of the KGM/SPI films. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the moderate drying rate and lower Deff associated with TR drying facilitated a controlled reduction in molecular distance (D spacing) and promoted an ordered rearrangement of the macromolecular network. This structural refinement, characterized by enhanced hydrogen bonding and a more compact fractal network, directly transferred into superior mechanical performance. Specifically, the denser protein-polysaccharide matrix in TR-dried films provided higher resistance to deformation (increased tensile strength) and improved structural integrity (increased elongation at break). Furthermore, the reduction in free volume and the burial of hydrophobic groups collectively enhanced the water barrier properties, as evidenced by the lower WVP and higher WCA values. These results demonstrate that the drying-induced structural modifications are the primary drivers of the improved practical performance of the KGM/SPI assembly.Fig. 7Mechanism illustration of the effects of TR and TC drying methods.Fig. 7

Conclusions

Comparison of the effect of TR and TC drying methods on the drying rates, effective moisture diffusivity, molecular interactions, microstructures and practical properties was made in this study. During drying process, the shortened molecular distance and the restricted movement ability enabled the occurrence of molecular interactions between KGM and SPI as a result of moisture evaporation, contributing to the increment of hydrogen bonds. This further promoted the burying of hydrophobic groups on the surface of SPI, leading to the reduction of hydrophobic interactions. Simultaneously, the burying of hydrophobic groups of SPI destroyed α-helix to some extent and the polypeptide chains self-assembled in parallel into β-sheet. The occurrence of intermolecular interaction between KGM and SPI further inhibited the SPI aggregation, which resulted in smaller d values for all samples.

With the assistance of flowing air under TC, higher drying rates and larger Deff were generated due to rapid moisture evaporation. The relatively severe moisture evaporation probably caused the local aggregation of SPI (reflected by larger nanoscale scatter's size d) with wider size ditribution (reflected by larger Δq), probably leading to looser stacking of SPI aggregates, and thus a looser network structure in samples. Conversely, the smaller scatter size (d) and more interactions between components for samples dried by TR method strengthened network structure, improving mechanical properties and increasing their water barrier properties (with decreased WVP and increased WCA value). In conclusion, these findings establish a robust theoretical framework for understanding the correlation between thermal drying mechanisms and the structural-functional properties of KGM/SPI assemblies. From an industrial perspective, our study highlights thermal radiation (TR) drying as a high-performance and energy-efficient alternative to traditional hot-air drying. By facilitating a moderate moisture evaporation rate and lower Deff, TR drying promotes a more compact and ordered molecular network, which significantly enhances the mechanical durability and moisture-barrier performance of the resulting films. These improvements are critical for the development of sustainable food packaging materials, as they provide better protection against environmental humidity and mechanical damage, thereby potentially extending the shelf-life of packaged goods. Furthermore, the targeted use of TR drying aligns with the growing industrial demand for energy-saving technologies and high-quality bio-based products.
