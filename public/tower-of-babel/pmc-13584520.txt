Physics-Informed Graph Diffusion Model for Cross-Scale Performance Prediction of Silver Nanowire Percolation Networks
Authors: Wei Xiong, Xun He, Zhen Huang, Haibo Wang, Xiping Zeng, Xi Liu, Lin Lyu, Bin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Silver nanowire (AgNW) networks are promising transparent conductive electrodes (TCEs) for flexible optoelectronics, yet their industrial quality control is hindered by the gap between invisible microstructures and unpredictable macroscopic performance. Herein, we propose a Physics-informed Graph Diffusion Model that injects the physical laws governing TCE conduction into deep learning as an inductive bias. From a single scanning electron micrograph, we construct a topology graph and predict the sheet resistance, Rs. A physics-informed GIN encoder, pretrained on a resistor-network-derived conductance label, provides a robust cold-start representation; a target-posterior diffusion model then supplies a calibrated per-prediction uncertainty rather than performing latent manifold augmentation. The encoder is trained on a multisource graph data set that fuses synthetic percolation models with real SEM micrographs of AgNW films at three sheet-resistance levels, augmented by an external continuous-Rs data set. Under a leakage-free, group-aware evaluation, the two-stage model predicts Rs with a relative error of 7.27% (R2 = 0.840) while delivering calibrated uncertainty (empirical 1σ/2σ coverage of 63.6%/92.4% versus the nominal 68.3%/95.4%). Film-scale-level validation across the three held-out film-scale groups (the held-out in-house film at both fields of view plus one held-out external film) gives 6.21% ± 4.66% (mean ± std), and a stricter leave-one-film-out protocol over the three in-house films gives a 5.51% mean (4.75/7.72/4.06%). The diffusion module’s distinctive contribution is calibrated uncertainty quantification; its point accuracy is statistically indistinguishable from a no-diffusion baseline. Requiring only 108k parameters and trained on 13 source SEM images, the model matches strong graph baselines while uniquely supplying calibrated prediction intervals for industrial TCE inspection.

Introduction

With the rapid development of flexible electronics and touch screens, silver nanowire (AgNW) networks have emerged as highly promising transparent conductive electrodes (TCEs). , In high-throughput roll-to-roll (R2R) manufacturing, nondestructive real-time monitoring of optoelectronic properties, particularly sheet resistance (Rs) and transmittance (T), is crucial. The traditional four-probe method is contact-based and risks damaging delicate nanocoatings. Consequently, predicting performance from a limited number of existing scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images has become an urgent industrial need. However, computer vision-based prediction faces two severe challenges. The first is the “cross-scale prediction” dilemma: macroscopic conductivity depends on global connectivity, making it inherently difficult to accurately infer global macroscopic performance from localized microscopic images (e.g., a 10 μm field of view). The second challenge is the “small-sample” learning bottleneck. Unlike general image data sets, acquiring high-quality SEM images with precise electrical labels is extremely costly. With limited experimental paired data, traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are highly prone to overfitting, often memorizing background noise rather than essential topological rules.

Currently, AgNW performance prediction mainly relies on physical modeling and data-driven methods. Physical models, such as those proposed by Kumar and Kulkarni and Mutiso et al., establish analytical relationships for sheet resistance (Rs) but are constrained by ideal assumptions (e.g., “rigid straight rods”), making them ineffective for real networks containing nonideal defects like bending and fracture. In the realm of data-driven approaches, graph neural networks (GNNs) have demonstrated significant success in broader materials science applications, including crystal-structure property prediction , and microstructure modeling. However, GNNs still encounter challenges when processing complex AgNW networks with nonideal defects, particularly under small-sample conditions. To overcome data scarcity, generative augmentation has gained attention. While transfer learning has been used for simulated networks, diffusion models have recently surpassed traditional methods in generation diversity. Studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of diffusion models for tabular data augmentation and manifold expansion to resolve small-sample regression overfitting, yet their application to real, cross-scale SEM image prediction remains largely unexplored. To tackle these bottlenecks, we propose a physics-informed graph diffusion prediction model that combines explicit modeling with generative calibration. Instead of using physical equations as hard constraints in the loss function, , our model incorporates percolation laws as a flexible “inductive bias.” Specifically, we convert AgNW SEM images into physical graph structures via an explicit skeletonization algorithm, enabling the model to focus on conductive paths rather than raw pixels. We then pretrain an edge-aware Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) encoder on resistor-network-derived conductance labels to resolve the cold-start problem. Furthermore, a target-posterior diffusion model learns , the conditional distribution p(log G | z graph), providing calibrated per-prediction uncertainty rather than performing latent manifold augmentation. Finally, we design a two-stage sequential architecture: Stage 1 fine-tunes the encoder with a deterministic head, yielding a point estimate of log G, while Stage 2 trains the target-posterior diffusion, yielding the target-posterior mean and its calibrated standard deviation, synergistically optimizing point accuracy and uncertainty quantification. We refer to this two-stage model as B2; its deterministic Stage 1 head and target-posterior diffusion Stage 2 head are denoted b2_det and b2_diff, respectively, and yield the point estimate g det and the posterior mean g diff (with calibrated σ).

Ultimately, the proposed model achieves strong cross-scale prediction performance using micrographs cropped from 13 source SEM images (3 in-house films +10 DeepBlue) and 108k model parameters. At the patch level under the leakage-free group-aware split, Rs is predicted with a relative error of 7.27% (R2 = 0.840) by the Stage-2 target-posterior diffusion model (b2_diff) and 7.54% (R2 = 0.865) by the Stage-1 deterministic model (b2_det), with 6.21 ± 4.66% under film-scale-level held-out aggregation (5.51% mean under a stricter leave-one-film-out protocol). This work mitigates the limitations of purely data-driven “black-box” models, showing that combining physical priors with target-posterior diffusion is a viable direction for accurate, nondestructive optoelectronic performance prediction in industrial applications.

The external DeepBlue set plays three complementary, nonconflicting roles in this study. Its continuous resistance labels provide a training prior that bridges the discrete in-house {6, 25, 40} Ω/sq values. One DeepBlue film (db_028) is held out within the leakage-free, group-aware split as an instrument-domain diagnostic (Table , “External” row; SI S11), while the remaining nine DeepBlue films contribute the continuous Rs prior during training. The full DeepBlue set is also used as a zero-shot, cross-domain transfer probe to assess instrument-invariant generalization (SI S7, db2ours). The Evaluation Protocol consolidates every train/calibration/test assignment and confirms that no source film leaks across partitions.

Results and Discussion

Addressing the unique challenges of cross-scale performance prediction and small-sample overfitting in AgNW TCEs, we developed a physics-informed GDM. Rather than relying on purely data-driven “black-box” approaches, this framework extracts explicit physical topologies from raw SEM images. As briefly illustrated in the overall architecture (Figure ) and the specific graph extraction pipeline (Figure ), the system standardizes raw SEM inputs into topological graphs via rigorous artifact removal, center cropping, and skeletonization. This physical translation enables the GNNs to process nodes (physical junctions) and edges (conductive paths) directly, capturing the essential percolation physics while maintaining geometric invariance.

With the physics-informed framework and multisource data set established, we transitioned to the quantitative evaluation of the model’s predictive performance on R2R-prepared AgNW networks. All experiments were conducted using the PyTorch framework on a server equipped with an NVIDIA L20 GPU. The data set comprises 855 in-house cropped micrographs (39,414 graph patches) cropped from 3 in-house AgNW films ({6, 25, 40} Ω/sq), each imaged at two magnifications (450 macro-images at 100 μm and 405 microimages at 10 μm). It also includes 2,000 sets of physics-constrained synthetic data and 13,500 graph patches (from 270 cropped micrographs of an external Layer-by-Layer-assembled (bilayer) DeepBlue data set of 10 source SEM images)­21, providing a continuous-Rs prior spanning 2.38–27.57 Ω/sq. All results use a group-aware split that keeps every patch of one source film within a single fold (data-split seed = 2026; (training seed = 42)), together with a strict leave-one-film-out protocol for cross-scale generalization. This design eliminates inter-ROI data leakage. The in-house films span a narrow sheet-resistance range ({6, 25, 40} Ω/sq), so generalization across broader Rs levels and alternative fabrication lines remains open. Detailed imaging parameters (magnification, pixel scale, SNR, accelerating voltage, working distance) for all source SEM sets, and film-level aggregation of the three in-house physical films, are provided in Supporting Information Tables S14 and S15, respectively.

Regarding the model architecture and hyperparameter settings for the AgNW transparent electrode prediction task, the Graph Encoder adopted a 3-layer, edge-aware Graph Isomorphism Network (GINE variant) with a hidden dimension of 128; the edge projection MLP maps the 4-dimensional edge feature into the node feature space at each layer. The target-posterior diffusion model operates in the 1-D target space (log G) with a DPM-Solver++ sampler using 20 denoising steps. The two-stage sequential architecture first fine-tunes the encoder with a deterministic MLP head (Stage 1, yielding the point model b2_det), then freezes the encoder and trains the target-posterior diffusion head (Stage 2, yielding b2_diff). Edge features comprise [L, cos θ, sin θ, 1/L] (4-dimensional), where the direction-cosine pair replaces the raw absolute angle used in an earlier formulation, resolving the translation-invariance concern present in the earlier formulation. All training scripts use a fixed training seed of 42 (distinct from the data-split seed of 2026) to ensure reproducibility.

To evaluate the model’s performance comprehensively and with physical meaning, we adopted three core metrics for quantitative analysis: Relative Error (RE) for Rs prediction, the coefficient of determination (R2) for variance explanation, and the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). For transmittance, which is estimated from geometry rather than learned, we report Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and RMSE rather than a learned R2. We note that the calibration of uncertainty is assessed by the empirical coverage of 1σ and 2σ prediction intervals against their nominal levels (68.3% and 95.4%, respectively). First, RE quantifies the normalized prediction error; its calculation is shown in eq ): 1 RE=∑i=1N|ytrue,i−ypred,i|∑i=1Nytrue,i×100%

Second, RMSE imposes heavier penalties on samples with large errors. In industrial quality inspection scenarios, RMSE directly reflects the stability of the modelthat is, whether the model can avoid catastrophic prediction deviations. Its calculation is shown in eq : 2 RMSE=1N∑i=1N(ypred,i−ytrue,i)2

Finally, R2 measures the model’s ability to explain the variance of the true data, where ytrue denotes the arithmetic mean of the true values across all test samples. The closer R2 is to 1, the more accurately the model captures the nonlinear relationship between the nanowire network topology and macroscopic conductivity. The data points fit tightly against the identity line (y = x), validating strong patch-level agreement (R2 = 0.865 for the Stage 1 deterministic model, b2_det). Since this model is trained based on a physics-informed framework, to address the issue of whether the model’s predictive behavior conforms to real physical laws, we conducted in-depth verifications from two dimensions. The first is linear consistency. As shown in Figure , the predicted values are highly linearly correlated with the ground truth, as measured by the four-probe method. Given that the true measurements follow Ohm’s law and percolation theory, the high convergence of the predictions to the true values is statistically consistent with the model having captured the essential topological features governing conductivity rather than spurious correlations. The second is cross-scale self-consistency. Mean-field theory indicates that as the scale increases, microscopic random fluctuations should smooth out. Our experimental results show that the prediction error remains bounded across the micro (10 μm) and macro (100 μm) scales; quantitatively, the Stage-1 deterministic model (b2_det) achieves 4.02% RE on sem_10 and 11.37% on sem_100, while the Stage-2 diffusion model (b2_diff) achieves 3.36% and 11.59%, respectively. We interpret the cross-scale stability as supporting evidence rather than proof of causal reconstruction of the percolation physics.

We report all baselines on our own data under an identical, leakage-free, group-aware split. Note that all baseline and ablation results are single-run outcomes at a fixed seed; in place of per-bar standard deviations, we therefore report the run-to-run variation observed in repeated sanity reruns (<0.5 percentage points, reflecting nondeterministic GPU scheduling), which is smaller than the between-model gaps discussed below and within normal reproducibility bounds. Benefiting from high-quality preprocessing and the percolation-inspired inductive bias, our single-stream (no diffusion) model already reaches competitive accuracy; the diffusion-augmented two-stage model is comparable rather than strictly superior, and its distinct value is calibrated uncertainty quantification.

Against this internal baseline, the Graph Diffusion framework proposed in this work is competitive rather than strictly superior in point accuracy: GraphTransformer (6.65%) and ResNet-18 (7.14%) achieve marginally lower RE than our b2_diff (7.27%), but the gaps (0.62 and 0.13 percentage points, respectively) fall well within the film-level cross-validation variance (per-film standard deviation ±4.66%), and neither baseline provides uncertainty quantification. Critically, our model achieves this competitive accuracy with only 108k parameters1.5× fewer than GraphTransformer (161k) and over 100× fewer than ResNet-18 (11.25M)trained on merely 13 source SEM images (3 in-house films at {6, 25, 40} Ω/sq, each imaged at two magnifications, +10 DeepBlue). This parameter and data efficiency is a direct benefit of the physics-informed inductive bias: the resistor-network pretraining injects domain knowledge that would otherwise require far more data to learn, and explains why data-hungry architectures such as ViT-S collapse entirely (31.76% RE) in this small-sample regime.

To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we conducted a series of ablation studies comparing the “Full Model (Ours, two-stage + diffusion)” with its no-diffusion single-stream variant. Table now reports all three architecture variantssingle-stream (pretrained encoder only) → Stage-1 b2_det → Stage-2 b2_diffat pooled ALL accuracy: under film-level held-out aggregation, the two-stage model reaches 6.21 ± 4.66%, while the no-diffusion single-stream reaches 6.33 ± 3.67%; the difference is not statistically significant (the film-level 95% confidence intervals overlap substantially: [0.94, 11.48] vs [2.18, 10.48]). The diffusion module’s role is therefore best understood as the main driver of calibrated uncertainty quantification rather than as a source of large point-accuracy gains: the target-posterior diffusion yields per-prediction 1σ/2σ intervals with empirical coverage of 63.6%/92.4% against the nominal 68.3%/95.4%. Crucially, when the physics-informed pretraining is removed (from-scratch ablation), the deterministic point estimate is largely preserved (b2_det: 7.54% pretrained vs 7.86% from-scratch, a 0.32 pp change), whereas the diffusion model’s point accuracy degrades without pretraining (b2_diff: 7.27% → 10.02%). The contrast is even sharper in uncertainty quality: the pretrained model’s raw (preconformal) posterior is already close to nominal (70.76%/92.78%) and requires only mild conformal correction, whereas the from-scratch variant’s raw posterior is severely overconfident (39.5%/66.7%) and reaches nominal coverage only after conformal inflation of its intervals by 2.4× on average (mean posterior σ of 0.197 vs 0.082). Physics-informed pretraining therefore yields intrinsically well-calibrated uncertainties together with better point accuracy, rather than relying on post hoc correction to manufacture coverage. Complementary ablations further isolate the accuracy drivers: replacing the physics-informed node/edge features with a w/o physics topology (nodedim = 2, edgedim = 1) degrades RE to 16.47% (R2 = 0.668), while removing weight decay or injecting 5% label noise increases it only modestly to 9.04% and 9.33%confirming that the physics-informed inductive bias, rather than model capacity or regularization alone, is the dominant driver of point accuracy (Table ).

We stress that cross-instrument generalization is not symmetric at the device level: the film-level (aggregated) reverse DeepBlue-trained → in-house (db2ours) transfer of Ours (b2_diff) reaches only 51.64% mean Rs RE (95% CI 9.64–93.64%; Supporting Information Table S6 Note). The favorable 3.69% patch-level cross-instrument figure (main text Table , External row) should therefore be read as a near-matched-scale result rather than evidence of universal portability.

Regarding transmittance, we replaced the brittle, learned T head (which previously degraded in ablation, yielding poor quantitative agreement with measured T) with the physically grounded estimate T ≈ 1 – coverage, where coverage is the metal area fraction obtained directly from the segmented network. This first-order geometric-optics relation removes the degradation entirely and keeps Rs accuracy intact. On evaluated micrographs, it yields MAE 21.21% and RMSE 23.82% against measured T, and it is rank-consistent within a fabrication process (Spearman ρ = 0.50 for sem_10, ρ = 0.75 for sem_100, ρ = 0.26 across the cross-instrument DeepBlue set). We report it as a qualitative not quantitative, predictor, and we do not trade Rs accuracy for T.

Beyond within-laboratory performance, the topological representation also transfers across instruments. Our model, trained on the in-house films, generalizes zero-shot to the external DeepBlue data set 21 (R2 = 0.934, RE = 3.69%), remaining robust at the matched 10 μm scale but degrading at the unmatched 100 μm scale. The reverse direction (DeepBlue-trained → in-house) reaches only 51.64% film-level RE (SI Table S6 Note), confirming that the learned representation captures instrument-invariant percolation physics rather than data set-specific artifacts.

Finally, addressing the pressing need for real-time online inspection in industrial R2R production lines, we quantitatively evaluated the model’s practical inference efficiency. On a single GPU (batch 256, film-group split), the measured forward pass is a single-stream 1.45 ms per 256-patch batch (≈0.0057 ms/patch amortized; 1.76 × 105 patches·s–1). The two-stage target-posterior sampling draws K = 30 Monte Carlo samples, each via a 20-step DPM-Solver++ solver, which measures ≈1.18 ms/patch on an NVIDIA L20 GPU. This two-stage figure is from an independent L20 benchmark and is distinct from the canonical single-stream encoder benchmark of 1.45 ms per 256-patch batch reported above; the production 20-step setting is slightly faster. Even with diffusion, the ≈1 ms per patch cost is well within the real-time inspection requirements for typical roll-to-roll line speeds (a 104 -patch film is processed in ≈12 s). This paradigm of “heavy generation during training, light load during inference” clears the temporal hurdles for deploying diffusion models in high-throughput industrial quality inspection. On CPU (same host), the encoder-only single-stream path runs at ∼2.54 ms/patch and the full two-stage posterior at ∼603.8 ms/patch, whereas characterization on low-power edge devices remains future work.

Limitations

Despite the competitive accuracy and calibrated uncertainty demonstrated above, several inherent limitations of the present framework must be stated objectively. First, the model processes only the 2D planar projection of the nanowire network extracted from a single SEM micrograph; it does not represent through-thickness morphology or three-dimensional percolation pathways, so its predictions apply strictly to planar single-layer films. Second, the model is trained and evaluated on as-fabricated microstructures and predicts sheet resistance from a static image; it cannot forecast how Rs evolves under long-term aging, thermal cycling, or environmental degradation, because no temporal or accelerated-aging data are included in the present study. Third, prediction error is elevated for multilayer stacked or bilayer films, since the 2D graph cannot resolve interlayer contacts and buried conductive pathsa behavior already observed on the external bilayer DeepBlue set, where reverse (DeepBlue → in-house) transfer degrades to 51.64% RE. These constraints bound the method’s applicability; we therefore caution against extrapolating the reported accuracy to out-of-scope film architectures or to postaging performance, and we identify 3D morphology encoding, aging-aware modeling, and multilayer representation as explicit directions for future work.

Conclusions

To address the twin challenges of cross-scale performance prediction and data scarcity in AgNW transparent conductive films, we propose a staged, interpretable, physics-informed GDM. Unlike “black-box” models, we explicitly convert AgNW SEM images into topological graphs, injecting physical laws as an inductive bias. Pretraining the edge-aware GIN encoder on a resistor-network-derived conductance label enables the model to grasp microscopic conductive mechanisms early. Crucially, the target-posterior diffusion model learns p(log G | z_graph), providing calibrated per-prediction uncertainty rather than performing latent manifold augmentation. The two-stage sequential architecture trains a deterministic point estimate (Stage 1) followed by the posterior mean and calibrated standard deviation. Under a leakage-free group-aware split, the Stage 1 deterministic model predicts Rs with RE = 7.54% (R2 = 0.865), and the Stage 2 diffusion model (b2_diff) with RE = 7.27% (R2 = 0.840), while delivering calibrated 1σ/2σ coverage (63.6%/92.4%). The diffusion module does not significantly improve point accuracy (the film-level 95% confidence intervals of the diffusion and single-stream variants overlap substantially), but its unique, demonstrated contribution is trustworthy uncertainty quantification. The learned topological representation further generalizes zero-shot across instruments (cross-instrument R2 = 0.934, RE = 3.69% at matched 10-μm scale), confirming that the physics-informed graph representation captures transferable percolation structure rather than data set-specific features. Moreover, this competitive performance is achieved with only 108k parameters trained on patches cropped from only 13 source SEM images (3 in-house films +10 DeepBlue), demonstrating that physics-informed inductive bias enables data-efficient learning where conventional architectures either require far more parameters (ResNet-18:11.25M) or fail to converge entirely (ViT-S: 31.76% RE). Because the conformally calibrated 2σ interval is empirically covered at 92.4%, the prediction is trustworthy enough for risk-aware screening in high-throughput R2R quality control, a capability no deterministic baseline provides.

Methods

Synthesis of AgNW Ink

Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, 0.63 g, Mw ≈ 55,000), KCl (1.73 mg), and KBr (1.41 mg) were sequentially added to a vessel containing 50 mL of ethylene glycol (EG). The mixture was stirred moderately for 10 min to form solution A. Separately, AgNO3 powder (0.225 g) was dissolved in 5 mL of EG under vigorously stirred until fully dissolved to form solution B. Solution A was heated to 150 °C for 0.5 h, followed by the dropwise addition of solution B over a period of 10 min. The resulting mixture was then maintained at 150 °C for 3 h. After the reaction, the product was washed three times with acetone and ethanol, and finally stored in ethanol. Prior to use, the silver nanowires were formulated into a coating ink consisting of 2% acrylic resin, 0.1% HPMC, 0.9% silver nanowires, 95% deionized water, and 2% ethanol. The ink was subsequently scaled up for roll-to-roll mass production using this process.

R2R Fabrication of AgNW TCE Films

The two-shaft linkage machine (FOM sigmaR2R) was utilized for roll-to-roll production. A roll of PET was wrapped around the back shaft and moved forward at a speed of 4 m/min. The slot-die length was 650 mm and the width was 188 μm. The injection speed was 26 mL/min. The coating temperature followed a gradient ramping profile: 60 °C → 80 °C → 100 °C → 60 °C. The morphologies of the samples were probed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM, HITACHI SU8100).

Overall Architecture of the Physics-Informed GDM

Our framework predicts the sheet resistance Rs (equivalently, the sheet conductance G = 1/Rs) of silver nanowire (AgNW) transparent conductive films from a single scanning electron micrograph (SEM). It proceeds in four steps (Figure ): Graph constructionthe segmented nanowire skeleton is converted into a graph whose nodes are wire junctions/end points and whose edges are wire segments, with translation-invariant edge features. Physics-informed encoder pretrainingan edge-aware Graph Isomorphism Network (GINE-variant) encoder is pretrained to regress the physically supervised sheet conductance, log G eff obtained from a resistor network solver on the constructed graph. This injects a percolation/physics inductive bias before any downstream fine-tuning. Target-posterior diffusioninstead of modeling the latent space, a diffusion model learns the target posterior p(log G | zgraph ), where zgraph is the encoder’s graph-level embedding used as a conditioning variable; sampling uses a 20-step DPM-Solver++ solver. Two-stage sequential calibrationStage 1 yields a deterministic point estimate g det ; Stage 2 yields the target-posterior mean g diff and its calibrated standard deviation σ, providing per-prediction uncertainty. The transmittance is estimated from first principles as T ≈ 1 – coverage, where coverage is the metal-area fraction of the segmented network, removing the need for a brittle learned T head.

Data Preprocessing and Graph Topology Extraction

To overcome the limitations of traditional convolutional neural networks in processing sparse data, a rigorous engineering approach must be adopted to execute the complete conversion pipeline from raw SEM images to graph structures (Figure ). We first designed a physics scale-based preprocessing strategy: on the one hand, artifact removal automatically identifies and crops scale bars and text labels at the bottom of SEM images; on the other hand, it establishes the mapping between pixels and micrometers by reading metadata, crops a fixed area from the center based on the target field of view, and strictly prohibits interpolation scaling. After preprocessing, we employ a classical explicit geometric modeling algorithm. We define the extracted topological network as a graph G = (V, E). For the image, an adaptive Otsu thresholding method is used for binarization, followed by the scikit-image skeletonization algorithm to extract the skeleton. Nodes (V) represent intersections (degree ≥ 3) and end points (degree 1); edges (E) represent conductive paths connecting the intersections. Each edge carries a translation-invariant feature vector as defined in (eq ), where L ij is the Euclidean length of the segment and (cos θ ij , sin θ ij ) is the unit direction vector of the edge. We deliberately encode orientation as the direction-cosine pair rather than an absolute angle because the pair is invariant to the choice of coordinate origin and to the sign/orientation of the edge. The 1/L ij term acts as an edge-conductance prior under a first-order wire-resistance model. This length-based encoding is invariant to translation of the raw micrograph and greatly reduces the model’s reliance on data volume; practical rotational tolerance is obtained through rotation augmentation during training rather than exact equivariance.

Multisource Physics-Constrained Data Set Construction

To support the aforementioned architecture and validate its practical utility, we constructed a multisource hybrid data set (Table ). First, we generated physics-constrained synthetic data based on percolation theory: − in terms of topological evolution, a curvature constraint was introduced to control the random-walk process to simulate flexible bending; regarding visual alignment, adaptive Gaussian blur and additive Gaussian noise were applied to simulate electron-beam interactions and thermal noise. This section generated 2,000 images providing precise topological labels for pretraining. Subsequently, we collected the real data: this study fabricated 3 AgNW TCE films at distinct sheet resistances ({6, 25, 40} Ω/sq); each film was imaged by SEM at two magnifications, and the resulting micrographs were cropped at multiple nonoverlapping spatial positions into 405 microscale (10 μm) and 450 macro-scale (100 μm) patch-level subimages (855 crop micrographs in total), with labels obtained through actual four-probe measurements. In addition, we incorporated an external DeepBlue data set providing 10 SEM images with continuous Rs values spanning 2.38–27.57 Ω/sq, from which 270 crop micrographs (FOV 5–10 μm; 7.27 nm/pixel) were further processed into 13,500 graph patches. Note that during graph construction, each patch-level micrograph was further subdivided into multiple nonoverlapping graph patches (subgraphs) for GNN processing, yielding 52,914 real-image graph-level instances (39,414 in-house +13,500 DeepBlue) that, together with 99,999 synthetic instances, form the 152,913-patch corpus (137,520 training/1,350 validation/14,043 held-out test); all evaluation metrics (Table ) are reported at this graph-patch level on the held-out fold. The nine non held-out DeepBlue films serve as a continuous-Rs training priorbridging the three discrete Rs values {6, 25, 40} Ω/sq in our in-house datawhile one DeepBlue film (db_028) is held out within the group-aware split as an instrument-domain diagnostic (Table , “External” row); that row is a diagnostic within the main split rather than an independently retrained external test set.

Two-Stage Sequential Calibration

The final predictor is trained in two sequential stages that share the pretrained GIN encoder (Figure ). Stage 1 (deterministic) fine-tunes the encoder together with a deterministic MLP head, yielding a point estimate g det = MLP_α­(zgraph)a direct regression of log G (L det = MSE). Stage 2 (posterior) freezes the encoder and trains the target-posterior diffusion described above (L diff = noise-prediction MSE), yielding g diff and the calibrated uncertainty σ. No consistency loss between the two stages is required; the deterministic head’s accuracy is fully established in Stage 1, and the target-posterior diffusion’s calibration is established independently in Stage 2. In the Results, we refer to the Stage-1 deterministic configuration and the Stage-2 target-posterior diffusion configuration as b2_det and b2_diff, respectively.

The diffusion module (stage 2) contributes exclusively through the calibrated target posterior; the deterministic head (stage 1) is optimized independently with a standard MSE objective. The two stages are trained sequentially and not jointly.

Stage-1 objective: Ldet = MSE­(gdet, y); Stage-2 objective: Ldiff = MSE­(εpred, ε), where y is the measured log G and ε is the diffusion noise. The final reported Rs is exp­(−gdiff) with a 1σ interval exp­(−(gdiff ± σ)). During inference, the Stage-1 point model (b2_det) can be used alone for real-time deployment (0.0057 ms/patch on a GPU), while the full two-stage model with posterior sampling (b2_diff) provides calibrated uncertainty at ≈1.18 ms/patch (K = 30 Monte Carlo samples, each via the same 20-step DPM-Solver++ solver; measured with K = 30 posterior samples on an L20 GPU, 256-patch batch).

Evaluation Protocol

All results use a group-aware split that keeps every patch of one source film within a single fold (data-split seed = 2026; (training seed = 42)), together with a strict leave-one-film-out protocol that rotates the three in-house films ({6, 25, 40} Ω/sq) in turn as the held-out test fold; the external DeepBlue and synthetic data remain in the training fold throughout, serving as continuous-Rs and topological priors. This design eliminates inter-ROI leakage. Rs prediction is evaluated by Relative Error (RE) and R2. Transmittance is reported as MAE/RMSE and Spearman ρ, without claiming a learned R2. Uncertainty calibration is assessed by the empirical coverage of 1σ/2σ prediction intervals. The external DeepBlue data set is incorporated as a continuous-Rs training prior, not as an independent external test set. Every real SEM patch inherits the four-point-probe Rs of its parent source film, so all patches cropped from one film share the same ground-truth Rs label. The group-aware held-out film-level mean is the unweighted average over the three held-out film-scale groups (the held-out in-house film at 10 μm and at 100 μm, plus db_028): 6.21% ± 4.66%, whereas the pooled 7.27% aggregates every held-out patch; the two metrics use different aggregation and their confidence intervals overlap, so they are not contradictory. A stricter leave-one-film-out protocol that retrains a model for each held-out film gives per-film errors of 4.75%/7.72%/4.06% (mean 5.51%). In the primary group-aware training, the nine retained DeepBlue films and the synthetic data serve as continuous-Rs/topological priors, and the held-out test fold comprises one in-house film (imaged at both FOVs) plus the DeepBlue film db_028; the external cross-lab row of Table reports db_028’s results from this same split rather than from a separate retraining run.
