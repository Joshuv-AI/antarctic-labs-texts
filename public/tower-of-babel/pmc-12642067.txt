Comparative efficacy of multiple drugs for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease: a Bayesian network meta-analysis
Authors: Zhile Xiao, Xiaonan Li, Jiahong Gong, Feng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), now referred to as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), is the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide. However, it remains unclear which drug treatment is more effective. Therefore, we conducted a network meta-analysis to comprehensively compare the efficacy of several potentially beneficial hypoglycemic drugs and vitamin E in treating patients with NAFLD.MethodsThis Bayesian network meta-analysis searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for randomized controlled trials (RCTs). We employed random-effects models to calculate mean differences (MD), relative risk (RR), and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The study outcomes included anthropometric measurements, biological markers, hepatic fat content, and liver biopsy results. The protocol for this systematic review with network meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024532600).ResultsThis analysis included 26 RCTs with 2143 patients. Based on the surface under the cumulative ranking curve and the network meta-analysis matrix, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) demonstrated superior efficacy compared to other interventions, particularly in terms of weight loss, improvement in liver enzymes, resolution of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), reduction of hepatic fat content, glycemic control, and improvement in insulin resistance. Additionally, compared to placebo, sodium–glucose cotransporter protein-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2I) showed moderate benefits in weight loss (body mass index: MD − 1.20, 95% CI − 1.83 to − 0.71; waist circumference: MD − 2.03, 95% CI − 3.32 to − 0.74) and reduction in liver enzyme levels (alanine aminotransferase: MD − 12.96, 95% CI − 19.24 to − 6.86; aspartate aminotransferase: MD − 9.53, 95% CI − 14.20 to − 5.06). Overall, thiazolidinediones (TZD) provided significant histological benefits; however, they also carried a risk of weight gain. Vitamin E was also found to improve liver enzyme levels and histological features. According to the GRADE framework assessment, most of these findings are supported by evidence of moderate certainty.ConclusionsThe present study suggests that GLP-1RA may be the optimal treatment for NAFLD patients. In addition, current evidence does not sufficiently evaluate the impact of SGLT-2I on reducing liver fat content or histological outcomes. The potential risk of weight gain associated with TZD appears to be a limitation to their use. Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has recently been renamed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), a change that is widely supported by the multisociety Delphi consensus statement [1]. However, the majority of past randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were based on the diagnostic criteria for NAFLD. To maintain consistency, we will continue to use the term NAFLD in our future work.

NAFLD is the most common liver disease worldwide, and its overall global prevalence has increased significantly from a previous estimate of 25.2% to the current estimate of 32.4% [2–4]. NAFLD is defined by more than 5% of hepatocytes displaying macrovesicular steatosis in the absence of a readily identifiable alternative cause of steatosis and excessive alcohol consumption [5]. Histologically, NAFLD encompasses a disease continuum that ranges from simple steatosis of the liver (non-alcoholic fatty liver, NAFL) to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), with the latter covering fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma progression [6].

There is a close bidirectional association between NAFLD and metabolic syndrome [7]. On the one hand, insulin resistance and systemic inflammation are key pathophysiologic mechanisms in NAFLD [8]. On the other hand, a significant proportion of patients with obesity and type 2 diabetes have NAFLD [9, 10]. Concurrently, NAFLD is associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, hepatocellular carcinoma, and extrahepatic cancer, and represents the second leading cause of liver transplantation [11–14]. NAFLD is currently recognized as a global public health challenge [15].

Lifestyle interventions, including weight loss, a healthy diet, physical activity, and regular exercise, represent the fundamental treatment for NAFLD [5]. However, the absence of large-scale studies with adequate statistical power to guide prescriptions, as well as the influence of psychological factors, presents a challenge to the implementation of effective lifestyle interventions [16, 17]. Therefore, a practical and feasible pharmacological treatment is in the spotlight. Although regulatory agencies currently approve no drug for NAFLD or NASH, several antidiabetic medications, diet pills, and vitamin E have shown benefit in multiple clinical trials and are considered possible treatments by current guidelines [5].

Antidiabetic medications or diet pills that may be used in the treatment of NAFLD or NASH include thiazolidinediones (TZD), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), and sodium–glucose cotransporter protein-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2I). Other candidates, including metformin and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4I), have been limited in the treatment of NASH due to their inability to provide meaningful histological benefits in several RCTs [18–21].

However, few studies have directly compared the effects of GLP-1RA, TZD, SGLT-2I, and vitamin E in treating patients with NAFLD or NASH. Furthermore, existing meta-analyses lack a mixed efficacy comparison of vitamin E and the other three drugs. Therefore, we conducted a Bayesian network meta-analysis (NMA) to comprehensively compare the efficacy of these four potentially beneficial drugs on anthropometric, biological, imaging, and liver biopsy-based outcomes, aiming to provide a clinically useful summary of the results of the multi-treatment meta-analysis and to provide a meaningful reference for the development of clinical decisions.

Methods

This study was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-2020, Table S1) and the PRISMA Extension Statement, which is an extension statement for reporting systematic reviews that incorporate network meta-analyses of healthcare interventions [22, 23]. The protocol for this systematic review with network meta-analysis was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024532600).

Search strategy and selection criteria

We searched four major electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, from inception through December 21, 2023. Databases were searched using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and entry terms (Table S2). No other search restrictions were applied. Additionally, we manually searched the relevant literature for references to identify potentially eligible RCTs.

Screening of the literature was performed by three independent reviewers, who read the title, abstract, and full text. Any disagreements that arose during the screening process were resolved through a consultative discussion with a fourth reviewer. The inclusion–exclusion criteria for the literature were developed following the PICOS principles (Table S3). EndNote 20 software managed the references and eliminated duplicate literature from different databases. The process of literature screening is presented in the form of a flowchart based on the PRISMA-2020 guidelines. Table S4 provides information about studies excluded after a full-text assessment.

Study outcomes

Firstly, a comprehensive analysis was conducted on anthropometric outcomes (such as body mass index [BMI] and waist circumference [WC]) and biological outcomes (such as alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], total cholesterol [TC], triglyceride [TG], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], fasting plasma glucose [FPG], and Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance [HOMA-IR]). The HOMA-IR was calculated as follows: fasting glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (μIU/mL)/22.5. In addition, for imaging outcomes based on non-invasive techniques, hepatic fat content, measured by proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) and MRI proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF), was analyzed. Meanwhile, liver biopsy-based outcomes, including resolution of NASH, steatosis score, fibrosis score, and NAFLD activity score (NAS), were assessed. These histology scores were calculated based on the NASH Clinical Research Network scoring criteria [24]. According to the same criteria, the resolution of NASH is defined as the absence of more than mild residual inflammatory cells, no hepatocyte ballooning, and no worsening of liver fibrosis. The primary outcomes were resolution of NASH, BMI, WC, ALT, and AST. Secondary histologic outcomes included steatosis score, fibrosis score, and NAS. Other secondary outcomes included TC, TG, LDL-C, HbA1c, FPG, and HOMA-IR.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

For each eligible study, a standardized data extraction form was pre-designed according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [25], and the following were extracted independently by two researchers: study characteristics (authors, year of publication, major country, duration), population characteristics (number of people in each group, age, gender, diabetes status, NAFLD or NASH status, BMI and HbA1c at baseline), interventions (drug, dose, cointervention), and outcomes. If the study reported the mean and standard deviation of the change before and after the intervention, these data were used directly. If studies did not report a standard deviation equivalent to the change from baseline, or only provided standard errors, 95% confidence intervals (CI), medians, or ranges, calculations were made using the formulas recommended in the Cochrane Handbook and the methodology proposed by Wan et al. [25, 26].

To evaluate the quality of the included studies, two researchers independently assessed the risk of bias using the revised Cochrane Randomized Trials Risk of Bias Tool (2.0), which provides for bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in the selection of the reported result [27]. Each domain was judged as low, unclear, or high risk of bias. After mutual review, any disagreement will be resolved through discussion between the two researchers or mediation by a third researcher.

Statistical analysis

The statistical effect sizes for the outcomes were the weighted mean difference (WMD) for continuous variables, the relative risk (RR) for dichotomous variables (resolution of NASH), and their respective 95% CI. We used drugs in the same category as a treatment node. Since there was no explicit target dose, the specific drug dose was not considered in calculating the combined effect size. Stata version 17.0 was used to plot a comparative network graph.

First, we conducted a direct meta-analysis of the same interventions using a random-effects model to estimate the WMD or RR and 95% CI. We performed direct comparisons and drew forest plots using Review Manager version 5.4. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values greater than 50% considered significant.

Second, in a Bayesian framework, we conducted a network meta-analysis of random effects using Markov chain Monte Carlo methods with WinBUGS version 1.4.3 (MRC Biostatistics Unit, Cambridge, UK). This method combines direct and indirect evidence from each treatment comparison group. The parameter settings were as follows: the number of chains was 5, tuning iterations were 30,000, simulation iterations were 100,000, the thinning interval was 10, inference samples were 25,000, and the variance scaling factor was 2.5. To assess inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons, we used node-splitting models to analyze and calculate p-values, and inconsistency was considered when the p-value was < 0.05 [28]. In addition, we also assessed the probability that each intervention was the most effective option, the second best, the third best, and so on, and visualized the sort order by overlaying the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) and bar charts. To search for evidence of a small study effect in analyses with ten or more studies, we assessed publication bias using comparison-adjusted funnel plots and Egger’s test. The Egger’s test with p-value < 0.05 was considered to be publication bias.

Finally, to assess the stability and reliability of the results, a sensitivity analysis was performed after excluding studies with a high risk of bias.

Assessment of evidence certainty

We employed the methodology recommended by the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) Working Group for assessing the quality of evidence for all study results [29–32]. Assessments of the certainty of evidence for direct estimates included considerations of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. After selecting the best indirect comparison path, the lower of the ratings of the two direct estimates in the path is used as the certainty rating for that indirect estimate. The certainty of the evidence for network comparisons was based on the results of direct and indirect comparisons. The quality of evidence was downgraded if there was inconsistency or imprecision between the two. We used the highest certainty estimate (direct or indirect) as the best estimate of the treatment effect. The quality of the evidence for comparing the two interventions depended on the quality of the evidence for the respective comparisons (direct or indirect) for groups with only direct or indirect comparisons. Evidence certainty ratings for network comparisons include high, moderate, low, and very low.

Finally, we employed a minimally contextualized framework to draw conclusions and interpret results, allowing for the simultaneous consideration of the impact of intervention effect sizes, evidence credibility, and rankings on the interpretation of NMA results [33]. The conclusions include the most effective, the least effective, and the least effective for high certainty evidence, as well as the most effective, probably effective, and probably the least effective for low certainty evidence.

Results

Description of included studies

Based on a pre-defined search strategy, a total of 13,062 articles were obtained from database searches, followed by title and abstract review. After initial screening, the full text was read for detailed assessment. A total of 26 RCTs that met the PICOS principles and inclusion–exclusion criteria were ultimately included in this NMA [34–59]. The process of literature screening is presented in the form of a flowchart based on the PRISMA-2020 guidelines (Fig. 1). A total of 2143 patients were randomized to receive one of the five treatment strategies, and a network graph visualizes the relationship between each intervention (Fig. 2). Risk of bias

Figure 3 presents the risk of bias in each trial. Twenty-three (88.5%) of 26 trials were at low risk of bias in random sequence generation, and 16 (61.5%) were at low risk of bias in allocation concealment. Twenty trials (76.9%) reported blinding of participants and personnel, and 17 trials (65.4%) reported blinding of outcome assessment. Twenty-three trials (88.5%) were adjudicated as being at low risk of attrition bias, with no significant bias in selective reporting. Additionally, 12 trials (46.2%) were at low risk of bias from other sources of bias.

Body mass index and waist circumference

Seventeen trials, including 1303 patients, reported BMI during a median follow-up of 6 months. Nine trials, including 523 patients, reported WC during a median follow-up of 6 months. GLP-1RA and SGLT-2I treatment showed weight loss benefits; the most effective intervention was GLP-1RA (SUCRA: BMI and WC were 98.75% and 98.25%, respectively), followed by SGLT-2I (SUCRA: BMI and WC were 76.25% and 70.75%, respectively; Figure S1 and S2). Compared to TZD and vitamin E, both GLP-1RA and SGLT-2I were significantly more favorable for lower BMI and WC (Fig. 4). In addition, compared with placebo, there was a trend toward a possible risk of weight gain with TZD. Vitamin E may have a trend to increase BMI, but has no significant effect on WC. The results of the meta-analysis are summarized in Figs. 5, S3, and S4.

Alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase

Twenty trials, including 1536 patients, reported ALT during a median follow-up of 6 months. Nineteen trials, including 1462 patients, reported AST during a median follow-up of 6 months. Compared to placebo, GLP-1RA, SGLT-2I, TZD, and vitamin E all reduced the levels of ALT and AST, with the most effective intervention being the GLP-1RA (SUCRA: ALT and AST were 84.00% and 75.25%, respectively, Figure S1 and S2), followed by TZD (SUCRA: ALT and AST were 68.50% and 51.25%, respectively, Figure S1 and S2).

Liver biopsy-based outcomes

Seven trials, including 735 patients, reported resolution of NASH during a median follow-up of 18 months. Six trials, including 388 patients, reported steatosis scores during a median follow-up of 12 months. Seven trials, including 451 patients, reported fibrosis scores during a median follow-up of 12 months. Three trials, including 174 patients, reported NAS during a median follow-up of 12 months. Compared to the placebo, GLP-1RA, TZD, and vitamin E all showed benefits in the resolution of NASH (Fig. 6), with the most effective intervention being GLP-1RA (RR: 2.59, 95% CI 1.73–3.87, SUCRA: 79.33%, moderate certainty). In addition, compared with the placebo, TZD have shown effects in improving steatosis, fibrosis, and NAS, while vitamin E has shown effects in improving steatosis (Fig. 5). Although a statistical difference was not observed, there may be a potential trend for GLP-1RA to improve steatosis, fibrosis, and NAS compared to placebo (Fig. 5).

Hepatic fat content

Three trials, including 222 patients, reported the measurement of hepatic fat content by 1H-MRS during a median follow-up of 26 weeks. Four trials, including 347 patients, reported the measurement of hepatic fat content by MRI-PDFF during a median follow-up of 6 months. Compared with placebo, GLP-1RA treatment reduced hepatic fat content as measured by 1H-MRS (MD: − 6.26, 95%CI − 11.49 to − 1.17, SUCRA: 94.00%, low certainty), while the TZD did not show a statistical difference (MD: − 3.84, 95%CI − 9.04–1.22, SUCRA: 53.00%, low certainty). Both GLP-1RA and TZD were superior to placebo for reduction of hepatic fat content as measured by MRI-PDFF, with TZD likely to be the most effective (MD: − 4.30, 95%CI − 7.75 to − 0.85, SUCRA: 65.33%, moderate certainty). Although not statistically significant, SGLT-2I showed a beneficial trend in reducing MRI-PDFF (Fig. 5).

Other biological outcomes

As shown in Fig. 5, except vitamin E, GLP-1RA, SGLT-2I, and TZD all showed benefit in controlling diabetes-related markers, including FPG, HbA1c and HOMA-IR, with the most effective intervention being GLP-1RA (SUCRA: FPG, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR were 97.25%, 77.75% and 96.25%, respectively, Figure S1 and S2). It is important to note that although SGLT-2I did not show a statistically significant difference in HOMA-IR being superior to placebo, there was a trend toward reducing HOMA-IR as well. As shown in Figure S4, in terms of improvement in lipid profiles, GLP-1RA, SGLT-2I, TZD, and vitamin E all showed a trend toward reduction in TG compared with placebo, with GLP-1RA showing the highest probability (SUCRA: 87.50%); except GLP-1RA, the other three drugs did not show a trend toward reduction in TC; and none of the four drugs showed a trend toward a beneficial effect in improving LDL-C compared with placebo.

Sensitivity analysis and inconsistency assessment

Sensitivity analysis was performed after excluding studies with a high risk of bias. As shown in Table S5, the results of three comparisons in WC, two comparisons in FPG, and one comparison in TG exhibited some instability; however, the overall results remained relatively robust and similar to the original network estimates. Because there is no study with a high risk of bias or a small number of remaining studies, no sensitivity analysis was performed for other outcomes.

We used the node-splitting models to assess inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons. An inconsistency was observed in the comparison of the efficacy of TZD and SGLT-2I in improving HbA1c (P = 0.03, Table S6), as there are no available direct or indirect estimates, and no inconsistency analysis was performed for other outcomes. Overall, there are no significant differences between the direct and indirect comparisons. Thus, the results are relatively robust.

Publication bias

Because it is generally recommended that meta-analysis to draw a funnel plot requires the inclusion of at least ten studies [60], comparison-adjusted funnel plots and p-value calculations for Egger’s test were only performed for the primary outcomes that were provided by a sufficient number of studies. As shown in Figure S5, the Egger’s test did not show a significant publication bias (p-value > 0.05).

Certainty of evidence

We employed the methodology recommended by the GRADE Working Group to assess the quality of evidence for all study results, as shown in Table S7. This table describes the results of all quality of evidence assessments and the reasons for each downgrade. For the primary outcomes, a moderate or high level of quality of evidence was shown for improvements in BMI, WC, and resolution of NASH, while some low or very low quality of evidence was assessed for reductions in ALT and AST. Discussion

This network meta-analysis comprehensively evaluated the therapeutic effects of several potentially beneficial drugs for NAFLD or NASH, assessing conclusions within the minimally contextualized framework recommended by the GRADE working group. The study’s findings may offer clinically valuable summaries and inspire novel directions for future clinical trials.

The 2023 AASLD practice guidance on NAFLD and NASH discusses the potential impacts of GLP-1RA, SGLT-2I, TZD, and vitamin E, yet does not compare their relative efficacy [5]. This meta-analysis provides the first comparative assessment of these four drugs in NAFLD, addressing a gap in efficacy comparisons, particularly regarding vitamin E.

Lifestyle modification and weight management are fundamental to treating NAFLD. Studies indicate that weight loss in overweight or obese patients is associated with cardiovascular and hepatic benefits, ≥ 5% weight loss reduces hepatic steatosis, ≥ 7% weight loss achieves NASH resolution, ≥ 10% weight loss promotes regression or stabilization of hepatic fibrosis, and a weight loss of around 15 kg can lead to remission of most cases of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [61–64]. Patients with NAFLD and a normal BMI may also benefit from appropriate weight loss. However, achieving and maintaining lifestyle modification and weight loss remains challenging. Currently, GLP-1RAs constitute over one-third of drugs approved for weight loss indications, including semaglutide and liraglutide. Additionally, SGLT-2Is are suggested to promote weight loss and have become an evidence-based therapeutic option for overweight or obese patients with T2DM [65]. The results of this NMA indicated that GLP-1RA and SGLT-2I significantly reduced BMI and WC in NAFLD patients, with moderate to high certainty, providing weight-loss benefits. GLP-1RA was considered the most effective. Although TZD demonstrated superior histological benefits overall and were effective in lowering liver enzymes and improving insulin resistance, their potential adverse effects—including weight gain, edema, induction or exacerbation of cardiac insufficiency, and fracture risk—may limit their use in NAFLD [66–68].

In evaluating NAFLD, measuring ALT and AST levels helps assess the extent of liver damage and monitor disease progression. ALT serves as a marker of NAFLD, and in the absence of other chronic liver diseases and significant alcohol intake, elevated ALT levels may indicate NAFLD, warranting further clinical evaluation [69]. A retrospective cohort study in Japan found that ALT was not significantly reduced from baseline and may serve as a predictor of histological progression in NASH patients [70]. Another cohort study reported that higher baseline and elevated AST levels were associated with progression of hepatic fibrosis among NAFLD patients [71]. This NMA suggests that GLP-1RA, SGLT-2I, TZD, and vitamin E improve serum ALT and AST levels in NAFLD patients, with GLP-1RA likely being the most effective intervention for reducing ALT and AST (although with low certainty), followed by TZD (with moderate to high certainty).

The interactions between NAFLD and diabetes are bidirectional and complex. Patients with NAFLD typically exhibit insulin resistance, while T2DM is often accompanied by NAFLD, with NAFLD further promoting the progression of T2DM [72]. Thus, early preventive and therapeutic strategies effective for prediabetes and diabetes, such as TZD, GLP-1RA, and SGLT-2I, should also be considered for NAFLD treatment [73]. Additionally, insulin resistance in fatty liver results in the overproduction of triglyceride-rich very low density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL) particles, which contributes to increased serum TG and LDL levels, further elevating atherosclerotic risk [7]. Unsurprisingly, all three hypoglycemic agents in this study, except for vitamin E, demonstrated glycemic-related benefits. The HOMA-IR is a widely accepted alternative measure of insulin resistance. For improving HOMA-IR, GLP-1RA appeared to be the most effective intervention, followed by TZD, with SGLT-2I showing a potentially beneficial trend; however, all were assessed with low certainty of evidence.

Regarding lipid improvement, all four interventions showed a possible trend toward lowering TG, with GLP-1RA demonstrating a benefit over placebo in sensitivity analyses (MD: − 0.39, 95% CI − 0.77 to − 0.02). Except for GLP-1RA, none of the interventions indicated a trend toward lowering TC, and all four drugs showed no significant benefit over placebo in improving LDL-C. However, conclusions should account for the high heterogeneity in direct comparisons.

Beyond insulin resistance, oxidative stress and systemic inflammation are also recognized as key pathogenic factors in NAFLD, with a strong link observed between NAFLD severity and oxidative stress levels [8, 74]. Given the favorable anti-apoptotic and antioxidant properties of vitamin E, its use has been proposed for the treatment of NAFLD. Most clinical trials evaluating vitamin E in NAFLD have been limited to nondiabetic patients, emphasizing its potential for use in nondiabetic NASH patients. However, a retrospective study involving 236 patients with NASH and advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis demonstrated that vitamin E treatment reduced the risk of death or transplantation and the rate of hepatic decompensation, with benefits observed in both diabetic and nondiabetic patients [75]. More clinical evidence is needed to support these findings in broader populations. Nonetheless, safety concerns regarding vitamin E have been noted; high doses (especially > 400 IU/day) may increase the risks of all-cause mortality, hemorrhagic stroke, and prostate cancer, though evidence remains conflicting and of low certainty [76–80]. Additional prospective data are necessary to confirm these risks. Overall, vitamin E appears relatively safe and may be considered for selective use.

Liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing NAFLD, though its use in studies is limited due to invasiveness, sampling errors, risk of complications, and subjective evaluation bias. Non-invasive imaging techniques for quantifying liver fat content and assessing hepatic steatosis are valuable for early diagnosis of NAFLD and selecting clinical treatment options, with MRI-PDFF considered to have the highest diagnostic accuracy [81]. This NMA suggests that TZD offer better histological benefits overall and reduce MRI-PDFF. GLP-1RA showed the most promising results for NASH regression and liver fat reduction; however, more head-to-head studies are needed to confirm improvements in steatosis, fibrosis, and NAS. Additionally, vitamin E appears beneficial for the regression of steatosis and NASH, but there are currently no studies focusing on liver fat content. SGLT-2I demonstrated a beneficial trend in reducing MRI-PDFF, although studies assessing liver biopsy results remain limited.

This study’s analysis of histological endpoints is based on clinical trial data from patients with biopsy-confirmed NASH, aligning with the current gold standard for drug clinical trials. However, in clinical practice, the majority of NAFLD patients are diagnosed through non-invasive methods such as ultrasound, transient elastography, or serum biomarkers. This population vastly exceeds the biopsy-confirmed cohort and exhibits significant heterogeneity. Translating findings from strictly defined trial populations to broader clinical cohorts presents particular challenges. The drug’s mechanism of action, evaluated by this institute, targets the core pathophysiology of NAFLD. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that it may also demonstrate potential benefits in earlier stages of NAFLD, such as simple steatosis, although its absolute efficacy may be smaller than that observed in NASH patients. Consequently, future effectiveness studies involving broader populations of NAFLD patients based on non-invasive diagnostics are needed to further validate the drug’s efficacy across different disease stages.

Our study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this NMA is the first to include GLP-1RA, SGLT-2I, TZD, and vitamin E in a single comparative network, and to explore the efficacy of different types of drugs for NAFLD in terms of their drug classes. This approach offers a new direction for developing novel treatments for NAFLD and selecting medications for clinical trials. Second, we comprehensively assessed the potential efficacy of various therapeutic strategies using both invasive and non-invasive outcome measures.

Our study also has limitations. First, the search terms covered only a limited number of individual drugs, as many were either in clinical trials or did not meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria. However, the medications included, such as semaglutide, liraglutide, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, and pioglitazone, are still representative. Future trials into novel therapies based on other drugs will be crucial for determining optimal treatment strategies. Second, because of the invasive and risky nature of liver biopsy, as well as the high cost and limited accessibility of MRI, there is limited literature on liver biopsy results and hepatic fat content. Furthermore, this study has not evaluated the impact of SGLT-2I on liver histology and the effect of vitamin E on hepatic fat content, which needs to be probed by more head-to-head studies in the future. Additionally, a limitation of this study is that the number of included trials was insufficient to perform subgroup analyses based on drug dose and diabetes status. For dose-dependent drugs like GLP-1RA, this might affect the precise estimation of effect sizes. More head-to-head RCTs with fixed-dose will be needed in the future to directly compare these drugs, thereby providing more accurate guidance for clinical practice. Lastly, due to limited literature, a small portion of the results relied solely on direct or indirect comparisons, requiring caution when interpreting findings related to histology and liver fat content.

Conclusions

Overall, GLP-1RA is effective in weight management, reducing liver enzymes and hepatic fat content, improving insulin resistance, and slowing histological progression in patients with NAFLD or NASH. It may be the treatment of choice in specific circumstances. SGLT-2I offer modest benefits in weight loss, liver enzyme reduction, and glycemic control; however, current evidence does not sufficiently evaluate their impact on reducing liver fat content or histological outcomes, warranting further investigation in future studies. TZD generally provide superior histological benefits compared to other treatments, but the associated risk of weight gain should be considered. Vitamin E has also demonstrated positive liver-related effects and may be a suitable option for nondiabetic patients with NAFLD or NASH, although its efficacy in those with comorbid diabetes remains unproven. Notably, improvements in liver histology or biomarkers have not been definitively demonstrated to translate into reduced risks of long-term clinical outcomes such as cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, cardiovascular events, or all-cause mortality. Further head-to-head clinical trials are needed to validate the specific effects and potential mechanisms of these drugs on NAFLD or NASH, laying the groundwork for viable pharmacological treatment options.
