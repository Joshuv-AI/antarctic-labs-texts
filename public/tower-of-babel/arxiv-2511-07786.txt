A Closed-Form Framework for Schrödinger Bridges Between Arbitrary Densities
Hanwen Huang

Abstract
Score-based generative models have recently attracted significant attention for their ability to generate high-fidelity data by learning maps from simple Gaussian priors to complex data distributions. A natural generalization of this idea to transformations between arbitrary probability distributions leads to the Schrödinger Bridge (SB) problem. However, SB solutions rarely admit closed-form expressions and are commonly obtained through iterative stochastic simulation procedures, which are computationally intensive and can be unstable. In this work, we introduce a unified closed-form framework for representing the stochastic dynamics of SB systems. Our formulation subsumes previously known analytical solutions—including the Schrödinger–Föllmer process and the Gaussian SB—as specific instances. Notably, the classical Gaussian SB solution, previously derived using substantially more sophisticated tools such as Riemannian geometry and generator theory, follows directly from our formulation as an immediate corollary. Leveraging this framework, we develop a simulation-free algorithm that infers SB dynamics directly from samples of the source and target distributions. We demonstrate the versatility of our approach in two settings: (i) modeling developmental trajectories in single-cell genomics and (ii) solving image restoration tasks such as inpainting and deblurring. This work opens a new direction for efficient and scalable nonlinear diffusion modeling across scientific and machine learning applications.



Keywords:
Diffusion process; Generative model; Neural network; Single-cell; Stochastic differential equation

1
Introduction

Diffusion-based generative models have seen rapid development and broad application across machine learning domains, including computer vision
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
)
, image analysis
(
Wang and
Ponce 2021
;
Pu
et al. 2015
)
, natural language processing
(
Shah 2021
;
Fu 2019
)
, and drug discovery
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
. These models construct a stochastic process that transforms a simple reference distribution, such as a multivariate Gaussian, into a complex data distribution. A central class within this family is score-based generative models (SGMs)
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho
et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2020
;
Song
et al. 2021
)
, which currently achieve state-of-the-art sample quality across multiple domains. SGMs couple two stochastic processes: a forward diffusion process that gradually corrupts data into noise, and a reverse denoising process that reconstructs data from noise. Both can be described as stochastic differential equations whose endpoints match the data and prior distributions.

While SGMs excel at data synthesis, many scientific applications—such as single-cell analysis—require learning the stochastic dynamics that interpolate between two observed distributions, rather than generating samples from noise. In these settings, the goal is to infer the most likely probabilistic paths connecting initial and terminal state distributions. This has led to growing interest in the Schrödinger Bridge (SB) framework
(
Bernton
et al. 2019
;
Chen
et al. 2023
;
Bortoli
et al. 2023
;
Shi
et al. 2023
;
Chen
et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Stromme 2023
)
, which generalizes diffusion-based models. SB seeks a stochastic process that evolves between two marginals while staying close to a reference process, typically Brownian motion.

In general, SB problems lack closed-form solutions except in special cases (e.g., both marginals Gaussian
(
Bunne
et al. 2023
)
). As a result, most methods rely on iterative numerical approximations
(
Bortoli
et al. 2023
;
Chen
et al. 2023
)
, which can be unstable and scale poorly in high dimensions
(
Shi
et al. 2023
)
. To overcome these computational limitations, we propose a closed-form framework for learning the stochastic dynamics between two arbitrary distributions. We derive, for the first time, an explicit analytical relation between the dynamic formulation of the SB problem and the solution to its corresponding static problem. Specifically, we show that the solutions of the SB system admit analytic expressions that depend solely on integrations with respect to the endpoint joint distributions. Remarkably, this result yields a unifying perspective that encompasses all previously known closed-form SB solutions, including the Schrödinger–Föllmer process—which transports a degenerate Dirac delta distribution at time zero to a target distribution at time one—and the Gaussian Schrödinger Bridge, which transports one multivariate Gaussian to another, as special cases.

Establishing this closed-form link between the dynamic SB formulation and static endpoint densities enables an efficient and tractable computation of interpolation dynamics, implemented as Algorithm 1, without resorting to iterative proportional fitting or neural network–based regression. For paired data, Algorithm 1 can be directly applied to compute the SB drift. For unpaired data, paired samples can first be generated using an optimal transport algorithm and then used within Algorithm 1 to recover the stochastic dynamics. Furthermore, our formulation admits an equivalent variational (minimization) representation, from which we derive a simulation-free algorithm, implemented as Algorithm 2, for inferring stochastic dynamics directly from samples drawn from arbitrary source and target distributions. This method provides a training-free framework that avoids the computational complexity and instability often associated with iterative, training-based approaches. Moreover, it generalizes and unifies several existing simulation-free formulations—including those proposed in
Liu et al. 2023
;
Tong et al. 2024
—as special cases.

Beyond SB-based methods, several other approaches have recently been proposed for learning nonlinear transformations between arbitrary distributions. A prominent class among them is the flow-based models, or continuous normalizing flows (CNFs), which assume a deterministic continuous-time generative process governed by an ordinary differential equation (ODE) that transports the source density to the target density. Similar to score-based generative models, CNFs admit closed-form solutions only when the source distribution is Gaussian. For arbitrary source distributions, their applicability has been limited by simulation-based training objectives that require expensive numerical integration of the ODE during training. Recent advances have introduced simulation-free training objectives that improve the efficiency of CNFs and make them competitive with SGMs. However, these methods still require training neural networks and, more importantly, do not extend to stochastic dynamics, which are essential both for generative modeling and for recovering the underlying dynamics of complex systems.

Our specific contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We develop a closed-form framework for studying Schrödinger Bridge (SB) systems that connect arbitrary probability distributions. This framework unifies and generalizes all previously known closed-form SB solutions, including the Schrödinger–Föllmer process and the Gaussian Schrödinger Bridge, as special cases. In particular, the well-known closed-form solution to the Gaussian Schrödinger Bridge problem—previously derived using considerably more sophisticated tools such as Riemannian geometry and generator theory in
Bunne
et al. 2023
—emerges as an immediate corollary of our framework (see Corollary 1). To the best of our knowledge, this constitutes the most general closed-form result to date in the line of research on analytical solutions to the SB problem.

•

Building on this framework, we introduce a training-free algorithm (Algorithm 1) for learning stochastic dynamics from both paired and unpaired datasets. This one-step method eliminates the need for neural network training and the associated challenges of architecture selection, making it computationally efficient and easy to implement. The algorithm proves particularly effective for image restoration and modeling the temporal evolution of single-cell genomic data.

•

Through an equivalent variational formulation, we further derive a simulation-free algorithm (Algorithm 2) for inferring stochastic dynamics directly from samples drawn from arbitrary source and target distributions. Unlike prior simulation-free approaches—such as those in
Liu et al. 2023
;
Tong et al. 2024
—which restrict the reference process to standard Brownian motion, our formulation accommodates arbitrary Itô processes as references. As demonstrated in Section
4
, careful selection of the reference process can lead to substantial performance gains.

4

2
A closed-form framework for solving Schrödinger Bridge problem

In this section, we first provide background on the Schrödinger Bridge (SB) process. We then introduce a tractable formulation that explicitly connects the dynamic formulation of the SB problem to the solution of its corresponding static problem. Finally, we apply this formulation to the Gaussian SB case and rederive its well-known closed-form solutions—previously obtained through considerably more sophisticated frameworks such as Riemannian geometry and generator theory.

2.1
Background on Schrödinger Bridge

We are interested in find nonlinear functions that transfor one distribution
\mu_{0}({\bf x})
to another distribution
\mu_{1}({\bf x})
for
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our algorithms are implemented through constructing a stochastic diffusion process
\{{\bf x}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{1}
indexed by a continuous time variable
t\in[0,1]
such that
{\bf x}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
, the initial distribution, and
{\bf x}_{1}\sim\mu_{1}
, the target distribution. Denote by
\Omega=C([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space consisting of all
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued continuous functions on the time interval
[0,1]
and
\mathbb{P}
the measure over
\Omega
induced by the following SDE

\displaystyle d{\bf x}_{t}={\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)dt+\sigma(t)d{\bf w}_{t},

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{0}\sim\mu_{0},

(2.1)

where
{\bf w}_{t}
is the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion,
{\bf b}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a vector-valued function called the drift coefficient of
{\bf x}_{t}
, and
\sigma(t):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a scalar function known as the diffusion coefficient of
{\bf x}_{t}
. When
{\bf b}({\bf x},t)=0
and
\sigma(t)=1
,
{\bf x}_{t}
is just the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. The SDE (
2.1
) has a unique strong solution as long as the drift coefficient function
{\bf b}({\bf x},t)
is globally Lipschitz in both state
{\bf x}
and time
t

(
Oksendal 1992
)
.

Denote by
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
the marginal probability law at time
t
for the probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
on
\Omega
. We write
{\cal D}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=\{\mathbb{Q}:\mathbb{Q}_{0}=\mu_{0},\mathbb{Q}_{1}=\mu_{1}\}
for the set of all path measures with given marginal distribution
\mu_{0}
at the initial time and
\mu_{1}
at the final time. Then the solution of the SB problem with respect to the reference measure
\mathbb{P}
can be formulated by the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{\star}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{argmin}_{\mathbb{Q}\in{\cal D}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}D(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{P}),

(2.2)

where
D(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{P})
denotes the relative entropy between two probability measures on
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
which is defined as

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{P})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\int\log(d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P})d\mathbb{Q}&if~\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}\\
\infty&otherwise\end{array}\right.,

where
\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}
denotes that
\mathbb{Q}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
and
d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}
represents the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mathbb{Q}
w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
.

2.2
A closed-form fromulation to the Schrödinger Bridge problem

Denote
\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
the set of all couplings between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. The static Schrodinger bridge consists in finding the joint distribution
\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
which is closest to the reference prior subject to marginal constraints, that is

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}=\text{argmin}_{\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}D(\mu\|\mathbb{P}_{01}),

(2.6)

where
\mathbb{P}_{01}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
denotes the joint distribution at times
0
and
1
for the reference measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\Omega
which is given by
\mathbb{P}_{01}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=\mu_{0}({\bf x}_{0})q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})
, where with
q(t_{1},{\bf x},t_{2},{\bf y})
denotes the transition density of
{\bf x}_{t_{2}}={\bf y}
at time
t_{2}
given
{\bf x}_{t_{1}}={\bf x}
at time
t_{1}
for stochastic process
{\bf x}_{t}
governed by the reference SDE (
2.1
). For Brownian motion reference process, i.e.
{\bf b}({\bf x},t)=0
and
\sigma(t)=\sigma
, (
2.6
) becomes the entropically-regularized optimal transport problem defined as

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}=\text{argmin}_{\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\left\{\int\frac{\|{\bf x}_{1}-{\bf x}_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}d\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})+D(\mu\|\mu_{0}\otimes\mu_{1})\right\},

(2.7)

where
\mu_{0}\otimes\mu_{1}
is the joint distribution over
{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}
in which
{\bf x}_{0}
and
{\bf x}_{1}
are independent.

Denote the gradient of a smooth function
f({\bf x})
by
\nabla f({\bf x})
and the partial derivative with respect to
{\bf x}
of
\psi({\bf x},{\bf y})
for
({\bf x},{\bf y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\nabla_{{\bf x}}\psi({\bf x},{\bf y})
.
We state in the following theorem the solution
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
to the SB problem (
2.2
) whose reference measure
\mathbb{P}
is induced by the SDE (
2.1
).

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
\sigma(t)\in C^{1}([0,1])
and the components of
{\bf b}({\bf x},t)
are bounded continuous and satisfy Hölder conditions with respect to
{\bf x}
, i.e., there are real constants
C\geq 0,~\alpha\textgreater 0
such that
|b_{i}({\bf x},t)-b_{i}({\bf y},t)|\leq C\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|^{\alpha}
for all
i=1,\cdots,d
and
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the solution probability measures
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
to the SB problem (
2.2
) is induced by the following SDE with a modified drift:

\displaystyle d{\bf x}_{t}=[{\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)+{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x}_{t},t)]dt+\sigma(t)d{\bf w}_{t},~~~{\bf x}_{0}\sim\mu_{0},

(2.8)

where the drift term
{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)
is given by

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\int s_{{\bf x}_{1}}({\bf x},t)g_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\mu^{\star}(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})}{\int g_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\mu^{\star}(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})},

(2.9)

where
\mu^{\star}
is the solution to the static problem (
2.6
),
s_{{\bf x}_{1}}({\bf x},t)
is the conditional score defined as

\displaystyle{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}}(t,{\bf x}_{t})=\nabla_{{\bf x}_{t}}\log q(t,{\bf x}_{t},1,{\bf x}_{1}),

(2.10)

and

\displaystyle g_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{q(0,{\bf x}_{0},t,{\bf x})q(t,{\bf x},1,{\bf x}_{1})}{q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})}.

(2.11)

The proof is given in Appendix
B
. Theorem
1
shows that we can start from initial sample
{\bf x}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
and update the values of
\{{\bf x}_{t}:0<t\leq 1\}
according to the SDE (
2.8
) in continuous time, the value
{\bf x}_{1}
has the desired distributional property, that is,
{\bf x}_{1}\sim\mu_{1}
.

For the solution to the SB problem (
2.8

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\int s_{{\bf x}_{1}}({\bf x},t)\frac{q(t,{\bf x},1,{\bf x}_{1})}{q(0,{\bf a},1,{\bf x}_{1})}\mu_{1}(d{\bf x}_{1})}{\int\frac{q(t,{\bf x},1,{\bf x}_{1})}{q(0,{\bf a},1,{\bf x}_{1})}\mu_{1}(d{\bf x}_{1})}.

(2.12)

This is a well-known result that has been studied in

;

;
Tzen and

. When the reference process

is induced by (

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x}_{t},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{argmin}_{{\bf u}({\bf x}_{t},t)}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int|{\bf u}({\bf x}_{t},t)|^{2}dt\right],

(2.13)

s.t.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}d{\bf x}_{t}=[{\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)+{\bf u}({\bf x}_{t},t)]dt+\sigma(t)d{\bf w}_{t},\\

We can simplify (

\pi({\bf x}_{1}|{\bf x})\equiv q(t,{\bf x},1,{\bf x}_{1})

\pi({\bf x}|{\bf x}_{0})\equiv q(0,{\bf x}_{0},t,{\bf x})

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\int\frac{{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}}(t,{\bf x})\pi({\bf x}|{\bf x}_{0})\pi({\bf x}_{1}|{\bf x})}{\pi({\bf x}_{1}|{\bf x}_{0})}\mu^{\star}(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})}{\int\frac{\pi({\bf x}|{\bf x}_{0})\pi({\bf x}_{1}|{\bf x})}{\pi({\bf x}_{1}|{\bf x}_{0})}\mu^{\star}(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\int{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}}(t,{\bf x})\pi({\bf x}|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\mu^{\star}(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})}{\int\pi({\bf x}|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\mu^{\star}(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})}

\displaystyle\sigma(t)^{2}\int{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}}(t,{\bf x})\pi({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}|{\bf x})\mu^{\star}(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})

\displaystyle\sigma(t)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\pi({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}|{\bf x})}{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}}(t,{\bf x}),

2.3
Exact Solution of the Schrödinger Bridge Problem for Multivariate Gaussian Measures

Note that in (
2.9
), the transition kernel corresponds to the reference process defined by (
2.1
). To simplify computation, one may select a reference SDE that admits a closed-form solution. In this paper, we consider a general class of reference measures
\mathbb{P}
that encompasses most processes commonly used in machine learning applications of Schrödinger bridges (SBs). Specifically, given an initial condition
{\bf x}_{0}
, we define
\mathbb{P}
as the measure induced by the following linear stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\displaystyle d{\bf x}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{c(t){\bf x}_{t}+\alpha(t)}dt+\sigma(t)d{\bf w}_{t},

(2.18)

where
c(\cdot):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha(\cdot):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\tau(t)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds\right)
. Then, the solution to (
2.1
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{t}=\tau(t)\left({\bf x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\alpha(s)}{\tau(s)},ds+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sigma(s)}{\tau(s)}d{\bf w}_{s}\right).

(2.19)

Using the independent increments of
{\bf x}_{t}
and Itô’s isometry, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}({\bf x}_{t}|{\bf x}_{0})=\tau(t)\left({\bf x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\alpha(s)}{\tau(s)}ds\right)=\eta(t),

and, for any
t^{\prime}\geq t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[({\bf x}_{t}-\eta(t))({\bf x}_{t^{\prime}}-\eta(t^{\prime}))^{T}|{\bf x}_{0}]=\big(\tau(t)\tau(t^{\prime})\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sigma(s)^{2}}{\tau(s)^{2}}ds\big){\bf I}_{d}=\kappa(t,t^{\prime}){\bf I}_{d}.

Importantly, the expression in (
2.18
) subsumes all three reference SDEs proposed in
Song et al. 2020
, which have been successfully employed in a variety of probabilistic generative modeling tasks:

•

Variance Exploding (VE) SDE:

\displaystyle c(t)=0,~~\alpha(t)=0.

When
\sigma(t)=\sigma
, this corresponds to the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.

•

Variance Preserving (VP) SDE:

\displaystyle c(t)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t),\alpha(t)=0,\sigma(t)=\sqrt{\beta(t)},

(2.20)

where
\beta(\cdot):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. When
\beta(t)=1
, the process reduces to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.

•

Sub-Variance Preserving (sub-VP) SDE:

\displaystyle c(t)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t),\alpha(t)=0,\sigma(t)=\sqrt{\beta(t)\left(1-e^{-2\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s),ds}\right)}.

The VE SDE produces trajectories with diverging variance as
t\to\infty
, whereas the VP SDE yields processes with bounded variance. In particular, if the initial distribution
{\bf x}_{0}
has unit variance, the VP SDE preserves this property for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. The two principal classes of score-based generative models—score matching with Langevin dynamics (SMLD) and denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM)—can be viewed as discrete approximations of the VE and VP SDEs, respectively
(
Song et al. 2020
)
.

The following corollary provides the analytic solution to the SB problem between Gaussian measures.

Corollary 1
.

.
Let
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{N}(\nu_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{0})
and
\mu_{1}=\mathcal{N}(\nu_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1})
be two arbitrary Gaussian distributions. Then, for the reference measure defined by (
2.18
), the closed-form expression of (
2.9
) is given by

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(t)^{2}\left(\frac{\tau(1)}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}[r(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}+\bar{r}(t)C_{\sigma_{\star}}^{T}]-\frac{\tau(1)^{2}}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}[\bar{r}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{0}+r(t)C_{\sigma_{\star}}]-\rho(t){\bf I}_{d}\right)

(2.21)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{t}^{-1}\left\{{\bf x}-\bar{r}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{0}-r(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{1}-\zeta(t)+r(t)\zeta(1)\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\tau(1)}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{1}-\zeta(1))-\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\tau(1)^{2}}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{0},

where
\sigma_{\star}^{2}=\kappa(1,1)/\tau(1)
,

\displaystyle D_{\sigma}=(4\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{0}^{1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{0}^{1/2}+\sigma^{4}{\bf I}_{d})^{1/2},

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{0}^{1/2}D_{\sigma}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{0}^{-1/2}-\sigma^{2}{\bf I}_{d}),

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{t}={\bf I}_{d}+\frac{\tau(t)^{2}\kappa_{1}(t,t)}{\kappa(t,t)\kappa(1,1)}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{0}+\frac{\tau(1)^{2}\kappa(t,t)}{\tau(t)^{2}\kappa_{1}(t,t)\kappa(1,1)}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}+\frac{\tau(1)}{\kappa(1,1)}(C_{\sigma_{\star}}+C_{\sigma_{\star}}^{T}),

and the functions
\tau(t),\zeta(t),\kappa(t,t),\kappa_{1}(t,t),r(t),\bar{r}(t),\rho(t)
are all defined in Appendix
C
.

The complete derivation is presented in Appendix
C
, where it is verified that (
2.21
) exactly matches the well-known result in Theorem 3 of
Bunne
et al. 2023
. Although the algebraic computation is somewhat tedious, the derivation itself is conceptually straightforward, as all transition kernels and the joint distribution
\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
in (
2.9
) are multivariate Gaussians. In contrast, the proof in
Bunne
et al. 2023
relies on considerably more sophisticated tools from entropic optimal transport, Riemannian geometry, and generator theory.

Practical Implementation

In this section, we detail the implementation of our closed-form framework described in Theorem

3.1
Training-free algorithm

The drift term (
2.9
) involves integration over the joint distribution
\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
, which typically lacks a closed-form expression. To evaluate (
2.9
) accurately, one must estimate the probability density
\hat{\mu}^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
from available samples. This estimation is particularly challenging in high-dimensional settings, as
\mu^{\star}
is often multi-modal or concentrated on a low-dimensional manifold, making it difficult to approximate using simple distributional families such as Gaussians or Gaussian mixtures. Classical density estimation methods include kernel-based techniques
(
Parzen 1962
)
, interpolation-based methods
(
Wahba 1975
)
, and diffusion-based approaches
(
Masry 1983
)
. However, these techniques are primarily effective in low-dimensional settings and do not scale efficiently to higher dimensions.

In this work, we exploit two key properties of formula (
2.9
) to construct a direct estimator for the drift coefficient
{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)
.
First, since (
2.9
) involves only expectations under
\mu^{\star}
, these integrals can be approximated by empirical averages:

\displaystyle\int g_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\mu(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)},{\bf x}_{1}^{(i)}),

(3.1)

which leads to the following estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf u}}^{\star}({\bf x},t)=\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}^{(i)}}({\bf x},t)g_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)},{\bf x}_{1}^{(i)})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{t}({\bf x},{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)},{\bf x}_{1}^{(i)})}.

(3.2)

This formulation completely avoids neural network training, thereby eliminating architectural design considerations and substantially reducing computational cost. The second advantage arises from the fact that the transition density
q(t_{1},{\bf x},t_{2},{\bf y})
in (
2.11
) is defined with respect to the reference process, which can often be computed in closed form for suitable choices of the drift
{\bf b}({\bf x},t)
and diffusion
\sigma(t)
in (
2.1
). For example, when
{\bf b}({\bf x},t)
is affine in
{\bf x}
, as in (
2.18
), the transition kernel becomes Gaussian.

The joint distribution
\hat{\mu}^{\star}
in (
2.9
) corresponds to the solution of the static Schrödinger Bridge problem (
2.6
), which is equivalent to the entropically regularized optimal transport (EOT) problem:

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}=\text{argmin}_{\mu\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\left\{\int\|{\bf x}_{1}-(\tau(1){\bf x}_{0}+\zeta(1))\|^{2}d\mu+2\kappa(1,1)\int\log\mu,d\mu\right\},

(3.3)

under the reference SDE (
2.18
). Given paired data
\left\{{\bf x}_{0}^{(i)},{\bf x}_{1}^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we can minimize the empirical objective

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bf x}_{1}^{(i)}-(\tau(1){\bf x}_{0}^{(i)}+\zeta(1))\|^{2},

to estimate the parameters
\hat{\tau}(1)
and
\hat{\zeta}(1)
, which in turn define an optimal reference SDE process. Once the reference SDE is specified, the corresponding function
g_{t}
in (
2.9
) can be substituted to obtain the drift estimator in (
3.2
).

In practice, paired samples are rarely available. Instead, we often have access only to i.i.d. samples forming two empirical marginal distributions
\hat{\mu}_{0}
and
\hat{\mu}_{1}
, of size
m
and
n
. In this case, one can obtain synthetic paired samples by solving the EOT problem (
3.3
) between
\hat{\mu}_{0}
and
\hat{\mu}_{1}
. Importantly, empirical approximations of EOT remain accurate even in high-dimensional spaces
(
Aude
et al. 2016
)
, and recent results show that the Schrödinger Bridge inherits this favorable property
(
Stromme 2023
)
. The EOT plan between empirical distributions can be efficiently computed using the Sinkhorn algorithm
(
Cuturi 2013
)
, which has
{\cal O}(n^{2})
complexity
(
Altschuler et al. 2017
)
, or via stochastic optimization methods
(
Aude
et al. 2016
)
. The complete training-free Schrödinger Bridge (TFSB) estimation procedure is summarized in Algorithm

3.2
Simulation-free deep neural network training algorithm

Leveraging insights from score-based generative learning, we introduce the following theorem, which provides an alternative characterization of the drift term
{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)
as a conditional expectation. This formulation naturally leads to a quadratic objective function for which
{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)
emerges as the unique minimizer.

Theorem 2
.

The drift term
{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x}_{t},t)
in (
2.8
) is the time-varying vector field
{\bf u}({\bf x}_{t},t)
that minimizes the quadratic objective

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bf Q}}\left\|\sigma(t)^{2}{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}}(t,{\bf x}_{t})-{\bf u}({\bf x}_{t},t)\right\|^{2},

(3.4)

where
{\bf Q}=[t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)]\otimes\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\otimes\pi({\bf x}_{t}|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
, the conditional score is defined in (
2.10
), and the conditional bridge distribution is given by

\displaystyle\pi({\bf x}_{t}|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=\frac{q(0,{\bf x}_{0},t,{\bf x}_{t})\,q(t,{\bf x}_{t},1,{\bf x}_{1})}{q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})}.

(3.5)

Equation (
3.4
) provides a practical foundation for designing deep learning algorithms to estimate
{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x}_{t},t)
nonparametrically. In particular, one can approximate
{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)
by training a neural network
{\bf u}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}({\bf x},t)
on i.i.d. samples
t_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
,
({\bf x}_{0,i},{\bf x}_{1,i})\sim\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
, and
{\bf x}_{t,i}\sim\pi({\bf x}_{t,i}|{\bf x}_{0,i},{\bf x}_{1,i})
. The resulting regression problem seeks to minimize the expected squared error in (
3.4
), thereby learning the time-dependent drift
{\bf u}^{\star}
as a function of
({\bf x},t)
given joint samples
({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})\sim\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
. In this way, the estimation task reduces to a supervised learning problem for the drift field.

It is worth noting that Theorem
2
encompasses several existing simulation-free estimation methods for the Schrödinger Bridge problem as special cases. For instance,
Tong et al. 2024
propose a simulation-free score and flow matching framework for learning stochastic dynamics from unpaired samples drawn from arbitrary source and target distributions. Their method introduces two neural networks to match the conditional ODE flow
{\bf u}^{o}({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
and the conditional score
\nabla_{{\bf x}}\log p_{t}({\bf x}|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
separately, after which the conditional drift is reconstructed as

{\bf u}({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})={\bf u}^{o}({\bf x},t|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})+\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla_{{\bf x}}\log p_{t}({\bf x}|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}).

The regression-based training objective in (

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our algorithm on both simulated and real datasets. We begin with two-dimensional toy examples to illustrate the ability of our method to learn multimodal distributions. We then show how our approach can be adapted for modeling single-cell dynamics. Finally, we validate our method on a range of image restoration tasks—including inpainting, super-resolution, deblurring, and JPEG restoration—using the CIFAR-10 datasets.

Once the drift function
{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)
is obtained, the diffusion process (
2.8
) can be used to generate samples from the target distribution
\mu_{1}
by transporting the initial distribution
\mu_{0}
at
t=0
to
\mu_{1}
at
t=1
. Since the SDE (
2.8
) is defined over the finite interval
[0,1]
, we implement this procedure numerically using the Euler–Maruyama discretization. Specifically, we divide
[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
into
N\geq 2
grid points,
\epsilon=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=1-\epsilon
with step sizes
\delta_{j}=t_{j+1}-t_{j}
. To mitigate numerical instability near the boundaries
t=0
and
t=1
, we truncate the interval using a small
\epsilon>0
. The discretized update rule is given by

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{t_{j+1}}={\bf x}_{t_{j}}+\delta_{j}[{\bf b}({\bf x}_{t_{j}},t_{j})+{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x}_{t_{j}},t_{j})]+\sigma(t_{j})\sqrt{\delta_{j}}\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{j},\quad j=0,1,\ldots,N-1,

(4.1)

where
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are i.i.d. random vectors drawn from
N(0,{\bf I}_{d})
. The drift term
{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x}_{t_{j}},t_{j})
is estimated using either Algorithm
1
or Algorithm
2
.

4.3
Image restoration

Image restoration is a fundamental problem in computer vision and image processing, with applications ranging from optimal filtering
(
Mary Mathew et al. 2023
)
and data compression
(
Wallace 1992
)
to adversarial defense
(
Nie et al. 2022
)
and safety-critical domains such as medical imaging and robotics
(
Li
et al. 2021
)
. Recent advances have leveraged diffusion and score-based generative models for restoration tasks; see, for example,
Saharia
et al. 2022
;
Kawar
et al. 2022
. However, as noted by
Liu et al. 2023
, most diffusion-based restoration methods initiate the generative process from Gaussian white noise, which contains little to no structural information about the underlying clean image distribution.

This observation highlights the advantage of Schrödinger bridge (SB) based diffusion models for image restoration: the degraded observation itself provides a structurally informative prior, in stark contrast to random noise. In particular, Theorem
1
shows that the SB dynamics admit analytic solutions when provided with boundary pairs (clean and degraded images). This leads directly to a training-free framework (TFSB) or a simulation-free framework (SFSB), both of which avoid the computational overhead typically associated with diffusion model training or trajectory simulation.

We evaluate our method on four standard restoration tasks—super-resolution, deblurring, inpainting, and JPEG artifact removal—using CIFAR-10 images of size
3\times 32\times 32
. For 4× super-resolution, we downsample images to
8\times 8
and then upsample them back to
32\times 32
so that the clean and degraded domains share the same resolution. Inpainting is performed using masks that remove the central 25% of pixels. Deblurring is done using Gaussian blur. For JPEG artifact removal, we apply JPEG compression with a quality factor of 10.

Because TFSB directly constructs diffusion bridges between the clean and degraded domains, the resulting generative trajectories are more interpretable: the restored image emerges progressively from the degraded input, as illustrated in Figures
2
and
3
. This structural guidance also improves sampling efficiency, since the starting point of generation is already close to the target image manifold. Figures
2
and
3
visualize the full restoration trajectories obtained using TFSB under the reference VP-SDE (
2.20
), demonstrating successful recovery of the target images.

Finally, all experiments were conducted on a consumer-grade laptop without access to a dedicated GPU. This prevents direct comparison with GPU-based methods or scaling to larger datasets such as ImageNet (
3\times 256\times 256
). With sufficient computational resources, we expect comparable performance to
Liu et al. 2023
, as their simulation-free training procedure can be viewed as a special case of our more general framework.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a closed-form framework for learning the stochastic dynamics of Schrödinger bridge systems by deriving an explicit expression for the drift function of the associated SDE. This result provides a unified theoretical perspective that encompasses all previously known closed-form SB solutions. The formulation is notably simple, requiring only the transition probabilities of the reference process and integration with respect to the joint target distribution. Building on this insight, we developed a training-free algorithm (Algorithm 1) capable of learning stochastic dynamics from both paired and unpaired data. The method operates in a single step, avoiding neural network training and the associated challenges of architectural design, making it computationally efficient and straightforward to implement. Through an equivalent variational formulation, we further proposed a simulation-free procedure for inferring dynamics directly from samples drawn from arbitrary source and target distributions. These approaches prove particularly effective for image restoration and for modeling temporal evolution in single-cell genomic data. Experimental results on both low-dimensional multi-modal distributions and high-dimensional image datasets demonstrate that our method achieves competitive performance relative to state-of-the-art models while remaining simple, efficient, and computationally lightweight. Moreover, unlike previous simulation-free approaches which restrict the reference dynamics to Brownian motion, our framework accommodates general Itô reference processes.

A known limitation, however, is that the closed-form drift solution (
3.2
) tends to memorize samples, which constrains its ability to generate genuinely novel outputs
(
Pidstrigach 2022
)
. To mitigate this issue,
Scarvelis et al. 2025
proposed smoothing the closed-form score using convolution with zero-mean noise together with efficient nearest-neighbor approximations. Their modification enables novel sample generation while retaining a training-free formulation. As future work, we intend to incorporate similar smoothing strategies into our framework to enhance generative capability. In addition, we plan to explore the integration of convolutional architectures from recent neural methods into our smoothing-based, training-free TFSB approach to further improve empirical performance.

Our formulation also suggests several promising extensions. One natural direction is to consider conditional settings in which the stochastic dynamics are learned between two marginal distributions conditioned on an auxiliary variable
{\bf z}
. This can be achieved by introducing a neural function
{\bf u}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}({\bf x},{\bf z},t)
in (
3.6
) to incorporate the conditional dependence, similar to conditional SGMs
(
Song et al. 2020
)
and conditional SB formulations
(
Huang and
Huang 2025
)
. Another direction is to generalize the approach to multi-marginal problems, following recent developments in multi-marginal Schrödinger bridges
(
Shen
et al. 2025
)
. We expect these avenues to broaden the applicability of our framework to a wider class of nonlinear diffusion models.

Appendix A
Static Formulation

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mu^{\star}
denote the solution of the static Schrödinger Bridge problem, i.e. the joint distribution
\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
that minimizes the Kullback–Leibler divergence to a given reference distribution
\bar{\mu}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
subject to prescribed marginals
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. Formally,

\mu^{\star}=\text{argmin}_{\mu\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}D(\mu|\tilde{\mu}),

(A.1)

where
\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
is the set of couplings with marginals
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. Assume the reference transition density
q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})
is positive on the relevant domain. Then there exist strictly positive functions
\hat{\rho}_{0},\rho_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that the optimal coupling factors as

\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1}).

(A.2)

Moreover,
\mu^{\star}
attains the prescribed marginals, which can be written in the form

\mu_{0}({\bf x}_{0})=\rho_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0}),\qquad\mu_{1}({\bf x}_{1})=\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1})\hat{\rho}_{1}({\bf x}_{1}),

(A.3)

with the consistency relations

\displaystyle\rho_{0}({\bf x}_{0})=\int q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1})d{\bf x}_{1},

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{1}({\bf x}_{1})=\int q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0})d{\bf x}_{0}.

Proof.

The optimization problem (
A.1
) can be written as:

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=\text{argmin}_{\mu\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\int\left\{\log[\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})]-\log[q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})]\right\}\mu(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1}).

(A.4)

Introduce the constrained minimization (
A.4
) by adjoining dual potentials (Lagrange multipliers)
\lambda_{0}({\bf x}_{0})
and
\lambda_{1}({\bf x}_{1})
to form the Lagrangian by

\displaystyle{\cal L}(\mu,\mu_{0},\mu_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left\{\log[\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})]-\log[\bar{\mu}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})]\right\}\mu(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})

(A.5)

\displaystyle+\int\lambda_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\left(\int\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})d{\bf x}_{1}-\mu_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\right)d{\bf x}_{0}

\displaystyle+\int\lambda_{1}({\bf x}_{1})\left(\int\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})d{\bf x}_{0}-\mu_{1}({\bf x}_{1})\right)d{\bf x}_{1}.

Then, differentiating the Lagrangian with respect to
\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
yields the first-order optimality condition

\displaystyle\log\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})-\log[\bar{\mu}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})]+1+\lambda_{0}({\bf x}_{0})+\lambda_{1}({\bf x}_{1})=0.

Rearranging, we get

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=\mu_{0}({\bf x}_{0})q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\exp(-\lambda_{0}({\bf x}_{0})-\lambda_{1}({\bf x}_{1})-1),

which we can re-express in terms of the auxiliary potentials
\hat{\rho}_{0},\rho_{1}
:

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0})q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1}),

satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\int q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1})d{\bf x}_{1}=\mu_{0}({\bf x}_{0}),

\displaystyle\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1})\int q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0})d{\bf x}_{0}=\mu_{1}({\bf x}_{1}),

where we re-label the terms with the integrals to

\displaystyle\rho_{0}({\bf x}_{0})=\int q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1})d{\bf x}_{1},

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{1}({\bf x}_{1})=\int q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0})d{\bf x}_{0}.

These relations are equivalently written as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\rho_{0}({\bf x}_{0})=\mu_{0}({\bf x}_{0}),

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{1}({\bf x}_{1})\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1})=\mu_{1}({\bf x}_{1}),

which is the Schrödinger system (or Sinkhorn scaling equations) for the pair
(\hat{\rho}_{0},\rho_{1})
.
∎

Remark.

The scaling functions
\hat{\rho}_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
are determined only up to the multiplicative normalization
(\hat{\rho}_{0},\rho_{1})\rightarrow(c\hat{\rho}_{0},c^{-1}\rho_{1})
for

, which leaves the coupling

invariant. Given any choice of
\hat{\rho}_{0}
and
\rho_{1}

the functions

\displaystyle\rho_{t}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\int q(t,{\bf x},1,{\bf x}_{1}),\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1}),d{\bf x}_{1},

(A.6)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{t}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\int q(0,{\bf x}_{0},t,{\bf x}),\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0}),d{\bf x}_{0},

(A.7)

where
q(s,{\bf x}_{s},t,{\bf x}_{t})
denotes the transition density of the reference process (
2.1
). By standard properties of transition kernels,
\hat{\rho}_{t}({\bf x}_{t})
satisfies the forward Fokker–Planck equation associated with (
2.1

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}_{t}({\bf x}_{t})=-\nabla_{{\bf x}_{t}}\cdot\{{\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)\hat{\rho}_{t}({\bf x}_{t})\}+\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}}{2}\bigtriangleup\hat{\rho}_{t}({\bf x}_{t}),

(A.8)

while

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}({\bf x}_{t})=-\{\nabla_{{\bf x}_{t}}\rho_{t}({\bf x}_{t})\}\cdot{\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)-\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}}{2}\bigtriangleup\rho_{t}({\bf x}_{t}).

(A.9)

Set

A.8
) and (
A.9
) shows that

satisfies the forward Fokker–Planck equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{t}({\bf x}_{t})=-\nabla_{{\bf x}_{t}}\cdot[\{{\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)+\sigma(t)^{2}\nabla_{{\bf x}_{t}}\log\rho_{t}({\bf x}_{t})\}p_{t}({\bf x}_{t})]+\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}}{2}\bigtriangleup p_{t}({\bf x}_{t}).

Consequently,
p_{t}
is the time-

marginal of the diffusion process governed by the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle d{\bf x}_{t}=[{\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)+\sigma(t)^{2}\nabla_{{\bf x}_{t}}\log\rho_{t}({\bf x}_{t})]dt+\sigma(t)d{\bf w}_{t},~~{\bf x}_{0}=\mu_{0}.

(A.10)

In other words, the drift correction
\sigma(t)^{2}\nabla_{{\bf x}_{t}}\log\rho_{t}({\bf x}_{t})
transforms the reference dynamics into the optimal dynamics whose marginals are given by

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
1

To prove Theorem
1
, we begin by reformulating the KL divergence
D(\mathbb{Q}|\mathbb{P})
so as to disentangle the contribution of the terminal distribution
\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
from that of the path measures. This separation is made possible by the disintegration theorem, which decomposes the divergence into one part depending only on the terminal distributions and another part depending on the conditional path laws. We then relate this decomposition to the drift and diffusion terms of the SDEs in (
2.1
) and (
2.8
) via Girsanov’s theorem.

Recall that
\mathbb{Q}_{01}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
denotes the terminal distribution of
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\mathbb{Q}^{{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}=\mathbb{Q}(({\bf x}_{t})_{t\in(0,1)}|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})

{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}
under
\mathbb{Q}
. Then
\mathbb{Q}(({\bf x}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]})=\mathbb{Q}^{{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}\mathbb{Q}_{01}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
. Analogously, under the reference measure
\mathbb{P}
, we define
\mathbb{P}^{{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}=\mathbb{P}(({\bf x}_{t})_{t\in(0,1)}|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
. If
\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}
(so
D(\mathbb{Q}|\mathbb{P})<\infty
), the disintegration theorem yields

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{Q}|\mathbb{P})=D(\mathbb{Q}_{01}|\mathbb{P}_{01})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{Q}_{01}(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})D(\mathbb{Q}^{{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}|\mathbb{P}^{{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}).

(B.1)

Denote by
\mu
and
\bar{\mu}
the terminal joint laws of
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
, respectively, i.e.,
\mathbb{Q}_{01}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=\mu({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
and
\mathbb{P}_{01}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=\bar{\mu}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
. Since
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
, the marginals of
\mu
are
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. The marginal distribution of
\bar{\mu}
with respect to
{\bf x}_{0}
is
\mu_{0}
, and it admits the factorization
\bar{\mu}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=\mu_{0}({\bf x}_{0})q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})
. Substituting into (
B.1
) gives

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{Q}|\mathbb{P})=D(\mu|\bar{\mu})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(d{\bf x}_{0},d{\bf x}_{1})D(\mathbb{Q}^{{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}|\mathbb{P}^{{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}})\geq D(\mu|\bar{\mu}).

This inequality has a clear interpretation: the divergence between
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
is always at least as large as the divergence between their terminal distributions. Equality holds only when
\mathbb{Q}^{{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}=\mathbb{P}^{{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}
for

, i.e. when the conditional path laws coincide. It follows that

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in{\cal D}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}D(\mathbb{Q}|\mathbb{P})=D(\mu^{\star}|\bar{\mu}),

(B.2)

with minimizer given by
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}=\mathbb{Q}^{{}^{\star}{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
, where
\mathbb{Q}^{{}^{\star}{\bf x}_{0}{\bf x}_{1}}=\mathbb{P}(({\bf x}_{t})_{t\in(0,1)}|{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})
and
\mu^{\star}
is the solution to the static SB problem

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}=\inf_{\mu\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}D(\mu|\bar{\mu}),

with
\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
denotes the set of all couplings between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. In other words, the optimal

is a
\mu^{\star}
-weighted mixture of reference trajectories. This representation often admits a closed-form expression and highlights how the target distribution
\mu^{\star}
reweights the reference process.

According to Lemma
1
, there exists a pair of functions
\hat{\rho}_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the measure
\mu^{\star}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1})=q(0,{\bf x}_{0},1,{\bf x}_{1})\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1})

(B.3)

which has marginals
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}

\displaystyle\mu_{0}({\bf x}_{0})=\rho_{0}({\bf x}_{0})\hat{\rho}_{0}({\bf x}_{0}),

\displaystyle\mu_{1}({\bf x}_{1})=\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1})\hat{\rho}_{1}({\bf x}_{1}),

(B.4)

where

Recall that the reference path measure
\mathbb{P}
corresponds to the solution of the SDE

\displaystyle d{\bf x}_{t}={\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)dt+\sigma(t)d{\bf w}_{t},\qquad{\bf x}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}.

(B.5)

The Hölder condition imposed on

To construct the optimal

\displaystyle d{\bf x}_{t}=\big[{\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)-\sigma(t)^{2}\nabla\phi({\bf x}_{t},t)\big]dt+\sigma(t)d{\bf w}_{t},\qquad{\bf x}_{0}\sim\mu_{0},

where

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\sigma(t)\nabla\phi({\bf x}_{t},t)\cdot d{\bf w}_{t}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(\partial_{t}\phi({\bf x}_{t},t)+\nabla\phi({\bf x}_{t},t)\cdot{\bf b}({\bf x}_{t},t)+\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}}{2}\bigtriangleup\phi({\bf x}_{t},t)\right)dt+\phi({\bf x}_{0},0)-\phi({\bf x}_{1},1).

Substituting back into (

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\phi({\bf x}_{0},0)-\phi({\bf x}_{1},1)\right.

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(t)^{2}\nabla\log\rho_{t}({\bf x})=\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\int\nabla_{{\bf x}}q(t,{\bf x},1,{\bf x}_{1})\rho_{1}({\bf x}_{1})d{\bf x}_{1}}{\rho_{t}({\bf x})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{t}+\sigma(t)^{2}\left(\frac{\tau(1)}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}[r(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}+\bar{r}(t)C_{\sigma^{\star}}]-\frac{\tau(1)^{2}}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}[\bar{r}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{0}+r(t)C_{\sigma^{\star}}^{T}]-\rho(t){\bf I}_{d}\right).

With this notation, equation (29) in Theorem 3 of
Bunne
et al. 2023
can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle S(t)^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{t}^{-1}\left\{{\bf x}-\bar{r}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{0}-r(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{1}-\zeta(t)+r(t)\zeta(1)\right\}+\dot{\bar{r}}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{0}+\dot{r}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{1}+c(t)\zeta(t)+\alpha(t)-\dot{r}(t)\zeta(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(t)^{2}\left(\frac{\tau(1)}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}[r(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{1}+\bar{r}(t)C_{\sigma^{\star}}^{T}]-\frac{\tau(1)^{2}}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}[\bar{r}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{0}+r(t)C_{\sigma^{\star}}]-\rho(t){\bf I}_{d}\right)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{t}^{-1}\left\{{\bf x}-\bar{r}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{0}-r(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{1}-\zeta(t)+r(t)\zeta(1)\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\tau(1)}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{1}-\zeta(1))-\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}\tau(1)^{2}}{\tau(t)\kappa(1,1)}\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{0}+c(t){\bf x}+\alpha(t),

which coincides exactly with (
C.87
). It is worth noting that Theorem 3 in
Bunne
et al. 2023
establishes this result using more advanced mathematical machinery—specifically, the central identity from quantum field theory and the infinitesimal generator framework. In contrast, our derivation arrives at the same expression through direct and elementary manipulations of the multivariate normal density, offering a more transparent and computationally accessible perspective.

For the SDE in (

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x},t)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bf Q}_{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}|{\bf x}_{t}={\bf x}}}\left[\sigma(t)^{2}{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}}(t,{\bf x}_{t})\right],

as shown in (
2.17

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bf Q}_{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{t}}}\left[\left|\sigma(t)^{2}{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}}(t,{\bf x}_{t})-{\bf u}({\bf x}_{t},t)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bf Q}_{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{t}}}\left[\left|\sigma(t)^{2}{\bf s}_{{\bf x}_{1}}(t,{\bf x}_{t})-{\bf u}^{\star}({\bf x}_{t},t)\right|^{2}\right].

), note that the inner product term vanishes:
