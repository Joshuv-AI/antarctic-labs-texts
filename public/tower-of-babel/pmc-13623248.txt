Hindi translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the Liverpool University Neuroleptic Side-effect Rating Scale (LUNSERS): A cognitive debriefing study
Authors: Hemant Choudhary, Tulika Shukla, Vaibhav Patil, Koushik S. Deb, Rakesh K. Chadda, Mamta Sood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The Liverpool University Neuroleptic Side-Effect Rating Scale (LUNSERS) is a comprehensive self-report tool for assessing subjective adverse events during antipsychotic treatment.Aim:We aimed to translate LUNSERS into Hindi for its application in the Indian population.Methods:We followed the translation methodology provided by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) 2005 task force (English to Hindi). We completed the forward (n = 2) and back translation (n = 2), reconciliation by an expert panel (n = 5), and cognitive debriefing (20 participants). After suggested culturally relevant changes, the final version was drafted.Results:Participants included Hindi-speaking adult male participants on second-generation antipsychotics. Reported side effects included sedation, tiredness, tension, restlessness, difficulty concentrating, and memory problems. A total of eight items were revised following cognitive debriefing. The influence of “Hinglish” was a factor influencing a change in interpretation.Conclusion:The Hindi-translated self-report tool (LUNSERS) can help screen antipsychotic-related side effects in a busy Indian clinical setting.

INTRODUCTION

The use of antipsychotics has encountered a shift in recent decades.[1] Among other factors, side effects due to the use of antipsychotics guide the choice of the agent for treatment. Numerous tools exist to record antipsychotic side effects, which can help guide drug selection and dosing. Self-report tools can be easily filled out by patients while waiting for their consultation, saving crucial time during busy clinics.[2] The Liverpool University Neuroleptic Side-Effect Rating Scale (LUNSERS) is one such comprehensive self-report instrument for assessing and quantifying the subjective adverse events during antipsychotic treatment.[3] When required, it can also be administered by mental health workers if the client is unable to complete it. It is available in English, German, and Romanian.[3]

Hindi is a common language in the northern part of India.[4] There is limited availability of self-reported measures for antipsychotic side-effect rating in the Hindi language. The Glasgow Antipsychotic Side-Effect Scale (GASS) is available but limited to assessment of antipsychotic side-effects related to second-generation antipsychotics. Its official translated version is not readily available in the database.[5] LUNSERS provides a more comprehensive assessment, including side-effect domains related to first-generation antipsychotics as well. Additionally, it has “red-herring” items to monitor for overreporting during the assessment.[3] Translation of a patient-reported outcome measure into an Indian language has its own set of challenges, including low health and questionnaire literacy, cultural mediation of health expressions, response style biases, and requires a systematic approach.[67] To date, no validated or adapted Hindi version of LUNSERS exists, limiting its use in routine clinical practice in Northern India. Therefore, we planned this study with the aim of translating the LUNSERS scale into Hindi using standard translation methodology.

METHODS

The validity and reliability of LUNSERS are established.[389] It consists of 51 items, with each item scored on a five-point Likert rating scale of 0–4. For the purpose of cross-cultural adaptation of the LUNSERS, we adopted the methodology provided in the translation guidelines of the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) 2005 task force.[1011] The guidelines comprise several steps for adopting a translation of patient-reported measures. These include obtaining permission from the developers of the scale, constituting a bilingual expert panel, two forward translations into the target language (e.g., English to Hindi), two backward translations into the original language, and cognitive debriefing. The cognitive debriefing was conducted with a selected number of participants (e.g., 10) to check for cultural and linguistic acceptability. The final draft was compiled after reconciliation of inputs from cognitive debriefing and shared with the developers. The translation process was completed at a tertiary center in North India. The ethics approval for the study was obtained from the Institute Ethics Committee (IEC-409/02.07.21). The authors adopted the following steps for translation [Supplementary Figure 1]:

Phase 1: Forward and back translations of the LUNSERS

The authors of the original LUNSERS were contacted, and permission was obtained for the Hindi translation. A panel of five bilingual (proficiency in reading and writing both English and Hindi) experts working in the field of mental health was formed. Forward translations (English to Hindi) by two bilingual individuals were later compiled into a single draft (LUNSERS Hindi V1). This draft was given to two other bilingual people who had not seen the original scale before, and they back-translated it (Hindi to English). This was reviewed by the expert panel, and the final draft was made by consensus of all the experts (LUNSERS Hindi V2).

Phase 2: Cognitive Debriefing of the LUNSERS Hindi V2 draft

After the developers’ review and approval, the cognitive debriefing was conducted with the LUNSERS Hindi V2 draft. The questionnaire for the cognitive debriefing interview was developed to pre-test the instrument on the target population (10–15 participants) using in-depth personal interviews.[10]

For cognitive debriefing, 20 participants aged 18 to 60 years, of either sex, and currently on daily antipsychotic medications for greater than 2 weeks were recruited from the psychiatry outpatient clinic. Interviews for cognitive debriefing were conducted by a single interviewer who was fluent in both languages and was a psychiatrist familiar with the application of LUNSERS. We included participants having a history of any affective or non-affective psychotic illness as per ICD-10 criteria. Those with severe psychiatric or medical illness hindering the interview or unwilling to participate were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient or their family members. Participant data were collected cross-sectionally from December 2024 to February 2025.

The participants were interviewed systematically, using the cognitive debriefing questionnaire, which had questions for each item of the scale. The questions asked about their understanding of the item, rephrasing in their own words, their choice of answer, understanding of all words, any unacceptable or offensive words, and any alternative expressions. The responses were audio-recorded after taking consent. Written notes were also taken during the interview.

After the interviews, there was a cognitive debriefing review by the expert panel. The audio records were referred to where required. The questions for cognitive debriefing were organized into domains of understandability (yes/no), unacceptable words or phrases (yes/no), and suggested alternatives for expression. The data on specific domains were categorized based on the number of participants. The expert panel reviewed the responses and suggested alternatives, with the compilation of a final draft (LUNSERS Hindi V3) after discussion. Descriptive analysis was used to summarize the participant data.

RESULTS

The LUNSERS Hindi V2 was compiled after forward and backward translation and expert discussion regarding several items, as provided in Table 1 and Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. The participants for cognitive debriefing included adult males (median 30 years) (n = 14) belonging to middle socio-economic status (n = 13). Common diagnoses in the participants included bipolar disorder (n = 11) and schizophrenia (n = 7). All participants were receiving second-generation antipsychotics for a median of 6 months [Supplementary Table S3].

DISCUSSION

We translated the LUNSERS into Hindi. During our cognitive debriefing, we found that several participants had difficulty understanding the translated terms used to describe “periods” and “difficulty achieving climax.” This could be related to the hesitancy surrounding the discussion of topics related to sexual health.[12] The language or “slang” used to describe various sexual terms differs from the classical Hindi terminologies.[12]

Another important highlight was the suggestion of English language terms by the participants for better expression of certain translated terms in the LUNSERS Hindi V2 draft. An example is the term “concentrating,” where several participants suggested the use of the term “focus” in place of the Hindi-translated word “ekagrata”. This could possibly be due to the uncommon use of the more formal and complex Hindi language terminologies and integration of adopted words from English literature into the Hindi language.[13] It also reflects the integration of English literature with the Hindi language in day-to-day communication for the general population. Recent literature supports this notion, highlighting the rise of “Hinglish” speaking individuals.[13]

The strength of our study is that we followed the WHO and ISPOR 2005 task force guidelines and frequently requested inputs from the developers. The study limitations include having no psychometric validation, with a small, single-center sample, and difficulties in generalizability to all Hindi-speaking individuals. This translated version requires further validation research.

This study provides a perspective on the translation of a self-reported instrument. We selected LUNSERS for translation into Hindi after considering its comprehensive nature, clinical relevance, reliability, validity, and relevance of the translated language to the population of our region. This translated self-report tool can help screen antipsychotic-related side effects in a busy clinic and inform clinicians about the side effects experienced by individuals at a glance. Future work on this translated version could elaborate further on other psychometric properties and improve the validity of the Hindi translated version of LUNSERS.
