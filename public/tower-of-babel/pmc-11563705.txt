Views on the prevention of SWI and selection strategy of its risk factors
Authors: Dongsheng Chen, Chun Liang, Libo Luo, Youbai Chen
Published: 2024

We are pleased to read your comments on our published study Risk factors for sternal wound infection after median sternotomy: A nested case–control study and time‐to‐event analysis. We agree with you on the significance of prevention and treatment of sternal wound infection (SWI) and we would like to share some opinions to your questions.

We previously performed a meta‐analysis 1 including 81 studies regarding the risk factors for SWI. Thirty‐two studies analysed the association between myocardial infarction (MI) and SWI and three of them 2 , 3 , 4 (9%) identified MI as an independent risk factor for SWI with a pooled odds ratio [OR] of 1.43. In addition, 64 studies analysed the association between age and SWI, and five of them (8%) identified age as an independent risk factor for SWI with a pooled OR of 1.26. So there are not many studies demonstrating that age is a risk factor for SWI. This is because that most adult patients who underwent median sternotomy and developed SWI have similar ages (around 60 years in our study). This concentrated distribution cannot show any significant association between age and SWI. To better understand the relationship between MI and age in our cohort, we performed Spearman correlation test and found that the correlation coefficient is 0.156 (p = 0.001). In other words, age is significantly associated with MI, but not SWI in our study because the study population belongs to the same age group. There might be some interaction effect between age and MI that affects the outcome, but the effect is not significant enough. Therefore, we believe that MI is an independent risk factor for SWI and its significance is not due to the old age of MI patients.

We agree with you that the conclusion may have more clinical significance if we could treat diabetes mellitus (DM) as a continuous variable, for example, the length of DM history, or even the mean value of blood glucose level. However, this information is occasionally missing in the electronic medical records considering the 10‐year time span of this study. So treating DM as a dichotomous variable is to maximize the use of data. This is also consistent with most previous studies that 76 studies in our meta‐analysis treated DM as a dichotomous variable. As for body mass index (BMI), the present study already treated it as a continuous data. Because of the highly concentrated distribution of BMI, a stratification of BMI and treating it as a categorical variable may lose some information and underestimate its significance on SWI. The current analysis is able to demonstrate that the greater the BMI values, the higher the risk of SWI. The HR of BMI is 1.083 (1.012–1.160), p = 0.022, indicating that each unit increase in BMI was associated with 8.3% higher risk of SWI.

We agree that the treatment of superfacial sternal wound infection (SSWI) and deep sternal wound infection (DSWI) is different and important. However, the scope of this study is to identify independent risk factors for SWI, including superficial or deep SWI. Although treatment of SWI is out of the scope of our study, we would like to share some views on the treatment of SWI. The treatment of SSWI only requires routine dressing change and nutrition enhancement. However, DSWI requires antibiotic use, negative pressure wound therapy, debridement, PRP therapy and even reconstruction with muscular flaps for those with extensive involvement. 5 Therefore, prediction of the risk of SWI preoperatively is very important. This is the reason that we construct an easy nomogram for clinical use. We strongly advocate that great attention should be paid and more rigorous perioperative management should be performed in patients with higher risk of SWI. For example, an multi‐disciplinary treatment including cardiac surgeon, plastic surgeon, nutritionist, pharmacist, infectionist and nurse may be helpful to prevent SWI in high‐risk patients.
