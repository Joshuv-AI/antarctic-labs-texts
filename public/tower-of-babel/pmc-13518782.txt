Ungulate geophagy maintains termite mound habitat heterogeneity and increases internal mound temperature
Authors: Stein R Moe, Cathrine Glosli, Ole Gunnar Støen, Paul Okullo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSavannas are globally important ecosystems and unique in supporting abundant and diverse populations of wild large mammals. Mound building fungus-farming Macrotermitinae are key determinants of savanna structure and function. Termite colonies are sensitive to shade, and their lifetime reproductive success is substantially reduced in forests compared to open savanna areas. Here we experimentally show how ungulates, predominantly impala modify mound soil and vegetation cover and consequently internal temperature in Macrotermes subhyalinus epigeal nests. There are large differences in soil nutrient concentrations between the eastern and western termite mound sector. Ungulates selectively feed on the eastern side of the mounds, reducing vegetation cover and exposing that sector to the warming morning sun. Soil sodium concentration, an essential nutrient for heterotrophs is high on the eastern open mound sector on termite mounds. Important plant nutrients are accumulated on the western side of mounds, facilitating growth of vegetation that shade mounds from the hot afternoon sun. When excluding large herbivores from mounds by fencing, differences in sodium soil concentrations between the eastern and western side of the mound are no longer evident while other soil concentration differences are less pronounced. Following fencing, trees gradually established on the eastern side and eventually mounds were completely covered by trees. Shading arising from ungulate exclusion reduce internal mound temperature by 1.6 degrees, resulting in sub-optimal conditions for termite colonies. This study reveals unknown relationships between key functional groups in tropical savannas. The abundance of large iconic wild herbivores is declining over large African savanna areas. Introduction

Termites are important determinants of African savanna structure and function1 and their epigeal termite nests or mounds are prominent features of many savanna landscapes across tropical areas2. Their wide distribution can be easily seen on aerial and satellite photos because, in many open savannah areas, mounds are often the only patches with dense tree growth2. Termites are major decomposers in savannas and important agents of heterogeneity through redistribution and concentration of soil nutrients3,4 organic matter and moisture5.

Many large Macrotermes mounds are covered with trees and grass6. Macrotermes mounds in Sub-Saharan Africa are used by a wide range of plant and animal species and, particularly in nutrient poor areas, mound vegetation serves as foraging hotspots for many ungulate herbivores7,8. Effects of termites and ungulate herbivores interact in determining spatial variations in savanna plant9,10, bird11 and small mammal12 species composition, abundance and diversity.

Macrotermitinae (Family Termitidae) comprises fungus-farming termites that have an ectosymbiotic relationship with Basidiomycete fungi13. The symbiotic relationship between the termites and the fungi is a classic example of mutualism in ecological literature. The fungi decompose substances like lignin and cellulose, thereby reducing the C:N ratio of termite food. In return the fungi benefit by an optimal microclimate, accessing digestible plant material, protection from competitors and prevention of microbial infection13.

Regulations of temperature, CO2 and moisture pose a challenge for termite colonies and associated fungus cultures. Large, mound building termites use several strategies to regulate temperature and CO2. The most well know example is the magnetic termites (Amitermes meridonalis) of Australia where mounds are oriented north–south and termites concentrate around the eastern side where mounds are heated by the morning sun14.

External habitat conditions may also be important for termite fitness. Sub-optimal forest habitat with lower ambient temperatures compared to more open savanna can reduce lifetime reproductive success of termite colonies15.

Located just south of the equator, Lake Mburo National Park has high densities of large Macrotermes subhyalinus mounds (henceforth simply termed termite mounds). The mounds are characteristic features in the landscape, with substantial higher woody cover than the surrounding open savanna. A typical mound has a dense canopy cover shading it from the sun, except for a clearly demarcated open sector with little or no woody vegetation typically in an easterly direction. In this study we set out to illuminate any role and origin of these spatial differences in the density of woody cover on the mounds. Specifically, we aimed to determine whether the spatial differences in mound vegetation cover were caused directly by termite activities, or by external factors like for example ungulate herbivory.

Methods

Field survey and experiment

The study was conducted in Lake Mburo National Park in the south-west of Uganda (00° 30′–00° 42′S and 30° 47′–31° 04′E). The area has two rainy seasons (October–December and February–June), with an average annual rainfall and average maximum temperature in the nearby city of Mbarara of 1032 mm and 26.4 °C, respectively (http://www.worldclimate.com). The vegetation comprises relatively dry open savanna, woodland and swamps. Thicket clumps (9 ha−1), typically associated with termite mounds (10 ha−1), are a prominent feature of the savanna landscape6. Lake Mburo National Park has a biomass density of 86.64 kg ha−1 of wild ungulate herbivores. Common species are impala Aepyceros melampus, bushbuck Tragelaphus scriptus, zebra Equus quagga, waterbuck Kobus ellipsiprymnus, African buffalo Syncerus caffer and warthog Phacochoerus africanus 16.

In an experimental set-up we selected nine pairs of Macrotermes mounds within a 260 km2 area between June-July 2005. At each site we randomly selected one of the paired mounds to be fenced. To exclude ungulate herbivores, we used 2 m high galvanized fences with 5 cm mesh. The fence was supported by 2 m high steel angle-bars and fixed to the ground with concrete (Fig. 1). The plots varied from 90–260 m2 depending on the size of the mounds at the site and the fenced and unfenced mounds were of the same size at each site. Radius of the experimental mounds varied from 2.38 to 5.13 m (mean 3.23 m (± 0.71 SD), n = 18) and mean mound height was 1.83 m (± 0.46 SD). The inter-mound distances varied from 22 to 53 m with mean of 35 m (± 0.11 SD). Only termite mounds that were active (i.e. termites present in the mounds) at the onset of the experiment were included.

Fenced and unfenced Macrotermes mounds. The small photo shows a fenced mound in February 2006, 7 months after fencing. Re-growth has already started on the open sector. The large picture shows the same mound in September 2008, when the open sector is hardly visible. The unfenced experimental mound to the left, as well as two other mounds in the background of the picture, have a distinct open sector pointing east.

To study the landscape scale (i.e. at the scale of our 260 km2 study area) variability of the number of mounds with the area of the mound with sparse or no vegetation (henceforth termed open sector) pointing east we randomly (i.e. we drove a random distance on the park road network, randomly selected a compassing bearing from the road and then walked a random distance in the selected compass direction and finally selected the closest mound to our arrival point) selected 39 termite mounds for the estimation of the direction of the open sector versus the sector with a denser vegetation cover (henceforth termed covered sector). Each mound has a clearly demarcated boundary between the open and covered sector where the open sector has sparse or no woody vegetation, and the covered sector has relatively denser tree cover (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). The border between the open and covered sector of the mound could be visually determined relatively easy (see Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Standing at the base of the mound the compass direction of each of the open sector boundaries were determined. We determined the main compass direction of the open zone by calculating the mid-point between its sector boundaries.

To study seedling regeneration on mounds with and without fencing, we recorded seedlings, from March 2007 to March 2008, on open and covered zones17. We counted all seedlings 1–150 cm in height and with a basal diameter > 1 mm within fenced and open mounds on all sites. Re-sprouts could be separated from seedlings because re-sprouts have characteristic leaves resembling those of mature trees, re-sprouts typically also have multiple stems and relatively large basal diameters compared to seedlings.

The adjacent savanna matrix has only scattered woody vegetation that generally not shade the mounds and mounds could therefore easily be inspected from a distance see (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). In January 2014, we visually estimated vegetation cover, on the open and covered sector of the mounds. We defined vegetation cover as the proportion of the ground surface of the mound that was covered by the tree canopy by standing 10 m away from the middle of the open sector and then 10 m away from the opposite side (i.e. covered sector) of the mounds. This was done on all the 9 pairs of experimental mounds, and to increase sample size on 21 additional randomly selected mounds.

We determined ungulate feeding position in relation to open and covered sector on 275 different mounds in June and July 2007. We used a car on the road network in the park. Whenever ungulate herbivores were observed feeding on a mound, their feeding position on the mounds (i.e. open or covered sector) was noted using an instantaneous scan sampling technique18. Following the feeding observations on each of these mounds, the bearings of the open sector was recorded and used to estimate the area of the open and covered sector. In total 610 animals (366 impala, 89 zebra, 65 waterbuck, 55 warthog, 28 topi, 4 bushbuck, 2 reedbuck and 1 buffalo) were observed feeding on the 275 different mounds.

Soil samples were collected in 2008 and 2009. To standardise sampling, we measured the height of the mound and sampled soil halfway between the top and the base of the mound. Samples were taken from the upper 15 cm soil layer in the middle of the open sector and the covered sector, respectively. Soil samples from all mounds from the experimental set-up (n = 18 from the open and covered sector of the mounds) was analysed for Na, P, K, Mg, Ca, C, Total N (TN) and Kjeldahl-N (TKN). To increase sample size, we sampled 10 additional random mounds that were analysed for Na, P, K, Mg and Ca. Soil samples were analysed at the Eurofins laboratory, Moss, Norway. Samples were crushed and passed through a 2-mm sieve and the < 2-mm soil fraction was analysed according to Egner, Riehm & Domingo (1960)19. The exchangeable calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na) and potassium (K) were extracted with 100 mL of 0.1 M ammonium lactate + 0.4 M acetic acid (pH 3.75). Na and K were determined by a flame photometer; Ca and Mg by atomic absorption spectrophotometry; and phosphorus by colorimetric technique. Organic carbon (C) was determined by the Walkley–Black method20, total nitrogen (TN) by the Dumas combustion method and Total Kjeldahl nitrogen (TKN) by the Kjeldahl method.

To explore how spatial differences in mound vegetation may affect internal thermoregulation soil temperature was measured on the 18 experimental mounds. Two temperature loggers (I button DS1921G; Maxim Integrated, San Jose, California, USA) were placed inside each mound. To standardise, the loggers were placed 80 cm above outside level ground and 50 cm deep, from the mound surface into the mound soil. One logger was inserted from the covered sector and one from the open sector. Ambient temperature was also measured. Loggers were put out in 2019 from 5th February 1800 (local time) to 8th February 1100. Monthly temperature variation in the area is less than 2 °C http://www.worldclimate.com). Each logger recorded soil temperature every 10 min. Two loggers malfunctioned; thus 34 loggers remained for recording.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using R version 4.5.0 (https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/). The direction of the open sector was tested with a Watson test for circular uniformity in the package circular in R (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/circular/index.html).

Linear mixed-effects models (lmer) were used to analyse soil parameters as a function of fencing (fenced vs. unfenced), sector (covered vs. open) and their interaction, with site included as a random intercept. Model selection started by fitting the full model which was subsequently simplified using likelihood ratio tests.

Ungulate feeding position relative to open and covered sector of the termite mounds were quantified by assigning each observation to one of three categories: soil feeding, ground feeding (grass, forbs and small seedlings) or canopy feeding (leaves and twigs). Since the areas of open and covered sector generally differed on the mounds data were converted to animals m-2. We did not model animal selection as a function of species and feeding type since impala was the only species recorded in all feeding categories while the other common species were primarily ground feeding. We therefore used paired t-tests within the different feeding categories combining all the animals and run separate tests for ground feeding impala, zebra, waterbuck, warthog and topi.

Linear mixed-effects models (lmer) were used to test the difference in mound temperature between fenced and unfenced mounds. We used fencing and time as fixed effects and temperature logger ID as a random intercept.

Results

The woody vegetation cover (mean = 38.17% ± 15.84 SD) on the open sector of Macrotermes mounds was about half as dense as that of the covered sector (mean = 80.67% ± 16.33 SD), (Paired t29 = 14.54, P < 0.0001).

Three years after fencing, seedling regeneration on the open sector of the fenced mounds were more than three times higher than unfenced mounds (0.39 seedlings m−2 ± 0.25 SD on the open sector of the unfenced mounds versus 1.11 ± 0.89 SD seedlings m−2 on the open sector of the fenced mounds, Paired t8 = 2.58, P = 0.03).

Nine years after ungulate herbivore exclusion the open sectors were no longer visible on the fenced mounds, because of re-generating seedlings and expansion of branches from trees that grew on the covered sector prior to fencing (mean woody cover of fenced mounds open sector: 76.67 ± 14.14 SD vs covered sector: 78.89% ± 19.65 SD, Paired t8 = 0.36, P = 0.73, Figs. 1 and 2).

Impala was the only species consuming soil from the mounds, and all these observations were from the open mound sector (Paired t18 = 4.18, P = 0006, Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 2). Their preference for the open sector followed a similar pattern as when all species were combined (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 2). Impala, topi, warthog, waterbuck and zebra had sufficient ground feeding observations for statistical comparisons, but only impala showed any sector-based preference (Supplementary Table 1).

Many mounds in the area have distinct signs of geophagy from the open sector and such soil disturbance substantially reduce plant establishment and growth (Supplementary Fig. 1). In terms of canopy feeding, ungulates did not discriminate between the open and covered sector (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 2). The covered sector had a much higher availability of browse compared to the open sector, which is likely to influence these results.

Mound soil properties differed between open and covered sector (Figs. 6 and 7, see supplementary Table 2 for statistical analysis). Mg, C, TKN and TN where higher on covered sector, but ungulate exclusion by fencing reduced the differences between open and covered sector (Fig. 6). Sodium concentrations were substantially higher on open sector compared with covered on unfenced mounds, but when mounds were fenced there was no longer any difference between the two mound sectors (Fig. 6). P, K, Ca and pH were higher on the covered mounds sector, but sector did not interact with fencing (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

The vast majority of the mounds had an open sector facing east. Such pattern could potentially be driven by ungulates (mainly impala) or by termites. Unfenced mounds had significantly higher sodium concentration on the open sector. Sodium is vital in herbivorous mammals’ metabolic processes, digestion and ultimately survival. In plants, sodium is of little importance, and plant consumers are therefore faced with low sodium concentrations in their food21. Furthermore, a previous study from African savannas have documented that sodium deficiency was the main driver behind ungulate geophage habits on Macroteremes mounds22. However, our findings indicate that impala was the driver of the differences in soil sodium between open and covered sector. Soil consuming impala seems to be the main reason why the open sector is maintained on the mounds. Three years after fencing, the mounds protected from ungulates no longer had differences in sodium between the open and covered sector (Fig. 9). Potential explanations for this pattern could be that impala preferentially feed and consume mound soil in the morning sun, triggered by higher morning temperature or morning dew on the exposed mound sector. We cannot rule out that geophagy on the east sector removed soil over time, potentially exposing a different horizon. However, the consistent selectivity by impala of the eastern side suggests that they are responding to real sodium availability in the exposed soil layer, regardless of its origin. Through geophagy they may expose deeper mineral salts (e.g. sodium rich soil) on the eastern open sector and consequently initiate positive feedback.

There could also be other micronutrients involved, not analysed in this study. For example, Se, Mo, and Co has been suggested as important determinants of geophagy in Ngorongoro Conservation Area in Tanzania23. The detoxifying effects of clay have also been suggested as reason for animal geophagy24.

Fencing reduced soil differences in Na, Mg, C and nitrogen between open and covered sector, but fencing did not affect spatial distribution of P, K and Ca, indicating that for these elements, either the duration of fencing is too short to have an effect or that there are spatial differences in soil nutrient concentrations on mounds that are not caused by large ungulates. Tropical seedling growth is strongly nutrient limited, and K is particularly important for seedlings25. Thus, accumulation of plant nutrients on the covered sector may facilitate the establishment and growth of woody vegetation on this part of the mounds.

By maintaining an open zone on mounds towards the eastern morning sun, impala increase mound temperature by 1.6 °C, which is closer to the optimal internal nest temperature of 30 °C (colder, but suboptimal nest temperature is 26–29 °C) given for the species Macrotermes bellicosus26. The temperature difference between the exposed and non-exposed mound is relatively small and we cannot conclude that it has fitness effects. However, several social insects, including magnetic termites, the harvester ant Pogonomyrmex occidentalis, and the wood ant Formica polyctena, construct nests whose eastern sides receive morning solar radiation14. The insects relocate to these warmer areas at dawn, indicating that this thermally driven behaviour likely confers fitness advantages. In Uganda, M. subhyalinus has been recorded in areas with mean annual minimum temperatures ranging from 9 to 22 °C and mean annual maximum temperatures between 24 and 32 °C, with highest densities of mounds in areas with 16 °C and 28 °C for minimum and maximum temperatures, respectively27. In our study area, annual average low is around 16 °C while annual average high is around 26 °C, illustrating the potential benefits of heating the mounds by the early morning sun. Minimum temperature is a key predictor of the distribution of M. subhyalinus28, highlighting the importance of maintaining the elevated mound temperatures, as we documented in the unfenced termite mounds in our study.

Shade has been shown to have severe negative fitness consequences for termite colonies in that lifetime reproductive success of M. bellicosus colonies in open shrub savanna is 20 times higher than colonies in forests15. Thus, declining ungulate population predict sub-optimal conditions for termites and consequently for litter decomposition. A recent study found that termites enhanced litter decomposition with as much as 41% globally29.

This study indicates intimate links between key functional groups in African savanna (Fig. 9). It is well known that impala feed preferentially on mound vegetation, but here we show that herbivory and geophagy are strongly concentrated on the eastern open sector of mounds. This pattern apparently creates positive feedback were ungulates access sodium from deeper soil.

Our study echoes the sentiment of another recent finding from an East African savanna, illustrating complex outcomes of multispecies interactions30. Invasive ants were found to disrupt mutualist interactions between native ants and the whistling-thorn tree (Vachellia drepanolobium), resulting in reduced tree cover, higher visibility and consequently reduced lion (Panthera leo) predation on zebra30.

Directional effects of Macrotermes mounds on soil nutrients, vegetation communities and on grazing lawns has been documented from Kruger South Africa, but these effects are context dependent and from mounds with little or no woody vegetation31. In Kruger, effects were associated with an aspect where tall mounds create shadow differences between the southern and northern part of the mound31. Our study area is located immediately south of equator, excluding effects of mound shading in the north–south direction.

Resource-rich vegetated termite mounds are found throughout African savannas2. Many tree species are exclusively associated with termite mounds6. Mounds with associated vegetation increase dryland ecosystem resilience to climate change by enabling vegetation to persist or change more gradually under reduced rainfall, as well as reducing the threshold for recovery2.

Large wild mammals are under increasing threat globally and African vertebrates has declined by 76% between 1970 and 202032. There are strong landscape-scale positive associations among African savanna herbivores33. For example, the decline of African ungulates has been associated with loss of bird mutualists34. Although there are several species of Macrotermes on African savannas, spatial species overlap is low, and consequently functional redundancy is limited. Ongoing reductions of mammal populations with concomitant reduced termite abundance may have severe impact on decomposition rates and consequently for entire savanna ecosystem functions.
