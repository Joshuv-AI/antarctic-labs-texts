Genetic diversity and gut microbiome of Anopheles mosquitoes in Tamil Nadu by using COI DNA barcoding and 16S rRNA metagenomics
Authors: Sathishkumar Vinayagam, Ipsita Pal Bhowmick, Devianjana Rajendran, Dhanush Kumar Arumugam, Kathirvel Sekar, Kaviyarasi Renu, Harpreet Kaur, Kamaraj Sattu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnopheles mosquitoes transmit infections to humans. Identifying the right mosquito species is crucial for vector control evaluation. This study uses COI gene DNA barcoding and 16S rRNA metagenomics to show the genetic diversity and gut microbial profile of undiscovered mosquito species. Three genera were found, including eight morphologically different Anopheles mosquitoes, and six mosquito species were molecularly validated, including An. moghulensis. The analysis of genetic diversity indicated that there is a state of balanced natural selection present. The species An. maculatus s.s. and An. stephensi exhibited nearly identical mutations, while An. moghulensis demonstrated evidence of purifying selection within the studied population. The gut microbiomes of An. moghulensis (149,377 reads), An. maculatus (51,016 reads), and An. dravidicus (33,126 reads) mosquitoes were also revealed. Afipia felis and Prevotella copri were the leading bacterial species, followed by other phyla including Proteobacteriota, Spirochaetes, and Firmicuteota. In An. moghulensis, alpha diversity assessments of Chao I incidence were dominating, whereas Shannon index was plentiful in An. maculatus s.s. mosquitoes. The mosquito’s distinct bacterial species and shared microbial community are shown in the Venn diagram. These results suggest that the discovered bacterial taxa might be exploited to create vector control techniques for vector-borne illnesses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48529-9.Keywords: Anopheles, Genetic diversity, COI, 16S rRNA metagenomics, Gut-microbiomeSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Genetics, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Mosquitoes transmit diseases such as malaria, chikungunya, dengue, filaria, Zika, Japanese encephalitis, yellow fever, and West Nile fever. More than 700,000 infectious cases of mosquito-borne diseases occur annually throughout the world. This disease burden is still common in tropical and subtropical countries1. Particularly, malaria is caused by Plasmodium species such as Plasmodium falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae and P. knowlesi and transmitted by the Anopheles mosquito species2. There are two different vectors existing in Anopheles mosquitoes based on their vector competencies and the environmental factors, including primary and secondary vectors. These vectors’ preferences can vary from one geographical region to another. For instance, the primary malaria vector in Africa is the Anopheles gambiae complex and Anopheles funestus3. Similarly, in India, Anopheles culicifacies, and An. stephensi acts as the primary vector as well as An. darlingi in South America4. Likewise, the secondary vector also differs depending on the geographical location, including Anopheles arabiensis, An. coustani, An. pharoensis, and An. ziemanni in Africa and Anopheles fluviatilis, An. minimus, An. sundaicus, and An. dirus in India3,4. Due to several other reasons, the vector competence becomes varied, including habitats and climatic factors such as temperature, precipitation, altitude, wind speed and pressure2. In other aspects, genetic variation and structure are the main causes of mosquitoes being the vectors for several diseases and the possibility of conversion from non-vectors to vectors. These vector mosquitoes can be controlled by limiting their population is the easiest way to control disease transmission and prevention. The government sector included the strategies using insecticides to control mosquitoes and prevent the spread of disease. However, the emergence of is a serious issue in controlling vectors, as it arises by causing mutation or may occur through cross-breeding with a resistant strain5.

Identification of mosquito species is crucial to identify the specific species and population distribution, which is essential to monitoring and the implementation of proper vector control strategies and to prevent the transmission of diseases6. Morphological based taxonomical classification is the gold standard method for mosquito identification. However, the misidentification of species may happen due to the damage or loss of key scaling markers during field collection, a lack of taxonomical experts, undocumented species and novel or cryptic species7. To overcome this, molecular-based approaches were utilized to identify the mosquito species. It is helpful to identify the organism at the species and subspecies level, as it describes the evolutionary lineage and current molecular status of the particular species8,9.

Researchers studied the genetic diversity and structure, insecticide resistance, as well as evolutionary dynamics of vector mosquitoes among the population using molecular tools, to develop novel approaches to control vector mosquitoes10. In other divergent studies, the characterization of midgut microbiota in mosquitoes, including bacteria, fungi, viruses and protozoa to understand the host pathogen interaction and disease transmission dynamics. Microbiota plays an essential role in the development, fitness, and immune response, as well as fecundity and reproduction. To some extent, gut microbes affect the disease susceptibility of mosquitoes, or they act against pathogens within the mosquito gut environment by competing for nutrients11,12. Some gut bacteria present in mosquitoes that activate gene expression, like cytochrome oxidase, to neutralize insecticides, as well as immune genes such as Toll pathways, Imd pathway, Jak-STAT pathway and ROS-activating genes, act against self-colonization or foreign bodies13,14. Several studies have reported that the composition of mosquito gut microbiota exhibits variation across species, sex, and ecological habitats13,15,16. The gut microbiota studies on Aedes aegypti and Culex quinquefasciatus reveal that the laboratory-reared mosquitoes exhibit less diverse and more stable bacterial communities, and field-collected mosquitoes show highly diverse and more variable bacterial communities, supported by environmental factors, insecticide exposure and host physiology17,18. Similar studies in An. darlingii and An. stephensi have exhibited the low diversity and stable bacterial population in laboratory-reared mosquitoes, whereas a highly diverse and more varied bacterial population was exhibited in wild mosquitoes19,20. However, the gut microbiota composition also varies across mosquito species. In Aedes spp., the community is typically dominated by Enterobacteriaceae, Acetobacteraceae, and Serratia. In contrast, Culex spp. are characterized by a higher abundance of Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Staphylococcus, whereas Anopheles spp. frequently harbour Elizabethkingia, Asaia, and Pseudomonas. 16,21. Elucidating the population patterns and functional roles of the mosquito gut microbiome among various species is crucial for advancing our understanding of host–microbe interactions that influence pathogen transmission and vector competence. Here, we investigated the genetic diversity of Anopheles mosquito species using the cytochrome oxidase subunit-I (COI) gene and compared it across different geographical regions. Additionally, we analyzed the gut microbiome composition of An. maculatus, An. mogulensis and An. dravidicus were collected from the Vathalmalai region of the Dharmapuri district, Tamil Nadu, India.

To characterize six mosquito species, the cytochrome oxidase I (COI) gene sequences of morphologically identified specimens were compared with reference sequences which were available in the NCBI GenBank database. Microbiome analysis

Gut microbiome profiling of An. moghulensis, An. maculatus, and An. dravidicus using 16S rRNA metagenomic sequencing revealed the presence of nine dominant bacterial phyla of Spirochaetes, Proteobacteriota, Firmicuteota, Deinococcota, Chloroflexi, Cyanobacteria, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota, and Acidobacteria (Fig. 4a and b). In addition, a small proportion of sequences were grouped under “other bacterial phyla,” and several taxa remained unclassified with in the total read abundance of 1,49,377, 51,016 and 33,126. Huge reads difference was observed between the mosquito’s species level. Across all three mosquito species, Proteobacteriota were the most abundant phylum, accounting for 48.5% in An. moghulensis, 55.5% in An. maculatus, and 55.7% in An. dravidicus. This was followed by Firmicuteota, while Bacteroidota and Actinobacteriota represented the next most prevalent groups. Acidobacteria were absent in An. moghulensis but present in both An. maculatus and An. dravidicus. Chloroflexi were detected exclusively in An. moghulensis and absent in the other two species. All three mosquito species harboured sequences assigned to unclassified bacterial phyla, indicating the presence of potentially novel or underrepresented taxa. These findings demonstrate that while the gut microbiome of the three Anopheles species shares a broadly similar phylum-level composition dominated by Proteobacteriota, notable differences exist in the occurrence of specific bacterial groups such as Acidobacteria and Chloroflexi phylum. Discussion

The current investigation identified a varied collection of mosquito species from three genera (Anopheles, Culex, and Aedes) obtained by various sampling methods in Vathalmail, Dharmaouri district, Tamil Nadu, India. Seven species of Anopheles mosquitoes were discovered, including members of the maculatus group, mogulensis, stephensi, annularis, vruna, subpictus, and vagus12,22–24. The detection of An. moghulensis for the first time in this region is a significant finding, expanding the known distribution of this species and suggesting possible ecological adaptation or dispersal. However, the existence of An. moghulensis were documented in various regions of India. Especially in Odisha22. An. moghulensis were recognised one of the dominant and important malaria vectors in Iran25. Moreover, the occurrence of An. stephensi in hilltop habitats further highlights the ecological plasticity of this important malaria vector. A recent study has been highlighted the stephensi mosquito’s larvae if being resistance towards temephos formulation as per WHO recommendation26. Furthermore, an another study reported that the shorten oocyst maturation duration were found and the density of oocyst varies from different region of An. stephensi mosquitoes where the malaria endemic region and non-malarial endemic region27. In India, An. subpictus and vagus mosquitoes were widely present in the paddy fields, ponds and stagnant fresh water bodies. The present study witnessed their existence in the hills also and these two mosquito’s species have been documented that they are susceptible with both P. falciparum and P. vivax malaria parasite. However, in India these two species were not considered has a malaria vector, rather, Neibauer countries like Sri Lanka it is a potential malaria vector12,28,29. In the northeastern states of India, the Maculatus group mosquito’s species of An. maculatus s.s, An. rampae and An. willmori were documented as a vector. An. dravidicus also were documented in India30. More recently, the other maculatus group mosquito’s species of An. pseudowillmori and An. sawadwongporni were documented in the Kolkata state, India by molecular tools along with other medically important malaria vectors31. The seasonal variation strongly influenced mosquito abundance32. In our study the mosquito’s samples were peak collections that occurred during the rainy season, particularly from August to November, when water bodies provided optimal breeding habitats. Conversely, mosquito populations were minimal during dry seasons when streams were absent. Culex mosquitoes were abundant in stagnant water environments, consistent with their inherent preference for unclean or motionless circumstances. These results highlight the importance of the water bodies in mosquito dynamics and the potential of disease transmission risks. In contrast with our findings, the other study showed the detailed entomological severance was conducted in the Kerala state, India, which is one of the neighbouring states of Tamil Nadu. In that 14 disease-spreading vectors and 108 non-vector species, which belong to the 28 genera, have been identified. Which suggested the diverse mosquito species were present in the same ecosystem, which can lead to severe disease outbreaks33.

To categorise the morphologically similar species complexes in their species if very difficult. Therefore, to overcome these difficulties molecular methods have been used, which includes rDNA of ITS2 region, D3 region cox1 and cox2 region of the mitochondrial DNA of the particular species34–36. In our study, mitochondrial COI sequence-based phylogenetic analysis offered robust molecular confirmation of Anopheles mosquito species. However, our study population in Tamil Nadu showed divergence, suggesting localized genetic structuring. The existence of two unique populations in Tamil Nadu indicates the process of possible microevolution or ecological isolation. Both An. maculatus s.s. and An. dravidicus were closely related to Indian and other Asian countries’ sequences within the NCBI GenBank database9,30. The outgroup of An. sinensis provided an exact split from the An. maculatus group of mosquitoes, affirming its unique evolutionary history. The second phylogenetic tree, which consists of morphology classified six species of An. maculatus, An. dravidicus, An. pallidus, An. varuna, An. moghulensis, and An. stephensi, was further validated with corresponding species-specific GenBank reference sequences. Each phylogenetic tree clade was matched with exact reference sequences. The An. moghulensis COI sequences were lacking in the GenBank database. Most recently, in Iran, molecular species diagnostic tools have been used to differentiate the An. moghulensis from the An. superpictus based on the rDNA-ITS2 (ribosomal DNA internal transcribed spacers) sequences37.

The genetic diversity of six mosquito species was substantial (0.13 ± 0.1), which supports taxonomic distinctness. The pairwise distance of An. maculatus s. s. and An. dravidicus revealed the moderate difference, whereas the An. moghulensis showed the higher genetic divergence (0.1061–0.1175) from other species, which is consistent with the novel detection in this region. Further, An. maculatus s.s. mosquito’s sequences showed border distributional connectivity with other Asian countries and An. dravidicus were restricted with regionally38. A. dravidicus exhibited high haplotype diversity (Hd = 0.857), and significant neutrality test results indicated that the population was expanding or balancing. The presence of two sub-clades in the phylogenetic tree supports these interpretations, and it’s showing the genetic differentiation within the populations. In contrast, An. maculatus s.s. exhibited lower genetic diversity, and the non-significant results from neutrality tests suggest a stable genetic structure with limited variation. An. moghulensis mosquitoes displayed moderate genetic diversity with negative Fu’s Fs values. With the limited sequences, this species is complicated for interpretation. However, the identified genetic divergence underscores its genetic identity. Similarly, An. stephensi mosquitoes showed the moderate genetic diversity that suggests stable populations. Other mosquito species are small in number, and it shows higher genetic variability within these mosquito species39.

This study presents the inaugural comprehensive characterization of the gut microbiome of three mosquito species utilizing a 16S rRNA metagenomic approach: An. maculatus s.s., and An. dravidicus, belonging to the An. maculatus group and An. moghulensis mosquitoes. All these three mosquito’s species were sampled in the same location and session were utilised for the microbiome diversity analysis for their gut. In all these mosquitos’ species in phylum level wide similarities were observed, notable interspecific difference was observed, which under covering the microbial complex and mosquitoes species-specific associated microbial communities. Furthermore, unclassified bacterial phyla across all the species were highlights the importance of novel or unrepresented with in the mosquitoes, warranting deeper taxonomic and functional explorations. However, adult mosquitoes were showed lesser microbial diversity as compared to their early stage, larger bacterial communities were removed due to metamorphosis which they acquired in early life stages.

The phylum of Proteobacteriota was dominant in all these three mosquito species, which is stable phyla is observed in the previous research reports in Anopheles and other mosquito taxa. Due to their metabolic versatility and adaptability, these bacterial phyla were frequently expressed as core microbiota in the diverse mosquito species around the country and world12,20,40,41. Other bacterial phyla of Firmicuteota, Bacteroides, and Actinobacteriota were also prevalent, representing their common role in nutrient metabolism and host-microbiome interactions. In Anopheles mosquitoes it observed consistently that predominance phylum of Proteobacteriota followed by Firmicuteota19,41–44. In An. moghulensis, Acidobacteria were absent, and they are present in the other two maculatus group mosquitoes of An. maculatus s.s. and An. dravidicus rather, Chloroflexi were exclusively present in the An. moghulensis. These findings gave insights into the certain bacterial communities that were significantly associated with specific mosquito hosts, potentially influenced by ecological niches, feeding behaviours, or physiological traits14,43.

The genus Afipia was regularly dominant across all three species, followed by Prevotella. The Afipia files have a stable association with these mosquito species, suggesting that it may be linked with metabolic adaptability. The other shared genera, like Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, Bifidobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Roseburia, were also commonly present in all mosquito species and suggest the conserved core microbiome of these species and in has been reported in other mosquitoes species45,46. The species-specific genera of Paenibacillus in An. moghulensis, Thiospirochaeta in An. maculatus, and Ralstonia in An. dravidicus highlight the unique microbial characteristics of each individual mosquito species. OTU analysis of bacterial species gave further clear differences in the microbiome analysis in the three mosquito species. At the species level, Afipia felis and Prevotella copri were present in a huge amount of the present study’s mosquito species. which is reported previously in the southern state of India, which compared with three different malaria transmission dynamics places of An. stephensi mosquitoes47,48. Some of the mosquito’s species-specific bacterial taxa, such as Ralstonia pickettii in An. dravidicus, Thiospirochaeta perfilievii in An. maculatus, and finally Paenibacillus napththalenovorans in An. moghulensis, were observed and Roseburia faecis, were detected, but their functional role in the mosquitoes’ physiology remained unknown49. Ralstonia picketti is only of the opportunist pathogen, which case nosocomial infection and outbreak of these bacterial infection was showed in Germany50. These study finds that the bacterial taxa which identified through 16SrRNA metagenomics will be exploited for microbial based vector control strategies. The current research presents significant data on the presence of novel An. moghulensis mosquitoes. Two species of the An. maculatus group, An. maculatus s.s. and An. dravidicus, were existed in the same location. Additionally, An. stephensi mosquitoes were identified at the hilltop for the first time in our research conducted in Tamil Nadu, India. In all three genera of mosquitoes, species were identified, with Anopheles mosquitoes validated by morphological and COI-based genetic study. The genetic diversity investigation revealed that these mosquito species exhibited modest genetic variety based on the extracted gene sequences from the GenBank database. Our investigation demonstrated that An. moghulensis exhibited high gut microbial diversity, followed by the other two species in the maculatus group, An. maculatus s.s. and An. dravinicus. However, substantial differences were observed in the gut microbial genera and species of each mosquito species. Each mosquito exhibited a variety of bacterial species, including species-specific bacteria. The investigation of bacterial makeup in these mosquito species will facilitate the development of potential microbial-mediated vector control techniques. Future study must concentrate on analysing bacterial species and assessing their interactions with mosquitoes and mosquito-borne illnesses.

Materials and methods

Study location

The present study was conducted in Vathalmail hill station (Latitute:11.9454797, Longitude:78.0871216), which is in Dharmapuri district, Tamil Nadu, India. Recently, the state government announced a new tourist spot, and the villages have a human population of around 1700 as per 2011 census data. Notably, malaria cases have not been reported in this district, except for a few instances of traveller malaria cases. However, these regions boast notable tourist attractions due to the beautiful nature attractions and climatic conditions. Furthermore, the current study area is situated near the urban area where the national and international peoples can visit frequently.

Anopheles mosquitoes sampling

Mosquito’s samples were collected from suitable breeding spots over the one-year period of June 2024 to June 2025 by using CDC light traps drown to dusk (18:00 to 06:00). Simultaneously, mosquitoes larval also collected in the suitable breeding spots by dipping techniques, matured larvae and pupa were gathered and transported into laboratory and kept inside the mosquito’s cage to matured an adult mosquito. Then adults were anesthetized using chloroform and subsequently stored in a refrigerator, with each specimen labelled according to its collection date and location.

Morphological identification of mosquito’s species

Morphological characterization of field-collected Anopheline mosquitoes was identified utilizing taxonomic keys, with specimens collected from light traps and larvae that developed into adult mosquitoes. The abdominal physiological state of mosquitoes gathered from light traps was evaluated and classified into three categories: unfed (UF), blood-fed (BF), or semi-blood-fed (SF). Further, the identified mosquito’s species were stored in silica beads with cotton in the sampling tubes along with samples name, collection date and blood fed status. The marked samples were stored in the refrigerator for until further process.

Genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (gDNA) extraction

Field collected Anopheles-mosquitoes were carefully examined for mites on their bodies under stereomicroscope to prevent contamination with mite DNA, specimens containing mites were excluded from subsequent DNA extraction and analyses. We individually surface-sterilized each mosquito using 70% ethanol and then washed it three times with 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Under a dissection microscope, the head/thorax and abdomen (midgut) were separated on clean, sterile microscopic slides. We separated the midgut from the abdomen, washed it with sterile 1X PBS, and then transferred it into clean 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. As per manufacturer’s instructions QIAGEN DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit was used to extracted the mosquitoes gDNA.

Molecular characterization of Anopheles mosquitoes

We used mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase-1 (mtCOI-1) gene barcoding primers developed by Flomer et al. (1994) to identify the mosquito species. We used the COI forward primer (LCO1490F: 5’-GGTCAACAAATCATAAAGATATTGG-3’) and the reverse primer (HCO2198R: 5’-TAAACTTCAGGGTGACCAAAAAATCA-3’). A 1:10 dilution of the stock primer at 100 µM/µl was used to produce a working stock of 10 µM/µl. The total 25 µl PCR reaction was performed along with 2X GoTaq G2 Promega master mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and 0.5 µM/µl of each primer; nuclease-free water has been used to make the appropriate volume. The PCR reaction was performed in the Applied Biosystems VeritiPro thermal cycler for 35 cycles of 95°C for 30s of denaturation and 43°C for 1 min of annealing and extension at 72°C for 1.5 min. The initial denaturation was kept at 95°C for 2 min, and the final extension was at 72°C for 10 min, followed by an infinite 4°C holding temperature. To confirm the PCR reactions, we used a 1.5% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide (EtBr) in 1X TAE buffer alongside a 100 kb DNA ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and positive and negative controls. The PCR products were visualized in a UV transilluminator. The positive PCR products were purified by the Promega Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Furthermore, agarose gel electrophoresis was used to assess the quality of the purified PCR products. Moreover, randomly selected samples from each collection site were sequenced by Sanger sequencing in both forward and reverse orientations with the appropriate COI primers.

Sequence analysis

The forward and reverse cytochrome oxidase I (COI) sequences of Anopheles mosquitoes were subjected to rigorous quality assessment and manually curated using BioEdit software (version 7.2). The obtained high-quality sequences were submitted in the GenBank database and acquired accession numbers. To determine the genetic diversity analysis and construct phylogenetic tree similarity sequences were retrieved from NCBI-GenBank database by using BLAST and exported into MEGA XII software.

Phylogenetic tree analysis

Genetic divergence within and between species of the An. maculatus group was estimated in MEGA 12 (Molecular Evolutionary Genetic Analysis) using the Kimura 2-parameter (K2P) distance model. Intraspecific, interspecific, and overall divergences were calculated separately for the present study COI sequences of the maculatus group along with retrieved gene sequences of other An. maculatus group mosquitoes and overall, six mosquito species analysed separately along with the two maculatus groups mosquito’s species of present study. Two phylogenetic trees were constructed to represent species relationships. The first was constructed only with An. maculatus group mosquitoes which retrieved from GenBank database along with obtained from present study. Phylogenetic reconstruction was conducted using the Maximum Likelihood approach with the Tamura 3-parameter model (G + I), corroborated by 1,000 bootstrap repetitions. Analyses were performed in MEGA12 using up to 8 parallel processing threads. The phylogenetic tree was created using MEGA (version XII) and subsequently exported in Newick format. The produced Newick file was later integrated into iTOL v7 to create a refined and visually appealing phylogenetic tree.

The same approach was applied to construct the second tree, which included An. sinensis and the other mosquito species for comparative representation.

Haplotype analysis

Initially, high-quality aligned sequences were scrutinized, and subsequently, the sequences were converted into Nexus format using MEGA XII, then exported. Subsequently, the haplotype data file was acquired from DnaSP v6.0 software and heat map was generated using SRplot, an online tool available at https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/login_en/.

Genetic diversity determination

To evaluate genetic divergence, the obtained Anopheles mosquitoes COI sequences were used to find the global sourced sequences from NCBI data base. Further, these sequences were aligned utilizing MEGA XII. The alignments obtained were transformed into Nexus format and analyzed for genetic diversity using DnaSP v6.0. Genetic diversity analysis was calculated with number of haplotypes (H), haplotype diversity (Hd), nucleotide diversity (π) and total number of segregating sites (S). Furthermore, to identify any deviations from neutral evolution we conducted Neutrality tests specifically Jajima’s D, Fu and Li’s D* and Li’s F* were calculated.

16S rRNA sequencing

The bacterial 16S rRNA V3-V4 regions from pooled mosquito samples’ gut genomic DNA were amplified by a two-step PCR protocol. Initially, the first PCR was conducted with a template of the mosquito’s gut gDNA along with KAPA HiFi HotStart Ready-mix (Roche) and overhanging primers with a total volume of 25 µl. The PCR reactions were kept at 95ºC for 3 min for initial denaturation, followed by 25 cycles of 95ºC for 30s for initial denaturation; annealing was at 55ºC for 30s, and extension was at 72ºC for 30s. The final extension was 72ºC for 5 min. The PCR amplicon (~ 550) was confirmed by using a DNA Bioanalyzer 1000 chip. Further, these products were purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Library preparation employed the Nextera XT indexing kit for barcode incorporation. The second PCR (50 µl) was set up with template DNA, Nextera XT primers, KAPA HiFi HotStart Ready-mix, and nuclease-free water, following manufacturer instructions. Appropriate negative controls were used in the PCR reactions but excluded from metagenomic sequencing. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (V3 kit) by 2 × 300 bp paired-end reads.

Quality control and preprocessing

Raw paired-end reads were assessed for sequence quality and artefacts to retain only high-confidence data for downstream analysis.

Trimming and filtering

Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were removed using Trim Galore, which integrates Cutadapt and FastQC. Stringent thresholds were applied to discard poor-quality reads.

Taxonomic profiling

Overlapping paired-end reads were merged with FLASh, and subsequent refinement was performed using the DADA2 pipeline in R. This included filtering, denoising, and chimera removal, yielding Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) with single-nucleotide resolution. Taxonomic assignment was carried out using the DADA2 classifier against the Genome Taxonomy Database Species Rank (GSR v3–v4).

Microbiome analysis

Community composition was explored through taxonomic bar plots, alpha diversity indices of Chao 1 incidence, Shannon index and Simpson index. Relative abundance plots were generated from ASV classifications. Visualization and statistical analyses were conducted in R using phyloseq and ampvis2.
