Understanding the tradeoffs between quality and morality: Insights from consumer response to animal welfare eggs in China
Authors: Junyan Zhang, Yujie Liu, Jingjing Wang, Xiaoting Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Public interest in improving farm animal welfare has increased globally, but research on consumer demand for enhanced welfare practices lags, particularly in China. The tradeoffs between ethical and quality concerns driving consumer acceptance of animal welfare, which support sustainable food consumption, remain unexplored. This study identifies emerging market consumers' true preferences for laying hen welfare attributes and underlying psychological drivers. Specifically, using survey data from 1,021 Chinese egg consumers, we employed a discrete choice experiment embedded in random parameter logit model and latent class model to elicit perceptions. Results reveal consumers are willing to pay 45% and 78% premiums for nest-box use and free-range systems. Further analysis indicates that these preferences are primarily driven by food quality concerns rather than ethical concerns, consistent with the dual attributes (self-interest vs. altruism) framework of moral products. Furthermore, consumers' preferences display pronounced demographic heterogeneity, with stronger willingness to pay among women, younger cohorts, higher-income households, and more educated individuals. Latent class analysis further identified four distinct groups: 31.5% "novel welfare preference", 11% "unbalanced welfare-price", 42.9% "balanced welfare-price", and 14.6% "traditional welfare preference" consumers. The findings suggest that, in China's emerging economy, market-driven advances in laying hen welfare should prioritize tangible food quality cues over pure ethical narratives and facilitate differentiated welfare labels to capture targeted groups. These insights contribute implications for enhancing farm animal welfare through the consumer market and provide valuable guidance for the development of welfare standards, product differentiation, and cost-benefit evaluation of welfare policies in emerging markets.

Introduction

Due to the growing public interest and consumer demand for enhanced animal welfare in food production, marketplaces are increasingly offering welfare-related labeled products (Schuck-Paim et al., 2021). Modern livestock farming systems have increased intensive production and achieved economic gains, but also generated public and private concerns related to animal welfare (Boogaard et al., 2010). Particularly, as one of the farm animal species raised at massive scale, the global laying hen industry faces the profound challenge of farm animal welfare (Kuruc & McFadden, 2023). China, the world's largest producer and consumer of eggs (Chen et al., 2024), still relies predominantly on conventional cage systems. In 2023, China raised approximately 3.3 billion laying hens (FAOSTAT, 2025), yet only about 10% were kept in free-range systems (Yang et al., 2026), which long suppressed hens' basic behavioral needs, including foraging, dust bathing, and nesting, causing severe and prolonged suffering (Kuruc & McFadden, 2023). Enormous evidence reveals that enhancing animal welfare can elevate productivity, lower mortality, and reduce production disruptions, thereby delivering tangible economic benefits for producers (Kauppinen et al., 2012) and public health benefits for consumers (Aminzare et al., 2024; Chinmayee et al., 2024; Donat et al., 2024).

Animals are sentient beings capable of experiencing pain, fear, and pleasure (Proctor, 2012; Browning & Birch, 2022). The introduction of animal welfare has extended past the avoidance of negative welfare states to focus more heavily on positive emotions and experiences of the animals, such as satiety, vitality, reward, contentment, curiosity and playfulness, in order to provide a “life worth living” (Green & Mellor, 2011; Webster, 2016; Yeates & Main, 2008). Extensive research considers using the Five Domains model to assess animal welfare; the model covers five domains: nutrition, physical environment, health, behavioral interactions, and mental state (Mellor & Reid, 1994; Mellor et al., 2020). The Welfare Quality assessment framework holds that the fundamental principles for evaluating farm animal welfare include: (i) adequate feed and water; (ii) an appropriate physical environment; (iii) maintenance of health; and (iv) opportunity to express normal behaviors (Welfare Quality®, 2009).

As a species of small poultry, laying hens in China increasingly suffer under highly intensive production systems, resulting in poor animal welfare outcomes (He et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024). In China, approximately 90% of laying hens remain housed in cage systems (Chen et al., 2023). This physical environment deprives birds of the space needed to express highly motivated behaviors, such as walking, wing-flapping, nesting, foraging, perching, and dust bathing. Such long-term deprivation of behavioral expression can trigger feather pecking, fear responses, and aggressive behavior (Cordiner & Savory, 2001; Donaldson & O'Connell, 2012; Newberry et al., 2001). Given the cost pressures facing welfare improvements on the production side, assessing Chinese consumers' willingness to pay (WTP) for improved laying hen welfare is critically important (Clark et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2024). To date, however, consumer research on laying hen welfare has concentrated on North America and Europe (Doyon et al., 2016; Dunne & Siettou, 2020; Estévez-Moreno et al., 2025; López-Galán et al., 2013; Ochs et al., 2019), while empirical evidence on WTP in developing economies, especially China, remains scant. Because preferences for welfare attributes are shaped by complex socio-economic and cultural forces, eliciting Chinese consumers’ valuations is indispensable for understanding demand in emerging markets.

Existing consumer studies on laying hen welfare typically examine four attributes: (i) housing type (free-range system, cage, barn) (He & Xiong, 2024; Varziri et al., 2024); (ii) beak trimming to prevent injurious pecking (Dunne & Siettou, 2020); (iii) certification logos (organic, green) (Liu et al., 2022; Varziri et al., 2024); and (iv) nutritionally fortified feed (Calcium-enriched or Selenium-enriched) (Liu et al., 2022). Qualitative work has additionally explored attitudes toward nest-box use (Trimania et al., 2022). These studies supply producers with quantifiable premium ranges that can offset the incremental costs of welfare improvements. Building on these dimensions, this study operationally defines "welfare eggs" as eggs produced under one or more farming practices designed to exceed conventional production standards and improve hen welfare (e.g., free-range systems, nest box use, antibiotic-free certification, or nutritionally enhanced feeding). It should be particularly emphasized that nesting is an instinctive behavior of laying hens; nest-box use mitigates stress and satisfies an essential physiological need (Blokhuis et al., 2007). To date, limited studies have systematically quantified Chinese consumers' willingness to pay for the attribute of Nest boxes, constituting an area that remains insufficiently explored.

Moreover, welfare eggs are not mere food products; they embody moral attributes (Garlet et al., 2024). Purchase decisions, therefore, entail simultaneous evaluations of quality, price, ethics, and emotion. As public concern for farm animal welfare escalates (Schuck-Paim et al., 2021), policymakers and producers confront a strategic question: should welfare improvements be marketed primarily through quality premiums, or through ethical positioning that leverages consumers’ moral identification?

Methodologically, the extant literature predominantly relies on the random parameter logit (RPL) model to accommodate preference heterogeneity, interacting each welfare attribute with demographic variables (He & Xiong, 2024; Liu et al., 2022; Varziri et al., 2024). However, the model results can only show the average marginal effect of the sample, making it difficult to identify subgroups within the group that may have completely conflicting preferences. To overcome this limitation, the present study employs a latent class model (LCM) to segment the market based on underlying preference structures. Although LCM has been applied to consumer preference research on other animal-derived products, such as broiler chicken welfare (Heinola et al., 2023), this represents its first application in the domain of laying hen welfare, thereby offering a novel analytical tool for targeted marketing strategies in this field.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows. First of all, this study innovatively incorporated the laying environment into the attribute category of egg selection experiments, and conducted a quantitative analysis of Chinese consumers' preferences and willingness to pay regarding the nest-box use. By extending the analysis beyond the single dimension of the housing system to a multi-attribute welfare, we offered a granular account of how consumers trade off competing welfare dimensions. Second, diverging from prior studies that directly estimate welfare preferences without theoretical grounding, we systematically embedded the dual-pathway framework of food quality concern versus ethical concern (Napolitano et al., 2010; Rondoni et al., 2020) within the Chinese market for welfare eggs. Meanwhile, this paper introduced the dual-attribute framework of moral products' self-interest and altruism (Garlet et al., 2024) to analyze the relative influence of quality demands and ethical concerns. This adds to the literature on animal welfare research concerning consumer payment motivations. Third, beyond heterogeneity analyses based on random parameter logit (He & Xiong, 2024; Liu et al., 2022; Varziri et al., 2024), this study innovatively introduces the latent class model approach, previously applied in areas such as broiler welfare (Heinola et al., 2023), into laying hen welfare market segmentation, providing actionable market segmentation insights for supply chain stakeholders.

Conceptual framework and hypotheses

Laying Hen welfare information and willingness to pay

Rondoni et al. (2020) show that consumers' preference for eggs is mainly driven by the intrinsic and extrinsic characteristics of eggs as well as socio-cultural factors. Among them, extrinsic characteristics and intrinsic characteristics are the factors that consumers pay the most attention to, and there is an interaction at the consumer perception level between the two. Intrinsic characteristics refer to the direct sensory attributes of eggs (such as the size of eggs, the color of eggshells and egg yolks) and nutritional characteristics, while extrinsic characteristics cover the production methods of eggs and other contents. Consumers believe that different production methods of eggs may have an impact on the intrinsic characteristics of eggs, namely, sensory characteristics and nutritional characteristics. Layer welfare information belongs to the extrinsic characteristics of egg production methods, which will increase consumers' expectations for the sensory characteristics and nutritional components of eggs. For example, consumers believe that eggs laid by layers raised in accordance with higher animal welfare standards are safer (Ochs et al., 2018), and consumers in the UK (Pettersson et al., 2016) and Australia (Bray & Ankeny, 2017) believe that free-range eggs have a better taste than caged eggs. To sum up, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1Consumers exhibit positive preference and willingness to pay for laying hen welfare attributes.

Consumers exhibit positive preference and willingness to pay for laying hen welfare attributes.

In the classical literature on food quality, the food quality analysis model proposed by Peri (2006) places safety on par with sensory and nutritional needs as a fundamental consumer requirement for food quality. Rondoni et al. (2020) similarly note that consumers infer the healthfulness, safety, and sensory characteristics of eggs from production methods. Building on this, the present study defines food quality as the perceived intrinsic attribute advantages of eggs from the consumer's perspective, encompassing three dimensions: sensory, nutritional, and safety attributes, while food quality concern refers to the degree of attention and importance consumers place on these food quality dimensions. Existing empirical research provides direct evidence linking quality-level perceptions to preferences for welfare attributes. Based on a choice experiment in the Chinese egg market, He & Xiong (2024) found that consumers who believed larger living space improves the nutritional quality of eggs showed a significantly stronger preference for free-range eggs. This suggests that consumer preferences for welfare attributes can arise through a quality-perception pathway. The present study further posits that consumers with greater food quality concern regarding welfare-friendly eggs are more likely to attend to and rely on the quality signals carried by welfare attributes, thereby exhibiting a stronger preference for such attributes. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1aThe strength of this preference increases with consumers’ food quality concerns.

The strength of this preference increases with consumers’ food quality concerns.

Socio-cultural factors, such as consumers' attitudes towards animal welfare, also influence consumers' preferences and purchasing behaviors regarding welfare eggs from laying hens. For example, consumers in Spain (López-Galán et al., 2013), the United States (Norwood & Lusk, 2011; Spain et al., 2018), Brazil, Chile (Lemos Teixeira et al., 2018), and the United Kingdom (Bennett et al., 2016) have all shown concern about the welfare of farm animals and a higher willingness to pay for cage-free eggs. Based on this, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1bThe strength of the preference for laying hen welfare attributes increases with consumers’ ethical concern.

The strength of the preference for laying hen welfare attributes increases with consumers’ ethical concern.

Heterogeneity in consumers' Laying Hen welfare preferences

Extant studies document heterogeneity in preferences for animal welfare attributes. Demographic variables (gender, age, education, income, and location) systematically influence WTP (He & Xiong, 2024; Varziri et al., 2024; Liu & Wang, 2023). Rondoni et al. (2020) identify physiological traits (age, gender), socio-cultural factors (income, education), and contextual factors (residence) as determinants of purchase behavior, perceptions, and preferences for welfare eggs. Accordingly:

H2Consumers’ preferences for laying hen welfare attributes are heterogeneous.

Consumers’ preferences for laying hen welfare attributes are heterogeneous.

Relative influence of food quality concern versus ethical concern

When consumers purchase animal welfare products, their decision-making process may be simultaneously driven by internal ethical and moral motivations as well as external considerations of product quality. Garlet et al. (2024) point out that animal welfare products, as a type of moral product, possess both self-interested and altruistic moral attributes. Among these, the self-interested moral attribute reflects consumers' pursuit of personal interests, while the altruistic moral attribute embodies concern for social public interests. Taking laying hen welfare eggs as an example, as a type of animal welfare product, they also integrate self-interested and altruistic moral attributes, i.e., the combination of personal interests and social public interests. From the perspective of consumer behavior theory, when evaluating moral products, the consumer market pays more attention to product performance, which is one of the basic motivations of human behavior (Das et al., 2021; Luchs et al., 2010; Mai et al., 2019). Davies and Gutsche (2016); Andersch et al. (2019), and Smith & Johns (2019) point out that self-interested attributes are often more effective in driving purchase decisions. Because in a cultural context that emphasizes individualism, comfort, well-being, and quality of life, the dissolution of personal interests into collective interests is difficult to achieve (Lipovetsky, 2002). For example, Kossmann & Gomez - Suarez (2018) conclude: "At the end of the day, the consumer is not buying Fairtrade wine, but wine. It needs to appeal to her taste." Therefore, in consumers' purchase decisions of moral products, self-interested attributes occupy a more important position compared to altruistic attributes. As a type of moral product, consumers' purchase decisions of laying hen welfare eggs are not only influenced by product quality factors but also driven internally by ethical and moral factors. Consumers' attention to the quality of welfare eggs reflects the self-interested attribute of the product, while their ethical concern for the state of laying hens in the egg production system reflects the altruistic characteristic. Based on this, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3Relative to ethical concern, food quality concern exerts a stronger positive effect on consumers’ preference for laying hen welfare attributes.

Relative to ethical concern, food quality concern exerts a stronger positive effect on consumers’ preference for laying hen welfare attributes.

Discrete choice experiment (DCE)

Auction experiments are one of the mainstream methods widely used to estimate consumers' willingness to pay for specific product attributes; they require participants to bid with real money for physical goods, thereby obtaining willingness-to-pay data under incentive-compatible conditions (Canavari et al., 2019). However, this method presupposes the actual existence of the target goods (Canavari et al., 2019). Given that no eggs are currently produced in China using an on-farm nest-box system, auction experiments are difficult to implement in this study. Choice experiments, as an effective instrument for dissecting consumers’ stated preferences, allow the valuation of attributes embedded in products under hypothetical scenarios. Building on this, we conducted a discrete choice experiment to assess different consumers' preferences for hen welfare eggs. Participants were provided with a set of choices (i.e., choice tasks) comprised of hen welfare eggs (see Section 3.3). Each participant was randomly assigned one of three possible blocks of choice sets, with each block containing ten choice sets. They were asked to choose one of three alternative options (egg A, egg B, or Neither of Them).

The specific combination of levels for each attribute in Option A and B were selected through a multi-step process following Contu et al. (2016). This process, using the Apollo software (Hess & Palma, 2019), made the design more efficient by limiting the number of possible combinations. In the initial phase of data collection, combinations were extracted from a Full factorial design. This was first administered to a pilot sample of around 126 participants via Sojump, a leading data collection service provider in China. Using this pilot data, we estimated Multinomial Logit (MNL) and Random Parameters Logit (RPL) models, obtaining priors to be included within a Bayesian Efficient design. Based on these priors, we generated a Bayesian efficient design containing 30 choice sets using Ngene software. Considering that each respondent can complete approximately 8-16 choice tasks before experiencing fatigue (Allenby et al., 2019), to ensure the quality of participants' decision-making, we used Ngene's blocking function to divide the choice sets into three blocks of ten choice sets each.

Participants

We administered the formal survey online via Sojump in April 2025. We inserted the "cheap talk" script immediately before the choice tasks. The script explicitly instructed respondents to treat the scenario as a real purchase. This further mitigated hypothetical bias.

We collected a total of 1,325 questionnaires through the Sojump platform. We then applied two quality control checks to assess data quality, following the approach of Cai et al. (2025). First, we examined questionnaire completion times to identify respondents who answered too quickly, removing the fastest 5% of the sample, resulting in the exclusion of 67 respondents. Second, among the remaining sample, we excluded participants who gave inconsistent answers on a repeated-choice task included as an internal consistency check, resulting in the exclusion of 237 respondents. After this screening process, 1,021 participants were retained for the final analytical sample, with the demographic composition of this sample detailed in Section 4.1.

Hen welfare egg attributes

The research team summarized a candidate attribute set for laying hen welfare eggs through literature review, covering housing systems, beak trimming, nutrient-fortified feed (selenium, calcium enrichment), certification labels (organic, green, and antibiotic-free certification), laying environment, and price dimensions (He & Xiong, 2024; Varziri et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022; Dunne & Siettou, 2020). Based on this, a focus group comprising four graduate students and two university faculty members discussed the candidate attributes' potential influence on Chinese consumers' purchasing decisions, as well as their practical applicability in the Chinese egg market. Following discussion, the group agreed that beak trimming was poorly recognized among Chinese consumers and lacked a corresponding labeling system in the market, making it difficult to construct an effective choice scenario. Although certifications such as organic and green labels were already applied in the market, their meanings were considered too broad to precisely correspond to specific improvements in laying hen welfare. Accordingly, the attributes ultimately selected for the experimental design were housing system, laying environment, antibiotic-free certification, selenium-enriched feed, and price. Housing system. Previous work on consumer valuations of animal welfare eggs has centered almost exclusively on housing system (He & Xiong, 2024; Varziri et al., 2024); we therefore retain this attribute and operationalize it as cage versus free-range system. Cage rearing refers to a farming system where laying hens are confined within conventional cages, which is a form of intensive farming. Free-range system rearing, on the other hand, provides laying hens with a larger space for movement, additional foraging opportunities, and the freedom to express natural behaviors (such as walking and wing-flapping). From an ethical standpoint, free-range hens enjoy greater locomotor space, additional foraging opportunities, and natural behavior expression. These elements represent welfare improvements (Yilmaz Dikmen et al., 2016). From a quality perspective, several studies report superior internal egg quality in free-range systems relative to cage systems (He & Xiong, 2024). Thus, free-range systems combine strong ethical effects and strong food quality effects.

Laying environment. The laying environment can be categorized into two types: nest-box use and no nest-box use. Pre-laying nesting behavior is an instinctive need for laying hens. As hens approach oviposition, they show a strong preference for concealed, enclosed nest sites and are willing to pay a high cost to gain access to one, with nest-site acquisition taking behavioral priority over feeding at this time (Weeks & Nicol, 2006). The absence of a suitable laying site leads to behavioral indicators of impaired welfare, including increased pacing, restlessness, redirected behavior, and frustration-induced aggression (Yue & Duncan, 2003; Cooper & Appleby, 1995; Hunniford & Widowski, 2016). In the European Union LayWel project's (a welfare assessment project on laying hen housing systems funded under the European Union's Sixth Framework Programme) comprehensive welfare assessment of various laying hen housing systems, nest provision was identified as the most important environmental enrichment for laying hens (Blokhuis et al., 2007). From an ethical standpoint, therefore, nest-box use carries a strong ethical effect. From a food quality perspective, eggs laid outside the nest box under free-range systems tend to be dirty eggs, with significantly higher aerobic bacterial counts on the shell compared with eggs laid inside the nest box (Holt et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2015), indicating that nest-box provision helps improve eggshell hygiene under free-range conditions. In caged systems, however, shell bacterial loads are similar between furnished and conventional cages (Jones et al., 2015), suggesting that nest boxes primarily maintain rather than enhance egg quality. Moreover, regardless of the housing system, nest-box use does not alter the internal nutritional composition or chemical residue levels of eggs. Therefore, this study defines nest-box use as an attribute with a weak food quality effect.

Antibiotic-free certification. This certification guarantees that no antibiotics are used on laying hens throughout the entire production cycle. In egg production, antibiotics are commonly used for disease prevention and treatment, but after administration, the drugs can be absorbed through the intestine and circulate via the bloodstream to the ovaries and oviduct, subsequently depositing as residues in the yolk and albumen (Owusu-Doubreh et al., 2023). Residue classes detected in eggs include coccidiostats, sulfonamides, nitrofurans, and other antibiotic types (Owusu-Doubreh et al., 2023). Consuming eggs with antibiotic residue levels exceeding maximum residue limits may pose risks such as allergic reactions, hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and teratogenicity in consumers (Owusu-Doubreh et al., 2023). Antibiotic-free certification eliminates the risk of veterinary drug residues in egg products at the source, addressing consumers' food safety concerns regarding antibiotic residues. Antibiotic-free certification therefore carries a strong food quality effect. On the ethical dimension, on the one hand, reducing antibiotic use objectively requires producers to adopt good husbandry management practices (encompassing animal nutrition status, housing and rearing conditions, and feed quality and quantity) to lower disease risk (Lhermie et al., 2017), which may indirectly improve laying hen welfare. On the other hand, once this certification commands a market premium, maintaining the certification itself constitutes an economic incentive for producers. Yet once a sick animal has been treated with antibiotics, its products can no longer be sold under this label, forcing producers to bear the corresponding loss, which may in turn lead to delayed treatment (Brock et al., 2021). Accordingly, the ultimate direction of the welfare effect depends on production choices left unregulated by the certification itself, rather than being directly determined by the certification. Antibiotic-free certification therefore carries a weak ethical effect.

Selenium-enriched feed. Selenium-enriched eggs already enjoy conspicuous market success in China as functional foods (Liu, 2025). We therefore incorporate a Selenium-enriched feed attribute. Selenium is a trace nutrient with immune-enhancing and antioxidant properties. By regulating the feeding conditions of hens to precisely control their nutrient intake, hens can be induced to produce selenium-rich eggs. Appropriate daily consumption of selenium-enriched eggs has become an effective strategy to address selenium deficiency in the Chinese diet (Kieliszek & Serrano Sandoval, 2023). Therefore, selenium-enriched feed exhibits strong food quality effects. From an ethical standpoint, compared with conventional feeding, the additional selenium intake for producing selenium-enriched eggs does not alter laying hens' egg production performance or the health indicators of organs such as the liver, heart, and spleen (Qiu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025). The protective effects of additional selenium intake, namely alleviating oxidative damage and immunosuppression, mainly become apparent when laying hens are subjected to heat stress (Wang et al., 2022). Selenium-enriched feed therefore provides a potential physiological reserve that requires the trigger of stress conditions to take effect, rather than directly improving the hens' everyday welfare status. Accordingly, selenium-enriched feed exerts a weak ethical effect.

Price. In the choice design, the price of eggs plays a key role. The levels of the egg price attribute in this study were determined based on the comparative evaluation results of market egg prices and existing research findings (He & Xiong, 2024). To capture the impact of price on consumer preferences, this study set the middle price of a box (10 eggs) at ¥12, with prices adjusted in increments or decrements of ¥3, ranging from ¥6 to ¥18.

The second section collected standard demographic information. The third section assessed participants’ concern for the food quality of eggs produced under improved welfare conditions and their general concern for animal welfare. Eggs are widely recognized for their rich nutritional content (Walker & Baum, 2022). Prior research has found that nutritional value is the quality dimension consumers perceive most strongly in eggs (Song et al., 2026). At the same time, food quality is a multidimensional and abstract concept. Consumers often struggle to form a clear and consistent judgment of it in survey contexts. Nutritional value, by contrast, is specific and easy to understand. Accordingly, participants' food quality concern for eggs produced under improved welfare conditions was measured using the statement: "The nutritional value of eggs related to laying hen welfare is receiving increasing attention." Animal welfare concern was measured by the following statement: “Farm animals should be guaranteed a happy and content life.” Both statements were measured by a 5-point Likert scale asking for respondents’ agreement.

Analysis plan and models

This section outlines the functional forms of the multinomial logit (MNL), random parameter logit (RPL), and latent class model (LCM) used to analyze the discrete choice experiment (DCE) data. All estimations were executed with the Apollo package (Hess & Palma, 2019) in R.

Choice experiments rest on McFadden’s (1974) random utility theory and Lancaster’s (1966) theory of value. This experiment, as a stated preference research method, assumes that individuals will choose the option that brings the maximum utility among multiple choices. Utility functions are usually divided into two components: the deterministic part (i.e., the observable part V) and the stochastic part (i.e., the error term ε). According to classical random utility theory, the utility of individual i choosing alternative j in choice set t can be described as follows:(1)Uijt=Vijt+εijt

For the Multinomial Logit model (MNL), one assumes that error terms are independently and identically distributed (I.I.D.) to define the random component, using a Gumbel Distribution. The MNL model also implies independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) and assumes taste homogeneity across respondents (e.g., Strazzera et al., 2012). However, this assumption of preference homogeneity might be considered too restrictive. To overcome this restriction, the RPL relaxes this assumption, allowing for preference heterogeneity (Hensher and Greene, 2003; Revelt and Train, 1998). This model’s utility function assumes a continuous distribution, with a mean value βk and idiosyncratic random deviation ηik for each respondent i and each attribute k:(3)Uijt=βkX′kjt+ηikX′kjt+εijt

The RPL requires a simulated maximum likelihood estimation, whereby the researcher must specify the number of draws. This number can influence the stability and precision of results, such that a sufficiently large number of draws is important. Our models use 500 inter-person Halton draws.

Another way of modelling preference heterogeneity is through a latent class model (Boxall & Adamowicz, 2002). This model is particularly applicable when the preferences within specific groups are relatively homogeneous, but there are significant differences between groups. Compared with the RPL model, the latent class model has advantages because researchers do not need to choose a distribution function. However, the model requires researchers to make more assumptions when determining the number of modeling categories. Results

Descriptive results

The sample characteristics are presented in Table 3. Among the 1,021 survey respondents, 64.6% were female, and 35.4% were male. Taken together, these findings provide consistent evidence in favor of H3.

In terms of the characteristics of Class A members, compared with the benchmark consumers of Class B, Class A consumers are relatively younger (at the 5% significance level), have higher income levels (at the 5% significance level), have higher education levels (at the 5% significance level), and have a higher degree of concern about the quality of animal welfare eggs (at the 1% significance level).

Although Class B consumers for each welfare attribute is significantly stronger than that of Class C consumers, they are the most sensitive to the price attribute among the four types of consumers. Thus, they can be classified as “Unbalanced welfare-price consumers”, and their consumption behavior tends to seek cost-effective welfare eggs.

Class C consumers have positive preferences for all welfare attributes, but their coefficients are generally lower than those of Class A and Class B consumers. Their price sensitivity is slightly lower than that of Class B consumers. They show a dislike for the "neither" option at the 1% significance level and are more inclined to choose between Egg A and Egg B. Therefore, they can be summarized as “Balanced welfare-price consumers”. This category of consumers accounts for the largest share among the four categories, at 42.9%. In terms of member characteristics, using Category B as the reference group, consumers in Category C are relatively more likely to be male (at the 10% significance level), tend to have relatively lower income levels (at the 10% significance level), and are more concentrated in urban areas (at the 5% significance level).

Consumers in Class D show the strongest preference for the free-range traditional welfare attribute at the 1% significance level, with its coefficient much higher than other attributes. This group of consumers has a relatively high concern for the egg quality of animal welfare eggs at the 10% significance level, second only to consumers in Class A. Therefore, they can be summarized as “Traditional welfare preference consumers”.

Overall, the four consumer classes differ markedly in their welfare-attribute preferences and price constraints. Class A (novel welfare preference) consumers show the highest preference coefficients among all four classes for nest-box use, antibiotic-free certification, and selenium-enriched feed, and are insensitive to the price attribute, resulting in relatively higher willingness to pay for these three attributes. Class D (traditional welfare preference) consumers have a preference coefficient for free-range systems that far exceeds that of the other three classes, with an insignificant price coefficient, indicating that this group's willingness to pay is most pronounced for the traditional welfare attribute of free-range rearing. By contrast, although Class B and Class C consumers hold positive preferences for all welfare attributes, both groups have negative price coefficients that are highly significant at the 1% level, making them the most price-sensitive of the four classes, which substantially constrains their willingness to pay. Within this pair, Class B has higher preference coefficients across attributes than Class C but also somewhat greater price sensitivity, reflecting a stronger tension between welfare preferences and price. Class C has the lowest preference coefficients across attributes, characterizing it as a group with mild preferences and moderate price sensitivity.

Discussion

This study aims to explore consumers' preferences and willingness to pay for laying hen welfare attributes, as well as the impact of food quality concerns and ethical concerns on attribute preferences through a discrete choice experiment. To achieve this goal, we collected data from 1021 consumers on the Sojump platform. The study found that consumers have positive preferences and willingness to pay for laying hen welfare attributes, and that food quality concerns have a greater influence on consumers' preferences compared to ethical concerns.

Consumer preferences and willingness to pay for welfare attributes of laying hens

Concerning consumer preferences for the housing system of laying hens, the findings of this study align with those of Liu et al. (2022), in that Chinese consumers show a positive preference for and willingness to pay for eggs produced under free-range systems. Using the midpoint of the price range in the choice experiment, ¥12, as the baseline, this study finds a premium rate of 78.4% for this attribute, lower than the 110.9% reported by Liu et al. (2022). This gap may be related to differences in the design of the two studies. Liu et al. (2022) used a labeled choice experiment, in which options within the choice sets were labeled as "regular eggs" and "eco-friendly eggs," respectively. In a comparative study of labeled versus unlabeled choice experiments, de Bekker-Grob et al. (2010) found that about 22% of respondents exhibited a dominant preference for option labels, meaning they answered based on the label alone rather than weighing the attributes. In this study, the options are labeled neutrally as "Egg A," "Egg B," and "Neither," carrying no evaluative information, so the estimation results may more closely reflect the marginal contribution of the attributes themselves. In addition, Liu et al. (2022) collected data through face-to-face household interviews. Existing research indicates that in face-to-face interview settings, respondents may exhibit social desirability effects on options involving ecological and welfare issues, yielding willingness-to-pay estimates twenty-three to twenty-nine percent higher than those obtained through self-administered methods (Leggett et al., 2003). This study used an anonymous online survey format in which respondents completed the questionnaire independently, which can help mitigate social desirability bias to some extent in this setting.

Regarding the welfare of the laying environment for hens, this study is the first to incorporate the laying environment as an independent attribute in the choice experiment, and the results show that Chinese consumers exhibit a positive preference for and willingness to pay for eggs produced with nest-box use. Using the midpoint of the price range in the choice experiment, ¥12, as the baseline, the premium rate for this attribute is 45.08%, the lowest among the four hen welfare attributes examined. This result indicates that corresponding market demand already exists in the Chinese market, and its policy implications depend on whether the premium can cover the incremental cost associated with providing nest boxes. When the premium exceeds the incremental cost, a laying-environment welfare standard could be introduced in China as a reference for producers. Producers willing to participate in such a standard could offer differentiated egg products to Chinese consumers; eggs that do not currently exist in the market would then become available to meet the demand of consumers willing to pay for laying-environment welfare, thereby increasing the economic welfare of both the participating consumers and producers to some extent. Consumers unwilling to pay the premium could still purchase conventional eggs at the original price, and producers who choose not to participate in the standard would remain unaffected, resulting in a Pareto improvement in social welfare.

Regarding consumer preferences for antibiotic-free certification, our findings reveal that Chinese consumers exhibit positive preferences and willingness to pay for this attribute. Using the same baseline, this attribute commands a premium of 72.5%. This estimate is comparable to the findings of Liu & Wang (2023), who report a 60% premium for antibiotic-free certification in pork production.

Regarding consumer preferences for laying hens fed with selenium-enriched feed, the results of this study show that Chinese consumers have a positive preference and willingness to pay for the attribute of selenium-enriched feed, with a premium rate of 83%, which is the highest among the four laying hen welfare attributes. Given that this attribute has a strong food quality effect and a weak ethical effect, this indicates that Chinese consumers' preference and recognition of laying hen welfare are more derived from the private good attribute of laying hen welfare, namely the food quality effect, which verifies for the first time the research conclusion that, in the Chinese market, food quality concerns have a greater influence on consumer preferences than ethical concerns.

Heterogeneity in consumer preferences

Our research results show that consumers have obvious heterogeneity in their preferences for laying hen welfare attributes, which are significantly influenced by demographic characteristics. Overall, female, young, highly educated, and high-income consumers prefer welfare eggs from laying hens, which is consistent with existing studies that women pay significantly more attention to animal welfare than men, young groups also pay more attention, and this attention will gradually decrease with age (Clark et al., 2016), as well as that the premium amount consumers are willing to pay usually increases with the improvement of income and education levels (Clark et al., 2016; Lagerkvist & Hess, 2010). The difference is that, in terms of the rearing space attribute, compared with young consumers, older consumers show a higher preference for eggs from free-range systems. This phenomenon may be related to the fact that older consumers in China are more familiar with the traditional breeding mode of raising hens on grasslands around rural families. The above content provides research references for the entry of laying hen welfare eggs into the Chinese market. Producers targeting Chinese consumers can consider market characteristics to formulate marketing strategies and focus on providing laying hen welfare eggs to relevant groups.

Food quality concerns, ethical concerns, and preference for welfare attributes of laying hens

The results of this study indicate that consumers' food quality concerns have a significant positive impact on their preference for laying hen welfare attributes, while ethical concern has no significant impact on it. This result is consistent with the research conclusions of He & Xiong (2024) on consumer preference motivations in the laying hen welfare field in the Chinese market, but differs from the findings of relevant studies in developed markets. Relevant studies in developed markets point out that consumers have an ethical premium for laying hen welfare (Dunne & Siettou, 2020; Ochs et al., 2019). As an emerging country, China has differences in the level of ethical consumption compared with developed country markets, which may be because the country-level affluence has a certain impact on ethical consumption (Prikshat et al., 2024), and ethical consumerism increases with the growth of national wealth (Summers & VanHeuvelen, 2017).

Notably, in our study's descriptive statistics, participants' mean level of concern for animal welfare was similar to their mean level of concern for the nutritional value of welfare-friendly eggs, suggesting that participants expressed attitudinal endorsement of animal welfare at a level nearly equivalent to their food quality concern. However, the choice experiment results show that only food quality concern had a significant positive effect on preferences for laying hen welfare attributes, while animal welfare concern was not significant. This gap between attitude and behavior has also been documented in research on public attitudes toward animal welfare (Jiang et al., 2024). As animal welfare is a credence attribute, consumers are unable to verify it either before or after purchase and must depend on third-party certification for confirmation, which may explain this gap between attitude and behavior (Schrobback et al., 2023). China's farm animal welfare standards are still at an early stage. Animal products that meet these standards have not gained wide recognition, and the market still lacks a comprehensive certification and labeling system for them (Jiang et al., 2024). This asymmetric distribution of animal welfare information may undermine consumer trust and reduce consumers' willingness to pay a premium for these attributes (Jiang et al., 2024).

Currently, most studies on consumers' preference for laying hen welfare attributes focus on developed countries (Doyon et al., 2016; Dunne & Siettou, 2020; López-Galán et al., 2013; Ochs et al., 2019). Therefore, another important contribution of this study lies in investigating consumers' preference for multi-dimensional laying hen welfare attributes in emerging markets, which helps producers and marketers targeting emerging market consumers to gain a clearer understanding of consumer preferences and their driving factors.

Furthermore, the findings of this study once again verify the view that food quality concerns have a stronger influence on consumers' preferences for laying hen welfare attributes compared to ethical concerns in China, indicating that food quality concerns are the core driving force in Chinese consumers' decision-making process for purchasing laying hen welfare egg products. This is consistent with the research by He & Xiong (2024) in the Chinese market and the findings of studies in developed markets. Therefore, for the Chinese market, if producers expect to gain profits through laying hen welfare premiums, they need to incorporate improved laying hen welfare into a broader concept of product quality. Specifically, relevant enterprises need to measure the impact of improved laying hen welfare on egg quality and develop certification labels to more clearly convey product quality information to Chinese consumers, thereby enhancing product competitiveness.

Overall, this study demonstrates that Chinese consumers exhibit significant positive preferences and willingness-to-pay (WTP) premiums for laying hen welfare attributes. More detailed analysis suggests that these preferences are primarily driven by food quality concerns rather than ethical concerns. In terms of preference heterogeneity, we find that female, younger, better-educated, and higher-income consumers display stronger preferences for laying hen welfare. Furthermore, latent class analysis identifies four distinct consumer segments: "Novel welfare preference" consumers (31.5%), "Unbalanced welfare-price" consumers (11%), "Balanced welfare-price" consumers (42.9%), and "Traditional welfare preference" consumers (14.6%).

These findings offer important implications for promoting hen welfare through consumer market mechanisms. (i) Studies show that Chinese consumers have a certain willingness to pay for nest-box use and are willing to pay a 45% premium for it. However, compared with the other three welfare attributes, consumers' willingness to pay for nest-box use is relatively low. Based on this, the costs and benefits of using laying nest boxes can be evaluated. If there is a possibility of profit, relevant standards for the nest-box use for laying hens can be introduced or formulated in China. The differentiated products provided by producers in accordance with this standard will be able to meet the needs of some Chinese consumers, thereby improving the level of social welfare. (ii) Given that Chinese consumers' WTP for welfare improvements is predominantly motivated by food quality concerns rather than ethical concerns, voluntary certification standards that signal credence quality attributes appear more appropriate than mandatory welfare regulations in the current market context. To support this, establishing credible certification and monitoring systems is essential to prevent fraudulent labeling and maintain consumer trust. For producers, we recommend prioritizing welfare investments that can be translated into perceivable quality cues, such as enhanced nutritional profiles or safety guarantees. (iii) Additionally, the identification of four distinct market segments suggests that China's policy design should account for heterogeneous consumer acceptance of welfare standards; avoiding uniform, "one-size-fits-all" mandates can help minimize market inefficiencies and welfare losses.

It should be noted that this study has certain limitations. First, the present study set only two levels for the housing systems attribute, battery cage and free-range systems, without covering intermediate rearing modes that fall between the two (such as enriched cages, aviaries, and barns). Future research could expand the design of attribute levels, providing a more actionable reference for the tiered development of laying hen welfare standards in China. Second, the choice experiment used in this paper still belongs to the stated preference method, and there may be certain deviations in the process of measuring consumers' preferences for farm animal welfare. As farm animal welfare attributes enter the market, auction experiments and other methods will be further used to measure consumers' preferences for laying hen welfare in the future.
