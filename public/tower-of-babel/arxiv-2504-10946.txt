Maximum principles and spectral analysis for the superposition of operators of fractional order
Serena Dipierro, Edoardo Proietti Lippi, Caterina Sportelli and Enrico Valdinoci

Abstract
. We consider a “superposition operator” obtained through the continuous superposition of operators of mixed fractional order, modulated by a signed finite Borel measure defined over the set [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . The relevance of this operator is rooted in the fact that it incorporates special and significant cases of interest, like the mixed operator − Δ + ( − Δ ) s -\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s} , the (possibly) infinite sum of fractional Laplacians and allows to consider operators carrying a “wrong sign”. We first outline weak and strong maximum principles for this type of operators. Then, we complete the spectral analysis for the related Dirichlet eigenvalue problem started in [ 7 ] .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Why superposition operators?

Superposition operators naturally arise in the study of anomalous diffusion, with a motivating example coming from population dynamics. Suppose that
a population consists of different groups of individuals, where each group follows a distinct diffusion pattern—such as a Gaussian process or a Lévy flight with varying diffusive exponents. The overall displacement of the population is then governed by an evolution equation in which the diffusion operator is a linear combination of the various operators describing each group’s diffusion pattern (see
[
11
]
).

Superposition operators, where the coefficients of these linear combinations are allowed to change sign, are also useful in describing population density patterns. Positive coefficients correspond to diffusive phenomena, while negative contributions model “reverse parabolic” features, which help describe population concentration (see
[
12
,
7
]
.

In this paper we carry on the study of operators derived from the (both finite and infinite,
both discrete and continuous) superposition of operators of mixed fractional order, focusing on two key topics: the validity of some maximum principles and the analysis of the spectral properties of this kind of operators.

1.2.
The mathematical setting

The superposition of operators that we consider takes the form

(1.1)

L_{\mu}u:=\int_{[0,1]}(-\Delta)^{s}u\,d\mu(s),

where
\mu
denotes the signed measure of the form

(1.2)

\mu=\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}.

Here above,
\mu^{+}
and
\mu^{-}
are assumed to be two nonnegative finite Borel measures over the interval
[0,1]
.
Also, in (
1.1
), the notation
(-\Delta)^{s}
stands for the fractional Laplacian, defined, for all
s\in(0,1)
, as

(1.3)

(-\Delta)^{s}\,u(x)=c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{2u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy.

The positive normalizing constant
c_{N,s}
, defined by

(1.4)

c_{N,s}:=\frac{2^{2s-1}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{N+2s}{2}\right)}{\pi^{N/2}\,\Gamma(2-s)}\,s(1-s),

being
\Gamma
the Gamma function,
is chosen in such a way that, for
u
smooth and rapidly decaying, the Fourier transform of
(-\Delta)^{s}u
returns
(2\pi|\xi|)^{2s}
times the Fourier transform of
u
and provides consistent limits as
s\nearrow 1
and as
s\searrow 0
, namely

\lim_{s\nearrow 1}(-\Delta)^{s}u=(-\Delta)^{1}u=-\Delta u\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\lim_{s\searrow 0}(-\Delta)^{s}u=(-\Delta)^{0}u=u.

We refer the interested reader to
[
4
,
1
]
for a detailed presentation of the fractional Laplace operator.

The setting in which we operate is the same as the one originally introduced in
[
5
]
. In
[
6
]
, that framework has been further broadened to the superposition of fractional
p
-Laplacians (see also
[
14
]
). In addition, the case of fractional
p
-Laplacians of different orders, namely for
s\in[0,1]
and
p\in(1,N)
, has been considered in
[
8
]
, whereas the superposition of fractional operators under Neumann boundary conditions has been examined in
[
10
,
9
]
.

Now we introduce our setting with all the details. We let
\mu
be as in (
1.2
).
The components of the measure tied to higher fractional exponents are supposed to be positive, whereas the negative components (if any) must be appropriately reabsorbed into the positive ones.
This is formally expressed by requiring the existence of
\overline{s}\in(0,1]
and
\gamma\geqslant 0
(to be chosen small enough) such that the following properties are satisfied:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mu^{+}\big([\overline{s},1]\big)>0,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mu^{-}\big([\overline{s},1]\big)=0,

(1.7)

and

\displaystyle\mu^{-}\big([0,\overline{s})\big)\leqslant\gamma\mu^{+}\big([\overline{s},1]\big).

The exponent
\overline{s}
will serve as a Sobolev critical exponent, its role being enhanced in the upcoming Proposition
2.3
.

We now delve into the two key topics of the paper: the validity of a maximum principle and the analysis of the spectral properties of the operator (
1.1
).

1.3.
Maximum principles

We point out that, if the measure in (
1.2
) comes with no negative contributions, that is
\mu^{-}\equiv 0
, then the operator in (
1.1
) boils down to

(1.8)

L_{\mu}^{+}u:=\int_{[0,1]}(-\Delta)^{s}u\,d\mu^{+}(s).

Our first set of results deals with maximum principles for the operator
L^{+}_{\mu}
in (
1.8
). We present different formulations, depending on whether weak or classical solutions are being sought.

To this aim, we take

and we introduce the space

,
which is given by the completion of compactly supported smooth functions
with respect to a specific norm which takes into account the presence of the measure
\mu^{+}
(see the forthcoming definition in (

)).
The weak maximum principles for the operator

reads as follows:

.

Let

be an open and bounded subset of

with Lipschitz boundary. Let

Let

in

in the weak sense. Suppose

1

When

at

For classical solutions, the previous statement is enhanced in this way:

1.4.
Failure of the maximum principle

We point out that the validity of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
is heavily impacted by the choice of the leading operator. One might be tempted to consider the more general operator
L_{\mu}
defined in (
1.1
) in place of
L^{+}_{\mu}
in the statements of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.
Unfortunately, this is not possible.
Indeed, a classical result by Bony, Courrège and Priouret
(see
[
3
, Theorem 3, page 391]
) states that for linear translation operators the maximum principle holds if and only if the measure
\mu
in the operator in (
1.1
) has constant sign, that is it holds by taking either
\mu^{+}\equiv 0
or
\mu^{-}\equiv 0
in (
1.2
).

For the sake of completeness, we exhibit an explicit counterexample which disproves the validity of the maximum principle in the presence of a negative contribution in the measure
\mu
.
More precisely, we will show that, for any
s\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)
,

(1.10)

the maximum principle does not hold for the operator
-\Delta-\alpha(-\Delta)^{s}
.

To this aim, we will take
\Omega:=(-1,1)
and construct a function
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
u\in C(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{2}(\Omega)
and

-\Delta u-\alpha(-\Delta)^{s}u\geqslant 0\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\quad\mbox{and}\quad u\geqslant 0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}\setminus\Omega,

but
u(x)<0
for any
x\in\Omega
.

Notice that the operator introduced in (
1.10
) corresponds to the choice
\mu:=\delta_{1}-\alpha\delta_{s}
, being
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{s}
the Dirac measures centered at the points
1
and
s\in(0,1)
, respectively,
thus the operator
-\Delta-\alpha(-\Delta)^{s}
occurs as a particular case of
L_{\mu}
.

We also point out that the assumptions in (
1.5
) and (
1.6
)
for
-\Delta-\alpha(-\Delta)^{s}
are satisfied, and, by taking
\alpha
sufficiently small, the assumption in (
1.7
)
can be fulfilled as well. This says that not even a “reabsorbing” property
can make the maximum principle hold true.

The proof of (
1.10
) will be presented in Appendix
A
.
Also, we refer the interested reader to
[
2
, Appendix A]
for a counterexample in which the “wrong” sign occurs in the Laplacian and the maximum principle is proved to fail for
the operator
\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}

for any
s\in(0,1/2)
.

1.5.
Eigenvalues and eigenfunctions

The other topic that we tackle in this paper is the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem driven by the operator
L_{\mu}
.
To this aim, we let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and we take into account the problem

(1.11)

\begin{cases}L_{\mu}u=\lambda u&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega.\end{cases}

The existence of the first positive eigenvalue of problem (
1.11
), as well as its properties, have been established in
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
.
Here, we complete the spectral analysis of the operator
L_{\mu}

by constructing a diverging sequence of eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
for the problem (
1.11
) such that each eigenvalue has finite multiplicity.
In addition, the sequence of eigenfunctions
e_{k}
corresponding to
\lambda_{k}
establishes an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega)
and an orthogonal basis for the space
X(\Omega)
, which is defined as the set of functions which belong to
X(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and vanish outside
\Omega
.

To state our result, we denote by
[u]_{s}
the Gagliardo semi-norm of
u
, with the
slight abuse of notation that
[u]_{0}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and
[u]_{1}=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
(see
the forthcoming formula (
2.1
)).
Moreover, we point out that we write

(1.12)

\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)

with an abuse of notation. Indeed, to be precise, one should write

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mu^{+}(\{0\})\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\,dx+\mu^{+}(\{1\})\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)\,dx.

We have the following result:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mu
satisfy (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) and take
N>2\overline{s}
.
Let
\Omega
be an open and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with Lipschitz boundary.

Then, there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
, depending only on
N
and
\Omega
, such that if
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{0}]
the following statements hold true.

There exists a sequence
\lambda_{k}
of eigenvalues of problem (
1.11
) such that
0<\lambda_{1}\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\lambda_{k}\leqslant\cdots
, and

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\lambda_{k}=+\infty.

Moreover,
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k+1}

are given recursively by

(1.13)

\lambda_{k+1}=\min_{u\in E_{k+1}\setminus\{0\}}\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]}[u]_{s}^{2}\,d\mu^{+}(s)-\int_{[0,\overline{s})}[u]^{2}_{s}d\mu^{-}(s)}{\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}},

where
where
E_{1}:=X(\Omega)
and, for all
k\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle E_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Bigg\{u\in X(\Omega)\;{\mbox{ s.t. for all }}j=1,\dots,k

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))(e_{j}(x)-e_{j}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu(s)=0\Bigg\}.

Furthermore, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
the function

is an eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k+1}
and achieves the minimum in (

).

Also, the sequence of eigenfunctions

constitutes an orthonormal basis of

and an orthogonal basis of

Each eigenvalue

has finite multiplicity, namely, if there exists

such that

3.
Maximum principles

In this section we provide the proofs of some maximum principles for the operator
L^{+}_{\mu}
.

Our first main result establishes the maximum principle for weak solutions, in the sense of the following definition (recall that the “abuse of notation” in (

Definition 3.1
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
We say that

is a weak solution of

(3.1)

\begin{cases}L_{\mu}^{+}u=f&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\

v\in X(\Omega)
,

\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\,dx.

Moreover, we say that
u\in X(\mathbb{R}^{N})
satisfies
L^{+}_{\mu}u\geqslant f
in
\Omega
in the weak sense if, for any nonnegative
v\in X(\Omega)

(3.2)

\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)\geqslant\int_{\Omega}f(x)v(x)\,dx.

(3.3)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1)}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle+\mu^{+}(\{1\})\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla u^{-}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1)}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u^{+}(x)-u^{+}(y))(u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle-\int_{[0,1)}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{|u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)-\mu^{+}(\{1\})\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{-}(x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{[0,1)}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{u^{+}(x)u^{-}(y)+u^{+}(y)u^{-}(x)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle-\int_{[0,1)}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{|u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)-\mu^{+}(\{1\})\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{-}(x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant-\int_{[0,1)}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{|u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)-\mu^{+}(\{1\})\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{-}(x)|^{2}\,dx.

Now, if
\mu^{+}(\{1\})>0
, we deduce from this that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{-}(x)|^{2}\,dx\leqslant 0

and therefore
u^{-}
is constant, and therefore constantly equal to zero, given its trace values along
\partial\Omega
(recall the notation in footnote
1
). This provides a contradiction with the existence
of the above mentioned set
E
.

Hence, we can now suppose that
\mu^{+}(\{1\})=0
. In this case, we infer from (
1.5
)
that
\mu^{+}\big([\overline{s},1)\big)>0
, and thus by (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle 0\geqslant\int_{[\overline{s},1)}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{|u^{-}(x)-u^{-}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle\qquad\geqslant\int_{[\overline{s},1)}c_{N,s}\iint_{E\times(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega)}\frac{|u^{-}(x)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)>0.

This contradiction completes the proof of
Theorem
1.1
.
∎

We now introduce the definition of classical solutions of problem (
3.1
). To this end, we define
the norm

(3.4)

\|u\|_{L^{1}_{\mu^{+}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}:=\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)|}{1+|x|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,d\mu^{+}(s)

and the space

(3.5)

C_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):=\left\{u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N})\mbox{ such that }\|u\|_{L^{1}_{\mu^{+}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}<+\infty\right\}.

This definition should be compared with the one of the space
C_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

given e.g. in
[
2
, formula (2.9)]
when the operator
L^{+}_{\mu}
boils down to
-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}
.

We stress that the integration in (
3.4
) is on
s\in(0,1)
and does not extend to
s=0
nor
s=1
, since the identity map and the classical Laplacian do not require any decay assumption of the function under consideration.

We also introduce the notation

(3.6)

\overline{\Gamma}_{N}:=\max_{s\in[0,1]}\dfrac{\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\dfrac{N+2s}{2}\right)}{\displaystyle\Gamma(2-s)}

Our next result shows that the space
C_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is the proper one to handle classical solutions for the operator
L^{+}_{\mu}
. Indeed, for
u\in C_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega)
, one can compute
L_{\mu}^{+}u
pointwise, according to the following statement:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
u\in C_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega)
.
Then,
L^{+}_{\mu}u\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\Omega)
and, for all
x\in\Omega

such that
B_{r}(x)\subset\Omega
for some
r\in(0,1)
,

|L^{+}_{\mu}u(x)|\leqslant C\left(\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}+|u(x)|+\|u\|_{L^{1}_{\mu^{+}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\right),

for some
C>0
, depending on
N
,
\Omega
,
r
and
\mu^{+}
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\Omega
and let
r\in(0,1)
be such that
B_{r}(x)\subset\Omega
. Recalling the definitions in (
1.8
) and (
1.3
), we can write

(3.7)

\displaystyle L_{\mu}^{+}u(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{2u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle+\mu^{+}(\{0\})u(x)-\mu^{+}(\{1\})\Delta u(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{B_{r}}\frac{2u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle+\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{2u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle+\mu^{+}(\{0\})u(x)-\mu^{+}(\{1\})\Delta u(x).

Now, when
y\in B_{r}
, a second order Taylor expansion for both
u(x+y)
and
u(x-y)
gives that

(3.8)

\begin{split}\Bigg|\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}&\int_{B_{r}}\frac{2u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)\Bigg|\\
&\leqslant 2\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{B_{r}}\frac{dy}{|y|^{N+2s-2}}\,d\mu^{+}(s)\\
&=\omega_{N-1}\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}\int_{(0,1)}\frac{c_{N,s}r^{2-2s}}{1-s}\,d\mu^{+}(s),\end{split}

where
\omega_{N-1}
is the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

We now recall (
1.4
) and see that

\displaystyle\frac{c_{N,s}}{1-s}=\frac{2^{2s-1}s\,\Gamma\left(\frac{N+2s}{2}\right)}{\pi^{N/2}\,\Gamma(2-s)}\leqslant\frac{2\overline{\Gamma}_{N}}{\pi^{N/2}},

where the notation in (
3.6
) has been used.
Exploiting this information into (
3.8
), we thus obtain that

(3.9)

\begin{split}&\Bigg|\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{B_{r}}\frac{2u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)\Bigg|\\
&\qquad\qquad\leqslant\frac{2\overline{\Gamma}_{N}\omega_{N-1}\mu^{+}([0,1])}{\pi^{N/2}}\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}.\end{split}

Furthermore, up to a suitable change of variables, we can write

(3.10)

\begin{split}&\Bigg|\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{2u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)\Bigg|\\
&\quad\leqslant 2|u(x)|\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{dy}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,d\mu^{+}(s)+2\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{|u(x+y)|}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s).\end{split}

We have that

\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{dy}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,d\mu^{+}(s)=\omega_{N-1}\int_{(0,1)}\frac{c_{N,s}}{2sr^{2s}}\,d\mu^{+}(s).

From (
1.4
) we see that

\frac{c_{N,s}}{s}=\frac{2^{2s-1}(1-s)\,\Gamma\left(\frac{N+2s}{2}\right)}{\pi^{N/2}\,\Gamma(2-s)}\leqslant\frac{2\overline{\Gamma}_{N}}{\pi^{N/2}},

and thus

(3.11)

\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{dy}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,d\mu^{+}(s)\leqslant\frac{\overline{\Gamma}_{N}\omega_{N-1}\mu^{+}((0,1))}{r^{2}\pi^{N/2}}.

Now we claim that there exists
C>0

such that, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}
and all
s\in[0,1]
,

(3.12)

\frac{1+|x+y|^{N+2s}}{|y|^{N+2s}}\leqslant C.

To check this, we define the function

f(y,s):=\frac{1+|x+y|^{N+2s}}{|y|^{N+2s}}

and we observe that

\lim_{|y|\to+\infty}f(y,s)=1\quad\mbox{uniformly for any }s\in[0,1].

As a consequence,
there exists
M>1
such that, if
|y|\geqslant M
, then
f(y,s)\leqslant 2
.

Also, if
y\in B_{M}\setminus B_{r}
, then

f(y,s)\leqslant\frac{1+(|x|+M)^{N+2s}}{r^{N+2s}}\leqslant\frac{1+(|x|+M)^{N+2}}{r^{N+2}}.

These considerations establish the claim in (
3.12
).

From the bound in (
3.12
) we thus obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{|u(x+y)|}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)\leqslant C\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{|u(x+y)|}{1+|x+y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s).

Hence, changing variable
z:=x+y
and recalling that
u\in C_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
we find that

\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{|u(x+y)|}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)\leqslant C\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(z)|}{1+|z|^{N+2s}}\,dz\,d\mu^{+}(s)=C\|u\|_{L^{1}_{\mu^{+}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

Plugging this and (
3.11
) into (
3.10
), we thus
obtain that

\begin{split}&\Bigg|\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{2u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)\Bigg|\\
&\qquad\leqslant\frac{2\overline{\Gamma}_{N}\omega_{N-1}\mu^{+}((0,1))|u(x)|}{r^{2}\pi^{N/2}}+2C\|u\|_{L^{1}_{\mu^{+}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.\end{split}

Combining this with (
3.7
) and (
3.9
), we conclude that

\displaystyle|L_{\mu}^{+}u(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{2\overline{\Gamma}_{N}\omega_{N-1}\mu^{+}((0,1))}{\pi^{N/2}}\left(\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}+\frac{|u(x)|}{r^{2}}\right)+2C\|u\|_{L^{1}_{\mu^{+}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mu^{+}(\{0\})|u(x)|+\mu^{+}(\{1\})|\Delta u(x)|,

which gives the desired result.
∎

We can now give the following:

Definition 3.3
.

Let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be any measurable function. We say that a function
u\in C_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap C^{2}(\Omega)
is a classical solution
of problem (
3.1
) if
L_{\mu}^{+}u(x)=f(x)
for any
x\in\Omega
and
u(x)=0
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

If
\mu^{+}((0,1))=0
, then the problem boils down to the classical
maximum principle for the Laplace operator, see e.g.
[
13
, Theorem 2 on page 329 and Theorem 4 on page 333]
, hence we can suppose
that
\mu^{+}((0,1))>0
.

We want to prove that
u\geqslant 0
in
\Omega
. For this, we assume by contradiction that there exists some
\overline{x}\in\Omega

such that
u(\overline{x})<0
.
Then, we prove that

(3.13)

u
cannot have an interior minimum, unless it is constant.

To this end, we assume by contradiction that
u
is not constant
and that it possesses a minimum at a point
x_{0}\in\Omega
. This implies that
\Delta u(x_{0})\geqslant 0
and
u(x_{0})\leqslant u(\overline{x})<0
.
Moreover, for all
s\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{0})=c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{2u(x_{0})-u(x_{0}+y)-u(x_{0}-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\leqslant 0.

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle 0\leqslant L^{+}_{\mu}u(x_{0})=\int_{[0,1]}(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{0})\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\mu^{+}(\{1\})\Delta u(x_{0})+\int_{(0,1)}(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{0})\,d\mu^{+}(s)+\mu^{+}(\{0\})u(x_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\int_{(0,1)}(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{0})\,d\mu^{+}(s)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{(0,1)}\left[c_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{2u(x_{0})-u(x_{0}+y)-u(x_{0}-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\right]\,d\mu^{+}(s)\leqslant 0.

Consequently, for almost every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\int_{(0,1)}c_{N,s}\frac{2u(x_{0})-u(x_{0}+y)-u(x_{0}-y)}{|y|^{N+2s}}\,d\mu^{+}(s)=0.

As a result, since
u
is not constant (and therefore
2u(x_{0})-u(x_{0}+y)-u(x_{0}-y)

is not identically null), we find that, for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
,

\int_{(0,1)}\frac{c_{N,s}}{|y|^{2s}}\,d\mu^{+}(s)=0.

Since the integrand is strictly positive, we have thus derived a contradiction
and we have completed the proof of (
3.13
).

Now, the statement in (
3.13
) and the fact that
u(\overline{x})<0
are in contradiction with
the assumption that
u\geqslant 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
.
Hence, we conclude that
u\geqslant 0
in
\Omega
, which is (
1.9
).

Now we assume that there exists
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that
u(x_{0})=0
.
Since we already know that
u(x)\geqslant 0
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it follows that
x_{0}
is a minimum point
for
u
. Hence, by (
3.13
),
we have that
u
vanishes identically. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
∎

4.
The eigenvalue problem

We start this section by introducing some preliminary notations.
We recall that the space
X(\Omega)
has been defined in (
2.3
).

The weak formulation of the eigenvalue problem (
1.11
) is given by

(4.1)

\begin{split}&\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)\\
&\quad-\int_{[0,\overline{s})}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{-}(s)\\
&=\lambda\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\,dx\qquad\mbox{ for any }v\in X(\Omega),\end{split}

where the “abuse of notation” in (
1.12
) is assumed.

We recall that if there exists a nontrivial solution
u\in X(\Omega)
of (
4.1
), then
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is called an eigenvalue of the operator
L_{\mu}
. Any solution
u\in X(\Omega)
is called an eigenfunction associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

Furthermore, let
I:X(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
be the functional defined as

(4.2)

I(u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]}[u]^{2}_{s}\,d\mu^{+}(s)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,\overline{s})}[u]^{2}_{s}\,d\mu^{-}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2},

where
\|u\|
is the norm given in (
2.10
).

We recall the following result proved in
[
7
, Lemma 3.1]
:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mu
satisfy (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
1.7
).

Let
X_{0}
be a nonempty, weakly closed subspace of
X(\Omega)
and

\mathscr{M}:=\big\{u\in X_{0}\;{\mbox{ s.t. }}\;\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\big\}.

Then, there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
, depending only on
N
and
\Omega
,
such that if
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{0}]
the following statements hold true.

There exists
u_{0}\in\mathscr{M}
such that

(4.3)

\min_{u\in\mathscr{M}}I(u)=I(u_{0})>0.

In addition, for any
v\in X_{0}
,

(4.4)

\begin{split}&\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{+}(s)\\
&\quad-\int_{[0,\overline{s})}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu^{-}(s)=2I(u_{0})\int_{\Omega}u_{0}(x)v(x)\,dx.\end{split}

The next two results provide some useful properties of the eigenfunctions and will be useful when dealing with problem (
1.11
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
be two different eigenvalues of (
1.11
) and let
e_{1}
,
e_{2}\in X(\Omega)
be associated eigenfunctions.

Then,

\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(e_{1}(x)-e_{1}(y))(e_{2}(x)-e_{2}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu(s)=\int_{\Omega}e_{1}(x)e_{2}(x)\,dx=0.

Proof.

Up to a normalization,
we can assume that
\|e_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|e_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
.

Testing the equation in (
4.1
) for
e_{1}
against
e_{2}
, we get

(4.5)

\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(e_{1}(x)-e_{1}(y))(e_{2}(x)-e_{2}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu(s)=\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}e_{1}(x)e_{2}(x)\,dx.

Similarly, testing (
4.1
) for
e_{2}
against
e_{1}
, we obtain that

(4.6)

\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(e_{2}(x)-e_{2}(y))(e_{1}(x)-e_{1}(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu(s)=\lambda_{2}\int_{\Omega}e_{1}(x)e_{2}(x)\,dx.

Combining (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), we infer that

(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\int_{\Omega}e_{1}(x)e_{2}(x)\,dx=0.

As a consequence, since
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
,

\int_{\Omega}e_{1}(x)e_{2}(x)\,dx=0.

Using this information into (
4.5
) (or equivalently (
4.6
)),
we complete the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of (
1.11
) and let
e\in X(\Omega)
be an associated eigenfunction.

Then,

\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{|e(x)-e(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu(s)=\lambda\|e\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

The desired result plainly follows by testing the equation (
4.1
) for
e
against itself.
∎

We now establish some properties of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions for
the operator
L_{\mu}
of classical flavour.
The proofs are inspired by
[
15
, Proposition 9]
,
where the case of the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
is taken into account.

We first prove that the set of eigenvalues of problem (
1.11
) consists of a sequence
\lambda_{k}
which diverges as
k
goes to infinity:

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mu
satisfy (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
1.7
). Take
N>2\overline{s}
.

Then, there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
, depending only on
N
and
\Omega
, such that if
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{0}]
the following statements hold true.

There exists a sequence
\lambda_{k}
of eigenvalues of problem (
1.11
) such that

(4.7)

0<\lambda_{1}\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\lambda_{k}\leqslant\cdots

and

(4.8)

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\lambda_{k}=+\infty.

In addition, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geqslant 1
, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k+1}

are given recursively by

(4.9)

\lambda_{k+1}=\min_{u\in E_{k+1}\setminus\{0\}}\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]}[u]^{2}_{s}\,d\mu^{+}(s)-\int_{[0,\overline{s})}[u]^{2}_{s}\,d\mu^{-}(s)}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}\,dx},

where
E_{1}:=X(\Omega)
and, for all
k\geqslant 1
,

(4.10)

E_{k+1}:=\big\{u\in X(\Omega)\;{\mbox{ s.t. }}\;\langle u,e_{j}\rangle=0\ \mbox{ for all }j=1,\dots,k\big\}.

Moreover, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geqslant 1
,
the function
e_{k+1}\in E_{k+1}
is an eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k+1}
and achieves the minimum in (
4.9
).

Proof.

The existence of the first eigenvalue is guaranteed by
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
. Hence, we now focus on the statements
for
\lambda_{k+1}
with
k\geqslant 1
.

We observe that

(4.11)

E_{k+1}\mbox{ is a weakly closed subspace of\penalty\ $X(\Omega)$}.

Indeed, let
u_{n}\subset E_{k+1}
be such that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\langle u_{n},v\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle\quad\mbox{ for any }v\in X(\Omega).

Now, for any
j=1,\dots,k
, we have that

0=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\langle u_{n},e_{j}\rangle=\langle u,e_{j}\rangle,

which gives that
u\in E_{k+1}
. This proves (
4.11
).

In light of (
4.11
), we can apply Lemma
4.1
with

X_{0}=:E_{k+1}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\mathscr{M}:=\big\{u\in E_{k+1}\;{\mbox{ s.t. }}\;\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\big\}.

Accordingly, formula (
4.3
) gives that the minimum in (
4.9
) is attained at some
e_{k+1}\in E_{k+1}
, with
\|e_{k+1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
.

Now, by (
4.10
), we deduce that
E_{k+1}\subseteq E_{k}\subseteq X(\Omega)
, and therefore
\lambda_{k}\leqslant\lambda_{k+1}
. Thus, (
4.7
), is proved.

In order to show that
\lambda_{k+1}
defined in (
4.9
) is an eigenvalue of problem (
1.11
) with corresponding eigenfunction
e_{k+1}
, we need to show that

(4.12)

\begin{split}&\int_{[0,1]}c_{N,s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\frac{(e_{k+1}(x)-e_{k+1}(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\,d\mu(s)\\
&\quad=\lambda_{k+1}\int_{\Omega}e_{k+1}(x)v(x)\,dx\qquad\mbox{ for any }v\in X(\Omega).\end{split}

To this end, we perform an inductive argument.
We observe that if
k=0
, then the desired result follows from
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
. Now, we assume that (
4.12
) holds true
for
k
and we prove that it is true for

We write

&\qquad=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}\langle e_{k+1},e_{j}\rangle-\lambda_{k+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}\int_{\Omega}e_{k+1}(x)e_{j}(x)\,dx.\end{split}

From this and (

) and (
A.7
), we find that

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{R}(x)=2x\log\left(\frac{1+R+x}{1+R-x}\right)-4-4R.

We observe that the function

h_{R}(x):=2x\log\left(\frac{1+R+x}{1+R-x}\right)-4-4R

is even and has a maximum at
x=1
in
[0,1]
. Moreover,

\lim_{R\to+\infty}h_{R}(1)=\lim_{R\to+\infty}2\log\left(\frac{2+R}{R}\right)-4-4R=-\infty.

Thus, for any
\alpha>0
there exists
R_{0}>0
such that

h_{R_{0}}(1)\leqslant-\frac{2}{\alpha}.

This proves the claim in (
A.2
) and then (
A.1
) is established also in the case
s=1/2
.

The proof of (
1.10
) is thereby complete.

Appendix B
Proof of (
4.23
)

In this appendix we prove that the sequence of eigenfunctions
e_{k}
defined in Proposition
4.4
is a basis for the space
X(\Omega)
.

To this aim, let
f\in X(\Omega)
. For any
i
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\widetilde{e_{i}}:=\frac{e_{i}}{\|e_{i}\|}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad f_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\widetilde{e_{i}}\,\langle f,\widetilde{e_{i}}\rangle.

Observe that, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

(B.1)

f_{j}\in\mbox{span}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{j}\}.

Let
v_{j}:=f-f_{j}
. Then, by the orthonormality of
\widetilde{e_{k}}
in
X(\Omega)
with respect to the norm defined in (
2.10
) (see Proposition
2.5
), we get that

0\leqslant\|v_{j}\|^{2}=\|f\|^{2}+\|f_{j}\|^{2}-2\langle f,f_{j}\rangle=\|f\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\langle f,\widetilde{e_{i}}\rangle^{2}.

Thus, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{j}\langle f,\widetilde{e_{i}}\rangle^{2}\leqslant\|f\|^{2}.

This implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}\langle f,\widetilde{e_{i}}\rangle^{2}\mbox{ is a convergent series}.

Hence, we define

\tau_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\langle f,\widetilde{e_{i}}\rangle^{2}

and we have that

(B.2)

\tau_{j}\mbox{ is a Cauchy sequence in }\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, again by the orthonormality of
\widetilde{e_{k}}
in
X(\Omega)
, if
l>j
, we see that

\|v_{l}-v_{j}\|^{2}=\left\|\sum_{i=j+1}^{l}\widetilde{e_{i}}\langle f,\widetilde{e_{i}}\rangle\right\|^{2}=\sum_{i=j+1}^{l}\langle f,\widetilde{e_{i}}\rangle^{2}=\tau_{l}-\tau_{j}.

This and (
B.2
) imply that
v_{j}
is a Cauchy sequence in
X(\Omega)
. By (
2.4
),

(B.3)

v_{j}
converges strongly to some
v
in
X(\Omega)
.

We observe that, for any
j\geqslant k
,

\langle v_{j},\widetilde{e_{k}}\rangle=\langle f,\widetilde{e_{k}}\rangle-\langle f_{j},\widetilde{e_{k}}\rangle=\langle f,\widetilde{e_{k}}\rangle-\langle f,\widetilde{e_{k}}\rangle=0.

Taking the limit as
j
goes to infinity, by (
B.3
)
we infer that
\langle v,\widetilde{e_{k}}\rangle=0
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,
the statement in (
4.21
) implies that
v\equiv 0
.

Recalling that
f_{j}=f-v_{j}
, we thereby obtain that

f_{j}
converges strongly to
f
in
X(\Omega)
.

This, together with (
B.1
), gives (
4.23
),
as desired.
