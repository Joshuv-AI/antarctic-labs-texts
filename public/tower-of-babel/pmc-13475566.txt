Consensus definition and classification of neglected fractures and dislocations: protocol for an international modified Delphi study
Authors: Alazar Menbere Haile, Fentahun Bantigegn Seyoum, Tinsae Yoseph Mekasha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeglected fractures and dislocations are a common and clinically important problem in low- and middle-income countries, often attributed to limited access to timely orthopaedic care, financial barriers, referral delays and prior non-formal treatment. Despite their clinical significance, no standardised definition or classification exists. Reported time thresholds, defining criteria and classification approaches vary substantially across and even within anatomical regions, injury types and practice settings, limiting clinical communication, research comparability and burden estimation. This study aims to develop an expert consensus-based definition and classification system for neglected fractures and dislocations, focusing on identifying a common denominator applicable across various anatomical regions and injury types, using a modified Delphi methodology.Methods and analysisA three-round, international, online modified Delphi study will be conducted involving purposively selected experts representing diverse geographical regions, subspecialties and practice settings, with approximately 200 experts invited to achieve a final panel of 20–30 participants. Questionnaire development was informed by a concurrent scoping review of the literature. Round 1 will explore the definitional elements and scope of the term as applied in orthopaedic practice, including the relative importance of temporal, biological, clinical and treatment-related factors that are often used to describe this entity, whether neglected fractures and dislocations are best conceptualised as a binary entity or a graded clinical spectrum, and whether defining criteria should vary across anatomical regions or different injury contexts. Round 2 will refine and present unresolved concepts and evaluate anatomic-specific operationalisation, including specific time thresholds and characteristic clinical and biological features. Round 3 will seek final consensus on the proposed definition and classification, including clarity, acceptability and perceived clinical usability. Consensus will be predefined as ≥70% agreement (7–9 on a 9-point Likert scale), with strong consensus defined as ≥80%. Free-text responses will undergo directed thematic content analysis. The study will be designed, conducted and reported in accordance with the Conducting and REporting of DElphi Studies (CREDES) checklist.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been obtained from the Addis Ababa University Institutional Review Board (reference: 007/26/Ortho). Findings will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed publication and presentations at scientific meetings.Trial registration details https://osf.io/jx4ew/overview?view_only=5bcabab130964c90ba0d2d1f17b8f247

Introduction

Burden and context of neglected musculoskeletal injury

Injuries account for approximately 8% of global mortality and contribute an estimated 220 million disability-adjusted life-years annually, exceeding the combined burden of tuberculosis, HIV, malaria and COVID-19.1 2 A substantial proportion of this burden arises from musculoskeletal trauma, 80–90% of which is concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where limitations in trauma systems, referral pathways and access to timely surgical care are common.2 Despite this disproportionate impact, high-quality epidemiological data on musculoskeletal trauma in these settings remain scarce.3–5

Within this broader injury burden, neglected fractures and dislocations represent an important but poorly defined aspect of musculoskeletal trauma. Patients in many LMIC settings commonly present weeks, months or even years after injury because of geographical barriers, financial constraints, prolonged referral delays, limited surgical capacity and reliance on traditional or informal musculoskeletal care.6–9 Rang observed that nearly two-thirds of the world’s population lacks access to orthopaedic care, living in what he termed an ‘orthopedic desert’, a characterisation that, despite being made three decades ago, remains broadly accurate today.10 11 Despite the apparent frequency in clinical practice, the burden of neglected fractures and dislocations is poorly quantified. Few population-based studies have directly addressed neglected musculoskeletal injuries. One notable exception is the nationwide cluster-randomised population surveys conducted in Sierra Leone and Nepal, which reported that approximately 6–10 per 1000 individuals were living with untreated fractures, highlighting the scale of the problem in resource-limited settings.12

Heterogeneity of existing definitions and the need for standardisation

A major barrier to quantifying and comparing neglected fractures and dislocations across studies is the absence of a standardised operational definition. Three broad definitional paradigms are identifiable in the literature. Time-based definitions are the most commonly used, anchoring neglect to a prespecified elapsed time from injury; however, these thresholds are sometimes adopted for operational convenience rather than biological rationale, and are frequently inconsistent even within the same anatomical region. Biology-based definitions anchor the concept of neglect to observed structural or tissue changes at presentation, such as callus formation, malunion, established nonunion, fixed deformity or degenerative joint change, rather than to a specific elapsed time. Prior-treatment definitions, increasingly prominent in LMIC literature, centre on receipt of non-medical or suboptimal care (such as traditional bone setter treatment or inappropriate immobilisation) as an independent driver of surgical complexity, irrespective of time elapsed.

This definitional heterogeneity is evident across all major anatomical regions. For cervical spine injuries, neglect has been defined as presentation after 3 weeks in some reports, while others avoid specifying a time threshold altogether.13 14 Posterior hip dislocations have been described as neglected after delays ranging from 3 days to 1 week, and there is evidence that avascular necrosis risk escalates sharply even within this narrow window.15 16 For acetabular fractures, neglect has been defined as treatment delayed beyond 3 weeks, yet poor outcomes have been reported after delays as short as 11 days.17 18 For shoulder dislocations, both 3-week and 6-week thresholds have been used in the literature to define neglect.19 20 Among paediatric injuries, supracondylar fractures have been described as neglected after 14 days, despite callus formation occurring as early as 5–7 days post-injury, with some defining neglect as early as 2 days.21

Clinical significance of delayed presentation and factors beyond time

The clinical significance of delayed presentation varies by anatomical region and injury pattern. Long-bone shaft fractures, even after prolonged delay, may remain technically manageable using conventional strategies. In contrast, the management of periarticular and intra-articular injuries, such as femoral neck fractures, acetabular injuries, major joint dislocations and paediatric elbow injuries, often becomes substantially more complex with delay, requiring corrective osteotomy, joint-preserving reconstruction, arthroplasty or other salvage procedures.18 22 23 This likely explains the concentration of neglected-injury literature in these regions. Furthermore, clinical and biological changes in some anatomical regions alter management and prognosis independent of elapsed time. In neglected paediatric supracondylar fractures, early callus formation and soft-tissue contracture may render standard closed reduction techniques no longer feasible, necessitating open reduction.21 In neglected intracapsular femoral neck fractures, assessment of femoral head viability on imaging and changes in fracture-site morphology are at least as important as duration of delay in guiding management strategy.24 In open injuries, established infection at presentation may independently alter treatment strategy and prognosis.25 These considerations collectively demonstrate that neglected fractures and dislocations cannot be adequately defined by time alone, and that a clinically meaningful definition must incorporate the biological and treatment-related consequences of neglected presentation. Although the literature suggests that the terms ‘neglected fracture’ or ‘neglected dislocation’, as used in orthopaedic practice, convey more than delayed presentation, their defining features remain inconsistently described.

Rationale for a consensus approach

The present study seeks to establish consensus on what constitutes a neglected fracture or dislocation in orthopaedic practice. Without such a consensus, orthopaedic surgeons lack a common language, musculoskeletal researchers cannot reliably compare outcomes across studies, and health systems cannot accurately quantify the burden of neglected musculoskeletal injuries.

Some authors also suggest that neglect may be better conceptualised as a graded clinical spectrum rather than a binary condition defined by a single time threshold.26 27 Neglected presentation appears to produce progressively different biological changes, operative challenges and prognostic implications across anatomical regions and injury patterns. A clinically meaningful classification should therefore capture not only whether an injury is neglected, but also the stage of structural and biological change at presentation, while accounting for anatomy, injury pattern and patient-specific factors. Given the substantial heterogeneity across anatomical regions and injury types, the goal of this study is not to establish a universally uniform definition applicable identically across all contexts, but rather to develop a unifying conceptual framework with anatomy-specific operational adaptations. Because neglected presentation is shaped by context, anatomy, biology and healthcare access, and because empirical evidence on definitional thresholds remains limited, expert consensus represents the most appropriate first step toward standardisation. Accordingly, this study uses a modified Delphi methodology to establish expert consensus on what constitutes a neglected fracture or dislocation in orthopaedic practice and to develop an internationally informed definition and classification for subsequent evaluation of its reliability, usability and clinical validity.

Methods and analysis

Study design

The study will employ a three-round, international, online modified Delphi methodology to develop expert consensus on the definition and classification of neglected fractures and dislocations.28 A modified Delphi approach is appropriate because neglected fractures and dislocations lack a universally accepted definition, existing terminology is heterogeneous across anatomical regions and injury types, and empirical evidence from which to derive standardised criteria remains limited. In such contexts, structured iterative consensus methods provide a rigorous and transparent means of synthesising expert clinical judgement. The ‘modified’ designation reflects the use of a concurrent scoping review to systematically inform Round 1 item generation.

All Delphi rounds will be conducted electronically using Tally, a web-based survey platform. Participants will remain anonymous to one another throughout the study to minimise dominance bias and encourage independent judgement. The study coordinating team will oversee questionnaire development, round administration, response synthesis and data analysis. The study will be designed, conducted and reported in accordance with the guidance on Conducting and REporting of DElphi Studies (CREDES) checklist.29

Panel eligibility and recruitment

A purposive sampling strategy will be used to recruit an international multidisciplinary expert panel of orthopaedic surgeons with relevant clinical and academic expertise in the management of neglected fractures and dislocations. Eligibility will require that participants meet at least one of the following criteria: (1) a minimum of 10 years of post-fellowship clinical experience in the management of fractures and/or dislocations, including delayed or neglected presentations; (2) a senior academic appointment, trauma fellowship directorship or leadership role in an orthopaedic or trauma professional society at national or international level; or (3) authorship of at least one peer-reviewed publication related to neglected musculoskeletal injury, fracture or dislocation classification systems or global orthopaedic care. The survey will be administered in English, and additional eligibility will require sufficient English proficiency to complete all rounds independently. Efforts will be made to ensure broad geographical representation, with participation sought from Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin America, the Middle East and high-income countries with relevant experience in treating neglected injuries (table 1). Diversity in subspecialty background, practice setting and gender will be considered during recruitment to maximise breadth of expertise and contextual relevance. Potential participants will be identified through professional networks, published literature, conference faculty lists and peer nomination. Eligible experts will be invited by email and provided with study information, consent materials and detailed participation instructions outlining the study objectives, Delphi procedures, anticipated time commitment and intended use of collected data, consistent with recommendations for optimising participant engagement and retention in Delphi studies.30 Participation will be voluntary and uncompensated. At enrolment, participants will be encouraged to commit to completion of all Delphi rounds to support continuity and stability of the consensus development process. Experts who complete all study rounds will be acknowledged in the resulting publication, subject to their consent.

Sample size

No formal sample size calculation will be performed, consistent with Delphi methodology. A minimum target of approximately 20–30 participants completing all Delphi rounds will be sought, in line with established methodological guidance, and is considered sufficient to support stable consensus formation while maintaining feasibility across multiple rounds.31 32 Approximately 200 eligible experts will be invited at study initiation to account for expected non-response and attrition across sequential Delphi rounds. This approach is consistent with prior international Delphi studies in musculoskeletal research, in which large invitation pools yielded substantially smaller final expert panels after exclusions, non-participation and round attrition. Recruitment targets were therefore intentionally inflated to preserve adequate geographical representation, subspecialty diversity and overall panel robustness. No replacement participants will be recruited after completion of Round 1. However, if fewer than 20 experts complete Round 1, the response window may be extended before proceeding to subsequent rounds.

Questionnaire development and piloting

Questionnaires for the Delphi study were developed by the investigators using findings from a scoping review conducted in April 2026 across nine bibliographical and grey literature databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, Scopus, Google Scholar, BASE, AJOL and DOAJ). The search strategies combined terms relating to fractures and dislocations, trauma and injury mechanism and delayed or neglected presentation, with anatomically stratified terms spanning the upper limb, lower limb, spine and pelvis. Of the 8346 records identified, 3772 remained after deduplication in Rayyan, of which 996 underwent full-text review. The review synthesised existing definitions, terminology variants, reported time thresholds and clinical factors associated with neglected presentation. These findings informed item generation and the development of the initial Delphi questionnaire. Survey instruments were designed to progress from broad conceptual exploration toward increasing refinement and consensus on definitional and classification elements (figure 1).

Study design and workflow.

Round 1 will focus on establishing the conceptual foundations of neglected fractures and dislocations (please refer to the online supplemental material). Participants will evaluate the contribution of multiple candidate definitional elements identified from the literature, that is, time from injury, biological or structural change, prior non-formal or suboptimal treatment, increased surgical complexity and altered expected prognosis. They will also assess an integrated draft definition, consider whether missed injuries should be included within the concept of neglected injuries and evaluate whether classification should be independent of the underlying cause of delay. Additional items will explore whether neglected fractures and dislocations are best conceptualised as a binary entity or as a graded clinical spectrum, and whether defining criteria should differ in special contexts, such as paediatric injuries and open fractures. Round 2 will present anonymised aggregated findings from Round 1 and invite participants to reconsider revised statements and unresolved concepts. It will additionally explore anatomical and injury-specific operationalisation, including clinically meaningful time thresholds across selected anatomical regions and injury patterns, and the clinical and radiographic features most characteristic of neglected presentation. Round 3 will seek final agreement on the proposed definition and classification of neglected fractures and dislocations, including its clarity, acceptability and perceived usability in routine clinical practice.

Questionnaires will primarily use structured Likert-scale items (1–9), ranking tasks, multiple-choice responses and targeted open-ended questions to enable both quantitative consensus measurement and qualitative refinement.33 Before distribution, the Round 1 questionnaire will undergo cognitive pilot testing with two to three independent clinicians to assess clarity, comprehensibility, usability and content validity. Feedback from pilot participants will inform revisions to wording, item structure and survey flow prior to study launch.

Delphi procedure

The Delphi process will consist of three sequential online rounds, each open for approximately 2–4 weeks, with interim periods allocated for data analysis, questionnaire refinement and preparation of structured feedback. Reminder emails will be sent during each round to optimise response rates and reduce attrition.

At the beginning of each subsequent round, participants will receive an aggregated summary of the preceding round’s results, including quantitative response distributions and anonymised, synthesised qualitative comments. Participants will be invited to reconsider their responses in light of the group feedback while maintaining independent judgement.

Consensus will be assessed using predefined agreement and convergence criteria. Agreement will be evaluated using the proportion of participants rating an item within the agreement range (7–9 on the 9-point Likert scale), excluding ‘unable to assess’ responses and responses from participants indicating that an item falls outside their scope of practice.34 Consensus categories will be interpreted as follows: strong consensus (≥80% agreement and IQR ≤2), moderate consensus (70–79% agreement and IQR ≤2), partial consensus (60–69% agreement and/or IQR >2) and no consensus (<60% agreement). Items meeting predefined strong or moderate consensus thresholds will be retained. Items demonstrating partial consensus or wider response dispersion will be revised and re-presented in subsequent rounds, where conceptually justified. Items with lack of consensus may be excluded following a qualitative review of participant feedback. Changes in agreement proportions and IQRs across rounds will be examined descriptively as indicators of convergence.

Data management and analysis

All study data will be collected electronically using a secure web-based survey platform. Data will be anonymised before analysis and stored on a secure local drive for a period of 10 years. Only de-identified, aggregated data will be used in reporting and dissemination. Quantitative analyses will be conducted using R or Stata. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise participant characteristics and questionnaire responses across all rounds. For Likert scale items, responses will be summarised using medians, IQRs and percentage agreement. For ranked items, weighted ranking scores will be calculated and agreement among panellists will be assessed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance, where appropriate. If clinical vignettes or case scenarios are incorporated in Round 3 to assess usability of the proposed classification, inter-rater agreement will be assessed using Fleiss’ kappa.

Open-ended responses will be analysed using directed thematic content analysis via Taguette or QualCoder. The analysis will be guided by a preliminary conceptual framework derived from the scoping review and the literature-informed conceptual domains used to develop the Round 1 questionnaire. Two investigators will independently code responses into framework-informed thematic categories while remaining open to novel concepts not represented within the initial framework. Coding discrepancies will be resolved through discussion, with adjudication by a third reviewer if required. Coded themes will be used to refine questionnaire items and to contextualise quantitative findings between rounds. No inferential paired statistical testing will be performed, consistent with the exploratory and consensus-based nature of the study. Response convergence will be assessed descriptively across rounds. Attrition rates will be reported for each round; if response rates fall below 70% in any round, reminder emails and targeted follow-up contact will be used to improve retention. To assess whether differential attrition introduces bias toward overestimation of consensus, agreement proportions and IQR values will be compared between complete-case respondents (participants completing all rounds) and all available responses at each round. However, where attrition exceeds 20% between any two consecutive rounds, findings will be flagged and results be interpreted with explicit caution.

Ethics and dissemination

Participation will be voluntary, and an information and consent page will precede each round. The study collects expert opinion only, contains no patient-level data and poses minimal risk to participants. Although participants will remain anonymous to one another throughout the Delphi process, the study team will retain participant identities for purposes of round administration and follow-up, reflecting the ‘quasi-anonymous’ structure characteristic of most Delphi studies.35 Ethical approval has been obtained from the Addis Ababa University Institutional Review Board (reference number: 007/26/Ortho). Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and presentations at national and international meetings and conferences.

This study represents the first phase of a staged research programme on neglected fractures and dislocations; subsequent phases will assess reliability, feasibility and clinical validity of the agreed definition and classification in routine practice.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design or conduct of this study because the present phase is a clinician-expert consensus exercise focused on terminology, conceptual definitions and operational classification. Patient and public involvement will be considered in later validation work assessing feasibility, usability and clinical acceptability.
