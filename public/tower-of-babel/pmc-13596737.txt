Accent type modulates frequency-specific neural tracking of speech
Authors: Anna M Czepiel, Holly Bradley, Christina M Vanden Bosch der Nederlanden, Elizabeth K Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the real world, listeners often find speech in a non-native (L2) accent harder to understand than speech in their own native (L1) accent. In ideal laboratory listening settings, these struggles can become smaller. For this reason, most research on L2 accent processing uses speech-in-noise paradigms to avoid ceiling effects. Here, we explore whether neural tracking provides a sensitive measure of L2-processing difficulties, even in ideal listening conditions. In our electroencephalography (EEG) study, Canadian English-speaking adults were presented with Canadian (L1) and Mandarin-accented (L2) English. We assessed neural tracking of speech by analyzing coherence between the speech envelope and EEG. We found significantly stronger tracking for L1-accented speech, in canonical delta-theta frequencies, as well as higher-frequency bands associated with fine-grained phonemic encoding. Exploratory analyses illustrated that transcription errors correlated with neural tracking and that neural tracking was greater for L1- than L2-accented speech at the word and phoneme rate. These findings indicate that accent type modulates neural tracking of speech at specific frequencies, even in passive, quiet settings. This study highlights the sensitivity of frequency-specific neural tracking methods for elucidating subtle accent-related variations in speech, offering a foundation for future work on accent processing across more diverse speaker and listener populations.

Introduction

Listeners find non-native (L2) varieties of their native language more difficult to understand than their own native (L1) accent (Stevenage et al. 2012; Yu et al. 2021; Verbeke and Simon 2023). In noisy listening settings, like having a conversation in a noisy restaurant, the difficulties associated with L2 speech processing can render meaningful conversations extremely difficult. Why is L2 speech processing so difficult, especially in noisy settings? The most common explanation is the additional resources it takes to adapt to the sound patterns present in other-accented speech (Lecumberri et al. 2010; Cristia et al. 2012; Van Engen and Peelle 2014; Brown et al. 2020). Despite the preponderance of work demonstrating listeners’ difficulties with accented speech in challenging listening conditions, there is little examining how the human brain responds to L2 accents in optimal listening conditions. Is processing L2 speech substantially more effortful even under optimal listening conditions, where there are minimal observable deficits in comprehension? And if so, what aspect of L2 speech makes it more effortful to process? In the current study, we use EEG to examine frequency-specific neural tracking of L1- and L2-accented speech. Our approach complements previous work by providing a continuous, temporally sensitive measure of how the brain aligns with the rhythmic structure of speech at multiple linguistic levels (e.g., phrase, word, syllable, and phoneme). Our aim is to demonstrate the sensitivity of neural tracking as a tool to probe how accented speech is processed in the absence of laboratory-induced task demands or artificially introduced noise.

Neural activity tracks the rhythms of speech across multiple frequencies (Luo and Poeppel 2007; Giraud and Poeppel 2012; Peelle and Davis 2012; Kolozsvári et al. 2021). These frequencies carry different units of linguistic structure unfolding rhythmically; words occur around 4 Hz (every ~ 250 ms), syllables at 8 Hz (~125 ms), and phonemes varying from 8–12 Hz (~83–125 ms) or 30–40 Hz (~25–30 ms; Keitel et al. 2018; Ding et al. 2017). Neural activity aligns with these rhythms in corresponding frequency bands, delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), beta (~12–30 Hz), and gamma (~30–50 Hz), each sensitive to different linguistic units (Meyer, 2018; Myers et al. 2019). Prominent speech rhythms are readily captured by the amplitude envelope of speech, where alignment of the neural activity to the speech envelope can index how well listeners attend or comprehend speech as it unfolds in time (Ahissar et al. 2001; Luo and Poeppel 2007; Zion Golumbic et al. 2013; Harding et al. 2019; Lizarazu, Lallier, and Molinaro, 2019; Vanden Bosch der Nederlanden et al. 2020; Vanden Bosch der Nederlanden et al. 2022). Several studies show a consistent relationship between greater neural tracking of speech and improved comprehension, particularly in the delta-theta band (e.g., Peelle and Davis 2012; Etard and Reichenbach 2019; Gillis et al. 2021). Delta-theta neural tracking is also reduced in difficult listening situations, including acoustic manipulations of speech (Vocoded: Peelle et al. 2013; altered envelopes: Doelling et al. 2014; time-compressed: Ahissar et al. 2001; Vanden Bosch der Nederlanden et al. 2020) and when speech is embedded in different types of noise (Ershaid et al. 2024; Etard and Reichenbach 2019; Rimmele et al. 2015; Schmitt et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023; for a review, see Zoefel and Kösem, 2024). In short, neural tracking can be an index of how successfully speech is encoded by the brain.

While this growing body of research shows that acoustically manipulating speech or presenting speech in noise is useful for understanding speech processing under challenging conditions, there is little neural work looking at other naturalistic situations that tax comprehension, such as differences between L1- and L2-accented speech. L2-accented speech is more difficult to comprehend than L1-accented speech both in quiet (Stevenage et al. 2012; Verbeke and Simon 2023) and noisy conditions (Yu et al. 2021). As described above, worse comprehension of L2-accented speech may be driven by a mismatch between incoming speech and expectations, which requires additional cognitive resources, such as listening effort and working memory, to resolve (Van Engen and Peelle 2014). Indeed, L2- compared to L1-accented speech is processed more slowly (Munro and Derwing 1998; Adank et al. 2009) and transcriptions are more error-prone (Yu et al. 2026). However, listeners adapt to an L2 accent relatively quickly (Clarke and Garrett 2004; Bradlow and Bent 2008; Xie et al. 2018; Brown et al. 2020; Wu and Holt 2022; Rovetti et al. 2023). Neurophysiological responses, like event-related potentials (ERPs), show L1- and L2-accent differences. For example, the P200 component is lower in L2- compared to L1-accented speech, suggesting listeners have difficulty extracting detailed spectral information (Romero-Rivas et al. 2015). However, ERP studies showing language differences between L1- and L2-accented speech (e.g., Romero-Rivas et al. 2015; Romero-Rivas et al. 2016; Grey et al. 2019) are largely limited to brain responses of single words, whereas neural tracking can uncover neural processing of longer utterances.

A few studies have used neural tracking to examine how accented speech is processed, but they report mixed results, likely due to differences in stimuli and task demands. For example, Song and Iverson (2018) presented English L1- and L2-accented sentences dichotically, instructing participants to attend to one speaker only. L1 listeners showed greater delta-theta coherence and larger N400 responses when attending to the L2- compared to the L1-accented talker, which the authors interpreted as greater processing effort for the less familiar L2-accented speech. However, it is difficult to disentangle whether this effect is driven by accent-specific processing or the inhibition of the easier to process L1-accented speech in the unattended ear. In a more recent naturalistic study, Dutch listeners were presented with English short stories spoken in either Dutch-accented English or American-accented English. Neural tracking was reduced in American-accented English compared to Dutch-accented English at the phonetic and lexical processing time-window (Brodbeck et al. 2024). However, the study compared tracking for different accents in the participant’s L2 (Dutch speakers listening to English), rather than their L1 (Dutch speakers listening to Dutch). Additionally, both studies were limited in terms of the neural frequency range examined, with neural tracking either collapsed across a broad frequency range (Brodbeck et al. 2024) or a single band (2–8 Hz; Song and Iverson 2018).

Previous work on the neural processing of L1 and L2 accents has also failed to explore individual differences in listeners’ neural tracking success, and how these differences might relate predictably with specific life experiences. That is, how individuals understand accented speech varies widely (Bent et al. 2016; McLaughlin et al. 2018) based on their linguistic background (Munro et al. 2006). The amount of exposure to an accent is likely an important factor, where listeners who regularly encounter a wide range of accents are better able to adapt to variation in phonetic and prosodic patterns. Listeners who have experienced more geographic mobility (Clopper and Pisoni 2006) and greater accent diversity (multi-accent exposure) are better able to recognize (Sumner and Samuel 2009), repeat (Levy et al. 2019), and learn new speech patterns (Kartushina et al. 2022), perhaps due to more flexible perceptual systems that generalize more easily across phonetic variability (Clopper 2014). Music training is another individual difference that may impact accented speech processing. Training in music may enhance auditory precision and flexibility (Patel 2012) as well as executive function skills (Okada and Slevc 2018). Indeed, participants with more music training tend to have stronger neural tracking of piano music (Doelling and Poeppel 2015) and songs (Harding et al. 2019), greater fidelity of auditory brainstem responses to speech (Skoe and Kraus 2013; Bidelman et al. 2014, but see Whiteford et al. 2025), and better ability to process speech in noise (Slater and Kraus 2016; Coffey et al. 2017, but see Boebinger et al. 2015). The impact of these individual differences remains largely unexplored, despite their relevance for understanding how listeners adapt to phonetic variability in accented speech.

The current study is the first to examine listeners’ frequency-specific neural tracking of their own L1 accent versus a relatively unfamiliar L2 accent, without the distraction of a competing speech stream or added noise. We recorded EEG from Canadian adults while they listened to English sentences presented in their own L1 accent (the local dominant variety of Canadian English) and an L2 (Mandarin-accented) variety of English. This allowed us to explore accent-related processing differences for specific linguistic timescales (e.g., word, syllable, and phoneme) by analyzing tracking across distinct frequency bands, offering a more granular picture of how the brain responds to L1- vs L2-accented speech. Based on prior findings linking stronger neural tracking to more efficient and successful speech processing, we predicted that neural tracking would be greater for L1-accented than L2-accented speech. Our primary aim was to examine whether neural tracking could capture a new vantage point on challenges associated with processing L2-accented speech under optimal listening conditions, where behavioral measures fall short of revealing subtle differences. To achieve this aim, we compared neural tracking of L1 versus L2 speech and carried out an exploratory analysis comparing how sentence-by-sentence neural tracking relates to sentence-by-sentence speech unit rates and transcription accuracy. A secondary aim was to explore whether individual differences in the neural processing of accented speech might be related to listeners’ musical training or frequency of exposure to other accents.

Materials and methods

Methods and hypotheses were pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/bhw8u/overview), where the final data frames and analysis code are also available (https://osf.io/bhw8u/overview).

Participants

For our predicted main effect of accent type, we conducted an a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 (Faul, Erdfelder, and Lang, 2007) to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) with 80% power at a 5% significance level, resulting in a recommended sample of 34 participants. We recruited 34 adults (25 female, eight male, 1 non-binary, aged 18–22 years, mean = 19.73, SD = 1.43) from the University of Toronto at Mississauga’s Undergraduate Psychology participant pool. This study was approved by the University of Toronto Research Ethics Board (Protocol number: 00045684). All participants gave written consent before participation and were compensated with course credit. Four additional participants were excluded from the current sample due to equipment error. All participants were native English speakers, who learned English before age 6 and reported currently using it as their dominant language. All participants self-reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing.

Stimuli

The stimuli consisted of 40 sentences spoken by eight female speakers—four Canadian (L1) and four Mandarin (L2)—yielding 320 sentence stimuli (used in previous studies, Yu et al. 2021, 2026). The Canadian English and Mandarin English speakers recorded sentences that were matched in length (15–21 syllables per sentence) and were acoustically matched within each accent group for average pitch (f0) and pitch variability (mean and SD of f0; cf. Yu et al. 2021). All sentences were between 2.4 and 5.6 s long (Canadian-accented sentences ranged from 2.4 to 4.3 s, Mandarin-accented sentences ranged from 3.2 to 5.6 s). To ensure that Canadian speakers had similar accents, they were all from the Greater Toronto Area. Although it was not possible to record Mandarin speakers from one metropolitan area, two independent native Mandarin speakers recognized the speakers as a standard Mandarin variety. Mandarin speakers were also late learners of English (i.e., learned after age 5), which was evident in the salience of their L2 accents. To gauge differences between accented sentences, and to facilitate comparisons with our neural tracking data, a separate group of listeners transcribed both Canadian- and Mandarin-accented speech samples in quiet and noise (see Section Behavioral transcription of stimuli).

Behavioral transcription of stimuli

Fifty participants independent from our main EEG sample (age mean = 19.78, SD = 1.91) completed a transcription task on Gorilla (https://www.gorilla.sc; Anwyl-Irvine et al. 2020). Participants spoke Canadian-accented English, learned English before age 7 (mean = 2.04 years old, SD = 1.91) and reported little-to-no self-reported exposure with Mandarin or Cantonese languages or accents (mean = 9.56% of time per day, SD = 9.41%). Participants were instructed to transcribe each sentence as accurately as possible using a computer keyboard after hearing only one presentation of the sentence (no replays). Participants heard each of the 40 sentences once (each participant heard 20 sentences in Canadian-accented speech and 20 in Mandarin-accented speech). Across all participants, accent and speaker were counter-balanced so that each sentence was heard in Canadian- and Mandarin-accented conditions for all eight speakers (see 2.2 Stimuli). Stimuli were presented over headphones at 48,000 Hz sampling rate. Twenty-five participants listened to the sentences presented clearly (i.e., no background noise) and twenty-five participants listened to the sentences in noise (Gaussian white noise, 0 decibels (dB) Signal-to-Noise-Ratio (SNR). Native English speakers assessed transcription accuracy by giving correctly transcribed words a value of 1 and any missing or wrongly transcribed words a value of 0 (Nilsson et al. 1994). Each participant’s accuracy was assessed by two independent raters; inter-rater reliability had high agreement, κ = 0.942, 95% CI [0.937, 0.947], P < 0.001. Transcription accuracy was converted to error for analysis, that is, correctly transcribed words were marked as “0” and all transcription errors were marked as “1.” Transcription results are henceforth interpreted as the percent of transcription errors per sentence.

Linear mixed effects models (LMEMs), with fixed effects of accent (Canadian and Mandarin accented sentences) and noise (clear or noise at 0 SNR) and random intercepts for subject, speaker, and sentence, revealed both accent type and the presence of noise significantly predicted transcription errors with a medium effect size (Marginal R2 = 0.437, see Supplementary Materials, Table S1), confirming that the Mandarin accent was less intelligible than the Canadian accent. Estimated marginal means showed that the Mandarin-accented sentences had a higher percentage of transcription error (M = 38.96% incorrectly transcribed words, SD = 2.73, 95% CI [32.53, 45.49]) than Canadian-accented sentences (M = 18.02% incorrectly transcribed words, SD = 2.73, 95% CI [11.58, 24.45]; t(618.45) = 16.62, P < 0.001). Significantly more transcription errors occurred in noise (M = 46.2, SE = 3.12, 95% CI [39.37, 53.01]) than clear (M = 10.78, SE = 3.12, 95% CI [3.96, 17.61]) conditions (see Fig. 1A, t(55.31) = 10.85, P < 0.001). There was a significant interaction between accent type and noise. Estimated marginal means revealed a smaller error difference between accent types in clear (M = 9.19, SE = 2.46, 95% CI [4.26, 14.10]) than noise (M = 32.91, SE = 2.46, 95% CI [27.97, 37.80], t(48) = 6.83, P < 0.001). There was no significant main effect (P = 0.776) or interaction of time (Ps > 0.297), suggesting there was no learning effect.

Stimulus power spectrum analysis

Neural tracking is inherently related to the acoustic properties of the stimulus, so we characterized peaks in the stimulus spectra related to critical time-domain elements of speech (e.g., phrase, word, syllable, phoneme rate). Using discrete Fourier transforms, we extracted the amplitude envelope power spectra (see Fig. 1C) for all stimuli and detrended (log-linear transformation) the resulting spectra to remove 1/f. Peaks related to different aspects of continuous speech around 4–5 Hz, ~ 7 Hz, and from 15–20 Hz. Power spectra between accent conditions were compared across our frequencies of interest (0.5–40 Hz) using cluster-based permutation tests (10,000 permutations, see accompanying MatLab code). We note that data in Fig. 1 has been smoothed for visualization purposes (Loess method, i.e., locally weighted regression), but unsmoothed values were used in the model. Frequency power for accent conditions were highly similar, though Mandarin- was greater than Canadian-accented stimulus power in the delta band considering raw (0.72–1.45 Hz, t = 135.3, Pcluster = 0.001; 1.64–2.73 Hz, t = 28.67, Pcluster < 0.001; 3.27–4.18 Hz, t = 14.88, Pcluster < 0.001), though this was not a robust effect as it was not replicated in the detrended signals (see gray shading in Fig. 1C and D). Since the differences between accents are all below 4 Hz, accents likely differed at the word or phrasal level (Ding and Simon, 2014, 2017). This may also reflect Mandarin speakers’ slightly slower speech rate than Canadian speakers.

Speech unit rate

We assessed whether peaks observed in Fig. 1C and D were related to word and syllable amplitude envelope changes by calculating speech unit rates for words, syllables, and phonemes (Keitel et al. 2018). We segmented sentences in PRAAT (Boersma et al. 2026) at the word, syllable, and phoneme level for all sentences (four per accent type), calculated the duration of these units, and converted to frequency (Hz). On average, words unfolded at 4.34 Hz (SD = 3.35 Hz), syllables at 5.69 (SD = 3.12 Hz), and phonemes at 15.84 Hz (SD = 10.90 Hz; see Fig. 1B, where these rates are plotted via the arrows and labels in Fig. 1C and D), which is comparable to Keitel et al. (2018). Unit rates are likely only loosely related to exact peaks in the speech envelope spectra because averaging unit rates to a single value loses some of the natural variability common to speech rhythms (i.e., more distributed spectral peaks or multiple smaller peaks). We compared unit rates between Canadian- and Mandarin-accented sentences and t-tests revealed that word, syllable, and phoneme rates differed significantly between accent conditions, where estimated marginal means showed that Canadian- were faster than Mandarin-accented speech units at the word, syllable, and phoneme levels (Bonferroni corrected P < 0.001, see Fig. 1D).

Procedure

Participants were fitted with a BrainVision (BrainProducts GmBH, Gilching, Germany) 32-channel EEG cap. Electrodes were placed according to the 10–20 system and impedances were kept below 20 Ω. Continuous EEG data was recorded at 500 Hz sampling rate and had a default reference at Cz (the predefined reference channel for BrainProducts, section 1.2: https://pressrelease.brainproducts.com/referencing/). Participants were instructed to keep still while they passively listened to stimuli in a quiet booth watching a silent movie or cartoons for ~ 35 minutes. Stimuli were presented using Psychtoolbox (PTB-3, Kleiner et al. 2007) in MatLab (2024a) via an external soundcard (Focusrite Scarlett Solo 3rd Gen USB audio interface) through two JBL Series 3 LSR305 studio speakers at 65–70 dB SPL at 48,000 Hz. Stimulus onset triggers were recorded using a BrainVision StimTrak to ensure precise synchronization between the acoustic signal and EEG recordings. Listeners heard all 320 utterances across eight blocks of Canadian- and Mandarin-accented sentences, with 1.5 s between trials. Sentence order and accent type were pseudo-randomized, where the same accent was not presented more than three times in a row. Before the experiment, participants completed a demographic questionnaire including questions on accent exposure (scale of 1–7 how much accent exposure they experience, 1 = “always have heard local Toronto-Canadian accents” to 7 = “always have heard other accents”) and musical training (Musical Training subscale, Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index, Gold-MSI; Müllensiefen et al. 2014).

E‌EG recordings and preprocessing

All data were preprocessed offline using EEGLAB (Delorme and Makeig 2004) and FieldTrip Software (Oostenveld et al. 2011). First, any noisy channels were interpolated. Then, data were high-pass filtered at 0.5 Hz and low-pass filtered at 40 Hz using a Hamming windowed sinc FIR (finite impulse response) filter. Sections of noisy data (e.g. excessive movement artifacts) were manually removed. Artifacts (i.e., eyeblinks, horizontal and vertical eye movements, noisy electrodes, muscle, and heartbeat activity) were removed using independent components analysis (ICA). Components were evaluated based on both the scalp topographies and temporal characteristics, using stereotypical artifact signatures (e.g., large deflections in frontal electrodes were identified as blinks, high-frequency bursts identified as muscle artifacts). To aid identification of non-brain components, we used EEGLAB’s function pop_iclabel and compared event-related potentials (ERPs) before and after IC removal to check expected auditory ERPs were not substantially attenuated. We removed around four components per subject (mean = 4.5, SD = 1.022). The data were then re-referenced to the average of all electrodes for each participant. We note that this re-reference to the average was not specified in our pre-registration, but this is a crucial step to best represent our auditory-generated potentials. It is not recommended that the reference electrode be at the site of the predicted topographical location of effects (Cohen 2014, pp 82–83), as such, it was necessary to re-reference EEG data to average of all electrodes as is widely done in auditory EEG studies (Luck 2014, pp 156–162). The data were epoched by being time-locked to sentence onsets with a 1000 ms pre-stimulus baseline. Any remaining noisy trials containing an electrode value exceeding ±80 mV were automatically flagged for rejection and were double-checked via manual screening. On average, 28 trials (8% of trials, SD = 16.72) were removed as part of this data cleaning. Data were down-sampled to 200 Hz. Acoustic envelopes were extracted using a gammatone filter bank (128 bands between 180 Hz to Nyquist) and taking the Hilbert transform for each band. The absolute values were summed, low-pass filtered (Butterworth, 6th order, 50 Hz cutoff), and downsampled to 200 Hz to match EEG.

EEG data and acoustic envelopes were transformed into the frequency domain using the FieldTrip ft_freqanalysis function with a multitaper method to frequencies 0.5–40 Hz (in 0.25 Hz steps) separately for each accent condition and block. From the power and cross spectral density output, cerebral acoustic coherence (henceforth, coherence) was calculated with ft_connectivityanalysis across all trials (see also Kolozsvári et al. 2021; Vanden Bosch der Nederlanden et al. 2022). This metric assesses how consistently aligned the EEG signal is to the amplitude envelope of the stimulus across trials for each frequency. Speech-brain coherence measured across trials provides a stronger means of assessing what frequencies participants reliably (across multiple speech excerpts) tracked while listening to speech, compared to single trial estimates. To account for individual differences in average coherence and to ensure that coherence indexes neural tracking of a specific sentence and not just coherence to standard features of sentences common to all speech, we calculated z-score transformations using the mean and SD of multiple iterations of random EEG-audio pairings. This null distribution was calculated by obtaining coherence for 100 random pairings (i.e. label shuffling) of EEG data and stimulus envelopes for each participant separately, which were then used to z-score transform the true coherence values. Statistics and figures all use the z-score transformations (though see supporting code and Table S3 showing that we replicate results with raw coherence). Coherence values were averaged over auditory electrodes, namely Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, F7, F8, FC1, FC2, FC5, FC6, C3, C4, CP1, CP2, and Fz (Vanden Bosch der Nederlanden et al. 2020) and topographic plots confirmed that coherence was prominent in these electrodes (see Fig. 2A).

Statistical analysis

To examine neural tracking frequencies that were significantly above the null distribution, we ran one-sample t-tests with cluster-based permutation (Monte Carlo) testing across all accents, then separately for each accent (n permutations = 10,000). Following our pre-registration, we examined whether accent condition, musical training (Gold-MSI scores) and accent exposure (all fixed effects) were related to neural tracking across all frequencies using LMEMs via lmer (Kuznetsova et al. 2017) and lme4 (Bates et al. 2015) packages. In addition to our pre-registered analyses, we further examined individual differences by computing separate Pearson’s correlations between neural tracking, accent exposure, and the musical training subscale of the Gold-MSI. We examined frequency-specific neural tracking (Di Liberto et al. 2015; Meyer, 2018; Poeppel 2014) by adding the frequency bands identified in cluster-based permutations as another fixed variable. For interpretation, we report results of the LMEMs using Estimated Marginal Means with emmeans package to allow for comparisons of main effects and interactions without reference to the first level of each variable entered into the model. Although our pre-registration specified random effects for participant, stimulus speaker ID, and sentence, the coherence analysis we chose to use (FieldTrip’s ft_connectivity) does not retain trial level data but rather provides the power-normalized summed magnitude of the cross-spectral density between EEG and audio for a single condition. Consequently, we deviated from our pre-registration to directly study the speech frequencies that listeners reliably (consistently) track across all L1- and L2-accented sentences: instead of trial, we used block as a random intercept. An additional model with experimental half (first four blocks versus last four blocks) revealed no effect of learning (see Supplementary Materials, Table S6). Finally, we ran two additional exploratory analyses to further understand how accent type related to neural tracking. First, we correlated neural tracking with behavioral sentence transcription errors. Second, we compared coherence values at each sentences’ individual word, syllable, and phoneme frequencies for each accent type.

Results

Significant neural tracking across accent types

Cluster-based permutation testing revealed that coherence was significantly above the null distribution for all frequencies (0.49–40.04 Hz, t = 894.88, Pcluster < 0.001, see Table 1, Fig. 2A). Canadian-accented sentences also had significant coherence across all frequencies (see Table 1, Fig. 2B). In contrast, Mandarin-accented sentences only had significant coherence in three frequency clusters ranging between 0.49 and 13.79 Hz (see Table 1, Fig. 2C).

Neural tracking stronger in L1-accented speech

To test our pre-registered hypotheses, we ran an LMEM to assess whether neural tracking was predicted by accent type (Hypothesis 1), music training (Hypothesis 2), and multi-accent exposure (Hypothesis 3). Neural tracking across all significant frequencies (0.49–40.04 Hz, see Table 1, Fig. 2A) was significantly predicted by accent type even while accounting for individual differences in music training and multi-accent exposure (Table 2). Estimated marginal means (Bonferroni corrected), showed that Mandarin-accented sentences (M = 0.07, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [.05 .10]) had significantly less tracking than Canadian-accented sentences (M = 0.21, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [.18 .23]) overall (t(497) = 15.298, P < 0.001). Neither music training nor accent exposure significantly predicted neural tracking, nor impacted the main effect of accent type (see Table 2 - left panel). For predicting neural tracking, the best model included only the fixed effect of accent type which had the best Akaike Information Criterion (AIC; see Table 2 – right panel or Table S2 for full models). These results were replicated with raw (non z-scored) coherence (see Figure S1, Table S3). We additionally ran correlations for music training and accent exposure with coherence averaged across all significant frequencies (0.49–40.04 Hz). After Bonferroni corrections, no significant correlations were present for either music training (Ps > 0.891) or accent exposure (Ps > 0.117) for either accent condition (see Fig. 3).

In addition to our pre-registered analyses, we investigated whether the effect of accent type differed by frequency band. We ran cluster-based permutations to compare accent conditions across all frequencies. Clusters with significant accent differences were in the delta band (0.73–4.52 Hz), theta band (5.25–7.69 Hz), beta band (clusters 14.77–16.48 Hz and 17.70–25.51 Hz) and gamma band (clusters 27.95–34.79 Hz and 35.03–40.04 Hz) (see Table 1, see Fig. 2D). We compared neural tracking between accent conditions averaged across these frequency clusters using an LMEM (see Table S4 for both full and error-free models). Canadian-accented speech had stronger coherence than Mandarin-accented speech in delta, theta, beta, and gamma frequency band clusters (estimated marginal means shown in Table 3, see Fig. 2D).

Neural tracking relates to sentence transcription errors

To better understand what the observed differences in neural tracking reflect in terms of behavior, we correlated neural tracking and behavioral transcription errors at the sentence (trial) level. We removed one behavioral outlier sentence, though we also ran the same analysis with the outlier (see Figure S2). To obtain neural tracking at a trial level, we ran a jack-knifing (leave-one-out) procedure (Richter, 2015). A larger decrease in coherence for one trial in the leave-one-out procedure means the omitted trial contributed more to the overall coherence than a trial with a smaller decrease in coherence. For ease of interpretation, the new jack-knifed values were subtracted from overall coherence so that larger jack-knifed values indicate a greater contribution from the omitted trial. We averaged jack-knifed coherence across all significant frequencies (0.49–40.04 Hz) as well as per frequency band (significant clusters from Results section above) where all significant beta band ranges were averaged together, and all gamma band ranges were averaged together. After z-scoring, we correlated coherence with transcription errors at the sentence level. After Bonferroni corrections, we found a significant negative correlation with transcription errors and jack-knifed coherence across all frequencies (r = 0.31, Pcorrected = 0.029) and for the theta band (r = 0.31, Pcorrected = 0.029), meaning that stronger neural tracking across all frequencies, but perhaps especially in the theta band, corresponded to lower transcriptions errors (see Fig. 4). All other frequency bands were non-significant (delta, P = 1.00; beta, P = 0.500; gamma, P = 0.174). When the outlier was included we replicated the significant correlation across all frequencies (r = 0.35, Pcorrected = 0.006), but the correlation in the theta band became marginally significant (r = 0.027, Pcorrected = 0.080), and the previously non-significant correlation in the gamma band reached significance (r = 0.30, Pcorrected = 0.035) (see Fig. 4).

Neural tracking relates to speech units

Since our speech unit rate analysis showed different peak frequencies for each accent type that spanned canonical theta, beta, and gamma frequency bands, we extracted coherence at the frequencies unique to each speech unit rate (word, syllable, and phoneme, see Section Speech unit rate). We again used the jack-knifed coherence estimate described above. For each sentence, the jack-knifed coherence at the frequency nearest each word, syllable, and phoneme rate was selected and averaged per sentence. LMEM (see Table S5) and estimated marginal means revealed that coherence was higher in Canadian-accented sentences compared to Mandarin-accented sentences overall, but specifically for phoneme units (P = 0.013) and word units (P = 0.014), but not syllable units (P = 0.20) (see Fig. 5).

Discussion

Understanding spoken language comes with various challenges, including adapting to diverse accents. It is well-known that L2-accented speech is more difficult to comprehend than L1-accented speech, particularly in challenging listening conditions (Lecumberri et al. 2010; Cristia et al. 2012). In the current study, we use both behavioral and EEG measures to ask whether neural tracking can capture the often subtle differences in listeners’ ability to process L1 and L2 accents in ideal listening conditions. We robustly replicated the behavioral impact of L2-accented speech on intelligibility (Yu et al. 2021, 2026) in both clear and noisy settings, with a significantly smaller effect between L2- and L1-accented speech in clear settings. We investigated whether this small behavioral effect could reliably be observed using EEG and we are the first to demonstrate stronger neural tracking in L1- than L2-accents, consistent with the well-established additional challenges listeners face in the processing and understanding of L2- versus L1-accented speech. Our exploratory analyses comparing neural tracking performance with participants’ transcription accuracy further supports the notion that these tracking differences reflect processing difficulty.

Robust differences in neural tracking between L1- and L2-accented speech were found across all significant frequencies (0.5–40 Hz), but in particular at canonical delta (0.5–4 Hz) and theta (5–8 Hz) bands as well as in beta (14.7–25.5 Hz) and gamma (27.5–40 Hz) bands, where L1-accented speech elicited significantly higher neural tracking than L2-accented speech. Key speech rhythms in our stimuli—words (~3–5 Hz), syllables (~5–7 Hz), and phonemes (~14–18 Hz)—broadly fall within these frequency bands, suggesting that participants tracked word, syllable, and phoneme level information better in L1-accented speech than L2-accented speech. Indeed, by extracting trial-level coherence specifically at each sentence’s word, syllable, and phoneme frequencies, we found stronger tracking for L1-accented sentences than L2-accented sentences at the word and phoneme levels. It could be argued that the neural tracking observed here may not represent processing in terms of comprehension but represents differences in the way listeners process different acoustic signals. Our acoustic analyses of the stimulus spectra revealed accent type differences in the delta band (0.73–4.52 Hz, see Fig. 1C) with greater power in Mandarin- than Canadian-accented stimuli. However, if the differences in neural tracking in the delta band were related to the acoustic differences, then there would be greater coherence for Mandarin- compared to Canadian-accented speech. As we find the opposite, we maintain that neural tracking appears to be most clearly related to ease-of-processing and comprehension.

To further bolster our assertion that higher neural tracking for L1- than L2-accented speech represents better comprehension of L1-accented speech, behavioral transcription errors were significantly correlated with coherence across all frequencies. This is consistent with prior studies showing robust neural tracking differences in the delta and theta bands related to comprehension (Peelle et al. 2013; Keitel et al. 2018). In addition to differences in the oft-cited delta and theta bands, neural tracking was also stronger in beta and gamma frequencies for L1- than L2-accented speech. Beta and lower gamma bands have been linked to sub-lexical processing and phonemic encoding (Lehongre et al. 2011; Di Liberto et al. 2015; Meyer 2018; Brodbeck et al. 2024), as well as to comprehension more broadly (Keitel et al. 2018). Although our neural data correlated with transcription errors overall, there were differences for the frequency-specific analyses dependent on the inclusion or exclusion of an outlier sentence with high transcription error. Transcription errors significantly correlated with neural tracking in the theta band when excluding the outlier, but not when including the outlier, and the opposite pattern was found for the gamma band. This suggests that theta and gamma neural tracking are both important for behavioral understanding, with some evidence for a unique role of the theta and gamma bands for comprehension of accented speech. However, these frequency-specific effects are weaker than the effect considering all frequencies. These results highlight the importance for future work to assess what sentence-specific factors beyond accent (e.g., semantic probability, syntactic or phonological complexity, and length) impact neural tracking at specific frequencies or whether neural tracking correlates differently with behavior when sentence difficulty is above a certain threshold.

One lingering question from our results is what stimulus specific features were being encoded at higher frequencies (~27–40 Hz)? The exact frequency range associated with phoneme processing remains debated: while some literature relates gamma (>30 Hz) to phoneme transitions (Lizarazu et al. 2019), others suggest that relevant activity may lie around 8–12 Hz (Keitel et al. 2018). We observed robust tracking at the phoneme rate when we took each sentence’s unique phoneme rate into account (around 14–18 Hz, see Fig. 1D). One possible explanation for the robust tracking in the gamma band is that phoneme-rate estimates reflect average unit timing, whereas the acoustic envelope also contains faster fluctuations generated by rapid transient events, such as consonant bursts that form just one part of a phoneme differentiated by voice onset time or fast formant transitions. These brief transients introduce higher-frequency changes in the speech envelope that are not captured by the average phoneme rate. Consistent with this interpretation, the stimulus spectrum (Fig. 1C) shows considerable variability with energy extending into the ~ 30–40 Hz range and above. If these rapid acoustic features are less predictable or harder to parse in L2-accented speech, listeners may show reduced neural tracking of them. In contrast, familiarity with L1-accented speech may allow for more efficient tracking of these fast phonetic features. In summary, the observed high-frequency tracking differences suggest that accent familiarity modulates how the brain aligns to phonemes and fine-grained acoustic–phonetic features in speech.

Beyond exposure to one’s own accent, we hypothesized that individual differences in exposure to a wide range of accents might also influence the neural tracking of speech. Greater accent diversity might tune the auditory system to handle unfamiliar phonetic variation more efficiently. Indeed, behavioral research shows that multi-accent exposure improves perception of a wider range of accents (Clopper and Pisoni 2006; Sumner and Samuel 2009; Clopper 2014). Our data did not show significant correlations between multi-accent exposure and neural tracking of L1 or L2 speech. It is possible that only frequent, high-quality exposure—such as regular conversational interaction—produces measurable neural adaptation. We also examined whether musical training, which can enhance rhythmic perception (Rammsayer and Altenmüller 2006; Zatorre et al. 2007; Kraus and Chandrasekaran 2010) and speech-in-noise (Coffey et al. 2017; Slater and Kraus, 2016) processing, was associated with neural tracking. Contrary to expectations, we found no relationship, which aligns with recent findings showing limited effects of musical training on speech perception (Schüller et al. 2025; Riegel et al. 2024; Whiteford et al. 2025; Boebinger et al. 2015). In sum, neither accent exposure nor musical training predicted neural tracking strength. However, these analyses were exploratory, and our sample may have lacked sufficient variability, especially in musical expertise and for individuals with very little multi-accent exposure. Future research with more musically diverse participants could clarify whether musical training selectively supports rhythmic and speech-in-noise processing, or also enhances sensitivity to accent-driven phonetic variability.

The current study focused on a single L1 and a single L2 accent and it is striking that despite using speech produced by highly proficient L2 talkers, we still found robust differences in the neural tracking of own L1 versus other L2 accents. Future work should look at a wider variety of accents and in more naturalistic interactions to assess generalizability of our findings. For example, examining multiple accents would replicate and extend previous work in this area (e.g., Peelle et al. 2013; Rimmele et al. 2015; Etard and Reichenbach 2019). Further, different accents could have a variety of frequency-specific differences, which might not mainly rely on the phonemic frequencies. For example, L2-speakers of different languages may carry over prosody/stress rhythms of their native tongue in different ways (Chakraborty and Goffman, 2011), which might be reflected by different bands of frequency-specific neural tracking. Neural tracking also opens new possibilities for studying how other-accented speech is processed in multilingual communities and children. Since we know both bilinguals and children struggle more than monolingual adults with speech in noise—confounding accent-issues with high load task demands—using EEG in addition to traditional behavioral approaches may be a fruitful avenue for studying accent adaptation in these populations (Skoe and Karayanidi 2019; Bsharat-Maalouf and Karawani 2022).

Conclusion

We examined how neural tracking of speech is influenced by accent familiarity using naturalistic L1- and L2-accented speech in a passive listening task. Overall, our results show that significant neural tracking occurred for both accent types, with stronger tracking in L1-accented speech than L2-accented speech in all frequency bands thought to be related to phrasal, word, syllable, and phoneme levels. This work contributes to the broader understanding of how the brain tracks real-world variability in speech, and sets the stage for future research in children, bilinguals, and other diverse listener populations.
