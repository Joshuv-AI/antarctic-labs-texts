Development and evaluation of a novel nebulized dihydromyricetin formulation: enhanced pulmonary delivery and pharmacokinetic properties
Authors: Kunyan Xu, Jianing Yang, Meng Yan, Dan Li, Wenli Du, Jing Bai, Bai Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRespiratory diseases are a major global health burden, and inhalation therapy offers efficient pulmonary targeting and therapeutic effectiveness. Dihydromyricetin (DMY), a natural flavonoid with anti-inflammatory and antiviral activities, shows limited clinical application due to poor solubility, instability, and low oral bioavailability caused by extensive first-pass metabolism.MethodsA nebulized inhalation formulation of DMY was developed to enhance solubility, stability, and bioavailability. Pharmacokinetics were evaluated in rats following inhalation and oral administration. Safety was assessed through pulmonary function testing and histological examination of major organs.ResultsInhalation delivery significantly improved DMY bioavailability in both lung and systemic circulation compared with oral dosing. No differences in pulmonary function or tissue morphology were observed between the inhalation and saline groups after short-term administration, suggesting preliminary safety, which warrants further investigation.ConclusionThe nebulized DMY formulation demonstrates enhanced bioavailability, effective pulmonary targeting, and favorable safety, providing a promising therapeutic strategy for respiratory diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03410-7.

Introduction

Respiratory diseases, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and viral respiratory infections, remain a significant public health challenge worldwide [1]. These conditions are associated with high morbidity and mortality, and their prevalence continues to rise due to factors such as air pollution, population aging, and recurrent viral outbreaks [2, 3]. The frequent occurrence of respiratory infections underscores the need for effective local therapeutic strategies to alleviate airway inflammation and prevent secondary complications [4]. Although several antiviral and anti-inflammatory agents have been developed, the efficacy and safety of many existing treatments remain unsatisfactory, highlighting the need for improved or alternative therapeutic approaches for respiratory diseases [5, 6]. Effective pulmonary drug delivery plays a critical role in managing respiratory diseases by enabling high local drug concentrations, rapid onset of action, and reduced systemic side effects [4]. Among various administration routes, inhalation therapy has gained widespread use in clinical practice due to its superior targeting efficiency and improved patient compliance [5]. These advantages make it particularly well suited for both chronic respiratory disorders and acute viral infections.

Flavonoids have various pharmacological effects, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antiviral, and neuroprotective effects [6]. Flavonoids have also recently been recognized as agents with promising antiviral effects on a variety of viruses, including influenza A (H1N1) virus [7], Ebola virus (EBOV) [8], and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) [9]. Among these, dihydromyricetin (DMY), a flavonoid derived from vine tea, has demonstrated inhibitory effects on key viral enzymes and significant anti-inflammatory activity [10]. It has also shown the ability to alleviate pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis, which are common pathological features in various respiratory diseases [11]. Although DMY has been investigated in the context of COVID-19, its therapeutic potential extends to a broader spectrum of respiratory conditions, including viral pneumonitis and fibrotic lung injury, making it a promising candidate for inhalation-based treatment strategies.

In recent years, DMY has shown therapeutic potential in various pulmonary disease models, including COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, acute lung injury (ALI), and lung cancer. In COPD, DMY improved airway epithelial function by reducing mucus hypersecretion and restoring ciliary structure in air-liquid interface cultures derived from patient bronchial cells [12]. In pulmonary fibrosis, DMY attenuated bleomycin-induced fibroblast activation and tissue remodelling via the STAT3/p-STAT3/GLUT1 pathway [13]. For ALI, oral DMY mitigated lung histopathological damage and suppressed MAPK signalling in LPS- or sepsis-induced mouse models [14, 15]. Moreover, DMY has also demonstrated anti-tumour activity in lung cancer models [16]. However, most of these studies have relied on systemic administration, and the therapeutic effect of DMY is limited by its poor water solubility, poor stability, and high hepatic and intestinal first-pass metabolism, resulting in extremely low oral bioavailability [17]. To improve the delivery efficiency of DMY, researchers have begun exploring nanocarrier systems such as liposomes [18], micelles [19], nanoparticles [20], or self-emulsifying drug delivery systems (SEDDS) [17]. However, these approaches remain limited, and systematic investigations of its pulmonary delivery are scarce. Meanwhile, other flavonoids (e.g., quercetin) have been explored for nebulized inhalation in animal experiments, but most of these studies lacked comprehensive evaluations of pharmacokinetic profiles, local tissue distribution, and long-term safety [21, 22].

Many current pandemic threats are caused by viruses that infect the respiratory tract, but notably, most antiviral drugs are administered via routes other than the respiratory route. From this perspective, we believe that in the search for new therapeutic strategies against respiratory viral infections, the pulmonary route of administration deserves more attention [23]. Considering the characteristics of viral infection and lung disease, DMY was formulated as an inhalation solution, which has more advantages than conventional DMY administration because the respiratory delivery route ensures that DMY is delivered to the lungs and bloodstream at lower doses and at higher concentrations than oral formulations are used; this approach has minimal or even no side effects and better therapeutic efficacy [24]. Additionally, reduced dosing frequency and improved convenience of inhaled formulations may contribute to better patient compliance and clinical outcomes in the management of both chronic and acute respiratory conditions [25]. These considerations illustrate the potential value of pulmonary delivery for flavonoid-based antiviral agents.

Therefore, inhalation formulations have great promise for addressing the challenges of the oral absorption of DMY and increasing its pulmonary antiviral efficacy. In this study, DMY was formulated as an inhalation solution and comprehensively evaluated for the first time in terms of pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and safety. This approach enabled targeted delivery to the lungs, improving drug stability and local exposure compared with conventional oral administration, while minimizing systemic side effects. These findings provide important preclinical evidence supporting the feasibility of DMY pulmonary delivery and highlight the potential of inhalation formulation strategies for improving the bioavailability and therapeutic performance of flavonoid compounds.

Materials and methods

Material

All reagents and materials used in this study were of analytical grade, unless specified otherwise. DMY (content > 98%) was purchased from Shanghai Shengde Medical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). PBS was obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Methanol (chromatographically pure) was obtained from OCEANPAK (Tianjin, China). Tween-80 was supplied by Shandong Yousheng Chemical Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Glycerol was obtained from the Second Pharmaceutical Factory of Xingtai City, Hebei Province (Hebei, China). Ethanol and propylene glycol were both acquired from Tianjin Yongda Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Ascorbic acid was provided by Xiangteng Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Sodium metabisulfite was purchased from Tianjin Bodi Chemical Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na) was sourced from Beijing Chemical Factory (Beijing, China).

Animals

Healthy male Kunming (KM) mice weighing 20–30 g and healthy male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats weighing 200–250 g were both obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Hebei Medical University (Shijiazhuang, China). The animals were housed in clean cages under conditions of free access to water and food, controlled temperature (25±2 °C) and humidity (RH 50–75%), and a 12-h light cycle. All procedures involving animal housing and treatment were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and approved by the Institutional Authority for Laboratory Animal Care of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University. (Approval No. IACUC-Hebmu4th-20250511).

Preparation of liquid DMY inhalation formulations

Chromatographic conditions

The chromatographic column was Kromasil ™C18 (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm); the mobile phase was methanol–0.1% aqueous phosphoric acid (30:70, V/V); the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min; the detection wavelength was 291 nm; the column temperature was 30 °C; and the injection volume was 20 µL.

pH screening of inhalation formulations

Determination of the pH value of the formulation: According to the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China (2020), the pH of inhaled liquid preparations should be in the range of 3–10 before use. Solubility examination: Appropriate amounts of DMY were added to 2 mL of PBS solutions with pH values ranging from 3 to 10. The mixtures were subjected to ultrasonic treatment until no further dissolution of DMY was observed. The suspensions were then filtered through a 0.45 μm microporous membrane to obtain clear saturated DMY solutions at each pH level. The solubilities of DMY in buffer solutions with different pH values were determined. Solution stability study: An appropriate amount of DMY was dissolved to prepare a 1 mg/mL stock solution. A 1 mL aliquot of this stock solution was transferred into a 10 mL volumetric flask and diluted to volume with PBS buffers of varying pH values (3–10). The resulting solutions were stored at room temperature (25 °C). At predetermined time intervals (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 h), aliquots were withdrawn and analyzed by HPLC under the chromatographic conditions described in Section "Chromatographic conditions". For each time point, three independent replicates (n = 3) were tested. Stability was defined as retention of at least 90% of the initial concentration (time 0).

Screening of cosolvents for inhalation formulations

Tween-80, glycerol, ethanol, and propylene glycol were selected by checking the types of cosolvents available for FDA inhalation formulations, as well as the maximum amount of cosolvents to obtain saturated solutions of clarified DMY under different cosolvents and to determine the maximum solubility of DMY in different cosolvents.

Screening of stabilizers for inhalation formulations

Ascorbic acid, sodium metabisulfite, and EDTA-2Na were selected by checking the types of stabilizers available for FDA inhalation formulations, as well as the maximum dosage. The formulations were prepared with different stabilizers. The solutions were placed at ambient (25 °C) and 60 °C environments, and samples were collected at different intervals to determine the DMY content.

Pharmacokinetic studies of inhaled liquid formulations of DMY

Chromatographic conditions

The chromatographic column was Kromasil ™C18 (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm); the mobile phase was methanol–0.1% aqueous phosphoric acid (35:65, V/V); the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min; the detection wavelength was 291 nm; the column temperature was 30 °C; and the injection volume was 20 µL. The internal standard was dihydroquercetin.

Preparation of standard solutions

Standard stock solutions were prepared as follows: DMY was weighed in a 25-mL volumetric flask, dissolved in methanol, diluted to scale, and shaken well to obtain 100 µg/mL DMY control stock solutions. The samples were stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C.

The appropriate amount of dihydroquercetin was weighed, placed in a 25-mL volumetric flask, dissolved in methanol, diluted to scale, and shaken well to obtain a 100 µg/mL dihydroquercetin internal standard solution, which was stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C.

Dosing regimen

Inhalation Group: The inhaled dose was calculated using the formula [26]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D=C\times V_{m}\times t\times Fr$$\end{document}

where D is the delivered dose (mg/kg), C is the drug concentration in the inhalation chamber (determined by collecting the aerosolized drug, quantifying it using HPLC, and converting to chamber concentration), Vm is the respiratory minute volume (~ 1.0 L/min/kg for mice), t is the exposure duration (30 min), and Fr is the fraction of inhaled drug deposited in the lungs (~ 0.3 for mice). Using this method, the inhaled dose was estimated to be approximately 13.2 mg/kg.

Oral Group: Dihydromyricetin was suspended in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose to prepare a 20 mg/mL suspension, which was administered by oral gavage at a dose of 200 mg/kg. This oral dose was chosen based on previous studies demonstrating tolerable administration and measurable systemic exposure in mice.

Method of administration

Seventy-two adult Kunming female mice were randomly divided into a gavage group and a nebulized inhalation group; 36 mice were included in each group, with 4 mice assessed at each of the 9 time points. The mice were fasted for 12 h and provided ad libitum access to water. Based on the relevant literature, the sampling time points for the pharmacokinetic and tissue distribution assays of DMY in the mice were determined. Blood was collected from the orbits at 5, 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, and 150 min after drug administration, placed in sodium heparin-containing tubes, labelled, and centrifuged immediately at 4000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were placed in a freezer at -20 °C until use. The lung, heart, liver and kidney tissues were also removed, and after the surface was rinsed with low-temperature saline to remove the blood and contents, filter paper was used to absorb the water, which was stored at -80 °C until use.

Data processing

The in vivo pharmacokinetic parameters of DMY, including the maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), time to reach Cmax (Tmax), area under the drug–time curve (AUC), mean residence time (MRT) and half-life (T1/2), were calculated and analysed via DAS 3.0 pharmacokinetic software with a noncompartmental model.

The area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC0−∞) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal method. To ensure comparability between administration routes, AUC values were dose-normalized (AUC/Dose) prior to the calculation of bioavailability. The relative bioavailability (F%) was determined as (AUC (nebulized) / Dose (nebulized)) / (AUC (gavage) / Dose (gavage)) × 100%. This normalization eliminates the bias caused by different administered doses and allows a more accurate comparison of systemic exposure.

Preliminary safety evaluation of DMY inhalation liquid formulations

Method of administration

Six healthy female Sprague‒Dawley rats were randomly selected and divided into two groups: the preparation group and the saline group. The rats were placed in the semi-closed device, the inlet of the device was connected to the outlet of the nebulizer to ensure that the nebulized gas entered the device efficiently, 2 mL of the DMY inhalation solution (2 mg/mL) or saline was added into the nebulizing cup, and the nebulizing device was turned on for nebulized drug administration. The drug was administered once a day for 7 consecutive days. At the time of administration on the 7th day, the lung function of the rats was tested, and after measurement, the heart, liver, lung, and kidney were dissected and isolated, and the sections were stained for observation.

Lung function tests in rats

Pulmonary function was assessed using a whole-body plethysmography (WBP) system for small animals (Data Sciences International, USA). During operation, airflow changes were detected by sensors to assess respiratory parameters and pressure signals, from which a series of parameters related to the animal’s lung function were derived.

Histopathological analysis

Six rats were randomly selected, and the nebulizing device was turned on for nebulized drug administration. The drug was administered once a day for 7 consecutive days. At the time of administration on the 7th day, a lung function test was performed on the rats. After the test was complete, the heart, liver, lung, and kidney were dissected and isolated. Anatomically isolated heart, liver, lung and kidney tissues were immediately rinsed with saline to remove blood, filter paper was used to blot the tissues dry, and the tissues were fixed with a 4% formaldehyde solution. Then, the tissues were dehydrated, embedded, and sectioned; finally, H&E staining of the sections was performed. The irritation of lung tissues by DMY inhalation preparations was examined by placing them under a microscope and evaluating alveolar swelling and congestion, infiltration, hyperplasia, and other lesions in the lung tissues. Lung tissue (target organ) and major non-target organs (heart, liver, and kidney) were evaluated using a semi-quantitative “Severity × Extent” scoring system. Severity (0–3) was graded based on the degree of cellular degeneration or inflammation, and extent (0–3) was graded based on the percentage of tissue involvement. The final score for each organ was calculated as Severity × Extent. All slides were independently reviewed by two blinded pathologists, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Results and discussion

Results of the screen for DMY inhalation formulations

To identify optimal formulation conditions for DMY inhalation solutions, the effects of pH, cosolvents, and stabilizers on its solubility and stability were systematically investigated (Fig. 1). DMY exhibited limited solubility under acidic to neutral conditions, with a pronounced pH-dependent increase in solubility observed as pH rose (Fig. 1A). Within the pH range of 3–6, the solution remained stable for up to 8 h, with no apparent color change or loss of drug content (Fig. 1B). In contrast, at pH ≥ 7, a rapid decline in drug content was observed [27], accompanied by a color shift from colorless to brick red within 1 h, indicative of chalcone formation. This transformation is attributed to the deprotonation of hydroxyl groups on the DMY molecule in alkaline conditions [28]. At pH > 10, DMY underwent oxidative degradation and ring-opening reactions, resulting in significant alterations to its molecular conformation. These findings suggest that DMY exhibits optimal physicochemical stability under mildly acidic to neutral conditions, providing a rational basis for pH selection in inhalation formulation [29]. Consequently, pH 6 was selected as the optimal condition for subsequent solution preparation.

To optimize the excipient composition of DMY inhalation formulations, cosolvent and stabilizer screening was performed in combination with solubility and thermal stability assessments. Among the four cosolvents evaluated, only propylene glycol significantly enhanced the solubility of DMY to a level sufficient for formulation development (Fig. 1C). A concentration gradient of propylene glycol (0–25%) was further tested, and the solubility threshold was met only in formulations containing 20% or 25% propylene glycol (Fig. 1D).

To identify a suitable stabilizer, the drug content of DMY was monitored over time at room temperature (25 °C) and elevated temperature (60 °C). Sodium metabisulfite demonstrated superior stabilizing effects under both conditions and was selected for subsequent formulation studies (Figs. 1E, F).

Based on these findings, the optimized formulation was composed of DMY, propylene glycol (as cosolvent), and sodium metabisulfite (as antioxidant stabilizer), with the pH adjusted to 6.0. This combination effectively minimized DMY degradation during storage. The final preparation process involved dissolving 2 mg or 4 mg of DMY in 1.5 or 1.6 mL PBS (pH 6.0), followed by the addition of 0.4 or 0.5 mL propylene glycol and 6 mg sodium metabisulfite. The solution was mixed by ultrasonic dispersion and sterilized through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, yielding the DMY inhalation formulation (Table 2). This optimized formulation process ensures reproducibility, stability, and suitability for further inhalation delivery evaluations.

DMY inhalation formulations

In this study, a concentration of 20–25% propylene glycol (PG) was used as a co-solvent to enhance the solubility and stability of DMY in the nebulized formulation. Although PG is widely applied in pharmaceutical and inhalation products, its use at relatively high concentrations may raise concerns regarding potential airway irritation. Previous studies have shown that Montharu et al. did not observe significant irritation when using a higher concentration (30%) of PG in a rat inhalation model [30]. In the nasal inhalation study by TB Langston et al., the highest dose, equivalent to 1,152 mg/kg/day, did not produce any adverse effects [31]. Moreover, acute exposure studies in humans demonstrated no significant alterations in lung function following moderate PG inhalation [32]. These findings suggest that PG is generally well tolerated within certain exposure ranges; however, the possibility of local irritancy at higher concentrations cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, further preclinical and clinical investigations are warranted to comprehensively assess the long-term pulmonary safety of PG-containing nebulized formulations.

Results of pharmacokinetic and tissue distribution studies of inhaled liquid formulations of DMY

Compared with oral DMY formulations, nebulized DMY inhalation formulations allowed more drug flow to the lungs, resulting in faster and greater blood entry, increased relative bioavailability, and greater drug accumulation in the lungs, relatively prolonging the drug’s residence time in the body (Fig. 2) [33]. Based on the concentration–time curves and the relevant pharmacokinetic parameters, after aerosolized administration, the DMY concentration peaked in both plasma and lung tissues and then decreased sharply, suggesting the accumulation of DMY inhalation formulations in the lungs and blood of the mice, after which DMY is distributed to tissues such as the heart, liver, and kidney. In plasma, inhalation administration of DMY resulted in significantly shorter Tmax, higher Cmax, and increased AUC (P < 0.05), indicating faster systemic absorption and higher peak exposure. In contrast, t₁/₂ and mean residence time (MRT) showed non-significant differences (P ≥ 0.05), suggesting that the elimination kinetics were largely unchanged In lung tissue, Tmax was significantly shorter following inhalation (P < 0.05), whereas Cmax, t₁/₂, MRT, and AUC did not differ significantly between the two groups, indicating faster local absorption without substantial changes in overall lung exposure or elimination. The dose of DMY administered by nebulization was 13.2 mg/kg, the dose of DMY administered by oral was 200 mg/kg, and the relative bioavailability F = AUC0−∞/D (dose) nebulized/AUC0−∞/D (dose) gavage × 100% = 2266.12%, indicating that the DMY inhalation formulation could significantly increase drug exposure and improve bioavailability in mice. Hepatic, cardiac, pulmonary, and renal DMY concentrations peaked at 30 min after administration, and the drug content of DMY in each tissue was close to 10% of the maximum concentration at 150 min, suggesting that DMY was eliminated more rapidly in mice and did not easily accumulate. DMY was detected in all major organs of the mice, possibly suggesting that DMY is widely distributed in the body [34].

To evaluate the robustness of this calculation, a brief sensitivity analysis was performed by varying the deposition fraction (Fr, 0.25–0.35) and minute volume (Vm, 0.8–1.2 L/min/kg) within physiologically reasonable ranges reported for mice. The resulting F% values varied by less than 15%, indicating that the enhanced bioavailability observed with inhaled DMY is not an artifact of parameter uncertainty but reflects the genuine advantage of pulmonary delivery.

In this study, we observed significant pharmacokinetic differences between inhaled and orally administered DMY. These differences can be largely attributed to the inherent advantages of pulmonary delivery, including bypassing first-pass metabolism, achieving rapid systemic absorption, and providing high local drug concentrations in the lung. Nevertheless, we also acknowledge that the oral and inhalation formulations were not identical, and the use of different excipients may have contributed to part of the observed differences. Importantly, even when accounting for this potential influence, the consistent enhancement in lung exposure and systemic availability with inhalation strongly supports the superiority of pulmonary delivery. Future studies employing a unified vehicle for both administration routes would help distinguish the effects of the formulation itself from those of the delivery route, which would be of significant value.

Despite the clear trends observed, this study has limitations that must be considered. Most notably, we observed considerable inter-individual variability in the quantitative pharmacokinetic parameters, as indicated by the relatively high standard deviations. This variability is a recognized challenge in preclinical pulmonary delivery models, where minor differences in administration technique (e.g., depth of inhalation, distribution within the airways) can significantly impact the initial dose deposited and subsequent absorption kinetics. Furthermore, inherent physiological differences between animals may contribute to variations in drug distribution and clearance. While this variability does not detract from the consistency of our primary conclusion—that the formulation provides sustained lung exposure and high relative bioavailability—it introduces uncertainty in the precise estimation of PK values. Therefore, these parameters should be interpreted with this caveat in mind, and future work with larger cohorts will be essential to refine these estimates.

In conclusion, pharmacokinetic and tissue distribution analyses demonstrated that nebulized administration of DMY resulted in faster absorption and higher peak plasma and lung concentrations compared with oral administration, while elimination kinetics (half-life and mean residence time) remained largely unchanged. Inhalation delivery enhanced lung targeting while relatively reducing off-target distribution. Furthermore, the rapid systemic entry and elevated bioavailability of the inhaled formulation support its potential for clinical application as an effective pulmonary drug delivery strategy [35].

Lung function tests in rats

Lung function testing is a valuable tool for assessing pulmonary health in animal models, offering insights into both functional impairment and therapeutic efficacy [36]. In rats, these assessments provide detailed information on parameters such as ventilation, airway resistance, and breathing rhythm, which are considered sensitive indicators of respiratory status. Although there are anatomical and physiological differences between the respiratory systems of rodents and humans, the rat model remains a widely accepted and essential platform for the preclinical evaluation of inhaled therapeutics. Lung function tests in rodents not only reflect pulmonary injury or dysfunction but also offer translational relevance by informing the early diagnosis, progression, and prognosis of respiratory diseases in humans, as well as supporting therapeutic regimen selection and efficacy evaluation [37].

In this study, pulmonary function was assessed using whole-body plethysmography (WBP), a non-invasive and reliable technique recognized by the ICH S7A guidelines for respiratory safety evaluation in small animals [38, 39]. The WBP not only reveals relevant indices of pulmonary ventilation function but also detects respiratory mechanic indices related to the influence of airway resistance or lung compliance [40]. No significant differences were observed between the DMY inhalation group and the saline control in key respiratory parameters—such as respiratory frequency, tidal volume, minute ventilation, airway narrowing index, and maximal inspiratory flow rate—indicating that repeated short-term inhalation of DMY did not adversely affect lung function (Fig. 3). These multi-dimensional indices serve as robust biomarkers of respiratory function and drug-induced impairment, collectively indicating a preliminary pulmonary safety profile and acceptable short-term tolerability of the inhaled DMY formulation, which warrants further confirmation in extended and disease-relevant studies.

Histopathological analysis

In addition to functional assessments, histopathological examination was performed to evaluate the local and systemic safety of the DMY inhalation formulation following short-term nebulized administration. The lung, liver, kidney, and heart were selected for microscopic analysis due to their relevance in drug absorption, metabolism, distribution, and potential toxicity. To further quantify the histopathological changes, a semiquantitative scoring method using H&E staining was employed to assess the severity scores of tissues including the lung, liver, kidney, and heart (Fig. 4). Minor alterations, such as slight haemorrhaging, alveolar swelling, and mild inflammatory cell infiltration, were observed in both the DMY-treated and saline control groups, indicating that these findings were not formulation-specific and likely within the normal range of physiological variation. According to established toxicological criteria, these mild, non-progressive changes are generally considered non-adverse and acceptable in preclinical inhalation studies [41]. Furthermore, the absence of significant histopathological abnormalities aligned with the pulmonary function test results, which demonstrated preserved respiratory capacity and no evidence of lung injury. These findings suggest that no apparent tissue toxicity was observed in this preliminary short-term evaluation. However, given the limited sample size and study duration, these results should be interpreted as indicative rather than conclusive. Further long-term and larger-scale studies are warranted to comprehensively assess systemic safety.

Although this study did not assess therapeutic efficacy in disease models, the baseline data obtained here clearly indicate the direction for future research. Upcoming studies will further validate the therapeutic relevance of inhaled DMY in pathological models, including evaluations of its anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury and bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis models, as well as its potential antiviral and lung-protective activities in viral infection settings. These investigations will help bridge the gap between proof-of-concept and clinical application, promoting the further development of DMY as a novel nebulized inhalation therapy for respiratory diseases.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel nebulized inhalation formulation of dihydromyricetin (DMY) was successfully developed and evaluated for pulmonary delivery. The formulation significantly improved the solubility, stability, and bioavailability of DMY, and achieved strong lung-targeting efficiency while minimizing systemic distribution. Pharmacokinetic studies revealed that nebulized administration extended the residence time and half-life of DMY compared with oral administration, and resulted in higher drug concentrations in both lung tissue and systemic circulation. A small animal whole-body volume scanning system was used to test the lung function of the rats, and the effects of drug administration on heart, liver, lung and kidney tissues were observed by staining tissue sections. The two methods complemented each other and together showed that multiple administrations of the DMY inhalation formulation over a short period had no significant effect on the lung function or tissues of the rats compared with the saline group, indicating that the formulation exhibits a relatively favorable preliminary safety profile. Overall, the inhaled DMY formulation provides a new strategy for the treatment of respiratory diseases, with potential therapeutic value pending validation in disease models and longer-term safety studies.
