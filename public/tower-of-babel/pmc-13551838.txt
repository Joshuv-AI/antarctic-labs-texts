Comparison of microdrop administration using a bottle adapter of 2.5% phenylephrine and 0.8% tropicamide with standard drop for ROP examination: a randomized controlled noninferiority trial
Authors: Vivetha Elango, Narayanan E, Muthukumaran N
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharmacologic mydriasis is essential for retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) screening; however, conventional ophthalmic drops substantially exceed neonatal conjunctival sac volume, leading to systemic absorption and potential adverse effects. Microdrop administration may reduce drug exposure while preserving adequate pupillary dilation. This study aimed to determine whether microdrop administration of phenylephrine and tropicamide is noninferior to standard drops in achieving adequate mydriasis for ROP screening via a bottle adapter.MethodsThis single-center, randomized, parallel-group noninferiority trial was conducted in a tertiary neonatal unit in Chennai, India. Preterm infants undergoing routine ROP screening were randomized to receive either microdrops (10.26 µL) or standard drops (26.58 µL) of 2.5% phenylephrine and 0.8% tropicamide. Three doses were administered at 10-minute intervals. The primary outcome was the mean pupil diameter at 45 min after the first instillation. The secondary outcomes included physiological parameters (heart rate, oxygen saturation, and blood pressure), systemic and local adverse events, and the adequacy of ROP examination. A noninferiority margin of − 0.5 mm was prespecified.ResultsAmong the 127 infants assessed for eligibility, 102 were randomized (microdrop group, n = 52; standard group, n = 50), and all completed the study. Baseline characteristics were comparable between the groups. At 45 min, mean pupil diameter was 6.54 ± 0.52 mm in the microdrop group and 6.38 ± 0.63 mm in the standard-drop group (mean difference, 0.16 mm; 95% CI, − 0.07 to 0.38 mm; p for noninferiority < 0.001). As the lower bound of the 95% CI (− 0.07 mm) was above the prespecified noninferiority margin of − 0.5 mm, noninferiority was established. Physiological parameters, including heart rate, oxygen saturation, and blood pressure, remained stable and comparable between the groups. Systemic adverse events were rare and similar across groups, and no local ocular complications were observed. ROP examination was successfully completed in all infants.ConclusionMicrodrop administration of phenylephrine and tropicamide achieved mydriasis comparable to standard drops, with no significant difference in adverse effects between the groups. This approach may represent a safer, clinically feasible and more physiologically appropriate strategy for ROP screening by reducing drug exposure in vulnerable preterm infants.Trial registrationThe study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Madras Medical College, Chennai (IEC No. MMC/Approval/12052025). Introduction

Preterm neonates undergo multiple examinations for retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) during their stay in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) and after discharge for early diagnosis and timely management. Various mydriatic regimens with different compositions are used worldwide. The commonly used topical agents include a combination of adrenergic agonist and anticholinergic agents. Up to 80% of instilled ophthalmic drops may undergo systemic absorption, particularly in neonates, due to a reduced conjunctival sac volume and immature drug metabolism [1, 2]. Standard ophthalmic drops (~ 26 µL) substantially exceed neonatal conjunctival cul de sac volume capacity (0.5–2 µL), resulting in overflow and nasolacrimal drainage [3]. In contrast, microdrops (5–15 µL) better approximate the physiological conjunctival cul de sac volume and may reduce systemic exposure while maintaining efficacy [4–6].

Several studies have demonstrated that reduced-volume mydriatic administration via micropipettes and modified cannulas achieves comparable pupillary dilation with reduced systemic exposure [4–8].

Currently, there are no studies evaluating the mydriatic regimen commonly used in India for neonatal ROP screening with a simplified delivery method, such as bottle adapters or calibrated droppers, which could facilitate the widespread use of microdrop instillation across neonatal units. The present study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of microdrops (10.26 µL) compared with standard drops (26.58 µL) of the recommended formulation containing 2.5% phenylephrine and 0.8% tropicamide(TrophthaTM−P-Semi) in achieving adequate mydriasis for ROP examination.

Sample size

On the basis of a power of 90%, an assumed standard deviation of 0.75 mm (based on a study by Alpay et al.), and a predefined noninferiority margin of − 0.5 mm for the pupillary diameter, a minimum sample size of 78 infants was needed [10]. Considering a potential 20% dropout rate, the total sample size was 98 infants, with 49 infants in each group.

The noninferiority margin of 0.5 mm was determined on the basis of clinical consensus as the largest difference that would not meaningfully affect the adequacy of mydriasis needed for ROP screening. Prior trials evaluating microdrop mydriasis have used similar margins, supporting the clinical relevance of this range [1, 7, 8]. Setting the noninferiority margin at this unit mitigates measurement error and ensures alignment between the statistical sensitivity and the practical limits of clinical precision. This margin was closely aligned with the − 0.4-mm minimum clinically acceptable difference used in the MyMiROPS noninferiority trial and also corresponded to one increment (0.5 mm) of the pupil-diameter measurement scale used in the present study [8].

Results

Participants

Standard drop group (comparator)

A standard drop volume of 26.58 µL was instilled directly from a conventional synthetic dropper bottle.

The same mydriatic formulation was used in both groups:

Phenylephrine 2.5% and tropicamide 0.8% (TrophthaTM−P-semi).

Phenylephrine 2.5% and tropicamide 0.8% (TrophthaTM−P-semi).

The microdrop volume was determined by independent gravimetric analysis of the study formulation; using the measured formulation density (0.9896), the bottle-adapter system delivered a mean volume of 10.26 µL/drop, compared with 26.58 µL/drop for standard drop.

In both groups, three doses were administered by the nurse, with one drop instilled into each eye at 10-minute intervals.

During administration, the infant’s eyelids were gently retracted by the nurse, and one drop was instilled into each eye at each dosing interval.

During the procedure, infants were swaddled and continuously monitored for vital parameters via a multiparameter monitor. ROP examination was performed by an experienced ophthalmologist via the pediatric wide field fundus camera(RetCam, Clarity USA).

Prior to examination, topical prilocaine anesthetic eye drops were instilled, after which an eyelid speculum was gently inserted. Retinal examination and staging were performed and documented via RetCam images.

A formal pharmacological washout period was not prespecified; however, ROP screening examinations were performed at weekly intervals in our unit.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All randomized infants who received the assigned intervention and had baseline measurements were included in the analysis.

Continuous variables (gestational age, postmenstrual age, birth weight, present weight, pupil diameter, heart rate, SpO₂, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and mean blood pressure) are expressed as the mean (SD) or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and categorical variables (sex, comorbidities, ROP stage, adverse events, respiratory support, and examination adequacy) are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The normality of the distribution was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Baseline characteristics were compared between groups via the independent samples t test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

The primary outcome was the mean pupil diameter at 45 min (T45). The noninferiority of microdrops compared with standard drops was evaluated via the mean difference (microdrop − standard) and the corresponding two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI). A noninferiority margin of − 0.5 mm was predefined. Noninferiority was concluded if the lower bound of the 95% CI was above the prespecified margin [16–18].

Between-group comparisons of pupil diameter at T45, T90, T120, and postprocedure were performed via independent samples t tests. Within-group changes from baseline were analyzed via paired t tests.

Physiological parameters (heart rate, SpO₂, and systolic, diastolic, and mean blood pressure) at baseline and subsequent time points were compared between groups via independent samples t tests.

The incidence of systemic and local adverse events and the adequacy of ROP examination were compared via the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

A predefined subgroup analysis was performed according to gestational age (< 34 weeks and ≥ 34weeks). All tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. No missing data or protocol deviations were observed; therefore, no imputation or sensitivity analyses were performed.

Results

A total of 127 infants were assessed for eligibility, 102 of whom were randomized: 52 to the microdrop group and 50 to the standard drop group. Twenty-five infants were excluded (18 did not meet the inclusion criteria, 5 declined participation, and 2 were unable to provide consent). All randomized participants received the allocated intervention and were included in the final analysis, with no loss to follow-up. Baseline characteristics

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced between the groups, with no statistically significant differences. The mean (SD) gestational age was 30.9 (3.2) weeks in the microdrop group and 30.7 (3.3) weeks in the standard group. The mean (SD) birth weights were 1401 (563) g and 1327 (606) g, respectively. The distributions of ROP stages and comorbidities, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia, patent ductus arteriosus, necrotizing enterocolitis, and sepsis, were comparable between the groups. Discussion

This randomized controlled noninferiority trial demonstrated that compared with standard drop instillation, microdrop administration of 2.5% phenylephrine and 0.8% tropicamide results in noninferior pupillary dilation. All examinations were successfully completed with comparable mydriatic efficacy at 45, 90, and 120 min post-instillation. No significant differences in heart rate, oxygen saturation, or blood pressure were observed between the groups. This finding extends emerging evidence supporting reduced-volume mydriatic regimens as safe, effective alternatives to standard dosing in preterm infants [7, 8].

Previous studies have demonstrated effective mydriasis using microdrop volumes of approximately 5–7 µL with varying mydriatic regimens. Wheatcroft et al. used 5 µL microdrops of 2.5% phenylephrine and 0.5% cyclopentolate, compared with 26 µL standard drops of the same regimen [4]. The Little Eye Drop Study used approximately 7 µL microdrops and demonstrated noninferiority of a very-low-dose regimen (0.5% phenylephrine and 0.1% cyclopentolate) compared with a low-dose regimen (1% phenylephrine and 0.2% cyclopentolate) [7]. The MyMiROPS trial used 6.5 µL microdrops of 1.67% phenylephrine and 0.33% tropicamide and demonstrated superior mydriatic efficacy at 45 min and fewer oxygen desaturation events compared with standard-volume drops [8].Current Indian ROP guidelines recommend pupillary dilatation with a combination of 2.5% phenylephrine and 0.5–1% tropicamide, administered as one drop two to three times at 10–15-minute intervals before examination, with measures to minimize systemic absorption [19]. Our findings using 2.5% phenylephrine and 0.8% tropicamide demonstrate that the principle of volume reduction applies across a wide range of drug concentrations. The 2019 systematic review by Kremer et al. revealed that published evidence supporting current mydriatic guidelines was largely underpowered with a high risk of bias, despite the widespread use of adult-equivalent dosing in neonatal units [1]. The convergence of recent trials across geographies and formulations validates that reduced-volume delivery represents a fundamental principle for optimizing the risk‒benefit profile of mydriatic therapy. The safety advantage of microdrop administration is grounded in neonatal pharmacology. Standard drops (approximately 26 µL) substantially exceed neonatal conjunctival sac volume capacity (0.5–2 µL), resulting in excessive nasolacrimal drainage and systemic absorption [3]. Up to 80% of instilled mydriatics may undergo systemic absorption, which is particularly significant in neonates because of their low circulating blood volume and immature hepatic metabolism [1, 2]. Microdrops (5–15 µL) more closely approximate physiological conjunctival sac volumes, optimizing the local drug effect while minimizing systemic exposure [4–6]. Both groups achieved mean pupillary diameters exceeding 6 mm at 45 min, which is well above the threshold required for adequate retinal visualization. All examinations were successfully completed, including identification of treatment-requiring ROP. These findings confirm that a reduction in drop volume does not compromise diagnostic efficacy [7–8].

Physiological monitoring revealed no significant differences in adverse effects between the groups. Importantly, apnea was already prevalent in this preterm population before the ROP examination. In the preceding 24 h, apnea was documented in 8 infants (15.4%) in the microdrop group and 10 infants (20.0%) in the standard-drop group. Post procedure apnea was reported in 9 infants (17.3%) and 12 infants (24.0%), respectively. Thus, the proportion of infants with apnea was only modestly higher after the procedure, with no significant difference between the treatment groups. No gastrointestinal complications or serious adverse events were observed. These findings are consistent with previous studies documenting the physiological instability associated with ROP examinations [20].

Unlike earlier microdrop studies requiring micropipette delivery, the use of a bottle adapter system (Nanodropper) demonstrated feasibility for routine clinical practice. From a practical perspective, the Nanodropper is a sterile, disposable bottle adapter composed of medical-grade silicone and high-density polyethylene and is intended for use with a single compatible ophthalmic bottle. It is attached directly to the bottle and enables microdrop administration in a manner similar to conventional eye-drop instillation, without requiring specialized equipment or additional technical skills. Previous studies have demonstrated consistent reduction in drop volume with the Nanodropper across different ophthalmic formulations and effective mydriasis with approximately 10-µL drops in children [21]. The adapter is not intended for resterilization or reuse with another bottle, and a no-touch technique should be maintained to avoid contact of the dispensing tip with the ocular surface or other surfaces and minimize microbial contamination. Although commercially available, geographic availability and cost may influence its wider implementation, particularly in resource-limited settings.

This investigation employed rigorous methodology, including randomized allocation with concealment, blinded outcome assessment, stratification by gestational age, and a prospectively calculated sample size, which is consistent with established standards for noninferiority trials [16–18]. However, this single-center study may not fully represent other healthcare settings. Although adequately powered for the primary outcome, the sample size may be insufficient to detect rare adverse events. Manual measurement of pupillary diameter introduces potential error compared with automated methods; the use of photograph-based pupillometry in prior studies represents a methodological improvement [7]. Infants with ROP stage > 2 were excluded because advanced ROP may itself impair pupillary dilatation and thereby confound assessment of the pharmacological mydriatic response. Consequently, the findings of this study should be extrapolated cautiously to infants with advanced ROP.The exact timing, duration, and interventions required for individual post-procedure apnea episodes were not prospectively recorded, limiting assessment of their temporal relationship to mydriatic administration or the ROP examination. In addition, ROP screening in the present study was performed using a pediatric wide-field fundus camera (RetCam); cardiorespiratory responses may differ with indirect ophthalmoscopy and scleral depression, which may limit the generalizability of the safety findings to other screening techniques. Although clinical adverse events were monitored for 48 h, serial physiological monitoring was limited to 120 min; therefore, delayed physiological effects cannot be excluded. The lack of pharmacokinetic analysis limits mechanistic interpretation; however, prior studies have demonstrated increased systemic bioavailability with standard drops, supporting volume reduction strategies [8].

Current evidence suggests that transitioning from standard large-volume drops to reduced-volume microdrops is a preferred approach for neonatal ROP screening [7, 8]. This aligns with clinical guidelines emphasizing safe and effective mydriatic use in preterm infants [19]. Future multicenter trials with larger sample sizes are needed to detect rare adverse events and assess cumulative exposure across repeated ROP examinations. Comparative effectiveness studies evaluating clinically meaningful outcomes, including necrotizing enterocolitis, sepsis, and mortality, would provide stronger evidence for widespread adoption.

Conclusion

Microdrop administration of phenylephrine and tropicamide achieved mydriasis comparable to standard drops, with no significant difference in adverse effects between the groups. These findings support the use of reduced-volume mydriatic regimens as safe, effective, and scalable alternatives for ROP screening in preterm infants.
