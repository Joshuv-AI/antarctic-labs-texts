Enzymes assembled pickering emulsion as self-cascade bioreactor to reprogram inflammatory microenvironment for gouty arthritis therapy
Authors: Yuanru Liu, Jingqing Gan, Zunfu Hu, Yang Zhang, Baochan Yang, Fengqiang Cao, Xiuwen Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Gouty arthritis (GA) is the most prevalent metabolic inflammatory arthropathy worldwide, imposing an escalating burden on both patients and health-care systems [1]. Clinically, GA manifests as acute-onset erythema, swelling, and stiffness with excruciating pain, predominantly affecting the first metatarsophalangeal joint [2, 3]. Without timely intervention, recurrent attacks progress to tophi formation, joint deformity, and permanent disability [4]. At the molecular epicenter of this pathology lies the precipitation of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals within the synovial space [5]. Once phagocytosed by macrophages, these crystals ignite the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering a burst of interleukin (IL)−1β and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α that propagates a self-amplifying inflammatory circuit via NF-κB crosstalk. As a result, there is increased infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils, which release excessive amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and superoxide anions (•O2−) [6, 7]. This cascade triggers oxidative stress and amplifies inflammatory signaling, causing disruption of the immune microenvironment in the joint cavity [8]. Moreover, the abnormal activation of macrophages exacerbates tissue damage and impairs the immune environment necessary for tissue repair, making it difficult for the tissue to recover, and even leading to tissue degeneration [9].

Uricase (URI) is an enzyme that catalyzes the degradation of uric acid (UA) into allantoin, playing a vital role in rapidly reducing UA levels in the body, particularly by effectively eliminating large MSU crystals [10, 11]. Yet this reaction stoichiometrically generates cytotoxic H2O2 that, within the relatively avascular and enclosed joint cavity, accumulates rapidly and precipitates oxidative damage, offsetting the therapeutic benefit of URI [12]. Coupling URI with catalase (CAT) in a cascade offers an elegant solution by immediately decomposing H2O2 into innocuous water and oxygen [13]. However, improper assembly of the two enzymes may hinder the accessibility of active sites and the transport of intermediates, ultimately reducing catalytic efficiency. More gravely, this strategy still leaves the joint awash in oxidative stress conditions, which disrupts immune homeostasis and drives the joint microenvironment into irreversible structural collapse and functional loss [14].

Pickering emulsions, solid-particle-stabilized dispersions, have emerged as versatile microreactors that can spatially confine enzymes at the oil-water interface, thereby accelerating intermediate transfer and shielding proteins from denaturing stresses [15, 16]. Van Hest et al. reported that polymer vesicles were cross-linked to better maintain structural integrity and retain enzymes inside, which can be used for single reactions and multi-enzyme cascades at the emulsion interface [17]. Ngai et al. engineered a robust water-in-oil Pickering emulsion stabilized by hydrophobic silica nanoparticles in concert with pH-responsive PDEAEMA microgels. These microgels not only armored the droplet interface but also served as nanoreactors, sequestering enzymes and orchestrating catalysis directly at the water–oil boundary [18]. The synergistic architecture delivered a fivefold boost in conversion efficiency versus the simple biphasic control, underscoring its potency as a programmable biocatalytic platform. These strategies utilize particles as carriers to enhance the catalytic efficiency of enzymes at the emulsion interface. Moreover, leveraging the hierarchical architecture of Pickering emulsions, targeting ligands can be anchored on the droplet surface while immunomodulators are sequestered within the hydrophobic core [19, 20]. This spatially programmed design simultaneously (i) amplifies enrichment in the inflamed joint and immune cells targeting, (ii) synergizes with co-encapsulated enzymes to inhibit oxidative stress, and (iii) intercepts key inflammatory signaling cascades [21]. Operating as a microscale bioreactor, the emulsion system coordinates cascade catalysis with on-demand regulation of cellular oxidative stress, reprograming the inflammatory microenvironment and restoring immune homeostasis, thereby delivering a multi-level, synergistic therapeutic regimen for GA.

Herein, URI and CAT were arranged at the Pickering emulsion interface, forming self-cascade bioreactors that exhibited excellent cascade catalytic capabilities and H2O2 scavenging potential. The tight adsorption of enzyme molecules at the interface reduces interfacial tension and increases the stability of PEBR. The droplet surface was modified with mannose ligands via a clinically approved 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-polyethylene glycol (DSPE-PEG) linker to actively target CD206-expressing inflammatory macrophages. Simultaneously, the oil core was loaded with methotrexate (MTX), a potent anti-folate that suppresses ROS generation and cytokine production while steering macrophage polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype [22]. Notably, PEBR can be readily synthesized through a one-step ultrasonic method, which obviates harsh chemistry and preserves native enzyme activity. We anticipate that PEBR will not only deplete MSU deposits with unprecedented efficiency but will also re-programmer the inflammatory microenvironment, thereby offering a safe, clinically translatable and function-integrated platform for the precision treatment of GA (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Cell lines and animals

RAW 264.7 cells, derived from mouse mononuclear macrophages (catalog number: STCC20020), were cultured in a high-glucose DMEM complete medium (Servicebio, G4511), sourced from Servicebio. C57 BL/6J and BALB/c female mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were supplied by Jinan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. The mice were provided with ad libitum access to food and water and were maintained under controlled environmental conditions, including a temperature range of 22–24 °C, humidity levels between 30 and 70%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the guidelines approved by Jinan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Co., Ltd (license number: SCXK 20220006).

Materials

Bovine serum albumin (BSA, 96%) were procured from Aladdin, while CAT was obtained from Sigma. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), Cy7, and MTX (99%) were acquired from Solarbio. Squalene (99%), URI, 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB, ≥ 99%), Cy3 (95%), Cy5 (≥ 95%), and UA were sourced from Macklin. MSU was synthesized by Shanghai Yuanye Bio Technology Co., Ltd. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7.4), 4% paraformaldehyde, JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit, and 0.25% trypsin were obtained from Servicebio. Additionally, the ROS assay kit and cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) were purchased from Beyotime.

Preparation of PEBR

PEBR was prepared via a one-pot self-assembly strategy. In brief, 200 µL of URI and 200 µL of CAT were sequentially introduced into buffer containing DSPE-PEG-mannose. Subsequently, 30 µL of MTX-loaded squalene was incorporated into the mixture. And ultrasonication (100 W, 2 min) at low-temperature was performed to ensure complete encapsulation and stabilize the emulsion.

MTX loading efficiency

The MTX loading efficiency was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (Agress 1100). MTX was dissolved in squalene and formulated into PEBR. The emulsions were diluted to the working range, then centrifuged (10,000×rpm, 10 min, 20 °C). The subnatant was withdrawn, filtered (0.22 μm), and injected (20 µL) onto a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) equipped with a matching guard cartridge. Elution (isocratic 60% acetonitrile/40% water, 0.5 mL/min, 30 °C) was monitored at 302 nm. Finally, encapsulation efficiency was calculated from peak areas against identically prepared MTX standards, with triplicate determinations.

Mannose location

To visualize the localization of mannose within PEBR, FITC-conjugated mannose was formulated into the emulsion. Stimulated emission depletion microscopy (STED; Leica, Mannheim, Germany) was used to acquire FITC (488 nm excitation/520 nm emission) signals, thereby mapping the spatial distribution of the mannose within individual droplets.

The Spatial confinement of URI and CAT

The spatial confinement of URI and CAT were evaluated via fluorescence resonance transfer (FRET) analysis. Cy3-labeled CAT (donor) and Cy5-labeled URI (acceptor) were prepared at equal molar ratios and incorporated into three formulations including C-U (free CAT and free URI were mixed), DPE (the mixture of CAT stabilized-emulsion and URI stabilized-emulsion are separately made into Pickering emulsion and mixed) and PEBR. All samples were excited at 550 nm (Cy3 λex) and emission spectra from 550 to 700 nm were recorded. The sensitized emission of Cy5 and the concomitant quenching of Cy3 were analyzed to calculate FRET efficiency (E) and donor-acceptor distance, thereby quantifying the degree of enzyme co-localization within each architecture.

Assays of enzymatic reaction

URI activity was quantified spectrophotometrically by monitoring the decrease of UA absorbance at 290 nm. UA (100 µM) was incubated with URI, PEU, DPE or PEBR in PBS (pH = 7.4, 25 °C), and the change of absorbance was recorded continuously for 30 min. Additionally, the evaluation of CAT activities was carried out by quantifying the dissolved O2 production. 100 µL of PEBR was introduced into the MSU-containing buffer (pH = 7.4, 25 °C), and the dissolved O2 levels in the solutions were probed at defined time points.

Cell viability assay

RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 104 cells per well in 100 µL DMEM medium and incubated overnight. Subsequently, the medium was then replaced with MSU crystals and PEBR. After 24/48 h, cell viability was evaluated by CCK-8 assay kit following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Biosafety assay

Mouse blood (1 mL) was collected from the retro-orbital plexus using heparinized capillaries and immediately centrifuged (3,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). The resulting red blood cell (RBC) pellet was washed three times with ice-cold PBS, then resuspended in PBS to obtain a 10% (v/v) RBC stock. Aliquots (100 µL) of this suspension were mixed with C-U, DPE, or PEBR. Among them, water was used as the control group. After incubation at 37 °C for 0.5 h, the supernatant was collected for analysis.

Cellular uptake

Intracellular uptake was visualized by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, SP8, Leica). RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in laser confocal dish and cultured for 24 h. The medium was replaced with fresh DMEM, and FITC-CAT and Cy5-URI formulated as C-U, DPE, PEBR, or PECU (without mannose) were introduced into different laser confocal dish. After 6 h of incubation, the cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Fluorescence signals (FITC, Cy5 and DAPI) were imaged immediately using CLSM.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Intracellular ROS levels were quantified with 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). Initially, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. Following stimulation with MSU crystals (0.5 mg/mL), the medium was supplemented with PBS, C-U, DPE and PEBR (20 µg/mL). After 6 h, cells were stained with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C. The intracellular ROS levels were qualitatively analyzed by an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus U-RFL-T) at 450 nm.

Mitochondrial damage assay

RAW 264.7 was inoculated in 6-well plates and incubated overnight. Except the blank group, all wells received MSU crystals (0.5 mg/mL) for 6 h. Subsequently, C-U, DPE or PEBR (20 µg/mL) were added to the culture medium and incubated for 24 h. Mitochondrial membrane potential was then assessed by staining with pre-warmed JC-1 (10 µM) and DAPI (5 µg/mL) for 30 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2. After two PBS washes to remove unbound dye, red (JC-1 aggregates) and green (JC-1 monomers) fluorescence were immediately visualized on a fluorescence microscope, and the red/green fluorescence ratio was quantified to evaluate mitochondrial damage.

Western blot

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated overnight. Following stimulation with MSU crystals (0.5 mg/mL), the medium was supplemented with C-U, DPE or PEBR and incubated for 24 h. Cells were harvested and washed thrice with ice-cold PBS, lysed in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors on ice for 30 min with intermittent agitation, and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C). Supernatant protein was quantified by BCA. Equal aliquots (30 µg) were resolved on 8% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF, blocked with 3% BSA in TBST (2 h, RT), and probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NLRP3, Caspase-1 and NF-κB. Bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence (FluorChem R) and analyzed using ImageJ.

Macrophage reprogramming

Raw 264.7 cells were seeded in 24 well plates and incubated in 1mL DMEM medium (10% FBS and 1% antibiotics) for 24 h. After stimulation with MSU crystals (0.5 mg/mL) for 12 h, the fresh medium containing PBS, C-U, DPE or PEBR was replaced and incubated for a further 12 h. The cells were collected, and washed with ice-cold PBS and blocked with 1% BSA-PBS (30 min, 4 °C, dark). They were then stained with anti-F4/80-APC, anti-CD86-FITC and anti-CD206-PE antibodies (30 min, 4 °C, dark), washed twice and resuspended in 300 µL PBS for immediate flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX).

In vivo biodistribution study

To investigate the in vivo biodistribution of PEBR, female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks) received a single intra-articular injection of formulations containing Cy5-URI conjugate (excitation/emission: 648/662 nm) and Cy7-CAT conjugate (excitation/emission: 750/770 nm). Fluorescence signals were dynamically monitored using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) at predetermined time points (2, 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection). Regions of interest (ROIs) encompassing the injection site and major organs (liver, kidneys, spleen, heart, lungs) were quantified with Carestream Molecular Imaging Software.

Animal model of GA

The mouse model of GA was established by intra-articular injection of MSU crystals. Model mice received a 0.5 mg/dose of MSU suspension in PBS directly into the knee joint, while control mice were administered an equal volume of PBS alone. The entire procedure was performed under 2.5% isoflurane anesthesia to ensure animal welfare. Ankle swelling and stride gait response that occurred 2 h after MSU injection were regarded as the criteria for successful model establishment.

Histological evaluation

The ankle joints of GA model mice were harvested and sectioned for histological examination following treatment with various formulations. Specifically, ankle joints were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then decalcified with EDTA for 3 days. The specimens were subsequently embedded in paraffin for histological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were used for analysis and morphological evaluation.

Swelling evaluation

After the successful establishment of the GA model, the mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 6): (1) PBS; (2) C-U; (3) DPE; (4) PEBR. Measure the paw thickness in each group using a vernier caliper at 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h after injection, and calculate the swelling elimination rate.

Diseases targets prediction, gene enrichment analysis, and protein network analysis

Disease-associated gene targets for GA were compiled from GeneCards, OMIM and TTD. Methotrexate (MTX) targets were retrieved from SwissTargetPrediction (http://swisstargetprediction.ch), Super-PRED, and NCBI. Overlapping targets between GA and MTX were identified via SwissTargetPrediction using the provided gene list. Pathway and process enrichment analyses were subsequently performed on the shared targets, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05 after multiple-testing correction.

Molecular Docking analysis

Molecular docking was employed to quantify the binding affinities between MTX and its putative targets. The top five hub proteins identified by the MCC algorithm in the PPI network were selected as receptors. Crystal structures of these proteins were retrieved from the RCSB PDB, while the three-dimensional SDF structure of MTX was downloaded from PubChem. All docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina after standard receptor preparation and ligand energy minimisation.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

The total RNA (> 1 µg, RIN > 7, OD260/280 > 1.8) of synovial tissue in the joint of MSU model mice was extracted with RNeasy kit, verified on Bioanalyzer. mRNA was captured with poly-T beads, fragmented, and converted to cDNA using random hexamers, M-MuLV and DNA Pol I. After end-repair, adaptor ligation and AMPure XP size-selection (250–300 bp), libraries were PCR-enriched with Phusion polymerase, quantified on Qseq 100, clustered on cBot and sequenced 150-bp paired-end on NovaSeq 6000.

Results and discussions

Preparation and characterization of PEBR

In terms of the preparation of self-cascade bioreactor, MTX wrapped in squalene was employed as the oil (O) phase, while URI, CAT, and DSEP-PEG-Mannose, serving as stabilizers were arranged at the O/W interface by single-step ultrasonic emulsification (Fig. 2a). To screen the efficient catalytic activity and high stability of PEBR, a series of optimizations were conducted [23]. Catalytic mapping revealed that a URI: CAT molar ratio ≤ 4:1 eliminated H2O2 completely, meanwhile the 4:1 ratio delivered the fastest MSU crystals clearance (Figure S1). Simultaneously, hydrodynamic size and zeta potential profiling demonstrated that the 4:1 ratio yielded the smallest and most negatively charged droplets, which are hallmarks of colloidal stability. Buffer type and aqueous pH were subsequently tuned to lock in these parameters (Figure S2) [24].

The stepwise hierarchical assembly process was subsequently monitored [25]. The emulsion stabilized by URI/CAT (PECU) exhibited an average hydrodynamic diameter of 309.7 nm, which increased to 453.3 nm following surface modification with mannose (PECU-D) and further expanded to 813.8 nm upon the incorporation of MTX, resulting in PEBR (Fig. 2b). Concurrently, the zeta potential underwent a significant shift towards more negative values (Fig. 2c), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) revealed distinct endothermic signatures [26], confirming the successful integration of both MTX and mannose into the bioreactor architecture (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, we recorded FTIR spectra at each assembly step. After the co-adsorption of URI/CAT, new peaks corresponding to amide I (1655 cm⁻¹) and amide II (1540 cm⁻¹) emerged, confirming protein loading. When MTX was encapsulated in the oil core, the C = N stretching vibration of the pteridine ring (1605 cm⁻¹) and the γ(OH) stretching vibration of glutamic acid (1380 cm⁻¹) appeared. Finally, during mannose assembly, distinct phosphate and sugar C–O vibration peaks were observed in the range of 1050–1080 cm⁻¹. The gradual accumulation of these characteristic bands indicates that URI, CAT, MTX, and mannose were sequentially introduced onto the PEBR, thereby reinforcing the evidence for their simultaneous immobilization (Figure S3a). To further verify the location of URI and CAT on PEBR, we labeled URI with FITC and CAT with Cy5, respectively. CLSM showed a perfect overlap between the green (URI) and red (CAT) fluorescence signals, demonstrating the co-assembly of URI and CAT into emulsion droplets at the interface (Figure S3b). The loading efficiency of URI and CAT was calculated as the ratio of the mass of immobilized enzyme to the initial mass of enzyme used in the immobilization process, with both reaching approximately 100%. Specifically, the total protein concentration of the PEBR is determined to be 2.39 mg/mL. Regarding the absolute enzyme loading, consistent with the URI: CAT mass ratio of 4:1, the absolute loading of URI enzyme is 0.92 mg and that of CAT enzyme is 0.23 mg. In addition, the interfacial enzyme density of the PEBR has been calculated and supplemented as 4.55 × 10− 6 μm², with the interfacial density of URI enzyme being 5.17 × 10− 6 μm² and that of CAT enzyme being 3.80 × 10− 5 μm². Additionally, CLSM and stimulated emission depletion (STED) imaging confirmed the homogeneous distribution of mannose at the interface (Fig. 2e). Then scanning electron microscope (SEM) revealed that the PECU, PECU-D and PEBR exhibited all globular-like morphology (Figure S3c-e). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) exhibited well-dispersed spherical droplets and distinct boundaries. The clear contrast between the droplets and the background suggests effective adsorption of URI and CAT particles at the oil-water interface. These structural characteristics demonstrate the effective emulsifying and stabilizing capability of URI and CAT in constructing a stable PEBR (Fig. 2f). Meanwhile, MTX was localized within the lipophilic core, with the emulsion prepared at concentrations of 0.1 mg/mL and 0.5 mg/mL achieving an encapsulation efficiency exceeding 80%, indicative of a high loading efficiency for the fat-soluble immunomodulator (Fig. 2g). Collectively, these findings indicate that URI, CAT, and mannose are uniformly assembled at the interface, while MTX is encapsulated within the oil core, resulting in a stable self-cascade bioreactor (Fig. 2h).

The stability of the PEBR was systematically evaluated based on the low interfacial tension properties of URI, CAT, and lipid-polymer conjugates [27]. After day 20 of storage at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), the emulsion demonstrated robust structural integrity, with no signs of demulsification or phase separation under optical microscopy. Moreover, the emulsion droplets maintained a spherical morphology with a moderate level of polydispersity index (PDI = 0.28 ± 0.03), indicating effective stabilization through interfacial adsorption mechanisms (Fig. 2i). These findings collectively validate the enhanced colloidal stability of the formulated PEBR after ambient storage conditions. Additionally, activity retention was defined as the percentage of immobilized enzyme activity relative to the initial activity of the corresponding free enzyme (prior to immobilization), which was determined to be 98% for both URI and CAT. These findings confirm that the immobilization process achieved compatibility of the construct with maintaining enzymatic functionality. CAT, URI, mannose and MXT exhibited a synchronized release, which stabilized after 72 h at approximately 40% (Figure S4a-d). This collective release was pH-responsive, occurring more rapidly at pH = 6.8 than at pH = 7.4 as a result of the acid-triggered disintegration of the emulsion droplets. The operational half-lives of CAT and URI under simulated inflammatory conditions (pH = 6.8, 37 °C) were quantified by monitoring their activity decay over 72 h using established spectrophotometric assays. The half-life was determined to be 14 h for CAT and 16 h for URI, calculated from the time-dependent activity loss. These quantitative stability data, confirm that both enzymes remain functional over a timescale relevant to the application.

Biocatalytic cascade of PEBR

The strong affinity for the substrate is a remarkable trait for enhanced cascade catalytic reaction. To elucidate the interaction mechanisms between PEBR and MSU crystallization, quartz crystal microbalance with quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring (QCM-D) was employed for in-situ characterization. URI and CAT assembled at the oil/water interface, where their high specific surface area and hydrophobicity led to a pronounced increase in dissipation energy, indicating a quantitatively enhanced physicochemical affinity. Compared to free enzymes, the assembled system achieved a 75.53% decrease in ∆F and significantly boosted affinity for MSU crystals (Fig. 3a). After MSU crystals attach to the bioreactor surface, the URI (upstream enzyme) and CAT (downstream enzyme) need to be strategically positioned to reduce the diffusion loss of intermediate products. By covalently conjugating Cy3 to CAT and Cy5 to URI, we created a Förster distance-sensitive biosensor system. Spectral overlap analysis of the Cy3 excitation (567 nm) and Cy5 emission (667 nm) confirmed energy transfer efficiency exceeding 85% under PEBR conditions, demonstrating the nanoscale proximity (< 10 nm) required for efficient product channeling (Fig. 3b). Thereby, the spatial proximity effects of PEBR not only reduced diffusion limitations but also created a microenvironment conducive to accelerated catalytic turnover for high-efficiency cascade catalysis.

The abilities to catalyze the degradation of UA and decompose H2O2 were then evaluated. We measured the specific activity of both immobilized and free URI and CAT under identical assay conditions. The results showed that the specific activity of immobilized URI (3.53 U/mg) and CAT (0.58 U/mg) was significantly higher than that of their free counterparts (free URI: 2.35 U/mg; free CAT: 0.123 U/mg) (Figure S4e-f). To further understand the catalytic behavior, Lineweaver-Burk curves were constructed for different groups [22]. According to the results, the Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) of PEBR was lower compared to free URI and DPE (Fig. 3d), suggesting that PEBR has higher enzyme activity and better enzymatic kinetic properties due to the high affinity and efficient transport of intermediate products. Upon contact with PEBR, UA was almost completely degraded within 30 min. In contrast, free URI could only degrade 43.5% of UA within the same time frame, demonstrating the remarkable degradation effect of PEBR (Fig. 3e). It is also worth noting that H2O2, as a metabolite, can penetrate the cell membrane and exhibit significant cytotoxicity. A portable dissolved oxygen detector is used to monitor changes in dissolved O2 in the solution, indirectly measuring the activity of CAT. As shown in Fig. 3f, the dissolved O2 concentration of free URI was 20 mg/L at 3 min, while PEBR was 3.5 times that of free URI and maintained a decomposition rate throughout 30 min. This indicates that PEBR not only promotes the catalytic elimination of harmful H2O2, but also accelerates the degradation of UA through the generated O2.

Additionally, ensuring catalytic activity in harsh environments is key to the effective functioning of PEBR in vivo [28]. Consequently, we conducted a more investigation into the impacts of environmental factors, specifically pH and temperature, on enzyme activity. As depicted in Fig. 3g, within the measured temperature spectrum, the residual activity of PEBR was notably higher than that of free URI. At a temperature of 70 °C, it retains over 50% of its activity. Moreover, it is well-established that the pH within the joint cavity of GA typically exists in a mildly acidic milieu. Through in vitro simulations across a broad pH range, we observed that the residual activity of PEBR was also superior to that of free URI (Fig. 3h). These results comprehensively indicate that, in comparison to free URI, PEBR based on the Pickering emulsion structure, exhibited a significantly enhanced tolerance to variations in pH and high temperatures. This enhanced resistance further validates the potential of PEBR as a more stable and effective alternative for the application of the enzyme in vivo.

PEBR promoted cellular uptake and mitochondrial homeostasis

The safety profile of PEBR was assessed by a cytotoxicity assay. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, cell viability remained above 90% following 24 h and 48 h treatments with various concentrations of PEBR in the absence of MSU crystals, underscoring its excellent biocompatibility. Conversely, the data in Fig. 4b demonstrate a distinct effect of PEBR on MSU-induced cell viability reduction. Cells treated with 50 mg/mL MSU crystals alone displayed a significant decrease in viability, dropping to roughly 50%. In contrast, co-incubation of cells with 50 mg/mL MSU crystals and gradient concentrations of PEBR resulted in a concentration-dependent recovery of cell viability. Notably, even at a low PEBR concentration of 8 µg/mL, the cell viability was markedly elevated to 95%. We attribute this rescue to the synergistic clearance of MSU crystals and the concomitant dismutation of cytotoxic H2O2 by the URI-CAT cascade embedded within PEBR. To further evaluate the biocompatibility of PEBR, we assessed its effect on the viability of human skin fibroblasts (HSF) as a representative normal cell line. As shown in Figure S5a, after 24/48 h of incubation with PEBR at concentrations ranging from 0 to 80 µg/mL, HSF cell viability remained above 95% in all treatment groups. These results collectively indicate that PEBR exhibits minimal cytotoxicity and favorable biosafety toward normal cells. Hemocompatibility was further conducted, confirming that C-U, DPE, and PEBR elicited negligible erythrocyte lysis, thereby corroborating their in vitro safety profile (Figure S5b). To evaluate the oxidative stress after treatment with PEBR, DCFH-DA was employed to monitor intracellular ROS levels. As shown in Fig. 4f and g, MSU crystals triggered robust intracellular green fluorescence signals via DCFH-DA staining, which is indicative of ROS generation. After incubation with C-U, DPE, and PEBR, the average fluorescence intensities were reduced to 58%, 49%, and 25%, respectively. To assess the integrity of the mitochondrial membrane under conditions of oxidative stress and subsequent treatment, assays utilizing the JC-1 probe were performed. Under physiological conditions, functional mitochondria are characterized by red fluorescence due to JC-1 aggregates, whereas mitochondrial depolarization results in a shift to green fluorescence, indicative of JC-1 monomers. Fluorescence signals revealed significant mitochondrial damage in MSU-challenged controls, as evidenced by predominant green fluorescence. Conversely, cells treated with PEBR displayed intense red fluorescence, signifying well-preserved mitochondrial membrane potential and minimal structural damage (Fig. 4h). These observations collectively suggest that PEBR confers protective effects against oxidative mitochondrial dysfunction induced by ROS.

Anti-inflammatory effect of acute GA

To probe the therapeutic potential of PEBR in vivo, a GA model was established by intra-articular injection of 20 µL (25 mg/mL) of MSU suspension into the knee joint [31, 32], while the contralateral knee joint received an equal volume of PBS as a blank control. The histopathological evaluation confirmed the successful establishment of the model, as indicated by significant swelling in the ipsilateral hind paw within 2 h following injection. Subsequent fluorescence biodistribution analysis, conducted using the IVIS imaging system, aimed to compare the retention efficiency specific to the lesion site [33]. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, the PEBR formulation demonstrated significantly higher fluorescence signal intensity in the inflamed left knee joint at 2 h post-injection compared to that in C-U group (P < 0.001). Notably, by 12 h post-administration, the fluorescence associated with PEBR remained localized at the lesion site, with no evidence of systemic diffusion (Fig. 5b and c). Additionally, we analyzed the fluorescence data by applying a first-order kinetic model to the fluorescence intensity decay of URI and CAT within the joint cavity. Linear regression of the natural logarithm of normalized intensity versus time yielded a degradation rate constant (k) of −0.0123 h− 1 for URI, corresponding to a half-life (t1/2 = ln(2)/k) of 86.3 h (R2 = 0.963). Similarly, the decay profile of CAT also conformed well to first-order kinetics (R2 = 0.986), with an estimated half-life of approximately 89.7 h. Notably, both URI and CAT exhibited significantly longer half-lives than those of C-U (URI: 52.1 h; CAT: 57.4 h). This indicated that the PEBR effectively protected the enzymes from rapid degradation and clearance in vivo, which was consistent with our in vitro stability observations against proteolytic digestion. This finding highlights the importance of the strong affinity with cells and mannose modification in facilitating on-site retention efficiency and macrophage-specific receptor recognition. Terminal euthanasia at 48 h post-injection allowed for ex vivo organ biodistribution analysis, which revealed no significant fluorescence signals in major organs, including the liver, spleen, lungs, heart, and kidneys (Figure S7a). This was corroborated by histopathological examination, indicating minimized systemic toxicity (Figure S7b). At the same time, the biochemical indicators were evaluated in mice, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), which further confirmed that PEBR had good biological safety and did not cause additional damage or potential risks (Figure S7c-g). After the completion of the treatment course, we continued to monitor the experimental animals for an additional 2 weeks. During this follow-up period, we collected blood samples from the animals and performed a comprehensive detection of key biochemical indicators related to organ function and toxicity, including ALT, AST, ALP, LDH, and BUN. The results showed that there were no significant differences in the levels of these biochemical indicators between the PEBR treatment group, and the normal group at 2 weeks post-treatment(Figure S7h-l). This indicated that PEBR did not cause obvious long-term damage to the liver, kidneys, or other important organs of the animals, confirming its favorable long-term biosafety profile. Additionally, body weight changes in mice before and after treatment were provided in Figure S7m. No significant body weight loss in the organs was observed, collectively indicating the negligible systemic toxicity of the material. Collectively, the combination of enhanced enzyme retention and localized delivery may alleviate acute inflammation and modulate immune cell activity.

To further evaluate the treatment effects, 12 h following the modeling procedure, intra-articular injections of C-U, DPE, and PEBR were successfully performed (Fig. 5d). Then, the forepaws and hindpaws of the mice were marked with red and black ink, respectively. The mice were allowed to walk freely across a track, and their footprints were recorded [34]. Untreated mice displayed the shortest stride length and step width, the longest fore/hind prints, and the lowest paw withdrawal threshold (PWT), collectively indicating the most severe pain state (Fig. 5e). Following treatment with C-U, DPE, and PEBR, the step width gradually improved, and the degree of fingerprint separation decreased. Furthermore, the joints of mice treated with PEBR returned to a normal state within 24 h [35], whereas the C-U group and DPE group displayed persistent swelling (Fig. 5f and g). Notably, 72 h following PEBR treatment, the alignment of the fore and hind paw prints closely resembled that of the control group, indicating no significant differences. This observation suggests that the motor function of the treated mice began to return to normal. Moreover, upon successful establishment of the model, local inflammation resulted in an increase in joint temperature to 34.6 °C (Fig. 5h). At the 72 h post-treatment mark, joint temperatures in mice treated with C-U, DPE, PEBR, and PBS demonstrated varying degrees of reduction. Notably, the temperatures in the DPE and PEBR groups approached those of normal mice, signifying substantial therapeutic efficacy.

The observed ankle swelling, gait disturbances, and fever were attributed to inflammatory responses induced by MSU crystal, and may be accompanied by significant inflammatory cell infiltration in synovial tissues [36]. Thus, to comprehensively assess the anti-inflammatory efficacy of PEBR, histopathological analyses were performed on murine knee joints. Synovial tissues extracted from the joint cavities were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The results revealed extensive immune cell infiltration in the synovial lining of GA mice treated with PBS, whereas the PEBR group exhibited only marginal inflammatory cell infiltration at the tissue margins (Fig. 5i). Furthermore, macroscopic examination of knee joints in the PBS group demonstrated severe cartilage degradation characterized by pronounced fibrillation and matrix loss. This pathological phenotype was hypothesized to result from hyperplastic synovial tissue invading subchondral bone structures, leading to osteoarticular destruction. Notably, PEBR intervention markedly reduced osteophyte formation, significantly inhibited cartilage degeneration and wear, and demonstrated pronounced chondroprotective effects (Fig. 5j). These findings collectively suggest that PEBR exerts dual therapeutic benefits through both anti-inflammatory activity and promotion of articular tissue repair in GA pathogenesis.

Therapeutic mechanism at molecular and protein levels

To comprehensively explore the pathways through which PEBR orchestrates therapeutic remission in GA, a high-throughput RNA-Sequencing (RNA-Seq) was performed on ankle synovial tissues [37, 38]. Compared to GA model mice (CK group), PEBR-treated mice exhibited significant transcriptional reprogramming, with 334 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified using stringent criteria (|log2FC| ≥ 1.0, adjusted q-value ≤ 0.001) (Fig. 6a). Functional annotation clustering by Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed pronounced enrichment across all three ontologies. Biological process (BP) terms were dominated by cell migration and chemotaxis (Figure S8a). Cellular component (CC) terms were enriched for specific granules and cell receptor complexes (Figure S8b). Molecular function (MF) terms were strongly enriched for cytokine activity, chemokine activity, receptor-ligand activity, and receptor binding (Fig. 6b). Consistently, pathway-centric interrogation via Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis further demonstrated that PEBR selectively suppressed key inflammatory signaling cascades, including the TNF signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and NF-κB signaling pathway, while simultaneously downregulating metabolic disease-associated pathways (Fig. 6c). To validate these transcriptomic findings, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on bulk RNA-seq data, which consistently demonstrated that PEBR treatment significantly suppressed the MSU-induced rheumatoid arthritis and NF-κB gene signature compared to CK (Fig. 6d and Figure S8c). To validate the RNA-seq findings, we performed qPCR analysis of key differentially expressed genes (DEGs) implicated in the NF-κB and NLRP3 pathways, including the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, along with the anti-inflammatory marker IL-10. Consistent with the transcriptomic data, PEBR treatment significantly downregulated TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β expression while markedly upregulating IL-10 (Figure S9). These results corroborate the robustness of our RNA-seq analysis and confirm the anti-inflammatory mechanism of PEBR at the transcriptional level. Furthermore, Western blot analysis confirmed that PEBR-treated group exhibited marked reductions in key inflammatory mediators, including NLRP3 inflammasome components, cleaved Caspase-1 and NF-κB. These findings collectively indicate that PEBR exerts its therapeutic effects in GA through coordinated suppression of NF-κB-driven inflammatory pathways and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby reinstating immune homeostasis (Fig. 6e and f). To rigorously validate the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory potency of PEBR, we performed Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) analyses on synovial lavage fluids. In accordance with its transcriptomic profile, PEBR markedly reduced protein levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α to 43.69%, 32.04%, and 36.95% of those observed in the MSU group, while simultaneously elevating the anti-inflammatory mediator IL-10 five-fold (all P < 0.01; Figure S10).

We propose that PEBR orchestrates a significant reprogramming of inflammatory pathways through two synergistic mechanisms: (i) the rapid enzymatic dissolution of MSU crystals, thereby eliminating the primary danger signal, and (ii) the targeted release of methotrexate (MTX) within the inflamed synovium [39, 40]. To elucidate the molecular framework supporting this dual action [41], we constructed a high-confidence protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of overlapping MTX–GA targets using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org). As illustrated in Fig. 7a and Figure S11, MCODE clustering (Metascape) identified densely interconnected modules predominantly enriched for cytokine signaling, leukocyte chemotaxis, and NF-κB-mediated inflammatory pathways. These findings suggest that MTX acts as a precision modulator, recalibrating immune homeostasis by intercepting the pathways that drive GA inflammation.

Next, we used molecular docking techniques to quantitatively evaluate the binding strength between MTX and its potential targets. Based on the ranking of the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm in the PPI network, we selected the top 4 related targets for molecular docking with MTX. In this docking process, MTX was designated as the ligand, while the key targets served as receptors. The docking was carried out using AutoDock software, and the docking scores were meticulously recorded. A fundamental principle in molecular docking is that the lower the binding energy, the stronger and more stable the interaction between the drug and the target protein. The binding energies of MTX to the target proteins, Matrix Metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), TNF, IL-6, and IL-10, were determined to be −8.4 kcal/mol, −7.9 kcal/mol, −6.9 kcal/mol, and − 6.5 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 7b). These results indicate that MTX can spontaneously bind to immune-related targeting proteins, suggesting that it may have stable and specific effects on these proteins.

PEBR enhanced inflammatory microenvironment remodeling

To corroborate that PEBR has enhanced inflammatory microenvironment remodeling capacity, the expression of key inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-10) were assessed in macrophage pretreated with MSU and subsequently incubated with PBS, C-U, DPE, or PEBR. The results showed that PEBR significantly reduced the secretion of IL-1β (P < 0.001), IL-6 (P < 0.01), and TNF-α (P < 0.05), while increasing the level of IL-10 (P < 0.01) compared to other groups (Fig. 7c-f). These findings suggested that PEBR promoted the Reprograming of the inflammatory microenvironment toward a resolution phenotype. Considering that oxidative stress and the cytokine environment determine macrophage differentiation, we proceeded to analyze PEBR-induced M1/M2 reprogramming using flow cytometry [36]. Within 24 h after exposure, PEBR downregulated the expression of CD86+ in pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, with the expression level being approximately 18.83% that of the C-U group and 40.80% that of the DPE group. Meanwhile, PEBR upregulated the expression of CD206+ in anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, and the expression level was 3.2-fold higher than that in the C-U group and 1.8-fold higher than that in the DPE group, respectively (Fig. 7g and h). These marked reciprocal changes indicated that PEBR mediates a pivotal immunometabolic reprogramming, reshaping the pro-inflammatory M1-dominated microenvironment into a restorative M2-polarized niche, which in turn promoted inflammation resolution and accelerates joint repair. Collectively, these findings establish PEBR as a dual-function platform that mitigates inflammatory signaling and orchestrates macrophage polarization, thereby promoting joint homeostasis and repair.

Conclusions

In summary, the cascade catalytic bioreactor based on Pickering emulsions significantly eliminated MSU deposits and ROS, programmed the inflammatory microenvironment, and alleviated tissue damage, thereby providing a potent, clinically translatable therapy for GA. By precisely positioning URI and CAT at the O/W interface, the architecture optimizes substrate channeling, preserves individual enzyme kinetics, and drives an efficient self-cascade reaction. DSPE-PEG-mannose forms a dense glycan corona that amplifies macrophage contact and boosts endocytosis by approximately 3-fold compared with non-targeted controls. Encapsulating low-soluble methotrexate within the squalene core markedly increases its apparent solubility and bioavailability, while co-delivery with uricase, an FDA-endorsed strategy, mitigates anti-drug antibody formation and minimizes off-target toxicity. Indeed, PEBR elicited markedly lower enzyme-specific antibody titres, corroborating its superior safety profile. Collectively, these attributes establish PEBR as a precision nanomedicine that integrates catalytic detoxification, targeted immunomodulation, and enhanced drug delivery into a single, synergistic platform for GA therapy (Fig. 1).

In this study, we introduce a novel one-step ultrasonic emulsification approach that integrates uricase, catalase, methotrexate, and mannose-decorated lipids into a unified, autonomous bioreactor without the need for intricate chemical processes or multi-step methodologies. PEBR resided in inflamed synovium, effectively dissolved MSU crystals both intra- and extracellularly, neutralized reactive oxygen species, and facilitated a critical polarization of macrophages from the M1 to M2 phenotype, thereby reprogramming the joint microenvironment from a pro-inflammatory to a pro-resolving state. In vivo, this coordinated cascade alleviated joint swelling, restored normal gait, and accelerated tissue repair.

To address potential immunogenicity concerns, serum samples were collected from experimental animals following treatment. Enzyme specific IgG antibodies against URI and CAT were quantified by indirect ELISA. The results revealed that antibody levels in all treatment groups remained consistently low (Figure S12), confirming the minimal immunogenicity of the therapeutic system in vivo.

More broadly, PEBR serves as a model for next-generation bioreactors that leverage enzyme cascade catalysis and multi-component synergy to address inflammatory diseases. This strategy of leveraging multi-component synergy and cascade reactions aligns with the cutting-edge design principles of next-generation nanotherapeutics, particularly in oncology, where such integrated systems have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in reprogramming the tumor microenvironment and overcoming biological barriers [42–45].
