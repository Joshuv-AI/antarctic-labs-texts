End-of-life healthcare utilisation and costs of older patients on kidney replacement therapy – analysis of Dutch health insurance claims data
Authors: Micha Jongejan, Manon J. M. van Oosten, Martijn J. H. Leegte, Mattijs E. Numans, G. Ardine de Wit, Marjolijn van Buren, Alferso C. Abrahams, Willem Jan W. Bos, Carlijn G. N. Voorend
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients on kidney replacement therapy (KRT) face high symptom and treatment burden, especially at end of life. Yet insights into end-of-life healthcare utilisation and costs remain limited. We aimed to describe healthcare utilisation and costs during the last year of life across KRT modalities.MethodsWe used Dutch health insurance claims data to identify incident and prevalent patients aged ≥ 65 years, treated with KRT during the year preceding death, and who deceased between June 2016 and December 2021. Healthcare utilisation and costs in the last year of life were analysed for different KRT modalities and compared with controls without kidney disease-related claims, with controls being matched on sex, age, socio-economic status, and year of death.ResultsWe identified 7279 patients on KRT (4614 haemodialysis [HD] patients, 766 peritoneal dialysis [PD] patients and 1899 kidney transplant [KTx] recipients) and 14,558 controls. During their last year of life, 85% of patients on KRT experienced ≥ 1 hospital admission, with 27% requiring intensive care - representing to 2.6- and 3.5-fold higher proportions than in their controls, with minimal variation across KRT modalities. Similar differences were observed for emergency department visits, outpatient visits, and long-term care use. Healthcare utilisation significantly increased in the last three months preceding death, more markedly among patients on KRT than controls (p < 0.001). Average healthcare costs were 5.9, 5.5 and 3.0 times higher for patients on HD (€117,520), PD (€108.294), and KTx recipients (€59,489), respectively, compared to controls (€19,820). KRT-unrelated costs, encompassing 40% to 74% of total costs, were 2.2 to 2.6 times higher in patients on KRT compared to controls, with specialist hospital care costs being three times higher.ConclusionIn the last year of life, healthcare utilisation is higher for patients on KRT compared to controls, with a further increase towards end-of-life, along with higher KRT-unrelated costs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04605-2.

Introduction

Patients on kidney replacement therapy (KRT) experience high symptom and treatment burden, which translates into substantial healthcare utilisation [1]. The majority of this healthcare use is related to dialysis or kidney transplantation [2, 3], but costs vary between patients. Among haemodialysis patients, frail patients incur 22% higher healthcare costs than non-frail patients, primarily attributable to increased inpatient care [4]. This difference in healthcare utilisation between frail and non-frail patients further diverged among those approaching the end of life [4]. The last phase of life is accompanied by a decline in health-related quality of life and increased healthcare utilisation [5, 6]. Towards the end of life, treatment goals ideally shift from prolonging life to maximising comfort, particularly when the burden of interventions outweigh the anticipated benefits. For example, in patients with cancer, in-hospital death, intensive care unit admission and chemotherapy in the last week of life were associated with lower quality of life [7]. Additionally, patients with kidney failure are less frequently engaged in advance care planning (ACP) than patients with cancer [8]. Study outcomes reflect intensive end-of-life care among CKD patients, with many patients experiencing unplanned hospital admissions, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, surgeries and mechanical ventilation [6, 9, 10]. However, for patients receiving KRT a detailed description of healthcare utilisation and the associated costs during the last year of life is lacking. Gaining insight in healthcare use is an important initial step in understanding treatment patterns, may lay a foundation for discussion on end-of-life care, and aid policymakers in anticipating future healthcare demands. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to describe healthcare utilisation and costs during the last year of life of patients aged ≥ 65 year on KRT, stratified by KRT modality, and to compare them with patients without kidney disease. Our secondary aim is to examine changes in healthcare utilisation as patients approach the end of life and to assess whether these changes differ between patients on KRT and their controls. We hypothesized that the high healthcare utilisation further increases in the final months of life, with a more pronounced increase among patients on KRT.

Methods

Data source

A nationwide cohort was defined within the Vektis database, comprising health insurance claims covered by the Dutch Health Insurance Act and Long-Term Care Act, linked with demographic data [11]. The Dutch Health Insurance Act provides mandatory health insurance coverage for medical care (e.g., primary care, hospital care, medication and mental health care). The Long-Term Care Act provides comprehensive long-term care services at home or institutionalised to individuals in need of ongoing assistance with activities of daily living due to chronic illness, disability, or aging. These two acts regulate and cover the vast majority of healthcare services in the Netherlands, where 99% of inhabitants are insured, with the Vektis database encompassing 99% of these insured individuals [12]. Privacy was ensured by pseudonymisation of patients’ national identification number.

Study population

We identified individuals aged ≥ 65 years, who deceased between June 1st, 2016, and December 31st, 2021, with a health insurance claim for dialysis or kidney transplantation during their last year of life. Individuals who initiated HD, PD or KTx more than 365 days before death were categorised as prevalent patients, those who initiated HD, PD or KTx within the year preceding death as incident patients. This classification resulted in six groups. Incident KTx recipients included both pre-emptive transplanted patients and those who underwent dialysis before transplantation (see Supplemental Materials for detailed definition of groups). Further details regarding the proportions of prevalent and incident patients within this cohort, as well as treatment modality switches during the last year of life, were published previously [13]. To prevent the inclusion of individuals with acute kidney injury requiring transient dialysis needs, we excluded incident patients with a dialysis duration of less than 28 days. For prevalent patients, we excluded individuals who were treated with dialysis at 12 months before death but had less than 6 months dialysis during their last year of life. The latter exclusion criterion was applied to account for cases involving transient kidney failure. Analyses were restricted to patients aged ≥ 65 years, who comprise a large fraction of the KRT population and an even larger fraction of decedents, and in whom the balance between life prolongation and quality of life may differ from that in younger patients. After classification, we validated the number of dialysis and KTx patients for each calendar year using an external database, the Dutch Renal Registry [14]. The agreement between the two databases ranged from 90.3% to 100% annually (see Supplemental Materials).

Healthcare utilisation and costs variables

To assess healthcare utilisation, we derived the number of hospital admissions, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, emergency department (ED) visits, outpatient visits and long-term care outside the hospital. Dialysis treatments were not included in the count of outpatient visits. Variables of healthcare utilisation are based on hospital activity codes and Long-Term Care Act performance codes (listed in Supplemental Materials).

All healthcare costs in the year preceding death were derived and classified for its relation to kidney disease care. KRT-related costs encompassed all expenses related to the dialysis procedure (e.g., access surgery and revisions, dialysis equipment, medication used during dialysis, diagnostics related to dialysis treatment and staff expenses) or kidney transplantation (e.g., transplant surgery, pre- and post-transplant care and donor expenses). KRT-unrelated costs were defined as all remaining costs not directly related to KRT. If we could not definitively attribute expenses to KRT exclusively, we categorised them as KRT-unrelated costs. KRT-unrelated costs were differentiated into the following segments: costs related to primary care, specialist care delivered in the hospital (inpatient and outpatient), mental health care, medication, transport, others (i.e., medical devices, healthcare abroad) and care provided within the Long-term Care Act. In the Netherlands, hospital claims are derived from negotiated administrative prices for high-level groups of diagnoses within a specific time period and cover care provided for a maximum period of 120 days [15]. As such, health insurance claims data provide insights into expenditure, which may be different from actual costs. In this paper, we will use the term costs when we actually refer to expenditure. All costs of a claim are denoted to the first day of that period, irrespective of the actual distribution of care use within the 120-day period. Within the Vektis database, long-term care costs are declared on a date basis. All other costs (i.e., primary care, mental health care, medication, transport and others) are available per calendar year. To account for varying time intervals, we calculated the duration between the index date and the end of that calendar year (i.e., the calendar year before death). We then obtained a proportionate representation of these costs by multiplying the prior year’s costs by the calculated time and adding them to the costs of the calendar year of death. Cost data are reported in euros and adjusted for inflation according to the Dutch Consumer Price index, with 2021 as the base year [16].

Statistical analysis

Patient and treatment characteristics (sex, age at death, KRT duration, KRT switch, and comorbidities) were presented per treatment modality as means with standard deviation for continuous variables and as percentages for categorical variables. We assessed comorbidities using claims of hospital-, pharmaceutical- and primary care, as described elsewhere by Van Oosten et al. [17] and in Supplement 2. Each KRT patient was matched with two controls, which were randomly selected decedents from the Vektis database, provided they had no healthcare claim for CKD or KRT. Controls were matched for sex, age, socio-economic status and year of death. See Supplemental Materials for details on how age and socio-economic status were derived. Records with incomplete demographic data for matching controls were excluded. We assessed covariate balance between patients on KRT and their matched controls using standardized mean differences (SMD). SMD is a summary statistic used for comparison of data, calculated by dividing the mean difference by the pooled standard deviation. Values < 0.1 are considered indicative of a balanced comparison between groups. The chi-square test was performed for comparison of categorical measures and the independent t-test for continuous measures of healthcare utilisation between groups. To compare healthcare utilisation in the quarter preceding death to the prior nine months, we included only prevalent patients for this specific analysis. Since the initiation period of KRT is associated with intensified healthcare utilisation, including incident patients could distort the results based on the timing of their KRT initiation relative to their death. Therefore, for this single analysis, we excluded incident patients. To account for the difference in observation period, healthcare utilisation during the first nine months was averaged per three-month interval, enabling direct comparison with the last three months of life. Costs were presented as means per person per year. Instead of using standard deviations, we presented interquartile ranges as they more accurately reflect the data distribution. Additionally, we calculated the percentage of users that incurred costs.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). This article adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [18].

Results

Baseline characteristics

We enrolled 7279 patients receiving KRT (4614 on HD, 766 on PD and 1899 KTx recipients) and 14,558 matched controls (Fig. 1). Mean age of patients on KRT was 78 ± 7 years, with 63% being male. This was similar for the control group, reflecting successful matching. Comorbidities were more prevalent in patients on KRT compared to controls. The mean KRT duration was 51 ± 31 months, with variation across modalities. KTx recipients had a younger age at death (74 ± 5 years) compared to HD and PD patients (79 ± 7 and 78 ± 6 years respectively). Furthermore, a larger proportion of dialysis patients initiated KRT in the year preceding death (21% of HD and PD patients combined versus 3% of KTx recipients; 16% overall), see Table 1.

Healthcare utilisation in the last year of life

Figure 2 and Table 2 show that patients on KRT were 2.6 times more likely to be admitted to the hospital compared to controls (85% versus 33%), with a median of 14 [4, 29] versus 0 [0, 4] hospital days. The proportion of patients admitted to the ICU was 3.5 times higher for patients on KRT (27% versus 8% in controls. Additionally, patients on KRT were 2.4 and 1.7 times more likely to visit the emergency department and outpatient department, respectively, along with 1.2 times more often long-term care use. Healthcare utilisation was similar across KRT modalities, apart from long-term care. Compared to controls, patients on HD more frequently used long-term care (RR = 1.3, p < 0.01), while patients on PD or KTx showed lower long-term care use (RR = 0.8). In general, incident patients had higher levels of healthcare utilisation compared to prevalent patients, see Supplemental Table S1A-C.

Healthcare utilisation in the last three months prior to death

Healthcare utilisation intensified in the three months prior to death compared to the preceding nine months in both patients on KRT and their controls. Figure 3 illustrates increases across all groups in hospital and ICU admission days during the last three month, along with increases in the number of ED presentations, outpatient visits and long-term care use. Among prevalent HD patients, the mean number of hospital admission days was 8.4 days in the final three months of life, compared to 2.6 days per quarter in the previous nine months (p < 0.001). In prevalent PD patients and KTx recipients, mean hospital days increased from 3.3 to 12.3 and 3.2 to 10.7 days per quarter, respectively (p < 0.001 for both), see Supplemental Table S2A-C. Patients on KRT exhibited greater increases in healthcare utilisation in the last three months of life compared to controls; the corresponding values are represented in Supplemental Figure S1.

Healthcare costs in the last year of life

Mean total healthcare costs of the last year of life were €117,520 for HD, €108,294 for PD and €59,489 for KTx patients. This was higher compared to the controls, i.e., €19,820, see Fig. 4; Table 3. Total healthcare costs in dialysis patients were primarily driven by KRT-related costs, constituting on average €65,216 (55% of total costs) for HD patients and €64,559 (60% of total costs) for PD patients. For kidney transplant recipients, the costs related to KRT varied widely depending on the time since transplantation, with a mean of €3,537 for prevalent patients and €88,491 for those who received a KTx during their last year of life. See Supplemental Table S3 for information on costs for prevalent, incident, switching and non-switching patients per KRT modality.

Discussion

In this study we examined healthcare utilisation and costs in the last year of life in a large cohort of deceased patients on KRT. We found significantly higher healthcare utilisation among patients on KRT than among their controls, across all measures and largely irrespective of KRT modality. The increased healthcare utilisation translated into higher healthcare expenditure, with mean KRT-unrelated healthcare expenses in the last year of life more than double those of matched controls without kidney disease. In the final three months of life, patients across all KRT modalities exhibited a greater increase in healthcare utilisation compared to controls.

Consistent with studies involving patients with CKD or kidney failure, we observed extensive levels of healthcare use at end of life in patient on KRT, regardless of the KRT modality [6, 10, 19]. Population-based studies, not focused on kidney disease, have reported a rise in healthcare utilisation as patients near the end of life, particularly in the final months and weeks [20–23]. We found comparable patterns of increasing healthcare utilisation towards death, with a more pronounced rise among patients on KRT compared to controls. Studies in patients with CKD stage 4–5 or on KRT found that 65% preferred to die at home or in a hospice, and an increased likelihood of dialysis withdrawal among those who engage in ACP [24–26], indicating a desire for reduced healthcare intensity at end of life. In our study, depending on the KRT modality, 25–30% of patients were admitted to the ICU at least once in the last year of life. Some other studies in CKD and dialysis patients have indicated higher rates of hospital care use, with ICU admissions ranging from 40 to 49% in the month prior to death [9, 27]. Variations in end-of-life care may be explained by multiple factors in end-of-life settings, whether community-based or hospital-based, including cultural norms towards death, the implementation of ACP, access to palliative care services and healthcare policy. However, fragmentary research on end-of-life healthcare utilisation in patients on KRT limits insights into healthcare system differences. As such, the observations in the present study may differ from end-of-life care provided in other countries, and extrapolation should be approached with caution.

Although several studies have examined healthcare costs in the last year of life [21, 28–30], no research specifically addresses costs in this period for older patients on KRT. Comparing our findings in the last year of life to literature on a typical year showed that KRT-related costs were similar, whereas KRT-unrelated costs were approximately three times higher in the year preceding death [2]. Notably, in our study, we also observed higher costs for KRT-unrelated care in patients on KRT compared to their controls, primarily driven by specialist care within hospital settings. This increased healthcare use may be attributed to a higher prevalence of comorbidities and complications associated with kidney failure. Previous studies have also shown an association between frailty and healthcare utilisation and costs [31, 32].

The main strength of our study lies in its comprehensiveness, with nationwide coverage of health insurance claims data across multiple healthcare domains and a time span of 5.5 years. This enabled us to obtain a thorough overview of costs incurred across all these domains. By using claims data, we avoided response-related selection bias and recall bias. Furthermore, we validated our cohort to the Dutch Renal Registry, ensuring accuracy and completeness of our data.

The primary limitation of our study is the lack of clinical context, which limits our ability to assess whether healthcare utilisation in the last year of life was appropriate or whether clinical decisions were justifiable. Using a health insurance claims database, we were unable to determine whether patients received ACP or how ACP may have influenced healthcare utilisation and costs. This limitation underscores the need to incorporate clinical data in future research to provide a more nuanced understanding of healthcare practices in end-of-life care. We suggest future research to explore variations in end-of-life healthcare utilisation, evaluate how increased healthcare utilisation impacts quality of life and whether enhanced goal setting and ACP could reduce burdensome healthcare utilisation and costs at end-of-life. As a second limitation, our data did not cover two categories of healthcare use that may be used by KRT patients, namely hospice care and home-based care that fall under legislation other than the Long-term Care Act. Palliative care is often embedded within other types of care, and because health insurance claims data lack clinical context, it is not possible to distinguish whether a hospital admission is life-prolonging or palliative in nature. Despite this, we were able to provide robust estimates as the vast majority of healthcare costs were covered. Third, our analysis likely underestimated healthcare costs. Costs are linked to the start date of a claim, which could fall prior to the index date of 365 days before death. Consequently, some costs incurred in the last year of life were not included in our analysis. The non-normal distribution of costs prevented us from imputing these expenses or estimating their magnitude accurately. Furthermore, our study coincided in part with the COVID-19 pandemic, during which routine healthcare services were significantly reduced across all healthcare domains. On the other hand, the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to an overestimation of hospital admissions and ICU admissions, as studies have reported increased COVID-19 related admission rates for patients on KRT compared to the general population, along with an increased mortality risk [33–35]. The effects of the COVID-19 pandemic likely applied equally to the control group, minimizing their impact on relative differences. Considering the study duration of 5.5 years, the influence of the COVID-19 pandemic on our results is probably relatively low.

Our findings provide valuable insights for clinicians and policymakers by highlighting the healthcare intensity and economic burden of KRT at the end of life. It is reasonable to assume that healthcare utilisation may entail treatment burden for patients. The intensive healthcare utilisation observed in our study therefore suggests a considerable burden. Accordingly, our results emphasize the importance of exploring care strategies to alleviate this treatment burden. In addition, these insights into the intensity of care can raise awareness among healthcare professionals and inform ACP by supporting discussions between patients and healthcare providers, while also serving as a leverage point in discussions onthe allocation of resources to meet patient needs and ensure the long-term sustainability of care.

In conclusion, our study reveals that patients on KRT have significantly higher healthcare utilisation during their last year of life compared to matched controls, with a further increase towards the end of life. This was accompanied by substantial higher costs, extending beyond KRT-related expenses.
