Myocardial infarction treatment with a composite hydrogel containing metformin-induced vesicles of adipose-derived stem cells
Authors: Jing Qi, Zhipeng Cao, Lu Yin, Hong Jin, Cheng Cheng, Xiaohong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myocardial infarction (MI) leads to irreversible cardiomyocyte loss and adverse remodeling. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) have shown promise in cardiac repair, but their clinical translation has been strictly limited by the short retention time and rapid clearance phenomena at the injury site. Metformin preconditioning of adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) is an effective way to enhance the yield of the EVs (Met-EVs). In this study, we incorporated Met-EVs into a composite hydrogel composed of decellularized extracellular matrices (dECMs) derived from porcine cardiac muscle and aortic adventitia, enabling sustained EV release and attenuation of inflammation after MI. In a rat MI model, the Met-EVs-laden dECM hydrogel (Met-EVs-dECM) significantly improved cardiac function recovery by enhancing cardiomyocyte survival, reducing cardiac apoptosis, and promoting angiogenesis. C1q/tumor necrosis factor-related protein 1 was identified as a contributor to the biological effects of Met-EVs, at least in part through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the therapeutic potential of the Met-EV-loaded dECM hydrogel for myocardial repair after MI.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality [1,2], with a poor long-term prognosis largely attributable to irreversible cardiomyocyte loss and adverse ventricular remodeling [3,4]. Current clinical management prioritizes rapid revascularization via percutaneous coronary intervention, thrombolytic therapy, or coronary artery bypass grafting [5,6]. However, these strategies cannot repair the infarcted myocardium because of the inherently limited regenerative capacity of the adult cardiomyocytes [7]. Hydrogel-based injectable biomaterials offer promising approaches for addressing this issue. Among the injectable biomaterials, stem cells and their extracellular vesicles (EVs), especially the EVs-loaded hydrogel systems, have emerged as promising platforms for enhancing the myocardial repair functions post-MI [[8], [9], [10]].

EVs, as the natural cell-free carriers of bioactive cargo (e.g., miRNAs and proteins), function as key intercellular mediators that orchestrate the multiple post-MI repair processes, including angiogenesis, apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis [[11], [12], [13]]. At present, the clinical advantages of the cell-free EVs, such as the improved human body safety and the easier clinical translation policy, are strictly constrained by the challenges, including the short retention time and rapid clearance at the injury site [14]. To overcome these constraints, various engineering approaches have been developed to prolong the in vivo persistence of the EVs.

The first approach involves optimizing the cellular sources of EVs to improve their biological functions through cell preconditioning (e.g., hypoxia [15] or drug pretreatment [16]) with the selected ideal parental cells [17]. A prime example of the parental cell preconditioning is metformin, a widely used drug that can augment both the biogenesis and functionalities of the mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived EVs. It has been proven that the therapeutic efficacy of the metformin-pretreated EVs can be enhanced remarkably via cell autophagy and proteome alterations to enrich the reparative proteins [18,19]. The direct benefits include mitigating cellular senescence, enhancing the oxidative phosphorylation in the aged tissues, and rescuing the senescence-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. These findings underscore the potential of using metformin as a tool for optimizing the EV sources to treat MI.

The second approach focuses on developing advanced delivery platforms to overcome the pharmacokinetic limitations of the EVs. Notably, injectable hydrogels can serve as ideal cardiac repair reservoirs by boosting the EVs' bioavailability and MI treatment efficacy [14,20]. Especially, the decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM)-based hydrogels could offer advantages in cardiac repair by preserving the native bioactive components, thus providing a biomimetic microenvironment that supports the endogenous healing processes, modulating the immune responses [21], promoting the angiogenesis procedures [22], and inhibiting the adverse remodeling tendencies [23]. These findings suggest that a synergistic effect could be produced by combining the bioactive dECM hydrogels with the metformin preconditioning MSC-derived EVs (Met-EVs) [[23], [24], [25]].

In this study, we used an injectable composite hydrogel to controllably release the Met-EVs. The composite hydrogel, designated CA, was fabricated by mixing porcine cardiac dECM (cECM) and aortic adventitia dECM (Adv) at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio to integrate the complementary biological properties of the two ECM sources. When the Met-EVs were embedded evenly in the CA, the Met-EVs-dECM hydrogel could act as a localized delivery system to synergistically enhance vascularization, inhibit apoptosis, and modulate the post-infarct microenvironment. The therapeutic potential of the Met-EVs-dECM hydrogel in improving cardiac function, reducing fibrosis, and promoting angiogenesis was further evaluated in a rat MI model. It is a promising strategy for cardiac tissue repair and regeneration post-MI.

Materials and methods

Preparation of the dECM hydrogels

The dECM hydrogels were prepared from porcine left ventricular myocardium and aortic adventitia according to the adapted protocols [[26], [27], [28]].

Preparation of the cECM

Fresh porcine cardiac muscles were first dissected to isolate the ventricular myocardium. The obtained cardiac muscles were minced into small pieces after being washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Decellularization was performed by continuous agitation of the tissue pieces in 1% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution at room temperature for 4-5 days, with daily solution changes. The samples were subsequently agitated for another 3 days and washed with PBS to completely remove the residual SDS, as indicated by the absence of foam upon shaking. After the resulting samples were frozen at −80°C, lyophilized, and terminally sterilized using gamma irradiation from a 60Co source, cECM powder was obtained.

Preparation of the Adv pregel

The adventitial layer was meticulously isolated from a fresh porcine aorta. Decellularization was achieved using a sequential detergent-enzymatic process. Briefly, the fresh porcine aorta was first treated with a solution of 1 M NaCl, 8 mM CHAPS (Aladdin®, C105622), and 25 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) under oscillation at 37°C for 24 h, before being washed with PBS and distilled water [28]. Subsequently, the samples were incubated in 0.1% trypsin/0.04% EDTA to detach and disrupt the cells, and treated with 0.5% SDS, 1 M NaCl, and 25 mM EDTA at 37°C for 24 h to remove the residual cells. Finally, the samples were washed thoroughly with PBS before being frozen at −80°C and lyophilized. The Adv powder was collected after lyophilization.

Preparation of the cECM-Adv hydrogels

The lyophilized cECM and Adv powders were separately digested in 0.01 M hydrochloric acid containing 10 mg/mL pepsin at a concentration of 15 mg ECM powder per mL for 48 h. Based on preliminary optimization, 15 mg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments due to its stable gel formation (Fig. S1). The digestates were then neutralized with sodium hydroxide and buffered to physiological pH (i.e., 7.4), yielding the pre-gel solutions that underwent thermal gelation at 37°C. The composite hydrogel, designated as the CA hydrogel as stated above, was formed by mixing the cECM and Adv pre-gel solutions in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio.

Assessment of the decellularization efficiency and dECM composition

Histological and nuclear staining

Tissue samples were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound, sectioned to a thickness of 8 μm, and stained. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Servicebio, G1005) staining was used to evaluate general tissue architecture and cellular removal. Nuclei were visualized using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining (Solarbio, C0065). The presence of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and collagen fibers was assessed using Alcian blue (Beyotime, C0155S) and picrosirius red staining (Phygene, PH1098), respectively.

DNA content analysis

The residual DNA was quantitatively and qualitatively analyzed. The total DNA was extracted from weighed tissue samples using a commercial genomic DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, DP304-02) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop One Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For qualitative assessment, the extracted DNA fragments were resolved by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel.

Collagen content quantification

The total collagen content was determined using a hydroxyproline assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A030-2-1), following the manufacturer's protocol. Tissue samples were hydrolyzed, and the hydroxyproline content was measured colorimetrically at 550 nm using a spectrophotometer. The total collagen content was calculated based on the principle that hydroxyproline constitutes approximately 14.3% of the total collagen mass [29].

Cell isolation and culture

Isolation and culture of the neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs)

The NRVMs were isolated from 2-4-day-old Sprague–Dawley (SD, Huafukang Biological Co., Beijing, China) rats, as previously described [30,31]. Ventricles were excised, minced, and subjected to sequential enzymatic digestion using 0.8% (w/v) type II collagenase (Solarbio, C8150) in a 37°C water bath. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 200 × g for 15 min at room temperature. To enrich cardiomyocyte purity, the pelleted cells were resuspended and subjected to differential plating (pre-plating) for 2 h in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Eallbio, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (v/v; Biosharp, BL505A). Non-adherent cardiomyocytes were then collected and maintained in DMEM/F12 (10% FBS) containing 100 μM 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (5-BrdU, Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., S18116) to inhibit fibroblast overgrowth. The NRVMs spontaneously contracting by Day 3-4 of culture were used for the subsequent experiments.

Culture of the human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs)

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; iCell-h110) were obtained from a collaborating laboratory. The cells were originally purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Shanghai) Inc. (Shanghai, China). Cells at passages 5–7 were used for subsequent experiments. They were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Eallbio, China) in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37°C (Thermo, USA).

Culture of ADSCs

Rat ADSCs (Cell Bank HyCyte™, catalog #ADSC-C106) were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Eallbio, Beijing, China) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells between passages 6 and 7 were used for EV isolation, and conditioned culture supernatants were collected.

In vitro biocompatibility studies

Hemolysis test

In vitro hemolysis tests were performed on the cECM, Adv, and CA hydrogels to evaluate their hemocompatibility. The hydrogel extracts were incubated with a 2% (v/v) suspension of rat red blood cells at 37°C for 1 h. After centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm. The hemolysis ratio was calculated using the following equation, where the absorbance values of the samples were given as A, the positive control was distilled water given as AP, and the negative control was PBS given as AN:Hemolysisrate(%)=(A−AN)(AP−AN)×100

CCK-8 assay

After treating the cells with hydrogel extracts for 1, 2, and 3 days, cell viability was determined using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Biosharp, BS350B) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm after 2 h of incubation at 37°C.

Live/dead staining

Following treatment with the extracts, cell viability and cytotoxicity were directly visualized using the calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) double-stain kit (Solarbio, CA1630). Cells were incubated with the staining solution at 37°C for 30 min and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Cell state and angiogenic morphogenesis analyses on the hydrogels

Hydrogel preparation and cell seeding: The cECM, Adv, and CA pre-gel solutions were pipetted into 24-well plates and underwent thermal gelation at 37°C for 1 h. Thereafter, the hydrogels were gently rinsed with PBS. The HUVECs and NRVMs were seeded directly onto the surface of the gels for immunofluorescence staining and angiogenesis assays. The NRVMs were immunostained for Connexin 43 (Cx43) using a specific antibody (Abcam, ab314908). After 7 days of culture, the HUVECs were stained for F-actin with phalloidin (Abbkine, BMD0084, 1:100 dilution) at 37°C for 60 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The capillary-like structures formed by the HUVECs were quantified using the “Angiogenesis Analyzer” plugin in Fiji software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Isolation and characterization of the ADSCs-EVs

After treatment with 5 mM metformin (Macklin, M813341) for 24 h, the ADSCs were washed three times with PBS and subsequently cultured in fresh medium supplemented with EV-depleted FBS for an additional 24 h. The conditioned medium was then collected for the isolation of ADSC-derived EVs. The EVs were isolated according to the MISEV2018 guidelines. The cell supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Biosharp, BS-PES-22), followed by a series of differential centrifugation steps: at 2000 × g for 10 min, 10,000 × g for 30 min, and 100,000 × g for 90 min at 4°C. The EV-containing pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 90 min at 4°C. For clarity, the EVs directly from the ADSCs were termed EVs, while those from the metformin-treated ADSCs were termed as Met-EVs. The protein concentrations of the isolated EVs were quantified using a Bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0012) following the manufacturer's instructions.

The morphology of the EVs was visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) by loading them onto a carbon-coated copper grid and staining them with uranyl acetate. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; ZetaView S/N22-772, Particle Metrix, Germany) was used to determine the size distribution and concentration of EVs. The size distribution data were acquired using a Zetaview-PMX120-Z system (Particle Metrix, Germany). EVs labeled with PKH26 (Solarbio, China) were incubated with cells for the internalization assay. Subsequently, this labeling strategy was applied to confirm the uniform distribution of EVs within the hydrogels using fluorescence microscopy.

Cell migration and tube formation assays on the hydrogels

For the Transwell assay, 1 × 104 HUVECs were seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell plate (8.0 μm pore size, Corning, 3422) with serum-free medium, and media with different EVs were added to the lower chamber. After incubation for 24 h, the migrated cells in the lower surface of the membrane were counted after staining with a 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio, G1062) for 30 min.

For the tube formation assay, Matrigel (Mogengel-Bio, 082704T) was coated as the base of pre-cooled plates, and starved HUVECs were seeded onto Matrigel-coated plates with different EVs. Cells were incubated for 6 h, following which they were photographed and quantified using Fiji.

Cell proliferation and apoptosis assays

Cell proliferation (EdU)

After the HUVECs were treated with EVs or Met-EVs for 24 h, the cell proliferation rate was assessed using EdU Imaging Kit 488 (APExBIO, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were incubated with EdU for 2 h, fixed, permeabilized, and stained with Apollo 488 and Hoechst 33342. EdU-positive nuclei were visualized and counted using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell apoptosis

The NRVMs were subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) to mimic ischemic injury by incubation in glucose-free DMEM (Procell, China) in a tri-gas incubator (94% N2, 1% O2, 5% CO2) for 24 h [32]. They were pretreated with different concentrations of EVs, Met-EVs, recombinant mouse C1q/tumor necrosis factor-related protein-1 (C1QTNF1,Aviscera Bioscience, 00083-02-100), or Ca1 for 24 h. Apoptosis was evaluated using the mitochondrial membrane potential-sensitive dye JC-1 (Beyotime, C2003S) and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated (TUNEL) staining (Roche, 11684795910, Switzerland) based on the respective kit instructions.

Western blot

The cells were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor (Beyotime, P1045) and 1% phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime, P1045). Denatured proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto 0.22 μm polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, ISEQ00010) using a Trans-Blot system (Tanon, China). The membranes were blocked with blocking buffer (Yamei, PS108) for 20 min at room temperature, and then they were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C for 14-16 h. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using Tanon™ High-sig ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Tanon, 180-501), and images were captured using an automated chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

The following primary antibodies were used in this study: anti-CD63 (1:1000; Proteintech, 67605-1), anti-HSP70 (1:1000; Abcam, ab181606), anti-TSG101 (1:1000; Abcam, ab125011), anti-Cleaved-caspase3 (1:1000; CST, 9661T), anti-caspase3 (1:1000; CST, 14220T), anti-C1QTNF1 (1:500; Novus Biologicals, NBP1-76626), anti-AKT (1:1000; CST, 4691T), anti-p-AKT Ser473 (1:1000; CST, 4060T), anti-PI3K p85 (1:1000; CST, 4257T), anti-p-PI3Kp85 (1:1000; CST, 17366), anti-Calnexin (1:3000; Proteintech, 66903-1) and anti-β-actin (1:2000; Proteintech, 66009-1). The secondary antibodies used were anti-mouse IgG, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked (Bioss, bs-0296G), and anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked (Invitrogen, C31460100).

In vivo experiments in rats

All the animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of China Medical University. Male SD rats were obtained from HFK Biotechnology (Beijing, China).

In accordance with the approved animal protocol (IACUC approval No. CMU20241387), male SD rats weighing 220–260 g were used to maintain consistency with our established acute myocardial infarction (AMI) model and to enable internally consistent comparisons among treatment groups in this initial proof-of-concept study. The rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, intubated with a 16-G trocar, and ventilated via tracheal intubation connected to a rodent ventilator. AMI was induced by ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery [33]. The rats were divided into six groups (sham, MI, MI + CA, MI + CA + EVs, MI + CA + Met-EVs, and MI + Met-EVs; n = 6 per group). Thereafter, 200 μL PBS, 200 μL hydrogel, or 200 μL hydrogel containing 100 μg EV (20 μL, 5 μg/μL) or Met-EVs (20 μL, 5 μg/μL) was injected into the pericardial cavity adjacent to the infarct area, or Met-EVs (20 μL, 5 μg/μL) were injected into the border zone of the infarct area at three sites. The sham group underwent the same procedure without ligation or injection. Under isoflurane anesthesia, transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a Vevo 2100 system (VisualSonics). Left ventricular function parameters, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS), left ventricular end-diastolic internal diameter (LVIDD), and left ventricular end-systolic internal diameter (LVIDS), were measured and calculated, as previously described.

Histological staining

All the rats were sacrificed 28 days after MI induction, following the final echocardiographic measurements were recorded. The samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde phosphate buffer for 24 h, following which they were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm slices. H&E staining was used to evaluate the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration, and Masson's trichrome staining was performed to quantify the extent of fibrosis and infarct size in the left ventricle. The number of apoptotic cells in the tissue sections was determined using a TUNEL assay (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The cross-sectional areas of cardiomyocytes were determined by staining with FITC-conjugated wheat germ agglutinin (WGA, Servicebio, G1730). To assess the distribution of microvessels and neovascularization four weeks after MI, the sections were co-stained with antibodies against CD31 (Servicebio, GB11063-2), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; Cell Signaling Technology, 19245), and cardiac troponin I (cTnI; Proteintech, 66376-1-Ig). Nuclei were visualized using DAPI counterstaining. Images were obtained using a microscope (Leica).

Immunohistochemical staining

Deparaffinized and rehydrated tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval by heating in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). After blocking endogenous peroxidase with 3% H2O2 and non-specific sites with 10% normal goat serum, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody against cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000, CST, #9661T) and then with appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated with HRP at room temperature for 60 min. Detection was performed using a Histostain-SP kit (Boster) with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine(DAB) as the chromogen, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (Leica, Germany).

Proteomic analysis

Proteins from EVs derived from control or metformin-treated ADSCs were isolated and analyzed using data-independent acquisition quantitative proteomics technology. The procedure was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Novogene Co., Ltd., China). Subsequently, the raw data were processed and analyzed using Thermo Proteome Discoverer software. Differentially expressed proteins were identified based on the threshold of a 1.5-fold change between the control and treated samples. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using InterProScan. BLAST was used for KEGG pathway analysis and Clusters of Orthologous Genes annotations.

Lentiviral transduction

Lentiviral vectors (Shanghai Shenggong) that reduced rat C1qtnf1 expression were generated using a lentiviral vector plasmid system. The ADSCs at passage 2 were transduced individually or in combination with lentiviral vectors at a multiplicity of infection of 100 [34]. The transduced ADSCs with C1qtnf1 knockdown were selected using 10 μg/mL puromycin. After a 2-week selection period, successful transduction was confirmed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). The expression of C1QTNF1 was verified by western blot analysis. Once characterized, the cells were cultured in EV-free serum until reaching passages 5-6, after which the culture supernatant was collected for EV extraction.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

The total RNA from the ADSCs' samples was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, RR047A). The qRT-PCR was subsequently performed with TB Green Premix Ex TaqII (Takara, RR820A) on a LightCycler 96 System (Roche, Switzerland) following the manufacturer's protocols. C1qtnf1 expression was quantified using the primers listed in Supplementary Materials Table 2, with Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase serving as an endogenous control for normalization.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed and analyzed using GraphPad Prism software, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All continuous variables were tested for normality. Upon confirming the homogeneity of variance across groups, independent-sample t-tests were used to compare two groups, whereas one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's post-hoc test was employed for three or more groups. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Preparation and characterization of the dECM hydrogels

To develop an ECM-like hydrogel that better recapitulates the biochemical composition of the native heart tissues, we prepared the cECM and Adv hydrogels separately before merging them together (Fig. 1A). These hydrogels exhibited several key characteristics. First, the cellular components were effectively removed from the porcine heart and aortic adventitia via the previously described decellularization methods [[26], [27], [28]]. This phenomenon was clarified through several experiments. On the one hand, the decellularization efficiency was evaluated by the H&E and DAPI staining results (Fig. 1B). In contrast to the native heart tissue, no nuclear residue was detected in the dECMs, confirming the effective removal of the cellular components. On the other hand, the Alcian blue staining results revealed the presence of the retained GAGs in both the cECM and Adv samples, as evidenced by the distinct blue areas. It is recognized that GAGs are crucial nonfibrillar components of the ECM and are vital for cell signaling, angiogenesis, and vasculogenesis [35,36]. Furthermore, under polarized light, the picrosirius red staining revealed a well-preserved collagen framework characterized by the abundant red-orange collagen fibers (type I) and a minor proportion of green fibers (type III) (Fig. 1B). The preservation of a structurally intact collagen framework is critical, as it provides fundamental mechanical support while actively orchestrating tissue regeneration and repair [37,38]. Additionally, the hydroxyproline content was significantly increased in the dECM samples relative to the native heart tissues. Second, the residual DNA content was very low. In all the resulting samples the residual DNA content wase less than 50 ng/mg (Fig. 1D and E), consistent with the successful cell removal results. Following digestion and dissolution with pepsin (10 mg/mL), both the cECM and Adv powders were induced to form hydrogels at the physiological pH (i.e. 7.4) and temperature (37°C) utilizing the kinetics of collagen fibril self-assembly (Fig. 1F). All three hydrogels exhibited highly porous microstructures upon SEM examination after freeze-drying (Fig. S2). SEM analysis showed that the average pore sizes of the cECM, Adv, and CA hydrogels were 155.32 ± 20.49 μm, 88.05 ± 16.30 μm, and 117.72 ± 1.91 μm, respectively (n = 3). This porous structure is functionally beneficial for tissue formation, as it promotes molecular transport and cellular infiltration, especially for bioactive signal delivery. Turbidimetric analysis showed that the cECM hydrogel had a significantly longer t1/2 than the Adv hydrogel, indicating slower gelation kinetics (Holm-adjusted P = 0.0080) (Fig. S3A, Table 1). Rheological and mechanical properties of the dECM hydrogels were evaluated (Fig. S3). All hydrogels exhibited shear-thinning behavior, indicating favorable injectability. Frequency-sweep analysis showed that the storage modulus (G′) remained higher than the loss modulus (G″), indicating stable elastic networks. Compression testing revealed distinct compressive moduli among the hydrogels, with CA showing intermediate characteristics between cECM and Adv. Proteomic analysis further demonstrated distinct ECM signatures between cECM and Adv (Fig. S4). Specifically, cECM was enriched in basement membrane-related proteins, whereas Adv ECM exhibited relatively higher abundance of fibrillar collagen and matrix-remodeling-associated proteins. These findings suggest that the two ECM sources provide complementary biochemical components for constructing the composite hydrogel.

Third, the hydrogels exhibited good cytocompatibility. The calcein-AM/PI staining assay, a standard method for evaluating cytocompatibility, was used to assess the cell viability following a 3-day culture in the hydrogel extracts. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, HUVECs treated with the three hydrogel extracts exhibited a uniform distribution and healthy, normal morphology compared to the control group. The propidium iodide/calcein-AM ratio was < 0.1% in all groups (control, cECM, Adv, and CA), with no statistically significant differences. Immunofluorescence staining showed that the isolated NRVMs were positive for cTnT, confirming their cardiomyocyte identity (Fig. S5). In contrast, the primary NRVMs (Fig. 2F and G) were notably larger and spindle-shaped. Despite the slightly higher PI/calcein-AM ratios in all NRVM groups, no statistically significant difference was found compared to the control group. Two-dimensional (2D) culture of the HUVECs and NRVMs on the dECM hydrogels

When HUVECs were seeded onto the hydrogels, robust capillary networks formed on the surface. As shown in Fig. 3A, the capillary-like networks formed were significantly longer after seven days of 2D culture on the Adv than those on the cECM and CA hydrogels. Similarly, the number of nodes (primary stage of angiogenesis) and branches (intermediate angiogenesis stage) significantly increased with increasing Adv in the composite hydrogel (Fig. 3D). Discussion

As stated above, MI remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, with a poor long-term prognosis largely attributable to irreversible cardiomyocyte loss and adverse ventricular remodeling [3,4]. The healing process after MI involves a complex pathological cascade, including cardiomyocyte apoptosis, inflammation, insufficient vascularization, and fibrosis. The adult mammalian heart exhibits limited regenerative capacity following ischemic injury, and massive cardiomyocyte loss remains a major barrier to functional recovery [45,46]. Moreover, inadequate restoration of the vascular network within the infarcted myocardium results in persistent hypoxia, sustained inflammation, and maladaptive fibrosis, ultimately impairing cardiac repair and promoting heart failure [47,48]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies capable of simultaneously providing a regenerative microenvironment and supporting vascular reconstruction are highly desirable for post-MI repair.

Although cECM provides a cardiac-specific microenvironment for myocardial regeneration, effective repair after MI also requires vascular support and ECM remodeling. Therefore, Adv was incorporated as a complementary component. Adv contains abundant matrix components involved in vascular regulation and remodeling, and previous studies have demonstrated its vascular-supportive properties [49,50]. Consistently, our proteomic analysis revealed ECM signatures between cECM and Adv, with cECM enriched in basement membrane-associated components and Adv containing relatively higher levels of fibrillar collagen and matrix-regulatory proteins. These complementary biochemical properties suggest that Adv provides additional vascular-supportive and matrix-remodeling cues, potentially enhancing the regenerative capacity of the cardiac ECM-based hydrogel.

In addition to optimizing the local extracellular microenvironment, sustained delivery of therapeutic factors represents another critical challenge for myocardial repair. Previous studies have demonstrated that hydrogel-based delivery systems can improve the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-derived EVs after MI by prolonging EV retention and enhancing their local availability [[51], [52], [53]]. However, the therapeutic effects of approaches based on a single bioactive agent remain limited due to their restricted mechanisms and short-lived activity within the hostile infarct microenvironment. Therefore, integrated strategies that combine regenerative ECM scaffolds with sustained delivery of bioactive EVs may provide a more comprehensive approach to preserving viable myocardium and promoting vascular regeneration after MI.

Previously, a study utilized a decellularized porcine cardiac ECM hydrogel to deliver the curcumin-encapsulating exosomes for antifibrotic purposes [23]. In the present study, we developed a composite CA hydrogel (i.e., the CA + Met-EVs) to deliver the Met-EVs. The experimental results demonstrated that the composite CA + Met-EVs system could orchestrate a multifaceted reparative response that reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, promoted robust angiogenesis, and ultimately improved ventricular systolic function, as evidenced by the elevated LVEF values (Fig. 9). There are several outstanding features of this CA + Met-EVs system for MI treatment. First, the bioactive signal-coupled CA-based hydrogels can be a multifunctional platform for repair of injured myocardium. The composition and microarchitecture can closely mimic the native myocardial ECM, providing a biomimetic scaffold support. The injectable hydrogel can form an in situ pericardial patch to achieve localized and sustained EV delivery. Compared with the conventional intramyocardial EVs injection route, this approach could eliminate the risks of direct myocardial injury and potential tumorigenesis, providing a minimally invasive alternative with superior tissue compatibility [40]. Beyond its structural role, the composite CA-based hydrogel has actively fostered a pro-regenerative microenvironment.

Second, the CA-based hydrogels applied in the MI models could recruit cardiac-resident macrophages, activate fibroblasts, and enhance the development of lymphatic and vascular systems with the surrounding cardiomyocytes, as revealed by the coupled spatial and single-nucleus analyses [54]. The clinical translatability of this novel biomaterial approach was supported by a first-in-human trial that established the safety and feasibility of the intracardiac hydrogel injections in post-MI patients [21]. Importantly, the therapeutic potential of the CA-based hydrogels has been underpinned by their capability to orchestrate a sophisticated proregenerative microenvironment, as indicated by the high-resolution spatial and single-cell analysis results (Fig. 7).

The Adv hydrogel may promote angiogenesis by stimulating endothelial sprouting through bFGF and stabilizing newly formed vessels via α2β1/TGF-β-mediated enhancement of pericyte contractility [28,50]. By integrating cECM and Adv, the composite CA hydrogel provides complementary biochemical cues for multicellular myocardial repair. Consistent with previous studies showing that ECM composition and matrix stiffness can modulate Cx43-related cardiomyocyte phenotypes [40,41], the increased Cx43 immunofluorescence intensity and Gja1 mRNA expression observed in the cECM and CA groups suggest that cardiac ECM-containing hydrogels provide a favorable microenvironment for maintaining a Cx43-associated cardiomyocyte phenotype. Together with the enhanced pro-angiogenic response, these effects may contribute to the reconstruction of a more favorable microenvironment for myocardial repair [55]. However, functional electrical coupling remains to be verified by direct electrophysiological assays.

Third, the metformin preconditioning of the ADSCs is an effective way to enhance the yield of the EVs. Recently, the MSC-derived EVs have emerged as promising cell-free therapeutic agents. Their principal advantage lies in recapitulating the dual therapeutic actions of the parental MSCs, modulating the immune responses, and delivering the pro-regenerative signals while circumventing the risks associated with whole-cell therapies [56,57]. This potential has been further exemplified by studies showing that the EVs from the specialized progenitor cells can enhance cardiac repair by mitigating cardiomyocyte death and stimulating angiogenesis [12]. A key strategy to amplify these inherent benefits is the preconditioning of MSCs (e.g., via LPS, metformin, or hypoxia), which reprograms their EVs by enriching them with tailored cargo, thereby transforming them into more potent nanotherapeutics with amplified anti-inflammatory, pro-survival, and pro-regenerative functions [18,19,58,59]. Our findings suggest that metformin preconditioning affects both the abundance and protein characteristics of ADSC-derived EVs. Metformin treatment increased CD63-associated signals in ADSCs, while the resulting Met-EV preparations also exhibited higher CD63 signal intensity than control EV preparations. Our findings suggest that metformin preconditioning modulates both the yield and molecular characteristics of ADSC-derived EVs. Although increased CD63-associated signals in ADSCs and Met-EVs indicate altered EV-associated characteristics, enhanced EV production was primarily supported by NTA analysis. Furthermore, metformin preconditioning generated Met-EVs with improved therapeutic effects, including reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis and enhanced endothelial angiogenic capacity, consistent with previous reports demonstrating the role of EVs in cardiac repair through promoting cell survival and angiogenesis [12]. A potential concern is that residual metformin may co-purify with Met-EVs and contribute to their biological effects. LC–MS/MS analysis indicated that residual metformin in the purified Met-EV preparation was below the operational LOD of approximately 1 ng/mL. Therefore, a direct pharmacological effect of detectable residual metformin was considered unlikely. However, trace amounts below the LOD cannot be excluded, and the absence of an equivalent trace-metformin control should be acknowledged as a limitation.

Notably, a recent study on skin repair provides compelling mechanistic insights into our findings, suggesting that the Met-EVs can remodel the cellular energy metabolism by enhancing mitophagy and restoring oxidative phosphorylation [19]. This metabolic reprogramming offers a plausible upstream driver for improving the cell survival rate and biological function observed in our cardiac model. Given that the pathogenesis of the acute MI involves significant apoptosis mediated through both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, the Met-EV-based therapeutic strategy is capable of inhibiting cell apoptosis effectively and is crucial for safeguarding cardiac functions [60].

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is a master regulator of multiple cell death pathways, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis [[61], [62], [63], [64], [65], [66]]. In this study, MI treatment with Met-EVs has activated this pathway, as evidenced by the increased phosphorylation. Proteomic analysis identified C1QTNF1 as a candidate mediator of the biological activity of Met-EVs. Functional experiments showed that C1QTNF1 knockdown partially attenuated Met-EV-induced PI3K/AKT activation, supporting a contributory role for C1QTNF1 in the observed pro-survival signaling. However, the biological effects of Met-EVs are likely mediated by multiple EV-associated components. C1QTNF1 is a recently identified adipokine involved in the regulation of metabolism and inflammation [67]. The cardioprotective role of C1QTNF1 has been supported by extensive literature. It protected against pathological cardiac hypertrophy, doxorubicin- and LPS-induced injury, and cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury by activating the pro-survival pathways, such as AKT, and inhibiting apoptosis [43,44,68]. This function was conserved within the protein family, as observed in the homologous cardioprotective adipokine CTRP3 [69].

Nevertheless, the role of C1QTNF1 is context-dependent and exhibits a duality defined by its cellular targets. In immune cells, such as macrophages, it can promote pro-inflammatory states and elevate systemic levels, correlating with adverse clinical outcomes [70,71]. This apparent contradiction underscores the critical importance of the delivery context. Our Met-EVs delivery system has also addressed this issue. By using a biomimetic hydrogel as a localized EV delivery system, we have selectively harnessed C1QTNF1-mediated beneficial PI3K/AKT signaling within cardiomyocytes for acute MI repair while minimizing systemic exposure and off-target inflammatory effects. The composite CA hydrogel has demonstrated the sophistication required to exploit the therapeutic potential of potent, context-sensitive molecules.

From a translational perspective, the present delivery system is primarily intended as an early intervention following acute myocardial infarction. In this study, the Met-EV-loaded CA hydrogel was administered intrapericardially immediately after LAD ligation. Such early local delivery may enhance EV retention within the injured myocardium, reduce acute cardiomyocyte loss, promote angiogenesis, and thereby limit subsequent adverse ventricular remodeling. However, because treatment was initiated at the time of infarction induction, the efficacy of this system following delayed administration, during subacute post-infarction remodeling, or in established chronic infarct scars remains unknown. Clinically, this strategy may be applicable during surgical revascularization or shortly after acute reperfusion therapy. Nevertheless, the feasibility, safety, and optimal timing of minimally invasive intrapericardial delivery require further investigation.

Additionally, this study has several limitations. First, therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in a rodent model of acute MI, and validation in clinically relevant large-animal models is required before clinical translation. Moreover, delayed administration studies are needed to determine whether this system applies to subacute or chronic after MI repair. Second, only male rats were included. Because biological sex may influence post-MI inflammation, fibrosis, ventricular remodeling, and functional recovery [72], the present findings cannot be directly generalized to females. Future studies including both male and female animals are needed to evaluate potential sex-dependent treatment responses. Third, although the 1:1 cECM/Adv ECM ratio was formulated to integrate complementary cardiac and vascular ECM cues, systematic optimization of alternative ratios was not performed. Future studies are needed to further refine the ECM composition. Finally, the molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of Met-EVs, including their potential influence on mitochondrial function, remain to be fully elucidated. Although C1QTNF1 may contribute to the cardioprotective effects of Met-EVs, EV-mediated repair is likely multifactorial, and the contributions of other bioactive components, particularly EV-associated RNAs, require further investigation. In addition, although EV dosage was normalized based on total protein content in this study, future investigations using particle number-based normalization are warranted to further distinguish the relative contributions of EV quantity and EV-specific biological activity.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed an injectable CA + Met-EVs hydrogel for myocardial repair. By integrating cECM, Adv, and bioactive Met-EVs, this system enhanced EV retention and promoted cardiomyocyte survival, angiogenesis, and cardiac functional recovery following MI. The CA hydrogel enhanced the local retention of Met-EVs, whereas Met-EV treatment was associated with increased PI3K/AKT signaling. C1QTNF1 knockdown attenuated this response, supporting a contributory role for EV-associated C1QTNF1 in the observed cardioprotective effects. Nevertheless, the therapeutic activity of Met-EVs is likely multifactorial and cannot be attributed exclusively to C1QTNF1. This multifunctional approach, combining biomaterial design, cellular preconditioning, and localized molecular delivery, may provide a useful framework for developing next-generation MI therapies.
