On the integrable six-wave interaction system and its space-time shifted reduction
Mark J. Ablowitz, Ramesh Gupta, Ziad H. Musslimani, Nicholas J. Ossi

Abstract
The multi-dimensional six-wave interaction system is derived in the context of nonlinear optics. Starting from Maxwell’s equations, a reduced system of equations governing the dynamics of the electric and polarization fields are obtained. Using a space-time multi-scale asymptotic expansion, a hierarchy of coupled equations describing the spatio-temporal evolution of the perturbed electric and polarization fields are derived. The leading order equation admits a six-wave ansatz satisfying a triad resonance condition. By removing secular terms at next order, a first order in space and time quadratically nonlinear coupled six-wave interaction system is obtained. This resulting system is tied to its integrable counterpart which was postulated by Ablowitz and Haberman in the 1970s. A reduction to a space-time shifted nonlocal three-wave system is presented. The resulting system is solved using the inverse scattering transform, which employs nonlocal symmetries between the associated eigenfunctions and scattering data; soliton solutions are then obtained. Finally, an infinite set of conservation laws for the six-wave system is derived; one is shown to be connected to its Hamiltonian structure.

Introduction

Multi-wave resonant interactions constitute one of the fundamental concepts ubiquitous to nonlinear dispersive systems. In its simplest form,
it amounts to a nonlinear conversion process by which two incoming waves corresponding to wave vectors
{\bf k}_{1}
and
{\bf k}_{2}
and frequencies

\omega_{1}\equiv\omega({\bf k}_{1})
and

\omega_{2}\equiv\omega({\bf k}_{2})
, where
\omega({\bf k})
is the dispersion relation, combine to generate a new (third) wave with
frequency
\omega_{3}
and wave vector
{\bf k}_{3}
satisfying the so-called resonant triad:

\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{k}_{3}=0\,,\;\;\;\;\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}+\omega_{3}=0\,,

(1.1)

or three-wave resonant interaction. When
{\bf k}_{2}={\bf k}_{1}
we term this a two-wave resonant interaction. Physically speaking, such coherent “nonlinear addition” of waves are special: it requires a match between
both
the wave vectors and their respective frequencies – a property not every dispersive medium allows. For example, in deep water without surface tension the dispersion relation
\omega=\pm\sqrt{g|{\bf k}|}
does not satisfy the resonance condition (
1.1
). In that case a four-wave resonant interaction is found.

[

2

[

[

showed that three-wave or triad resonance satisfying conditions such as (
1.1
) can occur in deep water waves with surface tension. Simmons
[
5

then gave a geometrical argument for three-wave resonance in deep water waves with surface tension and found an asymptotic reduction to three wave equations with slowly varying amplitudes in both space and time. Water wave experiments were carried out that demonstrated the interaction phenomena by McGoldrick
[

2
The six-wave interaction system and reductions

We begin with the above mentioned six-wave interaction system in the generic form for a
3\times 3
off-diagonal matrix system whose components satisfy

\partial_{t}N_{\ell j}(x,y,t)-\alpha_{\ell j}\partial_{x}N_{\ell j}(x,y,t)-\beta_{\ell j}\partial_{y}N_{\ell j}(x,y,t)=(\alpha_{\ell m}-\alpha_{mj})N_{\ell m}(x,y,t)N_{mj}(x,y,t)\,,

(2.1)

where the indices
\ell,j,m\in 1,2,3
are distinct, i.e.
\ell\neq j,\;j\neq m,\;m\neq\ell
. Furthermore, the constant off-diagonal matrices
\alpha,\beta
are defined by:
\alpha_{23}=\alpha_{32}\equiv-C_{1}
,
\alpha_{31}=\alpha_{13}\equiv-C_{2}
,
\alpha_{12}=\alpha_{21}\equiv-C_{3}
;

\beta_{23}=\beta_{32}\equiv-C_{1}(C_{2}+C_{3})
,
\beta_{31}=\beta_{13}\equiv-C_{2}(C_{1}+C_{3})
,

\beta_{12}=\beta_{21}\equiv-C_{3}(C_{1}+C_{2}).
Here, we take

C_{1}<C_{2}<C_{3}
. With the rescaling

\displaystyle N_{23}=\frac{-iQ_{1}}{\sqrt{(C_{2}-C_{1})(C_{3}-C_{1})}}\,,\;N_{31}=\frac{-iQ_{2}}{\sqrt{(C_{2}-C_{1})(C_{3}-C_{2})}}\,,\;N_{12}=\frac{-iQ_{3}}{\sqrt{(C_{3}-C_{1})(C_{3}-C_{2})}}\,,

(2.2a)

\displaystyle N_{32}=\frac{-iR_{1}}{\sqrt{(C_{2}-C_{1})(C_{3}-C_{1})}}\,,\;N_{13}=\frac{iR_{2}}{\sqrt{(C_{2}-C_{1})(C_{3}-C_{2})}}\,,\;N_{21}=\frac{-iR_{3}}{\sqrt{(C_{3}-C_{1})(C_{3}-C_{2})}}\,,

(2.2b)

(note that the lack of a negative sign in the relationship between
N_{13}
and
R_{2}
is purposeful) the system (
4.1
) can be written as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{1}(x,y,t)+C^{(x)}_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}(x,y,t)+C^{(y)}_{1}\partial_{y}Q_{1}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-iR_{2}(x,y,t)R_{3}(x,y,t)\,,

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{2}(x,y,t)+C^{(x)}_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}(x,y,t)+C^{(y)}_{2}\partial_{y}Q_{2}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-iR_{3}(x,y,t)R_{1}(x,y,t)\,,

(2.3b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{3}(x,y,t)+C^{(x)}_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}(x,y,t)+C^{(y)}_{3}\partial_{y}Q_{3}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-iR_{1}(x,y,t)R_{2}(x,y,t)\,,

(2.3c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}R_{1}(x,y,t)+C^{(x)}_{1}\partial_{x}R_{1}(x,y,t)+C^{(y)}_{1}\partial_{y}R_{1}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle iQ_{2}(x,y,t)Q_{3}(x,y,t)\,,

(2.3d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}R_{2}(x,y,t)+C^{(x)}_{2}\partial_{x}R_{2}(x,y,t)+C^{(y)}_{2}\partial_{y}R_{2}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle iQ_{3}(x,y,t)Q_{1}(x,y,t)\,,

(2.3e)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}R_{3}(x,y,t)+C^{(x)}_{3}\partial_{x}R_{3}(x,y,t)+C^{(y)}_{3}\partial_{y}R_{3}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle iQ_{1}(x,y,t)Q_{2}(x,y,t)\,.

(2.3f)

Here,
C^{(x)}_{j}=C_{j}
and
C^{(y)}_{1}=C_{1}(C_{2}+C_{3}),\,C^{(y)}_{2}=C_{2}(C_{1}+C_{3}),\,C^{(y)}_{3}=C_{3}(C_{1}+C_{2})
; note also that
C^{(y)}_{1}<C^{(y)}_{2}<C^{(y)}_{3}
.
Note that the system (2.3) is PT symmetric, i.e., it is invariant under the transformation

\{Q_{j}(x,y,t),R_{j}(x,y,t)\}\longrightarrow\{Q^{*}_{j}(-x,-y,-t),R^{*}_{j}(-x,-y,-t)\}\;.

(2.4)

We remark that PT symmetric integrable systems were originally proposed in
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
Upon imposing the symmetry reduction

R_{j}(x,y,t)=-\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}Q_{j}^{*}(x,y,t)\,,\;\;\;\;\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}\tilde{\epsilon}_{3}=-1\,,

(2.5)

where
\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}=\pm 1,\,j=1,2,3,
the system (
2.3
) becomes the classical three-wave resonant interaction equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{1}+C^{(x)}_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}+C^{(y)}_{1}\partial_{y}Q_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}Q_{2}^{*}Q_{3}^{*}\,,

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{2}+C^{(x)}_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}+C^{(y)}_{2}\partial_{y}Q_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}Q_{3}^{*}Q_{1}^{*}\,,

(2.6b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{3}+C^{(x)}_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}+C^{(y)}_{3}\partial_{y}Q_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\tilde{\epsilon}_{3}Q_{1}^{*}Q_{2}^{*}\,.

(2.6c)

Alternatively, a reverse space-time nonlocal symmetry reduction was introduced in
[
27
]
. Presently, we generalize this reduction, as was done in
[
28
,
29
]
for NLS-type systems, to include nonlocal space-time shifts in addition to reflections. In particular, if we enforce

\displaystyle R_{j}(x,y,t)=(-1)^{j+1}\epsilon_{j}Q_{j}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,,\;\;\;\;\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{3}=+1\,,

(2.7)

then (
2.3
) reduces to the complex space-time shifted three-wave interaction system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{1}+C^{(x)}_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}+C^{(y)}_{1}\partial_{y}Q_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\epsilon_{1}Q_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)Q_{3}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,,

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{2}+C^{(x)}_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}+C^{(y)}_{2}\partial_{y}Q_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\epsilon_{2}Q_{3}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)Q_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,,

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q_{3}+C^{(x)}_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}+C^{(y)}_{3}\partial_{y}Q_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\epsilon_{3}Q_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)Q_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,.

(2.8c)

Note that this is not a translationally invariant system in space or time. However, the shifting parameters supply (
2.8
) with a “pseudo-translational symmetry” in the sense that translating a solution of (
2.8
) gives a solution of another system in the same family. Specifically, if (
2.8
) is solved by
Q_{j}(x,y,t)
,
j=1,2,3
, then

\tilde{Q}_{j}(x,y,t)\equiv Q_{j}\Big(x+\frac{1}{2}(x_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}),y+\frac{1}{2}(y_{0}-\tilde{y}_{0}),t+\frac{1}{2}(t_{0}-\tilde{t}_{0})\Big)\,,

(2.9)

solves the same system but with
x_{0},y_{0}
and
t_{0}
replaced by
\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{y}_{0}
and
\tilde{t}_{0}
, respectively.

3
Derivation of the six-wave system in nonlinear optics

Before discussing the integrability of (
2.8
), it is worth reiterating that the general six-wave system (
2.3
) has been shown to arise as a complex asymptotic reduction of dispersive models with quadratic nonlinearity. In
[
12
]
, the six-wave system was obtained from the classical water-wave equations with surface tension. Here, we demonstrate that this system can be derived in the context of optical media with quadratically nonlinear polarization.

3.1
Susceptibility model

We start by considering Maxwell’s equations that govern the dynamics of the electric and magnetic fields,

\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
, respectively

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}=0\,,\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0\,,\qquad\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}=\nabla\times\mathbf{H}\,,\qquad\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}=-\nabla\times\mathbf{E}\,.

(3.1)

In (
3.1
),
\mathbf{B}\equiv\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}
is the magnetic induction while
\mathbf{D}\equiv\varepsilon_{0}(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P(\mathbf{E})})
is the electric displacement with
\mathbf{P}
being the polarization vector (which depends on the electric field).
The constants
\mu_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
denote the magnetic permeability and the electric permittivity of free space respectively. After some algebra, Eqns. (
3.1
) give rise to the coupled system:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial^{2}_{t}\left(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{E})\right)+\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})-\nabla^{2}\mathbf{E}=0\;,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{E})\right)=0\;,

(3.3)

where
c^{2}=1/\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}
. Each vector field depends on the spatial variables
\mathbf{r}=(x,y,z)
and time
t
. Here,
\nabla^{2}
denotes the three-dimensional spatial Laplacian. The polarization field has two contributions: linear and nonlinear. Depending on the material response, the nonlinear part could depend quadratically or cubically on the electric field. Since in this paper we are interested in deriving six-wave interaction equations, we will assume that the medium response is quadratic (and ignore higher order contributions). With this in mind, the polarization vector is written in the form:

\mathbf{P}=\underbrace{\chi^{(L)}\star\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{P}^{(L)}}+\underbrace{\chi^{(NL)}\star\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{P}^{(NL)}}+\cdots\;,

(3.4)

where
\chi^{(L)}
and
\chi^{(NL)}
are rank 2 and 3 tensors that depend only on time (both are set to zero for
t<0
) and star denotes temporal convolution. Specifically, we have

P_{j}^{(L)}(\mathbf{r},t)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{jk}^{(L)}(t-t_{1})E_{k}(\mathbf{r},t_{1})dt_{1}\;,

(3.5)

P_{j}^{(NL)}(\mathbf{r},t)=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{3}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{jk\ell}^{(NL)}(t-t_{1},t-t_{2})E_{k}(\mathbf{r},t_{1})E_{\ell}(\mathbf{r},t_{2})dt_{1}dt_{2}\;,

(3.6)

where the indices
j=1,2,3
correspond to the
x,y,z
directions respectively. Consider the asymptotic expansion

\mathbf{E}=\epsilon\mathbf{E}^{(1)}+\epsilon^{2}\mathbf{E}^{(2)}+\cdots\;.

(3.7)

Substituting (
3.7
) into (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial^{2}_{t}\left(\mathbf{E}^{(1)}+\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})+\epsilon\mathbf{E}^{(2)}+\epsilon\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(2)})+\epsilon\mathbf{P}^{(NL)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})\right)

\displaystyle+\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}^{(1)})-\nabla^{2}\mathbf{E}^{(1)}+\epsilon\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}^{(2)})-\epsilon\nabla^{2}\mathbf{E}^{(2)}=O(\epsilon^{2})\;,

(3.8)

and

\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{E}^{(1)}+\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})+\epsilon\mathbf{E}^{(2)}+\epsilon\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(2)})+\epsilon\mathbf{P}^{(NL)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})\right)=O(\epsilon^{2})\;.

(3.9)

Next, introduce the slow time and space variables

T=\epsilon t\,,\;\;\;\ \mathbf{R}=\epsilon\mathbf{r}\,,\;\;\;\nabla\rightarrow\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}+\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\;,\;\;\;\;\;\partial_{t}\rightarrow\partial_{t}+\epsilon\partial_{T}\;.

(3.10)

Substituting (
3.10
) into (
3.1
) and (
3.9
) leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left(\partial^{2}_{t}+2\epsilon\partial_{tT}\right)\left(\mathbf{E}^{(1)}+\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})+\epsilon\mathbf{E}^{(2)}+\epsilon\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(2)})+\epsilon\mathbf{P}^{(NL)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})\right)

\displaystyle+\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}(\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{(1)})+\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{(1)})+\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}(\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{(1)})

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}^{2}\mathbf{E}^{(1)}-2\epsilon\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\right)\mathbf{E}^{(1)}+\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}(\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{(2)})-\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}^{2}\mathbf{E}^{(2)}=O(\epsilon^{3})\;,

(3.11)

and

\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}+\epsilon\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{E}^{(1)}+\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})+\epsilon\mathbf{E}^{(2)}+\epsilon\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(2)})+\epsilon\mathbf{P}^{(NL)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})\right)=O(\epsilon^{3})\;.

(3.12)

At this stage, we still need to determine if the polarization fields,
\mathbf{P}^{(L)}
and
\mathbf{P}^{(NL)}
have any “hidden” dependence
on
\epsilon
due to the action of the space-time multi-scales on their convolutional representations. Before resolving this issue, we first make the following assumptions: The susceptibility tensor
\chi_{jk}^{(L)}
vanishes whenever
j\neq k.
Furthermore, the only nonzero elements of the
\chi_{jk\ell}^{(NL)}

tensor arise from indices satisfying
j=k=\ell
and assume that the leading order electric field
is in the
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
direction and is
z
independent. Thus, we write

\mathbf{E}^{(1)}\left(\mathbf{r},t;\mathbf{R},T\right)=E^{(1)}_{z}\left(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t;\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T\right)\hat{\mathbf{z}}\;,

(3.13)

(recall:
E^{(1)}_{z}=E^{(1)}_{1}
) where
\mathbf{r}_{\perp}
is the two-dimensional transverse
(x,y)
plane and
\mathbf{R}_{\perp}\equiv\epsilon\mathbf{r}_{\perp}.
Note that subscripts appearing on the electric and polarization fields do
not
represent partial derivatives. We next assume that the leading order electric field takes the form

E^{(1)}_{z}\left(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t;\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\Big[A_{j}(\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T)e^{i\theta_{j}(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t)}+B_{j}(\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T)e^{-i\theta_{j}(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t)}\Big]\,,

(3.14)

where
\theta_{j}\equiv\mathbf{k}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\perp}-\omega_{j}t
and
\omega_{j}\equiv\omega(\mathbf{k}_{j}).
The wave vectors
\mathbf{k}_{j}

and the corresponding frequencies
\omega_{j}
are assumed to satisfy the resonant triad condition for all
\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t
:

\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}=0\,.

(3.15)

The dispersion relation
\omega(\mathbf{k})
will be later derived for a generic susceptibility tensor. A typical case will be given for which an explicit formula for the dispersion relation is readily available and can be used to establish a resonant triad. Note that in (
3.14
),
we do not assume
A_{j}
and
B_{j}
are complex conjugates of each other. This is a key assumption that enables us to derive the triad six-wave interaction equations. If instead, the relation
A_{j}=B^{*}_{j}
is imposed (at this stage)
this would lead to the three wave triad system.

Next, we examine the multi-scale structure of
\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)}).
From the assumption on the linear susceptibility
\chi_{jk}^{(L)}
, and from (
3.5
), we conclude that the leading order linear polarization field also
points in the
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
direction and is
z
independent. In this case, we have

\mathbf{P}^{(L)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})\left(\mathbf{r},t;\mathbf{R},T\right)=P^{(1)}_{L,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})\left(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t;\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T\right)\hat{\mathbf{z}}\;,

(3.16)

where,

P^{(1)}_{L,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})\left(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t;\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{zz}^{(L)}(t-t_{1})E^{(1)}_{z}\left(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t_{1};\mathbf{R}_{\perp},\epsilon t_{1}\right)dt_{1}\;.

(3.17)

It is customary in nonlinear optics to relabel
\chi_{33}^{(L)}
and
\chi_{333}^{(NL)}
as
\chi_{zz}^{(L)}
and
\chi_{zzz}^{(NL)}
(here, subscripts do not indicate partial derivatives). Substituting (
3.14
) into (
3.17
) leads to (for ease of presentation, we do not indicate the dependence of the amplitudes and phases on the spatial slow and fast scales)

\displaystyle P^{(1)}_{L,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{zz}^{(L)}(t-t_{1})\left(A_{j}(\epsilon t_{1})e^{i\theta_{j}(t_{1})}+B_{j}(\epsilon t_{1})e^{-i\theta_{j}(t_{1})}\right)dt_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\chi_{zz}^{(L)}(\tau)e^{i\omega_{j}\tau}A_{j}(\epsilon t-\epsilon\tau)e^{i\theta_{j}(t)}+\chi_{zz}^{(L)}(\tau)e^{-i\omega_{j}\tau}B_{j}(\epsilon t-\epsilon\tau)e^{-i\theta_{j}(t)}\right)d\tau\;.

Next, we Taylor expand the amplitudes around the point
\epsilon t.
We find

\displaystyle P^{(1)}_{L,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{zz}^{(L)}(\tau)e^{i\omega_{j}\tau}\left(A_{j}(T)-\epsilon\tau\partial_{T}A_{j}\right)e^{i\theta_{j}(t)}d\tau

(3.19)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{zz}^{(L)}(\tau)e^{-i\omega_{j}\tau}\left(B_{j}(T)-\epsilon\tau\partial_{T}B_{j}\right)e^{-i\theta_{j}(t)}d\tau+O(\epsilon^{2})\;.

Define the Fourier transform

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{zz}^{(L)}(\omega_{j})=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{zz}^{(L)}(\tau)e^{i\omega_{j}\tau}d\tau\;.

(3.20)

Using the Fourier representation of the susceptibility, Eq. (
3.19
) now reads

P^{(1)}_{L,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})=P^{(1,1)}_{L,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})+\epsilon P^{(1,2)}_{L,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})+O(\epsilon^{2})\;,

(3.21)

where

P^{(1,1)}_{L,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(\hat{\chi}_{zz}^{(L)}(\omega_{j})A_{j}(T)e^{i\theta_{j}(t)}+\hat{\chi}_{zz}^{(L)}(-\omega_{j})B_{j}(T)e^{-i\theta_{j}(t)}\right)\;,

(3.22)

P^{(1,2)}_{L,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(i\partial_{\omega}\hat{\chi}_{zz}^{(L)}(\omega_{j})\partial_{T}A_{j}(T)e^{i\theta_{j}(t)}+i\partial_{\omega}\hat{\chi}_{zz}^{(L)}(-\omega_{j})\partial_{T}B_{j}(T)e^{-i\theta_{j}(t)}\right)\;.

(3.23)

Next, we turn our attention to the nonlinear polarization vector field to find its leading order contribution which is all that is needed for this calculation.
From the constraints on the nonlinear susceptibility tensor, the only non-zero component of the leading order
vector
\mathbf{P}^{(NL)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})
is given by

\mathbf{P}^{(NL)}(\mathbf{E}^{(1)})\left(\mathbf{r},t;\mathbf{R},T\right)=P^{(1)}_{NL,z}(E_{z}^{(1)})\left(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t;\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T\right)\hat{\mathbf{z}}\;,

(3.24)

where,

\displaystyle\left(A_{m}(\epsilon t_{2})e^{i\theta_{m}(t_{2})}+B_{m}(\epsilon t_{2})e^{-i\theta_{m}(t_{2})}\right)dt_{1}dt_{2}\;.

\tau_{1}=t-t_{1}

Note that

From (

\displaystyle P^{(1)}_{NL,res}(E_{z}^{(1)})

(3.29)

3.2
Laser model

In this section, we provide an alternative derivation of the six-wave interaction equations starting from a model commonly used in laser optics (see
[
20
]
and related references therein). The starting point is the coupled system (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) that govern
the dynamics of the electric field. Unlike the case discussed in Sec. (3.1) where the polarization vector is related to the electric field via a convolution integral; here the polarization field is determined from a dynamical model used in lasers. Specifically, we will assume that the polarization component

P_{j}
obeys the equation

\partial_{t}^{2}P_{j}+\omega_{0}^{2}P_{j}+\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{3}d_{jk\ell}P_{k}P_{\ell}-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\mu_{0}c^{2}}E_{j}=0\,,\;\;\;j=1,2,3\,,

(3.43)

where
d_{jk\ell}=d_{j\ell k}
,
\omega_{0}
, and
\Omega
are inherent to the given medium. We shall follow similar assumptions made in Sec. (3.1) regarding the electric and polarization fields. That is to say, we let both
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{P}
be
z
independent and have their third component be the only nonzero element. With the notation
\mathbf{E}=E\hat{\mathbf{z}}
and
\mathbf{P}=P\hat{\mathbf{z}}
we arrive at the quadratically nonlinear coupled system:

\partial_{t}^{2}E-c^{2}\nabla^{2}E+\mu_{0}c^{2}\partial_{t}^{2}P=0\,,

(3.44)

\partial_{t}^{2}P+\omega_{0}^{2}P+d_{zzz}P^{2}-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\mu_{0}c^{2}}E=0\,,

(3.45)

where the notation (from optics)
d_{333}\equiv d_{zzz}
has been adopted. Next, introduce the slow time and space variables (
3.10
) and the perturbation expansions

E=\epsilon E_{1}+\epsilon^{2}E_{2}+\cdots\,,\;\;\;P=\epsilon P_{1}+\epsilon^{2}P_{2}+\cdots\,.

(3.46)

Substituting (
3.46
) into (
3.44
) and (
3.45
), we arrive at the leading order equation

\mathbb{L}\begin{bmatrix}E_{1}\\
\\
P_{1}\end{bmatrix}=0\;,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\mathbb{L}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\partial^{2}_{t}-c^{2}\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{r}}&&\mu_{0}c^{2}\partial^{2}_{t}\\
\\
-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\mu_{0}c^{2}}&&\partial^{2}_{t}+\omega_{0}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\;.

(3.47)

To determine the dispersion relation, we seek a plane wave solution in the form

E_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{1}e^{i\theta}\,,\;\;\;P_{1}=\mathcal{P}_{1}e^{i\theta}\,,\;\;\;\theta=\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t\,.

(3.48)

Then we find

c^{2}|\mathbf{k}|^{2}=\omega^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}\Omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}}\,,\;\;\;\;\;\mathbf{V}_{g}=\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}\omega=\frac{c^{2}\mathbf{k}}{\omega\left(1+\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}\Omega^{2}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}}\right)}\,.

(3.49)

The corresponding eigenvector is given by:

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{E}_{1}\\
\\
\mathcal{P}_{1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\\
\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\mu_{0}c^{2}(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\end{bmatrix}\;.

(3.50)

The following adjoint problem will be later used to determine the six-wave interaction equations:

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-\omega^{2}+c^{2}|\mathbf{k}|^{2}&-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\mu_{0}c^{2}}\\
\\
-\mu_{0}c^{2}\omega^{2}&-\omega^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbb{L}^{A}}\mathbf{w}^{A}=0\;,\;\;\;\;\;\mathbf{w}^{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\\
\frac{\mu_{0}c^{2}\omega^{2}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\end{bmatrix}\;,

(3.51)

where
\mathbf{w}^{A}
denotes the adjoint eigenvector.
The equation at order
\epsilon
reads

\mathbb{L}\begin{bmatrix}E_{2}\\
\\
P_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\mathcal{F}\;,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\mathcal{F}=\begin{bmatrix}-2\partial_{tT}E_{1}-2\mu_{0}c^{2}\partial_{tT}P_{1}+2c^{2}\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\right)E_{1}\\
\\
-2\partial_{tT}P_{1}-P_{1}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\;.

(3.52)

We now make a six-wave ansatz in the form given by (
3.14
) for the electric field and

P_{1}\left(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t;\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T\right)=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\mu_{0}c^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})}\Big[A_{j}(\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T)e^{i\theta_{j}(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t)}+B_{j}(\mathbf{R}_{\perp},T)e^{-i\theta_{j}(\mathbf{r}_{\perp},t)}\Big]\,.

(3.53)

Taking the square leads to

\displaystyle P^{2}_{1}=\frac{2\Omega^{4}}{\mu^{2}_{0}c^{4}}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{j}e^{i\theta_{j}}+\mathbb{P}^{(2)}_{j}e^{-i\theta_{j}}\right)+\text{non resonant terms}\,,

(3.54)

where
\mathbb{P}^{(k)}_{j},\;j=1,2,3,\;k=1,2
depends on the frequencies and the amplitudes
A_{j},B_{j}
given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{1}=\frac{B_{2}B_{3}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2})(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{3}^{2})}\;,\;\;\;\;\;\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{2}=\frac{B_{1}B_{3}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{1}^{2})(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{3}^{2})}\;,\;\;\;\;\;\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{3}=\frac{B_{1}B_{2}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{1}^{2})(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2})}\;,

(3.55)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(2)}_{1}=\frac{A_{2}A_{3}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2})(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{3}^{2})}\;,\;\;\;\;\;\mathbb{P}^{(2)}_{2}=\frac{A_{1}A_{3}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{1}^{2})(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{3}^{2})}\;,\;\;\;\;\;\mathbb{P}^{(2)}_{3}=\frac{A_{1}A_{2}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{1}^{2})(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{2}^{2})}\;.

(3.56)

We now decompose the vector
\mathcal{F}
as

\mathcal{F}=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{j}e^{i\theta_{j}}+\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{j}e^{-i\theta_{j}}\right)\;,

(3.57)

where

\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}2i\omega_{j}\partial_{T}A_{j}+\frac{2i\omega_{j}\Omega^{2}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})}\partial_{T}A_{j}+2ic^{2}\left(\mathbf{k}_{j}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\right)A_{j}\\
\\
\frac{2i\omega_{j}\Omega^{2}}{\mu_{0}c^{2}(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})}\partial_{T}A_{j}-\frac{2\Omega^{4}}{\mu^{2}_{0}c^{4}}\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{j}\end{bmatrix}\;,

(3.58)

and

\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}-2i\omega_{j}\partial_{T}B_{j}-\frac{2i\omega_{j}\Omega^{2}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})}\partial_{T}B_{j}-2ic^{2}\left(\mathbf{k}_{j}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\right)B_{j}\\
\\
-\frac{2i\omega_{j}\Omega^{2}}{\mu_{0}c^{2}(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})}\partial_{T}B_{j}-\frac{2\Omega^{4}}{\mu^{2}_{0}c^{4}}\mathbb{P}^{(2)}_{j}\end{bmatrix}\;.

(3.59)

From the Fredholm alternative, the solvability condition associated with (
3.52
) is expressed as

\langle\mathbf{w}^{A},\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}_{j}\rangle=0\;,\;\;\;\ell=1,2,\;\;\;j=1,2,3\;,

(3.60)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes vector dot product. As we shall see later, (
3.60
) will lead to the six-wave interaction equations. Taking the vector inner product, we arrive at

\partial_{T}A_{j}+\left(\mathbf{V}_{g,j}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\right)A_{j}=-\frac{i\Omega^{4}\omega_{j}(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})}{\mu_{0}c^{2}\left((\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})^{2}+\Omega^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}\right)}\mathbb{P}^{(1)}_{j}\;,

(3.61)

\partial_{T}B_{j}+\left(\mathbf{V}_{g,j}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\right)B_{j}=\frac{i\Omega^{4}\omega_{j}(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})}{\mu_{0}c^{2}\left((\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})^{2}+\Omega^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}\right)}\mathbb{P}^{(2)}_{j}\;,

(3.62)

where the group velocity is given by

\mathbf{V}_{g,j}=\frac{c^{2}\mathbf{k}_{j}}{\omega_{j}\left(1+\frac{\Omega^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{j}^{2})^{2})}\right)}\;,\;\;\;j=1,2,3\;.

(3.63)

This group velocity coincides with the expression given by (
3.41
) when
\hat{\chi}^{(L)}_{zz}(\omega_{j})
is given by (
3.36
).

4
Inverse scattering transform for the 1+1D space-time shifted three-wave system

In what follows we discuss the six-wave system in the form (
2.1
)
in
(1+1)
dimensions with no
y
dependence, i.e.

\partial_{t}N_{\ell j}(x,t)-\alpha_{\ell j}\partial_{x}N_{\ell j}(x,t)=(\alpha_{\ell m}-\alpha_{mj})N_{\ell m}(x,t)N_{mj}(x,t)\,,

(4.1)

which is an integrable system that arises as a compatibility condition
between the
3\times 3
scattering problem

\partial_{x}v(x,t,k)=[ikD+N(x,t)]v(x,t,k)\,,

(4.2)

where

D=\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}&0&0\\
0&d_{2}&0\\
0&0&d_{3}\end{bmatrix},\;\;\;N(x,t)=\begin{bmatrix}0&N_{12}(x,t)&N_{13}(x,t)\\
N_{21}(x,t)&0&N_{13}(x,t)\\
N_{31}(x,t)&N_{32}(x,t)&0\end{bmatrix}\,,

(4.3)

with
d_{j}=-C_{j}
(note that
d_{1}>d_{2}>d_{3}
) and the time evolution equation

\partial_{t}v(x,t,k)=\mathcal{T}v(x,t,k)\,,\;\;\;\mathcal{T}=\begin{bmatrix}-id_{2}d_{3}k&d_{3}N_{12}(x,t)&d_{2}N_{13}(x,t)\\
d_{3}N_{21}(x,t)&-id_{1}d_{3}k&d_{1}N_{23}(x,t)\\
d_{2}N_{31}(x,t)&d_{1}N_{32}(x,t)&-id_{1}d_{2}k\\
\end{bmatrix}\,.

(4.4)

By exploiting its connection to these two linear problems, the above six-wave system is solvable by the inverse scattering transform (IST).
As mentioned earlier the IST for the classical three-wave system is well known. Space-time nonlocal variants of the three-wave system were considered by Gerdjikov et al in 2016
[
34
]
; associated soliton solutions were obtained by dressing methods. Soliton solutions were also obtained using Darboux transformations in
[
35
]
.
The detailed IST and associated bound states and soliton solutions necessary to solve the reverse space-time nonlocal three-wave system in comparison with the classical case were formulated in
[
27
]
, whose notations and procedure we will follow in the present work.

Here, we consider the nonlocal space-time shifted case (
2.8
) which can be obtained by applying the symmetry reduction

N(x,t)=\Lambda N^{\dagger}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\Lambda\,,\;\;\;\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon_{1}&0&0\\
0&\epsilon_{2}&0\\
0&0&\epsilon_{3}\end{bmatrix}\,,\;\;\;\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{3}=+1\,,

(4.5)

(where
\dagger
is the conjugate-transpose) to the matrix
N(x,t)
followed by the rescaling in (
2.2a
). Note that applying (
4.5
) followed by (
2.2
) is equivalent to applying (
2.2
) followed by (
2.7
). Throughout this section, we give a brief overview of this solution process and give the relevant adaptations (particularly in the symmetries of the scattering data) to the case of the space-time shifted system. For a more detailed treatment, we refer the reader to
[
27
]
.

4.1
Direct scattering problem

We assume that the potential matrix
N(x,t)
decays to zero sufficiently rapidly as
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
, and define eigenfunctions of (
4.2
) according to the following boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\phi_{j}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle e^{ikd_{j}x}\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{j}\,,\;\;\;\text{as}\;x\rightarrow-\infty\,,

(4.6a)

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle e^{ikd_{j}x}\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{j}\,,\;\;\;\text{as}\;x\rightarrow+\infty\,,\;\;\;j=1,2,3\,.

(4.6b)

Here and throughout the rest of the paper,
\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{1}=(1,0,0)^{T}
,
\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{2}=(0,1,0)^{T}
, and
\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{3}=(0,0,1)^{T}
. The two sets of eigenfunctions
\phi_{j}(x,t,k)
and
\psi_{j}(x,t,k)
each form a linearly independent set of solutions of the
3\times 3
system (
4.2
). As such, they can be expressed in terms of each other as follows:

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{11}(t,k)\psi_{1}(x,t,k)+a_{12}(t,k)\psi_{2}(x,t,k)+a_{13}(t,k)\psi_{3}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.7a)

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{21}(t,k)\psi_{1}(x,t,k)+a_{22}(t,k)\psi_{2}(x,t,k)+a_{23}(t,k)\psi_{3}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.7b)

\displaystyle\phi_{3}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{31}(t,k)\psi_{1}(x,t,k)+a_{32}(t,k)\psi_{2}(x,t,k)+a_{33}(t,k)\psi_{3}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.7c)

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{11}(t,k)\phi_{1}(x,t,k)+b_{12}(t,k)\phi_{2}(x,t,k)+b_{13}(t,k)\phi_{3}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.7d)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{21}(t,k)\phi_{1}(x,t,k)+b_{22}(t,k)\phi_{2}(x,t,k)+b_{23}(t,k)\phi_{3}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.7e)

\displaystyle\psi_{3}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{31}(t,k)\phi_{1}(x,t,k)+b_{32}(t,k)\phi_{2}(x,t,k)+b_{33}(t,k)\phi_{3}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.7f)

where
a_{\ell j}(t,k)
and
b_{\ell j}(t,k),\;\ell,j=1,2,3
will be referred to as the scattering coefficients. The diagonal entries in
A=[a_{\ell j}]
and
B=[b_{\ell j}]
are the (inverse) transmission coefficients, and the off-diagonal entries determine the reflection coefficients

\rho^{(a)}_{\ell j}(t,k)=\frac{a_{\ell j}(t,k)}{a_{\ell\ell}(t,k)},\;\;\;\rho^{(b)}_{\ell j}(t,k)=\frac{b_{\ell j}(t,k)}{b_{\ell\ell}(t,k)}\,,\;\;\;\ell\neq j\,.

(4.8)

It will also be useful to define the following Jost functions which satisfy constant boundary conditions:

M_{j}(x,t,k)=\phi_{j}(x,t,k)e^{-ikd_{j}x}\,,\;\;\;N_{j}(x,t,k)=\psi_{j}(x,t,k)e^{-ikd_{j}x}\;.

(4.9)

Indeed, it can be proven that
M_{3}(x,t,k)
and
N_{1}(x,t,k)
are analytic functions of the complex parameter
k
in the upper-half plane, and as a consequence so are
a_{33}(t,k)
and
b_{11}(t,k)
while
M_{1}(x,t,k)
and
N_{3}(x,t,k)
, along with
a_{11}(t,k)
and
b_{33}(t,k),
are analytic functions of
k
in the lower-half plane. However, no such analyticity can be established for
M_{2}(x,t,k)
or
N_{2}(x,t,k)
. To replace them with analytic eigenfunctions, a standard procedure is to define the adjoint (auxiliary) scattering problem

\partial_{x}v^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)=-[ikD+N^{T}(x,t)]v^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.10)

whose solutions are related to solutions of the original scattering problem via
\partial_{x}(v^{T}v^{\text{ad}})=0
. In a similar fashion, we define the adjoint eigenfunctions

\displaystyle\phi^{\text{ad}}_{j}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}e^{-ikd_{j}x}\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{j}\,,\;\;\;\text{as}\;x\rightarrow-\infty\,,

(4.11a)

\displaystyle\psi^{\text{ad}}_{j}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}e^{-ikd_{j}x}\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{j}\,,\;\;\;\text{as}\;x\rightarrow+\infty\,,

(4.11b)

and the corresponding adjoint Jost functions

M^{\text{ad}}_{j}(x,t,k)=\phi^{\text{ad}}_{j}(x,t,k)e^{ikd_{j}x}\,,\;\;\;N^{\text{ad}}_{j}(x,t,k)=\psi^{\text{ad}}_{j}(x,t,k)e^{ikd_{j}x}\;.

(4.12)

It can again be proven that
M^{\text{ad}}_{3}(x,t,k)
and
N^{\text{ad}}_{1}(x,t,k)
are analytic in the upper-half
k
-plane, while
M^{\text{ad}}_{1}(x,t,k)
and
N^{\text{ad}}_{3}(x,t,k)
are analytic in the lower-half
k
-plane. Now, if
u^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)
and
w^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)
are two arbitrary solutions of (
4.10
) then

v(x,t,k)=e^{ikdx}\big(u^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)\times w^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)\big)\,,

(4.13)

where
\times
denotes the vector cross product and
d=d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}
, will be a solution of (
4.2
). So, we define the following two additional eigenfunctions of (
4.2
),

\displaystyle\tau(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{ikdx}\big(\phi_{1}^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)\times\psi_{3}^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)\big)\,,

(4.14a)

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{ikdx}\big(\phi_{3}^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)\times\psi_{1}^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)\big)\,,

(4.14b)

and the corresponding Jost functions

\chi(x,t,k)=\tau(x,t,k)e^{-ikd_{2}x}\,,\;\;\;\bar{\chi}(x,t,k)=\bar{\tau}(x,t,k)e^{-ikd_{2}x}\,,

(4.15)

which are analytic functions of
k
in the upper- and lower-half planes, respectively. Furthermore, by using (
4.13
) and comparing boundary conditions, one can establish the following useful representations for
\tau(x,t,k)
and
\bar{\tau}(x,t,k)
:

\displaystyle\tau(x,t,k)=b_{21}(t,k)\psi_{1}(x,t,k)-b_{11}(t,k)\psi_{2}(x,t,k)=a_{23}(t,k)\phi_{3}(x,t,k)-a_{33}(t,k)\phi_{2}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.16a)

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}(x,t,k)=b_{33}(t,k)\psi_{2}(x,t,k)-b_{23}(k)\psi_{3}(x,t,k)=a_{11}(t,k)\phi_{2}(x,t,k)-a_{21}(t,k)\phi_{1}(x,t,k)\,.

(4.16b)

Constructing the additional analytic structure of the scattering eigenfunctions from adjoint eigenfunctions is a convenient technique for determining fundamental analytic solutions of the
3\times 3
scattering problem (
4.2
), see e.g.
[
15
,
33
]
. Alternative methods for constructing fundamental analytic solutions have been studied (see for instance
[
36
,
37
,
39
]
); they have
an advantage of constructing fundamental analytic solutions for suitable
n\times n
Lax operators. However, the underlying discrete scattering data are more difficult to characterize. For the present 3×3 case, the use of the adjoint problem suffices.

4.2
Symmetries of the scattering data

It can be shown that the two scattering problems (
4.2
) and (
4.10
) with the reduction (
4.5
) admit the following symmetry:

•

If
v(x,t,k)
is a solution of the scattering problem (
4.2
) with symmetry reduction (
4.5
), then
\Lambda v^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})
is a solution of the adjoint scattering problem (
4.10
).

From this fact, we can derive space-time shifted symmetries connecting the eigenfunctions of both scattering problems. For instance,
\psi_{2}(x,t,k)
and
\psi_{3}(x,t,k)
are solutions of the direct problem, so
e^{-ikdx}(\psi_{2}(x,t,k)\times\psi_{3}(x,t,k))
must be a solution of the adjoint problem, and it satisfies the same boundary condition as
\psi_{1}^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)
, but without the factor of
\epsilon_{1}
. So, we have

\epsilon_{1}\psi_{1}^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)=e^{-ikdx}\big(\psi_{2}(x,t,k)\times\psi_{3}(x,t,k)\big)\,.

(4.17)

Furthermore, taking into account the above symmetry then,
e^{ikdx}(\Lambda\psi_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})\times\Lambda\psi_{3}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*}))
is a solution of the direct scattering problem. Then, comparing boundary conditions gives

e^{ik(d_{2}+d_{3})x_{0}}\phi_{1}(x,t,k)=e^{ikdx}\big(\psi_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})\times\psi_{3}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})\big)\,.

(4.18)

Conjugating and letting
x\rightarrow x_{0}-x
,
t\rightarrow t_{0}-t
,
k\rightarrow-k^{*}
gives

e^{ik(d_{2}+d_{3})x_{0}}\phi_{1}^{*}\big(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})=e^{ikd(x_{0}-x)}(\psi_{2}(x,t,k)\times\psi_{3}(x,t,k)\big)\,.

(4.19)

Comparing with (
4.17
) shows that

\psi_{1}^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)=\epsilon_{1}e^{-ikd_{1}x_{0}}\phi_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})\,.

(4.20)

Following similar arguments, for
j=1,2,3
, one can get

\displaystyle\psi_{j}^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}e^{-ikd_{j}x_{0}}\phi_{j}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})\,,

(4.21a)

\displaystyle\phi_{j}^{\text{ad}}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}e^{-ikd_{j}x_{0}}\psi_{j}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})\,.

(4.21b)

In turn, corresponding symmetries of the scattering coefficients can be obtained. For example, we have

\displaystyle a_{\ell 1}(t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\det\big[\phi_{\ell}(x,t,k),\psi_{2}(x,t,k),\psi_{3}(x,t,k)\big]}{\det\big[\psi_{1}(x,t,k),\psi_{2}(x,t,k),\psi_{3}(x,t,k)\big]}\,,

(4.22a)

\displaystyle a_{1\ell}(t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\det\big[\psi^{\text{ad}}_{\ell}(x,t,k),\phi^{\text{ad}}_{2}(x,t,k),\phi^{\text{ad}}_{3}(x,t,k)\big]}{\det\big[\phi^{\text{ad}}_{1}(x,t,k),\phi^{\text{ad}}_{2}(x,t,k),\phi^{\text{ad}}_{3}(x,t,k)\big]}\,.

(4.22b)

Substituting the symmetries (
4.21
) into (
4.22b
) and comparing with (
4.22a
) gives

\displaystyle a_{\ell 1}(t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\ell}\epsilon_{1}e^{ik(d_{\ell}-d_{1})x_{0}}a^{*}_{1\ell}(t_{0}-t,-k^{*})\,.

(4.23)

By similar logic, for
\ell,j=1,2,3
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle a_{\ell j}(t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\ell}\epsilon_{j}e^{ik(d_{\ell}-d_{j})x_{0}}a^{*}_{j\ell}(t_{0}-t,-k^{*})\,,

(4.24a)

\displaystyle b_{\ell j}(t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\ell}\epsilon_{j}e^{ik(d_{\ell}-d_{j})x_{0}}b^{*}_{j\ell}(t_{0}-t,-k^{*})\,.

(4.24b)

4.3
Time evolution and inverse scattering

The evolution equation (
4.4
) (in the limits
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
) can be used to determine the time dependence of the scattering coefficients. Since the situation is identical to
[
27
]
, we simply state the results:

\displaystyle a_{\ell\ell}(t,k)=a_{\ell\ell}(0,k)\,,\;\;\;

\displaystyle b_{\ell\ell}(t,k)=b_{\ell\ell}(0,k)\,,

(4.25a)

\displaystyle a_{\ell j}(t,k)=a_{\ell j}(0,k)e^{-i(d_{\ell}-d_{j})d_{m}kt}\,,\;\;\;

\displaystyle b_{\ell j}(t,k)=b_{\ell j}(0,k)e^{-i(d_{\ell}-d_{j})d_{m}kt}\,,

(4.25b)

where in (
4.25b
),
\ell\neq j,\;j\neq m,\;m\neq\ell
,
j,k,\ell=1,2,3
.
The inverse scattering problem associated with (
4.2
) can be formulated as a Riemann-Hilbert problem in the upper/lower half
k
-plane with a jump across the real
k
axis. For ease of presentation we will consider only the reflectionless case, where
\rho_{\ell j}^{(a)}=\rho_{\ell j}^{(b)}\equiv 0
. The discrete eigenvalues are the zeros of the diagonal scattering coefficients
a_{11}(k)
,
a_{33}(k)
,
b_{11}(k)
, and
b_{33}(k)
, which are independent of time according to (
4.25a
). We assume that
a_{33}(k)
and
b_{11}(k)
have simple zeros
\alpha_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,J
and
\beta_{n}
,
n=1,\ldots,N
, respectively, in the upper-half plane; and that
b_{33}(k)
and
a_{11}(k)
have simple zeros
\bar{\alpha}_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,\bar{J}
and
\bar{\beta}_{n}
,
n=1,\ldots,\bar{N}
, respectively, in the lower-half plane. Furthermore, for simplicity, we take all discrete eigenvalues to lie on the imaginary
k
axis. Note that in general, due to the symmetry (
4.24
), if
\alpha_{j}
is a discrete eigenvalue, then so is
-\alpha_{j}^{*}
. In this simplified case, the solution of the Riemann-Hilbert problem reduces to the following linear system (again, we refer the reader to
[
27
]
for details),

\displaystyle N_{1}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{1}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\bar{N}}\frac{\bar{\chi}(x,t,\bar{\beta}_{\ell})e^{i\bar{\beta}_{\ell}(d_{2}-d_{1})x}}{a_{21}(t,\bar{\beta}_{\ell})(k-\bar{\beta}_{\ell})\partial_{k}a_{11}(\bar{\beta}_{\ell})}\,,

(4.26a)

\displaystyle N_{3}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{3}+\sum_{m=1}^{J}\frac{\chi(x,t,\alpha_{m})e^{i\alpha_{m}(d_{2}-d_{3})x}}{a_{23}(t,\alpha_{m})(k-\alpha_{m})\partial_{k}a_{33}(\alpha_{m})}\,,

(4.26b)

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\chi}(x,t,k)}{b_{33}(t,k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{2}-\sum_{p=1}^{N}\frac{b_{21}(t,\beta_{p})N_{1}(x,t,\beta_{p})e^{i\beta_{p}(d_{1}-d_{2})x}}{(k-\beta_{p})\partial_{k}b_{11}(\beta_{p})}

(4.26c)

\displaystyle-\,\sum_{q=1}^{\bar{J}}\frac{b_{23}(t,\bar{\alpha}_{q})N_{3}(x,t,\bar{\alpha}_{q})e^{i\bar{\alpha}_{q}(d_{3}-d_{2})x}}{(k-\bar{\alpha}_{q})\partial_{k}b_{33}(\bar{\alpha}_{q})}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\chi(x,t,k)}{b_{11}(t,k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{2}+\sum_{p=1}^{N}\frac{b_{21}(t,\beta_{p})N_{1}(x,t,\beta_{p})e^{i\beta_{p}(d_{1}-d_{2})x}}{(k-\beta_{p})\partial_{k}b_{11}(\beta_{p})}

(4.26d)

\displaystyle+\,\sum_{q=1}^{\bar{J}}\frac{b_{23}(t,\bar{\alpha}_{q})N_{3}(x,t,\bar{\alpha}_{q})e^{i\bar{\alpha}_{q}(d_{3}-d_{2})x}}{(k-\bar{\alpha}_{q})\partial_{k}b_{33}(\bar{\alpha}_{q})}\,,

where
a_{21}(t,\bar{\beta}_{\ell})
,
a_{23}(t,\alpha_{m})
,
b_{21}(t,\beta_{p})
, and
b_{23}(t,\bar{\alpha}_{q})
are so-called reduced normalization coefficients. Note that the off-diagonal scattering coefficients do not generically admit analytic continuation off the real
k
-axis, and the way we denote the reduced normalization coefficients is only for notational convenience.

The Jost functions can be found from the above after solving the linear system with
J+\bar{J}+N+\bar{N}
equations and unknowns obtained by evaluating (
4.26
) at the appropriate discrete eigenvalues. Finally, once the system above has been solved, the desired potentials
N_{12}(x,t)
,
N_{23}(x,t)
, and
N_{31}(x,t)
which determine the solution of the space-time shifted three-wave system can be recovered by studying the asymptotic behavior of the Jost functions and comparing with (
4.26
). Indeed, it can be proven that

\displaystyle N_{21}(x,t)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle ik(d_{1}-d_{2})N_{1}^{(2)}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.27a)

\displaystyle N_{23}(x,t)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-ik(d_{2}-d_{3})N_{3}^{(2)}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.27b)

\displaystyle N_{31}(x,t)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle ik(d_{1}-d_{3})N_{1}^{(3)}(x,t,k)\,,

(4.27c)

as
|k|\rightarrow\infty
, where the superscripts on the right-hand side denote the vector component.

5
Soliton solutions

The 1-soliton solution can be obtained by setting
J=\bar{J}=N=\bar{N}=1
. In this case, (
4.26
) reduces to:

\displaystyle N_{1}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{1}-\frac{\bar{\chi}(x,t,\bar{\beta}_{1})e^{i\bar{\beta}_{1}(d_{2}-d_{1})x}}{a_{21}(t,\bar{\beta}_{1})(k-\bar{\beta}_{1})\partial_{k}a_{11}(\bar{\beta}_{1})}\,,

(5.1a)

\displaystyle N_{3}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{3}+\frac{\chi(x,t,\alpha_{1})e^{i\alpha_{1}(d_{2}-d_{3})x}}{a_{23}(t,\alpha_{1})(k-\alpha_{1})\partial_{k}a_{33}(\alpha_{1})}\,,

(5.1b)

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\chi}(x,t,k)}{b_{33}(t,k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{2}-\frac{b_{21}(t,\beta_{1})N_{1}(x,t,\beta_{1})e^{i\beta_{1}(d_{1}-d_{2})x}}{(k-\beta_{1})\partial_{k}b_{11}(\beta_{1})}-\frac{b_{23}(t,\bar{\alpha}_{1})N_{3}(x,t,\bar{\alpha}_{1})e^{i\bar{\alpha}_{1}(d_{3}-d_{2})x}}{(k-\bar{\alpha}_{1})\partial_{k}b_{33}(\bar{\alpha}_{1})}\,,

(5.1c)

\displaystyle\frac{\chi(x,t,k)}{b_{11}(t,k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{2}\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{2}+\frac{b_{21}(t,\beta_{1})N_{1}(x,t,\beta_{1})e^{i\beta_{1}(d_{1}-d_{2})x}}{(k-\beta_{1})\partial_{k}b_{11}(\beta_{1})}+\frac{b_{23}(t,\bar{\alpha}_{1})N_{3}(x,t,\bar{\alpha}_{1})e^{i\bar{\alpha}_{1}(d_{3}-d_{2})x}}{(k-\bar{\alpha}_{1})\partial_{k}b_{33}(\bar{\alpha}_{1})}\,.

(5.1d)

It is straightforward to solve this system generically, but to specialize the result to the space-time shifted case, we need to reduce the degrees of freedom by expressing the scattering coefficients in terms of the discrete eigenvalues. First, we can express the diagonal scattering coefficients (and the necessary derivatives with respect to
k
) in terms of the eigenvalues by using established (see
[
27
]
) trace formulae:

\displaystyle\displaystyle a_{33}(k)=\frac{k-\alpha_{1}}{k-\bar{\alpha}_{1}}\,,\;\;\;

\displaystyle b_{33}(k)=\frac{k-\bar{\alpha}_{1}}{k-\alpha_{1}}\,,

(5.2a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle b_{11}(k)=\frac{k-\beta_{1}}{k-\bar{\beta}_{1}}\,,\;\;\;

\displaystyle a_{11}(k)=\frac{k-\bar{\beta}_{1}}{k-\beta_{1}}\,.

(5.2b)

To find expressions for the reduced normalization coefficients, we need to employ additional symmetries. From (
4.16
) we have

\displaystyle\tau(x,t,\alpha_{1})=a_{23}(t,\alpha_{1})\phi_{3}(x,t,\alpha_{1})\,,\;\;\;

\displaystyle\tau(x,t,\beta_{1})=b_{21}(t,\beta_{1})\psi_{1}(x,t,\beta_{1})\,,

(5.3a)

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}(x,t,\bar{\alpha}_{1})=-b_{23}(t,\bar{\alpha}_{1})\psi_{3}(x,t,\bar{\alpha}_{1})\,,\;\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}(x,t,\bar{\beta}_{1})=-a_{21}(t,\bar{\beta}_{1})\phi_{1}(x,t,\bar{\beta}_{1})\,.

(5.3b)

By applying the symmetries of the eigenfunctions (
4.21
), it can be deduced that if the eigenvalues are purely imaginary, then we have

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon_{3}b^{*}_{21}(t,\beta_{1})b_{21}(t_{0}-t,\beta_{1})}{a_{33}(\beta_{1})}e^{i\beta_{1}(d_{1}-d_{2})x_{0}}=1\,,

(5.4a)

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon_{1}b^{*}_{23}(t,\bar{\alpha}_{1})b_{23}(t_{0}-t,\bar{\alpha}_{1})}{a_{11}(\bar{\alpha}_{1})}e^{i\bar{\alpha}_{1}(d_{3}-d_{2})x_{0}}=1\,,

(5.4b)

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon_{3}a^{*}_{21}(t,\bar{\beta}_{1})a_{21}(t_{0}-t,\bar{\beta}_{1})}{b_{33}(\bar{\beta}_{1})}e^{i\bar{\beta}_{1}(d_{1}-d_{2})x_{0}}=1\,,

(5.4c)

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon_{1}a^{*}_{23}(t,\alpha_{1})a_{23}(t_{0}-t,\alpha_{1})}{b_{11}(\alpha_{1})}e^{i\alpha_{1}(d_{3}-d_{2})x_{0}}=1\,.

(5.4d)

If we let
\alpha_{1}=iv_{1}
,
\beta_{1}=iv_{2}
,
\bar{\alpha}_{1}=-i\bar{v}_{1}
,
\bar{\beta}_{1}=-i\bar{v}_{2}
with
v_{1},v_{2},\bar{v}_{1},\bar{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, recall that we defined

for
j=1,2,3
, and incorporate the trace formulae (
5.2
) and the time evolution (
4.25b
), then from (
5.4
) we get

\displaystyle|b_{21}(0,iv_{2})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{3}\frac{v_{2}-v_{1}}{\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2}}e^{v_{2}(C_{2}-C_{1})(x_{0}-C_{3}t_{0})}\,,

(5.5a)

\displaystyle|b_{23}(0,-i\bar{v}_{1})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}\frac{\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1}}{\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2}}e^{-\bar{v}_{1}(C_{2}-C_{3})(x_{0}-C_{1}t_{0})}\,,

(5.5b)

\displaystyle|a_{21}(0,-i\bar{v}_{2})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{3}\frac{\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1}}{v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2}}e^{-\bar{v}_{2}(C_{2}-C_{1})(x_{0}-C_{3}t_{0})}\,,

(5.5c)

\displaystyle|a_{23}(0,iv_{1})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}\frac{v_{2}-v_{1}}{v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2}}e^{v_{1}(C_{2}-C_{3})(x_{0}-C_{1}t_{0})}\,.

(5.5d)

Finally, the system (
5.1
) can be solved and the scattering data can be written fully in terms of the eigenvalues using (

First, if we consider the case

\displaystyle b_{21}(0,iv_{2})

(5.6)

\displaystyle b_{23}(0,-i\bar{v}_{1})

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1}}{\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2}}}e^{-\bar{v}_{1}(C_{2}-C_{3})(x_{0}-C_{1}t_{0})/2+i\theta_{2}}\,,

(5.7)

\displaystyle a_{21}(0,-i\bar{v}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1}}{{v}_{1}+\bar{v}_{2}}}e^{-\bar{v}_{2}(C_{2}-C_{1})(x_{0}-C_{3}t_{0})/2+i\theta_{3}}\,,

\displaystyle a_{23}(0,iv_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{v_{2}-v_{1}}{{v}_{1}+\bar{v}_{2}}}e^{v_{1}(C_{2}-C_{3})(x_{0}-C_{1}t_{0})/2+i\theta_{4}}\,,

(5.9)

where the
\theta_{j}
are arbitrary phases. After simplification, one can obtain the solution

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{1}(x,t)=\frac{iG_{1}(v_{1}+\bar{v}_{1})}{D(x,t)}\sqrt{\frac{v_{2}-v_{1}}{v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2}}}e^{\xi_{1}(x,t)}\Bigg[1+\sqrt{\frac{(\bar{v}_{2}+v_{1})(\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1})}{(v_{2}-v_{1})(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2})}}e^{\xi_{2}(x,t)+\bar{\xi}_{2}(x,t)}\Bigg]\,,

(5.10a)

(5.10b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{3}(x,t)=\frac{iG_{3}(v_{2}+\bar{v}_{2})}{D^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)}\sqrt{\frac{\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1}}{v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2}}}e^{-\bar{\xi}_{2}(x,t)}\Bigg[1+\sqrt{\frac{(\bar{v}_{2}+v_{1})({v}_{2}-{v}_{1})}{(\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1})(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2})}}e^{-\xi_{1}(x,t)-\bar{\xi}_{1}(x,t)}\Bigg]\,,

(5.10c)

where

\displaystyle D(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigg[1-\sqrt{\frac{(v_{2}-v_{1})(\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1})}{(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2})(v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2})}}e^{\xi_{2}(x,t)+\bar{\xi}_{2}(x,t)}\Bigg]\Bigg[1+\sqrt{\frac{(v_{2}-v_{1})(\bar{v}_{2}-\bar{v}_{1})}{(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2})(v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2})}}e^{\xi_{1}(x,t)+\bar{\xi}_{1}(x,t)}\Bigg]

(5.11)

\displaystyle-\,\frac{(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{1})(\bar{v}_{2}+v_{2})}{(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2})(v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2})}e^{\xi_{1}(x,t)+\bar{\xi}_{1}(x,t)+\xi_{2}(x,t)+\bar{\xi}_{2}(x,t)}\,,

and

\displaystyle\xi_{1}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\theta_{4}+v_{1}(C_{2}-C_{3})[(x-C_{1}t)-(x_{0}-C_{1}t_{0})/2]\,,

(5.12a)

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}_{1}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\theta_{2}+\bar{v}_{1}(C_{2}-C_{3})[(x-C_{1}t)-(x_{0}-C_{1}t_{0})/2]\,,

(5.12b)

\displaystyle\xi_{2}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\theta_{1}+v_{2}(C_{1}-C_{2})[(x-C_{3}t)-(x_{0}-C_{3}t_{0})/2]\,,

(5.12c)

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}_{2}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\theta_{3}+v_{2}(C_{1}-C_{2})[(x-C_{3}t)-(x_{0}-C_{3}t_{0})/2]\,,

(5.12d)

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(C_{2}-C_{3})\sqrt{(C_{2}-C_{1})(C_{3}-C_{1})}\,,

(5.12e)

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(C_{3}-C_{1})\sqrt{(C_{2}-C_{1})(C_{3}-C_{2})}\,,

(5.12f)

\displaystyle G_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(C_{1}-C_{2})\sqrt{(C_{3}-C_{1})(C_{3}-C_{2})}\,.

(5.12g)

This solution is guaranteed to be non-singular if we choose the phases such that
\theta_{1}-\theta_{3}=\pi+2k_{1}\pi

.

On the other hand, if

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{v_{1}-v_{2}}{\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2}}}e^{v_{2}(C_{2}-C_{1})(x_{0}-C_{3}t_{0})/2+i\theta_{1}}\,,

(5.13)

\displaystyle b_{23}(-i\bar{v}_{1},0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\bar{v}_{1}-\bar{v}_{2}}{\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2}}}e^{-\bar{v}_{1}(C_{2}-C_{3})(x_{0}-C_{1}t_{0})/2+i\theta_{2}}\,,

(5.14)

(5.15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{v_{1}-v_{2}}{{v}_{1}+\bar{v}_{2}}}e^{v_{1}(C_{2}-C_{3})(x_{0}-C_{1}t_{0})/2+i\theta_{4}}\,.

(5.16)

The solution in this case is

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{1}(x,t)=\frac{iG_{1}(v_{1}+\bar{v}_{1})}{D(x,t)}\sqrt{\frac{v_{1}-v_{2}}{v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2}}}e^{\xi_{1}(x,t)}\Bigg[1+\sqrt{\frac{(\bar{v}_{2}+v_{1})(\bar{v}_{1}-\bar{v}_{2})}{(v_{1}-v_{2})(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2})}}e^{\xi_{2}(x,t)+\bar{\xi}_{2}(x,t)}\Bigg]\,,

(5.17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{2}(x,t)=\frac{iG_{2}}{D(x,t)}\frac{(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{1})(v_{2}+\bar{v}_{2})}{\sqrt{(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2})(v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2})}}e^{\bar{\xi}_{1}(x,t)+\bar{\xi}_{2}(x,t)}\,,

(5.18)

(5.19)

where

\displaystyle-\,\frac{(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{1})(\bar{v}_{2}+v_{2})}{(\bar{v}_{1}+v_{2})(v_{1}+\bar{v}_{2})}e^{\xi_{1}(x,t)+\bar{\xi}_{1}(x,t)+\xi_{2}(x,t)+\bar{\xi}_{2}(x,t)}\,,

with the other definitions the same as above.

6
Conservation laws and Hamiltonian structure

In
[
41
]
, an infinite set of conservation laws (generated by a recurrence) for the general
N\times N
version of the scattering problem (
4.2
) associated with the six-wave interaction system was derived. In this section, we follow
that work but specialize the result to both the classical and space-time shifted nonlocal three-wave systems. Finally, we connect some of the conserved quantities that we obtain from the recurrence to the infinite-dimensional Hamiltonian structure of the six-wave system. Throughout this section, we suppress
(x,t)
dependence when it is not crucial.

6.1
Infinite set of conservation laws

Consider a fundamental analytic matrix solution
\Phi=[\Phi_{\ell j}]
of (
4.2
) that has the large-
k
asymptotic behavior

\Phi\sim\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}e^{ikd_{1}x}&0&0\\
0&a_{2}e^{ikd_{2}x}&0\\
0&0&a_{3}e^{ikd_{3}x}\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}(1/k)\,,\;\;\;\text{as}\;|k|\rightarrow\infty\,,

(6.1)

where the
a_{j}
are independent of
k
. For example, we could take
\Phi=(\psi_{1},\tau,\phi_{3})
in the upper-half
k
plane. Define

\Gamma_{\ell j}\equiv\frac{\Phi_{\ell j}}{\Phi_{jj}}\,,\;\ell\neq j\,,\;\;\;\Gamma_{jj}\equiv 1\,,

(6.2)

noting that
\Gamma_{\ell j}\sim\delta_{\ell j}+\mathcal{O}(1/k)
as
|k|\rightarrow\infty
.
Now, starting from

\partial_{x}\left[\partial_{t}\log\Phi_{jj}\right]=\partial_{t}\left[\partial_{x}\log\Phi_{jj}\right]\implies\partial_{x}\left[\frac{\partial_{t}\Phi_{jj}}{\Phi_{jj}}\right]=\partial_{t}\left[\frac{\partial_{x}\Phi_{jj}}{\Phi_{jj}}\right]\,,

(6.3)

and applying (
4.2
) and (
4.4
) we get

\partial_{x}\sum_{\ell\neq j}\alpha_{j\ell}N_{j\ell}\Gamma_{\ell j}=\partial_{t}\sum_{\ell\neq j}N_{j\ell}\Gamma_{\ell j}\,,

(6.4)

with
\alpha_{j\ell}
defined as below (
4.1
). Based on its large-
k
asymptotic behavior, assume a Laurent series expansion for
\Gamma_{\ell j}
with
\ell\neq j
in the form

\Gamma_{\ell j}=\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p)}}{k^{p}}\,.

(6.5)

Upon substituting (
6.5
) and equating coefficients of
1/k^{p}
, (
6.4
) gives an infinite set of local conservation laws:

\partial_{x}\sum_{\ell\neq j}\alpha_{j\ell}N_{j\ell}\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p)}=\partial_{t}\sum_{\ell\neq j}N_{j\ell}\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p)}\,,\;\;\;j=1,2,3\,,\;\;\;p=1,\ldots,\infty\,,

(6.6)

which can be integrated to obtain the global conserved quantities:

\mathcal{C}_{j}^{(p)}=\sum_{\ell\neq j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}N_{j\ell}(x,t)\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p)}(x,t)\,dx\,,\;\;\;j=1,2,3\,,\;\;\;p=1,\ldots,\infty\,.

(6.7)

Furthermore, directly from (
4.2
), it can be found that
\Gamma_{\ell j}
satisfies the Riccati-type differential equation

ik(d_{\ell}-d_{j})\Gamma_{\ell j}=\partial_{x}\Gamma_{\ell j}-N_{\ell m}\Gamma_{mj}+\Gamma_{\ell j}N_{jm}\Gamma_{mj}\,,

(6.8)

with
m\neq j
and
m\neq\ell
. Substituting the Laurent series, with the additional
p=0
term corresponding to
\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(0)}\equiv\delta_{\ell j}
, into (
6.8
) gives

ik(d_{\ell}-d_{j})\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p)}}{k^{p}}=\partial_{x}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p)}}{k^{p}}-N_{\ell m}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{mj}^{(p)}}{k^{p}}+\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p)}}{k^{p}}N_{jm}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{mj}^{(s)}}{k^{s}}\,.

(6.9)

The
\mathcal{O}(k)
terms automatically cancel, and the
\mathcal{O}(1)
terms give

\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(1)}=\frac{iN_{\ell j}}{d_{\ell}-d_{j}}\,,\;\;\;\ell\neq j\,.

(6.10)

The
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
terms give

\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{(d_{\ell}-d_{j})^{2}}\partial_{x}N_{\ell j}-\frac{1}{(d_{\ell}-d_{j})(d_{m}-d_{j})}N_{\ell m}N_{mj}\,,\;\;\;\ell\neq j,\;j\neq m,\;m\neq\ell\,.

(6.11)

All subsequent terms can be determined by a recurrence. In particular, after rewriting the double sum, for
\ell\neq j
we have

i(d_{\ell}-d_{j})\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p+1)}}{k^{p}}=\partial_{x}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p)}}{k^{p}}-N_{\ell m}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma_{mj}^{(p)}}{k^{p}}+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\sum_{s=1}^{p-1}N_{jm}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p+s)}\Gamma_{mj}^{(s)}}{k^{p}}\,.

(6.12)

Equating coefficients of
1/k^{p}
for
p\geq 2
gives

i(d_{\ell}-d_{j})\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p+1)}=\partial_{x}\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p)}-N_{\ell m}\Gamma_{mj}^{(p)}+\sum_{s=1}^{p-1}N_{jm}\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(p+s)}\Gamma_{mj}^{(s)}\,.

(6.13)

6.2
The first few conserved quantities

From (
6.7
) and (
6.10
), the quantities

\sum_{\ell\neq j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}N_{j\ell}\Gamma_{\ell j}^{(1)}\,dx=\sum_{\ell\neq j}\frac{i}{d_{\ell}-d_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}N_{j\ell}N_{\ell j}\,dx\,,

(6.14)

are conserved. Setting
j=1,2,3
, performing the rescaling in (
2.2
), and putting
d_{j}=-C_{j}
, the first three conserved quantities for the generic six-wave system are (proportional to):

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{2}R_{2}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{3}R_{3}\,dx\,,

(6.15a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{3}R_{3}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}R_{1}\,dx\,,

(6.15b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}R_{1}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{2}R_{2}\,dx\,.

(6.15c)

These conserved quantities are well-known, and be also be derived directly from the six-wave system. Note that any one of them could be written as a linear combination of the other two. If we incorporate the classical symmetry reduction (
2.5
), then these can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{2}(x,t)Q^{*}_{2}(x,t)\,dx-\tilde{\epsilon}_{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{3}(x,t)Q^{*}_{3}(x,t)\,dx\,,

(6.16a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{3}(x,t)Q^{*}_{3}(x,t)\,dx-\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}(x,t)Q^{*}_{1}(x,t)\,dx\,,

(6.16b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}(x,t)Q^{*}_{1}(x,t)\,dx-\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{2}(x,t)Q^{*}_{2}(x,t)\,dx\,.

(6.16c)

On the other hand, if we apply the shifted symmetry reduction (
2.7
), then they become

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{2}(x,t)Q_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\,dx+\epsilon_{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{3}(x,t)Q_{3}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\,dx\,,

(6.17a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{3}(x,t)Q_{3}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\,dx-\epsilon_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}(x,t)Q_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\,dx\,,

(6.17b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}(x,t)Q_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\,dx+\epsilon_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{2}(x,t)Q_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\,dx\,.

(6.17c)

From (
6.7
) and (
6.11
), the second set of conserved quantities is

\sum_{\ell\neq j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}N_{j\ell}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{(d_{\ell}-d_{j})^{2}}\partial_{x}N_{\ell j}-\frac{1}{(d_{\ell}-d_{j})(d_{m}-d_{j})}N_{\ell m}N_{mj}\Bigg\}\,dx\,.

(6.18)

with
j=1,2,3
,
\ell\neq j
, and
m\neq\ell,j
. After simplification and applying the rescaling (
2.2
), we find that these three quantities are (proportional to):

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[(C_{1}-C_{2})R_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}+(C_{1}-C_{3})R_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}-iQ_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}-iR_{1}R_{2}R_{3}\bigg]\,dx\,,

(6.19a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[(C_{2}-C_{3})R_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}+(C_{2}-C_{1})R_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}-iQ_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}-iR_{1}R_{2}R_{3}\bigg]\,dx\,,

(6.19b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[(C_{3}-C_{1})R_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}+(C_{3}-C_{2})R_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}-iQ_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}-iR_{1}R_{2}R_{3}\bigg]\,dx\,.

(6.19c)

Applying the classical symmetry reduction (
2.5
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}(C_{1}-C_{2})Q^{*}_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{3}(C_{1}-C_{3})Q^{*}_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}\bigg]\,dx+2i\,\text{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}\,dx\,,

(6.20a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[\tilde{\epsilon}_{3}(C_{2}-C_{3})Q^{*}_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}(C_{2}-C_{1})Q^{*}_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}\bigg]\,dx+2i\,\text{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}\,dx\,,

(6.20b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}(C_{3}-C_{1})Q^{*}_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}(C_{3}-C_{2})Q^{*}_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}\bigg]\,dx+2i\,\text{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}\,dx\,.

(6.20c)

Applying the shifted symmetry reduction (
2.7
) gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[-\epsilon_{2}(C_{1}-C_{2})Q^{*}_{2}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{2}(x,t)

(6.21a)

\displaystyle\qquad+\,\epsilon_{3}(C_{1}-C_{3})Q^{*}_{3}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{3}(x,t)\bigg]\,dx-i\Upsilon\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[\epsilon_{3}(C_{2}-C_{3})Q^{*}_{3}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{3}(x,t)

(6.21b)

\displaystyle\qquad+\,\epsilon_{1}(C_{2}-C_{1})Q^{*}_{1}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{1}(x,t)\bigg]\,dx-i\Upsilon\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[\epsilon_{1}(C_{3}-C_{1})Q^{*}_{1}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{1}(x,t)

(6.21c)

\displaystyle\qquad-\,\epsilon_{2}(C_{3}-C_{2})Q^{*}_{2}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{2}(x,t)\bigg]\,dx-i\Upsilon\,,

where in the above, we have defined

\Upsilon=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[Q_{1}(x,t)Q_{2}(x,t)Q_{3}(x,t)-Q_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)Q_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)Q_{3}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\bigg]\,dx\,.

(6.22)

Again, note that one of these quantities could be written as a linear combination of the other two.

6.3
Hamiltonian structure

It turns out that the second set of conserved quantities (
6.19
) that one obtains from the infinite recurrence is directly connected to the infinite-dimensional Hamiltonian structure of the integrable six-wave interaction system. Particularly, a Hamiltonian for (
2.3
) is:

\mathcal{H}=-i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[C_{1}R_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}+C_{2}R_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}+C_{3}R_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}+iQ_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}+iR_{1}R_{2}R_{3}\bigg]\,dx\,.

(6.23)

Indeed, one can verify that (
2.3
) is equivalent to

i\partial_{t}Q_{j}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta R_{j}}\,,\qquad i\partial_{t}R_{j}=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta Q_{j}}\,.

(6.24)

Furthermore, if we define the Poisson bracket

\{F,G\}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_{k}(z)}\frac{\delta G}{\delta R_{k}(z)}-\frac{\delta F}{\delta R_{k}(z)}\frac{\delta G}{\delta Q_{k}(z)}\bigg)\,dz\,,

(6.25)

then the canonical variables
Q_{j}(x)
and
R_{j}(x)
satisfy

\displaystyle\{Q_{j}(x),R_{j^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\}=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta(x-x^{\prime})\,,

(6.26a)

\displaystyle\{Q_{j}(x),Q_{j^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\}=0\,,\;\;\;\{R_{j}(x),R_{j^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\}=0\,.

(6.26b)

We also have that

\{F,Q_{j}(x)\}=-\frac{\delta F}{\delta R_{j}(x)}\,,\qquad\{F,R_{j}(x)\}=\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_{j}(x)}\,,

(6.27)

in which case Hamilton’s equations (
6.24
) can be expressed in terms of the bracket as

i\partial_{t}Q_{j}=\{Q_{j},\mathcal{H}\}\,,\qquad i\partial_{t}R_{j}=\{R_{j},\mathcal{H}\}\,.

(6.28)

Observe that the Hamiltonian (
6.23
) can be obtained as a linear combination of the three conserved quantities listed in (
6.19
) (in many ways, since they are linearly dependent). Specifically,

-i\mathcal{H}=\eta_{1}\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(2)}+\eta_{2}\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{2}+\eta_{3}\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{3}\,,

(6.29)

for any
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3}
satisfying the conditions

C_{1}\eta_{1}+C_{2}\eta_{2}+C_{3}\eta_{3}=0
and
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}=1
. Applying the symmetry reduction (
2.5
), the Hamiltonian for the classical three-wave system is

\mathcal{H}=i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}C_{1}Q^{*}_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}C_{2}Q^{*}_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{3}C_{3}Q^{*}_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}\bigg]\,dx+2\,\text{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}\,dx\,.

(6.30)

Note that using integration by parts, one can verify that
\mathcal{H}
is real. Finally, applying (
2.7
), the Hamiltonian for the space-time shifted three wave system is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg[\epsilon_{1}C_{1}Q^{*}_{1}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{1}(x,t)-\epsilon_{2}C_{2}Q^{*}_{2}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{2}(x,t)

(6.31)

\displaystyle\qquad+\,\epsilon_{3}C_{3}Q^{*}_{3}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{3}(x,t)\bigg]\,dx+\Upsilon\,,

with
\Upsilon
defined as in (
6.22
). Using integration by parts, changing the variable of integration
x\rightarrow x_{0}-x
, and letting
t\rightarrow t_{0}-t
(since
\mathcal{H}
is conserved) shows that
\mathcal{H}
is purely imaginary, as required by (
6.24
) with the reduction (
2.7
).

Conserved quantities and Hamiltonian structure in 2+1 dimensions

We remark that one can generalize the above conservation laws to two spatial dimensions by inspection. The first set of conserved quantities (
6.15
) becomes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{2}R_{2}\,dx\,dy-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{3}R_{3}\,dx\,dy\,,

(6.32a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{3}R_{3}\,dx\,dy-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{1}R_{1}\,dx\,dy\,,

(6.32b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{1}R_{1}\,dx\,dy-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{2}R_{2}\,dx\,dy\,.

(6.32c)

Incorporating the symmetry reduction (
2.7
), in the space-time shifted case these are

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{2}(x,y,t)Q_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,dx\,dy

(6.33a)

\displaystyle+\,\epsilon_{3}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{3}(x,y,t)Q_{3}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,dx\,dy\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{3}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{3}(x,y,t)Q_{3}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,dx\,dy

(6.33b)

\displaystyle-\,\epsilon_{1}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{1}(x,y,t)Q_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,dx\,dy\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{1}(x,y,t)Q_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,dx\,dy

(6.33c)

\displaystyle+\,\epsilon_{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Q_{2}(x,y,t)Q_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\,dx\,dy\,.

The generalization of the second set of conserved quantities (
6.19
) is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[(C^{(x)}_{1}-C^{(x)}_{2})R_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}+(C^{(x)}_{1}-C^{(x)}_{3})R_{3}\partial_{x}Q_{3}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[(C^{(x)}_{3}-C^{(x)}_{1})R_{1}\partial_{x}Q_{1}+(C^{(x)}_{3}-C^{(x)}_{2})R_{2}\partial_{x}Q_{2}

Applying the shifted symmetry reduction (

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[-\epsilon_{2}(C_{1}^{(x)}-C^{(x)}_{2})Q^{*}_{2}(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)\partial_{x}Q_{2}(x,y,t)

(6.35a)

7
Conclusion

In this paper,
(2+1)
dimensional first order quadratically coupled nonlinear six-wave interaction equations were derived from the physics of
nonlinear optics. This was accomplished by considering two model examples. The first is based on Maxwell’s equations where the polarization field
is expressed in terms of a time-convolution between the electric field and medium susceptibility. The other case is based on a laser model where the polarization function satisfies a quadratically nonlinear differential equation. Upon employing a space-time multi-scale
asymptotic expansion (on both models), a hierarchy of linearly coupled nonhomogeneous equations is derived. Importantly, the leading order equation admits a solution in the form of a linear superposition of six plane waves whose wave-vector and corresponding frequency satisfy the triad resonance condition
(
1.1
). Importantly, the slowly modulated amplitudes of each individual plane wave are not assumed to be complex conjugates of each other.
By removing secular terms at order
\epsilon
(the small expansion parameter), first order in space and time quadratically coupled six-wave
equations in
2+1
dimensions are obtained.
Remarkably, this resulting system is shown to be connected to its integrable counterpart which was mathematically derived by Ablowitz and Haberman in the 1970s. Several integrable reductions to space-time shifted nonlocal three-wave equations are obtained and shown to form an integrable Hamiltonian system. In this regard, a set of integrals of motions are derived. The nonlocal space-time shifted three-wave equations are solved using the inverse scattering transform. Nonlocal symmetries between the associated eigenfunctions and scattering data are derived.
A Riemann-Hilbert problem is formulated which is used to obtain soliton solutions.
