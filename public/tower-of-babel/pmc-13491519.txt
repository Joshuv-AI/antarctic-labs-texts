Direct Electron Transfer-Type Gas Diffusion Electrode for Efficient CO2‑to-Formate Bioconversion across Broad and Low CO2 Levels
Authors: Ami Kobayashi, Taiki Adachi, Konatsu Ichikawa, Yuki Kitazumi, Osamu Shirai, Keisei Sowa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bioelectrocatalytic CO2 reduction to value-added chemicals offers a sustainable route for carbon recycling; however, achieving high activity under low CO2 conditions remains a major challenge. Herein, we report a direct electron transfer (DET)-type gas diffusion electrode (GDE) utilizing formate dehydrogenase 1 (FoDH1) from Methylorubrum extorquens AM1 for efficient CO2-to-formate conversion across a broad range of low CO2 levels. The electrode surface was modified with pyrene derivatives using multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) to tailor the enzyme–electrode interface, enabling favorable enzyme orientation for rapid electron transfer. A 1-pyrenemethylamine (PyNH2)-modified GDE with FoDH1 exhibited a current density of −1.2 mA cm–2 at a reduction potential of −0.9 V, which was twice that of the pristine electrode, and generated formate with a Faradaic efficiency of 82 ± 5%, outperforming previously reported GDE systems. Kinetic analyses under controlled CO2 conditions (0.04–100%) revealed a notably low apparent Michaelis constant (K M,app) of 2.6 ± 0.3%, which is less than one-third of that of the solution system, indicating high CO2 affinity. This enhancement is attributed to the direct supply of gaseous CO2 to the catalytic layer, avoiding equilibrium limitations in the electrolyte solution. These results lead to a promising tool for enzymatic CO2 reduction using emission gases from industrial sources and provide key design principles for practical CO2-recycling technologies.

Introduction

Escalating greenhouse gas emissions pose a significant threat to the global environment. The use of fossil fuels has increased atmospheric CO2 levels since the industrial revolution, leading to climate change and global warming. − To mitigate the atmospheric CO2 level, “Carbon dioxide Capture and Utilization (CCU)” technology has emerged as a promising strategy to reuse CO2 as value-added resource for realizing a sustainable society. − During CO2 utilization, CO2 serves as a precursor for a variety of valuable chemicals, including C1 chemicals (e.g., methane, methanol, formaldehyde, and formate), C2 chemicals (e.g., ethanol and acetic acid), and other organic building blocks for the chemical industry. ,

Electrocatalytic CO2 conversion is a widely researched approach in which electrocatalysts facilitate the reaction by promoting electron transfer (ET) between CO2 and electrodes. − The electrocatalysts are constructed using a variety of inorganic/organic materials tailored for the target transformation. In particular, transition metals such as Cu, Zn, and Ni have been extensively studied for their unique electronic structures and ability to facilitate diverse reaction pathways. Recent advances have introduced alloy-based catalysts, such as bismuth–copper nanostructures, which achieve >90% formate selectivity at low overpotentials, and Co–Ni alloys encapsulated in ceramic matrices, which demonstrate 90% energy efficiency and long-term stability at 800 °C for CO production. These remarkable developments in electrocatalysts have overcome traditional challenges such as enhancing catalyst stability and selectivity toward the desired products. Nevertheless, even though some systems now exhibit excellent energy efficiency, critical challenges remain. Many material-based electrocatalysts still face energy losses, need additional CO2 capture steps for separating impurities and increasing the CO2 concentration, and require extreme operating temperatures. Furthermore, expanding the range of complex multicarbon products remains a significant challenge. ,,

Given the current situation, significant attention has shifted toward bioelectrocatalytic systems as promising conversion strategies, which utilize biological processes in living organisms by integrating biocatalysts and electrode materials. − These systems offer remarkable selectivity and energy efficiency under mild conditions (neutral pH, room temperature, and atmospheric pressure) and can produce valuable multicarbon chemicals. Two main types of bioelectrocatalytic systems exist, enzymes and microbes. In enzymatic systems, the biocatalysts are redox enzymes isolated from microbes. − In particular, formate dehydrogenase (FoDH) and carbon monoxide dehydrogenase (CODH) can reduce CO2 to formate or CO as initial steps for further conversion. − In contrast, microbial systems utilize electroactive microbes, such as Geobacter sp., which are implemented as biofilms or mixed cultures in electrocatalytic systems, driving the multistep conversion of CO2 into chemicals such as acetate or methane. − ET in bioelectrocatalytic systems occurs through electronic interactions between the redox centers of enzymes and electrodes, either directly or via mediators. − In general, bioelectrocatalysis requires redox mediators to shuttle electrons between an electrode and enzyme, primarily because the redox-active sites are typically buried within the enzyme structure. This process is referred to as a mediated electron transfer (MET)-type reaction. In contrast, a limited number of enzymes can proceed via direct electron transfer (DET)-type reactions. DET-type reactions can reduce thermodynamic energy loss owing to the absence of mediators and simplify electrode fabrication. −

Among biocatalysts, FoDHs are popular because of their rapid and efficient CO2 reduction into formate, which is a beneficial product that can be used as a renewable energy resource, hydrogen carrier, and feedstock. − FoDHs play vital roles in metabolic functions by catalyzing the two-electron oxidation of formate to CO2. They can be divided into two classes: metal-dependent (containing a Mo or W cofactor at the active site) and metal-independent FoDHs. Metal-dependent FoDHs are markedly superior for driving the reverse reaction (two-electron reduction of CO2 to formate) because of their higher catalytic efficiency, lower overpotentials, and distinct reaction mechanisms. ,,,

Recent advances in electrode construction have aimed to enhance electrochemical compatibility between FoDHs and electrodes. The kinetics factor in DET-type bioelectrocatalysis strongly depends on the composition and architecture of the enzyme–electrode interface because the ET rate decreases exponentially with increasing transfer distance. Therefore, optimizing the enzyme–electrode interface through appropriate immobilization strategies, conductive nanomaterials, and orientation control of the enzyme is crucial for facilitating efficient ET and achieving high catalytic current densities. , Reda et al. first applied the W-containing FoDH from Syntrophobacter fumaroxidans (SfFoDH) to a DET-type reaction through its immobilization on a bare pyrolytic graphite edge electrode. Bassegoda et al. constructed graphite-epoxy electrodes that adsorbed Mo-containing FoDH from Escherichia coli (EcFoDH), achieving a maximum current density of −62 μA cm–2 for CO2 reduction.

Our group has focused on the W-containing formate dehydrogenase 1 from Methylorubrum extorquens AM1 (FoDH1). FoDH1 is a heterodimer of α and β subunits that catalyzes the reversible interconversion of two redox couples at the respective active sites: CO2/formate at W-pterin and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+/NADH) at noncovalent flavin mononucleotide (FMN). , Although most other FoDHs are sensitive to oxygen, FoDH1 is oxygen tolerant and maintains sufficient activity during cell expression and enzyme purification in an aerobic environment. Additionally, the three-dimensional structure of FoDH1 has recently been revealed at 2.2 Å with cryo-electron microscopy and single particle analysis (Figure ), enabling a deeper discussion of the interactions between the enzyme and the electrode. The study, which focused on the substrate oxidation reaction of FoDH1, suggested that iron–sulfur (Fe–S) clusters may function as intramolecular ET pathways and electroactive sites.

Three-dimensional structure of FoDH1 analyzed by cryo-electron microscopy (PDB: 7VW6).

When utilizing gaseous substrates, a gas diffusion electrode (GDE) has been developed to promote direct substrate supply in various bioelectrocatalytic systems, , including H2/O2 biofuel cells, − microbial electrosynthesis − and CO2 reduction. − The GDE design establishes gas–liquid–solid three-phase interfaces to introduce gaseous substrates from the gas phase to the enzyme-adsorbed electrode surface. Some studies, including ours, have developed a GDE under 100% gaseous CO2. For example, a MET-type GDE with FoDH1 was constructed by using KB-coated carbon cloth as an electrode and 1,1′-trimethylene-2,2′-bipyridinium dibromide (TQ) as a mediator, realizing a significantly high bioelectrocatalytic CO2-reducing current of approximately −20 mA cm–2. In a similar MET-type GDE example using benzyl viologen (BV2+) as a mediator, the Faradaic efficiency remained at 20 ± 10%, making efficiency improvement one of the key challenges. Furthermore, recent bio/hybrid electrochemical systems combining GDEs with enzymatic cascades or microbial catalysis have emerged as advancing approaches for efficient CO2 conversion. In these systems, electrochemically generated reducing equivalents, such as H2, are supplied to enzymatic or microbial processes for the production of value-added chemicals. − These studies highlight the growing importance of integrating gas-diffusion system with biological catalysis for sustainable CO2 utilization.

In this study, we introduced DET-type CO2 reduction into a GDE system with FoDH1 to construct an enzymatic electrode that operates efficiently across broad and low CO2 concentrations, including those at the atmospheric level. Pyrene derivatives have been used for the noncovalent modification of carbon nanotubes (CNTs). ,− Pyrene is a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon, and its planar aromatic structure enables π–π stacking interactions with the graphene layer of the CNTs. The electrode modification scheme is illustrated in Figure . We first explored the surface modification of the electrode to realize ideal DET with FoDH1 in a conventional solution system. This finding was applied to the GDE system, and the Faradaic efficiency was calculated by quantifying formate through the electrolysis of gaseous CO2. In addition, because previously reported studies on CO2-reducing GDEs were conducted only under CO2-saturated conditions, characterization was performed at various CO2 partial pressures with the aim of directly capturing the low concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere. Furthermore, the apparent substrate affinity for CO2 in the GDE system is discussed.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Chemicals

Waterproof carbon cloth (WPCC, EC-CC1-060T, thickness: 0.2 mm) was purchased from ElectroChem, Inc. (USA), and a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane filter (T050A025A, pore size: 0.5 μm, thickness: 90 μm) was purchased from Advantec Co. LCC (Japan). Titanium mesh (Ti mesh, TI-458100, thickness: 0.1 mm) was purchased from Nilaco (Japan). Multiwalled CNTs (MWCNTs, 755133-5G, outer diameter: 9.5 nm, length: 1.5 μm), 1-pyrenemethylamine (PyNH2) hydrochloride, 1-pyrenecarboxylic acid (PyCA), and pyrene (Py) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (USA). Toyopearl DEAE-650 M and Toyopearl Butyl-650 M for enzyme purification were purchased from Tosoh (Japan). Protein markers and 7.5% polyacrylamide gels (e-PAGEL, gel size 90 × 83 mm) for sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) were purchased from Bio-Rad (USA) and Atto Co. (Japan). Other chemicals were obtained from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Industries Ltd. (Japan), and all solutions were prepared using distilled water.

Enzyme Purification and Activity Assay

FoDH1 was purified as described previously. Briefly, purification was performed by ion-exchange chromatography using a Toyopearl DEAE-650 M column equilibrated with 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (KPB, pH 6.0) containing 80 mM NaCl. The enzyme was eluted by increasing the NaCl concentration from 100 to 220 mM. The collected fractions were further purified using hydrophobic interaction chromatography on a Toyopearl Butyl-650 M column equilibrated with 20 mM KPB (pH 6.0) containing 20% ammonium sulfate. The enzyme was eluted by decreasing the concentration of ammonium sulfate from 12 to 8%. The purified FoDH1 was finally concentrated and stored at −80 °C with 10% (w/v) sucrose.

The FoDH1 activity for formate oxidation in solution was analyzed according to the previous literature. The analysis was conducted in 1 mL of 0.1 M KPB (pH 7.0) containing 30 mM sodium formate, 0.2 mM 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol sodium salt (DCIP), and 0.05 mM phenazine methosulfate (PMS). The oxidation reactions were initiated by adding the FoDH1 solution, and the decrease in absorbance at 600 nm due to the reduction of DCIP was detected using a UV–VIS spectrophotometer (UV-1900i, Shimadzu Corp., Japan). One unit of the activity was defined as the amount of FoDH1 that reduces 1 μmol of DCIP per minute. The specific activity of FoDH1 used in this study was 52 ± 6 U mg–1, and the two major bands corresponding to the α and β subunits of FoDH1 were confirmed by SDS-PAGE (Figure S1). Errors were determined using Student’s t distribution at a 90% confidence level (n = 6).

Electrode Preparation for the Solution System

Glassy carbon electrodes (GCEs, diameter: 3 mm, BAS, Japan) were polished with 1.0 and 0.05 μm alumina dispersions and washed by sonication and rinsing with distilled water. The MWCNTs (25 mg) were dispersed in 10 mL of 2-propanol, and the suspension was sonicated for 120 min in an ultrasonic bath (UT-305S, Sharp Corporation, Japan) to obtain a homogeneous MWCNT slurry. The MWCNT slurry (10 μL, 0.25% (w/v)) was applied to the GCE and dried at 70 °C. This electrode was called MWCNT/GCE. The electrode was then immersed in a modification solution containing 5 mM pyrene or pyrene derivatives in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and held for 60 min Meanwhile, 4-(pyren-1-yl)-N-(pyridin-4-ylmethyl)­butanamide (PyPy) was synthesized according to the literature. The modified electrode was removed from the solution and rinsed with DMF, followed by 1.0 M KPB (pH 6.5). Finally, FoDH1 was adsorbed on the electrode surface by drop-casting 30 μL of 3.0 mg mL–1 FoDH1 dissolved in 1.0 M KPB (pH 6.5). The electrodes were placed in water-saturated conditions for 60 min at 4 °C.

Electrode Preparation for the Gas Diffusion System

GDEs were prepared as follows: 50 μL of a 0.25% (w/v) MWCNT slurry was drop-casted onto the circular area (diameter: 5.0 mm, area: 0.20 cm2) of a 1.0 cm2 piece of WPCC and dried at 70 °C. The MWCNT-coated area works as a basement for both surface modification and enzyme adsorption. Then, a PTFE membrane filter was attached to the opposite side of WPCC by pressure bonding to completely hold the electrolytes, referred to as MWCNT/WPCC (Figure ). Surface modification was achieved by drop-casting 100 μL of 5 mM pyrene derivative solution dissolved in distilled water. Pure water was used as the solvent because DMF can dissolve or degrade the polymeric waterproof coating on WPCC. After 60 min, the modified GDE was removed via rinsing with distilled water. Finally, a 50 μL of 3 mg mL–1 FoDH1 solution was drop-casted on the MWCNT-coated area. The electrode was dried under reduced pressure at 4 °C for approximately 120 min until just short of complete dryness.

(A) Photograph of MWCNT/WPCC attached to the PTFE membrane filter (B) enlarged view of the coated area of MWCNT/WPCC.

Electrochemical Measurements

In both the solution and gas diffusion systems, cyclic voltammograms (CVs) and chronoamperograms (CAs) were recorded using an electrochemical analyzer (ALS 701E, BAS Inc., Japan). A platinum wire and a homemade Ag|AgCl|sat. KCl electrode were used as counter and reference electrodes, respectively. All potentials in this study are in reference to the KCl reference electrode.

A handmade gas-diffusion-type electrolysis cell was constructed according to a previously reported method. The FoDH1-adsorbed electrode was inserted between the one-compartment cells, as shown in Figure . The projected surface area of the electrode was adjusted to 0.2 cm2 by covering the electrode with a cell vessel. The total volume of the electrolyte solution in the vessel was 1.5 mL. A Ti mesh was used as the current collector, and the counter and the reference electrodes were inserted through the opening at the top of the electrolysis cell. The measurements were performed in 1.0 M KPB (pH 6.5) at 25 °C.

(A) Schematic illustration of the gas diffusion electrode and electrolysis cell. (B) Photograph of the assembled gas diffusion system.

Quantification of Produced Formate

Formate quantification was performed using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Shimazu Corp., Japan), including a system controller (CBM-20A), degassing unit (DGU-12A), two pumps (LC-10AD), autosampler (SIL-10AP), column oven (CTO-10A), and electric conductivity detector (CDD-10AVP), according to the process described in the literature. One pump carried the precolumn mobile phase (5 mM perchloric acid solution), whereas the other pump carried the postcolumn pH-buffering solution (20 mM Bis-Tris solution containing 5 mM perchloric acid) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL min–1. The system connected three columns in a sequence: a Shim-pack SCR-102H column (50 mm length × 6.0 mm i.d., guard column), Shim-pack SCR-102H column (300 mm length × 8.0 mm i.d., 7.0 μm), and Shim-pack SPR-H column (250 mm length × 4.6 mm i.d., 8.0 μm). The temperature of the column oven was 40 °C. Each sample solution was injected into a 20 μL sample loop for formate quantification. Then, a calibration curve was created from the peak areas of the formate standard solutions prepared in 1.0 M KPB (pH 6.5) with concentrations ranging from 0 to 4 mM.

Results and Discussion

DET-Type CO2 Reduction on the Solution System

To explore the effect of surface functionalization on bioelectrocatalytic CO2 reduction, three different pyrene derivatives and pyrene were modified on the MWCNT/GCEs. Figure A shows the CVs recorded for the FoDH1-adsorbed MWCNT/GCEs, comparing the effects of modification on the DET-type reaction. Under CO2-saturated conditions, all FoDH1-absorbed electrodes exhibited catalytic currents for CO2 reduction at potentials more negative than −0.6 V, while the signals were not observed under Ar-saturated conditions. The current densities at −0.9 V are summarized in Figure B. The PyNH2-modified electrode yielded the highest reduction current density (−0.43 ± 0.03 mA cm–2), followed by PyPy (−0.30 ± 0.02 mA cm–2), PyCA (−0.25 ± 0.05 mA cm–2), pyrene (−0.23 ± 0.03 mA cm–2), and the pristine electrode (−0.20 ± 0.02 mA cm–2). This significant enhancement with PyNH2 modification indicated that positively charged interfaces can promote favorable FoDH1 orientations. PyPy modification produced a relatively high current density of −0.30 mA cm–2, implying that pyridine-containing structures may offer additional specific interactions with intramolecular Fe–S clusters. , The orientation effect of positive charge and pyridine rings for FoDH1 was previously reported: Sakai et al. demonstrated that a gold nanoparticle (AuNP)-embedded Ketjen Black (KB)-coated electrode treated with 4-mercaptopyridine (4-MP) successfully improved CO2-reducing activity. Yoshikawa et al. introduced 4-MP and 4-aminothiophenol (4-ATP) onto porous gold electrodes (PGEs) and observed a greater increase with 4-ATP-modified PGEs. Our results are consistent with these findings, indicating that the functional groups of the pyrene derivatives exhibit properties compatible with those of previously applied modifiers.

(A) CVs for CO2 reduction at the pristine electrode (black) and MWCNT/GCE modified with Py (green), PyPy (purple), PyCA (blue), and PyNH2 (red). FoDH1 was adsorbed onto all the electrodes. The solid lines represent the CVs under CO2-saturated conditions, and the black dashed lines represent the CV of the pristine electrode recorded under Ar-saturated conditions. All the measurement was performed in 1.0 M KPB (pH 6.5) at 25 °C under stirring condition. (B) CO2 reducing current density at −0.9 V for each modification. Errors were determined using Student’s t distribution at the 90% confidence level (n = 6).

Unexpectedly, the negatively charged PyCA-modified electrode showed a clear but statistically insignificant signal compared with that of the pristine electrode. Considering electrostatic interactions, the DET-type reaction is expected to deteriorate compared with that of the pristine electrode. However, because a reactivity equivalent to that of the pristine electrode was observed, factors other than electrostatic interactions were likely involved. This may be because of the complexity of FoDH1, which has multiple DET pathways. In addition, the Py-modified electrodes exhibited almost no significant difference from the pristine electrodes, confirming that the pyrene backbone itself does not significantly contribute to the catalytic performance. These results demonstrate that the observed enhancements arise from the individual functional groups rather than the pyrene scaffold and identify PyNH2 as the most effective modifier. Therefore, PyNH2 was employed in subsequent experiments.

DET-Type CO2 Reduction on Gas Diffusion System

As shown in Figure , FoDH1-adsorbed PyNH2-modified GDE (PyNH2/MWCNT/WPCC) exhibited markedly higher DET-type activity, with its current density of −1.2 mA cm–2 at −0.9 V more than twice as high as that of the pristine GDE (FoDH1-adsorbed MWCNT/WPCC). This clear increase indicated that the effect of PyNH2 modification observed in the solution system remained valid and reproducible in the GDE system. This result confirmed that the interactions between the functionalized electrode surface and the enzyme were sufficiently robust, even at the more complex three-phase interfaces of the GDE.

CVs for CO2 reduction in the GDE system. MWCNT/WPCC (black solid line) and PyNH2/MWCNT/WPCC (red solid line) were measured under CO2-saturated conditions. FoDH1 was adsorbed onto all the electrodes. Dashed lines represent each CV recorded under Ar-saturated conditions.

Furthermore, to evaluate Faradaic efficiencies, CAs at −0.9 V for 60 min were recorded at FoDH1-adsorbed PyNH2/MWCNT/WPCC and pristine electrodes (Figure S2), and generated formate in the electrolyte was detected using a HPLC system (Figure S3). Then, quantification was carried out via calibration curve using peak areas of formate standards (Figure S4). As shown in Figure , the PyNH2-modified GDE achieved an average Faradaic efficiency of 82 ± 5%, which was approximately 30% higher than that of the pristine GDE. To investigate the influence of crossover effects, we constructed a separated GDE cell in which the counter electrode compartment was connected via a salt bridge. , No significant difference in Faradaic efficiency was observed between the one-compartment and separated cells, indicating that crossover effects did not measurably influence formate quantification under the present experimental conditions (Figure S5). In comparison, Adachi et al. constructed a formate-generating system based on MET-type CO2 reduction using FoDH1 with BV2+, achieving an average Faradaic efficiency of 20 ± 10%. Szczesny et al. reported an FoDH-adsorbed GDE system to evaluate both MET- and DET-type reactions, observing reduction current densities of −533 ± 126 μA cm–2 and −333 ± 273 μA cm–2, respectively. However, the Faradaic efficiency of the MET-type GDE remained low, producing at most 2.6 mg L–1 of formate, corresponding to only 3.7% of the theoretical maximum of 70 mg L–1. Our GDE system significantly exceeds the conversion efficiency of reported MET-type GDE systems and achieves a higher reaction rate for CO2 reduction. This improvement suggests that PyNH2 modification not only promotes the ET rate but also increases the reaction selectivity, effectively suppressing undesirable side reactions such as hydrogen evolution.

Faradaic efficiency for CO2 reduction with the pristine electrode (black) and PyNH2/MWCNT/WPCC (red) at −0.9 V under CO2-saturated conditions. FoDH1 was adsorbed onto all the electrodes. Errors were determined using Student’s t distribution at the 90% confidence level (n = 3).

Although the catalytic current gradually decreased during continuous electrolysis, approximately 40% of the initial current was retained after 120 min (Figure S6). The detailed causes of the current decline have not been fully clarified. However, possible factors may include desorption of electroactive enzyme and gradual enzymatic deactivation during electrochemical operation. Additionally, enzymatic deactivation may arise from structural disruption caused by external factors, as well as from local pH changes near the electrode surface. Thus, further investigation of stabilization strategies and enzyme–electrode interactions will be important for improving the long-term durability of the DET-type GDE system.

Electrochemical CO2 Reduction at Atmospheric-Level Concentrations

To quantitatively evaluate the affinity toward CO2 in the GDE system, the CO2 partial pressure was systematically controlled over the range of 0.04 to 100%, with the lower limit corresponding to the CO2 level in ambient air. This range provides a basis for assessing the minimum CO2 levels compatible with bioelectrocatalytic activity and represents the first demonstration of FoDH-driven CO2 reduction under a broad range of CO2 concentrations. Electrochemical measurements were performed by regulating the proportions of CO2 and Ar gases. Figure shows the current density recorded at −0.9 V during chronoamperometry after 100 s as a function of the CO2 partial pressure (P CO2 ), with the current recorded under Ar-saturated conditions subtracted. The system exhibited a typical Michaelis–Menten curve, and the GDE displayed a markedly steeper increase in the low-CO2 region (<10%), eventually reaching a plateau, which indicated that the CO2 supply of the GDE system was saturated under these conditions.

(A) Plots of the current density vs CO2 partial pressure at FoDH1-adsorbed PyNH2/MWCNT/WPCC. (B) Enlarged view where the CO2 partial pressure is below 10%. Errors were determined using Student’s t distribution at the 90% confidence level (n = 6).

Next, we normalized all the current densities to the average current density at 100% CO2 for the GDE, quiescent solution, and stirred solution systems to focus on the differences in the apparent CO2 affinity, independent of their maximum reaction rates. Figures S7 and S8 show the current density at −0.9 V recorded during chronoamperometry after 100 s as a function of the CO2 partial pressure for the quiescent and stirred solution systems, after subtraction of the Ar-saturated background current. Below 50%, compared with the solution systems, the GDE exhibited up to 2-fold higher normalized currents, indicating the superior reactivity of the GDE under CO2-limited conditions. Furthermore, the normalized current vs P CO2 plot was analyzed using the Michaelis–Menten equation to obtain the apparent Michaelis constant (K M,app) using nonlinear regression analysis in Gnuplot (Figure ). jjmax=11+KM,appPCO2 where j and j max are the measured and maximum current densities, respectively. K M,app represents the CO2 partial pressure required for the half-maximal reaction rate and is an indicator of the apparent substrate affinity. Compared with the K M,app values of 9 ± 1% and 8.3 ± 0.7% for the quiescent and stirred solution systems, respectively, the GDE system exhibited the lowest K M,app of 2.6 ± 0.3%. The significantly lower K M,app of the GDE can be attributed to the differences in the equilibrium state of CO2, with the state change affecting the available substrate concentration. In the buffer at a pH of 6.5, dissolved CO2 and bicarbonate (HCO3 –) coexist in similar proportions due to the proximity of the pH to the first dissociation constant (pK a ≈ 6.04). Meneghello et al. demonstrated that Mo/W-containing FoDHs preferentially react with CO2 rather than HCO3 – as the substrate, suggesting that FoDH1 is likely to show the same property. Therefore, exclusion of the dissolution process can contribute to the higher apparent reactivity observed at lower CO2 partial pressures by maintaining a direct substrate supply to the catalytic layer. This result highlights the intrinsic advantage of the GDE, and its high affinity is particularly relevant for emission gases that typically contain CO2 levels ranging from below 5 to above 10–15%. −

Conclusions

A DET-type GDE for efficient CO2 reduction was successfully constructed using wild-type FoDH1. To establish a favorable interface for rapid ET between FoDH1 and the electrode, we employed surface modification with pyrene derivatives. In the GDE system, FoDH1-adsorbed PyNH2/MWCNT/WPCC achieved a CO2-reducing current density of −1.2 mA cm–2 in CV, which was approximately twice that of the pristine electrode. The GDE enabled efficient formate production, with an excellent Faradaic efficiency of 82 ± 5%. Additionally, we examined the apparent CO2 affinity by regulating the CO2 concentration over a range of 0.04 to 100%. The resulting K M,app value of 2.6 ± 0.3% in the GDE system was markedly lower than that in the conventional solution system. This enhanced CO2 affinity can be attributed to the direct CO2 supply to the catalytic layer, which avoids the limitations arising from CO2 equilibrium shifts in the electrolytes. Our approach represents a viable platform for CO2 utilization under low CO2 conditions and provides design insights for future bioelectrocatalytic systems using gaseous CO2 feedstocks. However, the present system still requires improvements for industrial-scale application, especially for the current density and long-term stability. − In the broader context of CO2 utilization technologies, the performance achieved by state-of-the-art material-based electrocatalytic systems, including current densities exceeding 10 mA cm–2 and stable operation over 24 h with alloy-based catalysts, represents important benchmarks for the future development of enzymatic CO2 reduction systems.

In parallel, optimizing the GDE configuration and ensuring continuous operation will be important for future practical applications. Although the current system is at an early stage of development, with current densities and long-term stability remaining to be established for industrially applicable systems such as continuous-flow operation, this work represents an initial demonstration of a DET-type bioelectrocatalytic CO2-recycling system under low CO2 level and may serve as a basis toward scalable and environmentally friendly recycling technologies.
