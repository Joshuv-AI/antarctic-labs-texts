Towards Inclusive Toxic Content Moderation: Addressing Vulnerabilities to Adversarial Attacks in Toxicity Classifiers Tackling LLM-generated Content
Shaz Furniturewala, Arkaitz Zubiaga

Abstract
The volume of machine-generated content online has grown dramatically due to the widespread use of Large Language Models (LLMs), leading to new challenges for content moderation systems. Conventional content moderation classifiers, which are usually trained on text produced by humans, suffer from misclassifications due to LLM-generated text deviating from their training data and adversarial attacks that aim to avoid detection. Present-day defence tactics are reactive rather than proactive, since they rely on adversarial training or external detection models to identify attacks. In this work, we aim to identify the vulnerable components of toxicity classifiers that contribute to misclassification, proposing a novel strategy based on mechanistic interpretability techniques. Our study focuses on fine-tuned BERT and RoBERTa classifiers, testing on diverse datasets spanning a variety of minority groups. We use adversarial attacking techniques to identify vulnerable circuits. Finally, we suppress these vulnerable circuits, improving performance against adversarial attacks. We also provide demographic-level insights into these vulnerable circuits, exposing fairness and robustness gaps in model training. We find that models have distinct heads that are either crucial for performance or vulnerable to attack and suppressing the vulnerable heads improves performance on adversarial input. We also find that different heads are responsible for vulnerability across different demographic groups, which can inform more inclusive development of toxicity detection models.

Introduction

With the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs), the Internet is seeing more machine-generated content than ever before
(
Wu et al. 2025a
;
Brooks, Eggert, and Peskoff 2024
)
, including toxic content
(
Barman, Guo, and Conlan 2024
)
. While content moderation systems have previously struggled to accurately classify this content
(
Warner et al. 2025
)
, the ability of users to automatically generate such toxic content using LLMs introduces a new challenge that is currently understudied. Most toxicity classification models are trained on human-generated text and on labeled data from a specific source, for example, Twitter posts
(
Mollas et al. 2022
;
Caselli et al. 2020
)
. LLMs, on the other hand, are trained on a much broader variety of text and can generate content that does not necessarily align with the training data seen by these classifiers, hence hindering model generalizability due to the inability to deal with new, unseen types of toxic texts
(
Yin and Zubiaga 2021
)
. This misalignment can lead to two key issues: first, classifiers may misclassify the toxicity of LLM-generated text due to distributional differences between (i) training data composed solely of human-generated content, and (ii) test data containing LLM-generated content; second, adversarially generated toxic text can be designed to evade detection entirely
(
Hartvigsen et al. 2022
)
. Research into adversarial prompting shows that it is possible to reduce the accuracy of text-transformer classifiers to nearly 0 with an effectively imperceptible change in the input
(
Ebrahimi et al. 2023
;
Roth et al. 2021
;
Sadrizadeh, Dolamic, and Frossard 2023
;
Li et al. 2019
)
.

There has been a recent body of work addressing this issue, however existing work on making models robust to such adversarial inputs continues to treat them as black boxes. These approaches typically rely on external models to detect and undo adversarial perturbations
(
Chen, Xu et al. 2022
;
Yoo et al. 2022
)
or use adversarial training against known attack methods
(
Bespalov et al. 2024
;
Samory et al. 2021
)
. However, these strategies require continuously identifying new attack methods and retraining models against them, making them reactive rather than proactive.

In this work, our goal is to move beyond black-box defenses by leveraging mechanistic interpretability techniques to identify internal circuits within classification models that are responsible for misclassification. In the context of neural networks, circuits refer to interpretable subnetworks of neurons that correspond to specific features or decision-making processes
(
Olah et al. 2020
)
.
García-Carrasco, Maté, and Trujillo 2024
outline a broad framework to identify these circuits and demonstrate its efficacy on a toy classification task. We set out to extend and evaluate this framework to the task of toxicity classification using both human- and machine-generated datasets
(
Hartvigsen et al. 2022
;
cjadams et al. 2019
)
. We patch the activations of each attention head in a classifier by setting them to 0. This way, we are able to identify heads that are crucial for model performance

A significant challenge in this area is to extend robustness techniques beyond English-language toxicity classifiers. While most prior works have focused on English datasets, harmful and adversarial content exists across multiple languages, often manifesting in ways that differ structurally and contextually from English.
Research on toxicity against specific minority groups is also rather sparse, with works focusing on toxicity as a whole and without focusing on target minority groups, or categorizing it into types of toxicity (insults, threats, etc.). Our objective is to make our framework of identifying vulnerable circuits language agnostic and hope to attribute circuits to various minority groups, making the improvement in the field more balanced across stakeholders.

By conducting for the first time a comprehensive mechanistic interpretability study on toxic content moderation, our work makes the following novel contributions:

;

Related Work

Toxicity Detection

Toxicity detection has been widely studied as a subproblem of abusive language and hate speech classification, in a diverse set of languages beyond English
(
Khan et al. 2024
;
Bensalem, Rosso, and Zitouni 2024
;
Krak et al. 2024
)
, as well as in multi- and cross-lingual settings
(
Jiang and Zubiaga 2024
;
Mnassri et al. 2024
)
. Early systems relied on keyword lists and linear classifiers trained on bag-of-words features
(
Nobata et al. 2016
;
Wulczyn, Thain, and Dixon 2017
)
, but these approaches struggled to capture context and often failed to distinguish between toxic and non-toxic uses of the same words. The introduction of transformer-based models, such as BERT and its variants, brought substantial improvements on benchmark datasets like Jigsaw
(
cjadams et al. 2019
)
, allowing models to handle phenomena such as sarcasm, negation, and paraphrasing more effectively
(
Yin and Zubiaga 2021
)
.

Recent work has also expanded the task definition, moving from simple binary classification to multi-label setups that distinguish between threats, insults, identity attacks, and other forms of harm
(
Caselli et al. 2020
)
. Datasets targeting implicit or subtle toxicity have been introduced to address cases where harmful speech avoids explicit slurs or profanity, such as ETHOS
(
Mollas et al. 2022
)
and ToxiGen
(
Hartvigsen et al. 2022
)
. Multilingual datasets like the latest Perspective API corpus improve coverage of minority groups and non-English content
(
Lees et al. 2022
)
.

Despite these advances, toxicity classifiers are known to be brittle under distributional shift. They frequently over-rely on lexical cues, leading to false positives when identity terms appear in benign contexts and false negatives when toxicity is conveyed implicitly
(
Yin and Zubiaga 2021
)
. Prior studies have highlighted demographic disparities in error rates, with comments mentioning marginalized identities disproportionately flagged as toxic
(
Dixon et al. 2018
)
. To address these issues, researchers have proposed debiasing techniques such as counterfactual data augmentation, adversarial training, and fairness-aware loss functions
(
Dinan et al. 2020
;
Samory et al. 2021
)
. However, most evaluations still use static test splits, which do not capture the challenges posed by synthetic or adversarially manipulated text. This gap motivates our analysis of classifiers on both human-written and LLM-generated toxicity, with the goal of revealing model vulnerabilities through mechanistic interpretability.

LLM-Generated Content and Adversarial Attacks

With the advent of LLMs and the associated ease to produce LLM-generated content at scale, a new need has emerged for building classification models that identify if a text is human- or LLM-generated
(
Wu et al. 2025b
)
. Research in LLM-generated text detection has evolved from development of classifiers that rely on linguistic features
(
Fröhling and Zubiaga 2021
)
, to developing watermarking techniques to facilitate detection
(
Pan et al. 2024
)
, statistics-based detectors
(
Vasilatos et al. 2023
)
, and neural-based detectors
(
Mitchell et al. 2023
)
. Despite increasing interest in detecting LLM-generated text and identifying what distinguishes it from human text, the use of LLMs for generation of toxic content has been comparatively understudied.

Indeed, the rapid adoption of LLMs has led to large volumes of synthetic toxic text, raising new challenges for moderation systems
(
Wu et al. 2025a
;
Brooks, Eggert, and Peskoff 2024
)
. Because toxicity classifiers are typically trained on human-written data, LLM outputs often fall outside their distribution, resulting in frequent misclassifications
(
Barman, Guo, and Conlan 2024
;
Warner et al. 2025
)
. Adversarial prompting further exacerbates this vulnerability, enabling attackers to generate toxic content that is semantically preserved but that circumvents detection
(
Ebrahimi et al. 2023
;
Roth et al. 2021
;
Sadrizadeh, Dolamic, and Frossard 2023
;
Li et al. 2019
)
. Prior defenses focus largely on adversarial training
(
Bespalov et al. 2024
;
Samory et al. 2021
)
or detection models that filter manipulated text
(
Chen, Xu et al. 2022
;
Yoo et al. 2022
)
. While it is effective against known perturbations, these approaches are reactive and struggle to anticipate new attack methods, therefore hindering future generalizability as attacking methods evolve.

Mechanistic Interpretability for Robustness

Mechanistic interpretability (MI) aims to identify functional circuits within neural networks, enabling a causal understanding of model behavior
(
Olah et al. 2020
;
Bereska and Gavves 2024
)
. Activation patching and causal tracing techniques have revealed specialized circuits in tasks such as syntactic role identification
(
Wang et al. 2022b
)
, arithmetic reasoning
(
Hanna, Balesni et al. 2023
)
, and factual recall
(
Conmy, Elhage et al. 2023
)
. Recently,
García-Carrasco, Maté, and Trujillo 2024
extended these methods to systematically discover circuits in smaller classification settings, demonstrating their potential for principled interventions. However, applications of MI to toxicity classification or adversarial robustness remain unexplored. By analyzing which attention heads are crucial for performance and which are vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, our work connects mechanistic interpretability with robustness research, providing a proactive strategy to mitigate attacks.

In contrast to prior work, we combine three threads: (1) the challenge of classifying both human- and LLM-generated toxic text, (2) the need for defenses that generalize across unseen adversarial attacks, and (3) the promise of mechanistic interpretability to expose internal model failure modes. Our approach identifies vulnerable circuits in transformer-based classifiers and suppresses them, improving robustness while revealing fairness gaps across demographic groups.

Methodology

To identify the components most vulnerable to misclassification, we follow the typical MI workflow
(
Conmy, Elhage et al. 2023
;
García-Carrasco, Maté, and Trujillo 2024
)
, which has been studied in other contexts including circuit identification for tasks like indirect object identification
(
Wang et al. 2022b
)
and mathematical ability
(
Hanna, Balesni et al. 2023
)
in GPT-2. We will perform these experiments on finetuned variants of BERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
as those are still the most common and efficient classifiers, and applied in the context of toxicity detection. We aim to identify the components that are affected leading to misclassifications, to then mitigate this effect.

Datasets and Metrics

For investigation within the context of the task of toxicity classification, and from the two proposed angles that include human- and LLM-generated toxic texts, we use the following two datasets:

ToxiGen

(
Hartvigsen et al. 2022
)
. This is a machine-generated English dataset containing 251k implicitly toxic and benign statements mentioning 13 minority groups annotated by humans. All the data was generated using GPT-3 and a constrained decoding tool that reduced the accuracy of PerspectiveAPI
(
Lees et al. 2022
)
to 40%. This data is evenly split between toxic and non-toxic samples. Table
2
shows examples from this dataset.

Jigsaw Dataset

(
cjadams et al. 2019
)
. This is a dataset originally containing 223k Wikipedia comments in English classified as toxic or non-toxic. There are also 6 toxicity subtypes that each comment is classified on, however, we don’t use those labels in this work. Given our interest in focusing on target groups, we used the subset of the Jigsaw dataset (50k samples) that has been annotated with 24 demographic groups. This data is split between 46k toxic and 4k non-toxic samples.

We selected these two datasets to capture complementary aspects of the toxicity classification problem. ToxiGen is fully machine-generated using GPT-3, which allows us to study how classifiers handle adversarial and implicit toxic text produced by LLMs. In contrast, the Jigsaw dataset consists of human-written comments from Wikipedia discussions, providing a natural benchmark for real-world moderation. By analyzing both, we can evaluate whether vulnerabilities arise specifically from the distributional shift of LLM-generated text or whether they reflect broader weaknesses in toxicity detection models.

We use two complementary evaluation metrics to quantify the model’s performance:

Binary Cross Entropy Loss.

It quantifies the difference between predicted probabilities and actual binary class labels (0 or 1). It is calculated as:

\text{Loss}=-\left[y\cdot\log(p)+(1-y)\cdot\log(1-p)\right]

where
y
is the true label and
p
is the predicted probability for class 1, i.e. the toxic class. The lower the value, the better the model’s performance.

Accuracy.

The fraction of labels correctly predicted by the model.

Step 1. Identifying Crucial Heads Using Activation Patching

Activation patching, involves replacing the activations of a component of the model with those generated by a “corrupted prompt” and comparing the difference in performance. Some works replace the activations with zeros
(
Olsson et al. 2022
)
, while others replace it with the mean activation to eliminate all information
(
Wang et al. 2022a
)
. We use the former method based on activations with zeros. This activation patching is sequentially performed on all attention heads of the model and if patching a particular head drastically decreases the model’s performance, then that head is significantly contributing to the task and it is a
crucial
head.

Step 2. Adversarial Attacks

Once we have identified the relevant circuits, we use adversarial attacks on the model by modifying the inputs and observing these circuits. We aim to isolate components that activate in response to specific types of attacks or aspects of the input such as specific minority groups or languages.

PGD BERT-Attack

(
Waghela, Sen, and Rakshit 2024
)
. This is a white-box adversarial attack that generates perturbations in the embedding space using Projected Gradient Descent (PGD). It first computes the gradient of the loss with respect to the input embeddings and iteratively applies small changes using another BERT model to maximize the loss, while keeping the perturbations within a bounded norm ball. These modified embeddings are then mapped back to the closest valid tokens for replacement. Figure

is a depiction of BERT-Attack, the attack that PGD BERT Attack is based on.

Experimental Setup

We conduct experiments to evaluate whether interpretable circuit-level interventions can improve the adversarial robustness of toxicity classifiers. Our pipeline consists of model training, adversarial input generation, mechanistic analysis of internal components, and targeted suppression of vulnerable attention heads. We used 1 H100 GPU to perform all the experiments.

Model Training

We trained a BERT classifier using each of our datasets under consideration. On the Jigsaw dataset, with 160k training samples and 63k test samples, we achieved an accuracy of 92.86%. On the Toxigen dataset, with 188k training samples and 63k test samples, we achieved an accuracy of 84.5%. In addition to the trained classifier, we also tested the RoBERTa classifier by
Logacheva et al. 2022
on Jigsaw which achieved an accuracy of 93.4%. We use these as our reference performance scores that we then use for comparison.

Adversarial Attack Procedure

For the test subsets of both datasets, we generated corresponding adversarial samples using the PGD BERT Attack. This attack method successfully perturbs 53.5% of the Jigsaw dataset and 98.2% of the Toxigen dataset. Of these successful perturbations we selected the perturbed samples that had a
>
0.95 cosine similarity with the original text. 54% of the successful Jigsaw samples and 19% of the successful Toxigen samples met this criterion.

Our findings demonstrate that mechanistic interpretability can improve the robustness and fairness of toxicity classifiers. Rather than relying solely on external detectors or adversarial training, we propose using model-internal signals to uncover and address failure modes. This proactive strategy enhances not only classification accuracy under attack but also trust in automated moderation systems.

Conclusion

In this work, we present a novel framework for improving the robustness of transformer-based toxicity classifiers against adversarially generated text, including content produced by Large Language Models (LLMs). Our approach moves beyond black-box defenses by leveraging mechanistic interpretability to identify attention heads that are either crucial to model performance or vulnerable to adversarial manipulation. Through extensive experiments on both human-written (Jigsaw) and machine-generated (ToxiGen) datasets, we demonstrate three key findings: (1) adversarial perturbations can severely degrade model performance, (2) crucial and vulnerable attention heads are disjoint, allowing us to suppress the latter without large losses in clean accuracy, and (3) vulnerabilities are not evenly distributed across demographic groups, revealing fairness concerns that must be addressed in robust content moderation systems. Together, these findings illustrate that mechanistic interpretability can play a central role in proactively hardening toxicity classifiers, improving both accuracy and fairness under attack.

Our results have important implications for the future of automated moderation. Rather than relying solely on adversarial training or external detectors, which are reactive and brittle to unseen attacks, our framework provides a proactive path toward defense by identifying and intervening on the internal components most responsible for misclassification. This contributes to building more trustworthy, transparent, and equitable toxicity detection models, supporting safer online discourse even in the presence of adaptive adversaries.

Limitations and Future Work

While our findings are promising, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, our experiments were restricted to English-language datasets and primarily evaluated BERT and RoBERTa models. As a result, it remains unclear whether our conclusions generalize to multilingual settings or to newer LLM-based classifiers used in production. Additionally, activation patching and adversarial generation are computationally expensive, limiting the scalability of our approach for very large models and real-time moderation. These limitations motivate several directions for future work:

Runtime attack detection and suppression

We aim to move from offline mitigation to online defense, we have seen in Figure 2 that the
vulnerable
attention heads still have some contribution to clean input accuracy. Indiscriminately setting them to zero is not the most efficient method of adversarial attack suppression. By monitoring the activations of known vulnerable heads during inference, we can proactively detect potential adversarial inputs. If such heads are triggered, their activations can be suppressed or replaced with robust alternatives. Further, the flagged inputs can be stored and used to build an adversarial training dataset.

Identifying circuits containing multiple attention heads across layers

So far we have only looked at the impact of single attention heads. From these experiments we observed that the highest impact was from heads in the first 2-3 layers. However, interpretability research has shown that heads in different layers often compose to form complex circuits that perform higher order thinking than a single head is capable of. We want to identify such pairs or triples and test whether suppressing them together further improves attack resistance.

Extension to Multilingual and Minority Group-Specific Classifiers

Given the disparities in adversarial vulnerabilities across demographics, future work will also focus on understanding how linguistic and cultural structures influence model robustness, especially in low-resource languages or for underrepresented groups.
