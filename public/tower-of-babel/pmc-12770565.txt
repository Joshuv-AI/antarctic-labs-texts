Fluorescence-based bioassay for the detection of changes in mitochondrial physiology during artemisinin-induced growth arrest of blood-stage Plasmodium falciparum
Authors: Narisara Whangviboonkij, Kasem Kulkeaw, Mallika Imwong, Porntida Kobpornchai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of artemisinin (ART) resistance in Plasmodium falciparum challenges global malaria treatment programs. To avoid ART-induced death, a proportion of ring-stage parasites arrest and resume cyclic intraerythrocytic growth, leading to recrudescent malaria. However, the direct tracking of mitochondrial physiological and dynamic changes underlying this survival mechanism in P. falciparum remains poorly characterized by traditional mitochondria-based fluorochromes. The aim of this study was to use the mitochondrion specific, pH-sensitive fluorochrome Mtphagy to monitor mitochondrial changes during ART exposure. Mtphagy dye can be detected in the intact mitochondria of all blood-stage parasites. After dihydroartemisinin (DHA) exposure, the fluorescence intensity of Mtphagy dye is greater in the early ring-form trophozoites, indicating a decrease in pH. Despite the presence of undetectable lysosomes, pH-sensitive Mtphagy and mitochondria-specific MitoTracker signals slightly reduced colocalization after DHA-induced growth arrest. Inhibition of mitochondrial fission and the PI3K-mediated pathway significantly reduced the number of Mtphagy-positive parasites and was associated with delayed recrudescence in the DHA-treated parasites. Taken together, these data demonstrate that the Mtphagy-based fluorescence assay serves as a useful tool for detecting mitochondrial changes during growth arrest. Therefore, targeting changes in mitochondrial dynamics may offer a promising strategy for identifying, monitoring, and potentially overcoming ART resistance in malaria parasites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29777-7.Keywords: Plasmodium falciparum, Growth-arrested parasite, Mitochondria-based fluorochromes, Artemisinin resistance, RecrudescenceSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cell biology, Diseases, Physiology

Introduction

Artemisinin (ART) and its derivatives are still the first-line treatments for malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum, which is one of the four Plasmodium species that has caused the greatest number of deaths1. ARTs specifically target the asexual developmental stages of parasites and are less effective when the parasites are in ring stages. Parasites in the early ring stage are less active in hemoglobin digestion. In the parasite food vacuole, ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) heme cleaves the endoperoxide bridge, resulting in the formation of carbon-centered radicals. This process leads to ART activation, which potentially participates in alkylating reactions with biological targets such as heme and proteins, contributing to parasite death2–4. Thus, ARTs are fast-acting drugs that eliminate all susceptible stages and are clinically used in combination with partner drugs to eliminate parasites in other blood stages that are tolerant to ARTs. ART-based combination therapies (ACTs) are recommended for the treatment of uncomplicated malaria. Despite the effectiveness of ACTs, ART resistance was first reported in western Cambodia, Pailin Province, in 2009 before it spread across the Greater Mekong Subregion (GMS)5 and other parts of the world, including sub-Saharan Africa6. On the basis of both genetic and clinical evidence, a point mutation in the parasite Kelch13 domain gene (K13) is reportedly associated with delayed clearance of the parasite in individuals infected with P. falciparum, conferring the primary cause of ART resistance7. Emerging resistance to ARTs challenges attempts to control and eradicate malaria. Therefore, establishing tools for a surveillance system in drug-resistant testing is urgently needed for ongoing research and the development of new antimalarial strategies.

In drug testing for blood-stage malaria-causing parasites, ART resistance or tolerance is defined as more than 1% parasite survival in the ring-stage survival assay (RSA), in which early ring-stage parasites are exposed to 700 nM dihydroartemisinin (DHA) for 6 h, followed by microscopic assessment of parasite survival 3 days later8. Morphological similarities between growth-arrested ring-stage parasites and pyknotic forms pose challenges to their precise differentiation under microscopic examination9. Both ART-sensitive and ART-resistant parasites are capable of temporarily arresting their growth to increase survival when exposed to drug pressure. However, different genetic backgrounds of malaria parasites potentially influence the timing of recrudescence in in vitro experiments10,11. Interestingly, early ring-stage P. falciparum arrests its growth by lowering metabolic levels12 but retains partial active mitochondria13. ARTs are associated with physiological changes in mitochondria, causing depolarized mitochondrial membranes and interference with the electron transfer chain in malaria-causing parasites14,15. Under temporary ART-induced growth arrest, Plasmodium mitochondrial morphology changes, including a shorter distance between mitochondria and nuclei and an enlarged mitochondrial mass16. Moreover, the inhibition of mitochondrial activity increases the susceptibility of K13 mutant parasites after DHA treatment, suggesting the pivotal role of mitochondria in parasite survival17. Therefore, tracking mitochondrial functions during DHA exposure and recrudescence provides insight into drug resistance mechanisms.

Current fluorochromes used to label the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) can serve as functional viability markers in Plasmodium parasites18–20. Nevertheless, their fluorophores have certain limitations. For example, rhodamine 123 and JC-1 have been reported to delay parasite regrowth21,22. Growth-arrested or early ring-stage parasites with low ΔΨm often exhibit weak or misleading MitoTracker signals, which can interrupt interpretation23. Importantly, none of the available fluorophores can reliably report changes in mitochondrial physiology in terms of dynamics in P. falciparum. Mtphagy dye is a multifunctional tool for studying mitochondrial functions, especially mitophagy24–27. The distinct chemical moiety of Mtphagy dye, specifically, the mitochondria-localizing triphenylphosphonium and highly sensitive fluorescence perylene-3,4-dicarboximide, allows the assessment of mitochondrial functions. Moreover, this fluorochrome contains chloromethyl and piperazine groups, which immobilize specific mitochondrial proteins and serve as pH sensors for mitochondrial acidification28. Thus, Mtphagy dye results in a brighter fluorescent signal when mitochondria are fused with the acidic lysosomes of parkin-expressing HeLa cells28. However, direct demonstration of mitochondrial dynamics, including mitochondrial fission, fusion, and mitophagy, remains limited for P. falciparum. The aim of this study is to use Mtphagy, a mitochondria-specific fluorescence dye, to monitor pH changes and mitochondrial physiological changes during ART exposure in early ring-stage P. falciparum. The use of Mtphagy dye allows tracking of alterations in the mitochondrial pH and dynamic changes associated with parasite survival during artemisinin-induced growth arrest. Flow cytometric analysis provides quantitative data that reveal a brighter fluorescent signal after ART treatment. Three-dimensional colocalization of the subcellular structure was used to assess the dynamic changes in mitochondria. Small molecule-based inhibition of mitochondrial fission and autophagosome formation confirmed the pivotal role of mitochondria in growth-arrested P. falciparum.

Results

The mitochondrial regulatory process is involved in DHA-induced growth arrest in P. falciparum

Direct evidence of selective mitochondrial degradation via autophagy has never been demonstrated in P. falciparum, particularly during the growth-arrested ring stage. Thus, we first examined transcriptional changes following DHA exposure. The gene candidates were selected from previous studies as alternative indicators of mitochondrial fission (dynamin-like proteins; PfDYN1 and PfDYN2), mitochondrial outer membrane integrity (voltage-dependent anion channel; PfVDAC), and autophagic membrane formation (autophagy-related protein 8; PfATG8). The transcript levels of PfDYN1, PfDYN2, and PfVDAC were significantly elevated in both the ART-sensitive K1 and ART-resistant IPC-5202 P. falciparum strains. In parallel, the mRNA level of PfATG8 increased approximately twofold (Fig. 1A). To further investigate the reversibility of these responses, we treated parasites with two small-molecule inhibitors: Mdivi-1, a selective inhibitor of DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission, and wortmannin, a PI3K inhibitor that blocks autophagy. The half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50s) of the small-molecule inhibitors against the K1 and IPC-5202 strains were first calculated. A fluorochrome-based flow cytometric assay was used to assess parasite viability. The viable parasites were Visafe green (VSG)-positive and propidium iodide (PI)-negative, whereas the dead parasites were positive for both VSG and PI. The percentage of dead parasites increased with increasing inhibitor concentration (see Supplementary Figure S1 online). The IC50 values of Mdivi-1 against the K1 and IPC-5202 parasites were 93.07 ± 1.46 and 106.8 ± 1.59 µM, respectively. Those of wortmannin against K1 and IPC-5202 parasites were 229 ± 1.11 µM and 84.98 ± 1.31 µM, respectively (Fig. 1B). Inhibitor concentrations lower than the cytotoxic dose were determined to be 10 µM Mdivi-1 and 20 µM wortmannin, on the basis of previous studies29,30. Following exposure to Mdivi-1 and wortmannin inhibitors, the gene expression of PfDYN1, PfDYN2, PfVDAC, and PfATG8 decreased (Fig. 1A). The downregulation of these genes upon inhibitor treatment indicates that DHA induces a temporary ring-stage growth arrest that involves mitochondrial remodeling and degradation processes. These findings support the interpretation that mitochondrial degradation through an autophagy-like mechanism plays a role in the parasite’s adaptive response under ART pressure.

Mtphagy dye does not impair parasite growth

To verify that Mtphagy dye can be used for live imaging of cells, its cytotoxic effects on parasite growth and viability were assessed. Early ring-stage P. falciparum parasites of K1 and IPC5202 were subjected to Mtphagy dye labeling before and after DHA exposure. In the first experimental setting, parasites were stained with the dye and then treated with 700 nM DHA or 0.1% DMSO, referred to as the “pre-drug Mtphagy staining” condition. In the second setting, parasites were first treated with 700 nM DHA or 0.1% DMSO for 6 h, after which the drug was removed and parasites were stained, referred to as the “post-drug Mtphagy staining” condition. The DHA- or DMSO-exposed parasites without Mthpagy dye staining were used as unstained parasite controls (Fig. 2A). Parasite growth was monitored via standard microscopic examination for 14 days of intraerythrocytic development. The Giemsa-stained parasitemia of the pre- and post-DMSO Mtphagy-stained parasites increased from an initial 2% to 12% for the K1 strain and 8% for the IPC-5202 strain on day 8, which was similar to the findings in the unstained parasite control (Fig. 2B). After DHA exposure, parasite recrudescence occurred on days 12 and 8 for the K1 and IPC-5202 strains, with a percentage of parasitemia of more than 2% (Fig. 2B and see Supplementary Figure S2 online). Moreover, the higher proportion of parasites at normal developmental stages, together with the reduced incidence of the condensed form demonstrated no growth defects of Mtphagy-stained K1 and IPC5202 parasites (see Supplementary Figure S2 online). To confirm the viability of the parasites, flow cytometry was performed as described previously31. In the flow cytometric profiles, a population of VSG-positive and PI-negative cells indicated viable parasites, whereas double-positive fluorescent cells were nonviable parasites (see Supplementary Figure S3 online). The population of viable parasites in the pre- and post-drug Mtphagy staining did not significantly differ from that in the unstained control. In contrast, a larger fraction of ART-resistant IPC-5202 stain appeared nonviable compared with the ART-sensitive K1 strain (see Supplementary Figures S3 online). On day 6 after monitoring the recrudescence of parasites, no change in ratio of surviving DHA-treated to surviving DMSO-treated parasites was detected in the pre- and post- drug Mtphagy staining, as determined by flow cytometry. Notably, the percentage of surviving DHA-treated to surviving DMSO-treated parasites in the ART-resistant IPC-5202 strain was greater than that of the ART-sensitive K1 strain (Fig. 2C). Together these data show that, despite a higher fraction of acutely damaged-appearing ART-resistant parasites at the measured timepoint, the parasites contained a viable subpopulation that resumes growth earlier than ART-sensitive parasites (see Supplementary Figures S2 online). Therefore, the Mtphagy dye did not retard parasite growth or impair parasite survival, making it suitable for live-cell imaging analysis.

Fluorescence signal of mtphagy dye is associated with growth arrest of P. falciparum in response to ART exposure

To ensure that Mtphagy dye accumulates in the mitochondria of malaria patients, parasites subjected to Mtphagy staining for 6 h before and after DMSO or DHA exposure were incubated with Mtphagy dye and MitoTracker CMXRos. In the confocal microscopy images, the emitted fluorescent signal of Mtphagy dye is shown in green, whereas that of the MitoTracker CMXRos is shown in red. The Mtphagy dye was located at the same position as MitoTracker CMXRos. Moreover, Mtphagy dye was also detected in the mitochondria of all asexual blood-stage parasites (see Supplementary Figure S4 online). To determine precisely the localization of the Mtphagy dye, the peaks of the fluorescence intensity distribution of the Mtphagy dye and MitoTracker CMXRos were overlaid in a line graph and showed the same pattern, implying that Mtphagy dye accumulates in the mitochondria (see Supplementary Figure S4 online). These results revealed that Mtphagy dye is a mitochondrion-specific probe in P. falciparum. To evaluate the ability of Mtphagy dye to monitor the mitochondrial changes of P. falciparum after DHA exposure, early ring stages at 0 to 3 hpi were incubated with 700 nM DHA or 0.1% DMSO (vehicle control) for 6 h before they were incubated with Mtphagy dye, Lyso dye, and MitoTracker Red CMXRos. By using a confocal microscope, a fluorescence signal of Mtphagy dye was observed in the parasitized erythrocytes of both the ART-sensitive K1 and ART-resistant IPC-5202 strains after DMSO and DHA exposure (Fig. 3A). The fluorescence intensity distribution demonstrated that the Mtphagy dye was localized at the same position as the MitoTracker (Fig. 3B). Notably, the fluorescent signal of Lyso dye was not observed in the DHA- and DMSO-exposed parasites (Fig. 3A and C). Nevertheless, the fluorescence enhancement of the Mtphagy dye was investigated in human neuroblastoma SHSY-5Y cells upon acidification at the lysosome compartment (see Supplementary Figure S5 online). To investigate the extent of the colocalization area in a 3D manner, holotomography imaging was performed. In the DMSO-exposed parasites, the localization of the Mtphagy dye was the same as that of the MitoTracker dye (yellow color in Fig. 3C and Supplementary Figure S6 online). On the other hand, the overlapping area of both fluorochromes decreased in the DHA-exposed parasites, confirming the change in the location of mitochondria when they were exposed to DHA (Fig. 3C). The overlapping area of both fluorescent signals was analyzed and displayed as an R value. The fluorescent signals of the Mtphagy dye mostly overlapped with those of MitoTracker Red CMXRos (R = 0.8) in the DMSO treatment control experiments with both the K1 and IPC-5202 strains. In contrast, the DHA treatment experiments revealed a slightly overlapping area of both fluorescent signals (R = 0.6) (Fig. 3D), implying a change in the positions of the Mtphagy-positive and MitoTracker-positive areas. Together, these fluorescence images suggest a dynamic change in the location of mitochondria in response to ART treatment.

Mtphagy dye facilitates the detection of growth-arrested P. falciparum

Since the fluorescent signal of Mtphagy dye is increased by acidification, the increase in fluorescence intensity reflects the early damaged mitochondria and fusion of autophagosomes after DHA exposure (Fig. 4A). To demonstrate the use of Mtphagy dye for detecting the growth arrest of P. falciparum after an antimalarial drug test, 3-hpi ring-stage parasites were first exposed to 700 nM DHA or 0.1% DMSO for 6 h. After DHA removal and continuous culture for 66 h, the parasites were subjected to staining with Mtphagy dye and a cell viability assay using a flow cytometer. The results are displayed as scatter plots, in which forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) of the fluorescence intensity were plotted on the X- and Y-axes on a logarithmically, respectively, to define the debris-free cell populations (gate R1, Fig. 4B). The cells in the gated R1 population were then further profiled on the basis of the fluorescence intensities of VSG and PI (X- and Y-axes, respectively). Four-quadrant (Q) channels were set to indicate a cutoff for positive and negative signals on the basis of the fluorescence intensity of the unstained sample. The population of VSG‒positive and PI‒negative cells in quadrant no. 3 indicated viable or growth-arrested parasites. For the DMSO exposure experiments, ring-stage P. falciparum were gated separately to exclude strong fluorescence intensity bias from the trophozoite and schizont stages (red box). Among these viable parasites, the Mtphagy dye-positive cells were gated separately, and the fluorescence intensity of these cells was compared to that of the unstained control (Gate Q5, Fig. 4B). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the percentages of surviving DHA-treated to surviving DMSO-treated parasites in ART-sensitive and ART-resistant strains were 7.8% and 15.4%, respectively (Fig. 4C). A greater number of Mtphagy dye-positive parasites were found in the DHA-exposed experiment (K1; 26.2% and IPC-5202; 21.2%). In contrast, the number of Mtphagy dye-positive parasites in the DMSO-exposed control decreased to 18.3% and 13.7% for K1 and IPC-5202, respectively. On the basis of the overlaid histogram of the emitted Mtphagy fluorescent signals from the two groups, the maximum fluorescence intensity of the DHA-exposed parasites (blue color) was slightly greater than that of the 0.1% DMSO-exposed parasites (orange color) (Fig. 4D). The percentages of Mtphagy dye-positive cells among the DHA-exposed parasites were greater than those among the DMSO-exposed control parasites (Fig. 4E, p < 0.01 for K1 and p < 0.05 for IPC-5202). Moreover, a significant increase in the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of Mtphagy dye was associated with the growth arrest of parasites after DHA exposure (Fig. 4F, p < 0.05 for K1 and IPC-5202). Thus, Mtphagy dye is useful for detecting growth arrest in DHA-exposed P. falciparum.

Mtphagy dye could reflect physiological changes in mitochondria during artemisinin-induced growth arrest in P. falciparum

To demonstrate the utility of Mtphagy dye for monitoring physiological changes in mitochondria during artemisinin-induced growth arrest, mitochondrial fission and autophagosome formation were investigated via small molecule inhibitors. Tightly synchronized ring-stage parasites were administered at two different time points: (a) the early phase (t = 3 h) and (b) the late phase (t = 18 h) after DHA exposure (Fig. 5A). The flow cytometric profiles of viable parasites (VSG+/PI‒) were set on the basis of the fluorescence intensity of the Mtphagy dye in comparison with that of unstained samples (see Supplementary Figure S7 online). In the early phase of inhibitor treatment, Mdivi significantly reduced the proportion of Mtphagy-positive cells in both ART-sensitive and ART-resistant parasites (Fig. 5B, subpanel a; p < 0.001 for K1 and p < 0.01 for IPC-5202). In contrast, the percentage of surviving ART-resistant parasites exposed to DHA plus wortmannin was not significantly different from those treated with DHA alone (Fig. 5B, subpanel a). In the late-phase experiment, the percentage of Mtphagy dye-positive cells significantly increased in the parasites exposed to only DHA (Fig. 5B, subpanel b; p < 0.001 for K1 and p < 0.01 for IPC-5202). When Mdivi-1 and wortmannin were added to the 18-h DHA-exposed parasites, the percentage of Mtphagy dye-positive cells significantly decreased (Fig. 5B, subpanel b; p < 0.01 for K1 and p < 0.05 for IPC-5202) compared with that in the DHA-exposed parasites. Next, the effects of inhibitors on the recrudescence of the DHA-exposed parasites were assessed via standard microscopy. Blood smears were prepared and stained with Giemsa dye. Parasitemia was monitored for a total of 14 days after DHA treatment (see Supplementary Figure S8 online). The morphology of DHA-exposed K1 parasites was observed over 8 days. In contrast, DHA-exposed ART-resistant parasites started to resume normal cyclic development at 6 days posttreatment (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Figure S9 online). Treatment with the mitophagy inhibitors Mdivi-1 and wortmannin prolonged the growth arrest of the DHA-exposed K1 and IPC-5202 strains (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Figure S9 online). These findings suggest that Mtphagy dye is applicable for monitoring mitochondrial changes during artemisinin-induced growth arrest in P. falciparum.

Discussion

The temporary arrest in the ring-to-trophozoite transition is strong evidence that P. falciparum can escape the lethal effect of artemisinin. These resistant parasites exhibit an irregular cellular ultrastructure with a condensed nucleus and scant cytoplasm but retain active mitochondria32. Growth arrest and quiescence are associated with alterations in mitochondrial physiology and function, which are essential for their survival16,17. Evidence of ART resistance has been reported in the interaction of the K13 protein with parasite mitochondria after DHA exposure33. In higher eukaryotes, dysfunctional mitochondria are regulated through fission, fusion, motility, and mitophagy—collectively known as mitochondrial dynamics—which effectively maintain intracellular homeostasis34. The beneficial effects of mitophagy include not only the removal of abnormal mitochondria but also the recovery of healthy mitochondria, which are essential for cell survival35,36. The inhibition of ART-induced mitophagy in breast cancer cells increases the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitochondrial damage, resulting in cell death37. ART induces mitophagy through the degradation of mitochondrial proteins and the activation of the PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) and E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin (PRKN)-dependent pathways38,39. However, this process has not been fully characterized in malaria parasites. This led us to further investigate whether the growth arrest of ring-stage parasites was involved in the changes in mitochondrial dynamics after ART exposure. Among the alternative-related mitochondrial dynamics genes, the P. falciparum-encoded dynamin-like protein 1 and 2 (PfDYN1 and PfDYN2) genes have similar DNA sequences and structural homology to dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) in mammalian cells40. The regulation of PfDYN1 and PfDYN2 gene expression plays a role in mitochondrial division41,42. Upon the increase in mitochondrial fission, PINK1 degradation by presenilin-associated rhomboid-like proteases (PARLs) is inhibited, resulting in the stabilization of PINK1 to promote the recruitment of PRKN to mitochondria. Then, activated PRKN induces the ubiquitination of mitochondrial substrates, including mitofusin 1 and 2 (MFN1, 2) and voltage-dependent anion channels 1 and 3 (VDAC1, 3)43, leading to recognition by the autophagic machinery. The ubiquitination of mitochondria is characterized by the presence of the autophagic membrane that is formed from MAP1LC3/LC3 (known as ATG8 in malaria)30. The ubiquitinated mitochondria enhance degradation via the autolysosome process. Finally, this process increases mitophagy, which subsequently facilitates cell survival. Our study revealed that DHA-induced growth arrest in early-ring-stage parasites is potentially attributed to mitochondrial fission and autophagic membrane formation through increased transcript levels of the PfDYN1, PfDYN2, PfVDAC and PfATG8 genes (Fig. 1). In addition, treatment with two small-molecule inhibitors, Mdivi-1, a mitochondrial fission inhibitor, and wortmannin, a PI3K inhibitor, resulted in downregulated expression of the PfDYN1, PfDYN2, PfVDAC, and PfATG8 genes, implying a decrease in mitochondrial division44 and autophagosome formation30,45. When the ART-sensitive and ART-resistant P. falciparum K1 and IPC-5202 strains were compared, the IPC-5202 parasites presented a lower level of mitochondrial function-related gene expression, implying the involvement of mutations in ART-induced mitochondrial toxicity. Considering the role of autophagy in ART resistance, the IC50 of wortmannin decreased twofold in the IPC-5202 strain, emphasizing the importance of PI3K-mediated pathways in the survival of ART-resistant parasites38,46. This information will aid in understanding how drug-resistant P. falciparum adapts after DHA exposure. Therefore, monitoring mitochondrial physiological changes using mitochondrial-based dyes represents a valuable approach for assessing parasite responses to ART-induced stress and identifying potential targets for overcoming drug resistance.

Recently, fluorescent probes have been developed to visualize mitophagy in real time with no requirement for plasmid transfection into cells28,47,48. Applicable for visualizing mitochondria‒lysosome complexes has been demonstrated in human epithelial49,50 and liver cells51. However, tracking autophagic‒mitochondrial interactions during DHA-induced growth arrest in P. falciparum remains elusive. This study revealed that the use of a mitochondrion specific, pH-sensitive fluorochrome, called Mtphagy, is simple, has low toxicity, and is stable in live-cell imaging for malaria parasites. Compared with conventional fluorophores such as MitoTracker and Rhodamine 123, Mtphagy dye comprises a mitochondria-targeting triphenylphosphonium moiety and a highly sensitive perylene-based fluorophore, which together allow precise detection of mitochondrial physiological changes without interfering with parasite viability. Moreover, Mtphagy dye provides clearer and more reliable mitochondrial signals, even in parasites undergoing growth arrest, likely because of its enhanced retention and sensitivity to pH-related changes in mitochondrial functions28. Here, brighter fluorescence intensity of the Mtphagy dye was detected in the DHA-exposed ART-sensitive and ART-resistant parasites than in the vehicle-treated control parasites, suggesting that the pH decreased. With higher-resolution 3D imaging via a holotomography microscope, DHA-induced growth-arrested P. falciparum displayed a reduction in the overlapping area of the Mtphagy dye and MitoTracker Red CMXRos, suggesting that the location of the mitochondria changed (Fig. 3C). However, the ultrastructures of ring-stage and growth-arrested P. falciparum after one day of DHA exposure revealed the absence of a vesicle-like structure or typical lysosome52, which is consistent with a negative readout of Lyso dye, a fluorescent indicator of lysosomes. Although the ability to stain Mtphagy and Lyso dyes confirmed the colocalization of the Mtphagy-positive fluorescent signal at the lysosomal compartment of human neuroblastoma SHSY-5Y cells upon acidification (see Supplementary Figure S5 online), the absence of a lysosome-detecting fluorescent dye needs to be verified to determine whether the DHA-induced growth arrest in P. falciparum is associated with early-stage processing of dysfunctional mitochondria without subsequent lysosomal degradation. The use of advanced image microscopy provides a more accurate structure and reduces artifacts due to fluorescent signals. For example, transmission electron microscopy enhances the resolution of imaging and allows observation of the ultrastructures of subcellular compartments to confirm morphological changes and colocalization of lysosomes and mitochondria during degradation in parasitophorous vacuoles52,53. Collectively, the Mtphagy dye effectively detects mitochondrial remodeling in response to DHA; however, the absence of lysosomal visualization and Mtphagy–lysosome colocalization suggests a disruption or incomplete execution of the mitophagy-like pathway in P. falciparum. Mtphagy dye may be used as an alternative mitochondrial indicator to assess drug-induced growth arrest in Plasmodium falciparum. In combination with other fluorochromes, e.g., DNA-binding fluorescent dyes, this approach is useful for characterizing the growth arrest of Plasmodium falciparum. To prevent DHA-induced parasite death, we improved conventional RSA, similar to the methods used in our previous study31. The growth arrest stage of the K1 and IPC-5202 parasites could be distinguished from nonviable cells via fluorescence-enhancing RSAs. This method allows the identification of viable and nonviable parasites using detergent and two DNA-binding fluorochromes: VSG and PI. Thus, the Mtphagy-positive signals resulted from growth-arrested but not dead parasites. Compared with that in DMSO-exposed ring-stage parasites, the increase in Mtphagy dye-positive cells was associated with DHA exposure. Nevertheless, no significant difference in the maximum fluorescence intensity of Mtphagy dye was observed between the ART-sensitive and ART-resistant P. falciparum strains following DHA exposure (Fig. 4). In this context, the different genetic backgrounds of K13 mutations need further investigation. Overall, the combination of Mtphagy dye and fluorochrome-based RSA was an effective strategy for detecting the susceptibility of P. falciparum to ARTs.

The application of Mtphagy dye in assessing mitochondrial responses and parasite survival has been evaluated during antimalarial treatment. Our findings revealed that DHA-induced growth arrest in early ring-stage parasites is potentially associated with the activation of mitochondrial dynamics. The addition of Mdivi, a mitochondrial fission inhibitor, and Wortmannin, a PI3K inhibitor that suppresses autophagosome formation, at different time points relative to DHA treatment produced distinct effects on mitochondrial responses. In the early phase (t = 3 h), the inhibitors were added to examine the parasite’s stress response during DHA treatment. The reduced proportion of Mtphagy-positive cells in parasites exposed to DHA plus Mdivi reflected early mitochondrial remodeling in both ART-sensitive K1 and ART-resistant IPC5202 strains. In contrast, when added after 18 h of DHA treatment at late phase, Mdivi-1 and Wortmannin significantly decreased the proportion of Mtphagy-positive K1 and IPC5202 parasites, suggesting DHA-induced growth-arrested parasites involving mitochondrial fission and autophagosome formation (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the recrudescence of parasites was delayed after treatment with DHA and inhibitors, implying that physiological alterations in mitochondria may influence parasite survival and recovery (Fig. 5C). Given the cellular uptake rate and exposure time, the use of small-molecule inhibitors has a transient effect. Gene deletion in P. falciparum needs to be further studied to result in permanent loss of protein function. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockdown of mitochondrial dynamics or autophagy-related genes confirmed the requirement of mitochondrial quality control in the DHA-induced growth arrest of both wild-type and mutant K13 parasites44. Additionally, dynamic changes in protein function during the dysfunctional mitochondrial regulatory process need to be examined at the protein level via antibodies or fluorescence protein tags. Nevertheless, our findings provide the fundamentals of parasite survival when parasites are exposed to antimalarial drugs. Malaria-causing parasites adapt to almost all drug regimens that have been developed. Therefore, Mtphagy dye provides a valuable tool for monitoring mitochondrial dynamics and functional integrity in P. falciparum, especially in the context of drug-induced stress responses.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The experiments involving the use of human samples were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University (COA 048/2023). Human erythrocytes and serum were obtained from healthy human participants in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All the samples were handled in accordance with approved protocols and carried out in accordance with ethical standards. Blood donors were informed about the purpose of the study and the risks before providing informed consent in accordance with approval by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University.

Parasite culture

Laboratory strains of Plasmodium falciparum: ART-sensitive K1 and ART-resistant IPC-5202 carrying the K13-point mutation (R539T) were obtained from BEI Resources (NIAID, NIH). The parasites were routinely cultured in malaria culture media (MCM), which contained RPMI 1640 (HyClone, Marlborough), 25 mM HEPES (Gibco, Singapore), 0.2% NaHCO3, and 10% (vol/vol) heat-inactivated human serum from Type B Rh + blood. The parasites were grown in O-positive (O+) human erythrocytes at 5% hematocrit and incubated in a 5% CO2 environment at 37 °C. To investigate parasite growth, a thin blood smear was prepared with 100% methanol and stained with Giemsa dye diluted with a 1:20 ratio of tap water for 15 min before being observed under a light microscope (Olympus BX53) via an objective lens at 100× magnification. The percent parasitemia was determined by calculating a minimum of 1,000 erythrocytes, which is equivalent to five view fields.

Preparation of P. falciparum at various asexual stages

To obtain a synchronized culture of the ring stages, the parasitized erythrocytes were exposed to 5% D-sorbitol to remove trophozoites and schizonts as previously described by Lambros and Vanderberg54. Briefly, the MCM was removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 5 volumes of prewarmed 5% D-sorbitol. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. After incubation, the cell suspension was subjected to centrifugation at 2,500 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and the mixture was subsequently washed twice with 5 volumes of RPMI media. In the final wash, the cell pellet was resuspended in MCM with an adjustment of 5% hematocrit. Early- to middle-stage trophozoites developed after culture for 24–30 h, whereas schizonts were observed at 40–48 h of culture, with a morphology of 8–24 small merozoites in single erythrocytes. To prepare early ring forms for the growth arrest experiment, 44–48-h schizonts or those containing merozoites with more than 10–12 nuclei were isolated using 60% Percoll. The isolated schizonts were subsequently mixed with uninfected red blood cells at the final 5% hematocrit. The isolated schizonts were cultured for exactly 3 h followed by synchronization by exposure to 5 volumes of 5% D-sorbitol to remove the remaining late-stage parasites. The parasites were resuspended in MCM and collected for further experiments.

Growth inhibition assay

The synchronized early ring-stage parasites at 0–3 h post invasion (hpi) at 1% parasitemia and 2.5% hematocrit were incubated with Mdivi-1 and wortmannin at various concentrations ranging from 0 to 360 µM in a 96-well culture plate with triplicate wells. The cell pellets were collected after 24 h, and parasite viability was assessed using a fluorochrome-based flow cytometric assay. The percentage of viable parasites was gated from the VSG-positive and PI-negative populations. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was estimated using the dose‒response function of GraphPad.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Tightly synchronized early-ring-stage P. falciparum were prepared at more than 10% parasitemia and 2.5% hematocrit in a T25 flask. The parasites were subjected to four different treatments: 0.1% DMSO, 700 nM DHA, DHA plus Mdivi-1, and DHA plus wortmannin for 3 h. Following drug removal for 6 h, the parasite pellet was collected by centrifugation at 2,500 rpm for 5 min and subsequently washed twice with 1x cold PBS. The cell pellets were mixed with 0.1% saponin and incubated for 5 min to lyse the uninfected erythrocytes. After being washed twice with PBS, the cells were stored at -80 °C until use. Total RNA was extracted from the frozen parasites using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Austin, TX), and subsequently, DNase I was used to remove genomic DNA (Promega, Fitchburg, WI). The DNA-free total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the iScript reverse transcription supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California). Specific forward (Fw) and reverse (Rw) primers were designed using Primer 3 software55 and are shown in Table 1. For qRT‒PCR analysis, the amplification reaction was performed using Luna Universal qPCR master mix (New England BioLabs, city, country) and consisted of a first denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and extension at 60 °C for 60 s. Melting curves of the amplicons were generated at 65 –95 °C. The data were calculated from relative mRNA expression via the comparative threshold cycle (Ct) method with the formula 2−ΔΔCt.

The specific forward and reverse primers used for qRT-PCR.

Growth arrest induction using DHA

A DHA-induced growth arrest experiment was performed via a standard RSA following a previously published method8. Briefly, early ring-stage parasites were obtained after 3 h of schizont culture (RSA 0–3 h) and adjusted to 1–3% parasitemia and 2.5% hematocrit in a final volume of 0.5 mL in a well of a 24-well microplate. Each well was cultured with MCM containing 700 nM DHA or 0.1% DMSO (as a vehicle control) and incubated in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C for 6 h. After DHA exposure, the culture media were removed, the mixture was washed once with RPMI 1640, and subsequently, the pellet in the MCM was resuspended. The percentage of viable parasites was examined at 6 h and 1, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 14 days after drug exposure using Giemsa staining and microscopy. The culture medium was replaced daily, and 5–10 µL of freshly prepared erythrocytes were added every two days to compensate for red blood cell loss during thin blood smear preparation and medium replacement. The percentage of parasite survival was calculated by dividing the number of viable DHA-treated parasites by that of viable DMSO-treated parasites.

DNA detection in ring-stage P. falciparum

The Plasmodium parasites were labeled via a set of DNA-binding fluorochromes, which can distinguish viable and nonviable parasites following the published protocol14. Briefly, a total of 1–2 µL of the cell pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of DNA staining solution consisting of 0.0003% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS, 10 µg/mL Visafe green (VSG) (Vivantis Technologies, Selangor, Malaysia) and 10 µg/mL propidium iodide (PI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Eugene, OR). The mixture tubes were incubated at 25 °C for 20 min in the dark and subsequently centrifuged at 2,500 rpm for 3 min to remove the excess dye. For selective mitochondrial degradation assessment, 100 nM Mtphagy dye was added to the DNA-stained parasites, which were then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing, the samples were resuspended in 300 µl of ice-cold buffer containing 1× PBS and 0.5% FBS, followed by flow cytometric analysis.

Flow cytometry

The fluorescent signal of the parasitized cells was analyzed via a FACSCalibur instrument equipped with 488- and 633-nm lasers and CellQuest™ software (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). The fluorescence emitted by VSG and PI was detected after passing through 525 ± 50-nm and 576 ± 26-nm filters, respectively. For the Mtphagy dye, a 670 ± 30-nm filter was used as a PerCP channel. A total of 250,000 cells flowed into the fluidic channel to be activated by the lasers. The cell suspension stained with a single dye was used to identify the overlapping detection of each fluorochrome by other detectors (see Supplementary Figure S10 online). The flow cytometric data were analyzed via FlowJo version 7.6 software (Tree Star, Inc., Ashland, OR). The analyzed cells are displayed as scatter plots, in which forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) of the fluorescence intensity are plotted on the X- and Y-axes on a logarithmic scale, respectively.

Fluorochrome labeling of mitochondria

The mitochondrial regulatory process of the DHA-exposed ring-stage parasites was examined using a mitochondrion specific, pH-sensitive fluorochrome called Mtphagy (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Rockville, MD). A total of 2 µL of the erythrocyte pellet was added to a 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tube and subjected to washing with 1× Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) twice by centrifugation at 2,500 rpm for 3 min. The cell pellet was incubated with a mixture containing 100 µL of 1× HBSS, 100 nM Mtphagy dye, 5 µM MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Eugene, OR) for a mitochondria tracker, and 1 µM Lyso dye (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Rockville, MD) for lysosomal staining for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation and centrifugation, the dye solution was removed before washing twice with 1× HBSS. The erythrocyte pellet was diluted with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.3% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to obtain a final concentration of 1% hematocrit. The samples were dropped on a glass slide and covered with a coverslip, and the edges were sealed with nail polish. The fluorescence images were analyzed using Nikon A1R (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and Zeiss LSM 910 (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany) confocal microscopes. The three-dimensional images were observed under the HT-X1 holotomographic microscope (Tomocube, Inc., Republic of Korea).

Microscopy image analysis

To visualize the localization of the Mtphagy dye in the intraerythrocytic parasites, the cells were observed under a confocal microscope (Nikon A1R, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan and Zeiss LSM 910, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany) equipped with a 60× objective lens. The emitted fluorescent signals were detected via differential interference contrast with 488-nm, 561-nm, and 640-nm argon-ion lasers. Colocalization and intensity correlation analyses were performed via Nikon’s NIS-Elements software. For three-dimensional image analysis, the parasites were subjected to visualization under the HT-X1 holotomographic microscope (Tomocube, Inc., Republic of Korea). The cellular compartments were visualized on the basis of the reflective index (RI) and fluorescence signal with 488-nm, 561-nm, and 640-nm lasers. Moreover, the 360° rotation of the light source allows the construction of a holographic image of cell localization. The cells were cultured in a cell culture dish in a centered quadrilateral well (TomoDish, Daejeon, South Korea) and then subjected to 3D imaging in real time.

Inhibition of mitochondrial fission and autophagosome formation

Synchronized early-ring stage parasites at 0–3 hpi were treated with small molecule-based inhibitors. Inhibition was performed in (a) the early phase (t = 3 h) and (b) the late phase (t = 18 h). In the early phase of the experiment, the parasites were incubated with 700 nM DHA or the vehicle control for 3 h, followed by the addition of 10 µM Mdivi-1 or 20 µM wortmannin. Six hours after adding inhibitors, the culture medium was removed by centrifugation at 2,500 rpm for 3 min, and the cells were subsequently washed once with RPMI 1640 medium. The cell pellet was subjected to microscopic examination and flow cytometric analysis. In the late-phase experiment, the cell pellet was washed at 6 h post-DHA exposure before 18 h of treatment with 10 µM Mdivi-1 or 20 µM wortmannin. The parasites were additionally incubated until the 0.1% DMSO-exposed parasites reached the ring stage of the next cycle (~ 48–54 h). The remaining samples were resuspended in MCM and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment for the recrudescence assay. To monitor parasite growth, thin blood smears were prepared on days 1–14, stained with Giemsa dye, and observed under a light microscope. The cultures were maintained by daily medium replacement, with 5–10 µL of freshly prepared erythrocytes added every two days to compensate for red blood cell loss during thin blood smear preparation and medium replacement.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and graphical presentation were performed via GraphPad Prism software version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). The results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistically significant differences between two groups were identified via unpaired Student’s t test, whereas statistically significant differences among more than three groups were determined via one-way ANOVA. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
