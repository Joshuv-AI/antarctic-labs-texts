Comment on “Early outcomes of transverse perineal support in obstructed defecation syndrome with pathological perineal descent”: a historical and methodological clarification
Authors: Jacques Beco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The recent study by Basiouny et al. on transverse perineal support for obstructed defecation syndrome highlights the importance of objective assessment of pathological perineal descent. We wish to provide a historical and methodological clarification regarding the measurement device used in the study. The instrument illustrated as a “perineometer” may correspond to the previously described Perineocaliper®, a device developed for the clinical measurement of perineal descent. Although objective measurement of perineal descent was first introduced by Henry and Parks using a different instrument, the Perineocaliper represents a distinct development. In addition, the term perineometer has traditionally been used in urogynecology for devices measuring pelvic floor muscle contraction strength. Accurate identification and terminology of measurement devices may improve methodological clarity and facilitate comparison between studies.

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Basiouny et al. [1] entitled “Early outcomes of transverse perineal support in obstructed defecation syndrome with pathological perineal descent”.

The authors should be congratulated for drawing attention to the important role of pathological perineal descent in obstructed defecation syndrome and for providing prospective clinical data on a surgical technique specifically targeting this defect.

We would, however, like to make a historical and methodological clarification regarding the measurement device described in the study. The instrument referred to as a “perineometer” may correspond to the Perineocaliper®, a device specifically developed for the clinical measurement of perineal descent relative to the ischial tuberosities.

We would like also to emphasize that we do not claim priority for the concept of objective measurement of perineal descent. To our knowledge, Henry and Parks [2] were among the first authors to describe a dedicated instrument for this purpose in patients with descending perineum syndrome. Their contribution remains an important milestone in the field. The Perineocaliper, however, was developed later as a different instrument based on the same anatomical landmarks but designed for routine clinical use and standardized measurements in the gynecological position. In contrast, Basiouny et al. performed their measurements with the patient in the left lateral position. This difference in patient positioning should be taken into account when comparing the two measurement approaches, which should not be regarded as strictly equivalent.

We would also note that, consistent with terminology used in some colorectal and perineology publications, Basiouny et al. refer to the device as a “perineometer.” Historically, however, Kegel [3] introduced this term for instruments intended to assess pelvic floor muscle contraction strength, and it remains commonly used in this sense in urogynecology. As the same term has therefore been applied to devices based on different measurement principles, more precise identification of the instrument would improve methodological clarity and facilitate comparison between studies.

The Perineocaliper was subsequently described and used in studies investigating descending perineum syndrome, levator plate sagging, pudendal neuropathy and pelvic floor dysfunction [4]. In particular, it was incorporated into our work on retro-anal levator plate myorrhaphy and on the clinical impact of perineal descent on pelvic floor symptoms [5].

Beyond the measurement of perineal descent itself, these studies highlighted the biomechanical concept of levator plate sagging and its relationship with dyschesia, urinary symptoms, dyspareunia, perineal pain, pelvic organ prolapse and signs of pudendal neuropathy.

We are pleased to see that objective assessment of perineal descent continues to be used in contemporary pelvic floor research. We believe that acknowledging both the pioneering work of Henry and Parks and the subsequent development and clinical application of the Perineocaliper would provide readers with a more complete understanding of the evolution of this field.

We congratulate the authors again for their valuable contribution and hope that this clarification may be of interest to readers.
