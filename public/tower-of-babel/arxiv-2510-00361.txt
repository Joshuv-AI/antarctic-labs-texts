Abstract
. AI question answering systems increasingly generate responses with attributions to sources. However, the task of verifying the actual content of these attributions is in most cases impractical. In this paper, we present attribution gradients as a solution. Attribution gradients provide integrated, incremental affordances for diving into an attributed passage. A user can decompose a sentence of an answer into its claims. For each claim, the user can view supporting and contradictory excerpts mined from sources. Those excerpts serve as clickable conduits into the source (in our application, scientific papers). When evidence itself contains more citations, the UI unpacks the evidence into excerpts from the cited sources. These features of attribution gradients facilitate concurrent interconnections among answer, claim, excerpt, and context. In a usability study, we observed greater engagement with sources and richer revision in a task where participants revised an attributed AI answer with attribution gradients and a baseline.

1.
Introduction

People increasingly rely on AI-assisted search engines to find answers
(
Memon and West 2024
;
Venkit et al. 2024b
)
. These systems retrieve relevant source documents, generate answers for the user, and insert citations to sources intended to support statements in the answer
(
Venkit et al. 2024b
)
.

In theory, checking the citations is straightforward. A user clicks on a citation and is taken to a passage in a source (e.g., as with
(
you.com [n. d.]
;
Perplexity [n. d.]
;
Mircosoft [n. d.]
)
). In reality, it can be rather more involved.
A close review requires a thorough reading of the generated response, understanding the constituent claims, accessing the source documents, collecting context, and keeping enough of the found information in one’s mind to draw conclusions. Of these stages, connecting the generated answer to relevant reference documents can be notably challenging
(
Shah and Bender 2024
)
. Furthermore, should they be unfamiliar with the subject matter or aspects of the source documents, readers may overlook critical information or misunderstand the nuance in the citations to source materials
(
Venkit et al. 2024b
)
.
These challenges are exacerbated by the fact that the answers of these systems have been observed to exhibit factual inconsistencies and citation inaccuracies
(
Belem et al. 2024
;
Wright et al. 2025
;
Laban et al. 2024a
;
Lindemann 2024
)
.

The complexity of the task suggests the need for substantial sensemaking support. The essentials of such support are present in prior HCI innovations. Such projects have supported sensemaking with aids for information integration including in-context clarifications
(
Head et al. 2021a
;
August et al. 2023a
)
, synthesized insights cutting across documents
(
Kang et al. 2022
)
, and attentional cues
(
Fok et al. 2023b
)
. But any one of these solutions provides only partial support for AI answer engines. Reviewing attributed answers requires not just in-document reading or across-document reading support, but rather concurrent connections among answer, claim, excerpt, and context.

To challenge the standard of the context-impoverished inline citation, we introduce the idea of
attribution gradients
.
By this we mean attributions that provide the necessary sensemaking support across multiple source documents. Attribution gradients are incrementally unfoldable and provide the needed interconnections among answer, claim, excerpt, and context. The entrance to this gradient is the decomposition of an attributed sentence into atomic claims, and the exit is contextualized passages in their source documents. In between, a gradient provides organized evidence for claims, evidence filters, and the ability to unravel nested citations into additional evidence-lending sources (Figure
1
).
The novelty of attribution gradients is in its tight arrangement of multi-source information fragments to accelerate foraging and synthesis within the conventional linear AI answer form.

In this paper, we introduce a design of attribution gradients in a research prototype.
We develop attribution gradients as an extension to a widely-used attributed scientific question answering engine OpenSciLM
(
Asai et al. 2024
)
. We select this as our domain because the documents are particularly tricky to read and because they contain particularly convincing evidence if cited correctly.
We evaluate its effect on critical examination of attributed AI answers with a lab usability study. We compare attribution gradients to other contemporary forms of AI answer support including a “chat with paper”
(
chatdoc.com [n. d.]
)
system and a generated literature comparison table
(
elicit.com [n. d.]
)
. Our study showed that, when given the tasks of revising AI answers for correctness and detail, participants produced better revisions, reflecting more effective engagement with the attributions during time-bound examination.
They also consulted more sources, for more time per visit. Our observations of usage found evidence filters and links to the source to be the most invoked stages of the gradient.

In summary, this paper contributes:

•

Attribution gradients, a novel approach to reading attributed AI answers that supports their examination by incrementally unfolding context along a continuum, from sentence decomposition to contextualized passages in a source, through simultaneous, tightly woven interconnections among the answer, claim, excerpt, and context.

•

Insights from a within-subjects usability study with 20 participants, providing:

–

evidence that attribution gradients led to closer examination of attributed generated answers, evidenced by higher-quality revisions made in an answer editing task.

–

evidence that attribution gradients led to longer and more frequent interactions with sources.

–

a characterization of the roles that each stage of the gradient played in critical examination of AI answers.

2.
Background and Related Work

2.1.
Attributed AI answer tools

Unlike traditional search engines that return a list of documents ordered by relevance, AI-assisted search engines typically produce answers using Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). In RAG, an AI combines a document retriever with a generative model (i.e. an LLM) to synthesize answers from retrieved passages
(
Fan et al. 2024
)
. These systems are intended to help answer questions with the long tail of information that LLMs have not yet indexed
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
. Many RAG-enabled UIs, including Perplexity AI
(
Perplexity [n. d.]
)
, Microsoft Copilot
(
Mircosoft [n. d.]
)
, OpenSciLM
(
Asai et al. 2024
)
, and You.com
(
you.com [n. d.]
)
, return answers informed by retrieved evidence. In this regard, they have the potential to reduce the need for users to manually explore documents extensively all while mitigating common generative AI issues such as hallucinations
(
Huang et al. 2025
;
Venkit et al. 2024a
)
. Typically, these tools offer academic paper-style citations as a means for users to get more context on model’s responses. Some tools go further: the Olmo
(
Groeneveld et al. 2024
)
online playground employs phrase-level highlights to help users pinpoint documents from the training set containing exact text matches. Deep Research
(
OpenAI [n. d.]
)
offers a status bar revealing intermediate steps in the answer generation process, reflecting the agent’s evolving goals and sources under consideration. ScholarQA and OpenSciLM surface previews of citation contents: hovering over a citation reveals a relevant snippet from the source paper
(
asta.allen.ai [n. d.]
;
Asai et al. 2024
)

2.2.
User needs with attributed answers

A growing body of research characterizes the user experience of consuming AI-generated attributed texts. Here, we review that research to help articulate a set of needs (
N1-4
) that attribution gradients are meant to address.

A central concern with these systems is their inherent opacity
(
Hayes 2023
)
—both the retriever and generator involve black box models, making the final generated answer difficult for users to scrutinize and understand. Although attribution mechanisms, such as citations, are intended to mitigate some opacity (e.g., around what sources informed part of a response), prior studies suggest they often fall short in providing the desired transparency
(
Venkit et al. 2024b
)
. This opacity results in users having very limited insight into how answers are constructed
(
Hayes 2023
)
, what biases these search engines might contain
(
Li and Sinnamon 2024
)
, and ultimately what information to actually take away
(
Venkit et al. 2024b
)
.
Venkit et al. 2024b
observe that attributed AI answer tools generate answers that contain a large proportion of statements unsupported by the sources they provide. Therefore, one need is simply to help users look up more support that might pertain to parts of the answer, whether currently attributed or not (N1).

An additional challenge of working with these systems is that they sometimes surface a narrow perspective
(
Lindemann 2024
)
and reinforce biased views
(
Sharma et al. 2024
)
. A study by
Lindemann 2024
terms the difficulty of accessing the gamut of potential answers to search queries as “sealed knowledges” and states that this in turn makes marginalized knowledge (perspectives that sit outside mainstream channels) difficult to find. Another need for users is therefore to help them reach the gamut of potential answers, in this case finding and making sense of a set of evidence that supports and contradicts a part of an answer (N2).

Moreover, a study by
Venkit et al. 2024a
shows that users of generative search engines tend to click on sources and engage with them less deeply compared to users of traditional search engines when querying technical questions. This was in part because of fewer and imprecise citation links between the generated answers and their sources. To us, this suggests two needs. One need is to reduce the cost of a citation link (N3), so that it is easier to eventually find those citations that
are
precise and to get to the useful context that may still accompany an imprecise citation. Additionally, if a link is imprecise simply because it cites other work rather than providing evidence itself, then those links could be made precise if users are connected more quickly to the cited work (N4).

These needs correspond closely to core tasks in classic sensemaking models
(
Pirolli and Card 2005
)
. Specifically, N1 and N2 reflect user needs to efficiently access and evaluate sources. N2 and N3 relate to the need to efficiently find certain kinds of information in those sources. N2 relates to the need to connect related information across multiple sources and explore relationships among sources. N4 too supports connections among sources, ultimately to feed back into efficient information finding.
In this paper, we explore how these gaps of sensemaking (source access, information retrieval, relational exploration) can be filled for attributed AI answers with a design that tightly interlinks answer, claim, collection, excerpt, and context.

2.3.
HCI systems for sensemaking

Sensemaking supports

Many initiatives have been undertaken to develop AI systems aimed at enhancing information-seeking and sensemaking, especially in contexts where researchers need to understand and review large collections of literature.
Kang et al. 2023
identify two main strategies for synthesizing extensive bodies of work: a bottom-up approach that helps researchers navigate literature at the paper level—illustrated by tools like Threddy
(
Kang et al. 2022
)
, Qlarify
(
Fok et al. 2023a
)
, and others
(
Fok et al. 2023b
;
Head et al. 2021a
;
Kim et al. 2023
;
Chang et al. 2023
;
Palani et al. 2023
;
August et al. 2023a
)
—and a top-down approach that provides a broader view of the landscape, as seen in tools like Marco
(
Fok et al. 2024
)
and PaperWeaver
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
. Tools like Synergi
(
Kang et al. 2023
)
and DiscipLink
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
combine these strategies, clustering papers for an overview while enabling customization through seed papers or user interests. Previous research has highlighted the importance of building context about an information space by enabling users to find, extract, and summarize relevant information from multiple sources into cohesive themes and knowledge
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2024
)
. Our work introduces a novel but related approach: attribution gradients. In contrast to prior systems that either offer static navigation across one document or synthesize broad thematic overviews, attribution gradients focus on citation sensemaking. Context is incrementally revealed: from cited sentences to fully contextualized passages in the source. This is especially salient in a multi-document setting, where evidence must be sorted across multiple documents and presented to the user in a centralized place. In doing so, attribution gradients facilitate simultaneous tight interconnections among answer, claim, excerpt, and context.

Literature-reading aids

Another thread of research focuses on reducing the cognitive effort involved in reading complex texts, such as research papers. This line of work includes tools that organize and display supporting evidence
(
Kang et al. 2022
)
, use faceted highlights to direct readers’ attention to salient content
(
Fok et al. 2023b
)
, gather related work sections for a given topic
(
Palani et al. 2023
)
, provide jargon definitions
(
Head et al. 2021b
)
, and generate plain-language summaries of key paper sections
(
August et al. 2023b
)
. We draw inspiration from these systems that augment and contextualize a single paper, then extend these methods to multiple papers that are tied to a generated answer. In particular, attribution gradients classify and highlight different kinds of evidence, unravel citation paths, simplify and contextualize information, and summarize clusters of evidence.

Linking text and context

Researchers have also examined how to enrich reading experiences by linking core text to supporting information
(
Kambhamettu et al. 2024
)
. This can involve creating textual narratives connected to underlying data
(
Sultanum and Srinivasan 2023
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Latif et al. 2021
;
Heer et al. 2023
)
, linking AI-generated text to evidence
(
Krishna et al. 2024
;
Hoque et al. 2024
;
Laban et al. 2024b
)
, interleaving generated text with symbolic references to data fields
(
Hennigen et al. 2023
)
, or examining multiple LLM outputs at once to compare similarities and differences
(
Gero et al. 2024
)
. Building on these approaches, our system links pieces of evidence within AI-generated statements back to their original contexts, alongside additional affordances. The novelty of the design resides in its concurrent links among answer, claim, excerpt, and context.

Digesting scientific text

LLMs have inspired many applications to support sensemaking within scholarly research and writing. There have been significant efforts in recent years to advance how AI, particularly LLMs, can help users get more out of scientific text. This includes tools for scientific idea or hypothesis generation
(
Radensky et al. 2024a
;
Pu et al. 2024
;
Ding et al. 2025
)
, research planning
(
Feng et al. 2024
)
, organization and synthesis of scientific support
(
Hsu et al. 2024
;
Newman et al. 2024
)
, academic document question-answering
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
, creation of research-paper blog posts
(
Radensky et al. 2024b
)
, and conversational podcasts based on academic PDFs
(
Yahagi et al. 2024
)
.

3.
System

To motivate the utility of a system like attribution gradients, we conducted an illustrative case study and assessed the extent to which claims in our target AI QA system (OpenSciLM) were supported by the papers they cited. We find that the citations in OpenSciLM often failed to support claims in a generated answer. We also found that useful support for claims sometimes resided in sources used for other claims in the answer. More details are reported in Section
A.1
. In the rest of this section, we detail the attribution gradients system.

3.1.
Narrative scenario

We now describe the design of our system, an interface that implements attribution gradients, a novel approach that incrementally unfolds layers of context from the cited sentences in an AI-generated answer to enable simultaneous tight interconnections among answer, claim, excerpt, and context. We demonstrate how each aspect of the system contributes to sensemaking of attributed AI answers. We describe the experience of interacting with attribution gradients in a narrative walkthrough.

Shelby, a junior researcher in natural language processing, has just joined a project on automated scientific discovery. She is looking to educate herself on the recommended approaches to evaluating scientific question answering systems, with the hopes of improving one such system she is working on. Knowing that scientific QA is an active research area, she turns to an attributed AI QA interface and asks, “What are leading approaches for evaluating complex scientific question answering systems in NLP?”

Base tool

The QA interface described here is an extension of OpenSciLM
(
Asai et al. 2024
)
, a recently-released scientific attributed QA platform with tens of thousands of users. The sources provided in attributed answers are scientific papers.
Here, the tool we describe Shelby using is a version of OpenSciLM extended with attribution gradients.

Figure 2
.
Inspecting the first sentence of an AI-generated answer. When the users clicks the button to open evidence for the sentence, the interface decomposes the sentence into atomic claims and displays these claims below the generated sentence.

Dissecting a sentence

Shelby skims over the model response. Eventually, her attention lands on a sentence about RAG and its evaluation. The sentence is quite complex, but it claims that RAG is “powerful” for answering complex questions. There is an attribution (“[1]”) in the sentence. But it is not clear from context whether the citation justifies that RAG is powerful, that it answers complex questions, or some part of the sentence. She clicks on an icon next to the sentence to investigate. This unpacks the sentence into its constituent atomic claims. The claims include “RAG systems are a solution for answering open-ended complex questions” and “RAG systems combine the strengths of retrieval systems with generative capabilities of LLMs” (Figure
2
).

Figure 3
.
Exploring different types of evidence for the claim. When the user clicks on a claim, the interface displays pieces of evidence that relate to the claim. There are four kinds of evidence (from left to right): evidence from retrieved papers that directly support the claim are colored in black; evidence that references other papers that support the claim are colored in gray; evidence that references other papers that directly contradict the claim are colored pink; and evidence from retrieved papers that directly contradict the claim are colored in red. Users can click on a tab of evidence type to filter down to that kind of support—here, only first-degree support is displayed. For visual succinctness, some excerpts are elided from the list above with a jagged line.

Overview of support for a claim

Shelby clicks on the claim “RAG systems are a solution for answering open-ended complex questions” to understand the nature and strength of the evaluation. As soon as Shelby clicks the claim, she sees a view of support that is more nuanced than an individual citation number (Figure
3
). There is a digest of all excerpts from the cited source or sources and the kinds of support they provide. Excerpts can either support a claim, or contradict it. Evidence can be first-degree, in that the source provides novel evidence itself, or second-degree, where the source cites other sources that provide the evidence. For the claim Shelby inspects, she sees that there are 7 excerpts providing first-degree support, 6 providing second-degree support, 2 providing second-degree contradiction, and 3 providing first-degree contradiction. Shelby begins to get the impression that the citation does not provide unequivocal support for the idea that RAG systems have the potential to reliably answer scientific questions.

The excerpts are color-coded to help Shelby scan particular kinds of evidence. This helps Shelby determine which excerpts to consider further before opening a source. Shelby can also filter to a particular class of evidence. She first toggles to see the first-degree supporting excerpts. Among them, she reads an excerpt with two interesting bits: that RAG seems to improve over baselines, and that it was evaluated using a methodology involving sub-questions (see Figure
3
). Because this is “primary” evidence, Shelby understands that she could visit the paper to likely find data supporting the claim. Then, she toggles to the contradictory pieces of evidence and views an evidence excerpt, “Interestingly, we find that while all answer engines cover core sub-questions more often than background or follow-up ones, they still miss around 50% of core sub-questions, revealing clear opportunities for improvement.”

Looking at the evidence

Shelby is curious now — both to understand more about RAG’s performance on complex QA, and the evaluation methods that show shortcomings. She clicks the “show source” button associated with the excerpt. This opens up a side panel with the PDF of the source. The document scrolls to the relevant passage (see Figure
4
). She sees right away from the placement of the passage that it is in the document’s abstract. From this she infers it is likely one of the most defended claims in the paper and there likely more detailed supporting data to be found.

Figure 4

Viewing passages in context. When a user clicks to jump to the source for a piece of evidence, it scrolls to that evidence in a source viewer on the right side of the screen. The evidence excerpt is highlighted. All other extracted passages of evidence are highlighted in a less salient color. The interface also shows “context for highlighted passage,” or a brief description meant to help relate the passage to the claim when the excerpt is read out of context.

Contextualizing evidence

In the header of the document reader Shelby can access a brief assistive text to help contextualize what she has found in terms of the claim she is inspecting. Here the text is simple: “Core sub-questions are important for complex questions. But RAG systems miss 50% of them.” The context clears up aspects of the passage that are left implied, like how sub-questions are used to evaluate complex questions and that the evaluation described targets RAG systems rather than others. In this way, attribution gradients helped Shelby get a more nuanced perspective about the capabilities of a relevant QA technology and its evaluation than afforded by the original citation.

After clicking the button to expand nested citations, excerpts from the cited source are shown that pertain to the claim. Next, consider Sai, another user, who is a junior applied AI researcher in medical informatics. She is exploring model choices for a new project that leverages clinical named entity recognition and sentiment analysis to detect adverse drug events in electronic health records. She wants to learn more about tasks for which fine-tuning smaller models is advantageous over using large language models. Wanting to understand the current consensus, grounded in scientific literature, she asks an attributed AI QA system the following question: “What are some tasks where fine tuning smaller models is beneficial over using LLMs?” Sai then starts reviewing the generated answer.

Unraveling evidence from referenced sources

Sai reads through the answer, and finds a claim that “Fine-tuning small models can outperform LLMs in certain cases.” Wanting to understand better about which cases, she expands the evidence for this claim. She sees a piece of second-degree evidence that “Fine-tuned models, such as DeltaLM+Zcode [118], still perform best on most rich-resource translation and extremely low-resource translation tasks.” This evidence is second-degree because its evidence comes from other sources. Sai wants to see those sources themselves.
So, she unravels the evidence.

She clicks on a button to unravel the evidence for the excerpt (Figure

She sees that the referenced paper ([“118”]) states that outputs from a smaller, fine-tuned model are preferred to those of a larger model.
In this way, Sai gets stronger support for the original claim than what came from the citations in the AI-generated answer.

Sai adds the excerpt about DeltaLM+Zcode so that she can recall later an instance where fine-tuned models outperform LLMs. She has also come across some other interesting snippets of evidence that intrigue her and she wants to review later (i.e. excerpts that further clarify performance of fine-tuning vs. LLMs on certain tasks). Where the initial response was broad, Sai has used the attribution gradients to rapidly collect a more nuanced understanding of the information presented. 3.2.
Relation to user needs

We put the affordances of the system in context by relating them back to the user needs identified in Section
2.2
.

•

N1: Look up more support for the answer.
Prior work highlights that readers struggle to connect specific segments of a generated sentence to concrete, verifiable details
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Venkit et al. 2024b
)
. Our design of attribution gradients helps a reader get a better sense of what part of an answer is supported by unpacking sentences into atomic claims, and rapidly exposing excerpts that relate to each of those claims.

•

N2: Reach the gamut of answers.
Readers also need to gauge how well a snippet supports
or
contradicts a claim, rather than passively accepting a one-sided narrative
(
Fok et al. 2024
;
Laban et al. 2024b
;
Venkit et al. 2024b
)
. The design of attribution gradients makes it possible to estimate the available evidence (at least in the available sources) by faceting the evidence into support and contradiction, and first- and second-degree evidence. This helps user reach into contradictory evidence if they have previously been focused on supporting evidence, and vice versa.

•

N3: Reduce the cost of inspecting a citation.
“Bare” links (citations that are shown without explanations or contextualizations) impose interaction costs: users must navigate away, locate the passage, and reconstruct context
(
Fok et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024
)
. While deep links into cited papers are increasingly common in RAG tools (e.g., opening directly to the referenced passage), we provide empirical evidence for their value. With attribution gradients, users stay in the interface. When they click an evidence excerpt that corresponds to a generated claim, the source opens to that location in the same window, scrolled into view with the evidence excerpt highlighted. To further help a reader digest a piece of evidence, the contextual explanations clarify why the passage is considered supportive, contradictory, or otherwise significant.

•

N4: Connect to nested evidence.
Prior work notes that imprecise attributions often rely on sources that themselves rely on other sources, creating nested chains that are costly to unravel
(
Venkit et al. 2024b
;
Gero et al. 2024
)
. Attribution gradients helps users process citations that are imprecise in the sense of relying on other sources. It helps them unravel these citations, looking up excerpts from the nested citations.

The PDFs for cited sources are fetched by following links to open access PDFs found using the Semantic Scholar API

.

Fetching second-degree sources

When evidence is second-degree, we attempt to process the source cited in the evidence.
We resolve references to second-degree sources by extracting reference strings for citation numbers using an LLM, and then matching reference strings to reference information for the paper fetched from the Semantic Scholar API.

UI

The interface is implemented in HTML, JavaScript, and CSS. For our prototype, processing of questions was done offline before usability study sessions.
To highlight excerpts in their source PDFs, we extracted precise locations of those excerpts.
We parsed PDFs with PaperMage

3.4.
Technical audit

To help characterize the capacity of AI to enable attribution gradients, we conducted a focused audit of the attribution gradients pipeline on one of the questions from the narrative scenario: “What are leading approaches for evaluating complex scientific question answering systems in NLP?” We audited all excerpts for all claims in the first sentence of the answer. For each excerpt, the last author inspected the source PDF to judge whether it constitutes precise, formal evidence for or against the claim. The last author followed the following strict grading pipeline: first tagging whether the evidence snippet clearly validates or undermines the claim, then tagging whether the evidence was a duplicate of an earlier excerpt, then tagging whether it is an assertion of a claim that does not have data or citations, and lastly tagging if the evidence is classified correctly. Evidence excerpts that satisfied all of these constraints were marked as correct. We report the results as follows: across 61 excerpts, 33 were labeled by the system as first-degree support (FDS): 6/33 (18%) were indeed first-degree support, 19/33 (58%) were topic-relevant but not precise evidence, 1/33 was a duplicate, 3/33 (9%) were assertion-only, 3/33 (9%) were actually second-degree support, and 1/33 was a first-degree contradiction. Of 20 second-degree support (SDS) items, 3/20 (15%) were correctly SDS, 15/20 (75%) were not precise evidence, 1/20 was assertion-only, and 1/20 was a second-degree contradiction. Of 2 second-degree contradictions (SDC), 1/2 was correctly SDC while the other was not precise evidence. All 6 first-degree contradictions (FDC) were not precise evidence.

Anecdotally, many excerpts did not directly validate or refute the claim but were plausibly implicative. For example, when evaluating the claim that fine-tuned models outperform LLMs on natural language inference (NLI), the last author often saw results on adjacent tasks (e.g., symbolic reasoning, medical diagnosis) that might suggest similar performance on NLI. However, to maintain a strict evidence policy, the last author counted such analogical or cross-task signals as not precise evidence for the claim. Importantly, our usability study indicates that users still benefit from this pipeline in the attribution gradients workflow: even with misclassifications, participants performed better than a baseline interface, because of the interaction affordances attribution gradients provides. This suggests that the benefit of attribution gradients lies less in classification accuracy and more in how its structured excerpts scaffold users’ reasoning, helping them perform better even when some labels are wrong. Nonetheless, for higher end-to-end accuracy, attribution gradients can be paired with more capable reasoning models (e.g., o3) while preserving its interaction design.

4.
Evaluation

We conducted an in-lab usability study to evaluate attribution gradients. Our goal was to understand the extent to which attribution gradients helped readers drill into generated attributed answers. The study was designed to answer the following research questions:

(1)

Do attribution gradients help people more critically examine attributed generated answers?

(2)

How do people make use of attribution gradients as they read an attributed generated answer?

The study consisted of a controlled comparison between attribution gradients and modern AI baselines that readers might use to drill down into the details of sources.

Participants

We recruited 20 participants from mailing lists at a private US university. Participants were 55% doctoral students, 40% Master’s students, and 5% research associates. Participants all reported some prior exposure to AI. 90% of participants had taken at least one course on NLP or AI, and the other participants indicated that they had worked on personal projects in those areas. This indicated that most were acquainted with the domain of the answers used as foci of the tasks. Participants had varying levels of experience using attributed question-answering AIs: 55% of participants used these systems daily, 10% weekly, 25% monthly, and 10% less than monthly.

Setup

All study sessions were held over Zoom remote conferencing software. The interface was served over a public URL via
ngrok
. Participants joined the meeting from their own computers.

Interfaces

This was a within-subjects study.
All participants completed one task with attribution gradients, and one task with a strong baseline.
Both attribution gradients and the baseline had the same base interface: a model response in the format provided by OpenSciLM, with a list of cited references and links to each reference. In-text citations could be clicked to view a tooltip containing the cited source’s title, authors, venue, year, and the abstract from the paper that were retrieved to generate the answer.
Responses included a list of cited references and links to each reference.
In-text citations could be clicked to view a tooltip containing the cited source’s title, authors, venue, year, and excerpts from the paper that were retrieved to generate the answer.

The baseline was additionally extended to have the affordances of modern AI document-reading support.
In the baseline condition, readers had access to complementary tools of:

•

ChatDOC
(
chatdoc.com [n. d.]
)
, a web app that allows readers to load in PDFs and ask questions about them. Answers are served with deep links into relevant passages. We preloaded ChatDOC with all sources cited from the response.

•

The Elicit
(
elicit.com [n. d.]
)
literature search tool, and specifically its literature table view. We loaded all of the cited sources into a collection of papers into Elicit. Then, we generated a summary table. The table included columns that summarized aspects of the papers including main findings, methodologies, interventions, outcomes measured, and limitations extracted from the papers. Each cell in the table was itself an attributed generation. The participant could click the cell to open up a passage containing relevant information from the source in a plaintext rendering of the text from the paper.

To reduce demand characteristics, we assigned pseudonyms to the tools in both conditions, and avoided telling participants which one we had a role in implementing. All cited sources were pre-loaded into both tools so participants could immediately ask questions and scan a summary table of findings, methods, interventions, outcomes, and limitations. 16/20 participants used both ChatDoc and the Elicit table during the baseline task, suggesting that the split-tab setup did not deter participants from engaging with the baseline’s AI features.

Training

Participants received a tutorial for each interface prior to the task where they used that interface.
Both tutorials took approximately the same amount of time to deliver.
The experimenter demonstrated each of the major features of the relevant interfaces.
Then, they asked the participant to practice those features on small practice tasks.
The responses used in the training task were different from the test tasks.
Interface condition was counterbalanced (AB/BA), while task order was fixed across participants.

Test tasks

We first sampled
\sim
20
ScholarQABench questions, screened them for relevance and citation availability, and pilot-tested with four users to set difficulty and timing. From these, we selected two tasks that required deep engagement with a generated attributed text. Specifically, we provided a generated attributed text. Then we asked participants to edit this text as if they were preparing to share it with a colleague with whom they were doing collaborative research. We asked that they both correct the text as well as embellish it. Participants were directed to focus on one specific paragraph of the text, because during our pilot sessions participants were overwhelmed by the unreasonable workload of closely examining an entire attributed text in the time provided. Generated answers in the tasks were not intentionally seeded with errors, though an author who was a domain expert later identified some inaccuracies.

Pipeline accuracy on task stimuli

To calibrate our own expectations about the performance of the pipeline, we audited its outputs, focusing on how often irrelevant excerpts were included. We randomly sampled 40 claim–evidence pairs (20 per task) from the study corpus. For each pair, the first author assessed whether the evidence was “relevant” or “not relevant.” We defined a piece of evidence as being relevant if the evidence provided useful and related context about the claim. The last author then independently assessed the relevance of the 40 claim-evidence pairs. Full agreement between the two raters was reached after resolving disagreements in ratings. Our analysis showed that the system was accurate overall, though with frequent inaccuracies. 52.5% pieces of evidence were judged relevant. Below, our study shows that with the current accuracy, attribution gradients are still adequate to improve performance. Section
5.1.4
discusses participants’ apparent capability in ignoring inaccurate classifications.

Measurements and analysis

We measured depth of investigation of the generated attributed text by analyzing the revisions participants made to their texts, making the assumption that participants that were more successful at clarifying their knowledge about the generated text would make more substantial revisions to the text.

Two domain experts served as judges in reviewing the revised answers. One was an author, and the other was a person external to the team. Both judges had technical expertise in the subject matter of the queries and reviewed all the participant-revised texts. Both were provided the set of revised texts without knowledge of the relationship between individual text and study condition. The first step consisted of annotating individual revision for:

•

Number of inserted facts
. The judge counted the number of facts added to the revised text versus the original text, with a fact typically consisting of a short sentence, and compound sentences occasionally counting as multiple facts,

•

Number of corrections
. The judge counted the number of facts that corrected a fact in the original text, which was typically too broad or misleading. We note that most of these corrections were minor.

•

Number of incorrect facts inserted
. The judge counted the number of inserted facts that were added in the revised text that were judged to be incorrect.

•

Shallowness of engagement
. The judge counted the number of facts added that were very general (i.e., did not require a source to be able to intuit), and were most likely based on participants’ prior knowledge rather than engagement with source content.

Once individual revisions were reviewed and annotated, the judge assigned an overall grade to each participant-revised response, ranging from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest). The aim of the overall grade was to assess the relative quality of the 20 revised responses, and the grades were roughly evenly distributed across the five grades.

The expectation was that, if attribution gradients served their intended purpose, the revised texts produced with the interface would be ranked as higher quality, with more added facts, more corrections, fewer incorrect facts inserted, and fewer additions that represented shallow engagement.

For overall quality, number of inserted facts, and number of corrections, both judges yields very similar outcomes in effect size and significance. A different outcome was observed for shallowness of engagement, though the difference is insignificant for both judges. We report the outcomes for the external judge in the findings below, and include the outcomes for the author judge in Appendix
A
.

We collected additional subjective measures through questionnaires given after each test task. In these questionnaires, we asked participants to report on the difficulty of the task they had just completed on a 5-point Likert scale.

Comparisons of revision-based annotations and Likert data between conditions were performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests of significance
(
Wilcoxon 1992
)
with a significance value of .05.

After each task, we asked a participant to enter the usefulness of the features they had used on a 5-point Likert scale. Then, the experimenter asked them to describe out loud which features they found most useful in the tool they used for the task. The experimenter took structured notes throughout the sessions, including counts of usages of key features, observations of user behavior, and themes from conversations. Our reports of feature usefulness and interaction patterns in Section
5
derive from a thematic analysis of the post-task probes and structured notes. The first author coded all notes from the studies to identify instances of perceived feature usefulness and why these features were used. The same author clustered codes into themes that explained when and why specific features helped participants.

5.1.3.
Reading patterns

Interaction traces revealed two complementary navigation styles that attribution gradients enables: breadth-first and depth-first reading. This demonstrates how readers find and make sense of evidence (N2 in section

Breadth-first reading

We define breadth-first reading as scanning many excerpts first to triage which are worth deeper inspection, then opening a selected subset in the PDF. We observed ten participants exhibiting breadth-first reading. Breadth-first readers opened on average 77% of the available excerpts in the task. A representative pattern of breadth-first reading is with P14, who opened up 18 snippets that corresponded to one claim in the RAG answer without opening up the evidence in the source view. P14 then clicked to jump to a couple of second-degree evidence snippets in the source view. P14 remarked:
“I wanted to look at a bunch of excerpts to see which would be most useful. Honestly the second-degree evidence was almost more important than the first…being able to read in context was really useful.”
P14’s final revision exemplified the BFS workflow’s strengths: it summarized many different approaches to sub-question coverage (the main component of the answer), articulating broadly how sub-question decomposition could augment RAG pipelines.

Depth-first reading

We define depth-first reading as deeply engaging with a single claim, a single evidence excerpt, and a single source. By “depth-first” we mean focusing on one branch before others. For example, P5 clicked on a claim about sub-question coverage and a first-degree supporting evidence snippet of that claim. P5 then jumped to the source view of that evidence excerpt. After reading the source, P5 revised the answer to more accurately reflect the source text. We observed seven participants exhibiting depth-first reading. Depth-first readers opened fewer panes (on average 46.7% of available excerpts), often making multiple short edits that related to a small number of evidence excerpts.

5.1.4.
Evidence of synthesis

Direct copying of evidence content, which might indicate a shallow information transfer rather than deep synthesis, was occasional. With attribution gradients, we observed 9 instances where a participant directly copied over information from an evidence excerpt verbatim across all sessions. All other edits did not involve directly copying information and likely relied on synthesis from one or multiple evidence excerpts. In the baseline, we observed 33 instances of participants directly copying over content from the ChatDOC response into the revised answer. No participant copied information from Elicit. This suggests that most often, using attribution gradients, participants synthesized sentences that spanned multiple snippets or added insights that they derived from information they engaged with in the sources.

5.1.5.
Seeing and ignoring inaccuracies.

Seven participants noted a misclassification during the study (most commonly secondary support labeled as primary), but no participant introduced incorrect information in their revisions in the attribution gradients condition. This indicates that, despite classification noise, observed inaccuracies did not propagate into edited answers.

Responses to the questionnaire, summarized in Figure

The ability to look up an evidence snippet in the PDF viewer stood out as one of the most useful features. Participants jumped to an excerpt in the paper a median of 7 times (

“I’m able to click into the excerpt and see the highlighted snippet [in the PDF viewer] and [say] ‘huh this makes sense’…it makes the whole process quicker to do.”
We observed a connection between viewing excerpts in the source and correcting the answer: in all 5 instances of corrections, participants corrected information in the answer while consulting the source. These corrections were often important. For instance, the answer to the question “What are leading approaches for evaluating complex scientific question answering systems in NLP?” described an evaluation method of assessing “the response’s ability to address multiple facets of the question.” After consulting the excerpts in the source, some participants corrected this by changing ‘the response’ to ‘RAG systems’ (3). “I can read the context alongside the snippets and say this all checks out.”
The context was especially useful when reading second-degree evidence snippets. As P14 states, “it’s usually not really clear what’s going on [in the second degree evidence snippets] from the excerpts…the context helps me figure out what’s important and skip to the information I need.”

6.
Discussion

Our study showed greater quality revisions and preference of attribution gradients for a task of embellishing a generated answer with details. Observations from the study showed regular use of and high reported usefulness of many of the facets of the gradient. These findings suggest the promise of the ideas behind attribution gradients, namely supporting users in critically assessing AI-generated content by facilitating connections between generated statements and their underlying evidence. In this section, we consider the generalizability of the findings and opportunities for advancing the research agenda of which attribution gradients is a part.

6.1.
Limitations

Our study has some limitations to consider. First, we evaluated the system exclusively using scientific literature as the source documents for RAG. Perhaps there would not be as much of a need for scaffolding with other kinds of articles, especially those written in plain language. Future studies should explore the applicability of such tools across other document types such as news articles. Second, our participant sample consisted primarily of individuals with limited domain expertise. Participants with greater domain expertise might have lower motivation to engage deeply with the provided evidence, as they could verify the information independently. This could alter the perceived utility of the tool.

Third, our evaluation relied on responses generated by OpenSciLLM, which demonstrated various shortcomings such as incorrect document retrieval and inaccuracies within the generated content. Here there are two conflicting effects—responses are in need of more validation with OpenSciLLM because the responses are less accurate. With better RAG, we might see tool use drop, because it is less needed. Though we might also see the gradients as more useful, as they point people to more useful resources. Improved RAG will probably result in mixed influence on usefulness of the features. These limitations could be addressed in future iterations—through more accurate retrieval methods or higher-performing models. Additionally, the current implementation exhibited several system inaccuracies, including misclassified evidence snippets, occasionally unhelpful contextual explanations, and failures to retrieve secondary-degree documents. These issues might prevent our system from working as well as it could. Future iterations should prioritize refining these components to provide more reliable interactions.

6.2.
Future work

Looking ahead, there are several promising directions for future work. Improving the underlying RAG components and adopting more sophisticated language models could substantially enhance both retrieval accuracy and generated responses.

Furthermore, the system can be expanded beyond the binary support-versus-contradict categorization to incorporate richer, multi-dimensional contextual relationships between claims and evidence. Evidence snippets can be annotated with more nuanced labels such as partial support, clarification, extension, or background context. With these relationship types, users can glean more meaning that go beyond whether evidence merely agrees or disagrees with a claim and gain a more precise understanding of the claim’s standing in the broader research conversation.

If raw data—such as tables, numeric results, or structured datasets—can be extracted from sources, then future interfaces could perform analyses on a user’s behalf, including running statistical tests or checking for logical consistency. For example, a user could see real-time analyses that verify a claim’s alignment with the underlying evidence. This approach could enable more advanced sensemaking scenarios, such as quickly validating reported findings in the figure of a scientific study. Future research could explore such structured verification techniques for more rigorous logical assessments of claims against data such as tables.

Another direction for future work is to expand beyond linking individual claims to singular evidence snippets and instead facilitating more sophisticated synthesis across multiple sources and for different sensemaking tasks. Future interfaces might be able to dynamically organize evidence from multiple documents beyond the original retrieved set based on user-inputted claims. They might also allow users to annotate or revise evidence snippets based on their evolving understanding and help close the sensemaking loop. By integrating synthesis and iterative editing, future interfaces might more dynamically allow meaningful comparisons and logical connections between different sources, further assisting users in their sensemaking processes.

7.
Conclusion

We introduce the notion of
attribution gradients
, a novel approach to facilitate a detailed understanding of attributed AI-generated responses by incrementally unfolding context from generated answer to source evidence. Attribution gradients establish simultaneous tight interconnections among answer, claim, excerpt, and context. We instantiate this idea in the setting of an attributed AI QA system grounded in scientific literature.
In a usability study, we found that using attribution gradients, participants produced higher-quality revisions when critically revising AI-generated answers. Nearly all levels of gradients supported deep inspection of attributed answers in some way, and all were reported as having some use.
Altogether, this work demonstrates the value of this design for bridging gaps in citation sensemaking of attributed AI-generated answers.

A.1.
Illustrative case study of OpenSciLM attributions

Following an annotation process adapted from
(
Wadden et al. 2022
)
, the first author analyzed how much support that retrieved documents lent to each of 108 claims extracted from OpenSciLM’s answers to 20 scientific questions randomly sampled from ScholarQABench
(
Asai et al. 2024
)
.

For each answer, the author manually identified claims as sentences that contained citations. The author then assessed the extent to which each claim was supported. Each claim was labeled with one of three levels of support: adequately-supported (the evidence fully supports the claim), topically-relevant (the evidence does not expressly support the claim, but provides some context about the claim), or unsupported (the evidence does not support or relate to the claim). Of the 108 claims, 35 (32.4%) were fully supported, 24 (22.2%) were topically relevant, and 49 (45.4%) were unsupported. In summary, for the RAG system used as a focus in this paper, citations often failed to support the claim in the generated answer.

The author additionally assessed how often there was at least
some
support for a claim from any of the documents cited by the answer (not restricted just to those cited by the claim sentence).

Of the 108 claims, 78 (72.2%) at least partially supported by at least one document in the full set of cited documents. This suggests that useful support for claims may sometimes reside in the sources used for other claims in the answer.
