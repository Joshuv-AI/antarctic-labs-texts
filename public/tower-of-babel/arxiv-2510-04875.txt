Rectangular Shrinking Targets on Self-Similar Carpets
Demi Allen, Thomas Jordan, Benjamin Ward

Abstract
Since the introduction of the shrinking target problem by Hill and Velani in 1995 there has been a surge of interest in the area. In this paper we consider the case where the target is a rectangle, rather than a ball, and the underlying space is a self-similar carpet. We calculate the exact Hausdorff dimension of the resulting shrinking target set. Interestingly the Hausdorff dimension depends on the centre of the target, a condition uncommon in most other shrinking target type problems. This extends a theorem of Wang and Wu [Theorem 12.1, Math. Ann. 2021].



1
Introduction

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space equipped with a Borel probability measure
\mu
and let
T:X\to X
be a measure preserving transformation; that is, for any Borel set
A\subset X
, we have
\mu(T^{-1}A)=\mu(A)
.
Suppose we are given a sequence
(B_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of measurable sets in
X
, and suppose that these sets are “shrinking” in some way. Then, the classical
shrinking target problem
is concerned with studying the set of points
x\in X
which “hit” infinitely many of the targets,
B_{n}
, when acted upon by
T
; i.e., one is often interested in the set

{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{x\in X:T^{n}x\in B_{n}\quad\text{for i.m. }n\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\},

where ‘i.m.’ denotes ‘infinitely many’.
Typically one is interested in studying the measure theoretic properties of the set
{\mathcal{B}}
. However, initiated by the work of Hill and Velani
[
12
,
13
]
there has been a great amount of interest in studying the Hausdorff dimension of shrinking target sets. Usually the underlying set
X
is a fractal set, in particular when
X
is a self-similar, self-conformal, or self-affine set and the sets
B_{n}
are balls where the radius shrinks exponentially. See for example
[
9
, Chapter 9]
,
[
7
, Chapters 2 and 4]
or
[
11
, §7-9]
for the relevant definitions and background on self-similar, self-conformal and self-affine sets.

In
[
13
]
, Hill and Velani studied the Hausdorff dimension of shrinking targets sets in self-conformal sets. This was followed up more recently with work relating to the Hausdorff measures of the same sets by Baker
[
2
]
, and the first author together with Bárány
[
1
]
. Baker also studied shrinking targets in self-similar sets with overlaps in
[
3
]
. The problem of shrinking targets on self-affine sets has also been studied, see
[
5
]
for the Bedford-McMullen carpets case,
[
17
]
for a “typical” self-affine set,
[
6
]
for generic (as introduced by Falconer
[
10
]
) self-affine sets, and
[
16
]
for self-affine sets where the rate of shrinking is path-dependent. Very recently, Baker and Koivusalo have studied shrinking targets in overlapping self-affine iterated function systems where the targets considered may be more exotic
[
4
]
. In
[
15
]
, the second author and Koivusalo consider shrinking targets in a class of self-affine sets. While the shrinking targets considered in
[
15
]
are balls, the structure of the self-affine sets makes the work similar in nature to the present work where we consider rectangular shrinking targets for self-similar systems.

Much of the work above focuses on the shrinking target problem for sequences of balls. Our aim in this paper is to study the Hausdorff dimension, denoted
\dimh
, of shrinking targets in self-similar carpets where our targets are rectangles rather than balls. Results relating to rectangular shrinking targets on a restrictive class of self-affine sets have been obtained by Wang and Wu
[
19
, Section 12]
, but they require the underlying self-affine set to be the Cartesian product of one-dimensional self-similar sets, see
[
19
]
for more details. We do not have this condition in our setup, but we do require the self-similar carpet under consideration to have a nice “grid structure”.

As a motivation for our setup, consider the following example. Let
V
denote the Vicsek set or Cross Fractal. That is,
V
is the set of points in
[0,1]^{2}
whose two dimensional base
3
expansion uses exclusively the digits
\{(0,0),(2,0),(0,2),(1,1),(2,2)\}
. Consider the transformation
\tilde{T}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]^{2}
defined by

\tilde{T}(x_{1},x_{2})=\left(3x_{1}\mod 1\,,\,3x_{2}\mod 1\,\right).

Our results applied to this setting give:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\xi\geq\lambda>0
, let
y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in V
be fixed, and consider the set

V_{\lambda,\xi}(y):=\left\{x\in V:\tilde{T}^{n}(x)\in[y_{1}-3^{-n\lambda},y_{1}+3^{-n\lambda}]\times[y_{2}-3^{-n\xi},y_{2}+3^{-n\xi}]\quad\text{ for i.m. }\,\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}\,.

Suppose that the digit frequencies of
y_{2}
exist for each digit base
3
. Then

\dimh{V}_{\lambda,\xi}(y)=\min\left\{\frac{\dimh V}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\dimh V+(\xi-\lambda)\dimh V_{y}\right)\right\},

where
V_{y}
denotes the horizontal slice through
V
at the point
y
.

The case where
\lambda=\xi
is the standard shrinking target problem for shrinking targets covered by
[
12
]
, see also
[
1
]
. The case with
\xi>\lambda
is given by our Theorem
2.1
which in turn follows from Theorem
2.6
.
See §
2
for the definition of digit frequency. As seen in §
2
the condition that the digit frequency of
y_{2}
exists can be removed. However, what this condition does allow us to do is state our result in terms of the Hausdorff dimension of the horizontal slice. Note that our general result (Theorem
2.6
) gives the exact Hausdorff dimension formula for any choice of
y\in V
in the above theorem, but the formula becomes more complicated when
y
is not a “typical” point in the fractal. See §
6
for further discussion on this matter.

Note interestingly that the dimension depends on the centre of our target. For example

\dimh V_{\lambda,\xi}((0,0))=\min\left\{\frac{\dimh V}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\dimh V+(\xi-\lambda)\dimh{\mathcal{K}}\right)\right\},

where
{\mathcal{K}}
denotes the middle-third Cantor set, but

\dimh V_{\lambda,\xi}\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\right)=\frac{\dimh V}{1+\xi}\,.

The paper is laid out accordingly. In the following subsection we give a generalised setup in which our results are applicable. In §
2
we state our main results, firstly in a special case where the Hausdorff dimension can be stated in a intuitive manner (Theorem
2.1
) and then in the general case where the Hausdorff dimension is a little more complicated (Theorem
2.6
). The weakening required to state the special case, in comparison to the general case, is discussed further in §
6
where various examples are presented. In §
3
and
4
we prove our main result and in §
5
our special case is deduced via the general case.

We should mention that at the time of preparation of this article we discovered that Edouard Daviaud was simultaneously proving a similar result, but with different techniques. This was done via mass transference principles and can be found in
[
8
]
. Theorem 3.4 in
[
8
]
is essentially the same result as our Corollary
6.1
.

1.1
Our setting

Fix
b\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
b\geq 2
and let
J\subset\{0,\dots,b-1\}^{2}
be a proper subset with
\#J\geq 2
. For each
(u,v)\in J
, let
f_{u,v}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]^{2}
be the map defined by

f_{u,v}(x,y)=\left(\frac{x+u}{b},\frac{y+v}{b}\right).

Consider the self-similar iterated function system

\Phi=\{f_{u,v}:(u,v)\in J\}

and let
\Lambda
be the attractor of
\Phi
. That is,
\Lambda
is the unique non-empty compact subset of
[0,1]^{2}
for which

\Lambda=\bigcup_{(u,v)\in J}{f_{u,v}(\Lambda)}.

The fact that such a set
\Lambda
exists is a classical result due to Hutchinson
[
14
]
. Since all of the maps in
\Phi
are similarities, we call
\Phi
a
self-similar iterated function system
and call
\Lambda
a
self-similar set
(or, to be more precise, the
self-similar set associated to
\Phi
).

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
\mathbf{t}=((u_{1},v_{1}),\dots,(u_{n},v_{n}))\in J^{n}
we will use the shorthand notation

f_{\mathbf{t}}=f_{u_{1},v_{1}}\circ\dots\circ f_{u_{n},v_{n}}.

For a point
(x,y)\in\Lambda
, let
f_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y)_{i}
denote the
i
th coordinate value of
f_{\mathbf{t}}(x,y)
. For each
a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}
, define

J_{1}(a)=\{(u,v)\in J:u=a\},

and

J_{2}(a)=\{(u,v)\in J:v=a\}.

If we consider taking the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
and splitting it into a
b\times b
grid of squares (with rows and columns indexed by
0,1,\dots,b-1
), each of the maps
f_{u,v}\in\Phi
maps the unit square to the smaller square of side-length
b^{-1}
in the
u
th column and
v
th row. The set
J_{2}(a)
therefore consists of the pairs
(u,v)\in J
corresponding to maps
f_{u,v}
with images belonging to the
a
th row of this
b\times b
grid. Similarly, the set
J_{1}(a)
consists of the pairs
(u,v)\in J
corresponding to maps
f_{u,v}
with images belonging to the
a
th column.

For any
(x,y)\in\Lambda
we may write

(x,y)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\frac{x_{i}}{b^{i}}},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\frac{y_{i}}{b^{i}}}\right)

(1.1)

where
(x_{i},y_{i})\in J
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
A priori
, the sequence
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in J^{{\mathbb{N}}}
yielding (
1.1
) may not be unique. Let
A(x,y)
denote all sequences
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in J^{{\mathbb{N}}}
satisfying (
1.1
). For each
(x,y)\in\Lambda
, we pick a unique representative sequence
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
from
A(x,y)
as follows: if
\#A(x,y)>1
, in the first instance choose the sequence such that

\prod_{i=1}^{N}\#J_{2}(y_{i})=\max_{(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in A(x,y)}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\#J_{2}(y_{i}^{\prime})\quad\text{for all }N\geq N_{0}

for some
N_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If more than one sequence satisfies the above equality, choose the one with the most
(0,0)
terms, then the most
(0,b-1)
terms, and lastly the most
(b-1,0)
terms. This ordering is not particularly important, it is simply to ensure uniqueness. Let
\bar{\Sigma}
denote the collection of all of our unique representative sequences. Generally, the sequences in
\bar{\Sigma}
are chosen so that, where possible, the elements of those sequences belong to the most populated row
J_{2}(a)
.

Define
T:\Lambda\to\Lambda
by

T(x,y)=T((x_{1},x_{2},\dots),(y_{1},y_{2},\dots))=(bx-x_{1},by-y_{1}).

where
(x,y)\in\bar{\Sigma}
.

Throughout let
(\lambda(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(\xi(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be sequences of positive integers with
\xi(n)\geq\lambda(n)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\lambda(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Let
(z,w)\in\Lambda
and consider the shrinking target set

\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\left\{(x,y)\in\Lambda\,:\,T^{n}(x,y)\in[z-b^{-\lambda(n)},z+b^{-\lambda(n)}]\times[w-b^{-\xi(n)},w+b^{-\xi(n)}]\text{ for i.m. }\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}.

Let us write

J^{*}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}{J^{n}}

to denote the set of all possible finite (non-empty) sequences consisting of elements from
J
.
Note that
\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)
is closely related to the set

\displaystyle W_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)

\displaystyle:=\left\{(x,y)\in\Lambda:\left|x-f_{\mathbf{t}}(z,w)_{1}\right|\leq b^{-\lambda(|\mathbf{t}|)-|\mathbf{t}|}\text{ and }\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{=========}\left.\left|y-f_{\mathbf{t}}(z,w)_{2}\right|\leq b^{-\xi(|\mathbf{t}|)-|\mathbf{t}|}\text{ for i.m. }\mathbf{t}\in J^{*}\right\},

(1.2)

which also appears in the literature, see for example
[
1
,
19
]
. In particular, we have the following relations between the two sets.

Proposition 1.2
.

Fix
(z,w)\in\Lambda
. Then,

(i)

If
z\not\in\{0,1\}
and
w\not\in\{0,1\}
, then
\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=W_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)
.

(ii)

If
z\in\{0,1\}
or
w\in\{0,1\}
, then
\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)\subseteq W_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)
and

W_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)\subseteq\bigcup_{(r,s)\in\{-1,0,1\}^{2}}\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z+r,w+s).

This proposition is proved towards the end of §
3
.

2
Statements of results

2.1
A special case

We prove the following statement regarding the Hausdorff dimension of
\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix
(z,w)\in\Lambda
and let
(z_{i},w_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}
be its unique representative sequence. Thus,

(z,w)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}z_{i}b^{-i},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}w_{i}b^{-i}\right).

Suppose the limits

\lambda:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda(n)}{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\xi:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\xi(n)}{n}

exist and that
\xi>\lambda
. Further, suppose that the limits

p_{a}(z,w):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\left\{1\leq i\leq n:w_{i}=a\right\}}{n}

exist for each
a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}
and that
p_{0}(z,w)<1
and
p_{b-1}(z,w)<1
. Then,

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\gamma(z,w)_{2}\right)\right\}\,,

where

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\dimh\Lambda=\frac{\log\#J}{\log b}

and

\displaystyle\gamma(z,w)_{2}

\displaystyle=\dimh\left(\Lambda\cap\left\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}:y=w\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log b}\sum_{a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}}p_{a}(z,w)\log\#J_{2}(a)\,.

Remark 2.2
.

In the above,
p_{a}(z,w)
is the limiting frequency of the digit
a
in the word
(w_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
\gamma
is the Hausdorff dimension of
\Lambda
, and
\gamma(z,w)_{2}
is the Hausdorff dimension of the horizontal slice through
\Lambda
including the point
(z,w)
. To see this, note that the horizontal slice at height
w
(as long as
w
does not have
2
different
b
-expansions which would imply
p_{0}(z,w)=1
or
p_{b-1}(z,w)=1
) can be expressed as

\Lambda^{w}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}b^{-i}:(x_{i},w_{i})\in J\right\}.

Following the methods in example 1.4.2 in Bishop and Peres
[
7
]
(they cover the case
b=2
but the general case is a simple adaptation of this) we get that

\dimh\Lambda^{w}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{a=0}^{b-1}n^{-1}\#\{1\leq i\leq n:w_{i}=a\}\log\#J_{2}(a)}{\log b}.

Thus, in the case where the limits

p_{a}(z,w):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\left\{1\leq i\leq n:w_{i}=a\right\}}{n}

exist for all
0\leq a\leq b-1
with
p_{0}(z,w)\neq 1
and
p_{b-1}(z,w)\neq 1
, we get that

\dimh\Lambda^{w}=\frac{\sum_{a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}}p_{a}(z,w)\log\#J_{2}(a)}{\log b}.

Remark 2.3
.

Theorem
2.1
follows from a more general result (Theorem
2.6
) given in the following section. At the end of the paper we comment on what happens when the conditions in Theorem
2.1
are relaxed.

Remark 2.4
.

Theorem
1.1
can be deduced from Theorem
2.1
as follows. The case when
\lambda=\xi
corresponds to the more traditional shrinking target problem for balls and is already known (see
[
1
]
and
[
12
]
). Thus, we may suppose
\xi>\lambda
. Next, notice in this case that
\xi(n)=n\xi
and
\lambda(n)=n\lambda
, so the limits
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda(n)}{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\xi(n)}{n}
exist. Furthermore, since we ask that the base
3
digit frequencies of
y_{2}
exist, we have that the limits
p_{a}(y_{1},y_{2})
exist for each
0\leq a\leq 2
. Now, it is entirely possible that we have
p_{0}(y_{1},y_{2})=1
or
p_{2}(y_{1},y_{2})=1
. However, in this case, this is not a problem due to the symmetry of the cross fractal; in particular, we have
\#J_{2}(0)=\#J_{2}(2)
. See (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) in §
6
for further details.

2.2
The general result

We begin by defining the following subset of words.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(z,w)\in\Lambda
and let
(z_{i},w_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}
be its unique representative sequence.
Let
(\xi(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(\lambda(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be sequences of positive integers with
\xi(n)\geq\lambda(n)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
M_{n}(z,w)
denote the set of words
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}
such that the following two conditions are met:

(1)

(i)

x_{i+n}=z_{i}
for
1\leq i<\lambda(n)

or

(ii)

there exists
1\leq j<\lambda(n)
such that
x_{i+n}=z_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq j-1
and either

(a)

x_{j+n}-z_{j}=-1
and
x_{i+n}-z_{i}=b-1
for
j+1\leq i<\lambda(n)
, or

(b)

x_{j+n}-z_{j}=1
and
z_{i}-x_{i+n}=b-1
for
j+1\leq i<\lambda(n)
.

(2)

(i)

y_{i+n}=w_{i}
for
1\leq i<\xi(n)

or

(ii)

there exists
1\leq j<\xi(n)
such that
y_{i+n}=w_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq j-1
and either

(a)

y_{j+n}-w_{j}=-1
and
y_{i+n}-w_{i}=b-1
for
j+1\leq i<\xi(n)
, or

(b)

y_{j+n}-w_{j}=1
and
w_{i}-y_{i+n}=b-1
for
j+1\leq i<\xi(n)
.

Essentially
M_{n}(z,w)
contains the set of all words
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}
that are close to
(z_{i},w_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, where closeness is determined by
n
and the integers
\lambda(n)
and
\xi(n)
. Generally, the larger
n
is, the closer the points in
M_{n}(z,w)
will be to
(z_{i},w_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Since each sequence
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}
maps to a unique point
(x,y)\in\Lambda
, we may often simply write ”
(x,y)\in M_{n}(z,w)
”, meaning that there exists a sequence
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in M_{n}(z,w)
such that
(x,y)=\Pi((x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}})
. Here,
\Pi:\bar{\Sigma}\to\Lambda
denotes the usual
projection mapping
, as defined in Section
3
. For a fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
(z_{i},w_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, there will exist a unique integer
k_{w}(n)
with
1\leq k_{w}(n)<\xi(n)
such that all words
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in M_{n}(z,w)
will satisfy

(x_{i+n},y_{i+n})_{i=1,\dots,\xi(n)}=(x_{i+n},w_{i})_{i=1,\dots,k_{w}(n)}(x_{j+n},y_{j+n})_{j=k_{w}(n)+1,\dots,\xi(n)}\,.

That is, for each
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in M_{n}(z,w)
,
(y_{i+n})_{i=1,\dots,k_{w}(n)}
will be the same as
(w_{i})_{i=1,\dots,k_{w}(n)}
irrespective of whether later digits agree or not.

For each
a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
y\in\Lambda_{2}=\{y:(x,y)\in\Lambda\text{ for some }x\}
, and
j\geq\lambda(n)
, let

p_{n,j}(a,y)=\#\{\lambda(n)\leq i\leq j:y_{i+n}=a\}

and

A_{n,j}(z,w)=\max_{(x,y)\in M_{n}(z,w)}\left\{\sum_{a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}}p_{n,j}(a,y)\log\#J_{2}(a)\right\}.

Note that for
j>\xi(n)
there are no restrictions on the subword
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=\xi(n)+1,\ldots,j}
of

(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in M_{n}(z,w)
, so the choice for these letters, when considering
A_{n,j}(z,w)
, would simply be the one where
\#J_{2}(a)
is largest. For
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}
, corresponding to a point
(x,y)\in\Lambda
, the quantity
p_{n,j}(a,y)
tells us about the frequency of the digit
a
in the finite subword
y_{\lambda(n)}y_{\lambda(n)+1}\dots y_{j}
of the word
(y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. The set
M_{n}(z,w)
is essentially constructed as follows: given a fixed
(z,w)\in\Lambda
, we approximate
z
in the horizontal axis by the
b
-adic interval of length
b^{-\lambda(n)}
containing
z
, and then include the two other
b
-adic intervals adjacent. We repeat this in the vertical axis by approximating
w
by a
b
-adic interval of length
b^{-\xi(n)}
containing
w
, and again include the two other adjacent intervals. Taking the product of these
3
intervals in each axis we obtain
9
rectangles with sidelengths
b^{-\lambda(n)}
and
b^{-\xi(n)}
in the horizontal and vertical axes respectively, and with the centre rectangle containing
(z,w)
. Then,
M_{n}(z,w)
is the collection of all words in
\bar{\Sigma}
with image (under the usual projection mapping) contained in the collection of rectangles constructed. To calculate the value
A_{n,j}(z,w)
we pick the rectangle, out of those constructed in describing
M_{n}(z,w)
, with the most
b
-adic cubes (of sidelength
b^{-\xi(n)}
) that have non-empty intersection with
\Lambda
. Then
A_{n,j}(z,w)
is the logarithm of the cardinality of the non-empty intersecting
b
-adic cubes in this rectangle.

We prove the following statement regarding the Hausdorff dimension of
\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let

s_{n}=\min\left\{\frac{n\log\#J+A_{n,j}(z,w)}{(n+j)\log b}\,:\,\lambda(n)\leq j\leq\xi(n)\right\}\,.

Then

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}\,.

3
Preliminaries

We recall the definitions of Hausdorff measure and Hausdorff dimension. For more details see
[
9
]
. For any
0<\rho\leq\infty
, any finite or countable collection of balls
(B_{i})_{i\geq 1}
contained in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

F\subseteq\bigcup_{i}B_{i}
and
r(B_{i})<\rho
for all
i
, is called a
\rho
-cover
of
F
. Here we use the notation
r(B)
to denote the radius of a ball
B
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. For
s>0
let

{\mathcal{H}}_{\rho}^{s}(F)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i}r(B_{i})^{s}:\{B_{i}\}\text{ is a $\rho$-cover of }F\right\}.

The
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
F
is defined to be

{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(F)=\lim_{\rho\to 0}{\mathcal{H}}_{\rho}^{s}(F).

The
Hausdorff dimension
of
F
, denoted by
\dimh F
, is defined as

\dimh F:=\inf\left\{s\geq 0\;:\;{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(F)=0\right\}\,.

A fairly standard approach to computing the Hausdorff dimension of a set is to split the computations into two cases; an upper bound calculation and a lower bound calculation. The upper bound calculation is often easier and can be realised by taking a standard cover of the set while establishing lower bounds can frequently be somewhat more challenging. To this end, the following
mass distribution principle
often proves to be useful (see, for example,
[
9
, Chapter 4.1]
).

Lemma 3.1

(Mass Distribution Principle)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on a subset
X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Suppose that for some
s>0
there exist constants
c{>0}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mu(B)\leq cr(B)^{s}

for all open balls
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with
r(B)<\varepsilon
. Then
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(X)\geq\frac{1}{c}{>0}
and thus
\dimh X\geq s
.

We use the notational conventions

\Sigma=J^{{\mathbb{N}}},\qquad\Sigma^{n}=J^{n},\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma^{*}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}J^{n}.

We say that a function
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies
f(n)=o(g)
, where
g:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, if
\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
\Pi:\Sigma\to\Lambda
denote the usual projection mapping; thus,

\Pi((c_{i},d_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}b^{-i},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_{i}b^{-i}\right)\,.

Observe that
\Pi
is injective when restricted to
\bar{\Sigma}\subset\Sigma
. For
(c,d)\in\Sigma
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let

[(c_{i},d_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m}]=\left\{(u_{i},v_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}:(u_{i},v_{i})=(c_{i},d_{i})\text{ for }i=1,\dots,m\right\}.

That is,
[(c_{i},d_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m}]
is the cylinder containing all words
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}
with their first
m
terms matching
(c_{i},d_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m}
. Finally, let
\sigma:\bar{\Sigma}\to\bar{\Sigma}
denote the usual left shift map; that is,

\sigma\left((c_{i},d_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\right)=(c_{i+1},d_{i+1})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}.

The following lemma follows immediately from the definition of the mapping
T
as given on page
1.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
(c_{i},d_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}
we have that

T\circ\Pi\left((c_{i},d_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\right)=\Pi\circ\sigma\left((c_{i},d_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\right).

The following lemma will be crucial
in the proof of Theorem

in Section

. It essentially tells us that

is a good collection of words such that the corresponding cylinders cover the rectangle

and are contained in the rectangle

R\left((z,w),b^{-\lambda(n)+2},b^{-\xi(n)+2}\right)=[z-b^{-\lambda(n)+2},z+b^{-\lambda(n)+2}]\times[w-b^{-\xi(n)+2},w+b^{-\xi(n)+2}].

Throughout, for
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, we write
R((x,y),\alpha,\beta)
to denote a rectangle with centre
(x,y)
and sidelengths
2\alpha
and

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(z,w)\in\Lambda
, let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and let
M_{n}(z,w)
be as in Definition
2.5
.

(a)

If

T^{n}(x,y)\in R\left((z,w),b^{-\lambda(n)},b^{-\xi(n)}\right),

then
(x,y)\in M_{n}(z,w)
.

(b)

If
(x,y)\in M_{n}(z,w)
, then

T^{n}(x,y)\in R\left((z,w),b^{-\lambda(n)+2},b^{-\xi(n)+2}\right).

Proof.

(a)
Writing
\|\cdot\|
to denote the usual supremum norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, observe that

\left\|T^{n}\circ\Pi\left((x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\right)-(z,w)\right\|=\left\|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i+n}-z_{i})b^{-i},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(y_{i+n}-w_{i})b^{-i}\right)\right\|.

Thus, if

T^{n}(x,y)\in R\left((z,w),b^{-\lambda(n)},b^{-\xi(n)}\right),

then

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i+n}-z_{i})b^{-i}\right|\leq b^{-\lambda(n)}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(y_{i+n}-w_{i})b^{-i}\right|\leq b^{-\xi(n)}.

Consider the first coordinate axis and suppose that
(x_{i+n})_{i=1\dots,\lambda(n)}
does not satisfy the conditions of Definition
2.5
. We consider the following two cases:

(i)

Suppose that for some
1\leq j<\lambda(n)
we have
x_{i+n}=z_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq j-1
, but
x_{j+n}-z_{j}\geq 2
. Then, by our hypothesis combined with the reverse triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i+n}-z_{i})b^{-i}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|(x_{j+n}-z_{j})b^{-j}+\sum_{i=j+1}^{\infty}(x_{i+n}-z_{i})b^{-i}\right|

\displaystyle{\geq\left|(x_{j+n}-z_{j})b^{-j}\right|-\sum_{i=j+1}^{\infty}{\left|x_{i+n}-z_{i}\right|b^{-i}}}

\displaystyle\geq 2b^{-j}-\sum_{i=j+1}^{\infty}{(b-1)b^{-i}}

\displaystyle=2b^{-j}-b^{-j}

\displaystyle>b^{-\lambda(n)}

and hence
T^{n}(x,y)\not\in R\left((z,w),b^{-\lambda(n)},b^{-\xi(n)}\right)
. A similar argument yields the same contradiction if
x_{i+n}=z_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq j-1
and
x_{j+n}-z_{j}\leq-2
.

(ii)

Suppose that for some
1\leq j<\lambda(n)
we have
x_{i+n}=z_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq j-1
and
x_{j+n}-z_{j}=1
, but
z_{t}-x_{t+n}\leq b-2
for some
j+1\leq t<\lambda(n)
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i+n}-z_{i})b^{-i}

\displaystyle=(x_{j+n}-z_{j})b^{-j}-\sum_{i=j+1}^{\infty}(z_{i}-x_{i+n})b^{-i}

\displaystyle=b^{-j}-\sum_{i=j+1}^{\infty}{(z_{i}-x_{i+n})b^{-i}}.

Now observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=j+1}^{\infty}{(z_{i}-x_{i+n})b^{-i}}

\displaystyle\leq-b^{-t}+\sum_{i=j+1}^{\infty}(b-1)b^{-i}

\displaystyle=-b^{-t}+b^{-j}\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i+n}-z_{i})b^{-i}

\displaystyle\geq b^{-t}>b^{-\lambda(n)}\,

and so
T^{n}(x,y)\not\in R\left((z,w),b^{-\lambda(n)},b^{-\xi(n)}\right)
. The same contradiction can be obtained via a similar argument in the case that
x_{i+n}=z_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq j-1
,
x_{j+n}-z_{j}=-1
, and
x_{t+n}-z_{t}\leq b-2
.

Analogous arguments can be applied in the case that
(y_{i+n})_{i=1,\dots,\xi(n)}
does not satisfy the conditions of Definition
2.5
. This concludes the proof of part
(a)
of the lemma.

(b)
Again, we start with the observation that

\left\|T^{n}\circ\Pi\left((x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\right)-(z,w)\right\|=\left\|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{i+n}-z_{i})b^{-i},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(y_{i+n}-w_{i})b^{-i}\right)\right\|.

Let
T^{n}(x,y)_{1}
denote the value of
T^{n}(x,y)
in the horizontal axis and let
T^{n}(x,y)_{2}
denote the value of
T^{n}(x,y)
in the vertical axis. If
x_{i+n}=z_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,\lambda(n)
, then

|T^{n}(x,y)_{1}-z|\leq\sum_{i=\lambda(n)+1}^{\infty}(b-1)b^{-i}=b^{-\lambda(n)}

and so
T^{n}(x,y)_{1}\in[z-b^{-\lambda(n)},z+b^{-\lambda(n)}]
. Similarly, if
y_{i+n}=w_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,\xi(n)
, then

|T^{n}(x,y)_{2}-w|\leq\sum_{i=\xi(n)+1}^{\infty}(b-1)b^{-i}=b^{-\xi(n)}

and so
T^{n}(x,y)_{2}\in[w-b^{-\xi(n)},w+b^{-\xi(n)}]
.

Next, we consider the case when, for some
1\leq j<\lambda(n)
, we have
x_{i+n}=z_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq j-1
,
x_{j+n}-z_{j}=-1
, and
x_{i+n}-z_{i}=b-1
for all
j+1\leq i<\lambda(n)
. In this case, we have

\displaystyle\left|T^{n}(x,y)_{1}-z\right|

\displaystyle{=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{x_{i+n}b^{-i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{z_{i}b^{-i}}\right|}

\displaystyle{=\left|\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}{(x_{i+n}-z_{i})b^{-i}}\right|}

\displaystyle=\left|-b^{-j}+\sum_{i=j+1}^{\lambda(n)-1}(b-1)b^{-i}+\sum_{k=\lambda(n)}^{\infty}(x_{k+n}-z_{k})b^{-k}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|-b^{-j}+b^{-j}-b^{-(\lambda(n)-1)}+\sum_{k=\lambda(n)}^{\infty}(x_{k+n}-z_{k})b^{-k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|b^{-\lambda(n)+1}\right|+\sum_{k=\lambda(n)}^{\infty}|(x_{k+n}-z_{k})|b^{-k}

\displaystyle\leq\left|b^{-\lambda(n)+1}\right|+\sum_{k=\lambda(n)}^{\infty}(b-1)b^{-k}

\displaystyle=2b^{-\lambda(n)+1}

\displaystyle\leq b^{-\lambda(n)+2}.

It can be deduced similarly that if
x_{i+n}=z_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq j-1
,
x_{j+n}-z_{j}=1
, and
z_{i}-x_{i+n}=b-1
for all
j+1\leq i<\lambda(n)
, then

|T^{n}(x,y)_{1}-z|\leq b^{-\lambda(n)+2}.

Thus, in either case,

T^{n}(x,y)_{1}\in[z-b^{-\lambda(n)+2},z+b^{-\lambda(n)+2}].

Similar calculations show that if
(x,y)\in M_{n}(z,w)
, then
T^{n}(x,y)_{2}\in[w-b^{-\xi(n)+2},w+b^{-\xi(n)+2}]
. Hence,

T^{n}(x,y)\in R\left((z,w),b^{-\lambda(n)+2},b^{-\xi(n)+2}\right),

as required.
∎

j(n)
is chosen such that

s_{n}=\frac{n\log\#J+A_{n,j(n)}(z,w)}{(n+j(n))\log b}.

Next, let
s=\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Choose
N_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
we have
s_{n}\leq s+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Let

s^{+}_{\varepsilon}=\max\left(\{s_{n}:1\leq n\leq N_{\varepsilon}\}\cup\{s\}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad j_{\varepsilon}=\max\left\{\frac{j(n)}{n}:1\leq n\leq N_{\varepsilon}\right\}.

Then, by our choice of
s
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{b^{-N}}^{s{+\varepsilon}}(\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq N}\sum_{B_{i}\in C_{n,j(n)}}b^{-(n+j(n))(s+\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq N}\#C_{n,j(n)}b^{-(n+j(n))(s+\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\leq 9\sum_{n\geq N}(\#J)^{n}\max_{(x,y)\in M_{n}(z,w)}\left\{\prod_{i=\lambda(n)}^{j(n)}(\#J_{2}(y_{i+n}))\right\}b^{-(n+j(n))(s+\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=9\sum_{n\geq N}b^{\frac{n\log\#J}{\log b}}b^{\frac{A_{n,j(n)}(z,w)}{\log b}}b^{-(n+j(n))(s+\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle{=9\sum_{n\geq N}b^{(n+j(n))\left(\frac{n\log\#J+A_{n,j(n)}(z,w)}{(n+j(n))\log b}\right)}b^{-(n+j(n))(s+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle{=9\sum_{n\geq N}b^{(n+j(n))s_{n}}b^{-(n+j(n))(s+\varepsilon)}}

\displaystyle{=9\sum_{n=N}^{N_{\varepsilon}}{b^{(n+j(n))(s_{n}-s-\varepsilon)}}+9\sum_{n=N_{\varepsilon}+1}^{\infty}{b^{(n+j(n))(s_{n}-s-\varepsilon)}}}

\displaystyle{\leq 9\sum_{n=N}^{N_{\varepsilon}}{b^{(n+j(n))(s^{+}_{\varepsilon}-s-\varepsilon)}}+9\sum_{n=N_{\varepsilon}+1}^{\infty}{b^{(n+j(n))\left(s+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-s-\varepsilon\right)}}}

\displaystyle{\leq 9\sum_{n=N}^{N_{\varepsilon}}{b^{n(1+j_{\varepsilon})(s^{+}_{\varepsilon}-s)}}+9\sum_{n=N_{\varepsilon}+1}^{\infty}{b^{-n\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}}

\displaystyle{\leq 9\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}{b^{n(1+j_{\varepsilon})(s^{+}_{\varepsilon}-s)}}+9\sum_{n=N_{\varepsilon}+1}^{\infty}{b^{-n\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}}\,.

Since this bound is independent of
N
it follows by letting
N\to\infty
that
{\mathcal{H}}^{s+\varepsilon}(\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w))<\infty
and so by the definition of Hausdorff dimension it follows that
\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)\leq s
. This completes the proof of the upper bound of Theorem
2.6
.

4.2
Lower bound

4.2.1
Outline of the strategy

For establishing the lower bound in Theorem
2.6
we use the mass distribution principle (Lemma
3.1
). Firstly we translate the problem of considering points in
\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)
to the symbolic space. We then construct a suitable measure on the symbolic space such that the projection of the support of the measure is a Cantor set contained in a subset of
\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)
. We then use the mass distribution principle to calculate a lower bound for the Hausdorff dimension of
\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)
by computing the Hölder exponent of the measure at an arbitrary point in the Cantor set.

4.2.2
Construction of a measure

Let
(n_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a strictly increasing sequence of integers such that:

(i)

There exists a fixed
\Delta\in(1,\infty)
such that for all
k\geq 1
we have

n_{k+1}>\Delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\xi(n_{i})+2)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad n_{k+1}>n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2.

(ii)

For some
\varepsilon>0
we have

s_{n_{k}}\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}-\varepsilon

for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define

\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}((z,w),n_{k})=\left\{(x,y)\in\Lambda:T^{n_{k}}(x,y)\in R\left((z,w),b^{-\lambda(n_{k})+2},b^{-\xi(n_{k})+2}\right)\right\}.

As above, choose
\lambda(n_{k})\leq j(n_{k})\leq\xi(n_{k})-1
such that
j(n_{k})
satisfies

\frac{n_{k}\log\#J+A_{n_{k},j(n_{k})}(z,w)}{(n_{k}+j(n_{k}))\log b}=\min\left\{\frac{n_{k}\log\#J+A_{n_{k},j}(z,w)}{(n_{k}+j)\log b}\,:\,\lambda(n_{k})\leq j\leq\xi(n_{k})-1\right\}\,.

Furthermore, for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, pick some word

(\hat{x}(k),\hat{y}(k))=\Pi((\hat{x}_{i}(k),\hat{y}_{i}(k))_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}})\in M_{n_{k}}(z,w)

such that

\prod_{i=\lambda(n_{k})}^{j(n_{k})}(\#J_{2}(\hat{y}_{i+n_{k}}(k)))=\max_{(x,y)\in M_{n_{k}}(z,w)}\left\{\prod_{i=\lambda(n_{k})}^{j(n_{k})}(\#J_{2}(y_{i+n_{k}}(k)))\right\}.

Next, we define a measure
\mu
on the cylinders

\{[(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}]:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ and }(u,v)\in\bar{\Sigma}\}.

To begin with, for
1\leq m\leq n_{1}
, we assign cylinders corresponding to words of length
m
mass
\frac{1}{(\#J)^{m}}
; that is,

\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m}])=\frac{1}{(\#J)^{m}}.

In general, for a cylinder
[(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m+1}]
corresponding to a word of length
m+1\geq n_{1}+1
, we assign it a portion of
\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m}])
as follows:

•

If
n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2<m+1\leq n_{k+1}
for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then distribute the mass evenly over the digits so that

\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m+1}])=\frac{\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m}])}{\#J}.

•

If
n_{k}<m+1\leq n_{k}+\lambda(n_{k})+2
for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then assign all the mass at each digit to the digit matching
(\hat{x}_{m+1}(k),\hat{y}_{m+1}(k))_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
so that

\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m+1}])=\begin{cases}\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m}])&\textrm{if }(u_{m+1},v_{m+1})=(\hat{x}_{m+1}(k),\hat{y}_{m+1}(k))\;,\\[4.30554pt]
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

•

If
n_{k}+\lambda(n_{k})+2<m+1\leq n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2
for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then distribute the mass at each digit evenly over the digits in the set
J_{2}(\hat{y}_{m+1}(k))
so that

\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m+1}])=\begin{cases}\frac{\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,m}])}{\#J_{2}(\hat{y}_{m+1}(k))}&\textrm{if }(u_{m+1},v_{m+1})\in J_{2}(\hat{y}_{m+1}(k))\;,\\[4.30554pt]
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Essentially, the measure
\mu
distributes the mass evenly over the first
n_{1}
digits in the construction set for
\Lambda
, then all mass is concentrated to the digits that match up with the word whose cylinder projects (by
\Pi
) to a ball containing the most populated rectangle formed in the definition of
M_{n}(z,w)
. When the sidelength of this projected cylinder is slightly smaller than the longest sidelength of the rectangle the measure
\mu
then begins to distribute the mass over digits whose words have cylinders that project to a populated ball in said rectangle. This continues until the projected cylinder’s sidelength is slightly smaller than the smallest sidelength of the rectangle. The cycle then begins again, i.e. the mass is evenly distributed over the digits in the construction set of
\Lambda
until we reach the
n_{2}
th digit etc.

Define
\nu=\mu\circ\Pi^{-1}
. It follows from the construction of the measure
\mu
that

\nu\left(\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}((z,w),n_{k})\right)=1.

4.2.3
Measure of a general ball

Fix
(x,y)\in\bigcap_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}((z,w),n_{k})
and let
r>0
. We will consider the measure
\nu(B((x,y),r))
of the ball
B((x,y),r)
. To this end, suppose that
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the unique integer such that

b^{-n-1}<r\leq b^{-n}

and notice that
B((x,y),r)
can intersect at most
9
projections (in terms of the projection mapping
\Pi
) of
n
-level cylinders. Thus

\nu(B((x,y),r))\leq 9\mu([(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}]).

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
be the unique value of
k
such that
n_{k}<n\leq n_{k+1}
. For convenience, let us also define

V(n)=\prod_{a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}}(\#J_{2}(a))^{-p^{*}_{n_{k},n-n_{k}}(a,\hat{y}(k))},

where

p^{*}_{n_{k},n-n_{k}}(a,\hat{y}(k))=\#\{\lambda(n_{k})+3\leq i\leq\min\{n-n_{k},\xi(n_{k})+2\}\,:\,\hat{y}_{i+n_{k}}(k)=a\}.

By the construction of the measure
\mu
and choice of
(x,y)
with unique representative sequence
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\bar{\Sigma}
, we have the following non-zero possibilities for the measure of a cylinder
\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}])
which may be intersected by
B((x,y),r)
:

•

If
n_{k}<n\leq n_{k}+\lambda(n_{k})+2
, then

\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}])=(\#J)^{-\left(n_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\xi(n_{i})+2)\right)}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\prod_{i=\lambda(n_{l})+3}^{\xi(n_{l})+2}(\#J_{2}(\hat{y}_{i+n_{l}}(l)))^{-1}=:U(n_{k}).

•

If
n_{k}+\lambda(n_{k})+2<n\leq n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2
, then

\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}])=U(n_{k})\prod_{i=\lambda(n_{k})+3}^{n-n_{k}}(\#J_{2}(\hat{y}_{i+n_{k}}(k)))^{-1}=U(n_{k})V(n).

•

If
n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2<n\leq n_{k+1}
, then

\mu([(u_{i},v_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}])=U(n_{k})V(n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2)(\#J)^{-(n-(n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2))}.

Notice that

\displaystyle U(n_{k})

\displaystyle=(\#J)^{-\left(n_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\xi(n_{i})+2)\right)}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\prod_{i=\lambda(n_{l})+3}^{\xi(n_{l})+2}\left(\#J_{2}(\hat{y}_{i+n_{l}}(l))\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq(\#J)^{-(n_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\xi(n_{i})+2))}

\displaystyle\leq(\#J)^{-n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1})}

\displaystyle=b^{-n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1})\frac{\log\#J}{\log b}},

where
\Delta
is as on page
i
.

Next, recall that

p_{n,j}(a,\hat{y}(k))=\#\{\lambda(n)\leq i\leq j\,:\,\hat{y}_{i+n}(k)=a\}\,,

and let

C=\left(\max_{a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}}\#J_{2}(a)\right)^{3}.

Counting the number of times each digit
a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}
appears in the string of digits
\left(\hat{y}_{n_{k}+i}(k)\right)_{i=\lambda(n_{k}),\ldots,n-n_{k}}
we get

\prod_{a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}}(\#J_{2}(a))^{-p_{n_{k},n-n_{k}}(a,\hat{y}(k))}.

This is essentially what
V(n)
does except for that
V(n)
starts counting digits at
n_{k}+\lambda(n_{k})+3
. Thus,

C^{-1}\prod_{a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}}(\#J_{2}(a))^{-p_{n_{k},n-n_{k}}(a,\hat{y}(k))}\leq V(n)\leq C\prod_{a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}}(\#J_{2}(a))^{-p_{n_{k},n-n_{k}}(a,\hat{y}(k))}\,.

Putting all of the above together, and recalling that
b^{-n-1}<r\leq b^{-n}
and
n_{k}<n\leq n_{k+1}
, we have the following cases:

•

If
n_{k}<n\leq n_{k}+\lambda(n_{k})+2
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\log\nu(B((x,y),r))}{\log r}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{-\log U(n_{k})}{n\log b}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1})\log\#J}{n\log b}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1})\log\#J}{(n_{k}+\lambda(n_{k})+2)\log b}.

•

If
n_{k}+\lambda(n_{k})+2<n\leq n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\log\nu(B((x,y),r))}{\log r}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{-\log U(n_{k})-\log V(n)}{n\log b}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1})\log\#J+A_{n_{k},n-n_{k}}(z,w)-\log C}{n\log b}.

•

If
n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2<n\leq n_{k+1}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\log\nu(B((x,y),r))}{\log r}

\displaystyle\phantom{=====}\geq\frac{-\log U(n_{k})-\log V(n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2)-\log(\#J)^{-(n-(n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2))}}{n\log b}

\displaystyle\phantom{=====}\geq\frac{(n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1})+n-(n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2))\log\#J+A_{n_{k},\xi(n_{k})+2}(z,w)-\log C}{n\log b}

\displaystyle\phantom{=====}\geq\frac{(n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1})+(n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2)-(n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2))\log\#J+A_{n_{k},\xi(n_{k})+2}(z,w)-\log C}{(n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2)\log b}

\displaystyle\phantom{=====}\geq\frac{(n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1}))\log\#J+A_{n_{k},\xi(n_{k})+2}(z,w)-\log C}{(n_{k}+\xi(n_{k})+2)\log b}.

Thus, noting that the endpoints of the second case correspond to the lower bounds for the first and third of the above cases and recalling that
\Delta>1
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\log\nu(B((x,y),r))}{\log r}

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{\frac{n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1})\log\#J+A_{n_{k},j}(z,w)-\log C}{(n_{k}+j)\log b}\quad:\,\lambda(n_{k})+2<j\leq\xi(n_{k})+2\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{\frac{n_{k}(1-\Delta^{-1})\log\#J+(1-\Delta^{-1})A_{n_{k},j}(z,w)-\log C}{(n_{k}+j)\log b}\quad:\,\lambda(n_{k})+2<j\leq\xi(n_{k})+2\right\}.

Since
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{-\log C}{n_{k}\log b}=0
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}s_{n_{k}}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}
,
we have for all sufficiently large
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
(which corresponds to all sufficiently small
r>0
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\log\nu(B((x,y),r))}{\log r}

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{\frac{(1-\Delta^{-1})\left(n_{k}\log\#J+A_{n_{k},j}(z,w)\right)}{(n_{k}+j)\log b}\quad:\,\lambda(n_{k})+2<j\leq\xi(n_{k})+2\right\}-\varepsilon

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta^{-1})s_{n_{k}}-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq(1-\Delta^{-1})\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}-2\varepsilon.

Since this holds for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
\Delta\in(1,\infty)
, by the mass distribution principle (Lemma
3.1
) we have that

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}.

\frac{k_{w}(n)}{n}=\frac{\xi(n)}{n}+o(1).

We will also use the following estimate:

\displaystyle(j-\lambda(n))p_{a}(z,w)+\mathit{o}(n)\,,

Recall that for

\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)\,.

For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
u(n)
be an integer with
\lambda(n)\leq u(n)\leq\xi(n)
such that

s_{n}=\frac{n\log\#J+A_{n,u(n)}(z,w)}{(n+u(n))\log b}.

We now choose a subsequence
(n_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

\lim_{i\to\infty}s_{n_{i}}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)

and for which

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{u(n_{i})}{n_{i}}=u\in[\lambda,\xi].

We can do this as
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{\lambda(n)}{n}=\lambda
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{\xi(n)}{n}=\xi
so
\left(\frac{u(n)}{n}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a bounded sequence with limit points in
[\lambda,\xi]
.
Applying Lemma
5.3
gives that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}s_{n_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{n_{i}\log\#J+(u(n_{i})-\lambda(n_{i}))\sum_{a=0}^{b-1}p_{a}(z,w)\log\#J_{2}(a)+\mathit{o}(n_{i})}{(n_{i}+u(n_{i}))\log b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+u}(\gamma+(u-\lambda)\gamma(z,w)_{2}).

We have that for
u
between
\lambda
and
\xi
the function

u\mapsto\frac{1}{1+u}(\gamma+(u-\lambda)\gamma(z,w)_{2})

is monotonic and hence

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}\geq\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\gamma(z,w)_{2})\right\}.

In the other direction we have

\displaystyle s_{n}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{n\log\#J+A_{n,j}(z,w)}{(n+j)\log b}:\lambda(n)\leq j\leq\xi(n)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{\frac{n\log\#J}{(n+\lambda(n))\log b},\frac{n\log\#J+A_{n,\xi(n)}(z,w)}{(n+\xi(n))\log b}\right\}.

Note that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\log\#J}{(n+\lambda(n))\log b}=\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda}.

Furthermore, by Lemma
5.3
, we also have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\log\#J+A_{n,\xi(n)}(z,w)}{(n+\xi(n))\log b}=\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\gamma(z,w)_{2}\right)\,.

Thus,

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)\leq\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\gamma(z,w)_{2}\right)\right\}

and the proof of Theorem
2.1
is complete.
∎

6
Further remarks

In this section we consider some examples illustrating why the assumptions in Theorem
2.1
are necessary and explain how they may be weakened in certain circumstances.

6.1
Digit frequencies:
p_{0}(z,w)<1
and
p_{b-1}(z,w)<1

We start by looking at the assumption that
p_{0}(z,w)<1
(the case where
p_{b-1}(z,w)<1
is analogous). This assumption is used to show that words
(x,y)\in M_{n}(z,w)
will satisfy that for
\lambda(n)<j\leq\xi(n)
,

p_{n,j}(a,y)=p_{a}(z,w)(j-\lambda(n))+\mathit{o}(n)\quad\text{ for all $0\leq a\leq b-1$.}

So we will now look at the case where
p_{0}(z,w)=1
, but all the other assumptions of Theorem
2.1
are met, in particular

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda(n)}{n}=\lambda<\xi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\xi(n)}{n}.

If
w=0
, then it will be the case that
p_{0}(z,w)=1
. Moreover, in this case, we have that
(x,y)\in M_{n}(z,w)
implies
y_{i+n}=w_{i}=0
for all
1\leq i<\xi(n)
. Thus, in this case, for
\lambda(n)\leq j\leq\xi(n)
we will have that

p_{n,j}(a,y)=p_{a}(z,w)(j-\lambda(n)+1)+o(n)

for all
0\leq a\leq b-1
(where the
o(n)
term is coming from the possibility that we may have
y_{n+\xi(n)}\neq w_{\xi(n)}
). In turn, this means in this case that

A_{n,j}(z,w)=(j-\lambda(n)+1)\log{\#J_{2}(0)}+o(n)

and hence

s_{n}=\min\left\{\frac{n\log{\#J}+(j-\lambda(n)+1)\log{\#J_{2}(0)}}{(n+j)\log{b}}:\lambda(n)\leq j\leq\xi(n)\right\}+o(1),

where
s_{n}
is as defined in Theorem
2.6
.
Thus, when the other assumptions of Theorem
2.1
are satisfied, it follows from Theorem
2.6
that

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\frac{\log\#J_{2}(0)}{\log b}\right)\right\}.

(6.1)

Following an analogous argument, when
w=1
(i.e.
w_{i}=b-1
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
), we get

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\frac{\log\#J_{2}(b-1)}{\log b}\right)\right\}.

(6.2)

This means we have the following corollary to Theorem
2.6
regarding typical points for an ergodic measure.

Corollary 6.1
.

Let
\mu
be a
T
-invariant ergodic probability measure with
\mu(\Lambda)=1
. If we have

\lambda=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda(n)}{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\xi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\xi(n)}{n}

exist, then for
\mu
-almost all
(z,w)\in\Lambda
we have that

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\gamma_{2}(\mu)\right)\right\}\,,

where

\gamma_{2}(\mu)=\frac{\sum_{a=0}^{b-1}p_{a}(\mu)\log\#J_{2}(a)}{\log b}

with
p_{a}(\mu)
as defined in (
6.3
).

Proof.

Let
\mu
be a
T
-invariant ergodic probability measure with
\mu(\Lambda)=1
(see Definitions 1.1 and 1.4 in
[
18
]
for the relevant definitions). First of all we show that for
\mu
-almost all
(z,w)
,
w
has a unique base
b
-expansion. To see this we consider the sets

A=\left\{\left(z,\tfrac{i}{b}\right)\in\Lambda:i\in\{1,\ldots,b-1\}\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad B=\left\{\left(z,\tfrac{i}{b}\right)\in\Lambda:i\in\{0,b\}\right\}\,.

Note that
w
does not have a unique base
b
-expansion if and only if
T^{n}(z,w)\in A
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
(note that if
(z,w)\in B
, then
w=0=(0,0,\dots)
or
w=1=(b-1,b-1,\dots)
and, in particular, has a unique base
b
-expansion). Furthermore, if
(z,w)\in A
, then
T(z,w)\in B
. We also have

A\cup B=T^{-1}(B)

which means that
\mu(A\cup B)=\mu(T^{-1}(B))=\mu(B)
. Since
A
and
B
are disjoint this gives
\mu(A)=0
and so

\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}T^{-n}(A)\right)=0\,.

Thus, since
\mu
is ergodic, it follows by the Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem (see Theorem 1.14 in
[
18
]
and the remark straight after) that for
\mu
-almost all
(z,w)

and for each
0\leq a\leq b-1
we have that

p_{a}(z,w)=\mu\left(\left\{(x,y)\in\Lambda:y\in\left[\tfrac{a}{b},\tfrac{(a+1)}{b}\right]\right\}\right)=:p_{a}(\mu)\,.

(6.3)

This means that for
\mu
-almost all
(z,w)
we have

\gamma(z,w)_{2}=\frac{\sum_{a=0}^{b-1}p_{a}(\mu)\log\#J_{2}(a)}{\log b}=\gamma_{2}(\mu)\,,

where
\gamma(z,w)_{2}
is as seen in Theorem 2.1.
Finally note that if
p_{0}(\mu)=1
then
w=0
for
\mu
-almost
all
(z,w)
and if
p_{b-1}(\mu)=1
then
w=1
for
\mu
-almost all
(z,w)
. Thus, either the assumptions for Theorem
2.1
are met or
w\in\{0,1\}
and so we are in the case dealt with above. Thus the desired result follows.
∎

In the case where
w=(w_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has
w_{i}\neq 0
for infinitely many
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
but where
p_{0}(z,w)=1
, it is possible that Theorem
2.1
will still hold. If we have that
\#J_{2}(b-1)\leq\#J_{2}(0)
, then

A_{n,j}(z,w)=(j-\lambda(n)+1)\log\#J_{2}(0)+o(n)

and so

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\frac{\log\#J_{2}(0)}{\log b}\right)\right\}.

In the case where
\#J_{2}(b-1)>\#J_{2}(0)
, for
a\in\{0,\ldots,b-1\}
let

k_{n}(w,a)=\inf\left\{j-n:w_{j}\neq a\text{ and }j>n\right\}\,.

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{k_{n}(w,0)}{n}=0
and
(x,y)\in M_{n}(z,w)
then

\left|p_{n,j}(0,y)-\#\left\{\lambda(n)\leq i\leq j:w_{i}=0\right\}\right|=\mathit{o}(n)\,.

Thus

A_{n,j}(z,w)=(j-\lambda(n)+1)\log\#J_{2}(0)+\mathit{o}({n})

and so we can again conclude from Theorem
2.6
that

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\frac{\log\#J_{2}(0)}{\log b}\right)\right\}.

Similarly, if the sequence
(w_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has
w_{i}\neq b-1
for infinitely many
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
but
p_{b-1}(z,w)=1
, and either
\#J_{2}(b-1)\geq\#J_{2}(0)
or
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{k_{n}(w,b-1)}{n}=0
, then

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\frac{\log\#J_{2}(b-1)}{\log b}\right)\right\}.

Now consider the case where the sequence
(w_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has
w_{i}\neq 0
for infinitely many
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

p_{0}(z,w)=1\,,\quad\#J_{2}(b-1)>\#J_{2}(0)\,,\quad\text{ and }\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{k_{n}(w,0)}{n}>0\,.

Here the outcome can be different. For example, suppose that
J_{2}(0)
is a proper subset of
J_{2}(b-1)
and
w
is chosen such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{k_{n}(w,0)}{n}=\eta>\xi-\lambda.

In this case we can find infinitely many values
(n_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
where there exists
g(n_{k})<\lambda(n_{k})
with
w_{g(n_{k})}\neq 0
and
w_{i}=0
for all
g(n_{k})<i\leq\xi(n_{k})
. Since we are assuming
J_{2}(b-1)\supset J_{2}(0)
this will mean that we can find
(z,y)\in M_{n_{k}}(z,w)
where
y_{g(n_{k})}=w_{g(n_{k})}-1
and
y_{i}=b-1
for all
g(n_{k})<i\leq\xi(n_{k})
. This means that we will have

A_{n_{k},j}(z,w)=(j-\lambda(n_{k})+1)\log\#J_{2}(b-1)

for all
\lambda(n_{k})<j\leq\xi(n_{k})
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and hence, by Theorem
2.6
, we have

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\frac{\log\#J_{2}(b-1)}{\log b}\right)\right\}.

Thus, if

\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda}>\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\frac{\log\#J_{2}(0)}{\log b}\right),

then we have that

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)>\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\frac{\log\#J_{2}(0)}{\log b}\right)\right\}

since
J_{2}(b-1)\supset J_{2}(0)
. Note that this is the value given by Theorem
2.1
.

6.2
Limits:
p_{a}(z,w)
always exists

We now turn to the assumption that the limits
p_{a}(z,w)
always exist. We will still assume that the limits

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda(n)}{n}=\lambda\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\xi(n)}{n}=\xi

exist and
\lambda<\xi
. Recall that

s_{n}=\min\left\{\frac{n\log\#J+A_{n,j}(z,w)}{(n+j)\log{b}}:\lambda(n)\leq j\leq\xi(n)\right\}.

Without the assumption that the limits
p_{a}(z,w)
exist we do not necessarily have that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\gamma(z,w)_{2}\right)\right\}

where

\gamma(z,w)_{2}=\dimh\left(\Lambda\cap\left\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}:y=w\right\}\right)\,.

In particular, the conclusion of Theorem
2.1
does not necessarily hold in this case.

To see this let
b=3
and
J=\{(0,0),(2,0),(0,2)\}
. We take a sequence of positive integers
(n_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
where

n_{1}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{n_{j+1}}{n_{j}}=\infty.

We then pick

(z,w)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}z_{i}b^{-i},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}w_{i}b^{-i}\right)

where
(z_{i},w_{i})=(0,0)
if
j
is odd and
n_{j}\leq i<n_{j+1}
and
(z_{i},w_{i})=(0,2)
if
j
is even and
n_{j}\leq i<n_{j+1}
.

Note if the limits
p_{a}(z,w)
do not exist, then we cannot write

\displaystyle\gamma(z,w)_{2}

\displaystyle=\dimh\left(\Lambda\cap\left\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}:y=w\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log b}\sum_{a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}}p_{a}(z,w)\log\#J_{2}(a)\,.

In keeping with Remark
2.2
, by considering the sequence along
(n_{2j+1})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\dimh\left(\Lambda\cap\left\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}:y=w\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{a=0}^{2}\frac{\#\{1\leq i\leq n:w_{i}=a\}}{n}\log\#J_{2}(a)}{\log 3}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq i\leq n:w_{i}=0\}}{n}\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}

\displaystyle=0\,.

So

\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{1}{1+\xi}\left(\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\gamma(z,w)_{2}\right)\right\}=\frac{\gamma}{1+\xi}.

However,

\displaystyle\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=\lim_{j\to\infty}s_{n_{2j}}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda},\frac{\gamma+(\xi-\lambda)\left(\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}\right)}{1+\xi}\right\}

\displaystyle>\frac{\gamma}{1+\xi}.

Moreover, it is also possible to choose
(z,w)
where it is not possible to just consider the cases
j=\lambda(n)
and
\xi(n)
when computing the minimum for
s_{n}
. In such cases we need to use the more general statement given by Theorem
2.6
.

6.3
Convergence:
\left(\frac{\lambda(n)}{n}\right)
and
\left(\frac{\xi(n)}{n}\right)
converge

Finally we consider the case where the limits
p_{a}(z,w)
exist for all
0\leq a\leq b-1
with
p_{0}(z,w)\neq 1
and
p_{b-1}(z,w)\neq 1
but where the sequences
\left(\frac{\lambda(n)}{n}\right)
and
\left(\frac{\xi(n)}{n}\right)
may not be convergent. By adapting the arguments in the proof of Theorem
2.1
we get that

\dimh\Lambda_{\lambda,\xi}(z,w)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{1+\left(\tfrac{\lambda(n)}{n}\right)},\frac{\gamma+\left(\tfrac{\xi(n)-\lambda(n)}{n}\right)\gamma(z,w)_{2}}{1+\left(\tfrac{\xi(n)}{n}\right)}\right\}.
