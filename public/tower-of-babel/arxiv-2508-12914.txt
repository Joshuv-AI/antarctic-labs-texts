Discrete Approximate Circle Bundles
Brad Turow, Jose A. Perea

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce discrete approximate circle bundles , a class of objects designed to serve as the data science analog of circle bundles from algebraic topology. We show that, under appropriate conditions, one can meaningfully and stably identify a discrete approximate circle bundle with an isomorphism class of true circle bundles. We also describe two cohomology invariants which uniquely determine the isomorphism class of a circle bundle, and provide algorithms to compute them given a discrete approximate representative. Finally, we propose a novel methodology for coordinatization and dimensionality reduction of circle bundle data. To illustrate the practical utility and viability of our algorithms, we present applications to both real and synthetic datasets from computer vision (e.g., modeling optical flow).

Background And Problem Statement

1.1
Motivation And Context

In recent years, it has been observed that high-dimensional datasets arising in fields such as computer vision, computational chemistry, and motion tracking often lie near highly non-linear, low-dimensional manifolds
[
TSL00
,
RS00
,
BN03
]
. These manifolds can exhibit complex geometric and topological structure, motivating the development of a range of techniques for identifying and leveraging such structures in tasks including dimensionality reduction, statistical analysis, pattern recognition, and data visualization
[
CL06
,
Sap01
]
. By modeling the intrinsic manifold geometry, these methods have been shown to improve compression, denoising, and feature extraction in domains where traditional linear techniques fail to capture the underlying structure of the data.

In this paper, we focus on a particular class of datasets which can be modeled as
circle bundles
. Roughly speaking, a circle bundle is a continuously-varying family of (topological) circles parameterized by another space, called the
base space
of the bundle. The individual circles are called
fibers
of the bundle, and the union of the fibers is the
total space
. The parametrization is given by a projection map
\pi:E\to B
from the total space to the base space –
E
is locally homeomorphic to the product
B\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, but globally the structure may be twisted. For instance, the torus and the Klein bottle can each be characterized as the total space of a circle bundle with base space
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, but the two are not homeomorphic (see Figure
1
). Similarly, the unit tangent bundle on the 2-sphere is a circle bundle with base
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, but one can show that there is no bundle isomorphism between this bundle and the product space
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

On the machine learning side, various datasets related to computer vision and graphics have been shown to have circle bundle structures.
For instance, in
[
Ada+20
]
, Adams et al. use local measurements and a feature map to support a torus model for high-contrast optical flow data. Similarly, in
[
Car+08
]
, the authors propose that a certain space of high-contrast natural image patches is concentrated around a Klein bottle embedded in high dimension. Beyond these examples, datasets arising from 3D objects with rotational symmetries — such as projection images of macromolecules in cryo-electron microscopy — naturally give rise to circle bundle structures, where each fiber corresponds to rotations of the object about a fixed axis
[
SS14
]
.

The possible circle bundle structures over a metric space
B
are completely described by a pair of discrete invariants called
characteristic classes
.
In particular, a non-vanishing class indicates the bundle is non-trivial, so there is no hope of constructing a coordinatization for the whole space as a product.
Additionally, characteristic classes are invariant under continuous perturbations, so one might hope to be able to stably recover them from a noisy finite sampling. Another key feature of characteristic classes is that they can be computed from local measurements alone, which makes them an ideal tool for working with complex, high-dimensional data not amenable to more direct techniques. Lastly, the characteristic classes of a bundle can be used to construct a so-called
bundle map
from the total space to a more familiar space which respects topological structure. This observation has recently been leveraged in works such as
[
Lee+25
]
and
[
SP23
]
to develop non-linear dimensionality reduction techniques based on vector bundles. In this paper, we extend this approach to circle bundles.

1.2
Our Contributions

Here we summarize our contributions:

1.

We define notions of discrete approximate circle bundles and local trivializations and show how these relate to other well-studied objects such as discrete approximate Čech cocycles. Building on results of
[
Tin22
]
and
[
SP23
]
, we give conditions under which a discrete approximate circle bundle can be meaningfully identified with a true class of circle bundles.

2.

We show that every true circle bundle is uniquely characterized by an orientation class and a twisted Euler class, and provide an algorithm for computing these classes from a family of discrete approximate local trivializations. We further introduce a metric on each space of discrete approximate local trivializations and show that two trivializations which are sufficiently close produce the same characteristic classes. In particular, we prove that if a discrete approximate local trivialization is sufficiently close to a true one, the characteristic classes obtained from our algorithm are the same.

3.

Given a discrete approximate circle bundle over a base space
B
and an open cover
\mathcal{U}
for
B
, we introduce a weights filtration on the nerve complex
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
and a corresponding notion of persistence for characteristic class representatives. We then propose a scheme for coordinatization and dimensionality reduction which produces a map from the dataset to the total space of the associated bundle
V(2,d)\times_{O(2)}\mathbb{S}^{1}
, where
V(2,d)
denotes the Stiefel manifold of 2-planes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our pipeline can be tuned to be compatible with any stage of the weights filtration on
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
, yielding a point cloud with a different global topology at each stage.

4.

We provide implementations of our algorithms on several real and synthetic datasets related to imaging and optical flow. In Section
7.1
, we use these tools to confirm the torus model proposed in
[
Ada+20
]
. A more detailed analysis of our findings will appear in an upcoming companion paper.

1.3
Prior Work

A number of approaches to local-to-global inference for analyzing geometrically and topologically complex datasets have recently emerged. The basic principle in each case is to model small pieces of the dataset and analyze how these parts glue together to determine the global shape. Given a dataset
X
, a common technique is to choose a feature map
\pi:X\to S
to some metric space
S
(e.g.,
\mathbb{R}
) and study how the level sets vary. The Mapper pipeline takes this approach to represent a point cloud as a graph with nodes representing local clusters. Similarly, in
[
SP22
]
the authors describe a pipeline called FibeRed which uses local PCA together with a feature map
\pi:X\to S
to obtain a low-dimensional representation of
X
which is faithful to both local geometry and global topology.

Another aspect of local-to-global analysis is the synchronization of local coordinate systems. Cohomological obstructions to synchronization are explored in works such as
[
GBM19
]
using the language of principal
G
-bundles. We note, however, that the theory provided there makes the highly restrictive assumption that the underlying bundle is
flat
. In
[
SP23
]
, the authors introduce a theory of discrete approximate Euclidean vector bundles which handles non-flat cases in the context of
O(d)
-synchronization. Building on the language and technology developed in
[
Tin22
]
, they show that under good conditions, one can uniquely and stably identify discrete approximate Euclidean vector bundles with true ones. They also provide stable algorithms for computing characteristic classes of vector bundles from discrete approximate cocycle representatives.

1.4
Structure Of Paper

The rest of the paper is structured as follows:

Section 3: Mathematical Background.
We review the essential mathematical foundations required for our results, including the general theory of fiber bundles, the classification of circle bundles and cohomology with local coefficients. This section establishes notation and key concepts that will be used throughout the paper.

Section 4: Discrete Approximate Circle Bundles.
We introduce the notion of discrete approximate circle bundles and explore their relationship with discrete approximate Čech cocycles and classifying maps as defined in
[
SP23
]
. We also discuss how these discrete constructions relate to classical circle bundles.

Section 5: Algorithms for Characteristic Classes.
We present algorithms to compute characteristic classes from discrete approximate local trivializations, and prove that our algorithms are stable.

Section 6: Weights Filtration and Persistence.
Given a discrete approximate circle bundle over a metric space
B
and an open cover
\mathcal{U}
of
B
, we introduce a simplexwise filtration on
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
and describe a type of persistent cohomology for characteristic class representatives with respect to this filtration.

Section 7: Coordinatization Pipeline.
We provide a pipeline for coordinatization and dimensionality reduction which integrates the Principal Stiefel Coordinates algorithm
[
Lee+25
]
. The result is a map from the dataset into a Stiefel manifold which is, in some sense which we explain, compatible with a chosen stage of the associated weights filtration.

Section 8: Implementations and Experiments.
We demonstrate our methods on both synthetic and real datasets, applying the sparse circular coordinates algorithm
[
Per21
]
to obtain discrete approximate local trivializations and showcasing the resulting computations of characteristic classes and coordinatization.

Section 9: Future Work.
We conclude with a discussion of potential extensions, open questions, and directions for further exploration in both theory and applications.

Fiber Bundle Construction Lemma:
The following well-known lemma essentially states that a fiber bundle is completely determined up to isomorphism by the transition maps associated with a family of local trivializations. It also gives a computationally-friendly way to determine whether two bundles over the same space are isomorphic:

Part A:
Let
B

and structure group

with transition maps
\{\Omega_{jk}\}_{(jk)\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})}

Part B:
Suppose

is another fiber bundle with fiber
F
and structure group

which is trivializable over

with transition maps

. If the action of

for all
(jk)\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
.

Note that if
\xi=(E,\pi,B)
and
\xi^{\prime}=(E^{\prime},\pi^{\prime},B)

and

Pullback Bundles:
In addition to associated bundles, another key way to obtain new fiber bundles from existing ones is via the pullback construction. Given a fiber bundle
\xi=(E,\pi,B)
and a continuous map
f:B^{\prime}\to B
, the
pullback bundle

f^{*}\xi
is the triple
(f^{*}E,\pi^{\prime},B^{\prime})
, where

f^{*}E=\{(b^{\prime},e)\in B^{\prime}\times E:\ f(b^{\prime})=\pi(e)\}\\

and
\pi^{\prime}:f^{*}E\to B^{\prime}
is given by
\pi^{\prime}(b^{\prime},e)=b^{\prime}
. Note that if
\xi
has fiber
F
then so does
f^{*}\xi
. Moreover, one can show that homotopic maps yield isomorphic pullback bundles.

A

For any paracompact space

\mathcal{P}_{G,\omega}(V)=\{\text{families }\{f_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{G}(U_{j}\cap V)\}:\ f_{j}|_{U_{j}\cap U_{k}\cap V}=\omega_{jk}\cdot f_{k}|_{U_{j}\cap U_{k}\cap V}\}

The sheaf
\mathcal{C}_{G,\omega}
of
continuous
G
-valued functions on
B
twisted by
\omega
is the sheafification of
\mathcal{P}_{G,\omega}
. Note that
\mathcal{C}_{G,\omega}
is equivalently defined as the sheaf of sections of the associated bundle
B_{\omega}\times_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}G
, where
p_{\omega}:B_{\omega}\to B
denotes the orientation double cover represented by
\omega
.

If
G
is a discrete group, we will write
G_{\omega}
instead of
\mathcal{C}_{G,\omega}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Suppose
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\check{Z}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\underline{\mathbb{Z}_{2}})
. Then, for any topological group
G
with left
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-action, the sheaves
\mathcal{C}_{G,\omega}
and
\mathcal{C}_{G,\omega^{\prime}}
are isomorphic if any only if
[\omega]=[\omega^{\prime}]
. Explicitly, if
\omega=\omega^{\prime}\cdot\delta^{0}\phi
, then the map which sends
f_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{G,\omega}(U_{j})
to
\phi_{j}\cdot f_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{G,\omega^{\prime}}(U_{j})
is an isomorphism of sheaves.

Proof.

Let
\{f_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{G}(U_{j})\}_{j\in J}
such that
f_{j}|_{U_{j}\cap U_{k}}=\omega_{jk}\cdot f_{k}|_{U_{j}\cap U_{k}}
whenever
U_{j}\cap U_{k}
is nonempty, and let
\{f^{\prime}_{j}=\phi_{j}\cdot f_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{G}(U_{j})\}_{j\in J}
. Since
\omega=\omega^{\prime}\cdot\delta^{0}\phi
, we have

\tilde{f}_{j}|_{U_{j}\cap U_{k}}=\phi_{j}\cdot f_{j}|_{U_{j}\cap U_{k}}=(\phi_{j}\omega_{jk})\cdot f_{k}|_{U_{j}\cap U_{k}}=\phi_{k}\cdot f_{k}|_{U_{j}\cap U_{k}}=\tilde{f}_{k}|_{U_{j}\cap U_{k}}

whenever
U_{j}\cap U_{k}
is nonempty. It follows that the assignment
\{f_{j}\}_{j\in J}\longmapsto\{\tilde{f}_{j}\}_{j\in J}
induces a sheaf morphism
\mathcal{C}_{G,\omega}\to\mathcal{C}_{G,\omega^{\prime}}
. Since the map on each open set is clearly a bijection, the resulting sheaf morphism is an isomorphism.

∎

For
G
abelian, a base space
B
and a local system
\mathcal{L}
with stalk
G
, one can define cohomology groups
H^{k}(B;\mathcal{L})
, called
cohomology with local coefficients
in several ways, which are equivalent under mild assumptions. Most directly (though most abstractly),
H^{k}(B;\mathcal{L})
can be interpreted as sheaf cohomology groups defined in terms of derived functors. If
B
is paracompact, then by Proposition
2.2
we have
\check{H}^{k}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{L})\cong H^{k}(B;\mathcal{L})
for any good open cover
\mathcal{U}
of
B
, so we can compute these groups with Čech cohomology.

One can also define a notion of singular (co)homology with local coefficients (in fact, any (co)homology theory can be promoted to a theory with local coefficients). The details are not important for us, but we note that when
B
is paracompact, the singular and Čech cohomology groups with coefficients in
\mathcal{L}
are isomorphic. We also have the following generalization of Poincare duality:

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
B
be a closed connected
n
-manifold, and let
\mathcal{L}
be a local system of abelian groups. Then
H^{k}(B;\mathcal{L})\cong H_{n-k}(B;\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{O})
for all
k
, where
\mathcal{O}
denotes the orientation sheaf of
B
.

2.3
Classification Of Circle Bundles

In this paper, we use the term
circle bundle
to mean a fiber bundle with fiber
\mathbb{S}^{1}
(note that some authors reserve this term for
principal

\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundles). A priori, the structure group for a circle bundle is
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. We have the following two central extensions of topological groups:

1\to\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\xrightarrow{\deg}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to 1

(2)

1\to\mathbb{Z}\to\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\xrightarrow{\widetilde{\exp}}\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to 1\\

(3)

Here
\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
denotes the group of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and
\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
denotes the universal cover of
\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, which can be modeled explicitly as

\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})=\{f\in\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{R}):\ f(x+1)=f(x)+1\ \text{ for all }x\}

(4)

The projection map
\widetilde{\exp}:\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is defined by
\widetilde{\exp}(\tilde{f})=\text{exp}\circ\tilde{f}\circ\text{exp}^{-1}
, where
\exp:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
is given by
\exp(\theta)=e^{2\pi i\theta}
.

Notice that sequence (
2
) splits, and the section
r:\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
induced by complex conjugation gives rise to a semidirect decomposition
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\cong\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\rtimes_{r}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. In more detail, we obtain a right
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-action on
\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
via the assignment
\pm 1\longmapsto\text{Adj}_{r(\pm 1)}
which induces a group multiplication law
(\Lambda_{1},\omega_{1})\cdot(\Lambda_{2},\omega_{2})=(\Lambda_{1}\text{Adj}_{r(\omega_{1})}(\Lambda_{2}),\omega_{1}\omega_{2})
. The map
\varphi:\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\rtimes_{r}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
given by
\varphi(\Lambda,\omega)=\Lambda r(\omega)
is then a group isomorphism.

Thus, for any open cover
\mathcal{U}
of a paracompact space
B
,
\varphi
induces an isomorphism on cohomology, where the cocycle condition is given explicitly by
(\Lambda_{jk},\omega_{jk})\cdot(\Lambda_{kl},\omega_{kl})=(\Lambda_{jl},\omega_{jl})
, or, equivalently,

\Lambda_{jk}\text{Adj}_{r(\omega_{jk})}\Lambda_{kl}=\Lambda_{jl}\quad\quad\omega_{jk}\omega_{kl}=\omega_{jl}\\

Consequently, for any
\omega\in\check{Z}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\underline{\mathbb{Z}_{2}})
, there is a natural bijection between cocycles
\Omega\in\check{Z}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})})
satisfying
\deg_{*}(\Omega)=\omega
and cochains
\Lambda\in\check{C}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})})
satisfying
\Lambda_{jk}\text{Adj}_{r(\omega_{jk})}\Lambda_{kl}=\Lambda_{jl}
for all
(jkl)\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
. Alternatively, we can interpret
\omega
as a local system and
\Lambda
as a Cech cocycle in
\check{Z}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega})
:

\check{Z}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})})=\coprod_{\omega\in\check{Z}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\underline{\mathbb{Z}_{2}})}\check{Z}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega})\\

(5)

The advantage of this perspective is suggested by the following proposition:

Proposition 2.10
.

Suppose
\omega\in\check{Z}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\underline{\mathbb{Z}_{2}})
for some open cover
\mathcal{U}
of a paracompact space
B
. The following sequence is short exact:

1\to\mathbb{Z}_{\omega}\to\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega}\xrightarrow{\widetilde{\exp}_{*}}\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega}\to 1

(6)

Moreover, the sheaf
\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega}
is soft, so the induced connecting homomorphism
\Delta_{\omega}:\check{H}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega})\to\check{H}^{2}(\mathcal{U};\mathbb{Z}_{\omega})
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-actions on
\mathbb{Z}
,
\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
canonically induce actions on the sheaves
\underline{\mathbb{Z}}
,
\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}
. Moreover,
\widetilde{\exp}
is equivariant with respect to the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
actions on
\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
in the sense that
\widetilde{\exp}(\omega\cdot\tilde{f})=\omega\cdot\widetilde{\exp}(\tilde{f})
for any
\tilde{f}\in\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
\omega\in\{\pm 1\}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
(clearly the inclusion of
\mathbb{Z}
into
\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is also equivariant in the same sense). Thus, it descends to a map between the sheaves
\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega}
.

At the level of stalks, sequence (
6
) reduces to exact sequence (
3
), hence it is also exact as a sequence of sheaves of abelian groups. By Proposition
2.2
, we therefore obtain a long exact sequence in Cech cohomology, and in particular, a connecting homomorphism
\Delta_{\omega}:\check{H}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega})\to\check{H}^{2}(\mathcal{U};\mathbb{Z}_{\omega})
. Moreover,
\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is contractible, so
\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),\omega}
is soft, so Proposition
2.2
implies
\Delta_{\omega}
is an isomorphism by exactness.

∎

The proposition above immediately leads to the following classification result:

Proposition 2.11
.

For any open cover
\mathcal{U}
of a paracompact space
B
, there is a bijective correspondence

\check{H}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})})\ \cong\ \coprod_{[\omega]\in\check{H}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\underline{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})}\check{H}^{2}(\mathcal{U};\mathbb{Z}_{\omega})

via the assignment
[\Omega]\longmapsto\left(w_{1}(\Omega),\tilde{e}(\Omega)\right)

given by
w_{1}(\Omega)=[\deg_{*}(\Omega)]
and
\tilde{e}(\Omega)=\Delta_{\omega}([\Omega])
.

We therefore have a complete classification of circle bundles over paracompact spaces in terms of a pair of discrete invariants. As we will later show, these two invariants are closely connected to the first Stiefel-Whitney class and Euler class for real plane bundles, so we will refer to
w_{1}(\Omega)
and
\tilde{e}(\Omega)
respectively as the
Stiefel-Whitney class
(or
orientation class
) and the
twisted Euler class
of the circle bundle represented by
\Omega
.

Remark 2.12
.

The notation in Proposition
2.11
is slightly abusive, since each cocycle
\omega
within a cohomology class determines a different local system
\mathbb{Z}_{\omega}
. On the other hand, by Proposition
2.8
, the local systems determined by any two cohomologous cocycles are isomorphic. In particular, if
\omega
is a coboundary, then
\mathbb{Z}_{\omega}\cong\underline{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Observe that when
B
is a locally finite 1-dimensional CW complex (e.g., a graph), the Proposition
2.11
implies that circle bundles over
B
are completely determined by their Stiefel-Whitney classes. In the case when
B
is a compact connected surface, the classification is also especially simple and explicit:

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
B
be a closed connected 2-manifold, and let
\omega\in H^{1}(B;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
determine a double cover
p_{\omega}:B_{\omega}\to B
. Then

\check{H}^{2}(B;\mathbb{Z}_{\omega})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&\text{if }\omega=w_{1}(B),\\
\mathbb{Z}_{2},&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

We therefore have the following classification of circle bundles over
B
:

\text{Bun}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(B)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}\ \coprod\ (\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{2^{2g}-1},&\text{if }B=\Sigma_{g}\text{ (orientable surface of genus }g),\\
\mathbb{Z}\ \coprod(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{2^{k}-1},&\text{if }B=\#_{k}\mathbb{RP}^{2}\text{ (non-orientable case)}\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Twisted Poincare duality asserts that for any compact connected
n
-manifold
M
and locally constant sheaf
\mathcal{L}
of Abelian groups on
M
(with fiber
G
), one has
H^{*}(M;\mathcal{L})\cong H_{n-*}(M;\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{O})
, where
\mathcal{O}
denotes the orientation sheaf of
M
. Moreover,
H_{0}(M;\mathcal{L})
is isomorphic to the coinvariants of the monodromy action
\omega_{\mathcal{L}}:\pi_{1}(M)\to\text{Aut}(G)
induced by
\mathcal{L}
, i.e.,

H_{0}(M;\mathcal{L})\cong G/\langle\omega_{\mathcal{L}}(\gamma)\cdot a-a\ |\ a\in G,\gamma\in\pi_{1}(M)\rangle

Thus, for any compact connected surface
B
and orientation character
\omega\in H^{1}(B;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, one has

\check{H}^{2}(B;\mathbb{Z}_{\omega})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&\text{if }\mathbb{Z}_{\omega}\otimes\mathcal{O}\cong\underline{\mathbb{Z}},\\
\mathbb{Z}_{2},&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Since
\mathbb{Z}_{\omega}\otimes\mathcal{O}\cong\underline{\mathbb{Z}}
if and only if
\omega+w_{1}(B)=0
(or, equivalently,
\omega=w_{1}(B)^{-1}=w_{1}(B)
), the proposition follows.

∎

In light of the proposition above, for any compact connected surface
B
and
\omega\in Z^{1}(B;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, one can define a
twisted fundamental class

[B]_{\omega}
which generates
\check{H}_{0}(B;\mathbb{Z}_{\omega})
– this choice is unique when
\omega\neq w_{1}(B)
and unique up to sign when
\omega=w_{1}(B)
. The pairing
\langle\tilde{e},[B]_{\omega}\rangle
then gives an explicit bijection between
\check{H}^{2}(B;\mathbb{Z}_{\omega})
and its image in
\mathbb{Z}
. We call the integer
\langle\tilde{e}(\Omega),[B]_{\omega}\rangle
the
twisted Euler number
of the bundle classified by
\Omega
.

2.4
Reduction Of Structure Group

The structure group
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is rather unwieldly – in particular, it is disconnected, non-abelian and infinite-dimensional, making it challenging to work with from a computational perspective. In this section, we identify reductions of this structure group to simpler ones.

Definition 2.14
.

We say that a circle bundle
\xi
is
orientable
if any of the following equivalent conditions are met:

1.

\xi
admits a family of local trivializations with
\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
-valued transition maps,

2.

The cohomology class
\Omega_{\xi}\in\check{H}^{1}(B;\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})})
lifts to a class in
\check{H}^{1}(B;\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})})
,

3.

The Stiefel-Whitney class
w_{1}(\Omega_{\xi})\in\check{H}^{1}(B;\underline{\mathbb{Z}_{2}})
vanishes.

The next proposition implies another useful characterization of orientable circle bundles:

Proposition 2.15
.

The inclusion
\iota:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is a homotopy equivalence.

Proof.

Consider the map
\tau:\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
defined by
\tau(f)=f(1)
. Observe that
\tau
is a continuous surjection, and the fiber of
\tau
over each
z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
contains the orientation-preserving homeomorphisms which map
1
to
z
. One can easily check that each
\tau^{-1}(z)
is contractible, so
\tau
is a homotopy inverse for
\iota
.

∎

An immediate consequence of the proposition above is that one can always reduce the structure group of an orientable circle bundle
\xi
to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
itself, which means every orientable circle bundle admits a principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-structure. In fact, one always has two non-isomorphic choices for the principal structure on
\xi
corresponding to the two choices of orientation, though the underlying fiber bundles are isomorphic. This is consistent with our earlier observation that the (twisted) Euler number of a circle bundle is only well-defined up to sign until an orientation class representative for the bundle is specified.

More generally, Proposition
2.15
implies that the inclusion of
\mathbb{S}^{1}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\text{Iso}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
into
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is a homotopy equivalence. It follows that over paracompact spaces, any circle bundle, no matter how wild, admits a family of trivializations with
\text{Iso}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
-valued transition maps.

While the inclusion
\iota:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
induces a one-to-one correspondence between oriented circle bundles and principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundles, we note that the retract
\tau:\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
defined in the proof of Proposition
2.15
is not a group homomorphism, hence does not induce a cochain map
\check{C}^{1}(B;\mathcal{C}_{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})})\to\check{C}^{1}(B;\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}})
which descends to an isomorphism on cohomology. In particular, the following proposition shows that no retract can induce such a map:

Proposition 2.16
.

There does not exist a retract
\tau:\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
which is also a group homomorphism.

Proof.

One can show
\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is a
perfect
group in the sense that every element is expressible as a product of commutators (see
[
Ghy01
]
Proposition 5.11). On the other hand, if
\tau:\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a group homomorphism, we must have
\tau([f,g])=[\tau(f),\tau(g)]
for all
g,h\in\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Since
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is Abelian and
\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is perfect, it follows immediately that
\tau(f)=1
for all
f
. We conclude that
\tau
cannot be a retract onto
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

∎

Thus, given an arbitrary family of trivializations for a circle bundle, it may be difficult to find explicit homotopies so that the new transitions maps take values in
\text{Iso}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. On the other hand, if we define the supremum distance on
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
by

d_{\infty}(f,g)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(f(z),g(z))\\

(7)

where
d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
denotes the geodesic distance metric on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the proposition below states that well-behaved homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
are close to isometries. Since the proof requires some technical digressions, we defer it to the appendix.

Proposition 2.17
.

If
f\in\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is
L
-Lipschitz, then
d_{\infty}(f,\text{Iso}(\mathbb{S}^{1}))\leq 2\pi(\frac{L-1}{L})
.

Note in particular that
d_{\infty}(f,\text{Iso}(\mathbb{S}^{1}))\to 0
as
L\to 1
.

From a computational perspective, the structure group
\text{Iso}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\cong\mathbb{S}^{1}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is vastly preferable to
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. In particular,
\text{Iso}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is a 1-dimensional Lie group (with two connected components), and it has a natural identification with the matrix group
O(2)
which gives rise to the following commutative diagram of split extensions:

(8)

Note that
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is identified with
SO(2)
, giving rise to the following commutative diagram of exponential sequences:

. Passing to Cech cohomology, it is clear that the Stiefel-Whitney and twisted Euler classes we defined to classify circle bundles correspond precisely to their classical counterparts for vector bundles. However, it is useful to know how these classes arise and can be explicitly computed from arbitrary
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
-valued transition data.

2.5
The Universal Circle Bundle And Dimensionality Reduction

A classifying map can be used to model the total space of a fiber bundle as an embedded submanifold of a more familiar space. Specifically, if
G
is a topological group and
\mu_{G}=(EG,p,BG)
is a universal
G
-bundle, then for any principal
G
-bundle
\xi
over a paracompact space
B
, we can construct a classifying map
f:B\to BG
such that such that
f^{*}\mu_{G}=\xi
. This map is covered by a bundle map
F:E(\xi)\to EG
which embeds the total space of
\xi
into
EG
. More generally, if
\xi_{F}
is the associated bundle of
\xi
with fiber
F
, then
\xi_{F}
is isomorphic to the pullback of the associated bundle
EG\times_{G}F\to BG
along the same classifying map.

In the context of circle bundles, the associated bundle construction gives bijective correspondences

\text{Bun}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(B)\quad\leftrightarrow\quad\text{Prin}_{O(2)}(B)\quad\leftrightarrow\quad\text{Vect}_{2,\mathbb{R}}(B)

for any paracompact space
B
. It follows that up to homotopy, every circle bundle
\xi
over
B
can be realized (up to isomorphism) as the sphere bundle of a rank-2 real vector bundle
\xi_{\text{vect}}
.

A model for the classifying space of real rank-2 vector bundles is the infinite Grassmann manifold
\text{Gr}(2)=\text{Gr}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\infty})
consisting of all 2-planes in
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
, topologized as the direct limit
\text{Gr}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset\text{Gr}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\subset\cdots
(see
[
MS74
]
for more details). Identifying each plane with its projection matrix gives an embedding of
\text{Gr}(2)
into
\mathbb{R}^{\infty\times\infty}
. The total space of the universal plane bundle
\mu
over
\text{Gr}(2)
can be modeled by the Stiefel manifold
V(2)=V(2,\infty)
:

V(2,\infty)=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty\times 2}:A^{T}A=I\}

The columns of each
A\in V(2)
form an orthonormal basis for a plane in
\text{Gr}(2)
whose projection matrix is given by
AA^{T}
. Thus, the universal plane bundle is then defined by the surjection
\pi_{Gr}:V(2)\to\text{Gr}(2)
given by
\pi_{Gr}(A)=AA^{T}
.

Given a circle bundle
\xi
over a paracompact space
B
, the associated plane bundle
\xi_{\text{vect}}
can be expressed as the pullback of
\mu
along a classifying map
f:B\to\text{Gr}(2)
. It follows that
\xi
is isomorphic to the pullback of the associated circle bundle
\mu_{\circ}=V(2)\times_{O(2)}\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\text{Gr}(2)
along
f
. In this sense
\mu_{\circ}
is a universal circle bundle, and
\xi
is classified by the same map as
\xi_{\text{vect}}
.

From the perspective of data science and dimensionality reduction, a major advantage of choosing
\mu_{\circ}
as our model for the universal circle bundle is that we can apply known techniques to obtain low-dimensional representations of point clouds in
V(2)\times_{O(2)}\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Our coordinatization pipeline for discrete approximate circle bundles integrates Principal Stiefel Coordinates
[
Lee+25
]
for this purpose.

3
Discrete Approximate Circle Bundles

In this section, we introduce discrete approximate notions of local trivializations, Cech cohomology and classifying maps for circle bundles. The purpose of these objects is to represent true circle bundles with finite data which can be algorithmically, efficiently and stably computed from a point cloud and a feature map.

3.1
Three Characterizations

Definition 3.1
.

A
discrete
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximate circle bundle
is a map
\pi:X\to B
between metric spaces together with an open cover
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of
B
and a family of maps

\{f_{j}:\pi^{-1}(U_{j})\to\mathbb{S}^{1}\}_{j\in J}
with the following properties:

1.

d_{H}(f_{l}(\pi^{-1}(U_{jkl})),\mathbb{S}^{1})<\delta
(with respect to Euclidean distance) for every
U_{jkl}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
.

2.

There exists a simplicial cochain
\Omega\in C^{1}(\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U});O(2))
such that

|f_{j}(x)-\Omega_{jk}f_{k}(x)|<\varepsilon

(10)

whenever
x\in\pi^{-1}(U_{jk})\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
.

The family
\{f_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is called a
discrete
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximate local trivialization
, and the set of discrete
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximate local trivializations for
\pi
subordinate to an open cover
\mathcal{U}
is denoted by
T_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\pi,\mathcal{U})
. Any
\Omega
satisfying (
10
) is said to be a
witness
that
\{f_{j}\}\in T_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\pi,\mathcal{U})
.

Given a discrete approximate local trivialization, we will refer to the quantity
\alpha\equiv\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\delta}
as the
trivialization quality
of the trivialization and write
T_{\alpha}(\pi,\mathcal{U})=\bigcup_{\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\delta}<\alpha}T_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\pi,\mathcal{U})
for any
\alpha\geq 0
. We define the following metric on
T_{\infty}(\pi,\mathcal{U})
:

d_{T}(\{f_{j}\}_{j\in J},\{g_{j}\}_{j\in J})=\sup_{j\in J}\sup_{x\in\pi^{-1}(U_{j})}|f_{j}(x)-g_{j}(x)|\\

(11)

Notice that we require witnesses to take values in
O(2)
rather than
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Next, we introduce discrete and approximate relaxations of classical Cech cohomology. As we have seen, Cech cohomology provides a useful framework for computing obstructions and classifications, so these objects will play a central role in our classification and coordinatization algorithms.

Definition 3.2
.

Given a collection of open sets
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{j}\}_{j\in J}
and a metric group
G
, the set of
G
-valued
\varepsilon
-approximate Cech cocycles
subordinate to
\mathcal{U}
is defined by

\check{Z}^{k}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{G})=\{\Omega\in\check{C}^{k}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{G}):\ d_{Z}(\delta^{k}\Omega,\mathbbm{1})<\varepsilon\}

(12)

where
d_{Z}
is the supremum metric on
\check{C}^{k}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{G})
:

d_{Z}(\Omega,\Lambda)=\sup_{U_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})^{(k)}}\sup_{b\in U_{\sigma}}d_{G}(\Omega_{\sigma}(b),\Lambda_{\sigma}(b))

(13)

The set of
G
-valued
discrete
\varepsilon
-approximate Cech cocycles
subordinate to
\mathcal{U}
is defined by

\check{Z}^{k}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{U};\underline{G})=\check{Z}^{k}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{G})\cap\check{C}^{k}(\mathcal{U};\underline{G})

(14)

For
k=1
, we define the
\varepsilon
-approximate cohomology set

\check{H}^{1}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{G})
as the orbit space of
\check{Z}^{1}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{G})
with respect to the left action
\check{C}^{0}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{G})\curvearrowright\check{C}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{G})
given by
(\phi\cdot\Omega)_{jk}=\phi_{j}\Omega_{jk}\phi_{k}^{-1}
.

We will primarily be interested in the case where
G=O(2)
, since every circle bundle over a paracompact space
B
is represented by a class in
\check{H}^{1}(B;\mathcal{C}_{O(2)})
. Here we endow
O(2)
with the Frobenius metric, since this is the most natural choice for a number of key results.

Finally, we introduce an approximate version of classifying maps. These will play an important role in our coordinatization pipeline.

Definition 3.3
.

Given a paracompact space
B
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, an
\varepsilon
-approximate classifying map
is a map
f:B\to\text{Gr}(2)^{\varepsilon}
.

3.2
Relationships Between Notions

In this section, we describe two key constructions which produce functions

\text{wit}:T_{\alpha}(\pi,\mathcal{U})\to\check{Z}^{1}_{(3\sqrt{2})\alpha}(\mathcal{U};\underline{O(2)})\hskip 28.45274pt\text{cl}:\check{Z}^{1}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{O(2)})\to\text{Maps}(B,\text{Gr}(2)^{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon})\\

We first show that discrete approximate local trivializations are witnessed by discrete approximate cocycles:

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose
\Omega
witnesses that
\{f_{j}\}\in T_{\alpha}(\pi,\mathcal{U})
for some
\alpha\geq 0
. Then
\Omega\in\check{Z}^{1}_{3\alpha\sqrt{2}}(\mathcal{U};\underline{O(2)})
.

Proof.

Assume
\{f_{j}\}\in T_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\pi,\mathcal{U})
, where
\alpha=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\delta}
. Suppose
x\in\pi^{-1}(U_{j}\cap U_{k}\cap U_{l})
. Let
v_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}=\Omega_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}f_{\tau^{\prime}}(x)-f_{\tau}(x)
for
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\{j,k,l\}
. Then,

\left|\Omega_{jk}\Omega_{kl}f_{l}(x)-\Omega_{jl}f_{l}(x)\right|=\left|\Omega_{jk}(v_{kl}+f_{k}(x))-\Omega_{jl}f_{l}(x)\right|

=\left|\Omega_{jk}v_{kl}+(f_{j}(x)+v_{jk})-(f_{j}(x)-v_{jl})\right|=\left|\Omega_{jk}v_{kl}+v_{jk}-v_{jl}\right|

\leq|v_{kl}|+|v_{jk}|+|v_{jl}|<3\varepsilon\\

The result then follows immediately from Proposition
A.10
.

∎

Next, we show how to algorithmically compute a witness for a discrete approximate circle bundle.

Construction 3.5
.

Let
X=\{x_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n}
be a finite set, and let
f,g:S\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Compute the values
\{\alpha_{s}=\cos^{-1}(f(x_{s}))\}^{n}_{s=1}
and
\{\beta_{s}=\cos^{-1}(g(x_{s}))\}^{n}_{s=1}
, then define
\gamma_{s}=\alpha_{s}-\beta_{s}
and
\tilde{\gamma}_{s}=\alpha_{s}+\beta_{s}
for all
s
. Find the respective midpoints
\theta
and
\widetilde{\theta}
of the shortest arc
C
containing
\{\gamma_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n}
and the shortest arc
\widetilde{C}
containing
\{\widetilde{\gamma}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n}
. Compute the values

d_{\text{rot}}=\text{max}_{s}|f(x_{s})-R_{\theta}g(x_{s})|,\hskip 28.45274ptd_{\text{ref}}=\text{max}_{s}|f(x_{s})-R_{\widetilde{\theta}}g(x_{s})|

If
d_{\text{rot}}<d_{\text{ref}}
, set
\Omega=R_{\theta}
; otherwise, set
\Omega=R_{\widetilde{\theta}}r
, where
R_{\theta}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos(\theta)&-\sin(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\\
\end{array}\right)
and
r=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\\
\end{array}\right)
.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
n>1
and
\text{diam}(\{\gamma_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n})<\pi
in Construction
3.5
above, then
\Omega\in O(2)
is the unique minimizer of
\text{max}_{s}|f(x_{s})-\Omega g(x_{s})|
.

Proof.

Let
L:O(2)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be defined by
L(\Omega)=\max_{s}|f(x_{s})-\Omega g(x_{s})|^{2}
. We have

\displaystyle|f(x_{s})-\Omega g(x_{s})|^{2}

\displaystyle=|f(x_{s})|^{2}+|g(x_{2})|^{2}-2f(x_{s})\cdot\Omega g(x_{s})

\displaystyle=2-2f(x_{s})\cdot\Omega g(x_{s})

for all
s
. If
\Omega=R_{\theta}
is a counter-clockwise rotation by
\theta
, then
f(x_{s})\cdot\Omega g(x_{s})=\cos(\gamma_{s}-\theta)
, so to minimize
L
over
SO(2)
we need to identify
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
for which the angular distance to the farthest
\gamma_{s}
is minimized. If the measure of
C
is less than
\pi
, then this is precisely achieved at the midpoint of the shortest arc
C
containing
\{\gamma_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n}
(furthermore, the requirement that
\text{diam}(\{\gamma_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n})<\pi
guarantees that
C
is unique). To see this, first note that the optimal
\theta
certainly lies in
C
, and for fixed
\theta\in C
,
\cos(\gamma_{s}-\theta)
is smallest when
\gamma_{s}
is an endpoint of
C
. By symmetry, the
\theta
which maximizes the minimum of the values
\{\cos(\gamma_{s}-\theta)\}_{s=1}^{n}
must be half way between the two end points (otherwise, a small perturbation of
\theta
towards the midpoint would move
\theta
closer to the more distant endpoint while still keeping
\theta
nearer to the closer endpoint. This would yield a more optimal choice for
\theta
).

Analogously, if
\Omega=R_{\widetilde{\theta}}r
for some
\widetilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}
, one can check that
f(x_{s})\cdot\Omega g(x_{s})=\cos(\widetilde{\gamma}_{s}-\theta)
. Thus, to minimize
L
over the component of
O(2)
with matrices of negative determinant, we need to identify
\widetilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
for which the angular distance to the farthest
\widetilde{\gamma}_{s}
is minimized. Finally, comparing
d_{\text{rot}}
and
d_{\text{ref}}
determines which component of
O(2)
contains the optimal
\Omega
. To see that these two values cannot be equal, observe that if
\text{diam}(\{\gamma_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n})<\pi
, then
\text{diam}(\{\widetilde{\gamma}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n})>\pi
. It follows that then
d_{\text{rot}}<\frac{\pi}{2}
and
d_{\text{ref}}>\frac{\pi}{2}
. Similarly, if
\text{diam}(\{\widetilde{\gamma}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n})<\pi
, then
\text{diam}(\{\gamma_{s}\}_{s=1}^{n})>\pi
, so
d_{\text{ref}}<d_{\text{rot}}
.

∎

Corollary 3.7
.

If
\{f_{j}\}\in T_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\pi,\mathcal{U})
for
\varepsilon<\sqrt{2}
and
|\pi^{-1}(U_{jk})|>1
for all
(jk)\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
, then Construction
3.5
yields a witness.

Proof.

The hypotheses imply

|f_{j}(x)-\Omega_{jk}f_{k}(x)|^{2}<\varepsilon^{2}

or, equivalently,

\gamma^{jk}_{s}=(f_{j}(x_{s}))\cdot(\Omega_{jk}f_{k}(x_{s}))>1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}

for all
x_{s}\in U_{j}\cap U_{k}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
. Then
|\gamma^{jk}_{s}|>\cos^{-1}(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2})
for all
x_{s}\in U_{j}\cap U_{k}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
, so
\text{diam}(\{\gamma^{jk}_{s}\})<2\cos^{-1}(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2})
. We conclude that if
\cos^{-1}(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2})<\frac{\pi}{2}
(or, equivalently, if
\varepsilon<\sqrt{2}
), then each
\Omega_{jk}
obtained using Construction
3.5
is the unique minimizer of
\text{max}_{x_{s}\in\pi^{-1}(U_{j}\cap U_{k})}|f_{j}(x_{s})-f_{k}(x_{s})|
. Thus by Proposition
3.4
,
\Omega
is a witness that
\{f_{j}\}\in T_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\pi,\mathcal{U})
.

4
Effective Computation Of Characteristic Classes

In this section, we give explicit algorithms for computing the characteristic classes of a circle bundle from a discrete approximate cocycle representative. One can then use Construction
3.5
to compute classes from discrete approximate trivializations. We also introduce a notion of persistence for these classes.

We first note some simplifications which occur in the computation of the twisted Euler class when the transition functions are
O(2)
-valued (rather than
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
-valued). Let
r:\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
denote the section of (
2
) described in Section
2.3
, and let
\varphi:\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\rtimes_{r}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
be the group isomorphism described there given by
\varphi(\Lambda,\omega)=\Lambda r(\omega)
. Relative to the reduced structure group
O(2)
, we have
r(\pm 1)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&\pm 1\\
\end{array}\right)
. Moreover,
Proposition
A.9
implies
\text{Adj}_{r(\omega)}\Lambda=\Lambda^{r(\omega)}
for any
\Lambda\in O(2)
and
\omega=\pm 1\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, so the group multiplication law in
\mathbb{S}^{1}\rtimes_{r}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
simplifies to

(\Lambda_{1},\omega_{1})\cdot(\Lambda_{2},\omega_{2})=(\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2}^{\omega_{1}},\omega_{1}\omega_{2})

(18)

Thus, the cocycle condition for
(\mathbb{S}^{1}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2})
-valued cocycles is given explicitly by
(\Lambda_{jk},\omega_{jk})\cdot(\Lambda_{kl},\omega_{kl})=(\Lambda_{jl},\omega_{jl})

\Lambda_{jk}\Lambda_{kl}^{\omega_{jk}}=\Lambda_{jl}\quad\quad\omega_{jk}\omega_{kl}=\omega_{jl}\\

(19)

It follows that in this context, the connecting homomorphism
\Delta_{\omega}
described in Proposition
2.10
reduces to

\Delta_{\omega}([\Omega])_{jkl}=\omega_{jk}\Theta_{kl}-\Theta_{jl}+\Theta_{jk}

(20)

where
\Omega=(\varphi^{-1})_{*}(\Lambda,\Theta)
. For later use, we define
\text{abs}:O(2)\to SO(2)
by
\text{abs}(\Omega)=\Omega r(\det(\Omega))
– note
\varphi^{-1}(\Omega)=(\text{abs}(\Omega),\det(\Omega))
. In fact, it is easy to see that if
\Omega\in\check{Z}^{1}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{O(2)})
for some
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\omega=\det_{*}(\Omega)
, then
\text{abs}_{*}(\Omega)\in\check{Z}^{1}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{SO(2),\omega})
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For

Proof.

Compute the Smith normal form

Remark 4.6

Although Čech theory is typically formulated in terms of cohomology, there is a natural dual notion of Čech homology with the property that
\check{H}_{*}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{L})\cong H_{*}(B;\mathcal{L})

\mathcal{U}

Proposition 4.7

The algorithm above computes the twisted Euler number associated to a twisted Euler class (up to choice of sign).

5
Persistence Of Characteristic Classes

Discrete approximate trivializations constructed from a real-world dataset are unlikely to satisfy the fairly restrictive criteria required for our theoretical guarantees to hold. Due to practical considerations such as non-uniform sampling density or the presence of outliers for which the feature map is ill-defined, we may have reason to believe that some of the local measurements incorporated into our model are more reliable than others. In other cases, we may want to make a deliberate choice to ignore or modify certain parts of the data so that the restricted discrete approximate cocycle representative becomes trivial. Given a family of discrete approximate local trivializations
\{f_{j}\}\in T_{\alpha}(\pi,\mathcal{U})
and a witness
\Omega
, we therefore introduce a weight function
w:\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
which measures the alignment quality of each pair

and

5.2
Interpretation

We would like to interpret each filtration stage
W^{r}\in\mathcal{W}
as the nerve of a modified open cover
\mathcal{U}^{r}
of a new base space
B^{r}
. For example, removing an edge
(jk)
from
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
effectively separates sets
U_{j}
and
U_{k}
. For the purposes of coordinatization, there are various choices one could make about how to handle data in
\pi^{-1}(U_{j}\cap U_{k})
, and the appropriate choice depends on context. One option would be to simply remove these points from the dataset – this would be a reasonable choice if the intersection is small and consists mostly of outliers with ill-defined projections. In other cases, one may want to keep the data while modifying
B
and
\mathcal{U}
. For instance, if the cobirth of a class representative is
\infty
(or all the weights are fairly small and close together), one may still want to introduce cuts in the base space in order to artificially create a trivial bundle which admits a global coordinate system. With this in mind, we offer a recursive approach for constructing a family
\{B^{r}\}
of spaces and corresponding open covers
\{\mathcal{U}^{r}\}
such that
W^{r}=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U}^{r})
.

[
MG15

[

[

7.2
3D Densities

Many applications of computer vision and imaging involve data in the form of density or intensity functions on some region of space or time. The ambient dimension for such data is inevitably very large, and distances computed using the standard Euclidean metric often fail to reflect one’s intuition about how densities should compare (for example, two densities which differ by a small translation may be very far apart with respect to Euclidean distance). One would ideally opt for an Earth-mover-type distance such as the Wasserstein metric
[
RTG00
]
, but these metrics are prohibitively expensive to compute, so an effective local-to-global approach to topological modeling which circumvents the need to construct a full distance matrix is highly desirable.

Consider the space
\mathcal{D}
of real-valued functions on the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{3}
. The space
\mathcal{D}
has a natural right
SO(3)
-action defined by
(\rho\cdot A)(x)=\rho(A^{T}x)
for all
\rho\in\mathcal{D}
,
A\in SO(3)
and
x\in\mathbb{B}^{3}
. In particular, the orbit of any fixed
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
is diffeomorphic to a quotient of
SO(3)
by the corresponding stabilizer subgroup. For our demonstration, we sampled the
SO(3)
-orbit of a density
\rho_{\text{prism}}\in\mathcal{D}
concentrated around the boundary of a triangular prism
P\subset\mathbb{B}^{3}
(see Figure
10
). Explicitly,
\rho_{\text{prism}}:\mathbb{B}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\rho_{\text{prism}}(p)=\exp\left(-\frac{d(p,\partial P)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

(28)

where
d(p,\partial P)
denotes the (Euclidean) distance from
p
to the boundary of
P
, and
\sigma=0.05
. Note that
\rho_{\text{prism}}
has a 3-fold rotational symmetry in the
yz
-plane as well as a 2-fold rotational symmetry along the
x
-axis. The orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\text{prism}}
is therefore homeomorphic to the quotient
L(6,1)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, where
L(6,1)
denotes the lens space
\mathbb{S}^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{6}\cong SO(3)/\mathbb{Z}_{3}
.

Figure 10
:
A sample of discrete 3D prism densities from
X_{\text{prism}}
. Each density is represented by its 2D projection along the
x
-axis as well as the corresponding rotation of the prism
P
used to generate
\rho_{\text{prism}}
. Note that any two rotated prisms which are related by an element of
\text{stab}(\rho_{\text{prism}})
result in indistinguishable 3D density functions.

Consider the feature map
\pi:\mathcal{O}_{\text{prism}}\to\mathbb{RP}^{2}
defined as follows: given any

8
Conclusion and Future Work

We have introduced a discrete, approximate analog of circle bundles, motivated by data-theoretic applications. We established conditions under which such an object can be identified with a true circle bundle and described algorithms for recovering the classification of the underlying bundle. We also proposed a coordinatization scheme that mimics the construction of a bundle map to a universal bundle, which can be tuned to capture different global topologies using a persistence-based approach on characteristic classes.

A natural direction for future research is to establish a recovery theorem providing conditions under which a finite metric space
X
and a feature map
\pi:X\to B
uniquely determine a true circle bundle, independent of the choice of discrete, approximate local trivializations.

Beyond circle bundles, the theory and algorithms presented here could be extended in several ways. One could consider discrete, approximate analogs of bundles with other fibers, such as disjoint unions of circles
\coprod_{j}\mathbb{S}^{1}_{j}
, higher-dimensional spheres
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, or tori
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Another promising direction is the study of more general topological objects, such as fibrations or stratified bundles, which allow for singularities in the base or fiber. These generalizations open the door to applications involving more complex or singular data structures.

A.1
The Group
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})

In this section, we prove some technical results about
\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})

\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
\text{Homeo}_{-}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, respectively. A model for the universal cover
\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
of each component is given by

\widetilde{\text{Homeo}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}=\{f\in\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{R}):f(x+1)=f(x)\pm 1\}

(29)

We then define
\widetilde{\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}=\widetilde{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\coprod\widetilde{\text{Homeo}_{-}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}
.

We denote the group of isometries of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
by
\text{Iso}(\mathbb{S}^{1})<\text{Homeo}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
and denote the connected components by
\text{Iso}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. We have a natural identification of
\text{Iso}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
with
\mathbb{S}^{1}
itself via the map
R^{+}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\text{Iso}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
which sends
e^{i\theta}
the counter-clockwise rotation
R^{+}_{\theta}
(equivalently,
R^{+}_{\theta}
denotes left-translation in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
by
e^{i\theta}
). Similarly, every orientation-reversing isometry is expressible as
R_{\theta}^{-}
for some
\theta
, where
R_{\theta}^{-}(z)=e^{i\theta}\bar{z}
for all

. We can model the universal covers
\widetilde{\text{Iso}}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
as subsets of
\widetilde{\text{Homeo}}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S}^{1})

\widetilde{\text{Iso}}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S}^{1})=\{T^{\pm}_{a}(x)=\pm x+a\ :\ a\in\mathbb{R}\}

(30)

Proposition A.1
.

Suppose
\tilde{f}\in\widetilde{\text{Homeo}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}
is
L
-Lipschitz. Then,
d_{\infty}(\tilde{f},T^{\pm}_{\tilde{f}(0)})\leq\frac{L-1}{L}

Proof.

Assume
\tilde{f}\in\widetilde{\text{Homeo}_{+}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}
(the proof for
\tilde{f}\in\widetilde{\text{Homeo}_{-}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

x\in(0,1)
, we have
\tilde{f}(x)-\tilde{f}(0)\leq Lx
and
\tilde{f}(1)-\tilde{f}(x)\leq L(1-x)

\tilde{f}(0)<\tilde{f}(x)<\tilde{f}(1)
for any

is constrained to lie in a polygonal region. In particular, the quantity

.

A.2
Circle Bundles Over
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
\mathbb{RP}^{2}

In this section, we provide the details of the constructions and classifications of the two non-trivial circle bundle models described in the Implementations section. Since both models are associated with quotients of
SO(3)
, we begin by describing principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle structures for
SO(3)
and its universal cover
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

For any
v\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
let
R_{v,\theta}\in SO(3)
denote the counterclockwise rotation by
\theta
about
v
. Fixing a
v_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we obtain a right
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-action defined by
A\cdot e^{i\theta}=AR_{v_{0},\theta}
. One can easily verify that this action is free and that the orbits are precisely the fibers of the map
\pi:SO(3)\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
defined by
\pi(A)=Av_{0}
. We conclude that
(SO(3),\pi,\mathbb{S}^{2})
is a principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle. Moreover, the isomorphism class of this bundle does not depend on the choice of
v_{0}
. To see this, observe that for any
v_{0},v_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
B\in SO(3)
such that
Bv_{0}=v_{1}
, the map
F:SO(3)\to SO(3)
by
F(A)=AB^{-1}
is an equivariant bundle isomorphism. We therefore take
v_{0}=e_{1}
without loss of generality in what follows.

The group
SO(3)
admits a (universal) double cover
\text{Spin}(3)\cong\mathbb{S}^{3}\subset\mathbb{H}
via the map
\text{Adj}:\mathbb{S}^{3}\to SO(3)
defined by
\text{Adj}_{q}(v)=qvq^{-1}
. Here we are identifying
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with the pure imaginary unit quaternions via
(x,y,z)\leftrightarrow xi+yj+zk
. Thinking of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
as a subgroup of the unit quaternions, we have a free right
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-action on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
by group multiplication. Moreover, the continuous surjection
\widetilde{\pi}:\mathbb{S}^{3}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
defined by
\widetilde{\pi}(q)=\text{Adj}_{q}(e_{1})
is invariant under this action, so
(\mathbb{S}^{3},\widetilde{\pi},\mathbb{S}^{2})
is a principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
bundle (called the
Hopf bundle
). In particular, we have the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{S}^{3}}

Note, however, that the two principal circle bundles are not isomorphic. Indeed,
Adj
is not a homeomorphism, nor is it
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-equivariant (in fact, one has
\text{Adj}_{q\cdot e^{i\theta}}=\text{Adj}_{q}\cdot e^{i(2\theta)}
). The Hopf bundle famously has Euler number -1 with respect to the standard orientation on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
(for a proof, see
[
Hus94
]
). The following proposition immediately implies that the Euler number of the principal bundle
(SO(3),\pi,\mathbb{S}^{2})
is
-2
, and will also be useful for classifying the circle bundles from our models in Section
7
:

Proposition A.5
.

Let
\xi=(E,\pi,B)
be a principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle, and for any
p\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\varphi_{p}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be the group homomorphism defined by
\varphi_{p}(z)=z^{p}
. Then
(\varphi_{p})_{*}\xi
is a principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle over the same base space with Euler number
e((\varphi_{p})_{*}\xi)=pe(\xi)
and total space
E\times_{\varphi_{p}}\mathbb{S}^{1}\cong E/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega\in\check{Z}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}})
be a cocycle representative of
\xi
subordinate to some open cover
\mathcal{U}
of
B
. By the results in Section
2.1
,
\varphi_{p}^{*}\xi
is a principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle which is represented by
\varphi_{*}\Omega
. If
\theta\in\check{C}^{1}(\mathcal{U};\mathbb{R})
is any lift of
\widetilde{\Omega}
(i.e.,
\Omega_{jk}(b)=e^{i\theta_{jk}(b)}
for all
b\in U_{j}\cap U_{k}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
), then

(\varphi_{*}\Omega)_{jk}(b)=\varphi\circ\Omega_{jk}(b)=\varphi(e^{i\theta_{jk}(b)})=e^{i(p\theta_{jk}(b))}

for all
b\in U_{j}\cap U_{k}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U})
, so
p\theta
is a lift of
\varphi_{*}\Omega
. The components of the Euler class
e(\varphi_{*}\xi)
are then given by

\displaystyle e(\varphi_{*}\xi)_{jkl}

\displaystyle=p\theta_{jk}(b)+p\theta_{kl}(b)-p\theta_{jl}(b)

\displaystyle=p(\theta_{jk}(b)+\theta_{kl}(b)-\theta_{jl}(b))

\displaystyle=pe(\xi)_{jkl}

We conclude
e(\varphi_{*}\xi)=pe(\xi)
.

∎

Corollary A.6
.

The principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle
(SO(3),\pi,\mathbb{S}^{2})
described above has Euler number
-2
with respect to the standard orientation on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\xi_{H}=(\mathbb{S}^{3},\widetilde{\pi},\mathbb{S}^{2})
and
\xi_{SO(3)}=(SO(3),\widetilde{\pi},\mathbb{S}^{2})
be the principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundles shown in (
31
), and consider the group homomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
defined by
\varphi(z)=z^{2}
. The total space of
\varphi_{*}\xi_{H}
is
\mathbb{S}^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong SO(3)
, and the adjoint map precisely satisfies
\text{Adj}_{q\cdot z}=\text{Adj}_{q}\cdot\varphi(z)
. We can therefore identify
\xi_{SO(3)}
with
\varphi_{*}\xi_{H}
, so by Proposition
A.5
,
e(\xi_{SO(3)})=2e(\xi_{H})=-2
.

∎

Since principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundles over
\mathbb{S}^{2}
are completely determined by their Euler numbers, Proposition
A.5
actually implies
every
such bundle can be realized as a quotient of the Hopf bundle. The quotient 3-manifold
\mathbb{S}^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is often denoted by
L(p,1)
and called a
lens space
. Note that for any
p\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
L(2p,1)\cong SO(3)/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and
(\varphi_{2p})_{*}\xi_{\text{Hopf}}\cong(\varphi_{p})_{*}\xi_{SO(3)}
.

The following proposition suggests how to construct the circle bundles over
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
:

Proposition A.7
.

Suppose
\xi=(E,\pi,B)
is a principal
G
-bundle, and
E
and
B
have free, proper right actions by a group
H
such that:

1.

\pi:E\to B
is
H
-equivariant,

2.

There is a representation
\varphi:H\to\text{Aut}(G)
such that
(e\cdot g)\cdot h=(e\cdot h)\cdot\varphi_{h}(g)
for all
e\in E
,
g\in G
and
h\in H
.

Then, the map
\pi^{\prime}:E/H\to B/H
defined by
\pi^{\prime}([e])=[\pi(e)]
is a fiber bundle with fiber
G
and structure group
G\rtimes_{\varphi}H
.

Proof.

We give a sketch – the details are fairly straightforward and are left to the reader.

First, observe that the hypotheses imply
E
admits a free right
(G\rtimes_{\varphi}H)
-action
e\cdot(g,h)=(e\cdot h)\cdot g
, and that the orbits of this action are precisely the fibers of the map
\Pi:E\to B/H
defined by
\Pi(e)=[\pi(e)]
. That
\Pi
is locally trivial follows directly from this property of
\pi
, so
(E,\Pi,B/H)
is a principal
(G\rtimes_{\varphi}H)
-bundle. Define a left
(G\rtimes_{\varphi}H)
-action on
G
by
(g,h)\cdot g^{\prime}=\varphi_{h}(g^{\prime})g^{-1}
and form the associated bundle
(E\times_{G\rtimes_{\varphi}H}G,\Pi^{\prime},B/H)
with fiber
G
. One can check that the assignment
(e,g)\longmapsto[e\cdot g]
is a homeomorphism which fits into the commutative diagram below:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E\times_{G\rtimes_{\varphi}H}G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E/H}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B/H}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Pi^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\prime}}

(32)

where
\pi^{\prime}:E/H\to B/H
is the map defined in the proposition. We conclude that
(E/H,\pi^{\prime},B/H)
is a fiber bundle with fiber
G
and structure group
G\rtimes_{\varphi}H
.

∎

Corollary A.8
.

For any integer
p
, let
(L(2p,1),\pi_{2p},\mathbb{S}^{2})
be the principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle over
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with Euler number
-2p
, and consider the free right
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-actions on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
L(2p,1)
defined respectively by
v\cdot(-1)=-v
and
[q]\cdot(-1)=[qj]
. The map
\pi_{2p}^{\prime}:L(2p,1)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to\mathbb{RP}^{2}
defined by
\pi_{2p}^{\prime}([e])=[\pi_{2p}(e)]
is a non-orientable circle bundle with (twisted) Euler number
\pm p
.

Now, suppose
X
is a Hausdorff space which admits a continuous right
SO(3)
-action. The orbit
\mathcal{O}_{x}\subset X
of any
x\in X
is homeomorphic to the quotient of
SO(3)
by the stabilizer subgroup
\text{stab}(x)=\{g\in SO(3):x\cdot g=x\}
. In particular, the map
T_{x}:SO(3)/\text{stab}(x)\to\mathcal{O}_{x}
defined by
T_{x}([A])=x\cdot A
is a homeomorphism.

If
\text{stab}(x)
is trivial, then
\mathcal{O}_{x}\cong SO(3)
, and
\mathcal{O}_{x}
inherits a principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-structure defined by the right
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-action
y\cdot e^{i\theta}=y\cdot R_{e_{1},\theta}
. The induced projection map
\widetilde{\pi}:\mathcal{O}_{x}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is defined by
\widetilde{\pi}(x\cdot A)=\pi(A)=Ae_{1}
for all
A\in SO(3)
, and
T_{x}
is an equivariant bundle isomorphism.

Now suppose
x\in X
has a
p
-fold rotational symmetry in the sense that
\text{stab}(x)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for some positive integer
p
. The orbit
\mathcal{O}_{x}
is then homeomorphic to the lens space
L(2p,1)\cong SO(3)/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Just as before,
\mathcal{O}_{x}
inherits a principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-structure such that
T_{x}
is an equivariant bundle isomorphism. We conclude
(\mathcal{O}_{x},\widetilde{\pi},\mathbb{S}^{2})
is a principal
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-bundle with Euler number
-2p
.

Lastly, suppose that in addition to a
p
-fold rotational symmetry in the plane perpendicular to
e_{1}
,
x\in X
also has an additional 2-fold rotational symmetry with respect to this plane (imagine a prism). Then
\text{stab}(x)
is the non-abelian group generated by
\{R_{e_{1},\frac{2\pi}{p}},R_{e_{2},\pi}\}
, where
R_{e_{1},\theta}R_{e_{2},\pi}=R_{e_{2},\pi}R_{e_{1},-\theta}
. In particular, observe that
R_{e_{2},\pi}
induces a free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-action on
SO(3)/\langle R_{e_{1},\frac{2\pi}{p}}\rangle
given by
[A]\cdot(-1)=[AR_{e_{2},\pi}]
. Moreover, one can easily verify that
\pi([A]\cdot(-1))=-\pi([A])
and
([A]\cdot R_{e_{1},\theta})\cdot(-1)=([A]\cdot(-1))\cdot R_{e_{1},-\theta}
for all
A\in SO(3)
. Thus, by Proposition
A.7
, the induced projection map
\pi^{\prime}:SO(3)/\langle R_{e_{1},\frac{2\pi}{p}},R_{e_{2},\pi}\rangle\to\mathbb{RP}^{2}
given by
\pi^{\prime}([A])=[\pi(A)]
is a non-orientable circle bundle. In fact, using the adjoint map to identify
\mathbb{S}^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
with
SO(3)
, this bundle is canonically isomorphic to the circle bundle
\pi^{\prime}_{2p}:L(2p,1)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to\mathbb{RP}^{2}
described in Corollary
A.8
(note in particular that
\text{Adj}_{j}=R_{e_{2},\pi}
).

A.3
Miscellaneous

Proposition A.9
.

Suppose
r,R\in O(2)
with
\det(r)=-1
and
\det(R)=1
(
r
is a reflection and
R
is a rotation). Then,
rRr=R^{-1}
.

Proof.

Let
\text{Adj}_{r}(R)=rRr^{-1}
. Note that
SO(2)\subset O(2)\subset GL(2,\mathbb{R})
, so
r
and
R
are invertible (in particular,
r=r^{-1}
). Moreover,
\text{det}(\text{Adj}_{r}(R))=1
, so
\text{Adj}_{r}
is a map from
SO(2)
to itself. In fact,
\text{Adj}_{r}
is clearly an involution, so we must have
\text{Adj}_{r}\in\text{Aut}(SO(2))\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. On the other hand,
\text{Adj}_{r}
cannot be the identity since this would imply
rR=Rr
, which we know is false by the semidirect decomposition of
O(2)
. We conclude that
\text{Adj}_{r}
is the inversion map on
SO(2)
, so
rRr=R^{-1}
.

∎

Proposition A.10
.

Suppose
A\in M_{n\times n}
and
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
|Ax|<\varepsilon
for all
x\in X
. If
d_{H}(X,\mathbb{S}^{n-1})<\delta
(with respect to the Euclidean metric), then
|A|_{F}<\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{1-\delta}
.

Proof.

Choose any
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and let
x\in X
such that
|v-x|<\delta
. Then,

|Av|\ \leq\ |A(v-x)|\ +\ |Ax|\ <\ \frac{|A|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}\delta\ +\ \varepsilon

Thus,

|A|_{F}\ <\ \sqrt{n}\left(\frac{|A|_{F}}{\sqrt{n}}\delta\ +\ \varepsilon\right)

or equivalently,
|A|_{F}<\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{1-\delta}
.

∎

Proposition A.11
.

Suppose
V=\{v_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
|v_{j}-v_{0}|<\sqrt{2}
for all
j
. Then
V
has a unique weighted Karcher mean with respect to the geodesic distance on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
(and any weights).

Proof.

The fact that
|v_{j}-v_{0}|<\sqrt{2}
for all
j
implies
d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(v_{j},v_{0})<\frac{\pi}{2}
for all
j
, so by the triangle inequality, the diameter of
V
(with respect to
d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
) is less than
\pi
. Since
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is geodesically convex on any subset of diameter less than
\pi
,
V
has a unique weighted Karcher mean with respect to any weights.

∎
