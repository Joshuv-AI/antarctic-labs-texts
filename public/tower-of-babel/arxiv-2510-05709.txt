Towards Reliable and Practical LLM Security Evaluations via Bayesian Modelling Thanks: † Research conducted while at the Alan Turing Institute.
Mary Llewellyn, Annie Gray, Josh Collyer, Michael Harries

Abstract
Before adopting a new large language model (LLM) architecture, it is critical to understand vulnerabilities accurately. Existing evaluations can be difficult to trust, often drawing conclusions from LLMs that are not meaningfully comparable, relying on heuristic inputs or employing metrics that fail to capture the inherent uncertainty. In this paper, we propose a principled and practical end-to-end framework for evaluating LLM vulnerabilities to prompt injection attacks. First, we propose practical approaches to experimental design, tackling unfair LLM comparisons by considering two practitioner scenarios: when training an LLM and when deploying a pre-trained LLM. Second, we address the analysis of experiments and propose a Bayesian hierarchical model with embedding-space clustering. This model is designed to improve uncertainty quantification in the common scenario that LLM outputs are not deterministic, test prompts are designed imperfectly, and practitioners only have a limited amount of compute to evaluate vulnerabilities. We show the improved inferential capabilities of the model in several prompt injection attack settings. Finally, we demonstrate the pipeline to evaluate the security of Transformer versus Mamba architectures. Our findings show that consideration of output variability can suggest less definitive findings. However, for some attacks, we find notably increased Transformer and Mamba-variant vulnerabilities across LLMs with the same training data or mathematical ability.



I
Introduction

Large language model (LLM) architectures are evolving rapidly. Transformer attention mechanisms
[
68
]
led to the development of several well-known generative pre-trained Transformer (GPT) models. Since then, countless derivative and alternative architectures have been proposed, including Transformer-based mixture of experts and distilled models, for example, in
[
29
]
and
[
47
]
. Architectures such as Mamba and xLSTMs
[
4
,
27
]
have simultaneously demonstrated the potential for efficient inference using alternative architectures. As architectures emerge and are embedded into systems, our reliance on LLMs increases. Thus, it is critical that we are able to accurately and usefully understand their vulnerabilities.

We focus on comparing LLM architectures’ robustness to black-box, prompt-based adversarial attacks, as part of, for example, practical considerations on the adoption of a new architecture. In this setting, multiple pitfalls, primarily centring on confounding variables and capturing uncertainty, can nullify experimental conclusions with real-world consequences
[
33
,
43
]
. Firstly, confounding variables arise from the substantial variation in pre-trained LLM specifications, including training data, pre-processing procedures and training hyper-parameters. Ignoring these variables when comparing LLMs makes it impossible to establish whether differences in vulnerability are due to architecture or other confounding factors. Secondly, uncertainty quantification is often not addressed when analysing results. Standard practices draw conclusions from point estimates, on a small number of data samples and using human-designed test prompts
[
70
,
19
]
. However, these approaches often do not capture how a given LLM is likely to behave in the future. Point estimates do not capture the probabilistic nature of LLM outputs
[
45
]
, test prompts are often heuristic and therefore do not test different LLMs fairly
[
2
]
, and sample sizes are often too small for classical statistics guarantees
[
7
]
.

In this paper, we propose a principled approach to addressing these two categories of challenge. This results in an end-to-end guide to LLM evaluations from experimental design to interpreting outputs. Precisely, we first define LLM groups to isolate the architecture effect using two practically-relevant perspectives: training specifications and performance. To quantify uncertainty in attack outcomes, while acknowledging that practitioners often have a limited amount of compute, we utilise repeated independent trials and develop a Bayesian hierarchical model. Such models have recently shown promise in reliable LLM evaluation
[
36
,
7
,
71
,
66
]
, providing a natural way to interpret uncertainty while allowing for limited data settings. To compensate for test prompt biases arising from human definitions, we propose a new approach that leverages embedding-space clustering to identify distinct concepts in the prompts and reduce bias. Further, we do not assume that the number of distinct concepts is known, removing human bias and improving scalability.

After introducing this methodology, we show the improved insights of this Bayesian model on a case study, which aims to identify and compare vulnerabilities in Transformer and Mamba models. Mamba models were introduced in
[
27
]
and, in brief, replace the attention mechanisms of Transformer models with a context-aware and computationally efficient state-space model. Many authors have reported that Mamba models outperform Transformers on a number of benchmarks
[
20
,
49
,
70
,
73
]
and over 5000 articles have cited the seminal Mamba paper since its release in December 2023. However, despite these LLMs being integrated into production-grade systems
[
31
,
1
]
, their security properties relative to Transformers are largely unexplored. In summary, we see
three main contributions
of this paper:

•

Practical guidance on experimental design.
We provide two practical and realistic approaches to improving the experimental design of LLM vulnerability evaluations, centred on addressing confounding LLM variables.

•

Improved inferential reliability via a Bayesian model.
We improve the reliability of conclusions drawn with limited data, and use clustering to reduce topic bias and allow for scalable inference without prior knowledge on the number of topics.

•

Demonstration of the end-to-end design via a real-world case study.
We demonstrate the framework by comparing the robustness of Mamba versus Transformer models to prompt injection attacks. By explicitly accounting for output variability, we find several relationships between architecture and adversarial robustness.

II
Background & related work

Throughout this paper, we consider black-box, prompt injection attacks on LLMs that can be validated from LLM outputs. That is, given query access to a model, an attacker aims to elicit an adversarial output from the LLM through their input prompts, without access to or knowledge of the LLM internals. When assessing the robustness of LLMs to such attacks, rigorous experimental design and analysis are crucial for trusting that conclusions will hold in reality. As discussed in Section
I
, there are two key challenges that are often overlooked: confounding variables and quantifying uncertainty. Here, we provide further detail on these challenges, how they are currently addressed and we motivate our proposed framework.

II-A

Confounding variables

In line with well-established scientific principles
[
21
]
, experiments must be carefully controlled when comparing LLMs. Previous work has shown how several LLM variables, including training data, model size and training hyper-parameters can confound and mask the true effect of variables of interest
[
72
,
75
,
42
,
58
,
30
]
. For example, many frontier LLMs are trained on internet-scraped data, creating a high likelihood that evaluation datasets have been seen during training
[
72
,
75
]
. This contamination can artificially inflate performance, confounding the effect of architecture
[
42
,
58
]
. While detection methods such as n-gram analysis exist, they often prove insufficient for comprehensive identification of data leakage
[
74
]
.

The challenge is now how to control experiments.
Ideally, one would train a set of LLMs using standardised training procedures, fixing the training data and hyper-parameters as in
[
70
,
35
,
30
]
. However, training a set of LLMs is a costly and thus often an infeasible endeavour. Alternative methods, such as ablation
[
76
]
, can be used to determine the effect of components of an architecture, but can still be costly. Instead, practical approaches often include relating trends across LLMs with different parameter counts and equating LLMs based on performance
[
44
,
65
]
. This can be thought of as collecting evidence about the properties of different architectures which can inform practitioners, without hastily making overconfident conclusions. We concretely define and implement two practical approaches in this paper, motivated by practitioner perspectives of choosing an LLM to train or for its downstream task performance.

II-B

Quantifying uncertainty

Even after controlling confounding variables as far as possible, drawing conclusions from the resulting experiments presents another set of challenges. Many early LLM evaluations rely on point estimates from small sample sizes that do not account for the probabilistic nature of LLMs. This approach can skew conclusions by being over confident from results that could simply be luck
[
6
]
. In line with this work, we first enable the quantification of uncertainty in results by repeating experiments.

Classical versus Bayesian inference:
Recent work has recommended repeated trials to enable reliable estimation of sample means and confidence intervals
[
45
,
6
,
39
]
. However, financial and time constraints often limit the number of available repetitions. This low sample size means that the underlying assumptions of classical statistical results (based on the central limit theorem) often do not hold, undermining the accuracy of sample means, confidence intervals and p-value comparisons
[
7
]
.
Instead, Bayesian models can compensate for small sample sizes while simplifying the probabilistic interpretation of results. Recent work demonstrates that Bayesian models improve the accuracy of uncertainty quantification in LLM evaluations when there are less than a few hundred outputs
[
7
]
. Other examples include the Bayesian approach to model ranking with limited data presented in
[
71
]
and the use of textual variables in
[
57
]
. While this literature considers Bayesian models for general LLM evaluations, we suggest that adversarial vulnerability evaluations in limited data settings should similarly adopt their use.

Embedding-space clustering:
When analysing LLM evaluation results under this Bayesian (or any) modelling framework, an important consideration is the independence of test prompts. Test prompts are often heuristic and designed by humans
[
12
]
. These prompts may, for example, test the same areas of LLM understanding or vulnerability, leading to inter-dependent prompts. In turn, this ultimately results in less reliable insights into performance. If an LLM performs robustly at a task over-included in a benchmark, the results will be unfairly skewed in favour of that LLM. This is demonstrated in
[
2
]
, where they show that major benchmarks exhibit bias from imperfect test prompts sets
within
specified tasks. Further, they demonstrate that comparative studies can be highly skewed in favour of a given LLM.

Recent work therefore highlights the need to factor in prompt clusters, often by grouping data based on whole benchmarks or by labelled tasks within a benchmark
[
36
,
7
]
. However, in line with the work in
[
2
]
, we propose that tasks should also be clustered at the prompt level, using an embedding-space approach, to improve reliability. Further, we do not assume a known labelling for these clusters, or even that the number of clusters is known, with two main benefits. Firstly, we reduce potential bias from the human labels: clustering is now, objectively, a result of the semantics similarities of the prompts. Secondly, our assumption that the number of distinct clusters is unknown is inherently scalable. We can fit the model separately to many different attacks, or evaluation tasks, within a large-scale evaluation, with little adaptation. Finally, partially deterministic clustering methods are applied to mitigate against unstable results when the number of items in a cluster is small, allowing for cases where a prompt may test a distinct topic.

II-C

Mamba versus Transformers

Finally, we end this section by discussing the motivation for our extended case study. Assessing the adversarial properties of emerging architecture types, such as Mamba architectures when compared to widely-applied Transformer architectures is a critical consideration for a practitioner. However, very little work evaluates the robustness of Mamba models in language applications. The work in
[
14
,
40
,
41
,
51
]
evaluates the adversarial robustness of vision Mamba models and the related mechanistic determiners of vulnerability to attack (such as parameter size and back propagation). The results on robustness and comparison to Transformer methods are extremely mixed but several mechanistic vulnerabilities are identified that lead to worse robustness than Transformers, including around image centres and when scaling the model. However, this work does not explore the robustness of Mamba language models. In
[
52
]
, the authors evaluate the benefit of adversarial training to primitive Mamba models on the well-known MNIST and CIFAR-10 data sets, reporting little benefit to robustness of adversarial training. They also hypothesise that attention mechanisms are more adversarially robust and introduce an adaptive scaling mechanism that aims to mimic scaling in Transformers. This improves adversarial training performance. This paper provides a good general starting point for evaluating the susceptibility of Mamba variants to adversarial attacks but has limited scope in terms of application to real-world examples. We apply our methodology to uncover more about the adversarial properties of Mamba versus Transformer architectures.

and Bayesian hierarchical model in Section
IV
, with model validation and our case study of Mamba versus Transformer models in Sections
V
and
VI

III
Experimental design: controlling confounding variables

We begin the description of our methodology with how we propose to control for confounding variables. We provide two perspectives on how experiments should be controlled, motivated by key questions relevant to practitioners: training and performance comparability.

III-A

Similar LLMs by training

Ideally, one would be able to draw definitive answers about the relative security of different LLM architectures. However, as previously discussed, this is unattainable for most practitioners. In lieu of being able to train an LLMs, we seek to isolate the effect of architecture as much as we can. This means controlling as many confounding variables as possible from pre-trained LLMs.

Our approach involves first identifying groups of LLMs matched by their training data and by closely matching model specifications, such as layer counts. Although this does not entirely eliminate confounding variables, such as the effect of optimisation procedures on adversarial performance, comparing conclusions across groups that vary in model owner and training data
can
somewhat alleviate the effect of these confounders. If a supposed architectural effect holds across groups trained by different individuals and on different training sets, the effect is less likely to be due to a particular training setup and more likely to reflect a property of the architecture itself
[
8
,
46
]
. This approach allows practitioners to be reasonably confident about the effect of architecture on robustness without needing to train a new LLM for each new architecture they would like to test.

In our case study in Section
VI
, we demonstrate how we scoped the documentation for groups of LLMs with similar training specifications, which may be of practical relevance to readers.

III-B

Similar LLMs by performance

Practitioners often choose LLMs for their performance on a downstream task. If several LLMs perform similarly on this task, it is sensible to select the LLM with the greatest adversarial robustness. Thus, a reasonable and practical approach may compare adversarial robustness among LLMs with equal abilities. In addition to being a practically relevant method for comparing LLMs, this approach explicitly controls for confounding due to task-specific competence, and implicitly may also control for other confounding variables that correlate with competence, such as model size and training data.

A key challenge in this performance-based approach is finding benchmarking tasks that accurately reflect the nature of the downstream task. For a complete assessment, tasks must reflect all required capabilities, including all domain-specific knowledge, and the extent and nature of the required generative text abilities
[
9
]
. Particular challenges arise in the common case that generative capabilities are required. When LLM response accuracy is unambiguous, for example, a number or multiple-choice response, it is possible to automatically evaluate each response as correct or incorrect, for example, via a list of trigger words
[
23
]
. When responses are generative and there are multiple ways in which the response can be correct, human evaluation is often required. However, this human evaluation can be biased
[
16
]
and does not scale well. An active area of research is the use of LLMs as judges (also termed ‘autoraters’), for example, in
[
32
,
50
,
15
,
13
,
67
]
, which use highly-capable LLMs to evaluate the generative responses of other LLMs based on some rubric. While the capability of a judge LLM is crucial, the quality of the evaluation prompt also affects the reliability of the judgment
[
28
]
.
[
32
]
and
[
48
]
, for example, provide principled approaches. Our general advice, and the approach to our case study in Section
VI
, is to define performance using all required capabilities as the benchmark, carefully assessing response validation procedures. However, these challenges highlight that evaluating can be an additional source of uncertainty in robustness studies. To properly account for this variability, we next introduce our proposed Bayesian model.

IV
Analysis: quantifying uncertainty

To begin quantifying uncertainty in evaluation results, we repeat experiments several times. However, as discussed in Section
II
, there is a limit to how many repetitions are feasible in practice. While we recommend repeating queries as many times as possible, we acknowledge this is likely not enough for classical statistics guarantees to hold
[
7
]
. Moreover, the test prompts themselves are often heuristic and inter-dependent, and do not test different LLMs fairly
[
2
]
. Further, human evaluation for organising prompts into independent groups can be inaccurate and infeasible. In this section, we present the Bayesian hierarchical model with embedding-space clustering to tackle these challenges.

While prior work has assumed that prompts within a labelled task or subtask are independent, we first show why this assumption should be treated with caution. We begin with a simple demonstration using a set of attack prompts from the popular Garak LLM vulnerability scanner
[

, which aim to elicit JavaScript package hallucination. To understand the relationship between prompts, we first map them to the embedding space of a sentence Transformer,

. We can then visualise these embeddings, while reducing the dimension using firstly principal component analysis (PCA) then t-SNE
[

]
. Figure

illustrates this analysis for one attack dataset. While PCA fails to clearly reveal cluster structure, t-SNE successfully exposes distinct semantic groupings. We also show the percentage of the variance in the data explained by each principal components (PCs), validating that much of the structure in the prompt data can be explained by lower-dimensional features, implying redundancy and inter-dependence between prompt embeddings. ) in Appendix

.

The impact of ignoring the dependence structure between prompt embeddings on the accuracy of conclusions is demonstrated later in Section

. We now introduce the embedding-space clustering model, designed to disentangle these prompts and motivated by the evidence presented here. That is, if prompts are deemed to be in some way dependent and semantically linked, we seek to disentangle semantic similarity to avoid conclusions being skewed by over-inclusion of very similar prompts.

IV-B

Bayesian hierarchical model with embedding-space clustering

We focus on
one
particular attack, denoted
a
, with
n
prompts, and note the extension to other attacks or accuracy tasks is trivial. These
n
embeddings are denoted by
e_{1:n}
. First, we assume the practical scenario in which the number of clusters (topics) is unknown. We therefore assign the unknown number of clusters,
S
, a Bayesian prior distribution:

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\sim\text{Binomial}(50n,0.01),

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\min(n,B+1).

This prior is relatively diffuse, enabling sufficient probability across the range of possible cluster numbers, while bounding the maximum number of clusters by the maximum number of prompts. In a practical setting, this prior distribution could be informed by an arbitrarily vague notion of the number of prompt clusters.

We now apply agglomerative clustering
[
62
]
with Spearman correlation as the measure of similarity due to potentially non-linear and non-Gaussian relationships. We note that we do not treat cluster assignments as random variables given the number of clusters,
S
, as this leads to over-parameterisation with unstable results when the number of embeddings in a cluster is small. This process partitions the
n
prompt indices into
S
clusters,
\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{S}
, based on the resulting cluster assignment of the embedding vector. In other words, cluster
\mathcal{C}_{k}
contains the indices of the embeddings in cluster
k
. Each index belongs to one and only one cluster, i.e.,
\bigcup_{k=1}^{S}\mathcal{C}_{k}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap\mathcal{C}_{l}=\emptyset
for
k\neq l
.

We stipulate that the prompts within each cluster now have close semantic ties and thus model the probability of attack success as the same within each cluster. Further, we have several outputs for each unique prompt as part of our repeated experiments. That is, each of the
n
prompts is parsed to the LLM
m_{i}
times for a prompt index
i
. Therefore, the number of attack or accuracy task successes for each prompt
i
, denoted
x_{i}
, is a Binomial random variable with probability dependent on the cluster assignment and
m_{i}
total repetitions:

x_{i}\sim\text{Binomial}(m_{i},p_{k}),\text{ for }i\in\mathcal{C}_{k},

where
k\in\{1,\dots,S\}
,
i=1,\dots,n,
and we assign the following priors on the unknown probabilities:

p_{k}\sim\text{Beta}(1,1),\quad\text{for }k\in\{1,\dots,S\}.

Again, these priors are relatively diffuse and here avoid biasing conclusions. To find the overall probability of an attack succeeding, we then assume this probability is the average of all
distinct
attack or task topic probabilities:

\displaystyle p_{a}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k=1}^{S}p_{k}.

This effectively pools the variability between independent prompt groups, quantifying uncertainty at the topic level. From this model, we can formulate posterior distributions over the attack success rates that can be used to compare LLMs directly. Precisely, we can compare
p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})
,
p(p_{1:S}|x_{1:n})
or
p(S|x_{1:n})
between LLMs: respectively the posterior distributions of an entire attack or prompt category succeeding at eliciting adversarial outputs, and the posterior distribution for the number of distinct prompt categories identified.

IV-C

Implementation

The posterior distributions of interest, which may be
p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})
,
p(p_{1:S}|x_{1:n})
or
p(S|x_{1:n})
, depending on the question of interest, do not admit a closed-form expressions. Therefore, we use importance sampling to get exact samples from these distributions, as detailed below. Since
p_{a}
is a deterministic function of
p_{1:S}
and
S
, we start by considering the joint distribution,
p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})
. This joint distribution can be written as

p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})\propto p(S)p(p_{1:S}|x_{1:n},S)p(x_{1:n}|S).

(1)

Since the mapping from the number of clusters to cluster assignments is deterministic, we sample from this distribution by sampling
S
from its prior,
p(S)
, and
p_{1:S}
from the tractable conditional beta-binomial distribution,
p(p_{1:S}|x_{1:n},S)
. As per standard importance sampling procedures
[
55
]
and by considering Equation (
1
), we now weight these samples by the marginal likelihood,
p(x_{1:n}|S)
. This is the Beta-Binomial likelihood with the
p_{1:S}
integrated out. That is,

\displaystyle p(x_{1:n}|S)

\displaystyle=\int p(p_{1:S}|S)p(x_{1:n}|p_{1:S},S)dp_{1:S}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{S}\frac{1}{\beta(1,1)}\Big(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{m_{i}\choose x_{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\times\beta(1+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}x_{i},1+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}(m_{i}-x_{i})),

where
\beta
denotes the Beta function and this term is derived by relating the integration steps to the normalising constant of a Beta distribution. We can evaluate this marginal likelihood to calculate the weights up to proportionality and normalise, yielding weighted Monte Carlo samples directly from
p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})
. Extracting approximations of the marginal distributions from these samples is trivial, found by simply disregarding all but the samples for the variables of interest
[
55
]
. The approximation to the final target posterior,
p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})
, follows by averaging the sampled
p_{1:S}
in each cluster, with the weights already calculated. We summarise the entire importance sampling procedure in Algorithm
1
.

For further information, we provide a proof and detailed derivation of the importance sampling scheme in Appendix

. Importantly, the weighted samples can be used to formulate unbiased estimators for the corresponding true expectations. For example, the weighted average of samples for

is unbiased for the expected value
\mathbb{E}(p_{a}|x_{1:n})

V
Bayesian model validation

We evaluate the proposed Bayesian hierarchical model with embedding-space clustering against its ability to improve the reliability of inference from LLM security evaluations. The models in
[
7
]
and

36
]
were intended for different analysis settings and thus assume that the number of clusters and clustering approach are user-defined and known

We consider cases where it is not possible to determine the number of clusters before analysis either due to the scale of experiments or practical difficulties in determining which prompts are statistically independent. Thus, since no alternative automatic methods exist, we first assess how effective our clustering approach is at disentangling independent prompts, demonstrating theoretical validity, and we then compare the model to the remaining competitor: the same model without clustering. In this latter case, we simply implement the same model in Section

We use 5-fold cross-validation with approximate stratification to demonstrate out-of-sample performance while avoiding biasing the results by introducing new classes at prediction time. We first coarsely label the prompts with approximate classes and attempt to ensure that the training set contains exactly one example of each class guess. The rest of the data are then partitioned into the folds. We note that coarse labelling would not be required in a real-world example and does not inform or bias the proposed model directly here. For each fold, we then find the joint posterior distribution of the unknown parameters given the training set, approximated by the importance samples from Algorithm
1
. These weighted Monte Carlo samples are then applied to approximate the expected log predictive density (ELPD). As in
[
24
]
, the ELPD can be interpreted as a predictive likelihood of the observed value under each Bayesian model, capturing posterior uncertainty, making it an appropriate criterion for evaluating the fit of the posterior distributions. The approximate ELPDs from each LLM, attack and Bayesian model under test are presented in Table
II
. Higher ELPD indicates that the posterior generalises better, thus supporting more reliable explanatory conclusions.

Table
II
shows the favourable performance of the proposed model across the range of attacks and both LLMs considered. The difference in the ELPDs appears greatest for the
ansi escape
attack. This correlates with the extent of inter-dependence between prompts and the size of the clusters found as per Appendix
-C
, indicating that the proposed Bayesian model is particularly effective at improving the reliability of inference when there is strong inter-dependence between prompts. Conversely, smaller differences are observed for the attacks with more independent prompts, but even accounting for this lower level of inter-dependence has improved out-of-sample performance. We also note that predictive performance disadvantages the proposed model compared to the standard analysis scenario. We attempted to mitigate against some of this bias in our partitioning of training and test sets. However, for prediction, prompts that form new, unanticipated clusters must be allocated to an already-existing cluster, which we do via the closest average linkage.

VI-B

Comparing LLMs by performance

Although investigating the performance of LLMs across groups with the same training data can provide some practical insights on LLM vulnerabilities, the alternative method we propose compares LLMs based on their performance. Of practical relevance, we answer the question: when choosing between pre-trained LLMs offering the same performance, which LLM should I choose for robustness against attack?

Using the same LLMs as Section
VI-A
, we define performance as broad mathematics capabilities for the purposes of demonstration, but note that the approach generalises trivially to other required capabilities. We select five tasks aimed at testing various aspects of an LLM’s mathematical understanding, leveraging the
Language Model Evaluation Harness
software by EleutherAI
[
23
]
. We select the
MathQA
benchmark to test broad mathematical understanding, the
MMLU
elementary, high school and college-level mathematics benchmark tasks to assess different levels of mathematical understanding, and we select the
GSM8K
benchmark tasks to test generative mathematics abilities. For each task, we validated the triggers prescribed in
[
23
]
to assess whether the LLM answered correctly.

A key consideration in capturing accuracy from these benchmarks is, as is a theme of this paper, the independence of prompts. It is likely that the prompts are correlated in this case due to the shared subject matter. We therefore apply the same proposed Bayesian model in Section
IV-B
to cluster

However, we now select the sampling temperature that gives the highest posterior accuracy (mean with low variance) due to the performance focus here. We present the results in Figure

From Figure

More LLMs could be tested if this relationship is of interest. The LLMs with mid-range mathematics performance appear to reveal the most interesting differences due to architecture. For the
ansi escape
and
divergence
attacks, the Transformer-Mamba-2 distilled model (Llamba-3B) appears substantially more robust than the comparative single models and the Mamba-2-Transformer (mamba2attn-2.7b), but less robust to the
package hallucination
attack. For these LLMs with mid-range mathematical ability, architectures with any Mamba-2 component appear comparatively the most vulnerable to the
latent injection

The conclusions from this performance-based comparison are different from the previous training data comparison. This shows that different practitioner requirements may possibly require different architectures for robustness. For example, in this case, the falcon-mamba-7b model appears the most performant, while achieving fairly average adversarial robustness for most attacks (though is the least robust to the
ansi escape
attack), thus may be a reasonable performance-based choice depending on security requirements.

VII
Conclusions

In this paper, we provide a framework for end-to-end LLM security evaluation, from experimental design to analysis. Firstly, we consider careful experimental design and the control of confounding variables. Without training each LLM one would like to consider, which may be costly, eliminating
all
confounding variables is challenging. This leads to the question of whether the LLMs are similar
enough
given the downstream task required. We proposed two practical alternatives motivated by two common scenarios: when training an LLM and when deploying a pre-trained LLM. To eliminate as many confounding variables as possible, the selection of comparable LLMs should depend on the resources available and deployment setting.

Secondly, we addressed the analysis of experiments. Non-deterministic LLM outputs, limited evaluation data and inter-dependent test prompts via human design are common but do not satisfy the assumptions of current inferential approaches. The Bayesian hierarchical model with embedding-space clustering we proposed in Section
IV-B
provides marked improvements to out-of-sample performance and strengthens the reliability of conclusions drawn from the experiments. The model is also inherently scalable via the unknown numbers of clusters, removing the need for human cluster identification. The proposed approach also provides flexibility in prompt engineering, only requiring prompts that cover all required test areas, and not independence, for valid analysis.

After validating the proposed Bayesian model, we then demonstrated our end-to-end framework by comparing Transformer and Mamba architectures. In several cases, consideration of output variability via our proposed model reduced certainty in the findings. However, there were significant findings in some cases. For example, when comparing across several groups of LLMs with the same training data, we found some evidence of increased Transformer vulnerability to training data extraction via repeat string instruction but potentially increased robustness to instruction subversion via a French to English translation task and JavaScript package hallucination. Interestingly, when comparing across groups of LLMs with the same training data or similar mathematical abilities, distilling a Transformer to a Mamba-2 model altered the adversarial properties of the respective singular models. The robust choice of architecture, therefore, should depend on a practitioner’s deployment requirements and whether robustness to certain attacks should be preferred.

Our end-to-end approach could be extended to broader prompt-based evaluation settings trivially. One could evaluate architectural vulnerabilities to many further prompt injection attacks, or any prompt-based LLM evaluation task, such as accuracy benchmarking, as demonstrated in Section
VI-B
. Moreover, this approach could be applied to any safety-aligned LLM to evaluate alignment-based tasks.

We end this section by discussing several interesting extensions. Establishing independence between texts is challenging due to the complexity of language. One may be able to draw on further techniques from statistical independence testing and representation learning to help distinguish between attack prompts, for example, the methods in
[
26
]
and
[
53
]
. Further, although we chose to use the
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
embedding model based on the balance between speed and quality, many other options that could be tested, for example, those in
[
54
]
. Alternative clustering algorithms, such as DBSCAN
[
18
]
or k-means
[
34
]
, with comparisons in
[
56
]
, could also be explored. Finally, clustering could be applied as a data pre-processing step, leaving the practitioner to determine the number of clusters in the data. However, this is a non-trivial problem amounting to estimating the rank of a matrix
[
37
,
77
,
64
,
11
]
. These approaches may also be limited in their feasibility for large-scale evaluations compared to the Bayesian approach. A comprehensive study of Bayesian clustering can be found in
[
69
]
.

As with all Bayesian models, our approach depends on the selection of priors. Although we use diffuse priors throughout this work, which have demonstrated reliability in allowing the proposed model to capture underlying relationships, alternative prior distributions could be explored. For example, more complex Bayesian clustering priors, such as Dirichlet priors
[
3
,
63
]
, could be considered to infer the number of clusters, or more informative priors could be used given some vague knowledge of the number of clusters.

Finally, our current approach uses attacks and accuracy tasks with straightforward validation procedures. To incorporate additional tasks and attacks that are more difficult to assess, for example, those that require assessment beyond string matching, we may be able to use methods such as LLM judges
[
32
]
. However, this would require thorough validation of the judge LLM
[
61
]
to ensure accurate scoring before deployment and hence this is considered beyond the scope of this work.

Proof and derivation of importance sampling procedure

We derive the importance sampling procedure described in Section
IV-C
in full. Firstly, we note that the target distribution for an attack
a
is
p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})
. To show that the importance sampling procedure results in weighted samples from this distribution, we first show that the procedure targets the distribution
p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})
and then that the distribution for
p_{a}
is the result of marginalising over this extended space. First, the joint distribution for
p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})
can be written as

p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})\propto p(S)p(p_{1:S}|x_{1:n},S)p(x_{1:n}|S).

Since the mapping from the number of clusters to cluster assignments is deterministic, sampling from this distribution amounts to sampling
S
from its prior,
p(S)
,
p_{1:S}
from the conditional distribution
p(p_{1:S}|x_{1:n},S)
and weighting these samples according to the marginal likelihood,
p(x_{1:n}|S)
. Sampling from the prior is immediate and it is fairly easy to show that
p(p_{1:S}|x_{1:n},S)
amounts to sampling from separate Beta distributions due independence and the Beta-Binomial distribution conjugacy. Specifically, given the model in Section
IV
,

p_{k}|x_{1:n},S\sim\text{Beta}(1+\sum_{i\in C_{k}}x_{i},1+\sum_{i\in C_{k}}(m_{i}-x_{i})),

for
k\in{1,\dots,S}
. Given that we have these sampling schemes, we now derive an expression for the weights using the marginal likelihood. First, the marginal likelihood can be expressed as the integral over
p_{1:S}
as

\displaystyle p(x_{1:n}|S)

\displaystyle=\int p(p_{1:S}|S)p(x_{1:n}|p_{1:S},S)dp_{1:S},

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{S}\frac{1}{\beta(1,1)}\Big(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{m_{i}\choose x_{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\int p_{k}^{\sum x_{i}}(1-p_{k})^{\sum(m_{i}-x_{i})}dp_{k},

(2)

due to the distributional independence in the
p_{k}
. Now, since the integral in the second line of Equation (
2
) corresponds to the normalising constant of a Beta distribution, we have that

\displaystyle p(x_{1:n}|S)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{S}\frac{1}{\beta(1,1)}\Big(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{m_{i}\choose x_{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\beta(1+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}x_{i},1+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}(m_{i}-x_{i})),

where
\beta
denotes the Beta function. We can evaluate this marginal likelihood to calculate the weights up to proportionality and normalise so that they sum to one. Calculating these weights for the samples described, i.e. the first steps of our importance sampling procedure in Section
IV-C
, we draw weighted Monte Carlo samples directly from
p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})
.

We now show that the target posterior,
p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})
, is the result of marginalising over an extended space including the cluster-wise probabilities and the number of clusters, thus we can formulate a Monte Carlo approximation
p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})
. We can write
p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})
as the marginal of the joint distribution with the number of clusters,
S
, and the associated cluster probabilities,
p_{1:S}
:

p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})=\sum_{S=1}^{n}\int p(p_{a},p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})dp_{1:S}.

This can be written as

\displaystyle p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S=1}^{n}\int p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})p(p_{a}|p_{1:S},S,x_{1:n})dp_{1:S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S=1}^{n}\int p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})\delta_{p_{a}}(\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k=1}^{S}p_{k})dp_{1:S},

noting that, since
p_{a}
is deterministic given
p_{1:S}
and
S

p_{1:S}
and
S
as a delta function. Given
T
weighted Monte Carlo samples,
\{(p_{1:S}^{t},S^{t}),W^{t}\}
, from
p(p_{1:S},S|x_{1:n})

\displaystyle p(p_{a}

\displaystyle|x_{1:n})\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{S=1}^{n}\int\sum_{t=1}^{T}W^{t}\delta_{p_{a}}(\frac{1}{S^{t}}\sum_{k=1}^{S^{t}}p_{k}^{t})\delta_{(p_{1:S},S)}(p_{1:S}^{t},S^{t})dp_{1:S}

\implies p(p_{a}|x_{1:n})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}W^{t}\delta_{p_{a}}(\frac{1}{S^{t}}\sum_{k=1}^{S^{t}}p_{k}^{t}),

thus taking the average cluster-wise probabilities with weights
W^{1:T}
