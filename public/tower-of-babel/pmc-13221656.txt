TF-DWGNet: a directed weighted graph neural network with tensor fusion for multi-omics cancer subtype classification
Authors: Tiantian Yang, Zhiqian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegration and analysis of multi-omics data provide valuable insights for improving cancer subtype classification. However, such data are inherently heterogeneous, high-dimensional, and exhibit complex intra- and inter-modality dependencies. Graph neural networks provide a principled framework for modeling these structures, but existing approaches often rely on prior knowledge or predefined similarity networks that produce either undirected or unweighted graphs, failing to capture task-specific directionality and interaction strengths. Interpretability at both the modality and feature levels also remains limited. To address these challenges, we propose “TF-DWGNet,” a novel Graph Neural Network framework that combines tree-based Directed Weighted graph construction with Tensor Fusion for multiclass cancer subtype classification. TF-DWGNet introduces two key methodological innovations: (i) a supervised tree-based strategy that constructs directed weighted graphs tailored to each omics modality, and (ii) a tensor fusion mechanism that captures unimodal, bimodal, and trimodal interactions using low-rank decomposition for computational efficiency. Experiments on three real-world cancer datasets demonstrate that TF-DWGNet consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines across multiple metrics and statistical tests. In addition, the model provides biologically meaningful insights through modality level contribution scores and ranked feature importance. These results highlight that TF-DWGNet is an effective and interpretable solution for multi-omics integration in cancer research.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health challenge, marked by uncontrolled cell growth, genetic instability, and complex molecular alterations that vary across cancer types and among individual patients. This biological heterogeneity underscores the importance of accurate cancer subtype classification, which is central to precision medicine. Subtype classification enables personalized treatment strategies by leveraging individual molecular profiles, yet reliable prediction remains difficult due to the complexity and multilayered nature of tumor biology. Advances in high-throughput technologies now enable simultaneous profiling of multiple omics layers, such as DNA methylation, mRNA (messenger RNA) expression, and microRNA (miRNA) expression, providing a comprehensive molecular view of tumors [1, 2]. These modalities offer complementary biological signals that, when jointly analyzed, can reveal disease mechanisms not captured by single-omics analysis. However, multi-omics data integration remains challenging due to high dimensionality, modality heterogeneity, and complex cross-modal interactions [3]. Addressing these issues requires computational frameworks that are capable of modeling hierarchical and directional biological processes while preserving model interpretability.

Classical learning approaches such as random forest (RF) [4], eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [5], and deep feedforward networks (DFNs) [6, 7] have been widely applied to omics data, but their Euclidean assumptions make it difficult to capture relational structures, handle severe feature dimensionality, or provide task-relevant interpretability [8, 9]. Geometric deep learning, particularly graph neural networks (GNNs), provides a principled solution by modeling non-Euclidean structures such as biological networks [10, 11]. GNNs learn node representations by aggregating information from graph neighborhoods [12, 13], and architectures such as graph convolutional networks (GCNs) [14], graph attention networks (GATs) [15], graph isomorphism networks [16], and GraphSAGE [17] have demonstrated strong performance in biomedical tasks [18, 19]. Meanwhile, multimodal fusion strategies, such as encoder–decoder designs, attention-based methods, and tensor-based models, aim to address heterogeneity across modalities [3, 20], although many do not fully capture higher-order interactions.

Several GNN-based frameworks have been proposed for multi-omics integration in cancer subtype classification and survival prediction. For example, methods such as MOGONET [21] employ GCNs to process multiple omics modalities and incorporate a cross-omics discovery tensor and a view correlation discovery network for the final prediction. Architectures such as MODILM [22] extend these ideas using graph attention mechanisms to strengthen intra-omics modeling. Other frameworks, including SUPREME [23] and MOGAT [24], highlight patient similarity networks, GCNs or GATs, and basic concatenation to integrate multiple omics types. Additional approaches, such as DeepMoIC [25] and MoGCN [26], leverage autoencoder-based feature extraction and similarity network fusion before applying GNN modules. These approaches differ in terms of how they construct graphs, fuse multimodal information, and ensure interpretability. Despite these advances, existing frameworks exhibit several key limitations: (i) suboptimal graph construction: most rely on predefined or correlation-based similarity networks that are undirected, unweighted, and insensitive to task-specific directionality and heterogeneous interaction strengths, both of which are essential for modeling biological regulatory flow; (ii) incomplete multi-omics integration: many approaches use simple concatenation or pairwise modeling and therefore fail to capture higher-order (unimodal, bimodal, trimodal) interactions that may reveal synergistic mechanisms among omics layers; and (iii) limited interpretability: interpretability often depends on post hoc analyses, such as feature ablation, attention inspection, or gradient-based methods, which may not reflect the model’s true decision-making process and can be computationally intensive.

To address these challenges, we propose TF-DWGNet, a novel GNN-based framework for interpretable multi-omics integration and cancer subtype classification. The key novelties of TF-DWGNet lie in the integration of supervised directed weighted graph construction and low-rank tensor fusion, which together enable task-adaptive feature interaction modeling and efficient higher-order cross-omics learning. The primary contributions of this work are as follows:

Supervised directed weighted graph construction: we leverage XGBoost’s tree-splitting structure to construct biologically meaningful, task-specific, and variable-sized (i.e. data-adaptively determined) directed weighted graphs that encode both directional influence and interaction strength while performing data-driven feature reduction.

Directed weighted graph construction

We leverage XGBoost’s sequential boosting mechanism to capture task-specific feature dependencies and directional interaction patterns in multi-omics data. Unlike ensemble methods such as RF, which construct trees independently, XGBoost builds trees through successive gradient-based updates to the objective function, with each iteration refining the structure not captured by previous trees. This iterative refinement encourages the model to repeatedly focus on discriminative feature splits, leading to task-adaptive and stable feature interaction patterns when aggregated across the boosted ensemble. For each omics modality, an ensemble of boosted trees is trained. Each tree is viewed as a directed graph , where a directed edge indicates that feature appears before along the same root-to-leaf path. These edges do not represent causal biological relationships. Modeling intra- and inter-omics dependencies

In TF-DWGNet, dependencies are modeled through two complementary components. Within each modality, directed weighted graphs constructed via XGBoost capture structured, task-relevant relationships among features. Across modalities, interactions are modeled through the tensor fusion module, which operates on modality-specific embeddings and represents unimodal, bimodal, and trimodal interactions via outer products followed by low-rank CP decomposition. This design allows modality-specific graphs to encode intra-omics dependencies, while the tensor fusion module provides a unified mechanism to model both within-modality effects and higher-order cross-omics interactions without requiring a predefined cross-omics graph.

Residual classification network

In our architecture, the hidden width is fixed across all layers; therefore, only the first block requires projection and all subsequent blocks are identity blocks. This design stabilizes the optimization and enables deeper and more expressive feature transformations.

Technical details

XGBoost is implemented using the xgboost Python library, and all GNN and DFN components are trained in TensorFlow. Models are optimized using mini-batch training with the Adam optimizer, with ReLU as the activation function throughout and softmax in the output layer for multiclass classification. Training minimizes the categorical cross-entropy loss combined with dynamic regularization applied to all trainable weights from the three GNN branches and the DFN with residual blocks:

where is the true label; is the predicted probability; and is the regularization strength. We tune the number of estimators for XGBoost and optimize the architectural and training hyperparameters, including depth, hidden width, learning rate, batch size, and maximum epochs, for both the GNN and DFN components. Dropout, early stopping, and batch normalization are applied to mitigate overfitting in high-dimensional settings. Model evaluation is performed over 20 independent stratified training–validation–test splits (60%:20%:20%), with each run using a fixed random seed and different seeds across runs. These strategies act complementarily: dropout and regularization constrain model complexity during training, early stopping prevents excessive fitting to the training data, and repeated stratified splits provide a more robust assessment of generalization performance across different data partitions in high-dimensional multi-omics settings. In addition, we tune the tensor fusion rank and the number of residual blocks in the DFN. Unless otherwise specified, all components of TF-DWGNet fit exclusively on the training split. For each random split, XGBoost is trained on only the training samples, and directed weighted graphs are constructed from the tree-splitting paths on this data. The validation and test samples are then passed through the trained trees to obtain their reduced-feature representations, while the graph structure remains fixed within each split. This prevents any leakage of test information into the graph construction stage. All experiments are run on the Falcon supercomputer [38], with each job requesting four CPUs and 20 GB of memory.

Real data experiments

Datasets overview

We evaluate TF-DWGNet on three widely used multi-omics cancer subtype datasets: BRCA, UCEC, and KIPAN. The processed BRCA dataset is obtained from Wang et al. [21], while the original UCEC and KIPAN datasets are downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via the Broad GDAC Firehose and preprocessed following the procedure in Lu et al. [39]. BReast CArcinoma (BRCA) is used for PAM50 subtype classification with five molecular subtypes. Uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) contains three clinically relevant subgroups. KIPAN, the TCGA PAN-KIdney cohort, includes three kidney cancer subtypes. The datasets display varying degrees of class imbalance, including severely underrepresented subtypes such as HER2-enriched in BRCA and MSEAC in UCEC, offering a realistic and challenging evaluation setting. Although all datasets originate from TCGA, they represent biologically distinct cancer types with different imbalance structures and molecular characteristics. Baselines, ablation studies, and experimental setting

We compare TF-DWGNet with several conventional and deep learning baselines, including RF, XGBoost, DFN, GCN, and GEDFN, as well as “three ablation variants” designed to isolate the effects of graph construction, fusion strategy, and directionality: (i) replaces XGBoost with RF for graph construction, examining the effect of the tree-based estimator; (ii) replaces tensor fusion with direct concatenation, assessing the contribution of tensor modeling and CP decomposition; and (iii) symmetrizes the adjacency matrix to remove directional information, evaluating the importance of task-conditioned directionality.

All baselines are trained on column-wise concatenated multi-omics features, unless the model inherently incorporates graph structure. For fairness, both GCN and GEDFN use XGBoost-derived graphs, consistent with TF-DWGNet. All models are evaluated using the same 20 stratified training, validation, and test splits (60%/20%/20%), where each run uses a fixed random seed, with different seeds across runs to ensure robustness. For tree-based baselines, XGBoost and RF use 100 estimators. DFN, GCN, and GEDFN each use two hidden layers of width 64. TF-DWGNet employs one graph-embedded hidden layer of width fixed to the reduced feature dimension in each GNN branch, and three residual blocks in the DFN module, each with three hidden layers of width 64. The default TF-DWGNet model uses 100 trees in the XGBoost-based graph-construction module. The tensor fusion module uses a CP rank of . All models are trained using a learning rate of 0.0001, a batch size of 64, and a maximum of 1000 training epochs. Regularization strategies include dropout (0.5), L2 regularization (), and early stopping (patience = 10 epochs; minimum validation-loss improvement = 0.001). Evaluation metrics

We evaluate the model performance using accuracy, F1-weighted, and F1-macro scores. F1-macro computes the F1-score for each class independently and averages them equally, providing a balanced view of performance across all classes. In contrast, the F1-weighted score computes a weighted average of per-class F1-scores, with weights proportional to class frequencies, making it more sensitive to class imbalance. Across the three datasets, the edge-to-node ratios remain within a similar range despite differences in feature dimensionality and class distributions, indicating that the supervised graph construction yields consistent task-driven feature interaction structures. In practice, a well-constructed graph should remain relatively sparse while preserving connectivity among informative features: overly dense graphs may introduce noisy interactions, whereas excessively sparse graphs may fail to capture meaningful dependencies among biomarkers. The evaluated datasets also reflect typical high-dimensional multi-omics settings with hundreds of samples and tens of thousands of candidate biomarkers before feature selection, indicating that TF-DWGNet can operate effectively in small-, large- regimes commonly encountered in multi-omics studies.

Feature interpretability

Each input feature (biomarker) receives a ranking score after training. While the absolute magnitude of the score is not directly interpretable, the relative ranking provides meaningful insight into each feature’s contribution to subtype discrimination. High-ranking biomarkers may indicate strong statistical associations or potential mechanistic links to specific cancer types. To complement these rankings, we examine the global distribution of feature importance scores across datasets and modalities (see Supplementary Figs S1–S3). The distributions are consistently right-skewed, indicating that the predictive signal is concentrated on a small subset of informative biomarkers. This pattern suggests that the model does not rely on diffuse or noisy contributions, but instead identifies a stable set of highly informative biomarkers that drive subtype discrimination.

Consistent with this pattern, several top-ranked features in BRCA align with established cancer biology. For example, FAM134B, an ER-phagy receptor associated with hypoxic breast cancer survival, has been shown to suppress tumor proliferation when silenced [40]. Likewise, FOXC1, commonly overexpressed in basal-like breast cancer (e.g., BRCA1-mutant tumors), promotes proliferation and increases sensitivity to PARP inhibitors [41]. Such concordance supports TF-DWGNet’s ability to highlight biologically meaningful, subtype-relevant markers. Although our interpretability validation focuses on agreement with known biomarkers, a more systematic analysis, such as pathway enrichment or comparisons with curated biological knowledge, remains outside the scope of this study. Accordingly, the identified biomarkers should be interpreted as data-driven candidates rather than confirmed mechanistic drivers. The observed concordance with known biomarkers provides supportive evidence of biological relevance, while comprehensive biological validation is beyond the methodological focus of this work. TF-DWGNet is designed to provide statistically grounded, biologically plausible signals that can guide downstream investigations. In practice, groups of highly ranked biomarkers may reveal coordinated pathway activity or shared regulatory mechanisms that warrant further follow-up.

Modality interpretability

Figure 4 shows the relative contribution of each omics modality to TF-DWGNet predictions, derived from the aggregated feature-importance scores. The model adaptively reweights each modality based on its predictive utility. In BRCA, DNA methylation, mRNA expression, and miRNA expression contribute at comparable levels, suggesting complementary and non-redundant information across modalities. In contrast, for UCEC and KIPAN, DNA methylation emerges as the dominant source of predictive signal, while miRNA plays a comparatively smaller role. These patterns demonstrate TF-DWGNet’s ability to integrate heterogeneous omic sources in a data-driven manner, allowing the model to emphasize biologically relevant modalities without manual weighting or hand-engineered assumptions. While our analysis emphasizes statistical interpretability, validating modality-level contributions against known molecular mechanisms or pathway-level evidence remains an important direction for future work. The supervised graph construction mechanism is designed to capture task-relevant dependencies, providing a principled basis for the learned modality contributions and directional interactions.

Conclusion

We propose TF-DWGNet, a novel GNN framework for multi-omics cancer subtype classification that integrates directed weighted graph learning with tensor-based feature fusion in an end-to-end supervised architecture. By jointly modeling modality-specific graph structures and higher-order cross-omics interactions, TF-DWGNet provides a unified and expressive approach to capture the complexities inherent in heterogeneous omics data. The framework is also modality-flexible, enabling the incorporation of additional omics types through higher-order tensor fusion and low-rank CP decomposition and supporting future extensions to broader multi-omics settings. Comprehensive experiments across three real-world cancer datasets demonstrate that TF-DWGNet consistently outperforms or matches strong classical and deep learning baselines across all major evaluation metrics. These results highlight the model’s robustness, predictive accuracy, and generalization ability across cancers with distinct molecular architectures and varying degrees of class imbalance. Beyond improved predictive performance, TF-DWGNet provides interpretable outputs, including modality-level contribution scores and biomarker-level importance rankings. These insights help reveal potential molecular drivers and dataset-specific patterns, offering interpretability essential for downstream biological investigations and clinical hypothesis generation. Overall, TF-DWGNet offers a powerful and interpretable solution for multi-omics integration, highlighting how task-adaptive graph learning and structured tensor fusion can jointly improve predictive modeling and biological interpretability.
