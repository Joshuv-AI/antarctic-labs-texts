Development of an interpretable machine learning model for predicting stress urinary incontinence following the delivery of a first child
Authors: Siwen Tian, Jing Sha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe goal of this research is to use machine learning (ML) techniques to create a risk prediction model for postpartum stress urinary incontinence (PSUI). To improve screening accuracy and optimize clinical care techniques, the goal is to determine the best model for clinical screening.MethodsTelephone interviews and computerized medical records were used in this study to gather data, which was then combined into a follow-up data form. We recruited women who underwent surface electromyography (sEMG) at Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps Hospital’s postpartum rehabilitation clinic between January 2020 and January 2025. A total of 449 primiparous women were included in the study after the inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. A training set and a validation set were randomly selected from the dataset in a 7:3 ratio. Predictive models for persistent postpartum stress urinary incontinence (PSUI) were created using five machine learning algorithms: logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, support vector machine (SVM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). Model performance was evaluated using metrics including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Model interpretability was achieved through SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis.ResultsEight features were kept in the final model: neonatal birth weight, pre-pregnancy BMI, age at delivery, perineal laceration, mean pre-resting phase, mean post-resting phase, maximum fast-twitch phase, and mean slow-twitch phase. Out of the five algorithms, the Random Forest model performed the best, with a test set AUC of 0.920 (95% CI 0.8704–0.9688, P < 0.05), accuracy of 0.8444, precision of 0.6341, recall of 0.8125, specificity of 0.8544, and an F1 score of 0.7123. Ten-fold cross-test and the test set were used to confirm the model’s strong generalizability.ConclusionStrong clinical potential for PSUI risk prediction and screening is demonstrated by the random forest model. To further improve the model’s clinical usefulness, future research should increase the sample size and include multicenter data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09285-w.Keywords: Postpartum period, Predictive model, Machine learning, Risk assessment, Stress urinary incontinence

Brief summary

A Random Forest and SHAP-Based Prediction Model for Postpartum Urinary Incontinence (AUC 0.92): Suitable for Postpartum Clinical Screening.

Introduction

The first instance of involuntary pee leakage with physical exertion (including physical activity), sneezing, or coughing within a year after giving birth is known as postpartum stress urinary incontinence (PSUI) [1]. Perineal and pelvic floor muscle weakening and damage are known to be associated with pregnancy and childbirth. Stress urinary incontinence (SUI), or the involuntary urinary incontinence with elevated abdominal pressure, may be caused by several physiological changes that the urinary system experiences during pregnancy. Physical and hormonal factors, such as increased abdominal-pelvic pressure from the pregnant uterus, hormonal effects on urethral function, changes in bladder neck mobility, and changes in urethral closure pressure, can cause symptoms that start in early pregnancy and last throughout gestation [2]. Urinary incontinence (UI) affected 24.8% of women in China between the ages of 20 and 70, according to a recent large-scale epidemiological survey. The most common subtype among them was SUI (12.7%). Additionally, research shows that 89.5% of women with UI had their quality of life negatively impacted to varying degrees. Just 20.6% of women have sought medical attention [3]. SUI is a chronic illness that persistently impairs patients’ physical and emotional well-being, greatly lowering their quality of life and placing a heavy burden on public health due to hidden time costs, negative effects on mental health, and decreased quality of life. The intensity of symptoms raises the financial burden on women [4]. Despite these effects, only a tiny percentage of PSUI patients seek medical attention or talk about the problem with partners and family due to low disease awareness, feelings of shame, and financial limitations [5].

The field of urology and gynecological medicine has seen new technical advancements due to the quick development of artificial intelligence (AI), including machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) [6]. ML is a potent tool for evaluating enormous volumes of complicated medical data to improve the effectiveness and economy of healthcare services since it can describe, train, analyze, and predict data. It can effectively carry out standard, methodical duties, enhancing the capabilities of doctors [7]. ML provides better sensitivity, specificity, and predictive accuracy than conventional mathematical models. This includes increased prediction accuracy, the identification of nonlinear and higher-order correlations between exposures and confounders, and the quick processing of big datasets [8].ML is widely used by researchers worldwide to build and test disease risk prediction models. Through prognosis prediction, real-time therapy response evaluation, patient-specific treatment strategy development, and diagnostic optimization, these algorithmic tools are transforming clinical decision-making [9, 10]. Clinical decision-making requires transparent feature-level interpretability, which is provided by methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).

Through predictive modeling, several researchers have found important predictors of PSUI [11, 12], including vaginal delivery, age, high body mass index (BMI), excessive fetal weight gain, episiotomy, forceps delivery, and gestational urine incontinence [13].In the first year after giving birth, the prevalence of SUI rose from 24% at six weeks to 32% at twelve months [14]. To prevent PSUI, guidelines stress the significance of therapies including pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) and weight loss during pregnancy [15].PSUI incidence can therefore be decreased by early screening to find pregnant women who have not yet developed PSUI, successfully controlling controllable risk factors, and offering suitable therapy and management techniques to improve their condition. The results of this study have the potential to be a useful clinical tool for identifying postpartum women who are at high risk.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

The design used in this investigation was retrospective. Included were women who underwent surface electromyography (sEMG) at Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps Hospital’s postpartum rehabilitation clinic between January 2020 and January 2025. Telephone interviews and electronic medical records were the primary methods used to gather data, which was then combined into a standardized data collection form. It collected data on general information, pregnancy-related factors, and neonatal factors, primarily sourced from electronic medical records. Items such as educational level and postpartum SUI status were supplemented through telephone follow-ups. The full follow-up data form is available in Supplementary File 1. Postpartum women’s self-reported demographic data were gathered through telephone interviews. Pre-pregnancy body mass index(BMI), occupation, educational attainment and the incidence of PSUI within a year after giving birth were among these indicators. Participants’ family history and constipation history were also noted. Electronic medical records were used to extract pregnancy and delivery-related data. Maternal age at birth, height, pre-delivery weight, newborn weight, mode of delivery, number of pregnancies, history of miscarriage, and gestational weight increase were among these variables.

Further clinical information included postpartum pelvic floor electromyography, gestational age at birth, gestational diabetes, and gestational hypertension. Inclusion criteria: (a) primiparous women; (b) attended the postpartum rehabilitation clinic of our hospital at 42 days after delivery; (c) no severe postpartum complications (e.g., postpartum hemorrhage, puerperal infection, severe postpartum hypertension); (d) singleton pregnancy; (e) voluntarily participated and signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (a) multiparous women; (b) had absolute contraindications to vaginal delivery and ultimately underwent direct cesarean section; (c) diagnosed with any urogynecological disorder before pregnancy; (d) had major medical illnesses during pregnancy that could affect pelvic floor assessment; (e) refused or were unable to complete informed consent.

The locally manufactured Meland Pelvic Floor EMG Analysis System (B4Plus) was used to perform pelvic floor electromyography (EMG) testing. Every participant underwent pelvic floor EMG testing. Four phases of pelvic floor EMG data were gathered for this study [16]: (1) Pre-resting phase average value: evaluates the tension in the static pelvic floor muscles. Elevated resting tension, which may increase the risk of pelvic floor ischemia, is indicated by values more than 4 V.(2)Maximum value during the fast-twitch muscle (Type II) phase primarily evaluates the fast-twitch muscles’ (Type II fibers) dynamic muscle strength and reaction speed; (3)Average value during the slow-twitch muscle (Type I) phase primarily evaluates the slow-twitch muscles’ (Type I fibers) dynamic muscle strength and contraction control stability. Conditions like SUI and POP may be exacerbated by decreased muscle strength; (4)Average value in the post-resting phase: Measures static pelvic floor muscle tension. Elevated resting tension in the pelvic floor muscles is indicated by a reading greater than 4µV.

Stress urinary incontinence diagnostic standards

According to the diagnostic criteria of the International Continence Society (ICS) [1], stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is defined as the complaint of involuntary leakage of urine on effort or exertion, or on sneezing or coughing. In this study, diagnosis was made by clinicians based on a detailed history taking and the presence of these typical symptoms. Women who presented with only urgency symptoms or no leakage were excluded from the PSUI group and assigned to the non-PSUI group.

Data analysis

R 4.3.3 and SPSS 26.0 were used for data processing and analysis, as well as modeling. If continuous variables were regularly distributed, the statistical description utilized mean± standard deviation ( ± s), and independent two-sample t-tests were used for intergroup comparisons. The median (M) and interquartile range [M(P25, P75)] were included in the description if the data were not normally distributed. Independent two-sample rank sum tests were employed to compare the groups. The chi-square (χ²) test was used to compare groups for categorical data. The continuous-range chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was employed when the chi-square test was not appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Data preprocessing

Missing value management

First, features with more than 30% missing values were eliminated.To maintain feature correlations, the remaining missing data were imputed using multiple imputation (MI) with chained equations. Due to a high rate of missing data and lack of standardized measurement for the variable of urinary incontinence during pregnancy in the retrospective dataset, this variable was not included in the primary analysis. To guarantee independent model training and evaluation, all participants were split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) at random.Five machine learning methods were developed using the training set, and the model was evaluated using the test set.

Selection of features

The variable selection procedure in this study included two steps to guarantee that only factors that significantly contributed to PSUI risk prediction were included, eliminating unnecessary variables while preserving the model’s predictive power for the outcome event: Univariate Analysis: Using a significance level of P < 0.05, univariate analysis evaluated the relationship between each variable and PSUI.In order to improve data consistency and model correctness and lessen confounding effects on model performance, significant variables were moved on to the next stage. Multivariate logistic regression study: Categorical features were dummy-coded based on variables that were significant in univariate analysis. To find important risk factors, multivariate logistic regression was used to account for confounding effects.This method prevented overfitting, improved model stability and clinical application, streamlined model structure, and increased model performance and computing efficiency.

Building prediction models for machine learning

Five traditional machine learning methods were used to build an effective and empirically supported PSUI risk prediction model: logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, support vector machines (SVM), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XG Boost). These algorithms were chosen for their particular benefits in managing a variety of data characteristics and structural complexity, in addition to their extensive use and exceptional performance in classification tasks. A popular classical prediction model, logistic regression is renowned for its high interpretability and computational efficiency, and interpretability. Decision trees use a tree-like structure to divide data; nodes are split to create decision nodes, and the root node represents all of the data [17]. They are appropriate for clinical decision support because of their tree-like form, which graphically represents the decision path from characteristics to outcomes. Multiple decision trees make up the ensemble learning algorithms known as random forests, which greatly improve generalization skills. They evaluate variable contributions, efficiently manage high-dimensional features, and offer feature importance rankings. Although it can be applied to regression problems, SVM is mainly utilized for classification challenges. SVM is referred to as a non-probabilistic binary classifier because it divides data points into two classes. SVM achieves higher accuracy than several other methods.Nonetheless, it works well for issues with limited datasets [18]. Based on a gradient boosting framework, the XG Boost model significantly improves computing efficiency and model performance while controlling overfitting through regularization. These five algorithms, which range from sophisticated machine learning methods to conventional statistical models, offer technical assistance for thoroughly assessing the efficacy and application of PSUI risk prediction models.

Model interpretation

In order to overcome the “black box” aspect of machine learning models, a dual-level explanatory framework was established using SHAP analysis. It identified important predictors by quantifying the average contribution of characteristics to PSUI prediction at the global level. In order to improve transparency in the model’s reasoning process and explain prediction outcomes for each patient, it examined decision routes for particular cases at the individual level.

After applying strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final sample consisted of 344 women in the non-PSUI group and 105 women in the PSUI group. The incidence of PSUI was 30.52%. The mean age was 29.724 ± 3.720 years in the PSUI group and 28.581 ± 3.562 years in the non-PSUI group. Baseline characteristics of the two groups are presented in Table 1.

Initial feature selection

13 potential features linked to PSUI were found using univariate analysis (P < 0.05). Eight independent factors (P < 0.05) were chosen for modeling after multivariate logistic regression analysis. These variables included birth weight, pre-pregnancy BMI, age, perineal laceration, mean pre-resting phase, mean post-resting phase, maximal fast-twitch muscle, and mean slow-twitch muscle.

Model creation and evaluation of performance

Five machine learning risk prediction models—logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, SVM, and XG Boost—were created using these eight characteristics. During the model training phase, a 10-fold cross-validation approach was employed to mitigate the effects of randomization. Each model was evaluated using hyperparameter selection techniques that were most appropriate for the specific input data to which they were applied. On the training set (see Table 2; Fig. 1), the SVM model achieved excellent classification performance (AUC = 0.998, 95% CI: 0.996–1.000), but its AUC dropped to 0.904 on the validation set. Similarly, the XGBoost and random forest models also showed high performance on the training set, yet all experienced varying degrees of performance decline on the validation set. This discrepancy between training and validation performance is a common phenomenon in machine learning modeling and can be attributed mainly to the following factors: (1) the limited sample size of the training set, which may lead the model to learn some random noise or non-generalizable features; (2) the validation set in this study was an independent sample, whose data distribution naturally differed from that of the training set. Nevertheless, all models maintained good predictive ability on the validation set, with the random forest model performing the best: it achieved a validation AUC of 0.920 (95% CI: 0.870–0.969), followed by XGBoost and SVM (see Table 3; Fig. 2). The gold standard for evaluating a model’s generalizability is its predictive performance on an independent validation set. Based on this principle, we selected the random forest model, which showed the best overall performance on the validation set among the candidate models, as the final predictive model. This indicates that the model has good discriminative ability and clinical utility and can effectively identify individuals at high risk for PSUI.

Model interpretation using SHAP

SHAP global analysis identified four features that played a dominant role in the model’s classification decisions. In descending order of importance, these were: mean value during the pre‑rest phase, mean value during the post‑rest phase, maximum value during the fast‑twitch phase, and neonatal birth weight (Fig. 3A). Together, they served as the core drivers of the model’s predictions. SHAP dependence analysis further revealed (Fig. 3B) a consistent directional relationship between these four key features and the model output: higher values tended to push the predicted probability toward the negative outcome (non‑PSUI), whereas lower values were more likely to increase the predicted probability of PSUI, reflecting a clear linear driving effect.

Longer bars typically show that a characteristic has a bigger impact on model predictions. Generally, negative SHAP values are displayed in red, and positive SHAP values are indicated in blue. The model can also provide explanations for individual cases. For a non‑progressor case (Fig. 3C), even though the remaining features showed notable values, the higher mean values during the pre‑rest and post‑rest phases, the higher maximum value during the fast‑twitch phase, and higher neonatal birth weight together made positive contributions, whereas the other features had only weak effects on the final classification. In contrast, for a postpartum woman who progressed to PSUI (Fig. 3D), the prediction was mainly driven by the same four features—mean pre‑rest value, mean post‑rest value, maximum fast‑twitch value, and neonatal birth weight. These findings further confirm the dominant role of the core features and the negligible influence of the remaining ones.

Discussion

The health burden of PSUI extends beyond pelvic floor and urologic disorders in women. In addition to causing social isolation, sexual dysfunction, and psychological issues such as depression and anxiety, it also imposes a heavy economic burden on individuals, families, and society. In this study, we developed five machine learning models based on perinatal factors in primiparous women to predict the risk of PSUI and systematically compared their performance. The results showed that the random forest model offered better stability and generalizability and was ultimately selected as the model for clinical application, suggesting its potential for identifying high‑risk individuals and optimizing screening strategies. Among the factors predicting PSUI, pelvic floor sEMG parameters proved to be the most critical core features.

The SHAP framework overcomes key barriers to applying artificial intelligence in healthcare by providing both global feature importance rankings and individualized explanations. In our random forest model, four of the top five predictors were derived from pelvic floor surface electromyography (sEMG), highlighting the critical value of this measure in predicting PSUI. Moreover, the odds ratios for these four sEMG indicators were all below 1, meaning that lower values—reflecting reduced recruitment of pelvic floor muscle fibers—were associated with a higher likelihood of developing PSUI.

Surface electromyography (sEMG) is a well‑established method for quantifying neuromuscular activation and detecting early muscle dysfunction. In clinical practice, sEMG using a vaginal probe is one of the most common tools for assessing and treating PSUI. Chen et al. used artificial intelligence algorithms to intelligently identify pelvic floor muscle dysfunction and established normative reference values for pelvic floor sEMG parameters, providing a feasible approach for managing pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) and offering auxiliary diagnostic support for clinicians [18]. Li et al. found that women who underwent mediolateral episiotomy during their first delivery had lower pelvic floor muscle electrical activity, and that sEMG values were moderately negatively correlated with the incidence and severity of UI [19]. These findings align with our observation that pelvic floor sEMG can effectively predict the occurrence of PSUI.

As an objective and sensitive clinical indicator of pelvic floor function, sEMG has considerable value. It is widely used in disease assessment and has demonstrated strong clinical utility. We recommend timely sEMG screening for postpartum women to identify those at high risk, and when necessary, to provide interventions such as pelvic floor muscle training or electrical stimulation biofeedback, which may help reduce the risk of SUI in this population.

Among existing predictive models for PSUI, the most common predictor is stress urinary incontinence during pregnancy, followed by mode of delivery, age at delivery, gestational age, and constipation. Multiple studies have confirmed that these factors are important risk factors for PSUI [20, 21]. Our analysis showed that advanced maternal age, neonatal birth weight, and antenatal BMI were the main risk factors for PSUI, which is consistent with previous reports [12, 22]. Of note, among the non‑electromyographic factors, only neonatal birth weight emerged as a significant independent predictor. Although BMI, perineal laceration, and age contributed to model performance to some extent, their effects were relatively small. Several studies have indicated a positive association between higher fetal weight and the occurrence of PSUI [11], which aligns with our findings. A neonatal birth weight of 4000 g or more increased the risk of PSUI by 80% [23]. Regarding the effect of delivery mode, univariate analysis in the training set showed that women who delivered vaginally accounted for 78.4% of PSUI cases, markedly higher than the 21.6% among those who delivered by cesarean section. The risk of SUI in women with vaginal delivery is two to three times that in women with cesarean delivery. This difference is primarily attributed to direct damage to the pelvic floor support structures during labor: on the one hand, the muscles and fascial tissues supporting the urethra may be compromised by excessive stretching; on the other hand, delivery may also weaken the urethral closure mechanism, which is composed of both smooth and striated muscles of the urethral wall [24].

However, multivariable logistic regression in this study did not reveal a significant independent association between mode of delivery and the risk of PSUI. This result may suggest that the sample size of the cesarean section group in our training set was relatively small, leading to insufficient statistical power to detect a true difference. Alternatively, mode of delivery may influence PSUI indirectly through interactions with other factors such as perineal laceration or duration of the second stage of labor, rather than acting independently. Future studies with stratified analyses or larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings.

ML focuses on learning from data and continuously improving itself, and it holds greater power than traditional statistical methods in clinical decision‑making. ML has also been applied to early disease prediction or detection, thereby simplifying treatment complexity and increasing the likelihood of patient recovery [25]. This provides a scientific basis for personalized interventions, helping clinicians carry out early and precise interventions that can effectively reduce or delay the occurrence of PSUI. Notably, by using SHAP values to enhance interpretability, our study bridges the gap between machine learning models and real‑world clinical decision‑making, enabling healthcare providers to understand and trust the model’s predictions.

Furthermore, this study focused on women giving birth for the first time. Given that maternal age at first delivery has been steadily rising and fertility rates have been declining, more attention needs to be paid to the causes of PSUI in primiparous women and to improving their quality of life. Maternal age at first delivery is an important risk factor for pelvic floor dysfunction. Pelvic floor injury and the development of PSUI during the first delivery may have profound effects on a woman, potentially impacting her quality of life [26]. However, the mechanisms linking first‑time delivery to PSUI remain insufficiently explored [27]. Early identification facilitates timely intervention—for example, supervised pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), which has been shown to be effective in preventing and managing SUI, and may have even greater benefits for specific groups such as primiparous women, those in early pregnancy, or those carrying a large fetus [28, 29].

Existing models only support postpartum monitoring and intervention, rather than antenatal or intrapartum intervention. By integrating our tool into the antenatal, intrapartum and postpartum periods, clinicians can deliver targeted prevention strategies, thereby improving the overall quality of life for women after their first delivery and reducing the long‑term economic burden associated with PSUI treatment. This study also integrates multidimensional indicators, including pelvic floor muscle microstructure, into a dynamic risk assessment system covering the entire pregnancy, delivery, and postpartum periods. It clarifies the impact of various factors on primiparous women, addresses the lack of attention to first‑time mothers in traditional studies, and overcomes the problem of excessive confounding factors, with the goal of predicting the occurrence of PSUI.

Meanwhile, most existing prediction models have follow‑up periods ranging from six weeks to seven months postpartum. In contrast, our study extended the postpartum follow‑up to 12 months, challenging the current models. This extension improves the clinical applicability of our model, aligns with social trends, and provides a valuable tool for long‑term risk assessment. With the ongoing digitalization of medical data, valuable information such as sociodemographic details, antenatal examination records, and delivery characteristics can be archived and reviewed. These data are essential for assessing risk and predicting PSUI. Thus, using random forest, we can perform clinical assessments by inputting maternal data. The automation and objectivity of our model reduce subjective bias and improve screening consistency, which helps accurately identify high‑risk individuals and provides a scientific basis for developing personalized intervention strategies. Moreover, the model helps alleviate the economic burden of delayed treatment, optimizes the allocation of healthcare resources, promotes preventive care strategies, and ultimately improves overall health outcomes for postpartum women.

This study has several notable strengths, including the selection of the study population, the dynamic risk assessment system, an interpretability‑oriented design, and the use of ML models to enable real‑time clinical risk assessment and decision‑making. However, several limitations should also be considered. 1.Measurement bias: Telephone interviews may be subject to social desirability bias. The data collection form used in this study has not been psychometrically validated, and single‑item measurements have inherent limitations in terms of reliability and content coverage. Electronic medical record data are limited by the completeness of clinical documentation. 2.Diagnostic bias: PSUI was diagnosed based on self‑report rather than urodynamic studies or cough stress tests, which may affect diagnostic accuracy and model predictive performance. In addition, important variables such as urinary incontinence during pregnancy had high rates of missing data, which may lead to underestimation of model performance. 3.Generalizability: This study lacks large‑sample, multi‑center external validation. The applicability and stability of the model need further verification. Future research should conduct multi‑center prospective cohort studies using standardized pelvic floor assessment protocols, including pelvic floor ultrasound, assessment of urinary incontinence during pregnancy, and objective diagnostic criteria, and should establish an independent external validation set to further validate and optimize the clinical utility of our model. This is also the direction our team will pursue in future work.

Despite these limitations, this study has achieved its initial goal—to systematically evaluate the performance of five machine learning algorithms in predicting PSUI risk in primiparous women and to select the random forest model, which demonstrated the best stability and generalizability.

Conclusion

This study used five ML algorithms based on long postpartum follow-up periods to create a PSUI risk prediction model. The Random Forest model outperformed the others by a considerable margin, demonstrating its clinical promise for detection of high-risk people. This methodology offers a scientific basis for accurate screening and tailored treatments. Its clinical usefulness and universality are anticipated to be improved by future research centered on multicenter test, bigger sample sizes, and model optimization, providing important support for postpartum health management and preventative methods.
