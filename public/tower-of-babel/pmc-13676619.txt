A dataset on radical and extremist attitudes in Europe
Authors: Sebastian Jungkunz, Nina Fadarkhan Osenbrügge, Marc Helbling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset introduces the first cross-nationally validated measurement scales for left-wing and right-wing radicalism, and general political extremism, along with an implicit association test (IAT) for implicit political extremism in the Western world. It includes data from more than 18,000 participants collected through ten surveys conducted between 2022 and 2024 in France, Germany, Great Britain, Greece, Hungary, the Netherlands, and the United States. The dataset facilitates comparative analyses of the origins and consequences of radical and extremist attitudes across different political systems. Moreover, by linking attitudinal measures with behavioral indicators such as party support, it provides a foundation for examining how latent forms of extremism are translated into political action. Beyond extremism research, the dataset contributes to the broader study of democratic resilience, political polarization, and ideological belief structures.

Background & Summary

In recent years, political radicalism and extremism have moved from the fringes to the center of public debate across the Western world1–4. Parties and movements that reject or challenge liberal-democratic norms have gained visibility and influence, reshaping electoral competition and public discourse. While populist parties have been widely studied, their overlap with genuinely extremist organizations and supporters – those who oppose constitutional democracy itself – remains poorly understood. Addressing this gap requires not only conceptual clarity but also valid and comparable empirical tools to measure extremist attitudes across societies.

Political extremism refers to attitudes that reject the fundamental principles of the constitutional democratic state and seek to replace them with an alternative political order5,6. At its core, extremism is characterized by anti-democratic orientations that reject pluralism, political opposition, and democratic contestation as illegitimate. Extremist worldviews are typically built upon a claim to absolute truth, dogmatism, dualistic friend–foe distinctions, holistic and deterministic conceptions of society and history, identitarian constructions of the political community, and a fundamental rejection of the existing democratic order5. These characteristics are not confined to a particular ideological position but can emerge across the political spectrum, including left-wing, right-wing, and religiously motivated forms of extremism, all of which share a rejection of constitutional democracy and its core institutions.

Religious fundamentalism overlaps conceptually with political extremism in its absolutist and anti-pluralistic orientation but differs in its primary point of reference. Rather than centering on political ideology, religious fundamentalism is grounded in the belief that a single body of religious teachings constitutes the infallible truth about humanity and the divine, that this truth is threatened by forces of evil, and that these threats must be actively resisted7,8. Similar to political extremism, religious fundamentalism rejects individual autonomy and the fundamental equality of all human beings, thereby conflicting with core liberal-democratic principles.

Political radicalism, in contrast, accepts the procedural rules of democracy while opposing key normative foundations of liberal democracy9. Radicals advocate far-reaching political and societal transformation without necessarily rejecting democratic institutions themselves. Right-wing radicalism emphasizes nationalism, exclusionary conceptions of political community, and hierarchical understandings of social groups, whereas left-wing radicalism extends egalitarian principles to the point of subordinating individual liberties to collective goals and is commonly expressed through support for socialism and anti-capitalist, anti-imperialist, and anti-fascist positions6. Although radicals do not inherently reject the constitutional democratic state, extremists frequently combine anti-democratic attitudes with radical ideological commitments. Distinguishing between radicalism and extremism is therefore essential for identifying when strong ideological convictions cross the threshold into opposition to democracy itself.

Finally, we conceptualize political extremism, radicalism, and religious fundamentalism primarily as attitudinal phenomena. Although these orientations may be associated with political violence or militant action, neither violence nor illegal behavior constitutes a defining feature of these concepts. Conflating extremist attitudes with violent behavior obscures important conceptual distinctions between extremism and terrorism6,10. Moreover, these attitudinal orientations may exist both as explicit and implicit evaluations. Whereas explicit attitudes reflect consciously accessible and self-reported beliefs, implicit attitudes represent automatic evaluations that operate outside conscious awareness and may influence political judgment and behavior without deliberate reflection11.

The distinction between explicit and implicit attitudes is particularly relevant for the study of political extremism. Because anti-democratic views are often socially undesirable in democratic societies, respondents may conceal or underreport extremist beliefs in conventional surveys12. Implicit attitudes therefore provide insight into latent ideological predispositions that remain inaccessible to self-report measures while nevertheless shaping political preferences and behavior13–16. Assessing both explicit and implicit attitudes offers a more comprehensive understanding of extremist orientations and their potential mobilization under changing political opportunities or shifting social norms17.

Existing research on political extremism has relied heavily on behavioral indicators such as vote choice for radical parties or self-placement on the left-right spectrum. These measures, however, fail to capture latent extremist dispositions. Voting behavior can reflect protest, dissatisfaction, or strategic considerations rather than anti-democratic convictions18. Likewise, left-right self-placement varies in meaning across individuals, groups, and countries; respondents who consider themselves “moderate” may nonetheless endorse extremist ideas19,20. Furthermore, the boundaries of party systems and ideological labels shift over time, making behavioral and positional measures unreliable for longitudinal or cross-national comparison21–23. Identifying extremism at the attitudinal level before it manifests in political action is therefore critical for understanding processes of radicalization and democratic resilience.

Despite decades of research, the field lacks standardized, validated, and cross-nationally comparable measures of political radicalism and extremism24. Previous instruments, developed mainly in single-country studies, often conflate extremism with related but distinct constructs such as authoritarianism, prejudice, or approval of political violence25–27. Others emphasize issue-specific positions, such as racism or anti-capitalism, without addressing their relationship to democratic principles25,28. As a result, it has remained unclear whether these measures truly identify anti-democratic worldviews or merely capture strong ideological preferences.

The present dataset fills this gap by providing a theoretically grounded and empirically validated tool to measure political extremism and radicalism across six European democracies. Building on previous work17,29, the data include newly developed and psychometrically tested scales for general extremism, right-wing radicalism, and left-wing radicalism, as well as an implicit association test (IAT) for implicit extremist attitudes along with an established measure for religious fundamentalism. The conceptual framework distinguishes between a core “extremist belief system”, defined by anti-democratic and anti-pluralist attitudes, and ideological expressions on the right and left that emphasize hierarchy between nations and ethnicities or superimpose equality in socio-political context to an illiberal extreme. This two-dimensional structure allows for the systematic differentiation of radicals, extremists, and non-extremists. By combining the radicalism and extremism scales, right- and left-wing extremist attitudes can also be studied.

The data was collected in six European countries (France, Germany, Great Britain, Greece, Hungary, and the Netherlands) and the United States, to ensure variation in political culture and exposure to radical movements. The surveys include extensive socio-demographic and attitudinal variables, enabling research on the psychological, social, and political correlates of extremism. The inclusion of both explicit and implicit measures allows scholars to study the discrepancy between self-reported and latent extremist attitudes, an issue of particular importance given social desirability pressures in surveys on sensitive topics.

This resource substantially advances research on political extremism in three ways. First, it offers the first cross-nationally validated scales for left- and right-wing radicalism and general extremism in the Western world that clearly distinguish between democratic dissent and anti-democratic rejection. Second, it enables comparative analyses of the antecedents and consequences of extremist attitudes across political systems [see30]. Third, by linking attitudinal data to behavioral indicators such as party support, it provides a foundation for investigating how latent extremism translates into political action. Beyond extremism research, the dataset contributes to the broader study of democratic resilience, polarization, and ideological belief systems, offering an empirically rigorous basis for monitoring threats to liberal democracy over time.

Methods

Participants

The dataset comprises 18,432 participants from 10 surveys across 7 countries, including three pretests (one in the United States and two in Germany). In 2022, the data for the first main study were collected in Germany, Great Britain, and the Netherlands, and in 2024, the second study was conducted in Germany, France, Greece, and Hungary. Except for the pre-test in the United States (Mturk), participants were recruited through online access panels of Bilendi & respondi using a double-opt-in registration process. Participation in the panel is voluntary, and participants provided their written informed consent before the study started. For the main studies, quota sampling was applied to reach representativeness in terms of age (18 to 69 years), gender, and educational level. Muslims were oversampled in Germany and the UK in Study 1 and in France and Germany in Study 2 of the main studies, as well as in one of the pretests in Germany. This oversampling allows the comparison of radicalism, extremism, and religious fundamentalism between Muslims and non-Muslims. All pretests and main studies were administered in the respective official language of the countries. For the multi-country studies, questionnaires were prepared in English and then translated by expert translators of the survey company into the respective national languages. As survey participants were sampled via a double-opt-in online panel, this dataset cannot claim representativeness; quota sampling and weighting reduce but do not eliminate such biases31, and even probability samples now face declining response rates32. Point estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Non-probability samples nonetheless remain widespread in the social sciences33,34 and can recover relationships between variables comparably to probability samples31,35. Furthermore, compared with other online samples like MTurk, Bilendi & respondi (the provider for all studies except Pre-Test 1) achieved high data quality with respondents passing multiple attention checks at rates similar to lab samples36.

Ethics

The studies were approved by the ethics committee of the University of Mannheim to be conducted in the respective countries by Bilendi & respondi (EK Mannheim 38/2022 and 29/2024). Informed consent for participation and data sharing was retrieved from participants in written form before starting the survey.

Materials

The main purpose of the studies was the construction and validation of new scales for radical and extremist political attitudes29 and the development of an implicit association test (IAT) to capture implicit extremist attitudes17. Furthermore, the data included additional motivators of political extremism according to the “3N” approach, i.e. the individual needs, the ideological narrative, and the social network37. For individual needs, the data includes anomia, authoritarian traits, material deprivation, socio-emotional disintegration, and political alienation. For ideological narrative and social networks, the data includes regional identifiers which allow the merging of relevant variables on the meso and macro level (e.g., share of voters by various parties, numbers of migrants, unemployment rate, etc.). Additionally, the data includes measures that make the expression of extremist attitudes more or less likely (grit, self-control, social desirability tendencies). Finally, it includes related concepts like conspiracy beliefs, moral grandstanding, and political violence justification which can be used to assess the potential consequences of holding various forms of radical, extremist, or fundamentalist attitudes. General political extremism

The general political extremism scale (anti-democratic attitudes) is measured with a newly developed five-item battery (e.g., “Under some circumstances, a nondemocratic government can be preferable.” and “A concentration of power in one person guarantees order.”). For scale development purposes, the individual surveys also contain 34 additional items on general political extremism, which are not used for the final scale. Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neither agree nor disagree (3) to fully agree (5). The psychometric validity and construct validity was assessed in previous work29, indicating very good fit according to conventional cut-off criteria38.

Left-wing radicalism

The left-wing radicalism scale is measured with a newly developed six-item battery (e.g., “A decent living is only possible with socialism.” and “Fascism shows the true face of capitalism.”). For scale development purposes, the individual surveys also contain 44 additional items on left-wing radicalism, which are not used for the final scale. Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neither agree nor disagree (3) to fully agree (5). The psychometric validity and construct validity was assessed in previous work29, indicating very good fit according to conventional cut-off criteria38.

Right-wing radicalism

The right-wing radicalism scale is measured with a newly developed eight-item battery (e.g., “We should make sure we keep [nationality] pure and prevent nations mixing.” and “[Country] has become “too foreign” to a dangerous extent due to all the foreigners here.”). For scale development purposes, the individual surveys also contain 43 additional items on right-wing radicalism, which are not used for the final scale. Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neither agree nor disagree (3) to fully agree (5). The psychometric validity and construct validity was assessed in previous work29, indicating very good fit according to conventional cut-off criteria38.

Religious fundamentalism

Religious fundamentalism was captured with the 12-item scale by Altemeyer and Hunsberger8 (e.g., “God has given humanity, a complete unfailing guide to happiness and salvation, which must be totally followed.” and “When you get right down to it, there are basically two kinds of people in the world: the righteous who will be rewarded by God; and the rest who will not.).” Furthermore, there are 13 additional items capturing other aspects of religious fundamentalism. Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neither agree nor disagree (3) to fully agree (5).

Implicit political extremism

To assess implicit orientations toward political extremism, we administered a newly developed Implicit Association Test (IAT; see also17,39). The IAT measures the relative strength of automatic associations between target concepts and evaluative attributes by comparing participants’ response latencies during a series of categorization tasks40. Participants classify stimuli into paired categories using two response keys, with shorter reaction times indicating stronger underlying associative links between the combined concepts. Relative differences in response speed across compatible and incompatible category pairings provide an indirect measure of implicit attitudes that is less dependent on conscious self-report.

The IAT contrasted the target categories democratic and undemocratic, which were paired with positive and negative evaluative attributes. To operationalize the target concepts, we selected 15 terms reflecting democratic principles (e.g., “free elections”, “freedom of expression”, “rule of law”) and 13 terms representing undemocratic notions (e.g., “strong leader”, “right of the strongest”, “one-party rule”). Stimulus selection was guided by established conceptualizations of democracy and political extremism, while avoiding references to specific political actors, movements, or cultural contexts. Existing survey instruments on political extremism were additionally consulted to maximize content validity and facilitate comparisons between the implicit and explicit measures [see further41]. The evaluative stimuli were adapted from previous IAT applications42 and expanded with semantically related adjectives to better capture evaluations of democratic and anti-democratic concepts (e.g., “trustworthy” and “honorable” vs. “corrupt” and “untruthful”).

The task followed the standard seven-block IAT procedure. Participants first completed practice blocks in which they learned to categorize the target concepts and evaluative attributes separately. In the subsequent critical blocks, target and evaluative categories were combined into compatible (democratic & positive terms or undemocratic & negative words) and incompatible pairings (undemocratic & positive terms or democratic & negative words), with reaction times recorded for each trial. Implicit attitudes were quantified using the standardized D-score43, calculated from the latency difference between the two pairing conditions. Higher positive D-scores indicate relatively faster responses when extremist concepts are paired with positive attributes than when democratic concepts are paired with positive attributes, reflecting a comparatively stronger implicit preference for extremist over democratic concepts. The full procedure is explained in detail by Jungkunz et al. (2024)17.

Anomia

Anomia was captured with two separate batteries, a four-item scale by Fischer and Kohr44 (e.g., “Today, everything is changing so quickly that I often don’t know what to hold on to.”) and a four-item version of the scale developed by Srole45 (e.g., “No matter what some people say: The situation of ordinary people is not getting better, but worse”). Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neither agree nor disagree (3) to fully agree (5).

Authoritarianism

Authoritarianism was captured with a twelve-item scale46. Respondents were presented with two opposing statements, one of which represented authoritarian traits and one of which contained open-minded traits (e.g., “People who are not on my side are against me [vs.] I can accept people who are not on my side”). Response options ranged from fully agree with the statement on the left-hand side (-2) to difficult to say (0) to fully agree with the statement on the right-hand side (+2).

Political alienation

Political alienation was captured with seventeen items. Given the lack of a pre-existing scale, the items were gathered from several existing surveys (e.g., the German Longitudinal Election Study, GLES) (e.g., “Most politicians are engaged in politics simply for their own personal advantage.” and “Most parties and politicians are corrupt.”). Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neither agree nor disagree (3) to fully agree (5).

Material deprivation

For material deprivation, the data contains a five-item scale47 (e.g., “I feel deprived when I think about what I have compared to what other people like me have.” and “I feel resentful when I see how prosperous other people like me seem to be.”). Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neither agree nor disagree (3) to fully agree (5).

Socio-emotional disintegration

For socio-emotional disintegration, the data contains four items used in the group-focused enmity studies [GMF,48] (e.g., “Social relationships are becoming increasingly unstable these days.” and “Finding real friends is becoming increasingly difficult today.”). Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neither agree nor disagree (3) to fully agree (5).

Moral grandstanding

Moral grandstanding was captured with ten items49,50. These are grouped into two subdimensions: prestige-seeking grandstanding (e.g., “Even if expressing my views does not help anyone, it is important that I share them.”) and dominance-seeking grandstanding (e.g., “When I share my beliefs, I do so to show people who disagree with me that I am better than them.”). Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neutral (3) to fully agree (7).

Conspiracy beliefs

For conspiracy beliefs, the data contains a five-item scale51 (e.g., “I think that government agencies closely monitor all citizens.” and “I think that events which superficially seem to lack a connection are often the result of secret activities.”). Respondents were asked to indicate how likely the respective statements are true. Response options of all items ranged from 0%—certainly not to 100%—definitely (with ten percentage point intervals in between).

Self-control

Self-control was measured with a five-item scale52 (e.g., “I usually make up my mind through careful reasoning.” and “When I am upset I often act without thinking.”). Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neutral (3) to fully agree (5).

Grit

Grit was measured with an eight-item battery53. The battery captures two subdimensions of grit: consistency of interest (e.g., “I often set a goal but later choose to pursue a different one.”) and perseverance of effort (e.g., “I am a hard worker.”). Response options of all items ranged from fully applies to me (1) to applies to some degree to me (3) to does not apply at all to me (5).

Social desirability

The tendency for social desirability in responses to survey questions was captured with the 13-item Marlowe-Crowne Social Desirability Scale Form B54,55 (e.g., “I sometimes try to get even rather than forgive and forget.” and “I am always courteous, even to people who are disagreeable.”). Response options of all items ranged from agree (1) to disagree (2).

Political violence justification

Political violence justification was captured with two batteries. To measure a rather general support for political violence, the data include a five-item battery56 (e.g., “When politicians are damaging the country, citizens should send threats to scare them straight.”). Response options of all items ranged from fully disagree (1) to neither agree nor disagree (3) to fully agree (5). Furthermore, the data also contains six items that ask whether specific forms of political violence are acceptable57 (e.g., “Harassing a politician online in a way that makes them scared.”). Response options of all items ranged from never (1) to occationally (3) to always (5) acceptable behavior. Furthermore, there are 14 additional items capturing other aspects of political violence justification. All items are intended to be used separately (see further58,59).

Socio-demographics

All studies include additional items for political and religious identification, e.g., the left-right self-placement scale, past voting behavior and the intention to vote in the next election, religious affiliation, religiosity and support of religious organizations, as well as socio-demographic variables like age, sex, education, income, and occupation.

Regional identifiers

The data also include regional identifiers in Study 1 and Study 2. NUTS3-level identifiers are provided for France, Germany, Great Britain, Hungary, and the Netherlands. NUTS2-level identifiers are provided for Greece.

Weights

The dataset includes three types of weights to ensure representativeness according to age, gender, education, and religion (and region in Germany). First, a correction weight adjusts for the oversampling of Muslims based on their actual population shares in the respective countries [see60]. In addition, socio-structural weights account for differences in the distribution of age, gender, and education, while, for Germany, an additional adjustment corrects for regional disparities between East and West Germany. These weights were constructed using country-specific population statistics from Eurostat61,62 and the Federal Statistical Office of Germany63. Specifically, base weights were derived from these sources, and survey-specific weights were computed as the ratio of population totals to observed sample counts. The resulting weights were then merged with the survey dataset and checked for completeness.

Data processing

The data processing and harmonization were carried out systematically to ensure comparability across countries and studies and to prepare the datasets for analysis. Data import, management, cleaning, and transformation, were carried out in R64 via tidyverse65, sjlabelled66, and sjmisc67. The survey68 package was used to construct survey designs and compute weights, including raking procedures to adjust the sample to known population margins. Codebooks were created via codebookr69. Data quality checks were performed via careless70 and naniar71.

The following steps were implemented: Each dataset was loaded and, when needed, pre-processed for specific variables. If, for a study, the data were collected across different countries, the individual country datasets were merged to create a unified dataset before further processing. Several supplementary datasets were imported and prepared:

– Implicit Association Test (IAT) data: The D-Scores were calculated separately in Stata. This file was then merged with the main data sets [for the specific procedure, see17].

– Postal code and district data: German postal codes and district information were imported from official sources (German Federal Institute for Research on Building, Urban Affairs and Spatial Development (BBSR) and Deutsche Post) and harmonized for merging with survey data. For the final dataset, lower level regional identifiers were aggregated to NUTS3-level and NUTS2-level (only in Greece) regions in Study 1 and Study 2.

To construct the population weights, the following supplementary datasets were imported:

– Socio-demographic and population data: National population statistics were imported from Eurostat61,62 and the Federal Statistical Office63. Data included age, sex, education, and total population counts per country.

– Muslim population estimates: Pew Research Center data60 for the share of the Muslim population in European countries were combined with total population counts to calculate relative numbers and generate a variable which captures the share of Muslims within each country.

Afterwards, predefined sets of variables, including attitudes, socio-demographic measures, religious organization items, and country codes, were assigned harmonized value labels. Standardized coding schemes included:

– Attitude variables (1–5 or 1–7 scale)

– Socio-demographic binary indicators

– Country and country of origin codes

– Error and missing data labels

The merged survey datasets were recoded for consistency across countries and studies. Each respondent was assigned a unique ID and survey code. Attitudinal variables were recoded to harmonize naming and order across surveys. Age, sex, citizenship, birth country, educational attainment, occupation, household income, and household size were harmonized and transformed into comparable metrics across countries. Notably:

– Education levels were harmonized to ISCED 2011 categories.

– Income was converted to midpoints per category and to a numeric variable in euros, applying exchange rates for Hungary, Great Britain, and the USA. Voting behavior and party preference were recoded, handling missing values, inconsistencies, and country-specific differences.

In the data set of study 1, one duplicate was removed. The final, harmonized, and weighted datasets were saved for subsequent merging of all surveys and data analysis, ensuring reproducibility and consistency across studies.

Technical Validation

To assess the technical quality, reliability, and reusability of the dataset Radical and extremist attitudes in Europe, we conducted a series of validation procedures focusing on data completeness, attentiveness, internal consistency of measurement instruments, and the empirical validity of key constructs.

Data completeness

Data completeness was assessed at both the respondent and item level. Across studies, only fully completed questionnaires were retained in the final datasets. Completion rates were high; for example, in Study 2, 9,103 respondents completed the survey out of eligible 14,613 starters, corresponding to a completion rate of 62.29%. Duplicate observations were identified and removed by the panel provider (e.g., N = 695 in Study 2 based on identical panel IDs), ensuring that each record represents a unique respondent. One duplicate in Study 1 was removed by the researchers in the process of preparing for publication. The overall percentage of missing data across variables was 1.6% in Pretest 1, 10.3% in Pre-Test 2, 9.5% in Pre-Test 3, 6.1% in Study 1, and 4.8% in Study 2. To calculate the shares, we removed columns with design-based missings due to filter, randomization, or country-specific questions.

Attention check performance

Table 3 presents response quality checks for attentiveness, straightlining, extreme and midpoint responding, acquiescence, speeding, and long response times. Overall, there is little evidence for systematic bias. In some cases, we find higher extreme point responding (e.g., political violence justification or general extremism) but this is likely due to genuine disagreement with the quite radical or intensely worded items. The 100% extreme point responding rate for social desirability is the result of a two point scale where respondents can, by design, only answer with extreme endpoints. Across studies, we further tested for speeders (below 30% of median survey time, ntotal = 154) and laggers (more than three times the median survey time, ntotal = 912, see also Table 1). Again, there is no systematic evidence for speeding or lagging. Since there is no evidence that both affect survey quality per se, we created flag variables so that users can decide to include or remove them individually73.

Scale validations

To assess the internal consistency of the attitudinal measures, Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω coefficients were calculated for all multi-item scales (see Table 2). Across studies, most scales show acceptable to high levels of internal consistency.

Furthermore, we provide CFA (Table 4) and measurement invariance results (Table 5) for all scales that are available in the dataset. First, according to the CFA results from the pooled data, all scales show good or decent fit. Secondly, almost all scales achieve metric invariance or are only slightly above the threshold for ΔCFI but still under the threshold for ΔRMSEA. Thus, effect sizes can be compared meaningfully across countries. Scalar invariance is achieved in most cases only after freeing some intercepts (mostly one or two), indicating that these scales achieved partial scalar invariance (except authoritarianism) which is still good enough to compare means across countries75.

Construct validity of radicalism, extremism, and fundamentalism

To validate the measurement of radicalism, extremism, and religious fundamentalism, we examined correlations between key constructs and theoretically related variables (see Table 6). The observed relationships are consistent with theoretical expectations, supporting the construct validity of the measures. We first explored the distribution of key variables, including the radicalism and extremism scales (see Fig. 1). Across all countries, the distributions are characterized by a pronounced left-skew, with most respondents reporting low levels of radical and extremist attitudes and progressively fewer respondents at higher scale values. This pattern is theoretically expected, given that only a minority of the population typically holds extremist attitudes6. Importantly, the presence of a non-negligible proportion of respondents at higher scale values indicates that the measures capture meaningful variation and are not dominated by floor effects. At the same time, the absence of clustering at the upper bound suggests that ceiling effects are negligible. Differences in the upper tail of the distributions across countries further point to substantively meaningful cross-national variation, e.g., high levels of right-wing radical attitudes in Greece (16.7%) and Hungary (13.8%) relative to other countries (<9%).

The dataset includes an Implicit Association Test (IAT) to measure implicit political extremism. The IAT was implemented following established procedures, and D-scores were computed based on standardized algorithms76,77. The validation of the IAT can be reviewed in earlier work17. The implicit association test returns a standardized D-Score, which ranges from a preference of democracy over extremism (negative values) to a preference of extremism over democracy (positive values). Since there may be a variety of reasons why respondents do or do not state their true attitudes, e.g., non-awareness, social desirability, the data allow for a contrast between implicit and explicit measures. Fig. 3 displays the values of the D-Score and the index of general political extremism. Although both measures are related (r = 0.268), they also capture different parts of the population. The data points show substantial variability, but the upward trend suggests that individuals with stronger implicit biases also tend to report higher levels of explicit extremism. This is in line with other findings which show that correlations between IATs and explicit measures can vary substantially between 0.10 and 0.7041. In most cases, correlations are small to moderate, but they are generally rather low for topics with high social sensitivity, as in the case of political extremism78,79.

Table 6 presents the correlates of political radicalism, extremism, and religious fundamentalism with various potential predictors and consequences. Overall, the concepts correlate moderately in most cases, with similarities across radicalism, extremism, and fundamentalism. However, there are also some important differences which can be studied cross-nationally or among various subgroups of the populations (see also30 for a detailed analysis of commonalities and differences between radicals, extremists, and fundamentalists).

Finally, the attitudinal measures predict voting for a particular political party. Figure 4 shows the vote propensity for various party families by political radicalism and extremism scale. As expected, there is a higher likelihood to vote for far-left parties among respondents with a higher degree of left-wing radicalism and a higher propensity to vote for the far right among respondents with a higher degree of right-wing radicalism. Higher general extremism is also associated with a higher likelihood of voting for the far right but not the far left. Future research can study these insights and relationships within the individual countries and respective subgroups, or include moderators that may impact the relationship between attitudes and behavior.
