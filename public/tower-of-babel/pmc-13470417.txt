Adolescent Perceptions and Barriers for Urological Transition: STREAMWAY Qualitative Study Protocol
Authors: Mauro Van den Ende, Anne-Françoise Spinoit, Piet Hoebeke, Lien Dossche, Karsten Vanden Wyngaert, George Bou Kheir, Ellen Vandamme, Mieke Waterschoot, Ann Raes, Karel Everaert, Erik Van Laecke, François Hervé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageTransition from pediatric to adult care is a vulnerable period for adolescents and young adults with childhood lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). Patient-centered insights are needed to guide clinicians toward more effective care for adolescents with LUTS.

Introduction and hypotheses

Adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with chronic health care needs face significant challenges when transferring from pediatric to adult care services, including navigation of complex health care systems while simultaneously managing the physical, emotional, and social changes associated with adolescence [1]. In response, transition programs have been developed to support AYAs beyond the practical transfer from one health care provider to another [2]. These programs reflect a patient-centered and coordinated approach that supports AYAs through both medical and nonmedical challenges, thereby strengthening patients’ medical self-management and overall well-being [3]. During this transition period, the balance between parental guidance and personal autonomy in their care pathways shifts significantly, an important factor to consider when planning treatment.

Childhood lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) encompass a broad spectrum of voiding difficulties, incontinence, and urgency symptoms, with subclassification defined according to the International Children’s Continence Society (ICCS; Table 1) [4]. The complexity of urological conditions in childhood and adolescence requires careful consideration of both medical and psychosocial factors [5], [6]. In urological conditions, this transition can be particularly challenging owing to the intimate nature of urinary symptoms and their potential impact on quality of life, social functioning, and psychological well-being. Current literature indicates that childhood urological symptoms often persist into adulthood, with severe pediatric cases only having LUTS resolution rates of 50–60% [7]. Compared with other medical subspecialities such as pediatric cardiology and endocrinology, there remains a lack of transition guidelines in urological health care services, specifically for patients with LUTS where no neurological, congenital, or syndromal cause has been identified [8], [9]. Compared with the existing transition programs, for example, for patients with spina bifida, poorly managed transitions for functional LUTS can also result in persisting LUTS in the AYA population owing to treatment nonadherence and adverse health outcomes [4], [10]. Despite evidence on the impact of LUTS on school participation and social well-being (eg, bullying), research on the medical management of AYAs with LUTS when transferring between urological care settings is lacking [11]. Understanding the needs and challenges experienced by young people with LUTS is crucial for identifying LUTS-specific strategies to improve subjective and objective outcomes.

The Study of Transition in Real-life Experiences Affecting Micturition and Well-being in Adolescent Years (STREAMWAY) aims to map perceptions, attitudes, and experiences of AYAs who have undergone the transfer from pediatric to adult urological care. Barriers and facilitators for transition in urological care will be explored as well as their relation with treatment adherence and symptom management. This study aspires to understand the underlying mechanisms with an aim to generate foundations for future treatment recommendations, optimizing urological transition programs.

Study design

Overview

This study uses a qualitative study design, incorporating in-depth semistructured interviews suitable for interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) [12]. In addition, standardized questionnaires and demographic data are used for the description of the population and to create an overall impression of the characteristics of the patient population. This research design enables detailed exploration of how patients are making sense of their personal and social world in relation to their medical needs.

This is an exploratory, observational study examining transition experiences in AYAs with LUTS.

Eligibility criteria

Patients who meet the following criteria are eligible for inclusion: (1) LUTS (according to the ICCS definition; Table 1) present during childhood; (2) participants receiving follow-up care for LUTS from a general practitioner, urologist, pediatric urologist, or pediatric nephrologist; and (3) individuals aged 16–25 yr who have recently transitioned to adult care or are currently undergoing the transition process.

Exclusion criteria are the following: (1) patients not comfortable conducting interviews or completing questionnaires in Dutch; (2) participants with neurological conditions or congenital anatomical anomalies that may cause LUTS, given that these individuals are already followed in established transition pathways and cannot be compared with the target population; and (3) individuals with cognitive impairments that impair their capacity to independently participate in interviews or complete questionnaires

Recruitment and center details

Currently, the follow-up of AYA patients with functional LUTS in Belgium varies according to local protocols without any structured transition protocol that applies. Both pediatric caregivers and adult urologists encounter these patients, and upon individual request of the patient, regardless of age, a transfer of care between caregivers during adolescence can occur. Therefore, patient recruitment in this study is performed by pediatric urologists, adult urologists, and pediatric nephrologists from the departments of urology and pediatric nephrology of Ghent University Hospital, an European Reference Network - Rare Congenital Uro-Recto-Genital Diseases & Complex Conditions (ERN eUROGEN)–accredited center in Flanders, Belgium. Additional recruitment occurs through lecture evenings organized by Ghent University Hospital for peripheral health care providers involved in the care of AYAs with LUTS, where contact details of eligible participants are forwarded to the trial coordinator of Ghent University Hospital. All eligible participants are enrolled at Ghent University Hospital.

At the initial visit, before enrolment, AYAs with LUTS undergo a complete diagnostic workup as part of standard care to determine the presence of LUTS at the time of consultation. Participants are informed about the study by their consulting health care professional with a study leaflet and contacted by a researcher for an introductory meeting. During this meeting, study objectives and procedures are explained in detail, and a written patient information sheet is distributed.

If the participant is willing to participate, written informed consent is obtained. For minors, this includes an informed consent form from the parent or legal guardian and an informed assent form adapted to the participant’s age. For adult participants (≥18 yr), informed consent is obtained directly from the participant. Once the informed consent process is completed by all required parties, each participant is assigned a unique pseudonymization code. An in-depth interview is then scheduled within 3 mo of the initial study contact.

Protocol overview

This is a single-cohort observational study, and a convenience sampling strategy is used.

Measurement

Participants receive the following assignments to complete before the in-depth interview:•Urological timeline completion: participants complete a blank timeline representing their urological care pathway from birth to the time of the in-depth interview (Supplementary Appendices 1 and 2). All health care contacts related to urological symptoms are documented, including consultations and hospitalizations. Ideally, the timeline is completed together with a parent or guardian to ensure no part of the care pathway is overlooked. The completed timeline is brought to the in-depth interview, where a new timeline, containing only the participant’s pseudonymization code, is created together with the researcher to ensure confidentiality.•Growth curve collection: participants are asked to obtain growth curves from birth to puberty (available through the digital platform of “Kind en Gezin,” a Flemish government agency providing mandatory preventive family support services and maintaining standardized growth curves for children in Flanders and Brussels). These growth curves are also brought to the in-depth interview and pseudonymized.

Data collection

Demographic data

The demographic data from each participant are derived from the electronic patient medical record.

In-depth interviews

In-depth interviews follow a semistructured format and are conducted by a single researcher to ensure consistency. A piloted, multidisciplinary-approved interview guide is used to pose open-ended questions exploring AYAs’ transition experiences, enabling IPA (Supplementary Table 1). Participants are encouraged to respond freely and are informed that they may discontinue the interview at any time. Interviews last ∼60 min and can take place at the hospital, at another preferred location, or via video call to enhance accessibility and reduce participation bias. As described in the informed consent and assent forms, both in-person and video interviews are audio recorded. Recordings are subsequently transcribed and coded in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation. To protect confidentiality, audio and video recordings are deleted immediately after transcription.

The interview guide covers several key thematic domains.

The first domain is introductory questions, which includes exploration of AYAs’ general perceptions of pediatric and adult care, including their differences.

The second domain is core questions, which focuses on AYAs’ personal experiences with urinary symptoms during childhood, guided by the precompleted timeline to review health care contacts and provider interactions. This section examines the impact of urinary symptoms on daily life, including school participation, social relationships, and experiences of bullying or stigma. Associated conditions such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorders, constipation, and genitourinary pain are also discussed.

The third domain is developmental and pubertal experiences, which includes reflection on pubertal development, including age at menarche (for females) and growth patterns. Pubertal timing is assessed using developmental charts, images, and self-reported comparisons with peers regarding growth rate, breast development for females, and body hair development. The interview further explores how symptoms have evolved over time and how they currently affect daily functioning and sleep.

The fourth domain is transition-specific questions, which includes AYAs’ experiences with, or expectations of, transitional care, including types of health care providers involved, information received, and follow-up arrangements. AYAs are also invited to describe their ideal transition process and articulate their needs and preferences.

The fifth domain is contextual and barrier assessment, which includes exploration of broader contextual factors that may influence health care access and experiences, including family dynamics, financial considerations, and other potential barriers to adequate urological care.

Safety criteria and reporting

This is a minimal-risk observational study involving interviews and questionnaires. Although no serious adverse events are expected, the sensitive nature of urological symptoms and the inclusion of questions about adverse childhood experiences may cause emotional discomfort for some participants.

During the consent process, all participants are informed that they may skip any questions they find uncomfortable and may withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. If a participant becomes distressed during the interview, the researcher will pause or terminate the interview and provide appropriate support, including referral to psychological services when necessary. Any instances of participant distress, early termination, or withdrawal owing to emotional discomfort will be documented and reported to the principal investigator (PI) and, when appropriate, to the hospital’s ethics committee as per institutional guidelines.

Data management and confidentiality

Data are entered manually in an online firewall-protected data registry on the password-encrypted server of Ghent University. Subsequently, data are stored in a central IBM SPSS version 11 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) database, also protected by the institutional firewall. Pseudonymized qualitative and quantitative data are registered in separate datasets, accessible to the PI, trial coordinator, and coinvestigators. Only the trial coordinator has access to a key file with pseudonymization codes linked to patient data.

Statistical analysis

Sample size determination

The sample size is guided by methodological literature on qualitative research. According to Guest et al. [13], thematic saturation can often be achieved within the first 12 interviews, with basic themes emerging as early as six. Braun and Clarke [14] similarly suggest that a sample of 6–15 participants is appropriate for small-scale qualitative studies using semistructured interviews. Given the focused nature of the research topic and anticipated heterogeneity of the participant group, a minimal sample size of 20 participants is considered sufficient to achieve data saturation and provide rich, in-depth data. Data saturation is defined as the point when four consecutive in-depth interviews reveal no additional insights into the primary study end points. In addition to this qualitative saturation threshold, demographic data will undergo expert validation to ensure that the participant group is representative of the broader population. This dual approach strengthens the credibility and completeness of the findings. Interim analyses are conducted throughout the study, and the sample size may be expanded through protocol amendment based on result variability. Conversely, recruitment may be concluded early if sufficient data are available and data saturation is achieved.

Outcomes and end points

Primary end point

The primary end point is to map the perceptions, attitudes, and experiences of AYAs who have undergone the transition from pediatric to adult urological care. IPA enables a detailed examination of the patient’s life world and attempts to explore personal experience during transition care. These insights will be derived from in-depth semistructured interviews and will contribute to improving transition programs in urological care and may be used to optimize care delivery for future AYAs.

Secondary end points

The secondary endpoint is to assess urinary symptoms and create a broad picture of other potentially associated complaints, including constipation, sleep problems, quality of life, genitourinary pain, and adverse childhood experiences, by administering standardized questionnaires. Demographic and symptom descriptions at the moment of the interview are reported.

Analysis plan

Qualitative data analysis

Qualitative data are analyzed through thematic coding and interpretative analysis to uncover key themes and insights, directly addressing the primary endpoint of this study. Analysis is supported by NVivo version 12 or higher and follows the six-phase approach by Braun and Clarke [14]:1.Familiarization with data: reading and rereading interview transcripts to become familiar with the content.2.Initial code generation: systematic coding of interesting features across the entire dataset.3.Theme identification: collating codes into potential themes and gathering relevant data for each theme.4.Theme review: checking themes against coded extracts and the entire dataset.5.Theme definition and naming: ongoing analysis to refine themes and generate clear definitions, including expert validation sessions.6.Report production: final analysis and selection of compelling extract examples.

Experiences are compared across demographic groups (age, sex, and symptom severity) to identify patterns and variations in transition experiences.

Aside from thematic coding, a phenomenological approach to interpretative analysis also involves a detailed examination of the participant’s lived experiences. A two-stage interpretation process or a double hermeneutic is involved where both participants are trying to make sense of their world, and the researcher is trying to make sense of the participants trying to make sense of their world [12].

Quantitative data analysis

All quantitative analyses are conducted using SPSS version 31.0.

Urological timeline data are screened to address the potential recall bias of the AYA participant and provide an overview of the frequency of health care contacts per year by provider type, duration of care episodes and gaps in care, and transition timing with associated health care use changes (if applicable).

Standardized questionnaire data are analyzed to create a holistic picture of associated symptoms and their relationships. Descriptive statistics include means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Caution needs to be made that no conclusions on temporality or causality are possible based on these cross-sectional data [15].

Growth curves are used to identify patterns in physical development that may correlate with urinary symptom onset or changes. Individual growth trajectories are plotted against standardized growth charts to identify periods of accelerated or delayed growth, adjusted for participants with different ethnic backgrounds or premature births. Potential critical periods, in which growth patterns coincide with symptom changes or health care use patterns, are identified.

Context and limitations

Epidemiologic data indicate that 3–16% of children aged 5–19 yr experience enuresis or LUTS, with 1–2% of cases persisting into adulthood [7]. A substantial proportion of these patients are lost to follow-up during adolescence, largely owing to assumptions of spontaneous symptom resolution. However, this assumption is not adequately supported by evidence, and the absence of comprehensive transitional programs for childhood LUTS risks adverse outcomes for young adults with continued bothersome symptoms [1]. A recent systematic review on transitional urology confirmed that the lack of standardized protocols and coordinated care has resulted in poor treatment adherence, increased complications, and gaps in long-term management, whereas structured programs incorporating dedicated transition coordinators and multidisciplinary collaboration were associated with improved follow-up retention and fewer emergency visits [16]. Despite this emerging evidence, current literature reports little consensus on appropriate transitional care specifically for functional LUTS. A review on enuresis further found that most pediatric studies were authored by pediatricians and adult studies by urologists, with general practitioners largely absent from both—a pattern that mirrors local clinical practice and underscores the need for multidisciplinary and first-line care collaboration in transitional urology [17]. Therefore, the primary and secondary outcomes of this study protocol aim to provide a patient-centered starting point for the development of transition care recommendations, grounded in the perceptions and lived experiences of AYAs with functional LUTS.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. Although a sample size of ∼20 participants is methodologically justified by established thematic saturation criteria, questions may arise regarding the generalizability of findings to the broader AYA population with persistent functional LUTS. This qualitative study aims to provide a rich summation of lived experiences, but caution is warranted when interpreting these findings as representative of the whole population. The single-center design at Ghent University Hospital, combined with convenience sampling, means that not all patient subgroups could be captured and therefore study conclusions should be understood as hypothesis generating rather than broadly generalizable. Findings can serve as a foundation for the adaptation of current clinical transitional care services rather than definitive recommendations applicable across all settings. Recall bias during urological timeline completion and in-depth interviews may additionally limit the comprehensiveness of reconstructed care pathways and personal experiences. Finally, the exclusion of patients with neurological, congenital, or syndromal causes of LUTS may limit the applicability of results to the wider AYA urological population. However, given that the perceptions and lived experiences of patients with functional LUTS may differ substantially from those managed within established transition pathways, this population represents an important and distinct focus warranting dedicated investigation.

Summary

AYAs with LUTS face significant challenges during the transition from pediatric to adult urological care. This transition period is often marked by disruptions in care continuity, treatment adherence, and psychosocial stress, but remains understudied in urology. STREAMWAY seeks to address this gap by mapping perceptions, attitudes, and lived experiences of adolescents during this critical phase. This exploratory, qualitative study uses semistructured in-depth interviews to engage in dialogue with study participants. Standardized validated questionnaires are used for patient population description. Participants aged 16–25 yr with childhood LUTS who are currently undergoing or have recently completed transition to adult care are recruited. Language proficiency is required, and neurological conditions, congenital abnormalities predisposing to persistent LUTS, and cognitive impairments are excluded. The primary end point is to map the perceptions, attitudes, and life-world experiences of adolescents who have undergone the transition from pediatric to adult urological care, identifying barriers and facilitators to successful transition. Secondary end points include assessment of current urinary symptoms, sleep quality, quality of life, bowel problems, genitourinary pain, and adverse childhood experiences through standardized questionnaires. Approximately 20 participants will be enrolled. Data collection includes preinterview completion of urological care timelines and growth curves, followed by 60-min semistructured interviews and administration of validated questionnaires (ICIQ-MLUTS/FLUTS, ICIQ-LUTSqol, PSQI, EQ-5D-5L, ACE-IQ, CCCS, and GUPI). Qualitative data are analyzed using thematic coding and IPA, and quantitative data are analyzed using descriptive statistics. Results are integrated to identify discrepancies between qualitative and quantitative findings.
