Digit expansions in rational and algebraic basis Journal: Discrete Mathematics
Lucía Rossi

Abstract
Consider α ∈ ℚ ⁡ ( i ) \alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(i) satisfying | α | > 1 |\alpha|>1 . Let 𝒟 = { 0 , 1 , … , | a 0 | − 1 } \mathcal{D}=\{0,1,\ldots,|a_{0}|-1\} , where a 0 a_{0} is the independent coefficient of the minimal primitive polynomial of α \alpha . We introduce a way of expanding complex numbers in base α \alpha with digits in 𝒟 \mathcal{D} that we call α \alpha -expansions, which generalize rational base number systems introduced in ( Akiyama et al. 2008 ) and are related to rational self-affine tiles introduced in ( Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015 ) . We define an algorithm to obtain the expansions for certain Gaussian integers and show results on the language. We then extend the expansions to all x ∈ ℂ x\in\mathbb{C} (or x ∈ ℝ x\in\mathbb{R} when α = a b ∈ ℚ \alpha=\tfrac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{Q} , the rational case will be our starting point) and show that they are unique almost everywhere. We relate them to tilings of the complex plane. We characterize α \alpha -expansions in terms of p p -adic completions of ℚ ⁡ ( i ) \mathbb{Q}(i) with respect to Gaussian primes.



1
Introduction.

The study of rational and algebraic digit systems can be motivated by the following question: can we define something analogous to the decimal system where the base is a rational or an algebraic number? A desirable property of a number system is that almost all numbers (in a measure-theoretic sense) can be expanded uniquely.

In
(
Kempner 1936
)
and
(
Rényi 1957
)
, expansions with respect to non-integral real bases are considered. A straightforward way to expand a real number in a non-integer base is through the greedy algorithm, as is the case for
\beta
-expansion introduced in
(
Parry 1960
)
(see also
(
Sidorov 2003
)
). One famous complex numeration system has base
-1+i
and digits
\{0,1\}
, introduced in
(
Penney 1965
)
, related to the Twin Dragon introduced in
(
Knuth 1998
)
. The golden ratio is another famous base that gives rise to binary expansions avoiding the digit sequence
11
. The examples above are all algebraic integers, that is, roots of monic integer polynomials. We want to consider algebraic bases that are not algebraic integers.

Akiyama, Frougny and Sakarovitch
(
Akiyama et al. 2008
)
studied representations of numbers in a rational base
\tfrac{a}{b}
where
a>b\geq 2
are coprime integers, using the digits
\{0,1,\ldots,a-1\}
. These representations are obtained through the so-called
modified division algorithm
, which produces less significant digits first. In particular, they obtain a unique expansion for non-negative integers. Morgenbesser, Steiner, and Thuswaldner showed patterns in these number systems in
(
Morgenbesser et al. 2013
)
related to uniform distribution of digits. In the first section of this paper, we will recall rational base number systems, relate them to
p
-adic numbers, and use them as a starting point to define algebraic number systems.

Digit systems are very often linked to self-affine sets.
Rational self-affine tiles
were introduced in
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
)
for algebraic bases by defining a certain non-Euclidean representation space in terms of
\mathfrak{p}
-adic completions of algebraic number fields with respect to prime ideals. We revisit some results of this paper (which is of great depth and complexity) and reframe some of their proofs in our setting. The article
(
Rossi and Thuswaldner 2022
)
serves as an introduction to the topic, as it centers around the particular example of the rational number system with base
-\frac{3}{2}
and digits
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1,2\}
using the representation space
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Q}_{2}
; Donald Knuth called the elements of this space
ambinumbers
in
(
Knuth 2022
)
, where he continued the study of the
-\frac{3}{2}
example. In
(
Rossi et al. 2023
)
, the authors generalize rational self-affine tiles in two directions: they consider basis that are expanding matrices with rational entries, and varying digit sets; the
p
-adic completions are replaced by projective limits.

The central idea of this paper is to consider digit expansions of complex numbers in base
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(i)\setminus\mathbb{R}
. We assume that
|\alpha|>1
. The digit set
\mathcal{D}
is of the form
\{0,1,\ldots,|a_{0}|-1\}
, where
a_{0}
is the independent coefficient of the minimal primitive polynomial
P_{\alpha}
of
\alpha
. We introduce a lattice
\Lambda_{\alpha}
in the complex plane and the so-called integer
\alpha
-expansions, which we will afterwards extend to the whole complex plane and relate to tilings and
p
-adic completions to show uniqueness almost everywhere, as well as results regarding the language of the expansions.

1.1
Outline and main results

In Section
2
, we revisit rational number systems introduced in
(
Akiyama et al. 2008
)
, we extend their algorithm to negative bases and characterize expansions in terms of
p
-adic completions.

In Section
3
, we introduce integer
\alpha
-expansions in base
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(i)\setminus\mathbb{R}
for points in the lattice
\Lambda_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]\cap\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{-1}]
through the backward division map
T_{\alpha}:\Lambda_{\alpha}\rightarrow\Lambda_{\alpha},N\mapsto\frac{N-d}{\alpha}
where
d\in\mathcal{D}
is uniquely determined so that
\frac{N-d}{\alpha}\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
.

The first issue we encounter is that this algorithm may not terminate, producing an expansion that is infinite to the left. In Section
4
, we show that this question is analogous to determining the finiteness property of certain shift radix systems, which is well studied, and provide an algorithm to determine this property for a given base
\alpha
.

An interesting aspect of algebraic number systems is the special shape of the expansions. In section
5
, we depict them as paths on trees and show results on the language
L_{\alpha}
of these expansions.

In Theorem
2
we show the following: let
L^{k}_{\alpha}
be the language of all integer
\alpha
-expansions of length
k
. Then each word in
L^{k}_{\alpha}
can be extended to the right to a word in
L^{k+1}_{\alpha}
by all digits
d\in\mathcal{D}
that belong to exactly one residue class modulo
a_{2}
, where
a_{2}
is the leading coefficient of
P_{\alpha}
. Consequently,
\#L_{\alpha}^{k}\leq\left\lceil\frac{|\mathcal{D}|}{a_{2}}\right\rceil^{k}
. Afterwards, we show that the length of the integer
\alpha
-expansion of
N
behaves like
\log_{|\alpha|}(|N|)+\mathcal{O}(1)
. We give algorithms for the addition and multiplication of integer
\alpha
-expansions.

The next natural step is to extend the expansions to all complex numbers; we call them
\alpha
-expansions. Their language corresponds to words in
D^{\omega}
whose prefixes are in
L_{\alpha}
. We introduce them in Section
6
, and show how to approximate the expansion of
x\in\mathbb{C}
to any desired precision.

In Section
7
, we represent
\alpha
-expansions through a family of tiles with fractal boundaries and show in Proposition
6
that they yield a tiling of
\mathbb{C}
(this is a consequence of
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
, Theorem 3)
). This will be used to show our main result, stated in Theorem
3
: that
\alpha
-expansions of complex numbers are unique almost everywhere with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

In Section
8
, we consider
p
-adic completions of the field
\mathbb{Q}(i)
where
p
is a Gaussian prime. In Theorem
4
we show the following: write
\alpha=\frac{num(\alpha)}{den(\alpha)}
where
num(\alpha),den(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
have no common Gaussian prime divisors. Then, an expansion

x=\sum_{j\leq k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}\qquad(d_{j}\in\mathcal{D})

(1)

is an
\alpha
-expansion of
x
if and only if it converges to
0
in
K_{p}
for every Gaussian prime
p
dividing
den(\alpha)
.
We consider pairs in a space that we denote
\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
, and define
\alpha
-expansions of points in this space. We obtain uniqueness almost everywhere.

At the end of the paper, we state some generalizations and pose some open questions. We mention that the restriction that
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(i)
is mostly done for simplicity and that one could consider a quadratic algebraic number instead; in that case,
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
may not be a unique factorization domain, so the completions would be defined in terms of prime ideals.

2
Rational base number systems

Let
\tfrac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{Q}
with
a
and
b
coprime integers and
a>b\geq 2
. Consider the digit set
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\ldots,a-1\}
. Akiyama, Frougny and Sakarovitch
(
Akiyama et al. 2008
)
introduced the following modified division algorithm.

Write
N=N_{0}
and define
N_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
by

bN_{j}=aN_{j+1}+d_{j},

(2)

where
d_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
is the unique digit satisfying
d_{j}\equiv bN_{j}\mod{a}
. Induction yields

N=\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{j+1}N_{j+1}+\tfrac{d_{j}}{b}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{j}+\cdots+\tfrac{d_{1}}{b}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)+\tfrac{d_{0}}{b}.

(3)

Since
N_{j+1}<N_{j}
, eventually
N_{k+1}=0
for some minimal
k
. Then

N=\frac{1}{b}\left(d_{k}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{k}+d_{k-1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{k-1}+\cdots+d_{1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)+d_{0}\right)\qquad(d_{j}\in\mathcal{D})

(4)

with
d_{k}\neq 0
.

We consider an analogous expansion for negative bases and show that the algorithm ends.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\tfrac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
\tfrac{a}{b}<0
with
-a>b>2
, and
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\ldots,|a|-1\}
. Let
N=N_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then the sequence
(N_{j})_{j\geq 0}
obtained through (
2
) is eventually zero.

Proof.

For every
j\geq 0
, we have

|N_{j+1}|=\Bigg|\frac{b}{a}N_{j}-\frac{d_{j}}{a}\Bigg|\leq\left|\frac{b}{a}\right||N_{j}|+\left|\frac{d_{j}}{a}\right|.

(5)

Since
|\frac{b}{a}|<1
and
d_{j}<|a|
, it follows that
|N_{j+1}|<|N_{j}|+1,
thus
(|N_{j}|)_{j\geq 0}
is bounded and non-increasing. Suppose that
|N_{j+1}|=|N_{j}|
for some
j
. This implies that either
N_{j+1}=N_{j}
or
N_{j+1}=-N_{j}
.
If
N_{j+1}=N_{j}
, then

bN_{j}=aN_{j}+d_{j},\quad\mbox{so}\quad(b-a)N_{j}=d_{j}.

Since
b-a=b+|a|>d_{j}
, we must have
N_{j}=d_{j}=0
.

If
N_{j+1}=-N_{j}
, then

bN_{j}=-aN_{j}+d_{j},\quad\mbox{so}\quad N_{j}=\frac{d_{j}}{a+b}.

Moreover,
d_{j}\equiv bN_{j}\mod{a}
. On the next step of the iteration, we have

bN_{j+1}=aN_{j+2}+d_{j+1}.

Substituting
bN_{j+1}=-bN_{j}
, we find
d_{j+1}\equiv-bN_{j}\mod{a}.
Since
d_{j},d_{j+1}\in\{0,\ldots,|a|-1\}
and
a<0
, it follows that
d_{j+1}=-a-d_{j}.
Substituting
bN_{j+1}=-bN_{j}=-b\frac{d_{j}}{a+b}
into the recurrence, we have

-b\frac{d_{j}}{a+b}=aN_{j+2}-a-d_{j},

which simplifies to

N_{j+2}=1+\frac{d_{j}}{a+b}=1+N_{j}.

Note that
N_{j}<0
, so it follows that

|N_{j+2}|=|N_{j}+1|<|N_{j}|.

Thus
(N_{j})_{j\geq 0}
is eventually zero.
∎

We are going to slightly rephrase their algorithm to understand these expansions better. If we consider
N=N_{0}\in b\mathbb{Z}
and remove the preceding
\frac{1}{b}
factor, then the expansions obtained are the same: define
N_{j}\in b\mathbb{Z}
for
j\geq 0
satisfying

N_{j}=\tfrac{a}{b}N_{j+1}+d_{j}

with
d_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
. In other words, we are considering the backward division map

T_{\tfrac{a}{b}}:b\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow b\mathbb{Z},\quad N\mapsto\frac{b}{a}(N-d)

where
d\in\mathcal{D}
is the unique digit such that
\frac{b}{a}(N-d)\in b\mathbb{Z}
. By restricting this map to the lattice
b\mathbb{Z}
instead of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{a}{b}]
, we obtain a unique expansion

N=d_{k}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{k}+d_{k-1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{k-1}+\cdots+d_{1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)+d_{0}\in b\mathbb{Z}.

(6)

We called it the integer
\tfrac{a}{b}
-expansion of
N
.

The expansion can be extended to real numbers.

Definition 1
.

Let
\tfrac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{Q}
with
a
and
b
coprime integers,
|a|\geq b\geq 2
, and
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\ldots,a-1\}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\frac{a}{b}>0
, assume
x>0
. An
\tfrac{a}{b}
-expansion of
x
is an expansion of the form

x=d_{k}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{k}+\cdots+d_{1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)+d_{0}+d_{-1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-1}+\cdots\qquad(d_{j}\in\mathcal{D})

(7)

with
d_{k}\neq 0
such that
(d_{k}\ldots d_{l})_{a/b}
corresponds to the integer
\tfrac{a}{b}
-expansion of some
N\in b\mathbb{Z}
for every
l\leq k
. We denote it

x=(d_{k}\ldots d_{0}.d_{-1}d_{-2}\ldots)_{a/b}.

It follows from
(
Akiyama et al. 2008
, Theorem 36)
that the
\tfrac{a}{b}
expansion of reals is unique almost everywhere for positive
\tfrac{a}{b}
, and the negative case can be deduced along similar lines.

Our next step is to characterize the
\tfrac{a}{b}
expansions of reals in terms of
p
-adic completions, following
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
)
. For more on the topic of
p
-adic numbers, we refer the reader to
(
Holly 2001
)
. We denote by
\nu_{p}
the
p
-adic valuation on
\mathbb{Q}
and by
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
the corresponding
p
-adic field.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\tfrac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\ldots,a-1\}
as before. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\frac{a}{b}>0
, assume
x>0
. A series

d_{k}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{k}+\cdots+d_{1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)+d_{0}+d_{-1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-1}+\cdots\qquad(d_{j}\in\mathcal{D})

(8)

converging to
x
in
\mathbb{C}
is an
\tfrac{a}{b}
-expansion of
x
if and only if it converges to
0
in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
for every prime
p
dividing
b
.

Proof.

Let
l\in\mathbb{Z}
so that
l\leq k
. The sum of the first
k-l
terms of the series (
8
) is

S_{l}=d_{k}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{k}+\cdots+d_{l+1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{l+1}+d_{l}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{l}.

Write

N_{l}=\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-l}S_{l}=d_{k}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{k-l}+\cdots+d_{l+1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)+d_{l}.

Assume that (
8
) is an
\tfrac{a}{b}
-expansion of
x
, then
N_{l}=\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-l}S_{l}\in b\mathbb{Z}
. Let
p
be a prime dividing
b
, then
\nu_{p}(S_{l})\geq-l+1
. The remaining tail of the series (
8
) is given by

d_{l-1}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{l-1}+d_{l-2}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{l-2}+\cdots

Note that
\nu_{p}(d_{t}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{t})\geq-l+1
for every
t\leq l-1
. Denote by
L_{p}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
to the limit of (
8
) in
\mathbb{Q}(p)
, then
\nu_{p}(L_{p})\geq-l+1
. Hence, letting
l\to-\infty
, the series (
8
) converges to
0
in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

For the converse, suppose there exists some
l
such that
N_{l}\notin b\mathbb{Z}
. Then, there exists some prime
p
dividing
b
and
r\geq 1
such that
p^{r}
divides
b
and
p^{r}
does not divide
N_{l}
. Then
\nu_{p}(S_{l})<r(-l+1)
. It holds that
\nu_{p}(d_{t}\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{t})\geq r(-l+1)
for every
t\leq l-1
, so
\nu_{p}(L_{p})<r(-l+1)
and therefore
L_{p}\neq 0
, that is, the series does not converge to
0
in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
∎

In the upcoming sections, we will generalize these expansions to the complex plane, where one can visualize them through tilings.

3
Algebraic number systems

Consider a Gaussian rational
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(i)
with
|\alpha|>1
and
\alpha\notin\mathbb{R}
. Then
\alpha
is a quadratic algebraic number, and is a root of

P_{\alpha}(X)=a_{2}X^{2}+a_{1}X+a_{0}

such that
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
are coprime, and assume
a_{2}>0
. We call
P_{\alpha}
the
minimal primitive polynomial
of
\alpha
.

Consider the set
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\ldots,|a_{0}|-1\}
. We call the pair
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
an
algebraic number system
. The set
\mathcal{D}
is a complete set of residues of
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
modulo
\alpha
; this is known in the literature as a standard digit set.

An
expansion
of
x\in\mathbb{C}
in base
\alpha
with digits in
\mathcal{D}
(also known as a representation) is an infinite sequence
(d_{j})_{j\leq k}
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
d_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
such that

x=\sum_{j\leq k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}.

(9)

When
d_{j}=0
for all
j<0
, the expansion is said to be an
integer expansion
. When
d_{j}=0
for all
j\geq 0
, it is said to be a
fractional expansion
.

Given a number system
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
and a sequence
(d_{j})_{j\leq k}
, we define the
evaluation map

\pi_{\alpha}
as

\pi_{\alpha}((d_{j})_{j\leq k})=\sum_{j\leq k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}.

We have seen that rational number systems arise by restricting the backward division map to a lattice. We will generalize this idea after giving some definitions and results.

Definition 2
.

Let

\Lambda_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]\cap\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{-1}],

with the structure of a
\mathbb{Z}
-module of
\mathbb{Q}(i)
.

We now give a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis for
\Lambda_{\alpha}.

Lemma 1
.

Let
P_{\alpha}(X)=a_{2}X^{2}+a_{1}X+a_{0}
be the minimal primitive polynomial of
\alpha
. The
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\Lambda_{\alpha}
is a lattice in
\mathbb{C}
that can be expressed as

\Lambda_{\alpha}=a_{2}\mathbb{Z}+(a_{2}\alpha+a_{1})\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

See
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
, Lemma 6.14)
.
∎

The set

\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}=\{a_{2},a_{2}\alpha+a_{1}\}

is known as the
Brunotte basis
of
\Lambda_{\alpha}
. This lattice will be key in the definition of our algorithm.

Lemma 2
.

\mathcal{D}=\{0,1,\ldots,|a_{0}|-1\}
is a complete set of residues of
\Lambda_{\alpha}/\alpha\Lambda_{\alpha}.
Consequently, the backward division map
T_{\alpha}
in
\Lambda_{\alpha}
given by

T_{\alpha}:\Lambda_{\alpha}\rightarrow\Lambda_{\alpha},\qquad x\mapsto\frac{x-d}{\alpha},

(10)

is well defined, where
d
is the unique element of
\mathcal{D}
such that
T_{\alpha}(x)\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
be arbitrary. Then

x=u\,a_{2}+v(a_{2}\alpha+a_{1})

for unique
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}
. There are unique
w\in\mathbb{Z}
and
d\in\mathcal{D}
such that
u\,a_{2}+v\,a_{1}=w\,a_{0}+d
. This yields

\begin{split}x&=v\,a_{2}\,\alpha+w\,a_{0}+d\\
&=v\,a_{2}\,\alpha+w\,(-a_{2}\,\alpha^{2}-a_{1}\,\alpha)+d\\
&=\alpha\,(v\,a_{2}-w\,(a_{2}\,\alpha+a_{1}))+d\end{split}

so
T_{\alpha}(x)=v\,a_{2}-w(a_{2}\,\alpha+a_{1})\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
.
∎

The following algorithm will yield the central definition of this paper.

Algorithm 1
.

Consider an algebraic number system
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
. Let
N=N_{0}\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
and define
N_{j}\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
for
j\geqslant 0
by

N_{j}=\alpha N_{j+1}+d_{j}

(11)

with
d_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
uniquely defined by Lemma
2
because
N_{j+1}=T_{\alpha}(N_{j})
.

Iteration of (
11
) yields

N=\alpha^{j+1}N_{j+1}+d_{j}\alpha^{j}+\cdots+d_{1}\alpha+d_{0}.

(12)

Definition 3
.

Suppose that the sequence
(N_{j})_{j\geq 0}
defined in (
11
) satisfies
N_{k+1}=0
for some minimal
k
. Then

N=d_{k}\alpha^{k}+d_{k-1}\alpha^{k-1}+\cdots+d_{1}\alpha+d_{0}\qquad(d_{j}\in\mathcal{D}),

(13)

with
d_{k}\neq 0
. We call this an
integer
\alpha
-expansion
of
N
, denoted

N=(d_{k}\ldots d_{0})_{\alpha}.

We note

\langle N\rangle_{\alpha}=d_{k}\ldots d_{1}d_{0}.

The expansion of zero is the empty word
\varepsilon
. We proceed to show a couple of examples.

Example 1
.

Let
\alpha=\frac{-1+3i}{2}
, root of
P_{\alpha}(X)=2X^{2}+2X+5
, and
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1,2,3,4\}
. The Brunotte basis for
\Lambda_{\alpha}
is
\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}=\{2,1+3i\}
.

Let
N=1+3i\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
. Then

1+3i=\frac{-1+3i}{2}\,2+2

so
N_{1}=2\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
and
d_{0}=2
. The next iteration is

2=\frac{-1+3i}{2}\,0+2

so
d_{1}=2
and
N_{2}=0
, and since we reached
0

1+3i=(22)_{\alpha}

Example 2

Let

, root of
P_{\alpha}(X)=9x^{2}-2x+13

. The lattice

\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}=\{9,-9+6i\}

Let
6i\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
. We have

6i=\frac{3+2i}{3}\,6i+4,

and therefore
N_{j}=6i
and
d_{j}=4
for all
j\geq 0
, and we do not obtain an integer
\alpha
-expansion (instead, we would get infinitely many
4
’s to the left).

In the next section, we will show how to determine whether the algorithm always stops for a given base.

4
Finiteness property and shift radix systems

Consider an algebraic number system
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
. We are only interested in bases
\alpha
where there exists an integer
\alpha
-expansion of
N
for every
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha},
and we want to determine when this happens. We now tackle this issue and relate it to a well-studied problem.

Definition 4
.

We say that
\alpha
has the
finiteness property
in
\Lambda_{\alpha}
if, for every
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
, the sequence
(N_{j})_{j\geq 0}
defined by (
11
) satisfies
N_{j}=0
for some
j\geq 0
.

The previous example shows that
\alpha=\frac{3+2i}{3}
does not have the finiteness property in
\Lambda_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
\alpha
has the finiteness property. Then every
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
has a unique integer
\alpha
-expansion.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition: if
\alpha
has the finiteness property, then an integer
\alpha
-expansion of
N
exists for every
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
, and the digits
d_{j}
are chosen uniquely through the formula (
11
).
∎

There is no simple characterization for the bases
\alpha
where the finiteness property holds in
\Lambda_{\alpha}
. This question is analogous to determining the finiteness property of a family of dynamical systems known as shift radix systems. We will show that the backward division map
T_{\alpha}
restricted to
\Lambda_{\alpha}
is conjugate to an almost linear map defined in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Given the Brunotte basis
\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}=\{a_{2},a_{2}\alpha+a_{1}\}
of
\Lambda_{\alpha}
, consider the map

\iota_{\alpha}:\mathbb{Q}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}(i),\qquad(z_{0},z_{1})\mapsto\mbox{sgn}(a_{0})(z_{0}a_{2}+z_{1}(a_{2}\alpha+a_{1})).

(14)

Then
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
if and only if
\iota_{\alpha}(z)\in\Lambda_{\alpha}.

Given
r\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a
shift radix system
is a map of the form

\tau_{r}:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n},\qquad(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n-1})\mapsto(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},-\lfloor r\cdot z\rfloor),

where “
\cdot
” denotes the scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Consider the vector
r=(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{0}},\frac{a_{1}}{a_{0}})
. The following map
\tau_{r}
is a type of shift radix system (see
(
Berthé et al. 2011
)
and
(
Kirschenhofer and Thuswaldner 2014
)
):

\tau_{r}:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2},\qquad(z_{0},z_{1})\mapsto\left(z_{1},-\left\lfloor\tfrac{a_{2}}{a_{0}}z_{0}+\tfrac{a_{1}}{a_{0}}z_{1}\right\rfloor\right),

It can be seen by direct computation that, for any
(z_{0},z_{1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
r=(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{0}},\frac{a_{1}}{a_{0}})
,

T_{\alpha}(\iota_{\alpha}(z_{0},z_{1}))=\iota_{\alpha}(\tau_{r}(z_{0},z_{1})).

(15)

The map
\tau_{r}
is said to have the finiteness property if, for every
(z_{0},z_{1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\tau_{r}^{k}(z_{0},z_{1})=0
. Since
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
gets mapped to the lattice
\Lambda_{\alpha}
via
\iota_{\alpha}
,
\alpha
has the finiteness property in
\Lambda_{\alpha}
if and only if
\tau_{r}
has the finiteness property.

There is no easy way to describe the set of parameters
r
such that
\tau_{r}
has the finiteness property (this is a highly nontrivial problem that has been well studied), but there is a fairly simple algorithm to determine it using what is known as a set of witnesses.

Definition 5
.

A
set of witnesses
associated with a parameter
r\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a set
\mathcal{V}_{r}
such that:

1.

\{\pm e_{1},\ldots,\pm e_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{V}_{r}
(here
e_{j}
is the
j
-th canonical vector) and

2.

z\in\mathcal{V}_{r}
implies
\{\tau_{r}(z),-\tau_{r}(-z)\}\subset\mathcal{V}_{r}
.

The next result can be found in
(
Akiyama et al. 2005
, Theorem 5.1)
.

Lemma 3
.

Given a set of witnesses
\mathcal{V}_{r}
for a parameter
r
, the map
\tau_{r}
has the finiteness property if and only if for every
z\in\mathcal{V}_{r}
there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\tau_{r}(z)^{k}=0.

In
(
Akiyama et al. 2005
, Section 5)
, the authors give an algorithm to find a set of witnesses. We adapt it to our setting.

Algorithm 2
.

Let
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
be an algebraic number system. Let
r=(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{0}},\frac{a_{1}}{a_{0}})
and
\tau_{r}
as before.

1.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\{\pm(1,0),\pm(0,1)\}
.

2.

For
j\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{V}_{j+1}=\mathcal{V}_{j}\cup\tau_{r}(\mathcal{V}_{j})\cup(-\tau_{r}(-\mathcal{V}_{j}))
.

3.

Since
\alpha
is expanding,
r
is contracting, so for some
j
we reach
\mathcal{V}_{j+1}=\mathcal{V}_{j}=:\mathcal{V}_{r}
.

Then
\mathcal{V}_{r}
is a set of witnesses for
r=(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{0}},\frac{a_{1}}{a_{0}})
. Consider the map
\iota_{\alpha}
as before and define
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}:=\iota_{\alpha}(\mathcal{V}_{r}).
We call
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}
a set of witnesses for
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
.

The following is a direct consequence of Lemma
3
and equation (
15
).

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}
be a set of witnesses for
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
as in Algorithm
2
. Then
\alpha
has the finiteness property in
\Lambda_{\alpha}
if and only if
N
has an integer
\alpha
-expansion for every
N\in\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}
.

We conclude this section with an example.

Example 3
.

We continue with
\alpha=\frac{-1+3i}{2}
and
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1,2,3,4\}
. Let

Then

Each point has an integer
\alpha

5
The language of integer
\alpha
-expansions

Let
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
be an algebraic number system. Denote by
\mathcal{D}^{*}
the set of all
words
or finite sequences
d_{k}\ldots d_{0}
with
d_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
and
k\geq 0
, together with the empty word. This set becomes a free monoid with the operation of concatenation. Denote by
\mathcal{D}^{\omega}
the set of infinite words with digits in
\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 6
.

Consider a number system
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
with the finiteness property. We define the
language

L_{\alpha}
as the subset of
\mathcal{D}^{*}
given by the set of all integer
\alpha
-expansions. For simplicity, we allow words to start with
0
. We denote by
L_{\alpha}^{k}
the words of length
k\geq 0
.

The monoid
\mathcal{D}^{*}
is classically represented through the infinite full
|a_{0}|
-ary tree. The language
L_{\alpha}
is prefix closed, that is, given a word
d_{k}\ldots d_{0}\in L_{\alpha},
any prefix
d_{k}\ldots d_{l},(0\leq l\leq k)
is also in
L_{\alpha}
(this follows from the definition of integer
\alpha
-expansions). Consequently,
L_{\alpha}
can be represented through a subtree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
of the full
|a_{0}|
-ary tree so that each path starting from the root corresponds to an integer
\alpha
-expansion (maybe preceded by zeroes).

More formally, for
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
consider

D(N):=\{d\in\mathcal{D}\,:\,\alpha N+d\in\Lambda_{\alpha}\}.

The tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
is constructed as follows: start by labeling the root
0
. The children of the root are labeled
d
for every
d\in D(0).
The node at the end of each child corresponds to
N=\pi_{\alpha}(d)
. Iteratively, each node corresponds to
\pi_{\alpha}(d_{k}\ldots d_{0})
where
d_{k}\ldots d_{0}
is the label of the path starting from the root ending in the node, and the children of the node are labeled
D(\pi_{\alpha}(d_{k}\ldots d_{0}))
.

The following theorem establishes that each node will branch into exactly one residue class modulo

.

Theorem 2
.

A word in
L_{\alpha}
can be extended to the right by all digits
d\in\mathcal{D}
that belong to exactly one residue class modulo
a_{2}
. In other words, for every
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
there exists some
r\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

D(N)=\mathcal{D}\cap(a_{2}\mathbb{Z}+r).

Proof.

If

then there exist unique
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{Z}

. Using that
a_{2}\alpha^{2}+a_{1}\alpha=-a_{0},
it holds that

\begin{split}\alpha N+d&=\lambda a_{2}\alpha+\mu(a_{2}\alpha^{2}+a_{1}\alpha)\\
&=\lambda(a_{2}\alpha+a_{1})-\lambda a_{1}-\mu a_{0}+d,\end{split}

(16)

so
\alpha N+d\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
if and only if
-\lambda a_{1}-\mu a_{0}+d\in a_{2}\mathbb{Z}

.

Next, we show that the length of an integer
\alpha
-expansion grows logarithmically with respect to the modulus of
N
. As usual, given a complex number
a+bi
we denote
|a+bi|=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
.

Proposition 4
.

If
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
has an integer
\alpha
-expansion

N=\sum_{j=0}^{k}d_{j}\alpha^{j},

then it holds that

k=\log_{|\alpha|}(|N|)+\mathcal{O}(1).

Proof.

Using geometric sums and the fact that
0\leq d_{j}\leq|a_{0}|-1
we get the upper bound

|N|=\left|\sum_{j=0}^{k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}\right|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k}|d_{j}||\alpha|^{j}\leq(|a_{0}|-1)\sum_{j=0}^{k}|\alpha|^{j}=(|a_{0}|-1)\frac{|\alpha|^{k+1}-1}{|\alpha|-1}.

For the lower bound,

|N|\geq|d_{k}||\alpha|^{k}-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|d_{j}||\alpha|^{j}.

Since
1\leq d_{k}\leq|a_{0}|-1
and
0\leq d_{j}\leq|a_{0}|-1
for
j<k
we get:

|N|\geq|\alpha|^{k}-(|a_{0}|-1)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\alpha|^{j}=|\alpha|^{k}-(|a_{0}|-1)\frac{|\alpha|^{k}-1}{|\alpha|-1}=\left(1-\frac{|a_{0}|-1}{|\alpha|-1}\right)|\alpha|^{k}+\frac{|a_{0}|-1}{|\alpha|-1}.

Therefore

\left(1-\frac{|a_{0}|-1}{|\alpha|-1}\right)|\alpha|^{k}+\frac{|a_{0}|-1}{|\alpha|-1}\leq|N|\leq(|a_{0}|-1)\frac{|\alpha|^{k+1}-1}{|\alpha|-1}.

Taking the logarithm in base
|\alpha|
on the inequality yields the desired result.
∎

6
Expansions of complex numbers

Our next step in the study of algebraic number systems is to consider expansions of complex numbers, by introducing a radix point, or in other words, by allowing digits
d_{j}
where
j<0
.

Definition 7
.

An
\alpha
-expansion
of a complex number
x
is an expansion of the form

x=\sum_{j\leq k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}\qquad(d_{j}\in\mathcal{D})

(18)

with
d_{k}\neq 0
, such that
(d_{k}\ldots d_{l})_{\alpha}
corresponds to the integer
\alpha
-expansion of some
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
for every
l\leq k
. We denote it

x=(d_{k}\ldots d_{0}.d_{-1}d_{-2}\ldots)_{\alpha}.

It follows from the definition of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
that (
18
) is an
\alpha
-expansion of
x
if and only if the sequence
(d_{j})_{j\leq k}
corresponds to an infinite downward path in the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
starting from the root.

Remark 1
.

If we add a trail of zeroes to the right of the integer
\alpha
-expansion of
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
, in general, we do not get an
\alpha
-expansion of
N
: take, for example,
\alpha=\frac{-1+3i}{2}
. Then
2=(2)_{\alpha}
, however
(2.0000\ldots)_{\alpha}
is not an
\alpha
-expansion of
2
, because the path
200
is not in the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
.

Next, we give an algorithm to approximate an
\alpha
-expansion of a given
x\in\mathbb{C}
. As a first step, we introduce a function
\lambda_{\alpha}
that maps
x
to a nearby point in
\Lambda_{\alpha}
.

Regard
\mathbb{C}
as a two dimensional
\mathbb{R}
-vector space with basis
\{1,i\}
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}=\{a_{2},a_{2}\alpha+a_{1}\}
be the Brunotte basis of
\Lambda_{\alpha}
. Consider the linear isomorphism

f:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\qquad\begin{cases}1\mapsto a_{2},\\
i\mapsto a_{2}\alpha+a_{1}.\end{cases}

(19)

Consider the complex floor function defined as
\lfloor a+bi\rfloor:=\lfloor a\rfloor+\lfloor b\rfloor i
. Define

\lambda_{\alpha}:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\Lambda_{\alpha},\qquad x\mapsto f(\lfloor f^{-1}(x)\rfloor).

Algorithm 3
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

1.

Compute
\lambda_{\alpha}(\alpha^{n}x)\in\Lambda_{\alpha}.

2.

Find its integer
\alpha
-expansion
\lambda_{\alpha}(\alpha^{n}x)=(d_{k}\ldots d_{0})_{\alpha}.

3.

Let
\textbf{w}_{n}:=d_{k}\ldots d_{k-n}.d_{k-n-1}\ldots d_{0}00\ldots\in\mathcal{D}^{\omega}
.

Algorithm
3
produces a sequence of expansions in base
\alpha
. They are, in general, not
\alpha
-expansions, but we will see that they converge to an
\alpha
-expansion of
x
in the following metric: given any
\textbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\textbf{y}=(y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{D}^{\omega}
consider the distance
dist(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=2^{-\inf\{j\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,x_{j}\neq y_{j}\}}
.

Proposition 5
.

The sequence
\textbf{w}_{n}
from Algorithm
3
converges to an
\alpha
-expansion of
x
.

Proof.

We first show that
x=\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{\alpha}(\textbf{w}_{n})
, where
\pi_{\alpha}
is the evaluation map in base
\alpha
. It is clear that

\pi_{\alpha}(\textbf{w}_{n})=\alpha^{-n}\pi_{\alpha}(d_{k}\ldots d_{0})=\alpha^{-n}\lambda_{\alpha}(\alpha^{n}x).

We have

\lambda_{\alpha}(\alpha^{n}x)=f(\lfloor f^{-1}(\alpha^{n}x)\rfloor)=f(f^{-1}(\alpha^{n}x)+\{f^{-1}(\alpha^{n}x)\}),

where
\{z\}=z-\lfloor z\rfloor
is the complex fractional part. Since
f
is a linear map,

f(f^{-1}(\alpha^{n}x)+\{f^{-1}(\alpha^{n}x)\})=\alpha^{n}x+f(\{f^{-1}(\alpha^{n}x)\}).

By definition of the complex floor, it holds that
|\{z\}|<2
for any complex number
z
. Since
f
is linear, it is bounded, and so
\varepsilon_{n}:=f(\{f^{-1}(\alpha^{n}x)\})
is bounded. We get

\pi_{\alpha}(\textbf{w}_{n})=\alpha^{-n}(\alpha^{n}x+\varepsilon_{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}x.

We show now that the sequence
\textbf{w}_{n}
converges in
\mathcal{D}^{\omega}
to some
w
. For that we need to show that
N_{n}=\lambda_{\alpha}(\alpha^{n}x)
and
N_{n+1}=\lambda_{\alpha}(\alpha^{n+1}x)
have integer
\alpha
-expansions with a common prefix whose length goes to infinity as
n
grows. We have

T_{\alpha}(N_{n+1})-N_{n}=\frac{N_{n+1}-d_{0}}{\alpha}-N_{n}=\frac{\varepsilon_{n+1}}{\alpha}-\varepsilon_{n}-\frac{d_{0}}{\alpha}.

The integer
\alpha
-expansion of
T_{\alpha}(N_{n+1})
is the integer
\alpha
-expansion of
N_{n+1}
with the last digit
d_{0}
removed. Let
\langle N_{n}\rangle_{\alpha}=d_{k}\ldots d_{0}
and
\langle T_{\alpha}(N_{n+1})\rangle_{\alpha}=d^{\prime}_{k}\ldots d_{0}^{\prime}
where we patch one of the expansions with zeroes to the left so that they have the same length. Let
l
be the largest index so that
d_{l}\neq d^{\prime}_{l}
. Then
|\alpha|^{l}\leq|T_{\alpha}(N_{n+1})-N_{n}|=\left|\frac{\varepsilon_{n+1}}{\alpha}-\varepsilon_{n}-\frac{d_{0}}{\alpha}\right|
, which is bounded, and therefore
l
is bounded. As a consequence, the distance in
\mathcal{D}^{\omega}
of
\textbf{w}_{n}
and
\textbf{w}_{n+1}
tends to
0
as
n
goes to infinity. Therefore, the sequence is Cauchy and since
\mathcal{D}^{\omega}
is complete, it converges to
\textbf{w}\in\mathcal{D}^{\omega}
. Since the prefixes of
w
are integer
\alpha
-expansions by definition,
w
is an
\alpha
-expansion of
x
.
∎

7
Tilings for algebraic number systems

We introduce next a family of sets that we denote
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
, originally studied in
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
)
, where they are called intersective tiles.
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
is the set of points in
\mathbb{C}
with an
\alpha
-expansion whose integer part is
N
. We will show later on that these sets form a tiling of the complex plane.

Definition 8
.

Let
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
be an algebraic number system and
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
. Let

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N):=\{x\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,x=(d_{k}\ldots d_{0}.d_{-1}d_{-2}\ldots)_{\alpha}\mbox{ and }N=(d_{k}\ldots d_{0})_{\alpha}\}.

In Figure
4
, we show
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
for
\alpha=\frac{-1+3i}{2}
for
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
whose integer
\alpha
-expansion has length at most three. To each picture corresponds one level of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
from Figure
1
. The lattice points are labeled (the place of the label is where the corresponding point is), as well as the digits of their integer
\alpha
-expansion. We chose a four-coloring for this tiling, and one can observe that, even though all of the tiles seem to be different, the ones of the same color have a similar shape. We will explain this in the next section.

Figure 4:
Tiles
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
for
\alpha=\frac{-1+3i}{2}
and
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}

Definition 9
.

A collection
\mathcal{C}
of compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
is called a
tiling
if it is a covering of
\mathbb{C}
that is pairwise essentially disjoint, that is, the intersection of any two distinct sets has Lebesgue measure zero.

Proposition 6
.

Let
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
be an algebraic number system. Then the boundary of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
has Lebesgue measure zero for every
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
and the collection
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)\,:\,N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}\}
forms a tiling of
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

This result can be found in
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
, Theorem 3)
. The authors define
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
in a different and more general way than us, by considering self-affine tiles in certain spaces defined in terms of
\mathfrak{p}
-adic completions of
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
with respect to prime ideals. The equivalence between the two definitions is a consequence of Theorem
4
from the next section.
∎

The intersection of two distinct sets in
\mathcal{C}
occurs on their boundary because they are compact. We have arrived at our main result.

Theorem 3
.

The
\alpha
-expansion of a complex number is unique almost everywhere.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{C}
and suppose it has two different
\alpha
-expansions

x=\sum_{j\leq k}\alpha^{j}d_{j}=\sum_{j\leq k}\alpha^{j}d^{\prime}_{j},

where
d_{k}\neq 0
and where we pad the second expansion with zeros if necessary. Let
m\leqslant k
be the largest integer such that
d_{m}\neq d^{\prime}_{m}
. Let
N:=(d_{k}\ldots d_{m+1}d_{m})_{\alpha}
and
N^{\prime}:=(d^{\prime}_{k}\ldots d^{\prime}_{m+1}d^{\prime}_{m})_{\alpha}
. By definition of
\alpha
-expansion, we have
N,N^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
and they are distinct. It holds that
\alpha^{-m}x\in\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)\cap\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N^{\prime}).

As tiles only overlap on their boundaries, it holds in particular that
x\in\alpha^{m}\partial\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
.
Therefore, a point
x\in\mathbb{C}
has two different expansions if and only if
x\in\bigcup_{N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}}\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha^{m}\partial\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
.
Then
x
is in a countable union of measure-zero sets.
∎

8
\alpha
-expansions and
p
-adic completions

We now characterize
\alpha
-expansions in terms of
p
-adic completions of
\mathbb{Q}(i)
, where
p
is a Gaussian prime. The ring of integers of the field of Gaussian rationals
\mathbb{Q}(i)
is the ring of Gaussian integers
\mathbb{Z}[i].
There are exactly four units of
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, namely
\{\pm 1,\pm i\}.

Definition 10
.

A Gaussian integer
p\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
is a
Gaussian prime
if
p\neq 0
,
p
is not a unit and whenever
p
divides a product
ab
with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
then
p
divides
a
or
p
divides
b
.
Given a Gaussian prime
p
and a unit
u
, the number
up
is a Gaussian prime associated to
p
.

Every Gaussian integer, and therefore every Gaussian rational, has a unique factorization into Gaussian primes. This is a classical result.

Proposition 7
.

Given a non-zero
x\in\mathbb{Q}(i)
, it can be expressed as a product

x=u\,p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{e_{k}}

(20)

where
u\in\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
,
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
are pairwise not associated. This factorization is unique up to associated primes and multiplication by units. Note that the exponents
e_{j}
might be negative because
\mathbb{Q}(i)
is the field of fractions of
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

The following generalizes the notion of
p
-adic valuation in
\mathbb{Q}
.

Definition 11
.

Let
p
be a Gaussian prime. We define the
p
-adic valuation
as

\nu_{p}:\mathbb{Q}(i)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z},\qquad x\mapsto\nu_{p}(x),

where
\nu_{p}(x)
is given by the exponent of
p
in the decomposition of
x
into Gaussian primes given in Proposition
7
whenever
x\neq 0
, and
\nu_{p}(0)=\infty
. We define the
p
-adic absolute value

|\cdot|_{p}:\mathbb{Q}(i)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\qquad x\mapsto N(p)^{-\nu_{p}(x)},

where
N(p)
is the complex norm of
p
, with the convention that
|0|_{p}=N(p)^{-\infty}=0.

Given a Gaussian rational
\alpha
, find two Gaussian integers
num(\alpha)
and
den(\alpha)
that are coprime (that is, they have no common Gaussian prime divisors), such that

\alpha=\frac{num(\alpha)}{den(\alpha)}.

It follows from
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
, Lemma 3.3)
that
N(num(\alpha))=|a_{0}|
and
N(den(\alpha))=a_{2}
.

The proof of the next result follows the proof of
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
, Lemma 6.2)
.

Theorem 4
.

An expansion

x=\sum_{j\leq k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}\qquad(d_{j}\in\mathcal{D})

(21)

is an
\alpha
-expansion of
x
if and only if it converges to
0
in
K_{p}
for every Gaussian prime
p
dividing
den(\alpha)
.

Proof.

Given
l\leq k
, consider the sum of the first
k-l
terms of the series (
21
),

S_{l}:=d_{k}\alpha^{k}+\cdots+d_{l+1}\alpha^{l+1}+d_{l}\alpha^{l}.

Let
N_{l}=\alpha^{-l}S_{l}
. By definition, the series is an
\alpha
-expansion if and only if
N_{l}\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
for all
l\leq k
.

Assume that (
21
) is an
\alpha
-expansion of
x\in\mathbb{C}
. Then, by definition of
\Lambda_{\alpha}
,
N_{l}\in\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]\cap\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{-1}]
and hence
S_{l}\in\alpha^{l-1}\mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{-1}]
. Let
p
be a Gaussian prime dividing
den(\alpha)
, then
p^{-l+1}
is a factor of the numerator of
\alpha^{l-1}
, and
p
does not appear in the denominator of any point of
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{-1}]
, therefore
\nu_{p}(S_{l})\geq-l+1
. Therefore,
\lim_{l\to-\infty}\nu_{p}(S_{l})=\infty
and consequently
|\sum_{j\leq k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}|_{p}=0
.

For the converse, suppose that (
21
) is not an
\alpha
-expansion, that is, there exists some
l\leq k
such that
N_{l}\notin\Lambda_{\alpha}
. Since clearly
N_{l}\in\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
, it holds that
N_{l}\notin\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{-1}]
, and hence
S_{l}\notin\alpha^{l-1}\mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{-1}]
. Note that
d_{j}\alpha^{j}\in\alpha^{l-1}\mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{-1}]
for every
j\leq l-1
.

Let
\varphi_{p}:\mathbb{Q}(i)\rightarrow K_{p}
be the canonical embedding of
\mathbb{Q}(i)
into its completion
K_{p}
. Then

\sum_{j\leq k}\varphi_{p}(d_{j}\alpha^{j})\notin\varphi_{p}(\alpha^{l-1}\mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{-1}])

and in particular

\sum_{j\leq k}\varphi_{p}(d_{j}\alpha^{j})\neq 0.

∎

We illustrate this result with our recurring example.

Example 6
.

Let
\alpha=\frac{-1+3i}{2}
, root of
P_{\alpha}(X)=2X^{2}+2X+5
, and
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1,2,3,4\}
. We can express
\alpha
as a quotient of coprime Gaussian integers as

\alpha=\frac{-2+i}{1+i}.

Consider the Gaussian prime
p=1+i
. We can define the
(1+i)
-adic valuation
\nu_{1+i}:\mathbb{Q}(i)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
. For example,
2
factors as

so
\nu_{1+i}(2)=2
and hence
|2|_{1+i}=\frac{1}{4}
. Let
K_{1+i}
be the completion of
\mathbb{Q}(i)
with respect to this absolute value. We can express an

-expansion as

\sum_{j\leq k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}=\sum_{j=-k}^{\infty}\frac{d_{-j}}{(-2+i)^{j}}(1+i)^{j}.

(22)

Following the proof of Theorem

, the series (

in

and therefore

. Each

and green whenever

. The next observation is that tiles of the same color seem to have a similar shape, that is,
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
and
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N^{\prime})
have a more similar shape whenever

and

where
\sum_{j\leq-1}\alpha^{j}d_{j}
converges to
-N
in

. One could therefore consider all series
\sum_{j\leq-1}\alpha^{j}d_{j}
and take the limit both in
\mathbb{C}
and in
K_{1+i}
, producing a set in
\mathbb{C}\times K_{1+i}
, and then
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N)
and
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(N^{\prime})
could be seen as ‘slices’ of this set, and therefore they look more alike when
N

8.1
Ambinumbers

Given
x\in\mathbb{C}
, there are in general many ways to expand it in base
\alpha
and digits
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\ldots,|a_{0}|-1\}
. We chose a particular kind of expansion and showed that it converges to
0
in
K_{p}
for all Gaussian primes
p
dividing
den(\alpha)
. It seems, therefore, reasonable to look for an expansion that converges to
x
in
\mathbb{C}
and to some fixed value
y_{p}
in each
K_{p}
. This is the setting introduced in
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
)
.

The article
(
Rossi and Thuswaldner 2022
)
considers a number system with base
-\frac{3}{2}
and digits
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1,2\}
using the representation space
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Q}_{2}
, and it is shown that each point in this space has an expansion in this number system that is unique almost everywhere with respect to a natural Haar measure; Donald Knuth called the elements of this space
ambinumbers
in
(
Knuth 2022
)
. We retake this name here and generalize this notion for algebraic number systems.

Definition 12
.

Let
\alpha=\frac{num(\alpha)}{den(\alpha)}
. Define

K_{den(\alpha)}:=\prod_{p|den(\alpha)}K_{p}

where
p
ranges over Gaussian primes. We call a pair
(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
an ambinumber.

This motivates the following definition.

Definition 13
.

Consider a base
\alpha
and digits
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\ldots,|a_{0}|-1\}
. Given an ambinumber
(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
, where
y=(y_{p})_{p|den(\alpha)}
, the series

\sum_{j\leq k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}\qquad(d_{j}\in\mathcal{D})

(24)

d_{k}\neq 0
whenever
k\geq 1
, is called an
\alpha
-expansion of
(x,y)
if the series converges to
x
in
\mathbb{C}
and to
y_{p}
in
K_{p}
for every
p|den(\alpha)
. We note it

(x,y)=(d_{k}\ldots d_{0}.d_{-1}d_{-2}\ldots)_{\alpha}

We show next that
\alpha
-expansions of ambinumbers can take any form, and there are no restrictions on the strings of digits allowed. This justifies the introduction of
\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
as a natural representation space.

Proposition 8
.

Let
(\alpha,\mathcal{D})
be an algebraic number system. A series of the form (
24
)
correspond to the
\alpha
-expansion of some ambinumber
(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
for any sequence
(d_{j})_{j\leq k}
.

Proof.

The series converges in
\mathbb{C}
because
|\alpha|>1
and
\mathcal{D}
is finite. Let
p
be a Gaussian prime dividing
den(\alpha)
, and let
r_{p}
be the exponent of
p
in the prime factorization of
den(\alpha)
. Let
l\leq k
and consider the partial sum
S_{l}=\sum_{j=l}^{k}d_{j}\alpha^{j}
. Then
\nu_{p}(S_{l}-S_{l+1})=\nu_{p}(d_{l}\alpha^{l})=\nu_{p}(d_{l})-l\,r_{p}
and so
|S_{l}-S_{l+1}|_{p}=N(p)^{r_{p}l-\nu_{p}(d_{l})}
with
l\to-\infty
, hence
(S_{l})
is Cauchy in the complete space
K_{p}
, so the series converges in
K_{p}
.
∎

Note that the
\alpha
-expansion of
x\in\mathbb{C}
is then the
\alpha
-expansion of the ambinumber
(x,0)
. The integer
\alpha
-expansion of
N\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
is the
\alpha
-expansion of the ambinumber
(N,N)
.

We now show a uniqueness result for
\alpha
-expansions of ambinumbers.

Theorem 5
.

The
\alpha
-expansion of an ambinumber
(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
is unique almost everywhere (with respect to a natural Haar measure).

Proof.

This result is a consequence of
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
, Theorem 2 and Corollary 1)
. The authors consider a set
\mathcal{F}
, which in our setting corresponds to all the ambinumbers with an expansion (
24
) with
k\leq-1
, that is, the set of fractional expansions. They show that this set gives a tiling of
\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
(in their setting, this is done more generally). Given the existence of a tiling, the uniqueness almost everywhere can be obtained analogously as in the proof of Theorem
3
.
∎

To finalize, we show how to compute the expansion of an ambinumber
(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
for the case
y\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
: we regard
\Lambda_{\alpha}
as a subset of
K_{den(\alpha)}
through the product of the canonical embeddings, that is,
y\hookrightarrow(y)_{p|den(\alpha)}
. Note that
\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
is a group with component-wise addition, therefore

(x,y)=(x,0)+(0,y).

We know how to compute the
\alpha
-expansion of
(x,0)
, because it is exactly the
\alpha
-expansion of
x
. We also know how to add
\alpha
-expansions (same algorithm as with integer
\alpha
-expansions, just adding the radix point), so it remains to find the expansion of
(0,y)
. The group
\Lambda_{\alpha}
acts on
\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}
by multiplication, hence, if
y\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
, it holds that

(0,y)=y\cdot(0,1)

Computing the
\alpha
-expansion of
(0,1)
is not hard: we can first compute the
\alpha
-expansion of
-1
, which converges to
-1
in
\mathbb{R}
and to
0
in
K_{den(\alpha)}
. Adding
1
to the digit left to the radix point of this expansion will converge to
0
in
\mathbb{R}
and
1
in
K_{p}
, yielding the expansion of
(0,1)
. Since
y\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
, we can compute its integer
\alpha
-expansion, and then multiply both expansions.

Example 7
.

As usual, let
\alpha=\frac{-1+3i}{2}
. Suppose we want to find the expansion of the ambinumber
(\sqrt{2},1+3i)\in\mathbb{C}\times K_{den(\alpha)}.
From Example
5
,

(\sqrt{2},0)=(2.234112142444002024120003\ldots)_{\alpha}

We also have

-1=(0.243100111243211314444112303\ldots)_{\alpha}

Consequently,

(0,1)=(1.243100111243211314444112303\ldots)_{\alpha}

Using
1+3i=(22)_{\alpha}
and the multiplication algorithm,

(0,1+3i)=(0.12320244240042344032002044\ldots)_{\alpha}

Using the addition algorithm, we get

(\sqrt{2},1+3i)=(\sqrt{2},0)+(0,1+3i)=(2.13231231234442321444022\ldots)_{\alpha}

One can check that this expansion converges to the desired point.

9
Generalizations and open questions

9.1
Generalizations

Throughout the paper, we assumed that
\alpha
is a Gaussian rational, however, the study of algebraic number systems can also be done when
\alpha
is an expanding quadratic algebraic number. The definition of the expansions is the same, and the main difference would be encountered when considering
p
-adic completions. If
\alpha
is not a Gaussian rational, then in general
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
is not a unique factorization domain. However, it is a Dedekind domain, and the
p
-adic completions can be defined in terms of prime ideals. This is contained in
(
Steiner and Thuswaldner 2015
)
and therefore the proofs that rely on results there would stay essentially the same. Moreover, if
\alpha
is an expanding algebraic number of degree
n
, instead of expansions in
\mathbb{C}
, one can consider expansions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
using Galois embeddings. We opted for
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(i)
for simplicity.

We considered digit sets of the form
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\ldots,|a_{0}|-1\}
, but it would suffice to consider a complete residue set for
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]/\alpha\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
that is primitive, that is, such that
\mathbb{Z}\langle\alpha,\mathcal{D}\rangle=\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
, where
\mathbb{Z}\langle\alpha,\mathcal{D}\rangle
is the smallest
\alpha
-invariant
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule of
\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]
containing the difference set
\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{D}
. Once again, for simplicity, we opted for this special shape of
\mathcal{D}
.

9.2
Open questions

We leave some open questions to motivate future research.

1.

Is the language of integer
\alpha
-expansions regular? Is it context-free? We conjecture it is not, as this is the case for rational base number systems (see Corollaries 7 and 9 of
(
Akiyama et al. 2008
)
).

2.

Is it possible to characterize, given a base
\alpha
, the set of points
x\in\mathbb{C}
that have multiple
\alpha
-expansions? What is the upper bound for the number of possible
\alpha
-expansions? What shape do these multiple expansions have? This question is answered for the rational case; however, the algebraic setting is quite different because complex numbers are not ordered.

3.

What can we say about the distribution of digits (and, respectively, finite words) in
\alpha
-expansions of complex numbers? We conjecture that, for almost all
x\in\mathbb{C}
, any two words of the same length in
D^{*}
appear with the same frequency in the
\alpha
-expansion of
x
. This was proven for rational base number systems in
(
Morgenbesser et al. 2013
)
. Not only is this not obvious, but it isn’t easy to prove even in the rational case, and the authors in
(
Morgenbesser et al. 2013
)
use sophisticated techniques such as Fourier analysis in Adèle rings.
