Neural mechanisms of cough: insights from multimodal magnetic resonance imaging—a narrative review
Authors: Xinyi Liu, Yijing Wang, Chen Chen, Chunwei Wu, Bo Zhang, Zhandong Liu, Ying Xie, Peijun Gui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveCough is an essential defensive reflex that plays a critical role in airway protection. Dysfunction of this reflex, often manifesting as chronic cough or cough hypersensitivity, involves complex alterations in both peripheral and central nervous systems. While previous studies primarily focused on brainstem-mediated reflexes, recent neuroimaging highlights the pivotal role of higher cortical areas in regulating both voluntary and involuntary cough. This review aims to comprehensively summarize the application of multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in delineating these central mechanisms and identifying therapeutic targets.MethodsFor this narrative review, we conducted a structured literature search using PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science to identify relevant studies published up to January 2026. Search terms included “cough”, “chronic cough”, “multimodal MRI”, “structural MRI (sMRI)”, “functional MRI (fMRI)”, and “diffusion MRI (dMRI)”. Relevant articles focusing on neural substrates of cough and neuroimaging-guided interventions were selected and synthesized.Key Content and FindingsBy integrating sMRI, fMRI, and dMRI, current research has delineated a dynamic cough-related neural network. Key hubs identified include the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS). The review further describes mechanisms of central sensitization and neuroplasticity that may characterize refractory cough. Additionally, we explore how these imaging biomarkers can guide pharmacological and non-invasive interventions, such as transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS).ConclusionsMultimodal MRI facilitates a deeper understanding of the central functional alterations associated with cough dysfunction. Identification of specific imaging biomarkers provides reliable neuroimaging targets for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, ultimately facilitating the development of precision medicine for patients with cough-related neurological disorders.Keywords: Cough, multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (multimodal MRI), neural mechanisms, therapeutic strategies

Introduction

Background

Cough is a defensive reflex of the respiratory system that involves coordinated activity in both lower and higher neural centers. The cough neural pathway constitutes a multilevel neural network primarily mediated by the vagus nerve. Peripheral receptors are primarily distributed in the airway mucosa, while central processing involves coordinated regulation of multiple nuclei within the brainstem, thalamus, and subcortical regions (1,2). In decerebrated animal models, the cough reflex can be preserved (3,4). However, when certain nuclei of the cerebellum are removed, experimental animals exhibit a significant reduction in the number of coughs (5). In humans, cough control is far more complex. It involves higher cortical areas that regulate both voluntary and involuntary coughing, as well as the unique sensory experience known as the “urge-to-cough” (6-8). Distinct from the motor act of coughing, the “urge-to-cough” is a sensory experience primarily processed by the insula and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). Clinically, chronic cough shares overlapping neural substrates with neuropathic pain and is associated with central sensitization involving activated astrocytes and microglia. This “cough hypersensitivity” mimics pain hyperalgesia and is compounded by impaired central inhibition within the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and ACC (9).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Patterns of cough impairment vary across different neurological and systemic diseases. Voluntary cough is significantly affected in patients with frontoparietal and basal ganglia stroke, Parkinson’s disease (PD), multiple system atrophy (MSA), and motor neuron disease (MND), but the degree of reflex cough is not the same. Voluntary cough function is significantly reduced in patients with stroke, whereas reflex cough shows no significant change (10). Both voluntary and reflex cough thresholds are increased in patients with PD (11), and impairment of pathways between the cerebral cortex and brainstem cough centers may contribute to these differences (12). To understand these complex central mechanisms, advanced neuroimaging is essential. Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [including structural MRI (sMRI), functional MRI (fMRI), and diffusion MRI (dMRI)] enables in vivo visualization of central cough mechanisms (13-15). However, a holistic synthesis integrating these findings into a coherent “cough network” remains lacking. Crucially, linking these neuroimaging markers with non-invasive neuromodulation [e.g., transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)] represents a significant gap for advancing clinical treatment.

Objective

This review synthesizes recent multimodal MRI findings to provide a comprehensive overview of the neural mechanisms governing cough. This review aims to map the structural and functional plasticity associated with cough hypersensitivity, identify key targets in the cortical control network, and evaluate the potential of these biomarkers in guiding interventions such as non-invasive brain stimulation. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0248/rc).

Methods

For this narrative review, a structured literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science to identify literature on multimodal MRI and neural mechanisms of cough published up to January 2026. Key search terms included “cough”, “chronic cough”, “MRI”, “functional MRI”, “structural MRI”, “diffusion tensor imaging”, and “neural pathways”, using both MeSH terms and free-text keywords. We also hand-searched bibliographies of relevant articles. Inclusion criteria comprised English-language original research articles or reviews involving human or animal subjects that utilized neuroimaging to explore central cough regulation or interventions (e.g., neuromodulation). Two authors (X.L. and P.G.) independently performed the literature screening. See Table 1 for a summary of the search strategy. Multimodal MRI findings and clinical implications

Multimodal MRI

Multimodal MRI refers to a multi-parameter MRI approach that enables assessment of both brain structure and function (13). It is broadly categorized into sMRI and fMRI. Specifically, it encompasses high-resolution anatomical imaging data, dMRI, susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), task-based fMRI (T-fMRI), resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), and proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS). Advances in fMRI provide a non-invasive means to investigate changes in brain structure and function from a clinical perspective, enabling intuitive identification of alterations in human brain neural circuit activity. It is widely applied to evaluate brain functional activity and associated neural responses during cough.

Suntrup-Krueger et al. (14) employed MRI to assess correlations among stroke lesion locations, abnormal swallowing patterns, cough reflex impairment, and pneumonia. They performed swallowing angiography on 200 patients with acute stroke within 96 hours of admission and monitored pneumonia incidence during hospitalization. The study revealed that the parietal and temporal lobes of the right hemisphere were associated with oropharyngeal residue and delayed swallowing response. The paralimbic regions of the right hemisphere and the sensory regions of the left hemisphere were linked to impaired cough reflex. Moreover, lesions in the paralimbic regions were uniquely associated with impaired cough reflex and with pneumonia. With the growing utilization of multimodal MRI, numerous studies have utilized fMRI to identify the brain regions involved in cough control, including the motor cortex, sensory cortex, and brainstem cough centers. These brain regions display specific activation patterns during the triggering and modulation of cough. For instance, Canning et al. demonstrated that the nucleus tractus solitaris in the brainstem plays a crucial role in the cough reflex (15). fMRI studies have identified several key brain regions involved in cough control, including the motor cortex, sensory cortex, and brainstem nuclei. sMRI

sMRI is commonly used to provide detailed visualization of brain anatomy. Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) quantifies differences in brain anatomy by measuring regional grey- and white-matter density or volume across MRI voxels (16). Arinze et al. (17) conducted voxel analysis and found a significant reduction in the volume of the cingulate cortex in patients with chronic cough. This region is associated with cough inhibition and sensory-motor processing related to cough, suggesting that impaired cough control and enhanced sensory input may contribute to chronic cough in adults. Compared to healthy controls, patients with chronic refractory cough exhibit specific alterations in brain structure and function (18). VBM indicated that these patients had reduced grey matter in the left middle frontal gyrus. Moreover, increased functional connectivity (FC) in the left frontoparietal network was linked to more severe cough symptoms and cognitive impairment. Both spontaneous and reflex cough activate overlapping brain regions. However, reflex coughs, such as those triggered by capsaicin, involve brain regions related to sensory input, such as the somatosensory cortex, indicating more automatic, sensory-driven responses. In contrast, voluntary cough mainly activates regions responsible for motor planning and execution (19). This difference in brain activation patterns may serve as an important therapeutic target for patients with chronic cough.

dMRI

Based on water diffusion principles, dMRI can detect the diffusion of water molecules within human tissues. This approach enables characterization of tissue microstructure and reconstruction of brain fiber tracts. This technique is primarily employed in the study of white matter and its connectivity (20). Among the various dMRI techniques, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is one of the most frequently used. Wu et al. (21) used DTI to examine the structural abnormalities in the frontal lobe, inferior parietal lobe, and olfactory cortex following long-term use of antitussive drugs. Currently, few studies have examined cough-related neural pathways using DTI alone, and patients with chronic cough may exhibit alterations in neural pathways. Future studies may combine DTI with fMRI techniques to further investigate higher-order functional brain changes associated with cough.

fMRI

Investigation of central alterations related to the cough reflex using rs-fMRI

rs-fMRI is primarily used to assess brain function through local regional activity and inter-regional connectivity. During rs-fMRI scanning, participants remain at rest, eliminating the need for complex task designs. This approach reduces variability arising from differences in task design and subject performance (22). Regional homogeneity (ReHo) and amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) are commonly used metrics for evaluating local neural activity. Analytical methods for investigating inter-regional brain connectivity include FC and seed-based correlation analysis. Numerous studies integrate multiple analytical approaches to examine brain functional activity.

ALFF amplitude is an effective metric for assessing local neural activity and correlates with its intensity (23). A decrease in ALFF values suggests reduced spontaneous neuronal activity, whereas an increase in ALFF values may reflect compensatory neural activity (24). Chen et al. (25) employed the ALFF method for analysis and discovered that patients with chronic cough exhibited elevated ALFF values in regions such as the cerebellum and cingulate gyrus, along with reduced ALFF values in cortical and limbic regions. These findings suggest a reduced capacity for central inhibition of cough.

ReHo is also a commonly used index in rs-fMRI for assessing local functional activity (26). It can reflect the consistency of brain activity in the time series (27). A reduction in ReHo values implies a decrease in the consistency of neuronal activity. Qiu et al. (28,29) used ReHo analysis and found reduced ReHo in the bilateral medial and lateral orbitofrontal cortices in patients who had taken antitussive drugs for an extended period. Troche et al. proposed (30) that the cognitive module of cough shapes emotional responses to airway stimulation by activating the orbitofrontal cortex (in conjunction with limbic regions like the cingulate). For instance, during coughing, the orbitofrontal cortex might participate in evaluating the threat level of airway stimuli, thus affecting cough triggering or suppression. Similar mechanisms have been noted in other sensory processes (such as pain and dyspnea). Moreover, McGovern et al. (31) reported that electrical stimulation of the orbital gyrus region in anesthetized cats can promptly suppress the cough reflex triggered by superior laryngeal nerve activation. These findings suggest that the orbitofrontal cortex may contribute to the suppression of cough.

Wu et al. (32) employed the FC method and found that, in comparison with healthy individuals, patients who had undergone lung cancer surgery and experienced chronic cough exhibited alterations in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) and ACC. These changes appear to be associated with increased cough sensitivity and anxiety. Farrell et al. (33) also identified three sub-networks activated after capsaicin inhalation through FC calculations. The first sub-network, centered on the inferior frontal gyrus, is primarily involved in cough control and response inhibition. The second sub-network consists of paralimbic, premotor, prefrontal, and subcortical regions, which participate in the intensity coding of airway input. The third sub-network, involving the somatosensory and posterior parietal cortices, most likely represents cough sensation. These networks reflect the coordinated responses of cough-related brain areas.

The default mode network (DMN) has been shown to be altered across a wide range of neurological and psychiatric conditions (34-36). Brain regions comprising the DMN are typically deactivated during externally oriented tasks but highly active at rest (37). It is generally hypothesized that the DMN is involved in fundamental brain functions such as emotion and self-mental representation (38). According to existing research, patients with chronic cough may exhibit altered coordination between the DMN [e.g., posterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex (PFC)] and the executive control network (e.g., DLPFC). This abnormality bears resemblance to the central sensitization mechanism of neuropathic pain. Specifically, overactivity of the DMN during the resting state might heighten persistent attention to cough, whereas insufficient inhibition of the executive control network could weaken the active regulatory capacity of the cough reflex (39-41). However, no studies to date have directly used fMRI to evaluate DMN alterations specifically related to cough.

Research on central changes associated with the cough reflex using task-state fMRI

T-fMRI involves the presentation of structured stimuli or tasks to elicit event-related neural responses. Blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) functional imaging is used to observe the activation of cerebral cortical regions corresponding to different events at different time periods (42). When neurons in the brain are active, more oxygen and glucose are needed (43). As a result, regional cerebral blood flow increases in activated areas. BOLD imaging non-invasively detects changes in blood oxygenation and flow that indirectly reflect neuronal activity (44). By monitoring cerebral blood flow changes during cough, fMRI provides detailed information on the neural mechanisms underlying the cough reflex, and supports functional localization and neural network analysis (45).

Evaluating the cough activation network in healthy individuals using BOLD sequence

Numerous studies using BOLD imaging to examine cough-related brain activation networks have shown that network characteristics vary across different populations. Ando et al. (46) utilized the BOLD method and found that capsaicin inhalation in healthy individuals can elicit urge-to-cough, accompanied by activation of networks involving the brainstem, cerebellum, insular cortex, thalamus, primary somatosensory cortex (SM1), PFC, and supplementary motor area (SMA) (47-49). Activation of distinct brain regions is associated with the encoding and processing of sensory information arising from airway stimulation. This includes assessing stimulus intensity, discriminating spatial location, and regulating cough movements. The primary sensory cortex encodes the intensity of the urge-to-cough, while the insular cortex encodes the magnitude of airway sensory input. The prefrontal and posterior parietal cortices are responsible for encoding the spatial localization of airway stimulation sites (50), and the SMA coordinates cough movements (41). Previous studies have reported that patients with dementia with Lewy bodies exhibit a reduced urge-to-cough, potentially related to damage involving the insula and its associated fibers (51-54). Evaluating the characteristics of the activation network in patients with chronic cough using BOLD sequence

Ando et al. (55) demonstrated that, in comparison to healthy controls, patients with chronic cough exhibited heightened urge-to-cough sensitivity during capsaicin inhalation. This increased sensitivity was associated with augmented activity in midbrain regions involved in descending nociceptive modulation pathways, including the cuneate nucleus, periaqueductal grey (PAG) matter, and dorsal raphe. Moreover, patients with chronic cough typically present with symptoms that are difficult to control. These symptoms are linked to diminished activity in the dorsal-medial PFC and anterior middle cingulate cortex. Placebo antitussive therapy can reduce urge-to-cough in participants. When urge-to-cough decreases, increased activation is observed in the middle frontal gyrus within the right DLPFC. The degree of activation is correlated with the degree of decline in urge-to-cough, suggesting that the right DLPFC may contribute to urge-to-cough inhibition (56).

Evaluating the characteristics of the cough activation network in smokers using BOLD sequence

In smokers, cough exhibits central changes similar to those observed in healthy individuals but is also associated with activation of cough-inhibitory networks, such as the cuneate nucleus. In a study by Mazzone et al. on the brain network associated with cough inhibition using BOLD (57), the right inferior frontal gyrus formed an inhibitory network with the supplementary premotor area, PFC, cingulate, and basal ganglia. In another study on cough hypersensitivity, Ando et al. (55) found that midbrain regions, including the dorsolateral PAG and the cuneiform nucleus, were selectively activated in individuals with cough hypersensitivity. This finding was also corroborated in the study by Moe et al. (58). Smokers demonstrated significantly higher BOLD signal intensity in the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) and the SM1 compared to non-smokers, suggesting altered sensorimotor integration and impaired inhibitory control (46). Moreover, nicotine withdrawal can enhance the activity of anxiety-related brain regions and reduce the efficiency of urge-to-cough inhibition, whereas nicotine intake can partially reverse this effect (59).

Using BOLD sequence to evaluate the characteristics of the cough-activation network in women

It is worth noting that BOLD imaging has revealed that brain regions activated during cough demonstrate greater activation in females. In clinical practice, the proportion of female patients with chronic cough is indeed higher. This gender difference only emerges after puberty (60). When exposed to inhaled capsaicin, women exhibit twice as much activation in the SM1 as men, and they exhibit a lower tolerance threshold for the stimulus (60). This heightened sensitivity may be associated with hormonal influences (e.g., reduced estrogen after menopause), as well as sex-related differences in the central processing of noxious airway sensations (60,61).

Using BOLD sequence to evaluate the overlap and difference between cough and pain

Pain has long been recognized to share substantial overlap with brain regions activated during cough and urge-to-cough. The co-activated brain regions of pain and cough encompass the ACC, insula, SM1, and secondary somatosensory cortex (SM2) (62-64). Regarding the differences, pain is more consistently associated with activation of the thalamus, amygdala, and cerebellum. In contrast, coughing is more reliant on the reflex center of the brainstem medulla and the SMA (65,66). Additionally, the PFC participates in active inhibition and cognitive control during coughing (67), whereas its role in pain is more associated with attention and emotion regulation (65). BOLD signals further indicated that pain-induced activities in the ACC and insula were linked to a persistent increase in blood oxygen metabolism, reflecting their emotional load. Brainstem activation during cough demonstrates brief, intense BOLD fluctuations consistent with reflexive motor activity (68,69). Abubakar et al. (70) also explored the interaction between pain and cough. Painful stimulation was associated with reduced urge-to-cough scores and cough frequency in individuals who inhaled capsaicin. This behavioral effect was accompanied by a reduction in BOLD signal responses in the brain regions activated by capsaicin inhalation (71).

BOLD imaging was used to assess differences in activation networks elicited by different stimuli, doses, and sensory pathways

Distinct BOLD activation patterns were observed between capsaicin- and adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-stimulated conditions. When evaluated using BOLD sequences during the inhalation of capsaicin and ATP, capsaicin exhibited a more extensive activation network than ATP, with a significant increase in activity in regions related to motor control functions. Previous studies have indicated that unpleasant respiratory sensations are processed by airway nodule and jugular vagal sensory neurons. The airway nodule pathway projects centrally to the NTS, while the jugular pathway inputs to the trigeminal brainstem nucleus. Stimulation of airway nodule and jugular neurons with capsaicin led to an increase in BOLD signals in the dorsolateral medulla oblongata, including regions housing the NTS and the para-trigeminal nucleus. In contrast, at perceptually similar stimulus intensities, the airway nodule-selective stimulus ATP produced BOLD signal changes largely restricted to the NTS (58,72). Moreover, capsaicin induces robust neuronal activity and substantial BOLD signal changes by activating transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) receptors (73,74). In contrast, ATP has a relatively weak effect. Its regulation of neuronal activity is mainly achieved indirectly through changes in calcium transients and ATP levels, and its BOLD signal changes are less significant than those of capsaicin (75,76). These findings suggest that different stimuli engage distinct brain regions with divergent downstream connectivity (77,78). Additionally, the brain regions activated by different doses of stimulants also vary slightly. In healthy individuals, BOLD responses following capsaicin inhalation demonstrated dose-dependent activation of the prefrontal and parietal cortices, and the premotor area and cerebellum were activated only at high doses of capsaicin (50).

Most existing studies examining central regulatory mechanisms of chronic cough are observational in nature. These studies focus on the corresponding increases or decreases in the activity of different brain regions when the body’s urge-to-cough changes. Nevertheless, the specific central regulatory mechanism remains unclear. Therefore, further research is needed to elucidate how the cerebral cortex regulates chronic cough and whether cough hypersensitivity aligns with sensory representation changes in the brain. Currently, there is a paucity of research on the body’s regulation of reflex cough. It has been hypothesized that reflex cough may be regulated through cortical modulation of brainstem cough-reflex sensitivity (79). Central neuroplasticity appears to play an important role in the development of chronic cough. Determining mechanisms of central sensitization and plasticity in cough-related neural circuits is crucial, as this may assist in identifying novel therapeutic targets for the improved treatment of cough patients.

Treatment of cough dysfunction

Abnormalities in cough function, including hyperfunction and hypofunction, can significantly influence clinical outcomes. Therefore, different approaches are employed to regulate cough function, generally categorized into drug interventions and non-drug interventions. In drug interventions, neuromodulators are frequently utilized. However, they present certain drawbacks, necessitating the combination of rehabilitation training to enhance the function of relevant muscles (80).

Non-invasive neuromodulation techniques that can modulate cortical activity hold promise for the central regulation of cough function, representing novel approaches for investigating and potentially treating chronic cough. These approaches include repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), tDCS, and transcranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS). A preliminary study suggested that applying tDCS to target cortical centers of sensory processing and motor control can potentially improve the cough reflex threshold. Anodal stimulation appeared to have a greater effect, and enhancing prefrontal brain activity may have potential therapeutic benefits for cough dysfunction (81). However, the primary supporting evidence (81) is derived exclusively from healthy individuals, which substantially limits its direct applicability to clinical populations. Future studies exploring multi-site stimulation strategies may help clarify the long-term efficacy of these approaches for treating chronic cough. Other non-invasive interventions, such as behavioral cough suppression therapy, may also contribute to modulation of central cough pathways. Modulating these higher neural circuits through non-invasive brain stimulation techniques or cognitive behavioral interventions may offer novel approaches for chronic cough treatment. However, few studies have combined multimodal MRI to evaluate the regulatory effects of different treatments on cough-related central functions. Therefore, future studies combining multimodal MRI with neuromodulation or behavioral therapies in clinical populations are needed to determine whether these approaches can produce durable and clinically meaningful changes in central cough processing.

Limitations

The numerous sequences in multimodal MRI generate a large volume of data. Efficient processing and analysis of these data remain challenging. Developing more advanced image-processing and data-fusion techniques is necessary to enhance the accuracy and reliability of the results. Furthermore, any factors influencing neurovascular coupling can impact the accuracy of image acquisition. For example, arterial carbon dioxide levels, pH, arterial oxygen partial pressure, and pulmonary pressure can affect cerebral blood flow. Additionally, the administration of certain drugs can influence cerebral metabolism and vascular response, and motion-correction techniques can mitigate some of these effects (82). The interference of vision and hearing on the BOLD signal may impede the accurate identification of brain regions activated solely by coughing. Moreover, when capsaicin is used as an experimental stimulus, brain activity related to negative emotions (e.g., anxiety triggered by auditory stimuli) may overlap with regions associated with coughing. Therefore, when designing experiments, the influence of these factors on the experimental results should be carefully considered and discussed (9).

Conclusions

In summary, the advancements in multimodal MRI have facilitated a deeper understanding of the central functional alterations associated with cough. Moreover, they offer reliable neuroimaging targets for the central regulation and treatment of cough. Additionally, less frequently explored imaging sequences, such as diffusional kurtosis imaging (DKI), SWI, and 1H-MRS, may be integrated with established modalities. This combination can further comprehensively and precisely validate the central functional changes related to cough, thereby providing robust neuroimaging evidence to support future clinical applications.
