Patients with persistent atrial fibrillation and metabolic comorbidities have an altered inflammatory state of atrial epicardial adipose tissue, which is linked to CT-attenuation
Authors: Eva R Meulendijks, Benedetta Fabrizi, Steffen Bruns, Etto C Eringa, Ron Hoebe, Thomas van den Boogert, Robin Wesselink, Rushd F M Al-Shama, Tim A C de Vries, Auriane C Ernault, H W M Niessen, Wim J P van Boven, Antoine H G Driessen, I Zbairi, Pietro Zappala, Damini Dey, Ivana Isgum, R Nils Planken, Sébastien P J Krul, Joris R de Groot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpicardial adipose tissue (EAT) is linked to both Atrial fibrillation (AF) and metabolic syndrome (MetS). Whether EAT inflammation relates to AF type, recurrence after ablation, or MetS, is incompletely known, likewise if it can be measured by CT angiography.AimTo establish the link between (1) atrial EAT inflammatory composition and AF type, AF recurrence, and metabolic comorbidities. (2) EAT inflammation and EAT-volume or density.MethodsPatients undergoing thoracoscopic ablation for advanced AF (that is, usually persistent, with enlarged left atria and previous failed ablations) with a cardiac CT-scan before and 6 months after surgery were enrolled. CT-EAT atrial volume and attenuation (density), were used for analyses. Patients’ left atrial appendages (LAA + EAT) were excised during ablation and stained for adipocytes and different inflammatory cells.ResultsAmong the 134 included patients, 113 had a LAA available for (immuno)histo-chemistry. Patients with persistent versus paroxysmal AF had more EAT neutrophils: 155[257] versus 63[106] cells/mm2, (p = 0.003), and less anti-inflammatory CD163 + macrophages: 126[134] versus 224[179], (p = 0.03). The AUC curve for differentiating persistent from paroxysmal AF through neutrophil-count was 0.75 (p value < 0.001, CI 0.63–0.87). EAT neutrophil-count related to CT-EAT-attenuation (multivariable analysis: expB 1.01, CI 1.00–1.02, (p = 0.04)). CT-EAT-attenuation distinguished persistent from paroxysmal AF: − 73.0 ± 4.6 versus − 75.3 ± 5.3HU, (p = 0.03). Patients with versus without recurrence had similar inflammatory cell counts, but larger adipocytes, multivariable analysis: ExpB 1.002, CI 1.00–1.003, (p = 0.02). Hypertensive and diabetic patients also had an increased adipocyte size.ConclusionPatients with persistent versus paroxysmal AF exhibited increased EAT neutrophils, which is reflected by CT-EAT-attenuation. Those with AF recurrence, hypertension and diabetes had adipocyte hypertrophy which may imply a common mechanism underlying these conditions.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02985-1.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) reduces quality of life and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality and imposes a growing burden on the healthcare system [1]. Adiposity of the heart, i.e. abundant epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), has been associated with the incidence, type, and recurrence of AF [2−5].

EAT reduces conduction velocity, and induces fibrosis of the myocardium and electrical heterogeneity [6−10]. Understanding EAT components contributing to the processes leading to the proarrhythmogenic changes above may contribute to AF risk assessment or treatment opportunities. Inflammatory cells residing in the EAT secrete proteins that may affect these processes [11, 12]. Furthermore, adipocyte hypertrophy is associated with increased secretion of cytokines in obese mice, for example TNF-α [13].

EAT is a depot of visceral fat, which is related to metabolic syndrome (MetS) [14], including dyslipidemia, hypertension and diabetes type 2. MetS is involved in an altered state of visceral fat metabolism, potentially contributing to adipocyte dysfunction and inflammation, which may underlie its relation with AF. During inflammation, increased vessel permeability allows for infiltration of inflammatory cells into the tissue and accumulation of extracellular fluid and edema, which increases tissue density. Tissue density can be quantified by computed tomography (CT) attenuation, which typically increases during inflammation. However, although altered EAT-attenuation in AF is commonly being interpretated as inflammation [15], there is a lack of data on local inflammatory markers that support this relationship, but also on the relationship between EAT inflammation and adipocyte morphology, and AF types or AF recurrence after ablation.

We investigated patients undergoing thoracoscopic AF ablation, in which, as opposed to catheter ablation (CA), LA tissue was systematically excised and used for histological analysis. We assessed (1) the relation between EAT inflammation and AF type, AF recurrence risk after ablation, and metabolic syndrome), (2) the link between local EAT inflammation and the CT-markers EAT-volume and attenuation, (3) the reversibility of CT-EAT markers of inflammation after ablation.

Methods

Patient enrollment

Patients who underwent thoracoscopic AF ablation between 2017 and 2020 and were enrolled in the prospective registry titled ‘Markers for Determining Remodeling in Patients with Atrial Fibrillation’ (MARK-AF, NL5006901819) were included in this study. Of note, these patients have advanced AF, defined as persistent AF, enlarged left atria, or previously failed CA [16]. All patients underwent a CT scan before the procedure, and 6 months postoperatively. Patients who had either only a preoperative or a postoperative CT-scan, incomplete scans, scans that were acquired with settings differing from those described in the section below, scans affected by artifacts, or scans in which the pericardium could not be distinguished were excluded. Other important exclusion criteria were chronic use of anti-inflammatory medication and left ventricular ejection fraction of < 35%. Thoracoscopic AF ablation was performed as described previously, and consisted of pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) in patients with paroxysmal AF, and PVI plus a superior line and a trigone line in case of persistent AF [17]. During the procedure, we tested all ablation lines for bidirectional block. We did not use an electro-anatomic mapping system (and did not create an activation map) on the operation room [17]. Unless contraindicated, the left atrial appendage (LAA) was excised in all patients. AF classification was based on the ESC guidelines for the management of AF [18]. AF was documented on ECG or Holter within the 12 months preceding the first presentation. For this study, patients were categorized into two groups: those with paroxysmal AF and those with persistent AF (including longstanding-persistent AF). We will hereafter refer to these groups as the different AF types. We assessed AF recurrence at the 6-month follow-up (FU) mark, as this was when the second CT-scan took place and was thus more suitable for analysis on the change of CT-EAT characteristics than outcome at one year. However, we report one-year outcome as a secondary outcome. Patients were followed-up in accordance with the current guidelines and the HRS/EHRA/ECAS consensus document [19], and includes visits to the outpatient clinic, ECGs and 24 h-Holters at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months after the ablation. MetS variables hypertension and diabetes were extracted, as well as the visceral fat depot CT-EAT-volume, which was shown to be a proxy for overall VAT, as EAT independently and accurately correlated with intra-abdominal VAT, better than waist circumference does [20]. The MARK AF registry was approved by the Amsterdam University Medical Center medical ethical committee and was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent.

Histological data retrieval and analysis

The entire basis of the LAA was stored in formaldehyde for histopathological analysis. Serial 5 µm sections were stained in batches per 40 patients, with hematoxylin–eosin for assessment of adipocytes, and immunohistochemically for assessment of neutrophils (myeloperoxidase, MPO), proinflammatory macrophages type 1 (CD68), macrophage type 2, generally interpretated as anti-inflammatory (CD163), leukocytes (CD45), and T-lymphocytes (CD3). Details on how immunohistochemical stainings and inflammatory cell quantifications were performed are presented in the appendix. The percentage of interstitial collagen, hereafter referred to as fibrosis (red) and myocardium (yellow) were quantified by picrosirius red (PSR) staining (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Sections were digitized at a 40 × magnification, 0.25 μm/pixel (Philips IntelliSite Ultra Fast Scanner), and analyzed in Qpath 3.2 [21]. EAT was defined as the area between the visceral epicardium and myocardium. Adipocytes were quantified in QuPath using a deep learning algorithm called Cellpose [22], trained in house with manually annotated images of EAT adipocyte sections (Supplementary Fig. S1b).

CT data retrieval and analysis

Detailed information regarding CT data analysis and EAT quantification is available in the appendix. In summary, scans were acquired using a third generation dual source CT-scanner (Siemens SOMATOM Force). Since body surface area (BSA) reflects cardiac dimensions [23], we indexed atrial and total CT-EAT-volume based on BSA, as done before [24]. As patients were scanned with different kV beam settings, we applied a kV correction based on coefficient derived from results from [25]. Atrial- and total EAT-volumes were semi-manually segmented in the research software QFAT version 2.0, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center [26]. EAT-attenuation was determined by averaging all pixels of the whole atrial EAT-volume and of the total EAT-volume (i.e. atrial plus ventricular EAT-volumes). EAT-volumes from 30 patients were assessed for inter-person variability by a second researcher (TvdB). Discrepancies were resolved by an experienced cardiovascular radiologist (NP).

Statistics

Detailed information regarding the statistical analyses is available in the appendix. In short, differences in clinical, histology and CT-scan parameters were tested between AF types, as well as between patients with and without AF recurrence, employing tests based on normality/non-normality. Due to skewed distribution, fibrosis was first log/transformed before statistical analyses. We assessed the potential diagnostic value of inflammatory cells and CT-EAT in differentiating AF outcomes through ROC analysis. To evaluate potential confounders influencing the relationship between CT-EAT-attenuation, histological findings, and AF outcomes, we conducted multivariable logistic and linear regression analyses, correcting for baseline variables that demonstrated a significant association (p < 0.05) with AF outcomes in univariate regression. After log-transforming skewed variables, the correlation between both CT-EAT-attenuations and volumes and inflammatory histological parameters was tested by Pearsons’ r. Data are presented as mean ± SD, median [IQR], or n (%). Inter-observer reliability for CT-EAT-volumes was determined using a two-way mixed intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 28.0, and figures were created in GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 160 patients underwent thoracoscopic AF ablation from 2017 to 2020, of whom 141 received a CT-scan both before and 6.5 ± 0.7 months after the procedure. Seven patients had incomplete scans or scan settings deviating from the standard, for example slice thickness > 1 mm, an undetectable pericardium, and significant artefacts of pacemaker leads, leaving 134 patients eligible for this analysis. Among the included patients, 107 had persistent and 27 paroxysmal AF (Table 1). Fifteen patients had a previous unsuccessful AF ablation. Patients with persistent compared to paroxysmal AF had a larger indexed left atrial volume (LAVI) 44.7[20.5] versus 42.7[17.3] ml/m2 (p = 0.04), and higher NT-proBNP levels 566.0[693.0] versus 170.5[293.0]ng/L, (p < 0.001) (Table 1). At 6 and 12 months after ablation, 27 (20.1%) and 44 (32.8%) patients had at least one AF recurrence, respectively. Recurrence rate was similar between AF types. Patients who developed AF recurrence were older 67.8 ± 6.4 versus 61.8 ± 7.8 years, (p < 0.001), were more often female 16(59.0%) versus 26(24.3%), (p = 0.001), had a higher serum CRP 2.8[6.2] versus 0.9[1.5] mg/L, (p = 0.02) and serum leukocyte count 8.1[2.7] versus 6.7[1.7]10E9/L, (p = 0.001) (Supplementary Table S1). In those with recurrences, a minor trend was visible towards higher number of patients with hypertension 16/27(59%) versus 44/107(41%) p = 0.09, and diabetes 3/27(11%) versus 5/107(4.7%), p = 0.21, respectively. These conditions were similar across AF types. At baseline, none of the patients used SGLT2 inhibitors or MRAs, and only two (one with and one without recurrence) used spironolactone at follow-up.

EAT inflammation relates to AF types

First, we assessed the relation between inflammatory cells in the atrial EAT and AF type. In patients with persistent compared to paroxysmal AF, the median number of neutrophils in the atrial EAT was higher and CD163 + macrophages lower; MPO +: 155[257] versus 63[106]cells/mm2, (p = 0.003), and CD163 +: 126[134] versus 224[178]cells/mm2, (p = 0.03) (Figs. 1, 2a and b). The numbers of CD68 + macrophages, leukocytes, and T-lymphocytes were similar between AF types (Table 2). Generally, neither CD68 + nor CD163 + cells co-localized with MPO + cells in serial sections, underscoring that MPO + CD68-/CD163- cells are indeed neutrophils. In patients with persistent and paroxysmal AF the mean adipocyte size was 1602 ± 461 versus 1610 ± 322 μm2, (p = 0.60), respectively.

EAT inflammatory cell counts, adipocyte size and fibrosis in relation to AF outcomes

After multivariable regression, neutrophil count remained higher in persistent versus paroxysmal AF, expB:1.01, CI 1.00–1.02, (p = 0.04), Supplementary Table S2. The AUC curve for neutrophil count in differentiating persistent from paroxysmal AF was 0.75 (p value < 0.001, CI 0.63–0.87, optimal cut-off value 44 neutrophils/mm2).

There was no significant difference in the percentage of fibrosis observed in either EAT or myocardium between patients with persistent and paroxysmal AF; 21.6 ± 15.0 versus 20.3 ± 12.0%, (p = 0.93, after log-transformation), and 9.2 ± 5.2 versus 9.1 ± 4.3%, (p = 0.90).

Most inflammatory cells in the EAT were associated with smaller adipocytes and myocardial fibrosis (Supplementary Table S3).

EAT inflammation in relation to atrial CT-EAT-volume and attenuation

Next, we assessed if EAT inflammation markers related to CT-EAT-volume and attenuation. Lower counts of EAT neutrophils and EAT adipocyte hypertrophy were associated with increased CT-EAT-volumes; r =− 0.29, (p = 0.004), and r = 0.28, (p = 0.005), respectively (Fig. 3a, c, Supplementary Table S3). Interestingly, a higher neutrophils count and a smaller adipocyte size were associated with a higher CT-EAT-attenuation r = 0.26, (p = 0.007), (r = − 0.20, p = 0.03) (Fig. 3b, d, Supplementary Table S3). After multivariable linear regression analysis, the association between CT-EAT-attenuation and neutrophil count persisted: B1.84, CI 0.06–3.6, (p = 0.04). There was no association between CT-EAT-volume or attenuation and EAT- or myocardial fibrosis.

AF type relates to CT-EAT-attenuation

CT-EAT-volume was 16.1 ± 7.3 versus 14.4 ± 5.9 ml/m2, (p = 0.15) in patients with persistent and paroxysmal AF, respectively (Fig. 4a), while CT-EAT-attenuation was significantly higher in patients with persistent than paroxysmal AF; − 73.0 ± 4.6 versus − 75.3 ± 5.3HU, (p = 0.03), and − 76.2 ± 4.5 versus − 78.4 ± 5.1HU, (p = 0.03) (Figs. 4b, 5a, b). After multivariable regression analysis, continuous atrial CT-EAT-attenuation remained significantly higher in persistent than in paroxysmal AF: expB:1.16, CI 1.03–1.32, (p = 0.02). The AUC for distinguishing between persistent and paroxysmal AF was modest with 0.63, (p = 0.04) with an optimal atrial CT-EAT-attenuation cut-off point of -77HU. Incorporating this cut-off value yielded an ExpB of 6.8, CI: 1.8–24.9, (p = 0.004) after multivariable analysis.

AF recurrence relates to adipocyte hypertrophy

Inflammatory cell counts were similar, while adipocytes were hypertrophic; 1780[451] versus 1582[398] μm2, (p = 0.008), in patients with versus without AF recurrence (Fig. 6, Table 2). Adipocyte hypertrophy remained associated with recurrence after performing multivariable regression analysis: ExpB:1.002, CI 1.00–1.003, (p = 0.02), Supplementary Table S4a and S4b. Percentage fibrosis of the EAT was 13.9[15.8] versus 18.9[20.6]%, (p = 0.29), and of the myocardium 9.0[7.3] versus 7.9[6.0]%, (p = 0.52), in patients with versus without AF recurrence, respectively.

Baseline atrial CT-EAT-volume was 16.7 ± 7.7 versus 15.4 ± 7.1 ml/m2, (p = 0.38), and total CT-EAT-volume was 61.8 ± 21.9 versus 58.9 ± 22.0 ml/m2, (p = 0.52), in patients with versus without AF recurrence, respectively. Atrial attenuation was − 74.5 ± 5.0 versus − 73.2 ± 4.8HU, (p = 0.21), and total CT-EAT-attenuation was − 78.2 ± 5.2 versus − 76 ± 4.5HU, (p = 0.055), respectively for recurrence versus non-recurrence, however after correcting for age and sex, expB was 0.98, CI 0.89–1.09, p = 0.78 (Supplementary Fig. S2a–d). CT-EAT-attenuation was higher in males (− 72.9 ± 4.7HU) than females (− 74.6 ± 4.8HU), (p = 0.05).

Hypertension and diabetes in relation to atrial EAT inflammation and CT variables

Since there was a minor trend between AF recurrence and both MetS conditions hypertension and diabetes, we assessed whether these also relate to EAT inflammation and CT-EAT-volume and/or attenuation.

Hypertensive AF patients (60/134) showed a trend towards a larger atrial CT-EAT-volume than non-hypertensive patients, 17.0 ± 7.6 versus 14.6 ± 6.7 ml/m2 (p = 0.06), and towards lower atrial CT-EAT-attenuation − 74.2 ± 5.3 versus − 72.8 ± 4.4HU, (p = 0.10), respectively. Their CD163 macrophage count was lower 112.9[135.9] versus 171.9[158.9] cells/mm2, (p = 0.008), while CD68 count and other local inflammatory markers were similar, as were CRP levels. Adipocyte size was larger in hypertensive patients 1762 ± 388.6 versus 1564.0 ± 330.1 μm2, (p = 0.002).

Diabetic AF patients (9/134) showed a trend towards a larger atrial CT-EAT-volume versus non-diabetics 17.9 ± 7.9 versus 15.5 ± 7.2 ml/m2, (p = 0.07), respectively, a significantly lower atrial CT-EAT-attenuation, − 76.5 ± 5.2 versus − 73.2 ± 4.7HU, (p = 0.05) and a strong trend towards increased CD68 macrophages 174.9[301.8] versus 102.8[132.3] cells/mm2, p = 0.06. CD163 macrophages were increased 253.1[292.2] versus 140.6[161.1] cells/mm2, as well as CD45 leukocytes 217.1[224.7] versus 145.1[141.1] cells/mm2, p < 0.05. There was a trend towards increased CD3 T-cells 76.8[114.9] versus 38.8[56.3] cells/mm2, p = 0.07. Neutrophil count and CRP were similar. Adipocytes were larger 2041.3 ± 369.0 versus 1625 ± 355.8 μm2, (p = 0.003).

Change in CT-EAT-volumes and attenuation during follow-up

At follow-up, atrial CT-EAT-volume decreased from 16.6 ± 7.0 to 11.59 ± 4.65 ml/m2 and total CT-EAT-volume from 59.5 ± 21.8 to 52.2 ± 19.3 ml/m2, (p < 0.001). At the same time, CT-EAT-attenuations increased from − 73.8 ± 4.8 to − 71.3 ± 5.3HU, and from − 76.6 ± 4.7 to − 74.9 ± 5.0HU, (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. S2a–d). The change of both CT-EAT-volume and attenuation was irrespective of developing AF recurrence within 6 months after the procedure.

Discussion

This study shows that patients with persistent AF have more neutrophils and fewer CD163 + macrophages in their left atrial EAT compared to patients with paroxysmal AF, suggesting that EAT inflammatory changes, are linked to the progression of AF. Second, neutrophils and adipocyte hypertrophy, at least partially account for the higher CT-EAT-attenuation. While in this study the diagnostic performance of CT-EAT-attenuation for AF type was significant but modest, it may be a promising future marker for EAT neutrophils and AF type. Nevertheless, this requires further research, particularly in patients with earlier stages of AF. Importantly, we describe distinctly different relations of histological and CT features between patients with persistent versus paroxysmal AF, and those with versus without AF recurrence after thoracoscopic ablation. This emphasizes that distinct mechanisms may underlie the types of AF and the recurrence of AF. While only a trend was visible between recurrence risk and both hypertension and diabetes, adipocyte hypertrophy was present in all three and could be a common EAT mechanism underlying these conditions.

The role of EAT inflammation in AF types

Neutrophils release MPO, which contributes to inflammation both directly and through NETosis, attenuating attraction of more inflammatory cells. Previously it was shown that MPO secretion and NETosis are increased in the atria, and especially in the EAT, of patients with persistent AF compared to those without AF [9, 27]. Furthermore, MPO was increased in atrial EAT long before the onset of AF, suggesting MPO deposition is primarily a cause and not the consequence of AF [8, 28]. One could argue that if the EAT is inflamed, the myocardium would show signs of inflammation as well. However, in our previous study with patients of the same cohort, there was no difference in myocardial neutrophil count between patients with persistent and paroxysmal AF.

We observed a lower number of CD163 + and a similar number of CD68 + macrophages in the EAT of patients with persistent versus paroxysmal AF. CD163 + macrophages belong to the type 2 macrophage category and have both anti-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic properties [29]. This could be consistent with a continuous inflammatory cycle between increased neutrophils and decreased anti-inflammatory macrophages in our patient cohort [30]. The numbers of most inflammatory cell types were associated with a smaller adipocyte size (Supplementary Table S3). The release of cytokines can lead to the decrease of adipocyte size, which may explain the decreased adipocyte size in an inflammatory EAT environment [31, 13, 32].

The number of EAT neutrophils, which was increased in patients with persistent versus paroxysmal AF, was not associated with the extent of EAT or myocardial fibrosis in our study. Previously we showed that neutrophils co-localize with fibrofatty EAT to myocardial infiltrations, more pronounced in persistent than paroxysmal AF. This may indicate that not the total amount of myocardial fibrosis but specific areas of disrupted myocardial conduction affect the substrate for AF [9]. This could also explain the absence of a difference in global fibrosis percentage between AF types (Table 2). Importantly, myocardial fibrosis affects conduction heterogeneity in AF [33], but the association between LA fibrosis and AF type is inconsistent [34]. Ishii et al. found more EAT and myocardial fibrosis in patients with persistent (14.5%) than paroxysmal AF (11%) [35]. However, their patients had less progressed AF compared to our cohort, with mostly persistent AF patients, enlarged left atria and several previously failed ablations. This may underlie the limited differences in fibrosis between patients with persistent and paroxysmal AF in our cohort, as shown previously [33].

However, a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with a history of previous ablations did not alter the relation between AF outcomes and EAT fibrosis, inflammation or attenuation (data not shown).

Non-invasive imaging by CT associates with EAT inflammation

We found that increased attenuation of EAT on CT-scans was associated with an increased number of neutrophils and decreased size of adipocytes. Previously, Antoniades et al. found no relation between CT-EAT-attenuation and tissue infiltration of macrophages (CD68 +), consistent with our findings [31]. We also confirm the finding of Antoniades et al. that smaller adipocytes associate with a higher CT-EAT-attenuation. After multivariable regression, only EAT neutrophil count remained associated with atrial CT-EAT-attenuation, suggesting that neutrophils are a more important determinant for increased attenuation than adipocyte size or fibrosis. The increased neutrophil infiltration in the EAT from persistent AF patients may be a sign of vessel leakage. Interestingly, myocardial interstitial edema caused by leaky vessels increased susceptibility for atrial arrhythmias in murine models [36].

AF type relates to attenuation on CT-scans

We demonstrated that atrial CT-EAT-attenuation was higher in patients with persistent AF compared to paroxysmal AF. Previous studies showed that atrial EAT-attenuation was higher in patients with AF versus those without, with absolute attenuation values similar to reported here, while subcutaneous AT was unaffected by AF presence, suggesting that the fat changes in AF are EAT specific [15, 37, 38]. The difference in attenuation between AF types is much smaller than that between patients with and without AF, suggesting that most of EAT remodeling occurs before the onset of AF.

In our study, atrial CT-EAT-volume was similar among AF types, in line with the findings of Vroomen et al. [24], despite the enlarged LAVI’s (with more surface available for EAT) in patients with persistent AF. It was shown earlier that a larger LA correlated negatively with atrial-EAT thickness in echocardiograms from patients with persistent AF, whereas this relation was positive in those with paroxysmal AF. This may be due to a reduction of EAT thickness through extended EAT fibro-fatty remodeling following AF progression [6, 39]. Other than patient characteristics, heterogeneity in CT-scans and quantification techniques could explain discrepancies in study outcomes on EAT [40].

AF recurrence relates to adipocyte hypertrophy

Contrary to AF type, adipocyte hypertrophy was associated with AF recurrence, while inflammatory cells were not associated with recurrence after AF ablation (Table 2, Supplementary Tables S4a and b). Also,in persistent AF there was a trend towards decreased instead of increased CT-EAT-attenuation, especially for total EAT. However, this was omitted after correcting for age and sex. Earlier studies either confirm the absence or show the presence of a relation between CT-EAT-attenuation and AF recurrence after ablation [41−43].

Our findings indicate that, although there is a correlation between persistent AF and AF ablation failure, the relation between EAT and AF type is a different one than between EAT and presence or absence of AF recurrence. Interestingly, patients with AF recurrence did exhibit larger adipocytes, also after correction for age and sex and other confounders. Adipocyte hypertrophy has been linked to alterations in fat metabolism but also to increased secretion of inflammatory adipokines, and a decreased secretion of anti-inflammatory adipokines. Several of these factors are involved in MetS diseases, such as leptin in diabetes and angiotensin in hypertension [44, 45, 46]. Adipocyte hypertrophy decreases adipocytes’ sensitivity to insulin, causing diabetes [47]. Furthermore, adipocyte hypertrophy also causes hypoxia., which causes secretion of proinflammatory proteins, which may directly affect arrhythmogenic processes such as TNF-a [48], but also secrete proteins that recruits both inflammatory (CD68), but also anti-inflammatory (CD163) macrophages to the EAT, the latter as part of the repair response to adipocyte hypertrophy [49]. Subsequently, the newly infiltrated macrophages may further contribute to inflammation. Interestingly, this study showed that both AF patients with hypertension and with diabetes have hypertrophic adipocytes. As those patients with recurrence have a trend towards having more of both diabetes and hypertension, adipocyte hypertrophy may connect recurrences, diabetes and hypertension. Furthermore, increased adipocyte size may implicate an increased number of white hypertrophic, and decreased number of protective brown and/or beige fat cells [50]. Alternatively, the size of adipocytes in EAT could be a reflection instead of cause of increased risk for AF recurrences. For instance, the heart-specific miRNA-208a, (re)activated during cardiac stress as in heart failure, induces myocyte hypertrophy and negatively affects cardiac function in mice [51]. Interestingly, mice treated with antimiR-208a for several weeks appeared leaner compared to those treated with a control medium, and notably, their adipocytes in both visceral AT and brown AT were considerably smaller [52]. It can be hypothesized that, upon ablation, improvement of myocardial function may reduce EAT adipocyte size through changed myocardial secretion of components such as miRNA-208a. However, this remains speculative, and we did not evaluate components in the myocardial secretome.

Consistent with our results, previous studies have also shown lower attenuation in women compared to men and an increase in attenuation with advancing age [25, 53]. Earlier findings suggest sex-related differences in the regulation mechanism of pericardial adipokines and adipose tissue (AT) physiology. This may be reflected by the attenuations measured here [54, 55].

In patients with persistent AF, interaction between trigger and substrate is more complex than in paroxysmal AF. Thus, the EAT markers used to differentiate persistent AF from paroxysmal AF may not necessarily be the same as those employed to distinguish patients with recurrence from those without.

We observed a decrease in CT-EAT-volume and an increase in attenuation 6 months after ablation, suggesting reversed remodeling of EAT, irrespective of AF type or recurrence status (Supplementary Fig. S3). We acknowledge that this is a non-interventional observation without evident causal consequences but speculate that the unexpected increase in attenuation may be attributed to a reduction in adipocyte hypertrophy or a shift from proinflammatory white adipocytes to protective brown adipocytes. Evidently, we have no tissue available at 6 months after the procedure for histological validation of these suggested mechanisms. Alternatively, we may speculate that adipocytes may decrease in size through changed levels of cardiokines after improvement of cardiac contractility during sinus rhythm after the procedure. Future studies are required to assess the relation between actual rhythm status and EAT adipocyte volume after ablation procedures. Furthermore, atrial EAT could have been manipulated by radiofrequent (RF) epicardial ablation. RF, commonly used in both endo and epicardial ablation [56], may indirectly reduce EAT-volume through thermal injury and may induce fibrotic or inflammatory remodeling, as shown in animal models [57, 58]. Furthermore, ablation may reduce EAT-volume through either thermal effects, microvascular disruption, or ablation-induced inflammatory mechanisms, or reduction in AF episodes [59]. However, in our study, a sub-analysis on the ventricular EAT specifically showed that the volume and attenuation of the ventricular EAT changed in a similar manner and in the same direction compared to that from atrial EAT (data not shown SGLT2 inhibitors or MRAs could induce EAT changes, and therefore potentially could have affected the EAT outcomes in our study. However, our cohort contained patients with stand-alone AF without heart-failure; None of the patients used SGLT2 inhibitors or MRAs at baseline, and only two patients used spironolactone at 6 months of follow-up. Therefore, these medications could not have affected EAT changes in our cohort. Furthermore, CT-EAT changes did not differ between patients with and without AF recurrence. This may implicate either a significant reduction in AF burden irrespective of AF recurrence resulting in similar EAT changes for all patients, or, alternatively, no effect of eliminating AF on EAT composition or volume.

Limitations

While specific mechanisms through which neutrophils affect the AF substrate were not investigated, we previously showed anincrease in EAT markers involved in oxidative stress and lipolysis (processes known to attract neutrophils [9]) in patients without AF who developed AF in the future compared to patients without future AF.

Small adipocytes in patients without recurrences observed in our study and the increase in attenuation after the procedure could both potentially indicate a higher proportion of browning of adipose tissue. Unfortunately, there are no histological samples from patients 6 months after surgery. This limits our ability to determine the long-term effects of treating AF on the histology EAT composition and reversed remodeling of EAT.

We performed our study in a thoracoscopic AF ablation cohort with the great advantage of the availability of left atrial tissue for histological analysis. However, this cohort also comes with the potential limitation of being not fully representative to all AF ablation cohorts comprising less advanced AF patients. While we fully adhered to the rhythm follow-up guidelines, sporadic AF episodes could have gone undetected, resulting in an underestimation of AF episodes. However, we do not expect this to affect our outcomes.

CT parameters such as kV selection impacts EAT-attenuation and therefore indirectly the relation between EAT-attenuation and AF. In our study kV differed between patients due to automatic kV selection based on factors as body composition, requiring kV correction. To overcome this issue, we derived a kV correction factor from results based on pericoronary EAT, which may slightly differ in composition compared to the larger EAT depot that we assessed. However, Okayama et al. showed a similar trend for total EAT [60]. While it would be important to validate a correction coefficient for atrial EAT, we expect the periconary-based correction factor applicable to atrial EAT as well.

The absence of parameters reflecting MetS including triglycerides and HDL, and also weight circumference, limited our ability to assess the involvement of MetS in EAT inflammation, volume and attenuation of AF patients. However, as EAT was shown to be a successful surrogate marker for abdominal visceral fat, weight circumference was indirectly assessed.

Clinical implications

While the diagnostic performance of CT-EAT-attenuation for distinguishing AF types and its relation to neutrophils is noteworthy yet moderate, it could be a promising future marker for detecting EAT neutrophil infiltration and AF types. Nevertheless, this necessitates further investigation among patients with less advanced AF and consequently a less pronounced substrate. Additionally, the observed increased neutrophil invasion and decreased presence of anti-inflammatory macrophages in the EAT suggest their potential role in the progression of AF. Better understanding the inflammatory mechanisms underlying AF could lead to the development of novel therapeutic targets.

Conclusion

Patients with persistent AF exhibit a higher count of neutrophils and a lower count of CD163 + macrophages in their EAT compared to patients with paroxysmal AF. A higher CT-EAT-attenuation was associated with both the abundance of infiltrated neutrophils and AF type. These findings give insight in the relation between EAT inflammation and AF. The role of CT-EAT-attenuation in AF progression is promising, nonetheless, it requires further investigation in patients with less advanced AF. Adipocyte hypertrophy was present in those with AF recurrence, hypertension and diabetes, and may potentially be involved in a common EAT mechanism underlying these conditions, potentially linking MetS to AF recurrence.
