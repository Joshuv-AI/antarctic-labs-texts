Assessment of hepatic steatosis during rat liver regeneration after 70% partial hepatectomy using stereology and MRI-PDFF
Authors: Andrea Lund, Uffe Kjaergaard, Lotte Bertelsen, Esben Søvsø Hansen, Katrine Holm Andersen, Steffen Ringgaard, Jens Randel Nyengaard, Christoffer Laustsen, Frank Viborg Mortensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPartial hepatectomy (PH) is the primary treatment for liver malignancies, leveraging the liver’s regenerative capacity and metabolic potential. Transient hepatic steatosis is a common occurrence following PH. This study aimed to evaluate the utility of MRI-derived proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) for assessing hepatic lipid content in the regenerating rat liver by correlating it with stereological lipid quantification, used as a histological reference standard. Rats were randomly assigned to 70%PH (n = 6) or no surgery (n = 6) and evaluated at postoperative day 1. Regeneration ratio was calculated, and lipid-specific biochemistry was measured. Hepatocyte proliferation and volume, as well as lipid volume per hepatocyte, were estimated using stereological analyses. Proton density fat fraction was quantified using MRI. Compared to controls, the PH group showed increased regeneration ratio, hepatocyte volume, and cell proliferation. Hepatic lipid accumulation was elevated, as demonstrated by both stereology and MRI. A strong positive correlation was observed between stereological lipid volume per hepatocyte and MRI-PDFF. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were reduced in the PH group. This study demonstrates that stereological analysis and MRI-PDFF are complementary and reliable methods for quantifying hepatic steatosis in the regenerating rat liver. Their strong correlation supports MRI-PDFF for non-invasive, longitudinal monitoring.Keywords: 70% partial hepatectomy; Liver regeneration; MRI, hepatic steatosis, stereologySubject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Gastroenterology, Medical research

Introduction

Partial hepatectomy (PH) remains the fundamental strategy in curative treatment of liver malignancies, owing to the liver’s unique ability to regenerate and its excess metabolic capacity. The primary challenge of surgical treatment of liver cancer is ensuring adequate and efficient liver regeneration to restore function after surgery1,2. Understanding the metabolic regenerative dynamics following PH offers valuable insights for developing new strategies to support liver regeneration and avoid post-hepatectomy liver failure3,4.

Liver regeneration is a complex process, driven by hepatocyte hypertrophy alone or, in the case of larger resections, through a combination of hypertrophy and cell division5,6. Following PH, the remaining hepatocytes must sustain essential metabolic functions while simultaneously supporting the energy-intensive process of liver regeneration. This dual demand imposes a significant metabolic burden, leading, among other things, to adaptive reprogramming of lipid metabolism, including alterations in lipid uptake, synthesis, and breakdown7. As a result, transient steatosis develops, characterized by dynamic changes in lipid accumulation8–10.

Emerging evidence suggests that steatosis following PH may play a critical role in liver regeneration by supplying essential lipids as structural components, energy substrates, and signalling molecules required for cell proliferation11–16. However, the precise interplay between lipid accumulation and hepatocyte proliferation during liver regeneration remains insufficiently understood.

Unlike chronic hepatic steatosis, which is marked by sustained triglyceride accumulation and metabolic dysregulation17, steatosis following PH is a dynamic and transient process, involving not only changes in lipid composition and volume but also dynamic shifts in cell size and cell number, necessitating a more nuanced approach to lipid quantification than in chronic liver disease.

Stereology is a well-established method for quantitatively assessing volume fractions of cellular components with high precision and reproducibility, and it has been widely used for quantifying hepatic steatosis in chronic liver disease18–20. However, its invasive nature limits its application for longitudinal studies evaluating temporal changes, underscoring the need for a complementary, non-invasive technique to enable continuous assessment of lipid accumulation in the same subject during liver regeneration.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the clinical gold standard for non-invasive quantification of hepatic steatosis using proton density fat fraction (PDFF)21–23, and it is widely used in clinical trials to monitor metabolic associated fatty liver disease23. While previous studies have correlated MRI-PDFF with histology24 and stereology18 in chronic liver disease, histological methods are prone to sampling bias, and chronic liver disease differs fundamentally from steatosis following PH in its pathophysiology, owing to the rapid and dynamic chances in hepatocyte size, number, and metabolic function following liver resection. These characteristics introduce complexities absent in chronic steatosis and highlight the necessity for independent validation of MRI-PDFF in the context of steatosis following PH.

This study aimed to evaluate the utility of MRI-PDFF for assessing hepatic lipid content in the regenerating rat liver by correlating it with stereological lipid quantification, used as a histological reference standard. Establishing this correlation provides a foundation for future studies exploring the regulation of lipid metabolism during liver regeneration – leveraging stereology for high-resolution cellular insights and MRI for non-invasive, longitudinal monitoring.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Danish Animal Experiments Inspectorate under the permit number 2021-15-0201–00978 and conducted in accordance with the US National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals25. Reporting adheres to the ARRIVE guidelines26. Previous animal studies have also been conducted under this permit12,27,28.

Healthy, 8-week-old male Wistar rats (Janvier Labs, Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France) with a mean pre-operative weight of 301 g (range: 242–356 g) were housed in standard animal laboratoy facilities. Animals where maintained at 23 °C under a 12-hour light-dark cycle, with unrestricted access to food and water, and remained in these facilities throught the study.

Animal welfare was monitored using the General Distress Score, a standardized quantitative scoring system used to assess the overall well-being of the animals27,29. Humane endpoints were predefined by a score of 10 or higher, as this is generally considered indicative of a critical condition27,29.

All animals were continuously monitored for the first 3 h following surgery, and subsequently monitored and scored at least twice daily. Animals reaching a threshold of 10 or higher were euthanised. Euthanasia was performed under inhalation anasthesia by cervical dislocation, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

Experimental design and surgical procedure

Twelve rats were randomized to either 70% PH (n = 6) or no surgery (n = 6). One animal in the PH group was euthanized prior to evaluation due to biting through its sutures. To maintain a total of six animals per group, one additional animal was subjected to PH.

In our previous work, the surgical30 and the anaesthetic27 procedures have been described in detail. These established protocols were applied in our current study without modification. Selected descriptions are reused with proper attribution. In brief, inhalation anaesthesia was initially induced in a glass champer and subsequently maintained via a rodent mask using a mixture of oxygen 0.3 L/min, nitrous oxide (0.15 L/min), and sevoflurane (3.5%, Baxter Medical, Kista, Sweden)27. A 70% PH was then performed by resecting the median and left lateral lobes, leaving a liver remnant consisting of the two right lobes and the two caudate lobes30.

Prior to euthanisation at postoperative day (POD) 1, MRI scans were conducted, blood samples were collected, and the liver remnant was harvested. Animals where euthanised by cervical dislocation under anaestesia.

Stereology

The posterior caudate lobe was prepared for stereological evaluations using an in-house protocol previously published28,31. This established method was applied without modification, and the subsequent descriptions of immunohistochemical staining and stereological procedures include reused content from previously published work, cited accordingly. Ki-67 (monoclonal mouse anti-rat Ki-67 specific antibody, clone 30–9; Ventana Medical Systems, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) was used as a proliferation marker, and beta-catenin (anti-beta-catenin antibody, clone β-Catenin-1; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, U.S.) as a hepatocyte cell surface marker28. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. All sections were azamined by the same blinded investigator using an Olympus BX50 microscope equipped for stereology with a motorized stage (Märzhäuser Wetzlar MFD, Wetzlar, Germany) and a digital camera (Olympus DP73, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), connected to a computer running newCAST version 2020.01.4.8088 software (Visiopharm, Hørsholm, Denmark). Microscopy was performed using a 60× oil objective lens (N.A., 1.30)28.

A 2D unbiased counting frame (65 × 45 µm2) was used to sample hepatocyte nuclei profiles, in, on average, 90 systematically uniformly and randomly selected fields of view per animal (Fig. 1). A proliferating hepatocyte was defined as a hepatocyte with a counterstained red oval nucleus. The 3D isotropic nucleator was applied on, on average, 270 hepatocytes with a beta-catenin-stained brown cell membrane per animal (three hepatocytes in each randomly selected field) to assess hepatocyte volume. A 6 × 4-point grid was used to estimate the lipid content within a hepatocyte. The lipid volume per hepatocyte was determined by multiplying the volume fraction of lipid per hepatocyte with the average hepatocyte volume.

Stereological probes applied to sections from a PH animal (1A) and a control animal (1B). A 2D counting frame (shown as red and green lines) was used to quantify proliferating hepatocyte profiless, visualized by red Ki-67-stained nucleus. Hepatocyte volume was estimated with the 3D isotropic nucleator (blue lines intersecting the center of the measured hepatocyte). Finally, a point grid (black cross marks) assessed the volume fraction of lipid per hepatocyte, with clear circular voids reflecting lipid droplets. Dark brow circular voids in 1B reflect nuclear localization of beta-catenin, a phenomenon occasionally seen in rodent liver tissue which may be caused by certain mutations.

For each section, the mean number of proliferating hepatocyte profiles, hepatocyte volume, and lipid content per hepatocyte were measured before ratio-of-sums for the three serial sections was calculated. Counting rules for positive Ki-67 hepatocytes profiles28 and lipid content per hepatocyte profile have been described previously32.

MRI

Prior to MRI scans on POD 1, rats were anesthetized with 2–3% sevoflurane. Monitoring of vital parameters (respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and temperature) was performed using an MRI compatible Small Animal instrument (SA Instruments, Stony Brook, NY). All MRI scans were performed on a 3 Tesla clinical scanner (GE, Healthcare, Brøndby, DK).

During free breathing, images were acquired using a 16-channel small flex coil (GE, Healthcare). For quantification of PDFF, a 3D IDEAL IQ with multiple echo times was acquired (FOV = 180 × 180 mm, matrix = 128 × 96, section thickness = 4 mm, TR = 11.1 ms, TE = 1.4/2.8/3.2/4.4/5.6/6.8 msec, flip angle = 4° degrees, number of averages = 8). PDFF, R2*, Water, and Fat maps were reconstructed on the scanner workstation.

Data and image analysis

For proton density fat fraction, regions of interest (ROIs) were drawn on water T1-weighted (IDEAL) images in the caudate liver lobe, using in-house developed software (Siswin version 0.95). To avoid motion and partial volume artifacts, ROIs were placed at a distance from the liver edges. Three ROIs were drawn on three adjacent slices and averaged. The ROIs were then copied to the PDFF maps. Reported mean values are averages from these three ROIs.

Biochemistry

Blood samples were processed, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and triglyceride were measured using an immunoassay and clinical chemistry analyser (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Inc., Erlangen, Germany). International normalized ratio (INR) was measured using an automated blood coagulation analyser (Sysmex CS-2100i, Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol was estimated from the Friedewald Eq.33.

Statistical analyses

All variables were tested for normality using histograms and QQ-plots. When normality was violated, data were log-transformed, analysed, and then back-transformed. Group comparisons were conducted using an unpaired t-test. Parametric data are presented as means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while non-parametric data are presented as median values with 95% CIs. P-values of < 0.05 are considered significant. A Spearman rank-order correlation was conducted to evaluate the association between lipid volume by hepatocyte measured by light microscopy and fat fraction assessed by MRI. All analyses were carried out using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, U.S.). Graphs were performed in GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0 for Mac (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA).

Discussion

This study investigated lipid accumulation and hepatocyte dynamics in the regenerating rat liver following 70% PH, compared to non-surgically treated controls. By combining stereological analyses and light microscopy as a quantitative method for assessing hepatic steatosis during liver regeneration, MRI, and biochemical measurements, we evaluated the potential of MRI-PDFF as a non-invasive tool for monitoring dynamic lipid changes over time in a rat model of 70% PH.

In the PH group, the regeneration ratio increased to 45%, along with increased hepatocyte volume and proliferation. These findings are consistent with established knowledge that regeneration initiates rapidly after 70% PH in rats30, and that hypertrophy alone is insufficient to restore full liver function and body homeostasis6. Hepatocyte hypertrophy is considered a precursor to cell division, as pre-division growth ensures the accurate distribution of cellular components and supports the viability of daughter cells6. The accumulation of building blocks, such as amino acids, carbohydrates, and lipids, is essential for the regeneration process, as these materials are used to meet the increased demands of cell proliferation and tissue remodeling8,34.

Consistent with this, we observed increased hepatic lipid accumulation in the remnant liver. This phenomenon is well-documented and is thought to result from PH-induced hyperglycemia, leading to systemic lipolysis and the release of free fatty acids (FFAs)35. These FFAs are taken up by hepatocytes and stored as triglycerides, contributing to transient steatosis35. In parallel, PH induces de novo lipogenesis, further increasing hepatic lipid content36. Aside from HDL-cholesterol, plasma lipid levels did not differ significantly between groups in our study. This finding may reflect the rapid hepatic lipid uptake that occurs immediately following PH, a process previously linked to improved recovery after major liver resection32. Alternatively, it is possible that peak changes in circulating lipid species occurred beyond our sampling window, or that key lipid classes – such as FFAs – were not captured, as they were not measured in this study. Although some studies report an increase in FFAs on POD 1 [10], the evidence remains inconsistent, highlighting the need for further investigation into the temporal dynamics of lipid metabolism following PH37.

Previous studies have demonstrated correlations between MRI-PDFF and histological grading of hepatic steatosis38,39. However, conventoinal histology remains semi-quantitative and prone to sampling bias and interobserver variability. In contrast, stereological techniques offer unbiased, quantitative measurements40,41 and is highly sensitive to subtle histological changes39. While one study correlated volumetric liver fat fraction by stereology with MRI-PDFF in chronic liver disease18, the regenerative setting presents a very different pathophysiological landscape – marked by rapid changes in hepatocyte size, number, and metabolic activity. To account for these changes, the present study employed stereological measurements normalized to hepatocyte volume, thereby minimizing confounding effects introduced by variations in cell size and number during regeneration. Due to fundamental methodological differences—both in setting (chronic liver disease versus dynamic regeneration) and in the parameters measured (volumetric liver fat fraction across the liver parenchyma versus lipid volume per hepatocyte)—direct comparisons with previous studies are not feasible.

A strong positive correlation was observed between stereologically derived lipid volume per hepatocyte and MRI-PDFF, indicating that both methodologies reliably detect hepatic lipid accumulation during the early regenerative phase. Nevertheless, quantitative histology might under-estimate very low fat fractions because minute lipid droplets might fall below the optical resolution, however, the use of a 60x oil lens with a NA of 1.30 provides a theoretical resolution of (0.61*550 nm/1.30) = 258 nm and is therefore unlikely to make this happen. The biggest disadvantage of quantitative histology and the use of paraffin sections is tissue shrinkage, which can amount up to 60% on a volume basis and the shrinkage can also differ between various tissue compartments42. By integrating signal over the entire parenchyma, MRI-PDFF maintains linearity down to ~ 1% fat and therefore offers superior sensitivity in the lower range43. Investigators should consider this divergence when interpreting data from mildly steatotic tissues.

While MRI and stereology each confer significant strengths in the study of transient steatosis after PH, several limitations merit consideration. First, intracellular lipid was identified indirectly as optically clear vacuoles in haematoxylin-stained sections 44.; lipid-specific stains (e.g., Oil Red O) or osmium tetroxide postfixation would improve specificity. Second, MRI was acquired during free breathing, which may have introduced motion artefacts. We reduced this risk by prolonged acquisition and signal averaging and by drawing ROIs with a margin from the liver edges; nonetheless, a small over-estimation of PDFF cannot be excluded, although it is likely comparable between groups.

This study demonstrates that 70% PH induces marked changes in hepatocyte proliferation and lipid accumulation, underscoring the highly dynamic nature of steatosis following PH. Unlike the relatively stable lipid deposition seen in chronic liver disease, transient steatosis during regeneration involves rapid shifts in cell size, number, and metabolic activity – requiring more nuanced and dynamic assessment tools. Our findings support the use of stereology as a precise and quantitative method for assessing intracellular lipid content at a single, terminal time point during rat liver regeneration. In contrast, MRI-PDFF emerges as a reliable, non-invasive technique capable of capturing changes in hepatic steatosis over time. Together, these complementary methods provide a powerful framework for investigating the metabolic dynamics of liver regeneration and for evaluating targeted interventions aimed at optimizing regenerative outcomes.
