Heavy metal exposure and dental caries in children and adolescents: a NHANES-based analysis integrating machine learning approaches
Authors: Haibo Shen, Yan Zhou, Jie Lu, Yao Chu, Xiangyin Liu, Jing Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental caries remains a major public health concern among children and adolescents. While established risk factors such as diet and oral hygiene are well recognized, the association between environmental heavy metal exposure and pediatric dental caries is not fully understood. This study examined the relationship between blood heavy metal concentrations and dental caries in a nationally representative pediatric population.MethodsData from 15,194 participants aged 5–19 years were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Dental caries was assessed using the DMFT/S index. Blood lead, cadmium, and mercury concentrations were measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. Among the machine learning models, XGBoost showed the best predictive performance (AUC = 0.75), with cadmium ranking as the most influential feature.ConclusionBlood heavy metal concentrations were significantly associated with dental caries in children and adolescents, suggesting that environmental exposure may be an important correlate of pediatric caries risk.Keywords: Dental caries, Heavy metals, Children, NHANES, Machine learning

Introduction

Dental caries remains one of the most common chronic diseases among children and adolescents globally, with a heavy public health and economic burden [1]. Despite the success of preventive measures such as fluoride application and dietary education in recent years, the prevalence of dental caries still varies significantly between socioeconomic groups and demographic characteristics [2]. A large number of existing studies have focused on the relationship between dietary sugars, poor oral hygiene habits or oral microecological disorders and dental caries, while neglecting the potential contribution of environmental factors, especially heavy metal exposure, to the development of dental caries [3–5].

Heavy metals, such as lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd) and mercury (Hg), are widespread pollutants in the environment with toxic effects on several physiological systems in the human body [6, 7]. There is growing evidence that even at low levels, these heavy metals may promote dental caries through mechanisms such as interfering with enamel mineralization, altering salivary components, or disrupting local oral immune responses. For example, lead exposure is strongly associated with delayed tooth eruption and undermined mineralization and cadmium may affect tooth integrity by interfering with calcium metabolism [8].

However, current epidemiologic studies on the relationship between heavy metals and dental caries generally suffer from methodological shortcomings, including small sample sizes, cross-sectional study designs, and failure to adequately adjust for key confounders such as nutritional intake and socioeconomic status [9]. In addition, the heterogeneity of the effects of heavy metal exposure on different developmental stages (e.g., deciduous vs. permanent dentition) and socioeconomic strata remains insufficiently elucidated. For example, low-income populations may face both more severe environmental exposures and fewer oral healthcare resources, leading to an increased risk of dental caries, whereas higher-income groups may be exposed to more Hg through dietary routes such as seafood, complicating the relationship between socio-economic factors and the effects of heavy metal exposure on oral health [10].

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative study, provides data from standardized clinical examinations and biomarker tests, which can effectively overcome the above methodological shortcomings. This study proposes to systematically explore the relationship between blood heavy metal levels and caries risk in children and adolescents using NHANES data from 1999 to 2020, and strictly control potential confounding variables such as socioeconomic status, nutritional intake, and demographic factors during the analysis. In addition, the study will further explore the variability of the relationship between heavy metal exposure and dental caries among different age groups and socioeconomic subgroups, in order to identify vulnerable populations and ultimately provide a scientific basis for the development of targeted public health intervention strategies.

Methods

Study population

Data for this study were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative, continuous cross-sectional survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). NHANES employs a complex, multistage probability sampling design to assess the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized U.S. population through standardized household interviews, physical examinations conducted in mobile examination centers, and laboratory measurements.

In the present study, we analyzed publicly available NHANES data collected between 1999 and 2020. All participants provided written informed consent, and the survey protocols were approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board. The NHANES data are fully anonymized and publicly accessible on the official website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes).

Assessment of dental caries

Dental caries status was assessed using the decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT) index, a widely used epidemiological indicator recommended by the World Health Organization for measuring lifetime caries experience [11, 12]. The DMFT index is calculated as the sum of the number of decayed (D), missing due to caries (M), and filled (F) permanent teeth (T), with scores ranging from 0 to 28 for permanent dentition.

Participants were categorized into a caries-free group (DMFT = 0) and a caries-experienced group (DMFT > 0), a commonly used classification in pediatric oral health research to reflect the presence of lifetime caries experience and to align with the primary study objective of assessing caries occurrence [13].

Heavy metal measurement

Whole blood concentrations of lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg) were measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) according to standardized protocols implemented in NHANES. Blood samples were collected in EDTA-anticoagulated tubes, thoroughly mixed, and diluted prior to analysis (1 part whole blood, 1 part deionized water, and 48 parts diluent). The diluent contained tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH, 0.4%), Triton X-100 (0.05%), ammonium pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate (APDC, 0.01%), and ethanol (1%) to facilitate the release of erythrocyte-bound metals and reduce matrix interference. Rhodium (Rh), iridium (Ir), and tellurium (Te) were used as internal standards to correct for instrumental drift and matrix effects. Metal concentrations were quantified by mass spectrometry following introduction into the ICP source. Detailed laboratory procedures and quality control measures are available in the NHANES laboratory documentation.

Given the markedly right-skewed distributions of blood metal concentrations, exposure levels were categorized using a distribution-based approach. Survey-weighted quartiles were calculated for each metal, and the median value was used as the cutoff to define low- and high-exposure groups. Participants with concentrations less than or equal to the median were classified as having low exposure, whereas those with concentrations above the median were classified as having high exposure. The median cutoff values were 0.74 µg/dL for Pb, 0.14 µg/L for Cd, and 0.39 µg/L for Hg. Blood concentrations of lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg) exhibited markedly right-skewed distributions. To reduce the influence of extreme values, descriptive statistics were summarized within the central 95% distribution range for each metal.

It should be noted that blood concentrations of heavy metals primarily reflect recent exposure and may not fully capture long-term or cumulative exposure relevant to the etiologically meaningful window for dental caries development, particularly for metals with shorter biological half-lives. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Covariate

Demographic and socioeconomic information, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, and poverty income ratio (PIR), was collected using standardized questionnaires administered as part of NHANES. These questionnaires are nationally representative, validated survey instruments designed and implemented by NCHS, with standardized administration procedures and rigorous quality control [14]. Hematological parameters, including white blood cell count, red blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, red blood cell distribution width, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, and neutrophil count, were obtained from laboratory examinations. To further assess the impact of socioeconomic status on the association between heavy metal exposure and dental caries, participants were categorized into three income groups based on PIR (< 1.3, 1.3–3.49, and ≥ 3.5). Micronutrient and vitamin intake, including calcium, magnesium, zinc, selenium, vitamin C, vitamin E, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folic acid, was assessed using two 24-hour dietary recall interviews conducted according to standardized NHANES dietary assessment protocols.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Group comparisons were conducted using independent t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or Pearson chi-square tests as appropriate. To assess the association between heavy metal exposure and dental caries, we constructed univariate logistic regression, metal-specific multivariable models (lead, cadmium, mercury), and a fully adjusted model incorporating age, sex, nutrient intake (e.g., calcium, magnesium, zinc), and poverty index to control for confounding.

Subgroup analyses were performed based on age and poverty-to-income ratio (PIR) using logistic regression. To build predictive models, five machine learning algorithms (logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, SVM, and naïve Bayes) were applied and evaluated using ROC curves. SHAP value analysis was further used to interpret feature importance. All analyses were conducted using Python, leveraging its data science and machine learning libraries.

Results

Basic characteristics of participants

A total of 108,194 participants were initially screened. After excluding individuals aged < 5 or > 19 years (n = 75,812), those lacking dental caries data (n = 5,218), heavy metal measurements (n = 10,054), or micronutrient intake data (n = 1,920), a total of 15,194 participants were included in the final analysis (caries group: 5,440; non-caries group: 9,754) (Fig. 1).

Participants with dental caries had a significantly lower poverty income ratio (PIR) compared with those without dental caries (P < 0.001), indicating an association with socioeconomic disadvantage. Small but statistically significant differences were also observed in sex distribution (P = 0.023) and racial/ethnic composition (P = 0.005).

Blood lead and cadmium concentrations were higher among participants with dental caries (both P < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed for blood mercury levels (P = 0.102). Differences were also noted in several hematological parameters, including red blood cell count, hemoglobin, and leukocyte subtypes.

In addition, participants with dental caries exhibited lower intake levels of several micronutrients, including calcium, selenium, vitamin B12, and total folate (all P < 0.05). Overall, these baseline characteristics suggest that dental caries is associated with multiple socioeconomic, nutritional, and environmental factors; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the observational nature of the study.

Heavy metals correlate with dental caries in children and adolescents

In this study, we examined the association between blood heavy metal levels and dental caries status among children and adolescents (Table 2). Participants in the high exposure groups for lead, cadmium, and mercury exhibited a higher prevalence of dental caries compared with those in the corresponding low exposure groups. Specifically, statistically significant associations were observed for lead (χ² = 29.83, P = 0.0016), cadmium (χ² = 34.02, P < 0.001), and mercury (χ² = 22.75, P = 0.0046). These findings indicate that higher blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury are associated with dental caries in this population.

Association between heavy metal exposure and dental caries in children and adolescents

Multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the associations between blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury and the occurrence of dental caries (Fig. 2 and Table X). In Model 1 (univariable logistic regression), higher blood levels of lead (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.13–1.29), cadmium (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.15–1.32), and mercury (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.10–1.26) were each significantly associated with a higher likelihood of dental caries (all P < 0.001).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis model controlling for covariates. Model 1: single-factor logistic regression; Model 2: Multi-factor analysis of lead, cadmium and mercury; Model 3: Full factor analysis including: age, sex, daily intake of calcium, magnesium, zinc, selenium, magnesium, vitamin C, vitamin E, vitamin B6, vitamin B12 and folic acid, and poverty index

In Model 2, in which all three heavy metals were simultaneously included, blood lead (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.11–1.27), blood cadmium (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.13–1.31), and blood mercury (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.07–1.23) remained significantly associated with dental caries (all P < 0.001), indicating that these associations were not substantially attenuated by mutual adjustment.

In Model 3, after further adjustment for potential confounders including age, sex, micronutrient intake, and poverty index, the associations persisted. Specifically, elevated blood lead (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.02–1.18, P = 0.009), cadmium (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.10–1.27, P < 0.001), and mercury (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.06–1.22, P < 0.001) levels were all independently associated with increased odds of dental caries.

Subgroup analysis

Given the developmental differences between children and adolescents, age-stratified analyses were conducted (Fig. 3). In children aged 5–12 years, blood lead (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.12–1.35, p < 0.001) and mercury (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01–1.21, p = 0.040) were significantly associated with caries risk, while cadmium was not (p = 0.121). In adolescents (13–19 years), all three metals were significantly associated with caries: lead (OR = 1.17), cadmium (OR = 1.27), and mercury (OR = 1.18; all p < 0.01), with cadmium showing the strongest effect. These findings suggest age-specific differences in the impact of heavy metal exposure, especially for cadmium in adolescents.

Age-based multivariate logistic regression analysis model

Considering the influence of socioeconomic status, further analysis based on PIR stratification was performed (Fig. 4). In the low-income group (PIR < 1.3), only cadmium (OR = 1.16, p = 0.007) and mercury (OR = 1.11, p = 0.048) were significantly associated with caries, while lead was not. In the middle-income group (1.3 ≤ PIR < 3.49), cadmium (OR = 1.32) had the strongest association, followed by lead (OR = 1.12, p = 0.042); mercury showed a borderline significance (p = 0.055). In the high-income group (PIR ≥ 3.5), all three metals were significantly associated with caries, particularly mercury (OR = 1.39, p < 0.001).

PIR-based multivariate logistic regression analysis model

These results indicate heterogeneity in heavy metal–caries associations across age and income levels. Notably, cadmium risk was more pronounced in adolescents and middle-to-high income populations, suggesting the need for age- and income-specific prevention strategies that consider both environmental and socioeconomic factors.

Multiple machine learning to build a caries diagnostic model for children and adolescents

To evaluate the predictive value of heavy metal exposure and related covariates for dental caries, five machine learning models were constructed, including logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, support vector machine (SVM), and naïve Bayes (Table 3; Fig. 5). Model performance was assessed in the test set using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Among all models, XGBoost demonstrated the best overall predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.75, an accuracy of 0.70, a sensitivity of 0.48, and a specificity of 0.82. This performance exceeded that of the random forest model (AUC = 0.71) and logistic regression (AUC = 0.66, sensitivity = 0.24). The SVM and naïve Bayes models showed comparatively lower discriminative ability.

To enhance model interpretability, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis was applied to the XGBoost model (Fig. 6). The SHAP summary plot indicated that blood cadmium concentration contributed most strongly to model predictions, with higher cadmium levels generally associated with higher predicted probabilities of dental caries. Blood mercury and lead also ranked among the top contributing features. In addition, demographic and socioeconomic variables, including age, poverty–income ratio (PIR), sex, and BMI, showed notable contributions, suggesting that caries prediction reflects a combination of environmental exposure and individual characteristics.

SHAP analysis for the XGB model

Overall, these findings demonstrate that machine learning models incorporating heavy metal exposure and relevant covariates can achieve moderate predictive performance for dental caries. The SHAP-based interpretation highlights the relative importance of different predictors within the model, providing complementary insights into the factors contributing to caries risk prediction.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates a significant association between blood concentrations of lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg) and dental caries prevalence among children and adolescents, after adjustment for a wide range of sociodemographic, nutritional, and hematological factors. Using data from a large, nationally representative sample of the U.S. population in NHANES (1999–2020), our findings add to the growing body of epidemiological evidence suggesting that environmental heavy metal exposure may be relevant to oral health outcomes. Notably, the observed associations varied across age and socioeconomic subgroups, indicating potential heterogeneity in susceptibility that warrants further investigation. Collectively, these results underscore the importance of considering environmental exposures, alongside conventional behavioral and nutritional factors, when examining population-level patterns of dental caries.

Environmental exposures to the heavy metals lead, cadmium and mercury are mainly from lead paint and pipes, industrial waste and smoking, coal combustion emissions and contaminated fish, which affect human health through air, water, soil and the food chain. Our results align with emerging evidence suggesting that heavy metal exposure may exacerbate caries risk through pathways independent of traditional dietary or behavioral factors. A systematic evaluation published in 2024 concluded, after integrating the various literatures, that there is a positive correlation between lead exposure and caries incidence in deciduous teeth, and that the results are consistent both with those measured during extractions and enamel biopsies, but that there is still a lack of evidence for a correlation in permanent teeth [15–18]. Previous studies suggesting that environmental cadmium exposure may be associated with an increased risk of caries in children’s milk teeth are the same as our findings, but the present study had a larger sample size, adjusted for more confounders, and used more sensitive analytical methods [19, 20]. The persistent associations observed across progressively adjusted models (from univariate to fully adjusted) underscore the robustness of these relationships. Notably, the independent contribution of cadmium remained particularly strong in adolescents and high-income subgroups, suggesting its role may be modulated by developmental or environmental cofactors.

The divergent effects of heavy metals across age groups—particularly the stronger association of cadmium with caries in adolescents (13–19 years) compared to younger children (5–12 years)—warrant further scrutiny. This pattern may reflect biological differences in tooth development stages, as permanent teeth in adolescents undergo prolonged post-eruptive mineralization, rendering them more vulnerable to metabolic disruptions caused by cadmium [21, 22]. Alternatively, behavioral factors such as increased autonomy in dietary choices or oral hygiene practices during adolescence could amplify the impact of environmental toxicants [23–26]. From a public health perspective, these findings advocate for age-specific interventions, such as intensified heavy metal screening in adolescent dental check-ups or targeted educational campaigns to mitigate risk behaviors in this population.

The heterogeneity observed in the association between heavy metal exposure and dental caries across income groups warrants cautious interpretation. Although caries is traditionally considered more prevalent in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, our findings indicate that significant associations were also present in higher-income groups, suggesting that environmental exposures may contribute to caries risk beyond socioeconomic factors alone. In higher-income populations, potential exposure pathways related to dietary patterns or residential environments may partly explain these associations, although such explanations remain speculative. Conversely, the weaker associations observed in lower-income groups may reflect the influence of multiple competing risk factors or unmeasured confounding, underscoring the multifactorial nature of dental caries and the importance of considering environmental exposures alongside socioeconomic determinants [27].

The machine learning analyses further complemented the conventional regression results by exploring the relative importance of multiple predictors in caries classification. Among the five models evaluated, XGBoost achieved the highest discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.75), indicating a moderate ability to distinguish between participants with and without dental caries. This level of performance suggests that the model is suitable for exploratory risk stratification rather than clinical diagnosis or individual-level prediction, particularly in the absence of external validation. SHAP-based interpretation was applied to enhance the transparency of the XGBoost model by quantifying feature contributions within the predictive framework. The results indicated that blood cadmium, mercury, and lead ranked among the most influential features, alongside demographic and socioeconomic variables such as age and poverty income ratio. Importantly, these SHAP values reflect feature importance within the context of the fitted model, rather than evidence of biological causality or mechanistic dominance. The prominence of cadmium in the model may reflect its statistical contribution to classification under the current feature set and outcome definition, rather than a direct etiological role. Taken together, these findings suggest that incorporating environmental exposure indicators into multivariable and machine learning models may improve the identification of population subgroups at higher caries risk.

The present findings suggest that environmental heavy metal exposure may be an underappreciated factor associated with dental caries risk in children and adolescents, complementing traditional behavioral and dietary determinants. From a public health perspective, increased attention to environmental exposures may help inform more comprehensive caries prevention strategies. Clinically, awareness of heavy metal burden could aid risk stratification in pediatric populations, particularly in high-exposure settings. In addition, incorporating basic environmental health education into community- and school-based oral health programs may support preventive efforts. These implications should be interpreted cautiously given the observational nature of the study and require confirmation in longitudinal investigations.

Despite several strengths, including the large nationally representative sample and comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, this study has important limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of NHANES precludes causal inference, as the temporal sequence between heavy metal exposure and dental caries development cannot be established. In particular, blood concentrations of heavy metals primarily reflect recent exposure (with partial exception for cadmium) and may not correspond to the etiologically relevant exposure window for caries formation, which typically develops over a prolonged period.

Second, although extensive covariates were included, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—such as detailed dietary patterns, fluoride exposure, oral hygiene behaviors, and oral microbiome composition—cannot be fully excluded. Third, the dichotomization of caries status using a DMFT/S score of 0 versus > 0, while commonly applied in epidemiological and machine learning studies, may have influenced effect size estimation and model performance by reducing information on disease severity.

Fourth, the machine learning models employed in this study were designed to explore predictive patterns rather than causal mechanisms. Although SHAP analyses improved interpretability, the identified feature importance should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of biological causation. Finally, while NHANES provides high-quality standardized data, the use of a single measurement of blood metals may underestimate cumulative exposure. Future longitudinal studies with repeated exposure assessments and standardized caries evaluations are warranted to confirm these findings and clarify causal pathways.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this large-scale NHANES-based study identifies significant associations between blood concentrations of lead, cadmium, and mercury and dental caries among children and adolescents. These associations persisted across multiple analytical approaches and population subgroups, highlighting potential heterogeneity by age and socioeconomic status. In addition, machine learning models demonstrated moderate predictive ability and consistently ranked heavy metals—particularly cadmium—among important contributors to caries risk. Taken together, these findings suggest that environmental heavy metal exposure may represent a relevant, yet often overlooked, correlate of pediatric dental caries. However, given the observational and cross-sectional nature of the data, the results should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Future longitudinal studies integrating repeated exposure assessments and comprehensive oral health evaluations are warranted to clarify temporal relationships and to better define the role of environmental exposures in caries prevention.
