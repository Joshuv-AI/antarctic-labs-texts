Decoding the cancer microbiome: multi-omics, AI, and translational opportunities
Authors: Yuhan Sun, Olivia J Cheng, Anjun Ma, Samia Shabnaz, Xuelian Huang, Jing Zhao, Aik Choon Tan, Qin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMulti-omics technologies, coupled with AI technologies, have the potential to enable the systematic investigation of complex cancer microbiome biology by uncovering informative patterns and associations across complementary datasets. Here, we review existing and emerging cancer microbiome data, discuss the development, interpretation, and validation of AI models as key considerations for their integration and analysis, and provide practical suggestions for improving the reliability and biological relevance of AI-driven discoveries. We further highlight the opportunities and challenges of translating these discoveries into clinical practice, emphasizing strategies to bridge the gap between research-oriented models and clinical implementations.

Introduction

Cancer remains a worldwide health problem, with approximately 20 million new cases annually and projections rising to 30.5 million annually by 2050 [1]. To address this burden, research has focused on understanding cancer development, progression, treatment response, and toxicity. These processes are shaped by tumor-intrinsic factors, such as mutations and dysregulated metabolic and signaling pathways within cancer cells [2–4], as well as tumor-extrinsic factors, including all non-cancer components in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and tumor macroenvironment (TMaE) [4–7]. This review focuses on the cancer microbiome as a key tumor-extrinsic factor, encompassing both the gut microbiome and the tumor-associated microbiome [6–10].

Early gut microbiome studies in cancer centered on colorectal cancers (CRC) because of their proximity to the tumor site. Research has since shown that the gut microbiome also acts systematically as part of the TMaE, modulating host immunity and metabolism beyond the gastrointestinal tract. Interest has therefore expanded to its role across various cancer types. Overall, the gut microbiome has been implicated in cancer tumorigenesis, progression, treatment response, and immune-related adverse events (irAEs) [10–12], with diet-driven modulation in microbiome composition playing an essential role [13–20].

Unlike the systemic effects of the gut microbiome, the tumor-associated microbiome acts locally within the TME. Its composition is cancer-type specific, it can localize extracellularly and intracellularly in both cancer and immune cells, and it may show spatial organization within tumors [21–23]. It can influence the TME through infection, inflammation, and the production of metabolites [24–27]. Evidence from experimental models and human datasets has linked the intratumoral microbiome to cancer progression, prognosis, and treatment response across multiple cancer types [22, 28–34].

Computational methods have been central to cancer microbiome research. Early studies relied on classical statistical tools, including differential abundance methods (e.g., LEfSe [35] and metagenomeSeq [36]), to identify taxa associated with cancer risk [37–39], survival [40, 41], and treatment response [42–46]. However, these methods often perform poorly on microbiome data because they are high-dimensional, sparse, zero-inflated, and compositional, which can reduce reproducibility [47–49].​ Network-based methods (e.g., SparCC [50], CoNet [51], and SPIEC-EASI [52]) extended this framework by modeling microbial interactions, identifying community structures associated with cancer processes [53–57], yet are limited in their ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships. More recently, artificial intelligence (AI) models, particularly deep learning, have enabled integration of microbiome and multi-omics data to improve modeling of nonlinear and higher-order interactions, enhancing biomarker discovery and clinical outcome prediction [58–62].

Despite this progress, major challenges still limit the reliability and interpretability of AI models, as well as the clinical translation of cancer microbiome discoveries. At the data level, the field lacks standardized protocols for sample collection, DNA extraction, sequencing, and taxonomic profiling, creating substantial technical variability and inconsistencies across platforms and reference databases [63–66]. Similarly, in tumor-associated microbiome studies, distinguishing genuine microbial signals from laboratory contaminants or misclassified reads remains challenging, particularly when microbial information is inferred from host RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data [67–69]. These issues contribute to batch effects and cohort-specific biases that weaken reproducibility and generalizability. At the model level, most AI rely on high-dimensional yet small-sample datasets, increasing overfitting risk and reducing biomarker stability, especially without strong external or prospective validation. Although interpretable AI methods, such as feature importance scores, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, and attention mechanisms have improved model transparency, deep learning frameworks largely remain semi-opaque, limiting their ability to distinguish spurious correlations from causal microbe–host mechanisms. Together, these challenges hinder the development of robust AI-derived microbiome biomarkers and their translation into clinically actionable applications.

In this context, this review provides an integrated framework for understanding how AI is reshaping cancer microbiome research, from data generation and preprocessing to predictive modeling, biological interpretation, and clinical translation. We first summarize existing and emerging cancer microbiome data resources and multi-omics technologies, followed by strategies for generating reliable AI-ready datasets. We then discuss AI modeling frameworks, their applications, and approaches for improving interpretability and reliability. Finally, we highlight how microbiome discoveries can progress from biological evidence to clinically actionable biomarkers and therapeutic strategies (Fig. 1).

AI-driven translational framework for cancer microbiome research. Cancer microbiome research progresses through five interconnected stages. First, diverse microbiome resources, including gut and tumor-associated microbiomes measured by multiple omics technologies, provide the foundation for downstream analyses. Second, raw sequencing data undergo quality control, decontamination, host-read removal, taxonomic profiling, normalization, batch effect correction, and microbial signal validation to generate AI-ready microbiome feature matrices. Third, AI models, including machine learning, deep learning, graph neural networks, transformers, and foundation models, support both prediction tasks and multi-omics integration while enabling model interpretation and rigorous validation. Fourth, AI-derived hypotheses are evaluated through association studies, mechanistic investigations, and perturbation experiments to establish biological evidence across multiple levels. Finally, validated findings are translated into clinical applications through biomarker development, analytical and clinical validation, and AI-assisted clinical decision support systems, ultimately advancing precision oncology

Landscape of cancer microbiome data generation technologies and computational pipelines

Genomics and metatranscriptomics data

High-throughput sequencing has advanced cancer microbiome research by enabling analysis of microbial composition in the gut and tissues [84]. A common target is the 16S rRNA gene, a conserved marker in bacteria and archaea with variable regions that support taxonomic classification. Partial 16S sequencing is widely used because it is affordable and can estimate community composition, usually at the genus level, but results depend on the variable region sequenced and may bias taxonomic assignment. Full-length 16S sequencing offers higher resolution, often to the species level, and reduces region-specific bias [70, 72]. Although 16S data is mainly used for taxonomy, it can also support indirect functional inference through reference databases [85]. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing provides a broader view by capturing all genes in a sample, enabling both taxonomic profiling and prediction of functions such as metabolic pathways and drug resistance. However, due to the differences in resolution, partial 16S, full-length 16S, and metagenomic sequencing can yield different compositional and functional inferences [70, 72].

While these DNA-based methods reveal the overall functional potential encoded in the genome, they do not capture which genes are actively expressed. In contrast, metatranscriptomics targets RNA to capture real-time gene expression and thus the dynamic microbial activity. However, metatranscriptomic analyses remain less widely applied due to technical challenges, including RNA instability, stringent sample handling requirements, and challenges in interpreting transcript-level microbial activity, which have limited their application in large-scale cancer microbiome studies [74, 86, 87]. Overall, genomic and transcriptomic approaches help define microbial composition, diversity, and functional signatures associated with cancer risk, progression, and therapy response [88–94].

Metaproteomics data

Proteomics provides a more direct view of microbial function by profiling expressed proteins [75]. A comparison of gut metaproteomes and metagenomes showed that each captures different functional categories, with metaproteomics enriched for translation, energy production, and carbohydrate metabolism [95]. Metaproteomics can identify not only microbial taxa but also protein biomarkers and functional or metabolic pathways linked to patient stratification and cancer pathogenesis [96]. Although gut microbiome metaproteomics has been widely used in other diseases to identify therapeutic targets and predict treatment response or adverse events [97–100], its use in cancer remains limited. As of June 2026, a PubMed search identified only nine studies that used metaproteomics to investigate the cancer microbiome, underscoring a substantial gap and an important opportunity for future research [101–109].

Metabolomics data

Microbes produce metabolites, such as sugars, amino acids, and fatty acids, that affect both host and microbial biology [110]. Metabolomics measures small molecules in a sample and provides a dynamic view of physiological states in response to biological changes or stimuli [111–113]. It can be performed using mass spectrometry (targeted, untargeted or spatial), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), or Raman spectroscopy (spatial resolution) [76]. Because only a small fraction of human-detectable metabolites are uniquely microbial or shared between host and microbes, identifying microbial-derived metabolites remains challenging [76]. This often requires reference databases alongside correlation and statistical approaches to link metabolites to microbial taxa and functions. For example, the Microbial Metabolites Database (MiMeDB) [114] connects known human-associated microbial metabolites to health outcomes, Natural Products Atlas (NPAtlas) [115] curates structural information on microbial compounds and source organisms, and the Mass Spectrometry Search Tool (MASST) [116] enables mass spectrometry spectra matching against public data to identify molecules of interest and associated metadata such as phylogeny, source, and biological activity. Metabolomics has been used to study gut and intratumoral microbial metabolites in cancer development, progression, and therapy [117–119]. Recent advances in spatial metabolomics further allow mapping of region-specific metabolic changes within tumors, improving understanding of localized cancer-microbiome interactions [120].

Advances in intratumoral microbiome detection

Unlike the gut microbiome, tumor-associated microbiome signals are generally low in abundance, sparse, and highly heterogeneous, with microbial burden varying across tumor sites. Tumors exposed to external environments, such as colorectal, gastric, and oral cavity tumors, tend to harbor relatively higher microbial biomass, whereas tumors in traditionally sterile or less externally exposed tissues, including pancreatic, liver, lung, and breast tumors, are generally considered low-biomass settings. Early tumor sequencing studies did not specifically target microbes, but recent work has reanalyzed these datasets to infer intratumoral microbial signals from non-human reads in bulk RNA-seq and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data [121–125]. However, due to the low abundance of intratumoral microbes, this approach of reanalyzing non-human reads may be subject to contamination and should be analyzed with caution [123, 125]. In fact, a recent large-scale tumor whole-genome analysis revealed that after host subtraction and decontamination, detectable microbiome signals were largely restricted to orodigestive cancers, whereas most other cancer types lacked signals distinguishable from background, highlighting the need for rigorous analysis [126].

Although bulk omics and reanalysis of existing datasets have provided useful insights, intratumoral microbial communities also show important spatial and cellular heterogeneity that require specialized methods. Several computational and experimental methods were developed to optimize the detection of microbial presence in host normal and tumor tissues. For example, the CSI_Microbe pipeline extracts microbial reads from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) whole-genome sequencing data and highlights the limitations of detecting microbes in host-focused sequencing datasets [82]. The SAHMI pipeline denoises and identifies host-associated microbial signals from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data in tumor tissues [83]. To directly detect microbes within single cells in the human TME, invasion–adhesion-directed expression sequencing (INVADEseq), a scRNA-seq method, was developed that adds a primer targeting the conserved 16S region, enabling microbial mapping within individual human cells [22, 79].

Additionally, spatial proteomics and spatial metabolomics through isotope-, fluorescence-, and mass spectrometry- based methods enable the understanding of how microbial products influence the TME [80]. For example, the spatial proteomics technique imaging mass cytometry with mass-tagged antibodies against bacterial components can visualize both microbial presence and host immune and tumor cells, revealing specific spatial localization of Gram-negative bacteria with specific immune and tumor markers [127]. Similarly, in spatial metabolomics, approaches such as desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (DESI-MSI) targeting taxon-specific markers enable the detection of metabolic markers indicative of microbial presence and the mapping of their spatial heterogeneity within tumor samples [81]. While CSI_Microbe, SAHMI, and INVADEseq were primarily demonstrated in tumor-associated datasets, spatial imaging and molecular profiling approaches such as imaging mass cytometry and DESI-MSI represent broader platforms that can be applied to diverse tissue microbiome contexts, although their performance depends on tissue-specific microbial abundance, detection sensitivity, and appropriate host-background controls.

Multi-omics and matched host–microbiome datasets

As multiple microbial omics layers become available, linking them with host data provides an important data foundation for understanding host–microbiome interactions in cancer [128, 129]. These datasets may combine microbial genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, or metabolomic profiles with matched host molecular, immune, clinical, spatial, or longitudinal measurements. By preserving sample-level correspondence across modalities, they support the identification of microbial biomarkers, potential mechanisms, therapeutic targets, and microbial features associated with treatment outcomes across cancer types and subtypes [130–137].

However, multi-omics studies face important challenges. Generating paired datasets for everyone is expensive, so cohorts are often small, increasing the risk of overfitting. Many public datasets are collected retrospectively or for different primary purposes, making matched host–microbiome samples across cohorts less common. In addition, different omics platforms produce different data structures and formats, complicating harmonization. These challenges are further compounded by cohort effects and by temporal and spatial heterogeneity across organs and tissues within the same individual [138–141].

Preparing cancer microbiome data for AI

The above multi-omics data enable culture-independent characterization of microbial communities. Despite differences in modalities, these data share core preprocessing steps, including quality control, decontamination, alignment, taxonomic profiling and batch correction. However, gut and tumor-associated microbiome data differ substantially in signal strength and contamination risk, necessitating distinct strategies.

Quality control and decontamination

For gut microbiome data, quality control typically relies on rule-based filtering of low-quality reads and adapter sequences [142–145]. While effective in high-biomass settings, these methods assume consistent error patterns and often lack robustness in heterogeneous datasets. Recent AI-based methods expand quality control to assembly- and sample-level evaluation. For example, DeepCheck and CheckM2 use neural networks and ensemble models to assess genome completeness and contamination of metagenome-assembled genomes [146, 147], whereas Deepurify uses a multi-modal deep language model to remove contaminated contigs, thereby improving downstream genome quality [148]. At the sample level, methods such as seqQscorer, AMAnD, and KADAIF enable automated detection of low-quality or anomalous samples [149–151]. Together, these methods reflect a shift toward more holistic, data-driven assessment.

Decontamination is a dominant challenge in tumor-associated microbiome research [63, 69, 152, 153]. More recent approaches primarily rely on statistical and probabilistic strategies to address shared external contamination arising from reagents, laboratory environments, or batch effects, and often assume that contaminant signals exhibit systematic patterns across samples [39, 153, 154]. Extending beyond this setting, SCRuB also models well-to-well contamination using processing layout information, although it does not explicitly infer pairwise donor–recipient contamination events [155]. In a further advance, CroCoDeEL uses random forest classifiers to detect cross-sample contamination directly from abundance profiles without requiring negative controls or contaminant databases [156]. These advances highlight the importance of jointly addressing shared external contamination and cross-sample contamination in low-signal settings.

Host-read removal and taxonomic profiling

Standard pipelines remove host reads using aligners such as Bowtie2 [157] or BWA [158] and classify microbial sequences using tools such as Kraken2 [159] or SqueezeMeta [160]. However, their reliance on reference databases limits detection of novel or low-abundance taxa. AI-based approaches instead leverage representation learning [161]. Methods such as DeepMicrobes integrate k-mer embeddings with recurrent neural networks and attention mechanisms [162], while MetaTransformer leverages transformers to model long-range dependencies in genomic sequences [163]. These approaches improve generalization to novel or low-abundance taxa by learning sequence-level representations rather than relying solely on exact or approximate matches to known references.

For tumor-associated microbiome data, incomplete host-read removal and reference-database limitations can cause human sequences to be misclassified as microbial, necessitating stringent host subtraction and conservative classification criteria. Specialized pipelines such as PathSeq [164] and PRISM [165] have been developed to address these challenges. PRISM is a benchmark-validated computational framework that integrates machine learning-based scoring to improve microorganism identification precision and filter low-confidence assignments. These methods reflect the need for more conservative and robust alignment strategies in low-signal settings, where distinguishing true biological signal from noise is especially critical.

Practical standards for credible tumor-associated microbial signals

Beyond computational decontamination and taxonomic profiling, practical standards are needed to determine whether tumor-associated microbial signals are biologically credible rather than contamination-driven or misclassified artifacts. Credible signals should be supported by multiple complementary criteria, including (i) appropriate negative controls, such as extraction blanks, reagent-only controls, no-template polymerase chain reaction (PCR) controls, and batch-matched environmental controls; (ii) conservative host-read subtraction and taxonomic classification using curated reference databases; (iii) evaluation of batch structure, sequencing depth, and background microbial profiles; and (iv) where possible, orthogonal validation. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) [166] or reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) [167] can confirm microbial DNA or RNA signals, culture-based assays can support microbial viability, and in situ hybridization, fluorescence in situ hybridization, immunohistochemistry for microbial components, or spatial imaging can assess whether microbial signals localize within tumor tissues or specific host-cell compartments. Together, these approaches help distinguish biologically credible tumor-associated microbial signals from reagent contaminants, environmental contamination, misclassified reads, and other technical artifacts. Consistent with this need, a recent study proposed a comprehensive practical checklist for reporting microbial species in human cancers, emphasizing contamination prevention, negative and positive controls, batch metadata, conservative bioinformatic analysis, contaminant removal, targeted and spatial validation, evidence of microbial viability or activity, and multiple independent lines of evidence [168].

Normalization and batch correction

After sequencing and taxonomic or functional profiling, microbiome-derived profiles often require normalization and, in cross-study settings, batch correction. These preprocessing steps are critical because unaddressed differences in sequencing depth, abundance scale, and technical procedures can obscure true host–microbe associations and reduce the robustness and cross-study generalizability of microbiome-based AI models. For microbial abundance data, commonly used normalization and transformation strategies include relative abundance transformation, rarefaction, Hellinger transformation, log-proportion transformation, and compositional log-ratio transformations such as additive log-ratio, centered log-ratio, and isometric log-ratio transformations. Benchmarking studies have shown that normalization choices can substantially influence statistical inference and predictive performance [169, 170], particularly because microbial abundance data are compositional and zero-inflated, which can induce spurious correlations if not properly handled [171]. As no single strategy is universally optimal, normalization methods should be selected according to data characteristics and analytical objectives [172, 173]. In cross-study analyses, batch effect correction is also essential for improving model transferability. ConQuR is a microbiome-specific approach based on conditional quantile regression [174], whereas DEBIAS-M formulates batch correction as a domain-adaptation problem by jointly learning batch alignment and phenotype prediction. By estimating taxon- and batch-specific correction factors, DEBIAS-M aims to preserve phenotype-associated microbial variation while improving cross-study prediction performance and reducing information leakage during model development [175].

For tumor-associated microbiome data, normalization and batch correction require greater caution because preprocessing choices can substantially alter feature distributions and downstream modeling results. Recent debates over TCGA-based cancer microbiome analyses illustrate this challenge. One reanalysis argued that normalization strategies could introduce artificial cancer-type-specific microbial signals [67], whereas a subsequent robustness study reported that raw, Voom-SNM-corrected, and ConQuR-corrected data produced comparable per-batch prediction performance and similar model features [124]. These contrasting findings indicate that normalization and batch correction in tumor microbiome studies should be evaluated empirically rather than applied or rejected a priori. Robust analyses should therefore include raw-versus-corrected comparisons and evaluate the stability of predictive features under alternative preprocessing strategies.

AI modeling for cancer microbiome analysis

Before selecting an AI model for cancer microbiome analysis, it is essential to determine whether the model is compatible with the structural characteristics of the dataset. Microbiome-derived feature matrices are often high-dimensional, sparse, compositional, and highly correlated, with the number of microbial features p approaching or exceeding the number of samples n. Therefore, small-sample settings should not be defined by sample size alone, but by the combined effects of sample size, feature dimensionality, class distribution, and cohort heterogeneity. When p>n, and especially when p≫n, model estimation, performance evaluation, and biomarker interpretation can become unstable, increasing the risk that models capture cohort-specific noise rather than reproducible microbiome–phenotype associations. Thus, algorithm selection should balance predictive performance, model complexity, interpretability, computational cost, and validation stability, rather than assuming that larger or more flexible models are always preferable.

Modeling frameworks

AI models in cancer microbiome research span classical machine learning, deep learning, and, more recently, foundation models. These paradigms differ in modeling assumptions, representational capacity, interpretability, data requirements, and computational cost, and provide complementary tools for addressing the high dimensionality, sparsity, and heterogeneity of microbiome data.

Classical approaches, including logistic regression, regularized linear models, support vector machines, random forests, and gradient boosting, rely on explicitly defined features and emphasize interpretability, computational efficiency, and robustness in small-sample settings. They are widely used to identify microbial signatures associated with tumor progression, explore host–microbe associations in multi-omics data, and develop interpretable models for clinical outcome prediction [176]. Their interpretability is particularly valuable for microbiome-based biomarker discovery: linear and regularized models can be interpreted through model coefficients or selected features, whereas tree-based models are commonly examined using feature-importance or permutation-based approaches. However, feature importance should be interpreted cautiously because correlated microbial features, batch effects, and cohort-specific signals can affect biomarker stability.

Recent benchmarking studies support the continued value of classical machine learning in microbiome-based classification. In a 16S rRNA benchmark with 490 subjects and 6,920 operational taxonomic unit (OTU) features, reduced sample size increased cross-validation performance variability, and L2-regularized logistic regression achieved performance comparable to random forest while requiring substantially less training time and offering greater interpretability [177]. A larger benchmark across 83 gut microbiome cohorts and 20 diseases further showed that Ridge regression and random forest ranked among the best-performing algorithms, whereas more complex models, including neural networks, XGBoost, and LightGBM, did not consistently outperform them [173]. These findings suggest that classical and regularized models remain strong baselines for data-limited microbiome studies, particularly when reproducible biomarker interpretation, computational efficiency, and validation stability are important.

Deep learning models provide flexible frameworks for modeling nonlinear and high-dimensional dependencies and support representation learning directly from cancer microbiome and multi-omics data. Multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) are suitable for tabular microbiome data and can capture nonlinear interactions among microbial, host, and clinical features, but they require sufficient training samples and appropriate regularization to reduce overfitting in sparse high-dimensional datasets [58, 62, 178–180]. Convolutional neural networks can capture local patterns in structured microbiome inputs through shared convolutional filters, but their performance depends on whether the input features have biologically meaningful ordering or spatial structure; otherwise, learned convolutional patterns may be difficult to interpret [181, 182]. Transformer-based models can learn complex inter-feature dependencies and support cancer-associated prediction tasks, including survival prediction and tumor microbiota profiling. However, they are computationally intensive, require relatively large training cohorts, and are not inherently interpretable, as raw attention weights should not be directly treated as indicators of biological importance without additional validation [60, 183]. Graph-based models, such as graph convolutional networks and graph attention networks, incorporate relational structure for modeling species interactions, microbe–sample associations, and cross-omics networks, while their reliability depends strongly on the quality of the constructed graph and may be affected by noisy or incomplete prior knowledge [184–186]. Autoencoders and variational autoencoders provide flexible representation-learning frameworks for generating compact latent representations and extracting microbiome features associated with clinical outcomes; however, their latent spaces may be difficult to interpret biologically and can capture technical variation without careful regularization and validation [59, 60, 187–190]. Overall, although deep learning expands the modeling capacity of cancer microbiome analysis, its application in small or heterogeneous cohorts requires careful control of model complexity, computational cost, overfitting, and interpretability.

Foundation models are large-scale architectures typically using self-supervised objectives and transformer-based backbones. Unlike task-specific deep learning models trained from scratch, foundation models learn transferable representations that can be adapted to downstream tasks through representation extraction, linear probing, fine-tuning, or task-specific prediction heads. They are particularly valuable when large-scale pretraining data are available or when cross-cohort transferability is needed, particularly in heterogeneous clinical settings where labeled cancer microbiome cohorts are limited. Foundation models may operate on microbial community profiles or directly on genomic sequences. For example, GenomeOcean learns representations across characterized and previously uncharacterized microbial diversity, whereas Evo2 models genomic sequences across multiple biological scales. More broadly, sequence-to-function models such as AlphaGenome illustrate the potential of pretrained genomic representations to predict functional outputs. However, their application in cancer microbiome research remains at an early stage. Fine-tuning foundation models on small cancer cohorts may still lead to overfitting, and their performance can be affected by domain mismatch between pretraining data and tumor-associated microbiome data. In addition, their high computational cost and limited interpretability remain important barriers [191–195].

Collectively, these frameworks provide complementary strengths: classical machine learning emphasizes interpretability and robustness in data-limited settings, deep learning enables nonlinear and structure-aware modeling, and pretrained foundation models offer scalable and transferable representations. The following sections examine how these approaches are applied across downstream analytical tasks.

Functional applications of AI models

Prediction of cancer status and treatment outcomes

Disease prediction is a major application in cancer microbiome research, including cancer detection, risk stratification, and disease subtyping. Early studies primarily focused on binary classification between cancer patients and healthy controls, but such coarse-grained tasks are insufficient to capture the biological and clinical heterogeneity of cancer populations [90, 196, 197]. Recent deep learning approaches have shifted toward more informative prediction tasks. For example, MV-CVIB distinguishes metastatic from non-metastatic CRC using multi-view gut microbial features within a variational information bottleneck [181], while GDmicro combines a graph convolutional network with a deep adaptation network to improve cross-cohort robustness by reducing source–target shifts in gut microbial compositional profiles caused by differences in region, ethnicity, diet, sample collection, and sequencing protocols [185]. Beyond binary prediction, multi-class frameworks better capture disease-specific signatures [198]. Foundation models such as MGM, pretrained on large-scale microbiome data, learns transferable microbial representations that mitigate data scarcity and improve cancer type discrimination [191]. Taken together, these studies illustrate a shift from simple binary classification toward more flexible diagnostic models that capture finer phenotypic structure and support more robust prediction across heterogeneous settings.

Treatment outcome prediction is clinically relevant because it relates to outcome assessment, risk stratification, and treatment response evaluation. The cancer microbiome has been linked to survival and treatment response [199–201], whereas traditional frameworks such as tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) staging often fail to capture microbial and molecular heterogeneity. AI-based models integrate microbiome-derived features to improve prognostic prediction, with deep learning outperforming classical approaches in survival estimation [178]. Performance further benefits from multi-omics integration, jointly modeling intratumoral microbial, genomic, and transcriptomic data [58–61]. In data-limited settings, methods such as DeepGeni improve robustness through data augmentation and representation learning [187, 202]. These approaches extend traditional prognostic modeling by enabling nonlinear and integrative analysis, although further improvements in data quality and cohort diversity are needed to enhance robustness and generalizability.

Multi-omics integration

Given the heterogeneous matched host–microbiome datasets described in Sect. "Multi-omics and matched host–microbiome datasets", computational integration methods are needed to model coordinated host–microbe interactions in cancer microbiome studies. Classical approaches such as DIABLO provide interpretable representations, but remain limited by linear assumptions [203, 204]. Deep learning frameworks overcome these constraints by learning nonlinear, high-order cross-modal relationships. Methods such as VTrans leverage large-scale pretraining and selective co-attention to integrate microbiome features with optional host transcriptomics and copy-number profiles, enhancing survival risk stratification in small cohorts [60]. Related approaches similarly combine microbial, genomic, and tumor microenvironment signals using neural networks, with gradient-based attribution methods enhancing interpretability [180]. Beyond molecular data, multimodal frameworks incorporate histopathology and environmental exposures [58, 184, 205]. MOCO-GCN constructs modality-specific sample similarity graphs to propagate information across gut microbiome- and exposome-derived networks, enabling effective multimodal fusion and interpretable predictions [184].

Collectively, these approaches highlight AI-based multimodal integration as a flexible framework for modeling complex host–microbe interactions, particularly when cross-modal relationships are complex and context-dependent. However, the benefits of multimodal modeling should be interpreted cautiously, as integrating multiple data layers can increase model complexity and exacerbate overfitting, sensitivity to missing modalities, and instability across cohorts. Robust integration therefore requires modality-aware preprocessing, appropriate handling of missing modalities, and validation of whether each added data layer provides reproducible predictive or biological value.

Model interpretability and causal inference

Model interpretability is essential for translating AI-based cancer microbiome predictions into biologically meaningful insights. In this context, interpretability refers to the ability to identify which microbial taxa, genes, pathways, communities, or host–microbe interactions contribute to model outputs and how these features are associated with disease status, prognosis, or treatment response. For classical models, interpretation often relies on model coefficients, selected features, or feature-importance scores, whereas deep learning models typically require post hoc attribution methods.

Widely used approaches such as SHAP and integrated gradients estimate the contribution of individual features to model predictions and can provide information on both the magnitude and direction of feature effects [180, 196, 206, 207]. However, their applications can be computationally demanding for high-dimensional microbiome data. Approaches such as GDmicro address this limitation by enabling efficient species-level feature selection without repeated retraining and by incorporating directional associations [185]. Beyond single taxa, graph-based models extend interpretability from individual taxa to microbial communities. For example, Micah constructs a species interaction network based on metabolic and phylogenetic relationships and identifies cancer-associated microbial communities, yielding more robust and biologically interpretable signatures [186]. Overall, AI-based interpretability in cancer microbiome research has evolved from simple feature ranking toward attribution-based, direction-aware, and network-level explanations. Nevertheless, model-derived important features should be interpreted as biological hypotheses rather than causal drivers. Their reliability requires feature-stability assessment and external cohort validation, together with the contamination-control procedures described in Sect. "Quality control and decontamination" and, when possible, the orthogonal experimental validation outlined in Sect. "Practical standards for credible tumor-associated microbial signals".

Looking forward, integrating AI with causal inference frameworks may provide a promising direction for modeling cancer microbiome data, with the potential to improve interpretability and move beyond purely associative analyses toward more mechanistic understanding. Recent advances show how causal inference can be integrated with deep learning for complex biological systems [208, 209]. For example, DAG-deepVASE combines deep neural networks with knockoff features to identify nonlinear causal relationships and estimate effect sizes in high-dimensional data [210]. Its applications to gut bacteria, nutrient intake, and BMI data, as well as TCGA breast cancer molecular and clinical data, illustrate the potential of causal AI for identifying nonlinear and directional relationships relevant to microbiome and cancer research. Similar strategies could be adapted to cancer microbiome studies to infer putative links among tumor-associated microbes, host pathways, immune features, and treatment responses. Nevertheless, causal inference from observational microbiome data remains hypothesis-generating and depends on assumptions about confounding, model specification, and conditional dependencies. Therefore, causal AI findings should be accompanied by explicit assumptions, sensitivity analyses, and rigorous model-level and biological validation before mechanistic claims are made.

Validation and reliability of AI models

These considerations underscore the need for rigorous validation in cancer microbiome AI studies. Because cancer microbiome datasets are often small, heterogeneous, high-dimensional, and contamination-prone, AI models are particularly vulnerable to overfitting, unmeasured confounding, contamination-derived signals, and unstable or inflated performance estimates. Validation should therefore ensure that model performance and model-derived microbial signatures reflect reproducible biological signals rather than dataset-specific artifacts.

A primary requirement for reliable validation is a leakage-free design. All data-adaptive preprocessing and model-development procedures, including normalization, batch correction, domain adaptation, supervised feature selection, imbalance correction, model selection, and hyperparameter tuning, should be fitted or optimized strictly within each training split rather than on the full dataset [174, 185, 211–213]. When cross-validation is used for these procedures, a nested design is preferred, with an inner loop for model development and tuning and an outer loop for internal performance estimation [214]. For datasets with repeated samples or strong grouping structures, group-aware validation strategies, such as leave-one-patient-out, leave-one-batch-out, leave-one-center-out, or leave-one-cohort-out validation, should be used to prevent group leakage [215]. Together, these designs reduce overly optimistic performance estimates by limiting data leakage, validation-set overuse during model selection, and the influence of group-specific signals. In parallel, biological, clinical, and technical confounders should be addressed through appropriate study design [216] and explicit covariate adjustment [217, 218].

After model development, predictive performance should be evaluated using metrics appropriate to the specific prediction task. In cancer microbiome research, common tasks include classification, survival prediction, and continuous outcome prediction. For classification tasks, discrimination metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision–recall curve (PR-AUC), threshold-dependent metrics such as recall, precision and F1-score, and calibration metrics should be considered, particularly in the presence of class imbalance and small validation cohorts. For survival prediction, metrics such as the concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent AUROC assess predictive accuracy over follow-up time, whereas regression-based metrics including root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) can evaluate continuous outcomes such as tumor response magnitude or quantitative biological phenotypes. Uncertainty-aware evaluation should accompany these metrics: confidence intervals quantify uncertainty around performance estimates, repeated cross-validation or bootstrap resampling assesses variability across data splits, and repeated runs with different random seeds evaluate model stochasticity, especially for deep learning models.

Robustness should then be assessed across heterogeneous cohorts and distributional shifts. Fully independent external test cohorts, ideally collected from different studies, centers, sequencing platforms, or preprocessing pipelines, provide the strongest evidence of model generalizability. However, fully independent external cohorts with comparable sample types, sequencing platforms, clinical annotations, and preprocessing pipelines are not always available in cancer microbiome research, because many datasets remain small, heterogeneous, and difficult to harmonize across studies [69, 219]. In such settings, nested cross-validation, repeated cross-validation, bootstrap resampling, and group-aware validation can provide useful evidence of internal stability, but they should not be regarded as substitutes for external cohort validation. External validation remains necessary to demonstrate cross-study generalizability. When cross-cancer generalizability is the intended goal, cancer type should be treated as a biological domain or stratification factor, and leave-one-cancer-type-out validation may be used to assess whether the model generalizes beyond cancer-type-specific signals.

Taken together, these considerations can be organized into a practical pipeline for reliable cancer microbiome AI analysis. First, microbial profiles should undergo stringent quality control, host-read removal, decontamination, normalization, batch effect assessment, and microbial signal validation, as described in Sects. "Quality control and decontamination"–"Normalization and batch correction", before downstream AI modeling. Second, all data-adaptive preprocessing and model-selection steps should be implemented within leakage-free training pipelines. Third, model development should account for biological, clinical, and technical confounders through appropriate study design and covariate adjustment. Fourth, model performance should be evaluated using task-appropriate metrics, calibration assessment, uncertainty estimation, repeated runs, and, when model selection is performed or grouped data structures are present, nested and group-aware cross-validation. Fifth, locked models and prioritized microbial signatures should be tested in independent external cohorts whenever possible. Finally, model-derived microbial signatures should be interpreted as candidate biomarkers or mechanistic hypotheses and undergo biological validation before being considered mechanistically or clinically actionable. Together, this pipeline provides a practical framework for improving the reliability, robustness, biological credibility, and translational potential of AI-based cancer microbiome models.

Cancer microbiome biology across levels of evidence

Evidence supporting the involvement of microbiome in cancer has emerged from diverse study types, including observational and computational analyses to unravel associations and preclinical mouse in vivo experiments to provide mechanistic evidence and causal support. This section discusses representative examples of different levels of evidence in various aspects of cancer biology.

Cancer microbiome and tumor initiation and progression

The microbiome and its associated metabolites can influence tumor initiation and progression through multiple cancer-related mechanisms, including induction of DNA damage, chronic inflammation, and immune modulation, which together shape the tumor microenvironment toward either tumor-promoting or antitumor states [220–223]. Conversely, some microbial metabolites have demonstrated cytotoxic and growth-inhibitory effects on cancer cells, underscoring the dual role of host–microbe interactions in oncogenesis [224, 225].

Across cancer types, studies have identified associations linking specific microbial taxa and microbial metabolites to tumorigenesis. For example, a large-scale metagenomic analysis across multiple cohorts identified an association between microbial choline metabolism with CRC progression [89]. Integrative microbiome sequencing and metabolomic profiling in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma have revealed distinct metabolic alterations associated with tumor tissues [226]. Complementing these association-based analyses, AI-assisted multi-omics approaches adopt a data-driven paradigm to detect patterns across heterogeneous datasets. These models can identify coordinated microbe–metabolite–host associations, infer putative interaction networks linked to tumor phenotypes and disease-specific differences [59, 227], and reveal pathway-level relationships in cancer-related contexts [203]. In this setting, their primary value lies in prioritizing candidate interactions for subsequent mechanistic investigation.

Experimental studies can further provide mechanistic evidence supporting causal roles for specific microbes and metabolites in tumorigenesis. For example, intratumoral Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) has been implicated in promoting tumor progression and metastasis through immune modulation and oncogenic signaling [31–33, 228]. Similarly, enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (B. fragilis) in the gut or breast promoted tumorigenesis and metastasis in breast cancer models through toxin-mediated inflammatory signaling [229]. Together, these findings highlight diverse microbial and metabolic factors that modulate cancer initiation and progression. Representative mechanistic studies are summarized in Table 2.

Cancer microbiome and treatment response

Recent studies have increasingly focused on how the microbiome shapes cancer treatment response and therapeutic efficacy. Here, we focus on chemotherapy and immunotherapy, where microbiome-informed analyses have been most extensively explored.

Microbiome-associated determinants of chemotherapy response and toxicity

Association-based studies across cancer types have identified microbial signatures linked to chemotherapy response and toxicity [230]. In breast cancer, lower gut alpha diversity and Faecalibacterium abundance at baseline predict greater chemotherapy-induced gastrointestinal symptoms [231]. In leukemia patients, a high relative abundance of Enterococcus predicts a longer duration of treatment-induced neutropenia [232]. In pancreatic cancer, taxa such as E. coli, Enterococcus faecalis, and F. nucleatum have been identified as negative predictors of chemotherapy response [233]. Longitudinal metagenomic sequencing of stool samples from CRC patients further revealed enrichment of menaquinol biosynthesis genes following capecitabine treatment; this association-guided observation was subsequently supported by mouse experiments showing that MK-4 supplementation reduces peripheral sensory neuropathy [234]. Together, these studies highlight that association-based studies identify microbial signatures related to chemotherapy response or toxicity and provide candidates for subsequent mechanistic investigation.

Mechanistic evidence for microbial modulation of chemotherapy efficacy and toxicity

Preclinical mechanistic studies further support direct roles for microbes in modulating chemotherapy efficacy. In CRC models, gut microbes including F. nucleatum and B. fragilis, previously implicated in tumorigenesis, also promote resistance to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and oxaliplatin [235]. Beyond gut microbes, intratumoral F. nucleatum induces chemoresistance via activation of autophagy pathways [236]. Similarly, intratumoral B. fragilis correlates with non-response in CRC patients and complementary in vitro experiments suggest that it can reduce cell sensitivity to chemo-induced apoptosis [237]. More broadly, gut microbiota can directly metabolize therapeutic drugs, mediating chemical modifications and altering the bioavailability of drugs [238–241]. Accumulating studies have therefore characterized microbial taxa and enzymes that modulate chemotherapy through mechanisms such as drug activation, inactivation, transport, and reactivation (Table 3).

Microbes also contribute to chemotherapy-associated toxicity. A representative example is bacterial enzyme β-glucuronidase, which reactivates the inactive glucuronidated metabolite of irinotecan (SN-38G) in the gut, contributing to irinotecan-induced diarrhea [242–245]. Emerging evidence further suggests that modulation of the gut microbiome can alleviate treatment-related toxicities through effects on oxidative stress, inflammation, and barrier integrity [229, 230]. Together, these mechanistic studies demonstrate the role of gut microbes in modulating chemotherapy-induced toxicity through enzymatic pathway and broader host–microbe interactions.

Microbiome-associated determinants of ICI efficacy and irAEs

Association-based clinical studies have linked both gut and intratumoral microbiota to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) efficacy and immune-related adverse events (irAEs) (Tables 4 and 5). For the gut microbiome, antibiotic use prior to ICI treatment is associated with poor response rates and overall survival, reinforcing the concept that microbial disruption may impact immunotherapy efficacy [248]. Across multiple cancers, specific gut microbial signatures have been reported, with increased Faecalibacterium abundance and higher microbial diversity associated with favorable responses in multiple cohorts, whereas taxa such as Fusobacterium are more frequently linked to resistance [44, 249–251]. Machine learning analyses further support the predictive value of microbiome-derived features for ICI response, although these models remain primarily correlative and require validation across independent cohorts [252].

Beyond the gut microbiome, intratumoral microbial communities are also associated with ICI outcomes. Specific tumor-associated taxa have been linked to resistance or response across cancer types, supporting the potential as predictive biomarkers (Tables 4 and 5). More recently, findings from the CIAO clinical trial showed that total intratumoral bacterial abundance was the only microbiome-related feature predictive of immune checkpoint blockade response in head and neck cancer. Higher bacterial abundance was associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, marked by neutrophil accumulation and depletion of T cells and other adaptive immune cells [253]. These findings suggest that intratumoral microbial features may reflect or contribute to immune-contexture differences relevant to ICI response, but mechanistic validation remains needed.

Moreover, the microbiome has also been implicated in the development of irAEs. Post-anti-PD-1 analyses show that melanoma patients who developed irAEs had higher abundance of gut Fusobacterium, Enterococcus, Veillonella, and lower abundance of Bifidobacterium and Agathobacter [254]. In addition, intratumoral Blautia luti and other Lachnospiraceae are enriched in melanoma patients responsive to anti-PD-1 treatment without developing irAEs [255]. Together, these association-based studies suggest that gut and intratumoral microbial signatures may help stratify ICI efficacy and toxicity. AI-based approaches provide a complementary framework for integrating microbiome and host multi-omics data to identify predictive signatures; however, these remain largely correlative and require mechanistic validation for clinical translation [208].

Mechanistic and perturbation evidence for microbial modulation of ICI efficacy and toxicity

Preclinical and perturbation studies provide stronger evidence that microbiota can functionally modulate immunotherapy efficacy. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from responder patients into germ-free or antibiotic-treated recipient models enhances response to anti-PD-1 therapy, demonstrating functional transferability of response-associated microbiota [44, 45]. At the species and metabolite level, studies across different tumor models have shown that taxa such as Akkermansia muciniphila, Enterococcus hirae, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, as well as metabolites including short-chain fatty acids and TMAO, can modulate antitumor immunity [256–260]. Diet-induced microbiome modulation further influences treatment response, underscoring the role of host–microbiome–environment interactions in shaping ICI efficacy [13–16].

Preclinical mechanistic models also suggest a causal role for gut microbes in irAE development. Vancomycin-induced depletion of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus exacerbated ICI-induced colitis in melanoma models, which was mitigated by reintroducing of these probiotics without compromising antitumor effect [261, 262]. However, administration of Bifidobacterium- and Lactobacillus-based probiotics in other melanoma models impaired response to anti-PD-L1 [14]. These divergent findings may be attributed to differences in microbial strains and experimental contexts, highlighting the need for further context-specific studies before clinical translation is possible. An important translational goal is therefore to disentangle microbial determinants of therapeutic benefit from those of toxicity.

Overall, association-based clinical studies and AI-assisted analyses can nominate microbial biomarkers and prioritize candidate host–microbe interactions, whereas controlled microbiome perturbation studies provide stronger evidence supporting causal mechanisms [263]. Translation of these mechanistic findings into clinical applications requires additional clinical validation, which is discussed in the following section.

Clinical translation of cancer microbiome discoveries

Building on the predictive and mechanistic insights described above, a key challenge is how to translate microbiome-derived knowledge into clinically actionable decision-making frameworks. We therefore describe the practical steps necessary for clinical translation, focusing on biomarker readiness and clinical utility, before turning to emerging AI-enabled systems that may facilitate future clinical decision support and iterative translational microbiome research.

Biomarker readiness and clinical utility

Translating cancer microbiome signatures into clinical practice requires progression through three distinct stages of evidence that are frequently conflated: (i) analytical validation, which establishes whether an assay measures microbial features accurately, reproducibly, and robustly across operators, batches, and platforms; (ii) clinical validation, which establishes whether the measured signature is reproducibly associated with a clinical state or outcome in independent populations; and (iii) demonstration of clinical utility, which establishes whether using the biomarker to guide decisions measurably improves patient outcomes. This forms an evidentiary hierarchy long established in biomarker development [264]. Judged against these criteria, the great majority of cancer microbiome biomarkers remain at the discovery or early analytical stage [265]. A recent multidisciplinary Delphi consensus identified the scarcity of validated, kitted analytical assays as the principal barrier to clinical qualification [265]. Standardized reporting frameworks such as Strengthening The Organization and Reporting of Microbiome Studies (STORMS) have been proposed to provide the cross-study comparability that biomarker qualification requires [216].

These readiness criteria map unevenly onto the biomarker classes. Fecal metagenomic classifiers for colorectal cancer are the most mature, with cross-cohort meta-analyses yielding reproducible taxonomic and functional signatures and models that retain accuracy (AUC≈0.8) in independent cohorts [89, 90]. Tumor-intrinsic microbial signatures sit further back: the influential pan-cancer tissue- and blood-based classifier was challenged on the grounds that host-read misclassification and normalization artifacts could generate spurious cancer-type-specific signals, prompting rebuttals and the development of more conservative, contamination-aware detection frameworks [67, 124, 165]. Predictive biomarkers for ICI response are intermediate, robustly associated with response at the group level, yet poorly generalizable across cohorts, with no single taxon serving as a consistent biomarker [266, 267]. Collectively, these cases underscore that AI-derived or statistically-derived signatures should be treated as candidate biomarkers until subjected to contamination-aware quality control, feature-stability assessment, and prospective external validation.

On the interventional side, microbiome modulation has produced encouraging but preliminary signals tempered by safety considerations. Early human clinical studies evaluating microbiome modulation strategies have provided preliminary evidence that responder-derived FMT may reinstate anti-PD-1 responses in a subset of patients with ICI-refractory melanoma, and first-line FMT has reported objective response rates of up to 65% in small phase I cohorts, motivating ongoing randomized, placebo-controlled trials in additional tumor types [268–270]; defined live biotherapeutics such as CBM588 improved clinical endpoints in small, open-label randomized trials while missing their microbial primary endpoints [271, 272]; and higher dietary fiber intake, but not probiotic use, was associated with improved progression-free survival on ICIs [14].

Against these signals, the transmission of drug-resistant organisms by FMT, including a fatal extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli bacteremia [273], together with donor- and strain-dependent variability, nonstandardized manufacturing and dosing, and regulatory requirements for live biotherapeutic products, constrains routine use. Similarly, the effects of probiotics on cancer outcomes may vary depending on bacterial strain, dose, timing, and treatment context. These considerations highlight the need for cautious interpretation and rigorous longitudinal trials [274] to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and context-specific applicability of microbiome-based interventions. The recent licensure of the first fecal-microbiota-derived products for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection [275, 276] offers a precedent for standardization, although oncology indications will require adequately powered efficacy and safety trials.

Advancing from these early signals to demonstrated clinical utility will require a coordinated, stage-gated effort rather than the further accumulation of association studies. At the analytical level, this means adopting STORMS-compliant reporting, contamination-aware bioinformatic pipelines, negative and positive controls, and shared reference materials and benchmark datasets, so that analytical validation is demonstrable and comparable across laboratories [67, 124, 165, 216, 265]. Candidate signatures should then be prespecified and evaluated in prospective, multicenter, and geographically diverse cohorts using leakage-free, externally validated models with explicit confounder adjustment, moving beyond retrospective, single-cohort discovery [266, 267]. Clinical utility, in turn, must be established by testing whether biomarker-guided decisions change outcomes in biomarker-stratified interventional trials, and by pursuing formal biomarker qualification or companion diagnostic pathways with regulatory agencies. On the interventional side, microbiome modulation strategies should be evaluated in adequately powered randomized trials with harmonized products, standardized donor and strain selection and manufacturing, safety-first designs, and active pharmacovigilance [14, 268–273]. Throughout, AI should be scoped as a tool to prioritize robust candidates, integrate host–microbiome multi-omics, and predict treatment response and immune-related toxicity, provided that every model is paired with uncertainty estimation and external validation and that model outputs are treated as hypotheses until clinically validated, particularly given the small, heterogeneous, and confounded nature of current datasets.

AI systems for clinical decision support and translational microbiome research

Building on these requirements, emerging AI systems may provide an infrastructure for moving cancer microbiome research beyond isolated prediction toward integrated and iterative translational pipelines. Agentic AI should not be viewed as a fundamentally separate class of models from predictive AI; rather, in many modern applications, it represents a system-level architecture that builds upon predictive foundation models, including large language models, and augments them with planning, retrieval, tool use, memory, and external execution capabilities. In microbiome research, systems such as Eubiota have been proposed to integrate heterogeneous resources, including sequencing-derived data, experimental phenotypes, domain databases, protocols, and biomedical literature, within a unified reasoning framework to support hypothesis generation and prioritization of candidate genes, microbial communities, and metabolites [277].

However, fully autonomous systems face several limitations, particularly in handling uncertainty and incorporating expert feedback. Current implementations often rely on coarse-grained or system-level feedback and lack explicit mechanisms for identifying uncertain cases or evaluating the impact of feedback on decision quality. Human-in-the-loop frameworks provide a more structured approach by enabling targeted, instance-level expert intervention. By focusing expert input on low-confidence predictions, these systems improve reliability and interpretability while reducing annotation burden, making them particularly suitable for high-risk or out-of-distribution scenarios [278]. Despite these advances, the application of such frameworks in cancer microbiome research remains limited. Most existing systems have been developed in broader biomedical or oncology contexts and do not yet directly incorporate microbiome-derived clinical data. This highlights a gap between methodological innovation and domain-specific clinical translation.

Looking forward, integrating microbiome data with host molecular profiles, imaging, and clinical variables will require continuous and adaptive computational infrastructures. In this context, digital twin–like frameworks may provide a conceptual model for integrating longitudinal data and supporting in silico exploration of disease progression and treatment response [279–281]. In summary, emerging AI paradigms, from agentic systems to human-in-the-loop frameworks and digital twin–like approaches, offer a potential pathway for advancing microbiome research from predictive modeling toward iterative, validation-aware clinical translation. However, these systems should be viewed as complementary tools that guide, rather than replace, experimental and clinical validation.

Conclusions and future directions

Cancer microbiome research is moving from descriptive association studies toward integrative analyses of host–microbe interactions across systemic and local tissue contexts. Although multi-omics profiling and AI-based models have expanded the ability to detect microbial signals, prioritize biomarkers, and generate mechanistic hypotheses, clinical translation remains constrained by low microbial biomass in tumor tissues, contamination risk, cohort heterogeneity, limited matched host–microbiome datasets, and insufficient prospective validation. Future progress will require standardized experimental and computational workflows, rigorous cross-cohort validation, longitudinal sampling, perturbation-based studies, and integration of microbiome data with host molecular, spatial, imaging, and clinical information.

Looking beyond the tumor and gut compartments, the oral cavity is a compelling adjacent reservoir. It is a cancer-associated niche, where oral pathobionts, most notably F. nucleatum, are enriched in and mechanistically implicated in head and neck, esophageal, gastric, and colorectal cancers [30, 31, 37, 228]. Meanwhile, it is among the most thoroughly catalogued microbial habitats of any body site, yet its therapeutic potential remains less developed than that of the gut. A natural extension of the framework described here is therefore the genome-guided design of oral probiotic consortia, in which strain-level functional capacity, colonization potential, and reverse liabilities are inferred directly from genome sequence to nominate candidate strains for targeted experimental validation.

Importantly, current microbiome signatures should not be interpreted as clinically actionable drivers without mechanistic and experimental validation. AI frameworks are therefore best viewed as tools to prioritize robust microbial candidates and guide iterative translation of cancer microbiome discoveries into clinically relevant applications. Realizing this potential will depend on the coordinated development of rigorous decontamination pipelines, deep learning approaches capable of modeling microbe–host–treatment relationships, high-throughput experimental validation, and prospective clinical validation, which together can move the field beyond observational correlations toward actionable, treatment-specific mechanisms and clinically meaningful applications in precision oncology.
