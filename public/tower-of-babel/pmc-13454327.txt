TLCD1 correlates with malignant progression of hepatocellular carcinoma and activation of the ERK signaling cascade
Authors: Pei Wen, Qiuyan Liu, Xuefeng Guo, Chao Tan, Xiaonian Zhu, Xiaofei Li, Yuanxiao Du, Qibin Zhang, Lingyun Liu, Chunhua Bei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is characterized by high invasiveness and poor prognosis, making the identification of key driver genes crucial for the development of effective treatment strategies. TLCD1 is a member of the TLC domain family, which participates in membrane trafficking and lipid metabolism regulation. While bioinformatics suggests that TLCD1 is abnormally expressed in HCC, experimental evidence for its functions and mechanisms is still lacking. This study aimed to explore the expression pattern, biological functions, and potential molecular mechanism of TLCD1 in HCC. By integrating transcriptome analysis and immunohistochemical detection, it was found that TLCD1 was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues, and its high expression was associated with shortened overall and disease‑free survival in HCC patients. With TLCD1 knockdown and overexpression cell models, CCK‑8, colony formation, wound healing, and transwell assays revealed that overexpression of TLCD1 significantly promoted HCC cell proliferation and metastasis, whereas TLCD1 knockdown exerted an inhibitory effect. Mechanistically, transcriptome data together with western blot analysis indicated that TLCD1 may activate the mitogen‑activated protein kinase (MAPK)/ERK signaling cascade by upregulating the phosphorylation levels of KRAS, ERK1/2, and c‑MYC. In conclusion, TLCD1 may drive the malignant progression of HCC by activating the ERK signaling pathway and may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55516-7.Keywords: TLCD1, Hepatocellular carcinoma, ERK signaling pathway, Malignant progression, Cell proliferationSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Cell biology, Oncology

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant subtype of primary liver cancer, accounting for 75–85% of all cases1. Its global incidence and mortality have risen steadily, making it the third leading cause of cancer-related death2,3. Recent epidemiological data estimate approximately 367,700 new cases and 317,000 deaths from primary liver cancer each year, ranking fourth for incidence and second for mortality among all malignancies4. The complex pathogenesis of HCC is largely due to its high level of heterogeneity and lack of symptoms during the early stages, which often leads to diagnosis at an advanced phase, as well as its strong propensity for progression to metastatic and recurrent disease5,6. Despite optimized treatment strategies for HCC—including liver transplantation, local ablation therapy, transarterial chemoembolization, and the use of novel targeted drugs—the disease still has a poor prognosis in its advanced state7,8. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms that drive HCC progression, particularly the key regulators and signaling pathways underlying its malignant behavior, is critical for improving patient outcomes.

TLCD1 is a member of the TLC superfamily, localized to human chromosome 17q11.2, encoding a TLC domain‑containing protein. The protein product of TLCD1 interacts with mitochondria in an evolutionarily conserved manner and directly regulates the PE composition of mitochondrial membranes, thereby modulating cellular metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory states9. TLCD1-driven lipid reshaping directly alters membrane fluidity, regulates immune responses and immune infiltration, and increases the permeability of chemotherapeutic agents, thereby facilitating tumorigenesis and progression10,11. Our previous transcriptome sequencing of HCC unexpectedly identified TLCD1 as highly expressed12; meanwhile, independent studies have reported that TLCD1 is significantly upregulated in HCC tissues and that its high expression predicts shorter overall survival11. Nevertheless, the precise biological role of TLCD1 in HCC and its downstream molecular mechanisms remain incompletely defined. Given the central role of dysregulated signaling pathways in cancer, we hypothesized that TLCD1 might exert its oncogenic functions through the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade. This highly conserved serine/threonine kinase module comprises four canonical branches: ERK, JNK, p38 and ERK513,14, which orchestrate proliferation, differentiation and migration15–18. In HCC, the core RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK axis is frequently constitutively activated through overexpression of upstream receptors (e.g., EGFR, FGFR) or mutations at key nodes (e.g., KRAS, BRAF), resulting in uncontrolled cell growth and invasion19–21. For instance, CsGRN overactivates the EGFR-driven RAS/MEK/ERK pathway, increasing both mRNA abundance and phosphorylation of EGFR, RAS and ERK, and consequently promotes proliferation, migration and invasion of HCC cells22. Clinically, ERK signaling is activated in ~ 50% of early-stage and nearly all advanced HCC cases, as evidenced by elevated MEK/ERK expression and phosphorylation23. Therefore, the constitutive activation of the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK pathway is a central driver of HCC and establishes it as a critical therapeutic target.

In summary, elucidating the aberrant expression of TLCD1 and its biological role in HCC, particularly whether it modulates the MAPK pathway to promote malignant progression, has become critical. A systematic dissection of TLCD1-mediated molecular network in HCC will provide an experimental foundation for improving patient prognosis.

Results

High TLCD1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in patients with HCC

Transcriptome sequencing results from our previous study on HCC showed that TLCD1 expression was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared with non-tumor controls (Fig. 1A). IHC staining further validated this observation, showing strong TLCD1 signals predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of HCC cells (Figs. 1B and C). Analysis of TCGA data further confirmed that TLCD1 mRNA levels were markedly higher in HCC samples than in adjacent normal liver tissues (Fig. 1D). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated that high TLCD1 expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in HCC patients. Specifically, the high-expression group exhibited significantly lower OS rates (Fig. 1E) and shorter DFS durations (Fig. 1F). These findings suggest that TLCD1 may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker associated with the progression and poor prognosis of HCC.

(B, C) Immunohistochemistry showing TLCD1 protein expression in HCC tissues. (D) TLCD1 gene expression in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. (E, F) Association between TLCD1 expression and patient prognosis in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. Asterisks above error bars indicate statistically significant differences between groups (*P < 0.05).

TLCD1 expression in HCC cell lines and validation of overexpression and knockdown constructs

RT-qPCR analysis showed that TLCD1 expression in HepG2, SK-HEP-1, SMMC-7721, and MHCC-97 H cells was upregulated by 2.55-, 4.01-, 4.56-, and 14.96-fold, respectively, compared with normal liver cells (Fig. 2A). Based on the TLCD1 expression levels screened in different HCC cell lines, TLCD1 overexpression, knockdown, and empty vector constructs were generated and transfected into target cells. In MHCC-97 H cells, TLCD1 mRNA expression was dramatically reduced by 68% in the TLCD1-SH1 group relative to the KDNC group, while the TLCD1-OE group increased TLCD1 mRNA levels by 4.45-fold compared to the OENC group (Fig. 2B). In SMMC-7721 cells, the TLCD1-SH1 group decreased TLCD1 mRNA expression by 53% relative to the KDNC group, whereas the TLCD1-OE group elevated TLCD1 mRNA levels by 8.15-fold compared to the OENC group (Fig. 2C). Western blot analysis further showed corresponding changes in TLCD1 protein expression in both cell lines: in MHCC-97 H cells, TLCD1 protein expression was reduced by 61% in the TLCD1-SH1 group (relative to the KDNC group) and increased by 1.93-fold in the TLCD1-OE group (relative to the OENC group) (Figs. 2D-G). Collectively, these results validated the establishment of TLCD1 overexpression and knockdown cell models.

TLCD1 promotes the proliferative capacity of HCC cells

From the functional analysis, the TLCD1-OE group in SMMC-7721 and MHCC-97 H cells significantly enhanced cell proliferative activity, whereas the TLCD1-SH1 group markedly inhibited cell proliferative activity (Figs. 3A and B). Consistent with these findings, colony formation assays revealed that the TLCD1-OE group in MHCC-97 H and SMMC-7721 cells increased colony-forming efficiency by 1.22- and 1.81-fold, respectively, compared with the OENC groups (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the TLCD1-SH1 group reduced the number of colonies by 57% and 34% in MHCC-97 H and SMMC-7721 cells, respectively (Fig. 3C). These findings preliminarily indicated that TLCD1 positively regulates the proliferative capacity of HCC cells, prompting further investigation into its effects on HCC cell migration and invasion.

TLCD1 promotes the migration and invasion of HCC cells

Wound healing assay results showed that after 48 h, MHCC-97 H cells in the TLCD1-OE group exhibited a significantly higher wound healing rate compared to the OENC group, with a 1.60-fold increase. In contrast, the healing rate of MHCC-97 H cells in the TLCD1-SH1 group was significantly reduced by 52% compared to that of the KDNC group (Fig. 4A). Similarly, the TLCD1-OE group in SMMC-7721 cells showed a significantly faster wound healing rate than the OENC group (a 1.20-fold increase), while the TLCD1-SH1 group showed a significantly slower healing rate, decreasing by 41% compared to the KDNC group (Fig. 4A). Transwell migration assays revealed that MHCC-97 H cells in the TLCD1-OE group led to a 2.32-fold increase in the number of cells that crossed the membrane, while the TLCD1-SH1 group reduced migration by 74% (Fig. 4B). In SMMC-7721 cells, the TLCD1-OE group increased the number of migrating cells by 2.34-fold relative to the OENC group, and the TLCD1-SH1 group decreased it by 16% (Fig. 4B).

(B) Transwell migration assay to detect the migration ability of TLCD1 in the WT, KDNC, TLCD1-SH1, OENC, and TLCD1-OE groups. (C) Transwell invasion assay to detect the invasion ability of TLCD1 in the WT, KDNC, TLCD1-SH1, OENC, and TLCD1-OE groups. In invasion assays, the TLCD1-OE group of MHCC-97 H cells exhibited approximately twice the invasive capacity of the OENC group, whereas the TLCD1-SH1 group showed a 26% decrease relative to the KDNC group (Fig. 4C). Similarly, in SMMC-7721 cells, the TLCD1-OE group displayed a markedly heightened invasive capacity relative to the OENC group, as evidenced by a 1.25-fold increase in the number of invaded cells. Conversely, SMMC-7721 cells in the TLCD1-SH1 group produced a significant reduction in invasiveness, exhibiting a 15% decrease compared to the KDNC group (Fig. 4C). Taken together, the results from the wound healing, migration, and invasion assays preliminarily suggest that TLCD1 enhances the migratory and invasive capacity of HCC cells, although the underlying molecular mechanisms require further investigation.

Transcriptome integration analysis suggests that TLCD1 may regulate tumor-associated transcriptional programs via the ERK signaling axis

Based on the TCGA-LIHC transcriptome dataset, volcano plot analysis identified a total of 1145 TLCD1-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 582 up-regulated and 563 down-regulated genes (Fig. 5A). The top 100 DEGs (50 upregulated and 50 downregulated) were selected to construct a heatmap, which clearly illustrates their distinct expression patterns (Fig. 5B). Upstream transcription factor prediction across six public databases (hTFtarget, KnockTF, FIMO-JASPAR, ENCODE, GTRD and ChIP-Atlas) consistently highlighted CREB1, a well-characterized regulator of ERK-dependent transcriptional programs (Fig. 5C). GO analysis revealed that TLCD1-regulated DEGs are significantly enriched in biological processes including MAPK signaling, cell proliferation, and positive regulation of the ERK1/2 cascade; their cellular components are mainly localized to transmembrane transporter complexes, synaptic membranes, and secretory granules; and their molecular functions include serine-type endopeptidase activity, cytokine activity, and DNA-binding transcription factor activity (Fig. 5D). KEGG pathway mapping further demonstrated significant enrichment of cancer-related signaling cascades, such as the MAPK, PI3K-Akt, and Wnt signaling pathways, as well as pathways regulating stem cell pluripotency, ECM–receptor interaction, and synaptic vesicle recycling (Fig. 5E).

TLCD1 correlates with ERK signaling pathway activation in HCC

RT-qPCR results showed that in the TLCD1-SH1 group of MHCC-97 H cells, the mRNA expression levels of KRAS, HRAS, MAP2K2, MAPK3, and MYC were significantly decreased by 69%, 64%, 51%, 77%, and 33%, respectively, whereas the mRNA expression level of EGFR was significantly increased by 2.83-fold. Conversely, in the TLCD1-OE group, the mRNA expression levels of KRAS, HRAS, MAP2K2, MAPK3, and MYC were significantly increased by 1.95-, 3.60-, 2.76-, 7.12-, and 1.67-fold, respectively, while the mRNA expression level of EGFR was significantly decreased by 65%. (Figs. 6A and B). Similarly, in SMMC-7721 cells, the TLCD1-SH1 group showed insignificant decreases in the mRNA expression levels of KRAS, HRAS, MAP2K2, MAPK3, and MYC by 57%, 55%, 58%, 68%, and 60%, respectively, whereas the mRNA expression level of EGFR was significantly increased by 3.03-fold. The TLCD1-OE group significantly increased the mRNA expression levels of KRAS, HRAS, MAP2K2, MAPK3, and MYC by 2.55-, 4.16-, 2.01-, 5.61-, and 2.26-fold, respectively, while the mRNA expression level of EGFR was significantly decreased by 51% (Figs. 6C and D). These results suggest a positive correlation between TLCD1 and ERK pathway effectors, and an inverse correlation with EGFR expression at the mRNA level.

(E-H) Western Blot to detect the protein phosphorylation levels of p-EGFR, p-KRAS, p-ERK1/2, p-c-MYC in the WT, KDNC, TLCD1-SH1, OENC, and TLCD1-OE groups. Western blot analysis revealed that, in MHCC-97 H cells, the TLCD1-SH1 group significantly decreased the phosphorylation levels of p-KRAS, p-ERK1/2, and p-c-MYC by 65%, 63%, and 44%, respectively, whereas the phosphorylation level of p-EGFR was significantly increased by 1.73-fold. In contrast, the TLCD1-OE group significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of p-KRAS, p-ERK1/2, and p-c-MYC by 1.95-, 2.00-, and 1.70-fold, respectively, while the phosphorylation level of p-EGFR was significantly decreased by 55% (Figs. 6E and G). In SMMC-7721 cells, the TLCD1-SH1 group markedly diminished the phosphorylation levels of p-KRAS, p-ERK1/2, and p-c-MYC by 44%, 63%, and 53%, respectively, whereas the phosphorylation level of p-EGFR was significantly increased by 1.54-fold. The TLCD1-OE group significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of p-KRAS, p-ERK1/2, and p-c-MYC by 1.21-, 2.00-, and 2.01-fold, respectively, while the phosphorylation level of p-EGFR was significantly decreased by 63% (Figs. 6F and H). These results are consistent with the mRNA-level findings, supporting a correlative role of TLCD1 in ERK signaling activity in HCC cells.

Interactions of TLCD1 with key molecules of the ERK signaling pathway and their clinical relevance

In the protein-protein interaction network, the downstream target genes of TLCD1 were significantly enriched in the ERK signaling pathway, including MAPK3, EGFR, MAPK9, KRAS, HRAS, MAP2K2, and MYC (Fig. 7A). Protein-protein docking simulation using the GRAMM algorithm revealed that TLCD1 has potential binding affinity for EGFR (two predicted interaction sites, binding free energy − 39.2 kcal/mol, Fig. 7B), for HRAS (three predicted sites, -19.2 kcal/mol, Fig. 7C), and for MAPK3 (six predicted sites, -26.0 kcal/mol, Fig. 7D). In these complexes, hydrogen bonds bridging amino acid residues at the interfaces contributed to the stability of the interactions. These in silico interactions require experimental validation. Pearson correlation analysis indicated a weak negative correlation between TLCD1 and EGFR expression (Fig. 7E), whereas positive correlations were observed between TLCD1 and HRAS (Fig. 7F), TLCD1 and MAP2K2 (Fig. 7G), and TLCD1 and HRAS (Fig. 7H). Box-plot comparisons showed that both HRAS and MAPK3 transcripts were significantly upregulated in HCC tissues compared with non-tumor liver tissues (Figs. 7J and L). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that HCC patients with high HRAS expression in tumors had significantly poorer overall survival than those with low expression; similarly, high MAPK3 expression was also associated with worse prognosis (Figs. 7N and P).The preliminary evidence from the protein-protein interaction network, molecular docking, correlation analysis, and prognostic associations suggests that TLCD1 may functionally interact with the ERK signaling pathway through multiple key molecules (e.g., EGFR, HRAS, MAPK3).

Discussion

Despite recent advances in the diagnosis and treatment of HCC, long‑term prognosis remains poor24,25. Tumor recurrence and metastasis continue to pose major clinical challenges26. Moreover, oncologic heterogeneity and drug resistance remain significant obstacles27,28. In this study, by measuring TLCD1 expression, we observed that the ERK signaling axis may be linked to the growth and invasiveness of HCC cells. These results show partial alignment with the findings of Li et al.29. Our work establishes a theoretical foundation for understanding the regulatory mechanism of TLCD1 in the ERK signaling transduction pathway in HCC, providing novel insights into potential therapeutic targets for HCC treatment.

TLCD1 has been identified and proposed as a candidate biomarker in several types of cancers, playing potential roles in vital tumorigenesis processes. In kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), Cai et al. reported that TLCD1 is aberrantly expressed and significantly correlates with poor prognosis, as determined by LASSO and univariate Cox regression analysis30. In gastric cancer (GC), TLCD1 was found to be associated with differentially expressed and m6A-methylated genes linked to clinicopathological features and prognosis, suggesting its potential as a novel marker for cancer-related m6A methylation31. In uveal melanoma (UM), TLCD1 was identified as an independent prognostic factor through sequential analyses including univariate Cox, robust likelihood survival, and multivariate Cox regression32. In HCC, TLCD1 expression correlates with infiltrated leukocyte cells, immunoregulatory factors, and sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs, and it has been proposed as a candidate marker for immunity-related research and biomedical applications for drug efficacy11. In this study, based on transcriptome sequencing, IHC analysis of HCC tissues, and TCGA data mining, we observed that TLCD1 is markedly upregulated in HCC tissues and that its expression level is closely associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes.

“Sustaining proliferative signalling” and “evading growth suppressors” are recognized as core hallmarks of cancer33. In normal tissues, cell division proceeds in an orderly fashion under stringent regulatory control to maintain tissue homeostasis and function34. By contrast, neoplastic cells undergo uncontrolled proliferation as a consequence of genetic and epigenetic alterations, leading to unchecked cell-cycle progression and clonal expansion35. In this study, CCK-8 results showed that TLCD1 knockdown markedly reduced cell viability relative to the control group, whereas TLCD1 overexpression significantly promoted viability. Colony formation assay corroborated these findings: TLCD1 depletion markedly suppressed the number and size of colonies, whereas TLCD1 overexpression yielded more numerous and larger colonies. Collectively, these data reveal that TLCD1 robustly enhances the proliferative and self-renewal potential of HCC cells. Nevertheless, it has been proposed that malignant tumor progression is governed by a “migration & proliferation dichotomy” in which the two phenotypes, rapid proliferation and invasive migration, act synergistically to drive tumor dissemination36. Tumor cell migration and invasion ultimately lead to metastasis, and metastatic nodules often exhibit resistance to therapeutic interventions, thereby exerting a negative impact on patient prognosis37. Based on this, TLCD1 knockdown significantly reduced HCC cell migration and invasion, while its overexpression counteracted these effects. These results indicate a positive correlation between TLCD1 expression levels and the migratory/invasive potential of HCC cells.

While our study suggests the mechanistic role of TLCD1 in promoting HCC proliferation and metastasis, notably, other clinicopathological factors associated with tumor invasiveness, such as microvascular invasion, tumor satellite nodules, intrahepatic vascular compression, and tumor location, were not included in the current analysis due to limitations in the available clinical metadata. These factors may act as confounders or exert synergistic effects with TLCD1 expression, potentially influencing disease progression. Future studies integrating comprehensive clinicopathological parameters are warranted to fully evaluate how TLCD1 interacts with these variables and to refine its prognostic and therapeutic relevance in HCC. Such analyses could clarify whether TLCD1 serves as an independent predictor or interacts with established markers of invasiveness, as suggested by recent studies on hepatic or portal vein compression and its association with microvascular invasion and satellite nodules38.

HCC development is known to be an evolutionary process that involves the gradual accrual of oncogene activation, tumor suppressor gene inactivation, and epigenetic alterations that lead to improper activation of core signaling pathways39–41. Based on this framework, to elucidate the mechanisms of TLCD1-induced HCC pathogenesis, this study performed GO/KEGG enrichment analyses on TLCD1-regulated DEGs to identify the biological processes and signaling pathways involved. Enrichment analysis indicated that TLCD1’s regulatory role in HCC is strongly associated with the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway. Protein–protein interaction and Pearson analysis further showed associations between TLCD1 expression and key MAPK/ERK pathway molecules (EGFR, KRAS, HRAS, MAPK3, and MYC) at both the protein interaction and transcriptional levels. In addition, protein-protein docking analysis suggested that TLCD1 may have a certain binding affinity for HRAS and MAPK3 proteins. The MAPK/ERK signaling cascade is mediated by MAPK3 and its upstream regulators EGFR and KRAS, which regulate cell proliferation and migration in accordance with well-validated canonical MAPK signaling theory42–44.

The role of TLCD1 in HCC pathogenesis was comprehensively analyzed by evaluating TLCD1-induced HCC cell lines for the specific expression of MAPK signaling cascade proteins and regulators. Transcriptionally, TLCD1 knockdown resulted in downregulation of KRAS, HRAS, MAP2K2, MAPK3, and MYC, while upregulating EGFR mRNA levels. At the protein level, TLCD1 knockdown did not markedly alter the total protein levels of EGFR, KRAS, ERK1/2, or c-MYC; however, it significantly reduced the activated (phosphorylated) forms of p-KRAS, p-ERK1/2, and p-c-MYC, while enhancing p-EGFR levels. Similarly, TLCD1 overexpression had no significant effect on total protein levels but markedly increased the phosphorylation levels of p-KRAS, p-ERK1/2, and p-c-MYC, while suppressing p-EGFR expression. These results indicate that TLCD1 may positively regulate ERK pathway effectors and inversely modulate EGFR expression at both the mRNA and protein level, and this regulation of ERK pathway effectors is independent of EGFR. The finding that TLCD1 coordinates this phosphorylation network suggests that TLCD1 may act as a signaling hub in HCC. Given the known oncogenic properties of ERK and c-MYC as transcription factors45, it is possible that TLCD1 contributes to the malignant progression of HCC, at least in part, through the activation of the ERK signaling pathway. This indirect inference is supported by an independent study showing that arsenic sulfide treatment effectively inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cell lines and significantly alleviated HCC progression in mouse models29. Transcriptomic analysis of arsenic sulfide-treated HepG2 cells further indicated that the inhibitory effect of arsenic sulfide on the MAPK signaling pathway is associated with downregulated TLCD1 expression29. Taken together, these cross-validating findings raise the possibility that TLCD1 may represent a novel node within this well-established oncogenic cascade. However, whether TLCD1 directly activates ERK signaling pathway requires further experimental validation. All functional and molecular findings presented herein are correlative and do not establish direct causality.

While our study focuses on the molecular correlation between TLCD1 and ERK pathway activation in HCC invasiveness, we acknowledge that tumor invasiveness is a multidimensional process involving not only molecular mechanisms but also surgical and imaging-related factors. Preoperative contrast-enhanced CT or MRI can detect hepatic or portal vein compression, which helps predict microvascular invasion and tumor invasiveness46; yet such imaging predictors were not included in our current analysis. Luo et al. demonstrated that middle hepatic vein (MHV) resection during hemi-hepatectomy significantly affects surgical outcomes and long-term prognosis in HCC patients, with MHV resection associated with increased postoperative complications and decreased OS47. These findings imply surgical strategies, particularly vascular resection, interacting with biological tumor characteristics to influence patient outcomes. Given that TLCD1 correlates with ERK pathway activation and HCC invasiveness, it is plausible that patients with high TLCD1 expression may have more aggressive tumor biology, which could affect the decision to perform MHV resection.

In addition to these clinical considerations, methodological limitations related to sample size and cellular models should also be acknowledged. The present study was conducted primarily using in vitro cell models, which may not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity and complex microenvironment of in vivo tumors. Despite the limited number of patient samples used for transcriptome sequencing (n = 3), the initial findings were consistently supported by analysis of the larger TCGA-LIHC cohort (424 HCC samples), which enhances the reliability and generalizability of the observed TLCD1-associated expression patterns. We acknowledge that small sample size may not fully capture the heterogeneity of HCC, and that the use of specific cell lines is only a partial representation of tumor cell diversity. Nevertheless, these cell lines offer a well-controlled experimental system to investigate the mechanistic role of TLCD1, allowing for reproducible functional validation. Together, the integration of small-scale patient-derived data, large-cohort analysis, and controlled cellular experiments provides a complementary approach, balancing depth and breadth. This supports the relevance of our findings while underscoring the need for further studies to explore inter-patient and intra-tumor heterogeneity.

This study preliminarily explored the role of TLCD1 in HCC and suggests that TLCD1 may promote tumor progression through activation of the ERK signaling pathway. By integrating transcriptome analysis from patient-derived samples, the large TCGA-LIHC cohort, and functional validation in HCC cell lines, our findings demonstrate that elevated TLCD1 expression is associated with increased proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells, potentially via the MAPK/ERK pathway. These results highlight the translational potential of TLCD1: its expression could be used to stratify patients by risk, enabling more personalized monitoring and follow-up strategies. Furthermore, the mechanistic insights provide preliminary rationale for considering TLCD1 as a potential therapeutic target in the future. Given the predominantly correlative nature of the current evidence, a direct causal link between TLCD1 and ERK pathway activation has not been firmly established. Future studies are warranted to validate the oncogenic function of TLCD1 using patient-derived xenograft or organoid models, to evaluate TLCD1 as an independent prognostic marker through multicenter, large-cohort clinical studies, and to directly verify the physical interactions between TLCD1 and key molecules of the ERK pathway via co-immunoprecipitation and luciferase reporter assays.

Conclusions

This study provides an initial exploration of TLCD1 in HCC, describing its specific expression pattern, possible biological functions and potential regulatory mechanisms. The findings demonstrate that high TLCD1 expression is associated with activated ERK signaling pathway and contributes to HCC progression, at least in part, by modulating the ERK pathway. Additionally, TLCD1 may serve as a potential indicator for poor prognosis in HCC patients. This work lays a foundation for further investigating TLCD1 as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for HCC.

Methods

Cell culture

All five cells, HepG2, SK-hep-1, SMMC-7721, MHCC-97 H & LO2, were inoculated in high glucose DMEM (Gibco, California, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (OriCell, Guangzhou, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin ( Beyotime, Shanghai, China), incubated at 37℃, 5% CO₂ thermostat (Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA); passages were digested with 0.25% EDTA-free trypsin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Plasmid transfection

The shRNA plasmid, overexpression plasmid, and empty vector for TLCD1 were constructed by Guangzhou Huijun Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Plasmids were extracted from the constructed E. coli containing the target gene fragments using the Endotoxin-free plasmid small lift medium volume rapid extraction kit (Aidlab, Beijing, China). The extracted plasmids were transfected against SMMC-7721 and MHCC-97 H with GFP green English light labelled genes, and the transfection of the cells was observed by fluorescence microscopy after 48 h of transfection culture. Cell transfection was performed using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, five groups were set up: wild‑type (WT), TLCD1 knockdown (TLCD1‑SH1), knockdown negative control (KDNC), TLCD1 overexpression (TLCD1‑OE), and overexpression negative control (OENC). For all groups except WT, transfection complexes were prepared by mixing 2.5 µg plasmid DNA with 4µL Lipo8000™ in 125 µL serum-free high-glucose DMEM, incubating the mixture at room temperature (RT) for 30 min, and then drop-wise adding it to the corresponding wells of a 6-well plate. All procedures strictly followed the manufacturers’ protocols.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Cells inoculated in 6-well plates and transfected with plasmids were lysed with TRIzol™ (Invitrogen, California, UAS); RNA yield and purity were detected using a microspectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA) before subsequent steps. First-strand cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription of 1 µg of Template RNA in a 20 µL reaction system using ToloScript All-in-one RT EasyMix for qPCR reagent (TOLOBIO, Shanghai, China). The primer sequences are shown in supplementary Table 1. 2 × Q5 SYBR qPCR Master Mix reagent was subsequently used (TOLOBIO, Shanghai, China) was used to perform quantitative PCR reactions on QuantStudioTM 6 Flex (Applied Biosystems™, California, USA).

Western blot

After aspirating the medium, transfected cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS. One hundred microliters of freshly prepared lysis solution, RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), 100 mM Phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and 100 × protease-inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) combined at 100:1:2, was then added to each well of the 6-well plate and kept on ice throughout. All steps were performed on ice. Cells were quickly scraped off with a pre-chilled cell scraper, and the lysate was transferred to pre-cooled 1.5 mL tubes, followed by 30 min of additional incubation on ice to ensure complete lysis. The lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g at 4℃ for 20 min, and the clarified supernatant was transferred to a refrigerated clean centrifuge tube. Protein yield was determined using a BCA protein quantification kit (Biosharp, Anhui, China). Protein labelling was performed using ColorMixed Protein Marker (11–180 KD, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Samples were equalized with lysis mixture, mixed 4:1 with 5 × SDS loading buffer (CWBIO, Jiangsu, China), and boiled at 100℃ for 10 min to denature the proteins. For each sample, 25 µg of protein was resolved on 6%, 10%- or 12.5%-PAGE gels (Abbkine, Wuhan, China) and transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes by wet blotting. The Membranes were closed with TBST solution containing 5% skimmed milk for 2 h at RT, followed by overnight incubation at 4℃ with the specific primary antibody. On the morning of the second day, the membranes were washed three times with TBST for 5 min each, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for 2 h at RT. Protein bands were developed by ECL chemiluminescent substrate (Biosharp, Anhui, China) and captured with a ChemiDoc system (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Densitometry was performed with ImageJ. The specific primary and secondary antibodies are shown in supplementary Table 2.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) and colony-formation assays

CCK-8 assay: Cells were plated in 96-well plates at 2 × 10³ cells per 100 µL complete DMEM and cultured at 37℃ with 5%CO₂ for 6, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h, then 10 µL CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added per well. After 2 h incubation, absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a Varioskan LUX spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA). Colony-formation assay: A low density of 500 cells per well was seeded into fresh six-well plates, with three replicate wells per group. After 14 days of culture, distinct clones were clearly visible. The medium was aspirated, and the wells were gently rinsed twice with PBS. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 20 min. Gently rinsed with distilled water and air-dried. Images were acquired and colonies > 50 μm in diameter were counted using ImageJ.

Wound healing and transwell assays

Wound-healing assay: Three vertical scratches of equal width were created in the cell monolayer with a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. After washing, the cells were maintained in 1% FBS-DMEM to suppress proliferation. The wound field was first photographed on an inverted fluorescence imaging system (Evident Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), capturing ≥ 3 separate regions per well. Cultures were placed back in the incubator, and identical locations were re-photographed at 24 h and 48 h. Scratch area was measured with ImageJ’s MRI Wound Healing Tool plugin.

Transwell migration & invasion assays: cells were serum-starved in DMEM without FBS for 12 h to eliminate serum-induced signaling. They were then detached with 0.25% trypsin (EDTA-free), collected by centrifugation, and resuspended in serum-free DMEM to 2.5 × 104 cells /mL. A 600 µL portion of complete medium containing 10%FBS was introduced into the lower well of 24-well plates; 200 µL of cell suspension was gently layered into the upper Transwell chamber. The plates were then incubated for 48 h. For invasion assays, the procedure matched the migration setup, except that 50 µL of Matrigel, previously diluted to 2 mg/mL, was evenly spread over the polycarbonate membrane and permitted to polymerize for 1 h at 37℃ prior to cell addition. After the 48 h incubation, inserts were taken out, medium from the upper compartment was aspirated, and membranes were rinsed twice with PBS. Each insert was transferred to a new well containing 600 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde and fixed at RT for 15 min. Following two additional PBS washes, membranes were stained with 600 µL of 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min at RT in the dark. Upper-surface cells that had not migrated or invaded were carefully wiped away with a cotton swab, and excess dye was eliminated by thorough rinsing with PBS. Cells that had traversed the membrane were visualized under an inverted fluorescence imaging system; at least five random fields per insert were photographed and counted.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

HCC specimens (n = 12 paired samples) were immediately fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h, rinsed under running water to remove residual fixative, and processed in an automated tissue processor. Samples were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm, floated on a 42℃-water bath, and mounted on positively charged slides. Dehydration was carried out through an ascending ethanol series (75%, 85%, 95%, and absolute ethanol, 5 min each), followed by three changes of xylene (15 min each) and paraffin infiltration. Sections were baked at 60℃ for 2 h, then deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated using a graded alcohol series to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was carried out by high-pressure heating in Tris-EDTA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using Peroxidase Blocking Reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min. After three washes with phosphate-buffered saline in PBST, 5 min each time, non-specific binding was minimized by incubation with 10% normal goat serum (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 min in a humidified chamber. Slides were then incubated overnight at 4℃ with a primary antibody against TLCD1 diluted 1:50 in antibody diluent. Following three PBS washes, sections were incubated with an HRP-labeled secondary antibody (Genetech, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at RT. Antibody binding was DAB (Genetech, Shanghai, China) substrate for 5 min until brown cytoplasmic staining appeared without background; distilled water rinse was then used to halt the reaction. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (Bakeman Bio, Changsha, China) for 5 min, subjected to differentiation with 1% acid alcohol for 3 s, and subsequently blued by a 10 min wash under running tap water. Sections were dehydrated through 85%, 95%, and absolute ethanol, cleared in xylene, and cover slipped with neutral balsam. Finaly, sections were scanned using a whole-slide scanning system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) to generate digital images. TLCD1 expression levels at each location within the HCC tissue were evaluated using QuPath (version 0.5.1), and corresponding scores were obtained.

Data sources, stratification of TLCD1 expression levels, and analyses of differential expression and survival

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on cancerous and para-cancerous tissues from HCC patients (n = 3 paired samples) who underwent surgical resection, and the sequencing was completed by Guangzhou Huijun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All participants provided written informed consent.

Additionally, we downloaded gene expression quantification data and corresponding clinical metadata for the TCGA-LIHC cohort from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Using the Rjson package (version 0.2.21) in R (version 4.4.0), Ensemble identifiers were mapped to official gene symbols, yielding a raw‑count expression matrix of HCC samples (n = 424). Batch effects were removed with the ComBat function from the sva package (version 3.42.0), and the adjusted matrix was used for downstream analysis. Normality of TLCD1 raw counts across all samples was examined with SPSS (version 27) using P‑P plots and Q‑Q plots; the data followed a normal distribution with a mean of 578.200. This mean was used as the cutoff to define high‑expression (>578.200) and low‑expression (≤ 578.200) groups. Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed with the edgeR package (version 3.42.4) using |log₂ fold change| > 1 and P < 0.05 as cutoffs. For survival analysis, overall survival time and vital status of the same 424 TCGA‑LIHC patients were retrieved. Using the same mean cutoff, Kaplan‑Meier survival curves were generated with the survival package (version 3.5‑5) and survminer package (version 0.4.9) in R, and the log‑rank test was applied to compare survival distributions; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. This survival analysis was univariate and did not include other clinical variables.

Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed gene

We performed functional enrichment analysis using the Metascape tool (https://metascape.org) to interpret the differentially expressed genes in the context of GO biological processes and KEGG pathways. Metascape is an online platform that offers experimental biologists an integrated toolkit for the annotation and in-depth analysis of gene lists48. The analysis employed cut-offs of P < 0.05, a minimum count of 7, and an enrichment factor > 1.5. To summarize the findings, the enriched terms were graphically displayed using bubble plots.

Protein-protein interaction analysis

In this study, protein-protein interaction information was predicted using the STRING database (version 11.5), with a combined score ≥ 0.9 set as the high-confidence screening threshold to ensure the reliability of the selected interactions. The resulting interaction data were extracted and imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) for network construction and visualization. Within the Cytoscape platform, network analysis and graphic rendering functions were employed to systematically arrange and refine the nodes and edges of the protein-protein interaction network. Through appropriate layout and style modifications, the topological position of TLCD1 within the global interaction network and its potential functional connections with other proteins were displayed, thereby providing support for understanding the molecular regulatory processes involving TLCD1.

Protein-protein docking analysis

Based on the potential interactors of TLCD1 identified from the previous protein-protein interaction analysis, molecular docking was performed to structurally investigate the binding modes and interaction mechanisms between TLCD1 and these proteins. The amino acid sequence of TLCD1 was retrieved from the UniProtKB database (https://www.uniprot.org/) and submitted to the SWISS-MODEL server for homology modeling. The three-dimensional structural model with the highest quality score was selected as the initial docking structure. Rigid protein-protein docking was then carried out using the GRAMM algorithm. After docking, ten predicted conformations were generated and ranked according to their docking scores. The conformation with the top rank was considered the best binding prediction.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t-test. To examine the influence of two categorical factors, we performed one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test to compare all five groups (WT, TLCD1-SH1, KDNC, TLCD1-OE, and OENC) and to identify significant differences between specific groups. All statistical analyses and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism (version 8.0), and final figures were assembled in Adobe Illustrator (version 2023). Each in vitro and molecular assay, including all functional experiments, was carried out at least three times on separate occasions, and every replicate produced outcomes consistent with the representative data shown Table 1 and 2.
