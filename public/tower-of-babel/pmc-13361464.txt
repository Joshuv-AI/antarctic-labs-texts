A multi-omics dissection of INHBA+ CAF-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition and immune suppression in colorectal cancer
Authors: Jie Wang, Qian He, Jingwen Hu, Lianchong Gao, Nengquan Sheng, Siyu Zhou, Jianfeng Gong, Yun Gu, Hongqi Chen, Yi Xu, Ting Yan, Zhigang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe progression of colorectal cancer (CRC) is profoundly influenced by the tumor microenvironment (TME). However, the spatial functional specialization of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in driving malignancy and immune evasion remains poorly defined. This study aims to delineate the spatial and functional heterogeneity of specific CAF subpopulations in CRC.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on CRC tissues and integrated public datasets to map the stromal landscape. Key cellular interactions inferred from scRNA-seq were validated using high-resolution Xenium in situ transcriptomics and further corroborated by public spatial transcriptomics (ST) data and immunohistochemistry (IHC).ResultsScRNA-seq identified two distinct, tumor-enriched CAF subpopulations: INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs, associated with an epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) signature, and INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs, which exhibited an immunoregulatory phenotype. Xenium spatial mapping localized these transcriptional identities to spatially segregated populations of INHBA+ myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs) and INHBA+ inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), respectively. Both subsets localized to ‘Invasive Tumor-Enriched Regions’ alongside EMT+ malignant epithelial cells. Spatial analysis further revealed that INHBA+ myCAFs frequently clustered adjacent to SPP1+ macrophages, whereas INHBA+ iCAFs resided within specialized immunosuppressive niches. Orthogonal validation using ST and IHC confirmed that INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs directly colocalized with the Malignant C2 (characterized by an EMT signature), while INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs were closely associated with CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs). These findings suggest that two spatially and functionally distinct INHBA+ CAF lineages coexist: one orchestrating EMT-driven invasion and the other fostering local immunosuppression via Treg recruitment.ConclusionOur study reveals a spatial division of labor among CAFs in CRC. The conserved expression of INHBA across both pro-tumorigenic lineages positions it as a central stromal regulator and a promising therapeutic target for simultaneously disrupting tumor invasion and immune evasion. These findings highlight the potential of stroma-targeted strategies in advancing CRC treatment.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08232-9.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1]. In 2022, global CRC incidence surpassed 1.9 million new cases with approximately 904,000 deaths, marking a steady increase in disease burden [2, 3]. For patients with advanced or metastatic CRC, advancements in chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy have offered significant benefits; however, treatment resistance and heterogeneous responses remain formidable challenges [4]. These clinical hurdles underscore the urgent need to decipher the complex biology of the tumor microenvironment (TME), which serves as a central orchestrator of tumor progression and therapeutic outcomes.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), the predominant stromal component of the TME, regulate tumor progression through diverse mechanisms. Pioneering studies have established CAF heterogeneity—the existence of distinct fibroblast subpopulations—as a key determinant of their multifaceted functions [5]. Subsequent pan-cancer single-cell analyses have confirmed the existence of conserved CAF subtypes across various malignancies, providing a systematic framework for understanding CAF heterogeneity [6]. CAFs promote malignancy by directly enhancing tumor cell proliferation through altered biochemical signaling [7], remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM), driving angiogenesis, and fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment [8–11]. Although the targeted depletion of specific CAF subsets has shown therapeutic promise [12], their profound heterogeneity remains a major obstacle that necessitates systematic investigation.

The importance of spatial context—where these cells are located relative to others—is increasingly recognized as a vital component of CAF biology. Even the initial characterization of iCAFs and myCAFs revealed distinct spatial patterns: myCAFs typically localize immediately adjacent to neoplastic cells, whereas iCAFs are positioned more distantly within the stroma [5]. Recent spatial multi-omics studies have significantly expanded this view. Liu et al. identified four conserved spatial CAF subtypes defined by unique neighboring cell compositions and interaction networks that shape the TME. Concurrently, Chen et al. demonstrated that spatial segregation can dictate specialized functions even within a single CAF lineage [13]. Despite these insights, most CRC-specific studies have focused on transcriptional profiles in isolation [14], leaving the spatial organization of CAF subtypes within the CRC microenvironment largely unexplored.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized our ability to deconstruct intricate tumor heterogeneity by identifying cellular subtypes with high precision. However, scRNA-seq requires tissue dissociation, which loses the original spatial architecture. To bridge this gap, spatial transcriptomics (ST) platforms map gene expression back onto native tissue. High-resolution in situ platforms, such as Xenium, allow for this profiling at single-cell and even subcellular resolution. The Xenium platform (10x Genomics) is an imaging-based ST technology that utilizes padlock probes (DNA fragments that bind specifically to target RNA) and rolling circle amplification (a process that creates many copies of that signal) to visualize targeted RNA molecules directly within tissue sections [15]. This allows for the precise mapping of cellular “neighborhoods.”

In this study, we applied an integrated multi-omics framework to dissect the spatially defined functions of CAFs in CRC. Through single-cell analysis, we identified two major tumor-enriched CAF subpopulations. High-resolution spatial mapping revealed that these subsets occupy distinct niches: one is positioned to drive the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)—a process in which tumor cells gain migratory properties—at the invasive leading edge. Meanwhile, the other colocalizes with CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) to establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Collectively, our work elucidates a coordinated “spatial division of labor” among CAFs in CRC, highlighting how stromal organization drives complementary pro-tumorigenic programs. Finally, with its conserved expression across these divergent subsets, inhibin βA (INHBA) functions both as a master stromal regulator and as a driver of T cell differentiation toward a CD4+ Treg phenotype, positioning it as a compelling therapeutic target for dual-mechanism intervention.

Baseline characteristics of the study patients

To enhance statistical power and the scope of our analysis, we integrated additional public scRNA-seq datasets (GSE178341, GSE132465, and GSE144735). Publicly available spatial transcriptomic (ST) data generated via the 10X Genomics platform were sourced from Wu et al. [16], and Xenium in situ transcriptomics data were obtained from Oliveira et al. [17]. Finally, bulk RNA-seq data and corresponding clinical information were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), encompassing both the colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) cohorts.

Processing, normalization, and integration of scRNA-seq data

We analyzed scRNA-seq data using Scanpy (v1.11.1), integrating public CRC datasets GSE132465, GSE144735, and GSE178341 with an in-house cohort (GSE294300). Low-quality cells were excluded based on the following criteria: expressing fewer than 200 or more than 6,000 genes, or possessing a mitochondrial gene proportion exceeding 20%. We selected 4,000 highly variable genes and performed batch integration—a process to remove technical noise across datasets—using scVI on raw counts. For downstream analysis, counts were normalized to 10,000 per cell and log-transformed.

Major cell types were annotated using canonical lineage markers. Differential expression was computed using the Scanpy framework, and pathway enrichment was assessed with GSEApy (v1.1.3). Transcription-factor programs were inferred with pySCENIC (v0.12.1). To understand the “future” state of cells, we estimated RNA velocity (a method that predicts cell differentiation based on the ratio of unspliced to spliced mRNA) using scVelo (v0.3.3) and cell2fate (v1.0.0). Trajectory inference was performed with Palantir (v1.4.1) to calculate pseudotime and branch probabilities.

Identification of tumor cell states (InferCnvpy)

To evaluate copy-number variations (CNVs)—which are large-scale genetic gains or losses typical of cancer cells—in epithelial cells, we applied inferCNVpy (v0.6.0; https://github.com/icbi-lab/infercnvpy) to the scRNA-seq data. Epithelial cells from normal samples served as external references for baseline genetic stability.

ST analysis

ST data were processed using Scanpy (v1.11.1) in Python (v3.11). Quality control retained genes detected in ≥ 3 cells and removed spots with <300 detected genes or < 600 Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs). To determine which cell types were present in each spatial spot, we performed cell-type deconvolution using cell2location (v0.1.4), which uses the scRNA-seq dataset as a reference to infer cell abundances in each tissue location.

Xenium in situ spatial transcriptomic analysis

We merged Xenium Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) CRC datasets (P1, P2, and P5) and performed standard quality control. Low-quality cells were removed using predefined filtering thresholds, and genes detected in fewer than 15 cells were discarded. Expression values were normalized to 10,000 counts per cell and log-transformed. Downstream transcriptomic analysis was performed using standard single-cell procedures, including highly variable gene selection (Seurat flavor), principal component analysis (20 PCs), Harmony batch correction using sample identity as the batch variable, construction of a transcriptomic k-nearest-neighbor graph (k = 15), and Leiden clustering (resolution = 0.6). Cell-type labels were subsequently harmonized into a unified subtype annotation field by directly overwriting labels according to precomputed cell indices. CAFs were further categorized into INHBA+ CXCL3+ inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), INHBA+CTHRC1+ myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), and other CAF populations based on marker expression patterns. Clustering robustness and annotation consistency were evaluated using silhouette score, adjusted Rand index (ARI), and marker interpretability. Differential expression analysis used false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Xenium spatial neighborhood and subtype–niche association analysis

Following transcriptional subtype annotation, spatial neighborhood analysis was performed using Xenium spatial coordinates. For each cell, a local neighborhood was defined as all surrounding cells within a fixed physical radius of 100 µm. Because Xenium cell coordinates are recorded in microns, this radius-based definition is directly interpretable in tissue space. Each local neighborhood was represented as a vector of transcriptional subtype composition within the corresponding spatial window. Neighborhood composition vectors were standardized within each sample using z-score transformation and clustered using pooled MiniBatchKMeans with sample-size-aware weighting to identify consensus cellular neighborhoods (CNs). To characterize subtype–niche associations, we compared the subtype composition of each CN with the overall subtype composition of the corresponding sample and calculated log2 fold-change enrichment scores, which were then aggregated across samples with equal weighting. For distance-based analysis, a cKDTree was built for each sample using two-dimensional spatial coordinates, and Euclidean distances from query cells to the nearest reference cells were computed. Distances were binned into 10-µm annuli from 0 to 100 µm to summarize gradient-dependent changes in local cellular composition. Finally, selected gene signatures were quantified on log-normalized expression data using scanpy.tl.score_genes with ctrl_size = 50.

Cell–cell interaction identification

Cell–cell communication was inferred using CellPhoneDB (v.5.0) to decode ligand–receptor signaling networks. Intercellular crosstalk was evaluated using a permutation test (n = 1,000), with significant interactions defined as p-values < 0.05.

Cell culture

The HEK293T, SW480, DLD-1, HCT116, and CT26.WT cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). SW480, DLD-1, HCT116, and PBMC were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA), while HEK293T cells, CT26.WT and CAFs were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, USA). For PBMC culture, recombinant human IL-2 (Sino Biological, China) was added at a final concentration of 30 U/mL. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment.

Isolation of primary CAFs from CRC tissues

Primary human CRC tissues were obtained from surgical specimens. Fresh tissues were washed three times with PBS containing 5% penicillin/streptomycin. The tissues were then incubated in DMEM containing 5% FBS, 10 μM Y-27632 (MCE, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin B, and HEPES (YEASEN, China) at 4 °C for 6 hours. After incubation, the tissues were minced into a paste using sterile ophthalmic scissors and transferred into a digestion solution of DMEM containing 5% FBS, 1–2 mg/mL collagenase I (Sigma, USA), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin B. Digestion was performed at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 8 hours with gentle agitation. The cell suspension was filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer (BioSharp, China) and washed 5 times with PBS. The cells were seeded into culture flasks and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 30–60 minutes to allow initial adhesion. Non-adherent cells were removed by discarding the supernatant, and this step was repeated once or twice. The adherent cells (enriched CAFs) were cultured in complete medium. Cells with good morphology and growth status were selected for passaging and subsequent experiments. CAFs were confirmed by Western Blot analysis.

Plasmid construction and validation

The pCDH-CMV-puro-INHBA overexpression plasmid was constructed by amplifying the INHBA coding sequence from HEK293T cDNA and cloning it into the pCDH-CMV-puro vector. For knockdown experiments, two short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting INHBA were designed, with the sense strands as follows: shINHBA#1: 5′-GGCAGAAATGAATGAACTTAT-3′, shINHBA#2: 5′-TCAACATCTGCTGTAAGAAAC-3′. Corresponding oligonucleotides containing these sequences, along with their reverse complements and a loop region, were synthesized, annealed, and cloned into the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The correct construction of all INHBA overexpression and knockdown vectors was confirmed by Sanger sequencing before use.

Transfection

For overexpression studies, HEK293T cells were seeded in 10-cm dishes (NEST, China) at a density of 1 × 106 cells per dish. After 24 hours, the cells were transfected with 10 µg of the pCDH-CMV-puro-INHBA plasmid or empty vector control using 20 µL of polyethyleneimine (PEI, Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For the production of knockdown virus, HEK293T cells were seeded at the same density and co-transfected with 5 µg of pLKO.1-shINHBA (or non-targeting control), along with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 (3.75 µg) and pMD2.G (1.25 µg), using PEI as described above. Six hours after transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. The viral supernatant was collected at 48–72 hours post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane, and either used for subsequent infection or stored at −80 °C.

Protein extraction and Western blot

Cellular and tissue samples were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Tissue samples were further homogenized using a tissue disruptor. Following centrifugation, the protein concentration in the supernatant was quantified using a BCA assay kit (Yeasen, China). Proteins were denatured by heating in 5× SDS-PAGE Protein Loading Buffer (Yeasen, China). Western blot analysis was performed using standard protocols: proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred by electroblotting, and detected via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL). Primary antibodies included anti-Inhibin β-A (Santa Cruz, sc-166503), anti-α-SMA (Proteintech, 14395-1-AP), and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 10494-1-AP). Species-matched secondary antibodies were subsequently applied [18].

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (TargetMol, USA). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and measured at different time points. For the CCK-8 assay, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Preparation of conditioned medium

Once CAFs reached 80% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM. After 48 hours of incubation, the CAF-conditioned supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 minutes to remove cellular debris. The clarified conditioned medium was then aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in six-well plates and cultured to 90% confluence. Uniform linear scratches were created in each well using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. Following the scratch procedure, each well was treated with 1 mL of medium containing 2% FBS and 1 mL of CAF-conditioned medium. Images of the wound areas were captured immediately (0 h) and after a specified incubation period.

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; Corning, USA). For the assay, 200 μL of cell suspension in serum-free medium was added to the upper chamber, while the lower chamber of the 24-well plate was filled with 400 μL of medium containing 20% FBS and 200 μL of conditioned medium. The plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 hours. After incubation, the cells on the membrane were washed with PBS, fixed with methanol, and stained with crystal violet. Excess dye was removed by rinsing with water. The membranes were dried, and images were captured from randomly selected fields for analysis.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using a commercial RNA extraction kit (Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was then synthesized from the extracted RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, China). qPCR was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China).

For human samples, the primer sequences used for amplifying INHBA were: forward primer, 5′-CCTCCCAAAGGATGTACCCAA-3′; reverse primer, 5′-CTCTATCTCCACATACCCGTTCT-3′. For the internal reference gene GAPDH, the primers were: forward, 5′-GGTGAAGGTCGGAGTCAACG-3′; reverse, 5′-CAAAGTTGTCATGGATGACC-3′.

For mouse samples, the primer sequences used for amplifying Foxp3 were: forward, 5′-CACCTATGCCACCCTTATCCG-3′; reverse, 5′-CATGCGAGTAAACCAATGGTAGA-3′. For Pdcd1, the primers were: forward, 5′-CAGCTTGTCCAACTGGTCG-3′; reverse, 5′-GCTCAAACCATTACAGAAGGCG-3′. For the internal reference gene Gapdh, the primers were: forward, 5′-AGGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTTG-3′; reverse, 5′-GGGGTCGTTGATGGCAACA-3′.

Immunofluorescence

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed using the Absin multicolor fluorescence kit (Abs50013) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The primary antibodies used were Inhibin β-A (Sino Biological, Cat# 111008-T02), α-SMA (Proteintech, 14395-1-AP), BNIP3 (Proteintech, 68091-1-Ig), SULF1 (Proteintech, 27438-1-AP), GLUT1 (ImmunoWay, YM6583), EPCAM (Proteintech, 21050-1-AP), CD4 (Proteintech, 67781-1-Ig), and Foxp3 (Proteintech, 22228-1-AP).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was then performed using EDTA buffer, followed by the quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity with hydrogen peroxide. To prevent non-specific binding, sections were incubated with 3% BSA. They were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies against Inhibin β-A (Sino Biological, Cat# 111008-T02), N-cadherin (Zenbio, Cat# R380671), and E-cadherin (Zenbio, Cat# 340341), followed by incubation with species-matched secondary antibodies. After each antibody incubation, tyramide signal amplification was performed, followed by heat-induced antigen retrieval and antibody stripping. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and sections were mounted in an antifade medium. IHC staining was semi-quantitatively evaluated by two independent pathologists using a combined scoring system (range 0–12). The final score for each sample was derived by multiplying the staining intensity score (0 = none, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong) by the percentage score of positive cells (0 = <5%, 1 = 5–25%, 2 = 26–50%, 3 = 51–75%, 4 = >75%). Discrepancies between the two pathologists were resolved through consensus.

Animal xenograft model

Male BALB/c nude mice (5–6 weeks old) were purchased from SPF Biotechnology (China) for xenograft establishment. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the protocols approved by the relevant Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. To establish the co-injection model, a mixture of SW480 cells (1 × 106) and human-derived CAFs (5 × 106) was suspended in PBS and injected subcutaneously into the right flanks of the mice.

When tumor volumes reached 50–100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to treatment and control groups (n = 5 per group). The treatment group received intraperitoneal injections of garetosmab (Sanyou Bio, China) at a dose of 100 µg per mouse (formulated as 1 mg/mL in 0.9% NaCl). Control mice received an equal volume (100 µL) of the vehicle (0.9% NaCl). Injections were administered every other day for a total of eight doses [19].

Tumor dimensions (length and width) and body weight were measured every other day. Tumor volume was calculated using the standard formula: Volume = (length × width2) / 2. Upon completion of the treatment regimen, mice were euthanized via CO₂ asphyxiation. Tumors were harvested, and a portion of each tumor was snap-frozen for cryosectioning. These cryosections were subsequently subjected to IHC analysis of the targeted markers.

For the CT26 model, male BALB/c mice (5–6 weeks old) were purchased from the same vendor. CT26 cells (5 × 105) were suspended in PBS and injected subcutaneously into the right flank of the mice. Tumor establishment, grouping, and monitoring were performed as described above for the SW480 model, with the treatment regimen detailed in Fig. 6l .

CAF-T cell co-culture assays

CAFs (shCtrl or shINHBA#2) were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Simultaneously, PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors and activated using anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies for 24 hours. The activated PBMCs were then added to the CAF cultures at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well (approximate CAF: PBMC ratio of 1:10). These co-cultures were maintained for 5 days in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 30 U/mL IL-2. Cells were then harvested, and CD4+ T cells were analyzed by flow cytometry for Treg frequency using antibodies against CD4, CD25, and FOXP3.

Flow cytometry analysis

To evaluate immune cell populations, flow cytometry was performed. Cell suspensions were incubated with anti-human CD4 (FITC, cat. no. 300505) and anti-human CD25 (PE, cat. no. 302605) antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C for surface marker detection. Following surface staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using a Fixation/Permeabilization buffer set (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 4 °C. Intracellular staining was then carried out by incubating the permeabilized cells with an Alexa FluorⓇ 647-conjugated anti-mouse/rat/human FOXP3 antibody (cat. no. 320214) for 30 min at room temperature. All antibodies were used at dilutions recommended by the manufacturer (BioLegend). Stained samples were acquired on an Cytek Aurora flow cytometer, and data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (v3.12). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between the two groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Survival data were analyzed using the log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons. All experiments were conducted in triplicate or more, where applicable.

Following quality control and batch-effect correction using scVI, 465,743 high-quality single cells were retained for downstream analysis. Specifically, tumor tissues exhibited a marked expansion of epithelial and myeloid populations, whereas B cells and plasma cells were significantly enriched in normal mucosa. This observed disparity highlights a profound remodeling of the TME.

We first distinguished structural stromal cells, including smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and pericytes (PCs), which are constitutively associated with the vasculature, from the remaining CAF populations [20]. We then compared subtype abundances between tumors and matched adjacent normal tissues to infer CAF remodeling during tumorigenesis. Tumors exhibited significant enrichment of INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs, INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs, PCs, and SMCs. Conversely, S100A4+ CAFs and CXCL13+ CAFs were depleted in tumor tissues, while PDGFD+, DPT+, and SPON1+ CAFs showed no significant difference (Fig. 2c). This analysis confirmed a profound segregation in tissue distribution: the fibroblast lineage is polarized into distinct normal-resident and tumor-specific compartments (Fig. 2d). Notably, while SMCs and PCs were present in both physiological and neoplastic contexts [21], the INHBA+ BNIP3+ and INHBA+ SULF1+ CAF subtypes exhibited near-absolute tumor specificity. Consequently, we focused our subsequent mechanistic investigations on these two INHBA-high, tumor-restricted subtypes (Fig. 2b).

Characterized by a core gene set linked to EMT and ECM remodeling, INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs were more strongly associated with EMT-related pathways (Fig. 2e, f). This phenotype was further driven by upregulated TGF-β signaling, which contributed to an immunosuppressive TME (Fig. S2d) [22, 23]. Conversely, INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs displayed a chemokine- and protease-rich profile associated with inflammatory responses and immune modulation, supported by enrichment in pathways such as hypoxia, MAPK, NF-κB, and TNFα signaling. Although to a lesser extent than INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs, the INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAF subset also showed enrichment in EMT-related pathways (Fig. 2b, e, f; Fig. S2b, d).

To assess the prognostic relevance of CAF subtypes, we used CIBERSORTx digital cytometry to estimate subtype abundances from bulk RNA-seq profiles in the TCGA-COAD and TCGA-READ cohorts [24]. Enrichment of INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (OS) (HR = 1.68, log-rank p = 0.01) and was elevated in patients with perineural or lymphatic invasion (Fig. 2h). While INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs did not exhibit a significant association with OS (Fig. 2g), the infiltration of both INHBA+ subtypes was more prevalent in patients with MSI-H (Fig. S2c).

To elucidate transcriptional regulatory networks governing fibroblast identity, we performed single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) analysis on scRNA-seq data. We identified the top 15 transcription factors (TFs) with prominent activity in each fibroblast subtype (Fig. 2i). Among these TFs, ZNF454 and ELK3 demonstrated high expression and regulatory activity in INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs, as quantified by the Regulon Specificity Score (RSS). Notably, ELK3 has been established as a pivotal regulator of EMT [25]. Similarly, RAX and FOXO1 emerged as potential master regulators in INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs. Consistent with their immune-modulatory phenotype, prior investigations have demonstrated that FOXO1 potently upregulates CCL20, promoting the recruitment of CD4+ Treg cells into the TME [26].

Using RNA velocity and Palantir cell-fate modeling, we defined CAF developmental trajectories and resolved their dynamic state transitions along pseudotime in UMAP. This revealed a potential hierarchy where INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs and INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs may derive from PDGFD+ CAFs (Fig. 2j; Fig. S2e).

Discovery of an EMT-related malignant subpopulation

Having delineated pro-tumorigenic CAF subsets, we next sought to identify cooperating programs within the malignant epithelial compartment. To define the transcriptional programs underlying malignant behavior, we interrogated epithelial functional heterogeneity using high-resolution scRNA-seq. Following data integration, we isolated epithelial cells by selecting for EPCAM+ /KRT8+ cells. To distinguish malignant cells from their non-malignant counterparts, we performed copy-number alteration analysis using inferCNVpy. By using normal epithelial and non-epithelial cells as a genomic baseline, we identified five non-malignant epithelial lineages: goblet cells, tuft cells, enteroendocrine cells, absorptive enterocytes, and one indeterminate cluster. Analysis of normal epithelial cells revealed near-diploid genomes with minimal CNV burden (Fig. S3a-c).

We partitioned the malignant epithelial compartment into four distinct transcriptional subtypes, designated Malignant C1–C4 (Fig. 3a, b; Fig. S3c). Notably, the Malignant C2 subtype was characterized by a pronounced EMT/invasion signature (Fig. 3c, d; Fig. S3d). The aggressive nature of this subtype was further evidenced by the high expression of SLC2A1 and LAMC2—genes previously established as drivers of invasiveness and metastasis in CRC [27, 28] (Fig. 3b). In summary, we defined Malignant C2, an EMT-high epithelial subtype associated with a poor prognosis. Given the parallel identification of an EMT-promoting CAF subset (INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs), we hypothesized that these two programs might spatially co-localize and functionally cooperate to drive tumor invasion.

Xenium spatially resolves EMT-linked and immunosuppressive CAF subtypes

To validate the spatial architecture of these cell states within intact tissue, we reanalyzed a single-cell resolution Xenium in situ transcriptomic dataset [17]. We annotated the TME into malignant epithelial subtypes, CAF subtypes, and major immune or stromal lineages: myeloid cells (macrophages, monocytes, and mast cells), lymphocytes (T, B, and plasma cells), endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells (Fig. S4a-c). We then encoded each center cell by the local composition of neighboring cell types. We performed unsupervised clustering on these neighborhood profiles, thereby defining nine distinct cellular neighborhoods (CNs) (Fig. 4b, c). Notably, CN3 emerged as a critical “invasive niche”. This neighborhood was dominated by EMT/invasion-high malignant cells, which recapitulated the transcriptomic features of the scRNA-seq Malignant C2 state and formed dense aggregates at the tumor–stroma interface (Fig. 4b, c).

Notably, CN3 displayed a high enrichment of both INHBA+ CTHRC1+ myCAFs and INHBA+ CXCL3+ iCAFs, which transcriptionally correspond to the INHBA+ SULF1+ CAF and INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAF subsets, respectively. Also, it showed a prominent myeloid component (Fig. 4c). This spatial convergence suggests that CN3 represents a specialized multicellular community where aggressive tumor cells, specific CAF subsets, and myeloid cells actively coordinate to drive invasion. To quantify this, we stratified the stroma into “Invasive Tumor-Enriched Regions” (high CN3 frequency) versus “Non-Invasive Tumor Niches”. Both INHBA+ CTHRC1+ myCAFs and INHBA+ CXCL3+ iCAFs were markedly enriched and tightly co-localized within the invasive regions. In contrast, they were largely absent in non-invasive niches (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, high-intensity INHBA expression spatially overlaid with both CAF subsets within these invasive regions of interest (ROIs), confirming a localized, high-intensity signaling axis (Fig. 4d).

We dissected the myeloid heterogeneity within this invasive niche. Spatial proximity analysis revealed distinct interactive partners for each CAF subset. SPP1+ macrophages were highly enriched in the immediate vicinity of INHBA+ CTHRC1+ myCAFs, with their density progressively decaying as distance increased, suggesting a coupled axis facilitating matrix remodeling (Fig. 4e). In contrast, MRC1+ macrophages—established mediators of immunosuppression [16]—accumulated preferentially around INHBA+ CXCL3+ iCAFs (Fig. 4e).

Functionally, the iCAF–macrophage niche correlated with a potent immunosuppressive environment. Specifically, “scavenger” and “complement” immunosuppressive scores [29] peaked in the immediate proximity of INHBA+ CXCL3+ iCAFs and declined with distance (Fig. 4f). Although overall T cell density was low, high-resolution mapping revealed that INHBA+ CXCL3+ iCAFs were surrounded by T cells exhibiting concurrent high cytotoxic and exhaustion scores (Fig. 4g). This spatial arrangement implies that INHBA+ CXCL3+ iCAFs organize a local immunosuppressive barrier that effectively counterbalances and exhausts infiltrating effector T cells.

Despite the similarities in gene expression profiles, we provided direct histological evidence from patient tissues for the existence of both INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs and INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs (Fig. 5b). Subsequent spatial analyses further confirmed the co-localization of INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs with Malignant C2, particularly at the invasive tumor front (Fig. 5c, d; Fig. S5c). Building upon the immunomodulatory role of INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs suggested by scRNA-seq, and their spatial enrichment within an immunosuppressive microenvironment as revealed by Xenium data, we investigated their potential cellular interaction partners. Spatial co‑localization analysis demonstrated frequent juxtaposition of CD4+ Tregs with INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs (Fig. 5e, f; Fig. S5d). The abundances of these cell types were positively correlated (Fig. 5h), suggesting that INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs may actively recruit or regulate CD4+ Tregs to maintain an immunosuppressive state. Moreover, INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs partially localized around Malignant C2, suggesting potential functional cooperation (Fig. 5g; Fig. S5e). ScRNA-seq analysis also revealed a network of positive correlations across patients, interconnecting the abundances of INHBA+ SULF1 + CAFs, INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs, and Malignant C2 (Fig. 5h). This CAF–epithelial niche at the invasive margin was associated with the formation of a dense, fibrotic ECM, which might impede immune cell infiltration.

To investigate cellular crosstalk in the TME, we employed intercellular communication analysis. We identified intense autocrine and reciprocal signaling between INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs and INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs, reinforcing the likelihood of a close developmental relationship. Moreover, INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs exhibited particularly rich interactions with Malignant C2 (Fig. S5g). Screening of CAF-tumor cell interactions revealed a predominance of ITGA family members. Further mechanistic investigation pinpointed the COL1A2-integrin α2β1 complex as a direct mediator (Fig. S5h), suggesting that these CAFs promote progression through specialized ECM modulation [30].

Within the co-localized INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAF and CD4+ Treg populations, we observed a high density of intercellular interactions (Fig. S5i). Most notably, the PVR-TIGIT ligand-receptor pair emerged as a dominant signaling axis (Fig. S5j). PVR, a key regulator of immune responses, is typically absent in healthy tissues but highly overexpressed in CRC, where it directly promotes tumor immune evasion by binding to the inhibitory receptor TIGIT on T cells [31].

Collectively, our results indicate that INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs and Malignant C2 might contribute to ECM remodeling, whereas the interactions between INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs and CD4+ Tregs likely foster an immunosuppressive niche in CRC.

CAF-derived inhibin βA drives migration and immunosuppression in CRC

Although INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs and INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs possess distinct functional roles, both are characterized by high INHBA expression and are linked to EMT (Fig. 2b, e). Clinically, elevated INHBA correlates with unfavorable features, including advanced tumor stage, perineural invasion, and poor prognosis (Fig. S6a–d).

To investigate the functional impact of INHBA, we isolated primary CAFs from tumor tissues and established stable INHBA knockdown cell lines using lentiviral transduction (Fig. S6e–h). Conditioned medium from INHBA-deficient CAFs, while showing no effect on tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 6a), significantly impaired their invasive capacity in vitro compared to that from INHBA-sufficient CAFs (Fig. 6b, c). Consistent with these findings, western blot and IHC analyses of clinical specimens detected higher levels of inhibin βA (the INHBA gene product) in tumor tissues than in matched adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 6d, e). To evaluate the immunosuppressive potential of CAF-derived inhibin βA suggested by our spatial analyses, we performed co-culture experiments using CRC-derived CAFs and activated PBMCs. After five days of co-culture, we observed that INHBA-deficient CAFs (shINHBA#2) induced a significantly lower frequency of CD4+ FOXP3+ Tregs compared to control CAFs (shCtrl vs. shINHBA#2: 15.33% vs. 6.33%, p < 0.001, Fig. 6h; Fig S6j). These results demonstrate that INHBA expression in CAFs directly facilitates Treg differentiation, complementing its pro-invasive role.

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis in a physiological setting, we generated a xenograft model by co-implanting CAFs with tumor cells (Fig. 6i). Notably, treatment with garetosmab—a monoclonal antibody that specifically neutralizes activin A (a homodimer of inhibin βA subunits)—substantially suppressed tumor progression in vivo (Fig. 6j; Fig. S6k, l). Mechanistical analysis of the xenograft tumors revealed that garetosmab treatment effectively inhibited the EMT process (Fig. 6k), indicating that its anti-tumor effect is mediated, at least in part, through blockade of INHBA/activin A-driven EMT. These results revealed the critical role of CAF-secreted inhibin βA in driving both EMT and an immunosuppressive microenvironment, positioning it as a compelling “dual-mechanism” therapeutic target in CRC.

To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy in an immunocompetent setting, we established a CT26.WT syngeneic mouse model (Fig. 6l), which is widely recognized as an immunologically “cold” tumor model characterized by an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Consistent with the findings in the xenograft model, garetosmab treatment significantly reduced tumor volume in immunocompetent mice (Fig. 6m; Fig. S6m, n). Additionally, analysis of collected tissue samples revealed decreased expression of Pdcd1 and Foxp3 in the treatment group, further supporting the therapeutic potential of targeting INHBA/activin A (Fig. S6o, p).

Discussion

Despite considerable efforts, a clear and universally accepted classification system for CAFs remains elusive [32]. Previous integrated scRNA-seq analyses across cancer types have proposed five major fibroblast subtypes: myofibroblastic (myFibro), ECM-remodeling (eFibro), immunoregulatory (iFibro), antigen-presenting (apFibro), and quiescent (qFibro) [33]. However, scRNA-seq studies of CRC have been limited by small cohort sizes, leaving CAF–tumor cell interactions and potential therapeutic targets insufficiently characterized. Our large-scale, multimodal analysis overcomes these gaps by combining scRNA-seq and ST to delineate nine distinct fibroblast subsets. Crucially, we identify two tumor-enriched, INHBA-high CAF populations that drive complementary pathways of malignancy. Critically, our spatial analysis addresses a fundamental limitation of traditional scRNA-seq by preserving and interrogating the tissue architecture, thereby defining the spatially segregated functional specialization of these two CAF subsets within the intact tumor microenvironment.

Functionally, INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs align with the eFibro and myFibro subtypes, while INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs exhibit features of the iFibro and myFibro subtypes. RNA-velocity analysis supports a bifurcated differentiation model: PDGFD+ CAFs serve as a common progenitor for both INHBA+ BNIP3+ and INHBA+ SULF1+ lineages. Localized microenvironmental cues, such as hypoxia, TGF-β signaling, or elevated matrix stiffness, are likely to drive the terminal fate choice during tumor progression.

Spatially, INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs are predominantly located at the invasive tumor front, aligning with a previously described MMP11-high subset involved in active ECM remodeling [34]. We demonstrate that Malignant C2 in this region are enriched for stromal transformation pathways and exhibit significant co-localization with INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs. This synergistic interaction appears to drive the assembly of a dense, perimeter-like “stromal ring”, which impedes T-cell infiltration and contributes to an immune-excluded phenotype. This structural barrier is further reinforced by an immunosuppressive cellular circuit involving a close spatial relationship between SPP1+ macrophages and myCAFs [35], an axis frequently associated with treatment resistance. Our findings are consistent with recent studies identifying SULF1-expressing CAFs as key mediators of ECM remodeling that accelerate liver metastasis in CRC [36]. Collectively, these data suggest that INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs coordinate a multi-layered defense—constructing a physical and immune barrier that may confer resistance to neoadjuvant therapy [37].

Similarly, our identification of INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs matches the myCAF signature that recruits Tregs and forecasts poor prognosis across cancers. For instance, in pancreatic cancer, BNIP3+ fibroblasts occupy hypoxic–inflammatory niches and predict immunotherapy failure [38]. The spatial characteristics of our INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs align with the previously defined S1-CAF subset in CRC, which resides in direct proximity to tumor cells. This spatial proximity correlates with upregulated CD4+ Tregs and reduced cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, collaboratively shaping an immunosuppressive TME [39].

A key functional attribute of INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs is the high expression of collagen-associated ECM genes, including COL15A1 and COL27A1 (Fig. S2b), implicating them in forming a dense, stiffened matrix. Since matrix stiffness is known to promote CD4+ Treg differentiation and suppressive function via mechanosensing and TGF-β activation [40], the collagen deposition by these CAFs likely constitutes a direct physical mechanism for fostering an immunosuppressive niche. Spatially, we further found INHBA+ iCAFs tightly intertwined with MRC1+ macrophages, suggesting that macrophages act as cooperative partners in CAF-driven immune evasion. Deconvolution of bulk RNA-seq profiles from the TCGA-COAD/READ cohort reveals that INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs are virtually undetectable in most tumors, precluding robust survival stratification. Their prognostic significance must therefore be re-evaluated in large-scale single-cell or spatially resolved cohorts.

The immunosuppressive role of INHBA+ CAFs was further supported by our functional co-culture experiments, which demonstrated that INHBA knockdown in CAFs significantly reduced Treg differentiation. This provides direct mechanistic evidence that INHBA+ CAFs actively drive CD4+Treg accumulation in the CRC microenvironment. Our findings align with recent reports on ovarian cancer [41], suggesting that INHBA+ CAFs represent a conserved immunomodulatory subset with broad therapeutic relevance across different malignancies. Future studies are warranted to dissect the molecular mechanisms by which CAF-derived inhibin βA signals to T cells and to evaluate whether targeting this axis can sensitize CRC to current immunotherapies.

Collectively, our spatial and functional analyses converge on INHBA as a central node at the invasive margin. We defined a specialized “Invasive Tumor-Enriched Region” composed of EMT-primed malignant cells and specialized CAFs. A crucial finding is that these CAFs, particularly the INHBA+ subsets, serve as the predominant stromal source of inhibin βA in this niche. Although INHBA‘s pro-invasive role via autocrine signaling in tumor cells is well-documented [42, 43], its paracrine function—specifically from CAFs—has remained poorly understood [41]. Our functional data demonstrate that CAF-derived inhibin βA is critical for promoting cancer cell invasion, defining a key tumor-stroma crosstalk axis.

Recent research indicates that inhibin βA can also drive M2-like macrophage polarization in CRC [44], which, in turn, can enhance pro-fibrotic CAF responses [45]. The pleiotropic expression of INHBA across both invasive and immunosuppressive CAF subsets suggests that it acts as a master regulator of a coordinated pro-fibrotic program that concurrently stiffens the matrix to promote invasion and modulates immune cells. Unraveling whether INHBA engages subset-specific downstream effectors warrants future investigation.

Given the central role of the CAF-derived inhibin βA axis, direct inhibition of this axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy. Clinical-stage agents such as the monoclonal antibody garetosmab, alongside endogenous antagonists like follistatin, offer the potential to disrupt this critical tumor-stroma dialogue [44, 46]. Future studies should prioritize testing these agents, particularly as part of combination therapies designed to dismantle the stromal-driven resistance mechanisms characterized in this study.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study that warrant further investigation. First, our classification of malignant epithelial cells using inferCNV, while widely adopted, has inherent limitations common to transcriptome-based CNV inference [47]. To ensure robust distinction between malignant and non-malignant cells and address potential sensitivity to sequencing depth, we employed the Cross-Platform Omics Pipeline [48]. This approach incorporates large-scale chromosomal pattern analysis and marker cross-validation, providing a reliable foundation for our epithelial subtype classifications. We acknowledge that while our computational framework is rigorous, orthogonal DNA-based validation (e.g., FISH or whole-genome sequencing) in future studies would further strengthen the genomic characterization of the malignant subtypes identified here. Second, while GLUT1 was validated as a protein-level marker for Malignant C2 cells at the invasive front, aligning with its role as a leader cell biomarker in collective invasion [49], functional assays directly demonstrating the invasive response of isolated Malignant C2 cells to CAF-derived signals are still needed. Future studies employing sorted Malignant C2 cells in co-culture or CAF-conditioned medium invasion assays will be essential to address this question.

In summary, our study delineates two novel INHBA-high CAF subpopulations—INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs and INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs—which orchestrate complementary pro-tumor pathways in CRC. We have established a spatially resolved framework for CAF heterogeneity, nominating INHBA as a central stromal mediator with dual roles in promoting tumor invasion and driving immunosuppression. Translating these findings will require several key investigations: validating the spatial cellular neighborhoods in larger, multi-center cohorts; employing immunocompetent models to dissect the causal relationship between INHBA+ CAFs and CD4+ Treg-mediated immunosuppression; and testing INHBA-targeted therapies, such as garetosmab, in combination with standard regimens to overcome stromal-mediated treatment resistance by simultaneously targeting both the invasive and immunosuppressive axes. Pursuing these directions will be essential to transform this spatially resolved framework into effective therapeutic strategies targeting the CAF-driven TME in CRC.

Conclusions

Using an integrated multi-omics approach, we identified two transcriptionally and spatially distinct, tumor-enriched CAF subsets in CRC. INHBA+ SULF1+ CAFs are predominantly localized at the invasive front and are associated with EMT, while INHBA+ BNIP3+ CAFs are spatially intertwined with CD4+ Tregs. The convergent, high-level expression of INHBA across these subsets positions it as a universal stromal mediator of malignancy. Our functional studies demonstrate that CAF-derived inhibin βA directly promotes tumor cell invasion and CD4+ Treg accumulation. Furthermore, pharmacological blockade of this axis using garetosmab successfully suppressed tumor growth and reversed the EMT phenotype in vivo. Collectively, these findings identify the INHBA/activin A axis as a central orchestrator of the pro-tumorigenic microenvironment and a promising therapeutic target to overcome stromal-mediated resistance in CRC.
