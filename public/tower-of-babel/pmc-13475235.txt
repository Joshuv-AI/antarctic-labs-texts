SELENOF functions as a tumor suppressor in skin cancer
Authors: Brenna Flowers, An Zhang, Weiwei Ma, Alexandra Zigrossi, Madeline Warrick, Jacob K Phillips, Mitchell F Denning, Maarten C Bosland, Li Liu, Alan M Diamond, Irida Kastrati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelenoprotein F (SELENOF) is one of the 25 human selenoproteins that is highly sensitive to fluctuations in bioavailable selenium, an essential micronutrient. Although SELENOF has been implicated in cancer etiology, its precise role remains unclear. To define its contribution to carcinogenesis, Selenof knockout (KO) mice were exposed to the potent carcinogen 7,12-dimethylbenz(a)anthracene (DMBA) and fed a high-fat, high-calorie Western diet. All Selenof KO mice developed tumors and did so more rapidly, whereas only 30% of wild-type mice developed tumors. This effect was further exacerbated by the Western diet regardless of Selenof status. Notably, the majority of DMBA-induced tumors in KO mice were skin lesions, predominantly cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs) and benign papillomas. These tumors occurred at a four-fold higher frequency in KO mice than in wild-type controls, establishing SELENOF as a key tumor suppressor in the skin.To assess the relevance of these findings to human disease, we analyzed a skin cancer microarray containing patient samples of SCC, basal cell carcinoma, melanomas, and matched adjacent normal tissue. SELENOF expression was dramatically reduced across all tumor types relative to normal skin. Although the skin of Selenof KO mice appears histologically normal, transcriptomic profiling followed by enrichment analysis revealed significant downregulation of pathways related to epidermal development and keratinocyte differentiation as well as the upregulation of oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial metabolic pathways. Collectively, these findings support a causal link between SELENOF loss and skin cancer development and highlight a previously unrecognized role for SELENOF in safeguarding against skin carcinogenesis. Significance: Skin cancers are the most common human malignancies. Introduction

Selenoprotein F (SELENOF; formerly SEP15) is one of the 25 human selenoproteins that incorporate selenium as selenocysteine through a recoding of an in-frame UGA codon [1,2]. This specialized translational mechanism confers enhanced redox capacity; however, while several selenoproteins function as antioxidant enzymes, not all share this role [3]. Unlike some selenoproteins whose expression remains stable across a range of selenium intake, SELENOF expression is highly sensitive to dietary selenium availability [4]. Selenium status strongly correlates with survival among breast cancer patients [5], suggesting that SELENOF may contribute to selenium's anti-cancer effects. Experimental studies demonstrate that restoring SELENOF levels suppresses breast cancer cell growth and promotes cell death, whereas SELENOF loss enhances proliferation and confers resistance to apoptosis and autophagy in non-tumorigenic breast epithelial cells [6,7]. Beyond breast cancer, emerging evidence indicates that SELENOF loss contributes to prostate cancer development, whereas in colon cancer, SELENOF deficiency appears to be protective (reviewed in Ref. [8]). Collectively, these observations underscore the need to investigate how SELENOF deficiency affects tumorigenesis, both broadly and specifically in breast cancer, which was the initial objective of the current study.

Recent reports indicate that SELENOF may also play a role in lipogenesis and glucose metabolism, although the underlying mechanisms remain poorly defined [[9], [10], [11]]. Loss of Selenof in male C57BL/6 mice results in increased weight gain, elevated blood glucose levels, and impaired glucose and insulin tolerance when Selenof knockout (KO) mice are fed a high-fat diet compared with wild-type controls [10]. In addition, Selenof KO mice exhibit altered expression of hepatic proteins involved in fatty acid synthesis, accompanied by significant changes in hepatic ATP and NADPH levels [10]. Collectively, these findings indicate a likely role for SELENOF in regulating glucose metabolism and lipogenesis (reviewed in Ref. [8]). SELENOF-associated metabolic dysfunction and obesity are of particular relevance to breast cancer, as obesity is an independent risk factor for breast cancer in post-menopausal women [[12], [13], [14]]. Obesity is thought to increase estrogen exposure in part through the storage of estrogens and their precursors in adipose tissue, as well as enhanced aromatization of androstenedione and testosterone to estrogens, leading to elevated circulating estrogen levels in some obese post-menopausal women [12,14]. Additional mechanisms likely contributing to obesity-associated breast cancer risk include dysregulated insulin/IGF signaling, chronic inflammation, altered cytokine profiles, and possibly changes in the gut microbiota [14].

To determine how loss of SELENOF influences breast tumorigenesis, the well-established 7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA)-induced carcinogenesis model was employed. DMBA is a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon and a potent environmental carcinogen that has historically been used in Sprague-Dawley rats and mice to study mammary tumorigenesis [15,16]. DMBA is metabolized by the cytochrome P450 enzymes CYP1A1 and CYP1B1 to produce reactive intermediates that form DNA adducts [17] which are repaired through nucleotide excision [18]. This repair process can be error-prone, leading to the accumulation of mutations [17,18]. DMBA-induced mammary tumorigenesis is most effective when the compound is administered prior to 10 weeks of age, coinciding with the proliferative phase of terminal end buds during mammary gland development. This developmental window allows maximal incorporation of DNA damage into the mammary epithelium during periods of heightened cell proliferation [16]. A key advantage of the DMBA model is that it recapitulates the multistep nature of mammary tumor development while enabling interrogation of the gene or protein of interest in an immune-intact mouse model [15,16]. In addition to DMBA exposure, mice were randomized to receive either a standard chow diet or a high-fat, high-calorie diet (hereafter referred to as a Western diet) to assess the impact of diet and Selenof status on mammary tumorigenesis.

We report here that loss of Selenof increases susceptibility to carcinogen-induced tumor formation, supporting a tumor-suppressive role for Selenof. Although mammary tumorigenesis following carcinogen exposure appeared to be relatively low at 25 weeks, squamous epithelial skin lesions predominated, indicating that Selenof plays a previously underappreciated role in protection against skin cancer. The high incidence of non-melanoma skin tumors in mice prompted analysis of human tumor samples, which revealed significantly reduced SELENOF levels in skin tumors compared with adjacent normal tissue. These findings highlight a critical gap in understanding how SELENOF loss influences tissue-specific cancer susceptibility, particularly in the skin, and potential implications are discussed.

Results

Selenof deficiency and Western diet exacerbate carcinogen-induced tumor formation in mice. To investigate how loss of SELENOF impacts tumorigenesis, Selenof knockout (KO) and wild-type (WT) mice were subjected to the well-established DMBA-induced carcinogenesis model, which closely recapitulates the multistep nature of tumor development [16]. The study was designed primarily to assess mammary tumorigenesis, however, DMBA is also one of the most commonly used topical carcinogens for inducing skin cancer in mice [19]. Five-week-old female WT and KO mice were exposed to six weekly doses of DMBA (1 mg per dose) by oral gavage and were expected to develop mammary tumors within 30–40 weeks following the final exposure [16]. Mice were randomized to receive either a standard chow diet (normal diet, ND) or a Western diet (WD), characterized by high fat, sucrose, and cholesterol content as well as low fiber that promotes obesity [20,21] and increases the risk of certain cancers (Fig. 1A) [22]. The study was terminated early at 25 weeks after the final DMBA exposure because the majority of mice had succumbed to skin tumor burden and met humane endpoint criteria (Table 1A). Loss of Selenof dramatically increased tumor incidence from 30% in WT mice (Fig. 1B, black) to 100% in KO mice (Fig. 1B–red) and this was further accelerated by the Western diet (Fig. 1B, blue vs yellow) by shortening the tumor latency.

Most of the skin lesions examined histopathologically were classified as squamous cell carcinomas (SCC), a few as sebaceous squamous cell carcinomas or benign squamous cell papillomas (Table 1B). The overall number of SCC in each group is shown in Fig. 2A and representative histology of these tumors is shown in Fig. 2B. Loss of SELENOF massively increased the incidence and number of SCC in mice on the normal diet, but only insignificantly in mice on the WD (Table 1B). Loss of SELENOF also increased the number of papillomas, particularly in mice fed the WD (Table 1B). A small number of animals developed generalized lymphomas, a known consequence of DMBA exposure; however, their incidence was not influenced by Selenof status or diet. In addition, one ovarian granulosa cell tumor was identified (Table 1A). These neoplasms and the two mammary carcinomas did not significantly affect the total number of tumor-bearing mice in the four groups (Table 1A). In summary, loss of Selenof significantly increased DMBA-induced skin tumorigenesis and enhanced skin tumor development by up to four-fold and SSC incidence from zero to 100% in mice fed the ND.

Exposure to WD increased body weights over time in both WT and Selenof KO mice. However, Selenof KO mice were more resistant to this diet-induced weight gain despite having similar baseline weights as the WT mice at the start of the study (Fig. 1C). Notably, although they gained less weight, Selenof KO mice exhibited higher blood glucose levels as the study progressed (Fig. 1D), indicating broader metabolic dysfunction in the absence of Selenof.

Loss of Selenof downregulates gene signatures associated with epidermal development and differentiation while enhancing mitochondrial metabolic programs in normal murine skin. Given the pronounced susceptibility to skin cancer observed with the loss of Selenof, we investigated the global transcriptomic consequences of Selenof loss in mouse skin. RNA sequencing followed by bioinformatic analysis was performed on histologically normal skin samples collected from both male and female WT and Selenof KO mice fed regular ND. We validated Selenof expression by RT-QPCR and confirmed the absence of amplification in skin samples from Selenof KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 3). We identified 1618 transcripts that were significantly differentially expressed between WT and KO samples (Fig. 3A). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed Epidermis Development, Skin Development, and Keratinocyte Differentiation as the most significantly downregulated pathways in Selenof KO skin (Fig. 3B, top panel). These findings suggest that loss of SELENOF disrupts normal keratinocyte differentiation, a hallmark phenotype commonly associated with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) [23]. We validated downregulation of key genes involved in epidermal development and differentiation, including Ptk6, Dlx3, Krt10, and Notch2, by RT-QPCR. Expression of these genes was significantly reduced; however, some exhibited sex-dependent effects (Fig. 3C). Notably, Krt10 and Notch2 exhibited significantly lower expression in female but not male mice. GSEA also identified Oxidative Phosphorylation, Mitochondrial Electron Transport Chain, and Metabolic Process as the most significantly upregulated gene signatures in Selenof KO skin (Fig. 3B, bottom panel). Consistent with this, multiple members of the Nduf gene family were significantly upregulated (Fig. 3C). These genes encode structural and accessory subunits of mitochondrial complex I, the largest enzyme complex of the electron transport chain, responsible for transferring electrons from NADH to ubiquinone to drive ATP synthesis [24], further reinforcing the presence of a metabolic shift in Selenof-deficient skin.

SELENOF levels are dramatically reduced in human skin tumors. To assess the relevance of these findings in humans, a skin cancer tissue microarray (TMA) comprising squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), basal cell carcinoma (BCC), and malignant melanoma (MM) samples, along with available matched normal adjacent tissue, was analyzed for SELENOF expression. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed using an antibody directed against human SELENOF, and expression was quantified at the single-cell level. This antibody has been validated and previously used for immunostaining of breast and prostate cancer samples [[25], [26], [27]]. The TMA included 5 cores from normal tissue from different patients, 42 cores from SCC, 14 cores from BCC, and 10 cores from melanoma samples. Tumors spanned grades 1-3 and clinical stages I–III/IV (Table 2). The median patient age was 60 years, with 17 females (25.8%) and 49 males (74.2%). Representative SELENOF-stained tumor cores are shown in Fig. 4A (right panels), alongside their matched normal adjacent tissues (left panels). Each tissue core was unique and contained thousands of cells (mean = 8413; SD = 4278; median = 7668; range = 1128–20,892). In normal tissue, SELENOF staining revealed robust expression in the epidermis, particularly within the spinous layer, whereas tumor cores were largely devoid of SELENOF expression (Fig. 4A). Only epithelial regions, as annotated by a pathologist, were included in the analysis. Given the skewed distribution of SELENOF expression, data were log-transformed prior to analysis. A linear mixed-effects regression model was used to evaluate differences in SELENOF levels between tissue types, with random core effects accounting for correlations between cell measurements in the same core. Notably, SELENOF expression was markedly reduced across all three skin cancer types compared with normal tissue (p < 0.001) shown in Fig. 4B, consistent with a tumor-suppressive role for SELENOF. MM and BCC samples exhibited the lowest SELENOF expression, whereas SCC samples showed higher mean expression but also the greatest variability; SCC accounted for 64% of tumor samples in this TMA. SELENOF staining predominantly localized throughout the cytoplasm, likely the endoplasmic reticulum consistent with prior reports [28], but was also detected at relatively lower levels in additional cellular compartments, including the nucleus and plasma membrane [26]. Accordingly, SELENOF levels were quantified at the whole-cell, nuclear, cytoplasmic, and membrane levels (Table 3). Mean SELENOF levels were highest in normal tissue across all cellular compartments. Among tumor samples, SCC exhibited higher mean SELENOF levels than BCC and MM across all cellular compartments except the membrane, whereas no significant differences were observed between BCC and MM at any subcellular location.

Differences at the tissue level were assessed by averaging single-cell measurements within each core (Table 4). Mean SELENOF expression differed significantly across tissue types, with the highest levels observed in normal tissue, followed by SCC, and the lowest levels in BCC and MM across all cellular compartments (p < 0.0001). Paired tests for matched tumor-to-normal comparisons within the same patients confirmed that mean SELENOF levels were significantly higher in normal tissue than in skin tumors across all cellular locations. Associations between tumor tissue SELENOF expression and pathological or clinical features were also examined, including skin cancer type, tumor stage, tumor grade, age and sex (Table 2). When stratified by skin cancer type, mean SELENOF levels were significantly higher in SCC (mean = 2.9, SD = 0.8) compared with BCC (mean = 2.1, SD = 0.8) or MM (mean = 1.7, SD = 0.4). SELENOF levels varied significantly by tumor stage (p = 0.007), largely due to lower expression in stage II tumors (mean = 2.3, SD = 0.8) compared with stage III/IV tumors (mean = 3.2, SD = 1.0, p = 0.007). No associations were detected with either sex, age, or tumor grade (Table 2).

SELENOF is tightly regulated at the level of translation, and aside from selenium availability, few regulators of its synthesis have been identified. In vitro, selenium supplementation in the form of sodium selenite increased SELENOF protein levels in both normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEK) and immortalized HaCaT cells (Supplementary Fig. 2A). To explore potential mechanisms regulating SELENOF in human tissue samples, the TMA was co-stained with an antibody against eIF4A3, an RNA helicase best known for its role in RNA splicing [29], but also reported to be elevated under selenium-deficient conditions and to negatively regulate SELENOF translation [30]. The TMA was also stained for UGGT1, a glucosyltransferase that functions as the folding sensor of the calnexin cycle. SELENOF typically localizes to the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [31], where it forms a high-affinity 1:1 complex with UGGT1 [32] and enhances UGGT1 enzymatic activity in glycoprotein maturation and ER redox quality control [33,34]. Consistent with this functional interaction, we previously demonstrated in breast epithelial cells that depletion of UGGT1 leads to reduced SELENOF protein levels and phenocopies the effects of SELENOF loss on ER stress responses [35]. Using cell-level data, no significant differences in mean eIF4A3 or UGGT1 levels across tissue types (normal or cancer) were found in any cellular compartment (Table 3). At the tissue level, UGGT1 did not exhibit significant associations with skin cancer type in any cellular compartment (Table 4). In contrast, eIF4A3 levels differed in the cytoplasm (p = 0.0454) but not in the nucleus (p = 0.0921). The cytoplasmic differences were largely driven by higher eIF4A3 levels in normal tissues (mean = 2.2, SD = 1.0) compared to MM (mean = 1.2, SD = 1.0), with no significant differences observed for BCC or SCC. UGGT1 did not exhibit significant associations among the available clinical features (Supplementary Table 1). In contrast, eIF4A3 (Supplementary Table 2) marginally varied by skin cancer type (p = 0.057), with mean levels significantly higher in SCC (mean = 1.8, SD = 0.6) than in MM (mean = 1.2, SD = 1.1).

Lastly, the correlation between SELENOF and eIF4A3 or SELENOF and UGGT1 at the tissue level stratified by cellular location and tissue types was examined (Supplementary Table 3). There are overall moderate positive correlations between eIF4A3 and SELENOF levels in whole-cell (r = 0.368, p = 0.002) and cytoplasmic compartment (r = 0.386, p = 0.001) regardless of the tissue types, but not in the nucleus. There was no overall significant correlation between UGGT1 and SELENOF levels in each cellular location. Surprisingly, when stratified by tissue types, UGGT1 was strongly positively correlated with SELENOF levels in MM in the cytoplasm (r = 0.631, p = 0.05) and marginally positively correlated at the cellular level (r = 0.587, p = 0.074), given a small sample size of 10.

Discussion

The loss of Selenof increased susceptibility to carcinogen-induced skin tumor formation indicating a tumor-suppressive role for Selenof. No significant difference in mammary tumor incidence between the Selenof KO and WT cohorts were observed, although mammary tumors developed only in mice fed the WD (Table 1A). The overall number of mammary tumors was too low to draw definitive conclusions; however, this observation is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that high dietary fat increases mammary tumor incidence in rats exposed to DMBA [36]. The expected rate of mammary tumorigenesis in this model ranges from 30 to 70% [16], yet fewer than 30% of mice in our study developed these tumors. Susceptibility to DMBA-induced mammary tumors varies by mouse strain, with FVB/N mice exhibiting the highest incidence and C57BL/6 mice the lowest (43% versus 23%) [16]. Because this study used mice on a C57BL/6 background [37], strain-dependent susceptibility may have contributed to the lower rate of mammary tumor formation observed. Elevated hormone levels promote mammary tumorigenesis, and pregnancy which is associated with increased hormone levels, is often used in mouse studies to increase mammary tumor incidence [38]; this approach should be considered in future studies. Importantly, early termination of the study due to tumor burden from skin lesions may also have reduced the observed incidence of mammary tumors. Mammary tumors typically develop 30-40 weeks after DMBA exposure; however, our study was terminated at 25 weeks post-DMBA treatment because many mice reached humane endpoint criteria earlier due to skin lesions.

A previous study by Zheng et al. reported that loss of Selenof resulted in increased body weight when mice were challenged with a high fat diet; however, this was not observed in this study [10]. In contrast, Selenof KO mice exhibited less weight gain than WT mice in both the ND and WD cohorts (Fig. 2A). One possible explanation is that the KO cohort had a higher overall tumor burden (measured as the total number of tumors) than the WT cohort, which may have contributed to reduced weight gain. Additionally, this study used only female mice, whereas Zheng et al. used male mice [10]. Female mice are less susceptible to WD-induced weight gain due to the protective effects of circulating estrogen [39,40]. Although ovariectomy would reduce estrogen levels and potentially promote weight gain comparable to that previously observed in male mice, this would have conflicted with our objective of examining mammary tumorigenesis, which requires estrogen signaling [16]. Notably, a trend toward increased fasting glucose levels in the Selenof KO cohorts was observed consistent with prior reports [10]. Whether these elevations in blood glucose contribute to the increased tumor burden observed in Selenof KO mice remains to be determined.

Unexpectedly, loss of Selenof was found to markedly predispose mice to cutaneous SCC when exposed to DMBA. Ingested DMBA undergoes sequential oxidation and hydrolysis to form the ultimate carcinogenic metabolite, 7,12-DMBA-3,4-oxide-diol-1,2-epoxide, which generates DNA adducts and drives mutagenesis [16]. These bulky, helix-distorting adducts are primarily repaired by the nucleotide excision repair pathway, which also repairs lesions induced by ultraviolet B radiation, the main skin carcinogen [19]. Together with the observation that SELENOF levels in human tumors from skin cancer patients are significantly lower than in adjacent normal tissue, and that loss of Selenof leads to downregulation of epidermal development and differentiation programs in mouse skin, these findings strongly support a tumor-suppressive role SELENOF in skin cancer. The limitation of using only female mice in the DMBA study is partially mitigated by transcriptomic analyses of skin from both male and female mice, as well as by the skin TMA study, in which most of the patient cohort was male and no association between SELENOF levels and sex was observed (Table 2). SELENOF levels were significantly lower in SCC and BCC tumors compared with normal epidermis, consistent with both SCC and BCC arising from epidermal keratinocytes [41,42]. SELENOF levels were also very low in MM; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as a reduction relative to melanocytes, the cells from which MM arise [43], cannot be made conclusively since melanocytes are sparse and more difficult to detect by IHC analysis.

Transcriptomic analysis of skin from Selenof KO mice revealed a previously unrecognized role for Selenof in promoting epidermal development and keratinocyte differentiation. This finding is consistent with a likely tumor-suppressive function for Selenof, as loss of keratinocyte differentiation is a hallmark of tumor-specific keratinocytes in cutaneous SCC [23]. The analysis also identified a novel role for Selenof in restraining mitochondrial metabolism, including oxidative phosphorylation and electron transport chain activity. Notably, mitochondrial respiration is elevated in the differentiating layers of the epidermis, and disruption of mitochondrial respiration through expression of the mitochondrial uncoupling protein UCP3 promotes epidermal differentiation and markedly inhibits chemically induced skin tumorigenesis [44,45]. Collectively, these findings support a model in which loss of Selenof enhances mitochondrial metabolism, thereby impairing keratinocyte differentiation and promoting skin cancer development, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation.

What controls SELENOF levels beyond selenium (Supplementary Fig. 2A) or ER stress, as previously shown in breast epithelial cells [35], remains unknown. DMBA itself did not alter Selenof levels in the mouse mammary gland (Supplementary Fig. 1C) or in MCF-10A breast epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Two additional reported regulators of Selenof were investigated: eIF4A3, an upstream translational inhibitor of SELENOF translation, and UGGT1, SELENOF's binding partner in the ER. Using cell-level data, no significant differences in mean eIF4A3 or UGGT1 expression across tissue types were observed. At the core-level, eIF4A3 expression varied modestly by skin cancer type, with mean levels significantly higher in SCC than in MM. Compartment-specific analysis revealed differences in eIF4A3 levels in the cytoplasm but not in the nucleus. This cytoplasmic variation was largely driven by higher eIF4A3 expression in normal tissue compared with MM, whereas no significant differences were observed for BCC or SCC. The distinct nuclear and cytoplasmic localization of eIF4A3 may reflect its dual functions as an RNA splicing factor primarily active in the nucleus [29], and as a regulator of selenoprotein synthesis in the cytosol [46]. In this context, we previously investigated the interaction between eIF4A3 and a functional polymorphism within the 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR) of the SELENOF mRNA [47]. This polymorphism is part of a haplotype proposed to regulate SELENOF translation by influencing recoding of the in-frame UGA codon. In prostate cancer, eIF4A3 expression is significantly elevated, and mechanistic studies demonstrated that eIF4A3 directly binds a regulatory region of SELENOF mRNA, attenuating UGA codon readthrough and thereby suppressing SELENOF translation [30]. In breast cancer, the minor haplotype was significantly more frequent among African American patients and its association with SELENOF expression varied according to HER2 status [27]. Whether these or other SELENOF polymorphisms contribute to skin cancer susceptibility or influence SELENOF levels remains unknown and warrants further investigation.

The potential of selenium for cancer prevention has been investigated for decades, prompted by studies dating back to the 1970s that reported an inverse association between selenium status (both dietary intake and serum levels) and mortality from several types of cancer [48,49]. This evidence paved the way for two landmark cancer prevention trials with selenium supplementation: the Nutritional Prevention of Cancer (NPC) study in skin cancer and the Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention trial (SELECT) in prostate cancer. Unfortunately, these trials failed to demonstrate a benefit for selenium supplementation [50,51]. Of particular concern, a follow-up report showed that selenium supplementation increased the risk of SCC and overall non-melanoma skin cancer [52]. This association was subsequently confirmed by both a prior Cochrane review and a more recent systematic review and meta-analysis [53,54]. While selenium is required to support selenoprotein synthesis, these findings underscore the potential limitations of selenium supplementation, as it broadly affects multiple selenoproteins, some of which may exert complex and even opposing effects on cancer [55]. Collectively, our data establishes a previously unrecognized tumor-suppressive role for SELENOF in the skin. Skin cancer is the most common malignancy in the United States, with approximately 1 in 5 individuals developing skin cancer during their lifetime (American Cancer Society, Cancer Facts & Figuresr 2026). Exposure to ultraviolet B radiation is the major environmental risk factor for skin cancer. Despite this knowledge, public health efforts relying on sunscreen use and awareness campaigns have not succeeded in reducing the overall incidence of skin cancer in the United States [56]. Therefore, identifying molecular drivers of skin carcinogenesis remains an urgent priority for prevention and treatment strategies. Our findings identifying SELENOF as a tumor suppressor in the skin support the concept that restoring or enhancing SELENOF may represent a novel strategy to prevent or limit skin cancer development.

Materials and methods

Reagents

DMBA and sodium selenite were purchased from Sigma. Antibodies specific to SELENOF (ab124840) eIF4A3 (ab32485), UGGT1 (sc-374565) and β-actin (A5441) were purchased from Abcam, Santa Cruz and Sigma. The ND and WD chows were obtained from Inotiv.

Cell lines, culture conditions and treatments

MCF-10A were purchased from ATCC. NHEK cells were obtained from neonatal foreskins with institutional approval as previously described [57]. HaCaT cells were originally provided by Dr. Norbert E. Fusenig. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2, and the media were changed every 2–3 days. MCF-10A cells were cultured in DMEM-F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 20 ng/mL epithelial growth factor, 0.5 mg/mL hydrocortisone, 100 ng/mL cholera toxin, and 10 μg/mL insulin. NHEK cells were cultured in Medium 154 containing 0.07 mM CaCl2 and Human Keratinocyte Growth Supplement. HaCaT cells were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

Selenof KO mice and genotyping

Selenof KO mice on a C57BL/6 background originally generated by Kasaikina et al. [37] were obtained and used to start our own colony. Genotyping of tail-snips was used to confirm Selenof status (Supplemental Fig. 1). Tail snips of 5 mm were taken from 3 weeks-old mice and genotyped according to published protocol [37].

DMBA-induced tumorigenesis

Mouse experiments were conducted at the Loyola University Chicago animal facility in accordance with institutional procedures and guidelines following prior approval by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. For the DMBA study, mice assigned to the Normal Diet (ND) cohort were fed standard natural ingredient diet (chow) containing 17% of calories from fat, 58% from carbohydrates, and 25% from protein, with an energy density of 3.1 kcal/g (Envigo/Inotiv, catalog no. 7912, irradiated). Mice assigned to the Western Diet (WD) cohort were fed a semi-purified atherogenic diet (Envigo/Inotiv, catalog no. TD.88137) containing 42% of calories from fat, 42% from carbohydrates, and 15.2% from protein, with an energy density of 4.5 kcal/g. Selenium content was comparable between the two diets (0.16 mg/kg and 0.11 mg/kg). Selenof (KO) mice were rederived, bred, and female mice were randomly assigned to the study. Age-matched wild type female mice were commercially obtained (Inotiv, strain: C57BL/6NHsd). All mice were housed together. DMBA was prepared by dissolving the compound in corn oil at a concentration of 10 mg/mL and by incubating at 37 °C with stirring while protected from light. The DMBA solution was stored at 4 °C in amber glass vials for the duration of the protocol. Mice received 100 μL of the DMBA solution by oral gavage once weekly for six weeks (1 mg DMBA per dose), beginning at approximately six weeks of age [16]. Animals were examined weekly for development of mammary tumors and skin lesions as well as general appearance and signs of ill health. Palpable and measurable lesions were measured with calipers on a weekly basis and recorded in millimeters.

Weight and glucose measurements

All mice were weighed weekly, and weights were recorded in grams. Glucose measurements were taken in the morning, after an overnight fast with an animal glucometer (lAlphaTRAK2 Blood Glucose Monitoring Meter and corresponding test strips). Three glucose measurements were taken, one before mice were randomized into their respective diet cohorts or DMBA (approximately 6 weeks old), one after they had finished the protocol (approximately 12 weeks old), and one final time at the end of the DMBA protocol for multiple weeks (18 weeks and older).

Tumor histology

Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. DMBA-induced lesions identified at necropsy were excised, placed in cassettes, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h. Samples were then washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored in PBS containing 4% paraformaldehyde (2 mL) until processing. Tissues were subsequently submitted for processing and paraffin embedding, sectioning, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (within one week of tissue collection) and histopathologically examined by a veterinary pathologist (MCB). Many of the skin lesions listed in Table 1 were confirmed by histopathological examination, whereas others were classified based on photographs obtained at necropsy or on gross observations recorded periodically throughout the study and at necropsy. Comparing histological images with photos at necropsy and gross descriptions, squamous cell carcinomas and sebaceous-squamous cell carcinomas were typically crater-like ulcerating lesions, while (sebaceous-) squamous papillomas were exophytic non-ulcerating growths that were smaller than most carcinomas. For two animals in the knockout group fed the western diet for which no slides or photos were available, the distinction between carcinoma and papilloma was made on gross description and growth rate, assuming fast growing lesions to be cancers and slow growing lesions to be papillomas. The resulting data constitute Table 1.

Skin TMA histology and imaging

The skin TMA was purchased from TissueArray (SK801d). The UIC Research Histology Core performed antibody optimization, and the final TMA staining. The TMA slide was stained with SELENOF (Opal 570), eIF4a3 (Opal 520) and UGGT1 (Opal 69). The slide was scanned at 20× resolution on an Akoya PhenoImager HT using software version 1.0.13 in the whole slide scanning mode with the DAPI, Opal 520, Opal 570, Opal 690 and autofluorescence filter cubes. Images were unmixed using inForm 2.6 and the built-in synthetic libraries. All tissue cores were included in the analysis. A tissue classifier for Tumor, Non-Tumor and Blank regions was trained, and further analysis was limited to the tumor areas. Nuclei, cytoplasm, and membrane cell compartments were segmented using the adaptive cell segmentation (InForm2.4, Akoya Biosciences) algorithm that considers variations in staining and backgrounds allowing the identification of cellular compartments over multiple image planes. The nuclear, cytoplasmic, membrane and whole cells mean and sum intensities were obtained for SELENOF, eIF4A3 and UGGT1.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

For the transcriptomics study, 7-week-old male and female mice (n = 2) were used. Littermate control male and female mice, WT and Selenof KO (n = 4 per group), were depilated and dorsal skin biopsies were collected. Tissue was homogenized in TriZol followed by RNA extraction. RNA quantity and quality were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. RNA-seq library preparation, sequencing, and initial quality control were performed by Novogene. Libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq platform employing a paired-end 150bp sequencing strategy. Downstream analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0) for differential expression analysis. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the GSEA desktop application (version 4.3.3, Broad Institute). The full gene expression dataset, quantified as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads), was used as input. Analysis was performed using the standard (non-pre-ranked) mode with 1000 phenotype permutations to assess statistical significance.

Western blot

Whole cell extracts were prepared using the M-PER reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific). Proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE (Invitrogen), transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using the iBlot 2 instrument (Invitrogen), blocked for 1 h in TBS/T buffer containing 5% non-fat dry milk, and incubated with the appropriate primary antibody (diluted in 5% milk in TBS/T) overnight. The next day, the corresponding secondary antibody was applied for 1 h, and the signal was visualized using an iBright CL1000 Imaging System (Invitrogen) with the Pierce Supersignal West Pico chemiluminescent substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific). Protein density quantification was conducted by ImageJ and normalized to β-Actin.

RT-Quantitative PCR (QPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol according to manufacturer's instructions. RNA (0.5 μg) was reverse transcribed in a total volume of 10 μL using 200U of M-MLV reverse transcriptase (0.5 μL), 100 ng of random hexamer (0.5 μL), 0.5 mM of deoxy-NTP (0.5 μL), and 100 mM of DTT (1 μL), DEPC-treated water (0.5 μL), 5X buffer (2 μL). The resulting cDNA was diluted with 90 μL of DEPC-treated water, then 2 μL cDNA were mixed with SYBR Green master mix (8 μL, 5 μL 2X SYBR green, 1 μL 1.25 μM forward and reverses Primer Mix, 2 μL DEPC-treated water). Amplifications were performed using the QuantStudio3 instrument (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The fold-change was calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. All QPCR primers used were validated and are available upon request.

Statistical methods

Due to skewed distributions, SELENOF, eIF4A3, and UGGT1 protein expression levels were log-transformed at the cell level prior to analysis. At cell level, linear mixed-effect regression was conducted to evaluate the difference in SELENOF, eIF4A3, UGGT1 expressions across tissue types, stratified by cellular compartments at the cell level, with random core effects accounting for correlations between cell measurements in the same core. Due to the large cell sample sizes, Type I error probability at cell level was controlled at 0.001. For core-level analysis, cell-level protein expression values (log scale) were averaged within each unique core. Paired t-tests were employed to compare the difference between matched normal and tumor tissue protein expression levels and Spearman correlation for ordinal variables. Two sample t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test were employed to compare demographic groups or tissue type differences. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess the correlations between continuous variables and protein expression levels and Spearman correlation for ordinal variables. Type I error probability for core level analysis was controlled at 0.05. These statistical analyses were performed using SAS (SAS 9.4, SAS Institute, Inc. 2004). Mouse skin tumor incidence and all tumor-bearing animal data were analyzed using the Fisher exact test, and tumor multiplicity data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney test (one- and two-sided P values were calculated). These statistical analyses were performed using Prizm software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA).
