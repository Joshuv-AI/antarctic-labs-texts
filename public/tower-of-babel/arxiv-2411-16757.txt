Deriving Tsallis entropy from non-extensive Hamiltonian within a statistical mechanics framework
Paradon Krisut, Sikarin Yoo-Kong

Abstract
The Tsallis entropy, which possesses non-extensive property, is derived from the first principle employing the non-extensive Hamiltonian or the q q -deformed Hamiltonian with the canonical ensemble assumption in statistical mechanics. Here, the q q -algebra and properties of q q -deformed functions are extensively used throughout the derivation. Consequently, the thermodynamic quantities, e.g. internal energy and Helmholtz free energy, are derived and they inheritly exhibit the non-extensiveness. From this intriguing connection between Tsallis entropy and the q q -deformed Hamiltonian, the parameter q q encapsulates the intrinsic degree of non-extensivity for the thermodynamic systems.

2
The
q
-deformed Hamiltonian

In this section, we will give a brief review of the multiplicative Lagrangian and Hamiltonian and also will point out how to construct the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian from the multiplicative one as well as an intriguing similar structure between the
q
-Hamiltonian and the Tsallis entropy.

To make this section consistently clear, let us first review some features of the Tsallis entropy given by

T_{q}\,=\,-k\,\frac{1-\sum_{i}p_{i}^{q}}{1-q}\,,\hskip 14.22636ptq\in\mathbb{R},

(2.1)

where
q
is the parameter and
k
is the Boltzmann constant. The key feature of Tsallis entropy is the non-additive property, also called the pseudo-additivity. If system 1 and system 2 are independent, the joint probability satisfies the product rule
\,p_{ij}^{12}\,=\,p_{i}^{1}p_{i}^{2}\,
resulting in

T_{q}^{12}=T_{q}^{1}+T_{q}^{2}+(1-q)\frac{T_{q}^{1}T_{q}^{2}}{k}\;.

(2.2)

Here,
\,T_{q}^{12},T_{q}^{1}
and
T_{q}^{2}\,
represent the Tsallis entropy of the total system 12, system 1 and system 2, respectively. Under the limit
q\rightarrow 1
, one obtains

S_{BG}\,=\,-k\sum_{i}p_{i}\ln\,p_{i}\;,

(2.3)

which is the standard Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy satisfying
S_{BG}^{12}\,=\,S_{BG}^{1}+S_{BG}^{2}
with the product rule of the joint probability.
There is another one-parameter entropy known as the Rényi entropy

R_{q}\,=\,\frac{k}{1-q}\ln\sum_{i}p_{i}^{q}\,,\hskip 14.22636ptq>0\;.

(2.4)

One of the interesting features is the additivity

R_{q}^{12}\,=\,R_{q}^{1}+R_{q}^{2}\;,

(2.5)

which seems to share this feature with the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy. Moreover, there is the relation between Tsallis entropy and Rényi entropy as

T_{q}\,=\,\frac{k}{1-q}[e^{(1-q)R_{q}/k}-1]\;,

(2.6)

or

R_{q}\,=\,\frac{k}{1-q}\ln[1+(1-q)T_{q}/k]\;.

(2.7)

Applying (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) in the appendix, we can write

T_{q}\,=\,k\,\ln_{q}\,(e^{R_{q}/k})\;,

(2.8)

R_{q}\,=\,k\,\ln\,(e_{q}^{T_{q}/k})\;.

(2.9)

Using (
A.4
) and (
A.3
), one has

e_{q}^{T_{q}/k}\,=\,e^{R_{q}/k}\;.

(2.10)

Now, we are ready to explore the properties of
q
deformed Hamiltonian. We shall first start with a bit of history. With the system of one degree of freedom and the equation of motion is given by

m\ddot{x}=-\frac{dV}{dx}\;,

(2.11)

it is not difficult to work out that the Lagrangian for this system is
L_{N}(\dot{x},x)=T(\dot{x})-V(x)
, where
T(\dot{x})=m\dot{x}^{2}/2
is the kinetic energy and
V(x)
is the potential energy. However, from the non-uniqueness property of the Lagrangian, one can multiply and add constants such that
\,L_{N}\to\beta L_{N}+\alpha\,
without affecting the equation of motion (
2.11
). Moreover, the total derivative term
\frac{df(x,t)}{dt}
can be directly added into the Lagrangian and, again, this process will not alter the equation of motion. This is quite trivial since the total derivative term becomes the boundary term and does not play a part in the variational process of the action leaving the same Euler-Lagrange equation.

In
[
38
]
, a simple question was addressed: Are there other forms of Lagrangian producing the equation of motion (
2.11
)? It turns out to be “YES”. A new form of the Lagrangian called the multiplicative form
L(\dot{x},x)=\,F(\dot{x})G(x)
is purposed, where
F(\dot{x})
and
G(x)
are to be determined. Employing the inverse problem of the calculus of variations together with the constraint (
2.11
), the multiplicative Lagrangian is obtained

L_{\lambda}(x,\dot{x})=m\lambda^{2}\left(e^{-\frac{\dot{x}^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}}+\frac{\dot{x}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{0}^{\dot{x}}e^{-\frac{{v}^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}}\,dv\right)e^{-\frac{V(x)}{m\lambda^{2}}}-m\lambda^{2}\;,

(2.12)

where
\lambda
is a parameter in the velocity unit. It is not difficult to see that this new form of the Lagrangian gives exactly the same equation of motion (
2.11
) and, under the limit
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, the standard Lagrangian
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}L_{\lambda}=L_{N}
is recovered. Therefore, (
2.12
) can be treated as a one-parameter version of the standard Lagrangian.

Applying the Legendre transformation

H_{\lambda}(p,x)\,\equiv\,p_{\lambda}\dot{x}-L_{\lambda}(\dot{x},x)\;,\;\;\text{where}\;\;p_{\lambda}=\frac{\partial L_{\lambda}}{\partial\dot{x}}\;,

(2.13)

one obtains

H_{\lambda}(p,x)\,=\,-m\lambda^{2}e^{-\frac{H_{N}}{m\lambda^{2}}}+m\lambda^{2}\;.

(2.14)

Here
\,H_{N}(p,x)\,=\,\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+V(x)\,
is the standard Hamiltonian. Again, it is not difficult to see that this new Hamiltonian gives exactly the same equation of motion (
2.11
) and also under the limit
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}H_{\lambda}=H_{N}
is recovered.
Now, if we reparameterise the parameter such that
\,-m\lambda^{2}\,=\,\frac{\gamma}{1-q}\,
, where
\gamma
is a constant in the unit of energy and
q\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the multiplicative Hamiltonian (
2.14
) becomes

H_{q}(p,x)=\frac{\gamma}{1-q}[e^{(1-q)H_{N}/\gamma}-1]\;,

(2.15)

which is now called a
q
-deformed Hamiltonian. Consequently, if we have
H_{N}^{12}\,=\,H_{N}^{1}+H_{N}^{2}\,
, the
H_{q}^{12}
will be

H_{q}^{12}\,=\,H_{q}^{1}+H_{q}^{2}+(1-q)\frac{H_{q}^{1}H_{q}^{2}}{\gamma}\;,

(2.16)

which obviously exhibits the non-extensive property as those given in (
2.2
) for Tsallis entropy. In the limit
q\rightarrow 1
, (
2.16
) becomes a standard additive between two Hamiltonian:
H_{1}^{12}=H_{1}^{1}+H_{1}^{2}=H_{N}^{12}
.

Next, we can express (
2.15
) in terms of
q
-exponential (
A.1
) and
q
-logarithm (
A.2
), resulting in

H_{q}\,=\,\gamma\,\ln_{q}\,e^{H_{N}/\gamma},

(2.17)

or

e_{q}^{H_{q}/\gamma}\,=\,e^{H_{N}/\gamma}.

(2.18)

With properties of the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian, it seems to suggest us that there must be a connection between Tsallis entropy and
q
-deformed Hamiltonian from those hints of either the pseudo-additive features (
2.2
) and (
2.16
) or the relations (
2.10
) and (
2.18
). But the connection between entropy and Hamiltonian is quite not straightforward since it depends on the structure of the system. From (
2.18
), we can interpret the left-hand side of the equation as the Tsallis power law distribution while the right-hand side seems to give the energy distribution in statistical mechanics. However,
[
44
]
shows that Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy and thermodynamic entropy, in general, they are not directly connected but only special cases where a system is in equilibrium with the reservoir known as a canonical ensemble.

Then, we are interested in the property of a canonical ensemble using the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian and find its corresponding entropy. This approach is not the same with MSE
[
2
]
which obtained the probability distribution or density from entropy with some constraints. Here, we rather start from the first principle to construct a statistical ensemble for probability density and use it to find the entropy. We note that this kind of strategy from the first principle has been done before in

]
and
[

with the standard Hamiltonian leading to the imperfect reservoir condition. The difference is that we take the reservoir to be perfect as one normally does in standard textbooks of statistical mechanics together with the help of
q

3.1
The definition of
q
-version of phase space density matrix

To construct a statistical ensemble with the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian, we first construct a microcanonical ensemble for an isolated system. In the case of a standard Hamiltonian, the phase space density matrix of the system is given by

\rho_{N}\,=\,\frac{\delta(E_{N}-H_{N})}{Z_{N}}\;,

(3.1)

where
\delta(x)
is a delta function and
Z_{N}
is a normalization constant. Here, a label
N
denotes for the standard energy in the the Newtonian context.
For the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian case, it is very natural to define the phase space density matrix in the same sense such that

\rho_{q}\,=\,\frac{\delta(E_{q}-H_{q})}{Z_{q}}\;,

(3.2)

where
Z_{q}
is a normalization constant in this case.
However, the issue arises when we consider the phase space density matrix of a large system
H_{q}^{12}
. According to non-extensive feature, one has
H_{q}^{12}=H_{q}^{2}\mathbin{\oplus_{q}}H_{q}^{2}
. Here is a point, in the case of standard Hamiltonian, with
H_{N}^{12}
, one can find the Hamiltonian of subsystem 1 trivially:
H_{N}^{1}=H_{N}^{12}-H_{N}^{2}
. One can process the same with
H_{q}^{12}
and would get
H_{q}^{1}\neq H_{q}^{12}-H_{q}^{2}=H_{q}^{1}\mathbin{\oplus_{q}}H_{q}^{2}-H_{q}^{2}
.
However, this problem can be fixed by introducing a factor such that

\frac{H_{q}^{12}-H_{q}^{2}}{1+(1-q)H_{q}^{2}/\gamma}=H_{q}^{1}\;.

(3.3)

With (
A.7
), we now arrive

H_{q}^{1}=H_{q}^{12}\mathbin{\ominus_{q}}H_{q}^{2}

(3.4)

and the phase space density for system
12
becomes

\rho_{q}^{12}\,=\,\frac{\delta(E_{q}^{12}-(H_{q}^{1}\mathbin{\oplus_{q}}H_{q}^{2}))}{Z_{q}}.

(3.5)

So far, we have fixed one problem and another problem to go with the probability property of the
\rho_{q}^{12}
.
The main issue directly comes from tracing out any subsystem since there is an extra term
(1-q)H_{q}^{1}H_{q}^{2}/\gamma
, see
[
36
]
.
But if we treat the parameter
q
as a measure of the degree of non-extensivity, we can generalize the standard subtraction (between
E_{q}^{12}
and
H_{q}^{12}
) into the
q
-algebra context. By doing this, it is easy to proceed with the calculation from the property of
q
-deformed algebra (
A.19
), which looks very supportive and more natural to do operation than the standard one.
We conclude that, with a
q
-deformed Hamiltonian, one should consider
E\mathbin{\ominus_{q}}H
. Then, our phase space density can be expressed in the form

\rho_{q}^{12}\,=\,\frac{\delta_{q}(E_{q}^{12}\mathbin{\ominus_{q}}H_{q}^{12})}{Z_{q}}.

(3.6)

The last point is that one needs to use the
q
-Dirac delta function and a reason shall be revealed in the next section.

\rho_{q}^{1}(p_{1},x_{1})=\frac{e^{-\beta_{q}H_{q}^{1}}_{q}}{Z_{q}}\;,

(3.26)

where

Z_{q}\equiv\frac{\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{12})}{\Omega_{q}^{12}(E_{q}^{12})}\;.

(3.27)

The partition function
Z_{q}
is defined as a normalization factor to ensure that the integral over the phase space density is 1

Z_{q}=\int_{\Sigma}d\Gamma\,e^{-\beta_{q}H_{q}}_{q}.

(3.28)

For our convenience, we will drop the subscript ‘1’ since it is understood that our system is subsystem 1 and subsystem 2 is nothing but the reservoir.

4
Candidate entropy and system at thermal equilibrium

4.1
Candidate entropy

By constructing the canonical ensemble with
q
-deformed Hamiltonian and obtaining the phase space density matrix for the system and subsystems, we now set a task to derive the entropy. The question arises: Is the second law of thermodynamics still applicable? The answer is yes. The ensemble average of the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian over phase space yields the internal energy as

U_{q}=\int_{\Sigma}d\Gamma\,\rho_{q}H_{q}\;.

(4.1)

From
[
45
,
12
]
, we take the variation of
U
resulting in

\delta U_{q}=\int_{\Sigma}d\Gamma\,(\delta\rho_{q})H_{q}+\int_{\Sigma}d\Gamma\,\rho_{q}(\delta H_{q})\;.

(4.2)

The first law of thermodynamics reads

\delta U=\delta Q+\delta W
.
Then, the second term on the right-hand side in (
4.2
) corresponds to
\delta W
. We, therefore, can identify the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.2
) as

\delta Q_{q}=\int_{\Sigma}d\Gamma\,(\delta\rho_{q})H_{q}\;.

(4.3)

According to the second law of thermodynamics, we have

\delta S=\frac{\delta Q}{T}\;,

(4.4)

where
T
is the temperature. We can then find the candidate entropy function
\altmathcal{S}
that satisfies the second law of thermodynamics as follows

\altmathcal{S}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\Sigma}\altmathcal{B}\,d\Gamma+C\;,

(4.5)

where
C
is constant of integration and will be fixed later. The function
\mathcal{B}
is given by

\mathcal{B}=\int_{0}^{\rho}H_{q}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\;.

(4.6)

From the results of the previous section, taking the
q
-logarithm of both sides of (
3.26
), we obtain

\ln_{q}\rho_{q}=-\beta_{q}H_{q}\mathbin{\ominus_{q}}\ln_{q}Z_{q}\;.

(4.7)

Next, we express the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian as a function of the phase space density matrix

H_{q}(\rho_{q})=-\frac{1}{\beta_{q}}\left[[1+(1-q)\ln_{q}Z_{q}]\ln_{q}\rho_{q}+\ln_{q}Z_{q}\right]\;.

(4.8)

Substituting
H_{q}(\rho_{q})
into
\mathcal{B}
, we get

\mathcal{B}=-\frac{(Z_{q})^{1-q}}{\beta_{q}(1-q)}\left[\frac{\rho^{2-q}}{2-q}-\rho\right]-\frac{\rho\ln_{q}Z_{q}}{\beta_{q}}\;.

(4.9)

To avoid the confusion of the index
q
between subscript and superscript, we drop it since the phase space density matrix is still depend on parameter
q
. Therefore, our candidate entropy function becomes

\mathcal{S}=-\frac{\ln_{q}Z_{q}}{T\beta_{q}}-\frac{(Z_{q})^{1-q}}{T\beta_{q}(1-q)}\int_{\Sigma}\left[\frac{\rho^{2-q}}{2-q}-\rho\right]d\Gamma+C\;.

(4.10)

By reparametising the index
q^{\prime}=2-q
and using the fact that

\frac{\rho^{q^{\prime}}}{q^{\prime}}-\rho=\frac{\rho^{q^{\prime}}-\rho}{q^{\prime}}+\frac{\rho(1-q^{\prime})}{q^{\prime}}\;,

(4.11)

the candidate entropy function becomes

\mathcal{S}=\frac{(Z_{2-q^{\prime}})^{q^{\prime}-1}}{q^{\prime}T\beta_{2-q^{\prime}}}\int_{\Sigma}\left[\frac{\rho-\rho^{q^{\prime}}}{q^{\prime}-1}\right]d\Gamma+\frac{1-\ln_{2-q^{\prime}}Z_{2-q^{\prime}}}{q^{\prime}T\beta_{2-q^{\prime}}}+C.

(4.12)

The last two terms vanish if we choose
C=-\frac{1-\ln_{2-q^{\prime}}Z_{2-q^{\prime}}}{q^{\prime}T\beta_{2-q^{\prime}}}
. Moreover, the integral

T_{q^{\prime}}\equiv\int_{\Sigma}\left[\frac{\rho-\rho^{q^{\prime}}}{q^{\prime}-1}\right]d\Gamma

is nothing but the Tsallis entropy. Therefore, the candidate entropy function reduces to

\mathcal{S}_{q^{\prime}}=\frac{(Z_{2-q^{\prime}})^{q^{\prime}-1}}{q^{\prime}T\beta_{2-q^{\prime}}}T_{q^{\prime}}.

(4.13)

We observe that if
\frac{(Z_{2-q^{\prime}})^{q^{\prime}-1}}{q^{\prime}T\beta_{2-q^{\prime}}}
is constant, our candidate entropy function has the same behavior as Tsallis entropy. We next need to verify whether
\beta_{2-q^{\prime}}
is constant in thermal equilibrium.

4.2
Thermal equilibrium condition: A property of
\beta_{q}

To analyze the thermal equilibrium condition for the system with the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian, we start to simplify the expression for
\beta_{q}
as

\beta_{q}^{12}=\left[1+(1-q)\frac{E_{q}^{12}}{\gamma}\right]\left[\frac{d}{dE_{q}^{12}}\ln_{q}\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{12})\right][\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{12})]^{q-1}\;.

(4.14)

Using the definition of the
q
-logarithm, the
\beta_{q}
can be expressed in the form

\beta_{q}^{12}=\left[1+(1-q)\frac{E_{q}^{12}}{\gamma}\right]\frac{d}{dE_{q}^{12}}\ln\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{12})\;.

(4.15)

For the standard Hamiltonian case
[
46
,
47
]
, the thermal equilibrium condition is derived by finding the probability density that subsystem 1 has a specific energy
E^{1}
. This probability density is given by

\mathcal{P}(E^{1})=\frac{\Omega^{2}(E^{12}-E^{1})\Omega^{1}{(E^{1})}}{\Omega^{12}(E^{12})}\;.

(4.16)

The most probable value of
E^{1}
, denoted by
\mathcal{E}^{1}
, is found by setting the derivative of
\mathcal{P}(E^{1})
with respect to
E^{1}
to zero

\frac{d}{dE^{1}}\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}(E^{12}-E^{1})\Omega^{1}{(E^{1})}}{\Omega^{12}(E^{12})}\right)=0\;.

(4.17)

This leads to

\left(\Omega^{2}(E^{12}-E^{1})\frac{d}{dE^{1}}\Omega^{1}(E^{1})+\Omega^{1}(E^{1})\frac{d}{dE^{1}}\Omega^{2}(E^{12}-E^{1})\right)\Bigg|_{\mathcal{E}^{1}}=0.

(4.18)

Given that
E^{1}+E^{2}=E^{12}
(a constant), we have
dE^{1}=-dE^{2}
. Thus, the equation becomes

\Omega^{2}(E^{2})\frac{d}{dE^{1}}\Omega^{1}(E^{1})\Bigg|_{\mathcal{E}^{1}}=\Omega^{1}(E^{1})\frac{d}{dE^{2}}\Omega^{2}(E^{2})\Bigg|_{E^{12}-\mathcal{E}^{1}}.

(4.19)

The above equation is

\ \beta^{1}=\beta^{2}
which is the standard thermodynamics equilibrium condition, where
\beta^{i}=\frac{d}{dE^{i}}\ln\Omega^{i}(E^{i})
.

In the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian case, the probability density that subsystem 1 has specific energy
E_{q}^{1}
should have the same form as the standard Hamiltonian case

\mathcal{P}_{q}(E_{q}^{1})=\frac{\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{12}\mathbin{\ominus_{q}}E_{q}^{1})\Omega_{q}^{1}{(E^{1})}}{\Omega_{q}^{12}(E_{q}^{12})}\;.

(4.20)

Then, we take the derivative and set it to zero. We note that one could take the
q
-derivative or the standard derivative resulting in exactly identical relation

\left(\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{12}\mathbin{\ominus_{q}}E_{q}^{1})\frac{d}{dE_{q}^{1}}\Omega_{q}^{1}(E_{q}^{1})+\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{1})\frac{d}{dE_{q}^{1}}\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{12}\mathbin{\ominus_{q}}E_{q}^{1})\right)\Bigg|_{\mathcal{E}_{q}^{1}}=0.

(4.21)

Although the composite system has a fixed energy, it does not imply
dE_{q}^{1}=-dE_{q}^{2}
, because of the non-extensive property of
q
-deformed Hamiltonian. To see what we have in this context, one considers

E_{q}^{1}+E_{q}^{2}+(1-q)\frac{E_{q}^{1}E_{q}^{2}}{\gamma}=E_{q}^{12}\;.

(4.22)

Differentiating (
4.22
) with respect to
E_{q}^{1}
, we obtain

1+\frac{dE_{q}^{2}}{dE_{q}^{1}}+\frac{(1-q)}{\gamma}\left(E_{q}^{1}\frac{dE_{q}^{2}}{dE_{q}^{1}}+E_{q}^{2}\right)=0\;.

(4.23)

We rearrange the terms in (
4.23
) to obtain
\frac{dE_{q}^{2}}{dE_{q}^{1}}
, resulting in

\frac{dE_{q}^{2}}{dE_{q}^{1}}=-\frac{1+\frac{(1-q)E_{q}^{2}}{\gamma}}{1+\frac{(1-q)E_{q}^{1}}{\gamma}}\;.

(4.24)

Substituting (
4.24
) into the equilibrium condition (
4.21
), we arrive

\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{2})\frac{d}{dE_{q}^{1}}\Omega_{q}^{1}(E_{q}^{1})\Bigg|_{\mathcal{E}_{q}^{1}}-\frac{1+\frac{(1-q)E_{q}^{2}}{\gamma}}{1+\frac{(1-q)E_{q}^{1}}{\gamma}}\Omega_{q}^{1}(E_{q}^{1})\frac{d}{dE_{q}^{2}}\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{2})\Bigg|_{E_{q}^{12}\mathbin{\ominus_{q}}\mathcal{E}_{q}^{1}}=0\;,

(4.25)

which can be simplified further to

\left[1+(1-q)\frac{E_{q}^{1}}{\gamma}\right]\frac{d}{dE_{q}^{1}}\ln\Omega_{q}^{1}(E_{q}^{1})\Bigg|_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}=\left[1+(1-q)\frac{E_{q}^{2}}{\gamma}\right]\frac{d}{dE_{q}^{2}}\ln\Omega_{q}^{2}(E_{q}^{2})\Bigg|_{E_{q}^{12}\mathbin{\ominus_{q}}\mathcal{E}_{1}}.

(4.26)

Recalling the definition of
\beta_{q}^{12}
, thus, we establish a thermal equilibrium condition for the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian

\beta_{q}^{1}=\beta_{q}^{2}\;.

(4.27)

Therefore, we conclude that the candidate entropy function (
4.13
) is equivalent to the Tsallis entropy, up to a scaling factor.

5.1
The effective phase space density

Thermodynamic phenomena in the macroscopic world are well understood as emergent behaviors arising from the underlying randomness at the microscopic level, as described by statistical mechanics. In the previous sections, we successfully establish the statistical mechanics with the
q
-deformed Hamiltonian. Now, we are going to make a connection with the thermodynamics. With the canonical ensemble defined in section

and

In the case of the

\beta_{q}

Using the above result, we have

(5.5)

Here, the first problem arises when multiplying by

throughout (
5.5

(5.6)

The left-hand side seems to give what we want in (
5.1

H_{q}

To fix this problem, we start to consider the operator

(5.7)

To obtain

\frac{\partial x}{\partial\beta_{q}}D_{(q)}e^{x}_{q}=H_{q}e_{q}^{x}.

(5.8)

Although this approach seems to provide a method for differentiating the partition function to obtain the internal energy. However, the second problem inevitably pops up since the operator
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\beta_{q}}D_{(q)}

To tackle this second problem, initially, we hypothesize that the

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{q}+\frac{\tilde{Z}_{q}^{1-q}}{\beta_{q}T}\mathcal{Q}-\frac{1}{\beta_{q}T}\left(\frac{\tilde{Z}_{q}^{1-q}-1}{1-q}\right)+C\;,

(6.10)

\displaystyle T_{q}-\tilde{Z}_{q}^{q-1}\ln_{q}(\tilde{Z}_{q}e_{q}^{-\mathcal{Q}})+C\;.

Here, we can choose the constant
C
to eliminate the second term as we did in the previous derivation. Therefore, the candidate entropy function is perfectly the Tasllis entropy
\mathcal{S}_{q}=T_{q}
.
