Moral injury in Dutch treatment-seeking veterans: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Mariëlle L. de Goede, F. Jackie June ter Heide, Elisa van Ee-Blankers, Rick de Haart, Kathleen Thomaes, Paul A. Boelen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Moral injury (MI) is a construct distinct from, yet related to, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). To date, no study has assessed MI among Dutch treatment-seeking veterans using a validated measure.Objective: This study aimed to (a) examine the factor structure and psychometric properties of the Dutch Expressions of Moral Injury Scale – Military Version (EMIS-M); (b) to identify profiles of PTSD and MI outcomes among treatment-seeking Dutch veterans, as well as their associations with comorbid psychopathology and family functioning.Method: Participants were 455 Dutch treatment-seeking veterans at four national psychotrauma centres. Data were drawn from pre-intake routine outcome monitoring assessments. We evaluated the internal consistency, temporal stability, and factor structure of the EMIS-M, and conducted latent profile analysis (LPA) to identify PTSD and MI profiles, followed by analyses of associated psychopathology and family functioning.Results: The EMIS-M demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported a two-factor structure (MI-self and MI-other), though fit indices suggested room for improvement. LPA revealed three profiles: ‘Subclinical MI and PTSD’ (n = 95, 20.9%), ‘Moderate MI and PTSD’ (n = 218, 47.9%), and ‘High MI and PTSD’ (n = 142, 31.2%). Higher levels of MI and PTSD were associated with greater comorbid psychopathology. Family functioning was significantly poorer in the Moderate and High profiles compared to the Subclinical profile.Conclusions: Findings indicate that the Dutch EMIS-M is a psychometrically acceptable instrument for assessing MI among treatment-seeking veterans. MI frequently co-occurred with PTSD and was associated with greater psychopathology and poorer family functioning, underscoring the need for careful assessment of MI in veteran care.

Introduction

The concept of moral injury (MI) first emerged in the literature on Vietnam veterans (Lifton, 1973; Shay, 1994). This literature described not only the profound hardships that Vietnam veterans endured, but also the moral transgressions they committed or witnessed, and the enduring moral burden many subsequently experienced. This moral burden became known as MI – a construct developed to capture what conceptualizations of post-traumatic stress disorder insufficiently addressed, namely the moral dimensions of post-deployment suffering (Shay, 1994). Interest in MI expanded significantly after the publication of Litz et al.'s (2009) seminal article, which defined it as ‘the lasting psychological, biological, spiritual, behavioural, and social impact of perpetrating, failing to prevent, or bearing witness to acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs and expectations’ (p. 697). Transgressive acts that may lead to MI are commonly termed potentially morally injurious events (PMIEs; Litz et al., 2009). The morally injurious impact of such events can be far-reaching, including disruptions in personal identity, self-harming behaviours, reduced connectedness, and changes in beliefs about life’s meaning or purpose (Litz et al., 2022). Given this potentially impairing impact, MI is increasingly recognised as a significant clinical concern.

Most research on PMIEs and MI following military deployment has been conducted among U.S. veterans (e.g. Griffin et al., 2025). Because psychosocial reactions to potentially traumatic experiences can differ across cultures (Hinton & Lewis-Fernández, 2011), it is important to clarify whether constructs that have demonstrated explanatory value in primarily US military populations are applicable to Dutch veterans. For example, the experiences of Dutch veterans may differ from their US counterparts due to different cultural and military contexts. Dutch troops have historically served in several UN peacekeeping missions (although not exclusively), which may have exposed them to different types of PMIEs than US combat veterans (cf. de Goede et al., 2024). Peacekeeping operations may be morally injurious, for example, when mandate restrictions limit the ability to intervene in unfolding violence (e.g. ter Heide et al., 2025). Among Dutch peacekeepers deployed to the former Yugoslavia during the Srebrenica genocide, greater PMIE exposure – particularly experiences of being powerless – was associated with increased odds of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD; American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013) 25 years later (de Goede et al., 2024). Additionally, Dutch veterans generally report reserved pre-deployment expectations, including an anticipated gap between political objectives and what can realistically be achieved on the ground (Molendijk, 2021). For example, rather than being motivated by abstract ideals (such as ‘serving the country’), Dutch service members tend to be attracted by more concrete appeals to adventure and comradeship (Molendijk, 2021). Such differences in pre-deployment appraisal frameworks may shape how veterans interpret and respond to PMIEs, for example, by influencing the extent to which unmet expectations give rise to feelings of moral failure or betrayal. Examining manifestations of MI among treatment-seeking Dutch veterans is therefore important for evaluating the cross-cultural validity and clinical utility of the construct.

To date, no study has systematically assessed MI among treatment-seeking Dutch veterans using a validated measure. The present study therefore examined MI among trauma treatment-seeking Dutch veterans using the Expressions of Moral Injury Scale-Military Version (EMIS-M; Currier et al., 2018). We first translated the EMIS-M into Dutch following guidelines for translation of research instruments (Sousa & Rojjanasrirat, 2011). We then examined the psychometric properties and factor structure of the Dutch EMIS-M. Subsequently, we explored profiles of MI and PTSD indicators as well as the extent to which these profiles predict comorbid psychopathology and family functioning.

The Expressions of Moral Injury Scale – Military version (EMIS-M)

The EMIS-M was one of the first instruments developed to assess expressions of MI (defined as dysphoric moral emotions and cognitions, as well as maladaptive behaviours to manage moral distress; Currier et al., 2018). The authors found support for a two-factor model, consisting of self-directed MI (distress from one's own perceived transgressions) and other-directed MI (distress from transgressions by others), as well as a bifactor model, which adds an overarching MI factor (Currier et al., 2018). All factors demonstrated strong internal consistency and temporal reliability over a six-month period. The scale was also found to have strong convergent validity with measures of PTSD, depression, and PMIEs; strong divergent validity with measures of social support, hope, and gratitude; and strong incremental validity. A four-item short form of the EMIS-M has also been validated (Currier et al., 2020). Although the factor structure of the EMIS-M has not been evaluated in other military samples, studies using the measure among military personnel have reported adequate reliability and convergent validity with relevant outcomes such as suicidal ideation and mental health symptoms (Hamrick et al., 2022; Kelley et al., 2021). Emerging evidence also suggests that the two subscales may differentially predict outcomes: self-directed MI has been found to be more strongly associated with suicidal ideation via reduced meaning in life, as well as guilt and shame, than other-directed MI (Currier et al., 2018; Kelley et al., 2021).

In recent years, the EMIS-M has been adapted for first responders (Tappenden et al., 2024) and civilians (Thomas et al., 2024). Among first responders, Tappenden et al. (2024) reported support for the bifactor model; however, the robustness of this finding may be compromised because they estimated the correlation between the two factors, which is inconsistent with the assumptions of a bifactor model. Among civilians, Thomas et al. (2024) found the two-factor model to be a good fit, while Morriss and Berle (2023) found the two-factor model and bifactor model both fit the data. The question thus remains which factor model is best. Apart from Currier et al. (2018), the EMIS-M has not been psychometrically evaluated in other military samples.

MI profiles and associations with psychopathology and family functioning

Conceptually, MI overlaps with PTSD in terms of features such as shame, guilt, anger, social withdrawal, and negative cognitions about the self, others, and the world (Litz et al., 2009, 2022). A recent meta-analysis of MI measures reported that MI shares around 40% of its variance with PTSD, underscoring that these conditions are strongly related and frequently co-occurring, but nevertheless distinct phenomena (Griffin et al., 2025). This raises important questions concerning the treatment needs of veterans presenting with both MI and PTSD and the extent to which existing trauma-focused treatments adequately address the moral dimensions of their suffering. Identifying clinical profiles in trauma-exposed veterans may provide valuable insights into these treatment needs, as well as into the proportion of veterans who may require interventions specifically targeting MI. Several studies of MI among military veterans using latent profile analysis (LPA) or latent class analysis (LCA) have consistently revealed distinct subgroups (profiles or classes) characterised by varying constellations of PTSD and MI indicators. Mensink et al. (2022), for example, among treatment-seeking veterans and police officers, identified four subgroups, including an MI subgroup (8%), a combined MI and PTSD subgroup (52%), a PTSD subgroup (34%), and a subgroup with neither MI nor PTSD (6%). Similarly, Smigelsky et al. (2019) identified three subgroups among U.S. veterans: a high PTSD and depression subgroup (48%), a moderate PTSD and depression subgroup (30%), and a potential MI subgroup (22%). Currier et al. (2021), using MI and complex PTSD indicators, found a high distress subgroup (80%) and a low distress subgroup (20%) among British veterans. Except for Currier et al. (2021), few studies have applied LPA/LCA using a validated MI questionnaire among an exclusively veteran population.

LPA may also be used to distinguish clinical differences across subgroups. Mensink et al. (2022) found, for example, that participants reporting more comorbid psychopathology were more likely to be in the MI-PTSD or MI subgroup than the PTSD or neither-MI-nor-PTSD subgroup. Clinical differences may also concern inter- rather than intrapersonal problems. A defining feature of MI is its negative social impact, which may manifest as social withdrawal, reduced empathy, or aggressive behaviour (Litz et al., 2022), and there is growing evidence that these effects extend to the family context (e.g. Blom et al., 2023; Ne’eman-Haviv et al., 2025; Williamson et al., 2020, 2023). Qualitative studies have found that morally injured veterans report family withdrawal, anger problems at home, and risk-taking behaviours distressing to relatives (Williamson et al., 2020, 2023), while MI has been found to uniquely predict relationship difficulties above and beyond PTSD (Fernandez & Currier, 2024). However, although MI appears associated with poorer family functioning, to our knowledge, no study has examined differences in family functioning among clinical profiles.

Current study

The overarching aim of this study was to assess the extent to which Dutch treatment-seeking veterans experience MI and to determine the clinical relevance of the construct in this population, with the goal of informing treatment policies in the Netherlands. To achieve this, our research objectives were (1) to evaluate the factor structure of the EMIS-M, as well as its internal consistency and temporal stability; (2) to identify clinical profiles based on PTSD and MI indicators and to determine if these profiles could be differentiated by comorbid psychopathology and family functioning.

Based on prior psychometric evaluations of the EMIS-M (Currier et al., 2018; Morriss & Berle, 2023; Thomas et al., 2024) and earlier studies that found self- and other-directed MI to differentially predict outcomes (Hamrick et al., 2022; Kelley et al., 2021), we hypothesised (1) to find a two-factor model consisting of self-directed and other-directed MI. Based on aforementioned psychometric evaluations, we also hypothesised (2) that the Dutch EMIS-M would exhibit satisfactory internal consistency and test-retest reliability. Based on studies that identified clinical profiles among morally injured veterans (Mensink et al., 2022; Smigelsky et al., 2019), we hypothesised (3) to find at least three profiles, characterised by the presence of MI without PTSD, MI with PTSD, and PTSD without MI. Moreover, we expected (4) profiles characterised by more severe MI and PTSD symptomatology to be associated with higher levels of comorbid psychopathology and lower levels of family functioning.

Method

Participants and procedure

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Social and Behavioral Sciences of Utrecht University [approval no. 22-0116; no. 22-0562]. Data were collected between May 2022 and November 2024. Eligible participants were Dutch veterans seeking psychological treatment at one of four centres specialised in the treatment of complex psychotrauma, including the treatment of military veterans: ARQ Centrum’45, GGZ Drenthe Mental Health Institute, Reinier van Arkel and Sinai Centrum. The study used data from the routine outcome monitoring (ROM) assessment conducted at intake. For this study and concurrent clinical use, the EMIS-M was added to the standard ROM battery. All other questionnaires were part of the regular ROM assessment, which veteran patients were requested by email to complete online before intake. Before filling in the assessment, patients were asked to provide informed consent for the use of their ROM data in research.

We aimed to recruit 400 participants, which is considered adequate for LPA and distal outcome analysis (Wolf et al., 2013). Moreover, a sample size of 250 participants is considered sufficient for stable results in a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of a small-scale model using patient data (White, 2022). During the data collection period, 516 veterans filled in the ROM; of these, 457 veterans (89%) consented to the use of their data for research. Two veterans were excluded for not filling out the EMIS-M. The resulting sample size was 455 participants. Participants’ age and sex are displayed in Table 1. To determine the short-term test-retest reliability of the EMIS-M, an additional sample was recruited through ARQ Centrum’45 and Sinai Centrum between May and November 2023. The sample size was determined a priori using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007), applying an exact test for a bivariate normal correlation. Based on an expected correlation of ρ = .80, a null hypothesis correlation of ρ = .50, α = .05, and power = .80, a minimum sample size of 23 was required. A question was added to the EMIS-M in the ROM battery, asking veterans to fill in the EMIS-M a second time. If participants selected yes, they received a follow-up email two weeks later with a link to the online assessment portal that provided additional information about the study, a consent form, and the second EMIS-M. The link worked for one week, ensuring that all second administrations were collected within two to three weeks. A minimum interval of two weeks is generally considered acceptable for determining short-term test-retest reliability (Mokkink et al., 2018). A total of 37 veterans consented to participate in the test-retest study; 25 completed the EMIS-M on both occasions. Data from the remaining 12 participants were excluded from the analyses.

Preregistration

This study was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) website. The preregistration, including hypotheses, sample size, and planned analyses, is accessible at: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/H9YBZ. Although the preregistration specified a data collection period of 18 months, recruitment continued for an additional year to achieve the required sample size. The original study protocol also included the questionnaire Research And Development-SF/36 (Rand-36; van der Zee & Sanderman, 2012) to assess physical and societal functioning, to examine if clinical profiles differed with regards to these variables. However, this questionnaire was replaced by the TRACK – a questionnaire developed by the Dutch Veterans Health Care for similar purposes – during the data collection period. In the final dataset, an insufficient number of participants had filled in either questionnaire (109 and 125 participants for the Rand-36 and TRACK respectively). Consequently, we excluded these questionnaires from the analysis. Moreover, in the preregistration we hypothesised to find two profiles (PTSD with and without MI); this was revised to at least three profiles based on more recent research (Mensink et al., 2022).

Measures

Expressions of Moral Injury Scale – Military version (EMIS-M)

The EMIS-M (Currier et al., 2018) is a 17-item self-report questionnaire that measures agreement with MI expressions within military populations. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘strongly disagree’) to 5 (‘strongly agree’). Higher scores indicate worse psychological outcomes. The EMIS-M does not offer a cut-off score for clinically significant MI. Consequently, we use the term morally injurious outcomes to refer to these measurable expressions, rather than implying a binary presence or absence of MI. The EMIS-M has demonstrated strong internal consistency (α = ≥.94) and temporal stability (Currier et al., 2018).

The EMIS-M was translated into Dutch following the guidelines by Sousa and Rojjanasrirat (2011). The translation procedure involved forward and back translation, carried out by two certified translators and two bilingual scientist-practitioners with expertise in MI. Discrepancies between the original and translated versions were resolved through discussion among the scientist-practitioners. Remaining ambiguities were addressed in consultation with the original developer of the scale. The translated version was reviewed at two separate stages by a panel of veteran patient representatives, MI experts, and social workers working in veteran care. A detailed description of the translation procedure is provided in Supplement 1. For the Dutch translation of the EMIS-M, see Supplement 2.

PTSD checklist-5 (PCL-5)

The PCL-5 (Hoeboer et al., 2024; Weathers et al., 2013) is a 20-item self-report instrument that assesses PTSD symptoms according to DSM-5 during the past month. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert-scale indicating symptom endorsement ranging from 0 (‘not at all’) to 4 (‘extremely’). The PCL-5 consists of four subscales that correspond to the four symptom clusters of PTSD: Intrusion (items 1–5), Avoidance (items 6–7), Negative Alterations in Cognition and Mood (items 8–14) and Alterations in Arousal and Reactivity (items 15–20). The total score indicates PTSD symptom severity (0–80). The current study used summed scores per subscale, ranging 0–20 for Intrusion, 0–8 for Avoidance, 0–28 for Negative Alternations in Cognition and Mood, and 0–24 for Alterations in Arousal and Reactivity. A threshold score of 33 was used as an indicator of probable PTSD according to the DSM-5 (Bovin et al., 2016). The Dutch translation of the PCL-5 has demonstrated good psychometric properties (Hoeboer et al., 2024). Internal consistency in the current sample was α = .93 for the total scale and .89, .84, .84, and .78 for the respective subscales.

Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI)

The BSI (Derogatis & Melisaratos, 1983; Dutch translation de Beurs & Zitman, 2006) is a 53-item self-report questionnaire for general psychopathology in the past seven days. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale indicating symptom endorsement from 0 (‘not at all’) to 4 (‘extremely’). The total score (used in this study) provides an index of general psychopathology. The Dutch translation of the scale has good psychometric properties (de Beurs & Zitman, 2006) and internal consistency in the current study was excellent, α = .97.

SCORE-15 index of family functioning and change (SCORE-15)

The SCORE-15 (Stratton et al., 2014; Dutch translation Lemmens et al., 2015) measures the quality of family functioning across three dimensions: Strengths and Adaptability, Overwhelmed by Difficulties, and Disrupted Communication. The scale consists of 15 items rated on a 5-point scale indicating agreement with the given statements, ranging from 1 (‘completely’) to 5 (‘not at all’), with lower scores indicating better functioning. Internal consistency in the current sample was good, α = .80.

Demographic characteristics

As part of the ROM assessment, participants were asked to indicate their age and sex (male/female).

Data analysis

First, we assessed the normality of the EMIS-M items by inspecting values of skewness and kurtosis. Next, we computed inter-item and item-total correlations. To identify potential outliers, boxplots were generated; no outliers were detected. All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27).

Confirmatory factor analysis and reliability

We conducted a CFA to evaluate the factor structure of the EMIS-M. We tested a one-factor model, a two-factor model consisting of MI-self and MI-other, and the bifactor model as specified by Currier et al. (2018). CFA was performed in Mplus (version 8.6; Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017) using the weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator, which is suitable for Likert-type data. No EMIS-M data were missing. Error variances were specified as uncorrelated.

To assess the adequacy of model fit, we examined several fit indices: chi-square, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardised root mean square residual (SRMR). Guidelines from Hu and Bentler (1999) suggest acceptable fit thresholds for these indices (CFI and TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA ≤ 0.06, and SRMR ≤ 0.08). However, McNeish and Wolf (2023) caution against relying on fixed cut-offs, noting that these indices are unstandardised and their interpretation can vary depending on model characteristics. We therefore used an open-source, web-based tool (Wolf & McNeish, 2020) that calculates dynamic fit index cut-offs tailored to the specific properties of the model and data, to obtain dynamic cut-offs for the CFI and RMSEA. We evaluated the TLI and SRMR based on the Hu and Bentler (1999) guideline of TLI ≥ 0.95 and SRMR ≤ 0.08. Additionally, we conducted the CFA using maximum likelihood estimation to compute the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better model fit. After completing the CFA, we assessed the internal consistency of the scale and emerging subscales using Cronbach’s alpha and evaluated the temporal stability of the items using Pearson correlation coefficients.

Latent profile analysis

Next, we conducted an LPA in Mplus (version 8.6; Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017) using emerging EMIS-M and PCL-5 subscales to identify profiles that showed similar patterns of PTSD and MI indicators. No data were missing. To ensure comparability across instruments, all scores were standardised into z-scores. We estimated models with one to six latent profiles and compared model fit indices to determine the most parsimonious and best-fitting solution. Additional latent profiles were added until profile sizes dropped below 10% of the total sample and improvements in fit became negligible. Theoretical interpretability and clinical relevance were also considered when selecting the optimal model. To avoid local likelihood maxima, we requested 500 random sets of starting values in the first step and 50 in the second step of optimisation. We also specified 50 initial stage iterations.

To evaluate model fit, we considered the BIC, the Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), and the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT). Lower BIC values suggest better model fit, while significant p-values for the BLRT and LMR-LRT indicate that including an additional profile significantly improves model fit (Nylund et al., 2007). In addition, we assessed classification accuracy by examining entropy values and average latent profile assignment probabilities; for both, values closer to 1.00 reflect more precise classification (Geiser, 2013). Entropy values at or above 0.80 are considered indicative of acceptable classification accuracy (Celeux & Soromenho, 1996). Upon selecting the optimal solution, we examined the conditional response probabilities per profile to label the profiles. Additionally, we reviewed plots of item z-scores for the solutions.

Last, we conducted two independent distal outcome analyses using the BCH method, with the BSI and SCORE-15 respectively, to examine whether the profiles predicted scores on these outcome variables. Individuals with missing values on distal outcomes were excluded from the analyses but retained in the latent profile estimation, ensuring that the profile solution remained based on the full sample. For the BSI, 0.44% of data points were missing; for the SCORE-15, 10.93%.

The one-factor model showed poor fit as the CFI and RMSEA did not meet the dynamic fit thresholds and only the SRMR met the recommended cut-off value. The two-factor model showed relatively acceptable model fit; the CFI and RMSEA met the dynamic fit indices for a mediocre fitting model and the SRMR indicated good fit. However, the TLI was still below the recommended value of ≥ 0.95 (Hu & Bentler, 1999). Inspection of the modification indices indicated that this was primarily driven by residual covariance between specific item pairs (items 1 and 7; 6 and 13; 8 and 14). As freeing residual covariances without theoretical justification complicates model interpretation and the potential for replication (Hermida, 2015), we retained the original factor structure. Notably, the SRMR was within acceptable limits, suggesting that the overall pattern of covariances is reasonably well reproduced by the model. The average total score for the MI-self subscale was 23.47 (SD = 8.22) and for the MI-other subscale was 25.15 (SD = 7.64).

Reliability

The EMIS-M’ internal consistency was excellent, with a Cronbach’s α of .92. For the two-factor model, the internal consistency was α = .89 (factor 1, MI-self) and α = .88 (factor 2, MI-other). To assess the temporal stability, we evaluated Pearson's correlation coefficients of the total scale and subscales. These were high, with coefficients of .82, .81 and .82 for the total scale, MI-self subscale and MI-other subscale, respectively (see Table 2). The mean interval between the first and second administration of the EMIS-M was 14.44 (SD = 1.94) days.

Latent profile analysis

Model fit indices for the latent profile analysis are presented in Table 4. The AIC and BIC were lowest for the six-profile solution, indicating better fit. Entropy values were acceptable for the three-, five-, and six-profile solutions, suggesting adequate classification quality. The LMR-LRT supported the three-profile solution, while the BLRT yielded significant p-values across all solutions. Despite some indices favouring the five- and six-profile models, both included profiles representing less than 10% of the sample, which may reflect sample-specific noise rather than meaningful profiles, limiting replicability and interpretability. We therefore inspected the item z-score plots to inform model selection. Discussion

The main objective of this study was to examine the extent to which Dutch treatment-seeking veterans experience MI. To do so, we first validated the EMIS-M in a clinical sample of 455 Dutch veterans, testing its internal consistency, temporal stability and factor structure. Secondly, we identified profiles of PTSD and MI outcomes and examined if these profiles predicted comorbid psychopathology and family functioning.

Psychometric properties of the EMIS-M

As hypothesised, the EMIS-M and its subscales showed excellent internal consistency. The temporal stability of the total scale and subscales was high over a two-week period in a subsample of 25 veterans. In the original study (Currier et al., 2018) test-retest reliability was favourable over a six-month period, indicating the EMIS-M has good temporal stability over shorter and longer intervals. Inter-item correlations were predominantly in the acceptable range.

Factor structure

We conducted CFA to evaluate the factor structure of the EMIS-M, using dynamic fit indices tailored to our sample. We tested a one-factor model and a two-factor model consisting of MI-self and MI-other, showing poor and mediocre fit respectively. Inspection of modification indices suggested that the mediocre fit of the two-factor model was attributable to residual covariance between specific item pairs rather than a fundamental misspecification of the factor structure. Due to inadequate power (i.e. a small sample size relative to the complexity of the model), we were unable to recover a stable bifactor solution. Given prior evidence suggesting that the EMIS-M may be better represented by a bifactor structure rather than a two-factor model (Morriss & Berle, 2023; Tappenden et al., 2024), a bifactor model possibly would have provided superior fit, accounting for variance not captured by the two-factor solution.

Symptom profiles, comorbid psychopathology and family functioning

Using LPA, we identified three latent profiles of PTSD and MI outcomes among treatment-seeking veterans. These profiles consisted of ‘Subclinical MI and PTSD’ (20.9%), ‘Moderate MI and PTSD’ (47.9%), and ‘High MI and PTSD’ (31.2%), all showing relatively parallel levels of PTSD and MI outcomes. Symptom levels showed that even veterans in the ‘Subclinical MI and PTSD’ profile endorsed PTSD and MI outcomes, although generally below clinical thresholds. Contrary to expectations, we did not find profiles characterised by high levels of MI alongside low levels of PTSD, or vice versa.

Previous research provides potential explanations for why we failed to find profiles with greater differentiation between PTSD and MI, or an MI-only profile. Mensink et al. (2022), for example, found that treatment-seeking veterans were most likely to be in the combined MI and PTSD subgroup, whereas the MI-only subgroup consisted mostly of police officers. Similarly, the LPA by Currier et al. (2021) among a clinical veteran sample, found two subgroups characterised by parallel levels of MI and (C)PTSD symptom severity. Studies employing LCA/LPA among police officers and refugees have also found subgroups with more differentiation between PTSD and MI (Mooren, Boelen, et al., 2024; Mooren, de la Rie, et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest that MI potentially more frequently co-occurs with PTSD in treatment-seeking veterans than in other populations. Prior research in the Netherlands has also demonstrated that treatment-seeking veterans report high levels of PMIE exposure (92%) and PMIE-related distress (86%; De Goede et al., manuscript submitted for publication). This suggests that MI may be more present among Dutch veterans than previously assumed, or that the moral burden of deployment experiences may make veterans more likely to seek out treatment. Taken together, these findings underscore the clinical relevance of MI in trauma-exposed, treatment-seeking Dutch veterans.

We also examined whether the three profiles predicted comorbid psychopathology and family functioning. As hypothesised, veterans in the ‘Moderate MI and PTSD’ profile reported significantly higher levels of comorbid psychopathology than those in the ‘Subclinical MI and PTSD’ profile; veterans in the ‘High MI and PTSD’ profile, in turn, reported significantly higher levels of comorbid psychopathology than both the ‘Subclinical MI and PTSD’ and ‘Moderate MI and PTSD’ profiles. This is in line with prior studies (e.g. Mensink et al., 2022; Mooren, de la Rie, et al., 2024).

With respect to family functioning, and in line with expectations, the ‘Subclinical MI and PTSD’ profile reported significantly better outcomes than the ‘Moderate MI and PTSD’ and ‘High MI and PTSD’ profiles. Although the ‘High MI and PTSD’ profile reported poorer family functioning than the ‘Moderate MI and PTSD’ profile, the difference was not significant. These findings suggest that family functioning problems warrant clinical attention whenever veterans experience clinically significant PTSD or MI symptoms, regardless of severity. The results align with preliminary research showing that MI often co-occurs with interpersonal difficulties that may affect family life, including anger problems, reduced empathy, feeling undeserving of a loving family, and social withdrawal (Litz et al., 2022; Williamson et al., 2020, 2023).

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to examine the presence of clinically significant MI among Dutch veterans. Moreover, it is among the first to apply LPA in an exclusively veteran population to identify clinical profiles, thereby offering insights that may guide treatment needs and clarify the proportion of treatment-seeking veterans who could benefit from MI interventions. The study also adds to the emerging literature on MI and family functioning. In addition, the study employed dynamic fit indices to evaluate the factor structure of the instrument–an innovative method for assessing model fit. Finally, the collaboration among four psychotrauma centres across the Netherlands ensured that the sample is representative of Dutch treatment-seeking veterans, enhancing the generalizability and clinical relevance of the findings.

Several limitations concern the analyses conducted in this study. The two-factor model showed suboptimal fit, which may limit the interpretation of the LPA results. Although modification indices suggested that model misfit was attributable to residual covariance between specific item pairs, some influence of this misfit on the profile solution cannot be ruled out. Relatedly, insufficient statistical power prevented us from examining the bifactor model, for which prior studies found some support (Currier et al., 2018; Morriss & Berle, 2023; Tappenden et al., 2024) and may have fit better. Additionally, LPA is an exploratory technique and the three-profile solution identified in this study should be considered tentative; replication in independent samples is needed to verify both the number and the nature of the profiles.

The use of the EMIS-M also has several limitations. The EMIS-M does not anchor expressions of MI to a PMIE; instead, it instructs the respondent to think of things that they did or saw in the military and does not specify a time-period. We also did not separately assess if participants experienced a PMIE; consequently, we were unable to determine if elevated scores reflected responses to a PMIE, a PTSD criterion-A event, or neither. The EMIS-M has also been criticised for containing items that capture both exposure and responses to events, and for failing to assess the functional impact of endorsed items (Litz et al., 2022; Morriss & Berle, 2023). As a result, elevated scores may reflect moral distress but could also partly capture broader post-traumatic suffering. This ambiguity is compounded by the conceptual overlap between MI and PTSD, reflected in several EMIS-M items (e.g. item 15, concerning loss of faith in humanity). It is therefore possible that the strong co-occurrence of MI and PTSD outcomes in our study was partly due to these measurement characteristics rather than reflecting their true co-occurrence in this population. Newer MI measures, such as the Moral Injury Outcome Scale (Litz et al., 2022), circumvent some of the issues, but were not yet available at the time the study commenced.

As a final limitation, deployment locations were not recorded as part of the ROM battery. We were therefore unable to distinguish between veterans who served in peacekeeping missions and those deployed to more combat-oriented operations (e.g. veterans who served in UN peacekeeping missions such as in the former Yugoslavia in the period 1996–2003 versus in Iraq in 2003–2012). Given emerging evidence that the meaning and impact of PMIEs may differ by operational context (Nordstrand et al., 2025), it would be informative for future research to examine whether MI profiles differ as a function of mission type.

Potential implications

The findings demonstrate that MI frequently co-occurs with PTSD among Dutch treatment-seeking veterans and that higher levels of both are associated with greater overall psychopathology. These results highlight the importance of carefully assessing treatment priorities within this group, including routine screening for exposure to PMIEs and morally injurious outcomes alongside PTSD. Clinically, the findings underscore the need to attend to veterans’ specific symptom profiles and help-seeking needs. For veterans whose PTSD symptoms co-occur with moral emotions such as guilt, shame, or anger, it may be helpful to consider interventions that target moral distress when formulating a treatment plan. Although preliminary evidence suggests that MI-focused interventions can reduce morally injurious outcomes (Aita et al., 2023), it is important to note that relatively little research has examined the extent to which standard TFT reduce MI or how PTSD and MI change concurrently over the course of treatment (Held et al., 2018).

Our findings also underscore the importance of addressing family functioning and engaging family members who may struggle to understand or support their veteran spouse or parent. Close relatives have reported being highly impacted by veterans’ moral distress, sometimes internalising the reasons for their withdrawal (Williamson et al., 2023). Both clinicians and veterans in the study by Williamson et al. (2023) described family-related problems as cyclical, with withdrawal from one’s family exacerbating psychological suffering over time. Moreover, morally injured veterans expressed concerns about transmitting their psychological problems to their children (Williamson et al., 2020). Current treatments for MI may not sufficiently address these relational dimensions of functioning, highlighting the need to systematically address family functioning in treatment. In addition, spouses and children may require treatment too. As cost-effective, low-threshold online interventions have been developed for MI (e.g. ter Heide et al., 2026), similar interventions could be developed to provide psychoeducation and support for family members.

Finally, the study points to directions for future research. Although the scope of the current study did not allow for an examination of the unique contribution of MI to psychopathology and family functioning beyond PTSD, this would be a valuable avenue for future research. Additionally, given the strong co-occurrence and parallel severity of PTSD and MI observed in this study, an important unanswered question is whether MI outcomes decrease during evidence-based trauma focused treatment (TFT) that primarily targets PTSD. Few studies have examined improvements in MI outcomes after TFT using validated MI measures. Longitudinal research assessing both PTSD and MI over the course of treatment is therefore needed to clarify whether current interventions sufficiently address moral suffering. Such work would provide essential guidance for optimising treatments and tailoring care to veterans’ full spectrum of post-traumatic difficulties.

Conclusion

This study examined the psychometric properties of the Dutch EMIS-M and the extent to which Dutch treatment-seeking veterans experience MI. The psychometric properties of the EMIS-M were favourable. Although newer instruments have been developed to address limitations of the EMIS-M, our findings indicate that the scale remains an acceptable measure for clinical use. LPA identified three clinical profiles – Subclinical, Moderate, and High MI and PTSD – showing that MI and PTSD tend to co-occur at parallel levels rather than in isolation in Dutch treatment-seeking veterans. Veterans with higher levels of both conditions reported significantly greater comorbid psychopathology and poorer family functioning, underscoring the additive burden of these difficulties. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of MI in this population and suggest that careful assessment of moral suffering alongside PTSD may be warranted, while further research is needed to clarify how MI responds to trauma-focused treatments.
