Thoracic and retroperitoneal lymphatic injuries following spine surgery: illustrative cases
Authors: Thiago P Rodrigues, Mariana A S Rodrigues, Katherine Jensen, Clayton L Rosinski, Mani Ratnesh S Sandhu, Satoshi Yamaguchi, Mauricio Amoedo, Matthew A Howard III, Patrick Hitchon

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDPostoperative lymphatic leakage is a rare complication of spine surgery caused by injury to the thoracic duct, cisterna chyli, or retroperitoneal lymphatic channels. Because of its rarity, diagnosis is often delayed and optimal management remains poorly defined. Lymphatic leakage is an uncommon complication of spine surgery resulting from injury to the thoracic duct, cisterna chyli, or retroperitoneal lymphatic vessels.1,2 Persistent lymphatic fistula may lead to nutritional depletion, immunosuppression, electrolyte abnormalities, delayed wound healing, prolonged hospitalization, and the need for additional invasive interventions.3 Because of the rarity of this complication, the true incidence of postoperative lymphatic injury in spine surgery remains poorly defined, with the current literature consisting predominantly of isolated case reports and small case series.

Most reported cases of lymphatic injury in spine surgery have followed anterior cervical approaches, particularly left-sided exposures, because of the anatomical course and termination of the thoracic duct at the left venous angle.1 Chylous leakage has also been described after thoracic and lumbar procedures, including anterior and lateral lumbar approaches involving the retroperitoneal space and the vicinity of the cisterna chyli and major lymphatic tributaries.4 In contrast, lymphatic injury following posterior thoracic spine surgery appears to be exceptionally uncommon.3 Anatomical variability of the thoracic duct and cisterna chyli may further increase susceptibility to iatrogenic injury during complex spinal exposures.

The diagnosis of postoperative lymphatic leakage is typically suspected in the setting of persistent milky drainage and may be confirmed by biochemical analysis demonstrating elevated triglyceride levels within the collected fluid.1,3,5 Initial treatment generally consists of drainage, dietary modification with restriction of long-chain triglycerides, total parenteral nutrition, and pharmacological adjuncts such as octreotide.1,3 However, persistent high-output leakage may require more invasive management strategies, including lymphangiography with embolization, thoracic duct ligation, or surgical exploration. In recent years, intranodal lymphangiography has emerged as both a diagnostic and therapeutic modality, allowing localization of the leak and, in selected cases, definitive treatment through embolization.6,7

Here, we present 3 cases of postoperative lymphatic leakage following spine surgery involving distinct anatomical mechanisms: a thoracic lymphatic injury after posterior T2–3 corpectomy, a right retroperitoneal approach to the lumbar spine, and a retroperitoneal lymphatic injury after multilevel oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF). We discuss the diagnostic workup, anatomical considerations, and multidisciplinary management strategies used in these rare complications.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 66-year-old man with no significant past medical history presented with 45 days of spontaneous bilateral shoulder pain followed by 10 days of circumferential sensory loss below the nipple line and progressive gait imbalance. Neurological examination demonstrated bilateral patellar and Achilles hyperreflexia and a sensory level at T4. CT and MRI revealed a destructive mass involving the T2–3 vertebral levels with severe spinal cord compression (Fig. 1).

Despite complete opacification of the thoracic duct, no discrete site of contrast extravasation was identified.

Systemic imaging demonstrated prominent left supraclavicular lymphadenopathy and a nearly occlusive inferior vena cava thrombus. Therapeutic anticoagulation was initiated, and ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration of the supraclavicular lymph nodes was performed. Cytopathological analysis demonstrated metastatic adenocarcinoma with positive CK7/CK20 staining and loss of BAP1 nuclear expression, findings concerning for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.

The patient underwent posterior cervicothoracic decompression and instrumented fusion through a midline approach with T2–3 corpectomy. T1–3 laminectomies with bilateral T1–2 and T2–3 facetectomies were performed. Right-sided costotransversectomies at T2 and T3 were then completed. During resection of the T3 rib head, a white milky fluid was noted within the operative field, raising concern for lymphatic injury versus pleural effusion due to violation of the parietal pleura. However, the precise origin of the fluid could not be identified intraoperatively, and the area was packed with absorbable gelatin sponge and fibrin sealant. Tumor resection and piecemeal T2–3 corpectomy were subsequently performed, followed by placement of an expandable cage for anterior column support. Instrumentation consisted of C5–6 lateral mass screws and T1, T3 (just on the left side), T4, T5, and T6 pedicle screws connected with bilateral rods (Fig. 1).

Although a possible lymphatic injury was suspected intraoperatively, no persistent leak was identified during the index procedure, and the patient remained clinically stable throughout the initial postoperative period, with postoperative chest radiographic imaging not demonstrating any pleural effusion. Given the absence of wound complications or respiratory symptoms, he was discharged on postoperative day 4 with plans for stereotactic radiosurgery. Three weeks later, he returned to the emergency department with cervicothoracic wound dehiscence. Chest radiographic imaging and CT demonstrated a moderate right pleural effusion. Surgical wound exploration and debridement were performed. Intraoperatively, we observed a large volume of chylous fluid that was in communication with the pleural cavity. Extensive exploration of the operative corridor, including the region of the previously resected ribs, failed to identify an active site of chylous extravasation. The wound was copiously irrigated and closed in standard fashion.

Serial chest CT scans subsequently demonstrated progressive enlargement of the right pleural effusion, prompting ­ultrasound-guided chest tube placement. Milky pleural fluid was obtained and the pleural fluid triglyceride concentration was 427 mg/dL, consistent with chylothorax. Because of persistent high-output drainage, the patient underwent intranodal lymphangiography. Under ultrasound guidance, the bilateral inguinal lymph nodes were accessed using 22-gauge needles, and Lipiodol contrast was slowly injected under fluoroscopic guidance. The cisterna chyli was subsequently visualized and directly accessed using a 21-gauge needle, allowing cannulation of the thoracic duct with a guidewire and microcatheter system. Although no discrete site of leakage was identified, thoracic duct embolization was performed using coils and n-butyl ­cyanoacrylate glue based on the overall clinical and radiographic findings (Fig. 1).

Despite embolization, chest tube output persisted at approximately 200 mL/day, and the patient subsequently underwent right thoracoscopy. During thoracoscopic exploration, the parietal pleura adjacent to the azygos vein was opened, and the suspected region of lymphatic leakage was circumferentially ligated using a Harken clamp-assisted technique. Talc pleurodesis was then performed, and a new chest tube was placed. Following thoracoscopy, chest tube output progressively decreased, and the patient was ultimately discharged from the hospital. At outpatient follow-up, no recurrence of pleural effusion or wound drainage was observed.

Case 2

A 75-year-old man presented with severe axial low back pain and neurogenic claudication that had progressively worsened over the preceding 2 years. His axial pain was refractory to conservative treatment, including epidural steroid injections and analgesic ­medications. Neurological examination demonstrated mildly diminished bilateral patellar and Achilles tendon reflexes. Lumbar MRI revealed multilevel degenerative spondylosis with severe central canal stenosis (Fig. 2). His recent medical history was significant for ischemic colitis requiring a left colectomy with the creation of a left-sided colostomy.

The patient was scheduled to undergo a staged procedure consisting of a right-sided OLIF, followed by posterior decompression and instrumented fusion. During the anterior retroperitoneal exposure performed by the vascular surgery team, a laceration of the right common iliac vein resulted in massive intraoperative hemorrhage. The spinal portion of the procedure was aborted, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for postoperative management.

Twenty days later, the patient developed a fluid collection within the surgical bed. CT-guided percutaneous drainage was performed, and fluid analysis demonstrated a triglyceride concentration of 288 mg/dL, consistent with a postoperative lymphatic leak.

The patient was managed conservatively with a low-fat diet enriched with medium-chain triglycerides. Drain output progressively decreased, allowing drain removal after 10 days. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no evidence of recurrent fluid collection.

Case 3

A 70-year-old woman presented with progressive low back pain refractory to medical management and epidural steroid injections. The pain had become severe enough to significantly impair ambulation. Standing radiographs demonstrated lumbar dextroscoliosis (Fig. 3), and CT and MRI studies revealed multilevel degenerative lumbar disease with associated deformity.

Case 3. Preoperative standing anteroposterior (A) and lateral (C) radiographs demonstrating degenerative lumbar dextroscoliosis. Postoperative standing anteroposterior (B) and lateral (D) radiographs demonstrating deformity correction and instrumented fusion from T10 to the pelvis following multilevel OLIF and posterior fixation.

The patient underwent staged deformity correction. First, L2–3, L3–4, and L4–5 OLIF procedures were performed through a left-sided anterolateral retroperitoneal approach by the vascular surgery team. On the following day, posterior deformity correction and instrumentation were completed through a posterior midline approach with T10–S2 alar-iliac instrumentation (Fig. 3). No intraoperative evidence of chylous leakage was identified during either procedure.

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 6 after an otherwise unremarkable postoperative course. However, she returned to the emergency department on postoperative day 10 complaining of progressive abdominal pain and abdominal fullness. Abdominal CT demonstrated a large left retroperitoneal fluid collection. Ultrasound-guided placement of a 10-Fr pigtail drainage catheter was subsequently performed.

Laboratory analysis of the drained fluid demonstrated a triglyceride concentration of 157 mg/dL with the presence of chylomicrons, consistent with lymphatic leakage. The patient was started on a low-fat diet and underwent intranodal lymphangiography. Under ultrasound guidance, bilateral inguinal lymph nodes were accessed using 22-gauge needles, followed by slow Lipiodol injection under fluoroscopic guidance. Imaging demonstrated a left paraspinal retroperitoneal lymphatic leak, which was successfully embolized using n-butyl cyanoacrylate glue (Fig. 4).

Following embolization, drain output progressively decreased, and the catheter was removed after 7 days. The patient was subsequently discharged and continues outpatient follow-up without evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Observations

The present report highlights distinct anatomical patterns of postoperative lymphatic injury following spine surgery: thoracic lymphatic leakage after posterior upper thoracic corpectomy and retroperitoneal lymphatic leakage after multilevel OLIF. Although uncommon, both complications are anatomically plausible based on the relationship between the surgical corridors and major lymphatic structures. In the first case, extensive right-sided costotransversectomy and rib head resection at the upper thoracic level likely resulted in injury to mediastinal lymphatic channels or thoracic duct tributaries near the costovertebral junction. In the second and third cases, the retroperitoneal exposure used for OLIF traversed the anatomical region of the cisterna chyli and lumbar lymphatic tributaries, creating risk for lymphatic disruption. Together, these cases demonstrate the broad spectrum of lymphatic complications that may occur during modern spinal approaches and emphasize the importance of early recognition and multidisciplinary management.

The thoracic duct originates from the cisterna chyli, typically located anterior to the L1–2 vertebral bodies, and ascends through the posterior mediastinum before crossing from right to left near the mid-thoracic region and terminating at the left venous angle. However, substantial anatomical variability exists regarding the origin, number of tributaries, and crossing pattern of the thoracic duct. Such variability may increase susceptibility to iatrogenic injury during thoracic and retroperitoneal spinal procedures.8 Previous reports of spine surgery–related chylous leakage have most commonly involved anterior cervical approaches, particularly left-sided exposures, because of the close relationship between the thoracic duct and the lower cervical surgical corridor. In contrast, posterior thoracic lymphatic injury remains exceptionally uncommon in the literature.9

In our first patient, delayed postoperative chylothorax developed after right T2 and T3 costotransversectomy and rib head resection. Although no discrete leak point was identified either intraoperatively or during lymphangiography, several findings strongly supported the diagnosis of thoracic lymphatic injury, including the intraoperative observation of milky fluid, development of delayed pleural effusion, elevated pleural fluid triglyceride concentration, and partial response to thoracic duct embolization. Extensive lateral dissection near the costovertebral junction and mediastinal pleura likely resulted in injury to thoracic duct tributaries or adjacent mediastinal lymphatic channels. The delayed presentation observed in this case is consistent with previous reports describing progressive postoperative accumulation of chylous fluid before clinical manifestation becomes evident. When lymphatic injury is suspected intraoperatively, meticulous inspection of the operative field should be performed to identify the site of leakage whenever feasible. Maneuvers to increase lymphatic flow, such as placing the patient in the Trendelenburg position and applying positive-pressure ventilation, may facilitate identification of the leak. If the injured lymphatic channel can be localized, direct suture ligation or clipping is recommended.1 If the precise source cannot be identified, as in our first case, application of fibrin sealant and hemostatic agents, avoidance of excessive dead space, and placement of a drain when clinically appropriate may help limit postoperative accumulation while allowing early recognition of a persistent leak. Close postoperative surveillance is warranted, as delayed chylous collections may develop despite the absence of an obvious intraoperative leak.

Retroperitoneal lymphatic injury after OLIF is also uncommon but anatomically understandable. The retroperitoneal corridor used during lateral and oblique lumbar approaches traverses the region containing lumbar lymphatic trunks and the cisterna chyli. The standard OLIF approach is typically performed from the left side to avoid manipulation of the inferior vena cava and to circumvent the liver, which may limit right-sided access at upper lumbar levels. In our second patient, a right-sided retroperitoneal approach was selected because of his history of left colectomy with the creation of a left-sided colostomy, thereby avoiding the adhesions associated with the prior abdominal surgery. Although vascular, neurological, and visceral complications of OLIF are widely discussed, lymphatic injury remains comparatively underrecognized.10 In our third patient, postoperative abdominal pain and fullness prompted imaging that demonstrated a large retroperitoneal fluid collection. The diagnosis was established through drainage and biochemical analysis demonstrating the presence of chylomicrons within the collected fluid. Unlike the first case, lymphangiography successfully localized the site of leakage, which was located inferior to the cisterna chyli, and permitted definitive embolization with glue, resulting in gradual resolution of the collection without the need for surgical exploration. Interestingly, the retroperitoneal fluid demonstrated a relatively modest triglyceride concentration (157 mg/dL) despite the presence of chylomicrons. Because chylomicrons are produced by intestinal enterocytes and transported through the intestinal lymphatic system to the cisterna chyli, their detection suggests communication with intestinal lymphatic drainage. This finding may be explained by the considerable anatomical variability of the retroperitoneal lymphatic system, which contains numerous collateral channels and variable connections between the intestinal and lumbar lymphatic trunks. Consequently, lymphatic injuries that appear anatomically distal to the cisterna chyli may still contain chylomicron-rich lymph, while dilution within the retroperitoneal collection may account for the relatively low triglyceride concentration observed in our patient.11

Diagnosis of postoperative lymphatic leakage may be challenging because symptoms are frequently nonspecific during the initial postoperative period. Persistent milky drainage, enlarging pleural effusion, abdominal distension, or unexplained retroperitoneal collections should raise suspicion for lymphatic injury. Biochemical confirmation is generally based on elevated triglyceride concentration and/or the presence of chylomicrons within the collected fluid. Cross-sectional imaging is useful to characterize associated collections and monitor progression over time.12

Management strategies vary according to the anatomical location and severity of the leak. Initial conservative measures typically include drainage, low-fat diet with restriction of long-chain triglycerides, total parenteral nutrition, and pharmacological adjuncts such as octreotide.13 However, persistent or symptomatic leaks may require invasive intervention. In recent years, intranodal lymphangiography has emerged as both a diagnostic and therapeutic modality. Lipiodol injection may itself promote spontaneous sealing of lymphatic leaks, while selective embolization using coils or n-butyl cyanoacrylate glue permits minimally invasive definitive treatment in selected cases.14

In our series, 2 patients underwent lymphangiography, highlighting its important role in the contemporary management of postoperative lymphatic complications. Nevertheless, the first patient ultimately required thoracoscopic intervention despite technically successful thoracic duct embolization. Thoracoscopic exploration permitted mass ligation of the suspected thoracic duct region adjacent to the azygos vein and additional talc pleurodesis, resulting in progressive reduction of chest tube output and eventual resolution of the chylothorax. These findings emphasize that refractory thoracic lymphatic leaks may still require surgical management despite advances in interventional radiology techniques.

Although rare, lymphatic injury should remain within the differential diagnosis of postoperative pleural or retroperitoneal fluid collections following thoracic and lateral lumbar spinal procedures. Early recognition, biochemical confirmation, and prompt multidisciplinary treatment involving spine surgery, interventional radiology, and thoracic surgery are essential to minimize morbidity and achieve successful resolution.

Proposed Management Algorithm for Postoperative Lymphatic Leakage Following Spine Surgery

Based on the available literature and our experience, we propose a practical management algorithm for postoperative lymphatic leakage following spine surgery (Fig. 5). Early recognition remains critical, as clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and may be delayed. Persistent milky wound drainage, enlarging pleural effusion, abdominal distension, or unexplained retroperitoneal fluid collections following thoracic, anterior lumbar, lateral lumbar, or oblique lumbar approaches should prompt consideration of lymphatic injury.3

Initial evaluation should include cross-sectional imaging and biochemical analysis of the collected fluid. The diagnosis is typically established by demonstrating elevated triglyceride concentrations and/or the presence of chylomicrons. Although triglyceride thresholds have been primarily derived from studies of chylothorax, the detection of chylomicrons may be particularly valuable in atypical retroperitoneal collections. Once the diagnosis is confirmed, initial management generally consists of drainage, dietary modification with restriction of long-chain triglycerides, nutritional support, and consideration of pharmacological adjuncts such as octreotide.15

Patients demonstrating clinical improvement and progressively decreasing drainage output may be managed conservatively with close observation. However, persistent high-output leakage, enlarging collections, nutritional compromise, respiratory symptoms, or failure of conservative measures should prompt escalation to advanced lymphatic imaging and intervention. Intranodal lymphangiography has emerged as the preferred next step because it serves both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, allowing localization of the leak and, in many cases, definitive treatment through embolization with coils or n-butyl cyanoacrylate glue.16 MR lymphangiography may serve as a complementary imaging modality in selected cases, particularly when detailed characterization of lymphatic anatomy is desired before intervention.17

When embolization is unsuccessful or when clinically significant leakage persists despite interventional treatment, surgical management should be considered. Thoracoscopic ligation of the thoracic duct or the suspected region of leakage, with or without pleurodesis, remains an effective option for refractory chylothorax. In contrast, retroperitoneal lymphatic leaks can frequently be managed successfully with percutaneous drainage and lymphatic embolization, making surgical exploration rarely necessary.14 A multidisciplinary approach involving spine surgery, interventional radiology, thoracic surgery, and nutritional support is essential to optimize outcomes and minimize the morbidity associated with these uncommon complications.

Lessons

Postoperative lymphatic leakage is a rare but important complication of spine surgery that may occur following both thoracic and retroperitoneal surgical approaches. The present cases illustrate two distinct anatomical patterns of lymphatic injury: delayed chylothorax after posterior upper thoracic corpectomy and retroperitoneal lymphatic leakage after multilevel OLIF. Because clinical presentation may be delayed and nonspecific, a high index of suspicion is necessary when postoperative pleural effusion, milky drainage, abdominal distension, or unexplained fluid collections develop after complex spinal procedures.

Lymphangiography plays a central role in both diagnosis and treatment and may allow definitive minimally invasive management through embolization. However, persistent thoracic lymphatic leakage may still require surgical intervention such as thoracoscopic ligation and pleurodesis. Early multidisciplinary management involving spine surgery, interventional radiology, and thoracic surgery is essential to reduce morbidity and achieve successful resolution of these uncommon complications.
