Stack Trace-Based Crash Deduplication with Transformer Adaptation
Md Afif Al Mamun, Gias Uddin, Lan Xia, Longyu Zhang

Abstract
Automated crash reporting systems generate large volumes of duplicate reports, overwhelming issue-tracking systems and increasing developer workload. Traditional stack trace-based deduplication methods—relying on string similarity, rule-based heuristics, or deep learning (DL) models—often fail to capture the contextual and structural relationships within stack traces. We propose dedupT , a transformer-based approach that models stack traces holistically rather than as isolated frames. dedupT first adapts a pretrained language model (PLM) to stack traces, then uses its embeddings to train a fully-connected network (FCN) to rank duplicate crashes effectively. Extensive experiments on real-world datasets show that dedupT outperforms existing DL and traditional methods (e.g., sequence alignment and information retrieval techniques) in both duplicate ranking and unique crash detection, significantly reducing manual triage effort. On four public datasets, dedupT improves Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) often by over 15% compared to the best DL baseline and up to 9% over traditional methods while achieving higher Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the Curve (ROC-AUC) in detecting unique crash reports. Our work advances the integration of modern natural language processing (NLP) techniques into software engineering, providing an effective solution for stack trace-based crash deduplication.

Additionally, dedupT proved significantly more effective in identifying unique crashes, achieving an ROC-AUC improvement of over 22% compared to most alignment-based methods, while remaining competitive with other DL models.

II
Related Work

Sequence matching and information retrieval based techniques.
Stack trace-based crash deduplication has been fairly studied in the literature. Early approaches mostly address this as a string matching problem. Brodie et al.
[
4
]
proposed Needleman-Wunch based algorithm to compare stack traces. Modani et al.
[
20
]
explored different methods for comparing stack traces including prefix matching, edit distance, etc., along with an indexing strategy for efficient retrieval. Bartz et al.
[
21
]
used logistic regression, and Dhaliwal et al.
[
5
]
introduced a two-step approach: generating stack trace signatures followed by Levenshtein distance computation to enhance similarity detection. These methods primarily rely on lexical matching, which may struggle with structurally different yet semantically similar traces.

Many approaches also adopted TF-IDF to find similar crash reports
[
22
,
1
,
2
]
. Among these, Tracesim
[
23
]
is the first to combine TF-IDF, Levensthein distance, and machine learning (ML) to optimize hyperparameters. The same authors later proposed another version of Tracesim
[
2
]
that provides a pipeline to evaluate a lot of existing approaches, including
[
9
,
20
,
4
,
3
]
. This version of Tracesim also handled scenarios when multiple stack traces exist in a crash report. Later, Rodrigues et al. proposed FaST
[
24
]
, which works in linear time by aligning stack frames closer to the top, considering these are the most important frames. While FaST achieves higher throughput, its retrieval accuracy is similar to TraceSim.

Deep learning based approaches.
Deep learning (DL) has been comparatively underexplored in the domain of stack trace-based crash deduplication. One of the earliest applications of DL in this area is S3M
[
6
]
, which employs a bidirectional LSTM-based siamese network to learn similarity metrics between stack traces. Another notable approach, developed by Shi et al.
[
25
]
, utilizes a pre-trained BERT model
[
12
]
to classify Linux kernel crashes. DeepCrash
[
10
]
also incorporates bi-LSTM networks but differs from S3M in its methodology. While S3M generates representations for each stack frame individually, DeepCrash decomposes frames into subframes, generates representations at the subframe level, and then aggregates them hierarchically to form the final stack trace representation.

Although these DL-based methods have demonstrated performance improvements over some traditional approaches and reduce the need for manual feature engineering, they exhibit certain limitations. Both S3M and DeepCrash encounter out-of-vocabulary (OOV) issues, which arise when encountering tokens absent from their training data. Furthermore, DeepCrash employs a similarity threshold to cluster crashes into groups, requiring manual tuning of this parameter to optimize performance.

This study differs from existing methods by leveraging transformer-based models for stack trace-based crash deduplication. Unlike prior approaches, it considers the topmost stack frames collectively, leveraging contextual embeddings to enhance representation learning. Transformer models also employ advanced tokenization strategies, such as SentencePiece
[
26
]
, which mitigate OOV issues by processing subword units. Additionally, this research provides a systematic analysis of adapting general-purpose pre-trained models for crash deduplication, aiming to improve performance through contrastive fine-tuning and contextualized feature extraction.

[

algorithm, they have at least 2 mismatched frames (i.e, the first 2 frames are different subroutines) and 6 gaps (i.e., frames that can not be aligned), which would lead to a low similarity score. Similarly, methods that rely on the direct alignment of stack frames
[
2
]
or keyword matching
[
4
]
would assign a low similarity score to this pair, failing to recognize their underlying relationship. Similarly, deep learning (DL) models are also explored to capture the sequential relationships for STBD. Specifically, LSTM networks have been explored
[
6
,
10
]
to encode stack frames. However, LSTMs generally struggle with longer sequences in stack traces due to their limited memory capacity
[
27
]
. Stack traces often contain deeply nested call structures with functionally equivalent but lexically different paths, making it difficult for LSTMs to generalize across variations. For instance, two stack traces may exhibit different function names (e.g.,
ClassA.getUser()
vs.
ClassB.findUser()
) or variations in framework-level calls while originating from the same root cause. Such differences arise due to alternative execution paths, middleware abstractions, or reordered stack frames. Since LSTMs process sequences token by token, they are highly sensitive to lexical variations and lack an inherent understanding of structural similarities, making them less effective in deduplication.

Building on these findings, we evaluate transformer-based embedding models that use self-attention
[
11
]
to capture structural and semantic patterns in stack traces for duplicate detection. Specifically, we adapt a pre-trained SBERT model
[
19
]
to generate high-quality stack trace embeddings. Unlike traditional DL methods (e.g., LSTMs), SBERT captures contextual relationships, identifying duplicates despite lexical differences. To refine its ability to distinguish similar and dissimilar traces (i.e., adaptation), we fine-tune it using a contrastive learning
[
28
]
objective. As shown in Figure
3
, this adapted model assigns a high similarity score (0.889) to a duplicate pair while assigning a very low score to an unrelated trace (0.309). This highlights how transformers generalize beyond exact frame matching, learning meaningful execution flow patterns for automated stack trace deduplication.

III-B

Stack trace adaptation of embedding models

Fig. 4
:
Cosine distance of stack trace pairs before and after EM training on Netbeans dataset.

PLM-based embedding models (EMs) are typically trained on natural language (NL) corpora like MSMARCO
[
29
]
and WikiHow
[
30
]
, etc. However, stack traces follow a completely different structure with hierarchical syntax of method calls, class names, and file paths. General-purpose EMs may fail to capture fine-grained similarities in stack traces, which are important for tasks like duplicate crash detection. Small differences in file paths, method signatures, or exception types can signal distinct issues, while semantically identical stack traces may vary due to runtime factors.

Thus, separately fine-tuning or adapting an EM on stack traces enables the model to learn domain-specific representations that are better suited for similarity-based tasks like deduplication. Unlike generic PLMs, which may fail to capture the structural and semantic nuances of stack traces, a fine-tuned model can differentiate better between meaningful variations in execution paths, exception types, and method calls, etc.

We utilize contrastive learning to improve the model’s ability to distinguish between duplicate and non-duplicate stack traces. Contrastive learning optimizes the embedding space by pulling semantically similar stack traces closer together while pushing dissimilar ones apart. This ensures that stack traces belonging to the same bucket are mapped closer together while unrelated ones are separated. As illustrated in Figure
4
, the embedding distance between negative pairs (i.e., stack trace pairs that are not duplicates) increases significantly after fine-tuning the model on a studied dataset, indicating that the model learns a more discriminative embedding space, reducing false positives in duplicate detection. Additionally, when we fine-tune EM separately, it is possible to precompute embeddings for all stack traces and cache them for both faster training and retrieval.

IV-A

Stacktrace Preprocessor

Stack traces differ across programming languages. A typical Java stack trace (Figure
1
) begins with an exception message, followed by a sequence of stack frames that represent method calls, each containing details such as the class name, method name, and line number. In contrast, C++ stack traces—often obtained using tools like
backtrace
or debuggers such as
gdb
—usually provide memory addresses alongside function names (Figure
2
).

Following Rodrigues et al.
[
2
]
, we extract stack traces from crash reports using the Java stacktrace extractor from Lerch et al.
[
22
]
and
Parse::StackTrace
for C++. Since pretrained models impose a fixed input sequence length, long stack traces must be pruned to avoid exceeding this limit. Redundant frames consume valuable token space without adding meaningful information, which can degrade embedding quality. To address this, we design a four-step preprocessing pipeline, where only the
Frame Cleaning
stage differs between different languages to account for their structural differences.

Duplicate Removal.
We remove duplicate frames in a stack trace following the approach of Brodie et al.
[
4
]
, which eliminates consecutive frames with the same subroutine name under the same package or module. This is particularly helpful in cases of recursion and repeated library calls.

Fig. 6
:
Stacktrace Preprocessor.

Sampling Relevant Frames.
Prior studies show that the top stack frames are most informative for debugging
[
7
]
, whereas frames from standard libraries or deep recursion often add little value. Moreover, pretrained language models impose token length constraints, making it impractical to include full traces. To balance informativeness with efficiency, we retain only the top
N
frames. The value of
N
is determined empirically by selecting the cutoff that best preserves both the distinctive characteristics of a stack trace and its embedding similarity to related traces.

Frame Cleaning.
PLMs are primarily trained on natural language, so they may misinterpret stack-trace syntax. In Java traces, for example, dots (.) indicate package hierarchies rather than sentence boundaries, and in C++ traces, special characters or debugger-specific prefixes (e.g., IA, GI) are common. If left unprocessed, these symbols can be misinterpreted as sentence breaks or irrelevant tokens. To mitigate this, we replace dots and other special characters with whitespace, lowercase all frames, and treat each frame as a sentence, with the full trace as a passage. Following prior work
[
3
,
6
]
, we also evaluate different levels of trimming for Java stack traces and discuss their impact in Section
VI-D
.

Positional Coding.
Finally, each stack frame is assigned positional information to encode execution order, allowing the model to distinguish between frames and capture the sequential structure of function calls essential for recognizing stack trace patterns.

An example illustrating how stack traces are cleaned and preprocessed for both Java and C++ is shown in Figure
6
. While this work focuses on two popular statically typed languages—Java and C++, the approach should be easily extendable to other languages by adapting the frame cleaning stage to handle the syntax and conventions.

IV-B

Embedding Model Adaptation

The adaptation of the embedding model involves two stages – 1) Training Pair Generation and 2) Fine Tuning.

Training Pair Generation.
Since we have labeled crash datasets, we employ supervised fine-tuning for the embedding model adaptation using stack trace pairs. Let
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{k}\}
be the set of all crash buckets, where each bucket
B_{i}
contains stack traces corresponding to duplicate crash reports.

For each training sample, we define an
anchor

x_{a}\in B_{i}
as a reference stack trace. The data generation process involves constructing an equal number of positive and negative pairs with respect to the anchor:

•

Positive pairs
(
x_{a},x_{p}
): Both stack traces belong to the same bucket
B_{i}
, i.e.,
x_{a},x_{p}\in B_{i}
, indicating duplicate crashes.

•

Negative pairs
(
x_{a},x_{n}
): The stack traces belong to different buckets, i.e.,
x_{a}\in B_{i}
and
x_{n}\in B_{j}
when
i\neq j
, indicating non-duplicate crashes. Following S3M
[
6
]
, we construct negative pairs by sampling from a random subset of stack traces drawn from the top 50 buckets identified by Lerch and Mezini
[
22
]
, excluding the correct pair.

The objective of fine-tuning is to optimize the embedding model such that the anchor
x_{a}
is mapped closer to its positive counterpart
x_{p}
than to its negative counterpart
x_{n}
in the embedding space.

Fine-Tuning.
The model is fine-tuned/adapted using the positive and negative pairs generated in the earlier stage through a contrastive objective, Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (MNR)
[
31
]
, defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MNR}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp\left(\text{CosSim}(f_{\theta}(x_{a}^{i}),f_{\theta}(x_{p}^{i}))/\tau\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp\left(\text{CosSim}(f_{\theta}(x_{a}^{i}),f_{\theta}(x_{n}^{j}))/\tau\right)}

(1)

where,
N
is the batch size,
M
is the number of negatives in the batch (with
M=N-1
under no-duplicates sampling),
\tau
is a temperature scaling factor that controls the sharpness of similarity scores. This loss function encourages the model to maximize the similarity between the anchor (
x_{a}
) and the positive (
x_{p}
) while minimizing the similarity between the anchor (
x_{a}
) and the negatives (
x_{n}
). This whole adaptation process only use data from the training split.

IV-C

Feature Extraction

Once the embedding model is fine-tuned with stack traces, it can be used to extract meaningful features from them. A significant advantage of separate fine-tuning is that all precomputed stack trace embeddings can be cached or stored, enabling efficient retrieval and eliminating the need to repeatedly calculate embeddings during training. Since embedding generation is the most expensive part of the pipeline, this approach allows for faster classifier training while maintaining scalability.

However, a crash report
Q
may contain multiple stack traces (MST)
\{s_{1}^{Q},s_{2}^{Q},\dots,s_{n}^{Q}\}
, as noted in
[
2
]
. Similarly, a candidate report
S
may have multiple stack traces
\{s_{1}^{S},s_{2}^{S},\dots,s_{m}^{S}\}
. To compare the similarity of the reports
Q
and
S
, it is essential to consider the relationships between all their respective stack traces.

Challenges with Multiple Stack Traces.
A simple and efficient way to combine them is by concatenating all stack traces before embedding generation:
S_{\text{concat}}=\{s_{1}\oplus s_{2}\oplus\dots\oplus s_{n}\}
. However, this method is constrained by the embedding model’s maximum sequence length and is more preferable when frames are short (e.g., C++ subroutines). Since Java stack traces are often verbose and require a large number of tokens, this approach becomes impractical for Java-based crash reports. In such cases, another approach is to aggregate all stack trace embeddings using
mean pooling
, defined as:

\mu(QE)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(s_{i}^{Q}),\quad\mu(SE)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f(s_{j}^{S}),

(2)

where
f(s)
is the embedding of stack trace
s
, and
\mu(QE)
,
\mu(SE)
represent the aggregated embeddings for
Q
and
S
, respectively. However, mean pooling assumes equal contribution from all stack traces, ignoring their varying relevance and averaging over noise, which may dilute meaningful representations.

MST Aggregation.

To address these challenges, we propose a hybrid approach called Parametric Max-Mean that captures both the most relevant stack trace pairs (max pooling) and the context of all stack traces (mean pooling). First, we identify the most similar pair of stack traces,
(s_{i}^{Q},s_{j}^{S})
, with the fine-tuned embedding model using cosine similarity and construct features
f(s_{i}^{Q})
and
f(s_{j}^{S})
from their embedding.

(s_{i}^{Q},s_{j}^{S})=\arg\max_{i,j}\text{CosSim}(s_{i}^{Q},s_{j}^{S}).

(3)

Then, we compute the mean-pooled embeddings of all stack traces,
\mu(QE)
and
\mu(SE)
, which aggregate the embeddings of all stack traces from crash reports
Q
and
S
, respectively. To allow flexibility in determining the importance of these features, we introduce a trainable priority parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
. The final representation of stack traces for crash report
Q
is computed by concatenating both:

f_{Q}=\alpha\cdot f(s_{i}^{Q})\parallel(1-\alpha)\cdot\mu(QE)

(4)

This approach ensures that the model captures both the most relevant stack trace information and the broader context of all stack traces while allowing the importance of each component to be learned during training.

IV-D

Duplicate Classifier

Architecture.
The classifier is implemented as a fully connected network (FCN) designed to take feature vectors derived from two crash report stack traces and output a duplication score. Following previous approaches
[
32
,
6
]
, we construct the feature vector
f_{QS}
for the classifier using the following equation:

f_{QS}=\big(|f_{Q}-f_{S}|,\frac{f_{Q}+f_{S}}{2},f_{Q}\odot f_{S}\big),

(5)

where
f_{Q}\text{ and }f_{S}
are the representations of stack traces computed in Equation
4
. The rationale behind this feature construction is to capture a comprehensive representation of both the differences and similarities between the two embeddings. These features are then passed through an FCN defined in Equation
6
to calculate the duplication score.

\text{DupScore}=\Phi_{2}\left(\sigma\left(\Phi_{1}\left(\text{Dropout}(f_{QS})\right)\right)\right)

(6)

Where
\Phi_{i}
is linear layer
i
and
\sigma
is an activation function. The classifier includes a dropout
[
33
]
layer with a probability
p=0.1
to prevent overfitting, followed by two linear layers. The first linear layer reduces the feature dimension by half, followed by a ReLU activation, and the second linear layer produces the final score. This score is calculated for all candidate reports and the query report to rank the candidates.

Classifier Training.

Following S3M
[
6
]
, we train the classifier using RankNet
[
34
]
loss. We generate triplet pairs (
x_{a},x_{p},x_{n}
), where
x_{a}
is the anchor,
x_{p}
is a randomly selected stack trace from the same bucket (i.e., duplicate), and
x_{n}
is a non-duplicated stack trace selected following the process described in Section
IV-B
. Specifically, for each triplet, we compute the following RankNet loss:

L=\log(1+\exp(-\Delta f(x_{a},x_{p})+\Delta f(x_{a},x_{n})))

(7)

where
\Delta f(x_{a},x_{p})
and
\Delta f(x_{a},x_{n})
are the model’s predicted differences in rank between the anchor and the positive, and the anchor and the negative, respectively. The loss function minimizes the rank inconsistency by optimizing the model to predict that the positive sample is closer to the anchor than the negative sample.

The model is trained with a batch size of 25 with Adam optimizer
[
35
]
with a learning rate of
1e^{-}4
to minimize the RankNet loss. All the training and evaluation were done on Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6148 CPU@2.40GHz with an NVIDIA V100 16GB GPU, although the VRAM usage typically remained
<3\text{GB}
during training.

Embedding Model Evaluation

Model Selection.
Choosing an embedding model is critical, as performance largely depends on the training corpora, with domain-specific models typically outperforming general-purpose ones
[
43
]
. Since no embedding model exists for stack traces, we adapt a PLM to this domain. Section
VI-D
shows that our fine-tuning approach achieves strong performance regardless of the underlying PLM. We select a few candidate models from the HuggingFace repository that can be used with the Sentence Transformer library. These models are: all-mpnet-v2 (mpnet)
[
44
]
, all-distilroberta-v1 (distilroberta)
[
45
]
, bge-base-en (bge)
[
46
]
, etc. However, for most experiments of this paper, we selected
bge
as our preferred embedding model due to its slightly better performance on our evaluation.

Evaluation Metrics.
While the end goal of this work is crash deduplication using stack traces, a significant portion of the approach involves tuning and evaluating the embedding models. This evaluation is an essential part of our method, as the quality of the embeddings directly impacts the effectiveness of similarity-based deduplication. Below, we describe the evaluation process used to assess the embedding model’s performance.

To quantify the similarity, we compute the cosine similarity of embeddings, defined as:

\text{CosSim}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{\langle f(x_{1}),f(x_{2})\rangle}{\|f(x_{1})\|\,\|f(x_{2})\|}

(8)

where
f(x)
denotes the embedding of stack trace
x
.

We then evaluate the correlation between the predicted similarity scores and the ground-truth labels (i.e., positive (1)/negative (0)) using Pearson and Spearman cosine correlation coefficients:

r_{\text{Pearson}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\text{sim}_{i}-\bar{\text{sim}})(\text{gt}_{i}-\bar{\text{gt}})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\text{sim}_{i}-\bar{\text{sim}})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\text{gt}_{i}-\bar{\text{gt}})^{2}}}

(9)

\rho_{\text{Spearman}}=1-\frac{6\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_{\text{sim},i}-r_{\text{gt},i})^{2}}{n(n^{2}-1)}

(10)

where,
\text{sim}_{i}
represents the predicted cosine similarity score for pair
i
,
\text{gt}_{i}
is the ground-truth similarity score,
r_{\text{sim},i}
and
r_{\text{gt},i}
are the ranks of
\text{sim}_{i}
and
\text{gt}_{i}
, respectively.
Additionally, we compute Euclidean similarity (negated Euclidean distance) between embeddings, defined as:

d_{E}(x_{1},x_{2})=-|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})|_{2}

(11)

Higher Pearson values indicate stronger linear alignment with the stack trace pair labels (i.e., positive/negative), while higher Spearman values capture better monotonic ordering of pairs by similarity. A higher Euclidean similarity means embeddings of similar stack traces lie closer in the vector space.

V-D

Evaluation Metrics for Deduplication

Duplicate Ranking.
Following previous studies
[
6
,
2
,
24
]
, we employed Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) and Recall Rate (RR@k) to evaluate the retrieval ranking of our approach compared to the baselines. MRR is defined as the average of the reciprocal ranks of the first relevant result:

MRR=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{i=1}^{|Q|}\frac{1}{rank_{i}}

(12)

where
Q
is the set of queries and
rank_{i}
is the position of the first relevant result for query
i
.

Recall Rate at rank
k
(RR@k) measures the proportion of queries for which at least one relevant result appears within the top
k
retrieved results:

RR@k=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{i=1}^{|Q|}\mathbb{1}(rank_{i}\leq k)

(13)

where
\mathbb{1}(\cdot)
is an indicator function that equals 1 if the condition holds and 0 otherwise. We set
k\leq 10
since, in practical scenarios, users rarely consider recommendations beyond the top 10 results.

Unique Crash Detection.
To evaluate how well the models perform in identifying unique crash reports, we adopted the Receiver Operating Characteristic - Area Under the Curve (ROC-AUC) metric, which quantifies the model’s ability to distinguish between duplicate and unique reports. The ROC-AUC score is computed as:

\text{ROC-AUC}=\int_{0}^{1}TPR(FPR)\,dFPR

(14)

where the True Positive Rate (TPR) and False Positive Rate (FPR) are defined as:

TPR=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad FPR=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}

(15)

Where
TP
,
FP
,
TN
, and
FN
denote true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives, respectively. The ROC curve plots TPR vs. FPR across thresholds, while AUC quantifies the likelihood of ranking a relevant report above a non-relevant one.

V-E

Threats to Validity.

External validity concerns the generalizability of our findings beyond the datasets and environments used in this study. Our evaluation focuses on stack trace-based crash deduplication using specific datasets, which may not fully represent all software projects, particularly those with different logging mechanisms or programming languages. Threats to internal validity concerns about biases in data cleaning or filtering. Our results depend on factors such as preprocessing steps and training stability. Since DeepCrash
[
10
]
had no publicly available code, we had to reproduce it based on the original paper and adapt it to our retrieval ranking pipeline, which may introduce slight variations from the original implementation. To mitigate these risks, we followed the method descriptions closely and ensured consistency in our evaluation. However, variations in data splits and potential biases in labeled duplicates may impact reproducibility.

Comparison with SOTA Embedding Models.

We observed improvements in MRR and RR@k after fine-tuning embedding models. A natural question is how these gains compare with state-of-the-art embeddings such as OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small

Multi-Stack Aggregation.

We evaluated four different stack trace aggregation techniques within our approach. Given two crash reports,
A
and
B
, each containing multiple stack traces, we define
S_{A}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\}
and
S_{B}=\{q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{m}\}
be the sets of stack traces for crash reports
A
and
B
, respectively. We explore the following aggregation methods:

Max Pooling.
This method selects the most similar stack trace pair across both reports by the cosine similarity of embeddings.

h=\max_{i,j}\text{sim}(s_{i},q_{j})

(16)

Mean Pooling.
When a crash report contains multiple stack traces, we compute the mean of their embeddings to obtain a single representative embedding:

h=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e(s_{i})

(17)

where
e(s_{i})
represents the embedding of the
i
-th stack trace. This aggregated embedding is then used for similarity computation.

Multi-Head Attention Aggregation.
We apply a multi-head attention mechanism over all stack traces to learn weighted representations dynamically:

h=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}e_{i},\quad\text{where }\alpha_{i}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{q}^{\top}e_{i})

(18)

Here,
e_{i}
are the stack trace embeddings, and
\mathbf{q}
is a learnable query vector.

Parametric Max-Mean Aggregation.
This method balances by weighting the most similar stack trace embeddings and the mean of the all embeddings. We compute this by using Equation
3
and Equation
4
.

Table

The poor performance of max pooling suggests that relying only on the most similar stack trace pair between crash reports leads to information loss. In contrast, mean pooling preserves more context by averaging all stack traces, making it more effective. Multihead attention likely struggled due to irrelevant attention weights or challenges in learning meaningful dependencies across multiple stack traces.

Stack Frame Selection.
Since we encode entire stack traces at once for contextual embeddings, token limits of the PLM constrain how many frames can be processed. Exceeding this may limit may result in incomplete frame at the end due to truncation. Thus, selecting the most relevant frames, as discussed in
IV-A
, is crucial—especially for verbose Java stack traces. In contrast, shorter C/C++ subroutines allow processing more frames. Prior research
[
7
]
shows the top 10 frames are most critical for debugging. Hence, we retain at least 10 frames and gradually increase the count to evaluate embedding similarity metrics before the adaptation stage, allowing us to determine an optimal frame budget for downstream tasks like EM adaptation and classifier training.

Pre-fine-tuning comparison of embedding similarity metrics with different numbers of stack frames.

As shown in Figure
8

The figure also illustrates the impact of using more frames for PLM fine-tuning and finding duplicates, showing that excessive frames (20 frames) lead to lower MRR. However, extreme reduction (e.g., keeping only the exception frame) may maximize these similarity metrics but will not necessarily improve deduplication. For example, two instances of
java.lang.NullPointerException
may appear highly similar yet lack sufficient context for root cause analysis or to find an appropriate duplicate. This is especially important in contrastive learning. If single frames are sampled from duplicate and non-duplicate stack trace pairs, the same frame may appear in both positive and negative pairs. Without sufficient surrounding context, such cases can mislead the model.

Comparison with LLM-based Approaches.

The advent of large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 has greatly advanced natural language processing, due to their strong capabilities in understanding, generating, and reasoning over text
[
50
]
. To evaluate their effectiveness in crash report deduplication, we prepared a retrieval pipeline using OpenAI’s embedding model
text-embedding-3-small
(TE3S) and the LLM
GPT-4o
(v. 2024-08-06). In this pipeline, all stack traces are first embedded using TE3S and stored in a vector database (ChromaDB
[
51
]
). For a given query stack trace, the top 20 candidate crash reports are initially retrieved based on embedding similarity. These candidates are then reranked by the LLM, which assigns a similarity score in the range
[0,1]
by reasoning over error types, method and class names, call structures, and relevant messages, producing a final ranking.

VIII
Conclusions

We introduce dedupT, a transformer-based crash report deduplication approach that relies solely on stack traces. dedupT surpasses existing baselines in ranking duplicates while maintaining strong performance in identifying unique ones. By fine-tuning pretrained language models on stack trace data, our method effectively captures structural and semantic similarities, improving retrieval accuracy. Future work will focus on adding support for more programming languages, enhancing efficiency for faster retrieval, etc. Additionally, we will explore reranking strategies with large language model (LLM) integration to further refine duplicate ranking performance. Overall, dedupT demonstrates the potential of treating stack traces holistically—aligning with how developers analyze them—rather than as isolated frames, paving the way for more effective crash deduplication in large-scale systems.
