Rocky Mountain spotted fever
Authors: Negin Masoudifar, Robert Kozak, Isaac Bogoch
Published: 2026

Rocky Mountain spotted fever (RMSF) is a severe tick-borne infection

The disease is caused by Rickettsia rickettsii and is transmitted by multiple tick species including the American dog tick, Rocky Mountain wood tick, and brown dog tick.1 Rickettsia rickettsii is considered endemic in certain regions of Canada,2 but human cases of RMSF are rare.

Recognition depends on clinical presentation and history of exposure

Symptoms typically appear 2 to 14 days after a tick bite and include fever, headache, myalgia, and gastrointestinal complaints. The hallmark maculopapular rash usually appears 2 to 5 days after fever onset, initially on the wrists and ankles and spreading centrally, but is absent in 9% to 12% of cases.3 Recent outdoor activity (e.g., hiking, camping, gardening) in endemic areas should raise suspicion for tick exposure. Thrombocytopenia, hyponatremia, and elevated liver enzymes also support the diagnosis.3,4

Awaiting test results should not delay treatment

When RMSF is suspected, testing at a public health laboratory should include RMSF antibody (immunoglobulin G) on serum and polymerase chain reaction on blood or skin biopsy.4 However, patients should be treated empirically when RSMF is suspected.

Doxycycline is the treatment of choice

Prophylactic doxycycline does not prevent RMSF. When RMSF is suspected, doxycycline should be started immediately (100 mg orally twice daily) for 5 to 7 days or at least 3 days after fever resolves.3

Severe cases are common and warrant hospital care and public health notification

In a study of 46 people with RMSF, 57% required hospital admission for severe manifestations, including encephalitis, respiratory failure, acute kidney injury, or hepatic dysfunction. Patients admitted to hospital should receive supportive care and doxycycline (100 mg oral or intravenous twice daily).3 Infectious diseases consultation and public health notification are recommended for surveillance, with tick and animal monitoring to characterize the regional epidemiology of R. rickettsii.4,5
