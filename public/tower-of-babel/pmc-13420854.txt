Endovenous laser ablation versus ligation plus stripping or sclerotherapy for primary lower limb varicose veins: a multicentric three-arm propensity score matched analysis
Authors: Walied Khereba, Almetwaly Ragab, Osama Moeen, Mohamed Ahmed Agena, Elsayed Hadhoud, Reda Othman Abbas, Ahmed Atef, Maisa A. Abdel Wahab, Sameh E. Elimam, Ahmed Khairy Sakr, Shimaa M. ABSTRACT
Background and aimVaricose veins (VVs) are one of the most common consequences of chronic venous disease. The present multicentric three-arm study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes of endovenous laser ablation (EVLA) versus ligation plus stripping or sclerotherapy for primary lower limb VVs.Patient and MethodsThe study included 300 patients with VVs. They received EVLA (GI, n = 100), ligation plus stripping (GII, n = 100) or ligation plus sclerotherapy (GIII, n = 100). Groups were matched using propensity score. The primary outcome measures were 1-year total occlusion and recurrence rates.ResultsAt 1 year follow up, no significant differences were found between the total occlusion rates for GI (75.0%), GII (76.0%) and GIII (83.0%). It was demonstrated that GII had significantly higher recurrence rate (24.0%) as compared to GI (6.0%) and GIII (5.0%). It was also found that GII group had significantly higher frequency of ecchymosis, hematoma and surgical site infection as compared to the other two groups. In contrast, GI had significantly higher frequency of skin ulceration/burn as compared to the other two groups. Also, it was found that GIII had significantly lower frequency of deep vein thrombosis than GI and GII.ConclusionLigation plus sclerotherapy achieved slightly better total occlusion rate in comparison to EVLA and ligation plus stripping while avoiding important side effects related to them mainly traumatic and thermal injury effects and recurrence.

Introduction

Varicose veins are one of the most common consequences of chronic venous disease [1] with significant consequences on patients’ physical and mental well-being [2]. Management of symptomatic patients encompasses multiple conservative and surgical approaches which include conventional surgery and minimally invasive endovascular approaches [3]. The latter have recently gained more popularity [4]. Minimally invasive approaches include thermal (radiofrequency ablation (RFA), endovenous laser ablation (EVLA), steam ablation) and nonthermal (sclerotherapy, echosclerotherapy, mechanochemical ablation) approaches [5].

Each strategy had its own advantages and disadvantages and selection of the optimal strategy is still debatable for most patients due to lack of high-quality evidence [6]. Generally, it has been suggested that endovenous thermal ablation (EVLA or RFA) is recommended as a first-line treatment [4]. However, one meta-analysis concluded that RFA has demonstrated better occlusion rates, fewer complications and lower recurrence rates when compared with EVLA [7].

Trying to maximize the therapeutic outcome, many studies investigated the use of hybrid treatment approaches. These included EVLA plus eco-guided foam sclerotherapy [8], conventional surgery combined with sclerotherapy [9–11], RFA and foam sclerotherapy [12].

However, the current evidence regarding the optimal surgical approach for managing varicose veins remains insufficiently robust and more high-quality research studies are need to establish more rigorous evidence [13]. The present three-arm multicentric study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes of EVLA versus ligation plus stripping or sclerotherapy for primary lower limb varicose veins.

Patients and methods

Setting, ethics, recruitment and propensity score calculation

The present retrospective multicentric study was conducted at five Egyptian academic centres. The study revised the clinical records of patients submitted to surgical interventions for VV during the period between 2016 and 2023. The study protocol was approved by the ethical committee of Al-Azhar University Faculty of Medicine and all participants provided informed consent in accordance with the recommendations of Helsinki Declaration on clinical research involving humans.

The study included adult patients with primary symptomatic varicose veins (grade C2 or higher according to the clinical, etiologic, anatomical, and pathophysiological (CEAP) classification system [14], and reflux of the great or small saphenous veins of more than 1 s on duplex ultrasonography. Patients were excluded if they had ongoing deep venous thrombosis (DVT) at time of screening, acute superficial vein thrombosis, lower limb arteriovenous fistulas or malformations, lower limb skin infections, lower limb ischemia, lower limb malignancy, known hypersensitivity to polidocanol or lidocaine, and any other contraindications to the use of microfoam or endolaser or to local or spinal anaesthesia.

We estimated propensity scores to reduce confounding factors when comparing the three treatment strategies. A multinomial logistic regression model was fitted with treatment group (Group I, Group II, Group III) as the outcome and age (continuous), sex (male/female) and baseline CEAP clinical class (C2–C6, entered as categorical) as predictor variables. The model produced three-group propensity probabilities for each patient; we then performed 1:1:1 nearest-neighbour matching without replacement to yield matched triplets, using the logit of the estimated propensity probabilities and a caliper of 0.2 of the pooled standard deviation of the logit to avoid poor matches. Matching was implemented until 100 patients remained in each treatment arm (total matched N = 300). Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed with standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD < 0.10 considered adequate balance; distributions of CEAP classes and age/sex were also inspected graphically.

Preoperative assessment

Comprehensive clinical and ultrasound-based evaluation was performed to confirm the diagnosis and determine disease severity. The assessment begins with a detailed history focused on symptoms such as leg pain, heaviness, swelling, skin changes, and any previous episodes of thrombophlebitis, bleeding, or ulceration. Duplex ultrasound mapping in the standing or reverse Trendelenburg position was done to provide detailed information on reflux duration, vein diameter, saphenofemoral and saphenopopliteal junction competence, perforator incompetence, and the status of the deep venous system.

Operative interventions

Endovenous laser ablation

The patient was placed in an anti-Trendelenburg position at the beginning of the procedure to facilitate the cannulation of the GSV either directly or under ultrasound guidance. The site of puncture was made a few centimetres below the knee level with an 18-gauge cannula. Patient was then placed in the Trendelenburg position for the rest of the procedure. A 5 F long introducer sheath was placed into the GSV over the guide wire. The bare-tipped fibre 600-lm diameter connected to a 980-nm diode laser was introduced through the sheath. The device was set with 10 W power and pulsed mode manner 10 s (on) and one second (off). The distal tip of the laser fibre was positioned 2 cm below the SFJ under duplex sonographic guidance and confirmed by direct visualization of the red aiming beam of the laser fibre tip through the skin.

On average, 500 ml of peri-venous tumescent anaesthesia (20–25 ml lidocaine 2% buffered with 1.4% sodium bicarbonate in 500 cc saline 0.9%) was injected into the fascial space surrounding the GSV under cross sectional sonographic guidance along its length. Allowing the laser energy to be fired, the laser fibre and sheath were then slowly pulled back till they reached one centimetre above the site of puncture to avoid skin burn.

Bandage compression was applied postoperatively for the first 24 h, after which the patients were asked to wear full-thigh class II compression stockings (30–40 mmHg) for one week. Patients were recommended to mobilize immediately after the effects of the spinal anaesthetic had worn off. Patients were generally discharged within 6 h of the procedure.

Open surgical stripping

Conventional stripping was conducted under spinal anesthesia. It was approached through an oblique 3 cm incision in the groin crease to the palpable femoral artery, the saphenofemoral junction (SFJ) and its tributaries were exposed and identified. Each of the main tributaries were then ligated and divided. High ligation of the great saphenous vein (GSV) was performed close to the femoral vein, with double ligation on the proximal stump. Care was taken to avoid narrowing of femoral vein. Targeted downward GSV stripping was performed. Incompetent accessory saphenous vein, if present, was ligated & stripped during the surgery. Stab evulsions using small skin incisions of marked varicose branches and ligation of grossly incompetent perforators was performed whenever needed. Following skin closure, elastic stockings were immediately applied. Stockings were kept on for one week, with complete bed rest for 24 h and early ambulation afterwards recommended to patients. To mitigate the risks associated with immobility, patients were encouraged during the bed rest period to perform simple lower limb movements, such as ankle pumps and toe exercises, to promote venous return and reduce venous stasis. Patients were generally discharged within 36 h of the procedure.

Ligation and sclerotherapy

Similar to stripping surgery, the procedure was conducted under spinal anesthesia and was approached through an oblique 3 cm incision in the groin crease, the saphenofemoral junction (SFJ) and its tributaries were exposed and identified. Each of the main tributaries were then ligated and divided.

High division of GSV was performed close to the femoral vein, with double ligation on the proximal stump followed by the sclerotherapy catheter. The catheter is positioned at 2 cm from the end of the GSV distal stump under vision. The stump is then ligated, followed by the closure of the groin wound and application of sterile dressing. Creep bandage on top of gauze padding is then applied over the entire course of the GSV, with application of graduated compression from the foot to the groin.

Polidocanol 3% microfoam is prepared with a 1:4 polidocanol to air ratio. A total of 2.5 ml of microfoam is injected for every 10 cm of length of the GSV. The catheter is slowly withdrawn at a speed of 10 cm per minute, to ensure a complete contact of the microfoam with the vein endothelium, until reaching the distal puncture site at the medial malleolus. Technical success is confirmed by stagnant retrograde flow.

Postoperative care

Patients were recommended complete bed rest for the first 6 h, with return to daily activities from post-operative day one. Compression bandage applied in operative theatres was removed after 1 week. Patients were generally discharged within 12 h of the procedure.

Follow-up and outcome measures

Patients were followed up and outcomes were assessed at one week, three month and one year using duplex ultrasound imaging. There were also seen at 1-month post-operatively for assessment of early potential post-operative complications.

The primary outcome measures were the rate of 1-year total occlusion of the GSV and the recurrence rate. Total occlusion was defined as complete absence of a patent lumen in the treated vein segment, with the main truncal vein entirely occluded on duplex ultrasound [15]. Recurrence was typically considered present when there is duplex evidence of reflux in a previously treated venous segment, recanalization of an occluded vein, or neovascularization at the saphenofemoral or saphenopopliteal junction [16].

Secondary outcome measures included rate of post-operative complications in first 30 post-operative days (such as DVT, ecchymosis, haematoma, surgical site infections, ulcerations, and burns). Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were also integrated into the follow-up visit schedule as follows: At the one-week follow-up, patients were asked to assess their pain levels using the Visual Analogue Pain Scale. At the three-month follow-up, patients were asked to report the number of postoperative days required to resume their normal preoperative activities. At the one-year follow-up, patients were asked to evaluate if they were generally satisfied with the effect of the procedure on their chief presenting complaint.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from this study were presented as number and percent, mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR). Comparative analysis was achieved using chi-square test, one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallice test as appropriate. All statistical calculations were computed using SPSS 27 with p value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study aimed to perform a head-to-head comparison between three modalities used for management of primary VVs. These included the minimally invasive EVLA (GI), the open ligation plus stripping (GII) and ligation plus sclerotherapy (GIII). To secure minimal effects of preoperative confounders, the study used propensity score matched analysis.

It was also shown GII patients had significantly higher frequency of ecchymosis, hematoma and surgical site infection as compared to the other two groups while GI group had significantly higher frequency of skin ulceration/burn as compared to the other two groups and GIII patients had significantly lower frequency of deep venous thrombosis as compared to the other two groups.

Results showed that at one year postoperative, GIII patients had higher frequency of total occlusion as compared to the other two groups which fell short of statistical significance but with significantly lower recurrence rate than GII. An addition, it was demonstrated that GI patients experienced significantly lower postoperative pain and faster return to normal activities in comparison to the other two groups.

Generally, the present study, shown that ligation plus sclerotherapy achieved slightly better total occlusion rate in comparison to EVLA and ligation plus stripping while avoiding important side effects related to them mainly traumatic and thermal injury effects and recurrence. These findings probably were responsible for the significantly higher patients’ satisfaction rate in those patients.

In support of our conclusions, it was found that adding sclerotherapy to various VV treatment options resulted in significant impact on clinical outcomes. In one study, it was shown that adding sclerotherapy to ablation techniques resulted in reduction of minor complications such as pain, bruising, and numbness and significantly better early quality of life [17]. In another study, it was reported that fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous high ligation combined with fluoroscopy-guided foam sclerotherapy to treat varicose veins of the great saphenous veins was associated an occlusion rate of 92% with no deep venous thrombosis or other severe adverse events [18].

In addition, the study of Jun et al. [19] demonstrated that adding foam sclerotherapy to endovenous thermal ablation was associated with reducing the recurrence of VVs. Likewise, a retrospective study concluded that sclerotherapy is effective, safe in the long term and well-accepted by patients [20].

Also, a recent consensus recommendation from the expert panel from the American Vein and Lymphatic Society highlighted the value of foam sclerotherapy as primary treatment of adjunctive treatment for after or concomitant to endovenous ablation procedures [21]. This recommendation is supported by another one from the Japanese Society of Phlebology [22].

In comparison to our findings, the study of Lawaetz et al. [23], noted that at 5-year follow up, no difference in the technical efficacy was found between endovenous radiofrequency ablation (RFA), endovenous laser ablation (EVLA), ultrasound guided foam sclerotherapy (UGFS) or high ligation and stripping.

Also, the study of Ran et al. [24], showed that the combination of high ligation and stripping with foam sclerotherapy showed equivalent efficacy to sclerotherapy alone for treating patients with VVs. The authors suggested that considering the increased operative and recovery times, FS alone is recommended to treat VVs in clinical practice. The benefits of sclerotherapy are probably attributed to the reduced distal venous hypertension and improved microcirculation [25].

In respect to the reported complications, the present study found that patients submitted to ligation plus stripping had significantly higher prevalence of ecchymosis, hematoma formation and surgical site infection as compared to the other two groups. This is consistent with the conclusions of one meta-analysis that included 13 studies and 2245 limbs submitted to EVLA and high ligation and stripping. The authors observed lower risk of bleeding, hematoma and wound infection in the EVLA arm [26]. A high prevalence of postoperative ecchymosis following high ligation and stripping has been consistently reported in the literature. For instance, Flessenkämper et al. [27] documented ecchymosis in 69.2% of patients, while Nakashima and Kobayashi [28] reported rates as high as 82.4%. In the present study, the incidence of DVT over one year follow up was 4.0% which is close to the 3.2% figure reported by the study of Itoga et al. [29]. In contrast, other studies reported markedly lower rates [30]. Also, in the ligation and stripping group, DVT was reported in 7% of patients which is consistent with the 7.7% rate reported by the study of Chen et al. [31] with other studies reporting lower rates [32]. Variations among different studies may be related to differences is technical or pharmacological protocols used.

In conclusion, the present study suggests that ligation plus sclerotherapy may be an effective option for the management of varicose veins, with the potential to avoid some of the adverse effects associated with EVLA and ligation plus stripping. These conclusions may be limited by the retrospective design of the study, the limited follow up duration of 1 year, use of 980 nm laser and bare fiber in EVLA group and lack of use of validated patient-reported outcome measures.
