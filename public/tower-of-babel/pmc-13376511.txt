Micronutrient–immune interactions in mood and psychotic disorders: a case–control study of vitamin C, iron, zinc, magnesium, and peripheral blood cell indices
Authors: Najmeh Shahini, Nima Fotovat, Hosein Bagheri, Leila Kashani, Yasser Bagheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Micronutrients play essential roles in metabolic regulation, neurotransmission, and immune function. Disturbances in elements such as zinc, iron, magnesium, and vitamin C have been linked to cognitive impairment, mood dysregulation, and altered immune responses in psychiatric populations. This study aimed to compare serum levels of vitamin C, iron, magnesium, and zinc in patients with mood and psychotic disorders and healthy controls, and to examine their associations with peripheral immune indices, including neutrophil and lymphocyte counts. In this case–control study, 60 psychiatric patients (mood and psychotic disorders) and 20 healthy controls were recruited from 5 Azar Hospital in Gorgan. Serum micronutrient levels were measured using biochemical methods, and complete blood counts were used to determine neutrophil and lymphocyte percentages. Statistical analyses, including group comparisons and correlation tests, were performed using SPSS version 23. The mean age of participants was 40.87 ± 12.58 years. Serum zinc levels were significantly higher in psychiatric patients compared with healthy controls (P < 0.001), with no significant differences between mood and psychotic disorder groups. Serum iron, magnesium, and vitamin C levels did not differ significantly among the three groups. Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between serum zinc and magnesium (r = 0.286, P = 0.027), zinc and iron (r = 0.366, P = 0.004), and iron with both WBC (r = 0.291, P = 0.024) and neutrophil counts (r = 0.313, P = 0.015). The findings suggest that micronutrient–immune interactions may contribute to the biological profile of mood and psychotic disorders. Although causal relationships cannot be inferred, routine assessment of key micronutrients and immune indices may provide additional insight into the metabolic and inflammatory status of psychiatric patients. Further research is warranted to clarify the mechanistic pathways linking micronutrient balance, immune function, and psychiatric symptomatology.

Introduction

Micronutrient deficiencies have long been implicated in the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders, with growing evidence linking disturbances in iron, zinc, magnesium, and vitamin C to alterations in neurotransmission, oxidative balance, and immune function. Iron deficiency, for example, has been associated with impaired cognitive performance, emotional dysregulation, and increased vulnerability to depression and anxiety, largely through its effects on oxygen transport and monoamine synthesis1,2. Zinc and magnesium similarly contribute to synaptic plasticity, glutamatergic modulation, and stress-response pathways, and deficiencies in these minerals have been linked to anxiety, depression, and irritability3,4.

Vitamin C, a key antioxidant and cofactor in neurotransmitter synthesis, plays an essential role in regulating dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways, and low levels have been associated with mood disturbances and cognitive impairment5,6.

Moreover, immune cells such as lymphocytes and neutrophils play a critical role in the body’s response to stress and infection. These immune components are essential for maintaining homeostasis but can be adversely affected by micronutrient deficiencies. Deficiencies in zinc, magnesium, and vitamins impair immune cell function and may exacerbate psychiatric symptoms through heightened inflammatory responses8. A comprehensive review has highlighted that nutritional supplementation may ameliorate psychiatric symptoms by modulating immune pathways, reducing inflammation, and restoring micronutrient balance9, while population-based research indicates that iron-deficiency anemia increases the risk of anxiety, depression, sleep disorders, and psychosis, with improvements following iron supplementation7. Vitamin C deficiency has similarly been associated with mood and cognitive disturbances8, and meta-analytic evidence suggests that zinc supplementation reduces depressive symptoms and lowers the risk of developing depression9. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of integrating nutritional and immunological perspectives in psychiatric research and clinical care10–12.

The intricate interplay between biochemical processes and immune responses has garnered increasing attention in psychiatric research, underscoring the close relationship between mental health and immunological regulation. Immune dysregulation has been shown to play a major role in the onset and progression of mood and psychotic disorders. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as cytokines and C-reactive protein (CRP) have consistently been reported in individuals with schizophrenia and depression, suggesting a state of persistent low-grade inflammation that may contribute to symptom severity and chronicity13,14.

The selection of vitamin C, zinc, iron, and magnesium in the present study is grounded in their well-established roles at the intersection of oxidative stress regulation, neurotransmitter synthesis, and immune function. These four micronutrients represent the most consistently implicated biochemical factors in mood and psychotic disorders, with prior studies demonstrating that disturbances in their levels contribute to neuroinflammation, redox imbalance, and altered monoaminergic activity. Zinc and magnesium modulate glutamatergic and GABAergic neurotransmission, iron is essential for dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways, and vitamin C functions as a key antioxidant and cofactor in catecholamine synthesis. Consequently, these micronutrients form a biologically coherent cluster relevant to the immune-metabolic mechanisms underlying psychiatric disorders.

Similarly, lymphocyte and neutrophil counts were selected as immune indices because they are among the most reliable and widely used peripheral markers of systemic inflammation and stress-related immune activation. Altered neutrophil-to-lymphocyte dynamics have been repeatedly documented in schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, OCD, and major depression, reflecting the immune-metabolic overlap characteristic of these conditions. These cell populations are sensitive to micronutrient status, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signaling, making them appropriate markers for examining the biochemical–immune interactions central to the present study.

Recent literature further highlights the central role of oxidative stress and immune dysregulation in psychiatric disorders. Elevated oxidative stress markers and impaired antioxidant defenses have been documented in OCD, ADHD, and psychotic disorders15,16.Oxidative DNA damage has been identified as a significant biological feature in OCD, suggesting that redox imbalance contributes to symptom severity and chronicity17. Emerging evidence also implicates kynurenine pathway metabolites in psychiatric symptom severity, particularly in OCD and mood disorders, linking metabolic–immune interactions to disease18. Parallel research has identified retinal structural changes as potential biomarkers of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in psychosis and bipolar disorder19,20. Additionally, disruptions in sleep and circadian rhythms have been shown to exacerbate oxidative stress and psychiatric burden, reflecting broader psychobiological imbalance21.

Trauma-related stress responses also intersect with immune and oxidative pathways. Studies examining PTSD among avalanche survivors and the long-term effects of childhood trauma demonstrate how early-life stress biologically embeds vulnerability to psychiatric disorders22,23 Dermatological–psychiatric research further illustrates how immune-mediated skin conditions influence anxiety, social appearance concerns, and psychological functioning24,25.

Despite the growing body of evidence linking micronutrient imbalance, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation to psychiatric symptomatology, few studies have simultaneously examined micronutrient levels alongside immune cell indices across distinct psychiatric subtypes. Accordingly, the present study aims to compare serum levels of vitamin C, iron, zinc, and magnesium in patients with mood and psychotic disorders versus healthy controls, and to evaluate their associations with neutrophil and lymphocyte counts. By integrating nutritional and immunological parameters within the same analytical framework, this study offers a novel perspective on the biological mechanisms underlying psychiatric disorders and contributes to the emerging field of nutritional psychiatry.

Method

Study design

This case–control, descriptive-analytical study was conducted at 5 Azar Hospital in Gorgan and Al-Jalil Hospital in Aq Qala. Recruitment, exposure assessment, and data collection were carried out between January 2023 and December 2023.

Participants

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: Individuals aged 18 years or older diagnosed with psychiatric disorders (psychotic, mood, or anxiety disorders) according to DSM-5 criteria and presenting to 5 Azar Hospital in Gorgan. Exclusion criteria: Participants who declined to participate or were taking any vitamin or mineral supplements were excluded.

Case ascertainment and control selection

Cases were recruited from the psychiatric ward of 5 Azar Hospital. Controls were selected from individuals attending the outpatient clinic who met the inclusion criteria and had no history of psychiatric disorders. All psychiatric diagnoses were confirmed based on DSM-5 guidelines.

Measurements

Serum micronutrient levels

Venous blood samples were collected to measure serum concentrations of vitamin C, zinc, iron, and magnesium. All biochemical analyses were performed using immunochemical methods at the Al-Jalil Diagnostic Laboratory in Aq Qala.

Immune cell indices

White blood cell counts and differential percentages of neutrophils and lymphocytes were determined using the Romanowsky staining method. Advanced inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP, IL-6, TNF-α) were not measured due to resource limitations.

Study size

Sample size estimation was based on Islam et al.26. A minimum of 20 participants per group was required; this number was increased to 30 per group to account for a potential 10% dropout rate. The study was powered for overall group comparisons; subgroup analyses (mood vs. psychotic disorders) may have reduced statistical power due to smaller sample sizes.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed normality. One-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for group comparisons, with Tukey’s post hoc tests for pairwise analyses. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.Effect sizes (η² for ANOVA and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of group differences.

Multivariate regression analyses were considered; however, due to the limited sample size and risk of model overfitting, such analyses were not performed.

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0, with significance set at 0.05.

Results

Description of study population

A total of 60 patients with psychiatric disorders (mood and psychotic) and 20 healthy controls were included in the study. The mean age (± SD) of participants was 40.87 (± 12.58) years. Overall, 18 participants (22.5%) were younger than 30 years, and 56 participants (70%) were female (Table 1).

Based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, serum zinc levels in healthy controls and vitamin C levels in patients did not follow a normal distribution (P < 0.001 and P = 0.006, respectively). In contrast, serum iron and magnesium levels were normally distributed. Healthy controls had significantly lower mean serum zinc levels compared with patients (P < 0.001). However, serum iron, magnesium, and vitamin C levels did not differ significantly between patients and controls (Table 2).

Effect size analysis showed a large effect for zinc (η² = 0.27), while vitamin C (η² = 0.03), iron (η² = 0.004), and magnesium (η² = 0.02) demonstrated small or negligible effects.

Group comparisons

One-way ANOVA and post hoc analyses demonstrated that serum zinc levels in healthy controls were significantly lower than in both mood and psychotic disorder groups (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in serum iron, magnesium, or vitamin C levels among the three groups.

Post hoc comparisons confirmed that zinc levels were significantly lower in healthy controls compared with both patient subgroups (P < 0.001 for each comparison). No significant differences were found between mood and psychotic disorder groups for any micronutrient.

Age and sex distributions did not differ significantly between mood and psychotic disorder groups (age: P = 0.296; sex: P = 0.091).

Additional comparisons

Further subgroup analyses revealed that zinc levels did not differ significantly between psychotic and mood disorder patients (P = 0.217). However, patients with four or more hospitalizations had significantly higher zinc levels (161.45 ± 23.18) compared with those with three or fewer hospitalizations (145.51 ± 23.18; P = 0.028).

The difference in zinc levels between patients with ≥ 4 versus ≤ 3 hospitalizations demonstrated a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.69), indicating a clinically meaningful difference despite the modest sample size.

Vitamin C and iron levels showed minor variations across subgroups, but none reached statistical significance (Vitamin C: P = 0.391, 0.069, 0.435; Iron: P = 0.745, 0.834, 0.519). Magnesium levels remained stable across all comparisons (P = 0.754, 0.464, 0.493) (Table 3).

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between serum iron and zinc levels (r = 0.366, P = 0.004). Zinc and magnesium levels were also positively correlated (r = 0.286, P = 0.027). Additionally, serum iron showed significant correlations with WBC count (r = 0.291, P = 0.024) and PMN count (r = 0.313, P = 0.015) (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between serum micronutrient levels and immune cell indices in patients with mood and psychotic disorders compared with healthy individuals. The finding that serum zinc levels were significantly lower in healthy controls than in psychiatric patients aligns with previous research demonstrating zinc’s essential role in neurotransmission, synaptic plasticity, and mood regulation27. Although previous studies frequently report zinc deficiency in psychiatric disorders, our findings showed elevated zinc levels in patients. This discrepancy may be explained by several factors, including psychotropic medication effects, compensatory antioxidant responses to chronic oxidative stress, dietary variability, and population-specific metabolic differences. These findings suggest that zinc dysregulation—rather than simple deficiency—may be more relevant in psychiatric pathophysiology.

The large effect size for zinc (η² = 0.27) indicates that this difference was not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful. One possible explanation is that elevated zinc may represent a compensatory neuroprotective response to heightened oxidative stress, given zinc’s critical role in antioxidant defense, glutamatergic regulation, and cellular protection against free-radical damage. This interpretation is supported by recent findings demonstrating increased oxidative stress markers, impaired redox homeostasis, and retinal neuroinflammation in psychiatric populations, particularly in obsessive–compulsive and psychotic disorders17,28,29. Another plausible explanation is that psychotropic medications—especially antipsychotics and mood stabilizers—may influence zinc metabolism through effects on appetite, gastrointestinal absorption, or hepatic processing. Additionally, nutritional variability, including irregular dietary patterns, supplementation, or institutional feeding environments, may contribute to the observed differences.

Importantly, zinc is closely linked to immune modulation, and elevated levels may reflect an adaptive response to chronic low-grade inflammation. Prior studies have shown that zinc availability influences neutrophil activity, lymphocyte proliferation, and cytokine regulation, suggesting that the elevated zinc levels in our patient groups may be part of a broader immune–metabolic adjustment to persistent inflammatory signaling. This interpretation is consistent with the immune dysregulation and oxidative DNA damage reported in recent neuroimmune studies17, supporting the notion that zinc alterations may serve as a biomarker of systemic stress rather than a simple nutritional deviation. Furthermore, the medium-to-large effect size for hospitalization-related zinc differences (Cohen’s d = 0.69) suggests that zinc alterations may be associated with illness chronicity or severity. This finding is consistent with meta-analytic evidence showing that zinc supplementation significantly reduces depressive symptoms and lowers the risk of developing depression9,30. The correlations observed between zinc levels and immune cell counts in this study further reinforce zinc’s role in modulating immune responses and influencing inflammatory pathways implicated in psychiatric disorders.

Although magnesium levels did not differ significantly between groups, its known effects on anxiety, depression, and neural excitability remain clinically relevant. Magnesium participates in numerous biochemical reactions that regulate NMDA receptor activity and stress-response pathways. Previous studies have highlighted magnesium’s efficacy in reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms31,32. The absence of significant differences in the present study may reflect sample size limitations, dietary variability, or unmeasured confounders. Nevertheless, the observed relationship between zinc and magnesium levels suggests a potential metabolic linkage that warrants further investigation.

Iron deficiency has been associated with cognitive impairments, emotional dysregulation, and altered neurotransmitter synthesis. It should be noted that cognitive performance was not assessed in this study; therefore, references to cognitive mechanisms are theoretical and based on prior literature rather than direct measurement. The lack of significant differences in serum iron levels between patients and controls in this study contrasts with findings from Lee et al.33, who reported a higher prevalence of psychiatric disorders among individuals with iron-deficiency anemia. However, the significant correlations between serum iron levels and WBC and PMN counts observed here indicate that iron may influence immune cell function, which could subsequently affect inflammatory responses and mental health. This interpretation aligns with studies showing that serum ferritin levels correlate with depression severity and fatigue in multiple sclerosis patients34,35. These findings also resonate with broader evidence linking iron metabolism to oxidative stress and immune dysregulation in psychiatric populations.

Although leukocyte, neutrophil, and lymphocyte indices were included in the study aims, group comparisons did not yield statistically meaningful differences. Therefore, correlation analyses were prioritized to explore the relationships between micronutrients and immune parameters. The observed associations between micronutrient levels and immune cell counts underscore the importance of considering nutritional and immunological factors together when examining psychiatric disorders.

Although vitamin C levels did not differ significantly between groups, its role in neurotransmitter synthesis, antioxidant defense, and immune modulation is well established. Vitamin C deficiency has been associated with mood disorders and cognitive impairments8,36. The correlations observed between vitamin C and other biochemical markers in this study highlight its interconnected role in maintaining mental health and immune function. Previous studies have demonstrated that vitamin C deficiency contributes to mood and cognitive disturbances8,37, supporting its relevance in psychiatric health.

The observed associations between micronutrient levels and immune cell counts underscore the importance of a holistic approach to psychiatric care. Integrating nutritional assessments and targeted supplementation into clinical practice may enhance treatment outcomes by addressing deficiencies in essential micronutrients and optimizing immune function. This approach aligns with growing evidence that nutritional interventions can improve psychiatric symptoms by modulating inflammatory and oxidative pathways38–40.

Clinical implications

The clinical implications of these findings merit consideration within a translational framework. Although the present study does not establish causal relationships, the observed associations between micronutrient levels and immune indices suggest that routine nutritional and inflammatory profiling may provide additional insight into the biological status of individuals with mood and psychotic disorders. Incorporating basic micronutrient assessments—such as zinc, iron, magnesium, and vitamin C—into psychiatric evaluations may help identify individuals who could benefit from closer monitoring of metabolic or immune alterations. Furthermore, the interplay between micronutrient status and immune activity supports the relevance of integrative psychiatric models that consider biological, psychological, and lifestyle factors together. While no specific clinical interventions can be inferred from this study alone, these results highlight the potential value of multidisciplinary approaches that include nutritional assessment, attention to inflammatory markers, and collaboration between mental health and general medical services. Such frameworks may ultimately contribute to more individualized and holistic care pathways for psychiatric populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the sample size was adequate for overall group comparisons, the statistical power for subgroup analyses (mood vs. psychotic disorders) was limited. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship between micronutrient levels and psychiatric disorders. Third, several potential confounding variables—including dietary intake, socioeconomic status, medication profiles, and comorbid metabolic conditions—were not systematically assessed and may have influenced the findings. Fourth, immune assessment was restricted to neutrophil and lymphocyte counts; the absence of cytokine markers such as CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α limits the depth of neuroimmunological interpretation. Fifth, group differences in leukocyte indices were not statistically significant, which limited the ability to perform meaningful between-group comparisons. Finally, multivariate regression analyses were not conducted due to sample size constraints, reducing the ability to identify independent predictors of psychiatric status.

Future research should incorporate larger samples, longitudinal designs, comprehensive inflammatory markers, and multivariate analytical approaches to better elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying psychiatric disorders.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that micronutrient–immune interactions may play a role in psychiatric disorders; however, these results should be interpreted cautiously due to the study’s limitations. Further research in larger and more diverse cohorts is needed to clarify the causal pathways linking micronutrient balance, immune function, and psychiatric symptomatology.
