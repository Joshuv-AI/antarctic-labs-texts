Exploring Women’s Experiences of Art Therapy for Fear of Childbirth During Pregnancy in Singapore: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Mei Qi Ang, Peiling Pearlyn Lee, Sujitha Kumar Lakshmana, Amelia Shuyi Tan, Priscilla Tan, Tanuja Nair, Sylvia Mun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeFear of childbirth is characterized by heightened fear and anxiety related to pregnancy, labor, and the birthing process. Engaging in creative activities may help women externalise and process their emotions and develop coping strategies to manage their fears. The study aimed to explore the experiences of women who had undergone Art Therapy for fear of childbirth during pregnancy.Patients and MethodsThis paper presents the qualitative component of a mixed methods study. A descriptive qualitative design was used. Women aged 21 to 50 years old, exhibited a high level of fear of childbirth measured using the Fear of Birth Scale (FOBS) and had undergone the Art Therapy intervention during pregnancy, were invited to participate in individual semi-structured interviews at four weeks after childbirth. Women were excluded if they had experienced obstetric complications during pregnancy, had a current diagnosis of depression requiring medical intervention, or had stillbirth.ResultsA total of 20 women took part in the interviews. The average duration of the interviews was 40.6 minutes. Participants’ age ranged from 25 to 39 years, with a mean age of 31.3 years (SD = 4.05). Thematic analysis generated three themes and ten subthemes. The main themes were: (i) The burden of anticipating childbirth, (ii) Art as a pathway to healing and support, (iii) Psychological adjustment in preparation for childbirth.ConclusionWomen reported that important aspects of their fear of childbirth were difficult to express through verbal communication. Art Therapy was described as providing a space to express and process their fears through visual means, supporting emotional safety and promoting shared understanding within their relationships. Women also perceived creative activities as helping them and their partners feel psychologically prepared for childbirth. Healthcare institutions should consider expanding antenatal assessment and support beyond conventional dialogue-based and content-focused approaches.Trial RegistrationThe study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on 5 February 2024 (Trial ID: NCT06257186).

Introduction

Fear of childbirth is a multidimensional psychological phenomenon characterized by heightened fear and anxiety related to pregnancy, labor, and the birthing process.1 In its more severe form, often referred to as tokophobia, fear of childbirth may lead women to avoid pregnancy, request elective caesarean birth in the absence of medical indications, or experience significant distress throughout pregnancy and after childbirth.2 Owing to differences in cultural perceptions of childbirth and variations in maternity care systems, the reported prevalence of tokophobia varies considerably across countries.1 In addition, the tools used to assess fear of childbirth differ across research and clinical settings. Several standardized instruments have been developed to measure fear of childbirth, including the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ), which is widely used to measure the intensity of childbirth-related fear during pregnancy and after birth,3 as well as shorter screening tools such as the Fear of Birth Scale (FOBS),4 Childbirth Fear Questionnaire (CFQ),5 The Oxford Worries about Labor Scale (OWLS),6 The Slade-Pais Expectations of Childbirth Scale and Fear Subscale (SPECS).7 Variations in the choice of assessment tools, as well as differences in cut-off scores used to define severe fear, further contribute to the inconsistencies in reported prevalence rates across populations and settings.8 Nevertheless, fear of childbirth is a significant and increasingly recognized concern among pregnant women worldwide, highlighting the importance of identifying effective strategies to support women in managing these fears.

Various non-pharmacological interventions have been developed to address fear of childbirth, with the aim of reducing anxiety and enhancing women’s confidence in their ability to cope with labor and childbirth. These interventions include approaches such as psychoeducation, cognitive-behavioral therapy, relaxation techniques, mindfulness-based strategies, antenatal education programs, and counselling. Overall, evidence from recent research suggests that non-pharmacological approaches may have the potential to reduce fear of childbirth and improve psychological well-being among pregnant women.9–15 However, mixed findings regarding their effects on broader psychological outcomes have also been reported. For example, O’Connell (2021) found limited evidence on the use of non-pharmacological interventions in depression scores.16 In addition, substantial heterogeneity exists across studies in terms of intervention types, delivery formats, outcome measures. Methodological limitations such as small sample sizes and potential risk of bias were also found in several primary studies included in these reviews, which restricted the strength and reliability of the conclusions that can be drawn. As a result, while non-pharmacological interventions appear promising and can be implemented in clinical practice to support women, the overall certainty of the evidence regarding their effectiveness remains limited.

Among the various approaches explored, Art Therapy, a therapeutic approach that uses creative expression, such as drawing, painting, and other visual arts to facilitate emotional exploration and communication, has increasingly been explored within maternal mental health contexts. A recent meta-analysis have indicated that art-based interventions may contribute to reducing depression and anxiety related to childbirth.12 In addition to quantitative research that evaluates effectiveness, it is also important to understand the experiential aspects to have a more comprehensive understanding of the fear of childbirth and the role of Art Therapy in supporting women with fear of childbirth. A small number of qualitative studies have provided insights into how women perceive and engage with Art Therapy during pregnancy, the meanings they attach to the creative process, and the ways in which Art Therapy may influence their emotions, perceptions, and preparation for childbirth.17 These studies suggest that engaging in creative activities may help women externalize and process their emotions, reflect on their expectations of childbirth, and develop greater self-awareness and coping strategies.17 Art Therapy has also been described as providing a supportive space in which women can communicate concerns that may be difficult to articulate verbally, thereby promoting emotional relief and psychological well-being during pregnancy.17 However, the qualitative evidence base remains limited. Further research is needed to better understand how women experience the therapeutic process, how they perceive the use of visual expression in exploring and communicating childbirth-related fears, and how participation in Art Therapy may influence interpersonal relationships and preparation for childbirth. In addition, there is limited qualitative evidence examining how women engage with and attribute meaning to the artwork throughout the therapy, as well as how changes in their thoughts, feelings and perceptions are experienced over time. Moreover, most existing studies have been conducted in Western contexts, particularly in the Scandinavian, and relatively little is known about how women in Asian settings experience Art Therapy during pregnancy. Cultural beliefs and social expectations surrounding childbirth may shape women’s perceptions of fear of childbirth as well as their engagement with therapeutic interventions.8,18 Therefore, there is a need to gain a deeper understanding of women’s experiences with Art Therapy within Asian contexts. Singapore provides a particularly relevant setting for such inquiry due to its multicultural population comprising diverse ethnic, cultural and linguistic backgrounds. These diverse perspectives may influence how women perceive, express and cope with childbirth-related fears. In this context, Art Therapy may offer a valuable avenue for emotional expression by enabling women to communicate emotions and concerns through visual means that may transcend linguistic and cultural differences. Examining women’s experiences in Singapore can therefore provide important insights into how culturally diverse populations engage with creative and psychologically oriented interventions during pregnancy.

The present study aimed to explore the experiences of women who had undergone Art Therapy for fear of childbirth during pregnancy in Singapore. Art Therapy was selected as the focus of this study due to its emphasis on visual and experiential processes, which may facilitate the expression of emotions, fears, and concerns that are difficult to articulate verbally. As such, it may be especially well-suited to address fear of childbirth compared with approaches that depend predominantly on verbal reflection and communication. The conceptual model of fear of childbirth among pregnant women was adopted to guide this study (Figure 1).19 This framework delineates the antecedents, attributes, and consequences of fear of childbirth, providing a structured way to understand how fear of childbirth develops, how it is experienced, and the potential outcomes associated with it. By using this model, the study sought to situate women’s lived experiences within a broader theoretical understanding of fear of childbirth, thereby enabling a more comprehensive interpretation of how antenatal Art Therapy may interact with and influence the emotional, cognitive, and behavioral dimensions of fear. Materials and Methods

Study Design

This paper presents the qualitative component of a mixed methods study, in which the earlier phase utilized a quasi-experimental design to offer Art Therapy to women experiencing high levels of fear of childbirth. Women who consented to receive Art Therapy alongside routine antenatal care were assigned to the Art Therapy group, while those who declined were assigned to the Standard Care group and received only routine antenatal care. Women in the Art Therapy group were subsequently invited to take part in the qualitative phase, which involved individual semi-structured interviews exploring their experiences with Art Therapy. A descriptive qualitative study design was used for this phase to provide in-depth insights into participants’ real-world experiences. The present qualitative study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR).

Setting and Sample

The study was conducted in a public maternity hospital in Singapore providing specialized obstetric and gynecological care. The hospital handles approximately 11,000 deliveries a year20 and is the largest maternity facility in Singapore.

A convenience sampling strategy was used in this study. Women were invited to participate if they were between 21 and 50 years old, proficient in English, carrying a live singleton intrauterine pregnancy, demonstrated a high level fear of childbirth during pregnancy as measured by the Fear of Birth Scale (FOBS),4 and had completed the Art Therapy intervention in an earlier phase of the study. Exclusion criteria included contraindications for vaginal birth, obstetric complications during pregnancy, current depression requiring medical intervention, or a had stillbirth.

The sample size in this research was determined based on the sufficiency of the information obtained in order to generate a rich and detailed understanding of women’s experiences of the Art Therapy. A previous study recruited 20 participants and analyzed interviews from 19 participants,21 which informed the target sample size of 20 for the present research. Recruitment of participants continued until data saturation was achieved, when no new meaningful insights relevant to the study aims were derived from successive interviews. The first 20 women allocated to the Art Therapy group who had completed all procedures in the quantitative phase were invited to participate in the interviews through phone call. Data collection and preliminary analysis were conducted concurrently to monitor the emergence of codes and themes, with saturation confirmed through the repetition of existing themes in the final interviews. Written consent was obtained from each woman for study participation and using of their direct quotes and photographs of artwork for publication purposes.

Intervention

In addition to routine antenatal care provided by their maternity care providers in the study setting, women in the Art Therapy group were offered a series of three to five Art Therapy sessions. These sessions could take place at any point after consent was obtained up to approximately 36 weeks’ gestation. Women in this group received an art kit comprising a sketchbook, pencils, colored pencils, brush markers, and clay, which they could use during and after the sessions. Each session, led by a qualified Art Therapist, lasted 60 to 90 minutes and was delivered either face-to-face within a designated room in the hospital or via telehealth. The main art directives for the first three sessions were: (i) what does childbirth look like, (ii) body map, and (iii) mother and child.

Data Collection

Data were collected via individual interviews conducted between May and July 2024. Women were approached for consent taking for the earlier phase of the study when they were in the second trimester (between 13 and 27 weeks) of pregnancy. A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the conceptual framework guiding this study and validated by the research team comprising nurses, midwives and therapists, which focused on women’s experiences of participating in the Art Therapy. Open-ended questions were used by the interviewer to allow probing with additional questions as new information emerge through the dialogue. A pilot interview was conducted to ensure that the questions were comprehensible.

Interviews were conducted in English through phone call at one month after birth by the first author, a trained midwife researcher who had prior training and experience in qualitative research. The interviewer was not involved in the Art Therapy sessions or the childbirth process of the women. Field notes were taken by the interviewer during the interviews. Interviews were audio-recorded with permission from the women.

Data Analysis

Audio-recordings of the interviews were transcribed verbatim by two members of the study team. To ensure accuracy, another team member reviewed the transcripts against the original audio files. Data analysis followed the thematic analysis approach, combining deductive coding guided by the conceptual framework with inductive coding to capture emerging themes. The research team followed the six steps as outlined by Braun and Clarke (2021): Becoming familiar with the data, generating initial codes, identifying potential themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and compiling the final report.22 The first author thoroughly read and reread the transcripts to develop an initial understanding. Concepts with similar meanings were color-coded, leading to the creation of initial codes. The initial coding process involved identifying data corresponding to the conceptual framework. A coding framework was then developed to condense the text into meaningful and manageable segments. The transcripts were repeatedly reviewed to identify patterns and meanings. Related concepts and categories were organized into subthemes, which were then grouped to form overarching themes. To minimize confirmation bias, the research team continuously reviewed the data for perspectives that extended, contradicted, or did not fit the framework. Emergent codes were then incorporated into the analysis through an inductive approach. This ensured that the data analysis remained grounded in the participants’ lived experiences while aligning with the constructs underpinning the study. The definitions of main themes and subthemes were subsequently established, and other members of the research team cross-verified the generated codes, themes, and subthemes. To enhance reflexivity, the researchers acknowledged and reflected on how their professional backgrounds and experiences might influence the research process. Emerging codes and themes were also reviewed collaboratively, and differences in interpretation were discussed until consensus was reached. Reflective notes were maintained during the analysis process to enhance transparency and minimize influence of researcher bias.

Ethical Consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Centralized Institutional Review Board (CIRB) prior to commencement of study. Written informed consent to participate in the study and the using of their direct quotes and photographs of artwork for publication purposes were sought from the participants in the earlier phase of the study. All audio-recorded data and transcriptions were coded to maintain anonymity of the participants. The mixed methods study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on 5 February 2024 (Trial ID: NCT06257186).23

Rigor of Study

The rigor of the study was established by four main criteria: credibility, dependability, transferability and confirmability.24 Credibility was enhanced through member checking, whereby the interviewer summarized participants’ responses and invited clarification or correction to ensure their perspectives were accurately represented. In addition, the first author conducted repeated reviews of the interview transcripts to verify that the emergent themes genuinely reflected the data. Dependability was supported by maintaining a systematic audit trail, comprising raw data such as field notes and transcripts, as well as detailed documentation of the coding process and theme development. Transferability was facilitated by providing rich, contextualized descriptions of the research setting, procedures, and participant characteristics, enabling readers to assess the applicability of findings to other contexts. Finally, confirmability was strengthened through ongoing dialogue within the research team, where themes and subthemes were critically examined to minimize bias and ensure that interpretations remained grounded in the participants’ accounts.

Results

A total of 20 women participated in the interviews. The average duration of interviews was 40.6 minutes. Although women aged 21 to 50 years old were eligible for participation, the ages of the participants recruited into the study ranged from 25 to 39 years, with a mean age of 31.3 years (SD = 4.05). Half of the women were Chinese (n = 10; 50%), and all except one were married (n = 19; 95%). Over half of the women held a Bachelor’s degree or equivalent (n = 12; 60%), and majority of the women were employed full-time (n = 17; 85%). Additionally, more than half were experiencing motherhood for the first time (n = 13; 65%). Themes and Subthemes

Theme 1: The Burden of Anticipating Childbirth

This theme captures the multifaceted nature of fear experienced by women during pregnancy. For many women, fear of childbirth was not a singular concern but a deeply embedded emotional and psychological burden that shaped their expectations of labor, birth, and early motherhood. Women described how this fear developed and intensified through a range of influences. Together, these experiences illustrated how fear of childbirth was lived both psychologically and physically, while often remaining hidden within social contexts.

Subtheme 1.1: Socially Constructed Fear of Childbirth

This subtheme highlights how women’s fear was shaped by narratives and beliefs encountered through family, peers, and wider society. Women described how stories of difficult or traumatic births shared by others were often portrayed as overwhelming, painful, and potentially dangerous. Theme 2: Art as a Pathway to Healing and Support

This theme explores how Art Therapy provided a psychologically supportive space for women experiencing fear of childbirth during pregnancy. Women described how the creative process allowed them to express and recognize their complex emotions in a safe and non-judgmental environment. With this, women were able to concretize and reflect on their thoughts. Central to this process was the therapeutic relationship with the Art Therapist, which fostered a sense of trust, emotional safety, and validation. The involvement and support of their husbands further contributed to the therapeutic experience.

Subtheme 2.1: Finding a Voice Through Art

This subtheme captures how women used Art Therapy to externalize their fears, emotions, and experiences that were previously difficult to express verbally. With the use of the art materials, women were able to create tangible representations of their internal states, which validated their emotions. By externalizing these hidden emotions, women discovered the cause of the fear that they had not previously recognized. when I actually had this very dark picture uh of this whole pain of fear right around the birth. Theme 3: Psychological Adjustment in Preparation for Childbirth

This theme captures the shifts in women’s perceptions, emotions and sense of preparedness for childbirth that emerged through their engagement in the Art Therapy sessions. Through creative expressions, women reported being able to regulate their feelings and transform fragmented fears into new meanings, resilience, and psychological preparedness for childbirth and motherhood. With this, women stated being able to approach childbirth with greater calmness and confidence.

Discussion

This study aimed to gain a comprehensive understanding of the experiences of women who had undergone Art Therapy for fear of childbirth during pregnancy. Many women shared the difficulties in communicating their fears regarding the unknown changes that pregnancy and childbirth may bring about in their lives. Women also reported that the Art Therapy sessions offered a safe and supportive environment, and the artwork served as instruments for them to deposit their feelings outside of them. As a result, women felt empowered to face the anticipated challenges during pregnancy and the initial postnatal period. Many women reported positive birth and postnatal experiences, even when situations became overwhelming. These findings guided an exploration of the role of Art Therapy in addressing anticipatory fear, supporting psychological preparedness, and shaping women’s experiences during and after childbirth.

This study has extended current understanding of fear of childbirth by conceptualizing it as an anticipatory psychological state maintained through repetitive mental simulations of pain, uncertainty, and anticipated negative outcomes, which was then transformed through a staged process of externalization and cognitive-emotional adjustments with Art Therapy. Fear of childbirth has been frequently characterized by anticipatory anxiety and concerns about unpredictability.9,18,19 Several women in this study reported feeling overwhelmed with unstructured cognitions accompanied by heightened emotions. In addition to anxiety surrounding the labor and birth process itself, women also expressed concerns relating to their perceived ability to cope with postpartum responsibilities. Previous research has similarly identified that women with heightened fear of childbirth often report lower self-efficacy regarding infant care and breastfeeding, as well as increased anxiety about meeting maternal expectations.25–27 Such fears reflected a broader dimension of fear of childbirth that may not only be linked to the birthing event itself but also to the anticipated demands of the postpartum period.19 Notably, rather than emerging dynamically throughout pregnancy, women in this study were able to identify these specific sources of fear at the outset of the Art Therapy, which then became the focus of the therapeutic work in the first session of the therapy. Furthermore, although loss of control is frequently theorized as a construct of fear of childbirth,18,19,26 majority of the women in the present study did not explicitly describe feeling out of control. Instead, their distress appeared to arise from the concurrent presence of intense thoughts and emotions that hindered them from being able to have clarity in their thoughts. These findings indicate that women’s fear of childbirth may be driven more by cognitive overload, and that fear was maintained through persistent mental stimulation rather than dynamic changes in the perceived threat.

Previous research has shown that creative expression can facilitate the externalization of internal fears, allowing individuals to safely explore distressing thoughts and images.9,17 Through the Art Therapy process, women shared that they were supported to temporarily disengage from the stressors, and the relief of tension facilitated the process to translate diffuse internal experiences into concrete visual representations. Women also described that externalizing their fears provided an opportunity to psychological distance from the fear, reprocess their distressing cognitions, and reappraise childbirth as a transitional life event rather than an imminent threat. These findings were consistent with cognitive reappraisal frameworks, in which meaning-making allows fear to be regulated rather than avoided,28 suggesting that Art Therapy could have potentially promoted cognitive organization and interrupt cycles of mental rumination in women with fear of childbirth rather than eliminating or suppressing fearful thoughts. As a result, women reported increased psychological preparedness for both childbirth and the transition to motherhood. Readiness was therefore described not as a fixed psychological state, but as an evolving, constructed process shaped through non-verbal meaning-making. Much of the existing literature has focused primarily on the measurable effectiveness of interventions through reduction in fear or anxiety scores, with limited attention to the psychological processes through which interventions exert their impact.12,13 The process-oriented understanding from this study has extended the understanding of the psychological mechanisms that helped women manage fear of childbirth.

Findings in this study also highlighted that fear of childbirth is experienced by women as largely non-verbal in nature, and that some fears may remain inaccessible through conventional clinical or interpersonal dialogue. This may explain why fear of childbirth is often not recognized by healthcare providers during routine antenatal visits. Although it has been recommended for antenatal care programs and healthcare services to be designed around women’s needs,29 a significant number of women with fear of childbirth persists. Importantly, women in this study reported that the externalization of fear during the Art Therapy process was also not confined to an intrapersonal process. Making fear visible created a shared reference point that enabled partners to access and understand women’s internal experiences, which was perceived by women to have strengthened their relationships during the antenatal period. The artworks were described by women as mediating objects that supported emotional disclosure, mutual understanding, and empathic attunement. Women reported being able to communicate fears visually, which created opportunities for partners to be able to engage more fully without the pressure to problem-solve, fostering emotional safety and shared meaning-making. This supported findings from a previous study that highlighted the importance of shared coping within the couple.30 This relational engagement may have enhanced dyadic preparedness for childbirth, positioning readiness not solely as an individual psychological outcome, but as a co-constructed process within the couple system.

As a multicultural society comprising diverse ethnic groups with varying beliefs, traditions and attitudes towards pregnancy and childbirth, Singapore provides a valuable context for understanding women’s experiences of fear of childbirth and engagement with supportive interventions. The present study has also provided culturally contextualized insights into how Singaporean women perceive and navigate childbirth-related fears. Across ethnic groups, women described experiencing stigma surrounding their fear of childbirth, while also highlighting the importance of developing trusting and supportive relationships with the Art Therapist. These findings suggest that although cultural norms may shape the emotional expression of women regarding their fears, a non-judgmental therapeutic space can facilitate openness and foster relational support across diverse cultural backgrounds. Furthermore, Singapore’s healthcare system is highly medicalized, with antenatal care typically focused on physical monitoring and health outcomes. Within this context, Art Therapy appeared to offer a unique opportunity for women to explore and communicate emotional experiences that may receive less attention during routine antenatal care. These contextual factors may have shaped women’s engagement with the intervention and should be considered when assessing the transferability of findings to other cultural and healthcare settings.

Implications for Practice and Recommendations for Future Research

The findings of this study have several important implications for clinical practice in maternal healthcare. Firstly, there is a need to adopt more women-centered approaches that allow space for emotional expression beyond the conventional clinical conversations. Fear of childbirth should be proactively assessed and identified among pregnant women in early pregnancy, and healthcare institutions should provide environments that prioritize psychological safety, validation of feelings and supportive interventions for fear of childbirth. Art Therapy, as a non-pharmacological and low-risk intervention, can be incorporated into antenatal care programs to support women experiencing fear of childbirth, providing a structured space for emotional expression, reflection, and coping skills development. Alternatively, healthcare institutions could explore incorporating non-verbal, experiential approaches within standard antenatal care pathways to enhance the effectiveness of content-focused antenatal care programs in meeting the emotional needs of women. Midwives, who are often the primary point of contact for pregnant women, can play a key role in identifying women who require support. This could be achieved even without the use of formal assessment tools, by observing cues such as women expressing difficulty articulating their fears or remaining anxious despite receiving antenatal education. In settings where access to trained art therapists is limited, midwives may still support this process by offering avenues for women to externalize, distance from, and reprocess their fears. Healthcare institutions could also incorporate topics of fear of childbirth during antenatal visits or antenatal education classes to normalize discussions around fear, reduce stigma, and foster help-seeking behaviors among pregnant women.

Future research is needed to further strengthen the evidence base for having supportive interventions for women experiencing fear of childbirth. The long-term impact of supportive interventions beyond the antenatal period with outcomes such as women’s childbirth satisfaction and postpartum psychological well-being would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the sustained benefits of the interventions. Furthermore, future research should be done to further examine how involvement of partner in the intervention can benefit couples experiencing fear of childbirth.

Strengths and Limitations

Several strengths were identified in this study. Employing a descriptive qualitative design and a semi-structured interview guide enabled an in-depth exploration of women’s postnatal experiences, yielding rich and detailed narratives. Additionally, conducting interviews in the postnatal period enabled the gathering of insights over a longer timeframe, providing meaningful perspectives on how the techniques learned during Art Therapy sessions might impact women during the postnatal phase.

Several limitations were also noted in this study. Firstly, the study involved a relatively small sample recruited from a single healthcare institution, which may limit the transferability of the findings to women receiving care in other settings. Additionally, the use of convenience sampling of women who completed the Art Therapy intervention and agreed to participate in interviews could have introduced selection bias, potentially affecting the range of perspectives captured. These women may have been more positively engaged with the intervention, potentially contributing to a greater emphasis on beneficial experiences and perceptions. Therefore, findings should be interpreted cautiously. The study’s focus on English-speaking women further narrowed the sample, excluding the views of non-English-speaking mothers. Lastly, there is a potential for recall bias, as interviews were conducted after women had given birth, which may have influenced the accuracy of their experiences with the Art Therapy sessions.

Conclusion

Fear of childbirth appeared to encompass important non-verbal dimensions, with women describing aspects of their fears that were difficult to express through routine antenatal assessments that rely largely on verbal communication. These findings suggest the potential value of broadening assessment and support strategies beyond conventional dialogue-based and content-focused approaches. Women also described Art Therapy as an avenue to externalize and communicate their fears visually and reported that involving their partners in the process fostered greater understanding and connection within the relationship through meaningful engagement without placing pressure on partners to solve the problems that they were facing. The shared experiences were perceived by women as contributing to feelings of emotional safety and shared understanding within the couple, suggesting that psychological preparedness for childbirth may be strengthened through relational processes and should be considered a dyadic, co-constructed outcome. The findings suggest that non-verbal, experiential interventions such as Art Therapy may offer a valuable complement to existing antenatal care pathways, particularly for women who struggle to articulate fear verbally. However, as the study was conducted with a small sample from a single healthcare institution in Singapore and included only women who completed the intervention and agreed to participate in the interviews, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of women from other cultural backgrounds or healthcare settings.
