Antibiotic loaded solid lipid nanoparticles target bacteria in a pyogenic spondylitis rat model
Authors: Min Je Kim, Gong Ho Han, Wan-Kyu Ko, Seong Jun Kim, Daye Lee, Gi-Beom Ju, Seung Hun Sheen, Je Beom Hong, Seil Sohn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPyogenic spondylitis is treated with high-dose antibiotics for at least six weeks. In this study, we aimed to develop a novel therapeutic agent that can enhance bacterial targeting. We used solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) containing the antibiotic ampicillin (AMP), which were functionalized with the positively charged peptide LL-37. AMP-loaded SLNs (AMP-SLNs) were prepared, and LL-37 was attached to the AMP-SLNs (LL-37@AMP-SLNs). The physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles were characterized by particle size, zeta potential, morphology, drug release behavior, and hemocompatibility analyses. Furthermore, antibacterial activity, bacterial-targeting capability, and therapeutic efficacy were evaluated using Enterococcus faecalis-infected mesenchymal stem cells and a rat model of pyogenic spondylitis. The antibacterial performance of LL-37@AMP-SLNs was compared with that of SLNs and AMP-SLNs. The average size of the SLNs was approximately 342.66 ± 48.04 nm, which increased to 528.58 ± 35.64 nm after AMP loading and 695.58 ± 75.58 nm following LL-37 modification. The LL-37@AMP-SLNs exhibited sustained AMP release over four days and demonstrated enhanced antibacterial activity and bacterial-targeting capability compared with unmodified SLNs and AMP-SLNs. LL-37@AMP-SLNs were shown to inhibit bacterial growth by targeting the cytoplasmic membrane and the cell walls of Gram-positive bacteria. Moreover, the LL-37@AMP-SLNs treatment led to significantly enhanced antibacterial effects. In vitro studies showed significantly greater inhibition of bacterial growth, while intravenous administration of LL-37@AMP-SLNs significantly reduced bacterial burden and improved therapeutic outcomes in the pyogenic spondylitis rat model compared with free AMP treatment. We suggest that LL-37@AMP-SLNs can be a useful therapeutic agent for targeting bacteria in pyogenic spondylitis.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04853-7.Keywords: Bacterial spinal infection, Gram-positive bacteria, Targeted drug delivery system, Solid lipid nanoparticles, Antibiotics

Introduction

Spinal infections are mainly caused by bacteria and affect the vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and paraspinal tissues of the spine [1–3]. Pyogenic spondylitis is usually treated with high-dose antibiotics such as ampicillin (AMP) for at least six weeks [4]. However, these long-term high-dose antibiotic therapies can lead to antibiotic resistance and tend to decrease patients’ compliance [5]. Spinal infections account for more than 7% of musculoskeletal infections, and their incidence is increasing rapidly [6, 7].

LL-37 is a positively charged peptide from the cathelicidin gene family and is composed of a small protein (up to 50 amino acids) [8–10]. It is a component of the innate immune system and plays a crucial role against pathogens, including bacteria [11, 12]. LL-37 has a unique ability to selectively bind to negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, enabling both bacterial targeting and immune modulation [13, 14].

Lipids, the main components of cell membranes, have been studied extensively for many years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, lipid nanoparticles were used in vaccines [15, 16].

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) are conjugated with peptides and ligands to deliver targeted drugs, providing sustained release and long-term stability [17]. In addition, SLNs have emerged as promising drug delivery carriers because of their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, physical stability, and ability to provide sustained drug release. Encapsulation of antibiotics within SLNs may improve drug retention, protect encapsulated drugs from premature degradation, and enhance therapeutic efficacy at sites of infection. Recent studies have explored the incorporation of antimicrobial peptides, including LL-37, into various nanocarrier systems to enhance antibacterial activity and improve targeted drug delivery. LL-37-functionalized nanoparticles have been reported to exhibit enhanced interactions with bacterial membranes owing to the cationic nature of the peptide and its affinity for negatively charged bacterial cell surfaces [18]. In addition to its direct antimicrobial activity, LL-37 has been utilized as a targeting ligand to improve the localization of therapeutic agents at sites of bacterial infection [19].

Despite these advances, the application of LL-37-functionalized solid lipid nanoparticles for targeted antibiotic delivery in pyogenic spondylitis has not been extensively investigated [20]. Furthermore, achieving and maintaining effective antibiotic concentrations at the infection site remains challenging because of limited vascularization and biofilm formation within infected tissues. Therefore, the development of targeted drug delivery systems capable of enhancing local antibacterial efficacy while reducing systemic exposure is of considerable clinical interest.

Therefore, we hypothesize that LL-37-conjugated SLNs containing AMP can offer a targeted antibiotic therapy.

Experimental section

Materials

Stearic acid(Cat. 57-11-4), Tween-20(Cat. 9005-64-5), and AMP(Cat. 69-53-4) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Cat 10569004, GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat. 12483-020, GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS, Cat. 15140-122, GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA). Thirty male Sprague-Dawley rats were commercially obtained from Raonbio Inc. (Yongin, Republic of Korea).

Characteristics of LL-37@AMP-SLNs

The SLNs and AMP-SLNs used in this study were prepared as described in the literature [21, 22]. Briefly, 100 mg of stearic acid powder, 30 mg of AMP powder, and 150 µL of Tween 20 were dissolved in 1.35 mL of deionized water at 80 ℃ for 30 min. After 30 min, the hot dissolved solution was loaded into a double-syringe mini-extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids, USA), and 15 cycles were extruded through a 100 nm polycarbonate membrane. The extruded solution was transferred to 50 mL of ice-cold deionized water and stirred for one hour. To attach LL-37 to the prepared AMP-SLNs, the AMP-SLNs pellet obtained by centrifugation was dissolved in 3 mL of normal saline. Afterwards, 30 µg of LL-37 peptide was added, and the solution was gently stirred for two hours. A transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the LL-37@AMP-SLNs was obtained from an H-7100 device, and dynamic light scattering (DLS) and the zeta potential were used to measure the sizes and surface charges, respectively.

Release test of AMP from AMP-SLNs in vitro

For the in vitro release study, AMP-SNPs suspensions dispersed in saline were aliquoted into separate Eppendorf tubes (1 mL per tube). Afterwards, the sample was incubated at 37 ℃ and 100 rpm for four days. Individual samples were centrifuged and the supernatants were analyzed for drug release by ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectrophotometry at each time point (2, 4, 6, 8 h, 1 day, 2 days, 3 days, and 4 days). The standard curve was generated from absorbance values at various concentrations of AMP. The drug concentration (100 µg/mL) was calculated from the drug loading amount of the solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) assuming complete drug release (100% release). Released AMP levels were expressed in %.

Erythrocyte hemolysis assay

The impact of the SLNs on RBCs (Sensititre™; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cleveland, OH, USA; Cat. 00-4333-57) was evaluated by hemolysis assays. To do this, 5 mL of whole blood was collected from the jugular vein of the rats. The RBCs were isolated from serum by centrifugation at 1,000 x g for 10 min. The RBCs were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline. Various concentrations of SLNs, AMP-SLNs, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs (100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 1,000 µg/ml), a negative control (treated with phosphate buffer saline, 0% lysis), and a positive control (treated with 20% TritonX-100, 100% lysis) were added to each different 500 µL RBC pellet. The RBC pellets were incubated at 37 ℃. After one hour, the samples were centrifuged to isolate the RBCs. Afterwards, 100 µL of the supernatant of all samples was transferred to a 96-well plate. Bacteriological culture

The E. faecalis [ATCC19433] was sourced from the Korean Collection for Type Cultures (KCTC). E. faecalis was used for both the in vitro antibacterial assays and the establishment of the pyogenic spondylitis infection model because it is a clinically relevant gram-positive pathogen associated with musculoskeletal infections. The E. faecalis colonies were seeded into 5 mL of brain heart infusion (BHI) broth overnight at 37 ℃. After incubation, the cultures were centrifuged at 1000 x g for ten minutes, and the cell pellet was washed with PBS. Afterwards, E. faecalis was cultured on BHI agar at 37 ℃ under aerobic conditions.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay

The E. faecalis was diluted with fresh BHI broth and standardized to an optical density of 1.0 at 600 nm using a microplate reader. The MIC assay was conducted in BHI broth medium using various concentrations of SLNs, AMP-SLNs, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs (100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 1,000 µg/mL) with adjusted bacteria and with incubation at 37 ℃ under aerobic conditions. After 24 h of incubation, the MIC was confirmed by visual turbidity using a microplate reader at 600 nm.

Primary culture of MSCs

For MSC differentiation, bone marrow (BM) cells derived from the femurs and tibias of the rats were collected and cultured. The BM cells were harvested by rinsing the bone’s inner cavity with 10 mL of a medium containing DMEM, 20% FBS, 2% glutamate, and 1% PS. The rinsed BM cells were centrifuged for 10 min at 500 x g. Erythrocytes were lysed using a red blood cell lysis buffer. Cells were incubated in a culture-treated petri dish for four hours at 37 ℃ to exclude resident macrophages. Afterwards, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged. The pellet was dissociated with 150 mL of DMEM containing 20% FBS, 2% glutamate, and 1% PS. An amount of 10 mL of the suspended cells was distributed to each petri dish. Each dish was cultivated at 37 ℃ in 5% CO2. Medium changes were performed every three days, and all dishes were cultured for 14 days. Finally, the MSCs were harvested and seeded for the subsequent experiments.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC) staining

ICC staining was conducted as previously described [23, 24]. We performed ICC staining to confirm that the SLNs-attached LL-37 (LL-37@SLNs) targeted E. faecalis in the MSCs. The MSCs were cultured for two days in DMEM with E. faecalis. After two days, 400 µg/mL of LL-37@SLNs was added, and culturing continued for one day. Afterwards, the cell medium was removed, and the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Fixed cells were treated with the primary antibody at 4℃ overnight. The E. faecalis antibody was used as the primary antibody (1:200; Cat. PA1-73120; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). After washing, Alexa Fluor 488 phalloidin (1:500; A12379; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) and Alexa Fluor 568 (1:500; A10042; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) secondary antibodies were applied for two hours at room temperature. Afterwards, the nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Cat. H-1800-10; Vector Laboratories, CA, USA). The expression of ICC staining was detected using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope.

Gram staining

The slides underwent air-drying before being fixed by heat. The fixed cells were stained using a BBL Gram stain kit (BD Biosciences, USA, Cat. BD 212539). All images were obtained under a light microscope (IX71: Olympus). The gram-positive bacteria in the region of interest (ROI) were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health (NIH), Bethesda, MD, USA).

Western blotting

Western blotting was conducted as previously described [25, 26]. Equal amounts of MSC protein (20 µg) were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to methanol-activated poly (vinylidene fluoride) membranes. The membranes were incubated with 5% skim milk for one hour to block nonspecific binding. They were probed with primary antibodies with p-ERK (1:1000; Cat. 4377 S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), p-JNK (1:1000; Cat. 4668 S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), p38 (p-p38; 1:1000; Cat. 9211 S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, US), and TNF-α (1:1000; Cat. A11534; ABclonal, Woburn, MA, USA). Subsequently, equal membranes were stripped and reprobed with t-ERK (1:1000; Cat. 9102 S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, US), t-JNK (1:100; Cat. 9258 S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, US), t-p38 (1:1000; Cat. 9212 S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, US), and β-actin (1:5000; Cat. G043; ABM, Richmond, BC, Canada). As an internal control, β-actin was also probed into the membranes. The visualized signal bands were detected using an ECL solution (Amersham, Cat. RPN2232, Buckinghamshire, UK) through a G: Box Chemi-XX6 gel doc system (Syngene, Frederick, MD, USA). The p/t form volumes and TNF-α/ β-actin volumes were calculated and quantified using Image-J software (NIH).

Development of the bacterial spinal infection rat model

Thirty male Sprague-Dawley rats were divided into a without-infection group (Normal, n = 3), an infection-only group (infection-only, n = 4), an infection + AMP group (AMP, n = 4), and an infection + LL-37@AMP-SLNs group (LL-37@AMP-SLNs, n = 4). Thirty male Sprague-Dawley rats were commercially obtained from Raonbio Inc. (Yongin, Republic of Korea).

Twelve rats were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane with oxygen. A midline incision was made in the back. A lumbar 2 total laminectomy was performed to expose the vertebral body. The exposed vertebral body was drilled with a 0.5 mm burr. After drilling, per 50 µL of E. faecalis was transplanted into the drilled hole. The surgical site was closed layer by layer. The rats were kept warm and housed separately, with free access to food. Seven days after transplantation, 20 mg/mL of AMP and 10 mg/mL of LL-37@AMP-SLNs were injected in the AMP group and the LL-37@AMP-SLNs group, respectively, via tail vein injection for seven consecutive days.

After seven days, all the groups were deeply anesthetized with Zoletil® (50 mg/kg, Virbac Laboratories, Carros, France) and Rompun® (10 mg/kg, Elanco Korea, Ansan, Korea) by transcardial perfusion. After cannulation of the left ventricular-ascending aorta, rapid perfusion was performed using ice-cold saline. When the became clear, 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline was perfused for 5 min.

Ethics statement

All rats were handled according to the regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of CHA University (approval number: IACUC220010) and according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health (NIH), Bethesda, MD, USA).

Post-mortem MRI

One week after the LL-37@AMP-SLNs treatment, all experimental rats were sacrificed, and the vertebral bodies were isolated. All vertebral bodies were fixed in a 10% formaldehyde solution. MRIs of the vertebral bodies were conducted using a 9.4 T Biospec 94/20 USR device (Bruker, Germany) one week after the treatment. The parameters were as follows: repetition time = 800 ms; echo time = 7.5 ms; slice thickness = 0.5 mm; slice gap = 0.5 mm.

Histological analysis

Seven days after the treatment, the vertebral bodies were collected for H&E staining and Gram staining. H&E staining was done as previously described [27, 28]. Briefly, the collected vertebral bodies were fixed with a 10% formaldehyde solution and decalcified in a 10% EDTA solution for two weeks. After fixed dehydration, paraffin-embedded slides were sectioned into Sect. 10 μm thick. All images were obtained under a slide scanner (ZEISS Axio Scan.Z1, USA).

Statistical analyses

Multiple comparisons among groups were performed by a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used as a post-hoc analysis method. Differences with p-values for which *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 were considered statistically significant.

Hemolytic activity of the synthesized SLNs

Red blood cells (RBCs) after exposure to various concentrations of synthesized SLNs (100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 1,000 µg/mL) for one hour are shown in Fig. 2A. A hemolysis assay found that all groups showed hemolytic damage at a concentration of 1,000 µg/mL (Fig. 2B, SLNs: 7.62% ± 0.16, AMP-SLNs: 7.61 ± 0.15, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs: 24.00% ± 0.06, ***p < 0.001 and $$$p < 0.001). However, not all groups showed hemolytic damage at a concentration of 500 µg/mL (Fig. 2B, SLNs: 1.07% ± 0.08, AMP-SLNs: 1.06% ± 0.03, and LL37@AMP-SLNs: 1.48 ± 0.01). According to the criterion in the Standard Test Method for Analysis of the Hemolytic Properties of Nanoparticles (ASTM) E2524-08, a hemolysis percentage of > 5% indicates that any RBC damage is caused by the test nanoparticles [30].

Antibiotic effects of LL-37@AMP-SLNs

We tested the Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis) growth rate in various concentrations (100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 1,000 µg/mL) of synthesized SLNs (Fig. 2C). The initial optical density was prepared as 1.0 (3 × 108 CFU/mL). After one day, treatments with concentrations of synthesized SLNs showed that 500 µg/mL of LL-37@AMP-SLNs inhibited bacterial growth more than SLNs and AMP-SLNs (Fig. 2C, SLNs: 0.98, AMP-SLNs: 0.95, and LL37@AMP-SLNs: 0.83). In addition, 1,000 µg/mL of LL-37@AMP-SLNs inhibited bacterial growth more than SLNs and AMP-SLNs (Fig. 2C, SLNs: 0.98, AMP-SLNs: 0.86, and LL37@AMP-SLNs: 0.52).

Based on the hemolytic effect and the bacterial growth outcomes, an antimicrobial effect was evaluated using a concentration of 500 µg/mL (Fig. 2D). After one day of treatment with the synthesized SLNs, the survival rates of gram-positive bacteria were more reduced in the SLNs, AMP-SLNs, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs cases compared to those in the control specimens. However, the LL-37@AMP-SLNs showed stronger antimicrobial effects compared to those of the SLNs and AMP-SLNs (Fig. 2E, SLNs: 100% ± 1.23, AMP-SLNs: 91.12% ± 2.80, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs: 42.59% ± 3.88, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001).

LL-37@AMP- SLNs for targeting

As shown in Fig. 3A, we confirmed that LL-37-bound SLNs targeted bacteria in mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) infected with E. faecalis. SLNs without LL-37 did not target MSC or E. faecalis, whereas SLNs with LL-37 targeted MSC and E. faecalis.

We examined the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway by means of western blotting to determine whether the macrophages were activated by LL-37@AMP-SLNs (Fig. 3B–F). The phosphorylated volume per total volume (p/t) of extracellular-signal-regulated kinase (ERK) in the LL-37@AMP-SLNs case was significantly increased relative to those in the Control, SLNs, and AMP-SLNs specimens (Fig. 3C, Control: 1.00 ± 0.03, SLNs: 1.11 ± 0.05, AMP-SLNs: 1.13 ± 0.07, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs: 1.56 ± 0.08, ***p < 0.001). The p/t ratio of c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) in the LL-37@AMP-SLNs was also significantly increased compared to those in the Control and SLNs specimens (Fig. 3D, Control: 1.00 ± 0.06, SLNs: 1.01 ± 0.01, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs: 1.23 ± 0.04, ***p < 0.001). The p/t ratio of p38 in the LL-37@AMP-SLNs was significantly increased compared to those in the Control, SLNs, and AMP-SLNs specimens (Fig. 3E, Control: 1.00 ± 0.01, SLNs: 0.98 ± 0.01, AMP-SLNs: 1.09 ± 0.01, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs: 1.12 ± 0.03, ***p < 0.001). The tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) per β-actin volume in the LL-37@AMP-SLNs was also significantly increased compared to those in the Control and SLNs specimens (Fig. 3F, Control: 1.00 ± 0.12, SLNs: 1.04 ± 0.09, AMP-SLNs: 1.30 ± 0.07, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs: 1.68 ± 0.05, ***p < 0.001).

In vivo study of LL-37@AMP-SLNs

The four groups of in vivo experiments are shown in Fig. 4, which illustrates the overall experimental design of the spinal bacterial infection rat model and the treatment schedule. Seven days after infection, systemic treatment was administered via tail vein injection of either ampicillin (AMP, 20 mg/mL) or LL-37-conjugated AMP-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles (LL-37@AMP-SLNs, 10 mg/mL). The animals were assigned into four groups: normal control (Group A), infection-only (Group B), AMP-treated (Group C), and LL-37@AMP-SLNs-treated (Group D), as summarized in Fig. 4. After seven days of treatment, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was conducted to verify that the infection was improved (Fig. S1A). The infection site is indicated by the yellow dashed lines in the image. We confirmed that the infection was treated more in the LL-37@AMP-SLNs group than in the infection-only and AMP groups.

To observe the LL37@AMP-SLNs antimicrobial effects in the Pyogenic spondylitis rat models, we stained the vertebral body in the normal, infection-only, AMP, and LL-37@AMP-SLNs groups with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Gram staining (Fig. 5A, B). Discussion

Bacterial toxins are responsible for excessive inflammatory responses and tissue damage that can lead to abscesses and cellulitis. Pyogenic spondylitis is usually treated with long-term high-dose antibiotics such as AMP [4].

Other researchers have reported experiments involving a rat infection model in which Gram-positive bacteria were inoculated into the tibia [31, 32]. We created a bacterial infection model by inoculating Gram-positive bacteria into the vertebral bodies and discs where clinical diseases occur (Fig. 4). We evaluated whether the LL-37 conjugated SLNs containing AMP targeted bacteria and produced a therapeutic effect (Fig. 1A–F). In the synthesized AMP-SLNs, approximately 80% of the AMP was released within one day and continued to be released for four days thereafter (Fig. 1G).

Following AMP encapsulation and LL-37 conjugation, particle size increased from 342.66 ± 48.04 nm for SLNs to 528.58 ± 35.64 nm for AMP-SLNs and 695.58 ± 75.58 nm for LL-37@AMP-SLNs, while the zeta potential changed from − 31.80 mV to − 14.19 mV. These changes are consistent with successful LL-37 surface modification and have been associated with enhanced interactions with bacterial membranes and improved antimicrobial activity. A hemolytic assay showed that LL-37@AMP-SLNs at a concentration of 500 µg/mL exhibited effective antibacterial effects without causing erythrocyte membrane damage (Fig. 2A–E). To evaluate bacterial targeting, MSCs infected with E. faecalis were treated with nanoparticles. LL-37@AMP-SLNs were shown to target infected MSCs more effectively (Fig. 3A), confirming their ability to bind to bacterial cytoplasmic membranes and the cell wall [8, 33]. In particular, LL-37 can target teichoic acid in the cell walls of bacteria [34]. Previous studies have also demonstrated that LL-37 reduces bacterial burden and exhibits potent antimicrobial activity [35, 36].

TNF-α induces inflammation by activating the main signal pathways of ERK, JNK, and p38 in the MAPK pathways [27, 37]. In addition, TNF-α is essential for macrophage activation, phagosome activation, and for the differentiation of monocytes. Moreover, LL-37 acts as a chemoattractant to guide monocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages to infection sites, and it regulates differentiation into M1 phenotype macrophages. Macrophages are responsible for the phagocytic uptake of bacteria [38].

However, bacteria suppress TNF-α production, which increases bacterial survival and exacerbates bacterial infectious diseases [39]. Therefore, TNF-α is essential for host defense, and depletion of TNF-α increases the risk of infection or disease reactivation. LL-37@AMP-SLNs increase the ERK, JNK, and p38 pathways as well as TNF-α production (Fig. 3B–F). The infection method and in vivo experimental design are illustrated in Fig. 4. After seven days of treatment, MRI was used to verify that the extent of infection had been reduced (Fig. S1A). Characteristic changes in the vertebral bodies and discs were found in T1-weighted images, and high-intensity signals due to infection could be seen on T2-weighted images [40]. In our in vivo study, MRI showed infections in both the vertebral body and intravertebral disc (Fig. S1A). LL-37@AMP-SLNs or AMP were injected into the tail vein for seven days, and the antibacterial effect of the LL-37@AMP-SLNs group was greater than that of the AMP group.

The histopathological changes and recovery from infection in the vertebral bodies were evaluated by means of Gram staining. In our results, the numbers of gram-positive bacteria were decreased in the LL-37@AMP-SLNs group more than they were in the infection-only and AMP groups (Fig. 5A, B). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to investigate the effects of drugs in a bacterial infection model created by inoculating the vertebral body and disc with Gram-positive bacteria. Several limitations should be acknowledged. The loading efficiency and surface density of LL-37 on AMP-SLNs were not quantitatively determined, and the serum stability, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, and in vivo targeting efficiency of the nanoparticles were not directly evaluated. Future studies should address these factors to further validate the therapeutic potential of LL-37@AMP-SLNs [19].

Therefore, we suggest that LL-37@AMP-SLNs can be a useful antibacterial therapy for bacterial infectious diseases, including pyogenic spondylitis.

Conclusion

LL-37@AMP-SLNs target gram-positive bacteria and are shown to be an effective antibacterial treatment in the bacterial spinal infection rat model. Therefore, we suggest that LL-37@AMP-SLNs can be a useful antibacterial therapy for pyogenic spondylitis.
