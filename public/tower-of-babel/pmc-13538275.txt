Arthroscopic Suprapectoral Biceps Tenodesis Using a Standard Suture Anchor Is Safe and Effective
Authors: Xavier A. Duralde, Caroline A. Heramb, Jennifer N. Blanda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the safety and efficacy of an arthroscopic technique of suprapectoral biceps tenodesis using a standard absorbable double‐loaded suture anchor.MethodsThis retrospective study includes all patients who underwent arthroscopic biceps tenodesis by the senior author between December 2017 and June 2020. Biceps tenodesis was performed in patients who had failed conservative management and exhibited evidence of biceps pathology at the time of surgery that appeared to be contributing to the patient's pain. Evaluation criteria included integrity of the repair by physical examination, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, visual analog scale for pain, and recording any complications and the need for revision surgery.ResultsA total of 80/96 patients (83%) were available for evaluation at a minimum of 2 years including 43 men and 37 women with an average age of 53 (range 17‐72). Postoperatively, the average in‐office follow‐up was 17 months (range 1.5‐79 months), and the average phone contact follow‐up was 55 months (range 24‐89 months). Ninety‐one percent (73/80) of patients in this series maintained normal biceps contour. The average American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score improved from 41 (range: 0‐82) to an average of 83 (range 18‐100) (P < .001). Visual analog scale pain score improved from 7 (range: 2‐10) to 3 (range: 0‐8) (P < .001). Minimal clinically important difference thresholds (1.47 for Visual analog scale, 14.3 for American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score) were met by 81% and 78.5% of patients, respectively. There were 5 minor complications. Four additional patients complained of pain at the bicipital groove, and a total of 5 patients required revision surgery for either biceps failure or ongoing pain.ConclusionsArthroscopic suprapectoral biceps tenodesis using a standard suture anchor is safe and effective in the management of symptomatic biceps pathology refractory to conservative management. It offers reliable improvements in pain and function while maintaining biceps contour. It has a low complication rate, avoiding many of the potential complications reported after an open approach.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, retrospective therapeutic study.

Pathology of the long head of the biceps brachii tendon (LHBT) can present in isolation or in association with other shoulder conditions and is a well‐recognized and often difficult‐to‐diagnose generator of shoulder pain and dysfunction. 1 Surgery is indicated in cases of failed conservative treatment. Conflicting literature exists with many studies suggesting that the results of tenodesis are superior to those of tenotomy because it maintains the tension‐length relation of the biceps muscle and decreases muscle spasm and fatigue. 2 , 3 Other recent Level I studies and systematic reviews refute this and have found that the major advantage of tenodesis is cosmetic. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Putting arguments regarding the benefits of tenodesis aside, one of the major arguments against tenodesis is the time and expense of additional implants often required in performing it. 6 Despite ongoing controversy regarding the benefits of tenodesis over tenotomy, the incidence of biceps tenodesis using both open and arthroscopic techniques continues to rise. 10

Multiple techniques have been published describing both arthroscopic and open biceps tenodesis, indicating that the ideal method has not been identified. 10 , 11 Techniques vary by location and type of fixation used. Sites of fixation can be divided into 2 main groups, namely, suprapectoral and subpectoral based on its location relative to the pectoralis major insertion. Fixation techniques are also varied and include bone tunnels, simple sutures, cortical buttons, interference screws, and various types of suture anchors. All have advantages and disadvantages.

Multiple challenges and complications have been described with all currently available techniques, the most common of which is failure of fixation with subsequent deformity of the biceps muscle belly with failure rates of 7% to 10% reported in clinical series. 4 , 7 Concerns have been raised by some investigators that suprapectoral fixation above the bicipital groove runs the risk of ongoing persistent shoulder pain postoperatively because of ongoing tension on a diseased portion of the tendon. 12 The infrapectoral technique is an open one that introduces a number of additional potential risks. Complications associated uniquely with the open surgery include nerve injury, 13 pathologic humeral fractures at the fixation site, and rare cases of infection. 14 Techniques in which the tendon is first released from the labrum and subsequently tenodesed require methods to determine the normal resting length of the biceps to match the natural resting length and avoid over‐ or undertensioning the tendon, both of which are associated with increased pain. 15

The subacromial locking stitch anchor (SALSA) technique provides a solution for the described challenges and complications associated with current techniques. 16 In the beach chair position, the arthroscope is placed via a posterior viewing portal, and a midlateral working portal is used for suture manipulation and knot tying. The anchor is placed percutaneously via an anterolateral puncture wound after localization using a spinal needle. This technique uses running, locking stitches from a double‐loaded suture anchor placed arthroscopically in the suprapectoral region of the bicipital groove that incorporate both the biceps tendon and the transverse humeral ligament to obtain multiple points of fixation (Figure 1). The locking stitch configuration is obtained by rotating the suture passer around the post suture prior to each pass through the tendon. The anchor is placed in the central area of the bicipital groove, and sutures incorporate this area of the biceps tendon in the tenodesis. Length/tension of the biceps is maintained anatomically because the tenodesis is performed with the LHBT in situ at normal resting length prior to tendon release from the labrum proximally. No axillary dissection is required, and the suture anchor is placed in cancellous metaphyseal bone, thereby avoiding the risk of pathologic fracture from a cortical stress riser associated with more inferior sites of tenodesis. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of an arthroscopic technique of suprapectoral biceps tenodesis using a standard absorbable double‐loaded suture anchor. We hypothesized that this technique is safe and effective in the treatment of long‐head of biceps pathology with low incidence of loss of reduction, complications, or need for reoperation.

METHODS

Patient Population

This retrospective study included all patients who had undergone arthroscopic biceps tenodesis using the SALSA technique between December 2017 and June 2020. All cases were performed by the senior author (X.A.D.), and only patients with a minimum of 2 years of follow‐up were included. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained from Piedmont Healthcare Institutional Review Board (1599060‐1).

All patients had failed a course of conservative management or a prior surgery. Biceps injury typically represented only a portion of the pathology encountered in these patients.

Patients were examined in the office after surgery and were contacted by phone postoperatively at which point the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) and visual analog scale (VAS) scores were obtained. Only 1 patient had an in‐person follow‐up of less than 3 months, and he had no complaints related to the biceps on telephone follow‐up. Evaluation criteria included integrity of the repair by physical examination, ASES, VAS for pain, and recording any complications and the need for revision surgery. Because almost all patients underwent concomitant procedures in the shoulder, attempts were made to distinguish between ongoing pain from the biceps and other sources of pain. Pain directly at the tenodesis site or pain/tenderness along the biceps muscle belly was attributed to the tenodesis.

Preoperative and postoperative VAS and ASES scores were compared both overall and stratified by Workers’ Compensation (WC) status. Paired t‐tests were used to assess within‐group differences (preop vs postop). Between‐group comparisons of score changes (postop‐preop) for VAS and ASES between patients receiving WC and those who were not were evaluated using 2‐sample pooled t‐tests. Outcomes were additionally analyzed and reported by sex in accordance with the Sex and Gender Equity in Research guidelines. 17 Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for ASES and VAS scores was calculated based on the study population using a distribution‐based approach, defined as one‐half the standard deviation of individual pre‐ to postoperative change scores (Δ). Patient acceptable symptom state and substantial clinical benefit analyses were not performed because no independent anchor questions assessing patient satisfaction or perceived improvement were available in this cohort. Analyses were conducted using a complete‐case approach, assuming data were missing completely at random. As noted in multiple previous studies, our WC patients displayed inferior results compared with non‐WC patients. 18

RESULTS

A total of 80 patients were available for 2‐year follow‐up after biceps tenodesis using the SALSA technique. Of the initial 96 patients in this cohort, 4 patients had died, 1 refused to participate with the follow‐up questionnaire, and 11 (12%) of patients could not be contacted despite multiple attempts. This left 80 patients (83%) for evaluation (Figure 2). There were 43 men and 37 women with an average age of 53 years (range 17‐72). Patients were examined in the office at an average follow‐up of 17 months (range 1‐579 months) after surgery and were contacted by phone at an average follow‐up of 55 months (range 24‐89 months) postoperatively. There were 32 WC patients and 48 non‐WC patients. Biceps pathology noted at the time of surgery included tendinitis/hypertrophy in 23, partial tears in 30, instability in 15, and SLAP tears in 12. Concomitant procedures were performed on most patients 89% (71). These included the following: acromioplasty 88% (70), rotator cuff repair 58% (46), and distal clavicle excision 84% (67). An isolated biceps tenodesis was performed in only 9 cases, all of which were revision cases of previously failed arthroscopic decompressions in whom the biceps was suspected of being the ongoing source of pain.

Flowchart of patient selection process including exclusion criteria for patients undergoing biceps tenodesis. (SALSA, subacromial locking stitch anchor.)

Normal biceps contour was maintained in 91% (73/80) of patients in this series after the primary SALSA procedure. In terms of repair integrity, there were 3 (4%) patients who complained of mild deformity in their biceps who did not seek further treatment. There were 4 frank failures with loss of biceps contour (5%), 2 of which occurred traumatically with falls, 1 because of noncompliance with weightlifting at 6 weeks postoperatively, and 1 with a jerk of the arm while sleeping. Of the patients whose tenodesis failed, 3 elected to undergo additional revision surgery. Revision surgery consisted of an open soft tissue technique using a short oblique incision just anterior to the deltoid insertion on the proximal humerus. The biceps muscle belly and tendon were mobilized, and the biceps muscle fascia was sutured to the deltoid insertion fascia. Because the revision surgeries were performed distal to the failure site, the exact location of the failure was not visualized and is unknown. The 1 patient who was not evaluated in the office past 6 weeks postoperatively said he was doing well at that visit and was working with PT. His ASES scores improved from 7 preop to 71 postop. VAS improved from 5 preop to 2.5 postop. He had no biceps complaints and described a normal contour on telephone evaluation at 31 months postoperatively.

The average ASES score improved from 41 (range 0‐82) preoperatively to an average of 83 (range 18‐100) postoperatively (P < .001). VAS pain score improved from 7 (range 2‐10) preoperatively to 3 (range 0‐8) postoperatively (P < .001). Neither ASES nor VAS pain scores in this series were felt to be a pure indication of the results of biceps tenodesis because in 90% of patients, concomitant procedures were performed. The MCID was calculated as one‐half the standard deviation of individual pre‐ to postoperative change scores, yielding MCID thresholds of 1.47 points for VAS and 14.3 points for ASES. Based on these thresholds, 81% of patients achieved the MCID for VAS pain scores, and 78.5% achieved the MCID for ASES scores, indicating that most patients experienced clinically meaningful improvements in pain and shoulder function.

In non‐WC patients, ASES improved from a preoperative average of 46 (range 5‐82) to a postoperative score of 92 (range 28‐100) (P < .001), and VAS improved from a preoperative score of 6 (range 2‐10) to a postoperative score of 2 (range 0‐8) (P < .001). This compares to our WC patients whose preoperative ASES score of 32 (range 0‐73) improved to only 71 (range 18‐100) postoperatively (P < .001) and their preoperative VAS score of 7 (range 2‐10) improved to a postoperative score of only 5 (range 0‐8) (P < .001) (Table 1). VAS improvement between the 2 groups was statistically worse for WC patients, but ASES improvement was equal between both groups. Overall, 78.5% of patients achieved the MCID for ASES scores (95% confidence interval [CI]: 69.4‐87.5). The proportion of patients achieving MCID for ASES was 68.8% (95% CI: 52.7‐84.8) in the WC group and 85.1% (95% CI: 74.9‐95.3) in the non‐WC group; this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .0815). For VAS pain scores, 81.1% of patients overall achieved the MCID (95% CI: 72.4‐89.7). A significantly greater proportion of non‐WC patients achieved MCID for VAS compared with WC patients (89.4% [95% CI: 2.4‐89.7] vs 68.8% [95% CI: 52.7‐84.8], P = .022) (Table 2).

There was a total of 5 additional complications aside from tenodesis failure. These included 3 cases of arthrofibrosis, 2 of which resolved with physical therapy. One of these stiff patients complained of ongoing pain at the tenodesis site, and arthroscopic debridement was performed. At the time of surgery, dense subacromial scarring was found although full range of motion was noted on exam under anesthesia. Her biceps tenodesis site was benign, and she continued to have pain without improvement after this procedure. There was 1 allergic reaction to Toradol causing temporary edema and 1 case of mild intermittent numbness in the hand. There were no wound problems noted, no significant neurologic injuries, and no fractures of the humerus.

Four patients complained about ongoing pain at the biceps tenodesis site. This includes the 1 patient with arthrofibrosis mentioned below. Two additional patients complained of localized pain but refused further surgery. One additional (non‐WC) patient with an intact tenodesis also complained of persistent pain at the site of the tenodesis and required a revision arthroscopic debridement. Dense scarring was noted between the undersurface of the deltoid fascia and the bicipital groove and was arthroscopically released with care being taken to protect the axillary nerve. She noted significant improvement in pain after this procedure and was very satisfied with a final ASES of 90 and VAS of 2. A total of 5 patients (6%) required revision surgery after biceps tenodesis using the SALSA technique including 3 revisions for failure, 1 arthroscopic subacromial debridement for postoperative arthrofibrosis, and 1 arthroscopic debridement for scarring and pain at the tenodesis site.

DISCUSSION

This clinical series shows that arthroscopic suprapectoral tenodesis of the long head of biceps using a standard suture anchor is safe and effective in the management of biceps pathology. Biceps contour was excellent in over 90% of patients. There were very few failures or complications, and revision surgery for ongoing biceps pain was required in very few patients. Most importantly, complications associated with some open tenodesis techniques including humeral fracture, musculocutaneous nerve injury, wound infection, and challenges with biceps tension were avoided by using this technique.

Multiple series and systematic reviews have shown no significant differences in the results of suprapectoral versus infrapectoral tenodesis techniques, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 9 , 13 but there are many technical advantages to the suprapectoral approach that deserve emphasis. These advantages are related to the required approach, fixation techniques, and ease of appropriate tensioning, all of which favor the suprapectoral approach. The arthroscopic technique of suprapectoral tenodesis described here requires 1 additional anterolateral puncture wound and avoids an axillary incision and dissection along with the complications associated with that approach. Infection rates of up to 4% have been reported with the axillary approach to open biceps tenodesis. 14 An increase in wound complications including hematomas and wound infections has been reported when open biceps tenodesis is added to arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, whereas this has not been the case with arthroscopic techniques of biceps tenodesis. 13 , 19 , 20 Axillary dissection has also been associated with nerve injuries ranging from transient musculocutaneous nerve injury to permanent brachial plexus injuries. 19 , 21 , 22 Reports of actual fixation of the musculocutaneous and median nerve to bone as well as median nerve transection have been reported as complications of open biceps tenodesis. 22 , 23 A large case series by Yeung et al. also found a decreased incidence of nerve injury with arthroscopic versus open technique (0.35% vs 1.47%, P < .01). Although length of time required to perform this technique was not studied, it was felt to be no longer than that required by the open axillary approach used previously by the senior author.

The site of tenodesis using the SALSA technique is in the bicipital groove in the area with the highest incidence of pathology, 12 thereby eliminating the potential for persistent pain because of ongoing tension. Although some investigators have brought up theoretical concerns regarding the location of the biceps tenodesis, McCrum and others found no difference in anterior shoulder pain, cramping, weakness, or deformity for suprapectoral versus subpectoral tenodesis. 2 , 11 , 24 Bicipital groove pain is poorly understood after tenodesis but is seen with both suprapectoral and infrapectoral approaches. 11 In our series, 4 patients had groove pain which may be related to scar tissue between the undersurface of the deltoid and the bicipital groove. Ongoing pain at the site of tenodesis requiring revision surgery was seen in 2 cases and was improved significantly by arthrolysis of deltoid adhesions to the area of tenodesis in the 1 patient with scar tissue in this area.

The SALSA technique uses a suture anchor in a metaphyseal location, avoiding the creation of a significant stress riser in the proximal humeral diaphysis as seen with some subpectoral techniques that employ a cortical drill hole. Proximal humeral fractures have been described after techniques using keyholes, interference screws, or cortical buttons for fixation of the LHBT. 25 There are multiple case reports reporting this phenomenon both in the English and non‐English literature. 26 , 27 A recent military database review of over 15,000 cases of biceps tenodesis found 12 instances of associated proximal humerus fracture, all involving the cortical drill hole used in open subpectoral biceps tenodesis. 15 Biomechanical studies have validated the notion that large diaphyseal drill holes decrease the torsional strength of the proximal humerus, making it susceptible to fracture. 28 This risk is especially important for athletes who participate in overhead rotational sports such as baseball and racquet sports. 27

An additional advantage specific to our described technique is that the in situ tenodesis easily allows the surgeon to avoid over‐ or undertensioning the LHBT. Many tenodesis techniques describe an initial tenotomy of the LHBT followed by tenodesis later in the procedure, which creates the opportunity for improper tensioning of the tendon. Undertensioning the LHBT can result in adverse outcomes like those seen with tenotomy, including persistent cramp‐like pain and fatigue discomfort isolated to the biceps. 29 Overtensioning the LHBT is not without risk because this increases pullout forces at the site of tenodesis, which can ultimately result in failure. 2 In our described technique, tenodesis is performed prior to tenotomy of the more proximal portion of the tendon from the superior labrum, maintaining the tendon at its ideal resting tension.

The strength of the fixation device used for tenodesis is critical for the success of the procedure. The SALSA technique combines 2 separate elements to obtain tendon fixation and avoid failure, an arthroscopic anchor as well as locking stitches which incorporate the transverse humeral ligament obtaining multiple points of fixation. A biomechanical study comparing 3 tenodesis techniques (interference screw, suture anchor, and compressive rivet) reported that suture anchors had a significantly higher load to failure compared with rivets and that no difference was observed between interference screws versus suture anchors and interference screws versus rivets. 30 Although a variety of successful in situ techniques have been described previously, 31 the Krackow suture is a proven technique that shows decreased gap formation and increased load to failure and is not difficult to perform arthroscopically. 32 The use of a simple double‐loaded suture is cheaper than a loop‐and‐tac device which can be used in a similar fashion. The suture passer represents an additional cost except in cases which were combined with rotator cuff repair because this device was already being used. This occurred in over half of the cases in our series. The arthroscopic technique described here may have applications in other joints such as the hip, namely, in repair of abductor muscle avulsions. The healing rate of the biceps tenodesis in our series was 93% comparable to previously published techniques. 5

The controversy between long head of biceps tenotomy and tenodesis has raged for decades and will most likely continue because of the relative merits of each technique. 3 , 4 Several authors have recommended using patient expectations as a basis for deciding between tenotomy and tenodesis. 8 These discussions balance the benefits of tenodesis which include cosmesis and perhaps maintenance of strength and lack of cramping against the required additional operative time, surgical expertise, and the additional cost of needed for hardware necessary to accomplish it. The SALSA technique provides a reliable method of robust fixation that avoids potential complications including cramping, fracture, neurologic injury, or cosmetic deformity.

Limitations

This series has a number of limitations. As a nonrandomized retrospective study, this analysis is subject to selection bias, potential confounding, and limitations inherent to retrospective data collection, which may impact the ability to draw causal conclusions. Cost and time analysis was not performed but neither was felt to be excessive. Because outcome analyses included only patients with complete follow‐up, missing data may introduce attrition bias and reduce statistical power, limiting generalizability. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted primarily as descriptive outcomes rather than definitive comparative efficacy, and nonsignificant results should be viewed with caution. There was a 17% (16 patients) loss to follow‐up, but most failures of this technique occur within the first several months after surgery, and the number of cases lost prior to 6 months constituted only 5 cases (5%) of cases so we are confident that the failure rate because of loss of reduction is accurately shown in this series. The determination of ongoing biceps pain was subjective, but the location of the tenodesis and the biceps muscle belly were rarely the location of complaints. The absence of anchor‐based measures precluded calculation of patient acceptable symptom state and substantial clinical benefit. Biceps pathology encountered was heterogeneous. Given the amount of additional pathology in these patients, other sources of pain appeared more likely to account for ongoing pain in many cases although the exact cause for ongoing pain or low ASES scores could not be definitively assigned to either the biceps or concomitant pathology. Only the ASES score and VAS were used to determine improvement in pain and function, and their specificity was limited because of the commonly performed concomitant procedures.

CONCLUSIONS

Arthroscopic suprapectoral biceps tenodesis using a standard suture anchor is safe and effective in the management of symptomatic biceps pathology refractory to conservative management. It offers reliable improvements in pain and function while maintaining biceps contour. It has a low complication rate, avoiding many of the potential complications reported after an open approach.
