A New Stage and Classification of Osteonecrosis of the Femoral Head Based on MRI With Pathophysiological Interpretation
Authors: Mengran Shen, Hongxu Li, Haoyang Liu, Qihan Ma, Bailiang Wang, Liming Cheng, Jinhui Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTo explore the influence of the new stage and classification system based on the correlation between MRI and pathological features on the collapse, symptoms, and function of femoral head necrosis. Existing radiographic staging systems primarily evaluate structural changes and lesion morphology but provide limited information regarding intraosseous biological activity and pathological heterogeneity. Clinically, patients within the same ARCO stage often demonstrate markedly different symptoms, collapse patterns, and treatment responses, highlighting the need for an MRI‐based classification reflecting underlying pathological status.MethodsThis retrospective study included 100 patients (200 hips) who were diagnosed with bilateral ONFH. According to the degree of fibrosis in the necrotic area in a mid‐coronal section on hip MRI, the MRI stage of ONFH can be divided into three types. F1, borderline fibrosis (banded low signal); F2, partial fibrosis in the necrotic area (partial low signal); F3, global fibrosis in the necrotic area (global low signal). Based on the level of inflammatory response, the MRI classification of ONFH can be divided into four. E0, pathological stable type (no bone marrow edema and inflammatory); E1, partial edema of the femoral head and neck; E2, global edema of the femoral head and neck; E3, extensive edema of the proximal femur. The radiological evaluations were based on plain X‐rays and MRI.ResultsThere was a significant difference in the collapse rate between F1 (7.1%), F2 (66.3%), and F3 (92.1%) (p < 0.001). The collapse rate in E0 (11.1%), E1 (66.7%), E2 (85.3%), and E3 (77.8%) was significantly different (p < 0.001). There was also a significant difference in pain level and hip function between MRI stage and classification (p < 0.001). The later the stage, the higher the pain level, and similar results in hip functions. With the aggravation of bone marrow edema, the patient's pain level increases and hip function decreases. ROC analysis demonstrated that MRI stage based on fibrosis showed the highest discriminative ability for femoral head collapse (AUC = 0.819), compared with ARCO stage (AUC = 0.756) and MRI classification based on inflammatory response (AUC = 0.714). However, given the cross‐sectional design and the potential for incorporation bias, these findings should be interpreted with caution.ConclusionMRI‐based assessment of fibrosis extent and inflammatory activity provides additional information on intraosseous pathological status that is not captured by conventional radiographic stage systems and is significantly associated with femoral head collapse, pain, and hip function. This classification may have potential value for disease stratification and clinical assessment. However, its role in guiding treatment decisions and predicting longitudinal outcomes requires further validation in prospective studies.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: fibrosis, inflammatory response, MRI, osteonecrosis, pathology

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a disease with a high rate of disability. The progression of ONFH is related to the inflammation in the femoral head [1, 2]. The treatment of femoral head necrosis includes conservative treatment and surgical treatment. Conservative treatment including restricted weightbearing activities of the hip joint, drug therapy, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, extracorporeal shock wave therapy [3]. Surgical treatment can be separated between the femoral head sparing procedures which include Core decompression, bone graft, osteotomies and the arthroplasty procedures [4]. However, the average of patients with osteonecrosis is younger than that of osteoarthritis, meaning that if surgery is needed, osteonecrosis patients will require multiple surgeries [5]. Studies have reported that patients classified with the same severity stage responded differently after similar joint‐preserving surgery [6, 7]. The study found that in some patients with ONFH, although the femoral head has collapsed, the degree of collapse of the femoral head is not progressing, and the function of the damaged hip joint can be maintained without surgical intervention [8]. This interesting phenomenon may be attributed to the limitations and defects of the existing imaging classification system, as well as inconsistencies between histopathology and radiological stage [7]. Therefore, a decision must be made on whether to undergo surgical treatment. However, determining the pathological state of ONFH is indeed a difficult challenge. In order to guide clinical treatment, based on the imaging features of ONFH, different classification systems have been developed to evaluate the stage of severity and prognosis of the disease, including the ARCO phase, Ficat classification, JIC classification and CJFH classification [8]. Most methods for predicting the progression of ONFH are to evaluate the location, size, and volume of the necrotic lesion by using X‐ray, CT and MRI. But these classifications rarely involve pathological conditions inside the femoral head which is associated with ONFH state of progression and has guiding significance for treatment. It is necessary to propose a pathological stage system that can reflect the progression of the disease.

The MRI can reflect the histological changes of ONFH, and it can describe in detail the size of lesions, enabling accurate staging of the disease, which is used to decide later therapeutic strategies. When osteonecrosis occurs, the tissue adjacent to the necrotic area reveals increased marrow reaction and inflammatory reaction which can reflect different signals on MRI [6, 9]. Researches indicate that when necrotic bone tissue is replaced with repair tissue, MRI T1‐weighted images reveal low signal intensity resembling fibrosis. Conversely, when inflammation triggers a bone marrow response, MRI T2‐weighted images demonstrate high signal intensity indicative of edema [10, 11, 12]. We hence need to conduct extensive research on the relationship between imaging features and histopathological features to further refine and update widely accepted classification systems.

In clinical practice, we found that inflammatory response plays an important role in the pathological progress of ONFH. Adapala et al. [13] proposed a possible mechanism of chronic inflammation during the repair process after ONFH and its role in the development of collapse of the femoral head. Necrosis of the bone tissue in the femoral head due to the loss of blood supply results in the release of alarmins, which are recognized by TLR4 on macrophages. The activation of TLR4 induces the inflammatory responses in macrophages by increasing proliferation and migration and inflammatory cytokine production. The sustained inflammation caused by this promotes the formation of fibrous vascular repair tissue, inhibits the formation of new bone, and increases bone resorption. Consequently, because of the loss of mechanical strength, the femoral head develops a flattened deformity. The inflammatory response of bone tissue shows high signal intensity on MRI T2 phase, which is bone marrow edema (BME). From a histological point of view, the BME corresponds to an inflammatory infiltrate with macrophages, lymphocytes T and B, plasma cells, and osteoclasts; the intensity of MRI signal correlates with the number of inflammatory cells in the site [14].

Therefore, this study intends to classify the manifestations of bone marrow edema like signal intensity and analyze the destruction of bone tissue by inflammatory reaction through case series. The result of the destruction and absorption of necrotic bone tissue structure is fibrosis. Fibrosis is the ultimate result of bone necrosis repair response or process. After fibrosis of bone tissue, it will not transform towards the direction of bone tissue. From the perspective of pathological reactions after osteonecrosis, fibrosis is the final stage of osteonecrosis repair response, which manifests as a fiber‐like hypointense signal on an MRI. This study also intends to analyze the correlation between the pathological stage of osteonecrosis and the clinical manifestations and other imaging findings, and to analyze its value in judging the progression of ONFH and guiding treatment.

Materials and Methods

Patients Data

The study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of China‐Japan Friendship Hospital (2024‐KY‐357). Inclusion criteria: ① Patients who visited our hospital from January 2022 to December 2022 with bilateral femoral head necrosis enrolled in this study; ② Unified method is used to take hip X‐rays and MRI; ③ Patients are willing to participate and cooperate with the study. Exclusion criteria: ① Incomplete clinical data; ② History of hip preservation surgery, along with conditions such as metabolic disorders in the femoral head, congenital malformations, joint tuberculosis, suppurative arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, etc. These patients were diagnosed with bilateral femoral head necrosis using X‐ray and MRI, and determined whether there is collapse of the femoral head based on radiological features. Two authors staged the patients using MRI data. If the stage by the two authors was consistent, the patient's stage was confirmed. If the stage was inconsistent, the corresponding author re‐staged the patient, and the final stage was determined. The Harris score was assessed for the patients using the same method to assess patients' hip joint function. Assess patients' pain levels using the Harris Hip Score and the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) pain assessment system and collect basic information of patients, such as etiology, gender, age, BMI. Inter‐ and intra‐observer reliability were assessed for the four core assessments, including MRI stage, ARCO typing, VAS score, and Harris hip score. Cohen's κ statistics were used for categorical variables, whereas intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated for continuous variables. Inter‐observer agreement was evaluated based on the first independent readings of two observers. Intra‐observer agreement was assessed by repeated readings performed at least 4 weeks apart. κ values were interpreted according to conventional criteria, and ICC values ≥ 0.75 were considered indicative of excellent reliability. Given that both hips from the same patient were included, generalized estimating equations (GEE) were used to account for within‐patient clustering. Patient ID was specified as the clustering variable, and an exchangeable working correlation structure was applied.

ONFH Stage and Classification in MRI Evaluation

Research indicates that the intensity of inflammatory responses influences the pace of bone tissue breakdown and reabsorption, determining whether osteonecrosis leads to destructive or reconstructive repair. A moderate inflammatory response is essential to uphold the equilibrium between resorption and repair within the bone tissue structure [2, 15]. The various pathological conditions observed following osteonecrosis, specifically the extent of fibrosis and bone marrow edema, indicate the progression of the pathological response. The stage of ONFH can be divided into three types (F1, F2, and F3) according to the extent of fibrosis in the necrotic area in a mid‐coronal section on hip on MRI sequence (Fibrotic like signal) (Figure 1). F1, borderline fibrosis (banded low signal); F2: partial fibrosis in the necrotic area (partial low signal); F3: global fibrosis in the necrotic area (global low signal). We classify based on the morphology and extent of fibrosis in necrotic tissue. F1: Fibrosis is present at the edge of necrotic tissue; F2: Building on F1, fibrosis occupies up to 2/3 of the necrotic tissue; F3: Building on F1, over 2/3 of the necrotic tissue is fibrotic. Based on the level of inflammatory response (edema‐like signal intensity), the MRI classification of ONFH can be divided into four types on MRI T2 sequence (E0, E1, E2, and E3) (Figure 2). E0: pathological stable type (no bone marrow edema and inflammatory); E1: Bone marrow edema in the femoral head and neck, involving less than half of the femoral head area, without affecting the proximal femur; E2: Overall swelling in the femoral head and neck exceeds half of the femoral head area, without affecting the proximal femur; E3: Edema area exceeds half of the femoral head area with extensive swelling in the proximal femur. Based on the proposed MRI‐based pathological stage, we further outline a conceptual treatment framework to illustrate how this classification may inform clinical decision‐making (Figure 3). This framework integrates MRI‐defined fibrosis burden and bone marrow edema status with conventional ARCO stage, aiming to reflect the underlying biological activity and mechanical stability of the femoral head. The framework serves to demonstrate how MRI‐pathological features may complement existing stage systems in stratifying patients and identifying those who may benefit from closer monitoring or joint‐preserving strategies.

Statistical Analysis

The data was analyzed using the SPSS version 26.0 statistical software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Mean ± standard deviation and frequencies were calculated for all general demographic and routine clinical data. Statistical data were expressed as rates, and χ 2 test or ANOVA test were used for comparison between groups. Spearman correlation analysis was used to test the relevance. p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To account for within‐patient clustering due to bilateral hips, GEE models were applied. Patient identification number was specified as the clustering variable, with an exchangeable working correlation structure. Femoral head collapse was modeled as a binary outcome. MRI‐based fibrosis stage and inflammatory classification were included as categorical predictors, with the lowest stage serving as the reference category. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Correlation Analysis

Correlation analysis results showed that the collapse rate had no correlation with sex (r = 0.005, p = 0.942), age (r = 0.103, p = 0.149), BMI (r = 0.025, p = 0.724) and etiology (r = 0.043, p = 0.542), but had correlation with MRI stage based on pathology (r = 0.55, p < 0.001) and MRI stage based on pathological stable state (r = 0.358, p < 0.001). Moreover, the result of correlation analysis reflected that the MRI stage based on pathology was correlated with the patient's pain level (r = 0.565, p < 0.001) and hip function (r = −0.571, p < 0.001), and the result also reflected that the MRI stage based on pathological stable state was correlated with pain level (r = 0.606, p < 0.001) and hip function (r = −0.527, p < 0.001).

Multivariate Analysis

Multivariate regression analysis on sex, age, BMI, etiology, MRI stage based on the degree of fibrosis, and MRI stage based on the level of inflammatory response showed that MRI stage based on the degree of fibrosis and MRI stage based on the level of inflammatory response were related to collapse (p < 0.05).

Discriminative Performance of Different Stage Systems

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed to evaluate the ability of different stage systems to discriminate femoral head collapse status at the time of imaging. MRI stage based on the degree of fibrosis demonstrated the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.819; 95% CI, 0.753–0.884), followed by ARCO stage (AUC = 0.756; 95% CI, 0.678–0.835) and MRI classification based on the level of inflammatory response (AUC = 0.714; 95% CI, 0.626–0.802) (Table 7). However, given the cross‐sectional design and the fact that femoral head collapse is intrinsically incorporated into ARCO stage III, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the potential for incorporation bias.

Discussion

In the present study, we proposed a new MRI‐based stage and classification system for ONFH grounded in the radiologic‐histopathological correlation. Lesions were stratified into three stages (F1–F3) according to the extent of fibrosis within the necrotic area, and into four classifications (E0–E3) according to the level of inflammatory response reflected by bone marrow edema. The principal findings can be summarized as follows: (1) the new MRI stage and classification were significantly associated with femoral head collapse, with later stages showing higher collapse rates; (2) the MRI stage based on the degree of fibrosis demonstrated the highest discriminative ability for collapse status (AUC = 0.819) when compared with ARCO stage (AUC = 0.756) and MRI classification based on the level of inflammatory response (AUC = 0.714); (3) both classifications were significantly correlated with patients' pain levels and hip function, with greater fibrosis or more extensive bone marrow edema corresponding to higher pain scores and poorer hip function; and (4) substantial heterogeneity in fibrosis and inflammatory involvement was observed within the same ARCO stage, indicating that conventional radiographic staging alone does not fully capture the underlying pathological status of ONFH.

Comparison With Existing Classification Systems

In this study, we proposed an MRI classification system based on the pathological changes observed in osteonecrosis of the femoral head. Based on the extent of fibrosis in the necrotic area, we classified it into three types. According to the presence and condition of bone marrow edema, we classified it into four types. We found that patients with the same ARCO stage exhibited different pathological stages. Therefore, the ARCO stage does not completely reflect the pathological conditions inside the hips. We examined the relationship between the new classification and femoral head collapse, pain level, and hip function. ROC analysis was performed to explore the comparative discriminative performance of different stage systems for femoral head collapse at the time of imaging. MRI‐based stage according to the extent of fibrosis demonstrated numerically higher AUC values compared with the ARCO stage and MRI classification based on inflammatory response.

However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Given the cross‐sectional design and the fact that femoral head collapse is intrinsically incorporated into ARCO Stage III, ROC‐based comparisons are subject to incorporation bias. Therefore, the present analysis should not be interpreted as a definitive comparison of predictive accuracy, but rather as an exploratory assessment of the relative ability of different stage systems to stratify disease severity at a given time point. From this perspective, MRI‐based stage may provide complementary information by capturing intraosseous pathological heterogeneity that is not fully reflected by conventional radiographic classification. In addition, retrospective analysis demonstrated that both the degree of fibrosis within the necrotic area and the extent of bone marrow edema were significantly associated with the presence of femoral head collapse at the time of imaging. We found that the MRI stage based on the degree of fibrosis was correlated with the collapse rate of the femoral head, with later stages showing higher collapse rates. Similarly, we observed that the MRI classification based on the pathological stable state was correlated with the collapse rate. As the classifications increased, the collapse rate was higher, except for E3. This finding appears counterintuitive at first glance but can be explained by several factors. First, E2 is typically characterized by an active reparative process with prominent bone marrow edema, which may reflect a mechanically unstable phase with a higher short‐term risk of subchondral failure. In contrast, E3 lesions, although often more extensive in size, may represent a relatively later or more stabilized stage with partial reparative sclerosis, potentially conferring transient mechanical stability. In addition, lead‐time bias may also contribute to this observation, as E3 lesions with more conspicuous imaging features are more likely to be detected earlier, prior to the occurrence of collapse. Furthermore, heterogeneity in subchondral bone involvement, particularly in relation to the primary weight‐bearing region, may play a critical role in collapse risk and is not fully captured by lesion extent alone. Additionally, we found that the new MRI stage and classification system for ONFH, based on the radiologic‐histopathological relationship, correlated with patients' pain levels and hip function. As the stage and classification increased, pain levels worsened and hip function deteriorated. Therefore, the proposed MRI‐based stage and classification system is significantly associated with femoral head collapse and shows strong correlations with patients' pain levels and hip function, suggesting potential clinical relevance for disease assessment and risk stratification.

Pathophysiological Basis of the New MRI Classification

The key to the treatment of ONFH is to prevent femoral head collapse because whether the collapse of the femoral head occurs is the key to the outcome of the disease [16]. Once collapse occurs, the articular cartilage and joint morphology will inevitably be damaged, ultimately leading to joint degeneration, hip pain, and loss of hip function. Therefore, the ability to identify hips at risk of collapse at an early stage, and to assess whether structural deterioration may be mitigated, is of critical clinical importance. Timely intervention aimed at preventing or correcting collapse remains central to effective hip preservation strategies [17]. Accordingly, multiple imaging‐based classification systems have been developed to improve the assessment of collapse risk in osteonecrosis of the femoral head. The collapse usually occurs in the necrotic repair period, which is mainly related to the inadequate bone reconstruction after necrotic [18]. Bone repair begins on the surface of the necrotic bone trabecula, resulting in the zone of hardening. On the medial side of the hardened margin, fibrous granulation tissue, intraosseous hypertension and bone resorption are formed and extend to the osteonecrosis area. Increased intraosseous pressure and obstruction of venous return hinder the repair of necrotic bone tissue, thus causing the deterioration of osteonecrosis. When bone repair reaches a certain degree, the inflammatory response, fibrous granulation tissue and hematoma show high signal on the T1 and T2 weighted image of MRI, and when the intramedullary hypertension, fibrosis, sclerosis occur in bone repair, it shows low signal on the T1 weighted image and high signal on the T2 weighted image of MRI. At last, when bone repair is dominated by fibrosis and sclerosis, it shows low signal on the T1 and T2 weighted image of MRI [9, 19, 20]. Moreover, bone trabecular microfracture in the necrotic area leads to changes in the biomechanical structure of the femoral head, which then leads to injury of intramedullary vessels, increase of bone pressure and obstruction of venous return, and finally leads to the bone marrow edema [21, 22]. Bone repair is related to inflammation and increasing evidence points to inflammatory osteoimmunology playing an indispensable role in the pathogenesis of ONFH. Chronic inflammation is a distinctive feature of osteonecrosis, and persistent inflammation influences bone repair, causing progressive collapse [1, 15, 23]. The bone marrow edema corresponds to an inflammatory infiltrate with macrophages, lymphocytes T and B, plasma cells and osteoclasts, which is related to hip pain and femoral head collapse [14, 24, 25]. The fibrotic tissue represents a definitive solution to the necrosis evolution. Sadile et al. reported that the degree of fibrosis in the necrotic region is related to the prognosis of ONFH and the higher the degree of fibrosis, the higher the core decompression failure rate in the treatment of ONFH [26]. Meier et al. reported that in patients with AVN, bone marrow edema of the femoral head represented a secondary sign of subchondral fracture and indicates ARCO stage III disease [27]. Theruvath et al. found that bone marrow edema in corticosteroid‐induced osteonecrosis predicts progression to bone collapse and is associated with subchondral fractures, and they also suggested that absence of bone marrow edema can justify a “wait and watch” approach [28]. Hence, the MRI signals were consistent with the progression of ONFH and have a guiding effect on clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Clinical Application and Advantages of the New Classification

In routine clinical practice, the proposed MRI‐based stage and classification system can be readily applied as an adjunct to conventional radiographic staging using standard hip MRI sequences without additional imaging or specialized software. The F‐stage reflects the extent of fibrotic repair within the necrotic region, whereas the E‐classification represents the degree of bone marrow edema and inflammatory activity. Together, these two dimensions provide complementary information regarding the biological status of the lesion that is not fully captured by morphology‐based classifications alone.

This dual‐axis system may assist clinical assessment and risk stratification. For example, hips classified as F1 + E0 may represent relatively stable lesions with limited fibrosis and minimal inflammatory activity, supporting a conservative “wait and watch” strategy with regular follow‐up. In contrast, lesions classified as F2/F3 with E1–3 were strongly associated with collapse, severe pain, and impaired hip function in our cohort, suggesting the need for closer monitoring and earlier consideration of joint‐preserving intervention.

The proposed classification also has several practical advantages. It is based on routinely acquired MRI sequences and visually identifiable signal patterns, making it simple and reproducible in daily practice, as supported by the favorable inter‐ and intra‐observer reliability observed in this study. In addition, the wide distribution of F‐ and E‐subtypes within the same ARCO stage highlights the pathological heterogeneity of ONFH and suggests that MRI‐based assessment may provide complementary information for individualized clinical evaluation and prognosis assessment. We also applied this newly proposed classification system to guide clinical treatment decision‐making, as illustrated in Supporting Information S1.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, cross‐sectional design lacks longitudinal follow‐up; therefore, femoral head collapse reflects structural status at imaging rather than a true prognostic outcome. Second, although GEEs were used to account for within‐patient clustering, lesion size, volume, and precise location were not quantitatively analyzed, which may limit assessment of their independent or additive prognostic value. Third, treatment strategies were not prospectively evaluated based on the proposed stage system; thus, the clinical implications discussed are hypothesis‐generating. Finally, external multicenter validation was not performed and is warranted in future prospective studies.

Conclusion

The extent of fibrosis within the necrotic region and the degree of bone marrow edema are significantly associated with femoral head collapse, pain severity, and hip function in osteonecrosis of the femoral head. The proposed MRI‐based stage and classification system provides additional information on intraosseous pathological status that is not captured by conventional radiographic stage systems such as ARCO, thereby reflecting disease heterogeneity more comprehensively. These findings may have potential implications for risk stratification and clinical monitoring. However, given the retrospective and cross‐sectional design, as well as the absence of lesion size, volume, and location parameters, the clinical utility of this classification for guiding treatment decisions remains to be established. Future prospective longitudinal studies are required to validate its role in predicting disease progression and informing treatment strategies. This system should not be interpreted as superior to existing classifications at this stage.
