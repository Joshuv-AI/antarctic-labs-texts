Association between coronary angiography-derived index of microcirculatory resistance and ischemic stroke in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction
Authors: Chenchen Cui, Zijia Sun, Mingchang Du, Peng Lu, Yuan Lu, He Zhang, Qihua Xiao, Fan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke is a devastating complication following ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Coronary microcirculatory dysfunction (CMD) is implicated in adverse post-STEMI outcomes, but its relationship with subsequent stroke is unknown. We aimed to investigate the association of the coronary angiography-derived index of microcirculatory resistance (caIMR), a novel non-invasive tool for assessing CMD, for new-onset ischemic stroke in STEMI patients undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI).MethodsThis single-center, retrospective cohort study enrolled 602 consecutive STEMI patients who underwent successful pPCI between July 2020 and February 2025. The caIMR was calculated post-procedure using specialized software. The primary endpoint was new-onset ischemic stroke. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression and restricted cubic spline analyses were used to assess the association between caIMR and stroke risk.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 29.3 months, 38 patients (6.3%) developed a new-onset ischemic stroke. In the multivariable Cox model, caIMR was independently associated with the risk of ischemic stroke (HR: 1.044, 95% CI: 1.022–1.065, P < 0.001), along with new-onset atrial fibrillation (HR: 9.113, 95% CI: 4.587–18.107, P < 0.001). A linear dose-response relationship was observed between caIMR levels and the risk of stroke (P for non-linearity = 0.975). Reclassification analysis showed that adding caIMR improved risk classification, with an IDI of 0.0307 (95% CI: 0.0031–0.0636; P = 0.030) and an NRI of 0.3887 (95% CI: 0.3156–0.4618; P < 0.001).ConclusionElevated caIMR is independently associated with an increased risk of long-term ischemic stroke in STEMI patients after pPCI. This easily accessible metric may serve as a valuable tool for future risk stratification in this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06348-w.

Introduction

ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) continues to represent a major global health burden, contributing substantially to morbidity and mortality worldwide. Despite significant advances in reperfusion therapy—particularly the widespread implementation of primary percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI)—a proportion of patients remains vulnerable to adverse long-term outcomes, even when timely and successful restoration of epicardial coronary blood flow is achieved [1]. Among these, stroke stands out as a severe neurological complication, with an incidence of 2–5% within the first year post-myocardial infarction, and is associated with increased mortality and morbidity [2, 3]. As the management of stroke continues to emphasize prevention, identifying novel risk factors is of paramount clinical importance.

In recent years, the role of coronary microcirculatory dysfunction (CMD) in the prognosis of patients with myocardial infarction has garnered increasing attention [4]. A significant proportion of STEMI patients exhibit CMD, even after successful epicardial blood flow restoration via pPCI. Extensive research has established a strong link between CMD and larger infarct sizes, adverse left ventricular remodeling, heart failure, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [5–7]. The Index of Microcirculatory Resistance (IMR) is a validated tool for assessing CMD. However, its clinical application is limited by its invasive nature and the requisite use of adenosine [8]. Recently, the coronary angiography-derived IMR (caIMR) has emerged as a promising, non-hyperemic method for evaluating coronary microvascular obstruction [9, 10]. Compared to conventional IMR, caIMR is safer and simpler to perform, and numerous studies have validated its prognostic significance in patients with coronary artery disease [11–13]. Moreover, a recent study demonstrated that caIMR is a powerful predictor for new-onset atrial fibrillation (NOAF) in patients with STEMI [14]. Nevertheless, the relationship between CMD and the risk of stroke in post-myocardial infarction patients remains elusive.

This study aimed to investigate the association between caIMR and new-onset ischemic stroke during long-term follow-up in STEMI patients, with the goal of providing a novel tool for clinical risk stratification.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study. We consecutively enrolled patients who were admitted to the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University between July 2020 and February 2025, with a final diagnosis of STEMI and who underwent pPCI. The diagnosis of STEMI was based on the latest universal guidelines [1, 15], defined as the presence of elevated cardiac biomarkers (high-sensitivity troponin T, hs-TnT) with evidence of myocardial ischemia and new ST-segment elevation in at least two contiguous leads or a new left bundle branch block. The present study was performed in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Ethics Approval No. XYFY2025-KL182-01). Given the retrospective and non-interventional design, the requirement for written informed consent was formally waived by the ethics committee. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) successful pPCI within 12 h of symptom onset, defined as residual stenosis < 20% and restoration of TIMI grade 3 flow in the infarct-related artery (IRA); and (3) complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior history of stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA); (2) atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter on admission or documented previously; (3) severe valvular heart disease, cardiomyopathy (hypertrophic or dilated), or congenital heart disease; (4) concomitant intracranial hemorrhage, severe active bleeding, or known bleeding diathesis; (5) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction [estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²]; (6) life expectancy less than one year; (7) inability to measure caIMR due to poor angiographic image quality; and (8) loss to follow-up. A total of 602 STEMI patients were ultimately included in the study (Fig. 1).

Data collection and caIMR measurement

Baseline data were collected from electronic medical records, including demographic characteristics (age, sex, height, weight), risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, smoking history), laboratory findings (peak hs-TnT, NT-proBNP, complete blood count, liver and kidney function, lipid profile), Killip class, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Information on medication use at discharge and during follow-up was also recorded. We calculated the caIMR using FlashAngio software (RainMed, Suzhou, China), which integrates a 3D coronary model with invasive blood pressure data. The 3D model was reconstructed from two angiographic views (≥ 30° angular separation), and the pressure was measured from the guiding catheter. The calculation is governed by a unique hemodynamic formula [14]: caIMR = Pdhyp × (L/K ∙ Vdiastole), where Pdhyp represents the mean distal pressure at maximal hyperemia, L is a constant modeling the length from the inlet to the distal location, Vdiastole is the mean diastolic flow velocity, and K is a constant. In this study, caIMR was calculated in the infarct-related artery after successful primary PCI. The caIMR value of the infarct-related artery was used as the final patient-level caIMR value for all analyses. An independent core laboratory, whose operators were blinded to all clinical information, performed the analysis of all standard coronary angiograms (Figure S1).

Endpoints and follow-up

The primary endpoint was new-onset ischemic stroke. Stroke was defined according to relevant guidelines as an acute, focal neurological deficit with corresponding new ischemic lesions confirmed by cranial CT or MRI, after excluding non-vascular causes [16]. All suspected endpoint events were independently and blindly adjudicated by an endpoint committee consisting of a neurologist and a cardiologist. To investigate the influence of AF on stroke, NOAF during follow-up was recorded. AF was defined as the absence of P waves, the presence of fibrillatory ‘f’ waves, and an irregularly irregular RR interval on a standard 12-lead ECG or a single-lead ECG strip lasting > 30 s [17]. All patients with AF received anticoagulation guidance at presentation according to clinical practice. All patients were followed up via outpatient visits or telephone calls at 1, 6, and 12 months post-procedure, and annually thereafter until the end of the study or the occurrence of an endpoint.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) and SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and compared using the independent samples t-test, or as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. To identify variables independently associated with new-onset stroke, univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 in the univariable analysis, along with clinically important variables, were included in the multivariable model using a stepwise selection procedure. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. No significant violation of the proportional hazards assumption was observed for caIMR or the overall model. Collinearity among candidate variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor, with a VIF > 5 indicating potential multicollinearity. To further explore the dose-response relationship between caIMR and stroke risk, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with 4 knots was performed based on the multivariable Cox model. Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test was used to compare stroke-free survival between groups stratified by the optimal caIMR cutoff value, which was determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. To evaluate the internal stability of the caIMR cut-off, bootstrap resampling was performed with 1000 iterations. In each bootstrap sample, the optimal caIMR threshold was recalculated using the Youden index. The distribution of bootstrap-derived cut-off values was used to assess the robustness of the original threshold of 28.25 U. To evaluate whether caIMR provided incremental risk information beyond conventional clinical variables, additional model performance analyses were performed. A clinical model was constructed based on variables identified from regression analysis and clinically established risk factors for stroke. An extended model was then generated by adding caIMR to the clinical model. Integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI) were calculated to further evaluate the incremental risk information provided by caIMR beyond the clinical model. All tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 602 STEMI patients who underwent pPCI were included. During a median follow-up of 29.3 (IQR: 20.0, 40.7) months, 38 patients (6.3%) experienced a new-onset ischemic stroke. Patients were divided into two groups according to the median value of caIMR (caIMR ≤ 23.0 U group and caIMR > 23.0 U group). Significant differences were observed between the two groups in caIMR, LVEF, age, the incidence of stroke during follow-up, and the prevalence of diabetes (all P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Cox proportional hazards analysis of stroke risk factors

In the univariable Cox regression analysis, age, diabetes, NOAF, LVEF, hs-TnT, ventricular thrombus, and caIMR level were significantly associated with the occurrence of stroke (Table S1). After adjusting for potential confounders (including age, diabetes, NOAF, LVEF, hs-TnT, ventricular thrombus, and caIMR) in the multivariable Cox regression model, NOAF (adjusted HR: 9.113, 95% CI: 4.587–18.107, P < 0.001) and caIMR (adjusted HR: 1.044, 95% CI: 1.022–1.065, P < 0.001) remained independently associated with new-onset stroke (Table 2). Each 1-unit increase in caIMR was associated with a 4.4% increase in the risk of stroke. The restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a linear, positive dose-response relationship between caIMR levels and the risk of stroke (P for non-linearity = 0.975, P for overall association < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The hazard ratio for stroke increased continuously with rising caIMR values.

Discriminative and incremental risk information of caIMR for stroke

The ROC analysis demonstrated that caIMR was moderately associated with new-onset stroke in STEMI patients (Fig. 3). The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.669 (95% CI: 0.572–0.767, P < 0.001). The optimal cutoff value, determined by maximizing the Youden index, was 28.25 U, which corresponded to a sensitivity of 63.1% and a specificity of 69.8%. Internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples showed that the median bootstrap-derived caIMR cut-off was 28.25 U, with an interquartile range of 28.25–36.55 U and a 95% percentile interval of 25.60-40.35 U. The original threshold of 28.25 U was the most frequently selected cut-off across bootstrap samples, supporting the internal stability of this threshold.

The discriminative performance of the clinical model and the extended model was further assessed. The clinical model, including atrial fibrillation, ventricular thrombus, LVEF, age, diabetes mellitus, and hs-TnT, yielded an AUC of 0.764. After adding caIMR to the clinical model, the AUC increased to 0.819 (Fig. 3). Reclassification analysis showed that adding caIMR improved risk classification, with an IDI of 0.0307 (95% CI: 0.0031–0.0636; P = 0.030) and an NRI of 0.3887 (95% CI: 0.3156–0.4618; P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Kaplan-meier survival analysis

Patients were stratified into a high-caIMR group (≥ 28.25 U) and a low-caIMR group (< 28.25 U) based on the optimal cutoff. The Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that the high-caIMR group had significantly lower stroke-free survival rates compared to the low-caIMR group (Log-rank P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). This indicates that patients with higher caIMR levels have a significantly increased risk of developing stroke during follow-up.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically investigate the relationship between CMD, quantified by caIMR, and long-term new-onset ischemic stroke in patients with STEMI treated with pPCI. Our principal finding is that after adjusting for traditional risk factors, an elevated caIMR is independently associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke, exhibiting a linear dose-response relationship.

Our results demonstrate that not only was caIMR significantly elevated in patients who developed a stroke, but in the multivariable model, each 1-unit increase in caIMR conferred a 4.4% increase in stroke risk (HR: 1.044, P < 0.001). This finding has important clinical and pathophysiological implications. Traditionally, post-MI stroke risk assessment has focused on NOAF, age, and large-artery atherosclerosis [18, 19]. The association of caIMR, a marker of cardiac microvascular integrity, with stroke suggests that the origins of stroke risk may be more profound and systemic. Several potential mechanisms could explain this association. First, coronary microvasculature is not an isolated system; its dysfunction often reflects systemic vascular endothelial injury [20]. Pathological drivers such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance are systemic in nature [21, 22]. Therefore, a high caIMR value may not only signify poor cardiac perfusion but also indicate a “vulnerable” state in the cerebral microvasculature, predisposing it to microthrombosis or increased sensitivity to blood pressure fluctuations. Our study provides new evidence for the interplay of the “Heart-Brain Axis” in atherosclerotic disease. Second, previous studies have established that a high caIMR is associated with a larger myocardial infarct size, worse ventricular remodeling, and a higher incidence of heart failure [7, 23, 24]. Although we adjusted for LVEF in our multivariable model, caIMR might reflect earlier and more subtle cardiac dysfunction than LVEF alone. Such subclinical cardiac impairment, even without a formal diagnosis of heart failure, could lead to hemodynamic alterations and intracardiac stasis, thereby increasing embolic risk [25]. Notably, a prior study by Chen et al. demonstrated a significant correlation between elevated caIMR and the risk of new-onset atrial fibrillation in post-MI patients [14]. In our study, NOAF was also a powerful independent risk factor for stroke, which may partially explain our findings from another perspective.

It is well-known that an IMR value of 25 U is a recognized threshold for CMD [26]. In our study, the optimal caIMR cutoff for predicting stroke was 28.25 U, which is proximate to this established threshold. However, given the single-center nature and limited sample size of our research, more robust evidence from multicenter, large-scale studies in myocardial infarction patients is warranted to confirm this cutoff.

After adding caIMR to the clinical model, the AUC increased from 0.764 to 0.819. Notably, IDI and NRI analyses showed that the addition of caIMR significantly improved risk discrimination and reclassification. Given that caIMR alone demonstrated only moderate discriminative ability, caIMR may be more suitable for clinical use as a complementary marker in combination with conventional risk factors, rather than as a standalone tool for stroke risk assessment. caIMR provides clinicians with a simple and safe tool to quantify CMD in MI patients, and its utility has been validated in numerous studies. Our research adds valuable information to the application of caIMR in this population. For patients with a markedly elevated caIMR, clinicians should be aware that their long-term risk of cerebrovascular events may also be elevated, suggesting that more aggressive secondary prevention strategies may be required.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the investigation was conducted at a single tertiary center and involved a relatively small cohort, which may have introduced selection bias and potentially constrained the external validity of the results. The external validity of our conclusions needs to be confirmed in multicenter, large-scale studies. Second, as an observational cohort study, we can only establish an association between caIMR and stroke, not a causal relationship. Although we adjusted for numerous known confounders in our multivariable model, residual or unmeasured confounding variables may still exist. Third, STEMI is a severe manifestation of coronary artery disease, and our conclusions should be interpreted within this context. The findings may require replication in different disease settings. Finally, although all patients with atrial fibrillation received anticoagulation guidance at presentation according to clinical practice, detailed longitudinal data regarding anticoagulation adherence, treatment interruption, and long-term medication persistence were not systematically available. In addition, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was not available in this cohort; therefore, infarct size, atrial remodeling, and atrial stretch could not be directly evaluated. Residual confounding related to AF-related embolic risk and infarct-related structural remodeling cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusion

Among patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction undergoing primary PCI, an elevated caIMR is independently associated with long-term new-onset ischemic stroke. The relationship between caIMR level and stroke risk appears to be linear and dose-dependent.
