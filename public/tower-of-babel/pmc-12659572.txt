Household food security, food hygiene practices and their interaction on self-reported health among pregnant women in the Bono region of Ghana: a population-based cluster survey
Authors: Dominic Doglikuu Be-Ikuu, Prudence Portia Mwini Nyaledzigbor, Alice Ayawine, Ebenezer Jones Amoah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFood security and food hygiene practices can negatively influence health. Poor food hygiene and food insecurity risk poor health. Studies have investigated the direct associations of food security and food hygiene practices on health outcomes, however, combine effects of these variables on self-reported health is under explored. This study aimed to assess household food security, food hygiene practices and their interaction on self-reported health among pregnant women in Bono Region, Ghana.MethodsCross-sectional study was conducted among 408 pregnant women in Sunyani West Municipality. Data collected were entered into SPSS version-22. Descriptive statistics, One-way ANOVA with Post Hoc test, and multivariable logistic regression models were used to analyze the data and results presented at 95% confident interval with p-value ≤ 0.05 considered significant.ResultsMean prevalence and 95%CI of self-reported health, household food security and food hygiene practices were: 1.69 (1.59—1.79), 5.36 (4.92—5.79) and 122.06 (106.12—137.99), respectively. Being Student/Apprentice (AOR = 0.44, 95%CI = 0.20—0.98), having: low food hygiene practices (AOR = 0.38, 95%CI = 0.18—0.83), low household food security (AOR = 4.85 95%CI: 2.44—9.61), interaction of moderate food hygiene practices with low food security (AOR = 2.51, 95%CI = 1.38—4.54) and interaction of low food hygiene practices with moderate food security (AOR = 2.93, 95%CI = 1.57—5.48) were associated with poor self-reported health.ConclusionHousehold food security food hygiene practices and their interaction were associated with poor self-reported health among pregnant women in Sunyani West Municipality. Public health officers in the municipality should consider implementing Behavior Change Communication Strategies (BCCS) and food safety training to promote positive food hygiene practices. The Municipal assembly should prioritize food subsidies and direct food aid/cash to targeted vulnerable households.Keywords: Household Food Insecurity, Food Hygiene Practices, Self-reported Maternal Health

Introduction

Food security and food hygiene practices remain major public health issues globally [1]. Food security, defined by the United Nations, is the measure of the individual's ability to access nutritious, safe and sufficient food for healthy living [2]. Lack of sufficient, safe, and nutritious food, can lead to malnutrition and other serious health consequences including increased risk of infections which can affects general health and wellbeing of people [2]. Food hygiene practices, on the other hand involved specific activities put in place to prevent spread of foodborne infections, including Salmonella and Listeria species, to humans [3, 4]. Poor food hygiene practices are major causes of human illness, and most food hygiene problems are associated with faulty handling during processing and mass rash for food [5]. Food insecurity and poor food hygiene practices could negatively impact general health and wellbeing of people including pregnant women. poor food hygiene practices and access to insufficient, and non-nutritious food can decreased immune response and precipitate anxiety and depression in people including pregnant women, and this can cumulatively affect their overall health and wellbeing [6, 7].

The mechanism through which poor food hygiene practices and food insecurity could act to influence general health and wellbeing among people is complex and multi-faceted [8, 9]. Food insecurity can lead to reduced food intake and dietary diversity, thus increasing risk of micronutrient deficiencies and overall malnutrition, which could cumulatively weakens the immune system of individuals, and make them susceptible to infections affecting general health [10]. Poor food hygiene practices could cause diarrheal diseases, and further hindered nutrients absorption and thus worsened malnutrition and immune response [11]. Stress produced as a result of worry about foods unavailability and the potential guilt as the results of the uncertainty in access to food could trigger anxiety and depression, and all these can affect general health and wellbeing of people including pregnant women [12].

Pregnancies and pregnancy-related health issues are of growing public health concern among women globally [13–15]. Household food security and food hygiene practices play significant roles in general health and wellbeing of pregnant women [16, 17]. In Iran, study revealed that pregnant women living in high food insecure households reported lowest score in quality of health when compared their counterpart in food secured households [18]. Another study in Togo reported that household food insecurity was perceived as significant barrier to utilization of maternal healthcare services, and this affected general health and wellbeing of women [19]. In Ghana, although we did not find any study reporting household food security and its direct association with self-reported health among pregnant women, study conducted in East Mamprusi Municipality, in the northern region indicates that food insecurity was associated with common mental disorders among mothers living in low social support households [20]. Another study in Central region also indicates similar finding. Report from this study found that moderate and high household food insecurity were significantly associated with depression levels among teenage pregnant girls [21]. Regarding food hygiene practices and self-reported health in Ghana, study demonstrated that poor food hygiene practice put pregnant women and their unborn babies at increased risk of foodborne illnesses, which could consequently leads to serious health outcomes like miscarriage or premature birth which affects the psychological, mental and general health and wellbeing of women [22].

Several studies in developed countries the world over have significantly demonstrated the direct association of household food security and food hygiene practices on health outcomes among pregnant women [23, 24], however, same cannot be said in developing countries including Ghana. Furthermore, knowledge gap exist regarding how household food security and food hygiene practices interact to influence health outcomes among vulnerable groups such as pregnant women. Since household food insecurity and poor food hygiene practices could interact in synergy or in tandem to influence health outcomes among pregnant women, it is important that we assess how these independent variables and their interaction could influence self-reported health among pregnant women to better provide information for policy direction. In line with this our study sought to examine how household food security, food hygiene practices and their interaction are associated with self-reported health among pregnant women at Sunyani West Municipality in the Bono region of Ghana.

Methodology

Study area

Sunyani West Municipality is one of the 12 administrative municipalities/districts established by the Legislative Instrument (LI) 1881 in November 1, 2007 in the Bono Region of Ghana. The municipality is located between latitude 7° 19´N and 7° 35´N and longitudes 2° 08´ W and 2° 31´ W. It shares boundaries with Wenchi Municipality to the North East, Tain District to the North, Berekum and Dormaa East to the West, Sunyani District and to Tano North and Ofinso North Districts Eastern and South East borders respectively [25]. In terms of land size, Sunyani West District has overall land mass of approximately 1,658.7 km2 as shown in Fig. 1.

District Map of Sunyani West District. Source: Sunyani West District Assembly (2014)

The municipality has 18 electoral areas, and hosts the two prestigious universities of the region thus, the Catholic University of Ghana, Fiapre and the University of Energy and Natural Resources also located in Fiapre. According to the 2020 Population and Housing Census report, Sunyani West has a population of 85,272, representing 3.7 percent of the region’s total population [26]. Females constitute 51.5 percent, the youthful and population (population less than 15 years) accounting for 38.3 percent with general Fertility Rate (GFR) is 95.8 births per 1000 among women between 15–49 years old. About 92.9 percent of the population is employed with majority engaged as skilled agricultural, forestry and fishery workers [26]. Considering the relatively youthful population, and the presence of the two higher institutions of learning which attract more youthful population into the municipality from different part of the country, it is important that we conduct this study to investigate how household food security and food hygiene practices could be associated with health outcome particularly among venerable group to serve as baseline evidence for decision making and health policy direction in the municipality. In this regard registered pregnant women receiving ANC services in recognized health facilities across the municipality were used in this study, and so results may not be total reflection of the entire population of the municipality.

Study design

Analytical cross-sectional study design was used to conduct this study among pregnant women in Sunyani West municipality. The municipality is made up of urban and rural communities with population living in different locations. This study design was adopted to enable us obtain a snapshot the problem across the population in the municipality.

Study population

The study population for this study was all pregnant women in Sunyani West municipality and the target population was all registered pregnant women seeking ANC services at health care facilities in the municipality.

Inclusion criteria

Pregnant women who were confirmed pregnant by a qualified medical doctor or midwife and were attending ANC were included into our study.

Exclusion criteria

Women who were pregnant but not attending ANC and pregnant women who were not physically medically and/or psychologically fit were excluded. We excluded these women because they represent a high-risk group with adverse health outcomes. Pregnant women who do not attend ANC are at significantly higher risk of complications such as preeclampsia, anemia, preterm birth, low birth weight, and stillbirths and all these can affect their general health. Including them would skew our study results toward adverse outcomes making it difficult for our study findings to be applicable to women access ANC services.

Sample size determination

We adopted single population proportion formula (n = (Z2 P (1-P))/e2) to estimate our sample size for this study. In this formula, the letter ‘n’ stands for the sample size, ‘Z’ is the population normal standard distribution of 1.96 for 95% confidence interval, ‘P’ is the true population proportion or prevalence of self-reported health among pregnant women in the study area, and ’e’ is the standard error of (5%). Study conducted in Bono and Bono East Regions reports that prevalence of self-reported health among women is 24% [27]. Since we could not find any study in Sunyani West municipality which reported prevalence of self-reported health among pregnant women, we use the Bono and Bono East regional prevalence of 24% as proxy to calculate our sample size. Thus, putting the values in the equation above, the sample size n was calculated to be 281. But since about 93 percent of the population in the municipality is employed with majority engaged as skilled agricultural, forestry and fishery workers, we estimated higher non-response rate among our study target population. To curtail this problem, contingency sample of 45% was added to our original sample size and therefore increase it 408 to take care of this non-response from the participants.

Sampling techniques

Three stage (multi-stage) simple random sampling technique was used to recruit the 408 pregnant women into our study. In the first stage of this sampling technique, we used simple random sampling to select 12 electoral areas out of 18 where at least two or more health facilities including private health facilities were providing maternity care services to pregnant women. In the second stage, each selected electoral area is visited and simple random sampling used to select one health facility rendering maternity care services to pregnant women. In the third stage, pregnant women registered and are seeking maternity care services in each of the selected health facility were selected using simple random sampling and, their consents sought and interview granted. This sampling process was repeated in all the 12 selected electoral areas until all the 408 participants were recruited and interviewed. This data collection process lasted for a period of 3 months (March to May, 2024).

Data collection procedure and measurement of study variables

This study was a researcher administered questionnaire study; all questionnaires were in English. The dependent variable in the study was self-reported health among pregnant women.

Self-reported health among pregnant women is defined as the subjective feeling of overall well-being, and this encompasses physical, mental, and psychosocial well-being as measured using the HAQ. The independent variables were demographic characteristics (age, marital status, place of residence, educational background, occupation, and family size), food hygiene practices and household food insecurity. Household food security is defined as the state of pregnant women having reliable access to sufficient quantity, affordable and healthy food. Food hygiene is defined as the proper cleaning of equipment and surfaces; proper hand washing; using separate cutting boards for raw and cooked foods; cooking food to safe internal temperatures, and storing food at appropriate places and in temperatures.

Measurement of self-reported health

Structured questionnaire was used to measure socio-demographic and socioeconomic factors. British Colombia Ministry of Health, the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ) was used to Assess self-reported health among pregnant women [28]. The HAQ is a 10 domain 23 items questionnaire. Nine domains asked questions ranging from ‘’how difficult do you have or experience in dressing yourself including tying shoelaces and doing buttons’’, to ‘’how difficult do have or experience in performing personal hygiene, doing household chores including light gardening.’’ The response from these items were ‘’0 = no difficulty; 1 = some difficulty; 2 = much difficulty; and 3 = unable to do’’. The 10th domain asked participants to self- report from a scale 0–10 how much pain they have or experienced in the past one week due to their ill-health. In the data analysis the 10th domain of HAQ was recoded as follows: Participants who reported 0 to 1 were judged to have no pain (no difficulty) = 0; those reported 2 to 4 were judged to have some pain (some difficulty) = 1; those who reported 5 to 7 were said to have much pain (much difficulty) = 2 and those who reported 8 to 10 were said to have severe pain (unable to do) = 3. In these responses, 0 was the minimum response and 3 was the maximum response. The 23 items in the HAQ were summed up to form one composite continue scale with minimum score 0, and maximum score 69. During data analysis the composite scale was categorized into three levels; participants who scored between 0–9 points were said to have no pain (no difficulty) and judged to have high health; those who scored 10–29 points were said to have some pain (some difficulty) and were said to have moderate health; and those who scored 30–69 points were judged to have much pain (much difficulty) or severe pain (unable to do) and, therefore judged to have poor health.

Measurement of food hygiene practices

Participants’ food hygiene practices were measured using a 4 domain, 18 items questionnaire covering (Food Storage, Food Cooking, Food Cleaning and Personal Hygiene). Each question from the 4 domains was in a likert scale with response 0 = strongly disagree; 1 = disagree; 2 = neutral; 3 = agree and 4 = strongly agree. In this response 0 was the minimum response, and number 4 the maximum response. During the data analysis the 18 items were also summed up to one composite scale with a minimum score of 0 and maximum score of 72. This scale was categorized into 3 levels. Participants who score 0–19 on the scale were said to have poor food hygiene practices; those who scored 20—39 points were said to moderate food hygiene practices and those who scored 40 and above were said to have good food hygiene practices.

Measurement of household food insecurity

Household food insecurity was measured using USAID Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance (FANTA) Program Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) [29]. The HFIAS is a 9 items scale, with questions ranging from ‘’how often did you worry that your household would not have enough foods, to how often did you or any household member goes a whole day and night without eating anything because there was not enough food?’’ These set of questions have three responses (never, rarely, sometimes and often). “Never” was assigned 0 score; “rarely or sometimes” was assigned 1 score; and response “often” was assigned 2 scores. Therefore, 0 was the minimum score, and 6 maximum score was. During data analysis the questionnaires were summed up to form composite scale and this was used to evaluate participants’ food insecurity. In the composite scale, participants who scored 0–1was said to have little to no household insecurity, those who scored 2–3 were said to have moderate household insecurity, while participants who score 4–6 were said to have severe household insecurity.

Quality assurance procedures

For quality assurance purposes, the questionnaires and various adopted scales used in the study were pre-tested on 20 pregnant women receiving ANC services at Sunyani Municipal Hospital to evaluate their internal validity before the main data collection. Cronbach’s alphas results from the pretest of British Columbia Ministry of Health HAQ was 0.78 and Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) was 0.93, indicating good internal consistency. Data collectors were recruited and trained before they were deployed to collect data. After the data collectors were trained the principal investigators tested their knowledge on the data collection tools to make sure that they were all asking the questions consistently from the questionnaires, and the various scales given to them. Where discrepancies existed they were corrected. The data collection was done at designated places at the various health facilities to ensure privacy. During the data collection process on the spot checks were done on daily basis for completeness; and each time incomplete questionnaires were identified, respondents were followed up for them to complete the missing data.

Statistical analysis

The data collected were entered into IBM SPSS version 20 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Kolmogorov–Smirnov test were performed to check normal distributions of the data after they were cleaned. Participant demographic variables were analyzed using descriptive statistics; household food security and food hygiene practices was analyzed using One-way ANOVA test with Post Hoc Bonferroni correction to compare the significant mean differences of these independent variables on the outcome variable. One-way ANOVA test was used because our characteristics of interest in this case household food insecurity and food hygiene practices came random sample that is normally distributed, and our outcome variable (group variable), in this case self-reported health is a continue variable categorized into three level thus poor moderate and high health. Multinomial logistic regression models were used to assess the association of participant demographic variables, household food security, food hygiene practices and their interactions on the self-reported health among pregnant women with p-value ≤ 0.05 set as statistically significant for all variables in our study.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the “Declaration of Helsinki.” The protocol was approved by the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC0023/04/24). Participants were assured of privacy/secrecy during the data collection. Data that give identity to participants were not collected. Participants were told that their participation in the study was absolutely voluntary, and they have the right to terminate their participation in our study at any time without having to give any reason or suffer any consequences thereafter. Each participant after accepting to participate in the study was formally requested to sign a written informed consent form before participating in the study.

Discussion

This study sought to investigate the relation between household food Security, food hygiene practices and their interaction for self-reported health among pregnant women in Sunyani West Municipality, and therefore the findings may not be applicable to the entire population of the municipality. However these findings clearly demonstrate the importance of household food security and food hygiene practices in improving health, and health outcomes among vulnerable groups. In recent times the general public have become increasingly aware of the potential risks household food insecurity and poor food hygiene practices posed to the health of general public, and women during pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum period [30].

Food insecurity can increase stress and depression, and reduce access to prenatal care, while poor hygiene increased the risk of infections for both mother and child [31]. The interplay of household food security and food hygiene practices on health outcomes are well documented in several studies the world over. For instance study conducted in Konso district indicates that household food security, dietary diversity, and latrine availability were significantly associated with undernutrition among pregnant women [32]. Although our study did not directly investigates how household food security and food hygiene practices influence specific health among pregnant women, its findings undoubtedly demonstrate that household food security and food hygiene practices can significantly influence self-reported health among pregnant women.

Our study found that being a student/apprentice, having; low household food security, and low food hygiene practices were significantly associated with poor self-reported health among pregnant women. These findings demonstrate the crucial roles socio-demographic variables such as education, income, food hygiene practices, and household food insecurity play in health and wellbeing of humans. Access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food is crucial for health [33]. However, education positively influences this through knowledge, behaviors and practices, which could enhance utilization, and thus commutatively promote good health. For instance access to unsafe foods such as foods contaminated with harmful bacteria, viruses, parasites and/or chemical substances could significantly cause symptoms of diseases, ranging from vomiting to diarrhoea, and this can cause nutrients mal-absorption and thus, create vicious cycle of disease and malnutrition. However, education can positively modulate this effect through knowledge, and behavior change and practices and consequently influence total health of individuals, particularly the vulnerable group such pregnant women and children [34] as reported in our study. Although our study objective was not to exclusively evaluate how education influence household food security and food hygiene practices for self-reported health among pregnant women, data demonstrates that education significantly influenced household food security and food hygiene practices through proper decision-making, safe food handling practices, food storage, pest controls, and improves personal hygiene, which cumulatively reduce risk of illness [35].

Household food security and food hygiene practices were significantly associated with poor self-reported health among pregnant women in our study. Several studies have demonstrated that access to food is strong determinant of good health and well-being [9, 36, 37] however, this can only be realized if people have access to safe, nutritious and hygienic foods at all times to meet their dietary needs. Food safety practices and household food security issues are top notch global public health concern particularly among pregnant women due to their significant link to foodborne diseases [38]. In a recent study conducted to assess nutritional status and its determinants among mothers, it is found that women facing poverty, food insecurity, and poor hygiene practices, particularly relating to poor hand hygiene practices and toilet use were significantly associated with higher odds of severe wasting [39].

In other related study women who faced water unavailability and food insecurity in households were shown to have higher odds of perceived stress [40]. These two independent studies by Sinha, R.K., et al., and Ahmed, F., et al. respectively, suggest the critical ways through which household food insecurity and poor food hygiene practices, could independently affect health outcomes. In our study, although we did not investigate the association of household food insecurity and food hygiene practice for severe wasting and perceived stress among women as Sinha R.K., et al., and Ahmed, F., et al., did respectively, the similarity between our studies was the fact that we all investigated how household food insecurity and food hygiene practices were related to health outcome among women. The differences however, were with the outcome variables. While Sinha, R.K., et al., and Ahmed, F., et al., focused on severe wasting and perceived stress respectively, we focused on self-reported health among pregnant women. These differences in our studies could be as a result of the study locations and the prevalence of the study outcome variables among the respondents at the time of the study. Despite the differences in our studies outcomes, we all concluded that household food insecurity and food hygiene practices could negatively influence health outcomes among vulnerable population especially pregnant women.

Away from this our study further found that interaction of moderate food hygiene practices with low household food security was associated with poor self-reported health while interaction of low food hygiene practices with moderate household food security was associated with poor self-reported health among pregnant women. To the best of our knowledge this study is the first to assess how household food security and food hygiene practices interact to influence self-reported health among pregnant women. Household food insecurity and food hygiene practices are serious environmental stressors in human life [41, 42], and these variables could be linked to health outcomes via biological, nutritional, and psychosocial pathways [43]. For instance, when individuals live in food insecure household they often have inadequate food to eat, and this could affects nutrients intake and health outcomes [44]. On the other hand, when people practice poor food hygiene and sanitation this may leads to consumption of contaminated or unsafe food resulting to increase risk of diarrhoeal diseases and infections and consequently prevent nutrients absorption, and thus cause vicious cycle of malnutrition and diseases [45]. When this happens individuals may be stressed up, and ailment worsen. Additionally, when people feel uncertain about how long food may last or when they feel ashamed or guilty for not able to provide food for the family, this may generate stress, anxiety and depression and thus affect the overall health and wellbeing especially among venerable groups such pregnant women. Despite the different pathways through which household food insecurity and food hygiene practices could act to influence health outcomes, how these variables interact to influence self-reported health among pregnant women is not well known which our study sought to feel this gap.

Although our study found significant associations between household food security, food hygiene practices and their interaction for self-reported health among pregnant women, we cannot conclude that cause—effect association exist for these variables because we used cross-sectional study which is weak study design to investigate these associations. Since cross sectional study cannot detect cause-and-effect association between variables, we cannot conclude on causal-effect association. Again we used a sample size of 408 pregnant in our study, but since cross-sectional study require large sample size in order to generalized findings to the entire population, this limitation too has constrained the generalizability of our study results and therefore we cannot generalize our finding that household food security, food hygiene practices and their interaction were associated with self-reported health among pregnant women in Sunyani West Municipality. Due to the weak study design and the other limitations stated above, we recommend that subsequent study should consider adopting more powerful study designs such as prospective cohort study or randomize clinical trial to evaluate this problem.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that household food security, food hygiene practices and the interaction between household food security and food hygiene practices were significantly associated with poor self-reported health among pregnant women. These findings have significantly enriched the literature concerning household food security and food hygiene practices for self-reported health among pregnant women. To the best of our knowledge this study is the first of its kind in low and middle income countries to evaluate the interaction of household food security and food hygiene practices on self-reported health among pregnant women.

Based on the results of this study we strongly recommend that individuals and families living in food insecure household should master courage, and avoid pride, and reach out to religious organizations, co-operate entities and civil societies within their catchment areas for help. The public health officers in the municipality should also immediately implement Social and Behavior Change Communication (SBCC) to promote positive food hygiene behaviors, and food safety training to meet the specific needs of the vulnerable groups in the municipality. The government should also act through the municipal assembly to provide direct food aid/cash transfers to vulnerable households, and subsidize essential food items to make them available and affordable to all alleviate the hunger.

Limitation

We used simple random sampling to select one health facility in each electoral area and interviewed registered pregnant women seeking ANC services from these facilities. This method of selection has excluded pregnant women not attending ANC services in the municipality, and this process might introduced bias in our study and limits the generalizability of study finding to the entire municipality.
