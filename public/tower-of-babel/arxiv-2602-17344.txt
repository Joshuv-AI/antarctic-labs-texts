Invertibility of the Fourier Diffraction Relation in Raster Scan Diffraction Tomography
Peter Elbau peter.elbau@univie.ac.at, Noemi Naujoks noemi.naujoks@univie.ac.at

Abstract
Diffraction tomography aims to recover an object’s scattering potential from measured wave fields. In the classical setting, the object is illuminated by plane waves from many directions, and the Fourier diffraction theorem gives a direct relation between the Fourier transform of the scattering potential of the object and the Fourier transformed measurements. In many practical imaging systems, however, focused beams are used instead of plane waves. These beams are then translated across the object to bring different regions of interest into focus. The Fourier diffraction relation adapted to this setting differs in one crucial point from the plane-wave case: while certain Fourier coefficients of the measurements still directly correspond to individual Fourier coefficients of the scattering potential, others are given by linear combinations of two Fourier coefficients of the scattering potential. This article investigates which Fourier coefficients of the scattering potential can be uniquely recovered from these relations. We show that in dimensions higher than two, all coefficients appearing in the equations are typically uniquely determined. In two dimensions, however, only part of the Fourier coverage is uniquely recoverable, while on the remaining subset distinct coefficients can produce identical data.

1. Introduction

Diffraction tomography is a widely used inverse scattering technique that aims to reconstruct the spatial distribution of an object’s scattering potential from measurements of scattered wave fields. A prominent application is ultrasound tomography
[
1
,
4
,
12
]
, where both amplitude and phase information of the scattered fields are available.

Diffraction tomography relies on the Born approximation, under which the dependence of the scattered field on the scattering potential becomes linear and the corresponding Fourier diffraction theorem
[
14
]
provides an explicit representation of the scattering potential in terms of the measurements in Fourier space. More precisely, it states that the Fourier-transformed measurements coincide with the Fourier transform of the scattering potential along a semicircle in two dimensions or a hemisphere in higher dimensions. Incorporating a collection of measurements from multiple views then generates data on a union of distinct hemispheres in Fourier space, enabling reconstruction of the scattering potential through
filtered backpropagation

[
3
,
7
,
9
,
14
]
.

However, the classical theory relies on idealized assumptions about both the incident field and the measurement geometry. In particular, the object is assumed to be illuminated by monochromatic plane waves arriving from a broad range of directions
[
7
,
11
,
14
]
.
In practice, though, many imaging systems, such as medical ultrasound
[
6
]
, use focused beams that concentrate the energy at a focal region to improve the spatial resolution.
Rather than illuminating the entire object from different directions, these beams are translated to bring different regions into focus.
This scanning setup differs fundamentally from the plane-wave, full-angle illumination assumed in the conventional framework, highlighting a gap between theory and practical setups.

To address this discrepancy, recent work
[
8
]
extended the classical diffraction tomography framework to account for focused beams by modeling them as superpositions of plane waves while still assuming full-angle data. Building on this,
[
5
]
further extended diffraction tomography to a raster scan geometry in which a focused beam is emitted from only one side and then translated across the object along a fixed hyperplane. Within this framework, a new Fourier diffraction relation was derived, expressing the Fourier-transformed measurements in terms of the Fourier coefficients of the scattering potential of the object. Here, depending on the scan configuration, some measured data correspond directly to individual coefficients, while others are linear combinations of two coefficients.

To obtain the involved Fourier data of the scattering potential, we therefore still have to solve a linear equation system. But before doing so, we want to check in this article for every scan setup which Fourier coefficients are in fact uniquely determined by these equations.

While the physically relevant case is cleary the three-dimensional setting, we would like to discuss the problem in arbitrary dimensions, with particular attention to the theoretically interesting two-dimensional case where the dimensions of the gathered data and the Fourier data of the scattering potential are equal.

Our results show that in dimensions higher than two, all Fourier coefficients appearing in the equations are, at least in the generic case, uniquely recoverable from the measured data. In two dimensions, however, only a subset of the coefficients is uniquely determined, while on the remaining region distinct values for the Fourier coefficients produce identical measurements.

2. Problem formulation

We begin by briefly outlining the experimental setup underlying to our analysis, see
Figure 1
. The aim is to image an unknown object, represented by its complex-valued scattering potential
f\in L^{1}(\mathds{R}^{d})
in the arbitrary spatial dimension
d\in\mathds{N}\setminus\{1\}
, where we are, of course, mainly interested in the physically relevant cases
d\in\{2,3\}
. We assume that the object is embedded in a homogeneous background medium. More precisely, we assume that
\supp(f)\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{d}_{r}
, where we denote by

\mathcal{B}_{r}^{d}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathds{R}^{d}\mid\left\|x\right\|<r\}

the open ball in
\mathds{R}^{d}
of radius
r>0
centered at the origin.

As incident wave, we consider a focused acoustic beam which propagates in the direction
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\coloneqq\partial\mathcal{B}_{1}^{d}
and which is then successively translated across the object along a predefined scan plane

\nu^{\perp}\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathds{R}^{d}\mid\left<x,\nu\right>=0\right\},

where
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
defines the scan normal and
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
is the Euclidean inner product. For each position
y\in\nu^{\perp}
, the incident beam
u^{\text{inc}}_{y}
satisfies the homogeneous Helmholtz equation

(\Delta+k_{0}^{2})u^{\text{inc}}_{y}(x)=0,\qquad x\in\mathds{R}^{d},

where
k_{0}>0
is the wave number, and is modeled as a translated Herglotz wave
[
2
]
,

\displaystyle u^{\text{inc}}_{{y}}(x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}}a(s)e^{i\left<x,s\right>}e^{-i\left<y,s\right>}dS({s}),\quad{x}\in\mathds{R}^{d},\ {y}\in\nu^{\perp},

with Herglotz density
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1})
. Here,
\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}\coloneqq\partial\mathcal{B}_{k_{0}}^{d}
denotes the sphere of radius
k_{0}
in
\mathds{R}^{d}
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1})
is the Lebesgue space of all complex-valued square-integrable functions on this sphere.
To reflect the fact that
u^{\text{inc}}
should propagate in the direction
\omega
, we exclude all plane wave components moving in a direction
s\in\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}
with
\left<s,\omega\right>\leq 0
, that is, we enforce
a(s)=0
for those directions.

As prime example for such an incident focused beam, we consider a Gaussian beam, which we obtain with a Herglotz density of the form of

\displaystyle a(s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}e^{-A\left\|\tilde{s}\right\|^{2}},\quad&\left<s,\omega\right>>0,\\
0,&\left<s,\omega\right>\leq 0,\end{cases}

where
\tilde{s}\coloneqq s-\left<s,\omega\right>\omega
is the component of
s
orthogonal to
\omega
. Motivated by this, we further assume for the later analysis that the Herglotz density
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1})
fulfills that

a(s)\neq 0\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\left<s,\omega\right>>0.

(1)

For the wave propagation, we work within the first Born approximation
[
14
]
, assuming that the values of
f
are sufficiently small such that multiple scattering effects can be neglected. For each scan position
y\in\nu^{\perp}
, the corresponding scattered field
u_{y}
is then governed by the inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation
[
10
, Section 3.3]

\displaystyle(\Delta+k_{0}^{2})u_{y}(x)=-f(x)u^{\text{inc}}_{y}(x),\qquad x\in\mathds{R}^{d},

together with the Sommerfeld radiation condition
[
13
]
, ensuring uniqueness of the solution.

For each scan position, measurements of the scattered field are collected on the
(d-1)
-dimensional hyperplane
\mathds{R}^{d-1}\times\{L\}
with
L>r
chosen sufficiently large such that this measurement plane does not intersect the support of the scattering potential of the object.

The inverse problem now consists in reconstructing the scattering potential
f
from the measured data

m\colon(\mathds{R}^{d-1}\times\{L\})\times\nu^{\perp}\to\mathds{C},\quad m(x,y)\coloneqq u_{y}(x).

(2)

Thus, the acquisition process yields a
2(d-1)
-dimensional dataset. In particular, the dimension of the data exceeds that of the object for
d>2
.

2.1. Fourier diffraction relation for scanning data

To recover from the measurement data in
Equation 2
the scattering potential, we perform a
2(d-1)
-dimensional Fourier transform of the data
m
.
The Fourier diffraction theorem adapted to scanning configurations, derived in
[
5
]
, then provides an algebraic relation between these Fourier-transformed measurements and the Fourier transform of the scattering potential. Before stating this result, we introduce some notations that will be used throughout this article.

For a non-zero vector
v\in\mathds{R}^{d}
, the associated open half-space is given by

\Omega_{v}\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathds{R}^{d}\mid\left<x,v\right>>0\right\},

(3)

and the hemisphere in the direction of
v
with radius
k_{0}
is denoted by

S_{v}\coloneqq\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}\cap\Omega_{v}.

(4)

Moreover, for
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, the reflection of a point
x
across the hyperplane
v^{\perp}
is given by the Householder transform
H_{v}\colon\mathds{R}^{d}\to\mathds{R}^{d}
,

H_{v}x\coloneqq x-2\left<x,v\right>v.

(5)

In particular, we consider the hemisphere
S_{\omega}
oriented along the beam direction
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and the reflection
H_{\nu}
across the scan plane
\nu^{\perp}
orthogonal to the scan normal
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. We then divide
S_{\omega}
into two disjoint parts
S_{\omega}=\Sigma_{1}\sqcup\Sigma_{2}
, where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}\coloneqq\{\sigma\in S_{\omega}\mid H_{\nu}\sigma\notin S_{\omega}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma_{2}\coloneqq\{\sigma\in S_{\omega}\mid H_{\nu}\sigma\in S_{\omega}\}.

(6)

See
2(a)
for an illustration of this decomposition.

(a)

Symmetry on
S_{\omega}
with respect to the scan plane
\nu^{\perp}
.

(b)

Union of upper semicircles centered at
-S_{\omega}
.

:

. The subset

In this configuration, the two regions do not intersect. In general, however,
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and

do not have to be disjoint, see

.

Let
\phi\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{d}f
denote the
d
-dimensional Fourier transform of the scattering potential
f
. According to
[
5
]
, the function
\phi
is then related via the Fourier diffraction relation

\hat{m}(\eta,\sigma)=\begin{cases}a(\sigma)\phi(\eta-\sigma)&\text{if }\sigma\in\Sigma_{1},\\
a(\sigma)\phi(\eta-\sigma)+a(H_{\nu}\sigma)\phi(\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma)&\text{if }\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\end{cases}

(7)

for all
\eta\in S_{e_{d}}
, with
e_{d}
denoting the
d
th standard basis vector in
\mathds{R}^{d}
, to the reduced measurements
\hat{m}\colon S_{e_{d}}\times S_{\omega}\to\mathds{C}
which are with some explicitly known prefactor
C\colon S_{e_{d}}\times S_{\omega}\to\mathds{C}
given by

\hat{m}(\eta,\sigma)\coloneqq C(\eta,\sigma)\int_{\nu^{\perp}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d-1}\times\{L\}}m(x,y)e^{-i\left<\eta,x\right>}e^{i\left<\sigma,y\right>}dS(x)dS(y).

Since the Herglotz density
a
is assumed to be given and the reduced measurements
\hat{m}
can be directly calculated from the measurements
m
,
Equation 7
is a linear equation system for the values of the Fourier transform
\phi
on the set
\mathcal{Y}_{1}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{2}
where

\mathcal{Y}_{1}\coloneqq\{\eta-\sigma\mid\eta\in S_{e_{d}},\ \sigma\in\Sigma_{1}\}\quad\text{\lx@nobreakspace and }\quad\mathcal{Y}_{2}\coloneqq\left\{\eta-\sigma\mid\eta\in S_{e_{d}},\ \sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\right\}

(8)

correspond to the points appearing in the first and second case of
Equation 7
, respectively.

Geometrically, the sets
\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{2}
can be seen as unions of hemispheres centered at
-\Sigma_{1}\coloneqq\{-\sigma\mid\sigma\in\Sigma_{1}\}
and
-\Sigma_{2}\coloneqq\{-\sigma\mid\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\}
, see
2(b)
.

2.2. Reconstructibility of the scattering potential

Unless we are in the case where
\Sigma_{2}=\emptyset
, that is, if
\nu
is parallel to
\omega
so that we are shifting the incident beam perpendicular to the incident direction
\omega
,
Equation 7
is only an implicit equation for the values of the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{d}f
of the scattering potential
f
. Even though we enforced with
Equation 1
that none of the coefficients of the linear system vanishes, it is a priori not clear if this system allows us to recover
\mathcal{F}_{d}f
.

In principle, we could first recover the Fourier coefficients obtained from the first, direct relation in
Equation 7
and then use analytic continuation to determine the remaining ones, since
f
is compactly supported and hence
\mathcal{F}_{d}f\in C^{\infty}(\mathds{R}^{d};\mathds{C})
is an analytic function. However, such arguments have limited practical value, as real measurement data are discrete.

We therefore do not want to rely on analyticity and will not impose any regularity on the solutions
\phi\colon\mathds{R}^{d}\to\mathds{C}
of
Equation 7
for now. The question is then for which points
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
the values
\phi(y)
are uniquely determined by
Equation 7
.

To get rid of one of the coefficients
a(\sigma)
and
a(H_{\nu}\sigma)
in
Equation 7
by normalizing it to one, we introduce for some arbitrarily chosen
\chi\in(0,1)
the function
b\colon\Sigma_{2,\chi}\to\mathds{C}\setminus\{0\}
on the open neighborhood

\Sigma_{2,\chi}\coloneqq\left\{\tilde{\chi}\sigma\,\middle|\,\sigma\in\Sigma_{2},\ \tilde{\chi}\in(1-\chi,\ 1+\chi)\right\}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}

(9)

of
\Sigma_{2}
by

b(\tilde{\chi}\sigma)\coloneqq\frac{a(\sigma)}{a(H_{\nu}\sigma)}\text{ for all }\sigma\in\Sigma_{2},\,\tilde{\chi}\in(1-\chi,1+\chi).

(10)

(Hereby, we only extended
b
to the open set
\Sigma_{2,\chi}
to be able to differentiate it like a function on
\mathds{R}^{d}
instead of having to use covariant derivatives on the sphere.)

Let
\phi
and
\tilde{\phi}
be two solutions of
Equation 7
. Since
a(\sigma)\neq 0
for every
\sigma\in\Sigma_{1}
and
a(H_{\nu}\sigma)\neq 0
for every
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
, we can divide the first equation in
Equation 7
by
a(\sigma)
and the second equation by
a(H_{\nu}\sigma)
. Then, the difference
g\coloneqq\phi-\tilde{\phi}
satisfies the homogeneous system

0=\begin{cases}g(\eta-\sigma)&\text{if }\sigma\in\Sigma_{1},\\
b(\sigma)g(\eta-\sigma)+g(\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma)&\text{if }\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\end{cases}

(11)

for all
\eta\in S_{e_{d}}
. Thus, we have for an arbitrary point
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
that
\phi(y)=\tilde{\phi}(y)
for all solutions
\phi
and
\tilde{\phi}
of
Equation 7
if and only if every solution of the homogeneous system satisfies
g(y)=0
.

The aim of this work is to characterize all such points
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
.

.

Let
d\in\mathds{N}\setminus\{1\}

from

for all

What is the largest set

Clearly, the first equation in

Suppose

.
Then, we have

3. Structure of the System through Coupling Sets

We start the analysis of
Section 2.2
by examining the second equation of
Equation 11
, namely

0=b(\sigma)g(\eta-\sigma)+g(\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma),\qquad\ \eta\in S_{e_{d}},\ \sigma\in\Sigma_{2}.

(13)

A key observation is that the representation of a point
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
via
y=\eta-\sigma
with
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
and
\eta\in S_{e_{d}}
is, in general, not unique. Different pairs
(\eta,\sigma)
can produce the same point
y
, but lead to distinct coupled points
\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma
.

Definition 3.1
.

For
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
we introduce the set
F_{y}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}
of all points
z\in\mathds{R}^{d}
for which there exist values
\eta\in S_{e_{d}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
with

y=\eta-\sigma\quad\text{and}\quad z=\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma.

We call
F_{y}
the
coupling set
of
y
.

In other words,
F_{y}
collects all points
z
whose images
g(z)
show up together with
g(y)
in at least one equation of the system. By this, we can group all equations containing the term
g(y)
.

Note that we have
F_{y}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
, as
\mathcal{Y}_{2}
is by definition the set of all points
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
of the form
y=\eta-\sigma
with
\eta\in S_{e_{d}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
which then leads to the point
\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma\in F_{y}
.
The following lemma provides an explicit characterization of the coupling set
F_{y}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

In the setting of
Section 2.2
, we have for the coupling set
F_{y}
of an arbitrary point
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
the identity

F_{y}=\left\{y+2\left<\sigma,\nu\right>\nu\mid\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y)\right\},

(14)

where
S_{e_{d}}-y=\{\sigma-y\in\mathds{R}^{d}\mid\sigma\in S_{e_{d}}\}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}
denotes the upper hemisphere centered at
-y
.

Proof
:

Let
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
. The set
M_{y}
of all points
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
for which there exists a value
\eta\in S_{e_{d}}
satisfying

can be written as the intersection

M_{y}=\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y).

The points
z
in
F_{y}
are then characterized by

z=(y+\sigma)-H_{\nu}\sigma=y+2\left<\sigma,\nu\right>\nu\quad\text{with }\sigma\in M_{y},

that is,
Equation 14
.

\square

Fix
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and let
z\in F_{y}
. By definition of
F_{y}
, there exists at least one
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y)
with

z-y=2\left<\sigma,\nu\right>\nu

and the corresponding value
\eta=\sigma+y
. For such a choice of
\sigma
we have

0=b(\sigma)g(y)+g(z).

In general, however, different
\sigma
may correspond to the same pair of points
(y,z)
, leading to different coefficients
b(\sigma)
, and thus to potentially distinct relations between
g(y)
and
g(z)
.

Definition 3.3
.

For every
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and every

, we define the set

C_{y,\lambda}\coloneqq\left\{\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y)\mid\left<\sigma,\nu\right>=\lambda\right\}

(15)

of all points in
\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y)
lying in the plane perpendicular to
\nu

With these definitions,

can equivalently be written as

0=b(\sigma)g(y)+g(z)\quad\text{for all}\quad y\in\mathds{R}^{d},\,z\in F_{y},\,\sigma\in C_{y,\frac{1}{2}\left<z-y,\nu\right>}.

(16)

Remark 3.4
.

In view of
Equation 16
, we see the fundamental difference between the different dimensions:

•

For
d>2
, the intersection of
\Sigma_{2}
and
S_{e_{d}}-y
in the characterization of

in

is, in the case where the intersection is transversal and non-trivial, a relatively open subset of a

-dimensional sphere. Consequently,

contains infinitely many points and the value

.

If

relating the terms

and

.

•

In three dimensions, the set

of a circle with a plane, and thus consists, in the non-degenerate case, of at most two points. For a fixed pair

of points, we will therefore, in general, not have multiple equations relating the two terms

and

.

But we will still have for most points

that

appears in infinitely many equations in

.

•

In two dimensions, finally, the set

contains according to

(since

and

3.1. Graph representation of the system in two dimensions

We first consider the two dimensional setting in
Section 2.2
. In this case, the term
g(y)
of each point
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}\setminus\{0\}
appears in the system in
Equation 16
in at most two equations. Unless we get two equations coupling
g(y)
twice with the same term
g(z)
, these equations alone are not sufficient to determine all occurring values uniquely. We therefore seek to combine these equations to a system with the same amount of unknowns and equations.

To visualize these relations in a clear and structured way, we will make use of graph representations. More precisely, we view the set
\mathcal{Y}_{2}
as vertices and we draw an edge between two points whenever there exists an equation that directly couples their images. In other words, two vertices
y,z\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
are connected by an edge exactly when
z\in F_{y}
.

Definition 3.5
.

We call
(\mathcal{Y}_{2},E)
the graph associated with the equation system in
Equation 16
, where the vertices are the points in
\mathcal{Y}_{2}
and the edges are given by

E\coloneqq\{\{y,z\}\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}\times\mathcal{Y}_{2}\mid z\in F_{y}\}.

Moreover, a point
z\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
is called a neighbor of
y
if
\{y,z\}\in E
.

For a given point
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
, we can explicitly determine its neighboring points.

Lemma 3.6
.

We consider the setting of
Section 2.2
for
d=2
. For all values
\eta\in S_{e_{2}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
with
\eta\neq\sigma
, the coupling set
F_{y}
of the point
y\coloneqq\eta-\sigma
is given by

\displaystyle F_{y}=\begin{cases}\left\{\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma,H_{\nu}\eta-\sigma\right\}\quad&\text{if }\eta\in(-\Sigma_{2})\cap S_{e_{2}},\ \sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap S_{-e_{2}},\\
\left\{\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma\right\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(17)

Proof
:

Let
y=\eta-\sigma
with
\eta\in S_{e_{2}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
. The circles
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{k_{0}}
and
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{k_{0}}-y
intersect in at most two points, namely
\sigma=\eta-y
and
-\eta=-\sigma-y
. (If
\eta=-\sigma
, the intersection reduces to
-\eta
.) Hence, we always have

\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{2}}-y)\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{1}\cap(\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{1}-1)=\{\sigma,-\eta\}.

The point
\sigma
is always contained in
\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{2}}-y)
, while the second point
-\eta
belongs to
\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{2}}-y)
if and only if

-\eta\in\Sigma_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad-\sigma=-\eta+y\in S_{e_{2}}.

We thus obtain

\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{2}}-y)=\begin{cases}\{\sigma,-\eta\}\qquad&\text{if }\eta\in(-\Sigma_{2})\cap S_{e_{2}},\ \sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap S_{-e_{2}},\\
\{\sigma\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Using the representation of
F_{y}
from
Equation 14
completes the proof.

\square

Let us mention some special cases of sets
F_{y}
for
y=\eta-\sigma
with
\eta\in S_{e_{2}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
:

•

If
\sigma\in\nu^{\perp}
or if
\eta\in\nu^{\perp}\cap(-\Sigma_{2})
and
\sigma\in S_{-e_{2}}
, then
y\in F_{y}
. This means that one equation in
Equation 16
is of the form
(1+b(\sigma))g(y)=0
.

•

If we have
\eta\in\{-\sigma,H_{\nu}\sigma\}
, then
\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma=H_{\nu}\eta-\sigma
. Thus,
F_{y}
consists at most of one point. Otherwise, the points
\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma
and
H_{\nu}\eta-\sigma
are distinct.

•

If
\eta=\sigma
, that is,
y=0
, the coupling set is given by

F_{0}=\{2\left<\sigma,\nu\right>\nu\mid\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap S_{e_{2}}\},

which forms an entire interval on the line spanned by
\nu
.

To avoid additional technicalities associated with these degenerate configurations, we will exclude all such points from the analysis of reconstructibility on
\mathcal{Y}_{2}
in two dimension. They will nevertheless only influence the reconstructibility on a set of measure zero.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathds{R}^{2}
be the set of all points
\eta-\sigma
with
\eta\in S_{e_{2}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
for which
\eta\in\nu^{\perp}
or
\sigma\in\nu^{\perp}
. We call
\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathcal{Y}_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}^{d}_{2k_{0}}\setminus(S\cup\nu^{\perp}\cup\mathds{R}\nu)
the set of non-degenerate points in
\mathcal{Y}_{2}
.

For such points
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{\prime}
, we can guarantee that the points appearing in
Lemma 3.6
are all distinct.

Lemma 3.8
.

We consider the setting of
Section 2.2
for
d=2
. Let
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{\prime}
be written in the form
y=\eta-\sigma
with
\eta\in S_{e_{2}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
.

We then have that all the points
\eta-\sigma
,
H_{\nu}\eta-\sigma
,
\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma
, and
H_{\nu}\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma
are distinct and not zero.

Proof
:

For one of the points to be zero, we would need that
\eta\in\{\sigma,H_{\nu}\sigma\}
, which would imply that
y=\eta-\sigma=0
or
y=\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma+2\left<\sigma,\nu\right>\nu=2\left<\sigma,\nu\right>\nu\in\mathds{R}\nu
.

For two points which differ by an application of
H_{\nu}
to one term to be equal, we would need that
\eta=H_{\nu}\eta
or
\sigma=H_{\nu}\sigma
, which would require
\eta\in\nu^{\perp}
or
\sigma\in\nu^{\perp}
.

For
H_{\nu}\eta-\sigma=\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma
, we would need that
\eta\in\{-\sigma,H_{\nu}\sigma\}
, which would imply
y=2\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{2k_{0}}
or, as before,
y\in\mathds{R}\nu
.

Finally, for
\eta-\sigma=H_{\nu}(\eta-\sigma)
, we would require
y=\eta-\sigma\in\nu^{\perp}
.

\square

With the characterization of the neighbors of a vertex
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
presented in
Lemma 3.6
, we can now analyze how the connected component of an arbitrary non-degenerate vertex looks like.

Lemma 3.9
.

We consider the setting of
Section 2.2
for
d=2
. Each connected component
(C,E_{C})
of the graph
(\mathcal{Y}_{2},E)
associated to the equation system defined in
Equation 16
containing a vertex from
\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{\prime}
has exactly one of the following forms:

(i)

for some
\eta\in S_{e_{2}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
with
\eta\notin(-\Sigma_{2})
or
\sigma,H_{\nu}\sigma\notin S_{-e_{2}}
;

(ii)

for some
\eta\in(-\Sigma_{2})\cap S_{e_{2}}
with
H_{\nu}\eta\notin S_{e_{2}}

and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap S_{-e_{2}}
with
H_{\nu}\sigma\notin S_{-e_{2}}
;

(iii)

for some
\eta\in(-\Sigma_{2})\cap S_{e_{2}}
with
H_{\nu}\eta\notin S_{e_{2}}

and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap S_{-e_{2}}
with
H_{\nu}\sigma\in S_{-e_{2}}
;

(iv)

for some
\eta\in(-\Sigma_{2})\cap S_{e_{2}}
with
H_{\nu}\eta\in S_{e_{2}}

and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap S_{-e_{2}}
with
H_{\nu}\sigma\in S_{-e_{2}}
.

Proof
:

Let
(C,E_{C})
be a connected component of the graph
(\mathcal{Y}_{2},E)
containing a non-degenerate point
y\in C\cap\mathcal{Y}_{2}^{\prime}
, which we write in the form
y=\eta-\sigma
with
\eta\in S_{e_{2}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
.

According to
Lemma 3.6
, the set
F_{y}
of neighboring points of
y
fulfills
F_{y}\subseteq\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
where
z_{1}\coloneqq\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma
and
z_{2}\coloneqq H_{\nu}\eta-\sigma
are non-zero according to
Lemma 3.8
. We analogously have that
F_{z_{1}}\subseteq\{y,z_{3}\}
. If
z_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
, then also
F_{z_{2}}\subseteq\{y,z_{3}\}
, where
z_{3}\coloneqq H_{\nu}\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma
is again non-zero. Thus, the second neighborhood of
y
is a subset of
N_{y}\coloneqq\{y,z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\}
. Moreover, since we find in the same way that
F_{z_{3}}\subseteq\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
if
z_{3}\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
, we see that
C\subseteq N_{y}
and
E_{C}\subseteq\{\{y,z_{1}\},\{y,z_{2}\},\{z_{1},z_{3}\},\{z_{2},z_{3}\}\}
.

Since there are no isolated points in the graph
(\mathcal{Y}_{2},E)
and we know from
Lemma 3.8
that all the four points
y
,
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
, and
z_{3}
are distinct, this leaves exactly the four possible connected components listed in the proposition.
To characterize which vertices correspond to which of these cases, we pick the reference point
\eta-\sigma
with
\eta\in S_{e_{2}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}
as shown in the graphs and check for each edge under which condition it exists.

•

By applying
Lemma 3.6
to
y=\eta-\sigma
, we see that the edge
\{y,z_{1}\}
always exists and

•

that the edge
\{y,z_{2}\}
exists if and only if
\eta\in-\Sigma_{2}
and
\sigma\in S_{-e_{2}}
.

•

Applying
Lemma 3.6
to
z_{1}=\eta-H_{\nu}\sigma
, we find that the edge
\{z_{1},z_{3}\}
exists if and only if
\eta\in-\Sigma_{2}
and
H_{\nu}\sigma\in S_{-e_{2}}
.

•

Finally, assuming that the edge
\{y,z_{2}\}
exists, we get by applying
Lemma 3.6
to
z_{2}=-\sigma+H_{\nu}\eta
that the edge
\{z_{2},z_{3}\}
exists if and only if
H_{\nu}\eta\in S_{e_{2}}
.

Putting this together, we get the asserted conditions.

\square

Thus, the graph associated with
Equation 16
decomposes into connected components, and the equation system can be studied separately on each of them.
If, on a given component, the number of vertices exceeds the number of edges (as in the first three cases in
Lemma 3.9
), then the induced linear system for the values of
g
on this component is underdetermined. Consequently, uniqueness cannot be expected unless the value at one of the vertices is already known to be zero from the first part of
Equation 11
.
If the numbers coincide as in the last case in
Lemma 3.9
, the component gives rise to a linear system with equally many equations as unknowns which could potentially be uniquely solvable.

We discuss both possibilities further in
Section 6
.

3.2. Properties of the coupling sets in higher dimensions

For
d>2
, the coupling sets
F_{y}
can contain a continuum of points. First, we show that each non-empty
F_{y}
is forms a relatively open subset of a closed line segment
L_{y}
on a line parallel to
\nu
.

Lemma 3.10
.

We consider the setting of
Section 2.2
for
d>2
. For every point
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
, the coupling set
F_{y}
is a relatively open subset of the closed line segment

L_{y}\coloneqq\left\{y+2\left<\sigma,\nu\right>\nu\mid\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}\cap(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}-y)\right\}=\left\{y+2\lambda\nu\mid\lambda\in\Lambda_{y}\right\},

(18)

with the closed interval

\Lambda_{y}\coloneqq\left\{\left<\sigma,\nu\right>\mid\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}\cap(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}-y)\right\}.

(19)

Proof
:

It is clear from the explicit representation in
Equation 14
that
F_{y}
is a subset of
L_{y}
.

For every point
z\in F_{y}
, we then find a point
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y)
with

z=y+2\lambda_{0}\nu,\quad\lambda_{0}\coloneqq\langle\sigma,\nu\rangle.

Since the set

\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y)=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}\cap(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}-y)\cap\Omega_{\omega}\cap\Omega_{H_{\nu}\omega}\cap(\Omega_{e_{d}}-y)

is relatively open in the circle
C\coloneqq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}\cap(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}-y)
, we find a ball
\mathcal{B}^{d}_{\rho}(\sigma)
with
\mathcal{B}^{d}_{\rho}(\sigma)\cap C\subseteq\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y)
. We thus have

\displaystyle L_{y}\cap\{y+2\lambda\nu\mid\lambda\in(\lambda_{0}-\rho,\lambda_{0}+\rho)\}

\displaystyle=\{y+2\lambda\nu\mid\lambda\in(\lambda_{0}-\rho,\lambda_{0}+\rho)\cap\Lambda_{y}\}

\displaystyle=\{y+2\langle\tilde{\sigma},\nu\rangle\nu\mid\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{B}^{d}_{\rho}(\sigma)\cap C\}\subseteq F_{y},

showing that
z
is an inner point of
F_{y}\subseteq L_{y}
.

\square

Next, we investigate how the coupling sets of two base points behave when the points are sufficiently close to each other.

Lemma 3.11
.

We consider the setting of
Section 2.2
for
d>2
. Let
y\in\mathcal{B}^{d}_{2k_{0}}
be a point for which the coupling set
F_{y}
is not empty.

Then, there exists a point
\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{B}^{d}_{2k_{0}}\cap(y+\mathds{R}\nu)
such that
F_{y}\cap F_{\tilde{y}}

contains a non-empty open line segment on
y+\mathds{R}\nu
.

Proof
:

Let us introduce the sets
L_{y}
and
\Lambda_{y}
as in
Lemma 3.10
and define the interior
\mathring{L}_{y}\coloneqq\{y+2\lambda\nu\mid\lambda\in\mathring{\Lambda}_{y}\}
of
L_{y}
in
y+\mathds{R}\nu
, which is not empty because of the assumption
y\in\mathcal{B}^{d}_{2k_{0}}
.

Since
F_{y}
is according to
Lemma 3.10
a relatively open subset of the line segment
L_{y}
, we can find a point
z\in F_{y}\cap\mathring{L}_{y}
. For this point, we then have a
\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y)
with

z=y+2\lambda\nu,\qquad\lambda\coloneqq\left<\sigma,\nu\right>\in\mathring{\Lambda}_{y}.

Furthermore, the openness of
F_{y}
allows us to find a parameter
\varepsilon>0
with
y+2\tilde{\lambda}\nu\in F_{y}
for all
\tilde{\lambda}\in(\lambda-\varepsilon,\lambda+\varepsilon)
.

Since the spheres
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}
and
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{k_{0}}-y
intersect transversally for
y\in\mathcal{B}^{d}_{2k_{0}}
, the intersection depends continuously on
y
. That is, we can choose
\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{B}^{d}_{\varepsilon}(y)\cap(y+\mathds{R}\nu)
close to
y
with
\mathring{L}_{y}\cap\mathring{L}_{\tilde{y}}\neq\emptyset
such that

\operatorname{dist}(\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y),\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-\tilde{y}))<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

In particular, we can find a point
\tilde{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-\tilde{y})
with
\left\|\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}\right\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Defining then the point

\tilde{z}\coloneqq\tilde{y}+2\left<\tilde{\sigma},\nu\right>\nu=z+(\tilde{y}-y)+2\left<\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma,\nu\right>\nu\in F_{\tilde{y}},

and remarking that
\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{y}-y)+\left<\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma,\nu\right>\nu=\hat{\lambda}\nu
for some
\hat{\lambda}\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, we find that
\tilde{z}=y+2\tilde{\lambda}\nu\in F_{y}\cap F_{\tilde{y}}
with
\tilde{\lambda}\coloneqq\lambda+\hat{\lambda}\in(\lambda-\varepsilon,\lambda+\varepsilon)
.

Thus,
F_{y}\cap F_{\tilde{y}}
is a non-empty, relatively open subset of the non-degenerate line segment
L_{y}\cap L_{\tilde{y}}
.

\square

4. Reconstructible Fourier coefficients in dimensions larger than three

In this section, we study the
Section 2.2
in the case
d>3
, exploiting the additional structure provided by the sets
C_{y,\lambda}
introduced in
Section 3
. Recall that for fixed
y\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and
z\in F_{y}
the
Equation 16
yields the family

0=b(\sigma)g(y)+g(z)\qquad\text{for all }\sigma\in C_{y,\frac{1}{2}\left<z-y,\nu\right>}

of equations. If at least two of these equations are linearly independent, then
g(y)=g(z)=0
.

Proposition 4.1
.

We consider the setting of
Section 2.2
for
d>3
. Let
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
and suppose that
g
solves the homogeneous system from
Equation 11
.

If there exists a value
\lambda\in\mathds{R}
such that the function
b
is not constant on the set
C_{y,\lambda}
, introduced in
Section 3
, then
g(y)=0
.

Proof
:

Since
d>3
and, by assumption,
b
is not constant on
C_{y,\lambda}
, there exist two distinct vectors
\sigma,\hat{\sigma}\in C_{y,\lambda}
with
b(\sigma)\neq b(\hat{\sigma})
. Then, the pair
(g(y),g(z))
with
z\coloneqq y+2\lambda\nu
satisfies the system

\displaystyle B\begin{pmatrix}g(y)\\
g(z)\end{pmatrix}=0,\quad\text{with }B\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}b(\sigma)&1\\
b(\hat{\sigma})&1\end{pmatrix}.

Since the determinant of
B
is

\det(B)=b(\sigma)-b(\hat{\sigma})\neq 0,

the only solution is
g(y)=g(z)=0
.

\square

Section 4
shows that
g(y)=0
for every
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
for which there exists a level
\lambda\in\mathds{R}
such that
b
is non-constant on
C_{y,\lambda}
. If we impose that the solution
g
shall be continuous, we can extend this point-wise result also to points
y
which do not fulfill the assumptions in
Section 4
, but for which we find in every neighborhood of
y
some point which does.

Lemma 4.2
.

We consider the setting of
Section 2.2
for
d>3
. Let
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2k_{0}}^{d}\setminus\mathds{R}\nu
. There then exist an open neighborhood
V\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}
of
y
and an open interval
I\subseteq\mathds{R}
such that the set
C_{y^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}
, introduced in
Section 3
, is a
(d-3)
-dimensional manifold for every
y^{\prime}\in V
and
\lambda^{\prime}\in I
.

Its tangent space is given by

\mathrm{T}_{\sigma^{\prime}}C_{y^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}=\big(\operatorname{span}\{y^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\nu\}\big)^{\perp}\quad\text{for every }\sigma^{\prime}\in C_{y^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}.

(20)

Proof
:

For an arbitrary value
\lambda\in\mathds{R}
, the set
C_{y,\lambda}
is by definition the intersection of the relatively open subset
M_{y}\coloneqq\Sigma_{2}\cap(S_{e_{d}}-y)
of
\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}\cap(\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}-y)
and the hyperplane
N_{\lambda}\coloneqq\{\sigma\in\mathds{R}^{d}\mid\left<\sigma,\nu\right>=\lambda\}
. Since
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
, we have that
M_{y}\neq\emptyset
, which is thus a
(d-2)
-dimensional submanifold of the
(d-2)
-dimensional sphere

\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}\cap(\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}-y)=\left\{\sigma\in\mathds{R}^{d}\;\middle|\;\left<\sigma-\frac{y}{2},y\right>=0,\,\left\|\sigma-\frac{y}{2}\right\|=r_{y}\right\}

with radius
r_{y}\coloneqq\sqrt{k_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left\|y\right\|^{2}}\in(0,k_{0})
around
\frac{y}{2}
in a plane orthogonal to
y
. The orthogonal complement of its tangent space at a point
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}\cap(\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}-y)
is thus given by
\operatorname{span}\{y,\sigma-\frac{y}{2}\}=\operatorname{span}\{y,\sigma\}
.

We denote by
\nu_{y}\coloneqq\nu-\left<\nu,y\right>\frac{y}{\left\|y\right\|^{2}}
the orthogonal projection of
\nu
onto the orthogonal complement of
y
. Since
y\notin\mathds{R}\nu
, we have that
\nu_{y}\neq 0
. For every point

\sigma\in\tilde{M}_{y}\coloneqq M_{y}\setminus\operatorname{span}\{y,\nu\}=M_{y}\setminus\left\{\frac{y}{2}-\frac{r_{y}}{\left\|\nu_{y}\right\|}\nu_{y},\frac{y}{2}+\frac{r_{y}}{\left\|\nu_{y}\right\|}\nu_{y}\right\}

the vectors
y
,
\sigma
, and
\nu
are linearly independent, which implies that the tangent spaces of
\tilde{M}_{y}

-dimensional manifold.

\square

5. Reconstructible Fourier coefficients in three dimensions

If we reduce in
Section 2.2
the dimensions to
d=3
, it is no longer possible to choose for every point
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
a value
\lambda\in\mathds{R}
such that the set
C_{y,\lambda}
, defined in
Section 3
, consists of a continuum of points. Hence, in contrast to the higher-dimensional case, we cannot obtain an infinite number of equations for the pair
(g(y),g(z))
with
z\coloneqq y+2t\nu
in
Equation 16
.

Instead, as discussed in
Section 3
, the set
C_{y,\lambda}
consists in the non-degenerate case of at most two points and for some
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
, there may be no value
\lambda
for which
C_{y,\lambda}
has more than a single point.

To overcome this, we exploit
Lemma 3.11
. It states that the coupling set
F_{\tilde{y}}
of a point
\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
is a relatively open subset of a typically non-degenerate line segment. Hence, we can choose an additional point
\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
on the line
\tilde{y}+\mathds{R}\nu
such that the intersection of the coupling sets
F_{\tilde{y}}
and
F_{\hat{y}}
is a non-empty, relatively open subset of a non-degenerate line segment. Selecting two distinct points
\tilde{z},\hat{z}\in F_{\tilde{y}}\cap F_{\hat{y}}
gives one equation for each of the pairs
(g(\tilde{y}),g(\tilde{z}))
,
(g(\tilde{y}),g(\hat{z}))
,
(g(\hat{y}),g(\tilde{z}))
, and
(g(\hat{y}),g(\hat{z}))
in
Equation 16
. If these equations are linearly independent, the only solution is
g(\tilde{y})=g(\hat{y})=g(\tilde{z})=g(\hat{z})=0
.

We now relate this back to the original point
y
: given
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
together with a point
z\in F_{y}
in its coupling set, we show that there typically exists in every neighborhood of
y
a point
\tilde{y}=y+w\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
which we can complete with
\hat{y}=y+w+\delta\nu
,
\tilde{z}=z+w
, and
z+w+\varepsilon\nu
to such a set of four points.

.

Lemma 5.1

In the setting of
Section 2.2
for

Proof

5.1. Analysis of the system determinant

Taken together,

, and

and

7. Conclusion

In this article, we analyzed the Fourier diffraction relation arising in the extension of diffraction tomography to general beam scanning geometries, as introduced in
[
5
]
. In this setting, the Fourier diffraction relation may couple two Fourier coefficients of the objects scattering potential through a single measurement, which raises the question of whether these coefficients can be uniquely recovered.

For dimensions
d\geq 3
, we proved that all Fourier coefficients appearing in the measurements can, under mild assumptions on the Herglotz density
a\colon\mathbb{S}_{k_{0}}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{C}
, be uniquely determined from the data. In two dimensions, however, only a subset of these Fourier coefficients is uniquely recoverable, while the remaining coefficients cannot be reconstructed from the measurements.

Nevertheless, even in that case, the recoverable Fourier coefficients go beyond those that can be directly read off from the Fourier data of the measurments.
