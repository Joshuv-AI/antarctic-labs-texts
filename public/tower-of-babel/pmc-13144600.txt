FOXO1-mediated cell cycle arrest enhances osteogenic differentiation of spheroid-cultured human periodontal ligament–derived multipotent mesenchymal stromal cells
Authors: Sho Hironaka, Satoru Onizuka, Kotaro Sano, Yuichiro Oka, Takenori Suga, Shoichiro Kokabu, Wataru Ariyoshi, Kohji Nakazawa, Shinichi Mochizuki, Michihiko Usui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPeriodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by progressive alveolar bone loss. Although spheroid culture enhances the osteogenic and regenerative potential of human periodontal ligament–derived multipotent mesenchymal stromal cells (hPDLMSCs), the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the transcriptional features of spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs, with a particular focus on cell cycle regulation and Forkhead Box O (FOXO) transcription factors.MethodsMonolayer- and spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs were subjected to transcriptome analysis using RNA sequencing. Differentially expressed genes and enriched signaling pathways were identified, followed by validation through qRT-PCR and Western blotting. Flow cytometric analysis was then performed to compare cell cycle characteristics between monolayer- and spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs. The functional roles of FOXO1 and FOXO4 were examined using siRNA-mediated knockdown combined with cell cycle and osteogenic differentiation analyses.ResultsTranscriptome analysis revealed significant alterations in cell cycle–related genes and FOXO signaling in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs, including downregulation of cyclin family genes associated with cell cycle progression. Spheroid culture induced cell cycle arrest, characterized by an increased G0/G1 phase population and elevated expression of FOXO1, FOXO4, and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKN1A, CDKN1B, and CDKN1C). FOXO1 knockdown promoted cell cycle progression and markedly reduced stemness and osteogenic marker expression as well as alkaline phosphatase activity. Similarly, FOXO4 knockdown decreased stemness-related gene expression but had a limited effect on osteogenic differentiation.ConclusionsFOXO1-mediated cell cycle regulation supports the maintenance of stemness and osteogenic potential in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs, suggesting that FOXO1 may serve as a promising target for periodontal tissue regeneration.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting tooth-supporting tissues and leading to progressive alveolar bone loss [1]. Dysbiotic bacteria trigger persistent inflammation, which promotes osteoclastogenesis and suppresses osteoblast activity [2]. Although various regenerative therapies have been investigated [3,4], their efficacy and predictability remain limited, underscoring the need for more effective strategies. Multipotent mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) hold great promise for tissue regeneration because of their self-renewal capacity and multilineage differentiation potential into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes [5,6]. They also exhibit low immunogenicity and possess immunosuppressive properties, enabling their therapeutic use in inflammatory diseases such as periodontitis [7,8].

In the dental field, human periodontal ligament-derived multipotent mesenchymal stromal cells (hPDLMSCs) have been identified and shown to possess similar multipotent characteristics [9]. Owing to these properties, hPDLMSCs have been employed in regenerative approaches targeting periodontal tissues [10].

Traditional two-dimensional (2D) culture is widely used for MSC expansion; however, it restricts cell-cell and cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions [11]. In contrast, spheroid (three-dimensional; 3D) culture better replicates native tissue architecture, promoting intercellular communication, ECM interactions, growth factor secretion, and osteogenic differentiation [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Consequently, spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs exhibit greater regenerative potential than their monolayer-cultured counterparts. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms underlying this enhancement remain insufficiently understood.

Another notable feature of MSC spheroids is that their in vitro formation extends the replicative lifespan of MSCs and delays cellular senescence [12]. It has been reported that proliferating cells are rarely observed in the inner regions of spheroids [16]. Consistent with this spatial restriction of cell proliferation, MSC spheroids as a whole exhibit a lower proliferative capacity compared with MSCs cultured in a monolayer [14,17]. Although MSC spheroid culture has been linked to enhanced regenerative properties, including altered proliferative behavior, the transcriptional landscape underlying these spheroid-associated characteristics has not yet been fully characterized.

In this study, we conducted transcriptome analysis to elucidate the molecular characteristics of MSC spheroids. Given the marked alterations in cell cycle–related genes, we further examined the involvement of Forkhead box O (FOXO) transcription factors in regulating cell cycle dynamics in spheroid-cultured MSCs.

Materials/methods

Cell culture

hPDLMSCs were isolated from extracted wisdom teeth free of periodontal and apical disease after obtaining written informed consent, as previously described [14]. Briefly, the periodontal ligament from the middle third of the root was digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase type I (Wako, Osaka, Japan) and 1200 PU/mL dispase (Wako) to obtain a single-cell suspension, which was then passed through a 70 μm strainer. The cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biocera, Nuaillé, France), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced after 24 h, and subculturing was performed every 4 days until passages 4-5.

Spheroid formation

Spheroids were formed using microwell chips prepared as previously described [14,[18], [19], [20]]. Briefly, microwell chips with a diameter and depth of 500 μm were fabricated using a milling system (PMT Corp., Fukuoka, Japan). The chip surface was coated with a layer of platinum using an ion sputter unit (Hitachi High-Tech Science Systems Corp., Ibaraki, Japan).

The chips were immersed in 5 mM polyethylene glycol (PEG) in ethanol, washed with distilled water, and rinsed with 50% ethanol to remove unattached PEG and achieve sterilization. Before use, the chips were immersed in culture medium. hPDLMSCs were seeded at a density of 2000 cells per well on the microwell chips placed in 35 mm culture dishes containing 2 mL of α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS.

The culture medium was changed every 2 days, and spheroid formation was carried out in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Transcriptome analysis

hPDLMSCs were isolated from extracted wisdom teeth of four patients. Cells from each donor were cultured in microwell chips for 3 days to generate spheroids, while hPDLMSCs from the same donors cultured in monolayer conditions for 3 days served as controls. Total RNA was extracted from both groups using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed using the Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Adaptor sequences and low-quality bases in the FASTQ files were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39). Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) with HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Gene expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM) using Cufflinks (v2.2.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between spheroid and monolayer groups were identified using Cuffcompare, Cuffquant, and Cuffdiff, as previously described [21]. DEGs were defined as those with a q-value <0.05 and a fold change in FPKM ≥2.0. The hg38 reference genome and RefSeq annotation were obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID; https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). KEGG pathway data were obtained from the NCBI database (ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/DATA/). Most RNA-seq plots and graphs were generated using ggplot2 (v3.0.0) and related R packages. Hierarchical clustering analysis was performed using the hclust function in R.

RNA interference

RNA interference was performed as previously described [20]. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting FOXO1 (s5258), FOXO4 (s8835), and a non-targeting control (4390843) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. After seeding 4.0 × 105 cells into the microwell chips, the cells were transfected with siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Five hours after transfection, the medium containing the transfection reagent was replaced with fresh α-MEM, and the cells were cultured for 3 days.

Alkaline phosphatase activity (ALP) assay

ALP activity in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs was measured using the LabAssay™ ALP kit (Wako) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs treated with siRNA or non-targeting control were cultured in hMSC Osteogenic Differentiation Medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) as an osteoinductive medium for 7 days. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer (Wako). The cell lysates were then incubated with p-nitrophenyl phosphate substrate at 37 °C for 15 min, after which a reaction stop solution was added. The optical density of the resulting p-nitrophenol was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices LLC, Union City, CA, USA). Protein concentration in each sample was determined using the BCA™ Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to normalize ALP enzyme activity.

Western blotting analysis

Western blotting was performed as previously described [20]. Cells from monolayer and spheroid cultures were rinsed with ice-cold PBS, and total cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (Wako) supplemented with sodium orthovanadate (V) (Wako), phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), and Halt™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA™ Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were separated on Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast Gels (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and transferred onto Immun-Blot PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Nonspecific binding sites were blocked by incubating the membranes in Blocking One buffer (Nacalai Tesque) for 30 min at room temperature.

The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies against CDKN1B/p27 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; #3698) and CDKN1C/p57 (Cell Signaling Technology, #2557). β-Actin (Cell Signaling Technology, #12262) was used as an endogenous loading control. The blots were visualized using ImmunoStar LD (Wako) and the ChemiDoc™ XRS Plus imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Cell cycle analysis

For monolayer cultures, hPDLMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and harvested after 3 days. The cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, and the resulting pellet was resuspended and fixed with 1000 μL of ice-cold 70% ethanol at 4 °C for 30 min.

For spheroid cultures, 500 spheroids were collected from the microwell chips, centrifuged, and the supernatant was removed. The pellets were resuspended in trypsin solution and gently pipetted for 10 min to obtain single-cell suspensions. These single cells were then centrifuged and fixed with ice-cold 70% ethanol following the same procedure as for the monolayer cultures.

After fixation, the cells were washed with PBS containing 2% bovine serum albumin. The supernatant was removed by centrifugation, and the cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI)/RNase solution (ImmunoStep, Salamanca, Spain) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Finally, the stained cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). RNA-seq analysis was performed using four independent donor samples, and all other experiments were performed using three independent donor samples and repeated at least three times. Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel software (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Comparisons between groups were analyzed using the paired Student's t-test, and p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Gene expression profile of spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs by RNA-seq

To compare gene expression profiles between hPDLMSCs cultured as monolayers and those cultured as spheroids, RNA-seq was performed. Transcriptomic analysis of the sequencing data identified genes that were upregulated in monolayer- and spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs, respectively. KEGG pathway analysis of the upregulated genes in monolayer-cultured hPDLMSCs revealed significant enrichment of the cell cycle pathway (Fig. 1A). In contrast, KEGG analysis of the upregulated genes in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs indicated significant enrichment of the FOXO signaling pathway, which is known to be associated with the inhibition of cell cycle progression (Fig. 1B). Based on these transcriptomic results, pathways related to cell cycle regulation were markedly altered in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs compared with monolayer-cultured hPDLMSCs. In light of these differences, we further examined cyclin genes as key regulators of cell cycle control. Analysis of multiple cyclin-related genes revealed distinct expression profiles between the two culture conditions. In spheroid cultures, genes promoting cell cycle progression (indicated by red boxes) were generally downregulated, whereas genes involved in cell cycle suppression (indicated by blue boxes) were upregulated (Fig. 1C). To further visualize the global transcriptional differences between the two culture conditions, a volcano plot was generated (Fig. 1D). Genes upregulated in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs are shown on the right side of the plot, whereas those upregulated in monolayer-cultured hPDLMSCs are shown on the left. Notably, osteogenesis-related genes, including BMP2, RUNX2, and BGLAP, were upregulated in spheroid cultures. Altered cell cycle-related gene expression and cell cycle profile in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs

We performed qRT-PCR analysis to compare the mRNA expression of cell cycle-related genes between monolayer- and spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs. First, we examined the expression of Cyclin A2 (CCNA2) and Cyclin E1 (CCNE1), which are key regulators of the G1/S phase transition and play essential roles in cell cycle progression. The mRNA expression of both CCNA2 and CCNE1 in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs were less than 10% of those in monolayer-cultured hPDLMSCs (Fig. 2A). Next, we evaluated the mRNA expression of FOXO transcription factors (FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXO4) and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKN1A/p21, CDKN1B/p27, CDKN1C/p57), which are known to inhibit cell cycle progression. The mRNA expression of FOXO1, FOXO3, and FOXO4 in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs was approximately 18-fold, 3-fold, and 8-fold higher, respectively, than that in monolayer-cultured hPDLMSCs. Furthermore, the mRNA expression of CDKN1A, CDKN1B, and CDKN1C in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs was approximately 2.5-fold, 10-fold, and 25-fold higher, respectively, than that in monolayer-cultured cells (Fig. 2B). Protein expression of CDKN1B and CDKN1C were also about twofold higher in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs compared with monolayer-cultured hPDLMSCs (Fig. 2C). Flow cytometric analysis was performed to quantify the cell population in each phase of the cell cycle (G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases) in both culture conditions. The role of FOXO1 and FOXO4 in maintaining stemness of spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs

To elucidate the role of FOXO transcription factors in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs, siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments were performed. FOXO1 and FOXO4 were selected for further analysis based on their relatively higher expression levels compared with FOXO3 in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs. First, the knockdown efficiency of the siRNAs was evaluated. Following siRNA treatment, the mRNA expression of both FOXO1 and FOXO4 in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs were reduced by approximately 60% (Fig. 3A). We next examined the effects of siFOXO1 and siFOXO4 on the mRNA expression of stemness markers OCT4 and NANOG in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs. The role of FOXO1 and FOXO4 in the cell cycle and osteogenesis of spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs

We next examined the function of FOXO transcription factors on cell cycle status and osteogenic differentiation in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs using siRNA-mediated assay. The mRNA expression of both CCNA2 and CCNE1 were increased following FOXO1 knockdown (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis revealed that the proportion of G0/G1-phase cells decreased, while the proportion of S-phase cells increased after FOXO1 knockdown. However, no change was observed in the G2/M-phase cell population (Fig. 4C). In contrast, after FOXO4 knockdown, the mRNA expression of CCNA2 and CCNE1 increased slightly, but these changes were not statistically significant (Fig. 4B). Discussion

In this study, transcriptome analysis revealed that spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs exhibited reduced expression of cell cycle-related genes (CCNA2 and CCNE1), accompanied by G0/G1-phase arrest. In contrast, FOXO transcription factors (FOXO1, FOXO3, and FOXO4) and their downstream cell cycle inhibitors (CDKN1A, CDKN1B, and CDKN1C) were upregulated. These results suggest that FOXO activation may contribute to cell cycle suppression in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs.

FOXO proteins regulate diverse cellular processes, including cell cycle arrest, oxidative stress response, apoptosis, and stemness maintenance [[23], [24], [25]]. Specifically, FOXO1 suppresses the G1/S transition by inhibiting CCND2 and inducing the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p27, thereby restricting early G1 progression [[26], [27], [28]]. This FOXO1-mediated cell cycle regulation is closely linked to cell fate determination and differentiation. G0/G1 arrest has been associated with the maintenance of stemness and enhanced osteogenic differentiation potential in MSCs [[29], [30], [31]]. However, whereas previous studies primarily focused on FOXO1 regulation during the early G1 phase via CCND2, its involvement in later stages of the cell cycle—particularly through CCNA2 and CCNE1, which regulate the late G1/S transition and S-phase progression—remains largely unexplored.

We identified that FOXO1 is involved in the regulation of CCNA2 and CCNE1 expression, suggesting that FOXO1-mediated cell cycle regulation is not confined to the early G1 phase but may also extend into the late G1/S transition and S-phase progression. In contrast, FOXO4 knockdown reduced the expression of stemness-associated genes without affecting osteogenic differentiation, indicating functional divergence among FOXO family members.

Previous studies have demonstrated that FOXO1 plays a critical role in osteogenic differentiation beyond its involvement in cell cycle regulation. In murine studies, FOXO1 has been reported to directly bind to the promoters of osteogenic genes such as Runx2 and Alpl [32]. Moreover, FOXO1 knockdown suppresses the expression of key osteogenic markers, including RUNX2, ALP, and OCN, leading to reduced matrix mineralization [33]. These findings establish FOXO1 as a transcriptional regulator that directly promotes osteoblast lineage commitment—a function that has not been observed for FOXO4.

Consistent with previous findings, FOXO1 was markedly upregulated in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs and was associated with cell cycle arrest and increased expression of stemness-related genes. FOXO1 depletion led to upregulation of CCNA2 and CCNE1, re-entry into the cell cycle, and decreased expression of osteogenic markers and ALP activity, indicating that FOXO1 is essential for maintaining a quiescent-like, osteogenesis-permissive state.

Although FOXO family members share a conserved DNA-binding domain and exhibit partial functional redundancy, this redundancy appears to be incomplete at the transcriptional level [34]. Previous studies have shown that FOXO1 deficiency alone disrupts osteoblast differentiation and skeletal development [35], at least in part through mechanisms distinct from cell cycle regulation, including the direct regulation of RUNX2 and its downstream osteogenic targets. Thus, the effects of FOXO1 knockdown may result from the combined loss of osteogenic gene regulation and cell cycle suppression, while other FOXO members, such as FOXO4, likely serve compensatory functions.

While FOXO1 has primarily been implicated in repressing early G1 regulators, its involvement in later stages of the cell cycle has remained unclear.

In the present study, we identified CCNA2 and CCNE1 as FOXO1-responsive genes, revealing an additional layer of FOXO1-dependent cell cycle regulation. By suppressing the late G1/S transition and S-phase progression, FOXO1 maintains a differentiation-permissive state in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs.

These findings support a model in which FOXO1 integrates cell cycle regulation, maintenance of stemness, and activation of osteogenic gene expression to promote osteoblast differentiation under spheroid culture conditions, whereas FOXO4 primarily regulates stemness-related transcription. This functional divergence underscores FOXO1 as a key regulatory nexus linking quiescence and osteogenic commitment.

In spheroid culture, the hypoxic and nutrient-deprived microenvironment induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, suppresses PI3K/AKT signaling, and stabilizes HIF-1α, thereby promoting FOXO activation [[36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. In addition, nutrient deprivation decreases cellular ATP levels and activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which further enhances FOXO transcriptional activity [[43], [44], [45]]. Collectively, these pathways may mediate FOXO activation within the spheroid microenvironment. This study did not directly assess ROS levels or PI3K/AKT, AMPK, and HIF-1 signaling in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs. Therefore, these pathways should be regarded as plausible upstream regulators of FOXO activation based on evidence from previous studies. Elucidating their respective contributions to FOXO-mediated cell cycle arrest and osteogenic differentiation may facilitate the optimization of spheroid culture and priming strategies to improve the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs.

Previous research has demonstrated that spheroid culture enhances MSC stemness and regenerative potential while suppressing cellular senescence compared with monolayer culture [[46], [47], [48]]. Although the precise molecular mechanisms remain unclear, our findings suggest that FOXO1-mediated cell cycle regulation contributes to these spheroid-induced effects, providing new mechanistic insight into how spheroid culture reinforces MSC stemness.

This study has several limitations. Although FOXO1 plays a critical role in cell cycle arrest and osteogenic potential in spheroid-cultured hPDLMSCs, its downstream target genes and associated signaling networks remain incompletely characterized. In addition, gene expression analyses in the present study were performed exclusively under osteogenic conditions. Therefore, whether the observed transcriptional changes are specific to osteogenic stimulation remains to be determined. Furthermore, our findings are limited to in vitro analyses, and the in vivo relevance of FOXO1 in periodontal tissue regeneration requires further validation. Future investigations combining transcriptomic or chromatin-based analyses with in vivo regeneration models will be necessary to elucidate the therapeutic potential of FOXO1.

Overall, these findings suggest that spheroid-induced FOXO1 activation coordinates cell cycle regulation and maintenance of stemness in hPDLMSCs, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation. While this study identifies FOXO1 as a key mediator of spheroid-induced phenotypes, in vivo validation using periodontal regeneration models will be required to confirm its physiological and therapeutic relevance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, spheroid culture modulates the function of hPDLMSCs through FOXO1-dependent regulation of cell cycle progression and maintenance of stemness, thereby enhancing their osteogenic potential. Although these findings are based on in vitro analyses, this study identifies FOXO1 as a promising molecular target for periodontal tissue regeneration that warrants further in vivo validation.
