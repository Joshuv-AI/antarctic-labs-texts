Soft computing models for predicting polymer viscosity across wide thermal and ionic-strength conditions
Authors: Farhood Navaie, Mehdi Mahdaviara, Mohammad Sharifi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Characterizing polymer properties under varying thermal and ionic-strength conditions is crucial across diverse fields, from materials science to environmental engineering. While experimental techniques are effective, they are often time-consuming and costly, underscoring the need for rapid, reliable computational alternatives. This study evaluates the performance of various soft computing techniques, ranging from traditional machine learning methods (MLP, SVR, RF, GBM, LGBM, and XGB) to advanced deep learning approaches (CNNs), for predicting polymer viscosity under diverse temperature and salinity conditions. The models are trained on a curated database of 4,366 experimental measurements spanning ten commercially used polymers and wide ranges of molecular weight, polymer concentration, temperature, salinity, and shear rate. Using hold-out validation, 10-fold cross-validation, and a strict unseen-polymer test, all models achieve high accuracy (test R² ≥ 0.98), with LGBM and CNN providing the best overall performance. Compared with traditional models (MLP and SVR), these methods reduce test-set error metrics by approximately 25–30%, and the CNN attains R² = 0.997 on the unseen-polymer dataset. Permutation-importance analysis identifies polymer concentration as the dominant predictor, followed by shear rate and molecular weight, in agreement with established rheological behavior. The results demonstrate that ensemble and deep learning models can serve as fast, reliable surrogates for polymer viscosity across high-temperature and high-salinity conditions, reducing experimental effort and supporting data-driven optimization of polymer selection and operating conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42546-4.

Introduction

The polymer industry has grown tremendously over the past century, with the development of synthetic polymers1. These materials now underpin applications across materials science and polymer processing, chemical engineering, biotechnology, pharmaceuticals, food science, environmental engineering, and petroleum engineering2. The technique of polymer flooding is a well-researched and commonly used method for enhanced oil recovery (EOR) that involves the recovery of residual or unswept oil from oil reservoirs3. Practically, polymers improve the microscopic sweep and displacement efficiency of the aqueous phase by increasing its viscosity and mobility ratio, subsequently4. Accurate characterization of apparent viscosity is therefore crucial, particularly in porous media where viscosity typically decreases with increasing flow velocity but can rise rapidly beyond a critical velocity due to shear-thickening. A commonly used polymer in this context is partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM) because of its high water solubility and cost-effectiveness4,5. HPAM is a water-soluble polyelectrolyte bearing negative charges along its chain6, available in a range of molecular weights and degrees of hydrolysis7.

Chemical-enhanced oil recovery and polymer flooding are integral components of EOR strategies. Polymer flooding, in particular, has emerged as a widely adopted method for increasing oil recovery rates8. The fundamental principle underlying polymer flooding involves the introduction of water-soluble polymers into the reservoir, effectively increasing the viscosity of the injected water4. This increase in viscosity improves mobility control and reduces water’s relative permeability compared to oil, thereby enhancing the displacement of hydrocarbons9. The types of polymers used in polymer flooding can vary significantly, including both synthetic and biopolymer categories. HPAM and xanthan are the most commonly utilized polymers in polymer flooding operations10. However, the rheological behavior of polymer solutions is influenced by various factors, including temperature (T), shear rate (SR), and salinity, which are crucial to understanding their performance in the context of EOR11–13.

Accurate measurement of polymer viscosity is foundational for characterizing such complex fluids. Conventional steady-shear measurements provide essential data but cannot fully capture fluid–structure interactions14,15. Dynamic oscillatory tests complement these by probing viscoelastic response under sinusoidal loading and thus offering insight into both viscosity and microstructural behavior16. However, laboratory measurements are time-consuming and resource-intensive. Preparing homogeneous polymer solutions can require several days per sample. Moreover, viscosity depends on many interacting parameters—molecular weight, degree of hydrolysis, polymer concentration, cation content, shear rate, and temperature—leading to strongly nonlinear relationships17–19. While experiments remain the gold standard, exhaustive testing becomes impractical as applications diversify and the design space expands14,20. This complexity also hampers the derivation of general theoretical correlations that simultaneously account for shear rate, ionic strength/hardness, concentration, salinity, and temperature20.

Data-driven modeling has therefore emerged as a promising complement to experimentation for predicting polymer viscosity under varying conditions21–29. Methods spanning multilayer perceptron (MLP), gradient boosting (GBM, XGB, LGBM), voting regressors (VR), random forests (RF), support vector regression (SVR), and convolutional neural networks (CNN) have proved effective in modeling complex, multivariate systems across scientific domains30–32. These soft-computing and machine-learning approaches accommodate nonlinear, interdependent relationships among inputs—polymer molecular weight, concentration, temperature, shear rate, salinity/ionic strength, and degree of hydrolysis—thereby improving prediction reliability and efficiency33–38. In this work, we employ MLP, SVR, RF, GBM, LGBM, XGB, VR, and CNN to predict polymer viscosity under high-temperature and high-salinity (ionic-strength) conditions relevant not only to subsurface operations but also to environmental and process applications.

Accurate viscosity prediction is essential for the design and optimization of polymer-enabled processes. Reliable models support decisions on polymer selection, target concentration, and operating windows (e.g., temperature and shear), improving mobility control and process stability while reducing material use and cost. Beyond subsurface engineering, such predictive capability aids dosage optimization in flocculation and sludge conditioning, consistency control in food and pharma formulations, and flow assurance in microfluidic and additive-manufacturing platforms39,40.

Prior studies often focused on limited datasets, narrow operating ranges, or single algorithm families, which constrain generalization across polymers and conditions. Unlike those efforts, the present study integrates ensemble and deep-learning models over a comprehensive database of 4,366 experimental measurements spanning ten polymers and wide ranges of temperature, salinity/ionic strength, concentration, and shear. We benchmark traditional, ensemble, and deep architectures under consistent protocols (hold-out, 10-fold cross-validation, and an unseen-polymer test) to provide a generalizable and interpretable framework suitable for cross-sector use.

Therefore, the overarching goal of this work is to develop a comprehensive approach to predict polymer viscosity across a broad range of operating conditions using the methods described above. Earlier investigations (Table 1) employed limited input data and modeling strategies. In contrast, we combine multiple learning paradigms and leverage an expansive database curated from experiments and literature, enabling a more inclusive analysis grounded in prior work. This multi-faceted methodology is intended to strengthen predictive fidelity, broaden applicability beyond oil-specific scenarios, and support environmentally relevant and sustainable polymer applications in modern process engineering. This study presents an innovative approach to understanding the behavior of polymer solutions in EOR applications, focusing on a broad range of operating conditions, including high-temperature and high-salinity environments. Data description

Recent research has consistently demonstrated that machine learning algorithms have superior predictive capabilities when applied to the intricate characterization of complex subsurface processes 30,31,49–52. The reliability and robustness of predictions generated by these models are notably contingent upon the scope and quality of the data utilized to train them. Hence, in pursuit of scientific rigor, we have compiled one of the most comprehensive experimental datasets currently accessible to train various models to forecast polymer viscosity in porous media. This extensive dataset meticulously captures the variations in viscosity concerning independent variables such as molecular weight, salinity, hardness, temperature, shear rate, and polymer concentration, curated from relevant literature sources 20,23,24,27. Model development

We applied machine-learning methods to model polymer viscosity by using two distinct methodologies: Ensemble Learning and Deep Learning. These approaches were meticulously applied to develop intelligent models that establish a robust relationship between the viscosity of polymers in porous media and a range of independent variables, including molecular weight (Mw), salinity, temperature (T), polymer concentration (Cp), and shear rate (SR). For the deep learning facet of our study, we used a convolutional neural network (CNN) 53 as a well-known smart technique. In parallel, the Ensemble Learning dimension employed Random Forest (RF) 36,54, Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) 36, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) 55, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) 56,57, along with the inclusion of Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) 58,59 and Support Vector Regression (SVR) 60 as a traditional machine learning model, to enhance the reliability of our predictions, we further integrated the Voting Regressor (VR) algorithm, which combines predictions from multiple ensemble models. A detailed description of the machine learning algorithms assessed in this study is presented in supplementary material.

The utilized models were chosen based on their strengths in handling nonlinear relationships, high-dimensional data, and complex interactions among polymer properties (Table 3 presents a short comparison of assessed models).

MLP and SVR are efficient and straightforward but may underperform on highly nonlinear datasets. Ensemble learning models, such as RF, GBM, LGBM, and XGB, improve predictive performance by combining multiple weak learners, making them more resilient to overfitting and capable of capturing intricate parameter interactions. These models have demonstrated strong results in previous studies, particularly in handling structured data with diverse input variables. Among these, LGBM was the most computationally efficient and highly accurate, making it an attractive choice for large-scale viscosity prediction. The VR was also included to explore whether combining multiple models could enhance prediction stability, but its performance did not significantly exceed that of the most substantial individual models. Finally, the deep learning model, a CNN, was selected for its ability to identify hidden patterns in high-dimensional data and to generalize well, particularly in capturing complex dependencies among polymer molecular structure, shear rate, temperature, and viscosity.

Data preprocessing

The rheological dataset was assembled from four primary sources, each comprising different measurements. All salinity values initially reported in ppm were converted to g/L (1 g/L ≈ 1000 ppm) for consistency, and viscosity values were converted to cP. Data entries with missing or ambiguous temperature, shear rate, or polymer concentration were discarded, and obvious typographical errors were corrected by cross-checking with the original tables/figures. We screened for non-physical outliers (e.g., negative viscosities, extreme values inconsistent with the original publications) and removed many points. The final curated dataset contains 4,366 measurements.

The databank did not initially follow a normal distribution (see the histograms in the diagonal plots of Fig. 2). To address this, we applied a log transformation to mitigate the data’s skewness and approximate it as closely as possible to a bell-curve distribution. The log transform normalization reduces the influence of the extreme value, stabilizes the variance, and makes the data distribution suitable for training the machine learning models (refer to the histograms in the diagonal plots of Fig. S1 for the data normalized using the log transform technique). Furthermore, it should be noted that using a log transform for normalization facilitates denormalizing the predicted viscosities, as the log transform is not dependent on extreme values, unlike other normalization methods, such as the min-max scaler and standard scaler.

For the traditional machine learning models, the input data were used in their tabular numerical format without any spatial reshaping. However, to adapt the tabular rheological dataset for CNN modeling, the input data were reshaped into a 2D format suitable for one-dimensional convolutions. For this purpose, the features were transformed into tensors of shape (N, n, 1), where N is the number of samples and n is the number of features. This structure enables convolutional layers to learn local feature interactions and hierarchical patterns that traditional fully connected architectures may not capture effectively.

Model training

Following data preprocessing, the dataset was partitioned to facilitate model training. The Hold-out technique achieved this objective by randomly designating 80% of the dataset as the training set. In comparison, the remaining 20% was assigned to the test set to facilitate validation. Following this, the input parameters encompassing molecular weight (mDA), salinity (g/L), temperature (°C), polymer concentration (ppm), and shear rate (1/s) alongside the target value (µp, cP) were directly fed into the deep and ensemble learning algorithms for the development of viscosity models via supervised learning.

After a thorough series of examinations, the parameters for refining the intelligent algorithms were fine-tuned. In this study, tuning was performed by manually adjusting the most influential hyperparameters (e.g., number of layers and kernel sizes for the CNN models, and number of estimators and maximum depth for the ensemble learning techniques). The goal was to achieve the highest possible performance while keeping computational costs low. The objective was to strike an optimal balance between addressing high variance (overfitting) and mitigating bias (underfitting). Model validation

The precision and applicability of the created models were scrutinized using a range of statistical methodologies and visualization tools. The following subsections outline the steps for the model assessment.

Hold-out validation

The databank was shuffled and randomly split into two subsets: the training set (80%) and the test set (20%). It is important to note that the model’s performance can vary depending on the chosen train/test split. Different splits may yield different outcomes, potentially leading to inaccurate overestimations or underestimations during model evaluation.

10-fold cross-validation

We employed a 10-fold cross-validation approach to assess the models’ performance and generalizability further. This technique involves dividing the dataset into ten equal parts or folds. In the iterative process, one of the ten folds is employed as the validation set, while the remaining nine folds serve as the training set. This tenfold repetition ensures that each fold functions as the validation set precisely once. Accordingly, we obtained a more robust evaluation of the models’ performance and confirmed their ability to generalize well to unseen data.

Validation of completely unseen data

We further assessed the generalizability of our models by predicting viscosities for an unseen dataset, specifically the FP 3230 polymer. This dataset was excluded from the training and testing phases to ensure novelty. For further details about this unseen dataset, please refer to Table 2.

Permutation importance assessment

During the final stage of our analysis, we used the Permutation Importance technique to assess the importance of individual predictive features within the models developed in this investigation. Permutation importance is a technique for evaluating feature importance in a predictive model. It entails the random rearrangement of values associated with a specific feature, followed by quantifying the resultant decline in the model’s performance. The greater the decrease, the more influential the feature is in shaping the model’s predictions.

Results and discussion

As mentioned, multiple traditional, ensemble, and deep learning models were trained to predict the viscosity of polymers under high-temperature, high-salinity conditions. These models were designed to predict viscosity as a function of various input parameters, including molecular weight, salinity, temperature, shear rate, and polymer concentration. The training data for these models were derived from an extensive dataset comprising experimental viscosity measurements for 10 polymers across a wide range of conditions, totaling 4366 data points.

To comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of these models, diverse statistical metrics, such as R-squared, Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Average Absolute Relative Error (AARE), were computed for the training (80%), testing (20%), and unseen data subsets. The results of these evaluations are presented in Table 7 and further illustrated in Fig. 3. As shown Fig. 3, the developed models achieve high performance, with overall R-squared values exceeding 0.98 and RMSE values below 0.0006 cp. in both the training and testing phases. Table 7 summarizes the performance of all models, including R², RMSE, AARE, and training, testing, 10-fold cross-validation, and unseen-polymer (FP 3230) execution times. Compared with traditional ML models (MLP and SVR), the best ensemble and deep learning models (particularly LGBM and CNN) reduce test-set AARE by approximately 25–30% while maintaining R² ≥ 0.99. For the unseen polymer, the CNN achieves R² = 0.997 and the lowest AARE, highlighting its superior generalization.

Figure 4 (a) to (h), the cross-plot representations provide additional insight into the quality of the predictions, displays the deviation between predicted viscosity values and measured values for all models. These plots show that most data points cluster closely around the unit-slope line, indicating a low prediction error. However, analysis of the relative residuals (Fig. S2) shows that the ensemble learning models, particularly LGBM, exhibit minimal residuals across the viscosity range. In contrast, the MLP and SVR models display larger error spreads. This observation is further supported by the error distribution histogram (Fig. S3), which shows that most relative errors in the LGBM model are near zero, whereas the MLP and SVR models exhibit a higher frequency of large errors.

We employed the permutation variable importance technique to assess the impact of input parameters on model performance, specifically evaluating the relationship between each input variable and the target variable (viscosity). This method involves randomly permuting the variable sequence and measuring the resulting variation in model performance. Figure 8 presents the outcomes across various models, consistently ranking polymer concentration as the most significant predictor, followed by shear rate, molecular weight, temperature, and salinity.

The findings align with classical polymer rheology, where increasing polymer concentration increases viscosity by increasing the overlap and entanglement density of polymer chains, leading to a transition from the dilute to the semi-dilute or entangled regime. Shear rate influences viscosity due to the shear-thinning or shear-thickening behavior of HPAM and similar polymers, which is a result of chain orientation and stretching in flow. Molecular weight controls chain length and hydrodynamic volume, affecting zero-shear viscosity and the onset of non-linear viscoelasticity.

Temperature and salinity have a lesser impact on viscosity in our models, consistent with the narrower ranges explored for these variables. Their effects are mostly indirect, mediated by coil contraction, ion screening, and possible thermo-mechanical degradation, rather than the more direct impact of concentration and shear rate on network formation. Notably, this pattern holds across all models, except for MLP and SVR, which rank salinity as more influential than temperature. Overall, the data-driven importance ranking is physically plausible and in line with prior rheological studies 61,62.

Conclusions

This study has demonstrated the capability of soft-computing techniques, including traditional machine learning (MLP and SVR), ensemble learning (RF, GBM, LGBM, XGB), and deep learning (CNN), to accurately predict polymer viscosity under diverse rheological conditions influenced by salinity/ionic strength, temperature, concentration, shear rate, and molecular weight. Polymer viscosity depends on nonlinear interactions among concentration, molecular weight, temperature, salinity, and shear rate, which complicate empirical modeling.

By accommodating these complexities, data-driven models enable precise characterization of polymer behavior across a broad range of application-relevant environments. The models developed here provide accurate viscosity predictions across broad rheological conditions, reducing experimental effort in formulation and process optimization, as well as in other sustainable process-engineering workflows. In these settings, reliable viscosity prediction helps optimize polymer selection, dosage, and operating windows (temperature and shear), thereby improving process stability, product quality, resource efficiency, and cost effectiveness while reducing reliance on extensive laboratory testing. A natural extension of this work is to couple our rheological database with molecular representations of polymer structure, enabling unified models that interpolate across broader families of chemistries, as recently explored in structure-aware polymer ML studies 42. Such models could bridge formulation-level and molecular-level design for viscosity control.

Overall, these findings confirm that soft-computing, data-driven viscosity prediction can substantially reduce experimental burden and accelerate development cycles for polymer-enabled technologies in environmental and process engineering contexts. Future work can expand the databank to additional polymer families and operating regimes; incorporate physics-informed learning, uncertainty quantification, and explainability; and explore transferability to online monitoring and digital-twin implementations for sustainable, resource-efficient manufacturing.
