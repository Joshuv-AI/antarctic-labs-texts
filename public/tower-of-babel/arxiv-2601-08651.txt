Examples of critically cyclic functions in the Dirichlet spaces of the ball
Pouriya Torkinejad Ziarati

Abstract
. In this work, we construct examples of holomorphic functions in D 2 ​ ( 𝔹 2 ) D_{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2}) , the Dirichlet space on 𝔹 2 \mathbb{B}_{2} , for which there exists an index α c ∈ [ 1 2 , 2 ] \alpha_{c}\in[\frac{1}{2},2] such that the function is cyclic in D α ​ ( 𝔹 2 ) D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{2}) if and only if α ≤ α c \alpha\leq\alpha_{c} . To this end, we use the notion of interpolation sets in smooth ball algebras, as studied by Bruna, Ortega, Chaumat, and Chollet.

1.
Introduction

Consider a Banach space of holomorphic functions
\mathcal{A}
defined on a domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. A function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is called
cyclic
if the linear span of
f
multiplied by all
multipliers
of
\mathcal{A}
is dense in
\mathcal{A}
. (Here,
m
is a multiplier of
\mathcal{A}
if
mf\in\mathcal{A}
for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
.) Determining whether a function is cyclic is a space-dependent task. Roughly speaking, on a fixed domain, a more restrictive(larger) norm makes the conditions for cyclicity harder to satisfy. For instance, a function that is not cyclic in the Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is not cyclic in the Dirichlet space
D_{1}(\mathbb{D})
either.

Another important factor in the study of cyclicity is the zero set of the function. Indeed, let
z_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
be such that the evaluation functional

\Lambda_{z_{0}}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C},\quad f\mapsto f(z_{0}),

is bounded. Then any function admitting a zero at
z_{0}
fails to be cyclic. In particular, this situation often occurs when
z_{0}\in\Omega
, which shifts the challenge to the study of boundary behavior of functions.

In Dirichlet-type spaces on the unit ball
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
, which are the subject of this study, one may define for any
f\in D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
a non-tangential boundary limit
f^{*}
outside a set of
\alpha
-capacity zero
[
5
]
. It turns out that, for
f
to be cyclic, the zero set of
f^{*}
,
\mathcal{Z}(f^{*})
, cannot have positive
\alpha
-capacity. A converse to this fact, however, is more subtle and has been the subject of many studies in one and several variables.

In one variable, in 1984, Brown and Shields conjectured that an outer function
f\in D_{1}(\mathbb{D})
is cyclic if and only if
\mathcal{Z}(f^{*})
has zero logarithmic capacity
[
2
]
. In 2009, El-Fallah, Kellay, and Ransford provided a partial answer for functions continuous up to the boundary with a specific type of zero set
[
10
]
, and later extended this result to Dirichlet-type spaces by replacing logarithmic capacity with
\alpha
-capacity
[
11
]
. Moreover, in harmonically weighted Dirichlet spaces, El-Fallah, Elmadani, and Labghail established a Brown–Shields type result under certain conditions on the weight measure
[
13
]
. In all of these results, the zero sets of
A^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
—the algebra of holomorphic functions that are
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-smooth up to the boundary—known as Beurling–Carleson sets, play an indispensable role. A modern treatment of these sets can be found in the work of Ivrii and Nicolau
[
15
]
.

In several variables, on the unit ball, the concepts of
interpolation sets
and
peak sets
in smooth ball algebras (which are more restrictive than zero sets) may provide a useful tool for studying cyclicity. In
[
19
]
the author used a result of Chaumat and Chollet
[
6
]
on the peak sets of
A^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
to obtain sharp cyclicity conditions for a class of holomorphic functions defined on a neighborhood of the closed unit ball, whenever their zero set is a compact complex-tangential manifold (which, by
[
6
]
, is a peak set of
A^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
), thereby providing further positive evidence for the conjecture posed in
[
20
]
.

Consider a closed set
E\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
and
\alpha_{c}\in[0,n]
such that
E
has zero
\alpha
-capacity if and only if
\alpha\leq\alpha_{c}
. Then any function in
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
whose zero set contains
E
is noncyclic whenever
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
. We call a function
f\in D_{n}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
a
critically cyclic function
for the set
E
if it has
E
as its zero set and is cyclic in
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
if and only if
\alpha\leq\alpha_{c}
.

Here, we use a sufficiency result due to Bruna and Ortega
[
3
]
on the interpolation sets of
A^{m,s}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
for
0<m+s<\infty
and one provided by Chaumat and Chollet
[
8
]
in
A^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
to provide examples of critically cyclic functions in Dirichlet-type spaces on the unit ball. When these sets are contained in a transversal curve on the boundary of the unit ball, they satisfy a property called the
\mathcal{K}
-property. In the one-dimensional case, they are also known as
porous sets
and have been studied in
[
1
]
,
[
9
]
.

As the results in
[
3
]
and
[
8
]
suggest, we study separately three interesting cases for the boundary zero set, namely the
transversal
, the
complex tangential
, and a case that is neither transversal nor complex tangential but is still
totally real
. Later in Section
2
, we will provide precise definitions of these cases.

In our examples, which are for the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, one may observe that, depending on the case, the relationship between the Hausdorff dimension of the zero set and the critical index
\alpha_{c}
varies. In the transversal case, the zero set of our example has dimension
d
and
\alpha_{c}=2-d
. In the complex tangential case, however, we have
\alpha_{c}=2-\frac{d}{2}
. We also construct another example by taking a compact subset, of dimension
d
, of a transversal curve and extending it along a complex tangential direction, which yields a totally real set of dimension
1+d
; in this case,
\alpha_{c}=\frac{3}{2}-d
.

In the transversal case, the following theorem gives us the necessary tool to prove our first main result:

Proposition 1
.

[
3
, Theorem 4.3]

Let
E
be a
\mathcal{K}
-set contained in a simple transverse curve
\Gamma
.
Then there exists
f\in A^{1}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
such that

(1)

|f(z)|\asymp d_{K}(z,E),\qquad z\in\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{n}.

Moreover,
f
has this property that
f^{k}\in A^{k}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
for
k
being a positive integer.

Here,
A^{k}(\mathbb{B}_{n})=\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{B}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}^{k}(\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{n})
and, by
d_{K}(z,E)
we mean the
Korányi pseudodistance
between
z\in\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{n}
and the set
E\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
, as defined in Definition
6
. We call the function
f
in (
1
) an
extremal function
for the set
E
, and we establish the following result for it, which constitutes our first main result:

Theorem 2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
,
E\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{2}
and
\Gamma
be as stated in Proposition
1
, moreover assume that whenever
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is small enough we have:

(2)

|E_{t}|=|\{z\in\Gamma:d_{K}(z,E)\leq t\}|\asymp t^{1-d},

where
|E_{t}|
denotes the one dimensional Lebesgue measure of
E_{t}
, and moreover assume that
d\in(0,1)
is the Hausdorff dimension of
E
. Then,
f
is cyclic in
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
if and only if
\alpha\leq 2-d
.

In the complex tangential case, the following, which is a corollary of Theorem II in
[
3
]
will serve as the main tool to settle our second main result:

Proposition 3
.

Let
M
be a compact
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-smooth complex tangential curve and
E\subseteq M
be compact. Given
0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}
, there exists
h\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
such that
\mathcal{Z}(h)\cap\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{2}=E
and for all
k
being a positive integer, we have:

(3)

|f(z)|\asymp d_{K}(z,E)^{1+\epsilon},

and,

(4)

|R^{k}f(z)|\lesssim d_{K}(z,E)^{-k+1+\epsilon},

where
Rf
denotes the radial derivative of
f
defined in Definition
4
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
M
,
E
and
f
be as in Proposition
3
and, moreover, assume that

(5)

|E_{t}|=|\{z\in M:\mathrm{dist}(z,E)\leq t\}|\asymp t^{1-d},

where
\mathrm{dist}
refers to the Euclidean distance and
d\in(0,1)
is the Hausdorff dimension of
E
. Then,
f
is cyclic in
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
if and only if
\alpha\leq 2-\frac{d}{2}
.

In the following, which is our third main result, we create examples of critically cyclic functions in Dirichlet-type spaces with a zero set which is not contained in a complex tangential manifold or a transversal curve but is still contained in a totally real manifold.

Theorem 5
.

Let
M\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{2}
be a totally real smooth manifold of dimension
2
, then given
d\in[0,1]
, there exists a compact subset
E\subset M
of Hausdorff dimension
1+d
for which there exists a function
f\in D_{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
such that
f
is cyclic in
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
if and only if
\alpha\leq\frac{3}{2}-d
.

2.
Preliminary Tools

2.1.
Basics and Notations

Notation 1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a domain. We denote by
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
the set of all holomorphic functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
.

Notation 2
.

We write
A\lesssim B
to indicate that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
A\leq CB
. If both
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
hold, we write
A\asymp B
. The dependence of the constant
C
on parameters will be specified whenever necessary.

Definition 1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. A function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
, with power series expansion

f(z)=\sum_{|k|=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k},\quad z\in\mathbb{B}_{n},

is said to belong to the Dirichlet-type space
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
if

\|f\|_{D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})}^{2}:=\sum_{|k|=0}^{\infty}(n+|k|)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{(n-1)!}{k!\,(n-1+|k|)!}\,|a_{k}|^{2}<\infty,

where
k
is a multi-index,
|k|=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}
,
k!=k_{1}!\cdots k_{n}!
, and
z^{k}=z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{k_{n}}
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. The
multiplier space

\mathcal{M}(D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n}))
is the set of all functions
\varphi\in D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
such that for every
f\in D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
, the product
\varphi f\in D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
.

The space
\mathcal{M}(D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n}))
is a Banach algebra when equipped with the operator norm induced by multiplication. In particular, when
\alpha=0
, so that
D_{0}(\mathbb{B}_{n})=H^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
, we have

\mathcal{M}(D_{0}(\mathbb{B}_{n}))=H^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}_{n}),

the space of bounded holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{B}_{n}
.

Definition 3
.

Here in this paper we denote by
A^{k,s}(\mathbb{B}_{n})=\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{B}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}^{k,s}(\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{n})
and when
s=0
, we will simply denote it by
A^{k}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
.

Definition 4
.

Given
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
,
radial derivative
of
f
, shown by
Rf
, is defined as follows:

Rf(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_{i}}(z).

The following Lemma is a direct consequence of the fact that
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})=A^{2}_{-(\alpha+1)}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
in the sense of the generalization given to the definition of Weighted Bergman Spaces in
[
21
]
.

Lemma 6
.

f\in D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
if and only if
R^{k}f\in D_{\alpha-2k}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
. Also whenever
\alpha<0
we have

(6)

||f||^{2}_{\alpha}\simeq\int_{\mathbb{B}_{n}}(1-|z|^{2})^{-(\alpha+1)}|f(z)|^{2}\,dv(z).

Corollary 7
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\alpha-2k<0
. If
f\in D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
and
R^{k}f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
, then
f\in\mathcal{M}(D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n}))
.

Definition 5
.

A function
f\in D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
is called a
shift-cyclic vector
if:

[f]:=\overline{\operatorname{span}\{pf:p\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]\}}=D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n}).

The next theorem provides a practical sufficient condition for the cyclicity of a function. For a detailed proof, one may consult
[
16
]
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
f\in D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
and assume that
f
does not vanish on
\mathbb{B}_{n}
. For
0<r<1
, define the radial dilation

f_{r}(z)=f(rz).

If

\sup_{0<r<1}\left\|\frac{f}{f_{r}}\right\|_{\alpha}<+\infty,

then
f
is cyclic in
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})
.

Definition 6
.

The Korányi pseudodistance on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is
defined by

d_{K}(z,w)=|1-\langle z,w\rangle|,

where
\langle z,w\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\bar{w}_{i}
denotes the standard Hermitian product.
This defines a pseudodistance on the unit sphere
\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
,
we shall use the notation to situations where one of the points lies outside
\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
.
For
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
r>0
, we denote by
K(z,r)
the
Korányi ball

centered at
z
with radius
r
.

Definition 7
.

For
\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
, the complex tangent space to the sphere is
defined by

T_{\zeta}^{\mathbb{C}}\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}:=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\langle v,\zeta\rangle=0\}.

Definition 8
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{\partial}\mathbb{B}_{n}
be a
C^{k}
real submanifold.
We say that
M
is
complex tangential
if, for every
\zeta\in M
,

T_{\zeta}M\subset T_{\zeta}^{\mathbb{C}}\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}.

Definition 9
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{\partial}\mathbb{B}_{n}
be a
C^{k}
real submanifold.
We say that
M
is
totally real
if, for every
\zeta\in M
,

T_{\zeta}M\cap iT_{\zeta}M=\{0\}.

Definition 10
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
be a
C^{k}
curve.
We say that
\Gamma
is
transversal
if, for every
\zeta\in\Gamma
,

T_{\zeta}\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}=T_{\zeta}\Gamma\oplus T_{\zeta}^{\mathbb{C}}\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}.

Remark 1
.

The following facts will be used repeatedly throughout the paper (see, for example, Section II in
[
3
]
).

(1)

If
M\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
is a totally real submanifold, then

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}M\leq n.

(2)

If
M\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
is a complex tangential submanifold, then

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}M\leq n-1.

(3)

If
M\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
is smooth and complex tangential and
z,w\in M
, then

d_{K}(z,w)\asymp|z-w|^{2},

(4)

If
\Gamma\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
is a smooth compact transversal curve and
z,w\in\Gamma
,
then

d_{K}(z,w)\asymp|z-w|.

Definition 11
.

[
3
]

A closed set
E\subset\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
is called a
\mathcal{K}
-set
if there exists a constant
c>0

such that, for every Korányi ball
K_{r}
of radius
r
,

(7)

\int_{0}^{r}N_{\varepsilon}(K_{r}\cap E)\,d\varepsilon\leq cr,

where
N_{\varepsilon}(K_{r}\cap E)
denotes the minimal number of Korányi balls
of radius
\varepsilon
required to cover
K_{r}\cap E
.

Definition 12
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{\partial}\mathbb{B}_{n}
be a Borel set and let
\mathcal{P}(E)
denote the set of all
Borel probability measures supported on
E
.
For
\alpha\in(0,n]
, define the positive kernel

\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,n}(t)=\begin{cases}t^{\,\alpha-n},&0<\alpha<n,\\[4.0pt]
\log\!\left(\dfrac{e}{t}\right),&\alpha=n.\end{cases}

For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
, the Riesz
\alpha
-energy of
\mu
is

I_{\alpha,n}[\mu]=\iint_{\mathbb{\partial}\mathbb{B}_{n}\times\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}}\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,n}\!\left(d_{K}(\zeta,\eta)\right)\,d\mu(\zeta)\,d\mu(\eta).

The
Riesz–
\alpha
capacity
of
E
is then defined by

\operatorname{cap}_{\alpha,n}(E)={\inf\{I_{\alpha,n}[\mu]:\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)\}^{-1}}.

The following is a useful tool to determine the non-cyclicity of a function

Theorem 9
.

[
18
, Theorem 4.4]

Suppose
f\in\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{B}_{n})\cap A(\mathbb{B}_{n})
satisfies
\operatorname{cap}_{\alpha,n}(Z(f)\cap\partial\mathbb{B}_{n})>0
.

is not cyclic in

3.
Proof of Theorem
2

Before proving Theorem
2
, we first introduce the following lemma that will serve as a technical tool in its proof.

Lemma 10
.

[
12
, Exercise 9.4.1]

Let
E\subset\mathbb{T}
be a closed set of measure zero.
Let
\phi:(0,\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a positive, decreasing, and differentiable function.
Then

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\phi\big(\mathrm{dist}(\zeta,E)\big)\,|d\zeta|=\int_{0}^{\pi}|\phi^{\prime}(t)|\,|E_{t}|\,dt+2\pi\phi(\pi),

where
E_{t}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{T}:\mathrm{dist}(\zeta,E)\leq t\}
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let
\alpha_{c}=2-d
. Using Lemma
6
and the fact that first order derivative of
f
are bounded, one can easily verify that
f\in D_{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
and in fact,
f\in\mathcal{M}(D_{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2}))
, we will prove that
g=f^{2}
is cyclic in
D_{\alpha_{c}}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
, and
f
is non-cyclic if
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
.

Note that cyclicity of
g
implies cyclicity of
f
, since
f
being a multiplier implies the following which is a direct consequence of the definition

[g]=[f^{2}]\subseteq[f].

To prove the cyclicity part it is enough to show that

(8)

\sup_{r\in(0,1)}\left\|\frac{g}{g_{r}}\right\|_{\alpha_{c}}<+\infty.

In this way, we can apply Theorem
8
to conclude that
g
is cyclic.
To prove (
8
), we use Lemma
6
to show that
\left\|R\frac{g}{g_{r}}\right\|_{\alpha_{c}-2}
is uniformly bounded.
To do so we write:

(9)

\left|R\frac{g}{g_{r}}\right|=\left|\frac{g_{r}Rg-gRg_{r}}{g^{2}_{r}}\right|=\left|\frac{(g_{r}-g)Rg+g(Rg-Rg_{r})}{g^{2}_{r}}\right|.

Let
g=\sum_{k\geq 0}g^{\prime}_{k}
and
f=\sum_{k\geq 0}f^{\prime}_{k}
where
g^{\prime}_{k}
’s and
f^{\prime}_{k}
’s are
k
-homogeneous polynomials. After applying the triangle inequality to the right hand side of (
9
) we may write

(10)

\displaystyle\left|R\frac{g}{g_{r}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{2(f_{r}+f)fRf\sum_{k}(1-r^{k})f^{\prime}_{k}}{g^{2}_{r}}\right|+\left|\frac{g\sum_{k}(1-r^{k})kg^{\prime}_{k}}{g^{2}_{r}}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\left(\left|\frac{f}{f_{r}}\right|+\left|\frac{g}{g_{r}}\right|\right)\left|\frac{\sum_{k}(1+r+\ldots+r^{k-1})f^{\prime}_{k}}{g_{r}}\right|+\left|\frac{g}{g_{r}}\right|\left|\frac{\sum_{k}(1+r+\ldots+r^{k-1})kg^{\prime}_{k}}{g_{r}}\right|\right)(1-r)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\left(\left|\frac{f}{f_{r}}\right|+\left|\frac{g}{g_{r}}\right|\right)\left|\frac{\sum_{k}kf^{\prime}_{k}}{g_{r}}\right|+\left|\frac{g}{g_{r}}\right|\left|\frac{\sum_{k}k^{2}g^{\prime}_{k}}{g_{r}}\right|\right)(1-r)

\displaystyle\lesssim(\left(\left|\frac{f}{f_{r}}\right|+\left|\frac{f}{f_{r}}\right|^{2}\right)\left|\frac{(1-r)}{f_{r}^{2}}\right|.

The last inequality in (
20
) comes from the fact that,
\sum_{k}k^{2}g^{\prime}_{k}=R^{2}g
and
\sum_{k}kf^{\prime}_{k}=Rf
are bounded in
\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
as
f\in A^{1}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
and
g\in A^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
, and also replacing
g=f^{2}
at the end. Now the claim is that
\frac{f}{f_{r}}
has a uniform bound in
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}_{2})
. Before proceeding to prove this claim note that this together with (
20
) implies:

(11)

\left\|R\frac{g}{g_{r}}\right\|_{\alpha_{c}-2}^{2}\lesssim(1-r)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{B}_{2}}\left|\frac{1}{f_{r}^{4}(z)}\right|(1-|z|^{2})^{1-\alpha_{c}}\,\mathrm{d}v(z).

To prove the claim, we may write:

(12)

\left|\frac{f}{f_{r}}\right|\leq\left|\frac{f-f_{r}}{f_{r}}\right|+1\lesssim\left|\frac{\sum_{k}(1-r^{k})f_{k}^{\prime}}{f_{r}}\right|+1\lesssim\left|\frac{1-r}{f_{r}}\right|+1.

Lemma 5.6 in
[
3
]
implies that, given
\zeta_{0}\in E
there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
\zeta_{0}
and a coordinate
Z(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
centered at
\zeta_{0}
and valid in
U\cap\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{2}
, where
Z(x_{1},0,0,0)
is in the inward normal direction to
\partial\mathbb{B}_{2}
when
x_{1}>0
,
x_{4}\in\mathbb{T}
,
Z(0,x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\partial\mathbb{B}_{n}
and
Z(0,0,0,x_{4})\in\Gamma
satisfying

(13)

d_{K}(Z,E)\asymp x_{1}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+\mathrm{dist}(Z(0,0,0,x_{4}),E).

Then, for
r>\tfrac{1}{2}
, there exists
c>0
such that

(14)

|f_{r}(Z)|\geq c\Big((1-r)+x_{1}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+\mathrm{dist}(Z(0,0,0,x_{4}),E)\Big).

Fixing the notation
t=\mathrm{dist(Z(0,0,0,x_{4}),E)}
, and noting that we can cover
E
by finitely many such
U
’s settles the claim using (
12
). Moreover, this, together with (
11
