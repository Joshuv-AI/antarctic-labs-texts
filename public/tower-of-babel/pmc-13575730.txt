Healthcare professionals' perspectives regarding the foundations of a patient reported measure for adults receiving Home Enteral Tube Feeding—A Delphi study
Authors: Andriana Korai, Sharon Carey, Margaret Allman‐Farinelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo seek input on the development of a tool for measuring treatment burden in Home Enteral Tube Feeding by healthcare professional consultation.MethodsA modified two‐round online Delphi study recruited experts via an open call to professional organisations and snowball sampling. Experts were healthcare professionals involved in the provision, coordination, or management of care for adults receiving Home Enteral Tube Feeding. In Round 1, participants rated 14 topics grouped under three themes derived from prior qualitative research with Australian Home Enteral Tube Feeding patients and a literature review. Agreement was defined as the proportion of the top two ratings on a five‐point Likert scale. Ratings and free‐text comments were summarised and presented in Round 2, where topics and themes were re‐rated and ranked for usefulness to clinical practice and service management.ResultsForty‐five dietitians and nurses completed Round 1 and 32 self‐nominated for Round 2, which was completed by 18 respondents. In Round 1, agreement for importance (n = 33–41, 75%–98%) and relevance (n = 28–40, 72%–95%) was high across all topics. In Round 2, agreement for usefulness to practice was 88%–100% (n = 14–18) and for management, 60%–100% (n = 3–5). When prioritised, the theme ‘Essential Treatment Work’ ranked highest (n = 9, 60%), with three of its six topics placed in the top tertile by at least half of respondents.ConclusionClinicians perceived measurement of the topics patients previously described as contributing to Home Enteral Tube Feeding burden as important for improving clinical care. Findings support use of these topics to develop a targeted, patient‐reported measure that produces clinically meaningful data.Keywords: enteral nutrition, patient‐reported outcome measures, tube feeding

INTRODUCTION

The Australian healthcare system has conventionally been financed using a fee‐for‐service model, although value‐based health care is built upon the tenet that services should instead be funded based on the outcomes they generate, if they are achieved in a cost‐effective manner, and if patients perceive value in the outcome. 1 There has been an international movement towards providing more value‐based health care in response to growing healthcare expenditure and concerns about the sustainability of current systems. 2 Health spending in Australia totalled $270.5 billion between 2023 and 2024, of which 69.6% was funded by federal and state governments with an additional $4.8 billion of government funded National Disability Insurance Scheme expenses ascribed to health‐related services. 3 The 2023 review of the National Health Reform Agreement explicated how current activity‐based models used to inform funding of public hospital activity, do not support provision of value‐based care or integration of services across primary, secondary and hospital settings which is essential for patients with chronic or complex care needs. 4

To provide value‐based health care, services must be able to measure the outcomes of importance to their users. Patient‐reported measures are instruments developed to measure subjective outcomes important to health, or experiences of care directly from patients. 5 Routine collection of patient‐reported measures have the potential to drive value‐based healthcare by making direct care more patient‐centred, improving service delivery and informing resource allocation. 6 , 7 Promoting the uptake of patient reported measures is part of Australia's National Health Reform Agreement and state‐wide programs have sought to integrate their use into routine clinical practice. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11

Individuals receiving Home Enteral Tube Feeding (HETF) have been described to experience a significant treatment burden and face systemic barriers in terms of fragmented care and poor care‐coordination. 12 , 13 Activity‐based funding is the primary mechanism by which governments fund HETF in Australia. Public providers report the number of patients receiving HETF under their care to the Commonwealth which calculates a cost for providing HETF at home. 14 The total cost of HETF delivery and accompanying services is shared between the federal and state governments. 8 State contribution to these funds and how they are distributed within each state and territory is guided by state‐level and local policies. For instance, some states completely fund enteral nutrition and consumables, while others deem enteral nutrition as a food product and do not deem this a cost the state should bear. 14 Similarly, the type and frequency of outpatient appointments available to HETF patients is reported to vary between and within states. 15 , 16 , 17 Many HETF recipients access care funded by the National Disability Insurance Scheme which similarly does not operate on a value‐based care model. Given the reported variation in HETF services and the national movement towards value‐based health care, employment of patient‐reported measures in routine clinical HETF care would be warranted.

A scoping review was conducted of existing patient‐reported measures administered to adults receiving HETF. 18 , 19 The findings of this review demonstrated few measures have been designed with meaningful consideration of HETF experiences and most instruments lacked adequate and appropriate psychometric testing. Developing and testing a novel tool centred on experiences of this cohort is necessary. A qualitative study with adults receiving HETF and their carers across Australia was thus conducted to determine what comprises HETF treatment burden in order to inform the content of a novel HETF tool. 20 The inquiry was based on existing HETF literature and the formative work used to develop the Patient Experience with Treatment and Self‐management questionnaire for adults with chronic conditions. 21 , 22 , 23 Study findings demonstrated how HETF burden could be conceptualised as comprising three interrelated components with 14 subthemes. This conceptual framework is proposed as the basis of a HETF‐specific patient‐reported tool. Successful integration of patient‐reported measures into routine clinical care requires consideration of all end‐users' experiences to ensure relevance and viability. 24 Therefore, this study aimed to describe healthcare professionals' perspectives on the suitability of the proposed construct, burden of treatment, and content of the proposed framework as the basis for a HETF‐specific patient‐reported measure, and to refine these based on expert consensus.

METHODS

A modified, two‐round Delphi was conducted and reported in line with the Delphi studies in social and health sciences–recommendations for interdisciplinary standardised reporting (DELPHISTAR) checklist (Appendix S1). 25 The study was conducted by a team of Accredited Practising Dietitians with expertise spanning clinical practice, research including the conduct of Delphi studies, and health service design and management. There was no additional consultation regarding study conduct. The study protocol was registered on Open Science Framework (doi: 10.17605/OSF.IO/D3WY5). 26 A Delphi approach was appropriate for achieving the study aims, as it identifies agreement amongst a multidisciplinary panel and provides an iterative process to inform refinements to the proposed topics, with opportunities for reconsideration in subsequent rounds. Classical Delphi methodology was modified through inclusion of predefined topics in the first round. The topics presented to respondents were developed from the findings of the preceding studies. 18 , 19 , 20

Treatment burden was proposed to consist of the essential treatment work required to deliver HETF and the impact of treatment on wellbeing. Essential treatment work includes six types of work that need to be enacted, and the treatment is described as impacting two areas of wellbeing. Six additional topics were identified as amplifiers of treatment burden, which included individual and systemic issues that increased HETF workload or impact on wellbeing. The Delphi survey was designed to capture expert consensus, where experts were defined as credentialled healthcare professionals considered part of the core team involved in providing care to adults receiving HETF, or co‐ordinating or managing such services, as they would be the end‐users of the data generated from a HETF‐specific instrument. No additional professional attributes were required to participate. Australian guidelines outline HETF care should be provided by a nutrition support team which may include a dietitian, registered nurse, treating physician, pharmacist and speech pathologist. 27 , 28 , 29 Experts were recruited for Round 1 through an open call to professional societies and networks between 23 October and 10 November 2025, combined with a snowballing approach (additional detail provided in Appendix S2). Recruiting through these avenues ensured respondents would be appropriately credentialled healthcare professionals included in nutrition support teams. No sample size was specified a priori for the total sample or specific professions. After completing Round 1, respondents self‐nominated to participate in Round 2 by providing their email and a direct invitation with two subsequent reminders were sent between 25 November and 8 December 2025. As both rounds were anonymous, responses across rounds could not be linked. The Delphi process was decided a priori to stop after completion of two rounds.

Questionnaires were developed using Research Electronic Data Capture tool, Version 15.3.3, hosted by The University of Sydney and tested for comprehensibility and function by two dietitians and PhD candidates. The Round 1 survey consisted of 26 questions across three sections—(A) demographics (six closed questions), (B) proposed instrument content (14 sets of two closed questions with a free‐text field, an additional open question) and (C) instrument use (three closed, two open questions). As the study sought perspectives from multiple healthcare professionals, in section B respondents were asked to rate topics on both importance of measurement and relevance to their practice on two 5‐point Likert scales (0: ‘not at all’, 1: ‘slightly', 2: ‘moderately’, 3: ‘very’, and 4: ‘extremely’). Respondents were also given the opportunity to provide feedback on each topic, the overall construct, and propose additional topics. To obtain additional contextual information relating to use of a future tool, in section C participants were asked about current use of patient‐reported measures and the desired attributes of a future tool. Agreement was calculated as the percentage of ‘very’ and ‘extremely’ ratings for each topic. Delphi methodology indicates results with agreement above 70% constitute consensus. 30 In the current study, it was decided a priori that all topics would be retained regardless of whether they met this consensus threshold to ensure the tool remained patient‐centred.

The second round included 22 questions across three sections—(A) demographics (six closed questions), (B) proposed instrument content rating (14 sets of one to three closed questions each with a free‐text field) and (C) proposed instrument content ranking (two closed questions). This round sought to ascertain agreement for the final topics after presenting the panel with a summary of expert responses and proposed changes, and to understand which topics were perceived as the most valuable. Aggregated Round 1 ratings and a summary of the free‐text responses were presented to participants for each topic. To reduce respondent burden in Round 2, if ratings for importance and relevance were very similar a decision would be made to only request ratings for usefulness to patient care and/or service‐level activities, based on responses to demographic questions in section A. Where changes were made to the topics, respondents were asked whether they agreed with these changes. In section C the panel were asked to rank the three overarching treatment burden themes (essential treatment work, impact of treatment, and amplifiers of burden) and 14 topics. For both rounds, no question was mandatory, and questions were presented in sequential order. Questionnaires are available in Appendix S3.

All analyses were carried out using Microsoft Excel (Version 16.101.2). Ratings and rankings were summarised using descriptive statistics. Raw responses are reported for subgroups where the sample was less than three. No statistical comparisons were conducted between subgroup responses given their small sample size. Free‐text responses were first analysed for whether they comprised substantive content (i.e., offered feedback on the proposed topics through dissent, proposed extensions or modifications). 31 Responses that simply expressed agreement without content to inform topic refinement were deemed insubstantial and not analysed. Substantive responses were synthesised to remove duplication before presentation in Round 2.

RESULTS

Seventy‐two individuals responded to the open call and provided demographic information in Round 1. This included 64 dietitians, six nurses, one speech pathologist, and one Aboriginal health worker (Appendix S4, Table S1), of which 45 dietitians and nurses provided responses to sections B and C, corresponding to a 63% response rate. Thirty‐two of these 45 experts agreed to participate in Round 2, of which 18 provided responses to sections B and C, which corresponded to a 56% response rate. An outline of the Delphi process is illustrated in Figure 2.

The Round 1 expert panel included 39 (87%) dietitians and nurses with over 5 years of experience as a healthcare professional, of which 24 (53%) had more than 5 years of experience providing clinical care to adults receiving HETF (Table 1). In addition to experience providing clinical care, 13 (29%) of the experts were involved in managing HETF services or conducting relevant research. Twenty‐six respondents (58%) had experience working in New South Wales and there were no responses to sections B and C from individuals with experience across South Australia and Tasmania. Healthcare professionals were recruited from inpatient, outpatient, and community settings, representing both single‐specialty services (e.g., oncology) and services catering to clinically diverse cohorts. The proportion of nurse experts in Round 2 was lower than in Round 1, although other expert characteristics remained closely aligned between rounds.

Eighty‐seven percent (n = 34) of responses rated measuring treatment burden as ‘very’ or ‘extremely’ important (median(Q1, Q3) = 3 (3, 4)). The main justification was the ability to facilitate comprehensive assessment, and in turn provision of more responsive services (n = 8, 21%). Targeted quality improvement and reducing preventable complications were also identified as important reasons to collect this data (n = 4, 10%). Additional reasons included use for auditing services, advocacy efforts and as an important contribution to an otherwise lacking evidence base. Some experts highlighted indirect benefits of widespread implementation of such a tool in a field where treatment burden is poorly understood. These included raising clinician awareness of treatment burden (n = 4, 10%), which could support patient‐centred care by enabling more meaningful discussions about treatment initiation (n = 5, 13%). Four experts described concerns which included respondent burden, uncertainty of applicability to select subgroups (e.g., end of life, short‐term HETF) and integration challenges. Four experts reported already using an instrument within their service. Existing instruments were valued for their ability to flag concerns that patients might not raise during appointments. Drawbacks included low user‐friendliness, difficulty understanding the tool, and high respondent burden. The attributes of a future tool rated as most essential were suitability for a proxy responder (n = 32, 84%), the need for minimal training prior to use (n = 28, 72%) and built‐in alerts for respondents with a high treatment burden (n = 24, 63%).

There was a high agreement of importance (n = 33–41, 75%–98%) and relevance (n = 28–40, 72%–95%) ratings for all 14 proposed topics in Round 1 (Table 2). Most importance and relevance ratings were rated the same (521/590, 88%) or varied by one point (60/590, 10%). Topics 1.3 (managing essential equipment and devices), 2.1 (physical and mental impact), and 3.6 (financial barriers) achieved a median rating of ‘extremely’ important and relevant. Topic 2.1 also reached the highest agreement across all ratings (n = 41, 98%). In addition to aggregate ratings, ratings for subgroups of participants based on their responses to the demographic questions (e.g., profession, years of experience) are presented in Appendix S4, Tables S2–S7. Agreement reached 70% for 460/488 subgroup ratings (95%). Seven of the 14 topics consistently reached 70% agreement for importance and relevance amongst all subgroups (1.1, 1.3, 1.6, 2.1, 2.2, 3.2, and 3.6). For experts working with oncology and gastroenterology cohorts, agreement for importance of 1.4 (attending appointments), relevance of 3.3 (healthcare professional obstacles), and both ratings of 3.4 (interpersonal challenges) and 3.5 (confusion about self‐management) did not reach 70% agreement. At least 75% of respondents with experience in New South Wales rated 3.6 as ‘extremely’ important (median(Q1, Q2) = 4 (4, 4)). There was a total of 69 comments from section 2 of Round 1 and 65 (94%) were corroborative of the importance or relevance of the topic. Most of these corroborative comments either contextualised ratings through examples of challenges faced in the expert's practice, described how subgroup nuances would influence data while still rating the topic highly, or described their perceived utility of the data. The remaining comments (n = 4) either suggested minor modifications to the topic or highlighted that some of the data collected would overlap with data collected as part of routine assessment.

In Round 2 agreement reached 100% for usefulness in clinical practice and service management for seven of the topics (1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 2.1, 3.2, 3.3, and 3.5). Topics 1.5 (monitoring HETF) and 3.4 (interpersonal challenges) only reached a 60% agreement of ‘very’ and ‘extremely’ useful to management of services, although agreement for usefulness to practice remained high at 88% and 94% respectively. Median ratings across all topics remained higher than 3 and the only median rating lower than Round 1 was usefulness to management (3.5 from 4) of topic 3.6. Two hundred and ninety‐one of the 305 ratings (95%) provided by all participants were 3 or 4. Of the 14 comments provided across 18 experts, most were corroborative. Single respondents highlighted data pertaining to 2.1 is often sought by carers and/or patients, which may influence data collection, and that while information relating to 3.6 is important it would not be as useful for services or jurisdictions with 100% government subsidisation of equipment, services and consumables.

Across both rounds, almost all ratings (54/56, 96%) achieved an agreement >70%. When asked to rank the relevant importance of the three themes, 9/15 respondents ranked ‘essential treatment work’ as the most crucial for inclusion in the proposed instrument (Table 3). The theme ‘impact of treatment’ was ranked first by four respondents, and second and third by five respondents. Thirteen respondents (93%) ranked ‘amplifiers of burden’ as second or third. Three of the six theme 1 topics (1.1, 1.2, and 1.3) were ranked in the top tertile for inclusion in the instrument by most respondents (n = 8–11, 67%–100%). Four of the six theme 3 topics (3.3, 3.4, 3.5, and 3.6) were ranked in the lowest tertile by most respondents (n = 7–10, 54%–91%). No theme 2 topic was ranked within a specific tertile by more than half of the respondents.

DISCUSSION

Through this Delphi process, we demonstrated that healthcare professionals considered the 14 topics previously identified by HETF patients and carers as encapsulating treatment burden, to be useful for inclusion in a patient‐reported measure. Agreement greater than 70% was recorded for almost all ratings of the 14 topics, and 87% of Round 1 respondents reported measuring treatment burden as described in this study to be very or extremely important. The high agreement with the proposed topics and overall construct suggests a tool based on this framework would be clinically well‐targeted.

Beyond agreement with the proposed construct, the relative ranking of themes and topics provides insight into which aspects of treatment burden health professionals consider most critical to measure. Most experts ranked ‘Essential Treatment Work’ and the topics within this theme amongst the highest priorities for measurement, suggesting its content is perceived as the most useful to improving patient care. Silver et al. showcased how individuals caring for older adults receiving HETF completed 61.9 h of HETF‐related care per week. 32 Experts described how collecting information pertaining to HETF workload can be used to inform adjustments to feeding regimens that may reduce the associated time‐commitment. The topic consistently ranked within the lowest tertile was ‘Interpersonal Challenges’ (3.4). This topic captures how strained or unsupportive personal relationships can heighten the perceived burden of HETF. Social workers and mental health professionals' remit typically includes addressing relational strain and social connectedness, although they are not included in the core members of nutrition support teams and were not represented in the Delphi panel. Dietitians and nurses may naturally prioritise clinical and procedural aspects of HETF over relational dynamics, although a broader multidisciplinary team may have ranked this topic higher.

The benefits and challenges raised regarding use of the proposed instrument were in keeping with the findings of a systematic review pertaining to use of patient‐reported measures in direct patient care. 24 Experts from the current study highlighted the potential of the proposed instrument to prioritise patient needs and enable more targeted care. Experts also described anticipating wide variations between HETF subgroups for particular topics, constituting some less relevant for certain groups. For instance, there are local policies in Tasmania, Queensland, the Northern Territory and Western Australia which ensure HETF consumable costs are completely or partially covered by the public health system. 14 The remaining states and territories do not have state‐wide policies, but subsidisation is still routinely offered to varying degrees. 14 The greatest out‐of‐pocket costs for patients registered with government‐funded services are reported for residents of New South Wales. 14 , 17 When stratified by state‐based experience, median ratings and levels of agreement for ‘Financial Barriers’ were highest for New South Wales. Regarding clinical cohorts, experts working within oncology settings had lower agreement as to the importance of ‘Attending Appointments’. This may be because oncology patients receiving HETF will typically see clinicians as part of a multidisciplinary clinic or during treatment. Results stratified by subgroups should be interpreted with caution given the small sample sizes when the panel is split. Appropriate validation of the final instrument will require known‐group comparisons to establish construct validity across subgroups. The Patient Experience with Treatment and Self‐management tool, which was based on the same underlying theories that informed the framework proposed here, has been adapted into an electronic point‐of‐care tool which allows respondents to select the domains of their care they most struggle with before completing subsequent questions. 33 A similar approach could be employed to cater for the clinically diverse HETF cohort and varied services and funding schemes available.

A strength of this study was the sample size achieved in Round 1. Identification and recruitment of experts has been highlighted as a major challenge in conducting Delphi studies. 30 This study employed an open call and snowballing approach to recruit experts which yielded 45 responses. A recent scoping review of 287 Delphi studies used in health sciences reported a median sample of 31 participants for the first round. 30 However, this scoping review also reported an average 19% drop in respondents from the first to final round, while our sample dropped by 60%. This study sought to obtain healthcare professionals' perspectives on existing topics, without removal of any topics, and employed closed questions with the option to comment. Given the high agreement levels reported in Round 1, respondents may have felt the proposed topics adequately covered the construct thus did not self‐nominate to participate in the second round. While we sought a multidisciplinary sample from across the country, a limitation of this study was the expert characteristics not included in our sample. Our recruitment yielded responses from only dietitians and nurses. These are the healthcare professions most commonly included in HETF nutrition support teams, and dietitians have been suggested as appropriate co‐ordinators of HETF care in Australia, thus these are the professions most likely to employ such an instrument. 27 , 34 It has also been suggested that each discipline in a panel include at least 10 participants. 30 Five nurses responded to round 1, although their ratings showed a pattern of high agreement (75%–100%) across most topics and greater agreement than dietitians for 22/28 ratings, demonstrating broad alignment with the overall results. Recruitment did not yield responses from professionals that had practised in South Australia or Tasmania with most participants experienced in HETF care in NSW. The most recent national benchmarking study reported the majority of HETF services and participants were in Victoria and NSW, thus it is anticipated a higher number of HETF clinicians would have experience practising in these states. 17

The high ratings in support of measuring treatment burden and agreement across the included topics justify progression of this work. Future efforts should focus on item generation, refinement and reduction for these proposed topics with involvement of patients and carers to ensure content validity of the resultant inventory. The instrument should then be comprehensively tested for broader validity, reliability and responsiveness amongst HETF recipients with varied demographic and clinical characteristics. As highlighted in the preceding scoping review, tool responsiveness is paramount to obtain clinically meaningful data that can inform care decisions and not just be used to monitor treatment. 19 Testing of the tool must be longitudinal and measurements need to be taken at informative and clinically meaningful timepoints such as surrounding treatment changes. 35 While the findings presented here support use of a future instrument in Australia, particularly in the states from which responses were obtained, this perceived value is not transferable to other regions given variations in health service organisation and delivery.

This study demonstrates healthcare professionals perceive the measurement of treatment burden to be valuable for improving patient care. This finding is in line with previous research conceptualising this notion through patient and carer interviews. Few modifications were proposed and through this Delphi process healthcare professionals had the opportunity to refine the proposed topics. For an instrument to be effective, patients and carers must be able to understand and see value in completing it, and clinicians must be able to interpret the results and recognise how they can guide decision‐making. The development process used to establish the 14 topics reflects the importance of integrating patient experiences with healthcare professional perspectives, ensuring that the development of a future instrument would be both meaningful to patients and practically useful to clinicians.
