Estimating PM2.5 concentrations at public schools in North Carolina using multiple data sources and interpolation methods
Authors: Rachel Carroll, Paul Bailey, Saugat Pandey, Dana McCalla, Thomas Snyder
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter (PM2.5), poses serious health risks to children. Public schools are key locations for assessing air quality exposure. This study estimates PM2.5 concentrations at North Carolina public schools using EPA and PurpleAir monitor data, applying Kriging and Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolation. Cross-validation is used to evaluate predictive accuracy. Whereas EPA monitors offer precise but spatially sparse data, PurpleAir monitors provide real-time, widespread coverage with variable accuracy. Combining both improves spatial resolution and predictive power. Kriging, which incorporates both distance and spatial correlation, consistently outperforms IDW in estimating PM2.5, especially near EPA sites. The addition of PurpleAir data enhances accuracy further, particularly in areas lacking EPA monitors. School-level PM2.5 estimates reveal suburban schools often face higher pollution levels. Linking these estimates to 8th grade reading scores indicates a negative relationship between PM2.5 exposure and academic performance, suggesting possible cognitive impacts of air pollution. These findings underscore the importance of integrating PurpleAir monitors at school locations to improve air quality monitoring. Enhanced monitoring would support public health protections and inform education policy. Overall, combining EPA and PurpleAir data using Kriging offers a more accurate, actionable approach to understanding and addressing environmental determinants of student well-being and achievement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26539-3.

Introduction

Air pollution, particularly fine particulate matter < 2.5 μm (PM2.5), poses significant health risks, especially to vulnerable populations such as children1,2. Public schools, where children spend a substantial portion of their day, are critical locations for monitoring air quality. Accurate estimation of PM2.5 levels at these locations is essential for assessing exposure and implementing mitigation strategies. This study aims to estimate PM2.5 concentrations at public school locations using two distinct data sources—EPA and PurpleAir monitors—and two interpolation methods: Kriging and Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW). By employing cross-validation, we compare the effectiveness of these data combinations and methods to identify the most reliable approach for estimating PM2.5 levels.

PM2.5, particulate matter with a diameter of less than 2.5 micrometers, can penetrate deep into the respiratory system, causing various health issues such as asthma, cardiovascular diseases, and premature death3,4. Numerous studies have highlighted the adverse health effects of PM2.5 exposure, particularly in children, who are more susceptible to air pollution due to their developing respiratory systems and higher inhalation rates relative to their body weight2,5,6.

Accurate estimation of PM2.5 levels relies on high-quality data. Common data sources include ground-based monitoring stations and satellite remote sensing7. In this study, we utilize data from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and PurpleAir monitors. EPA monitors, considered the gold standard for air quality measurement, provide precise, localized measurements but are often limited in spatial coverage. PurpleAir monitors, which are low-cost sensors, offer improved spatial coverage and real-time data but may have variability in accuracy. Combining these data sources can potentially leverage the strengths of each, providing a more comprehensive understanding of PM2.5 distribution.

Interpolation methods are used to estimate pollutant concentrations at unmonitored locations based on observed data. Kriging and Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) are two widely used geostatistical techniques8–10. Kriging, a sophisticated method that considers both the distance and the degree of variation between known data points, provides optimal and unbiased estimates. IDW, the typical method of choice, estimates values based on the inverse of the distance to known points, assuming that closer points have more influence on the estimated value. We considered spatiotemporal versions of these interpolation methods by considering distance in terms of space and time. Both methods have been extensively used in environmental studies, but their comparative performance in estimating PM2.5 at specific locations, such as public schools, with supplementing EPA monitoring using PurpleAir measures remains underexplored.

Cross-validation is a statistical technique used to assess the predictive performance of models. By partitioning the data into training and validation sets, cross-validation provides an unbiased evaluation of model accuracy11. In the context of PM2.5 estimation, cross-validation can help determine the most reliable combination of data sources and interpolation methods, ensuring robust and accurate predictions.

This study contributes to the existing body of knowledge by comparing the effectiveness of Kriging and IDW in estimating PM2.5 levels at public school locations using data from EPA and, potentially in combination with, PurpleAir monitors. The findings will inform future air quality monitoring efforts and support the development of targeted interventions to protect children’s health.

North Carolina was selected as the study location due to the availability of detailed school-level data, which enables precise geospatial analysis of PM2.5 exposure and student performance. Additional factors supporting this choice include the alignment of Local Education Agencies with county boundaries, the state’s diverse student demographics, and its varied geographic regions. These characteristics make North Carolina a compelling setting for evaluating air quality estimation methods in relation to school-based exposure.

Data sources

The EPA Air Quality System data offers daily average PM2.5 measures at 4,000 monitor locations across the US12. Data collection for each monitor begins as early as January 1 and as late as October 30, however, not all days are available at all monitor locations. One hundred and ten (110) of these monitor locations were determined to be within a 100-mile buffer of the state of North Carolina. The monitors reside in North Carolina, Georgia, South Carolina, Tennessee, and Virginia. We downloaded the data associated with the year 2023.

PurpleAir data is available worldwide from about 22,000 monitors13. These are privately owned sensors that individuals can purchase and place at a location of their choosing. Researchers can access the data from these monitors through an API with tokens that cost money; the rates depend on the number of tokens purchased and the number of tokens required depends on the number of rows in the requested data. We retrieved PurpleAir data for the same time period—January 1 to October 30, 2023—to ensure temporal consistency with the EPA data. We pull daily average PM2.5 measures for 236 sensors that were identified as either in North Carolina or the closest monitor to a North Carolina school.

Because of the private, individual owned nature of the PurpleAir monitors, more caution should be used in the data received. We took several steps to cleaning the data and reducing the number of monitors considered. These removal criteria are described below.

Results

Figure 1 displays the EPA and PurpleAir monitor as well as North Carolina public school locations. Most of the PurpleAir monitors used in this study are located within North Carolina, with only a few situated just beyond the state’s borders (see the Methods section for more details). Both EPA and PurpleAir monitors are more densely distributed in urban areas, which influences the spatial resolution of PM2.5 estimates across different school locales. The PurpleAir monitors offer some locations that would not otherwise be covered by EPA alone. Supplemental Table 1 displays mean observed values from EPA and PurpleAir monitors overall as well as subset by distance to nearest EPA monitor. Mean values tend to be higher when the analysis is restricted to monitors located closer to EPA sites. This pattern is largely driven by land use: monitors that are closer to EPA sites are disproportionately located in urban and suburban areas, where both EPA and PurpleAir monitors are more densely distributed. These areas typically experience higher PM2.5 concentrations due to increased traffic, industrial activity, and population density. There are statistically significant differences in the mean observed PM2.5 values between EPA and PurpleAir monitors across all distance-based subsets, as indicated by p-values less than 0.01. However, the direction of these differences varies depending on the distance restraint—for example, PurpleAir monitors report lower values than EPA monitors when no distance restraint is applied, but higher values when restricted to monitors within the median distance.

Application with 8th grade reading scores in North Carolina

To explore the use of these measures in a real application, we consider a North Carolina Department of Education data set which contains student achievement outcomes, including 8th grade reading scores, as well as student and school level characteristics. We apply a Bayesian spatiotemporal linear model that adjusts for the available student and school level characteristics as well as incorporated school, county, and temporal random (latent) effects14–20. With regards to the PM2.5 estimates in modeling, we consider overall, school day, and summertime annual averages. This results in four models - baseline (no PM2.5 measures), annual average PM2.5, school days only average PM2.5, and summer days only average PM2.5. See the Supplemental Materials for additional model details.

Table 3 displays the predictor coefficients for each of the models. Albeit borderline for the annual and school day only averages, the PM2.5 parameter coefficients suggest that as PM2.5 values increase, 8th grade reading scores decrease. The estimates for the other predictors stay largely the same across the models. The estimates for school locale indicate the largest shifts when we introduce PM2.5 measures suggesting that there is some interaction between locale and PM2.5 values. The four models can be compared in terms of model fit using the widely acceptable information criteria (WAIC) values21. For these values, lower indicates a better model fit, and a difference of 4–5 points is considered significant21. The WAIC values for these models is as follows: baseline − 42071.31, annual average − 42075.79, school days only average − 42069.87, weekend days only average − 42074.59, summer days only average − 42081.1, and testing days only average − 42075.33. These measures agree with the coefficient estimates in that including the PM2.5 measure as a predictor only marginally improves model fit for the annual average and schools days only average models.

Discussion

The results of this study highlight the significant impact of incorporating PurpleAir monitors alongside EPA monitors for estimating PM2.5 levels at North Carolina schools. The addition of PurpleAir data enhances spatial coverage, particularly in areas where EPA monitors are sparse, thereby improving the overall accuracy of PM2.5 predictions. This is evident from the cross validation results, which show that combined data models consistently outperform models using only EPA data, both in terms of error reduction and correlation improvement. This finding is consistent with prior work by Malings et al. and Barkjohn et al.22,23, which demonstrated that low-cost sensors like PurpleAir can effectively supplement regulatory networks, especially in areas with limited EPA coverage. Our results extend these findings by showing that the inclusion of PurpleAir data improves prediction accuracy not only in urban centers but also in suburban and rural school locales.

The kriging method, despite its computational intensity, proves to be superior in accuracy compared to the IDW method. This is particularly true when predicting PM2.5 levels at EPA monitor locations, where the kriging method shows less error and higher correlations. However, the inclusion of PurpleAir data in the combined kriging model further enhances prediction accuracy, suggesting that PurpleAir monitors provide valuable supplementary data. Finally, the Kriging method also demonstrated the ability to produce estimates that differ by day-type (annual average overall, school day, summertime, weekend, and testing period). This supports previous geostatistical research, such as Li and Heap8, which found that kriging consistently outperforms deterministic methods like IDW in environmental modeling. Our study adds to this literature by demonstrating that kriging not only improves accuracy but also captures temporal variability across day types more effectively than IDW.

The examination of PM2.5 estimates at North Carolina public schools reveals a clear relationship between locale and PM2.5 levels, with suburban areas experiencing higher levels compared to rural and urban areas. A geographic variation is also identified by the linear model results, which indicate that PM2.5 levels decrease as one moves eastward and northward. This pattern mirrors findings from EPA regional assessments and studies like Crouse et al.24, which documented higher PM2.5 concentrations in areas with dense traffic and industrial activity. Our results suggest that suburban sprawl may contribute to elevated pollution levels, challenging assumptions that urban centers are always the most affected.

The application of PM2.5 estimates to 8th grade reading scores demonstrates a negative association between PM2.5 levels and student performance. Although the inclusion of PM2.5 measures in the models only marginally improves model fit, the results suggest that higher PM2.5 levels are associated with lower reading scores. This finding underscores the potential health and cognitive impacts of air pollution on students and aligns with studies such as Zhang et al. and Heissel et al.25,26, which found that both short-term and chronic exposure to fine particulate matter can impair cognitive function and academic achievement. Notably, our finding that school-day PM2.5 averages explain more variation than annual or summer averages supports the hypothesis from Currie et al. that exposure during instructional time has a more direct impact on learning outcomes27.

It is important to note that these estimates are based on outdoor air quality monitors, and therefore primarily reflect outdoor exposure. Indoor PM2.5 levels may differ significantly depending on building characteristics, ventilation systems, and maintenance practices. Acknowledging this limitation is essential, as it suggests that the results are most applicable to outdoor school activities and may not fully capture students’ total exposure during the school day.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the integration of PurpleAir monitors with existing EPA monitors significantly enhances the spatial resolution and accuracy of PM2.5 estimates in North Carolina. The combined data models, particularly those using the kriging method, provide more reliable predictions, which are crucial for assessing air quality and its impacts on public health and education.

Based on these findings, it is recommended that PurpleAir monitors be added to school locations across North Carolina. This policy would provide several benefits:

Methods

Kriging is a geostatistical interpolation technique used to predict the value of a random field (such as the elevation of the land, temperature, or mineral concentration) at an unobserved location based on observations at nearby locations10,28. The method provides not only predictions but also an estimation of the uncertainty of those predictions. We performed the most common type of kriging - ordinary kriging - which assumes a constant but unknown mean across the study area. It is the most commonly used form of kriging.

Kriging relies on the principle that points closer together in space and time are more likely to have similar values than points farther apart. This can be quantified using a variogram and covariance functions either alone or in combination. These functions describe the degree of spatial and temporal dependence of the spatial random field or stochastic process. We applied a sum-metric model that allows for a combination of spatial, temporal, and metric models and introduces an anisotropy parameter29. Although we use spatio-temporal models and estimate PM2.5 at schools daily from January 6 to October 25, our student level data does not vary daily. As such, it makes most sense to create an annual average PM2.5 value to use for modeling. In addition to an overall annual average, we also compare and consider the use of average PM2.5 for certain day types defined as school days (Monday through Friday, not summer), weekends (Saturday and Sunday), summer days (all of June, July, and August), and testing days (Monday through Friday, month of May). Testing days are defined in this way because North Carolina schools administer the end of grade tests in the final 10 days of the school year. Whereas the exact timing of these final 10 days varies by district, it generally falls during the month of May. This results in a total of 12 PM2.5 estimates, an overall annual average as well as day types per interpolation method.
