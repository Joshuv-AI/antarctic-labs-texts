Cavity QED beyond the Jaynes-Cummings model
Abeer Al Ghamdi, Gin Jose, Almut Beige

Abstract
As atom-cavity systems are becoming more sophisticated, the limitations of the Jaynes-Cummings model are becoming more apparent. In this paper, we therefore take a more dynamical approach to the modelling of atom-cavity systems and do not reduce the electromagnetic field inside the resonator to a single mode. Our approach shows that the decay rate Γ cav \Gamma_{\rm cav} of an emitter inside a subwavelength cavity with metallic mirrors can be much larger than its free space decay rate Γ free \Gamma_{\rm free} due to constructive interference effects of the emitted light. In general, however, we find that Γ cav = Γ free \Gamma_{\rm cav}=\Gamma_{\rm free} to a very good approximation which might explain why many atom-cavity experiments have not been able to operate in the so-called strong coupling regime.

Another example, which challenges the usual single-mode description of the quantised electromagnetic field between two mirrors, are so-called Fabry-Perot cavities

As part of our theoretical approach, certain simplifications were introduced to render the problem analytically tractable. These are discussed in detail in the conclusion of the manuscript. \displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}\Delta t}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\langle 0_{\rm F}|{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{\cal E}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})\,U_{\rm F}(t,t^{\prime})\,{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})|0_{\rm F}\rangle\,\sigma^{-}\,\rho_{\rm A}(0)\,\sigma^{+}\,,

\displaystyle H_{\rm cond}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{\rm A}-\frac{{\rm i}e^{2}}{\hbar\Delta t}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\langle 0_{\rm F}|{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{\cal E}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})\,U_{\rm F}(t,t^{\prime})\,{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})|0_{\rm F}\rangle\,\sigma^{+}\sigma^{-}\,.

(25)

The above equations show that all we need to know to calculate
{\cal L}(\rho_{\rm A}(0))
and
H_{\rm cond}
is how local electric field excitations created at the position
\boldsymbol{r}_{0}
of the atom evolve in time in the absence of atom-field interactions. As we shall see in the next two sections, the dynamics of these local field excitations can be deduced from classical optics in a relatively straightforward way after decomposing the electric field observable
\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})
as in Eq. (
10
) into its
({\boldsymbol{s}},\lambda)
components.

II.3
The spontaneous decay rate
\Gamma

To obtain an expression for the spontaneous decay rate
\Gamma
which we introduced in Eq. (
II
), we need to neglect an atomic level shift, i.e. a term of the form
\hbar\Delta\,\sigma^{+}\sigma^{-}
, in the expression for the conditional Hamiltonian. This level shift is in general much smaller than the atomic transition frequency
\omega_{0}
and can therefore be absorbed into the energy
\hbar\omega_{0}
of the excited state
|1_{\rm A}\rangle
of the atom. To implement this approximation, we replace
H_{\rm cond}
in the following by the operator
(H_{\rm cond}-{\rm H.c.})/2
. Subsequently we find that the above expressions coincide indeed with the reset operator
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\rm A}(0))
and with the conditional Hamiltonian
H_{\rm cond}
in Eq. (
II
), if we define the (real) spontaneous decay rate
\Gamma
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}\,\Delta t}\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}={\sf H},{\sf V}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\int_{\cal S}{\rm d}^{2}s\int_{\cal S}{\rm d}^{2}s^{\prime}

(26)

\displaystyle\times{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\langle 0_{\rm F}|\,{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{\cal E}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})\,U^{\dagger}_{\rm F}(t^{\prime},0)

\displaystyle\hskip 59.75095pt\times{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot U_{\rm F}(t,0)\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})|0_{\rm F}\rangle

where Eq. (
10
) has been used.

III
A single atom in free space and in the presence of a partially transparent mirror

To check the validity of the above equations, we now use Eq. (
26
) to derive the well-known spontaneous decay rates
\Gamma_{\rm free}
and
\Gamma_{\rm mir}
of a single two-level atom in free space
Hegerfeldt
;
Stokes
and in the presence of a partially transparent mirror
Nick
;
Dorner
;
Kuraptsev
, respectively. In both cases, our result agrees with the predictions of previous authors.

III.1
Free space

Figure 2:
[Colour online] Schematic view of an atom at a position
\boldsymbol{r}_{0}=(x_{0},0,0)
with
{x_{0}>0}
in the presence of a partially transparent mirror in the
x=0

\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}=(-x_{0},0,0)
of the mirror image of the atom and interferes with light emitted away from the mirror. For small atom-mirror distances, interference is mostly destructive and the spontaneous decay rate of the atom decreases. Here
r_{a}
and
t_{a}
denote the (complex) reflection and transmission rates of the mirror interface.

From classical optics, we know that, in free space, light with a well defined direction
{\boldsymbol{s}}
of propagation travels at constant speed along straight lines. Hence

\displaystyle U_{\rm F}(t,0)\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})|0_{\rm F}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)|0_{\rm F}\rangle\,.

(27)

Substituting this equation into Eq. (
26
), we therefore find that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm free}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}\,\Delta t}\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}={\sf H},{\sf V}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\int_{\cal S}{\rm d}^{2}s\int_{\cal S}{\rm d}^{2}s^{\prime}

(28)

\displaystyle\times{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\langle 0_{\rm F}|\,{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{\cal E}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}ct^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 59.75095pt\times{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{\cal E}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)|0_{\rm F}\rangle\,.

Hence using Eq. (
13
) leads us to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm free}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm i}ce^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\hbar\,\Delta t}\,\sum_{\lambda={\sf H},{\sf V}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\int_{\cal S}{\rm d}^{2}s

(29)

\displaystyle\times{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\delta^{(3)}(ct-ct^{\prime})\,\big\|{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}\big\|^{2}\,.

To perform the time integrations in the above expression, we notice that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}t^{\prime}}\,\delta^{(3)}(ct-ct^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{{\rm i}\omega_{0}^{3}\over c^{4}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}t}

(30)

for times
t\in(0,\Delta t)
. If
t
is not between 0 and
\Delta t
, then the above integral becomes zero. This observation leads us to the spontaneous decay rate

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm free}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}\omega_{0}^{3}}{8\pi^{2}c^{3}\varepsilon_{0}\hbar}\sum_{\lambda={\sf H},{\sf V}}\int_{\cal S}{\rm d}^{2}s\,\big\|{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}\big\|^{2}\,.~~

(31)

To further simplify this expression, we now introduce the polar coordinates
\vartheta
and
\varphi
with
\vartheta\in(0,\pi)
and
\varphi\in(0,2\pi)
and write the direction vector
{\boldsymbol{s}}
and the polarisation vectors
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}}
as

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76228pt\left\{\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{s}{\sf H}},\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{s}{\sf V}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\cos\vartheta\\
\cos\varphi\,\sin\vartheta\\
\sin\varphi\,\sin\vartheta\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0\\
~\sin\varphi\\
-\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\sin\vartheta\\
-\cos\varphi\cos\vartheta\\
-\sin\varphi\cos\vartheta\end{pmatrix}\right\}.

One can easily check that the above vectors are pairwise orthonormal. Using Eq. (
9
) and the fact that
{\rm d}^{2}s={\rm d}\vartheta\,{\rm d}\varphi\,\sin\vartheta
, we find that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm free}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\|^{2}\omega_{0}^{3}}{8\pi^{2}c^{3}\varepsilon_{0}\hbar}\int_{0}^{\pi}{\rm d}\vartheta\sin\vartheta\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\rm d}\varphi

(33)

\displaystyle\times\left(\cos^{2}\varphi+\sin^{2}\varphi\cos^{2}\vartheta\right)\,.

After performing the
\varphi
integration, substituting
\xi=\cos\vartheta
and performing the remaining integration, we obtain the spontaneous decay rate

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm free}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\|^{2}\,\omega_{0}^{3}}{3\pi c^{3}\varepsilon_{0}\hbar}

(34)

of a single atom in free space which aligns with the quantum optics literature
Hegerfeldt
;
Stokes
.

III.2
A partially transparent mirror interface

A partially transparent mirror reduces electric field amplitudes and alters directions of propagation when light arrives at its surface. However, away from the mirror, light still moves along straight lines, as described by Eq. (
27
). Taking this into account, placing the mirror into the
x=0
plane and the atom at
\boldsymbol{r}_{0}=(x_{0},0,0)
with
x_{0}>0
, as illustrated in Fig.
2
, we now find that

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274ptU_{\rm F}(t,0)\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\Theta(-s_{x})\Theta(x_{0}+s_{x}ct)\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)\right.

\displaystyle+t_{a}\,\Theta(-s_{x})\Theta(-(x_{0}+s_{x}ct))\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)

\displaystyle+r_{a}\,\Theta(-s_{x})\Theta(-(x_{0}+s_{x}ct))\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)

\displaystyle\left.+\Theta(s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)\right]|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle\,.

Here the tilde symbol is used to add a minus sign to the
x
component of a vector. For example, if
{\boldsymbol{s}}=(s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})
, then
\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}=(-s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})
. Hence
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{0}=(-x_{0},0,0)
denotes the position of the mirror image of the atom. In addition,
r_{a}
and
t_{a}
denote the (complex) reflection and (real) transmission rates of the mirror interface, respectively
AMC
, with

\displaystyle|r_{a}|^{2}+t_{a}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\,.

(36)

In addition,
\Theta(x)
has been defined such that

\displaystyle\Theta(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\text{ for }&x\geq 0\,,\\
0&\text{ for }&x<0\,.\end{array}\right.

The first three terms in Eq. (
35
) describe light which travels away from the atom towards the mirror and is eventually reflected or transmitted (cf. Fig.
2
). Notice that the reflected electric field contributions seem to originate from the mirror image of the atom which is situated a distance of
x_{0}
behind the mirror. The final term in Eq. (
35
) describes light which has been emitted away from the mirror surface and propagates as it would in free space. Here we are especially interested in the case where the time it takes light to travel from the atom to the mirror surface is in general much shorter than
\Delta t
. This means,
\Theta\left(-(x_{0}+s_{x}ct)\right)=1
and
\Theta\left(x_{0}+s_{x}ct\right)=0
for almost all times
t\in(0,\Delta t)
and

\displaystyle U_{\rm F}(t,0)\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[t_{a}\,\Theta(-s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)\right.

(40)

\displaystyle+r_{a}\,\Theta(-s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)

\displaystyle\left.+\Theta(s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)\right]|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle~~~~~

to a very good approximation.

To calculate the spontaneous decay rate of a single atom in the presence of a partially-transparent mirror interface, we can now proceed as in the previous subsection. Substituting Eqs. (
10
) and (
40
) into Eq. (
26
) yields

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm mir}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}\,\Delta t}\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}={\sf H},{\sf V}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{d}^{2}s\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{d}^{2}s^{\prime}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})}\,

(41)

\displaystyle\times\langle 0_{\rm F}|\,{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\Big[t_{a}\,\Theta(-s_{x}^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}ct^{\prime})+r_{a}^{*}\,\Theta(-s_{x}^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}ct^{\prime})+\Theta(s_{x}^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}ct^{\prime})\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 19.63246pt\times{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\left[t_{a}\,\Theta(-s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)+r_{a}\,\Theta(-s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+\Theta(s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)\right]|0_{\rm F}\rangle\,.

To evaluate this expression, we employ again Eq. (
13
) and take into account that state vectors of the electromagnetic field with different final directions of propagation and different polarisations are pairwise orthogonal and therefore do not interfere. Since the atomic dipole moment
{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}
is parallel to the mirror surface, we have
{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}={\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}
. After performing the
s^{\prime}
integration, we therefore find that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm mir}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm i}ce^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\hbar\,\Delta t}\sum_{\lambda={\sf H},{\sf V}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{d}^{2}s\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})}\left[\left((t_{a}^{2}+|r_{a}|^{2})\Theta(-s_{x})+\Theta(s_{x})\right)\,\delta^{(3)}(ct-ct^{\prime})\right.

(42)

\displaystyle\left.+r_{a}^{*}\,\Theta(s_{x})\,\delta^{(3)}({\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0})+ct-ct^{\prime})+r_{a}\,\Theta(-s_{x})\,\delta^{(3)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}-\boldsymbol{r}_{0})+ct-ct^{\prime})\right]\big\|{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}\big\|^{2}\,.

Since
(t_{a}^{2}+|r_{a}|^{2})\Theta(-s_{x})+\Theta(s_{x})=1
and
{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{0}=-\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{0}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}
, this equation simplifies to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm mir}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm i}ce^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\hbar\,\Delta t}\sum_{\lambda={\sf H},{\sf V}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t\int_{0}^{\Delta t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{d}^{2}s\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{0}(t-t^{\prime})}

(43)

\displaystyle\times\left[\delta^{(3)}(ct-ct^{\prime})+{\rm Re}(r_{a})\,\delta^{(3)}(2{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+ct-ct^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\times\big\|{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}\big\|^{2},

in analogy to Eq. (
29
). The first term in this equation coincides with the free space decay rate
\Gamma_{\rm free}
. However, the additional terms is due to the far-field interference between reflected light and light which has been emitted away from the mirror.

can be shown equal

\displaystyle{\Gamma_{\rm mir}\over\Gamma_{\rm free}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+{3{\rm Re}(r_{a})\over 8\pi}\sum_{\lambda={\sf H},{\sf V}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{d}^{2}s\,{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}k_{0}{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{0}}\,\big\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\cal D}}_{01}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\left(\cos^{2}\varphi+\sin^{2}\varphi\cos^{2}\vartheta\right)

with

\displaystyle{\Gamma_{\rm mir}\over\Gamma_{\rm free}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+{3{\rm Re}(r_{a})\over 2}

\displaystyle\times\left[{\sin(2k_{0}d)\over 2k_{0}d}+{\cos(2k_{0}d)\over(2k_{0}d)^{2}}-{\sin(2k_{0}d)\over(2k_{0}d)^{3}}\right]~~~~

after again carrying out the

IV
The spontaneous decay of a single atom between two planar mirrors

There are many similarities between the quantum optics of an atom in front of a highly-reflecting mirror (cf. Fig.
2
) and of an atom between two highly-reflecting mirrors (cf. Fig.
5
). In both systems, the light emerging from the emitter is reflected by the mirror surfaces and the different contributions interfere. This observation suggests that an atom inside a cavity, like an atom near a mirror interface, has only a single spontaneous decay rate
\Gamma_{\rm cav}
which is in general different from
\Gamma_{\rm free}
. This is in contrast to the standard quantum optics approach to atom-cavity systems in Eq. (
2
) which considers two independent decay channels with decay rates
\Gamma
and
\kappa
. In the following, we calculate the dependence of
\Gamma_{\rm cav}
on the distance
d
and the reflection and transmission rates,
r_{a}
,
r_{b}
,
t_{a}
and
t_{b}
of the resonator mirrors using the same methodology as in the previous section.

For convenience, we place the emitter in the following into the origin of our coordinate system at position
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{0}=(0,0,0)
, as illustrated in Fig.
5
. Moreover, we place the cavity mirrors, which are parallel to the
x=0
plane, at positions
x=-d/2
and
x=d/2
along the
x
axis. Proceeding as in the previous Section and considering again sufficiently large times
t
, we now find that

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{F}}(t,0)\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(-s_{x})\,t_{a}\left[\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}({\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+r_{a}r_{b}\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(2{\boldsymbol{d}}+{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+r_{a}^{2}r_{b}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(4{\boldsymbol{d}}+{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+\ldots\,\right]|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle

(47)

\displaystyle+\Theta(-s_{x})\,r_{a}t_{b}\left[\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(-{\boldsymbol{d}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+r_{a}r_{b}\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(-3{\boldsymbol{d}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+r_{a}^{2}r_{b}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(-5{\boldsymbol{d}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+\ldots\,\right]|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle

\displaystyle+\Theta(s_{x})\,t_{b}\left[\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}({\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+r_{a}r_{b}\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(-2{\boldsymbol{d}}+{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+r_{a}^{2}r_{b}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(-4{\boldsymbol{d}}+{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+\ldots\,\right]|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle

\displaystyle+\Theta(s_{x})\,r_{b}t_{a}\left[\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}({\boldsymbol{d}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+r_{a}r_{b}\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(3{\boldsymbol{d}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+r_{a}^{2}r_{b}^{2}\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(5{\boldsymbol{d}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+\ldots\,\right]|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle

in the absence of light-matter interactions and with
{\boldsymbol{d}}=(d,0,0)
and
d>0
. The first line in this equation describes light which has been created at
t=0
, travels to the left and eventually leaves the cavity on the left hand side. Analogously, the second line describes light which also travels left initially but eventually leaves the cavity on the right. Analogously, the last two lines describe light which travels initially to the right. After combining the above terms into geometric sums, Eq. (
47
) simplifies to

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{F}}(t,0)\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{0}\right)|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(r_{a}r_{b}\right)^{n}\left[t_{a}\,\Theta(-s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(2n{\boldsymbol{d}}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)+t_{b}\,\Theta(s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{s}\lambda}(-2n{\boldsymbol{d}}+\boldsymbol{s}ct)\right.

(48)

\displaystyle\left.+r_{a}t_{b}\,\Theta(-s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}(-(2n+1){{\boldsymbol{d}}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)+r_{b}t_{a}\,\Theta(s_{x})\,\boldsymbol{\cal E}^{\dagger}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}\lambda}((2n+1){{\boldsymbol{d}}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}ct)\right]|0_{\mathrm{F}}\rangle

in analogy to Eq. (
40
). The above state is the (unnormalised) state of a single photon with polarisation
\lambda
at time
t
which has been emitted by the atom into the
{\boldsymbol{s}}
direction at
t=0
.

Proceeding as in the previous section and substituting Eq. (
48
) into Eq. (
26
), we therefore find that the spontaneous decay rate
\Gamma_{\rm cav}
of the atom inside the cavity equals

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm cav}

\displaystyle=

V
Conclusions

The so-called cooperativity parameter
C
is widely accepted as a measure for how fast an atom-cavity system looses its initial excitation. Within the framework of the Jaynes-Cummings model
theory
;
theory2
;
theory3
;
theory4
, this parameter can be written as
C=g^{2}/\kappa\Gamma_{\rm free}
where
g
is the atom-field interaction constant in Eq. (
1
), while
\kappa
and
\Gamma_{\rm free}
denote the cavity and the emitter free-space decay rates, respectively. In principle, the parameter
C
can assume any value. For example, it seems that it is possible to enter the so-called strong coupling regime with
C\gg 1
by simply increasing the reflectivity of the resonator mirrors which increases the lifetime
1/\kappa
of photons inside the resonator. Moreover, the Purcell effect
Purcell1946
suggests that reducing the distance of the cavity mirrors increases the atom-field coupling constant
g
by reducing the mode volume of the cavity photons. Entering the strong coupling regime is important, since many atom-cavity quantum computing schemes require
C
to be at least two or even three orders of magnitude larger than one
Metz
. Nevertheless, despite great efforts, experimentalists have found it very hard to realise atom-cavity systems with
C\gg 1
in the laboratory.

To better understand, why entering the so-called strong-coupling regime is so hard, this paper takes an alternative approach to the modelling of light propagation in atom-cavity systems. Usually it is assumed that the presence of the cavity mirrors imposes boundary conditions on the quantised electromagnetic field, thereby reducing its Hilbert space to a discrete set of standing wave modes. The available frequencies
\omega_{\rm cav}
of these modes depend only on the distance
d
of the resonator mirrors. In contrast to this, this paper assumes that the presence of a mirror surface does not alter the structure of the quantised electromagnetic field. It only alters the dynamics of the photons
Jake
;
AMC
. Taking this point of view, the cooperativity parameter
C
of optical cavities equals
\Gamma_{\rm cav}/\Gamma_{\rm free}
which we calculated in Eq. (
60
). Our calculations show that
C
is in general close to one and that realising a cooperativity parameter different from one is much harder than generally assumed.

The only exception that we found are atom-cavity systems where the distance
d
of the resonator mirrors is much smaller than the wavelength
\lambda_{0}
of the emitted light. In the case of positive mirror reflection rates
r_{\rm mir}
, the spontaneous decay rate
\Gamma_{\rm cav}
in Eq. (
57
) can become much larger than its free space decay rate
\Gamma_{\rm free}
(cf. Fig.
8
). In other words, the enhancement of spontaneous emission requires that the resonator mirrors are metasurfaces which do not change the sign of electric field amplitudes upon reflection, as it would be the case for light reflection by dielectric media
Hecht
;
AMC
. Such metasurfaces exist
complex
;
Barreda2021
;
Liu2025
. For example, experiments have shown that metallic mirrors with some surface roughness have complex reflection rates
Fox
due to the possibility of currents. However, in general, an emitter placed at the center between two planar mirrors decays at a rate similar to its free space decay rate
\Gamma_{\rm free}
(cf. Eq. (
61
)), independent of the reflection and transmission rates and the distance of the resonator mirrors.

In conclusion
, our equations confirm that it is difficult to enhance atom-cavity interactions while reducing photon emission. One way of achieving this is to consider subwavelength cavities with metallic mirrors. Alternatively, one could try to avoid one of the assumptions which we made in our calculations for simplicity. These are:

1.

Here we only consider cavities with planar mirrors which is normally not the case in atom-cavity experiments
Reichel
. Usually, the mirrors are shaped such that they refocus the light back onto the emitter to encourage re-absorption by the source. In our paper, the possible re-absorption of light has been neglected.

2.

Here we neglected the re-absorption of light due to the relatively small size of an atom compared to the wavelength
\lambda_{0}
of the emitted light (cf. Eq. (
48
)). Increasing the size of the emitter might alter interference effects and might increase re-absorption.

3.

Another assumption made in this paper is that the atom is placed exactly at the centre of the resonator (cf. Fig.
5
). Moving the emitter to a different position is likely to impact the interference of the light coming from the emitter. More detailed calculations are needed to study the dependence of
\Gamma_{\rm cav}
on the atomic position.

4.

Our calculations also assume that the reflection rates
r_{a}
and
r_{b}
on the insides of the resonator mirrors are both real and both same, i.e.
r_{a}=r_{b}=r_{\rm mir}
. Breaking this symmetry is also likely to change the amount of interference inside the resonator. However, our discussion in Section
III.2
suggests that changing the balance between
r_{a}
and
r_{b}
is unlikely to strongly affect the result in Eq. (
61
), as long as the distance of the atom from the cavity mirrors remains relatively large.

5.

Finally, let us point out that our calculations do not take into account that the reflection rate of a mirror surface depends on the angle of the incoming light. Instead, we assumed that
r_{\rm mir}
is always the same. However, we do not expect that this simplification has a big impact on our results, since we only consider dipole moment vectors
{\boldsymbol{D}}_{01}
which are parallel to the mirror surface (cf. Eq. (
9
)) and most of the emitted light approaches the mirror at the same right angle.

We believe that the relatively simple analysis that we present already provides some interesting new insights. For example, it explains why it is so hard to design optical cavities which operate in the so-called strong coupling regime. It also explains some dramatic differences in the emission dynamics of atoms in metallic subwavelength cavities and in photonic bandgap materials.
