Clinical, radiological, and pathological features of pulmonary hypertension associated with combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema
Authors: Mengyu He, Qinhua Zhao, Huikang Xie, Rui Zhang, Jinming Liu, Weiping Xie, Yanzhe Yu, Yan Wu, Jiayi Zhang, Chunyan Wu, Sugang Gong, Lan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCombined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema-associated pulmonary hypertension (CPFE-PH) has emerged as a distinct third category of PH, yet its detailed clinical-hemodynamic profile, pathological features, and comparative outcomes remain poorly defined. This study aims to characterize the phenotype of CPFE-PH and compare it with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease-related PH (COPD-PH) and idiopathic interstitial pneumonia-related PH (IIP-PH).MethodsIn this multicenter cohort study, we analyzed 113 patients with CPFE-PH, 277 with COPD-PH, and 78 with IIP-PH, all diagnosed by right heart catheterization. Clinical, physiological, imaging, pathological, treatment response, and survival data were statistically compared across groups.ResultsCPFE-PH patients were older, predominantly male smokers, and exhibited preserved ventilatory function but severely reduced diffusing capacity (DLCO 31.6% predicted, IQR 24.8–37.8). They had the most severe hemodynamic compromise (mean pulmonary arterial pressure 41 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance 7.4 Wood units), a high prevalence of mediastinal lymphadenopathy (81.4%), and universal capillary hemangiomatosis-like remodeling, a pathological feature absent in COPD-PH and rarely observed in IIP-PH. Pulmonary arterial hypertension-targeted therapy failed to improve functional or biomarker outcomes, and transplant-free survival was shortest in CPFE-PH (median 32 months) compared with COPD-PH and IIP-PH (P < 0.05).ConclusionsCPFE-PH appears to represent a severe phenotype within Group 3 PH, in which prominent capillary remodeling, including hemangiomatosis-like changes, may contribute to its clinical, radiological, and functional manifestations, distinguish it from other Group 3 PH types.Clinical trialChinese Clinical Study.org, No: ChiCTR2000032212, Registration Date: 2020-04-23; URL: http://www.chictr.org.cn/.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03828-7.

Introduction

Chronic lung diseases, including interstitial lung disease (ILD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE), are recognized causes of Group 3 pulmonary hypertension (PH). In ILD- and COPD-associated PH, hemodynamic impairment is typically mild to moderate [1–3]. In contrast, CPFE-associated PH displays higher prevalence, a distinctive clinical profile, and disproportionately severe hemodynamic compromise compared with other Group 3 PH, emerging as a unique and clinically relevant phenotype [4, 5].

CPFE is radiographically defined by coexistence of upper-lobe emphysema and lower-lobe fibrosis and is observed in approximately 26–54% of patients with idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IIPs) [6]. Clinically, CPFE is characterized by male predominance, heavy smoking history, relatively preserved spirometric lung volumes despite striking impairment in gas exchange, and severe exertional dyspnea [7]. The hallmark physiologic abnormality is a profound reduction in diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), a feature that overlaps with pulmonary veno-occlusive disease (PVOD) and pulmonary capillary hemangiomatosis (PCH), raising questions about shared vascular pathology [8].

PH pathogenesis involves progressive remodeling of pulmonary vasculature, affecting all arterial layers and leading to luminal narrowing, loss of compliance, and reduced distensibility [9]. In Group 3 PH, chronic alveolar hypoxia and cigarette smoke are believed to drive this process [10]. While COPD-PH and IPF-PH have been associated with well-described structural vascular changes, the vascular pathology underlying CPFE-PH remains poorly defined. To date, the relationship between vascular lesions and distinct clinical and hemodynamic phenotype of CPFE-PH has not been comprehensively characterized.

To address this gap, we performed a systematic comparison of clinical features, hemodynamic profiles, radiologic and histopathologic findings, responses to pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH)-targeted therapies, and survival between CPFE-PH, COPD-PH, and IIP-PH cohorts. Our objective was to delineate the unique nature of CPFE-PH and enhance understanding of its vascular pathology.

Study design and methods

Study cohort

This multicenter observational cohort utilized data from the Hemodynamics, Outcomes, and Prognosis in PH with Chronic Pulmonary Disease (HOPE) registry, which included four PH referral centers in China and was established to evaluate the clinical, hemodynamic, and pathologic characteristics of chronic pulmonary disease associated-PH (Figure S1). Between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2024, 901 adults (≥ 18 years) were consecutively enrolled. After excluding 65 patients who did not meet the hemodynamic definition of pre-capillary PH, 836 patients with chronic lung disease-associated PH were included. Pre‑capillary PH was confirmed by right heart catheterization (RHC) demonstrating a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg, pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 2 Wood units (WU) [11]. CPFE was defined on high‑resolution computed tomography (HRCT) as upper‑lobe predominant emphysema (emphysema‑to‑lung volume ratio > 5%) with lower‑lobe predominant fibrosis [6]. COPD was diagnosed per contemporaneous Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) definitions, and IIP according to international guidelines [12]. Patients with other chronic respiratory diseases, concomitant rheumatic or autoimmune disorders, or combined pulmonary embolism were excluded. Diagnoses were independently confirmed by two senior respiratory physicians.

Data sources

The diagnosis date was recorded as the date of RHC. Baseline data collected included body mass index (BMI), World Health Organization functional class (WHO-FC), 6-minute walking distance (6MWD), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood (PaCO2). Pulmonary function testing was conducted according to recommendations [13]. Forced vital capacity (FVC) of the predicted value, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) of the predicted value, FEV1/FVC, total lung capacity (TLC) of the predicted value, and DLCO of the predicted value were included.

Follow-up data for treatment response were collected at the most recent evaluation after initiating PAH-specific therapy. Treatment, including management of chronic lung diseases and PAH-specific therapy, was initiated after RHC at the discretion of attending physician. Newly obtained test results were recorded.

RHC

RHC was performed as part of routine diagnostic evaluation for suspected PH, based on symptoms, echocardiographic findings, or gas exchange abnormalities disproportionate to the underlying lung disease. All patients underwent RHC using a Swan-Ganz catheter via a central or peripheral venous approach. Hemodynamic parameters included diastolic, systolic and mean PAP, PAWP, and mixed-venous oxygen saturation (SvO2). Cardiac output was measured three times by thermodilution. PVR, cardiac index (CI), stroke volume index (SVI) and pulmonary arterial compliance (PAC) were calculated according to standard formulas. Severe pre-capillary PH was defined by either the 2022 ESC/ERS criteria (PVR > 5 WU, mPAP > 20 mmHg, PAWP ≤ 15 mmHg) [11] or the 2015 ESC/ERS criteria (mPAP ≥ 35 mmHg, or mPAP 25–34 mmHg with CI < 2.5 L/min/m²) [14].

HRCT

HRCT scans were evaluated by two experienced respiratory physicians and radiologists blinded to clinical data. Emphysema, defined as low-attenuation regions with thin/absent walls or bullae ≥ 1 cm in the upper lungs, was graded by percent low attenuation area. Interstitial fibrosis, identified by honeycombing, reticulation, ground-glass opacities, traction bronchiectasis, or thick-walled bullae, was classified by extent of involvement. Grading criteria are detailed in the online supplement based on previous studies [15–17] (Table S1-S2).

Histopathologic assessment

Lung specimens were obtained from patients at the time of lung transplantation, fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and Victoria blue staining. To ensure representative sampling, tissue blocks were systematically collected from multiple anatomical regions of each explanted lung, including fibrotic areas, emphysematous areas, and lung regions that appeared relatively preserved on gross. For each category, at least two tissue blocks were obtained per patient, resulting in a minimum of six blocks examined per explanted lung. From each block, serial sections were prepared for histological evaluation. All specimens were independently evaluated by two cardiopulmonary pathologists.

For vascular assessment, three pulmonary arteries (100–300 μm in external diameter) near respiratory or terminal bronchioles were randomly selected. Vascular changes were graded using the Heath-Edwards classification, and the mean of the three scores was recorded as the regional score [18]. Pulmonary venules within the same regions were evaluated for cellular and/or fibrotic intimal thickening and muscularization [19]. Capillary remodeling was also examined, with hemangiomatosis-like lesions defined as alveolar septa containing more than three rows of capillaries [20].

Follow-up and outcomes

Patient vital status was confirmed through clinic visits or telephone interviews. Follow-up duration for survival analysis was calculated from diagnosis to the study endpoint (February 1, 2025), with patients lost to follow-up censored at their last contact. Two survival outcomes were assessed. Transplant-free survival (TFS), defined as the time from PH diagnosis to either all-cause mortality or lung transplantation, was the primary endpoint. Overall survival focused exclusively on mortality events with transplant recipients censored at the time of surgery.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were expressed as median with interquartile ranges (IQRs), categorical data as frequencies and percentages. Intergroup comparisons between CPFE-PH cohort and the other two cohorts utilized Kruskal-Wallis tests with Bonferroni post hoc analysis for continuous data and Person’s Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data. Baseline and follow-up 6MWD, NT-proBNP and arterial blood gases were compared using Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Survival was analyzed with Kaplan-Meier method, with log-rank tests for intergroup comparisons. Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to adjust for age and sex. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was conducted with SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp) and Stata 17 (Stata Corp).

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study comprised 113 patients diagnosed with CPFE-PH, compared with two control groups: 277 COPD-PH patients and 78 IIP-PH patients. All patients with CPFE-PH were male, with a median age of 69.0 yrs (IQR 63.0–75.0), higher than COPD-PH [67.0 yrs (IQR 60.5–73.0), P = 0.012] and IIP-PH patients [66.0 yrs (IQR 57.5–71.0), P = 0.001]. A history of smoking was reported in 85.0% (96/113) of CPFE-PH, significantly higher than in COPD-PH (188/277, 67.9%, P < 0.001) and IIP-PH (35/78, 44.9%, P < 0.001). The cumulative smoking exposure, expressed as pack-years, was also markedly greater in CPFE-PH patients [40.0 (15.0–45.0)] compared with COPD-PH [27.5 (0–40.0), P = 0.048] and IIP-PH [0 (0–30.0), P < 0.001] patients. Coronary artery diseases were common in CPFE-PH group (20.4%, 23/113). At baseline, CPFE-PH patients had more advanced functional impairment, with WHO-FC Ⅲ in 61.1% (69/113) and Ⅳ in 11.5% (13/113). There were no significant differences among three groups in BMI, 6MWD, or NT-proBNP levels (Table 1).

Disproportionate diffusion and oxygenation decline

Compared with patients with COPD-PH and IIP-PH, those with CPFE-PH exhibited relatively preserved ventilatory function, evidenced by higher FEV1, FEV1/FVC, and TLC. In contrast, diffusing capacity was disproportionately impaired in CPFE-PH, with a median DLCO of 31.6% predicted (IQR 24.8–37.8)]. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed severe hypoxemia in CPFE-PH [PaO2, 57.8 mmHg (IQR 49.0-63.2)] and lower PaCO2 [35.2 mmHg (IQR 30.3–38.3)] compared with COPD-PH [PaO2, 68.0 mmHg (IQR 56.0-82.3), P < 0.001; PaCO2, 49.9 mmHg (IQR 40.8–58.7), P < 0.001] and IIP-PH [PaO2, 66.7 mmHg (IQR 55.3–84.9), P < 0.001; PaCO2, 39.1 mmHg (IQR 34.7–45.1), P = 0.004] (Table 1).

Severe hemodynamic derangements

Patients with CPFE-PH showed significantly greater hemodynamic impairment than those with COPD‐PH or IIP‐PH (Table S3). Median mPAP was markedly higher in CPFE‐PH [41.0 mmHg (IQR 34.0-48.5)] than in COPD‐PH [30.0 mmHg (IQR 25.0–36.0), P < 0.001] and IIP‐PH [32.5 mmHg (IQR 27.0-40.5), P < 0.001] (Fig. 1A). PVR was similarly elevated [7.4 WU (IQR 5.1–10.6) vs. 4.3 WU (IQR 3.1–6.2), P < 0.001, and 5.2 WU (IQR 4.2–7.3), P < 0.001], while PAC was lowest [1.2 mL/mmHg (IQR 0.9–1.6) vs. 1.8 mL/mmHg, P < 0.001, and 1.5 mL/mmHg, P = 0.007] (Fig. 1B-C). CI, SvO₂, and SVI were reduced compared with COPD‐PH (P < 0.01 for all) but not significantly different from IIP‐PH (Fig. 1D-F).

Based on 2022 ESC/ERS criteria, severe pre-capillary PH occurred in 77.0% (87/113) of CPFE‐PH cases, compared with 37.5% (104/277) of COPD‐PH and 55.1% (43/78) of IIP‐PH (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1G). Similar proportions were observed using the 2015 definition (77.0%, 35.0%, and 52.6%, respectively; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1H).

Distinctive HRCT features

All CPFE-PH patients exhibited emphysematous changes, with significantly greater severity than COPD-PH (P = 0.001). Fibrosis was milder in CPFE-PH than in IIP-PH (P < 0.001). Mediastinal lymphadenopathy occurred in 81.4% of CPFE-PH cases, markedly higher than in COPD-PH (24.9%) or IIP-PH (60.3%) (P < 0.001). Diffuse ground-glass opacities, predominantly distributed within relatively preserved lung parenchyma rather than areas of advanced structural destruction, were significantly more frequent in CPFE-PH patients (49.6%) compared with those with COPD-PH (13.0%) and IIP-PH (34.6%) (P < 0.001) (Table 2; Figure S2A-D).

Pathological findings of pulmonary vessels

Histological analysis was performed on explanted lungs from 7 CPFE-PH, 3 COPD-PH, and 6 IIP-PH patients. The Heath-Edwards scores of pulmonary arteries did not differ significantly between CPFE-PH and COPD-PH in emphysematous regions (P = 0.393), between CPFE-PH and IIP-PH in fibrotic regions (P = 0.534), or among the three groups in preserved regions (P = 0.053) (Table 3). In emphysematous area, small pulmonary arteries predominantly presented with medial thickening (Fig. 2A and B). In fibrotic areas, these arteries displayed more pronounced vascular remodeling, characterized by cellular intimal proliferation, and in some vessels, superimposed concentric intimal fibrosis (Fig. 2C and D). In preserved regions, small pulmonary arteries in CPFE-PH, COPD-PH, and IIP-PH also exhibited medial thickening, which appeared milder in COPD-PH, though without statistical significance. None of the three groups showed plexiform lesions. Within CPFE-PH lungs, small pulmonary arteries showed the most severe structural changes in fibrotic regions, compared with emphysematous (P = 0.011) and preserved (P = 0.001) areas. Assessment of pulmonary venules showed no significant differences between CPFE-PH and COPD-PH in emphysematous regions, between CPFE-PH and IIP-PH in fibrotic regions, or among the three groups in preserved lung regions (all P > 0.05; Fig. 2E and F; Table 3). Nevertheless, venular luminal narrowing in preserved areas was observed in more than half of CPFE-PH lungs (57.1%), compared with none of the COPD-PH lungs and 33.3% of the IIP-PH lungs, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.167).

Capillary pathology demonstrated marked disease-specific variation (Table 3). All CPFE-PH specimens displayed diffuse capillary hemangiomatosis-like lesions (7/7, 100%), most prominent in preserved parenchymal regions and characterized by dense alveolar capillary proliferation (Fig. 2G and H). These lesions were entirely absent in COPD-PH (0/3) and in IIP-PH were limited to rare, patchy foci of capillary hemangiomatosis-like changes in a single patient (1/6, 16.7%). The affected areas in IIP-PH showed only mild to moderate capillary dilation and were confined to small, localized regions, in contrast to the extensive, confluent involvement observed in CPFE-PH (Figure S3A-B).

Limited effects of PAH therapy

Due to marked hemodynamic impairment, 81.4% of CPFE-PH patients received PAH-targeted therapy, predominantly as monotherapy (61.9%) with fewer on combination regimens (19.5%), compared with 41.9% of COPD-PH and 46.2% of IIP-PH patients (Table 1). At a median follow-up of 6 months (IQR 4–14), CPFE-PH patients demonstrated substantial clinical deterioration, with 6MWD dropping from 273.0 m (177.5-322.5) to 90.0 m (50.0-290.0, P = 0.003) and NT-proBNP rising from 722.3 pg/mL (234.9–2484.0) to 2162.0 pg/mL (339.8-5171.5, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A-B). No improvements were observed in arterial blood gases (Fig. 3C-D). Functional recovery remained poor, with over 90% of patients in WHO-FC Ⅲ (67.6%) or Ⅳ (23.9%)(Fig. 3E). Patients with COPD-PH showed increased NT-proBNP (819.6 pg/mL to 1529.0 pg/mL, P = 0.017). In the IIP-PH group, a slightly decline in NT-proBNP (599.2 pg/mL to 397.0 pg/mL, P = 0.422) was observed. No significant changes in 6MWD, arterial blood gases, or WHO-FC were observed in COPD-PH or IIP-PH groups. At baseline and follow-up, there were no significant intergroup differences among CPFE-PH, COPD-PH, and IIP-PH in NT-proBNP, 6MWD, PaCO₂, or PaO₂ (all P > 0.05, Fig. 3A-D). No clinically significant pulmonary edema was observed during follow-up among patients with PCH-like lesions in CPFE-PH and IIP-PH groups.

Adverse long-term outcomes

Over a median follow‑up of 25 months (IQR 10–57), CPFE‑PH patients experienced high event rates, with 55 (48.7%) deaths, 10 (8.8%) lung transplantations, and 8 (8.5%) lost to follow‑up. TFS for CPFE‑PH was 62%, 41%, and 29% at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively, compared with 85%, 71%, and 58% for COPD‑PH, and 68%, 55%, and 45% for IIP‑PH. Median TFS was significantly shorter in CPFE‑PH (32.0 months) than COPD‑PH (87.0 months, log‑rank P < 0.001) or IIP‑PH (57.0 months, log‑rank P = 0.021) (Fig. 4A).

Similarly, overall survival at 1, 3, and 5 years was 66%, 45%, and 33% in CPFE-PH, markedly lower than in COPD-PH (86%, 71%, 58%) and IIP-PH (74%, 60%, 48%). Median survival was 34.0 months for CPFE-PH, significantly less than 87.0 months for COPD-PH (log‑rank P < 0.001) and 57.0 months for IIP-PH (log‑rank P = 0.016) (Fig. 4B).

In multivariable Cox analyses adjusted for age and sex, CPFE-PH conferred the highest risk of all-cause mortality, a 2.2-fold increase compared with COPD-PH [hazard ratio (HR) 0.454, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.285–0.575, P < 0.001] and 1.6-fold increase versus IIP-PH (HR 0.615, 95% CI 0.385–0.983, P = 0.042) (Fig. 4C). This hierarchy persisted for the composite endpoint of death or transplantation, with CPFE-PH carrying a 2.9-fold higher risk than COPD-PH (HR 2.85, 95% CI 2.05–3.51, P < 0.001) and a 1.7-fold higher risk than IIP-PH (HR 1.67, 95% CI 1.11–2.15, P = 0.019) (Fig. 4D).

Discussion

In this large, multicenter cohort with RHC-confirmed PH, we identified CPFE-PH as a unique form of Group 3 PH, characterized by a distinct capillary hemangiomatosis-like vascular phenotype. This pathological signature provides a unifying explanation for several clinical attributes of CPFE-PH, including severe hemodynamic impairment, preserved lung volumes despite markedly reduced DLCO, frequent mediastinal lymphadenopathy, suboptimal response to PAH-targeted therapies, and poor prognosis.

An intriguing finding in this substantial pathological series of CPFE-PH was the presence of distinctive vascular changes. In all seven CPFE-PH lung specimens, we consistently observed alveolar capillary multiplication and capillary hemangiomatosis-like lesions. Although capillary proliferation has previously been reported in an isolated case of CPFE-PH by Sato et al., such findings have not been systematically characterized [21]. While the severity of arterial remodeling, as assessed by Heath-Edwards scores, did not differ significantly across emphysematous, fibrotic, or preserved regions among the three groups, CPFE-PH appeared to exhibit pronounced vascular alterations in preserved lung areas. Consistently, Awano et al. reported that vascular remodeling was most severe in preserved regions of CPFE lungs [22]. Venous-venular involvement has also been described. Awano et al. showed prominent venous-venular remodeling in preserved regions of CPFE [22]. Colombat et al. reported venous and venular remodeling accompanied by focal alveolar capillary multiplication in IPF [23]. These observations have raised the possibility that capillary proliferation may occur, at least in part, as a consequence of impaired venous drainage. In our study, venular luminal narrowing in preserved lung regions was more frequently observed in CPFE-PH than in COPD-PH or IIP-PH, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, capillary hemangiomatosis-like lesions were consistently present in CPFE-PH but were absent in COPD-PH and only rarely observed in IIP-PH. These findings suggest that, while venular remodeling may contribute to pulmonary vascular pathology in CPFE-PH, it is unlikely to fully explain the striking predominance of capillary lesions. Together, these observations support that capillary remodeling may represent a particularly distinctive vascular feature of CPFE-PH. Nevertheless, given the limited pathological sample size, the mechanistic relationship between venular remodeling and capillary hemangiomatosis-like lesions remains speculative and warrants further investigation.

These combined vascular abnormalities may underlie the profound hemodynamic impairment characteristic of CPFE-PH, as shown in our study and supported by previous reports [22]. Chronic lung diseases at rest typically induce only mild-to-moderate pulmonary hypertension. COPD patients in GOLD Ⅱ–Ⅲ exhibit a mPAP of 25–34 mmHg (with only 3–5% of GOLD Ⅳ cases exceeding 35–40 mmHg) [1, 2, 24–26]. Similarly, ILD-PH registries report a baseline median mPAP of 39 mmHg and PVR of 7.6 WU, with 25% of patients having CPFE, whereas isolated IPF shows mPAP > 40 mmHg in only 9% of cases [27, 28]. In our COPD-PH and IIP-PH cohorts, the median mPAP was approximately 30 mmHg. In contrast, CPFE-PH patients showed significantly more severe hemodynamic impairment, with three-quarters meeting ESC/ERS criteria for severe pre-capillary PH.

Apart from severe hemodynamic impairment, our CPFE-PH patients exhibited a functional profile remarkably similar to that observed in PCH, distinguished by relatively preserved lung volumes in the presence of profoundly reduced DLCO [29, 30]. This pattern points to a primary defect in alveolar-capillary gas transfer [31]. Typically, DLCO can be reduced by alveolar-capillary membrane thickening, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, or loss of capillary surface area. In CPFE-PH patients described above, the median DLCO was only 31.6% of predicted, and this marked reduction strongly correlated with the histological presence of capillary hemangiomatosis-like lesions.

Extending the pathological insight to thoracic imaging, we found that mediastinal lymphadenopathy and bilateral, patchy ground-glass opacities were more common in CPFE-PH patients than in those with COPD-PH or IIP-PH. Ground-glass opacities represent a nonspecific radiological pattern encountered in a range of conditions, including ILD, infections, lymphoproliferative disorders, and PVOD/PCH [32, 33]. In our cohort, these ground-glass opacities were predominantly identified within lung regions that appeared relatively preserved on imaging, rather than within areas of advanced fibrosis or emphysematous destruction. Histologically, such regions were characterized by marked interstitial thickening accompanied by diffuse capillary proliferation and capillary hemangiomatosis-like changes. Importantly, the diffuse nature of capillary remodeling observed on pathology suggests a widespread microvascular process affecting preserved parenchyma throughout the lung, of which ground-glass opacities likely represent only a radiologically detectable manifestation rather than a spatially confined lesion. The presence of mediastinal lymphadenopathy, another common radiographic feature in CPFE-PH, may reflect lymphatic congestion secondary to vascular remodeling and chronic inflammation. Notably, mediastinal lymphadenopathy is a hallmark imaging finding in PVOD/PCH, further supporting the radiological and histopathological overlap between these entities and CPFE-PH.

To date, effective treatment strategies for CPFE-PH remain unclear, and evidence-based guidance is limited. Our findings, along with previous reports by Cottin et al. and Tanabe et al., has shown that PAH-targeted therapies fail to provide significant clinical benefits for this population [4, 34]. Although inhaled treprostinil has emerged as a promising agent in ILD-PH, no improvement in treatment response was observed in patients with CPFE [35, 36]. The limited efficacy of treprostinil in CPFE-PH likely reflects its distinct pulmonary pathology characterized by coexisting emphysema, fibrosis, and extensive pulmonary arterial remodeling, capillary hemangiomatosis-like changes.

Our study demonstrates that patients with CPFE-PH have significantly worse outcomes than those with COPD-PH or IIP-PH, including lower survival and TFS rates and a median overall survival of just 34 months. Multivariable analysis confirms a higher risk of death or transplantation in this group. These results are in line with previous studies and highlight the persistent challenge of managing CPFE-PH [37, 38]. Pathologically, the poor prognosis may be partly related to alveolar capillary proliferation similar to PCH, rather than the typical vascular remodeling seen in other types of Group 3 PH.

This study has several limitations. First, our study was descriptive and did not assess therapeutic efficacy through a prospective, randomized controlled trial. Second, we were unable to quantify or grade the extent of capillary hemangiomatosis-like lesions owing to the lack of established histopathological criteria. Finally, although we proposed that CPFE-PH resembles a capillary hemangiomatosis-like subtype, the lack of genetic testing prevents confirmation of whether specific pathogenic mutations underlie this phenotype.

Conclusions

Patients with CPFE-PH display a distinct clinical phenotype defined by a history of heavy smoking, profoundly reduced diffusing capacity, severe hemodynamic compromise, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, and diffuse capillary hemangioma-like lesions on histopathology, all of which portend a poor prognosis. These features align CPFE-PH more closely with a capillary hemangiomatosis-like phenotype and support its recognition as a unique entity rather than a conventional Group 3 PH subtype. Further studies incorporating comprehensive genomic profiling of CPFE-PH cohorts are imperative to uncover the molecular mechanisms driving this distinctive phenotype.
