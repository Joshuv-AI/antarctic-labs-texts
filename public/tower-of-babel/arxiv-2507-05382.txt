An inexact inertial projective splitting algorithm with strong convergence
M. Marques Alves, J. E. Navarro Caballero, R. T. Marcavillaca

Abstract
We propose and study a strongly convergent inexact inertial projective splitting (PS) algorithm for finding zeros of composite monotone inclusion problems involving the sum of finitely many maximal monotone operators. Strong convergence of the iterates is ensured by projections onto the intersection of appropriately defined half-spaces, even in the absence of inertial effects. We also establish iteration-complexity results for the proposed PS method, which likewise hold without requiring inertial terms. The algorithm includes two inertial sequences, controlled by parameters satisfying mild conditions, while preserving strong convergence and enabling iteration-complexity analysis. Furthermore, for more structured monotone inclusion problems, we derive two variants of the main algorithm that employ forward-backward and forward-backward-forward steps. 2000 Mathematics Subject Classification: 47H05, 49M27, 90C33. Key words: Projective splitting, inertial algorithms, strong convergence, forward-backward methods.

Introduction

The projective splitting (PS) algorithm was originally proposed by Eckstein and Svaiter in
[
19
,
20
]
and has since been studied and extended in various directions, including:
(i) PS variants for solving coupled and more general forms of monotone
inclusions
[
1
,
2
,
14
,
15
]
;
(ii) asynchronous and block-iterative implementations
[
17
]
;
(iii) modifications incorporating inertial effects in the iteration process
[
8
,
18
,
26
]
;
(iv) PS-type algorithms with forward and forward-backward steps for subproblems
[
23
,
24
]
;
(v) a proximal-Newton-type PS algorithm for solving inclusions with smooth operators
[
5
]
; and
(vi) the analysis of convergence rates and iteration-complexity
[
22
,
27
]
.

The PS method is designed to numerically solve multi-term (finite-sum) monotone inclusion problems involving finitely many maximal monotone maps, as well as compositions with bounded linear operators (see problem (
7
) below). The main idea is to embed (
7
) into an enlarged product space, reformulating the monotone inclusion problem as a convex feasibility problem that seeks a point in an extended-solution set (see (
9
)). Given the feasibility problem, cutting planes are constructed using affine
functionals (see (
11
) and (
12
) below) derived from
elements in the graph of (the enlargements of) the maximal monotone operators associated with the original problem (
7
).
The new framework is more flexible than existing operator splitting methods and demonstrates promising numerical performance in certain
applications (see, e.g.
[
24
]
).

It is well known that proximal algorithms typically converge weakly (see, e.g.,
[
21
,
31
]
), requiring modifications in the iteration mechanism to achieve strong convergence of iterates (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
13
]
). In the context of modern inexact relative-error proximal algorithms
[
28
,
32
]
, this approach was first introduced in
[
33
]
, where an inexact proximal-type algorithm with strong convergence guarantees for monotone inclusions was proposed.

In this paper, we propose and analyze an inertial inexact PS algorithm with strong convergence guarantees.
Our new PS variant (see Algorithm
1
below) maintains essentially all attractive properties of
previous versions while ensuring strong convergence of iterates to the best approximation of the initial guess in the extended-solution set. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time in the literature that an inertial inexact PS-type algorithm with strong convergence has been proposed. Furthermore, building on Algorithm
1
, we introduce two variants that incorporate forward-backward and forward-backward-forward (Tseng) steps for subproblems (see Algorithms
2
and
3
).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the general notation used in this paper and present some basic results. In Section
3
, we propose and analyze our main algorithm, namely Algorithm
1
. In Section
4
, we introduce two variants of Algorithm
1
by incorporating forward-backward and forward-backward-forward (Tseng) steps for subproblems.
Section
5
is devoted to concluding remarks.
In Appendix
A
, we prove that Algorithm
1
is well-defined, and in Appendix
B

Next we make some remarks and comments about Algorithm
1
:

(i)

Algorithm
1
operates on the augmented space
\bm{{\mathcal{H}}}={\mathcal{H}}_{0}\times{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{H}}_{n-1}
; recall that the extended-solution set
{\mathcal{S}}_{e}
is a subset of
\bm{{\mathcal{H}}}
and our main problem (
7
) is equivalent to the (convex feasibility) problem of finding a point
p=(z,w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1})
in
{\mathcal{S}}_{e}
. Also, recall that, by
Lemma
3.1
(b)
, the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{e}
is a subset of the “negative” half-space defined by each affine functional
\varphi_{k}(\cdot)
(see the definition of
H_{k}
as in (
23
)). This explains the “projective” nature of Algorithm
1
, where the iteration is defined, as in (
24
), by successive projections on the intersection of the half-spaces
H_{k}
and
W_{k}
. The main role of the latter half-space is to enforce the strong convergence of
the sequences generated Algorithm
1
(see Theorem
3.13
below).

(ii)

The extrapolation steps defined in (
17
)–(
19
) promote
inertial effects
on the iterations; these inertial effects are controlled by the parameters
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
.
We note that while the first extrapolation represented by
\widehat{z}^{k}
and
\widehat{w}_{i}^{k}
(
i=1,\dots,n-1
)
corresponds to the more typical type of extrapolation present in many inertial-based algorithms
(see, e.g.,
[
3
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
), the second type

\widetilde{z}^{k}
and
\widetilde{w}_{i}^{k}
(
i=1,\dots,n
) is introduced by considering the particular iteration mechanism of Algorithm 1.
Conditions on sequences
\left(\alpha_{k}\right)
and
\left(\beta_{k}\right)
to ensure the strong convergence and iteration-complexity of Algorithm
1
will be given in Assumption
3.9

(see also Assumption
3.6
).

(iii)

At each iteration, Algorithm
1
demands the approximate solution of
n
prox-subproblems, as in (
20
). These subproblems can be independently solved in parallel and inexactly within a relative-error criterion that is controlled by the parameter
\sigma\in[0,1)
. Note that if
\sigma=0
, then, by
(
20
), we have
\varepsilon_{i}^{k}=0
and

\lambda_{i}^{k}y_{i}^{k}+x_{i}^{k}-(G_{i}\widetilde{z}^{\,k}+\lambda_{i}^{k}\widetilde{w}_{i}^{\,k})=0
for
i=1,\dots,n
. So,
y_{i}^{k}\in T_{i}(x_{i}^{k})
and

\lambda_{i}^{k}y_{i}^{k}+x_{i}^{k}=G_{i}\widetilde{z}^{\,k}+\lambda_{i}^{k}\widetilde{w}_{i}^{\,k}

for all
i=1,\dots,n
(recall that
T^{[0]}=T
). This means that, for a given
\lambda_{i}^{k}>0
, the triple

(x_{i}^{k},y_{i}^{k},\varepsilon_{i}^{k})
defined as

\displaystyle x_{i}^{k}\coloneqq G_{i}\widetilde{z}^{\,k}+\lambda_{i}^{k}\widetilde{w}_{i}^{\,k}-\lambda_{i}^{k}y_{i}^{k},\qquad y_{i}^{k}\coloneqq(\lambda_{i}^{k}T_{i}+I)^{-1}(G_{i}\widetilde{z}^{\,k}+\lambda_{i}^{k}\widetilde{w}_{i}^{\,k}),\qquad\varepsilon_{i}^{k}\coloneqq 0

(31)

always satisfies the error criterion (
20
). The main limitation of (
31
) is the computation of the exact resolvent
(\lambda_{i}^{k}T_{i}+I)^{-1}
of each maximal monotone operator
T_{i}
, which may be
expensive from a computational viewpoint. The relative-error criterion (
20
) overcomes this limitation by allowing the computation of inexact solutions
(x_{i}^{k},y_{i}^{k},\varepsilon_{i}^{k})
, which in practical situations can be achieved by some other “inner solver”, depending on the specific structure of
T_{i}
(the inequality in
(
20
) being used as a stopping criterion for the “inner solver”);
see
[
7
]
for a practical implementation of a similar relative-error criterion in the context of the ADMM splitting algorithm).
We also mention that (
20
) is a generalization of a relative-error criterion for an inertial PS algorithm that appeared in
[
8
]
.
In Section
4
, we will show how Algorithm
1
can be used as a framework for the design of new operator splitting methods, where a triple
(x_{i}^{k},y_{i}^{k},\varepsilon_{i}^{k})
satisfying (
20
) can be computed directly by forward-backward/forward-backward-forward (Tseng) steps, under assumptions of cocoercivity/Lipschitz continuity on the operator
T_{i}
.

(iv)

In Corollary
3.3
, we will show that if Algorithm
1

stops (see (
21
)), then it follows that
(\overline{z},\overline{w}_{1},\dots,\overline{w}_{n-1})
belongs to the extended-solution set
{\mathcal{S}}_{e}
; subsequently, in order to analyze the convergence of Algorithm
1
, we will assume that it generates “infinite” sequences (see Remark
3.4
below).

(v)

The definition of the next iterate
(z^{k+1},w_{1}^{k+1},\dots,w_{n-1}^{k+1})
as in (
24
) by the orthogonal projection of
p^{0}
onto the intersection
H_{k}\cap W_{k}
of the half-spaces

and
W_{k}
is one of the main ingredients to guarantee the strong convergence of Algorithm

(note that

is a half-space in
\bm{{\mathcal{H}}}
by Lemma

(a)
). In the context of inexact relative-error proximal-point methods, this idea traces back to
[

and was also recently
exploited in
[

]
(in the context of inertial methods). The computation of the orthogonal projection
in (

linear
system (see
[

and
[

(vi)

It is worth mentioning here that the implementation as well as the guarantees of convergence of Algorithm

are independent of the computation of norms and inverses of the bounded linear operators

(1\leq i\leq n)

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed and analyzed an inertial inexact projective splitting (PS) algorithm with strong convergence guarantees for solving multi-term monotone inclusion problems. The proposed framework offers greater flexibility while preserving key theoretical properties. Our main contribution lies in establishing the strong convergence of the iterates to the best approximation of the initial guess in the extended-solution set. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first instance of an inertial (relative-error) inexact PS-type algorithm with such a guarantee. Additionally, we introduced two algorithmic variants incorporating forward-backward and forward-backward-forward (Tseng) steps, further expanding the applicability of the PS method.
Inertial methods, which trace back to the celebrated heavy-ball and Nesterov’s accelerated gradient algorithms, have gained significant attention in the context of proximal-type algorithms since the seminal work
[
3
]
. As the introduction of inertial variants of known methods is often associated with improved convergence behavior in practice (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
), we believe that the inertial algorithms proposed in this paper have strong potential to outperform their non-inertial counterparts in terms of practical efficiency.

A\colon{\mathcal{H}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{H}}
and
B\colon{\mathcal{G}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{G}}
be maximal monotone operators and let
G\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
be a bounded linear operator.
Assume that
\left((r^{k},a^{k})\right)
and
\left((s^{k},b^{k})\right)
are sequences in
\Gra A^{[\varepsilon^{a,k}]}
and

\Gra B^{[\varepsilon^{b,k}]}
, respectively, such that
r^{k}\rightharpoonup\overline{r}\in{\mathcal{H}}
and

b^{k}\rightharpoonup\overline{b}\in{\mathcal{G}}
, where
\varepsilon^{a,k}\to 0
and
\varepsilon^{b,k}\to 0
.
If
Gr^{k}-s^{k}\to 0
and
a^{k}+G^{\ast}b^{k}\to 0
, then

(\overline{r},-G^{\ast}\overline{b})\in\Gra A
and
(G\overline{r},\overline{b})\in\Gra B
.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
B.1
(in the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{G}}
). For this, let

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &T\coloneqq A\times B:{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{G}},\quad T(z,v)=A(z)\times B(v)\quad\mbox{and}\\[5.69054pt]
&V\coloneqq\{(z,v)\in{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\mid v=Gz\}.\end{aligned}

(82)

Note that
T
is maximal monotone, and
V
is a closed linear subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{G}}
. Using the assumptions on the sequences
\left((r^{k},a^{k})\right)
,
\left((s^{k},b^{k})\right)
, the definition of
T(\cdot)
as in (
82
) and Lemma

2.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &(x^{k},u^{k})\in\Gra T^{[\varepsilon^{k}]}\quad\mbox{for all}\quad k\geq 0\quad\mbox{where}\\[5.69054pt]
&x^{k}\coloneqq(r^{k},s^{k}),\quad u^{k}\coloneqq(a^{k},b^{k})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\varepsilon^{k}\coloneqq\varepsilon^{a,k}+\varepsilon^{b,k}.\end{aligned}

(83)

Assume now that

\displaystyle Gr^{k}-s^{k}\to 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad a^{k}+G^{\ast}b^{k}\to 0.

(84)

Then, in view of the assumptions that

r^{k}\rightharpoonup\overline{r}
and
b^{k}\rightharpoonup\overline{b}
, and the (weak) continuity of the linear operators
G
and
G^{\ast}
,
we also have
s^{k}\rightharpoonup G\,\overline{r}
and
a^{k}\rightharpoonup-G^{\ast}\,\overline{b}
. Hence, also using the assumptions that
both
\left(\varepsilon^{a,k}\right)
and
\left(\varepsilon^{b,k}\right)
converge to zero, we find

\displaystyle x^{k}\rightharpoonup(\overline{r},G\,\overline{r})\eqqcolon\overline{x},\quad u^{k}\rightharpoonup(-G^{\ast}\overline{b},\overline{b})\eqqcolon\overline{u}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\varepsilon^{k}\to 0,

(85)

where
x^{k}
,
u^{k}
and
\varepsilon^{k}
are as in (
83
).

Now, let us check the conditions
x^{k}-P_{V}x^{k}\to 0
and

P_{V}u^{k}\to 0
. From
[
2
, Lemma 3.1]
we know that, for all
w=(z,v)\in{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathcal{G}}
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &P_{V}w=\left((I+G^{\ast}G)^{-1}(z+G^{\ast}v),G(I+G^{\ast}G)^{-1}(z+G^{\ast}v)\right),\\[5.69054pt]
&P_{V^{\perp}}w=\left(G^{\ast}(I+GG^{\ast}))^{-1}(Gz-v),-(I+GG^{\ast})^{-1}(Gz-v)\right),\end{aligned}

which in turn combined with (
84
) yields

\displaystyle P_{V^{\perp}}x^{k}\to 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{V}u^{k}\to 0,

(86)

where
x^{k}
and
u^{k}
are as in (
83
). Hence, from (
83
), (
85
), (
86
)
and Lemma
B.1
we conclude, in particular, that
(\overline{x},\overline{u})\in\Gra T
, i.e,

\overline{u}\in T(\overline{x})
, which gives – see (
85
) –

\displaystyle(-G^{\ast}\overline{b},\overline{b})\in(A\times B)(\overline{r},G\,\overline{r})

and so
-G^{\ast}\overline{b}\in A(\overline{r})
and
\overline{b}\in B(G\overline{r})
, concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

In the next corollary, we assume that
{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq n
) are real Hilbert spaces with
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}={\mathcal{H}}_{0}
.

Corollary B.3
.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
T_{i}\colon{\mathcal{H}}_{i}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
be a maximal monotone operator, let
\left((x_{i}^{k},y_{i}^{k})\right)
be a sequence in
\Gra T_{i}^{[\varepsilon_{i}^{k}]}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}^{k}\to 0
, and assume
x_{n}^{k}\rightharpoonup\overline{z}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and

y_{i}^{k}\rightharpoonup\overline{w}_{i}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{i}

(
1\leq i\leq n-1
)
.
Also, for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, let
G_{i}\colon{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
be a bounded linear operator.
If
G_{i}x_{n}^{k}-x_{i}^{k}\to 0

(
1\leq i\leq n-1
)
and
y_{n}^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\,G_{i}^{*}y_{i}^{k}\to 0
, then

\displaystyle\overline{w}_{i}\in T_{i}(G_{i}\overline{z}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n-1,\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\,G_{i}^{*}\overline{w}_{i}\in T_{n}(\overline{z}).

(87)

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}\coloneqq{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
,
{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathcal{H}}_{n-1}
(endowed with the usual (Cartesian) inner product) and define the maximal monotone operators
A\colon{\mathcal{H}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{H}}
and
B\colon{\mathcal{G}}\rightrightarrows{\mathcal{G}}
by

\displaystyle A\coloneqq T_{n},\qquad B\coloneqq T_{1}\times\dots\times T_{n-1}.

Also, define the bounded linear operator
G\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{G}}
by
G=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{n-1})
, i.e,

\displaystyle Gz\coloneqq(G_{1}z,\ldots,G_{n-1}z)\quad\mbox{for all}\quad z\in{\mathcal{H}}.

Now, define the sequences

\displaystyle a^{k}\coloneqq y_{n}^{k},\quad\varepsilon^{a,k}\coloneqq\varepsilon_{n}^{k},\quad r^{k}\coloneqq x_{n}^{k},

\displaystyle b^{k}\coloneqq(y_{1}^{k},\ldots,y_{n-1}^{k}),\quad\varepsilon^{b,k}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\,\varepsilon_{i}^{k},\quad s^{k}\coloneqq(x_{1}^{k},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{k}).

Using the above definitions and Lemma
2.3
, we conclude that
\left((r^{k},a^{k})\right)
and
\left((s^{k},b^{k})\right)
are sequences in
\Gra A^{[\varepsilon^{a,k}]}
and
\Gra B^{[\varepsilon^{b,k}]}
, respectively. Moreover, using the assumptions of the corollary we also have
\varepsilon^{a,k}\to 0
,

\varepsilon^{b,k}\to 0
and

\displaystyle r^{k}\rightharpoonup\overline{z}\eqqcolon\overline{r}\quad\mbox{and}\quad b^{k}\rightharpoonup(\overline{w}_{1},\ldots,\overline{w}_{n-1})\eqqcolon\overline{b}.

In order to apply Lemma
B.2
, it remains to verify the conditions
Gr^{k}-s^{k}\to 0
and
a^{k}+G^{\ast}b^{k}\to 0
. To this end, note first that

\displaystyle Gr^{k}-s^{k}

\displaystyle=(G_{1}r^{k},\ldots,G_{n-1}r^{k})-(x_{1}^{k},\ldots,x_{n-1}^{k})

\displaystyle=(G_{1}x_{n}^{k}-x_{1}^{k},\ldots,G_{n-1}x_{n}^{k}-x_{n-1}^{k}),

and so, by the assumptions of the corollary, we get
Gr^{k}-s^{k}\to 0
. Using the fact that

G^{\ast}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}G_{i}^{\ast}w_{i}
, for every
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-1})\in{\mathcal{G}}
, we also obtain

\displaystyle a^{k}+G^{\ast}b^{k}=y_{n}^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}G_{i}^{\ast}y_{i}^{k},

which then converges to zero, also in view of the assumption that
y_{n}^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}G_{i}^{\ast}y_{i}^{k}\to 0
.

Therefore, by Lemma
B.2
, we conclude that
(\overline{r},-G^{\ast}\overline{b})\in\Gra A
and
(G\overline{r},\overline{b})\in\Gra B
, which happens to be equivalent to (
87
).
∎
