Electrodeposition-initiated, self-catalyzed growth of 2D amorphous Fe-group metal–boron alloy mesoporous films
Authors: Lei Fu, Yunqing Kang, Ho Ngoc Nam, Yuqi Guo, Chengze Ji, Kaiteng Wang, Zilin Zhou, Zekai Chen, Quan Manh Phung, Le Shi, Kai Wu, Jun Zhou, Yusuke Yamauchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Two-dimensional mesoporous amorphous metallic films, particularly those based on earth-abundant Fe-group metals (Ni, Co, Fe), are highly promising for various applications due to their high surface area, structural isotropy, and abundant undercoordinated sites. However, synthesizing continuous Fe-group amorphous metallic films with well-defined mesoporosity under mild conditions remains a challenge. Here, we develop an electrodeposition-initiated, self-catalyzed electroless growth strategy, enabling the synthesis of composition-tunable (from binary to quaternary) amorphous Fe-group metal–boron (M–B) mesoporous films (MFs) on various conductive substrates. This approach decouples the nucleation and growth stages, allowing precise control over film thickness, composition, and mesostructure. Thickness-time measurements suggest a self-accelerating growth process, whereas finite-element simulations indicate that substrate-dependent electric-field distributions modulate the nucleation and growth morphology. The synthesized multimetallic amorphous Ni–Co–Fe–B MF demonstrates high oxygen evolution performance and durability in alkaline simulated seawater, making them promising candidates for practical applications. Theoretical insights reveal preferential OH− adsorption over Cl− in the amorphous architecture, which may help mitigate chloride adsorption and facilitate the key reaction step. This study provides a method for synthesizing noble-metal-free amorphous M–B MFs.

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) mesoporous metallic films combine high surface area, efficient mass transport, and excellent electrical conductivity, making them promising for catalysis, sensing, and electronic devices1. Amorphous metallic alloys with long-range structural disorder and abundant undercoordinated sites often outperform crystalline counterparts in chemical and physical performance2. In particular, the 2D amorphous state yields isotropy, a grain-boundary-free morphology, and a high density of dangling/unpaired electrons, all of which strongly determine material properties2,3. For example, earth-abundant Fe-group (Ni, Co, and Fe) amorphous films exhibit soft magnetic behavior, high electrical resistivity, low coercivity, and isotropic magnetic response, traits that suit magnetic devices4. These magnetic metallic characteristics makes such films valuable for studying magnetic effects in electrochemical catalysis5. Unfortunately, the synthesis of continuous, well-defined mesoporous Fe-group amorphous metallic films remains challenging, as conventional 2D analogs are typically hydroxides with metals in an oxidized state, instead of the desired metallic form6–9.

Although conventional physical deposition methods (e.g., magnetron sputtering)10 and chemical vapor deposition11 can be used to prepare amorphous magnetic films, these techniques offer limited control over nanostructures due to the high-energy growth environment. In contrast, electrodeposition is a simple and versatile chemical method that enables rapid thin-film fabrication under mild conditions, and when combined with polymer micelle porogens, mesoporosity can be successfully introduced12. However, most reported polymer-micelle-templated electrodeposion studies have focus on crystalline noble-metal systems13–16; even with a more negative deposition potential, the electrodeposition of Fe-group metals typically yields only dense, crystalline films without porosity17.

Another approach is electroless plating, which, unlike electroplating, does not require a continuous supply of electrons from an external direct current power source. Instead, it relies on a chemical reducing agent to provide electrons for the reduction of Fe-group metal ions, a process also known as autocatalytic plating18. During this process, heteroatoms, such as boron or phosphorus, can be incorporated into the metal lattice to modify the crystal structure (for example, by inducing amorphization) and influence the performance19. However, electroless plating requires precise control of processing parameters (e.g., pH, temperature, and complexing agents), and the resulting films are typically dense bulk layers without mesopore structures18,19. Such dense and non-mesoporous layers are not ideal when high surface area and fast mass transport are required for catalysis and functional surface modification20. Although hydrogen bubbles generated during electroless plating can create pores in the material and enhance its catalytic performance21, the resulting structures exhibit irregular porosity and limited compositional tunability. A general strategy for synthesizing 2D amorphous Fe-group metal-based alloy with well-defined mesoporosity and a wide range of compositions remains underdeveloped.

Our previous studies have shown that the rapid introduction of nuclei in solution can trigger the self-catalyzed growth of amorphous Fe-group metal–boron (M–B) materials22–25. Although the resulting structures were still mesoporous spherical particles, this approach provides a valuable conceptual basis for the preparation of the 2D films reported in the present work. Here, we develop an electrodeposition-initiated, self-catalyzed electroless growth strategy enabling the synthesis of amorphous noble-metal-free Fe-group M–B mesoporous films (MFs) on conductive substrates. We conducted experiments to probe the feasibility of the electrodeposition-initiated self-catalyzed process. It is revealed that an ultrathin nucleation layer, electrodeposited at a controlled potential, can spontaneously induce continuous growth of the amorphous M–B nanostructure after current interruption. This approach provides precise control over film thickness and composition, from binary to quaternary M–B amorphous alloys (Fig. 1a), by tailoring the metal precursors and self-catalyzed growth time. In addition, the film growth pathway was found to be modulated by the surface electric field distribution of various conductive substrates. Results

Probing the synthesis of amorphous MFs

We began by exploring the synthesis of amorphous Fe-group M–B MFs on a gold–titanium (Au–Ti)-coated silicon (Si) substrate, which was selected because its smooth and clean surface allows for clear observation of the entire synthesis process. Our experimental framework involves three main steps (Fig. 1b): (1) preparation of the precursor reaction solution. The block copolymer polystyrene-block-poly(ethylene oxide) (PS-b-PEO) served as a pore-directing agent and was dissolved in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) to form a transparent solution. The addition of an aqueous solution containing deionized water, Fe-group metal ions, tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBAB), and dimethylamine borane ((CH3)2NH·BH3, DMAB) triggered micellization (Supplementary Fig. 1); (2) formation of thin-layer nuclei through a dynamic linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) electrodeposition process (Supplementary Video 1). In the electrodeposition, metal-ion-decorated micelles migrated to the working electrode, where the metal species were reduced and gradually grew into a metallic nucleation layer; (3) after the power supply was turned off, the generated nuclei continuously catalyzed the decomposition of DMAB, which served as both the reducing agent and the B source, leading to the formation of an amorphous phase (Supplementary Video 2). Subsequent removal of the polymer template (PS-b-PEO) yielded the final product. This highly effective soft-template strategy for creating mesoporous architectures has been well-demonstrated in previous works by Qiao and co-workers26,27. This electrodeposition-initiated, self-catalyzed method also allows the growth of a-Ni–B, a-Co–B, a-Ni–Co–B, a-Ni–Fe–B, a-Co–Fe–B, and a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MFs with desired compositions (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figs. 2–7).

Next, we characterized the as-obtained samples using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). A top-view SEM image in Fig. 1c reveals a uniform distribution of mesopores (~10 nm in diameter) across the typical a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF (Supplementary Fig. 8), which is similar to the recent work reported by our group22. The internal porous structure of the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B was evaluated by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), which exhibits a distinct scattering peak at q = 0.303 nm–1, corresponding to a pore-to-pore distance of ~21 nm (Supplementary Fig. 9)28. Further microstructural analysis was conducted using focused ion beam (FIB)-processed cross-sectional TEM specimens. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, the TEM image reveals a uniform film thickness with mesoporosity throughout the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B film, consistent with the SAXS result. A high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF shows no discernible lattice fringes, indicating a short-range ordered yet long-range disordered atomic arrangement (Fig. 1d)24. In addition, the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern confirms its amorphous nature (Supplementary Fig. 10b)29. TEM energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mappings show the distribution of Ni, Co, and Fe (Fig. 1e). Ni enrichment occurs near the Au interface, corresponding to the electrodeposition-dominated zone, whereas chemically reduced regions display uniform Ni/Co/Fe distribution with Co enrichment, which reflects differences in the kinetics of metal growth by different reduction processes. It should be noted that although B cannot be accurately detected by EDS due to its low atomic weight, its initial presence can be confirmed by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) analysis and subsequent X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements. The atomic ratio of Ni:Co:Fe:B in a typical a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF is 10.5:51.1:12.1:26.3, determined by ICP-OES (Supplementary Table 1).

Growth process and mechanistic study

We found that the nucleation and growth kinetics of the amorphous MFs vary with the composition of the amorphous alloy (i.e., a-Co–Fe–B, a-Ni–Fe–B, a-Ni–Co–Fe–B), although all of them exhibit amorphous structures, as determined by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. 2a). Different feeding metal compositions were selected to further investigate the kinetic growth process. As shown in Fig. 2b, a distinct reduction peak appears at –0.93 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) for a-Co–Fe–B, indicating the nucleation of the metal film on the substrate. The reduction peaks for a-Ni–Fe–B (−0.65 V vs. Ag/AgCl) and a-Ni–Co–Fe–B (−0.69 V vs. Ag/AgCl) occur at more positive potentials compared to a-Co–Fe–B, suggesting that Ni is preferentially deposited over Co during the electrodeposition process. This might be due to the more positive reduction potentials of Ni2+/Ni (E0 = −0.24 V vs. SHE) compared with Co2+/Co (E0 = −0.28 V vs. SHE, Fig. 1a). Notably, subsequent LSV curves exhibit redox peaks, further confirming the successful deposition of metals on the substrate (Supplementary Fig. 11). An SEM image shows that the initial metal alloy film features a mesoporous structure with randomly distributed spherical pores (Supplementary Fig. 12a). EDS analysis of the initially electrodeposited a-Ni–Co–Fe–B film reveals a uniform distribution of Ni, Co, and Fe. After electrodeposition nucleation, the working electrode was left undisturbed for varying durations to allow further alloy growth via electroless chemical reduction. In this self-catalyzed process, DMAB plays two crucial roles. First, DMAB serves as the reducing agent in this process, continuously undergoing hydrolysis catalyzed by the preformed Fe-group M–B nuclei. In the absence of such nuclei, the mixed metal ion solution remains stable, and DMAB cannot decompose at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 14). Therefore, the nuclei indeed act as the active sites for the subsequent DMAB decomposition, where the reduction of metal cations is coupled with the B incorporation into the alloy. This process ensures that the subsequent amorphous film grows exclusively at the preformed nucleation sites on the substrate, rather than in the surrounding solution (Supplementary Video 2). Although the decomposition of DMAB is accompanied by the formation of hydrogen bubbles, these bubbles quickly detach from the electrode surface, minimizing their impact on the mesoporous structure. However, it should be noted that as the M–B alloy continues to grow, the gradual increase in film thickness disrupt the deposition of the metal onto the original substrate, causing a flocculent precipitate to form in the solution and ultimately turning it black (Supplementary Fig. 15). Second, DMAB also serves as the B source, which incorporates into the Ni/Co/Fe lattice, leading to the formation of an amorphous structure30,31. In the absence of DMAB, although a continuous electron supply from an electrochemical workstation can still drive the deposition of Ni, Co, or Fe, the resulting structure is crystalline rather than amorphous (Supplementary Fig. 16). Additionally, the elemental ratio in the amorphous alloy can be tuned by adjusting the feeding amounts of the metal precursors (Supplementary Figs. 17–20 and Supplementary Table 2).

To further investigate the growth process of the amorphous films, time-dependent structural characterizations were performed (Fig. 2c, d). Time-dependent EDS analysis of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B showed continued uniform elemental distribution, and prolonged time of chemical reduction ultimately yields stable Ni/Co/Fe ratios (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 12). Specifically, the initial metal nucleation layer formed by electrodeposition exhibits a distinct Ni-enriched layer at the interface close to the substrate, implying that the electrodeposition kinetics favor the reduction of Ni due to the more positive redox potential of Ni. Further, the self-catalyzed process shows a preference for the reduction of Co, resulting in a high Co concentration in the final metal alloy film, which is consistent with the TEM-EDS mapping results. The mesoporous structure of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF is established during the initial nucleation and is maintained throughout the subsequent electroless growth, as confirmed by top-view and cross-section SEM images (Fig. 2d) and atomic force microscope (AFM) images. In the electrodeposition-initiated nucleation stage, AFM images demonstrate the film thickness increases from 3.8 to 29.7 nm as the number of LSV cycles increases (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 21). During the self-catalyzed growth process, surface AFM images show flat, porous films of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF with an average surface roughness below 2 nm, indicating highly uniform, conformal coverage despite the presence of pore channels (Supplementary Fig. 22). The preferential growth of Ni at the early stage (Fig. 2c) may be attributed to its slightly more positive redox potential compared to Co. It should be noted that the subsequent decomposition of DMAB is metal-composition-dependent (Supplementary Fig. 23), as the different metals exhibit varying abilities to catalyze DMAB hydrolysis32,33. Specifically, the Co–Fe composition exhibits higher catalytic activity for DMAB decomposition, resulting in a thicker film, whereas the Ni–Fe composition displays a relatively slower decomposition rate. Typically, the thickness of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MFs can be tuned by controlling the growth time, ranging from ~29.7 nm at 0 min to ~367.5 nm at 180 min (Fig. 2d). Amorphous MFs grown on various substrates

Having established the growth mechanism on Au–Ti-coated Si, we generalized the growth behavior of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B on a family of conductive substrates, including Cu foil, indium-tin oxide (ITO) glass, nickel foam, carbon cloth, and carbon paper. Smooth, planar substrates like Au–Ti-coated Si (Figs. 1c and 2d), Cu foil (Fig. 3a), and ITO glass (Fig. 3b) yield uniform, conformal MFs with homogeneous pore distributions across large areas, following the Frank–Van der Merwe (i.e., layer-by-layer) growth mechanism (Fig. 3f). In contrast, deposition on three-dimensional or fibrous conductive frameworks, such as nickel foam (Fig. 3c), carbon cloth (Fig. 3d), and carbon paper (Fig. 3e), results in partially continuous, nanoparticle-stacked MFs. On rougher, curved substrates like nickel foam, growth transitions to the Stranski–Krastanov mode (i.e., layer-plus-island), while on fibrous substrates (carbon cloth, carbon paper), the Volmer–Weber (i.e., island) mode dominates, with a-Ni–Co–Fe–B primarily forming islands that occasionally connect into film-like structures (Fig. 3f).

We selected carbon paper as the substrate to further confirm the island growth behavior. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 25, some tiny nanoparticles appear on the carbon paper after the initial electrodeposition. Subsequently, the nanoparticles gradually grow into a stacked film as the standing electroless plating time increases. The distribution of Ni, Co, and Fe on carbon paper was characterized by SEM-EDS (Supplementary Fig. 26). The a-Ni–Co–Fe–B mesoporous nanoparticles grown on carbon paper were ultrasonically detached for TEM observation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 27, the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B nanoparticle exhibits a diameter of ~400 nm with numerous mesopores penetrating from the surface to the core. The SAED pattern (Supplementary Fig. 27b) and HRTEM (Supplementary Fig. 27c) image also confirm the amorphous nature. TEM-EDS mapping images reveal the relative distribution of Ni, Co, and Fe (Supplementary Fig. 27d), consistent with the composition observed in the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF on Au–Ti-coated Si substrate.

We hypothesize that the observed variations in thin film growth stem from distinct local electric field distributions induced by different substrates during electrodeposition nucleation. To gain mechanistic insight, finite-element simulations of electric field distributions (Fig. 3g–i) were conducted. A uniform electric field across the Au–Ti-coated Si substrate (Fig. 3g) likely promotes homogeneous nucleation and lateral film growth. In contrast, nickel foam (Fig. 3h) and carbon paper (Fig. 3i) exhibit highly heterogeneous electric field intensities, concentrated at surface protrusions, pores, and fiber junctions, driving anisotropic ion reduction and preferential nucleation. Especially during the early stages of growth, the surface of nickel foam presents a coexistence of nanoparticles and uniform thin films (Supplementary Fig. 28), while the carbon paper surface consists primarily of nanoparticles. As the nanoparticles gradually grow, they eventually connect to form a uniform film structure (Supplementary Fig. 25). Therefore, the simulation results suggest a potential mechanism by which variations in electric field distributions lead to a shift from continuous to layer-plus-island or island-type growth (Fig. 3c–e), although further direct spatial evidence is needed to fully confirm the link to macro-uniformity. These findings highlight the versatility of this method for synthesizing amorphous mesoporous alloy films on various conductive substrates.

Electrocatalytic OER performance evaluation

Given that multimetallic alloys, including high-entropy alloys and medium-entropy alloys, have attracted considerable research interest for electrocatalysis owing to their excellent corrosion resistance, compositional tunability, and multielemental synergistic effects34, we evaluated the performance of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF in alkaline simulated seawater electrolysis. Seawater electrolysis is a promising route to sustainable hydrogen production because seawater constitutes ~96.5% of the planet’s water resources35. However, practical implementation is hindered by catalyst degradation of both the cathodic hydrogen evolution reaction and the anodic OER, primarily due to corrosion, chlorine-related side reactions, and metal precipitates36. To ensure chemical stability during electrocatalytic testing, the carbon-paper substrate was used in this study due to its chemical inertness.

First, the effects of deposition time, composition, and porous architecture of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B on OER activity were examined in O2-saturated 1 M KOH to select the optimal catalyst. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 29, the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B exhibits progressively enhanced OER activity with increasing deposition time. However, excessive deposition leads to diminished performance at high current densities, likely due to mass transport limitations caused by overloading37. The composition of these alloys was also optimized by varying the feed ratio of the metal precursors (Supplementary Fig. 30). In addition, a-Ni–Co–Fe–B exhibits enhanced OER compared with its non-porous counterpart (synthesized without using a pore-directing agent), confirming that mesoporosity is advantageous for improving the catalytic activity (Supplementary Fig. 31). The electrocatalytic activity of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B on nickel foam is similar to that on carbon paper (Supplementary Fig. 32). The a-Ni–Co–Fe–B catalyst also significantly outperforms the binder-free Ni–Co–Fe layered double hydroxide (LDH) synthesized by a conventional hydrothermal method, further demonstrating that the superior activity of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B originates from its properties.

Next, the optimized a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF is compared with its crystalline counterpart (i.e., c-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF) and commercial RuO2 for performance in both alkaline and alkaline simulated seawater conditions. While maintaining a similarly well-defined mesoporous structure, the crystallinity in c-Ni–Co–Fe–B is enhanced by controlling the calcination temperature of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B due to the temperature sensitivity of the amorphous phase (Supplementary Fig. 33). The crystalline structure of c-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF calcined at 350 °C is further confirmed by TEM analyses (Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35). Notably, the optimal a-Ni–Co–Fe–B requires an overpotential of 230 mV to achieve 10 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4a, b), outperforming the c-Ni–Co–Fe–B (253 mV) and commercial RuO2 (311 mV). Moreover, a-Ni–Co–Fe–B shows a low Tafel slope, suggesting superior reaction kinetics compared to the others (Supplementary Fig. 36). The double-layer capacitance (Cdl), which is proportional to the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA), was derived from cyclic voltammetry (CV) scans at various scan rates38. The a-Ni–Co–Fe–B and c-Ni–Co–Fe–B exhibit comparable Cdl values (Supplementary Fig. 37), both significantly higher than that of commercial RuO2, suggesting superior active site accessibility in the mesoporous structure. When tested in alkaline simulated seawater electrolyte (1 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl), a-Ni–Co–Fe–B still exhibits the best simulated seawater tolerance, with almost no increase in overpotential (Fig. 4a-b). In contrast, c-Ni–Co–Fe–B and RuO2 show increases of 3.1% and 33% in overpotential at a current density of 100 mA cm−2, respectively, compared with those measured in pure 1 M KOH without NaCl (Fig. 4b). The a-Ni–Co–Fe–B exhibits a lower Tafel slope and charge-transfer resistance than c-Ni–Co–Fe–B and RuO2, confirming its superior OER kinetics in simulated seawater (Supplementary Figs. 39, 40, and Supplementary Table 4). A high Faradaic efficiency of 98% for O2 generation was observed for the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 41).

Then, the stability was evaluated and compared with previously reported electrocatalysts and RuO2 (Fig. 4c–e). As shown in Fig. 4e, the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B demonstrates enhanced durability with a low degradation rate of 6 µV h−1 over 2000 h of continuous operation in 1 M KOH (Supplementary Table 5). In contrast, the commercial RuO2 shows a notable loss of activity within 300 h under identical conditions. Post-characterization SEM, XRD, and TEM images validate that the mesoporous structure of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B is well-preserved, with a few nanosheets observed on the surface after the stability test (Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43). The SAED pattern, HRTEM images, and XRD patterns further confirm that these nanosheets are in situ reconstructed oxides/hydroxides formed during the OER process40,41. XPS spectra after the stability test show the disappearance of the metallic state and B 1s peak, implying the surface reconstruction into metal hydroxides under oxidative conditions (Supplementary Fig. 44). The ICP-OES result clearly shows a significant concentration of leached B in the electrolyte, accompanied by low concentrations of Ni, Co, and Fe elements (Supplementary Fig. 45). Although surface B leaches into the electrolyte after the reaction, its assistance in forming the initial amorphous structure is beneficial for the subsequent electrochemical reconstruction42,43. Furthermore, a-Ni–Co–Fe–B remains stable at a high current density of 0.2 A cm−2 over 1000 h (Fig. 4e) despite potential Cl– corrosion, superior to RuO2. The morphology of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B is well maintained after the stability test, confirming the high resistance to anodic corrosion in the simulated seawater (Supplementary Fig. 46). The high OER performance in both 1 M KOH (Fig. 4c) and simulated seawater (Fig. 4d) of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B is also highly competitive among previously reported catalysts (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

Finally, to further evaluate the practical application feasibility of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B for overall seawater splitting, an anion exchange membrane (AEM) simulated seawater electrolyzer is assembled by employing a-Ni–Co–Fe–B (or RuO2) as the anode and commercial Pt black as the cathode catalyst (Fig. 4f). The polarization curves indicate that a-Ni–Co–Fe–B exhibits superior performance compared to commercial RuO2. Further, the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B delivers a cell voltage of 1.69 and 1.83 V to achieve a current density of 0.5 and 1.0 A cm−2 for the AEM simulated seawater electrolyzer at 80 °C, respectively (Fig. 4g). The estimated electricity consumption cost of this AEM electrolyzer is $0.91 per kilogram H244. The reversible and thermoneutral voltage efficiency were calculated as 72.8% and 87.6% at 0.5 A cm−2, respectively. Moreover, the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B can maintain stable operation at a current density of 0.5 A cm−2 for at least 100 h without noticeable performance decay (Fig. 4h). The excellent performance of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B also outperforms recently reported seawater-electrolysis catalysts and systems45–47, highlighting the promising application potential of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B for alkaline simulated-seawater splitting.

Theoretical calculations and mechanistic analysis

To elucidate the origin of the enhanced OER performance of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B, we conducted both experimental characterizations and theoretical analyses. The surface chemical states of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B and c-Ni–Co–Fe–B before OER were probed by XPS (Supplementary Fig. 47). The results show that both a-Ni–Co–Fe–B and c-Ni–Co–Fe–B exhibit metallic Ni/Co/Fe and their oxide species (Supplementary Fig. 47a–c). Compared to c-Ni–Co–Fe–B, the Ni 2p, Co 2p, and Fe 2p binding energies in a-Ni–Co–Fe–B show negative shifts, suggesting that a-Ni–Co–Fe–B may exhibit altered electronic interactions between the metal and boron. In addition, the B 1s spectrum of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B displays two distinct peaks at 187.9 eV and 192.0 eV, assigned to M–B and B–O bonds, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 47d). Upon annealing, the c-Ni–Co–Fe–B retains only the B–O feature, owing to the loss of relatively light B atoms during calcination48.

Indeed, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) was conducted to simulate the diffusion behavior of metal atoms under an annealing temperature. Figure 5a illustrates the probability density distribution of metal atoms as the diffusion trace in the system at 600 K, where B exhibits longer migration paths compared to other metals under the same conditions. The calculated time-average mean-square displacement also reveals the average distance that ions have moved from their original position (Fig. 5b). We observed that Ni and Co exhibit nearly identical diffusion trends with the lowest mobility. In contrast, Fe displays significantly higher mobility, which can be attributed to differences in its crystal structure. Although Ni, Co, and Fe have comparable atomic radii, Fe adopts a body-centered cubic structure in its pure form, which is less densely packed than the face-centered cubic (Ni) or hexagonal close-packed (Co) structures, offering more free volume for atomic movement. This structural difference might lead to lower activation energies and higher diffusivity for Fe compared to Ni and Co. Among all the elements, B atoms exhibit the highest diffusivity owing to their smaller atomic radius and lower atomic mass, enabling them to diffuse easily through interstitial spaces in the metallic matrix. Therefore, B tends to leak out from the system under annealing conditions, consistent with experimental observations. Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the OER mechanism and surface selectivity of the Ni–Co–Fe–B catalysts. Specifically, the OER is investigated via a four-electron transfer pathway, as depicted in Fig. 5c. This process involves the sequential adsorption of hydroxide ions and the formation of key intermediates (*OH, *O, and *OOH) on the active sites before the final release of molecular oxygen. To compare the activity of the amorphous (a-Ni–Co–Fe–B) and crystalline (c-Ni–Co–Fe–B) catalysts, the Gibbs free energy (ΔG) for each step of this mechanism was calculated at the equilibrium potential of U = 1.23 V vs. RHE across different surface models. Given the consistency of results across configurations, the discussion hereafter focuses on the first model (Fig. 5d), while the additional model is presented in Supplementary Fig. 48.

The free energy diagrams reveal that the amorphous surface binds all intermediates more strongly, as shown by more negative ΔG values. For both catalysts, the conversion of the adsorbed oxygen intermediate (*O) to the hydroperoxyl intermediate (*OOH) is the rate-determining step (RDS) of the overall reaction. However, the energy required for this step is vastly different between the two surfaces. The c-Ni–Co–Fe–B surface exhibits a ΔG of +2.77 eV, whereas a-Ni–Co–Fe–B requires a substantially lower ΔG of +2.05 eV. This significantly lower energy barrier for the RDS on the amorphous surface is due to the disproportionate stabilization of *OOH, indicating a much higher catalytic activity. Although this enhanced performance may partly arise from the properties of the amorphous structure, such as long-range disordered atomic arrangement, which creates a high density of low-coordination active sites, changes in the electronic properties of active sites are likely the primary reason. On both surfaces, calculations showed that the most crucial intermediate, *OOH, preferentially adsorbs on bimetallic bridge sites involving Co and Ni atoms. The catalytic efficiency is therefore dictated by the electronic structure of these Co–Ni sites. According to d-band theory, as the d-band center moves closer to the Fermi level (i.e., becomes more positive), the adsorption energy becomes more negative (stronger adsorption)49,50. On the amorphous surface, the d-band centers of Co (−1.396 eV) and Ni (−1.617 eV) are shifted closer to the Fermi level compared to their positions in the crystalline phase (Co: −1.428 eV; Ni: −1.733 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 49). This upward shift results in stronger adsorption of *OOH, consistent with its more negative ΔG value on a-Ni–Co–Fe–B (Fig. 5d).

To demonstrate the preferential selectivity of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B toward OH⁻ over Cl⁻, the Gibbs free energies of adsorption for both ions were evaluated at various active sites on two surface models representing the structures before and after reconstruction caused by B leaching (Fig. 5e, f). Although modest differences in adsorption energies are observed between the two models, both exhibit consistent selectivity trends: OH− adsorption is substantially more thermodynamically favorable across all tested sites, with ΔG values approximately 1 eV more negative than those for Cl−. This indicates a strong preference for binding the OH⁻ ions required for the OER pathway. By preferentially adsorbing OH⁻, the catalyst surface effectively limits the access of Cl⁻ ions to the active sites, thereby suppressing the undesirable and corrosive chlorine evolution reaction.

Discussion

We developed a general electrodeposition-initiated, self-catalyzed electroless growth strategy to prepare 2D amorphous Fe-group M–B MFs on various conductive substrates. A controlled ultrathin electrodeposited seed layer triggers DMAB decomposition (serving as both reductant and B source), decoupling precise nucleation from autocatalytic lateral growth and enabling polymer-templated mesoporosity, tunable thickness, and composition (from binary to quaternary). Substrate morphology and local electric fields steer growth modes from layer-by-layer on planar surfaces to island-type on the frameworks. The quaternary a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF delivers enhanced OER performance in alkaline and simulated seawater, with 230 mV overpotential at 10 mA cm−2, low Tafel slope, minimal charge-transfer resistance, and high stability (>2000 h in 1 M KOH; >1000 h at 0.2 A cm−2 in simulated seawater), outperforming crystalline analogs, RuO2, and some reported non-precious catalysts. In an AEM electrolyzer, it also achieves 1.69 V at 0.5 A cm−2 with high durability. Combined experimental and theoretical analyses suggest that the amorphous catalyst favors the adsorption of OH⁻ over Cl⁻, effectively suppressing corrosive chlorine evolution and leading to a lower energy barrier for the OER RDS. This work offers a versatile synthetic platform and mechanistic insights for the design of high-performance amorphous alloys, extending their application potential beyond efficient hydrogen production to encompass magnetic systems and coatings on various emerging materials, such as graphene, MXenes, conductive polymers, and semiconductors.

Methods

Materials

Nickel (II) acetate tetrahydrate (Ni(CH3COO)2·4H2O), cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate (Co(CH3COO)2·4H2O), anhydrous iron (III) chloride (FeCl3), TBAB, and DMAB were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The block copolymer PS5000-b-PEO2000 was obtained from Polymer Source. DMF and KOH were purchased from Nacalai Tesque.

Synthesis of a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MFs

The a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF was synthesized by a mild electrodeposition-triggered chemical reduction process. The precursor solution was prepared in advance. Typically, 10 mg of block copolymer PS5000-b-PEO2000 was dissolved in 3 mL DMF via sonication, followed by the addition of 3 mL deionized water to form a micellar solution. Precursor solutions containing 60 mM Co(CH3COO)2·4H2O, Ni(CH3COO)2·4H2O, and FeCl3 were prepared. To suppress the hydrolysis of FeCl3, the freshly prepared FeCl3 solution was continuously purged with argon. Subsequently, 1.0 mL Ni(CH3COO)2·4H2O, 2.2 mL Co(CH3COO)2·4H2O, and 0.8 mL FeCl3 solutions were mixed with 0.15 g TBAB until complete dissolution, then poured into the micellar solution. The metal ratio is controlled through the precise dosing of different metal salt precursors. Then, 6.0 mL of 0.5 M DMAB solution was introduced to form the electrolyte.

Electrodeposition was performed in a standard three-electrode system employing a Pt wire counter electrode, a saturated Ag/AgCl reference electrode, and an Au–Ti-coated Si substrate, Cu foil, ITO glass, nickel foam, carbon cloth, or carbon paper as the working electrode. LSV was conducted from −0.2 V to −2.0 V (vs Ag/AgCl) at 100 mV s−1 for 6 cycles with 1-min intervals between scans to minimize concentration gradients near the electrode. After electrodeposition, the system was maintained under a nitrogen atmosphere for 60, 120, 150, and 180 min to facilitate the subsequent electroless plating. The working electrode was then immersed in DMF to remove the copolymer template, yielding the a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF.

Synthesis of monometallic amorphous M–B (metal = Ni, Co) MFs

The a-Ni–B and a-Co–B MFs were synthesized using the same procedure as a-Ni–Co–Fe–B, except that the metal salt solutions were replaced with 4.0 mL of Ni(CH3COO)2·4H2O (60 mM) and 4.0 mL of Co(CH3COO)2·4H2O (60 mM) solution, respectively.

Synthesis of amorphous binary metal–Fe–B (metal = Ni, Co) MFs

The a-Co–Fe–B MF was synthesized by the same method as for a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF, but using Co(CH3COO)2·4H2O (3.2 mL) and FeCl3 (0.8 mL) as metal precursors. The a-Ni–Fe–B MF was synthesized by the same method as for a-Ni–Co–Fe–B, but using Ni(CH3COO)2·4H2O (3.2 mL) and FeCl3 (0.8 mL) as metal precursors.

Synthesis of crystalline Ni–Co–Fe–B MF

The crystalline c-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF was obtained by calcining the as-prepared a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF at 350 °C for 2 h under a 4% H2/Ar atmosphere. Different crystallinities can be obtained by varying the temperature.

Synthesis of non-porous Ni–Co–Fe–B

The np-Ni–Co–Fe–B was synthesized by the same method as for a-Ni–Co–Fe–B MF but without the pore-directing copolymer.

Synthesis of mesoporous Ni–Co–Fe

The mesoporous Ni–Co–Fe without B was prepared only by electrodeposition. The electrolyte was the same as that used for a-Ni–Co–Fe–B but without DMAB and TBAB. The mesoporous Ni–Co–Fe electrode was synthesized by electrodeposition at −1.2 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) for 3000 s. The electrode was then immersed in DMF to remove the copolymer template.

Synthesis of Ni–Co–Fe LDH

The nickel foam was soaked in a 2 M HCl solution for 15 min, and then washed with deionized water and ethanol. 1 mmol Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, 2.2 mmol Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 0.8 mmol Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, and 10 mmol urea were dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water. The solution was transferred to a 100 mL Teflon-lined steel autoclave with clean nickel foam. The autoclave was maintained at 120 °C for 8 h. After cooling, the nickel foam was washed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C overnight to obtain Ni–Co–Fe LDH.

Characterization

XRD analysis was conducted on a Rigaku SmartLab 3kW-BU diffractometer operating in continuous scan mode with a 0.02° step resolution. Surface morphology and elemental composition were evaluated through SEM imaging (Gemini 560) coupled with EDS (Bruker 5060 F detector). All SEM images were captured in secondary electron mode. Cross-sectional TEM specimens were prepared through focused ion beam milling (NX5000 FIB system) at 30 kV acceleration voltage. Microstructural details were examined using a Talos F200X TEM system configured with HRTEM capabilities and a Super-X G2 EDS detector. AFM was implemented on a Bruker Dimension Icon system with a ScanAsyst-Air probe. Surface chemical states were analyzed by XPS measurements performed on a PHI Quantes spectrometer. Elemental quantification was achieved through ICP-OES analysis using an Agilent 5800. SAXS measurements were performed on a Rigaku Nano-Viewer.

Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical characterizations were performed in 1 M KOH or 1 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl electrolyte using a CHI 660E electrochemical workstation. A three-electrode configuration was employed with catalyst-modified carbon paper as the working electrode, Hg/HgO as the reference electrode, and a graphite rod as the counter electrode. For reference, RuO2 samples, the catalyst ink was prepared by ultrasonically dispersing 5 mg of catalyst in a mixed solvent containing 975 μL ethanol and 25 μL 5% Nafion solution for 30 min. Subsequently, 260 μL of the homogeneous ink was drop-cast onto carbon paper and air-dried at ambient temperature, achieving a controlled catalyst loading of 1.3 mg cm−2. LSV measurements were performed at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1 with 95% iR compensation. EIS was conducted over a frequency range from 100 kHz to 50 mHz under potentiostatic control. The Cdl was determined through CV analysis at different scan rates from 20 to 100 mV/s. The ECSA was calculated according to ECSA = (Cdl/Cs) × A, where Cs is 40 μF cm−2 and A is the electrode area. The Faradaic efficiency was measured using a drainage method. Long-term stability assessment was performed via chronopotentiometric measurements under continuous operation.

AEM electrolyzer evaluation

The a-Ni–Co–Fe–B (electrode area: 1.0 cm2, mass loading: 0.92 mg cm−2) and commercial Pt black (electrode area: 1.0 cm2, mass loading: 2 mg cm−2) were used as the anode and cathode, respectively. For the cathode, 5 mg Pt black was ultrasonically dispersed in 975 μL ethanol, and 25 μL 5% Nafion solution, and then the catalyst ink was dropped on carbon paper with an approximate metal loading of 2 mg cm−2. The AEM was PiperION A60 with a thickness of 60 μm. Before use, the AEM was pretreated by immersion in a 1 M KOH solution for activation. The AEM electrolyzer device was assembled and clamped under 55 in-lb by a torque wrench. The 1 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl electrolyte was pumped into the system by two peristaltic pumps at a flow rate of 25 mL min−1. The AEM electrolyzer was operated at room temperature (25 °C) or 80 °C.

Computational method

The electric-field distribution was computed using the finite element method by solving the charge conservation equation under steady-state conditions51. Regarding the boundary conditions, a fixed electric potential of 1 V is applied to the bottom boundary of all models, while the top boundary is grounded (0 V). During the modeling process, the following assumptions are made: (1) the electric field is under steady-state conditions; (2) the coupling between the temperature field and the electric field is neglected; (3) space charge effects inside the domain are ignored, and the electric field is solely determined by the applied boundary potentials.

DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with a plane-wave basis set52. The projector augmented-wave method53 was employed with an energy cutoff of 450 eV, and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof generalized gradient approximation functional54 was used for the exchange-correlation energy. To account for weak van der Waals interactions between surfaces and adsorbates, the D3 correction with Becke–Johnson damping (D3-BJ) was included55. The crystalline Ni–Co–Fe–B model was constructed as a four-layer slab exposing the (111) facet with a cell size of 5 × 5 (containing 100 atoms) and lattice parameters of a = b = 11.596 Å and c = 20.5542 Å. A vacuum layer of 15 Å was added along the z-direction to eliminate spurious interactions due to periodic boundary conditions. All atomic positions in the slab were fully relaxed until the total energy and atomic forces converged to less than 10–4 eV and 0.03 eV Å–1, respectively. A Γ-centered 2 × 2 × 1 k-point mesh was used to sample the Brillouin zone. The amorphous model was constructed using AIMD simulations. To mimic the liquid-like nature of the amorphous phase, the initial crystalline structure was heated to 5000 K using a Nosé–Hoover thermostat with a time step of 3 fs, allowing atomic diffusion over a period of 5 ps. The system was then rapidly quenched to 300 K to capture the disordered structure. All AIMD simulations were performed using the Γ-point sampling. To account for the diversity of active sites on their surfaces, 500 random configurations were generated and structurally optimized using the machine-learning potential from the OC20 project56,57. The two lowest-energy configurations were subsequently selected for further catalysis investigation.

The Gibbs free energy of intermediates was calculated using the definition G = E + ZPE − TS, where E, ZPE, and TS correspond to the system’s total energy obtained by DFT, zero-point energy correction, and vibrational entropy of the adsorbates, respectively. The catalytic activity of amorphous and crystalline NiCoFeB was evaluated by calculating the relative free energy (ΔG) of OER intermediates with ΔG = ΔE + ΔEZPE ‒ TΔS (i.e., T = 298.15 K and p = 1 atm), where correction terms were calculated using the VASPKIT package58. On each surface, the free energy of the reaction intermediates was represented by the four most stable adsorption configurations, which were averaged to plot the reaction pathway. Gibbs free energy of ion adsorption (OH–, Cl–) was corrected by employing the implicit solvation model from VASPsol. For electrochemical steps involving a proton-electron transfer, the computational hydrogen electrode model was employed, where G[(H+ + e–)] equals G[1/2H2(g)]59. The electrode potential value (U = 1.23 V vs. RHE) was included by adding ΔGU = − neU to the Gibbs free energy.
