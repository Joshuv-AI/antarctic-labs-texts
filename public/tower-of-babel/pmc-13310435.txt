Prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kanyanuth Boonyoung, Waranun Buajeeb, Naruemon Panpradit, Nat Na-ek, Kununya Pimolbutr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral health challenges in older individuals are often intensified by physiological and pathological changes associated with aging, leading to several oral conditions. Xerostomia and hyposalivation are common problems affecting quality of life. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in older individuals aged 60 years and above.MethodsA comprehensive search strategy was conducted across four databases (MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus and ProQuest) for articles published from inception to April 2026. Studies assessing xerostomia or hyposalivation in participants aged ≥ 60 years were included, while studies involving patients with systemic conditions known to cause xerostomia and/or hyposalivation, or those who had previously undergone radiotherapy, were excluded. The quality assessment was performed using a tool adapted from Hoy et al. The pooled prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation were estimated using random-effects models. Subgroup analyses were also conducted to identify sources of heterogeneity.ResultsThe inclusion of 128 eligible publications comprising 174,698 participants reporting on the prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation. The included studies comprised a total of 170,803 individuals for xerostomia and 10,396 individuals for hyposalivation, with reported age ranges of 60–107 years and 60–103 years, respectively. The pooled prevalence of xerostomia (n = 119 study populations) was 32.4% (95% CI: 29.6%, 35.2%; I² = 99.3%), while that of hyposalivation (n = 32) was 33.0% (95% CI: 25.7%, 42.0%; I² = 98.6%). No evidence of publication bias was observed, and all sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the main analyses. Subgroup analyses did not reveal any apparent sources of heterogeneity. Most of the studies were assessed as having a high risk of bias for both xerostomia and hyposalivation.ConclusionThe prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation among older adults aged ≥ 60 years was approximately one-third, indicating a considerable burden in this population. However, the high degree of heterogeneity suggests substantial variability across studies, and the pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08579-x.Keywords: Prevalence, Xerostomia, Hyposalivation, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

The number of older people is increasing in both size and proportion in every country worldwide. While many older adults maintain good health and functional independence, some may experience age-related changes that increase their susceptibility to certain chronic conditions [1]. Among the oral health conditions that may affect this population are dental caries, tooth loss, periodontal diseases, and dry mouth [2].

The term “dry mouth” may refer to either the subjective sensation of oral dryness or the objective finding of reduced saliva production. Xerostomia refers specifically to the subjective feeling of dry mouth, which is typically assessed through self-reporting or the use of questionnaires [3, 4], such as xerostomia questionnaire [5], Xerostomia Inventory (XI) [6], or the Summated XI-Dutch version [7]. In contrast, hyposalivation refers to a measurable reduction in salivary flow. Although various cut-off values have been reported in the literature, it is most commonly defined as an unstimulated salivary flow rate of < 0.1 mL/min and a stimulated salivary flow rate of < 0.7 mL/min [3]. Xerostomia and hyposalivation are two separate entities that do not always coexist. Reduced salivary flow does not always result in a sensation of oral dryness, and conversely, some individuals with normal salivary flow may still experience symptoms of dry mouth [8]. It is also important to note that while a slight decline in salivary gland function may occur as part of normal physiological ageing, clinically significant hyposalivation is not an inevitable consequence of ageing and is more commonly associated with specific pathological conditions and risk factors such as medication use, systemic diseases, and radiotherapy.

Although some degree of change in salivary function may occur with normal ageing, clinically significant reductions in salivary flow are more commonly attributable to pathological causes rather than ageing itself. These contributing factors can be broadly categorized into four groups based on their underlying mechanisms [9–11]. First, medication-related factors represent one of the most common causes of dry mouth [12]. Older adults commonly present with multiple chronic conditions and are frequently exposed to polypharmacy [13, 14]. Many of the medications prescribed in this population - such as antihypertensives, anticholinergics, anxiolytics, hypnotics, anti-inflammatory drugs and treatments for osteoporosis, are known to exert xerogenic effects [15, 16]. Second, disease-related factors include various systemic conditions associated with salivary gland dysfunction, such as Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, diabetes mellitus, and amyloidosis [17, 18]. Sjögren’s syndrome, in particular, is a common autoimmune disorder that impairs the function of the salivary and lacrimal glands, resulting in symptoms such as dry mouth and dry eyes [19]. Third, treatment-related factors, specifically radiotherapy targeting the head and neck region, can cause fibrosis, acinar cell atrophy, and reduced blood supply, ultimately leading to irreversible damage to the salivary glands and making hyposalivation a frequent side effect [20]. Forth, psychological and ageing-associated factors, including stress, depression, and anxiety, may be more prevalent among some older adults due to age-related life changes and health challenges [21], and have shown to be associated with xerostomia and decreased salivary flow rate [22].

Patients with hyposalivation often experience oral discomfort along with difficulties in chewing, swallowing and speaking, all of which can greatly impact their quality of life. A reduction in saliva production can impair its protective functions, increasing the risk of dental caries and periodontal diseases [23]. These oral health problems can lead to dental pain and tooth loss, which further compromise chewing and alter facial aesthetics [18, 24–26].

The prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in this population has been reported in multiple studies. In 2017, Islas-Granillo et al. [24] reported prevalence rates of 25.2% for xerostomia, 59.7% for hyposalivation, and 16.5% for both conditions with no significant association between xerostomia and hyposalivation. Although previous systematic reviews have examined the prevalence of dry mouth in older adults, several important gaps in the existing literature remain. Affoo et al. [27] focused on comparing salivary flow rates between young and older adults rather than estimating prevalence. Pina et al. [28]. reported an overall hyposalivation prevalence of 33.4% in older adults; however, individuals with subjectively reported dry mouth (xerostomia) were excluded, and the literature search was conducted up to February 2019 only [28]. Agostini et al. [29] investigated the prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in the general population, reporting rates of 27.2% and 20.0%, respectively among older adults; however, the age cut-off used to define the older adult population was not specified, and the search was limited to studies published up to February 2018. Furthermore, none of these reviews simultaneously addressed both xerostomia and hyposalivation exclusively in older adults, and subgroup analyses in prior reviews were limited in scope, restricting the ability to fully explore the sources of heterogeneity across studies.

Many previous studies on the prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation have used different age thresholds to define older adults. The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies individuals aged 60 years and above as older adults [30] while other studies have used 65 years or older as the cut-off [31, 32]. Since dry mouth is associated with aging, and its likelihood increases with advancing age, comparing prevalence rates from studies using different age thresholds can provide valuable insight into the age at which xerostomia and hyposalivation become significantly more common, informing clinical practice and guiding health policy development for the prevention and management of dry mouth in older populations. Awareness of the prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in older adults is essential for recognizing the extent and severity of this problem. Understanding these conditions not only help in preventing oral complications linked to dry mouth but also support the development of personalized treatment strategies and the formulation of public health policies aimed at improving the oral health-related quality of life in the aging population.

Therefore, the present systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to address these gaps by providing an updated and comprehensive synthesis of the prevalence of both xerostomia and hyposalivation in older adults aged 60 years and above, incorporating evidence up to 2026 as well as to identify factors associated with both conditions.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) checklists (Table S1) [33]. This study was a systematic review of previously published data and therefore did not require ethical approval, as no human participants were involved. The study was registered at PROSPERO, International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number CRD42022292199).

Eligibility criteria

We included observational studies reporting the prevalence of xerostomia and/or hyposalivation that met the following criteria: (1) studies including exclusively participants aged ≥ 60 years (studies involving mixed-age populations were excluded); (2) studies evaluating xerostomia using validated questionnaires, including the Xerostomia Inventory (XI), the condensed version of the XI, Summated XI-Dutch version, the eating symptom questionnaire, the xerostomia questionnaire by Fox et al. [5], the xerostomia questionnaire by Carvalho et al. [34], the xerostomia questionnaire by Ferreiro et al. [35], the Likert-style XI, and the visual analogue scale (VAS) instrument or by self-reported experience of dry mouth, including its extent and frequency; (3) studies assessing hyposalivation using either unstimulated or stimulated salivary flow rates. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) review articles, case reports, conference abstracts, letters to editors, editorial/personal opinions and pilot study; (2) studies involving participants aged < 60 years; (3) studies conducted in patients with systemic conditions known to cause xerostomia and/or hyposalivation (e.g., Sjögren’s syndrome and systemic sclerosis); (4) studies involving patients who had previously received radiotherapy; and (5) studies with incomplete or inaccurate information.

Information sources and search strategy

A systemic literature search was conducted across four electronic databases (MEDLINE/PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus and ProQuest) up to April 2026 without any publication period and language restriction. The search strategy combined Subject Heading terms (MeSH terms for MEDLINE and Emtree for EMBASE) and keywords for xerostomia and hyposalivation as appropriate. The search terms included the following: (‘geriatric’ or ‘older adult’ or ‘aged’ or ‘elderly’) and (‘dry mouth’ or ‘xerostomia’ or ‘hyposalivation’ and ‘prevalence’). The details of the search strategies for four databases are demonstrated in Appendix S1. Additional relevant articles were manually searched by screening the reference lists of retrieved articles. All identified articles were managed using the Rayyan web application [36].

Study selection and data extraction

After removing duplicates, two authors (KB and KP) independently screened titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles following the eligibility criteria. Following this process, the full text of all potentially eligible articles was assessed by the same two reviewers. Any reviewer disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (NN). After the full-text assessment, the two independent reviewers extracted data from the included articles using a predefined spreadsheet. The following data were extracted: authors, year of publication, country, study design, study setting, age-cut off for older adults, demographic data of sample (age and sex), sample size, criteria used to define xerostomia, diagnostic methods and its cut-off point used to define hyposalivation, the prevalence of xerostomia, and the prevalence of hyposalivation. For longitudinal studies, prevalence data were extracted from baseline measurements.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias was independently assessed by the two reviewers (KB and KP) using a tool developed by Hoy et al. [37]. This comprises 10 items specifically designed to evaluate the risk of bias in prevalence studies, including aspects related to both internal and external validity. Details of risk of bias assessment are provided in Appendix S2. Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved by consensus. Following a discussion among the authors, items 6 and 7 were considered critical. They were scored as 0 for a low risk of bias and 1 for a high risk of bias. The remaining items, considered minor, were scored 0 and 0.5 for a low and high risk of bias respectively. Each of the selected studies was classified as high, moderate, or low risk of bias if the sum score is ≥ 1, 0.5, and 0, respectively [38].

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of the included studies, including study design, setting, country, age cut-off for older adults, diagnostic criteria and methods of assessment, and prevalence estimates, were summarized descriptively. Point prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation was calculated as the number of cases divided by the total number of participants. The method used to define hyposalivation was also recorded.

Meta-analyses were performed to estimate the pooled prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation. Given the anticipated heterogeneity across studies, a random-effects inverse-variance model with the DerSimonian–Laird estimator of between-study variance (τ²) was applied. To stabilize the variance of proportions, particularly extremely high and low ones, prevalence data were transformed using the arcsine method. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with I² values greater than 50% or p-values from Q < 0.1 considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity [39].

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted according to study design, setting, participant characteristics, diagnostic methods, and geographic region. Differences between subgroups were examined using Cochran’s Q test for heterogeneity across subgroups. Specifically, studies reporting xerostomia prevalence were stratified by study design, study setting, timing of xerostomia assessment, age cut-off point, sex, diagnostic method, risk of bias, country, and continent. Studies reporting hyposalivation prevalence were categorized by study design, setting, age cut-off point, sex, method used to define hyposalivation, risk of bias, country, and continent.

Potential publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry and Egger’s regression test, performed only when ten or more studies were available for a given meta-analysis. Additionally, several sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of findings. These include: (1) Influence analysis: A leave-one-out approach was applied, sequentially excluding each study to assess its influence on the pooled prevalence estimate and heterogeneity. (2) Transformation methods: Alternative variance-stabilizing transformations, including logit and Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine methods, were used to verify the consistency of results [40], and (3) Publication bias correction: Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill method was applied to assess the potential impact of unpublished or missing studies on the pooled estimates.

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA), primarily employing the metan commands. Data visualizations, including forest plots for subgroup analysis, were created using R version 4.3.1 with the forestplot package.

Results

Study selection

Following electronic searching in four databases, 44,934 studies were retrieved. After removing duplicates, 30,833 studies remained for titles and abstracts screening and 664 of them were selected for full-text evaluation. Ultimately, 128 articles comprising 174,698 participants were included in the present study. Among these, 116 studies (representing 119 study populations) reported data on the prevalence of xerostomia (xerostomia 47,310 cases), and 32 studies reported data on the prevalence of hyposalivation (hyposalivation 2,974 cases). Twenty studies provided information on both xerostomia and hyposalivation. A PRISMA flow diagram demonstrating the selection process with reasons for exclusion is shown in Fig. 1.

With regards to xerostomia, a total of 116 studies examined the prevalence of xerostomia with 3 out of these 116 studies reporting prevalence of xerostomia in more than one study population. A total of 119 study populations (obtained from 116 studies) were included in this review, comprising 170,803 participants with age ranged between 60 and 107 years. Of these, 9,341 were institutionalized participants, 160,709 were community-dwelling, and 753 were unspecified. The 102 studies were cross-sectional studies followed by 17 prospective studies. To determine xerostomia, data were gathered either through xerostomia questionnaires (16 studies): the Xerostomia Inventory (XI) (4 studies), the xerostomia questionnaire by Fox et al. [5] (3 studies), Condensed version of the XI (2 studies), Summated XI-Dutch version (1 study), the xerostomia questionnaire by Carvalho et al. [34] (1 study), the xerostomia questionnaire by Ferreiro et al. [35] (1 study), the eating symptom questionnaire (1 study), the Likert-style XI (1 study), the VAS scale (1 study), and unspecified questionnaire (1 study) or by asking if they experienced dry mouth sensation and the extent and frequency of this sensation (103 studies).

Regarding hyposalivation, there were 32 studies assessing the prevalence of hyposalivation in 10,396 participants aged 60 to 103 years. Twenty-eight studies were cross-sectional studies, while four studies were prospective studies. The participant pool included 9,203 individuals living in the community, 935 individuals in institutional settings, and 258 individuals with unspecified living arrangements. There were 2 methods used to measure salivary flow rates in order to define hyposalivation: (1) by measuring the volume of saliva collected using the spitting method (25 studies) and (2) by measuring the weight of the collected saliva or the saliva that absorbed by cotton placed or chewed in the mouth (7 studies). Salivary flow rates were measured either unstimulated salivary flow rates, stimulated salivary flow rates or both. There were 19 studies measuring unstimulated salivary flow rates with a cut-off point of less than 0.1 to 0.9 mL/min to define hyposalivation. To identify hyposalivation using stimulated salivary flow rates, 21 studies measuring stimulated salivary flow rates with a cut-off point of less than 0.5 to 1.0 mL/min. Only one study deployed both stimulated and unstimulated salivary flow rates to report the prevalence of hyposalivation. A summary of the main characteristics of the included studies reporting prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in older people is provided in Table S2 and Table S3, respectively.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias patterns were broadly similar across outcomes. For xerostomia (n = 116), domain 1 showed a high proportion of high risk of bias (91.4%), indicating limited population representativeness. Domains 2 (sampling frame representativeness), 5 (data collection method), 6 (case definition), and 8 (consistency of data collection) were predominantly low risk (< 10% high risk), while domains 3 (sampling method), 7 (measurement validity), 9 (prevalence period), and 10 (numerator/denominator appropriateness) showed moderate levels (15.5%–30.2%), and domain 4 (non-response bias) was higher (37.9%). For hyposalivation (n = 32), domain 1 similarly showed high risk (96.9%), while domains 2 (sampling frame representativeness) and 9 (prevalence period) were entirely low risk. Most domains demonstrated low-to-moderate high risk (0%–28.1%), except domain 7 (measurement validity), which was notably higher (62.5%).

A summary of the overall risk of bias for each included study that reported the prevalence of xerostomia is demonstrated in Table S4. Eighty-eight studies were evaluated as having a high risk of bias, and 28 studies had a moderate risk of bias.

The overall risk of bias for each study that reported the prevalence of hyposalivation is demonstrated in Table S5. Most studies were evaluated as having a high risk of bias, and only three studies had a moderate risk of bias.

Prevalence of xerostomia and subgroup analysis

The pooled prevalence of xerostomia was estimated at 32.4% (95% CI: 29.6%, 35.2%; I2 = 99.3%). The reported prevalence of xerostomia across the included studies ranged from 0.9% to 95.0%. Subgroup analyses of xerostomia prevalence in older adults are presented in the forest plots (Fig. 2a and b), with detailed results summarized in Table S6. The pooled prevalence of xerostomia of each group when stratified by defining an age cut-off point for older adults (60 vs. 65) (p = 0.96), diagnostic methods for xerostomia (p = 0.96), risk of bias (p = 0.63), and continent (p = 0.11) did not differ statistically significantly (p > 0.05) (Table S6).

According to study design stratification, the pooled prevalence of xerostomia in older adults in the cross-sectional studies was 33.4% (95% CI: 30.2%, 36.8%) which was greater than the pooled prevalence observed from the prospective studies of 25.8% (95% CI: 22.9%, 28.7%). The I2 of the pooled prevalence was 99.4% and 95.4%, respectively.

Regarding the study setting, the included participants were recruited from institutional care for older adults or community-dwelling older adults. The pooled prevalence of xerostomia among institutionalized residents was 43.6% (95% CI: 33.9%, 53.5%) followed by community-dwelling older adults (30.9%, 95% CI: 28.0%, 34.0%) and a combination between institutional and community-dwelling (18.9%, 95% CI: 9.5%, 30.6%). However, there was significant heterogeneity with the I2 of the pooled prevalence ranging between 92.8% and 99.4%.

When stratifying by time that individuals experienced xerostomia, there were five studies reporting the prevalence of xerostomia at night. The pooled prevalence of night-time xerostomia was 45.5% (95% CI: 42.5%, 48.5%) whereas the pooled prevalence of xerostomia during the day was 32.2% (95% CI: 29.4%, 35.0%). The I2 of the pooled prevalence was 89.7% and 99.3%, respectively.

In the included studies where participants were stratified by sex, the pooled prevalence of xerostomia in women was 33.2% (95% CI: 26.6%, 40.0%), surpassing the pooled prevalence in men (25.5%, 95% CI: 20.4%, 31.0%). In studies reporting both genders, the pooled prevalence of xerostomia was 33.5% (95% CI: 30.4%, 36.7%).

The pooled prevalence of xerostomia varies substantially when stratified by country. Hong Kong had the lowest prevalence of xerostomia at 8.4% (95% CI: 7.0%, 23.3%), while the highest prevalence of xerostomia was observed in Germany at 81.4% (95% CI: 62.6%, 94.7%). The degree of subgroup heterogeneity, indicated by the I² statistic for the pooled prevalence, varied from 76.5% to 99.7%, except for the I² statistic from two articles studied in India, which equaled 0.0%.

Based on continent, South America had the highest prevalence at 38.9% (95% CI: 33.2%, 44.7%), followed by Europe (33.2%, 95% CI: 28.4%, 38.1%), North America (31.9%, 95% CI: 20.3%, 44.7%) and Australia (31.3%, 95% CI: 22.9%, 40.3%). Asia reported the lowest prevalence at 28.7% (95% CI: 24.2%, 33.4%). The I² exceeded 90% across all continents with the exception of the two studies conducted in India, where I2 was 0.0%.

Prevalence of hyposalivation and subgroup analysis

The overall prevalence of hyposalivation was estimated to be 33.0% (95% CI: 25.7%, 40.7%; I2 = 98.5%). The reported prevalence of hyposalivation across the included studies ranged from 6.42% to 70%. The pooled prevalence of hyposalivation based on unstimulated saliva was 39.0% (95% CI: 27.9–50.7%; I² = 98.4%), while the pooled prevalence based on stimulated saliva was 29.9% (95% CI: 20.9–39.7%; I² = 98.6%). Subgroup analyses of hyposalivation prevalence in older adults are shown in the forest plots (Fig. 3a and b) with detailed results provided in Table S7. In subgroup analysis, the pooled prevalence of each group did not differ statistically significantly (p > 0.05) when stratifying data by study designs (p = 0.99), study settings (p = 0.20), age cut-off point (p = 0.17), sex (p = 0.14), methods to define hyposalivation (p = 0.29), and risk of bias (p = 0.77) (Table S7).

Stratifying by country and continent resulted in a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001 when stratifying data by country and p = 0.002 when stratifying data by continents) in the pooled prevalence of hyposalivation across each group.

With regard to country, the highest pooled prevalence was found in studies from Pakistan at 70.0% (95% CI: 60.9%, 77.8%), while the lowest prevalence was observed in studies from Denmark at 6.4% (95% CI: 5.1%, 8.0%). The I2 ranged between 85.8% and 99.3%.

Based on continent, South America had the highest prevalence at 56.4% (95% CI: 37.1%, 74.7%), followed by Asia (33.2%, 95% CI: 22.3%, 45.1%), Europe (32.2%, 95% CI: 20.1%, 45.5%), and North America (28.5, 95% CI: 14.7%, 44.8%). Australia reported the lowest prevalence at 22.3% (95% CI: 19.4%, 25.4%).

The pooled prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in older adults from all subgroup analyses are presented in Tables S6 and S7, respectively.

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

Egger’s test for the xerostomia outcome indicated evidence of small-study effects (p = 0.04; Figure S1), suggesting potential publication bias. After applying the trim-and-fill method as a sensitivity analysis, the pooled prevalence slightly increased from 32.4% (95% CI:29.6%, 35.2%) to 32.5.% (95% CI: 29.2%, 35.9%). For the hyposalivation outcome, Egger’s test showed no evidence of publication bias among studies reporting the prevalence of hyposalivation (p = 0.08) and Funnel plot analyses for the overall prevalence of hyposalivation in older adults (Fig. S2) confirmed this finding.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the pooled prevalence estimates for both xerostomia and hyposalivation. The leave-one-out analysis showed that no single study significantly influenced the overall results (Fig. S3a, S3b and Fig. S4). In addition, alternative variance-stabilizing transformations yielded comparable pooled estimates to the arcsine method. For xerostomia, the logit and Freeman–Tukey transformations produced pooled prevalences of 30.9% (95% CI: 28.3%, 33.6%; I² = 99.1%) and 32.3% (95% CI: 29.6%, 35.2%; I² = 99.3%), respectively. For hyposalivation, the corresponding values were 31.5% (95% CI: 24.5%, 39.4%; I² = 98.1%) and 32.9% (95% CI: 25.6%, 40.7%; I² = 98.5%). Results from Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill method further supported the robustness of the findings, as no studies were trimmed or filled.

Discussion

Hyposalivation and xerostomia impair essential oral functions such as taste, chewing, swallowing and speech [26], and increase the risk of dental caries, periodontal disease and oral infections. Affected individuals may also experience anxiety and reduced self-esteem, particularly in social situations involving speaking or eating [41]. Effective management can mitigate these complications; however, accurate diagnosis is essential to distinguish between xerostomia, hyposalivation, or their coexistence, as effective management depends on identifying the underlying condition. This systematic review and meta-analysis, supported by an extensive literature search, a large sample size and comprehensive subgroup analyses, thoroughly examined the overall estimated prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in older adults (≥ 60 years old). The pooled prevalence of xerostomia was 32.4%, and the overall prevalence of hyposalivation in our study was 33.0%.

In this review, the pooled prevalence of xerostomia was slightly higher to that reported by Agostini et al. [29] who reported a prevalence of 27.2% among older adults. The higher prevalence observed in our review (32.4% vs 27.2%) may reflect differences in the geographic and economic composition of the included studies. The majority of studies included in the previous systematic review were carried out in high-income countries whereas many studies included in our review were from lower to middle-income settings [42]. Although Agostini et al. identified age and geographic location as potential sources of heterogeneity, no statistically significant associations were found [29]. It should be noted that no formal subgroup analysis stratified by country income level was performed in the present review; however, the wide range of prevalence estimates observed across countries and continents suggests that economic factors, including differences in access to healthcare, oral health services, medication use patterns, and dietary habits, may contribute to this variability. This represents an important area for future research, and formal analyses stratified by country income level are warranted to better understand the potential influence of socioeconomic factors on the prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in older adults.

In our subgroup analyses by country and continent, neither factor accounted for the heterogeneity in the pooled prevalence of xerostomia. Interestingly, the pooled prevalence of xerostomia in India showed an I² of 0.0%, suggesting that the results of the two Indian studies were highly consistent in their prevalence estimates. Both studies were cross-sectional and assessed xerostomia among community residents. Data from both studies were collected during the same period (2022–2023) and relied on self-reported measures to identify xerostomia. Despite these methodological similarities, the investigations differed in their study populations. Specifically, Muneshwar et al. examined postmenopausal women attending a dental outpatient clinic in Maharashtra, whereas Dodderi et al. surveyed community-dwelling older adults in Mangalore, Karnataka [43, 44]. The apparent absence of heterogeneity likely reflects the alignment of study design and contextual factors. Nevertheless, this estimate is based on only two studies and should therefore be interpreted with caution, as it may not provide a reliable or stable representation of the true prevalence, and I² estimates derived from only two studies can be imprecise and may not confirm true statistical homogeneity.

With regard to the overall prevalence of hyposalivation among older adults, our findings were consistent with those reported by Pina et al. [28], who reported a prevalence of 33.4%. This estimate was higher than that reported by Agostini et al. who observed a prevalence of 20.0% [29]. However, the latter did not specify the age range included in the elderly group.

Although xerostomia and hyposalivation were analyzed as distinct outcomes in this review, it is important to recognize that these conditions are closely related but not entirely overlapping. Of the studies included in this review, ten reported the co-occurrence of both xerostomia and hyposalivation, providing some insight into the degree of overlap between the two conditions. While reduced salivary flow or hyposalivation may contribute to the sensation of dry mouth, the relationship between the two is not always consistent, and discordance has been reported in the literature [34, 45]. Some individuals may experience xerostomia despite normal salivary flow rates, while others with hyposalivation may not report symptoms. This variability may be influenced by several factors such as medication use, systemic health conditions, oral mucosal sensitivity, and psychological factors [46]. Therefore, the coexistence and potential discordance between xerostomia and hyposalivation should be considered when interpreting the findings of this meta-analysis, as they reflect the complex and multifactorial nature of salivary dysfunction in older adults.

In relation to the methods used to measure salivary flow, the prevalence of hyposalivation in our study was higher under the unstimulated condition (39.0%) than that under the stimulated condition (29.9%), which is in line with the previous findings reported by Pina et al. [28]. Moreover, the meta-analysis by Affoo et al. [27] observed that both unstimulated and stimulated whole salivary flow rates were lower in older individuals (aged ≥ 60 years). This decline may be explained by an increase in chronic health conditions and higher number of medications used among older adults, both of which can adversely affect salivary gland function [47].

The high heterogeneity observed in our analysis aligns with that reported in previous systematic reviews [28, 29]. This variability in prevalence estimates may be attributed to differences in the criteria used to identify individuals with xerostomia and hyposalivation. Previous studies assessed xerostomia through self-reported symptoms, either by directly asking participants about dry mouth or by employing questionnaires such as the Xerostomia Inventory (XI), the condensed version of the XI, the Summated XI-Dutch version the Eating Symptom Questionnaire, and the Xerostomia questionnaire [5]. However, even when similar instruments were used, some studies applied different diagnostic criteria. For instance, one study characterized dry mouth symptoms as dryness occurring only during meals, which may have led to underestimation of prevalence [48]. Similarly, the cut-off values used to define hyposalivation varied across studies, likely influencing the reported prevalence rates. An unusually high cut-off value (0.9 mL/min) for hyposalivation was reported in one study [49]. Despite this, the corresponding prevalence estimate (64.5%) was not an outlier compared with other studies reporting similarly high prevalence (approximately 63.3%–69.5%). In addition, the relatively small sample size of this study (n = 138) limited its influence on the pooled estimate. Sensitivity analyses further confirmed that its exclusion did not materially affect the overall results, suggesting that this study is unlikely to be a major contributor to the observed heterogeneity.

These findings highlight the need to standardize diagnostic criteria and measurement approaches for both xerostomia and hyposalivation, including the use of consistent thresholds for unstimulated and stimulated salivary flow rates. Future research should also aim to better characterize the relationship between subjective and objective measures of salivary dysfunction in older populations, for example by recruiting older adults and simultaneously assessing both xerostomia using validated questionnaires and salivary flow rates under standardized conditions, to determine the degree of concordance and discordance between the two measures. Importantly, the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies may itself represent a key finding of this meta-analysis, highlighting the variability in prevalence estimates across different populations, measurement approaches, and study settings. Although high heterogeneity is commonly observed in prevalence meta-analyses and does not necessarily preclude pooling, it does limit the precision and generalizability of the pooled estimates. Therefore, the findings of this meta-analysis should be interpreted in light of this variability, and the pooled prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation should not be regarded as a single definitive value applicable across all older adult populations.

Our subgroup analyses based on study settings revealed a higher prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation among institutionalized older adults compared with community dwelling individuals, consistent with the findings from previous studies. This difference may be attributed to poorer general health and the greater number of medications used by older adults residing in institutions compared to those living independently [18, 50]. Certain drug classes including antidepressants, antihistamines, and antihypertensives are known to reduce salivary flow [51]. Moreover, several studies have shown that older adults residing in institutional settings are more likely to experience depression, a condition often treated with medications that can further contribute to salivary gland hypofunction [16, 52]. However, it is important to note that information on participants’ comorbidities and medication use was not consistently reported in the included studies, limiting our ability to fully account for potential confounding factors related to salivary function.

An unexpected finding from our subgroup analyses was that studies including both institutionalized and community-dwelling older adults reported a lower pooled prevalence of xerostomia (18.9%) compared with studies focusing exclusively on institutionalized (44.0%) or community-dwelling (30.5%) populations. This counterintuitive finding may be explained by a dilution effect, whereby the higher prevalence among institutionalized participants is averaged with the lower prevalence among community-dwelling participants, particularly when the latter group substantially outnumbers the former within mixed studies. Additionally, mixed setting studies may have applied stricter inclusion criteria or used diagnostic tools that performed differently across settings, potentially leading to underestimation of prevalence. Given the limited number of studies reporting mixed populations, this estimate should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory. Future studies should aim to report prevalence estimates separately for institutionalized and community-dwelling participants, even when both groups are recruited within the same study, to allow for more meaningful comparisons across settings.

Consistent with previous studies [29, 53], our findings revealed a higher prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation in females. This may be attributed to anatomical differences in salivary glands between men and women. Inoue et al. [54] reported women had significantly smaller parotid and submandibular gland sizes and lower salivary flow rates than men. Moreover, Agha-Hosseini et al. [55] found that oral dryness in menopausal women was not linked to reduced salivary flow. Instead, they suggested that elevated salivary calcium - likely influenced by hormonal changes in oestrogen – may contribute to the sensation of oral dryness [55]. Furthermore, postmenopausal women exhibited significantly reduced salivary flow compared to menstruating women, with improvements following hormone replacement therapy [56, 57]. Women are also more likely than men to use multiple medications and experience depression [18], both of which may further contribute to salivary dysfunction in the older females.

To the best of our knowledge, this systematic review applied a comprehensive analytical approach to estimate the pooled prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation. This study has several strengths. First, the application of a clear age definition (≥ 60 years) allows for a more precise estimation of prevalence specifically in older adults, reducing ambiguity present in earlier reviews. Second, the inclusion of a substantially larger number of studies and participants provides a more comprehensive and robust synthesis of the available evidence. Third, by evaluating both xerostomia and hyposalivation as distinct yet related outcomes, this review offers a more integrated understanding of salivary dysfunction in older populations — an approach that better reflects clinical reality, where the two conditions may coexist or diverge. Furthermore, the extensive subgroup analyses and exploration of heterogeneity reveal important patterns in how prevalence varies across different populations, diagnostic methods, and settings, which may inform more targeted clinical screening and assessment strategies. Together, these methodological contributions provide not only updated prevalence estimates but also a deeper understanding of the complexity and diversity of salivary dysfunction in older adults, with implications for both clinical practice and future research. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The predominance of high risk in domain 1 across both outcomes suggests limited external validity, which may partly explain the substantial heterogeneity observed. The higher risk in domain 7 among hyposalivation studies further indicates outcome-specific methodological variability, particularly in measurement approaches. These factors highlight the need to interpret pooled prevalence estimates with caution. A high degree of heterogeneity was observed, and its sources could not be fully determined, despite subgroup analyses conducted to explore potential contributing factors. The complex interplay of risk factors for xerostomia and hyposalivation among older adults—which appears to be more intricate than in younger populations [58] — may account for this unexplained heterogeneity. A further limitation relates to the interpretation of determinants of xerostomia and hyposalivation. Although multiple factors, such as medication use, systemic diseases, and ageing-related physiological changes, are known to influence salivary function, it was not feasible to meaningfully synthesized these determinants across studies. This is primarily because most included studies did not report these variables in a standardized or extractable manner, particularly in prevalence-focused designs. As a result, grouping determinants into clinically meaningful categories (e.g., medication-related versus disease-related) was not possible within the present meta-analytic framework.

Instead, the observed heterogeneity is more plausibly explained by methodological differences across studies, including variability in diagnostic criteria, measurement instruments, salivary flow thresholds, study settings, and population characteristics. These methodological factors likely represent the dominant drivers of between-study variability in prevalence estimates. In addition, socio-economic factors, including access to healthcare, institutional care quality, and education level, may contribute to variability across populations and should be considered as potential sources of heterogeneity.

Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, as their generalizability may be limited. Meta-regression was considered; however, it was not performed due to incomplete and inconsistent reporting of key covariates across primary studies. Future research using harmonized datasets may enable more robust exploration of heterogeneity.

Future research should consider the multifactorial nature of these conditions and incorporate subgroup analyses stratified by multiple variables to better elucidate their underlying determinants. Longitudinal studies in older populations are also warranted, as they would enable the examination of changes in salivary function over time and help clarify causal relationships between risk factors and the development of xerostomia and hyposalivation.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that the prevalence of xerostomia and hyposalivation among older adults was 32.4% and 33.0%, respectively, indicating that both conditions are common in this population. Hyposalivation prevalence was higher when assessed using unstimulated compared with stimulated salivary flow rates. In addition, both xerostomia and hyposalivation were more frequently reported among institutionalized older adults and females. However, the high degree of heterogeneity observed across studies suggests substantial variability in prevalence estimates, and the pooled results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Raising awareness among healthcare providers and the public is essential to improve education, prevention, and early detection of xerostomia and hyposalivation.
