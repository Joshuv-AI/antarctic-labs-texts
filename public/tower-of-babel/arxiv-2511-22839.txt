Can industrial overcapacity enable seasonal flexibility in electricity use? A case study of aluminum smelting in China Journal: Nature Energy
Ruike Lyu, Anna Li, Jianxiao Wang, Hongxi Luo, Yan Shen, Hongye Guo, Ershun Du, Chongqing Kang, Jesse Jenkins*

Abstract
In many countries, declining demand in energy-intensive industries (EIIs) such as cement, steel, and aluminum is leading to industrial overcapacity. Although industrial overcapacity is traditionally envisioned as problematic and resource-wasteful, it could unlock EIIs’ flexibility in electricity use. Here, using China’s aluminum smelting industry as a case, we evaluate the system-level cost-benefit of retaining EII overcapacity for flexible electricity use in decarbonized energy systems. We find that overcapacity can enable aluminum smelters to adopt a seasonal operation paradigm, ceasing production during winter load peaks exacerbated by heating electrification and renewable seasonality. In the 2050-net-zero scenario, the seasonal operation paradigm can reduce China’s electricity system investment and operational costs by 15-72 billion CNY/year (or 8-34% of the aluminum smelting industry’s product value), sufficient to offset the costs of maintaining overcapacity and product storage. It also reduces workforce fluctuations across the aluminum smelting and thermal power generation sectors by up to 62%, potentially mitigating socio-economic disruptions from industrial restructuring and energy transition.



1
Introduction

Industrial restructuring — the transition of economies from heavy industry to higher-added-value sectors
[
37
]
— is leading to declining demand for industrial products and overcapacity in energy-intensive industries in many countries. China’s steel and cement industries, for instance, have averaged 20-30% overcapacity in the last decade, resulting in declining profitability and asset utilization
[
43
]
. China’s aluminum smelters will confront substantial excess capacity in the near future, as China’s domestic aluminum demand is increasingly met by recycled aluminum (potentially fulfilling 70% of future demand
[
24
]
). Traditionally, this structural overcapacity is viewed as problematic, causing resource waste and economic risks
[
47
]
. Consequently, governments typically strive to eliminate overcapacity through policy measures
[
14
]
, such as China’s Supply-Side Structural Reform (SSSR), which was initiated around 2016 to tackle massive industrial overcapacity.

On the other hand, excess capacity also unlocks operational flexibility for energy-intensive industries, as it frees them from operating at full capacity at all times to meet demand. In decarbonized power systems reliant on variable renewable energy, the demand-side flexibility of energy-intensive industries may become more valuable for balancing electricity supply and demand
[
6
,
18
,
41
]
. By shifting electricity consumption to periods of high renewable energy availability, energy-intensive industries can reduce both the energy system operational costs and carbon emissions
[
13
]
. Furthermore, unlike “fast-burst” demand-side resources such as electric vehicles and air conditioning
[
29
,
40
]
, industrial facilities with overcapacity and material storage could potentially provide long-duration flexibility by scheduling their production over longer time horizons. In decarbonized energy systems with increasing variability in electricity prices
[
2
]
, this flexibility is also essential for lowering the energy input costs of energy-intensive industries like aluminum smelting, where electricity costs exceed 30% of operational expenses
[
1
]
.

In this context, determining whether industrial overcapacity should be decommissioned or retained requires a system-level cost-benefit analysis of the two competing mechanisms: decommissioning excess capacity to reduce maintenance costs versus retaining it to reduce energy system costs and emissions. To quantitatively assess this trade-off, we select China’s aluminum smelting industry as a representative case.
China is likely to face over 50% overcapacity in the aluminum smelting industry by 2050
[
24
]
, due to a shift towards secondary production using aluminum scrap
[
33
,
11
]
. Furthermore, the energy input for the aluminum smelting industry is almost entirely from electricity, accounting for 7% of China’s electricity demand. The aluminum smelting industry also has technical capability for flexible operation
[
39
]
. These make it an ideal example of an industry for examining the benefits of flexibly operating in the context of a decarbonized electricity system.

Figure 1:
Conceptual framework for overcapacity-enabled seasonal flexibility in energy-intensive industries.
The left panel illustrates the spring to autumn time period when renewable energy generation is abundant, with aluminum smelters operating at full capacity, producing aluminum for both meeting immediate demand and storing in inventory. During this period, aluminum smelters shut down and meet demand by drawing from product inventory. This seasonal operational paradigm leverages excess capacity to create complementary patterns between industrial production and energy system dynamics, enabling cost-effective decarbonization.

Existing studies have demonstrated the rapid power adjusting capabilities of aluminum electrolytic cells, validating the technical feasibility for flexible operation
[
39
,
42
]
. While existing studies analyze plant-level thermal balance and restart processes of aluminum electrolytic cells
[
35
,
10
]
, a comprehensive assessment of the system-level value of this flexibility remains absent, particularly its combined impact on energy system and aluminum smelter operation. Understanding these integrated impacts and their key determinants is essential for charting a sustainable path for the aluminum industry. However, the computational complexity of modeling and optimizing the intricate coupling between two already large and complex systems—the electricity system and aluminum smelters—has been a significant barrier to such analysis.

In this work, we quantitatively evaluate the system-level flexibility value of retaining aluminum smelting overcapacity in China’s decarbonization pathway by embedding a refined aluminum smelter operation model
[
31
]
within a provincial-resolved power system capacity expansion model to co-optimize the hourly operations of both systems.
The problem is made computationally tractable by the dimensionality reduction framework developed by Lyu et al.
[
27
]
and a tailored iterative solution algorithm.
We find that in net-zero electricity systems, overcapacity enables aluminum smelters to shift operations away from periods of winter electricity supply scarcity, which are driven by low renewable availability and heating electrification (Figure
1
). This flexibility lowers overall electricity system costs by reducing peak loads, which in turn decreases the renewable capacity required to meet those peaks and lowers utilization of coal, natural gas, and nuclear generation.
In a 2050 net-zero scenario, retaining 30% aluminum-smelting overcapacity reduces electricity system costs by 15-72 billion CNY/year (or 8-34% of the aluminum smelting industry’s product value), as compared to a no-overcapacity case. We also find that, in such a scenario, aluminum production costs are lower by over 1,500 CNY/tonne, due to aluminum smelters consuming cheaper electricity.
Furthermore, because thermal power plants may also operate seasonally in decarbonized systems, complementary seasonal operation in the aluminum smelting industry could help reduce workforce fluctuations across the aluminum smelting and power generation sectors by 62%, potentially helping to stabilize employment.

Although we are using aluminum smelting as a case, our findings can be generalized to other energy-intensive industries, such as steel and cement industries, where seasonal operation could potentially bring significant energy input cost savings to offset the costs of maintaining overcapacity and product storage.
More broadly, our work suggests that industrial overcapacity should be reframed from a problem to an opportunity to reduce energy system costs, lower industrial production costs, and mitigate socio-economic disruptions from energy decarbonization.

Electricity system cost reduction as a result of overcapacity-enabled flexibility

Reduction in electricity system costs due to overcapacity-enabled flexibility in aluminum smelting, compared to the no-overcapacity case.
Colors represent different sources of changes in system cost: renewable investment costs (green) and non-renewable operational costs (orange) account for the largest system cost reductions. The reduction in renewable investment costs reflects improved renewable energy utilization efficiency through concentrating smelting during periods of high renewable generation. The reduction in non-renewable operational costs reflects a decreased reliance on firm generation resources (coal, gas, biomass, and nuclear) to ensure power supply reliability. Figure
3
shows the reduction in electricity system costs in a 2050 net-zero emissions scenario when the operation of aluminum smelters with overcapacity is co-optimized with the electric power system, compared to a no-overcapacity operation scenario, which represents the value of flexibility in smelting electricity use. Here, the power system cost includes operational costs and the annualized capital investment for new generation, storage, and transmission capacity. The costs of the aluminum smelters are not included in the electricity system costs, but are discussed later in the paper. The results show that the overcapacity of aluminum smelters adds a flexibility value of 15 to 72 billion CNY to the power system, which is equivalent to 8-34% of the aluminum smelting industry’s product value in 2050. National aggregated results are shown here, while a provincial resolution for smelter and electricity system modeling is implemented (see the Methods section and Supplementary Notes 3.2 and 4.2 for details).

Across all scenarios, the cost reductions are primarily driven by lower investment costs in renewable energy and lower operational costs for coal, natural gas, and nuclear energy (mainly fuel costs). Two mechanisms can explain the dominance of these cost reduction sources. First, wind and solar power exhibit high variability, resulting in low utilization rates without flexibility mechanisms such as storage or demand response. Flexible operation enables aluminum smelters to concentrate electricity consumption during periods of high renewable energy generation, thereby improving the efficiency of renewable energy utilization and reducing the required renewable capacity to meet electricity demand. Second, from a reliability perspective, flexible operation also reduces the need for firm energy (energy from non-renewable resources that can be dispatched whenever needed
[
29
]
) to maintain reliability and meet demand during periods of renewable scarcity. The reduction in non-renewable operational costs reflects this second mechanism, which reduces the need for coal- and/or natural gas-based generation (with carbon capture and storage) and nuclear power (see Supplementary 3.4 for details). However, non-renewable investment costs remain relatively unchanged, suggesting that flexible aluminum smelting primarily reduces the utilization of non-renewable resources, rather than replacing the need for non-renewable capacity.

Comparing the different scenarios, we find that the flexibility value of aluminum smelting is higher in scenarios with high aluminum demand, because the aluminum smelting sector is bigger and the impact of load flexibility is therefore larger. As smelter operational flexibility increases, the cost savings from reduced battery capacity become larger; however, these are secondary to reductions in renewable investment and non-renewable operating costs. The overall electricity system cost savings are also similar between the Non-constrained-flexibility scenario and the Mid-flexibility scenario, suggesting diminishing returns in increasing smelter operational flexibility.

We also observe that flexible operation of aluminum smelting results in more investment in heating electrification (purple), such as the deployment of electric heat pumps. The increased investment reflects the fact that aluminum smelters can reduce winter peak loads through flexible operation, thereby enabling the greater deployment of clean heating technologies.

3
Discussion

In power systems with increasing penetration of renewable energy, the volatility of electricity supply is often compounded by load fluctuations from heating electrification, creating significant seasonal variations in electricity costs. Concurrently, many traditional energy-intensive industries face structural overcapacity, which reduces capacity utilization and creates uncertainty on their profitability. This convergence of challenges makes it increasingly urgent to understand how energy-intensive industries can adapt and thrive in decarbonized energy systems.

In this work, we use China’s aluminum smelting industry—a highly energy-intensive sector facing unprecedented overcapacity in the coming recycling era—as a representative case to explore the flexibility value of overcapacity in decarbonized energy systems. We developed a comprehensive framework that co-optimizes power system capacity expansion planning with detailed smelter operations, capturing key technical constraints such as temperature limits, restart costs, and operational limitations. This co-optimization was evaluated across more than 500 scenarios, varying flexibility settings and competing technology costs, to inform cost-effective strategies for retaining overcapacity and leveraging it for seasonal flexibility.

Our findings reveal that overcapacity unlocks seasonal flexibility of smelting electricity use, allowing them to curtail production during the winter when peak demand is exacerbated by heating electrification and reduced renewable output. In the 2050-net-zero scenario, seasonal flexibility reduces annual power system costs by 15-72 billion CNY through improved renewable integration and decreased reliance on firm generation, sufficient to offset the maintenance and operational costs of excess capacity and product storage, thereby cutting aluminum production costs by approximately 9% of the current level. We identify an optimal overcapacity rate of approximately 30% for the aluminum smelting industry in China, which closely matches the fraction of the year that is the winter heating season, indicating that the seasonality of the energy system is the fundamental driver of a seasonal smelter operation paradigm.

Our findings suggest that the increasing seasonality of decarbonized energy systems can be strong enough to drive energy-intensive industries to shift to a seasonal operation paradigm. Energy-intensive industries, such as aluminum, steel, and cement, will be the first to experience these impacts, given their high exposure to energy cost variations and structural overcapacity. As electricity cost variability becomes more seasonal, these sectors can leverage overcapacity and product storage capabilities to adapt to volatile electricity prices, reducing both their own operational expenses and overall energy system costs.

Interestingly, overcapacity can facilitate complementary workforce patterns between energy-intensive industries and the energy sector, which can help mitigate social disruption from energy decarbonization and industrial restructuring. Nevertheless, converting the co-benefits of overcapacity into financial incentives for energy-intensive industries will require careful design of financial instruments, targeted subsidies or market mechanisms, as is required for the seasonally operating agriculture sector.

4
Methods

4.1
Scenario analysis framework

The factors that have significant and uncertain impacts on the value of overcapacity in the aluminum industry include the demand for primary aluminum (from smelting), the operational flexibility of the smelters, and the costs of competitive technologies for load flexibility (batteries, long-duration storage, etc.). This study employs a scenario analysis approach, grouping these factors into three-dimensional parameter sets (Table
1
) to explore the impact of different assumptions and uncertain parameters on the research findings.

In the model, China’s domestic aluminum smelters are required to meet the country’s annual primary aluminum demand, which is equal to the total aluminum demand minus the demand that can be met by recycled aluminum, as recycled aluminum is prioritized for production due to its per-unit mineral, energy, and investment costs being orders of magnitude lower than those for aluminum smelting. We focus exclusively on primary aluminum smelters and do not consider recycled aluminum production to be overcapacity, as its output is currently increasing and does not face overcapacity issues. Additionally, recycled aluminum production has limited grid interaction flexibility value due to its low electricity intensity (only 5% of the electricity used in primary aluminum production) and is not traditionally considered an energy-intensive industry.

Using performance data from numerical simulations of aluminum smelter operations, as well as existing literature on the shutdown, restart processes, and costs of real-world aluminum smelters, we developed a reduced-order model that represents the key characteristics of aluminum electrolytic cell temperature constraints and restart costs (see Supplementary note 3 for more details). However, the cost uncertainty is large because there are few historical cases of aluminum smelters being restarted. Thus, we evaluated three comparative scenarios:
Low: No progress has been made in improving smelter operational flexibility, and restart costs from historical high-cost reports are being used.
High: New technologies and methods have significantly improved smelter operational flexibility, and restart costs have dropped substantially. This represents the goal of ongoing research, development, and demonstration efforts to enhance smelter-grid interaction
[
26
]
.
Un-constrained: This is an idealized scenario that disregards the temperature constraints, startup/shutdown constraints, and restart costs of potlines. It only considers rated power constraints, and the value of grid interaction in this scenario represents the theoretical maximum.

Finally, we consider three main power system technology cost scenarios (see Table 1) that reflect different levels of power generation technology development. All scenarios assume that a variety of competitive, clean, and stable technologies are available, covering a range from high fixed costs and low variable costs to low fixed costs and high variable costs. We also assume the availability of cost-competitive long-duration storage technologies (in the form of hydrogen and hot water) and a certain degree of flexibility in residential heating. By modeling combinations of different scenarios for primary aluminum demand, smelter flexibility, and technology costs, we are able to evaluate the sensitivity of the main power system and aluminum industry outcomes to changes along these dimensions.

4.2
Aluminum demand projections

Our objective is to project China’s primary aluminum demand for the coming decades, which requires projecting both total aluminum demand and the future supply of recycled aluminum. Our methodology is based on the assumption that per capita aluminum in-use stock tends to saturate as the economy develops, a common approach for projecting the demand for metal raw materials like steel and aluminum
[
34
,
22
]
. To obtain historical aluminum in-use stock data, which is not explicitly tracked in official statistics, we sourced historical aluminum production data from the China Statistical Yearbook. We then calculated the aluminum stock by accumulating historical production and accounting for product retirement. Specifically, using China’s primary and secondary aluminum production data from 1993 to 2023 and assuming that the lifespan of aluminum products follows a Weibull distribution, we calculated the annual aluminum in-use stock up to 2024.
Next, we input this data into the EnergyPathways model
[
12
]
, a mature, open-source software for forecasting various types of demand, which has been used in the Net-Zero America
[
19
]
and Net-Zero Australia
[
9
]
projects. Using the stock-based method, with the projected population by
4
as the driver, we selected logistic regression as the extrapolation curve for future aluminum demand. The saturation level is set differently for various scenarios, as detailed in Supplementary Note 1. The EP model projects the annual aluminum stock, total aluminum demand, and retired aluminum products (the available scrap in that year) up to 2060. The calculation formula is as follows:

\text{Stock}_{\text{year}}=\text{Stock}_{\text{year}-1}+\text{Production}_{\text{year}}-\text{Scrap}_{\text{year}}

(1)

Here,
\text{Stock}_{\text{year}}
represents the extrapolated aluminum stock,
\text{Production}_{\text{year}}
is the total demand/production for aluminum, and scrap is calculated from the historical stock based on the assumption that product lifespan follows a Weibull distribution. Therefore, the total aluminum demand and available scrap can be calculated from the above formula. Based on the scrap, we can calculate the recycled aluminum production (
\text{Production}_{\text{recycle}}=\text{Scrap}\times\text{Recycle}_{\text{rate}}
). By subtracting the recycled aluminum production from the total aluminum demand for that year, we obtain the primary aluminum demand/production (
\text{Production}_{\text{primary}}=\text{Production}-\text{Production}_{\text{recycle}}
). China is also a major aluminum exporter. Based on current export levels, we have also established two scenarios: reduced exports and increased exports (see Table
1
), with further details provided in Supplementary Note 1. Having obtained the annual aluminum demand projections, we assume that the hourly aluminum demand remains constant throughout the year, which simplifies demand distribution yet imposes a stricter supply-demand balance. This demand must be satisfied either through production from aluminum smelters or by drawing from existing aluminum inventories.

4.3
Modeling smelter operational flexibility

This study employs a mixed-integer linear model, an initial version of which was developed by Shen et al.
[
31
]
as a linear model for production and energy consumption decisions in smelter operations. The model is capable of representing the relationships between internal temperature changes and electrolysis rates in response to applied voltage and current in a potline, as observed in both numerical simulations and experiments
[
49
,
50
]
. It is transformed into a linear programming model through piecewise linearization and numerical approximation. To model the shutdown and startup decisions and their associated costs for smelters, we introduced startup and shutdown constraints and costs into the aforementioned linear model, transforming it into a mixed-integer model (see Supplementary note 3 and 5). This enables our model to optimize the smelter’s operational decisions over a longer timescale. However, the introduction of integer variables makes it computationally infeasible to directly embed a plant-level operational model into a power system planning model. Therefore, we design a decomposition-based iterative algorithm for solving this problem. To determine the production capacity of smelters, based on the Chinese government’s policy that primary aluminum capacity will not exceed 45 million tonnes per year, we assume that the production capacity of aluminum smelters remains unchanged at the 2024 level of 45 million tonnes per year, unless early retirement is chosen. This assumption is based on the typical lifespan of an aluminum smelter, which exceeds 50 years, and the fact that more than 90% of China’s aluminum smelters were built after 2000.

Since not all provinces have complete statistical data on aluminum smelting capacity, we distributed the national capacity to each province based on its 2023 production. We assumed that the capacity share of each province remains unchanged when reducing overcapacity (see Supplementary Note 3.3 for more details). This approach is justified by the fact that China’s aluminum smelting industry currently operates at a high capacity utilization rate of approximately 97%, meaning that most provinces are running at near full capacity. For estimating the needed workforce for smelters and power plants, we use the average number of workers required per unit of operating capacity, assuming a certain proportion of variable workforce (see Supplementary Note 3.5).

4.4
Smelter costing

The investment, operation, and maintenance costs of aluminum smelters are estimated from annual reports from Chinese smelters
[
1
]
. However, it is challenging to determine the idling and startup/shutdown costs, as there are not many historical cases where smelters were idled for long periods or shut down due to insufficient demand. According to publicly available reports, the normalized cost for restarting potlines can vary from 110,000 CNY/MW to 760,000 CNY/MW
[
44
,
20
,
8
]
, which is 1000 times higher than restarting coal-fired units
[
32
]
. This study does not attempt to predict the future trajectory of smelter restart costs, but rather presents extreme cases in addition to the baseline case to represent the two extremes of the spectrum for the development of flexible aluminum smelting technologies, resulting in a total of four smelter flexibility cases. In the Low-flexibility and Mid-flexibility cases, the smelter flexibility parameters are set based on current literature and reports. The former’s restart cost is estimated at 760,000 CNY/MW in a case of a U.S.-owned smelter. The latter’s restart cost is from a documented restart event at a smelter in Yunnan, China, estimated at 110,000 CNY/MW.
For smelters to conduct seasonal production and help adapt to fluctuations in the power grid’s supply and demand, they may need to increase the storage period of aluminum products to act as a buffer for balancing production and demand. Therefore, we also need to model the warehouse costs of aluminum products. Product warehouse costs are estimated by multiplying the actual short-term warehouse rental fees in each region for 2023 by the required warehouse area
[
7
]
. However, this may lead to an overestimation, as long-term rental fees are typically lower. A more detailed description of the smelter operational cost calculation method is in Supplementary note 3.3.

4.5
Power system capacity expansion model

This study aims to evaluate the load flexibility value of aluminum smelters for China’s decarbonized power system. A provincial resolution is needed because China’s aluminum industry is unevenly distributed across multiple provinces, and the highly interconnected nature of China’s power grid means that the impact of this flexibility is likely to span across provincial boundaries. We set the net-zero year of 2050, even though China’s overall carbon neutrality target is 2060
[
45
]
, since the power sector is generally considered the primary and critical area for achieving this goal and requires deep decarbonization at an earlier stage. According to several studies and scenario analyses, China’s power sector is expected to achieve net-zero emissions between 2045 and 2055
[
25
]
. To compare the effects of different carbon reduction constraints, we set up scenarios with 20%, 60%, and 100% reductions in carbon emissions for 2030, 2040, and 2050, respectively, based on 2020 emission levels.

This study uses PyPSA, an open-source model described in detail elsewhere
[
3
]
, to explore China’s pathway to carbon emission reduction. The model uses 2020 as the base year to formulate a brownfield capacity expansion model, covering thirty-one provincial administrative regions of China, with each province aggregated into a single node. Each node not only handles electricity but also incorporates multiple energy carriers, including heat, hydrogen, biomass, methane, and coal, reflecting the integration of a multi-energy system. The connections between nodes include electricity transmission via high-voltage direct current (HVDC) and high-voltage alternating current (HVAC) lines, as well as hydrogen transmission via hydrogen pipelines. The PyPSA model uses linear optimization to minimize annual operational and investment costs while adhering to technical and physical constraints. The system operation is modeled with an 8,760-hour time resolution over a single meteorological year (2020 as the representative year). The optimization includes the capacity of generators, storage, converters, and transmission, as well as the hourly operational dispatch of each technology. Exogenous variables include: demand curves for different sectors, the ratio of district heating to decentralized heating, the capacity of hydro reservoirs and run-of-river generators, and pumped hydro storage capacity. HVAC and HVDC lines are assumed to be long-lasting and therefore permanent. Thus, transmission network assets can be reinforced but not removed. The detailed input data for the model are primarily derived from the PyPSA China-specific project, originally established and open-sourced by Zhou et al.
[
48
]
, and have been used in peer-reviewed publications.

4.6
Dimensionality reduction and tailored iterative solution

Our objective is to address the computational intractability that arises when integrating detailed smelter operation models (which include integer variables) into a large-scale power system planning model. We adopt an iterative decomposition framework based on the D3R method proposed by Lyu et al.
[
27
]
, which provides a computationally efficient linear approximation of the complex industrial load constraints. The process begins by embedding this linearized smelter model obtained through the D3R framework into the main linear capacity expansion model. Solving this initial integrated model yields a first estimate of electricity system capacity expansion results and the hourly local marginal prices (LMPs) for electricity in each province.
Then, these LMPs are fixed and used as an exogenous price signal for the detailed smelter operational models. This step, a core part of the decomposition, allows each smelter’s 8760-hourly cost-minimization problem (including all unit commitment constraints) to be solved independently and in parallel. Once the detailed optimal operational schedule (i.e., the 8760-hourly load profile) is determined for each smelter, we fix these schedules in the main capacity expansion model and then re-optimize all remaining power system planning and operational variables. This re-optimization generates a new, updated set of LMPs, which are fed back into the detailed smelter models. This entire process is iterated until the objective function of the planning model converges—which our tests show occurs within several iterations—indicating a consistent, optimal solution for both system planning and smelter operation has been found. For more details, see Supplementary note 5.

4.7
Limitations and opportunities

Our study has several limitations that offer avenues for future research. Our model employs a top-down approach, projecting nationwide aluminum demand, which may differ from granular end-use forecasts
[
21
]
. However, our projected trends align with those of bottom-up studies
[
46
]
. We also simplify the distinction between primary/recycled aluminum, as well as market/trade dynamics. Regarding smelter representation, our model aggregates plants at the provincial level (see Supplementary Note 5.2 for details) and, due to computational constraints, assumes fixed capacity ratios between provinces. Future work could leverage efficient frameworks to optimize the spatial distribution of smelter retirement or migration, thereby capturing regional flexibility value, or, with enough data, offer plant-level operational strategies. We also do not differentiate between smelting technologies, retrofitting options, or stoichiometry of aluminum smelting
[
30
,
36
]
. Finally, our co-optimization focuses on the aluminum smelting industry — the most electrified energy-intensive industry and one that is roughly ten times more energy-intensive per tonne of product than steelmaking
[
17
,
15
]
. The aluminum smelting’s high electricity use and operational flexibility make it a prime candidate for studying grid interaction, but subsequent research should explore the co-optimization of multiple energy-intensive sectors to develop a more general understanding of how other energy-intensive industries can collectively manage structural overcapacity and navigate the low-carbon transition. More broadly, the question of how to leverage industrial-energy sector coupling to mitigate the socio-economic impacts of energy decarbonization and industrial restructuring represents a promising future research direction.
