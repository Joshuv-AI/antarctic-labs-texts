Anaesthesia management and perioperative clinical outcomes in caesarean section patients with placental pathology: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Gizem Kırbaş, Ahmet Salih Tüzen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlacental pathologies, particularly placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) disorders and placenta previa, are major causes of maternal morbidity and mortality, largely due to the high risk of massive obstetric hemorrhage. Anesthetic management is crucial in cesarean deliveries involving these conditions, yet limited data exist regarding how anesthesia type influences perioperative outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study evaluated 70 pregnant women who underwent cesarean section for placenta previa or PAS disorders between June 2024 and April 2025. Patients were categorized into general anesthesia (GA) and regional anesthesia (RA) groups. Clinical, surgical, anesthetic, and neonatal data were collected and compared between groups. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on the propensity score was applied to minimize baseline confounding, followed by weighted regression analyses to estimate adjusted associations.ResultsGA was used in 65.7% of cases, often in patients with placenta percreta and those requiring radical hysterectomy. In unadjusted analyses, GA was associated with higher estimated blood loss (1000 mL vs. 715 mL, p = 0.047), higher transfusion needs (87.0% vs. 54.2%, p = 0.004), increased maternal ICU admission (76.1% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.001), longer ICU (2 vs. 0 days, p = 0.002) and hospital stays (6 vs. 4 days, p = 0.008), and lower neonatal APGAR scores at 1 and 5 min (p = 0.008 and p = 0.009, respectively). After adjustment using IPTW, GA remained significantly associated with a higher likelihood of maternal ICU admission and longer ICU length of stay. Associations between anesthesia type and transfusion requirement, neonatal ICU admission, hospital length of stay, postoperative complications, and maternal mortality were attenuated and did not reach statistical significance after weighting.ConclusionIn this retrospective cohort of cesarean deliveries complicated by placenta previa or PAS disorders, RA was associated with more favorable unadjusted maternal and neonatal outcomes. After accounting for baseline differences using IPTW, GA remained associated with increased maternal ICU utilization, reflecting its predominant use in clinically more complex and higher-risk cases.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03583-0.

Introduction

Placental pathology during pregnancy, especially conditions involving abnormal placental implantation, such as placenta previa and disorders within the placenta accreta spectrum (PAS), poses a significant and growing challenge to obstetric care. These disorders are strongly associated with severe maternal morbidity and mortality, primarily due to their propensity for causing massive obstetric hemorrhage, difficult surgical dissections, and an elevated risk of hysterectomy and intensive care admission [1, 2]. Among them, PAS disorders—including placenta accreta, increta, and percreta—are characterized by pathological adherence or invasion of the placental tissue into the myometrium and, in the most severe cases, beyond the uterine serosa and into adjacent pelvic organs such as the bladder [3–8]. The incidence of PAS has seen a significant global rise, closely correlated with the increasing rates of cesarean deliveries and other uterine surgical interventions. Studies have estimated that PAS affects approximately 1 in 272 pregnancies, with prevalence varying depending on the presence of risk factors such as prior cesarean section, placenta previa, and uterine curettage [9, 10]. Placenta previa, defined by the implantation of the placenta over or near the internal cervical os, is also associated with increased risks of hemorrhage, preterm delivery, and operative complications. When combined with PAS, the clinical scenario becomes even more complex, often necessitating multi-disciplinary team involvement and specialized surgical and anesthetic approaches [11, 12].

Anesthetic management in these cases is central to optimizing maternal and neonatal outcomes, given the hemodynamic instability that often accompanies surgical treatment of these pathologies. The choice between general anesthesia (GA) and regional anesthesia (RA) is nuanced and depends on several preoperative and intraoperative considerations. RA, typically spinal or combined spinal-epidural, is often preferred for its favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes, including lower rates of thromboembolic events, reduced postoperative pain, and decreased neonatal exposure to anesthetic agents [13, 14]. However, GA is frequently employed in scenarios involving anticipated massive blood loss, placenta percreta, surgical urgency, or maternal hemodynamic instability. Moreover, emergent conversions from RA to GA intraoperatively are not uncommon in these high-risk settings [15].

Despite the important role of anesthetic planning, much of the current literature focuses disproportionately on the surgical and hematologic aspects of placenta previa and PAS, such as surgical techniques and transfusion strategies. Limited research has systematically examined how different anesthetic strategies influence perioperative outcomes, including maternal morbidity, transfusion requirements, surgical duration, and neonatal condition. Current clinical practice is primarily guided by institutional experience and expert consensus rather than robust empirical data, highlighting a significant gap in evidence-based recommendations for anesthesiologists managing these complex cases [16–18].

Conventional unadjusted comparisons between anesthesia groups may be misleading, as observed differences in outcomes may primarily reflect underlying case complexity rather than the independent impact of anesthetic technique. Because anesthetic selection in PAS disorders is inherently influenced by surgical complexity and maternal risk, robust statistical adjustment is essential to distinguish anesthetic effects from disease severity. This retrospective cohort study was designed to address this critical gap by evaluating the anesthetic management strategies employed in a tertiary referral center and their associations with perioperative outcomes in 70 patients diagnosed intraoperatively with placenta previa or PAS abnormalities, using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to reduce confounding by indication. Specifically, we aim to analyze the utilization of GA versus RA, conversion rates, transfusion needs, intraoperative complications, and maternal and neonatal outcomes. The findings of this study are expected to provide meaningful insights into best practices and inform the development of future anesthetic guidelines for managing abnormal placentation.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective cohort study was conducted by reviewing medical records of patients who underwent cesarean section due to placental anomalies between 01/04/2024 and 01/04/2025 in Gaziantep City Hospital. Patients were analysed in two groups as GA group and RA group. Patients who started surgery with RA and then switched to GA were excluded. The study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Gaziantep City Hospital’s Health Sciences Ethics Committee (Approval No: 202/2025). The Committee waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the retrospective design, based on anonymized clinical data, and no risk to participants. This retrospective cohort study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [19].

Study population and eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were: Age ≥ 18 years, diagnosis of placental pathology confirmed intraoperatively, and complete availability of perioperative clinical and anesthetic data in the hospital records. Exclusion criteria included: Patients aged under 18 years, concomitant surgical procedures unrelated to cesarean section (appendectomy or ovarian mass excision), in cases where the surgical procedure commenced under RA but required intraoperative conversion to GA, and incomplete or missing medical records that would compromise the validity of outcome analysis. All cesarean sections included in the study were performed under urgent or emergent indications; no elective procedures were included.

Institutional anesthesia management protocols

Anesthetic care for PAS obstetric surgery is governed by an institutional protocol at Gaziantep City Hospital. RA is conducted to single-shot spinal anesthesia using 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine (8–10 mg) with fentanyl (20 µg), targeting a sensory block to at least T4. Hemodynamic management aims to maintain systolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg or ≥ 80% of baseline; hypotension is treated with ephedrine boluses as first-line vasopressor therapy, together with intravenous crystalloid coloading (10–15 mL/kg). Supplemental oxygen is delivered via facemask throughout. After delivery, intravenous non-opioid analgesics (paracetamol 15 mg/kg) are administered. Patients are transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) when hemodynamically stable, and discharged from the PACU once stable vital signs, adequate pain control, normothermia, and full lower-extremity motor recovery are confirmed. GA follows a fixed institutional sequence. Induction is achieved with propofol (2–3 mg/kg) and rocuronium (0.8–1.2 mg/kg), followed by endotracheal intubation using direct or video laryngoscopy as appropriate. Anesthesia is maintained with a 50:50 mixture of oxygen and air and 1.0% sevoflurane until delivery. After cord clamping, fentanyl (1–2 µg/kg) is administered for analgesia. Controlled ventilation is maintained with an end-tidal CO₂ target of 32–36 mmHg. At the end of surgery, intravenous paracetamol (15 mg/kg) is administered as a non-opioid analgesic, neuromuscular blockade is reversed with sugammadex (4 mg/kg), and awake extubation is performed after confirming adequate spontaneous ventilation and intact protective airway reflexes. All patients are observed in the PACU for at least one hour and are transferred to the obstetric ward or intensive care unit (ICU) once hemodynamically stable and fully responsive. Institutional monitoring follows standardized American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) guidelines and includes continuous electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, non-invasive blood pressure measurement, and capnography. Invasive arterial pressure monitoring is employed as an advanced modality in patients with suspected PAS, anticipated massive hemorrhage, or whenever continuous blood pressure assessment is clinically indicated. Urine output is routinely monitored in all cases using a Foley catheter to assess renal perfusion and guide fluid management.

Maternal ICU admission follows institutional criteria including hemodynamic instability requiring vasopressor support, massive transfusion, ongoing coagulopathy, mechanical ventilation, or complex surgery. Patients remain in the ICU until stable hemodynamics are maintained without vasopressors, coagulopathy is corrected, spontaneous ventilation is adequate, urine output exceeds 0.5 mL/kg/h, and multidisciplinary consensus deems ward transfer safe.

Institutional transfusion, hemostatic management and adjunctive pharmacotherapy protocol

Transfusion management for obstetric patients is guided by a standardized institutional protocol. Transfusion triggers are defined as hemoglobin < 8 g/dL in hemodynamically stable patients or at higher thresholds in the presence of ongoing hemorrhage, symptomatic anemia, or hemodynamic instability. Massive transfusion protocol activation is initiated for an estimated blood loss > 1500 mL, transfusion of ≥ 4 units of erythrocyte suspension (ES) within 1 h, or anticipated total loss > 2500 mL. Massive transfusion protocol component ratio is maintained at approximately 1:1:1 (ES: fresh frozen plasma (FFP): platelet concentrate), with cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate added when fibrinogen < 2 g/L. Calcium gluconate 10 mL (1 g) is administered for every four units of transfused blood products. All transfusion decisions and massive transfusion protocol activations are coordinated jointly by the attending anesthesiologist, obstetric surgeon, and blood bank team.

Tranexamic acid (1 g IV) is routinely administered within three minutes after cord clamping, unless contraindicated by a known thromboembolic disorder. A second 1 g dose may be administered if active bleeding persists or if blood loss becomes excessive. Following delivery, uterotonic agents are administered sequentially to promote uterine contraction and minimize hemorrhage. The standard regimen consists of oxytocin 5 IU IV bolus, followed by a continuous infusion of 20 IU diluted in 500 mL crystalloid at 125 mL/h. In cases of persistent uterine atony, carboprost tromethamine 250 µg IM may be repeated at 15–20 min intervals to a maximum cumulative dose of 2 mg, and misoprostol 800 µg rectally may be added as a secondary agent. All pharmacologic interventions are administered collaboratively by the attending obstetrician and anesthesiologist in accordance with institutional obstetric hemorrhage guidelines. Also, when clinically indicated and resources permit, interventional radiology procedures, including arterial embolization, iliac artery balloon catheterization or intra-aortic balloon occlusion, may be employed as adjuncts to surgical hemostasis in selected PAS cases. In our institution, the decision to implement these measures is made by the multidisciplinary team, based on preoperative imaging findings, anticipated bleeding risk, and intraoperative hemodynamic stability.

Variables and outcomes

Data were retrieved from the hospital information system, operative notes, anesthesia records, and postoperative follow-up forms. A standardized data abstraction template was used to ensure consistency across variables and minimize the risk of selection or information bias. Data were anonymized during extraction and stored on secure institutional servers for analysis.

Outcomes included in IPTW-weighted regression analyses were transfusion needs, maternal ICU admission, neonatal ICU admission, ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, hysterectomy, postoperative complications, and maternal mortality. The study’s primary outcome was to compare the need for intensive care admission and length of stay in intensive care between patients who underwent GA and those who underwent RA during caesarean section due to placental pathology. Secondary outcomes included intraoperative fluid resuscitation volume (crystalloids and colloids), operative urgency (emergency vs. elective), delivery-to-incision time, type of surgical incision, and maternal mortality if it occurred. Additionally, neonatal outcomes such as 1-minute and 5-minute APGAR scores and neonatal ICU admission were analyzed to assess the indirect effects of anesthetic strategy on neonatal well-being.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 2024.09.0 + 375). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables and as median [IQR 25–75] for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%). For comparisons between two independent groups, the Independent Samples t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. All primary analyses were conducted in the unmatched full cohort to preserve statistical power. To evaluate the impact of anesthesia type on clinical outcomes while minimizing confounding bias, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on the propensity score was applied. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included key baseline covariates: surgical incision type, urgency of cesarean section, ASA physical status classification, placental pathology (accreta, increta, percreta), maternal age, gestational age, gravida, and parity. Stabilized IPTW weights were calculated using the marginal probability of receiving GA versus RA to reduce variance inflation and maintain the representativeness of the weighted sample. Application of IPTW created a weighted pseudo-population in which the distribution of baseline covariates was independent of anesthesia type. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD < 0.10 indicating excellent balance and SMD < 0.25 considered acceptable. All covariates demonstrated substantial improvement in balance after IPTW application, and all post-weighting SMDs fell below acceptable thresholds. Following IPTW, weighted logistic, linear, and Firth logistic regression models were fitted to estimate adjusted associations between anesthesia type and clinical outcomes, including transfusion need, neonatal and maternal ICU admission, length of hospital stay, hysterectomy, postoperative complications, and mortality. Effect estimates were reported as odds ratios (ORs) or β coefficients, accompanied by 95% confidence intervals. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A power analysis was conducted using RStudio (version 2024.09.0 + 375) to estimate the minimum sample size required to detect a clinically meaningful difference in ICU admission rate between patients who received GA versus RA during cesarean section for PAS disorders. Based on the multivariable logistic regression results from similar study GA was significantly associated with increased ICU admission (odds ratio [OR] = 4.19; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.39–7.35; p < 0.001) [15]. This association corresponds to a Cohen’s h effect size of 0.70. Using a two-sided test with an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.80, the estimated minimum sample size required was approximately 64 patients. Allowing for a potential 10% dropout rate, the final sample size was detect to 70 patients.

Results

A total of 87 patients were assessed for eligibility for inclusion in the study. 17 patients were excluded due to predefined criteria. 70 patients met the inclusion criteria and were subsequently enrolled. Among these, 46 patients received GA, while 24 patients received RA. All included patients were analyzed for perioperative outcomes according to their assigned anesthesia group (Fig. 1).

Unadjusted baseline characteristics and surgical profiles

Baseline demographic, obstetric, and surgical characteristics of the unweighted cohort are summarized in Table 1. Of the patients included in the study, 46 (65.7%) received GA, while 24 (34.3%) underwent RA. The median maternal age was 30 years [27–35], with no statistically significant difference between the GA and RA groups (p = 0.133). Most patients were classified as ASA II (90.0%), with no significant intergroup difference (p = 0.569). Median gestational age at delivery was 34 weeks [33–36], also similar between groups (p = 0.083). Gravida and parity values showed a trend toward higher numbers in the GA group, but the differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.058 and p = 0.153, respectively). All cesarean sections were performed under urgent or emergent indications. Maternal indications for surgery were more common in the GA group, whereas fetal indications were similar between groups (p = 0.045 and p = 0.400, respectively). Significant differences were observed in surgical approach, with Pfannenstiel incision used more frequently in RA patients (62.5% vs. 19.6%, p = 0.001). Placenta previa was present in 34.3% of patients, and PAS disorders were identified in 75.7%, including accreta (24.3%), increta (4.3%), and percreta (47.1%). Placenta percreta was more frequently managed under GA than RA (54.3% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.017) (Table 1).

Unadjusted hemorrhagic, maternal, and neonatal outcomes

Unadjusted perioperative outcomes are presented in Table 2. Preoperative hemorrhage was observed in 30.0% of all patients, with no significant difference between the GA (26.1%) and RA (37.5%) groups (p = 0.412). However, the need for blood transfusion was significantly higher in the GA group compared to the RA group (87.0% vs. 54.2%, p = 0.004). Median estimated intraoperative blood loss was significantly greater in the GA group 1000 mL [700–1500] than in the RA group 715 mL [475–1350], p = 0.047). Accordingly, the GA group required more ES units (2 [2–3] vs. 1 [0–2], p = 0.002) (Table 2).

Preoperative and postoperative laboratory parameters

Preoperative and postoperative laboratory parameters are presented in Table 3, while comparative analyses of changes between the preoperative and postoperative groups are provided in Table 4. Baseline (preoperative) laboratory values, including hemoglobin, platelet count, coagulation, and biochemical parameters, were similar between the GA and RA groups (all p > 0.05). Preoperative coagulation profiles, fibrinogen levels, and other markers showed no significant intergroup differences (all p > 0.05) (Table 3). Postoperatively, hemoglobin levels decreased in both groups. Although the drop in hemoglobin was more pronounced in the RA group (from 11.56 to 10.11 g/dL, p = 0.001) than in the GA group (from 11.20 to 10.97 g/dL, p = 0.354). Platelet counts also declined significantly in the RA group (p < 0.001) and showed a borderline decrease in the GA group (p = 0.088). Fibrinogen levels decreased significantly postoperatively in both groups (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

IPTW-adjusted associations between anesthesia type and clinical outcomes

To minimize confounding and account for baseline differences between the anesthesia groups, an IPTW approach based on the propensity score was applied. Covariate balance before and after weighting is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Prior to weighting, substantial imbalance was observed for several clinically relevant variables, including placental pathology, incision type, and urgency of cesarean section. After weighting, covariate balance substantially improved across all included covariates, and all post-weighting SMDs fell below accepted thresholds, indicating adequate balance between groups (Supplementary Table S1).

IPTW-weighted regression analyses were performed to estimate adjusted associations between anesthesia type and clinical outcomes (Table 5). IPTW-weighted regression analyses demonstrated that GA was independently associated with a higher likelihood of maternal ICU admission (OR = 6.20, 95% CI: 1.26–30.61, p = 0.026), and longer ICU length of stay (β = 1.44 days, 95% CI: 0.15–2.73, p = 0.030). In contrast, associations observed in unadjusted analyses for transfusion requirement and hospital length of stay were attenuated after IPTW and did not reach statistical significance. GA was not independently associated with neonatal ICU admission, postoperative complications, or maternal mortality in weighted analyses. Although effect estimates consistently suggested higher risk with GA, confidence intervals were wide and statistical significance was not reached (Table 5).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study aimed to evaluate the relationship between anesthetic modality, and perioperative outcomes in pregnant women undergoing cesarean section for placental pathologies, including placenta previa and placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) disorders, using IPTW to mitigate confounding by indication. After statistical adjustment, GA remained independently associated with increased maternal ICU admission and longer ICU length of stay, whereas several differences observed in unadjusted analyses, such as transfusion requirement and total hospital length of stay, were attenuated and no longer statistically significant. These findings represent adjusted associations rather than causal effects, given the observational design.

Placental abnormalities such as previa and PAS are among the most serious causes of maternal morbidity and mortality globally, primarily due to their strong association with massive obstetric hemorrhage, emergency hysterectomy, and multiorgan complications [7, 8, 15, 20, 21]. The risk of PAS is significantly elevated in women with a history of multiple cesarean sections, uterine surgeries, advanced maternal age, and assisted reproductive technologies [22]. In the present study, conducted in a region with high parity and gravida rates, PAS pathology was frequent, and advanced forms -especially placenta percreta- were common. Prior population-based studies have demonstrated that multiparity is associated with increased risks of placenta previa, placental abruption, and abnormal placental implantation, even after adjustment for confounders [23, 24]. Mechanistically, repetitive pregnancies and deliveries may impair spiral artery remodeling, induce endometrial scarring, and compromise uteroplacental blood flow, which predispose the placenta to pathological changes [1, 25, 26]. Our findings of increased placental pathology in a high-gravida, high-parity cohort align with these observations, reinforcing the importance of close monitoring and targeted maternal–fetal health interventions in multiparous populations.

In unadjusted analyses, GA was more frequently employed in patients with advanced PAS pathology, particularly placenta percreta, and in those undergoing complex surgical interventions such as peripartum hysterectomy. This pattern reflects standard clinical practice and guideline recommendations, which favor GA in settings of anticipated massive hemorrhage, surgical escalation, or hemodynamic instability [9, 11, 12, 27–30]. The significantly greater estimated blood loss and transfusion volumes observed in the GA group are in line with existing literature that links PAS disorders, especially increta and percreta, to extensive vascular invasion and disruption of the myometrial and pelvic vasculature [3, 10, 31]. Non-adjusted comparisons showed higher blood loss, transfusion requirements, ICU admission rates, and longer hospital stays in the GA group. However, these differences largely reflect baseline disease severity and surgical complexity rather than independent anesthetic effects, a distinction that becomes evident after IPTW adjustment. After weighting, GA remained significantly associated with maternal ICU admission and prolonged ICU stay, suggesting that these outcomes may be less sensitive to baseline disease severity and more closely linked to perioperative physiological stress, anesthetic-related hemodynamic effects, or postoperative management pathways. Prior studies have emphasized that GA, although lifesaving in critical scenarios, may be associated with prolonged postoperative recovery, higher incidence of hypotension requiring vasopressors, and increased use of invasive monitoring [9, 32]. In contrast, the association between GA and transfusion requirement, which was strong in unadjusted analyses, was attenuated after IPTW and did not reach statistical significance. This finding suggests that transfusion burden is primarily driven by the extent of placental invasion and surgical complexity rather than anesthetic modality itself. Similarly, total hospital length of stay did not differ significantly between anesthesia groups after adjustment, reinforcing the importance of accounting for confounding by indication when interpreting perioperative outcomes.

From a neonatal perspective, unadjusted analyses showed higher 1- and 5-minute APGAR scores in the RA group, consistent with prior studies reporting favorable immediate neonatal outcomes under neuraxial techniques [12, 20, 33]. However, after IPTW adjustment, GA was not independently associated with neonatal ICU admission, indicating that differences in early neonatal condition may be influenced by gestational age, fetal indication for delivery, or maternal disease severity rather than anesthetic exposure alone. These findings align with previous reports suggesting that while neuraxial anesthesia may improve immediate neonatal adaptation, its effect on more distal neonatal outcomes is less consistent [15, 20].

Laboratory findings demonstrated postoperative decreases in hemoglobin, platelet count, and fibrinogen levels in both groups, reflecting the hemostatic burden of PAS surgery. Although unadjusted analyses suggested more pronounced coagulopathy in the GA group, these differences should be interpreted cautiously, as patients receiving GA were more likely to undergo hysterectomy and receive larger volumes of blood products. Consumptive and dilutional coagulopathy is well described in severe PAS and massive transfusion settings, irrespective of anesthetic technique [34]. The persistence of postoperative hematologic derangements in both groups underscores the need for vigilant perioperative monitoring and protocolized transfusion strategies. Previous studies have demonstrated that dedicated placenta accreta teams, composed of maternal-fetal medicine specialists, anesthesiologists, neonatologists, urologists, and vascular surgeons, significantly reduce blood loss, operative time, ICU stay, and maternal morbidity [32, 34]. Our study supports these conclusions and highlights the necessity of centralized, coordinated care in high-risk obstetric populations [35, 36]. The decision regarding anesthesia type should be individualized, based on clinical risk factors, placental invasion depth, anticipated surgical complexity, and institutional experience. Importantly, IPTW-adjusted analyses did not demonstrate statistically significant associations between GA and postoperative complications or maternal mortality, although point estimates consistently favored higher risk with GA. While GA is frequently necessary in patients with advanced placental invasion and complex surgical requirements, its association with increased maternal ICU utilization persists even after adjustment, suggesting a higher overall physiological and postoperative care burden. Conversely, RA appears to be a safe and effective option in carefully selected patients with less extensive disease, provided that experienced surgical teams, adequate preparation, and rapid conversion protocols are available. However, it is equally important to recognize that GA remains the modality of choice in high-risk or emergent cases, where hemodynamic instability, surgical unpredictability, or airway concerns dictate its use. The implementation of a multidisciplinary care model is a cornerstone in the successful management of PAS disorders and was consistently utilized in our institution.

This study has several noteworthy strengths that enhance the validity and relevance of its findings. First, it focuses on a highly specialized and clinically challenging population managed in a tertiary referral center, comprehensive perioperative data collection, and the application of IPTW to address confounding by indication. By incorporating both intraoperative and early postoperative variables, including transfusion requirements, ICU admissions, and neonatal APGAR scores, the study offers a multidimensional perspective on anesthetic impact. Importantly, the inclusion of all patients diagnosed intraoperatively with PAS or previa, rather than pre-selecting only high-risk cases, ensures the generalizability of the findings to routine clinical practice. At the same time, this study has several limitations. First, the retrospective and non-randomized design introduces potential selection bias, as the choice of anesthetic modality was based on clinician judgment rather than standardized criteria. Although IPTW-weighted models were applied to balance confounders, unmeasured or residual confounding may still influence the results. Second, the relatively small sample size may limit the statistical power to detect differences in rare outcomes such as maternal mortality or surgical complications. Third, this study did not assess long-term maternal or neonatal outcomes, such as breastfeeding rates, neurodevelopmental outcomes, or quality of life, limiting generalizability beyond the immediate perioperative period. Fourth, preoperative diagnostic information regarding antenatal suspicion or confirmation of PAS was not consistently available in the hospital records. The absence of this data limited our ability to assess its influence on anesthetic planning and may represent a source of residual selection bias. Finally, while this study reflects real-world practice in a tertiary center, institutional protocols and surgeon experience may vary, limiting extrapolation to other settings.

Conclusion

After IPTW adjustment, GA remained associated with higher maternal ICU admission and longer ICU length of stay among patients undergoing cesarean section for placenta previa or PAS disorders. In contrast, several differences observed in unadjusted comparisons - higher estimated blood loss and transfusion rates and lower neonatal APGAR scores - were attenuated after weighting and were no longer statistically significant, consistent with confounding by indication and baseline disease severity.
