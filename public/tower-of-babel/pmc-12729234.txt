From trigeminal ganglion to cortex: ATG7 emerges as a key integrator of migraine pathways via multi-omics profiling
Authors: Chengcheng Zhang, Yine Song, Baicheng Cao, Yuancan Pan, Yuhan Liu, Jiangyan Wei, Libin Zheng, Lu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine is a complex neurological disorder with poorly understood molecular mechanisms. Despite advances in genetic and omics research, the shared mechanisms between central and peripheral nervous systems in migraine pathogenesis remain unclear.MethodsWe employed a multi-omics approach, integrating human trigeminal ganglion (TG) single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data from eight major cortical cell types. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was used to prioritize susceptibility genes, followed by functional enrichment, molecular network mapping, and computational drug screening. Key findings were experimentally validated in the primary sensory cortex hindlimb area brain region and TG, given their established roles in pain processing.ResultsWe identified 586 migraine-associated genes in TG and 1,108 in the cortex, with 109 overlapping genes. These overlapping genes converge on pathways including autophagy and neuroinflammation, suggesting shared mechanisms of central and peripheral nervous systems. Five hub genes - HSP90AB1, EGFR, ERBB3, MET and ATG7 - were implicated in both TG and cortical tissues. Experimental validation identified five hub genes strongly linked to migraine, with ATG7 emerging as a key candidate. Immunofluorescence co-localization revealed ATG7’s prominent expression in both cortical astrocytes and neurons, suggesting its dual role in glial and neuronal pathways underlying migraine pathophysiology. Western blot analysis revealed that in the S1HL brain region of migraine model mice, the protein level of LC3-II showed an increasing trend, while the expressions of both LC3-I and p62 exhibited decreasing trends compared to the control group. Furthermore, both the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and the LC3-II/p62 ratio were significantly elevated in the model group, suggesting that the upregulation of ATG7 promotes the activation of autophagic flux in the migraine model, with the autophagic flux remaining unobstructed.ConclusionsOur study provides novel insights into migraine’s central and peripheral mechanisms, highlighting cell-type-specific genetic contributions and potential therapeutic targets. The integrative framework combining snRNA-seq, eQTL, GWAS, and MR enhances the understanding of migraine biology and accelerates drug discovery, offering a pathway toward more effective treatments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02222-w.Keywords: Migraine, Multi-omics, Autophagy, ATG7, Therapeutic targets

Background

Migraine is a complex neurological disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of severe headache, often accompanied by sensory hypersensitivity, nausea, and autonomic dysfunction [1]. Globally, it affects an estimated 1.1 billion individuals [2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, migraine has a worldwide prevalence of 15.2% and ranks among the top 20 causes of years lived with disability at the individual disease level [3, 4]. The socioeconomic burden of migraine is substantial, resulting in significant healthcare expenditures and productivity losses [5, 6]. Despite extensive research, the precise molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying migraine pathogenesis remain incompletely understood, hindering the development of effective therapies.

Current hypotheses regarding migraine pathogenesis involve a complex interplay between the central and peripheral nervous systems (CNS and PNS). The trigeminovascular system, particularly the trigeminal ganglion (TG), serves as a critical relay station for craniofacial pain transmission [7], while cortical spreading depression (CSD) is implicated in the aura phase [8, 9]. Dysregulation of neurotransmitters, such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), serotonin, and glutamate, further contributes to migraine pathophysiology [10]. However, existing preventive and acute treatments, including triptans, CGRP antagonists, and beta-blockers, exhibit limited efficacy and significant side effects [11]. This therapeutic gap highlights the critical need to systematically explore novel targets from both CNS and PNS perspectives. A unilateral focus on either system risks overlooking crucial pathogenic intersections - particularly the bidirectional crosstalk between trigeminovascular pathways and central pain processing networks [12, 13]. Thus, comprehensive investigations simultaneously targeting CNS and PNS elements could reveal synergistic therapeutic opportunities, yielding treatments with improved efficacy. This dual-system approach may fundamentally advance our ability to discover the pathogenesis of migraine.

Recent advances in single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) have revolutionized our ability to dissect cellular heterogeneity and gene expression profiles at unprecedented resolution. This technology enables the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across distinct cell types in tissues, providing unprecedented opportunities to dissect the cellular heterogeneity of CNS and PNS, and identify disease-relevant molecular pathways [14–17]. Meanwhile, Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a powerful tool to infer causal relationships between genetic variants and disease risk by leveraging genome-wide association study (GWAS) data [18, 19]. Migraine, though non-fatal, imposes a significant burden on patients, yet the lack of diseased brain tissue samples has hindered traditional target discovery. The scarcity of disease-state data complicates conventional analyses, necessitating alternative strategies to identify causal genes. To address this, we employed an integrative approach, leveraging both central and peripheral data, and combining MR with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and snRNA-seq datasets. This multi-modal framework enabled the identification of key pathways and candidate targets, despite limited tissue-related data. Our strategy highlights how integrating genetic and transcriptomic datasets can overcome tissue accessibility constraints, advancing understanding of migraine pathogenesis and potential therapeutic interventions. This integrative strategy has been widely applied to discover new therapeutic targets [20–22].

In this study, we employed a multi-omics approach to unravel the molecular underpinnings of migraine. First, we obtained PNS-derived snRNA-seq data from human TG to characterize cell-type-specific DEGs. We then identified CNS-derived single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the DEGs across eight major cortical cell types. Using MR analysis with migraine GWAS data, we pinpointed genes strongly associated with migraine susceptibility. Subsequent bioinformatic analyses, including functional enrichment, molecular network mapping, and computational drug screening, were conducted to investigate migraine pathogenesis from both CNS and PNS perspectives. We selected the primary sensory cortex hindlimb area (S1HL) brain region in the CNS and the TG in the PNS for experimental validation, as these areas have been demonstrated to be highly associated with pain [23–28]. Our experimental validation culminated in the identification of five pivotal hub genes, with ATG7 emerging as particularly noteworthy. Importantly, immunofluorescence (IF) revealed prominent ATG7 expression in both cortical astrocytes and neurons. Western blot (WB) analysis established that the upregulation of ATG7 drives autophagic flux activation in cortex. These findings position ATG7 as a promising molecular nexus warranting further investigation in migraine pathogenesis.

Our study provides novel insights into the central and peripheral mechanisms of migraine, highlighting the cell-type-specific genetic architecture of migraine and identifying promising therapeutic targets. By integrating snRNA-seq, eQTL, GWAS datasets and MR, we offer a robust framework for uncovering disease-relevant genes and pathways. These findings not only enhance our understanding of migraine biology but also pave the way for more effective treatments, potentially reducing the time and cost associated with drug development.

Methods

Processing of obtained single-nucleus RNA sequencing data

This study utilizes snRNA-seq data from human TG, which can be accessed through the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository under accession number GSE197289 [29]. According to the original description, samples were sequenced using the 10X Genomics Chromium device, and human nuclei with at least 1,000 unique genes, fewer than 15,000 total unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), and less than 5% of counts derived from mitochondrial genes were included in the analysis.

For subsequent analyses, we used the NormalizeData() function in the R package ‘Seurat’(version 5.1.0) [30] to normalize based on total gene expression and applied a scaling factor of 10,000, followed by a log transformation of the results. The FindVariableFeatures() function was used to identify features in the dataset with high intercellular variation. Expression levels for each gene across all cells were standardized with the ScaleData() function, and linear dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA). Based on the cell types provided in the source data, we visualized cell clusters using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) methods. We conducted differential expression analysis for each cell cluster using the FindAllMarkers() function, applying the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test and setting a minimum expression threshold for marker genes at 25%. The cell-type specific DEGs with an adjusted p-value less than 0.05 in each cell subtype were considered significant and were used in subsequent analyses.

eQTL datasets

Due to the lack of eQTL data related to the human TG, we obtained brain eQTL data from the PsychENCODE consortia (http://resource.psychencode.org) [31] and searched for eQTLs of the DEGs identified from the snRNA-seq analysis. The brain eQTL dataset included 1,387 samples from the prefrontal cortex, predominantly of European ancestry (Table 1). We downloaded all significant eQTLs (FDR < 0.05) for genes with expression levels exceeding 0.1 fragments per kilobase per million mapped fragments in at least 10 samples, along with comprehensive SNPs data.

We obtained the SNPs for each DEG from a study that conducted snRNA-seq on eight major cell types (astrocytes, endothelial cells, excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, microglia, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells/committed oligodendrocyte precursors (OPCs/COPs), and pericytes) in human brain [32]. This study’s data were primarily derived from the prefrontal and temporal cortex of 192 European individuals. The researchers quantified the expression of 14,595 genes and genotyped 5.3 million SNPs (Table 1).

Migraine GWAS datasets

In this study, the migraine summary statistics were sourced from a meta-analysis of GWAS conducted by the International Headache Genetics Consortium (IHGC) [33]. To protect participant privacy, data from the 23andMe cohort were excluded from the GWAS summary statistics used. The resulting dataset included 589,356 individuals of European ancestry, with 48,975 migraine cases and 540,381 controls (Table 1).

Two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis

Since migraine is a non-fatal disorder and brain tissue samples from clinical patients are difficult to obtain, this study conducted MR analysis using eQTLs corresponding to cell-type specific DEGs from snRNA-seq results of the human TG, along with migraine GWAS data, to investigate genetic factors associated with the pathogenesis of migraine. We performed MR analysis using the R package ‘TwoSampleMR’(version 0.5.11) [34].

We selected genes and their corresponding SNPs that meet the criteria of an adjusted P-value less than 0.05 in differential expression analysis and have a corresponding relationship in brain eQTL data. Subsequently, these SNPs were clumped using European samples from the 1000 Genomes Project [35], selecting SNPs within the same gene that met clumping criteria of a window size less than 10,000 kb and an r2 less than 0.001. These SNPs were harmonized to remove palindromic SNPs, and the filtered SNPs served as instrumental variables (IVs) for the MR analysis. When only one SNP was available, we used the Wald ratio method. When multiple SNPs were available, we applied the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method with random effects [36]. Additionally, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted: Cochran’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity among SNPs [37], and MR Egger’s intercept was used to evaluate pleiotropy [38].

To ensure the inclusion of sufficient SNPs, we set a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10− 4 for the eQTL data of eight major brain cell types, as done in previous study [39]. The subsequent analysis followed the same procedure as before. A p-value threshold of 0.05 was considered significant.

Enrichment analysis

To explore the functional characteristics and biological relevance of significant genes, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses using the R package ‘clusterProfiler’(version 4.13.3) [40] on three groups: genes in the TG that were significant in the MR analysis (TG-derived DEGs), genes in brain cells that were significant in the MR analysis (brain-derived DEGs), and significant genes common to both (the shared DEGs). The GO analysis results included three terms: Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). The KEGG results provided information on related metabolic pathways.

Protein-protein interaction network construction

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network can visually display interactions between proteins encoded by genes that were significant in the MR analysis. We used STRING (https://string-db.org/) [41] to construct PPI networks for genes in the intersection group, keeping the parameters at their default settings. Additionally, we used the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) method from the ‘cytohubba’ plugin in Cytoscape [42] to calculate highly aggregated modules and hub genes in the PPI network.

Candidate drug prediction and molecular docking

Drug Signatures Database (DSigDB, http://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) [43] is a sizable database with 22,527 gene sets and 17,389 unique compounds spanning 19,531 genes. We uploaded the genes in the intersection group to DSigDB to predict candidate drugs and evaluate the pharmacological activity of the identified genes.

We selected the top five hub genes with the highest connectivity in the PPI network and the top five ranked drugs with the greatest potential, analyzing the binding energies between candidate drugs and these hub genes through molecular docking calculations to explore their interaction patterns. We identified the best conformation according to the lowest energy conformation in the group with the most clusters and calculated the binding energy. By identifying ligands with high binding affinity and favorable interaction modes, we can prioritize these drugs for experimental validation and refine the design of potential drug candidates. Drug structure data were obtained from the PubChem Compound Database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [44], downloaded in SDF format, and converted to PDB format using OpenBabel 2.4.1. Protein structure data were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, http://www.rcsb.org/) and AlphaFold protein structure database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). Using AutoDock 4.2.6 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/) [45], we docked these drugs with the proteins encoded by their respective target genes and visualized the results using PyMol 3.0.2 (https://www.pymol.org/).

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted under the supervision of the Animal Ethics and Use Committee of Beijing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, in full compliance with ethical guidelines. The sample size for behavioral tests was determined as n = 12 per group using G*Power (v3.1) [46], with a statistical power set at 0.8. Wild-type C57BL/6J male mice (18–20 g) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and housed under controlled conditions (22 °C, 50% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to standard pellet diet and water. Mice were left to acclimatize for 1 week prior to starting experiments. To minimize bias, four blinded researchers independently performed surgery, migraine model establishing, behavioral testing, and data analysis.

Cranial surgical procedure and model establishment

As described in previous researches, all mice underwent catheter implantation surgery for dural injections [47]. During the procedure, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. A midline scalp incision was made to expose the skull, followed by creation of a 1-mm-diameter burr hole 1 mm lateral to the midline and 1 mm anterior to the bregma. A plastic cap containing a stainless-steel guide cannula was then implanted into the burr hole. Postoperatively, mice were singly housed with continuous monitoring of vital signs. On Day 1, Day 3, Day 5 and Day 7, each mouse received a 5 µL dural injection of inflammatory soup (IS) or saline under isoflurane anesthesia. The IS comprised 1 mM histamine, 1 mM 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), 1 mM bradykinin, and 0.1 mM prostaglandin E2.

Behavioral testing

Behavioral testing was conducted during the morning hours (09:00–12:00) at three critical time points: (1) 24 h prior to surgery (pre-operative baseline), (2) immediately before each IS/saline injection, and (3) prior to sacrifice on Day 9. This dual-timepoint baseline approach controlled for potential stress-induced variability while establishing robust reference values.

Tactile sensitivity testing

Paw mechanical hyperalgesia was evaluated by recording the paw withdrawal threshold by using the up-down paradigm. Mice are transferred to a testing chamber (14 cm × 8 cm) with a wire mesh floor and acrylic walls, and allowed to adapt for 30 min in the indoor environment. Von Frey filaments are applied to the plantar surface of each hind paw starting at 0.16 g, with a total of 5 applications, each lasting 3–5 s. A positive response is indicated by paw licking or withdrawal. If no response occurs, a filament with higher force is used. The withdrawal threshold was recorded as the lower force value when two consecutive filaments each elicited ≥ 3 positive responses. Left and right paws were recorded separately.

Thermal nociceptive threshold

Each C57/6J mouse was placed on a heated plate (16.5 cm × 16.5 cm) surrounded by acrylic walls to prevent escape. The plate temperature was maintained at 55 °C (± 0.5 °C). The latency to the first nociceptive response (licking, paw flicking, or jumping) was recorded. To prevent tissue damage, a cut-off time of 30 s was imposed for all animals; mice not exhibiting a response within this period were removed from the hot plate.

Western blot

On day 9, mice (n = 8 each group) were euthanized by CO₂, and brain tissues were promptly collected. The S1HL and TG were dissected on ice, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C. Tissues were homogenized in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) buffer (with phosphatase/protease inhibitors), lysed for 30 min on ice, and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm (10 min, 4 °C) to collect supernatants. Protein concentrations were measured by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China) and normalized, then denatured with 5× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer (Dingguo Biotech, Beijing, China) at 97 °C for 5 min before storage at -20 °C. Proteins were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) under constant voltage and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes via wet transfer. After blocking with 10% non-fat milk in tris-buffered saline Tween-20 (TBST) buffer for one hour at room temperature (RT), the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following three 5-minute TBST washes, they were exposed to horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT. Signals were developed using a 1:1 mixture of ECL substrate (SinotechSci, Beijing, China) for one minute before imaging. TIFF files were analyzed using ImageJ for band intensity quantification and statistics.

Immunofluorescence staining

C57/6J mice (n = 5 each group) were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 40 mL ice-cold saline followed by 30 mL chilled 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Harvested brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight and then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose phosphate-buffered solution (PBS) at 4 °C until tissue sinking. After dehydration, tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, snap-frozen at -20 °C and stored at -80 °C. Coronal sections of 6 μm thickness were cut using a cryostat. Sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 60 min at RT before overnight incubation with primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA at 4 °C. After three PBS washes, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for two hours at RT protected from light. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min and slides were coverslipped with antifade mounting medium. All sections were imaged under 20× or 40× objectives using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse C1, Japan) with identical exposure settings, and fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Two-way repeated measures ANOVA with the Bonferroni post hoc test were applied to behavioral data, while unpaired t test was used for WB and IF results.

Cell-type-specific Mendelian randomization analysis identified migraine-associated causal genes

We mapped the cell-type specific DEGs with an adjusted P-value of less than 0.05 from snRNA-seq data of human TG to brain eQTL data in PsychENCODE, yielding a total of 7,073 unique eQTLs, which exhibited the strongest genetic associations (Supplementary Table S2). We conducted MR analysis, identifying 586 significant genes (Supplementary Table S3). Among them, TMA7 (OR 0.83; P = 2.41E-06), ICA1L (OR 1.38; P = 3.22E-06), ATG13 (OR 1.74; P = 3.95E-06), MACF1 (OR 3.46; P = 5.14E-06), and SH3BGRL3 (OR 3.68; P = 5.49E-06) are the genes most significantly associated with migraine (Fig. 3). The full MR analysis results are available in Supplementary Table S4.

MR results of the most significant genes from the TG and brain

For eQTL data from eight major cell types in human brain, we obtained 7,911 eQTLs (Supplementary Table S5) and identified 1,108 unique genes with potential causal relationships with migraine (Supplementary Table S6). Among them, ARHGEF16 from astrocytes (OR 1.57; P = 4.75E-35), RIT1 from endothelial cells (OR 0.73; P = 9.03E-17), ACTR10 from excitatory neurons (OR 1.40; P = 1.16E-07), AVIL from inhibitory neurons (OR 1.42; P = 2.60E-35), MMS22L from microglia (OR 1.49; P = 1.18E-29), POC5 from oligodendrocytes (OR 1.18; P = 5.24E-09), MANEA from OPCs/COPs (OR 1.35; P = 1.76E-29), and MMS22L from pericytes (OR 0.80; P = 1.86E-20) are the genes most significantly associated with migraine (Fig. 3). The full MR analysis results are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

Enrichment analysis highlights migraine-relevant mechanisms

Functional enrichment analysis further elucidated migraine-relevant biological pathways. We conducted enrichment analysis for the significant genes in each group (TG-derived DEGs, brain-derived DEGs, and shared DEGs). The complete GO and KEGG enrichment analysis results for the three groups can be found in Supplementary Tables S8–S10.

In the GO analysis results for the TG-derived DEGs, BP was mainly enriched in pathways related to muscle development and circadian rhythm; MF was mainly enriched in cadherin binding, structural constituent of ribosome, and tubulin binding; and CC was primarily enriched in the ribosome, microtubule, and focal adhesion. The KEGG analysis results were primarily enriched in pathways related to autophagy, reactive oxygen species (ROS), salmonella infection and coronavirus disease (Fig. 4a).

The GO analysis results for the brain-derived DEGs indicated that BP was primarily enriched in pathways related to the positive regulation of cell projection organization, regulation of endocytosis and postsynapse organization. MF was mainly enriched in tubulin binding, cadherin binding and microtubule binding, while CC was enriched in cell leading edge, spindle, and cell cortex. Altered autophagy-related protein expression and cellular localization in primary sensory cortex hindlimb area and trigeminal ganglion of migraine model mice

To validate our bioinformatics findings, we conducted systematic in vivo experiments using C57BL/6J mice subjected to repeated dural IS infusions to establish a migraine model. Quantitative behavioral assessments revealed markedly reduced hind-paw withdrawal thresholds and prolonged hot plate latencies following both the 3rd and 4th IS infusions compared to vehicle-treated controls (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6), The consistent baseline pain thresholds across all experimental subjects prior to treatment confirmed the model’s validity for studying cutaneous allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia. Subsequent WB analysis of key candidates from our PPI network demonstrated region-specific protein expression patterns. Notably, ATG7 expression was significantly upregulated in the S1HL region of IS-treated mice (P < 0.01) (Fig. 7a, b), while TG showed a non-significant increasing trend (P = 0.1134) (Fig. 7f, g). In contrast, other hub genes (EGFR, HSP90AB1 and ERBB3) exhibited no significant differential expression in either neuroanatomical region (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 7c–e, h–j). The protein expression of MET remained undetectable by WB analysis, potentially due to its low basal expression levels in both S1HL and TG tissues under our experimental conditions. This selective ATG7 upregulation aligned precisely with our pathway enrichment results highlighting autophagy-related processes. IF co-localization studies in S1HL provided cellular resolution of ATG7 expression patterns. Quantitative image analysis confirmed significantly increased ATG7 fluorescence intensity in the migraine model (P < 0.05), corroborating the WB findings (Fig. 8a-c). Crucially, we observed co-localization of ATG7 with both astrocytic and neuronal markers, confirming the cell-type specificity predicted by our bioinformatics findings (Fig. 8a, b, d, e).

The activation of autophagic flux in primary sensory cortex hindlimb area of migraine model mice

To investigate whether the upregulation of ATG7 expression in the S1HL brain region promotes the activation of the classical autophagic flux, we further examined the expression levels of key autophagy marker proteins and proteins related to non-classical autophagy pathways (Fig. 9a). In the S1HL brain tissue, WB results showed that compared with the control group, the protein level of LC3-Ⅱ in the model group exhibited an increased trend, while the expression of both LC3-Ⅰ and p62 showed a decreased trend, although these changes did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05) (Fig. 9b-d). Notably, compared with the control group, both the LC3-Ⅱ/LC3-Ⅰ ratio and the LC3-Ⅱ/p62 ratio were significantly increased in the model group (LC3-Ⅱ/LC3-Ⅰ: P = 0.003; LC3-Ⅱ/p62: P = 0.0397) (Fig. 9e-f), indicating a significant increase in the number of autophagosomes, effective clearance of the autophagy substrate p62, and an unobstructed autophagic flux process. To further exclude the possibility that ATG7 might be involved in the disease mechanism by activating non-classical autophagy-related processes (such as LC3-associated phagocytosis, LAP), we examined the expression of Rubicon, a key regulatory protein in the LAP pathway. The results showed that the expression level of Rubicon in the model group was significantly decreased compared with the control group (P = 0.0006) (Fig. 9g).

Discussion

Migraine, a complex neurological disorder with heterogeneous pathophysiology, requires innovative approaches to unravel its mechanisms. Our study breaks new ground by employing an integrative multi-omics strategy that simultaneously examines both CNS and PNS. Leveraging human TG snRNA-seq data and eQTL data from eight major cortical cell types, we identified hub genes through MR analysis of migraine GWAS data. Subsequent bioinformatic analyses, including functional enrichment and PPI network construction, revealed key pathways and targets in migraine development, while drug prediction and molecular docking demonstrated the promising clinical potential of these hub genes. Experimental validation confirmed significant co-localization of ATG7 in both astrocytes and neurons within the S1HL region of the cerebral cortex, as evidenced by IF.

A key methodological advance of this study is the concurrent profiling of TG and brain tissues through snRNA-seq combined with MR. Unlike previous studies that predominantly examined the CNS or PNS in isolation [7–10], our dual-tissue paradigm provides an integrative framework to unravel the interplay between these systems in migraine pathogenesis. The cell-type-specific resolution of snRNA-seq further refines our understanding, revealing subtype-specific DEGs in nociceptors, glia, and vascular cells that may drive migraine susceptibility. For example, the enrichment of autophagy-related pathways in merged datasets underscores the emerging role of dysregulated cellular clearance mechanisms in migraine.

ATG7 is a critical regulator of autophagy, a process essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis by degrading damaged organelles and proteins. ATG7 cleaves the cytosolic form of LC3-I and ultimately conjugates LC3-I to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), converting it into the membrane-bound form LC3-II. LC3-II is a hallmark molecule of the autophagosome membrane and is essential for the elongation and closure of autophagosomes [48]. During normal autophagy, the cytoplasmic p62 protein is continuously degraded as autophagy progresses [49]. When autophagy is impaired or its activity is reduced, p62 accumulates in the cytoplasm. Therefore, p62 is one of the marker proteins that reflect autophagic activity [50]. Our study found that the expression of ATG7 was significantly upregulated in the S1HL region of a mouse migraine model. Driven by this upstream regulatory event, we further obtained downstream evidence indicating effective activation of the autophagic flux. WB analysis revealed a significant increase in the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio in the model group, suggesting a marked rise in the number of autophagosomes, which aligns with the enhanced autophagic initiation predicted by the upregulation of ATG7 expression. Notably, the accumulation of autophagosomes could result from either increased generation or impaired degradation [51]. To clearly distinguish between these two scenarios, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the autophagic degradation process. Although the p62 protein levels did not show a statistically significant difference between groups, a downward trend in its expression was observed in the model group. Moreover, the LC3-II/p62 ratio was significantly elevated in the model group, indicating that while autophagosomes increased, the autophagic substrate p62 was effectively cleared, thus demonstrating that the entire autophagic flux - from initiation to degradation - remained unimpeded.

Notably, preclinical studies in migraine models have previously demonstrated the critical involvement of autophagy in disease pathogenesis. Both clinical and preclinical studies have demonstrated that the PI3K/Akt pathway modulates neuronal signaling and promotes inflammation in migraine [52, 53]. This pathway is also one of the key regulators of autophagy, and its downstream effector mTOR directly participates in the regulation of autophagic responses [54]. In a mouse model of migraine (repeated nitroglycerin-induced), the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio was significantly increased, along with elevated p62 expression, indicating impaired autophagic flux. Specifically, autophagy initiation remained intact, but downstream processes were blocked [55]. Additionally, P2 × 7R expression was increased in this migraine mouse model. Application of a selective P2 × 7R antagonist restored autophagic flux, alleviated pain sensitization, reduced CGRP and c-fos expression, and suppressed microglial activation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [55]. This study suggests that microglial autophagy dysfunction likely contributes to the pathogenesis of migraine. Mitophagy impairment also contributes to migraine pathology. In migraine model mice induced by repeated dural IS injections, damaged mitochondria accompany with increased p62 and decreased PINK1 levels were observed, suggesting defective mitophagy, while Parkin and Beclin1 were unaffected, linking mitochondrial dysfunction to impaired mitophagy [56]. Dysfunctional autophagy may impair synaptic plasticity by affecting the formation of long-term potentiation and long-term depression, thereby disrupting the development of central sensitization, which is implicated in the pathogenesis of chronic migraine [57]. Beyond this, the classic migraine mediator CGRP has been found to potentially interact with autophagy in non-migraine disease studies [58]. However, no migraine-related research has yet explored ATG7-mediated autophagic alterations. Intriguingly, the observed activation of autophagic flux in this study diverges from some previous reports describing impaired autophagy or reduced flux in migraine models. We propose that this discrepancy may be explained by the possibility that the upregulation of ATG7 represents an adaptive protective response of cells under stress [59, 60]. Specifically, cortical neurons and glial cells may compensatorily upregulate core autophagy genes such as ATG7 in response to stimuli like neuroinflammation or oxidative stress, resulting in an adaptive and activated autophagic flux state. This process may counteract key pathological mechanisms in migraine pathogenesis by clearing damaged mitochondria, maintaining energy homeostasis, reducing oxidative damage, and suppressing inflammasome (e.g., NLRP3) activity to alleviate neuroinflammation and maintain neuronal homeostasis [61–63]. However, as autophagy inducers were not used for validation in this study, the role of increased autophagic flux in migraine requires further investigation.

In addition to autophagy, ATG7 is also involved in regulating non-canonical pathways such as LAP [64]. This process is independent of autophagosome formation and plays a role in immune regulation and cellular debris clearance [65]. During LAP, ATG7 is recruited to single-membrane phagocytic vesicles, mediating the lipidation of LC3-II. Rubicon stabilizes the binding of lipidated LC3-II to the single-membrane LAP-engaged phagosomes (LAPosomes), thereby promoting the fusion of phagocytic vesicles with lysosomes and the degradation of their contents. The LAP process does not form typical double-membrane autophagosomes and operates independently of autophagy initiation signals (such as the ULK1 complex) [66–69]. Nevertheless, no studies have yet explored the extent of LAP’s contribution to migraine phenotypes. Our research also demonstrated the expression level of Rubicon in the model group was significantly decreased in the S1HL brain region, ruling out the contribution of the LAP pathway to migraine phenotypes.

The S1HL region, which processes somatosensory input from the hindlimb, may serve as a cortical hub for integrating peripheral nociceptive signals during migraine attacks. Emerging evidence suggests this region contains specialized neural circuits that are critically involved in pain processing [27, 28, 70]. The prominent involvement of S1HL in our model provides compelling evidence that migraine pathophysiology extends beyond primary pain pathways to include maladaptive sensory integration in higher-order cortical regions. Growing evidence suggests that central sensitization in migraine results from dysfunctional neuron-glia interactions. Neuron-derived HMGB1, interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-18 can activate the NF-κB signaling pathway in astrocytes, which facilitates CSD [71]. In addition to astrocytes, neuronal HMGB1 can also activate microglia via toll-like receptors [72], triggering the release of IL-1α and TNF-α to promote the transition of astrocytes toward a pro-inflammatory A1 phenotype [73]. These processes amplify inflammatory signaling and exacerbate neuroinflammation. Within this context, our observation of ATG7 upregulation in S1HL - with co-localization in both neurons and astrocytes - suggests that ATG7 may exacerbate cortical sensitization by dually modulating autophagy and neuroinflammatory responses, thereby disrupting metabolic homeostasis and signal transduction between glia and neurons. This finding provides a novel mechanistic perspective on how “neuron-glia bidirectional dysregulation” contributes to migraine pathogenesis.

Through PPI network analysis, we identified several key molecular targets and performed molecular docking to discover potential anti-migraine compounds. This target-to-drug discovery pipeline provides new opportunities for migraine treatment development. For instance, gefitinib, as an EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor, demonstrates therapeutic potential beyond oncology, as evidenced by its ability to attenuate IL-6-induced Stat3 activation in microglia and modulate neuronal responses to noxious stimuli in arthritic models [74]. Additionally, research has shown that gefitinib enhances TRAIL-induced apoptosis, likely through autophagy-dependent mechanisms and JNK-mediated upregulation of death receptors, coupled with modulation of Akt and ERK phosphorylation [75]. Calcitriol exerts neuroprotective effects through multiple mechanisms, including oxidative stress reduction, neuroinflammation modulation, and autophagy enhancement [76, 77]. Calcitriol is the primary active hormonal form of vitamin D [78]. Notably, clinical randomized controlled trials in migraine patients have shown that vitamin D supplementation significantly improves headache frequency, intensity, and duration, while also markedly lowering serum iNOS levels - a key mediator of oxidative stress and inflammation [79]. Staurosporine shows promise in pain management by suppressing IL-6 production in synovial fibroblasts and alleviating thermal hyperalgesia through modulation of spinal cord synaptic transmission [80–82]. These findings collectively suggest that drugs capable of modulating neuroinflammation may provide valuable insights for developing novel therapeutic approaches for migraine.

Our study found that the expression of ATG7 in the S1HL of migraine model mice was significantly upregulated compared to the control group. As mentioned earlier, we speculate that this phenomenon does not imply that ATG7 is a promoter of the disease; rather, it is more likely to function as an endogenous protective factor [59, 60]. Staurosporine, as an inducer of cellular autophagy and apoptosis [83, 84], may exert its effects through mechanisms involving the inhibition of pathways such as AKT/mTOR [85, 86]. Although this compound itself is not suitable for direct clinical translation due to its broad kinase inhibition profile and high toxicity, these research results provide us with an important proof-of-concept insight: pharmacologically enhancing autophagy to intervene in migraine is a potentially viable therapeutic strategy [87]. Therefore, the value of this study lies in establishing the therapeutic potential of the “autophagy induction” approach itself. This discovery lays the foundation for the subsequent development of safer, highly selective autophagy inducers or the repurposing of existing well-tolerated autophagy-modulating drugs, while also opening new avenues for migraine treatment strategies based on enhancing endogenous neuroprotection.

While our study provides important insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although MR offers powerful causal inference, its reliability depends heavily on the quality and comprehensiveness of the GWAS and eQTL datasets used. Second, since our analyses primarily included individuals of European ancestry, the generalizability of our genetic findings to other ethnic groups remains uncertain - underscoring the critical need for more diverse genetic studies in migraine research. Furthermore, while enrichment analyses, PPI network and drug prediction analyses yielded valuable information, these computational approaches have inherent limitations. The models may not capture the full complexity of biological systems and protein interactions that occur in living organisms. Finally, the conclusions drawn from this study still require ultimate validation through morphological data and animal experiments that include pharmacological intervention in the autophagy process. In subsequent research, it would be necessary to employ techniques such as transmission electron microscopy to directly observe the ultrastructure of autophagosomes and autolysosomes, thereby providing visual evidence for the activation of autophagic flux in the migraine model. Our identification of migraine-associated targets - including ATG7 and other hub genes - represents just the beginning of this line of investigation. Although these findings are promising, the exact mechanisms by which these genes influence migraine pathophysiology require more extensive experimental validation.

Conclusions

This study integrates multi-omics datasets to bridge CNS and PNS mechanisms in advancing migraine research, highlighting that ATG7 serves as a key hub gene and that activating protective autophagy to promote glial-neuronal interactions may represent a critical adaptation mechanism. Our dual-system approach provides a holistic understanding of migraine pathogenesis, highlighting autophagy as a promising therapeutic target. These findings deepen mechanistic insights and pave the way for targeted drug development. Future work should expand this framework to larger cohorts and validate ATG7 in preclinical and clinical studies to accelerate translational progress.
