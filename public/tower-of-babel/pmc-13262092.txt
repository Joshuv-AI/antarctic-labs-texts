Elarekibep, an inhaled anti-IL-4 receptor alpha Anticalin, in moderate-to-severe asthma: a randomized, placebo-controlled phase 2a study (APATURA)
Authors: Tania Hugo, Andrew R Harper, Jacob Leander, Philip Gardiner, Robert Palmér, Pål Lundin, David Bock, Xiao-Hong Zhou, Irina Bondarenko, Steve Burns, Erol Wiegert, Mary F Fitzgerald, David R Close, David J Keeling, Hitesh Pandya, Maria G Belvisi, Paul M O’Byrne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground The pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13 and their cognate receptor IL-4 receptor-alpha are key targets for asthma therapy. Elarekibep is an inhaled Anticalin protein developed to inhibit IL-4 receptor-alpha signalling in patients with moderate-to-severe asthma not controlled with standard-of-care therapies. APATURA was a phase 2a study (NCT04643158) investigating the efficacy and safety of elarekibep in patients with moderate-to-severe asthma.Methods This was a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blinded, multicentre, two-part study investigating elarekibep administered via dry powder inhaler twice daily for 4 weeks. Part 1 evaluated the safety and pharmacokinetics of elarekibep in patients with controlled moderate asthma, and part 2 evaluated the efficacy of elarekibep compared with placebo in those with uncontrolled moderate-to-severe asthma. Results In total, 72 patients were included (part 1: n = 50; part 2: n = 22) in the study. In part 1, approximately half of the patients receiving elarekibep (n = 20; 58.8%) or placebo (n = 8; 50.0%) experienced at least one treatment-emergent adverse event. Most of the treatment-emergent adverse events reported were of mild intensity. Elarekibep was steadily absorbed, with a median time to peak drug concentration of approximately 3–4 hours post-dose. In part 2, there was a mean increase from baseline in pre-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 second of 196 mL with elarekibep 3 mg compared with placebo (95% confidence interval: 14.3, 378; p = 0.035 vs placebo) at week 4. Improvements in Asthma Control Questionnaire-6 scores at week 4 were numerically superior with elarekibep 1 mg and 3 mg than with placebo. Conclusion Elarekibep was clinically well tolerated with no identified short-term safety concerns. Development of elarekibep was terminated due to lung pathology findings in a 13-week non-human primate inhalation toxicology study, which were considered to be adverse and relevant for chronic clinical dosing. Nonetheless, the overall data demonstrate that there remains potential for the development of inhaled biologic therapies for patients with asthma that may offer advantages over systemically administered biologics.Trial registrationNCT04643158 (ClinicalTrials.gov). Introduction

Type 2 (T2) cytokines interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5 and IL-13 are key drivers of inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness in many patients with asthma [1, 2]. The IL-4 receptor-alpha (IL-4Rα), a well-established T2 mediator abundantly expressed on airway epithelial and smooth muscle cells, facilitates IL-4/IL-13-driven mucus hypersecretion, epithelial cytokine production, airway hyperresponsiveness and nitric oxide synthase activity [1]. As such, targeting IL-4Rα localized in the lung is a promising therapeutic strategy. The subcutaneously administered biologic therapy dupilumab, which targets IL-4Rα, has been shown to reduce both the rate of asthma exacerbations and levels of T2-associated inflammatory biomarkers [3, 4]. However, systemic biologics may have poor bioavailability in the lungs [5], and subcutaneous administration may be inconvenient for some patients [6, 7]. Inhaled biologics, in contrast, are non-invasive [8] and offer direct delivery to the lungs, achieving higher lung: plasma exposure and local target engagement at lower doses; this focuses the pharmacology in the airway and potentially reduces systemic exposure and any associated adverse effects [9, 10]. There are currently no inhaled biologics approved for the treatment of asthma [10], although recent reports have highlighted the safety and efficacy of inhaled antibody fragments targeting thymic stromal lymphopoietin [11–14].

Elarekibep (AZD1402/PRS-060) is an inhaled Anticalin® protein derived from naturally occurring human tear lipocalins, engineered to inhibit IL-4Rα signalling [15–17]. Elarekibep doses of 2 mg or greater previously demonstrated a reduction in fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) levels in patients with mild asthma during a multiple ascending dose study, indicating pulmonary target engagement; systemic pharmacokinetics were assessed to contextualize exposure-response and safety rather than to imply systemic mediation of the drug target [18, 19]. These results also verified localized pulmonary target engagement data from a pre-clinical study [15, 17]. Here, we report findings from the APATURA phase 2a study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04643158), which investigated the therapeutic potential of elarekibep in patients with moderate-to-severe asthma [20]. The study was terminated early [21] owing to the emergence of lung pathology findings in a 13-week non-human primate inhalation toxicology study. Total delivered dose levels of 0.248, 0.744 or 2.08 mg/kg/day were well tolerated but resulted in adverse histopathological findings in the lung, including perivascular mononuclear inflammatory cell infiltrates across the dose range, alveolar haemorrhage and pigmented alveolar macrophages (containing haemosiderin) at 0.248 mg/kg/day. The histopathological changes were multifocally distributed across multiple lung lobes and were accompanied by increased cellularity in the tracheobronchial lymph nodes and the presence of circulating anti-drug antibodies (ADAs). These findings are consistent with an adaptive immune response to elarekibep, resulting in the recruitment of mononuclear cells into the perivascular interstitium and blood–air barrier disruption, causing extravasation of erythrocytes into air spaces, with subsequent erythrophagocytosis and haemosiderin production by alveolar macrophages. Similar responses have been observed in toxicology studies with other inhaled proteins [22, 23]. Consequently, the non-clinical safety data did not support long-term clinical administration of relevant dose levels, precluding further development of elarekibep.

Methods

Study design and population

APATURA was a two-part, phase 2a, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, dose-ranging trial (Fig. 1). Part 1 (Fig. 1A) followed a lead-in dose progression and escalation cohort design, with three dose levels. Participants received dry-powder-formulated elarekibep or placebo twice daily (BID) for 28 days, with a residential stay at the study centre for the first 2 weeks. In part 1a, participants were randomized 1:1:1 to receive elarekibep 1 mg, 3 mg or placebo. In part 1b, participants were randomized 2:1 to receive elarekibep 10 mg or placebo. The study population for part 1 comprised adults aged 18–75 years with controlled asthma, defined as Asthma Control Questionnaire-6 (ACQ-6) of ≤ 1.0, who were receiving medium-dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) (total daily dose of > 400–800 µg of budesonide dry powder or equivalent), a long-acting beta-2 agonist (LABA) and rescue medication as needed. Participants must have had a pre-bronchodilator (BD) forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) of ≥ 70% predicted normal and no exacerbations requiring systemic treatment or hospitalizations/emergency department visits for asthma in the 12 months before study start. An unblinded safety review committee reviewed data from part 1a before progressing to part 1b (dose escalation), followed by part 2 (efficacy evaluation) (Fig. 1A).

Part 2 (Fig. 1B) evaluated elarekibep 1 mg, 3 mg or placebo BID for 28 days. Participants were randomized 2:1 to elarekibep or placebo initially, then 1:1 once the 1 mg study arm stopped recruiting. The study population for part 2 comprised adults aged 18–75 years with physician-diagnosed uncontrolled asthma, defined by an ACQ-6 score of ≥ 1.5 on a medium-to-high dose of ICS (total daily dose of > 400 µg budesonide dry powder formulation or equivalent) with LABA as maintenance treatment for ≥ 6 months before screening. At screening, participants must have had a pre-bronchodilator FEV1 of 40–85% predicted normal. Participants must also have had a blood eosinophil count of ≥ 150 cells/µL and a FeNO level of ≥ 25 parts per billion (ppb). A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Study outcomes

In part 1 of the study, the primary objective was to evaluate the safety and tolerability of different dose levels of elarekibep compared with placebo in adults with asthma controlled on medium-dose ICS + LABA. This included analysis of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs); protocol-defined adverse events of special interest (AESIs), including dyspnoea, wheeze, cough, FEV1 decline, infection and confirmed fever; serious adverse events (SAEs); vital signs; changes in clinical chemistry; haematology and coagulation parameters; immune biomarkers; electrocardiogram; FEV1; and FeNO.

The secondary objectives were to investigate the pharmacokinetic (PK) profile and immunogenicity of elarekibep and associated effects on safety by measuring PK parameters and ADAs.

In part 2, the primary objective was to investigate the efficacy of inhaled elarekibep compared with placebo in adults with uncontrolled asthma on medium-to-high dose ICS + LABA, via the primary endpoint of change from baseline in pre-BD FEV1 at week 4. The change in pre-BD FEV1 was chosen as a relevant endpoint to evaluate early signs of efficacy. Given the study’s relatively short duration, this measure enabled assessment of any rapid improvements in airway function that could indicate signs of potential efficacy.

Secondary efficacy endpoints included changes from baseline in ACQ-6 at week 4 and the mean over the treatment period; the proportion of patients with a decrease in ACQ-6 score of ≥ 0.5 from baseline (i.e. ACQ-6 responders) at week 4; changes from baseline in mean morning and evening peak expiratory flow (PEF); and changes from baseline in daily mean asthma symptom score throughout the treatment period. Additionally, secondary endpoints included the change from baseline in FeNO (in-clinic) at week 4 and the mean over the treatment period. Safety, tolerability and PK of elarekibep were also secondary objectives in part 2.

Statistical analysis

Demographics, disease characteristics, safety, PK and ADA data were summarized for each study part by treatment group (elarekibep, either by dose level or overall or placebo) using descriptive statistics. The primary endpoint in part 2, change from baseline in FEV1, was analysed using a mixed model for repeated measures adjusted for fixed effects of treatment group, week, country, baseline FEV1 and treatment-by-week interaction, with participant as a random effect. Secondary efficacy endpoint data in part 2 were summarized descriptively. PK parameters were estimated using standard non-compartmental methods. Further details of the statistical methods are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Patient disposition, baseline demographics and clinical characteristics

The study was conducted between 21 April 2021 and 20 July 2023 at 38 centres in 10 countries (Australia, Canada, Germany, Hungary, Poland, the Republic of Korea, Spain, Taiwan, the UK and Ukraine). In total, 72 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the study (Fig. 1C and D).

In part 1, 50 patients started treatment, and 45 (90.0%) completed treatment (Fig. 1C). Some patients were affected by specific global and country events (i.e. coronavirus disease 2019 [COVID-19] and war in Ukraine). All patients were randomized after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Five patients (10.0%) experienced at least one disruption (missed visit) owing to the war in Ukraine: two patients in the elarekibep 1 mg group and two patients in the placebo group had visits affected, and one patient withdrew.

In part 2, 22 patients started treatment and, of these, 20 (90.9%) completed treatment (Fig. 1D). None of the patients had study visits disrupted or withdrew from the study owing to specific global or country-level events.

Baseline demographics and characteristics were generally well balanced between the different study drug arms and placebo in both parts of the study (Table 1 and Supplemental Table S1). An exception was FeNO, for which the median levels in part 1 were numerically lower in the elarekibep 10 mg and placebo groups than in the elarekibep 1 and 3 mg groups (elarekibep 1 mg, 32.8 [range: 21.8, 93.8]; elarekibep 3 mg, 30.9 [6.5, 165.6]; elarekibep 10 mg, 18.0 [7.0, 57.8]; placebo, 25.9 [7.3, 179.8]).

Safety

In part 1, approximately half of the patients receiving elarekibep (58.8%) or placebo (50.0%) experienced at least one TEAE (Table 2). The most frequently reported TEAEs with elarekibep were dyspnoea (n = 8, 23.5%) and cough (n = 7, 20.6%). Dyspnoea and cough were prespecified AESI and were mostly mild, occasionally moderate; events were generally brief and self-limiting, and mostly not associated with rescue medication use. Consistent co-occurrence of symptoms was not evident beyond occasional temporal overlap. There were no SAEs reported in part 1 and all but one TEAE were of mild or moderate intensity. One patient (7.7%) receiving 10 mg elarekibep experienced a severe non-serious TEAE of back pain. In total, 17 (50.0%) patients receiving elarekibep and 8 (50.0%) receiving placebo reported AESIs. Four (11.8%) patients receiving elarekibep reported TEAEs considered possibly related to the study drug, as assessed by the Investigator. Two patients (5.9%) in the elarekibep group (elarekibep 3 mg, one patient [10.0%]; elarekibep 10 mg, one patient [7.7%]) discontinued dosing owing to adverse events (AEs); one discontinued because of two AEs (i.e. COVID-19 and increased transaminases) and another discontinued owing to an AE of asthma. One patient (6.3%) in the placebo group discontinued dosing owing to an AE (COVID-19). There were no safety findings in part 1 that precluded progression to part 2.

In part 2, eight (61.5%) patients in the total elarekibep group and six (66.7%) patients in the placebo group experienced at least one TEAE (Supplemental Table S2). No TEAEs led to discontinuation of the study drug or withdrawal from the study. Most TEAEs were of mild (elarekibep overall, n = 4 [30.8%]; placebo, n = 5 [55.6%]) or moderate (elarekibep overall, n = 3 [33.3%]; placebo, n = 1 [11.1%]) intensity (Supplemental Table S2). The most frequently reported events were COVID-19, asthma and allergic rhinitis (each n = 2, 15.4%). One patient (7.7%) in the elarekibep group reported an SAE of asthma exacerbation during the follow-up period. Overall, no clinically significant changes in haematology, clinical chemistry values, urinalysis, electrocardiograms or vital signs were identified between groups in either part of the study.

Pharmacokinetics and ADA

In part 1, elarekibep was steadily absorbed following dry powder inhaler administration, with a median time to reach peak or maximum observed concentration or response of 3–4 h, and elimination half-life ranging between 7.7 h and 20 h (Supplemental Fig. S1). Serum concentrations of elarekibep remained quantifiable on day 28 throughout the dosing interval at the 3 mg and 10 mg dose levels, and accumulation was observed following multiple doses (Fig. 2). PK data from part 2 were similar to those from part 1 (Supplemental Fig. S2).

The ADA response to elarekibep in part 1 is summarized in Supplemental Table S3. In total, 28 of 34 patients who received elarekibep (elarekibep 1 mg, n = 10; elarekibep 3 mg, n = 10; elarekibep 10 mg, n = 8), and none in the placebo group, were treatment-emergent anti-drug antibody (TE-ADA)-positive, with most TE-ADAs appearing from day 20 onwards (Fig. 3). Of the 28 patients who were TE-ADA-positive, 16 (57.1%) reported at least one TEAE (elarekibep 1 mg, n = 4 [40.0%]; elarekibep 3 mg, n = 6 [60.0%]; elarekibep 10 mg, n = 6 [75.0%]) (Supplemental Table S3). There was no clear difference in PK parameters between ADA-negative and TE-ADA-positive patients in the elarekibep 10 mg dose arm, and the high interpatient variability could not be attributed to differences in ADA category. All patients in the 1 mg and 3 mg dosing arms for whom PK parameters were calculable, except for one patient in the 1 mg arm, were TE-ADA positive. As such, comparisons of PK parameters by ADA response could not be performed for these dosing arms.

The ADA response to elarekibep in part 2 is summarized in Supplemental Table S4. Briefly, 10 of 13 patients in the total elarekibep group, and none in the placebo group, were TE-ADA-positive. Of the 10 patients who were TE-ADA-positive in the elarekibep group, 7 (70%) reported at least one TEAE. Efficacy

Lung function

At week 4 in part 2, the elarekibep 3 mg group showed a least-squares (LS) mean increase in pre-BD FEV1 of 196 mL compared with placebo (95% confidence interval [CI]: 14.3, 378; p = 0.035 vs. placebo), whereas the LS mean increase in the elarekibep 1 mg group was 23.6 mL (95% [confidence interval] CI: −193, 240; p = 0.828 vs. placebo) (Fig. 4). Over the 4-week treatment period, no meaningful trends were observed in the mean morning and evening PEF between elarekibep and placebo groups (Supplemental Table S5).

Asthma control

At week 4 in part 2, both the 1 mg and 3 mg elarekibep groups had a numerically lower ACQ-6 score than the placebo group, with numerically greater improvements from baseline (Table 3). During the treatment period, a higher proportion of ACQ-6 responders (score decrease of ≥ 0.5) was observed with elarekibep (n = 10 [76.9%]) than with placebo (n = 3 [33.3%]). The 1 mg elarekibep and placebo groups each had three responders (75.0% and 33.0%, respectively), whereas the 3 mg elarekibep group had seven responders (77.8%) (Table 3). No meaningful trends were observed in the changes from baseline in daily mean asthma symptom scores over the treatment period across treatment groups (Supplemental Table S5).

Discussion

The release of T2 cytokines, including IL-4 and IL-13, from immune cells leads to a pro-inflammatory response, airway remodelling and mucus production in patients with asthma [3, 24, 25]. As a result, IL-4, IL-13 and their cognate receptor, IL-4Rα, have become key therapeutic targets in asthma [3]. Elarekibep was designed to inhibit the clinically validated target IL-4Rα [15, 17] and to modulate its activity directly through inhalation [26]. This two-part phase 2a, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study evaluated the safety and PK of elarekibep in patients with moderate-to-severe asthma, and its efficacy in those with T2-high and uncontrolled moderate-to-severe asthma. Elarekibep was well tolerated over this short study duration, with no safety concerns and a profile consistent with previous safety findings [18, 19]. Elarekibep was steadily absorbed following dry powder inhaler administration, with a median time to reach peak or maximum observed concentration of approximately 3–4 h post-dose. No clear differences in PK parameters were observed between ADA-negative and TE-ADA-positive patients. It should be noted that the small sample size and modest effects observed in the study do not allow firm conclusions to be drawn on the associations between ADAs and AEs. There was, however, an observed increase in systemic accumulation of elarekibep starting from day 16, which might be attributed to the onset of ADAs and a subsequent change in pulmonary absorption and/or systemic clearance. PK data from the prior multiple ascending dose study of elarekibep and from other inhaled biologics, e.g. pitrakinra and CSJ117, have also shown a greater than expected systemic exposure after repeated dosing [13, 19, 27]. Overall, the safety and PK profiles of elarekibep support BID inhaled dosing. However, the study was stopped early due to new non-clinical findings that did not support continued long-term clinical development. As a result of the early study termination and a smaller-than-planned number of evaluable study participants, firm conclusions about efficacy could not be drawn.

In part 2 of this study, at the prespecified week 4 primary endpoint, inhaled elarekibep 3 mg was associated with a placebo-adjusted LS mean increase in pre-BD FEV1 of 196 mL (95% CI: 14, 378; nominal p = 0.035). The estimate for the elarekibep 1 mg dose was near zero and imprecise (LS mean difference 23.6 mL [95% CI: − 193, 240]), reflecting the very small evaluable sample at week 4 (n = 4). Across weeks, estimates were variable with wide CIs and did not show consistent separation of the 3 mg dose over the 1 mg dose, and secondary endpoints (PEF, ACQ-6 score, FeNO) also did not show concordant patterns. Notably, the numerical FeNO reductions seen at week 4 compared with baseline were less pronounced than those previously reported in a phase 1 elarekibep study involving patients with mild asthma and a baseline FeNO level of ≥ 35 ppb [18]. Further studies would be required to ascertain whether the smaller reductions in FeNO levels were related to differences in the patient population analysed in this trial or to the early termination of the study. Overall, these data could suggest a role for elarekibep in reducing airway obstruction and T2-driven airway inflammation in this population, with potential improvements in symptom control; however, the number of patients evaluated was too small to reach firm conclusions. Additionally, receptor occupancy and airway target engagement were not measured; therefore, mechanistic inference, including any exposure threshold, is not feasible. As such, these findings are exploratory and require confirmation in adequately powered studies.

Aligned with observations in this study, blocking IL-4Rα systemically with dupilumab has been shown to increase FEV1 and reduce FeNO levels compared with placebo, leading to fewer exacerbations and improved asthma control as well as lung function [28]. Systemically administered biologic therapies have been a highly valuable addition to asthma treatment over the past two decades. Although systemic biologics such as dupilumab are generally well tolerated, they are associated with various AEs, including conjunctivitis and other eye-related issues, eosinophilia and, most commonly, injection-site reactions [29]. The possibility of delivering targeted biologic therapy through inhalation is, therefore, appealing. The potential suitability of elarekibep for inhaled delivery could reduce the need for repeated injections, which are currently required for subcutaneous and intravenous biologics, and offer patients the convenience of a familiar delivery method [30–32]. Moreover, direct delivery of the biologic to the target tissue could reduce systemic exposure and permit dose adjustment more readily, leading to potential safety and tolerability advantages over systemic biologic treatment [33, 34]. Promising early data have been published for inhaled biologics targeting thymic stromal lymphopoietin, which have shown signs of efficacy and safety in patients with asthma [13, 14]. Additionally, non-antibody biologics such as elarekibep may offer potential advantages in stability and production over monoclonal antibodies [8].

This study had some limitations that should be considered. The small sample size of the cohorts and the early study termination limited the interpretation of the efficacy analysis. In addition, the relatively short treatment period of 4 weeks limits the conclusions that can be drawn about the safety of elarekibep. Furthermore, the study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic which, in addition to affecting recruitment, may have influenced results through patients’ altered daily activities and reduced environmental exposure, with potential subsequent effects on asthma symptoms.

Conclusions

In the APATURA study, elarekibep was clinically well tolerated with no identified short-term safety concerns. However, the study was stopped early due to new non-clinical findings that did not support continued long-term clinical development. As a result of the early study termination and a smaller-than-planned number of evaluable study participants, firm conclusions about efficacy could not be drawn. Despite recent advancements in asthma treatment, there is significant potential for the development of inhaled biologic therapies that may provide advantages over systemic biologics for patients with uncontrolled moderate-to-severe asthma.
