Outcomes after gastrectomy plus pancreatic resection for gastric cancer invading the pancreas
Authors: Shih-Chen Chang, Yi-Fu Chen, Hao-Wei Kou, Puo-Hsien Le, Tsung-Hsing Chen, Chia-Jung Kuo, Shang-Yu Wang, Wen-Chi Chou, Ta-Sen Yeh, Jun-Te Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadical resection is the primary treatment for resectable gastric cancer (GC). However, outcomes remain poor for patients requiring combined gastrectomy and pancreatic resection due to pancreatic invasion. This study compares surgical and survival outcomes between radical (R0) gastrectomy with pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) versus distal pancreatectomy (DP).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 81 patients with pancreatic-invading GC who underwent R0 gastrectomy with pancreatic resection (January 1994–June 2023). Demographics, surgical complications, adjuvant chemotherapy, and survival outcomes were assessed (median follow-up: 13.4 months).ResultsAmong the cohort, 36 patients underwent gastrectomy with PD, and 45 underwent gastrectomy with DP. The DP group had significantly more total gastrectomies (88.9% vs. 36.1%, p < 0.0001), tumors localized to the upper stomach (p < 0.0001), and a higher metastatic-to-total lymph node ratio (0.30 vs. 0.15, p = 0.024). Postoperative pancreatic fistula occurred less frequently in the DP group (17.8% vs. 61.1%, p < 0.0001), correlating with a shorter median hospital stay (17 vs. 22 days, p = 0.045). Patient age, tumor characteristics, mortality, lymph node retrieval, and adjuvant chemotherapy use were comparable. Two-year disease-free survival (14.5% PD vs. 20.9% DP, p = 0.988) and cancer-specific survival rates (26.8% PD vs. 32.1% DP, p = 0.815) showed no differences. Multivariate analysis identified stage IIIC (reference: IIIA) as an independent prognostic factor for both disease-free survival (adjusted hazard ratio 3.91, p = 0.015) and cancer-specific survival (adjusted hazard ratio 3.12, p = 0.045).ConclusionsGastrectomy with PD or DP yields similarly poor outcomes for pancreatic-invading GC. Tumor stage (nodal status) is the dominant prognostic factor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03866-4.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, with over one million newly diagnosed cases and approximately 650,000 cancer-related deaths annually, posing a significant global health burden [1]. Most patients with GC are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with poor prognosis, low survival rates and limited therapeutic options except in Japan and Korea, where early GC is more frequently diagnosed through screening programs [2]. For localized resectable disease, significant disparities between the East and West regarding in treatment strategies. Perioperative chemotherapy with surgery is preferable in the US and Europe [3–6]. However, in East Asia, upfront radical (R0) surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy is considered the gold standard [2, 7, 8]. Advances in chemotherapy, immunotherapy and targeted therapies have significantly transformed GC treatment. For stage IB or higher cancers, adjuvant chemoradiation or chemotherapy has been shown to improve survival [3, 4]. Recently neoadjuvant chemotherapy with triplet chemotherapy regimens—comprising a fluoropyrimidine, a platinum compound, and docetaxel is recommended for resectable advanced GC [9–14]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors to enhance immune response, targeted therapies, such as trastuzumab for HER2-positive patients, ramucirumab as an anti-angiogenic agent or zolbetuximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CLDN18.2 in combination with chemotherapy have shown promise in clinical trials for treating metastatic and unresectable GC or gastroesophageal junction cancer [13–21].

Surgical resection is indicated for locally advanced GC involving adjacent organs, whether through desmoplastic reaction or true pathological invasion. Achieving R0 resection remains the cornerstone of GC treatment and is considered the most beneficial for improving clinical outcomes. However, management becomes complex when GC invades adjacent organs or the distal duodenum. In such cases, gastrectomy combined with multiorgan resection (MOR)—including pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) or distal pancreatectomy (DP) with splenectomy—is often necessary to achieve R0 resection [22–24]. The potential risks of postoperative complications, disease recurrence, and delays in systemic therapy raise concerns about whether the benefits of achieving R0 resection outweigh these challenges [22, 25, 26]. Thus, careful patient selection is critical [22, 26].

Studies demonstrated that patients with pancreatic involvement had significantly worse prognoses compared to those with invasion of other adjacent organs [24–27]. Interestingly, patients undergoing PD for pancreatic invasion had survival outcomes comparable to those of gastrectomy with positive duodenal margins, suggesting that aggressive tumor biology, rather than anatomical involvement alone, may drive outcomes [25]. However, outcome differences within subpopulations of pancreatic invasion remain poorly understood. Specifically, it is unclear whether proximal pancreatic invasion differs from distal pancreatic invasion in terms of prognosis. In this study, we analyzed outcomes in GC patients with pancreatic invasion who underwent either R0 gastrectomy plus PD or DP with splenectomy. Our objective was to compares surgical and survival outcomes between radical (R0) gastrectomy with PD versus DP.

Material and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the 1996 version of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee (Institutional Review Board) of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital at Linkou, Taiwan. This study was reported following Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Patients with pathologic T4b GC involving pancreatic invasion who underwent R0 gastrectomy with PD or DP were recruited from Chang Gung Memorial Hospital in Linkou between 1994 and June 2023. Surgeons determined whether to perform radical total or subtotal gastrectomy based on tumor location, intraoperative findings, and margin status. We typically used a stapler to transect the pancreas during DP.

Clinicopathological data were extracted from a prospectively maintained medical database. Tumors with well or moderately differentiated histology were classified as differentiated, while those with poorly differentiated or signet-ring cell histology were categorized as undifferentiated. The anesthesia risk assessment followed the classification levels recommended by the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA). As part of our preoperative assessment, we evaluated comorbidities, including cardiac diseases (myocardial infarction, valvular disease, or congestive heart failure), peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular diseases, dementia, chronic pulmonary diseases, connective tissue diseases, peptic ulcer disease requiring treatment, liver fibrosis/cirrhosis, renal diseases, diabetes (with or without end-organ damage), leukemia, acquired immune deficiency syndrome, and other active solid malignancies. These prognostic comorbidities were classified according to the Charlson Comorbidity Index [28]. Tumors were staged based on the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [29]. Medically fit patients received adjuvant chemotherapy within 6 to 8 weeks after surgery. Preferred regimens included single-agent fluoropyrimidine or doublet therapy combining a fluoropyrimidine with a platinum compound or taxane.

Drain fluid amylase levels were measured on postoperative days 1, 3, 5, or beyond as clinically indicated. Postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF), intra-abdominal abscess/infection, wound infection and pneumonia, were defined as surgical complications. POPF was defined according to the 2016 ISGPS criteria based on amylase levels and clinical findings [30]. Grade A POPF is defined a biochemical leak without clinical importance; grade B is drains left in place for > 3 weeks or endoscopic or percutaneous interventions are performed for reposition; grade C is requiring reoperation or presence of organ failure and/or mortality related to the POPF. None of the patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

Postoperative follow-up assessments included imaging studies (e.g., abdominal computed tomography/ultrasonography, chest radiography, and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy) and laboratory tests (including liver enzyme assessments and tumor markers) every 3 to 6 months during the first 2 years after surgery, and annually thereafter.

Patients who died within 30 days following surgery (surgical mortality) or during the same hospitalization (hospital mortality) were included from the survival analysis. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the interval from surgery to the first documented recurrence, second primary malignancy, or death from any cause. Cancer-specific survival (CSS) was defined as the interval from surgery to death attributable to GC or treatment-related causes or last follow-up (June 6, 2024). Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from surgery to death from any cause or last follow-up. The median follow-up duration was 13.4 months (range: 0.92–137.13 months).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as percentages or mean values with standard deviations. Clinical records were compared using Student's t-test, Pearson's chi-square test, or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Patient DFS, CSS and OS rates were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with differences between subgroups assessed using the log-rank test. All variables were included in the univariate Cox regression analysis. Variables with a p value < 0.2 in the univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate analysis using a Cox proportional hazards model. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software for Windows, version 20 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Table 1 shows demographics of GC patients undergoing radical gastrectomy with PD or DP. Among the 81 patients, 36 underwent gastrectomy with PD, and 45 with DP. Patients in the DP group had significantly higher percentages of total gastrectomies (88.9% vs. 36.1%, p < 0.0001), a higher proportion of tumors located in the upper, middle and whole part of the stomach (p < 0.0001), and a higher ratio of metastatic to total lymph nodes retrieved (0.30 vs. 0.15, p = 0.024) compared to those in the PD group. The DP group also had a shorter median length of hospital stay (LOS) compared to the PD group (17 days vs 22 days, p = 0.045). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of patient age, gender, tumor size, tumor stage, tumor differentiation, or the presence of lymphatic, vascular, or perineural invasion. The rates of mortality and number of lymph nodes retrieved were also similar. One PD patient died from grade B POPF with uncontrolled infection. In the DP group, four deaths occurred: three from esophagojejunostomy leak with severe infection and one from pneumonia. Nodal status (stage) was identified as a significant prognostic factor for DFS, CSS, and OS (Tables 4, 5 and 6). Patients with stage IIIA had the longest median DFS, CSS, and OS [17.0 months, 95% confidence interval (CI), 5.9–28.0; 29.6 months, 95% CI, 4.1–55.1; and 29.6 months, 95% CI, 4.1–55.1, respectively], whereas those with stage IIIC had the shortest median DFS, CSS, and OS (5.4 months, 95% CI, 3.5–7.3; 11.8 months, 95% CI, 8.6–15.0; and 11.2 months, 95% CI, 7.3–15.1, respectively). Other factors, including age, sex, tumor size, tumor location, type of pancreatectomy (PD or DP), differentiation, lymphatic, vascular, or perineural invasion, and administration of chemotherapy, were not significantly associated with DFS, CSS, or OS. Discussion

Radical gastrectomy combined with MOR remains the standard treatment for locally advanced GC invading adjacent organs, either through desmoplastic reaction or true pathological invasion. However, the prognosis of pathologic T4b GC varies significantly depending on the specific organ involved. Despite aggressive surgical interventions and adjuvant chemotherapy, long-term outcomes remain unsatisfactory due to high recurrence rates and limited survival, particularly when critical organs such as the pancreas and spleen are affected. Our study represents the largest cohort to date evaluating outcomes of radical gastrectomy combined with PD or DP. We observed similarly poor survival between the two surgical approaches, and multivariate analysis identified tumor stage—specifically nodal status—as the independent prognostic factor for survival. Notably, the complexity of achieving R0 resection increases when pancreatic resection is required, especially in cases involving the pancreatic head, which is associated with high morbidity.

Previous studies have consistently shown that pancreatic invasion portends worse outcomes compared to other adjacent organ involvement [22, 25–27]. However, the differential impact of pancreatic head versus tail invasion on survival outcomes remains unclear. Moreover, few studies have assessed whether PD confers a survival benefit by achieving a clear distal duodenal margin. Our findings suggest that while tumor location influences the choice of surgical procedure, the biology of the tumor may have a greater impact on prognosis.

Interestingly, PD patients had a lower metastatic-to-total lymph node ratio (0.15 vs. 0. 30, p = 0.024; Table 1) compared to DP patients despite PD's technical complexity, suggesting less aggressive tumor biology in this group. Conversely, DP patients—all undergoing splenectomy—showed a higher nodal metastatic ratio, potentially worsened immune function, and more total gastrectomies, which may impair postoperative nutrition. Splenectomy is linked to increased complications, sepsis risk, and poorer survival [22, 26, 31]. Although the DP group had lower POPF rates and shorter LOS, hospital mortality was numerically higher (4/45, 8.9% vs. 1/36, 2.8%; p = 0.375), partly due to esophagojejunostomy leaks (n = 3). Larger studies are needed to validate these findings. Survival analyses showed no significant procedural impact—again emphasizing the role of tumor stage as the primary determinant of outcomes, consistent with US GC Collaborative findings where MOR with pancreatectomy predicted poor survival (HR 1.67, p = 0.044) [26]. These results emphasize balancing R0 resection benefits against extensive surgery risks.

Evidence from population-based studies, including a Finnish nationwide study by Junttila et al., supports our findings [31]. Patients undergoing R0 gastrectomy with splenectomy for oncologic reasons had significantly lower 5-year OS compared to those with spleen preservation or splenectomy due to injury (30.8% vs. 38.7% and 39.7%, respectively; p = 0.032). These data further suggest that tumor biology may play a more critical role than extensive local clearance in determining long-term outcomes.

Given the limited efficacy of upfront surgery for GC patients with pancreatic invasion, a multidisciplinary approach incorporating surgery, systemic therapy, and tumor biology is essential. In this context, neoadjuvant therapy is emerging as a promising strategy. Clinical trials have shown that neoadjuvant chemotherapy can improve DFS and OS by reducing tumor burden, increasing R0 resection rates, and addressing micrometastases early [9–11]. However, several challenges remain. The selection of patients for neoadjuvant therapy is based on imaging, which can lead to both over- and under-treatment due to limitations in staging accuracy. Some patients may progress during chemotherapy and become unresectable, while others may experience treatment-related toxicity that delays or precludes surgery. Furthermore, the optimal regimen—chemotherapy alone vs. chemoradiotherapy—and the lack of reliable predictive biomarkers remain unresolved issues. Careful patient selection and individualized treatment planning are thus crucial.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design inherently introduces selection bias. Second, the analysis was limited to patients treated at a single tertiary referral center, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Third, the study encompassed a 30-year period, during which substantial advances in systemic therapy occurred, including the introduction of targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors; these variations in treatment may have influenced survival outcomes. Fourth, the relatively small sample size limited the statistical power to detect subtle differences. Fifth, we did not assess tumor biology markers (immunohistochemistry, biomolecular, or genetic profiles) or their impact on outcomes. Nevertheless, DFS, CSS, and OS were comparable between radical gastrectomy with PD versus DP.

Conclusion

In summary, despite the small sample size, our study demonstrates that patients with pancreatic invasion undergoing radical gastrectomy with either PD or DP have similarly poor DFS, CSS, and OS, with tumor stage (nodal involvement) emerging as an independent prognostic factor for DFS and CSS. Large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate these findings. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy ollowed by surgery may be considered as a potential strategy to improve outcomes in this challenging patient population.
