Abstract
. This scoping review analyzes the use of Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) in game-based learning (GBL) settings, providing a thorough examination of contemporary trends and approaches. This review aims to clarify the evolving role of fNIRS in GBL research, highlighting key trends and gaps in the literature to guide future studies in educational neuroscience by offering practical suggestions on device selection, study design, and data analysis. Employing the PRISMA framework, an initial collection of 956 articles was methodically screened, resulting in 18 research papers that satisfied the inclusion criteria. Each chosen study was assessed based on many criteria, including measurable outcomes, equipment characteristics, and study design. The review categorizes fNIRS-based GBL research into two primary types: cognitive response studies, which analyze how the brain function during tasks and comparative studies, which evaluate finding across different study materials or methods based on neural activities. The analysis includes learning platforms, gaming devices, and various fNIRS devices that has been used. Additionally, study designs and data collection methodologies were reviewed to evaluate their impact on research results. A comprehensive analysis outlines the specifications of fNIRS devices used in diverse studies, including yearly publication trends categorized by learning type, gaming equipment, fNIRS study classification, and outcome measures such as learning improvements and cerebral pattern analysis. Furthermore, the study design and analysis techniques are detailed alongside the number of studies in each category, emphasizing methodological trends and analytical strategies. The studies presented in this paper shows that GBL is as effective as traditional methods and it reduce cognitive load, enhance learning outcomes and cognitive engagement with fNIRS providing objective, real-time insights beyond self-reported questionnaires.

3.
Methodology

Motivation and Research Questions

Although there have been multiple review studies on the subject, there are still certain aspects of GBL and fNIRS that have not been well investigated
(
Tinga et al. 2020
)
.
As a new researcher, choosing the right device and software for a study can be overwhelming, and determining the best methods for data analysis is often challenging. Additionally, designing a study to assess the effectiveness of GBL with fNIRS brings up questions like what is the current trend, which device to use, what type of analysis to perform, and how to structure the study. The aim of this review is to offer practical guidance on study design, device selection, and data analysis to help researchers confidently make these early decisions.

This review paper followed PRISMA for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR)
(
PRISMA 2024
)
to evaluate the effects of GBL on neural activity using fNIRS.
This study compares cognitive load and patterns of brain activation across several platforms, such as virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), desktop and mobile platforms. Our aim is to give a detailed knowledge of the impact of educational games on cognition and learning.
Our goal is to understand the block based and event based design strategy used in fNIRS study to help new researchers to select their own strategy. Our research differs from past studies by investigating several digital learning environments, providing a thorough understanding of how different technologies affect cognitive processes. By including study type and data analysis methods, we can understand various way of designing new studies not only for GBL based research but also for other educational research.
We are seeking to find out the following research questions:

•

How does fNIRS has been used for games-based learning?

•

How does fNIRS data analysis help to understand the neural activity and efficacy of the game during learning?

•

What are the research gaps and future direction of using fNIRS in learning in a game environment?

Search Method

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis and Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR)
(
PRISMA 2024
)
framework for conducting the review process, which includes searching and screening methods (see Figure
1
).
The search was conducted on June 13, 2024, using the specified keywords in four digital libraries: Google Scholar, Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), Association for Computing Machinery (ACM), ScienceDirect (SD), and PubMed.

Search Criteria

We searched in the libraries with the keywords "game-based learning" and fNIRS, "gamification" and fNIRS, "educational game" and fNIRS, and "serious game" and fNIRS.
To eliminate publications that do not meet the necessary qualifications or are not related to the topic of this literature review, the following exclusion criteria were employed during the screening phase: articles that did not include fNIRS, articles that were not published in English, studies that did not employ a game environment for learning, and publications that were review papers.

Search Procedure

We initially identified 956 articles related to learning in-game environments. From this set, we selected papers that specifically conducted studies using fNIRS data analysis. Initially, we search in the title, abstract, and keywords to locate relevant papers. Due to the absence of filters in Google Scholar, we were compelled to utilize a comprehensive search range by applying the whole body. We searched IEEE, ACM, and SD databases, focusing on the title, abstract, and author keywords. In PubMed, our search was limited to the title and abstract. Disregarding time limitations, the search yielded a total of 956 publications. Specifically, there were 6 pieces from IEEE, 35 articles from ACM, 888 articles from Google Scholar, 27 articles from SD, and 1 paper from PubMed.

Based on the criteria mentioned above, we carried out a total of four screening rounds. In the first round, we eliminated duplicate articles, English or non-English, books or reports, etc. We removed 43 review and systematic review articles from the high-level meta-analyses. In the second round and assessed the relevance of the titles and abstracts to find if the paper is related to the fNIRS study. This process was repeated in the third screening to find if the paper was related to learning in the game environment. At the end we evaluated the full texts for relevance in the fourth round, and in the fourth round, we applied explicit tags to the selected articles.
Following the screening process, a total of 938 articles were deemed ineligible and hence excluded. we conducted a thorough analysis of the 18 articles listed in
section 3
. All the studies were experimental.

Classification Characteristics

For the high-level analysis of the 18 articles, we collected potential terms for classification concerning the aspects of research and technologies for fNIRS in the learning environment with game the review and determined the following categories after iterative refinements.

Learning Platform

A wide range of categories, including GBL, educational games, serious games, and gamification, have been used for learning with games
(
Becker 2021
)
. GBL employs actual games created for educational objectives, emphasizing learning via interactive gameplay and problem-solving
(
Cakir et al. 2015
)
. Educational games have been carefully designed to impart lesson plans or educational knowledge by compelling gameplay, integrating learning objectives with enjoyment
(
Holly et al. 2024
;
Lei and Rau 2023
)
. Serious games are developed to address real-world issues or replicate real-world situations, focusing on training, education, or awareness with a significant objective beyond simple amusement
(
Holly et al. 2024
;
Larson 2020
)
. Gamification involves the incorporation of game components such as points, badges, and leaderboards into non-gaming environments to improve motivation and engagement, usually using extrinsic rewards
(
Larson 2020
)
. We were unable to find any papers that covered gamification and fNIRS studies in particular. As a result, we have divided our research into three categories: serious games, educational game, and game-based learning
(
Becker 2021
)
.

-

Game-based Learning:
Using actual games designed for educational purposes.

-

Educational Game:
Games specifically
designed for educational content with some learning goals in mind.

-

Serious Game:
Games designed to
solve real-world problems or simulate real-world scenarios rather than pure entertainment.

Game Device

The types of devices for playing games in educational purposes used in the articles were collected and classified into the following three categories:

-

2D Display:
Computer and portable devices such as desktops, tablets and iPads.

-

3D Display:
AR and VR devices such as HTC Vive or Oculus

-

Other:
Hands-on, Robots, etc.

fNIRs Device

Devices that are primarily used for measuring brain activity and cognitive responses in various GBL and educational environments include systems such as the NirSmart, the Brite23, NIRSPORT-2, the NirScan, the Imagent fNIRS, ETG-4000, and fNIRS Devices by LLC with block or event-based task structure.

Study Configuration and Data Collection

This section gives insights into the methodology and contexts of the studies by classifying the study designs, settings, durations, and fNIRS data types comprised in the reviewed articles.

-

Design:
We classified the articles by dividing two design groups such as
within-subject design
and
between-subject design
. In within-subject studies each individual is exposed to more than one treatment, allowing causal estimates to be obtained by examining changes in individual behavior under different experimental circumstances.In between-subject design each individual is exposed to only one treatment using random group assignment
(
Charness et al. 2012
)
.

-

Settings:

Based on the study settings the articles were classified into two categories such as
controlled
and
naturalistic
settings. A controlled setting is generally a laboratory experiment environment where the researcher manipulates variables and maintains strict control over all aspects of the environment whereas the naturalistic setting is based on real-world context with minimal manipulation by the researcher.

-

Duration:
We classified the articles according to the duration of the investigation, either at a single point in time (
cross-sectional
) or at multiple periods in time (
longitudinal
)
(
Rindfleisch et al. 2008
)
.

-

fNIRS Data:
We categorized fNIRS data by only oxygenated hemoglobin (
HbO
), both oxygenated and deoxygenated (
HbO-HbR
), and Oxy value calculated by subtracting HbR from HbO (
Oxy= HbO-HbR
).

Outcome Measurement

While reviewing the collected articles, we identified the article’s outcome measure and classified them into three categories

-

Brain Region Pattern Analysis:
In these articles the main focus was how the brain region pattern behaves during gameplay while learning.

-

Learning Gain:
The articles that focus on neural activities because of learning gain are included in this category. Learning gain has been calculated from the test score from the game.

-

Both:
In this category, all those papers that considered both brain region pattern and learning gain are included.

Study Type

We have classified the articles based on the study type into two following categories:

-

Comparative Studies:
The articles that compare two groups, two different materials, or two tasks to find out which one was more effective are categorized in this section.

-

Cognitive Responses Studies:
These articles focused on finding the emotional, expertise, or cognitive responses during a task rather than comparing the system like the comparative studies.

7.
Implications and Future Directions

In this section, we present how fNIRS data have been useful in understanding neural activity and the efficacy of games in learning (RQ2) and the research gaps and opportunities to open new paths of research in this challenging learning technology (RQ3).

7.1.
Implications in various Sector of Education

The research used fNIRS to evaluate the effectiveness of GBL techniques across various educational and training settings, demonstrating broad implications. For example, in the field of STEM education, the neural activity captured by fNIRS shows that serious games can improve cognitive engagement and the acquisition of knowledge
(
Sharmin et al. 2024
)
. Furthermore, research has shown that including game aspects into learning activities can enhance emotional and cognitive engagement without impacting performance
(
De Nicolò et al. 2023
)
. This suggests that game features have the ability to optimize learner engagement and motivation
(
De Nicolò et al. 2023
)
. In medical and nursing training, fNIRS provides accurate assessments of cognitive workload, complementing VR-based tools for training
(
Aksoy et al. 2019b
;
Aksoy et al. 2020
)
. In addition, integrating brain signals with emotional state data in gaming contexts provides a reliable approach to identifying gamer proficiency and customizing experiences accordingly
(
Andreu-Perez et al. 2021
)
. The use of fNIRS in math education allows for the examination of neural activities to digital arithmetic applications. This offers valuable insights into the ways in which students interact with math content and aids in the creation of efficient instructional tools
(
Ninaus et al. 2014b
;
Cakir et al. 2015
)
. Moreover, fNIRS is recognized as a valuable instrument for quantifying cognitive load measurement in realistic emergency simulations, hence aiding in the advancement of adaptive training settings
(
Sevcenko et al. 2022
)
. The results also highlight the possibility of using VR for cognitive and motor rehabilitation in patients with MCI
(
Sevcenko et al. 2022
)
. The study demonstrates the efficiency of using multi-source feedback and fNIRS to assess the progress of rehabilitation. In overall, these results emphasize the ability of neuroimaging and GBL to improve educational and training results in different fields. This provides useful knowledge for creating and using effective learning and training methods.

7.2.
Limitations in fNIRS Studies for Learning in Game Environment

This review emphasizes the increasing use of fNIRS in GBL, however, the reviewed studies have some limitations as follows.

Sample Size and Diversity Limitations:

The studies using fNIRS in GBL is limited to the size of the sample and the diversity of participants. The limited number of participants and the lack of diversity in the groups involved in these studies limit the capacity to apply the findings to a broader population
(
Sharmin et al. 2024
;
De Nicolò et al. 2023
;
Aksoy et al. 2019b
;
Aksoy et al. 2020
;
Andreu-Perez et al. 2021
;
Ninaus et al. 2014b
;
Shibata et al. 2019
;
Cakir et al. 2015
;
Lingelbach et al. 2023
;
Qu et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Sevcenko et al. 2022
;
Ge et al. 2021
;
Aksoy et al. 2019a
;
Landowska et al. 2024
;
Lamb et al. 2018
)
.

Specific Demographic Focus

Studies that focus on specific demographics, such as older adults or college students, may not be generalizable to other groups

(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Landowska et al. 2024
;
Lamb et al. 2018
)
.

Technical and Hardware Limitations

The implementation of fNIRS sensors in naturalistic situations is significantly challenging due to the physical and technical limitations they possess. These constraints can affect the accuracy and reliability of the collected data

(
Sharmin et al. 2024
;
De Nicolò et al. 2023
;
Aksoy et al. 2019b
;
Aksoy et al. 2020
;
Andreu-Perez et al. 2021
;
Qu et al. 2024
;
Aksoy et al. 2019a
;
Landowska et al. 2024
;
Lamb et al. 2018
)
.
Obtaining accurate data collection with fNIRS necessitates careful arrangement and calibration, which can be intricate and time-intensive. Streamlining these procedures is essential for enhancing data accuracy and alleviating the workload on researchers
(
De Nicolò et al. 2023
)
.

Task and Game Design Limitations

Some studies used tasks that are overly simplistic or narrow in scope, thereby failing to adequately reflect the intricate complexities of real-world settings. This constraint implies the necessity of creating a wider range of tasks that are more authentic and accurately represent the complex cognitive requirements of real-life situations

(
Shibata et al. 2019
;
Lingelbach et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Sevcenko et al. 2022
;
Ge et al. 2021
)
.

Game designs used in research may not sufficiently take into account the preferences and talents of various age groups or comprehensively depict all cognitive requirements

(
Ge et al. 2021
;
Aksoy et al. 2019a
)
.

Short-Term and Pilot Study Limitations

Short-term studies and pilot studies frequently restrict the capacity to evaluate long-term impacts and necessitate careful interpretation of findings. Longitudinal studies are advisable for gaining a deeper comprehension of the enduring effects of GBL and cognitive training
(
Ninaus et al. 2014b
;
Cakir et al. 2015
)
.

Equipment Constraints
The use of wired head-mounted displays (HMDs) and fNIRS systems can cause discomfort and limit practical application. Future research should explore wireless alternatives to improve user comfort and the feasibility of naturalistic studies. Wireless systems would facilitate more seamless and comfortable participation, thereby enhancing the quality and authenticity of the collected data

(
Aksoy et al. 2019a
)
.

Measurement and Methodological Challenges

fNIRS technology is limited in its ability to measure brain activations outside the prefrontal cortex. Additionally, there are challenges in maintaining game continuity while ensuring accurate neural activity monitoring. Combining fNIRS with other neuroimaging techniques, such as fMRI or EEG, could provide a more comprehensive understanding of cognitive processing in GBL

(
Lamb et al. 2018
;
Lei and Rau 2023
)
.

Future Directions

To validate and extend these findings, future research should aim to increase participant numbers and explore additional educational contexts

To understand the long-term impact and usefulness of VR and serious educational games across various topics, research should also look into these technologies in education, especially in environments with limited resources

Most studies have looked at problem-solving and memorization skills, but fNIRS and GBL could also be used to explore other educational outcomes. For instance, critical thinking, creativity, emotional regulation, and collaborative learning are all areas where this technology could offer valuable insights. Expanding fNIRS studies in GBL settings could help us better understand how these tools impact a broader range of cognitive and social skills in education.
Beyond problem solving, more studies should explore subjects like arts, commerce, and humanities using GBL and fNIRS to understand their effects on the brain.
Another important domain is the investigation of emotional and affective engagement, including frustration and motivation during gameplay, which has been infrequently assessed with fNIRS. The standardization of research techniques across studies would enhance the comparability and reproducibility of outcomes.

8.
Conclusion

This scoping review indicates an increasing interest in utilizing fNIRS to investigate cognitive processes within GBL contexts. This review categorizes studies into cognitive response and comparative studies, emphasizing the diversity in study approaches, instruments, and methodologies within the discipline. Our findings indicate that, despite advancements in comprehending brain activity in learning environments by fNIRS, significant deficiencies persist, especially in the standardization of research settings and data collection methodologies.

The discovered trends, including the categories of fNIRS equipment, educational platforms, and gaming devices utilized, provide a fundamental comprehension of contemporary approaches and their results. Moreover, our examination of publication trends reveals that although interest is growing, additional research is essential to enhance the application of fNIRS in education, especially for learning outcomes and cerebral pattern analysis. Future research can focus on improving experimental consistency, employing varied learning platforms, and adopting longitudinal methodologies to gain a deeper understanding of the effects of GBL on cognitive functioning.

This review highlights the promise of fNIRS as an important instrument in educational neuroscience.
Researchers may find it difficult to understand the wide range of work in this domain and the various methodologies employed to assess neural activity during game-based learning. This review aims to provide a clear and thorough reference detailing various game kinds, analytical methodologies, and developing developments in the field. We aim to assist researchers, regardless of their experience level, in understanding the applications of fNIRS and identifying potential areas for study.
By overcoming the constraints and expanding upon the findings of this review, subsequent research can enhance the comprehension of cognitive responses in GBL, thereby guiding the development of more effective, data-informed educational interventions.
