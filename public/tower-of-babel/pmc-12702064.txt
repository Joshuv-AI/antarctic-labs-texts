Prehospital care utilization and associated factors among trauma patients during armed conflict: A mixed-methods study in west Amhara, Ethiopia referral hospitals
Authors: Temesgen Ayenew, Mengistu Abebe Messelu, Haile Amha, Mamaru Getie Fetene, Fentahun Minwuyelet Yitayew, Anteneh Belayneh, Abere Kassie, Getinet Nibret, Bekele Getenet Tiruneh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrehospital care is vital for reducing mortality from traumatic injuries, especially in low- and middle-income countries. This study determined prehospital care utilization and associated factors among trauma patients in referral hospitals in West Amhara, Ethiopia.MethodsA concurrent triangulation mixed-methods design was employed from March–August 2024 in Debre Markos, Tibebe Ghion, and Felege Hiwot hospitals. Quantitative data were collected from 518 trauma patients via questionnaire, with proportional allocation based on patient flow, and analyzed using logistic regression to identify factors associated with prehospital care use. Qualitative data were collected through semi-structured interviews with healthcare professionals and trauma patients, reported in line with the Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist. Reflexive thematic analysis guided by the WHO Emergency Care System Framework was used, integrating quantitative and qualitative findings while maintaining reflexivity to understand prehospital care utilization.ResultsThe prehospital care utilization rate was 49.8 %. The majority of patients (84.2 %) arrived at hospitals via taxi or on foot, reflecting reliance on non-EMS transport. Rural residence (AOR = 4.80), lower education (AOR = 0.54), violence/quarrel-related injuries (AOR = 2.60), motor vehicle accidents (AOR = 2.00), gunshot injuries (AOR = 8.61), and penetrating injuries (AOR = 0.34) were significantly associated with prehospital care use.Qualitative findings revealed challenges across four themesLimited awareness and lack of emergency communication systems during initial contact and dispatch (Scene); scarce ambulances, long response times, and reliance on non-EMS transport in prehospital treatment and transportation (Transport); resource and personnel shortages that indirectly affect prehospital response within facility-based emergency care linkages (Facility); and the need for Advocacy and Public Education.ConclusionPrehospital care utilization is sub-optimal, influenced by sociodemographic and injury-related factors. Qualitative insights reveal systemic and community challenges that warrant further investigation through larger, more representative studies.Keywords: Prehospital care, Associated factors, Barriers, West Amhara, Ethiopia

Introduction

During traumatic events, an organized Emergency Medical Services (EMS) are necessary to reduce mortality and long-term consequences [1,2]. EMS is defined as the system that organizes all aspects of care provided to patients in the pre-hospital environment. Hence, EMS is a critical component of the health systems and is necessary to improve outcomes of injuries and other time-sensitive illnesses [3]. However, EMS systems in low-income settings are generally fragmented and largely limited to transportation without protocols for field triage, standards of care, or communication to receiving facilities. The lay public is often left to decide independently whether and where to transfer their acutely ill or injured patients [4,5]. Particularly during conflicts, there are gaps in prehospital care delivery (both during scene response and during transport) [1].

Injuries are a neglected epidemic in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), causing more than five million deaths each year, roughly equal to the number of deaths from HIV/AIDS, malaria, and tuberculosis combined [6]. Of these deaths, >90 % of them occur in low – and middle-income countries [[7], [8], [9]]. In Ethiopia, according to Health Demographic Surveillance (HDS) and Addis Ababa trauma reports 6.4 % and 7 % of all deaths are related to trauma, respectively. Traumatic injuries are the major causes of death and disability among the young, productive age groups, and road traffic accidents alone are the main cause, fundamentally in low- and middle-income countries [[10], [11], [12]].

Although the birth and expansion of trauma systems show a shift toward a bimodal distribution with a decline in late deaths, studies show that deaths occurring within minutes or a few hours of injury are largely unchanged. About one-fourth of patients died during primary assessment in the emergency department. This figure might even be higher in conflict areas [9,13,14].

In Ethiopia, although there are some improvements in human resources and infrastructures, pre-hospital care is still not proportional to the number of patients with trauma [15]. The magnitude of prehospital care provision might be decreased during armed conflicts because of poor infrastructure, persistent social distrust, scarce formal emergency care training, and lack of resources and supplies [1]. This study aimed to determine the prehospital care use, and associated factors among trauma patients visiting Emergency Departments of public hospitals.

Methods

The study was conducted in west Amhara Regional State from March 2024 to August 2024. The region is divided into several highland areas separated by deep river valleys, and the eastern and western escarpments and their associated lowlands. Specifically, the study was conducted in Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized hospital (DMCSH) of the east Gojjam zone, and Tibebe Ghion Specialized Teaching Hospital (TGSTH) and Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH) of Bahir Dar Town Administration of the region. DMCSH, which is located in the northwest 300 km distance from Addis Ababa, serves about 2451,959 total population (1199,952 males and 1252,006 females). Bahir Dar Town is located in the same direction at 485 km from Addis Ababa, the two hospitals in the town serves a total population of 2474,254 (1220,477 males and 1253,777 females) [16]. According to data from hospital registries, the six-months trauma case flow rate of DMCSH, TGSTH and FHCSH hospitals are 511, 522 and 562, respectively. The region is currently under a state of emergency due to armed conflicts taking place in the region.

This study employed a concurrent triangulation mixed-methods design. Quantitative and qualitative data were collected in parallel, analyzed separately, and then integrated during interpretation to provide both breadth and contextual depth. Integration occurred at the interpretation stage, where qualitative findings were used to explain, expand, and corroborate quantitative results. The qualitative arm of the study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [17] to ensure transparency and rigor. A purposive sampling strategy was used, and data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached. Interviews were conducted using a semi-structured guide, with the interviewer being a trained emergency and critical care nurse researcher. No prior relationships existed with participants, and informed consent was obtained before interviews. To maintain transparency, participants’ demographic characteristics (age, sex, occupation, injury background) were documented to support transferability of findings.

The study population was all trauma patients visiting EDs of referral hospitals in the west Amhara who fulfilled the eligibility criteria as well as selected healthcare professionals for in-depth and key informant interviews. All trauma patients who presented to EDs of referral hospitals in the west Amhara during the study period as well as key informant health care professionals (nurses and physicians) were included. However, trauma patients who presented to EDs of referral hospitals in the west Amhara but were unable to communicate and had no attendants to provide information on their behalf, along with key informant health care professionals with less than six months of work experience (as they were not considered permanent employees), were excluded.

For the quantitative study, a single population proportion formula were used to determine the required sample size The final sample size was 518 participants, calculated based on a previously reported prehospital care proportion(29 %) [18] with a 4 % margin of error and 5 % allowance for non-response. Qualitative data were collected from purposively selected healthcare professionals and trauma patients through key informant and in-depth interviews. Participants were chosen for their direct experience with trauma care, and interviews continued until data saturation was reached, ensuring comprehensive insights from both provider and patient perspectives.

There are three referral hospitals in the study area. There was proportional allocation of study participants for each hospital based on the estimated trauma patient flow to the EDs in the 6 months before inception of the study. Study participants in each hospital was recruited consecutively by assuming that patients come to the EDs of each hospital at random.

Prehospital care (primary outcome) was defined as any form of first-aid or medical intervention delivered at the scene and/or during transport prior to arrival at a health facility.

Quantitative data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire and a clinical checklist. The questionnaire was developed based on prior studies [9,[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]] and included four domains: sociodemographic characteristics, clinical variables, injury-related variables, and prehospital care and prehospital time. The full questionnaire is provided in Supplementary File 1. Data were obtained directly from conscious patients or, when patients were unresponsive, from attendants and medical records. Three nurse data collectors and two supervisors were assigned to each hospital.

For the qualitative component, purposive sampling was used to select healthcare professionals and trauma patients with direct experience in emergency and trauma care. Eligible healthcare providers included nurses and physicians from emergency and orthopaedic departments with at least six months of experience. Participants were drawn from the three referral hospitals to ensure a range of professional roles and perspectives. Two sets of structured interview guides were developed for healthcare professionals and patients (Supplementary file 1). Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved.

The quantitative data collection questionnaire and checklist were tested on 5 % of the total sample at the Debre Tabor comprehensive specialized hospital, and appropriate modification was done. To ensure linguistic accuracy, all instruments were translated and back-translated. A one-day training was given for data collectors and supervisors. Daily communication was made between the principal investigator, supervisor and data collectors throughout the data collection period. Obtained data was reviewed for accuracy, completeness, clarity and consistency before analysis.

The interview guides were pilot-tested and refined for clarity and flow. All interviewers received standardized training, aiming to reduce bias and ensure consistent questioning. Interviews were conducted in quiet settings, with participants' identities protected by unique codes. Trustworthiness was ensured through methodological triangulation, data saturation, purposive sampling for transferability, and dependability through coding, recoding, and peer examination.

Quantitative data collected using Kobo toolbox was exported to STATA 17 for analysis. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, proportions, and median were calculated. Bivariate binary logistic regression was done and variables with p-value of <0.25 were entered to multivariable binary logistic regression. In the multivariable regression variables with p-value of <0.05 were considered statistically significant with 95 % confidence interval. Model fitness was checked using Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test with p-value (0.5232) which is greater than 0.05 indicating that the model fits the data well. Multicollinearity was checked with Spearman Correlation Matrix and all correlation coefficients are below ±0.3, meaning there’s no strong or moderate correlation between any of these variables.

Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated into English. Data were analyzed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) as outlined by Braun and Clarke (2006). The WHO Emergency Care System Framework (ECSF) [24] was applied as an initial coding structure to guide deductive analysis, while allowing for emergent inductive themes that extended beyond the framework categories. Coding and theme development were conducted using Taguette software (https://www.taguette.org/), with cross-checking by multiple researchers to enhance dependability and ensure richness of interpretation.

The primary investigator is an emergency and critical care nurse with several years of professional experience in trauma management. This professional background may have influenced assumptions about systemic barriers to prehospital care. To minimize potential bias, reflexive memos were maintained during analysis, and coding was independently reviewed by two co-researchers with no clinical role in emergency care. Peer debriefings and consensus discussions were held to ensure that interpretations reflected the data rather than researcher expectations.

The study was approved by Debre Markos University’s College of Medicine and Health Science Ethical Review Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality and privacy were strictly maintained.

In this study, almost half (49.8 %) of the participants had received at list one form of prehospital care. The median prehospital time was 5 hours (IQR: 2–14) with the smallest to be 1 hour and the largest 24 hours. The common prehospital care services received include fluid administration, bleeding control, wound dressing, immobilization of suspected fracture and C-spine stabilization. Of these, 72.9 % received fluid administration and 62 % had received bleeding control. (Supplementary Table 1)

In the bivariable binary logistic regression analysis residence, educational level, cause of injury, and mechanism of injury were found to have a p-value of <0.25. In multivariable binary logistic regression, rural residence, educational level of read and write, cause of injuries with violence/quarrel, motor vehicle accident, and gun-shot, and penetrating mechanism of injury showed statistically significant association with prehospital care use.

The odds of using prehospital care among rural residents were 4.8 times higher than urban residents (AOR = 4.80; 95 % CI = 2.90, 8.00). In addition, the odds of prehospital care usage in those with educational level of read and write was associated with 46 % lower odds of prehospital care utilization compared to those who completed secondary school (AOR = 0.54; 95 %CI = 0.30, 0.96). Similarly, the odds of prehospital care utilization among those being injured with violence/quarrel were 2.6 times higher compared to those injured with accidental falls (AOR = 2.60; 95 % CI = 1.50, 4.53). The odds of prehospital care usage in those being injured with a motor vehicle accident (MVA) were 2.00 times higher compared to those injured with accidental falls (AOR = 2.00; 95 % CI = 1.05, 3.44). Those injured with gun-shot have 8.61 times higher odds of prehospital care utilization compared to those injured with accidental falls (AOR = 8.61; 95 % CI = 4.32, 17.2). Furthermore, the odds of using prehospital care were 66 % lower in those who were injured with penetrating mechanism of injuries compared to those with blunt injury (AOR = 0.34; 95 % CI = 0.20, 0.60). Ten individuals participated in interviews exploring the barriers and facilitators to the use of prehospital care, comprising five healthcare professionals and five trauma patients. The patient group was equally diverse in terms of occupational and demographic backgrounds. This selection ensured representation of different injury mechanisms and contexts of prehospital care use.

Thematic analysis synthesized interview data from healthcare professionals (HCWs) and patients in West Amhara to explore barriers, facilitators, and recommendations for improving prehospital care utilization among trauma patients. The analysis is structured using the WHO Emergency Care System Framework (ECSF), which categorizes findings into Scene, Transport, and Facility domains, while allowing for new emergent themes (Supplementary Table 2).

Scene: limited awareness, communication deficiencies, and systemic barriers to prehospital care

A lack of awareness about prehospital care services, compounded by the absence of an organized emergency communication and dispatch system, significantly hinders access and utilization in West Amhara. The most pressing need identified is the establishment of a reliable emergency communication system, akin to a 911 service, to enable rapid reporting of emergencies and coordinated medical response. Additional systemic barriers include resource shortages, such as a lack of ambulances, trained personnel, and medical supplies, as well as poor infrastructure, particularly in rural areas. Economic constraints, cultural practices such as reliance on traditional remedies, and the lack of supportive policies further exacerbate the already fragile state of prehospital care services in the region.

HCW03 pointed out, “The issue is not quality but lack of knowledge about prehospital care.” The absence of a well-publicized emergency contact system makes it difficult for people to access care when needed most, as HCW04 explained: “A major obstacle is the absence of a centralized, well-publicized emergency contact system, making it difficult for people to access care when needed most.”

Transport: barriers and facilitators in the transfer of trauma patients

One of the most critical barriers to timely prehospital care is the difficulty in transporting trauma patients to healthcare facilities. Many patients experience delays due to inadequate ambulance services, poor road infrastructure, and financial constraints. Some interviewees reported that trauma patients had to wait for long periods or improvise dangerous transportation methods due to the lack of available ambulances.

HCW01 stated, “There are no enough ambulances and necessary medical supplies”, highlighting the practical challenges faced by healthcare workers in pre-hospital settings. Patients shared firsthand experiences of these challenges. P02 remarked, "We then transported him using a horse to the highway, where car transportation was available." The financial burden associated with accessing care was further emphasized by P02, who stated, "…we are struggling financially to cover the cost of transportation… The surgery was delayed until this morning due to financial constraints which delayed our arrival to the hospital." Additionally, security concerns and armed conflict in some areas further deter safe and timely transport to medical facilities. P03 described these risks: "The presence of armed groups fighting with government forces… made us fearful."

Despite these barriers, some facilitators help mitigate transport challenges. Community solidarity plays a crucial role, as neighbors and local networks assist in finding transportation and financial support for injured individuals. Healthcare workers also attempt to bridge the gap by issuing referral letters and advising patients on the best ways to reach higher-level facilities.

P05 highlighted this communal support: "My friends also helped me financially… I truly value the people who were there for me." Similarly, P04 shared how proactive medical professionals helped: "They sutured the wound… issued a referral letter and strongly advised us to seek further treatment as soon as possible."

These facilitators demonstrate the resilience of communities and healthcare workers in overcoming the transport-related barriers to prehospital care.

Facility: strengthening the pre-hospital system (Infrastructure and human resources)

Participants consistently emphasized the need to strengthen the pre-hospital system through improvements in infrastructure, transport networks, and human resources. Limited ambulance availability, shortages of emergency personnel, and poor road access were identified as major barriers to timely emergency response. HCW01 underscored this point: “Having more ambulances and healthcare personnel is critical.”. The importance of early interventions in pre-hospital settings was also recognized. HCW03 noted: “Early hemorrhage control in prehospital settings can prevent trauma-related deaths.”

Participants strongly linked road and transport infrastructure to patient outcomes. Inadequate road networks were described as a frequent cause of delays in ambulance arrival and hospital transfers. HCW05 stated: “Better roads and more ambulances would significantly reduce delays in reaching patients.”

Emergent theme: advocacy and public education

Beyond system-level investments, participants highlighted the need for advocacy and public education to improve pre-hospital care. Community awareness was viewed as essential for encouraging bystander assistance, promoting the use of emergency services, and reducing delays in seeking care. HCW03 emphasized this: “Educating the community on how to respond to accidents could save many lives before patients reach the hospital.”.

Together, these perspectives suggest that building an effective pre-hospital care system requires not only technical and infrastructural improvements but also active advocacy and community education initiatives to ensure greater utilization and sustainability.

Overall, the qualitative findings revealed barriers and facilitators mapped onto the WHO ECSF, including themes related to understanding of prehospital care, barriers to prehospital care, and facility readiness. In addition, an emerging theme of advocacy and public education was identified, underscoring participants’ strong emphasis on the role of community awareness and stakeholder engagement in improving trauma care systems.

Discussion

EMS is the system that organizes all aspects of care provided to patients in the pre-hospital environment. Therefore, it is a critical component of the health systems and is necessary to improve outcomes of injuries and other time-sensitive illnesses [25]. Prehospital care is still not proportional with the number of patients with trauma in Ethiopia, although there are some improvements in human resources and infrastructures [15]. The provision of prehospital care might be further compromised during conflicts because of disruption in infrastructure, persistent social distrust, scarce formal emergency care training, and lack of resources and supplies [1]. This study determined the prehospital care use, and associated factors among trauma patients visiting emergency departments of public hospitals.

This study found that 49.8 % of trauma patients in west Amhara received at least one form of prehospital care. This proportion is notably higher than previous reports from Ethiopia, such as the 29 % found in earlier studies in the same region, and is also greater than figures reported in Addis Ababa (16.7 %) and other LMICs like India (2 %) and Nigeria (2.3 %) [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. The improvement may be attributed to increased community awareness and stakeholder engagement in trauma response, as well as recent investments in emergency care infrastructure. However, ambulance utilization remains low (15.8 %), with most patients arriving by contract taxi or on foot, reflecting ongoing challenges in EMS accessibility and public reliance on informal transport [26]. This implies that, while access to some form of prehospital care is improving, the quality and timeliness of this care are likely suboptimal, potentially affecting patient outcomes.

The median prehospital time was 5 hours (IQR: 2–14), significantly exceeding the "golden hour" recommended for optimal trauma outcomes [26]. Only about half of the patients arrived at a health facility within one hour of injury. This delay is consistent with other Ethiopian studies and highlights systemic barriers such as poor infrastructure, lack of organized EMS, and limited ambulance availability [26,30,31]. The most common prehospital interventions were fluid administration (72.9 %) and bleeding control (62.0 %), but these were often provided by relatives or bystanders rather than trained professionals, echoing findings from Addis Ababa and other LMICs where lay responders play a major role due to EMS limitations [26,32]. This implies that, despite some progress, the delay in reaching definitive care and the reliance on untrained first responders likely contribute to preventable morbidity and mortality.

Rural residents were 4.8 times more likely to receive prehospital care than urban residents, possibly due to greater travel distances and reliance on community-based first aid. This pattern is consistent with other studies in Ethiopia and LMICs, where injury severity and context influence care-seeking behavior [26,30,32]. This would suggest that targeted education and EMS expansion could improve prehospital care access, especially for less severe or less visible injuries.

Lower prehospital care utilization among individuals with "read and write" education (AOR = 0.54; 95 % CI = 0.30–0.96) aligns with evidence linking education to health literacy, where higher education improves awareness of emergency services, as demonstrated by studies in Germany and Wisconsin [33,34]. While education is an important factor influencing awareness of emergency services, the relationship between educational level and prehospital care utilization is not straightforward. The 66 % lower odds of prehospital care use for penetrating injuries (AOR = 0.34; 95 % CI = 0.20–0.60) may stem from distrust in formal EMS in conflict zones, acuity-driven decisions to use private transport for severe haemorrhage, and resource limitations hindering effective prehospital interventions for such injuries [35,36]. These findings suggest a need for targeted health literacy programs and community-based interventions to improve EMS utilization and address context-specific barriers.

The qualitative component of this concurrent mixed-methods study revealed critical themes influencing prehospital care in west Amhara. Interviewees attributed low ambulance utilization (quantitatively found to be 15.8 %) to limited availability and misconceptions about costs. These qualitative insights contextualize the quantitative findings: poor infrastructure and reliance on informal transport explain extended prehospital delays. Rural residents’ greater prehospital care use was explained by reliance on informal networks—neighbors, relatives, or community leaders—who provided first aid or arranged transport (horse carts or local vehicles). Urban patients tended to bypass informal interventions and proceed directly to hospitals. These findings highlight adaptive community responses to limited formal EMS. Furthermore, qualitative data reveals that the observed correlation between lower literacy and reduced prehospital care utilization (AOR=0.54) is linked to participants’ observations of lack of awareness hindering service recognition. Likewise, violence/MVA-related injuries prompted more prehospital care-seeking (AOR=2.6–8.61) due to community mobilization around severe, visible injuries despite systemic gaps.

The mixed-methods design and inclusion of multiple referral hospitals with a large sample provided comprehensive insights into quantitative outcomes and the lived experiences of stakeholders and patients. Qualitative data from nurses and physicians across key emergency services added valuable contextual understanding. Limitations include potential recall bias, exclusion of non-trauma patients, focus on referral hospitals, and a small purposive qualitative sample, which may limit representativeness. The qualitative strand complemented the quantitative analysis rather than generating statistically generalizable conclusions, and future studies with broader samples are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Prehospital care utilization among trauma patients in West Amhara has improved but remains inadequate, with delays and reliance on informal providers. Rural residence and injury severity increase the likelihood of receiving prehospital intervention, yet systemic and community-level barriers persist, particularly for less severe injuries. To improve outcomes, organized EMS should be expanded, ambulance availability increased, first responders trained, and public education strengthened, with special attention to conflict-affected and rural areas. Policymakers should prioritize EMS infrastructure and protocols to ensure timely, effective trauma care.
