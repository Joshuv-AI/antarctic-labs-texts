Impact of survivorship stories on the interdisciplinary critical care team: a qualitative study
Authors: Sara U Dorn, Daniel Van Dussen, Ashley K Poole
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceHealthcare providers (HCPs) working in intensive care units (ICUs) face high levels of stress, burnout and emotional exhaustion. There is limited research on interventions that foster resilience and connection among interdisciplinary ICU teams. Storytelling, particularly through ICU patient survivorship narratives, may offer a novel approach to improving team morale, reducing burnout and enhancing workplace belongingness.ObjectivesTo explore the impact of ICU survivor stories on the interdisciplinary ICU team’s perceptions of their role in survivorship, resilience, burnout and sense of belonging at work.Design, setting and participantsThis qualitative study used a cross-sectional survey and focus group design at a large academic hospital. Participants included 39 members of the ICU interdisciplinary team who reviewed ICU survivor profiles and completed a survey. A subset of participants engaged in focus groups.AnalysisSurvey data were analysed using descriptive statistics. Open-ended survey responses and focus group transcripts underwent thematic analysis using descriptive and in vivo coding, followed by consensus-based theme development.ResultsAll survey items had mean scores above 3.0, indicating overall agreement with the positive impact of the survivor profiles. Six key themes emerged from qualitative analysis: validation and closure, learning and growth, gratitude, team building and collaboration, burnout and sense of belonging. Participants reported that the profiles provided emotional closure, reinforced the value of their work and fostered interdisciplinary appreciation. Some participants noted disparities in recognition across disciplines, highlighting opportunities for more inclusive team-building strategies.ConclusionsICU survivor stories may serve as a meaningful intervention to support emotional well-being, reduce burnout and enhance team cohesion among critical care providers. These narratives offer a humanising perspective on patient recovery and may help staff reconnect with the purpose behind their work. Future research should explore the long-term impact of such interventions and their potential to improve both provider well-being and patient care outcomes.Keywords: Critical care; Burnout, Professional; Attitude of Health Personnel; Survivorship

Summary of main findings

Intensive care unit (ICU) survivor stories may serve as a meaningful intervention to support emotional well-being, reduce burnout and enhance team cohesion among critical care providers and may help staff reconnect with the purpose behind their work.

Background

The ICU is an environment in which healthcare providers (HCPs) experience cumulative and chronic stress resulting from challenging workplace conditions and daily witness to physical suffering and death. Burnout and attrition rates are higher among HCPs working in critical care when compared to other inpatient practice areas.1 2 Research shows that meaningful connections with patients and colleagues can enhance job satisfaction by reinforcing a sense of purpose.3 Therefore, fostering these connections through targeted interventions is essential for supporting HCP well-being, preventing burnout and potentially improving patient care outcomes.

A review of interventions for preventing burnout across various populations revealed that a combination of person- and organisation-directed interventions was most effective in combating burnout.4 Good communication, team cohesiveness, value alignment and having a clear understanding of roles have been shown to reduce burnout and improve job satisfaction and staff retention.25,7 Literature investigating specific interventions that foster connections between interdisciplinary HCPs and patients is sparse.

Objectives

HCPs are often drawn to the critical care setting for opportunities to work in a fast-paced environment with complex patient cases, team collaboration and for lifelong learning. Challenges related to working in critical care medicine include emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, isolation and reduced professional efficacy.8 9 Subsequently, burnout and intent to leave are common among critical care staff,10 11 with ICU nursing turnover at 26.7%1 and intensivist burnout rates up to 46.5%.5 This may lead to a disruption of workflow, reduced team morale and a reduction in quality of care, patient satisfaction and patient safety.1 3 12

Storytelling is known in psychological and social science fields as an effective strategy to improve ‘sense-making’ of experiences, enhance the social strength of communities13 and can be used to benefit clinical practice and health education.14 15 Schwartz Rounds, a forum where multidisciplinary staff share personal reflections on complex patient cases, have been associated with improved staff well-being, reduced feelings of isolation, greater team appreciation and perceived benefits for teamwork and patient care.16 A small qualitative study among ICU nursing staff suggests that the use of storytelling as a relational strategy may be protective against components of burnout, such as depersonalisation and cynicism.17 However, we are unaware of available research on storytelling on the broader interdisciplinary ICU team.

In response to the Critical Care Societies Collaborative call to action to develop sustainable strategies that promote staff resilience and wellness in critical care settings,18 this study explores the impact of sharing successful patient outcomes—referred to as ICU survivor profiles—with the critical care team. While most existing research on burnout and intent to leave focuses on critical care nurses, there is limited insight into workplace well-being across the broader interdisciplinary team. The objective of this study is to examine how ICU survivor profiles influence the interdisciplinary team’s perception of their role in ICU survivorship, their levels of resilience and burnout and their sense of belongingness at work.

Materials and methods

Design

This study employed a prospective cross-sectional survey-based design. Subjects who met the inclusion criteria participated via an anonymous online survey.

Setting

The study was conducted within the medical and surgical ICUs of a large academic teaching hospital in the Southeastern United States. In 2021, this health system established the Critical Care Recovery Center, a clinic providing ambulatory physical and occupational therapy to ICU survivors. Patients are referred to the clinic if they have an ICU stay of at least 4 days, are on mechanical ventilation, or experience other risk factors for Post Intensive Care Syndrome (PICS) with physical or cognitive impairment. At the end of their rehabilitation course, patients and their families were given the option to share their recovery experiences with ICU staff members via a ‘Meet Me Again’ patient profile (box 1). If willing, patients signed an authorisation for release of information, understanding that these stories would be printed, compiled in binders and made available to the ICU staff. These profiles were collected as a new initiative at this clinic; they had not been shared with the ICU staff prior to this study.

Participants

Participants included any member of the interdisciplinary team working either full-time, part-time or as needed on the medical and/or surgical ICUs. A call for participants was made via email and/or a flyer posted on the ICUs. Participants were invited to view the ‘Meet Me Again’ patient profiles and the survey was accessed via quick response (QR) code or anonymous email link. The survey ensured respondent anonymity and remained active for 60 days following distribution.

Survey procedure

This study was approved and determined exempt by the participating institutions’ Institutional Review Board (Pro00111217).

The ‘Meet Me Again’ patient profiles were compiled in two binders and stored in each ICU’s staff break room. In the call for participants, individuals were invited to view the profiles at a time convenient to them. The binders were left in the break rooms for staff to view as many times as they wanted during the study period. After viewing the profiles, individuals could scan a QR code or use the anonymous link provided to access the QualtricsXM Survey platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT). First, the details of the study were outlined, and participants were informed of the voluntary nature, benefits and risks of the study. Informed consent was implied when participants clicked on ‘Yes, I agree to participate’. After providing consent, participants were presented with the survey where they rated the impact of patient stories on a 5-point Likert scale, with optional free-text responses. The online survey was available for 60 days from April to June 2023. Participants also provided demographic information and details about their profession and practice area.

Focus groups

Following survey completion, participants were asked if they were willing to participate in a focus group to discuss their experience with the ICU survivor patient profiles. If a participant selected ‘yes’, they were taken to a non-anonymous survey where they provided their contact information. Focus groups were voluntary, and only participants who opted in were invited to participate. Participants were not obligated to answer any question for which they did not wish to respond, and each verbalised consent at the beginning of each focus group.

Three focus group sessions were conducted via Zoom and scheduled based on participant availability during September and October 2023 and each lasted approximately 90 min. Sample focus group questions are provided in box 2. The sessions were recorded, transcribed and saved on a secure server. One researcher moderated, while a second researcher took notes during and after the focus groups. Primary outcomes included perceived impact on burnout, resilience and sense of belonging. No confounders or effect modifiers were analysed due to the qualitative nature of the study.

Data analysis

Demographic variables of the survey respondents were reported using descriptive statistics, specifically frequencies and percentages. The mean and SD were reported for each item on the survey. No subgroup or interaction analyses were conducted.

Responses to the open-ended survey question and focus group transcripts underwent thematic analysis. Descriptive and in vivo coding were used in the first cycle of coding. Potential bias due to prior ICU experience of two authors was mitigated by including a neutral third researcher in the analysis. Responses were first independently coded by two researchers followed by a meeting to achieve consensus with the neutral third researcher using a collaborative open coding process. In the second cycle of coding, broader categories and themes that emerged from the data were identified by examining patterns across the previously coded segments.

Participants who did not complete the survey were excluded from analysis. No imputation was performed. Most respondents were female nurses aged 25–44 years with 1–10 years of experience.

Sixteen individuals initially volunteered to participate in the focus groups, and all were invited to participate. Of those, four did not respond on further inquiry to participate. Twelve participants were scheduled for focus groups, but three of them dropped out due to scheduling conflicts. Therefore, nine participants participated in and completed the focus groups. There were three participants in the first focus group, four participants in the second focus group and two participants in the third focus group. Across the focus groups, participation consisted of one medical doctor (MD), one physical therapist (PT), one occupational therapist (OT), two registered nurses (RNs), two speech-language pathologists (SLPs) and two pharmacists.

Six key themes were identified during three focus group sessions that captured the impact of the ‘Meet Me Again’ patient profiles on the interdisciplinary team: validation and closure, learning and growth, gratitude, team building and collaboration, burnout and sense of belonging. Each theme is described below with associated italicised quotes and is identified by the discipline who made the statement.

Validation and closure

Participants described difficulties associated with the ‘open-endedness’ of complex patient encounters and expressed that the profiles provided a sense of closure and accomplishment in a setting where it is uncommon to hear about what happens to their patients after ICU discharge.

MD: I think most people are working in the ICU have some angst over the fact that, like, we don’t actually know what ends up happening to the patient long term…We don’t know which of these, which of the patients we take care of have that success story at the end. So, it was just wonderful to see that this is the reason why we do it.

RN: I feel like, especially with so much turnover that nursing has now, like it might be a way for new critical care nurses, and you know it’s good for us who have been around a while to just to have like, like, I said before some closure and feel like, oh, we actually do good things for some people.

Others mentioned that having the patient’s room number associated with the profiles further contributed to the sense of closure.

RN: You get sometimes negative feelings about a certain room, depending on things that you experienced in that room. So then, having them say the actual room in the physical space, and knowing that something good that came from that is therapeutic.

Pharmacist: Part of this is like I experience on a daily basis, almost like trauma, right?…I like the room number. That’s my way of like, you know, attaching the patient to the room. But then also, like it humanizes it later in the end. And I get that closure. I don’'t know if that makes sense at all. But no, I detach, but then I try to reattach.

The profiles were also validating for the participants, signifying that their contributions were meaningful, recognised and appreciated.

RN: I was most heartened to hear what an impact nursing can actually make. And with their families and with their recovery.

OT: Through the profiles I can find a sense of purpose, and like confirm that for myself.

Pharmacist: It’s nice to hear how I am impacting the day to day.

Learning and growth

Participants acknowledged the existing gap in knowledge about the challenges patients experience following critical illness and viewed the profiles as learning opportunities. There was enthusiasm about the role of ICU staff in optimising long-term outcomes and using patient stories to generate ideas for improving patient care. Additionally, participants from several disciplines noted that sharing positive ICU survivor outcomes during training and onboarding could be beneficial for both new and experienced clinicians.

Pharmacist: Understanding what the challenges are was pretty eye opening.

OT: it’s great and encouraging to see success stories, hear about success stories, and very interesting to hear from the patient perspective about what they do remember and what they don’t, and their big takeaways or recommendations. I think that’s how we grow.

OT: I happened to be there when PT One was mentioning to the other therapist that he was in the profile, and that he should go check it out, and it just so happened that that therapist had a young resident with them…and I think it was really neat that they together were able to go reference the profile. That younger generation of professional was able to see the impact that the more seasoned professional was able to provide.

The participants indicated that the stories could also help garner multidisciplinary support for modernising evidence-based practice patterns.

Pharmacist: I think that they help me in that because the evidence it’s just like data doesn’t sometimes speak all the time in the ICU. But the stories do. And so, I think that they help yeah, just translate that that information better.

RN: It was hard for me as well as a lot of nurses, I think, when we started doing less sedation with patients. But now, seeing like how advantageous that is…thank you to the pharmacists for really pushing that and the care team. But it’s easier for me to like teach people like newer nurses how to work through that cause it’s definitely a skill.

Gratitude

There was a strong sense of gratitude felt among the participants for the opportunity to hear patient success stories. This reinforced the meaningfulness of their work and provided emotional fulfilment.

OT: I am just grateful that people took the time to do this and gave voice to some of the patients…they were able to feel like they could reach back out to staff…and kind of maybe round out that chapter for themselves. I think all around, this was a very touching and very meaningful thing to be a part of.

SLP: I love to know what people are up to and see that they’re thriving…I was happy.

Team building and collaboration

Participants recognised the importance of interdisciplinary efforts that contribute to patients’ recoveries through the survivor profiles. The collaborative nature of ICU work was highlighted and for some, the stories fostered a sense of team unity. The setting in which the stories were read influenced the degree of perceived positive impact on team building; reading with colleagues led to stronger feelings of team building.

MD: The assumption of most people…is that the doctor does this and everyone follows suit…that’s not true at all…it was heartening to see that patients recognized all the effort that all the people put into getting them better. So, I don’t wanna say like minimizes my role because I don’t think it does, but it helps puts a physician role in in the context of an ICU.

RN: I think the location of having them in the break room facilitated that a little bit, because, as you’re standing there reading them like someone from respiratory therapy would walk in, or the pharmacist or provider, and it just kind of helped us start conversation about those folks.

Pharmacist: I always feel like I read them by myself, and then I just had to go cry…To be honest like, I haven’t talked a lot about the individual stories with many members of the team… it was nice to see, you know some positives come out of it, because we saw so much tragedy.

Burnout

The survivor profiles were mostly seen as morale-boosting and subjectively helped reduce feelings of burnout by reminding staff of the positive outcomes of their work. The patient profiles were a tangible reminder of survivor success stories, which was particularly valuable on challenging days and humanised the work that is done in the ICU.

SLP: It was morale-boosting, especially on the hardest days to see that not everyone dies.

MD: I think it what it really helps with, the burnout aspect that it helped with the most, I think, is the purpose of what we do, and then kind of putting up with the day-to-day incremental change that we’re trying to make…It helped with improved engagement in patient care…fuel to do the ordinary stuff extraordinarily well

Sense of belonging

There were mixed responses regarding the survivor stories’ impact on sense of belonging on the interdisciplinary team. Some participants felt the profiles promoted their sense of belonging.

PT: I think it helped out tremendously with the sense of belonging for the team. There were a number of times if I wasn’t mentioned by name, my profession was, or even a colleague, and just having that notoriety and recognition that we helped them get better, and that we were part of the team was really nice.

SLP: I feel that I can share this experience with lots of people - feel like we’re in a club together.

Alternatively, there were several participants who did not feel the survivor stories enhanced their sense of belonging, mostly due to a sense of inequity in recognition by either patients or colleagues.

SLP: I feel like, we’re often the forgotten like we’re like the forgotten therapy.

OT: as an OT, usually, very frequently, and kind of comedically, we’re lumped in with PT… It’s nice to be recognized, it’s nice to feel like I’ve contributed, but as far as like a sense of belonging on the team, and like being seen, I think that there’s still room for that for improvement.

Discussion

This study explored the impact of ICU survivor stories on the interdisciplinary ICU team’s perceptions of their role in survivorship, resilience, burnout and sense of belonging at work. The strongest themes that emerged from this project were validation and closure, learning and growth and gratitude. Participants overwhelmingly felt that engaging with patient survivor stories offered a sense of closure on complex patient cases and reinforced their personal sense of value on the interdisciplinary team. Participants also noted that ICU survivor profiles could serve as a valuable tool for onboarding and training new staff. While HCPs felt that patient stories helped to reduce feelings of burnout, there were mixed perceptions on how the profiles impacted their sense of belonging on the interdisciplinary team.

While survey and focus group responses largely evoked positive feelings about the potential impact of survivor stories on burnout prevention, the strength of this effect may be lessened due to the multifactorial aetiology of burnout and that burnout is a condition that necessitates long-term management strategies.19 Strategies that are protective against burnout and intent to leave in critical care clinicians include a strong personal sense of purpose and value alignment in work20,22 in addition to effective communication and positive working relationships across HCPs.5 6 23 Studies show that involvement in ICU follow-up clinics can reduce burnout, enhance understanding of survivorship and support professional growth among multidisciplinary staff.24,29 Similarly, exposure to ICU survivor stories may foster a stronger sense of purpose and resilience, though such benefits are best realised as part of a broader, multifaceted approach to workforce well-being.

Despite the positive impact of survivor stories, comments from participants revealed that they do not fully bridge the gap in visibility and inclusion felt by some team members. Clinicians from the fields of speech-language pathology and occupational therapy expressed that, despite contributing meaningfully to patient care, their roles are often overlooked or misunderstood. This aligns with literature revealing that other healthcare professions, such as respiratory therapists, also experience challenges in being recognised for their role in caring for critically ill patients across the continuum of care.20 A physician echoed this theme from a different angle, noting that while doctors are often assumed to be the central figures in patient recovery, it was heartening to see patients acknowledge the collective effort of the entire team. Rather than diminishing the physician’s role, this recognition reframed it within a collaborative context—reinforcing that belonging in the ICU is rooted not in hierarchy, but in shared purpose and mutual respect. This sentiment is echoed in literature emphasising the importance of flattening hierarchies to foster effective interdisciplinary collaboration and team cohesion in critical care settings.30

Several suggestions were made to improve the effectiveness of the ICU survivor profiles. Focus group participants identified feeling a stronger sense of belonging when reading the profiles as a shared experience among colleagues versus reading profiles privately. Some participants suggested that there may be benefits to presenting the profiles in a more structured group setting to more effectively promote team building, participation, reflection and discussion among team members. The literature supports that moral distress can be diminished by promoting opportunities for HCPs to engage in respectful discussion that recognises the viewpoints and experiences of their colleagues.31 Reflecting on the patient survivor stories in a group setting may have positive implications for team functioning, leading to increased compassion satisfaction and group cohesion.32 33 Several participants commented that they would have appreciated the inclusion of video or digital correspondence, which has been shown to increase a sense of emotional connection with patient stories.34 There was also a suggestion to display the ICU survivor stories on the unit more permanently where they would be visible to patients and caregivers.

This project does not aim to address the organisational or environmental barriers to implementing optimal care, which are often drivers of burnout and emotional exhaustion.31 35 Institutional support, such as debriefings after traumatic events, peer-based support and professional psychological services, should continue to be prioritised as essential to addressing the mental health and well-being of HCPs.10 Patient survivor profiles may serve as a meaningful complement to individual wellness practices and stress management strategies. Their availability to critical care HCPs may offer opportunities for meaningful peer-to-peer interactions, learning and reflection on the unique contributions of each team member to high quality patient care.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It addresses a gap in the literature by exploring the impact of ICU survivor stories on the entire interdisciplinary team, not just nurses. The mixed methods design—combining quantitative survey data with qualitative focus group insights—provides a comprehensive understanding of the intervention’s effects. The inclusion of a broad range of ICU professionals strengthens the generalisability of findings across roles, and the thematic analysis offers emotional and professional depth. The use of a real-world, low-cost intervention grounded in authentic patient experiences enhances its feasibility and relevance. Ethical rigour was maintained by including a neutral third-party analyst to mitigate potential bias.

However, the study also has limitations. This study was performed at a single large academic institution which includes level 1 trauma and transplant patient populations, which may not be generalisable to other health systems. We cannot exclude the possibility of sampling bias, as responders participated as volunteers and personal and experiential differences may have contributed to desire to participate. Additionally, while we were able to include responses from a cross-section of the ICU team, our study was not designed to reach discipline-specific saturation levels; therefore, we cannot draw meaningful conclusions comparing disciplines or demographics. Finally, the definition of ‘job burnout’ has been defined in prior literature,36 though existing studies use inconsistent definitions and measurement tools, and diagnosis of burnout is imprecise.19 Due to the qualitative nature of this project, we did not choose to formally define ‘burnout’ and allowed participants to subjectively define their own threshold for symptoms of burnout.

Conclusion

The use of storytelling via patient profiles may be an effective method for sharing gratitude with HCPs and may provide closure and positive reinforcement, especially for early career clinicians. Both survey and focus group participants overwhelmingly perceived value in regularly receiving ICU survivor stories, noting that these narratives can foster internal reflection, peer-to-peer support and potentially inform future patient care practices.

Exposure to successful patient outcomes and humanising experiences following critical illness may play a vital role in sustaining the personal and team well-being of clinicians working in high-stress critical care environments. While it may not be feasible for all ICU staff to engage in longitudinal patient care, providing access to survivor stories could offer similar emotional and professional benefits.

This research contributes to a deeper understanding of how highlighting patient recovery narratives can impact ICU staff. We recommend that future studies evaluate the effectiveness of such interventions in enhancing the well-being of individual HCPs and the interdisciplinary care team. Additionally, future research should explore how these experiences differ across professional roles to tailor support strategies more effectively.
