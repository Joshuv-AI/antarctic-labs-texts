Admission Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index and 90-Day AECOPD-Related Unplanned Readmission: A Single-Center Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Qiong Chen, Sufang Xu, Jin Wang, Mei Feng, Shaowen Wang, Man Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) is derived from routine blood counts, but its incremental value for disease-specific early readmission after acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) remains uncertain. We evaluated the association between admission SII and 90-day AECOPD-related unplanned readmission and its value beyond available clinical variables.MethodsThis single-center retrospective development cohort included 207 unique patients hospitalized for AECOPD from January 2024 to January 2025; only the first eligible hospitalization per patient was analyzed. Readmissions were ascertained from electronic records and structured telephone follow-up completed on June 30, 2025. SII was calculated from the first blood count obtained within 24 hours of arrival. Logistic regression, receiver operating characteristic analysis, 1000-resample bootstrap internal validation, calibration assessment and decision curve analysis were performed. Bootstrap validation assessed optimism in discrimination, calibration and net benefit.ResultsSeventy-eight patients (37.7%) experienced AECOPD-related unplanned readmission within 90 days. Each 500-unit increase in SII was associated with higher adjusted odds of readmission (odds ratio 1.329, 95% confidence interval 1.153–1.530; p < 0.001). Apparent AUC was 0.809 (95% confidence interval 0.742–0.869) for the integrated model versus 0.752 (0.678–0.821) for the limited clinical model. The absolute increase was 0.057 (bootstrap 95% confidence interval 0.014–0.101), and adding SII improved model fit (likelihood-ratio p < 0.001). Optimism-corrected AUC, calibration intercept and slope were 0.785, −0.003 and 0.874. Otherwise identical NLR and PLR models had corrected AUCs of 0.775 and 0.765; pairwise differences were inconclusive.ConclusionHigher admission SII was associated with 90-day AECOPD-related unplanned readmission and improved discrimination beyond a limited clinical model. These exploratory single-center findings require external validation, recalibration and prospective impact assessment before clinical implementation.Keywords: acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, systemic immune-inflammation index, unplanned readmission, clinical prediction model, internal validation, inflammation

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) imposes a major burden on mortality, disability, health-care use and quality of life worldwide.1,2 Acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) accelerate lung-function decline, worsen symptoms and frequently require hospitalization.1,3 Readmission is common during the vulnerable period after discharge, and contemporary meta-analyses have documented substantial 30- and 90-day readmission rates.4,5

Existing readmission tools and systematic reviews identify previous admissions, comorbidities, dyspnea, functional status, frailty and hospitalization characteristics as important predictors.6–8 The PEARL score, for example, predicts 90-day readmission or death using previous admissions, extended Medical Research Council dyspnea score, age, right- and left-sided heart failure.7 Our dataset did not contain several of these domains; consequently, the comparator used here is explicitly a limited clinical model rather than a comprehensive contemporary risk tool. A recent two-center study externally validated a 90-day readmission model in older patients and included SII, NLR and other early-admission variables, further emphasizing the need to position single-center development findings cautiously.9

Systemic inflammation provides a biological rationale for evaluating blood-count-derived indices after AECOPD.2,10 NLR and PLR have been associated with adverse AECOPD outcomes.11,12 SII integrates platelet, neutrophil and lymphocyte counts and has been associated with adverse outcomes, prolonged hospitalization and frequent exacerbations in COPD populations.13–15 These indices overlap biologically and mathematically, so evidence that SII adds value should distinguish association, incremental prediction and comparative performance.

The primary objective was to estimate the adjusted association between admission SII and 90-day AECOPD-related unplanned readmission. Secondary objectives were to quantify the incremental prognostic value of SII beyond a limited model containing available clinical variables, compare otherwise identical models containing SII, NLR or PLR, and internally validate discrimination, calibration and net benefit. This was an exploratory development study without external validation.

Material and Methods

Study Design, Cohort Construction and Ethics

This single-center retrospective observational cohort study screened 323 potentially eligible patients aged at least 40 years who had been hospitalized with a suspected or provisional diagnosis of acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) at the Anhui No. 2 Provincial People’s Hospital between January 2024 and January 2025. At source-data retrieval, authorized clinical staff jointly used each patient’s unique inpatient number and national identification number to identify and de-duplicate source records. No patient was retrieved or entered into the screening cohort more than once. If a patient had more than one potentially eligible hospitalization, only the earliest eligible hospitalization was retained as the index hospitalization. The resulting 323 screening records therefore represented 323 unique patients and contained no repeated index hospitalizations. Any qualifying readmission within 90 days after index discharge was treated solely as an outcome event and was never reused as a new index hospitalization.

Clinical eligibility criteria were: (1) age at least 40 years; (2) COPD documented by a respiratory specialist, with persistent airflow obstruction supported by a recorded prior stable-state post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio <0.70; and (3) an index hospitalization primarily attributable to AECOPD, as defined below. Patients were excluded from the final complete-case analysis if they had severe hepatic or renal disease; pneumonia or another acute pulmonary infection judged to be the principal diagnosis rather than a precipitating factor for AECOPD; psychiatric illness or inability to cooperate with follow-up; incomplete prespecified clinical or laboratory data; an unascertained 90-day outcome because of loss to follow-up or unwillingness to continue follow-up; or death before complete 90-day outcome ascertainment. Severe renal disease was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, maintenance dialysis or established renal failure. Severe hepatic disease was defined as decompensated cirrhosis with ascites, hepatic encephalopathy or variceal bleeding. Of the 323 screened patients, 116 were excluded: 35 were lost to follow-up or unwilling to continue follow-up, 28 had incomplete clinical or laboratory data, 23 had severe hepatic or renal disease, 18 had pneumonia or another acute pulmonary infection as the principal diagnosis rather than AECOPD, seven had psychiatric illness or were unable to cooperate, and five died before complete follow-up. The final cohort comprised 207 patients, including 129 without and 78 with AECOPD-related unplanned readmission within 90 days. Cohort construction is shown in Figure 1.

Authorized clinical staff used protected identifiers solely for patient-level retrieval, linkage and de-duplication. The unique inpatient number and national identification number were jointly checked before de-identification; names, inpatient numbers, national identification numbers and other direct identifiers were removed before creation of the analytical dataset. The final spreadsheet contained only de-identified analysis IDs. Access to identifiable information was restricted, and all analyses were conducted using de-identified data. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical Definitions

An index hospitalization was classified as AECOPD when a patient with documented COPD experienced worsening dyspnea and/or cough and sputum over ≤14 days.16 For the present retrospective hospitalized cohort, the deterioration also had to exceed normal day-to-day variation, constitute the principal reason for admission, and require treatment beyond the patient’s usual maintenance therapy.1 Case classification used the documented discharge diagnosis together with the available presenting symptoms, clinical findings, laboratory results, electrocardiography and chest imaging. Admissions primarily explained by pneumonia, acute heart failure, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax or another condition were not classified as AECOPD.17 Acute heart failure was evaluated using clinical findings, BNP or NT-proBNP and echocardiography; suspected pulmonary embolism was assessed using D-dimer and computed-tomography pulmonary angiography when indicated; and pneumonia, pneumothorax and other alternative diagnoses were evaluated using clinical and imaging findings. Pulmonary function variables were retrospectively extracted from historical medical records and were not remeasured or recalculated for this study. FEV1% predicted and FEV1/FVC were taken from the most recent recorded stable-state post-bronchodilator spirometry before the index admission. Although the recorded FEV1/FVC ratio and reported FEV1% predicted were available, the original spirometry curves, individual test-session quality grades and the specific reference equations used to calculate FEV1% predicted were not consistently retained in the accessible records and could not be retrospectively verified. The fixed FEV1/FVC ratio used for eligibility did not itself require a predicted-value reference equation; however, the technical acceptability and repeatability of the historical tests could not be independently assessed.

Outcome Ascertainment and Follow-Up

The primary outcome was the first AECOPD-related unplanned readmission within 90 days after index discharge. Follow-up was completed on June 30, 2025, after all index admissions had the opportunity to complete 90 days. Outcome status was determined jointly from the hospital electronic medical record system and structured telephone follow-up conducted by clinicians. Follow-up recorded whether an unplanned hospitalization occurred, its date, hospital and principal reason. Readmissions to the study hospital and other institutions were included. Outside-hospital events were verified where possible using discharge records, diagnostic certificates or other medical documents; when complete documents were unavailable, classification used patient or family reports together with available records.

An event was classified as AECOPD-related when an unplanned hospitalization was prompted by acute worsening of dyspnea and/or cough and sputum and no alternative principal disease better explained the admission. Planned admissions and admissions primarily attributable to pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, acute heart failure, pneumothorax or another disease were not outcomes. Clinicians adjudicating the outcome were blinded to SII. Only the first qualifying readmission was counted. Thirty-five screened patients were lost to follow-up or unwilling to continue follow-up, and five died before complete 90-day ascertainment; these patients were excluded before the binary complete-case analysis. Because their complete predictor and time-to-event data were unavailable, a competing-risk analysis could not be performed.

Predictor Measurement

Demographic and clinical variables were extracted from the electronic medical record. Baseline laboratory indicators were the first results obtained within 24 hours after arrival. Prehospital and emergency-department records were reviewed for systemic corticosteroid and antibiotic exposure before sampling; however, treatment timing was incompletely documented for some patients, so the data cannot establish that every sample preceded treatment. Smoking history was ascertained by asking patients whether they had ever smoked. Any patient who reported any previous smoking was classified as having a smoking history; smoking frequency, packs per day, duration and pack-years were not collected. Drinking history indicated any documented alcohol use before admission based on the patient history; quantity, frequency and duration were not sufficiently complete to define hazardous use. History of stroke was ascertained from the hospital case-record system. Any documented cerebral infarction, including mild cerebral infarction, was counted as a history of stroke.

SII, NLR and PLR were calculated from counts reported in 109/L using the following formulas:18

SII = platelet count x neutrophil count/lymphocyte count

NLR = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; PLR = platelet count/lymphocyte count

The resulting SII unit was 109/L. For the primary regression, SII was scaled per 500 units (SII500 = SII/500).

Statistical Analysis

Reporting followed the TRIPOD+AI and TRIPOD statements, and the completed TRIPOD+AI checklist is supplied as a separate revision file.19,20

Revised analyses were performed in Python 3.12.13 using pandas 3.0.1, NumPy 2.3.5, SciPy 1.18.0 and scikit-learn 1.9.0. Distributional form was assessed using histograms, quantile plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are summarized as mean (standard deviation) and compared using Welch’s t test; skewed variables are summarized as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are n (%) and were compared using the chi-square or Fisher exact test.

All collected candidate predictors and their univariable logistic regression results are reported in Supplementary Table 1. For the reproducible revised model-building procedure, SII was forced as the focal predictor, while age, albumin and FEV1% predicted were retained as clinically selected core adjustment variables. To limit the integrated model to six slope parameters, two additional variables were selected by the smallest univariable Wald p values from six non-inflammatory demographic or history candidates: sex, smoking history, drinking history, diabetes, hypertension and history of stroke. Applied to the full dataset, this rule selected smoking history and drinking history, yielding the reported five-variable limited clinical model and the six-predictor integrated SII model. The same selection rule was repeated independently within every bootstrap resample; the clinical model and each inflammatory-index model used the clinical variables selected in that resample. Other laboratory markers were reported in Supplementary Table 1 but were not eligible for the limited non-inflammatory clinical comparator, and NLR and PLR were not entered concurrently with SII because of mathematical overlap. Instead, otherwise identical models replacing SII with NLR or PLR were fitted as secondary comparisons. This constrained strategy was defined for the revised exploratory analysis rather than in a prospectively registered protocol or statistical analysis plan. The comparator remains limited because prior admissions, exacerbation frequency, dyspnea, frailty, comorbidity burden, adherence, discharge treatment and post-discharge care were unavailable.

The full final model is reported using coefficients, standard errors, odds ratios, confidence intervals, p values, coding and intercept. Nonlinearity for SII, age, albumin and FEV1% predicted was tested by comparing linear terms with four-knot restricted cubic splines. A log2-transformed SII model estimated the effect per doubling. Leverage, standardized Pearson residuals and Cook’s distance were examined; a sensitivity analysis excluded observations with Cook’s distance >4/n. Variance inflation factors assessed collinearity.

Discrimination was summarized by AUC with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. The absolute AUC change between nested models was reported with a paired bootstrap confidence interval, supplemented by the DeLong and likelihood-ratio tests. Sensitivity and specificity at Youden-derived cutoffs are reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals; the cutoffs are exploratory and not proposed for clinical use.

Internal validation used 1000 bootstrap resamples. Each resample repeated the complete defined model-building procedure: the six demographic or history candidates were re-evaluated, two were selected by their univariable Wald p values, the selected variables were combined with the three core clinical variables, and the resulting clinical and inflammatory-index models were refitted. Optimism was estimated from bootstrap-sample minus original-sample performance using the model selected in that resample. Optimism-corrected AUC, calibration intercept, calibration slope and Brier score were reported. A flexible calibration curve used restricted cubic splines of the predicted log odds and pointwise bootstrap intervals. Decision curve analysis was likewise optimism-corrected over threshold probabilities from 10% to 50%, interpreted as the probability at which a clinician might consider enhanced post-discharge review rather than a mandated treatment.21–23

The retrospective sample size was fixed by available eligible records; no a priori prediction-model sample-size calculation was performed. Although 78 events for six slope parameters correspond to a nominal events-per-variable value of 13, this does not account for the larger candidate set, nonlinear terms or model uncertainty and is not interpreted as proof of adequate sample size (Supplementary Table 2). Missingness in the screened population is reported in Figure 1. The final 207-patient dataset contained no missing values in analyzed fields, so complete-case analysis was used. Individual-level comparison of included and excluded patients was not possible because key variables were unavailable for excluded records.

Results

Cohort Flow and Outcome Ascertainment

Among 323 screened patients, 116 were excluded: 35 were lost to follow-up or unwilling to continue follow-up, 28 had incomplete clinical/laboratory data, 23 had severe hepatic or renal disease, 18 had pneumonia or another acute pulmonary infection as the principal diagnosis rather than AECOPD, seven had psychiatric illness or inability to cooperate and five died before complete follow-up. Joint source-record verification using the unique inpatient number and national identification number confirmed that the final dataset comprised 207 unique patients and 207 first eligible index hospitalizations, with no duplicate retrieval or repeated index inclusion (Figure 1). Seventy-eight patients experienced AECOPD-related unplanned readmission within 90 days (37.7%); 58 qualifying readmissions occurred at the study hospital and 20 were identified at other institutions. The remaining 129 patients did not experience the primary outcome. The relatively high event rate likely reflects the elderly hospitalized population, disease-specific follow-up and capture of reported outside-hospital readmissions; nevertheless, misclassification and referral patterns cannot be excluded.

Median admission SII was 1377.63 (interquartile range 841.38–2677.44) in the readmission group and 928.63 (449.31–1590.47) in the non-readmission group (p < 0.001; Figure 2A). The corresponding ROC comparisons are shown in Figure 2B. CRP, WBC, neutrophils, NLR and PLR were also higher in the readmission group, while FEV1% predicted was lower. Self-reported ever-smoking history was present in 63 patients (30.4%). Because smoking frequency, packs per day, duration and pack-years were not collected, this variable represents only a binary history of any previous smoking and not cumulative tobacco exposure. History of stroke was documented in 95 patients (45.9%); this definition included mild cerebral infarction recorded in the case-record system.

Discrimination, Comparator Models and Internal Validation

SII alone showed modest discrimination (AUC 0.659, 95% CI 0.578–0.733). Its exploratory cutoff of 2042.3 had sensitivity 37.2% (95% CI 27.3–48.3%) and specificity 86.0% (79.0–91.0%), confirming that SII should not be used as a stand-alone screening marker. The limited clinical model had AUC 0.752 (0.678–0.821); adding SII increased AUC to 0.809 (0.742–0.869), an absolute change of 0.057 (bootstrap 95% CI 0.014–0.101; DeLong p = 0.010). The likelihood-ratio statistic for adding SII was 19.44 (p < 0.001) (Table 3 and Figure 2B).

Otherwise identical models containing NLR or PLR had apparent AUCs of 0.800 and 0.793 and optimism-corrected AUCs of 0.775 and 0.765, compared with 0.785 for the SII model (Supplementary Table 4). Functional Form, Influence and Decision Curves

Restricted cubic spline tests found no evidence of nonlinearity for SII, age, albumin or FEV1% predicted (all p for nonlinearity >0.20; Supplementary Table 5). In the log2 sensitivity model, each doubling of SII was associated with adjusted OR 1.822 (95% CI 1.355–2.451; p < 0.001), with AUC 0.807. Fifteen observations exceeded the leverage threshold, three had absolute standardized Pearson residuals >3 and 11 had Cook’s distance >4/n. Excluding these 11 observations strengthened, rather than reversed, the SII association (OR 1.525 per 500 units, 95% CI 1.274–1.825; p < 0.001).

Optimism-corrected decision curves are shown in Figure 5 and Table 5. At a 20% threshold, the corrected net benefit was 0.251 for the integrated model and 0.230 for the limited clinical model. The difference at 10% was only approximately 0.002, whereas larger differences were observed from approximately 20% to 50%. These exploratory results describe potential value for considering enhanced post-discharge review and do not establish a clinically effective intervention.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective development cohort, higher admission SII was independently associated with AECOPD-related unplanned readmission within 90 days. Adding SII to five available clinical variables improved apparent discrimination, and this increase remained evident when expressed as an absolute AUC change with a bootstrap confidence interval and a likelihood-ratio test. Bootstrap correction reduced the integrated AUC from 0.809 to 0.785 and the calibration slope to 0.874, demonstrating measurable optimism. The findings therefore support an association and an incremental signal within this dataset, not a validated decision tool.

The biological interpretation should remain cautious. Neutrophilia, lymphopenia and platelet-related signaling provide plausible context for SII, but admission SII can reflect acute infection, exacerbation severity, physiological stress, comorbidity and treatment exposure.2,10,13 The retrospective data do not establish persistent post-discharge inflammatory vulnerability or a causal contribution of inflammation to readmission. Incomplete documentation of prehospital or emergency treatment may also have influenced blood counts.

Comparative results further temper claims of novelty. SII, NLR and PLR are overlapping summaries. The SII model had numerically higher apparent and corrected AUCs, but direct pairwise intervals did not demonstrate that it outperformed NLR or PLR. A multicenter AECOPD cohort previously assessed these indices comparatively, and a recent two-center externally validated model incorporated both NLR and SII.9,13 The present study differs by focusing on a disease-specific 90-day outcome and quantifying incremental value relative to the limited clinical-comparator framework, but its contribution is preliminary rather than definitive.

The choice of disease-specific readmission improves respiratory specificity but differs from all-cause outcomes and from PEARL’s composite of readmission or death.7,24,25 Because five deaths were excluded before complete outcome ascertainment, the binary analysis is conditional on surviving with observed 90-day status and may be subject to survivor selection. Future studies should include time-to-event data and treat death as a competing event or part of a clinically meaningful composite endpoint.

The comparator model is deliberately described as limited. It omitted prior admissions, exacerbation history, dyspnea, frailty, comorbidity burden, discharge treatment, adherence and follow-up intensity, all of which are established or plausible readmission predictors.6–8 The observed AUC increase may therefore be larger than would occur against a more comprehensive model or PEARL. Prospective studies should compare SII with contemporary scores and evaluate whether it adds value after these domains are measured.

Decision curve results should not be equated with proven clinical utility. At a 10% threshold, the corrected net-benefit difference was negligible, and no intervention was tested. We defined the 10–50% range as thresholds at which clinicians might consider enhanced discharge review, such as earlier follow-up, inhaler assessment, smoking-cessation support or pulmonary rehabilitation referral.21,26 Whether SII-informed selection improves outcomes without unnecessary intervention requires a prospective impact study.

Several limitations merit emphasis. First, the retrospective single-center cohort was elderly and predominantly male, restricting transportability. The sample was not large enough for reliable subgroup-specific performance estimates, and no formal fairness analysis was undertaken. Second, complete-case requirements excluded 28 patients with incomplete laboratory data, 35 without usable follow-up and five deaths; individual-level comparison with included participants was unavailable, so selection bias cannot be quantified. Third, outside-hospital readmissions were captured by telephone and verified documents where possible, but some patient- or family-reported events lacked complete records and may have been misclassified. Fourth, pulmonary function variables were extracted from historical reports. The original spirometry curves and individual quality grades were unavailable, and the specific reference equations used to generate the reported FEV1% predicted values could not be identified consistently. We therefore could not independently verify test acceptability, repeatability or cross-patient standardization; measurement heterogeneity in FEV1% predicted may have affected its coefficient and the model’s performance and transportability, while classification bias related to unverified spirometry quality cannot be excluded. Fifth, smoking was limited to self-reported ever-smoking status without information on frequency, packs per day, duration or pack-years; alcohol and treatment exposures were subject to retrospective under-documentation. Sixth, the constrained six-slope model-building strategy was not supported by a prospective sample-size calculation; nominal EPV does not account for the candidate screening, nonlinear assessment or model-selection uncertainty. Although the entire defined selection and fitting procedure was repeated within the bootstrap, the clinically forced variables and two-variable selection cap were defined during the revised analysis and require external validation. Seventh, joint checking of the unique inpatient number and national identification number confirmed that all 207 records were unique, no patient was retrieved twice and only the first eligible hospitalization was used; however, the study did not analyze recurrent readmissions beyond the first outcome. Finally, internal bootstrap validation does not establish external validity, and the exploratory Youden cutoffs should not guide clinical care.19,22,23

External validation should prioritize calibration, recalibration, threshold stability and comparison with PEARL and models containing prior exacerbations, frailty and symptom burden. Repeated SII measurements may determine whether inflammatory trajectories add information beyond a single admission value. Ultimately, a prospective impact study is required before SII can influence discharge pathways.

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective development cohort, higher admission SII was associated with AECOPD-related unplanned readmission within 90 days and improved apparent discrimination when added to a limited clinical model. Bootstrap correction showed residual optimism, and SII was not proven superior to NLR or PLR. These hypothesis-generating findings require independent external validation, recalibration and prospective impact assessment before SII can be incorporated into routine discharge decision-making.
