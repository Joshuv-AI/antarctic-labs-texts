Autophagy-centered gene networks reveal prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in esophageal cancer
Authors: Shirin Salehi, Negar Mottaghi-Dastjerdi, Behzad Shahbazi, Nahid Ahmadi, Abozar Ghorbani, Mohammad Soltany-Rezaee-Rad, Fateme Yazdani, Farzane Khoshdel, Mohammad-Javad Niazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEsophageal cancer (EC) remains an extremely lethal cancer with few prognostic biomarkers and specific therapies. Although autophagy is increasingly recognized as a key force behind tumor adaptation and resistance to therapy, its systematic role at the EC progression level for prognostics, as well as for drug target prediction, remains unclear. Here, we attempted to build a systems-wide map linking autophagy- and signaling-related genes and transcriptional dysregulation, patient survival, and druggability in EC. Hub genes were found using an integrative bioinformatics pipeline based on protein–protein interaction networks and further explored using enrichment, expression, survival, and molecular docking analyses. Functional enrichment highlighted autophagy, mitophagy, ferroptosis, and immune signaling as central processes, converging with stress- and metabolism-associated pathways. Expression profile of TCGA-ESCA data demonstrated substantial overexpression of autophagy initiators and elongation factors (ATG3, ATG5, ATG7, ATG12, ATG13), upstream regulators (AMBRA1, UVRAG), and stress/metabolic mediators (TP53, MYD88, GAPDH). Kaplan–Meier analysis indicated three genes, including ATG4A, GABARAPL2, and GAPDH, that exhibited significant expression levels correlating with less survival and emphasizing their prognostic capacities. Screening for drugs also revealed AKT1, TP53, and PIK3R4 as druggable hubs, and many drugs (e.g., Everolimus, Dabrafenib, Trabectedin) showing high-affinity interactions. These findings collectively demonstrate that the progression of EC is supported by a coordinated program that integrates autophagy and metabolic reprogramming with stress and immunological signaling. The study discovers new prognostic markers (ATG4A, GABARAPL2, GAPDH) and druggable targets, which could lead to better risk stratification and smarter drug repurposing. Introduction

Cancer of the esophagus (EC) remains a highly lethal malignancy with persistently poor survival rates despite advances in multimodal therapies. A major challenge in improving patient outcomes is the incomplete understanding of the molecular regulatory dynamics governing tumor initiation, progression, and therapy resistance. Although large-scale genomic studies have identified recurrent mutations and dysregulated signaling pathways, most notably PI3K/AKT/mTOR and Wnt/β-catenin, the systems-level regulatory architecture underlying EC pathogenesis remains insufficiently characterized [1–4].

Esophageal cancer comprises two major histological subtypes, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), which differ substantially in etiological factors, genomic landscapes, and biological behavior. These distinctions are clinically relevant and suggest that stress-adaptation programs, including autophagy, may be differentially deployed across subtypes. Current mechanistic evidence regarding autophagy in EC has largely focused on ESCC, where autophagy has been implicated in tumor cell survival under hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and chemoradiotherapy-induced stress, often through crosstalk with PI3K/AKT/mTOR and other stress-response pathways [5].

The role of autophagy in EAC remains less extensively investigated. Transcriptomic and TCGA-based studies show that patterns and signatures of autophagy-related gene expression may be able to separate clinically important EAC subgroups. However, there is still a lack of understanding of how these patterns and signatures interact at the mechanistic and network levels. This difference between studies centered on ESCC and EAC indicates a significant deficiency: the lack of a systems-level methodology that situates autophagy-related genes within expansive interaction networks, while also considering histological variability [6].

Autophagy is becoming more and more understood as a process that depends on the situation in cancer. In early stages, it acts as a quality control system that stops tumors from growing. Later on, it helps with metabolic flexibility, stress tolerance, and resistance to treatment in tumors that are already there. Autophagy is not a single process. Different kinds of autophagy, like macroautophagy and selective processes like mitophagy, use various molecular components that may have different implications on how tumors behave. Network-based techniques are a strong way to figure out these modular relationships and find important regulatory nodes that could be useful in the clinic [7].

Because EC has aggressive biology and is resistant to treatment, we require a more comprehensive molecular characterization. Large-scale datasets like TCGA have created detailed genomic profiles of EC. However, these descriptive datasets have not yet been successfully turned into therapeutic strategies that can be used. Systems biology approaches that integrate differentially expressed genes into protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks and pathway analyses enable the identification of hub genes, regulatory modules, and candidate biomarkers or drug targets [8, 9]. Building on our prior experience in integrative gene expression and network-based biomarker discovery in various malignancies [10–22], we applied a unified systems-biology approach to delineate autophagy-centered molecular hubs with prognostic and therapeutic relevance in esophageal cancer.

Despite the advances, most EC studies remain focused on single genes or isolated pathways, yielding a fragmented view of disease biology. Regulatory network organization and pathway crosstalk, particularly involving autophagy and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, are still insufficiently explored at the whole-genome interaction level. Also, many computational analyses rely exclusively on secondary datasets without anchoring predictions in experimentally supported disease biology, limiting translational interpretability [6, 23, 24].

Building on experimentally supported EC-associated genes [20], we hypothesized that key autophagy-connected regulators occupy central positions within molecular interaction networks that link transcriptional dysregulation to clinical outcomes and therapeutic opportunities. To test this hypothesis, we reconstructed PPI networks, identified hub genes through topological analysis, performed functional enrichment and expression profiling, evaluated prognostic relevance using survival analysis, and assessed druggability through molecular docking. Through integration of experimental context with computational modeling, this study aims to provide a systems-level map of autophagy-centered regulatory networks with translational relevance in esophageal cancer.

Methods

Network analysis

To investigate protein–protein interactions (PPIs), the hub gene list was queried in STRING v12.0 using a minimum required interaction score of 0.4 (medium confidence) [25]. This threshold is commonly used in cancer network studies to balance sensitivity and specificity when reconstructing interactomes from heterogeneous evidence sources. The resulting PPI network was imported into Cytoscape v3.9.1, and cytoHubba v0.1 was used to prioritize key nodes using 12 topological algorithms (MCC, Betweenness, Degree, Closeness, MNC, BottleNeck, EcCentricity, Clustering Coefficient, EPC, DMNC, Stress, and Radiality). For each algorithm, the top 10 ranked genes were retrieved; these lists were then merged and de-duplicated, yielding 29 unique hub genes that were carried forward for downstream functional enrichment, expression, survival, and druggability analyses. A hub-only subnetwork was additionally visualized to depict interactions among the selected hub genes. Thus, the final hub set consisted of all genes that ranked within the top 10 in at least one of the 12 cytoHubba algorithms, without imposing an additional frequency cutoff, in order to retain both globally central and algorithm-specific high-impact nodes while minimizing selection bias.

Functional analysis

Functional enrichment of the hubs was carried out by the STRING v12.0 web-based application, focusing on KEGG pathways and Gene Ontology (GO) categories (molecular function (MF), cellular components (CC), and biological process (BP)) [25].

Cluster analysis

This study used CytoCluster v2.1.0 in clustering the network nodes with The IPCA algorithm (threshold: 0.8) [26]. Genes in each cluster were subsequently examined through STRING v12.0 to identify the KEGG pathways associated with these genes.

Expression analysis

The expression data of the twenty-nine hubs in tumor and normal esophageal samples was retrieved from the UALCAN portal [27]. TCGA-ESCA comprises both esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and esophageal adenocarcinoma. In the present study, analyses were conducted on the combined TCGA-ESCA cohort to maximize statistical power for discovery, and subtype-stratified validation is proposed as a necessary follow-up step.

Survival analysis

Overall survival was evaluated using the UALCAN Kaplan–Meier module based on TCGA-ESCA, which compares survival distributions between expression-defined patient groups and reports significance using the log-rank test [27]. For each hub gene, patients were stratified according to the UALCAN expression grouping (high vs. low/medium), and log-rank p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Because this analysis was conducted through the UALCAN Kaplan–Meier interface, covariate-adjusted multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling was not performed in the present study; therefore, survival results are presented as unadjusted (univariable) associations between transcript level and overall survival. All analyses were performed using the TCGA-ESCA cohort as a combined dataset, and results are therefore interpreted as subtype-agnostic associations reflecting shared molecular dependencies across esophageal cancer.

Drug screening analysis

Screening of drugs was conducted to find the hub protein inhibitors. Protein structures were downloaded from PDB [28], UniProt [29], and Robetta [30], giving priority to human proteins of high resolution and no bound ligands. Putative drugs were downloaded from the DGIdb 5.0 [31], and drugs corresponding to the target genes were chosen.

Structure preparation and molecular docking

For docking analysis, the crystal structures of AKT1 (3O96), β-catenin Armadillo (2Z6H), GAPDH (6M61), MyD88 TIR domain (2Z5V), wild-type P53 (8F2H), and PI3KC3 (8SOR) were extracted from RCSB. FOXO3 and MYC structures were predicted using Robetta (https://robetta.bakerlab.org) and checked through MolProbity, ProSA, ERRAT, and Ramachandran plot (SAVES v6.0) analysis [32, 33]. Receptor structures had the ligands/water removed and were prepared for docking in AutoDock v4.2.6 by adding hydrogens and charges. Structures of drugs were downloaded from PubChem in SDF format, converted, and prepared using OpenBabel and AutoDock tools [34].

Molecular docking

The molecular docking analysis of AKT1, Beta-Catenin Armadillo, GAPDH, MyD88, wild-type P53, human PI3KC3, FOXO3, and Myc molecules was carried out with drugs by Smina software. To obtain the best drugs with the highest binding affinities, the docking scores obtained from Smina were analyzed. The results were displayed with Chimera v1.17.3 software and Discovery Studio v4.5. All data used in this study were retrieved from publicly accessible repositories (TCGA, STRING, UALCAN, DGIdb, PDB, UniProt, and Robetta). For several targets, experimentally validated ligand-bound crystal structures and defined inhibitor binding pockets were unavailable; therefore, docking was performed in a blind and comparative manner without predefined active sites. Consequently, classical positive-control ligands and re-docking validation could not be applied. Docking scores were used for relative ranking of therapeutic compounds rather than as absolute predictors of binding efficacy.

Results

Network analysis

The interaction network contained 55 nodes and 569 edges (Figure S1 in Supplementary), which exhibited strong connectivity (average neighbors: 20.7; clustering coefficient: 0.79) and short path length, reflective of biologically relevant interactions (Table 2). These 29 hub genes represent the union of all nodes ranked within the top 10 by at least one of the 12 cytoHubba algorithms, as defined a priori in the Methods section. Analysis by CytoHubba using 12 algorithms repeatedly picked up AKT1, TP53, GAPDH, BECN1, MYC, and some autophagic genes (ATG5, ATG7, ATG12, BCL2L1) as core hubs. The 29-node and 289-edge subnetwork (Figure S2 in Supplementary) showed higher density (0.712) and clustering, which confirmed strong interrelationships of the hubs. Functional analysis

Functional enrichment analysis consistently indicated autophagy-centered processes as the key features of the hub gene network in EC (Fig. 1). Along with stress-related processes, including cellular component disassembly and response to nitrogen starvation, BP keywords were highly enriched for mitochondrion autophagy, autophagosome assembly, macroautophagy, and mitophagy.

Functional analysis of hub genes. Bubble plots of substantially enriched terms by Gene Ontology and pathways are presented. The bubble diameters refer to the gene count together with the color intensity denotes the statistical significance of the enrichment (FDR)

CC analysis localized key proteins to autophagy-related parts of the cell, including the autophagosome, phagophore assembly site, autolysosome, and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase complex (class III).

MF enrichment revealed strong representation of ubiquitin ligase binding, protein kinase binding, and enzyme binding, together with autophagy-specific activities such as Atg12 transferase and Atg8 peptidase.

The KEGG pathways validated the significance of autophagy and mitophagy, while also included ferroptosis, FoxO signaling, and infection-related terms such as Shigellosis and Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus infection.

Cluster analysis

The clustering outcomes and enriched pathway categories of the four identified clusters are summarized in Figure S3 in the Supplementary material and Fig. 2, and detailed in Table 3, providing an overview of the four subnetworks and their conserved and cluster-specific biological processes. In line with these summaries, clustering the PPI network revealed four clusters and 14 conserved pathways shared by the entire set of clusters. The latter can be summarized into four categories: cell death and quality control, neurodegeneration-related, infection-related, and regulatory/stress response pathways. Beyond the infection-related pathways common to all clusters, Cluster 3 uniquely revealed additional infection-associated pathways. Clusters 3 and 4 were also found to be enriched in processes directly linked to other cancers. The similarity in recurrence of cancer-specific processes reflects significant molecular overlap between EC and other carcinomas. Several additional pathways enriched in cluster analysis represent canonical signaling and regulatory circuits frequently implicated in oncogenesis (PI3K–Akt, JAK–STAT, ErbB, and AMPK), which together govern cell proliferation, survival, metabolism, and stress adaptation. Additional enriched functions can be grouped into wider categories, such as stress and survival strategies, metabolic processes, therapeutic resistance, signalization and immunological cross-communication, and synaptic activity. They signify the complication of EC in restructuring metabolic processes, pressure adjustment, immune response control, and treatment avoidance in the network system.

Expression analysis

Transcriptional analysis of the twenty-nine hubs in TCGA-ESCA showed severe dysregulation, with nineteen hubs exhibiting elevated expression between tumor and normal tissues (p < 0.05). The presence of genes that regulate autophagy, survival signaling molecules, and stress mediators indicates a disruption in cellular homeostasis that predisposes to cancer development. The expression details of all hubs are illustrated in Fig. 3 and in Table S2 and Table S3 in the Supplementary material.

Fold-change in expression of significantly dysregulated hub genes in EC. Bar plot showing fold-change (Tumor/Normal, TCGA-ESCA, UALCAN analysis) for 19 hub genes with p < 0.05. All 19 genes displayed significant upregulation in primary tumors compared to normal esophageal tissues

The expression of several ATG family genes (ATG3, ATG4B, ATG5, ATG7, ATG10, ATG12, and ATG13) was significantly higher in tumor tissues (Fold Change (FC): 1.5–2). The increased levels of expression of UVRAG (FC:1.77) and AMBRA1 (FC:1.51) indicate the important role played by the upstream regulators of the Beclin1-VPS34 complex to initiate autophagy in EC.

Their transcription levels increased in the mediators involved in signaling, regulators of cell survival, and normal autophagy genes. BCL2L1 (FC:1.94) and MYD88 (FC:2.54) are involved in resistance to apoptosis and immune signaling. GABARAPL2 overexpression (FC:1.60), the indispensable constituent of autophosome formation, results in enhanced autophagy flux. There appears to be a correlation among the autophagy, Wnt signal, and the epigenetics because there is an overexpression of two important transcriptional regulators, viz., CTNNB1 (FC:2.12) and EP300 (FC:1.50).

Some FCs were associated with numerous genes being highly overexpressed. Overexpression of the frequently mutated tumor suppressor TP53 (FC > 3) hints towards possible compensatory transcriptional activation or context-dependent overexpression. The amplified antiviral-like stress signal in cancer is supported by the overexpression of the innate immunity sensor DDX58 (FC:2.71) and the autophagy adapter protein SQSTM1 (FC > 2), linking ubiquitinated proteins to autophagosomes. GAPDH, traditionally supposed to be a housekeeping gene, presented a significant rise in the tumor samples (FC:3.24).

PIK3C3, MYC, MAP1LC3A, GABARAPL1, FOXO3, BECN1, ATG4A, AKT1, and ATG14 showed no significant changes (p > 0.05), indicating selective rather than uniform activation of autophagy regulators in EC. Notably, although ATG4A did not show a statistically significant difference between tumor and normal tissues, its expression variability within the tumor cohort enabled subsequent prognostic stratification, as evaluated by survival analysis.

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of the 29 hub genes using the UALCAN module (TCGA-ESCA) showed that higher expression of ATG4A, GABARAPL2, and GAPDH was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (log-rank p < 0.05), whereas the remaining hubs did not reach statistical significance under the same settings (Fig. 4; Table S4 of the Supplementary material). Specifically, high expression of ATG4A (p = 0.013), GABARAPL2 (p = 0.0096), and GAPDH (p = 0.039) was linked to reduced survival probability. These findings represent univariable survival associations derived from UALCAN’s Kaplan–Meier analysis and should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Confirmation using covariate-adjusted Cox regression, independent cohorts, and histology-stratified analyses (ESCC vs. EAC) is warranted. Although ATG4A did not exhibit significant differential expression between tumor and normal tissues, Kaplan–Meier analysis within the TCGA-ESCA tumor cohort revealed that higher intratumoral ATG4A expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (p = 0.013).

Drug screening analysis

After searching the DGidb database, it was observed that among the hub genes, only AKT1, ATG5, CTNNB1, PIK3R4, FOXO3, GAPDH, MYC, MYD88, and TP53 have drugs reported and available. A search was conducted for these genes in the PDB and Uniprot databases, as well as the online tool Robetta, to identify proteins with suitable structures. For all proteins, except ATG5, a suitable crystal structure for docking was found (Table S5 in the Supplementary material).

Receptor preparation

The 3D structures of FOXO3 and Myc were modeled using Robetta and validated by ProSA-web, Ramachandran plot, ERRAT, and MolProbity. The results of the evaluation are shown in Table S6 in the Supplementary material. The Myc model showed high quality with an ERRAT score of 92.3, MolProbity 0.95 (100% ideal resolution), clashscore 1.58 (99th percentile), 99.7% residues in allowed regions, and a ProSA Z-score of − 5.91. The FOXO3 model was comparable in performance, with an ERRAT of 94.2, MolProbity 1.00 (100% ideal), clashescore 1.53 (99th percentile), 99.6% residues in allowed regions, and a Z-score of − 7.95. These results again corroborate that both the FOXO3 and Myc models are correct in structure and valid for molecular docking (see Table S7 of the Supplementary material for details).

Discussion

This study mapped key autophagy and signaling genes linked to EC, revealing their transcriptional deregulation, prognostic relevance, and druggability promise. The significance of autophagy and stress responses in EC is confirmed by the enriched pathways and prioritized candidates discovered through the combination of network clustering, functional enrichment, expression profiling, survival analysis, and molecular docking.

The identified hub genes spanned key processes, including autophagy, apoptosis, immune signaling (MYD88, DDX58), and metabolic regulation (GAPDH, ENO1), with the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway emerging as a recurrent axis [3]. Generally, these observations imply strongly related regulatory modules trigger the development of EC, and the hub genes are potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. The novelty of this study does not reside in the discovery of previously unknown genes, but in the integrative, network-based sorting of autophagy-related regulators in esophageal cancer. By combining interaction topology, functional enrichment, transcriptional dysregulation, survival association, and druggability assessment within a unified approach, we identify specific autophagy checkpoints with prognostic and translational relevance, rather than reiterating autophagy as a generalized cancer hallmark. This systems-level perspective enables narrowing down useful biomarkers and therapeutic targets beyond prior single-gene or pathway-focused reports and generates testable hypotheses for subtype-aware validation.

Our data suggest that autophagic processes, including autophagosome construction, macroautophagy, and mitophagy, are central to EC biology. These are in agreement with prior reports of involvement of autophagy in tumor cell survival under treatment challenge and metabolic stress [35, 36], but our studies generalize these findings by unveiling relationship of autophagy with ferroptosis, FoxO signaling, and infection-associated pathways. Identification of neurodegeneration-associated signatures including ALS further highlights the generalizability of organelle quality control and stress regulation and supports the interpretation that EC progression exploits shared molecular features found in cancer and infection, and degenerative diseases.

When we examined the TCGA-ESCA data, we observed that the core autophagic machineries were significantly upregulated at the transcriptional level. The coordinated overexpression of ATG3, ATG4B, ATG5, ATG7, ATG10, ATG12, and ATG13, along with the upstream regulators UVRAG and AMBRA1, shows that the autophagic initiation and elongation modules are turned on. This is in line with previous research that linked overexpression of autophagy genes to aggressive tumor phenotypes [37, 38]. However, our findings highlight that EC tumors prefer to activate elongation and maturation arms of the autophagy process. Meanwhile, signaling regulators like BCL2L1, MYD88, CTNNB1, and EP300 are overexpressed, indicative of a convergence of apoptotic resistance, immune signaling, and transcriptional regulation. Notably, the significant increase of TP53, SQSTM1, DDX58, and GAPDH transcript levels is another piece of evidence showing that EC progression is associated with stress adaptation, evasion of the immune system, and metabolic reprogramming. Functionally, the enrichment of autophagy/mitophagy together with PI3K–AKT–mTOR, FoxO signaling, and ferroptosis-related pathways is consistent with a model in which autophagy supports metabolic plasticity under fluctuating nutrient and oxygen availability. Autophagy may supply substrates for mitochondrial and glycolytic metabolism, maintain redox balance, and preserve organelle fitness, potentially sustaining tumor growth during metabolic stress. In this context, the prominence of GAPDH within the hub set and its adverse survival association supports the view that metabolic rewiring and autophagy are coupled adaptive programs rather than independent processes. Also, activation of PI3K–AKT–mTOR signaling provides a plausible mechanistic bridge between survival signaling and autophagy regulation, aligning with the known role of this axis in coordinating growth cues with autophagic flux.

Accumulating evidence indicates that autophagy plays a context-dependent and stage-specific role in esophageal cancer, acting as both a tumor-suppressive and tumor-promoting mechanism depending on histological subtype, genetic background, and microenvironmental stress. Most mechanistic investigations to date have focused on ESCC, where autophagy has been shown to promote tumor cell survival under hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and chemoradiotherapy-induced stress through activation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR and p53-associated signaling pathways. However, autophagy has also been reported to exert tumor-suppressive effects in certain contexts, supporting a dual role that depends on stage and cellular stress conditions. The role of autophagy in EAC remains less well characterized, with emerging evidence suggesting divergent regulatory dependencies and metabolic adaptations between these subtypes [3, 39–41].

Our results suggest selective activation of autophagy checkpoints, rather than uniform upregulation of all autophagy regulators. Specifically, the network and enrichment patterns point to preferential engagement of macroautophagy- and mitophagy-related modules involved in autophagosome elongation and maturation. The prominence of ATG8-family processing components (e.g., ATG4A, GABARAPL2) together with mitochondrial quality-control pathways supports a model in which esophageal tumors may exploit discrete autophagic steps to sustain stress tolerance and cellular plasticity. Although our analysis was not stratified by histological subtype, this pathway-selective signature may reflect a convergent survival strategy across esophageal cancers, while still permitting subtype-specific modulation at the level of upstream regulators and signaling outputs. Overall, these findings align with the emerging view that autophagy functions as a modular and adaptable program, underscoring the need for subtype-aware and autophagy-subtype–informed therapeutic targeting.

Survival analysis identified three genes (ATG4A, GABARAPL2, and GAPDH) whose elevated expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival in esophageal cancer. Rather than reflecting a global activation of the autophagy machinery, these findings suggest that selective components of autophagosome processing and metabolic regulation may have greater prognostic relevance. While prior studies have reported an association between autophagy-related activity and survival outcomes in esophageal cancer [42, 43], most have emphasized autophagy as a broad process. The present analysis highlights specific steps within the autophagy pathway, including ATG4A-mediated processing of LC3 family proteins and GABARAPL2-associated autophagosome maturation, as more closely linked to adverse clinical outcomes. ATG4A showed prognostic association in the survival analysis despite lacking significant tumor–normal differential expression, suggesting that its expression level within tumors may be more informative for risk stratification than diagnostic separation. The unfavorable survival association observed for GAPDH, traditionally regarded as a housekeeping enzyme, further underscores the importance of metabolic rewiring and stress adaptation in tumor progression. Collectively, these observations support a model in which autophagy-related vesicle processing and metabolic regulation operate as interconnected contributors to esophageal cancer aggressiveness, warranting further validation in covariate-adjusted and subtype-stratified clinical cohorts. Because ESCC and EAC were analyzed together in this discovery-level approach, the identified autophagy-related hubs represent convergent regulatory features rather than subtype-specific mechanisms.

Mechanistically, ATG4A is a cysteine protease that primes ATG8/LC3-family proteins for lipidation and later delipidation, thereby regulating autophagosome biogenesis and flux; dysregulation of ATG4-family activity has been linked to enhanced autophagy capacity and malignant phenotypes in several cancers, supporting the possibility that higher ATG4A expression marks tumors with stronger stress-adaptation potential [44]. Likewise, GABARAPL2 is an ATG8-family member involved in autophagosome elongation/maturation and selective cargo recruitment; altered ATG8/GABARAP module activity has been associated with aggressive behavior and metastatic traits across solid tumors, consistent with our observation that higher GABARAPL2 levels track with worse outcome [45]. Finally, although GAPDH is widely used as a “housekeeping” gene, increasing evidence indicates it has oncogenic, pro-survival roles beyond glycolysis, including supporting metabolic plasticity and stress tolerance; accordingly, elevated GAPDH expression has been associated with adverse prognosis across cancers and may reflect heightened metabolic rewiring in aggressive disease [46].

Beyond cell-intrinsic survival mechanisms, autophagy is increasingly recognized as a key modulator of tumor–immune interactions. Several hub genes identified in this study, including MYD88, DDX58, and SQSTM1, link autophagic regulation to innate immune signaling, inflammatory stress responses, and antigen processing pathways. Activation of these axes may help immune evasion by reshaping cytokine signaling, dampening antiviral responses, and supporting tumor cell survival under immune-mediated pressure. Notably, autophagy-dependent regulation of pattern-recognition receptor signaling and NF-κB activity has been shown to influence immune infiltration, therapy resistance, and tumor progression in multiple solid malignancies, including esophageal cancer [47, 48].

The convergence of autophagy, metabolic reprogramming, and immune signaling observed in the present network analysis suggests that autophagy operates not as an isolated process, but as a central adaptive hub integrating stress tolerance, inflammation, and immune escape. This integrated approach provides a mechanistic basis for the observed prognostic relevance of ATG4A, GABARAPL2, and GAPDH, and supports the rationale for therapeutic strategies that jointly target autophagy, metabolism, and immune modulation in esophageal cancer [49].

The drug screening and molecular docking presented here further showed the translational potential. Among the docked candidates, AKT1, TP53, and PIK3C3 were prioritized for focused discussion based on complementary biological and network-based criteria. AKT1 consistently ranked as a topological hub across multiple cytoHubba algorithms and demonstrated the strongest predicted binding affinities with several FDA-approved agents, including Everolimus, supporting its central role within the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis. TP53 and PIK3C3 were emphasized due to their pathway centrality and regulatory importance in stress response and autophagy initiation, respectively, rather than solely docking score magnitude. Everolimus was highlighted because it clinically targets the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway downstream (via mTOR), and its strong predicted affinity within our hub-centered docking approach supports the pathway-level plausibility of prioritizing this axis. Collectively, this prioritization reflects an integrated consideration of network centrality, pathway relevance, and pharmacological context, rather than reliance on binding energy rankings alone. We predicted a number of hub genes, such as AKT1, TP53, CTNNB1, PIK3R4, FOXO3, GAPDH, MYC, and MYD88, to exhibit high binding affinities to various therapeutic compounds. AKT1 was the most favorable target with nanomolar-range affinities to Everolimus, Nintedanib, and Dabrafenib. Likewise, TP53 and PI3KC3 exhibited vigorous interactions with clinically actionable chemotherapeutics, including Trabectedin and Infigratinib, respectively. These findings suggest rationally directed drug repositioning of signaling and autophagic regulators could act to complement current EC treatment approaches. Whereas prior studies have mostly focused on classical chemotherapy agents [50, 51], our docking output expands the universe of autophagic targets that could be potentially druggable. Autophagy is also tightly linked to therapy resistance in esophageal cancer. Under cytotoxic stress, autophagy can function as a damage-mitigation program by removing injured mitochondria, limiting oxidative stress, and buffering proteotoxic stress, which collectively promote survival during chemotherapy and radiotherapy. The recurrent identification of PI3K–AKT–mTOR signaling, apoptosis regulators (e.g., BCL2L1), and stress-associated hubs in our network supports the plausibility of an autophagy-enabled tolerance phenotype. From a translational perspective, this provides a rationale for combining pathway-directed agents with autophagy-modulatory strategies to sensitize tumors to standard chemoradiotherapy, while acknowledging that the directionality of autophagy (pro-survival versus tumor-suppressive) may depend on context and must be experimentally validated.

The multifaceted research design integrating transcriptomic profiling, network clustering, survival analysis, and molecular docking to systematically uncover vulnerabilities in esophageal cancer represents a major strength of this study. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the exclusively in silico nature of the analyses precludes direct functional validation, and experimental assays will be required to confirm the biological roles of the identified genes and predicted drug–target interactions. In addition, the lack of validation using independent clinical cohorts limits the external generalizability of the prognostic associations reported here. Second, the clinical heterogeneity of esophageal cancer, especially the distinctions between squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, was not thoroughly analyzed, which may affect gene expression patterns and survival correlations. Also, key components of the tumor microenvironment, including stromal elements and immune cell infiltration, were not incorporated into the present analyses and may have precluded the identification of additional context-dependent regulatory interactions. Accordingly, subtype-stratified analyses (ESCC versus EAC) and microenvironment-aware modeling will be required to determine whether the identified autophagy checkpoints and prognostic associations are shared or subtype-specific. Third, survival analyses were based on univariate models, and future studies incorporating multivariable adjustment and independent cohort validation are necessary to establish clinical utility. Molecular docking was performed as a comparison, hypothesis-generating method due to the lack of empirically confirmed binding sites for several targets, which prevented the incorporation of positive controls, re-docking validation, and molecular dynamics simulations. So, the predicted interactions should be seen as indications of priority rather than proof that the treatment works. Experimental validation using in vitro and in vivo models is necessary to assess pharmacological activity and binding stability prior to deriving translational findings.

Future studies should focus on confirming the predictive value of ATG4A, GABARAPL2, and GAPDH through experimental and clinical tests that use histological stratification and treatment-response data. Additionally, functional analysis of AKT1, TP53, and PI3KC3 as therapeutic targets could help the development of strategies for treatment focused on autophagy and metabolism. Integration of these findings with immunotherapeutic and metabolic interventions may ultimately help the design of more effective, multifaceted treatment approaches for esophageal cancer.

Conclusions

This integrative analysis unveiled autophagy- and signaling-related hub genes to play core roles in EC, unveiling their transcriptional dysregulation, prognostic value, and therapeutic potential. Cancer cell transcriptomic profiling disclosed a coordinated regulation of autophagy elongation and processing modules, and survival analysis suggested ATG4A, GABARAPL2, and GAPDH to act independent predictors of adverse outcome. Drug screening and docking pointed to AKT1, TP53, and PI3KC3 to act potential druggable targets and suggested potential for rational repositioning of off-patent agents. Overall, these discoveries establish autophagy and metabolic reprogramming to play core axes in EC progression and offer translational and mechanism-driven insight. Although reliance upon in silico methods and univariable models of survival are limitations, the study is an effective blueprint for investigation of therapies and discovery of biomarkers. Future analyses will need to validate these targets experimentally, add histological stratification, and optimize multigene prognostic modeling to narrow and improve therapies and clinical utility in EC.
