Association of metabolic score for visceral fat and risk of arterial stiffness and long-term all-cause mortality: a 10-year prospective study
Authors: Yue Chen, Qinzhi Cao, Jin Sun, Nan Zhang, Shuaishuai Zhang, Yan Wang, Boyang Zeng, Jiaojiao Qiu, Guanchao Sun, Bangguo Qin, Man Li, Bokai Cheng, Zimei Chi, Qiligeer Bao, Shuang Cai, Shuxia Wang, Ping Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between the Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF) and all-cause mortality among community-dwelling older adults, and to evaluate the potential mediating effect of brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) in this relationship.MethodsA prospective cohort of 1,794 community-dwelling elderly Chinese (mean age 71.2) was followed for a median of 9.9 years. METS-VF and baPWV were measured at baseline. We performed Cox regression to determine hazard ratios across METS-VF quartiles, restricted cubic splines (RCS) to examine potential nonlinearity, and mediation analysis to assess the effect mediated through baPWVA.ResultsThe mean age of the 1,794 participants was 71.2 years. Over a median follow-up of 9.9 years, 328 deaths were recorded. After multivariable adjustment, individuals in the highest METS-VF quartile had a significantly higher risk of mortality compared to those in the lowest quartile (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.43, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.46–4.06, p < 0.001). The restricted cubic spline analysis revealed that a nonlinear dose-response relationship was observed between METS-VF and mortality (P for nonlinearity < 0.001). Mediation analysis revealed that baPWV played a significant role in explaining the association between METS-VF and mortality.ConclusionOur study confirms the independent association between METS-VF and all-cause mortality in the community-dwelling elderly. The findings are consistent with a partial mediation effect of arterial stiffness in this relationship, providing insights into a potential underlying mechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02066-z.

Background

With the global acceleration of population aging, cardiovascular and metabolic diseases have become major threats to the health of the elderly [1]. Among various contributing factors, visceral fat accumulation is increasingly recognized as a key driver of insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and atherosclerosis progression [2–4]. Conventional measures of adiposity, such as body mass index (BMI), have well-known limitations in reflecting actual fat distribution, leading to the so-called “obesity paradox” [5, 6], wherein individuals with higher BMI may paradoxically exhibit lower mortality risk². This discrepancy may obscure the true pathogenic role of visceral adiposity [7].

Recently, the Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF) was proposed as a composite index integrating age, sex, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and metabolic parameters (e.g., METS-IR) to estimate visceral fat accumulation more accurately [8]. Initial validations have shown that METS-VF performs better than traditional anthropometric measures such as BMI in predicting type 2 diabetes [9], hypertension [10], cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality [11]. Large-scale cohort studies, including those from the NHANES database in the United States [12]and Chinese longitudinal cohorts [13], have demonstrated a dose–response association between METS-VF and cardiometabolic risk and mortality, highlighting its potential utility as a metabolic risk stratification tool.

However, existing studies primarily focus on middle-aged or general adult populations. There remains a significant evidence gap regarding the association between METS-VF and long-term outcomes in older adults, who exhibit distinct physiological changes in fat distribution, insulin sensitivity, and vascular function. Furthermore, while METS-VF has been linked to cardiovascular morbidity, its relationship with hard outcomes—particularly all-cause mortality—and the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain insufficiently explored.

Arterial stiffness, an indicator of vascular aging and dysfunction, is a well-established predictor of cardiovascular events and mortality [14]. The brachial–ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) is widely used as a non-invasive measure of arterial stiffness in both clinical and epidemiological settings [15, 16]. Prior research indicates that visceral fat may promote arterial stiffness through multiple mechanisms, including adipokine imbalance, pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic states, oxidative stress, and endothelial damage [15, 17]. Thus, it is plausible that METS-VF may influence mortality risk via vascular aging pathways, particularly through increased arterial stiffness.

From a clinical standpoint, METS-VF holds promise as a practical and non-invasive tool for estimating visceral fat using routinely available clinical data. Nevertheless, it was originally calibrated against DXA-derived measurements, and its generalizability across diverse populations—particularly among different ethnicities, age groups, and health statuses—requires external validation.

In summary, while METS-VF has emerged as a promising indicator of cardiometabolic risk and mortality, evidence in older adults is still scarce. Mechanistic insights are also lacking. By examining the association between METS-VF and all-cause mortality, and incorporating baPWV as a potential mediator, the present study aims to elucidate the biological pathways linking visceral adiposity to health outcomes in the elderly. These findings may inform future risk stratification and intervention strategies for cardiovascular prevention in aging populations.

Methods

Study design and participants

All participants of this study were recruited from a community-based cross-sectional survey of people aged 60 years or above, which was conducted in the Wanshou Road Community of Haidian District in Beijing, China between September 2009 and June 2010. This survey used a two-stage stratified sampling method and a total of 2162 persons from 60 to 95 years were selected as a representative sample of the elderly residing in Wanshou Road Community and were prospectively followed through December 2020, as described elsewhere [18–21]. 48 participants with incomplete demographic data and 32 with missing blood biochemical parameters were excluded. During the 10-year follow-up period (2010–2020), an additional 268 participants were lost to follow-up. The final analytical cohort comprised 1,794 eligible participants. Study procedures and measurements

Baseline data were collected by a structured questionnaire which was designed by a group of experts, including demographic factors, medical and medication history, lifestyle and physical function. Height and weight were measured using an automated instrument with participants standing barefoot and maintaining an upright posture. Waist circumference was assessed at the narrowest point between the lowest rib and iliac crest at end-expiration using a non-stretch measuring tape, while hip circumference was measured at the maximal hip width. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height² (m), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) as waist (cm)/height (cm). Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured twice in the right arm after a minimum 5-minute rest period using a sphygmomanometer. Fasting blood samples were collected after an 8-hour overnight fast. Plasma total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were quantified using enzymatic assays. Additional biochemical parameters included fasting plasma glucose (FPG), serum creatinine (Scr), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). All anthropometric measurements were performed by trained personnel following standardized protocols.

Hypertension (HTN) and its severity were defined as follows: Grade 0: No hypertension. Grade 1: SBP 140–159 mmHg or DBP 90–99 mmHg. Grade 2: SBP 160–179 mmHg or DBP 100–109 mmHg. Grade 3: SBP ≥ 180 mmHg or DBP ≥ 110 mmHg. A physician-confirmed diagnosis or current use of antihypertensive medication also led to classification in an appropriate grade based on available clinical information. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined by fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, prior clinical diagnosis, or active antidiabetic treatment. Coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke status were ascertained through standardized self-reported medical history assessments validated against clinical records. Smoking history was defined as being a current smoker or having a history of regular smoking. Alcohol drinking history was defined as being a current drinker or having a history of regular alcohol consumption. Educational level was categorized into four groups: illiterate or primary school, junior high school, senior high school, and college or above. Marital status was classified as married, divorced, or widowed.

The metabolic score for IR (METS-IR) was calculated as follow: METS-IR = Ln [2 × FPG (mg/dL) + TG (mg/dL)] × BMI (kg/m2)/Ln [HDL-C (mg/dL)]; the METS-VF was calculated as follow: METS-VF8 = 4.466 + 0.011 × [Ln (METS-IR)]3 + 3.239 × [Ln (WHtR)]3 + 0.319 × Sex (male = 1, female = 0) + 0.594 × [Ln (Age)(year)].

Arterial stiffness was measured using an automatic plethysmographic instrument (VP-1000; Colin, Japan) as previously described [22, 23]. Briefly, electrodes were placed on both wrists, and cuffs were wrapped around both the upper arms and ankles. After simultaneous measurement of blood pressure and waveforms in all four limbs, the time interval between the brachial and ankle waveforms (ΔTba) was determined. The distance between the brachium and the ankle (La − Lb) was estimated automatically according to the subject’s height. After these data were collected, baPWV was calculated using the following equation: baPWV = (La − Lb)/ΔTba (in cm/s). Both baPWV values were measured after allowing the patient to rest in a supine position for at least 5 min. The mean of the right and left baPWV was used as a marker of arterial stiffness. The mean value of left and right baPWV measurements was used for analysis. In this study, elevated arterial stiffness was defined as a baPWV greater than the 75th percentile of the measured distribution, which corresponded to a cutoff value of 2290 cm/s.14,567,500.

All-cause mortality

The study endpoint was vital status (alive/deceased), ascertained through telephone interviews with participants or their immediate family members. For deceased participants, the date of death was obtained either from family members during telephone follow-up or through medical record verification.The end time of follow-up was December 2020. The follow-up time (in years) was calculated for each participant from the enrollment date to the date of death or the last contact date for survivors.

Statistical analyses

All participants were stratified by the cut-off levels of METS-VF as previously described. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were reported as frequency (percentage). Participants were categorized into quartiles based on METS-VF levels, with stratification thresholds defined as the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the cohort distribution. Baseline differences across METS-VF quartiles were evaluated using one-way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the chi-squared test for categorical variables. Linear regression and logistic regression models were used to estimate β coefficients and odds ratios (ORs), respectively, for associations between METS-VF and baPWV (continuous outcome) or high baPWV (binary outcome).

The Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank test was used to compare survival curves across groups. The proportional hazards assumption, assessed via Schoenfeld residuals. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate the association between METS-VF and all-cause mortality, with confounding variables selected based on previous literature and clinical knowledge. These confounders included: (1) demographic and anthropometric factors (age, sex, BMI); (2) comorbidities (hypertension, coronary artery disease, stroke history); (3) lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status and educational level); and (4) biochemical parameters (TC, Scr, HbA1c); (5) vascular function parameter: brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV). The potential nonlinear relationship between METS-VF and all-cause mortality was examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Missing values in continuous variables were imputed with the mean, while those in categorical variables were imputed with the mode. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential modifying factors of the METS-VF–all-cause mortality association. We assessed multicollinearity among all covariates included in the multivariable Cox regression models using the variance inflation factor (VIF). A VIF value of < 5 was considered to indicate no significant multicollinearity.

To investigate the mediating role of baPWV in the relationship between METS-VF and long-term all-cause mortality, mediation models were constructed to estimate total, direct, and indirect effects. Specifically, the direct effect represented the association of METS-VF with all-cause death independent of baPWV, the indirect effect reflected the pathway through baPWV, and the total effect encompassed both direct and indirect associations. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P < 0.05.

To quantify the potential impact of unmeasured confounding on the observed association between METS-VF and all-cause mortality, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the E-value.

All analyses were performed on SPSS software (version 29.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and R(Version 4.4.3). Unless otherwise specified, the significance level α was set at 0.05 for all statistical tests in this study.

Association of METS-VF with all-cause mortality

During a mean follow-up of 9.9 ± 2.4 years, 328 deaths occurred. Mortality rates increased significantly across quartile groups of METS-VF levels: 55 deaths (12.2%) in Group 1 (lowest quartile), 66 (14.7%) in Group 2, 85 (18.9%) in Group 3, and 122 (27.2%) in Group 4 (highest quartile, P < 0.05 for trend). We assessed the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals. The overall global test was not significant (p = 0.160), and the test for our primary variable, METS-VF, was also non-significant (p = 0.647), supporting the assumption’s validity (Supplementary Figure S2). Multicollinearity was assessed, and all variance inflation factor (VIF) values were below 5, indicating no severe multicollinearity among the covariates (Supplementary Figure S3). Kaplan-Meier survival curves showed significant differences in survival distributions among the four groups (log-rank P < 0.01) (Supplementary Figure S1). Pairwise comparisons revealed that survival in Group 1 was significantly better than in Group 3 and Group 4, but not significantly different from Group 2. Results from Breslow and Tarone-Ware tests were consistent with log-rank findings.

As shown in Table 2; Fig. 2, the Cox proportional hazards regression models adjusted for age, gender, BMI, smoking, drinking, Scr, TC, HbA1c, HTN, CHD and stroke, higher METS-VF levels were significantly associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality in Chinese elderly community-dwelling adults. Each standard deviation (SD) increase in METS-VF corresponded to a 2.67-fold elevated mortality risk (95%CI: 1.66–4.31, p < 0.001). When stratified by METS-VF quartiles, participants in the highest quartile (Q4) exhibited a 2.43-fold higher mortality risk compared to the lowest quartile (95% CI: 1.46–4.06, p < 0.001), with a progressive increase across quartiles. The restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant nonlinear relationship between METS-VF and all-cause mortality (Overall P < 0.001; P for nonlinearity < 0.001). The dose-response curve exhibited a J-shaped pattern with an inflection point at a METS-VF of 6.94. Beyond the inflection point of 6.94, a sharp, monotonic increase in mortality risk was observed (supplementary figure S2 and table S8).

Subgroup analysis for association of METS-VF with all-cause mortality

Stratified analyses were conducted by age, sex, and baPWV levels. Subsequent categorization according to METS-VF quartiles revealed differential mortality associations across strata (Fig. 3). Among female participants, the highest METS-VF quartile (Group 4) exhibited significantly elevated mortality risk compared to Group 1 (HR = 3.01, 95% CI: 1.39–6.48), whereas males showed the same association (Group 4 vs. Group 1: HR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.46–4.06). In the high baPWV stratum, progressive METS-VF increases correlated with mortality risk escalation (Group 2: HR = 2.24; Group 3: HR = 3.24; Group 4: HR = 5.37). No significant gradient was observed in the non-high baPWV subgroup. In age-stratified analyses, participants in higher METS-VF groups (Groups 4) within the advanced elderly subgroup exhibited a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality, while no significant association was observed in the younger elderly group. Associations between METS-VF with BaPWV or high BaPWV

Logistic regression revealed a strong association between METS-VF and high baPWV risk (Supplementary Table S4). Per SD increment in METS-VF was linked to a 3.54-fold higher odds of high baPWV (95% CI: 2.20–5.70, P < 0.001) in the fully adjusted model. Similarly, In multivariable linear regression analyses, higher METS-VF levels were significantly associated with elevated baPWV across all models (Supplementary Table S5). After adjustment for demographic, clinical, and biochemical covariates, a 1-SD increase in METS-VF was associated with a 238.2 cm/s rise in baPWV (95% CI:165.7–310.8.7.8, P < 0.001). Stratified by quartiles, participants in Q4 exhibited 4.76 -fold increased odds versus Q1 (95% CI: 2.75–8.25, P < 0.001), with progressive risk escalation from Q2 to Q4 (P < 0.001) (supplementary figure S3).

Mediation effect of the predictors of arterial stiffness

To further investigate the mediating role of baPWV in the association between METS-VF and all-cause mortality risk, we constructed a mediation model. Results of the mediation analysis are presented in Table 3. We found that baPWV significantly mediated the association between METS-VF and all-cause mortality. The total effect of METS-VF on mortality was statistically significant (P < 0.001). The indirect effect mediated through baPWV was also significant (β = 0.017, 95% CI: 0.010–0.026, P < 0.001), accounting for 15.4% of the total effect (mediation proportion). These findings indicate that baPWV partially mediates the association between METS-VF and all-cause mortality risk as a key mediator.

A sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis using the E-value was performed to assess the robustness of our primary finding. For the observed association between the highest METS-VF quartile and all-cause mortality (HR = 2.76, 95% CI: 1.71–4.45), the E-value was 3.42 for the point estimate and 2.25 for the lower confidence interval. This suggests that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both high METS-VF and mortality by risk ratios of at least 3.42 each to explain away the observed association, indicating a relatively robust finding (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

Our findings suggest that METS-VF serves as an independent predictor of long-term all-cause mortality in older adults. This association remained significant after rigorous adjustment for demographic characteristics, cardiometabolic comorbidities, lifestyle factors, and biochemical profiles, highlighting an important role of visceral adiposity in geriatric mortality risk. Additionally, we observed a significant cross-sectional association between elevated METS-VF and increased arterial stiffness (baPWV), which is consistent with a potential mechanistic link between visceral adiposity and vascular aging. Accordingly, mediation analysis revealed that baPWV partially mediated the association between METS-VF and mortality.

The metabolic score for visceral fat (METS-VF) represents a novel, non-invasive surrogate index for estimating intra-abdominal adipose tissue volume and visceral adiposity. Derived from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) data, this metric integrates a non-insulin-based insulin resistance indicator (METS-IR), anthropometric measures of body-fat distribution (waist-to-height ratio, WHtR), sex, and age [8]. Notably, a growing body of validation studies supports its enhanced utility in evaluating metabolic risk profiles and predicting incident cardiometabolic diseases, such as diabetes and hypertension [8–11]. A large cohort study conducted by NHANES in the United States indicates that METS-VF, can independently predict the long-term risks of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and cancer mortality. Moreover, its predictive value exceeds that of traditional obesity-related indicators [12]. A cohort study conducted in China followed 6,827 individuals aged over 40 years with diverse glucose tolerance statuses for 5 years. Results showed a significant dose-response relationship between METS-VF and both cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality [13]. However, the association between METS-VF and all-cause mortality remains unreported in older populations. Our study addresses this critical research gap. Notably, we observed a significant dose-dependent increase in mortality risk with higher METS-VF values. After comprehensive adjustment for all potential confounding factors, older adults in the highest METS-VF category exhibited a 155% elevated risk of all-cause mortality compared to those in the lowest category, underscoring the pivotal role of METS-VF in predicting mortality outcomes among older individuals.

Arterial stiffness, quantified as the resistance to deformation in arterial walls, cannot be directly measured in vivo but is reliably assessed via pulse wave velocity (PWV), where higher values indicate greater stiffness [15]. Our study confirms a significant association between METS-VF and elevated baPWV, underscoring the link between visceral fat accumulation and arterial stiffening in older adults. Furthermore, mediation analysis revealed that baPWV partially mediates the relationship between METS-VF and all-cause mortality, suggesting vascular dysfunction contributes to adiposity-related mortality risk. Notably, This mediation is consistent with the pronounced mortality risk escalation observed in high baPWV subgroups, suggesting arterial stiffness acts as both a mediator and effect amplifier. This mechanistic pathway may involve age-related alterations in fat distribution, chronic low-grade inflammation, and insulin resistance (IR) [1, 6]. Gealekman et al. demonstrated that visceral adipose tissue (VAT) exhibits lower capillary density and impaired angiogenic capacity compared to subcutaneous fat (SAT) [24]. This mismatch between adipose expansion and vascular supply creates a hypoxic microenvironment, triggering oxidative stress, adipocyte fibrosis, and eventual necrosis [25, 26]. Within this hypoxic milieu, tissue-resident immune cells overexpress proinflammatory genes—a pathogenic cascade further amplified by VAT’s direct secretion of inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) [25–29]. These factors propagate low-grade systemic inflammation, accelerating atherosclerosis and contributing to residual cardiovascular risk [30]. In addition, visceral adipose tissue drives IR and dysregulates adipokine secretion (e.g., reduced adiponectin, elevated leptin), promoting prothrombotic and proinflammatory states [17, 31]. Concurrently, impaired cytokine signaling in visceral fat exacerbates endothelial dysfunction, accelerating arterial collagen deposition and elastin degradation. These processes collectively foster macrovascular damage (e.g., atherosclerosis) and microvascular impairment, heightening susceptibility to death [32–34].

The relationship between METS-VF and all-cause mortality demonstrates clinically relevant variations across population subgroups, shaped by intersecting biological and physiological factors. Women appear disproportionately vulnerable to mortality risks at elevated METS-VF levels, possibly reflecting the postmenopausal metabolic shift wherein estrogen withdrawal amplifies visceral adiposity-related endothelial pathology [35–37]. This association also intensifies with advancing age—likely a consequence of lifelong lipid toxicity compounded by diminished organ reserve capacity in later life. Particularly compelling is the interaction between visceral adiposity and vascular health [38]. When arterial stiffness coexists with high METS-VF, mortality risk escalates nonlinearly. This synergy suggests shared pathophysiological terrain where chronic inflammatory processes simultaneously drive adipose tissue dysfunction and vascular remodeling. In such contexts, baPWV may serve not merely as a risk marker but as a functional indicator of microcirculatory compromise in end organs. Clinically, this implies METS-VF screening should prioritize older women, particularly those with elevated baPWV.

This study presents several novel contributions. To our knowledge, it is the first to comprehensively evaluate the association between METS-VF and long-term all-cause mortality exclusively in a community-based Chinese elderly cohort. The community-based design enhances the generalizability of our findings to the broader aging population. With a substantial median follow-up of 9.9 years, our study robustly captures the long-term impact of visceral adiposity on mortality risk. Furthermore, we are the first to investigate and identify baPWV, a marker of arterial stiffness, as a significant mediating pathway in this association. This mechanistic insight moves beyond a mere association and provides a plausible biological explanation for how visceral fat may ultimately confer an elevated risk of death. Collectively, our findings position METS-VF not only as a robust prognostic indicator but also illuminate a potential target for interventions aimed at mitigating mortality risk in the elderly.

Our finding that METS-VF is a predictor of mortality in an elderly Chinese cohort both aligns with and extends previous work. While earlier studies in predominantly middle-aged Western populations have linked visceral adiposity to adverse outcomes. The modestly stronger association observed in our study may be attributed to several factors [35–37]. It is primarily driven by our exclusive focus on the elderly, a population with heightened susceptibility to the metabolic consequences of visceral adiposity. This is further augmented by ethnic-specific differences in body composition and cardiovascular risk profiles. Methodologically, our analysis was strengthened by the inclusion of a more comprehensive panel of biochemical indicators and the objective quantification of arterial stiffness via baPWV, allowing for a more precise and mechanistically informed assessment.

The robust association we observed is not merely statistical but is supported by a plausible biological pathway. Our novel mediation analysis confirms that arterial stiffness, measured by baPWV, is a significant intermediary. This suggests a mechanistic link where visceral adiposity exacerbates systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, leading to increased arterial stiffness, which in turn elevates cardiac afterload and promotes end-organ damage, ultimately increasing mortality risk. This pathway explains a portion of the total effect and provides a tangible target for intervention.

The implications of our findings are twofold for clinical practice. First, the METS-VF, derived from routine clinical measures, offers a simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective tool for identifying high-risk elderly individuals in community and primary care settings. Second, the identification of baPWV as a mediator suggests that interventions aimed at reducing visceral fat may confer a dual benefit by directly improving metabolic health and indirectly attenuating the progression of arterial stiffness. Implementing routine assessment of METS-VF could therefore facilitate earlier, more personalized preventive strategies to reduce the long-term mortality burden in our aging population.

This study has several limitations. First, the participants were all elderly Chinese individuals from a single community. While this is suitable for studying this specific age group, it means our findings may not apply to younger adults or other populations. Second, despite despite adjusting for major clinical confounders, residual confounding by unmeasured factors (e.g., socioeconomic status) cannot be ruled out. Although all participants came from the same community, which might make their backgrounds somewhat similar, the lack of this data remains a potential limitation. Similarly, detailed information on specific medications was not fully collected, which could have affected the results. Finally, because we did not have access to the specific causes of death, our study could only examine the relationship with overall mortality, not with mortality from specific diseases. This is an important question that should be addressed in future studies.

Conclusions

In this community-based elderly Chinese cohort, a higher METS-VF was independently associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. Our findings suggest that arterial stiffness, as measured by baPWV, may represent a significant mediating pathway in this association. These results highlight the potential clinical value of assessing both visceral adiposity and arterial stiffness in the elderly. Future research should investigate whether the incorporation of METS-VF and baPWV into routine geriatric evaluations can effectively improve the screening of high-risk patients and the stratification of mortality risk.

Why does this paper matter?

This work addresses a significant gap in geriatric metabolic research by validating the clinical utility of the Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF) while exploring the role of arterial stiffness in visceral adiposity-related mortality. These results advance visceral fat risk stratification for elderly populations and propose combined METS-VF/baPWV assessment to identify high-risk individuals.
