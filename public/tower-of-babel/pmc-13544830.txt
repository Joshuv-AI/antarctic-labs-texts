Gastrointestinal stromal tumor of the round ligament with synchronous colorectal adenocarcinoma and hepatic metastases: a unique case report
Authors: Anas Derkaoui, Tarik Deflaoui, Intissar Aissi, Abdelali Guellil, Rachid Jabi, Mohammed Bouziane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal neoplasms of the digestive tract. While they usually arise in the stomach or small intestine, primary involvement of the hepatic round ligament is exceedingly rare. The synchronous coexistence of such an unusual GIST with colorectal adenocarcinoma (CRC) represents a major diagnostic and therapeutic challenge.Case presentation:We report the case of a 66-year-old man admitted with acute intestinal obstruction associated with epigastric pain and abdominal distension. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed two intra-abdominal masses associated with multiple hepatic nodules. Exploratory laparotomy identified a tumor arising from the hepatic round ligament, a necrotic mesenteric mass causing bowel obstruction, and multiple suspicious liver nodules. Surgical management included en bloc resection of the round ligament tumor, biopsies of the mesenteric and hepatic lesions, and a proximal ileostomy. Histopathological examination demonstrated a high-grade spindle-cell GIST of the round ligament, positive for CD117, DOG1, and CD34, with a high Ki-67 proliferation index, confirming its independent mesenchymal lineage. Concurrently, the mesenteric and hepatic lesions corresponded to metastatic, moderately differentiated colorectal adenocarcinoma expressing CK20 and CDX2. Imaging findings alone were insufficient to distinguish metastatic dissemination from two separate primary tumors. Rigorous histopathological and immunohistochemical characterization of each suspicious lesion was therefore crucial because therapeutic strategies differ substantially between GIST and metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma. The rarity of round ligament GISTs further contributes to the originality of this observation.Conclusion:Primary GIST of the hepatic round ligament is exceptionally rare. Its synchronous association with metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma has not been previously reported. This case emphasizes the importance of systematic histological evaluation of atypical lesions and multidisciplinary management to optimize therapeutic decision-making.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal tumors of the gastrointestinal tract, accounting for approximately 1–3% of digestive neoplasms[1]. They most frequently arise in the stomach (60%) and small intestine (30%), whereas extra-gastrointestinal stromal tumors (EGISTs) remain uncommon and represent less than 10% of cases[2].

The hepatic round ligament (ligamentum teres hepatis), an embryological remnant of the umbilical vein, is an exceptionally rare location for GIST development, with only isolated cases reported in the literature[3,4].

The synchronous occurrence of GIST and colorectal adenocarcinoma has occasionally been described, with an estimated incidence ranging from 2% to 4%[5,6]. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain this association, including common carcinogenic exposure, molecular susceptibility, or incidental coexistence[7,8]. However, synchronous colorectal adenocarcinoma associated with a primary round ligament GIST has not, to our knowledge, been previously reported.

We present a unique case of a primary high-grade GIST arising from the hepatic round ligament, discovered in association with synchronous metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma presenting as acute bowel obstruction. This work has been reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[9].

Case report

A 66-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 2-month history of progressive epigastric pain, recurrent vomiting, abdominal distension, and weight loss. He had no history of diabetes, hypertension, inflammatory bowel disease, or previous abdominal surgery. He was not taking any chronic medications. Family history was negative for gastrointestinal malignancies or hereditary cancer syndromes. The patient was a non-smoker and denied alcohol consumption.

On physical examination, the patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. Abdominal examination revealed marked distension associated with epigastric tenderness, without signs of generalized peritonitis.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated elevated inflammatory markers, with a C-reactive protein level of 143 mg/L. Liver and renal function tests were within normal ranges.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (Fig. 1) revealed two heterogeneous, necrotic intra-abdominal masses. The first lesion was located anteriorly along the hepatic round ligament and appeared relatively well-circumscribed. The second lesion was a large necrotic mesenteric mass in close contact with the ileum and the inferior liver surface, causing mechanical small-bowel obstruction. Exploratory laparotomy confirmed a complex intra-abdominal process responsible for bowel obstruction. A firm, encapsulated tumor measuring approximately 5 cm originated from the hepatic round ligament without macroscopic hepatic invasion (Fig. 2). A second large necrotic mesenteric mass infiltrated the ileal mesentery and compressed the ileum, explaining the obstructive presentation (Fig. 3). Multiple hepatic nodules involving both liver lobes were identified. No peritoneal carcinomatosis was observed. Given the uncertain histological nature of the lesions, an en bloc resection of the round ligament mass was performed. Multiple biopsies of the mesenteric and hepatic lesions were obtained, and a proximal ileostomy was created to relieve the obstruction.

Microscopically, the lesion consisted predominantly of spindle-cell proliferation arranged in intersecting fascicles with moderate nuclear atypia and focal tumor necrosis. Mitotic activity exceeded 5 mitoses per 50 high-power fields. Immunohistochemical examination demonstrated diffuse positivity for CD117, DOG1, and CD34, whereas SMA and S100 were negative. The Ki-67 proliferation index was assessed at 15%, further confirming the aggressive features of this mesenchymal neoplasm. According to the modified NIH classification, the tumor was categorized as high risk. Molecular analysis for KIT and PDGFRA mutations could not be performed due to limited technical availability.

Histopathological examination of the mesenteric and hepatic biopsies demonstrated a glandular architectural pattern lined by atypical columnar cells with stratified, hyperchromatic nuclei and dirty necrosis, compatible with moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma. To firmly establish the primary origin and exclude any trans-lineage relationship or collision phenomenon with the GIST, the immunohistochemical panel was expanded. The adenocarcinoma cells showed strong, diffuse nuclear positivity for CDX2 and cytoplasmic positivity for CK20, while remaining entirely negative for CK7, CD117, and DOG1. This clear divergence in immunohistochemical profiles provided robust evidence confirming the synchronous existence of two pathologically independent primary malignancies: an epithelial colorectal adenocarcinoma with hepatic and mesenteric metastases, and a distinct mesenchymal EGIST of the round ligament.

Postoperative colonoscopy subsequently revealed an ulcerative sigmoid colonic tumor, and endoscopic biopsies confirmed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma mirroring the exact immunohistochemical and morphological profile (CDX2+ and CK20+) observed in the hepatic and mesenteric secondary lesions. The final diagnosis was synchronous primary high-risk GIST of the hepatic round ligament associated with metastatic sigmoid colorectal adenocarcinoma. The postoperative course was uneventful. The case was discussed during a multidisciplinary tumor board meeting involving surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, and oncologists. Treatment with imatinib was initiated for the high-risk GIST. The patient was also referred for systemic chemotherapy for metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma with a FOLFOX-based regimen. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable under oncologic treatment.

Discussion

GISTs are mesenchymal tumors originating from the interstitial cells of Cajal and are characterized by KIT or PDGFRA pathway activation in most cases[1]. EGISTs represent a rare subgroup occurring outside the digestive tract and are thought to arise from displaced mesenchymal precursor cells[2]. The hepatic round ligament is one of the rarest reported locations for EGIST development. Only isolated cases involving the falciform or round ligament have been documented in the literature[3]. The embryological derivation of the ligament from the ventral mesentery may explain the persistence of mesenchymal progenitor cells capable of neoplastic transformation. Histologically, our case demonstrated the classic spindle-cell morphology commonly observed in GISTs, associated with diffuse CD117 and DOG1 positivity. Additional CD34 positivity and negative SMA and S100 staining further supported the diagnosis. The elevated Ki-67 index (15%) aligned with the mitotic count to justify its high-risk classification. According to the modified NIH classification, the tumor was considered high risk because of its elevated mitotic index and tumor size. The synchronous occurrence of GIST and colorectal adenocarcinoma remains uncommon but has been increasingly reported[5,6]. Several theories have been proposed to explain this association. Some authors suggest a common carcinogenic exposure or molecular susceptibility, whereas others consider the coexistence to be incidental because of increased imaging and endoscopic investigations[7,8]. However, no definitive pathogenic relationship has been established. Although synchronous GIST and colorectal adenocarcinoma have been sporadically reported in the literature, the majority of published cases have involved gastric or small intestinal GISTs associated with localized colorectal tumors[5–8]. To our knowledge, no previous case of a primary GIST arising from the hepatic round ligament associated with synchronous metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma has been reported. This makes our observation unique. Furthermore, unlike previously published cases, our patient presented with multiple abdominal masses and hepatic nodules, initially mimicking disseminated metastatic disease originating from a single primary tumor, thereby creating a major diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma.

To unequivocally validate the synchronous nature of these tumors and eliminate the possibility of a diagnostic artifact, strict histopathological criteria were applied. First, the two lesions arose from anatomically separate sites without continuity or transitional zones. Second, they displayed distinct cellular lineages – mesenchymal spindle cells for the EGIST and epithelial glandular elements for the adenocarcinoma. Finally, the diametrically opposed immunophenotypic signatures (CD117+/DOG1+/CD34+ versus CK20+/CDX2+) ruled out any shared clonal origin or transdifferentiation.

The major diagnostic challenge in our case was differentiating metastatic dissemination from two independent primary malignancies. Radiologically, the mesenteric and hepatic lesions could easily have been interpreted as metastatic GIST. Such misdiagnosis would have resulted in inappropriate therapeutic management based exclusively on tyrosine kinase inhibitors while delaying chemotherapy for colorectal adenocarcinoma. This observation, therefore, underlines the importance of systematic histological confirmation of all suspicious lesions, especially when imaging findings appear atypical or discordant. Therapeutic implications were substantial because management strategies differ considerably between GIST and colorectal adenocarcinoma. High-risk GISTs require complete surgical resection followed by adjuvant imatinib therapy when appropriate[10]. Conversely, metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma is primarily treated using systemic chemotherapy such as FOLFOX or FOLFIRI regimens with or without targeted therapy according to molecular status[11]. This case also emphasizes the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration, integrating surgery, pathology, radiology, gastroenterology, and oncology expertise to establish an individualized treatment strategy.

Conclusion

We describe a unique case of a primary GIST arising from the hepatic round ligament, associated with synchronous metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma.

This exceptionally rare coexistence created a major diagnostic and therapeutic challenge because imaging findings alone were insufficient to distinguish metastatic dissemination from synchronous primary malignancies.

Our case highlights the critical importance of systematic histopathological confirmation of all suspicious lesions in complex oncologic presentations, as accurate diagnosis has a direct impact on therapeutic decision-making and prognosis.

Multidisciplinary management remains essential to optimize individualized treatment strategies in such rare synchronous malignancies.
