Palliative Care education in undergraduate pharmacy curricula in Brazil: a descriptive cross-sectional study
Authors: Ricardo Augusto dos Santos Silva, Maria Clara Pedrosa Ferreira, Giovana Flora Martine Teixeira, João Pedro Vasconcelos Paolinelli, Alexandre Ernesto Silva, Mariana Linhares Pereira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPalliative Care is essential for improving the quality of life of individuals with life-threatening conditions. Despite its growing relevance, the education of health professionals in this field remains insufficient. In Brazil, the implementation of the National Policy for Palliative Care reinforces the need to qualify the health workforce. In this context, the present study aimed to analyze the availability of Palliative Care courses in undergraduate Pharmacy programs in Brazil, as well as to characterize their content.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional, descriptive, documentary analysis study, based on data identifying active Pharmacy programs in Brazil as of April 2025. Higher Education Institutions with recognized programs were included, while duplicate, incomplete, or discontinued records were excluded. Data collection was carried out between April and December 2025 by two independent researchers through the analysis of Curricular Matrices and Pedagogical Course Projects available on institutional websites. The variables analyzed included type of institution, geographic location, and course characteristics, and were described using descriptive statistics.ResultsA total of 608 Higher Education Institutions were identified, most of them private and located in the Southeast and Northeast regions. A total of 530 curricular documents were available for analysis, and Palliative Care courses were identified in only four institutions (0.75%), being mandatory in only one. The courses varied in workload, objectives, and content. The content was grouped into the following categories: Humanization and Spirituality, Pharmacist’s Role, Multidisciplinary Care Management, and Introduction to Palliative Care.ConclusionsThis study highlights the limited availability of Palliative Care education in undergraduate Pharmacy programs in Brazil, showing that only four institutions offer such courses. Given the increasing life expectancy and the consequent rise in chronic disease-related suffering in the coming years, it is both necessary and timely for health professionals to receive basic training in this field. Therefore, expanding the availability of Palliative Care courses in undergraduate health programs in Brazil is essential to better prepare future multidisciplinary teams, in addition to the need to provide education that reflects the Brazilian context.

Background

Palliative Care is defined as active holistic care for individuals of all ages experiencing health-related suffering due to serious illness, particularly those approaching the end of life. Its primary goal is to improve the quality of life of patients, families, and caregivers through the prevention and relief of suffering, including the management of physical symptoms and the provision of psychosocial and spiritual support [1]. Despite its importance, an estimated 58.7 million people worldwide require Palliative Care, yet only 14% receive it [2].

The global need for Palliative Care is expected to increase substantially over the coming decades, driven by population aging and the growing burden of non-communicable diseases [3–5] Brazil follows this trend, with projections indicating that more than one million people will require Palliative Care services by 2040 [6].

Pharmacists are essential to the management of pharmacotherapy in Palliative Care, contributing to medication access, symptom control, identification of drug therapy problems, medication safety, and treatment adherence. Evidence has demonstrated the positive impact of pharmacists within multidisciplinary teams, particularly in symptom management and the prevention of adverse events [7–9].

Despite the progress achieved, gaps still remain in the education and training of pharmacists for Palliative Care practice. Several educational initiatives have been developed across different health professions, including the Education in Palliative and End-of-Life Care (EPEC) [10], aimed at teaching Palliative Care in medicine; the Palliative Education Assessment Tool (PEAT), created to assess Palliative Care education in undergraduate medical programs [11]; the End-of-Life Nursing Education Consortium (ELNEC), focused on nursing education [12]; and, in pharmacy, the Strategic Planning Summit for the Advancement of Pain and Palliative Care Pharmacy, which proposed specific competencies and curricular recommendations [13]. These initiatives reflect an international movement toward strengthening Palliative Care education; however, the current status of Palliative Care education in undergraduate pharmacy programs in Brazil remains largely unknown.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the availability of Palliative Care courses in undergraduate Pharmacy programs in Brazil and to characterize their curricular structure and educational content.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional, descriptive study based on documentary analysis, reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Statement (STROBE) [14]. Information on undergraduate Pharmacy programs in Brazil was obtained from publicly available data provided by the Brazilian Ministry of Education (MEC) (https://emec.mec.gov.br/). Data regarding the availability of Palliative Care courses were extracted from the official websites of Higher Education Institutions (HEIs).

Data collection procedures

Identification of programs

The search for Pharmacy programs in the MEC database was conducted in April 2025. To this end, a search was performed by selecting the “Undergraduate Program” option and using the keyword “Pharmacy.” Programs that were deregistered by the MEC, in the process of closure, or already discontinued were excluded. The data were exported to a spreadsheet using Microsoft Excel 2016®. After removing duplicates and incomplete records, the dataset was used for analysis. Duplicate records were defined as Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) with the same registration number or the same institutional acronym.

Identification of HEIs offering Palliative Care courses

The identification of courses related to Palliative Care in undergraduate Pharmacy programs was carried out between April and June 2025. Subsequently, in June 2026, a complementary search was conducted using the term “thanatology”, expanding the survey to verify whether content related to the theme appeared under other denominations within the curricula. The process was carried out by two independent, previously trained, researchers who were blinded to partial results. In cases of disagreement, a third reviewer was consulted to reach consensus.

The search strategy was adapted from a previously published methodology designed to identify specific disciplines within health-related undergraduate curricula [15–17]. The search was conducted directly on the official websites of Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) to locate the Curricular Matrix (CM) or the Pedagogical Course Project (PCP).

The identification of courses was conducted using the Portuguese-language descriptors “paliativ*” (“palliative*”) and “vida” (“life”). The use of the truncated search term “paliativ” allowed the retrieval of variations such as “paliativo” and “paliativa”, ensuring a comprehensive search strategy. The Portuguese-language descriptor “vida” (“life”) was incorporated to increase the sensitivity of the search process, enabling the identification of courses whose titles did not explicitly include the expression “Cuidados Paliativos” (“Palliative Care”) but addressed related content. Notably, in the nursing field, the expression “cuidados em fim de vida” (“end-of-life care”) and Tanatologia (“thanatology”) is commonly used to refer to palliative care practices, which justified its inclusion as a complementary search term [16, 18].

Descriptive analysis of course characteristics and educational content

After course identification, the corresponding curricular documents were reviewed to extract information regarding course type (mandatory or elective), workload, prerequisites, learning objectives, and educational content. The documents were stored locally and, when available only online, the respective access link was recorded in the data collection spreadsheet.

At this stage, information regarding document type (CM or PCP), geographic location (federative unit), and administrative status of the institution (public or private) was also collected. For analysis, the most recently updated version of the document available on the institution’s website was considered, acknowledging that some documents may have been outdated due to delays in institutional updates.

After the identification of the courses, the educational content was extracted from the curricular documents and subjected to descriptive analysis. The identified content was grouped into educational themes based on conceptual similarity, allowing the characterization of the educational profile of the courses analyzed. The development of the thematic categories was informed by the educational frameworks of the Palliative Education Assessment Tool (PEAT) [11] and the recommendations of the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) for Palliative Care education in undergraduate Pharmacy programs [13]. However, the analysis was not restricted to the domains proposed by these frameworks. Instead, they were used to support the organization, interpretation, and discussion of the identified content while preserving the inclusion of emerging themes observed in the curricula analyzed.

The thematic categories identified included: foundations of Palliative Care, pain management, management of non-pain symptoms, ethical and legal aspects, psychosocial, family, and spiritual aspects, communication and teamwork, the pharmacist’s role in Palliative Care, and additional themes identified in the curricula.

Statistical analysis

Data regarding program identification, availability of CM and/or PCP, and presence of Palliative Care content were analyzed using descriptive statistics, with absolute and relative frequencies.

The dataset supporting the conclusions of this study is available in the Open Science Framework repository: https://osf.io/sqmgn/files/4zng8.

Ethical considerations

According to Resolution No. 510/2016 of the Brazilian National Health Council, this study did not require approval by a Research Ethics Committee involving human subjects, as it is based on publicly available data and does not involve direct participation of human subjects.

Results

A total of 608 Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) offering undergraduate Pharmacy programs were identified across Brazil. Of these, 530 curricular documents were retrieved, of which 84.3% (n = 447) were curriculum matrices. Details of the identification and selection process of the curricula are presented in Fig. 1.

Most programs were offered in the Southeast region (35.9%, n = 219) and Northeast region (27.9%, n = 170). This was followed by the South (15.7%, n = 96), North (9.4%, n = 57), Midwest (9.0%, n = 55), and the Federal District (1.8%, n = 11). The majority of these programs were offered by private institutions, which accounted for 89.1% (n = 542).

Among the 530 curricula analyzed, Palliative Care was offered in only four HEIs (0.75%), with two private institutions located in the Northeast region (50.0%) and two public institutions in the Southeast region (50.0%). The course was mandatory only at Faculdade Paraibana de Saúde (FPS), where it is titled “Cuidados paliativos, saúde e espiritualidade e psicologia aplicada a profissão farmacêutica” (“Palliative Care, Health, Spirituality, and Psychology Applied to Pharmacy Practice”) In the other institutions, it was offered as an elective course under different titles: at Universidade Federal de São João del-Rei (UFSJ), it is named “Cuidados Farmacêuticos em Cuidados Paliativos” (“Pharmaceutical Care in Palliative Care”); at Faculdade de Quixeramobim (UNIQ), “Farmácia Clínica em Cuidados Paliativos” (“Clinical Pharmacy in Palliative Care”); and at Universidade Federal de Ouro Preto (UFOP), “Princípios dos Cuidados Paliativos na Prática Farmacêutica” (“Principles of Palliative Care in Pharmaceutical Practice”). No courses related to the terms end-of-life care and thanatology were found, highlighting the absence of explicit content on these topics within the curricula analyzed. The educational content demonstrated areas of convergence across the courses analyzed, particularly regarding introductory aspects of Palliative Care and interprofessional teamwork. The courses offered by UFSJ and UFOP placed greater emphasis on the management of pain and non-pain symptoms, topics that were not explicitly described in the FPS course syllabus. However, this absence may reflect limitations in the syllabus description, as symptom management was included among the general learning objectives of the course.

UFSJ was distinguished by the inclusion of content specifically related to the pharmacist’s role in Palliative Care and evidence-based practice, topics that were not identified in the other courses. In contrast, FPS was the only institution to explicitly address the spiritual dimension of care and, together with UFOP, included ethical and legal aspects related to Palliative Care. Discussion

This is the first study to map the teaching of Palliative Care in undergraduate Pharmacy programs in Brazil. The limited availability of Palliative Care courses identified in this study is consistent with findings from other health-related disciplines, such as Medicine, where only 14.0% of undergraduate programs included Palliative Care content in their curricula in 2018 [19], and Nursing, where only 18.6% of the courses analyzed offered a discipline related to Palliative Care [16].

Palliative Care courses were offered only in the Southeast and Northeast regions of Brazil, which also concentrated the majority of the undergraduate Pharmacy programs included in the analysis. A similar distribution pattern was also identified in studies assessing undergraduate Medical and Nursing curricula [16, 19]. Furthermore, these regions have the greatest availability of Palliative Care services and hospital beds in the country, which may facilitate the incorporation of Palliative Care into undergraduate curricula through access to clinical practice settings, specialized faculty, and institutional initiatives focused on the fiel [20].

At the international level, no recent studies mapping the teaching of Palliative Care in undergraduate Pharmacy programs were identified. Only one study, conducted in the United States and published in 2013, was found. Among the colleges and schools of pharmacy that responded to the survey, only 11.0% reported offering a specific Palliative Care course within their undergraduate Pharmacy curriculum [21].

Although several strategies have been developed internationally for the teaching of Palliative Care in undergraduate health programs [10, 12], at the international level, for undergraduate Pharmacy programs there is the model proposed by IASP [13]. In Brazil, there is no specific guideline for undergraduate Pharmacy education, as demonstrated in the present study, which highlighted inconsistencies in how certain contents are presented across course syllabi.

Beyond Brazil, the challenge of how to deliver Palliative Care content within undergraduate Pharmacy education also appears to persist. This is evidenced by the study, which, in a meta-analysis, observed that Palliative Care experiences improved students’ confidence, attitudes, and perceptions. However, there was considerable heterogeneity regarding the number of required teaching hours, content, and teaching methodologies. Nevertheless, interprofessional experiences appear to contribute positively to education in this field [22].

Introductory aspects of Palliative Care are also present in all universities and are essential, since they may represent students’ first contact with this subject [19]. When there is prior exposure, the content is often associated with stigma. These introductory components are also included in the proposals of the IASP, EPEC, and ELNEC [11–13].

Interdisciplinary/multidisciplinary care is an essential pillar for understanding the individual’s dimensions in palliative care, as physical, psychological, social, and spiritual suffering may be present and require support from different professionals [22, 23]. This is described by all universities and pharmaceutical assistance to address symptoms and know when to refer to a professional, so that no suffering persists.

Bioethical aspects were present in two curricula and highlight the need for bioethical training aligned with the perspective of Palliative Care, as they address important topics such as legal issues related to end-of-life care, living wills, and advance directives. The Bioethics course is included in Pharmacy undergraduate programs; however, due to the breadth and complexity of its content, it is not always possible to adequately address specific thematic areas [24].

Palliative Care teaching models emphasize symptom management, particularly pain management, which is present in all curricula and is essential for pharmacists in Palliative Care [10, 13, 18, 22]. Although FPS does not detail this content in its syllabus, one of its objectives is to “identify the management of signs and symptoms: pain control and symptom relief, as well as to list essential medications in PC.” This need is reinforced by the already consolidated knowledge in pharmacotherapy, which expands the pharmacist’s contribution to comprehensive patient care.

Communication is considered a fundamental element of care, as the involvement of the healthcare team, patients, and their families enables shared decision-making and fosters the development of a strong therapeutic relationship [11, 13]. However, it is described in the syllabus of only one course.

The Consensus Recommendations from The Strategic Planning Sumit for Pain and Palliative Care Pharmacy Practice recommends a minimum workload of 300 min (5 h), divided into six 50-minute sessions [13]. All programs exceeded this workload; however, UFOP had the highest workload, which may be related to the greater amount of curricular content.

At FPS, the course is mandatory, whereas in the other three institutions it is offered as an elective. Although this allows some students to pursue more in-depth knowledge in specific areas, it may compromise general training, given that undergraduate programs in Brazil are structured to provide a generalist education [25]. Consequently, even students with specific interests may later work in various fields, including Palliative Care, as established by Resolution 572/2013 of the Federal Council of Pharmacy [26].

This study has some limitations, including difficulties in obtaining data from Course Pedagogical Projects and Curricular Matrices on the websites of Higher Education Institutions (HEIs), as 13.0% of the institutions did not make these documents available online. In addition, due to the data collection period, some HEIs may have introduced Palliative Care courses after the beginning of the analysis and were therefore not captured in the results.

Another limitation is related to the identification of courses delivered in a transversal manner, which prevented the assessment of whether Palliative Care content is integrated into other undergraduate courses. In Brazil, however, there is no consensus regarding the teaching of Palliative Care in undergraduate Pharmacy programs, nor is there an instrument available for the horizontal analysis of this content. This limits the ability to perform a comprehensive horizontal evaluation.

It should also be considered that Palliative Care content may be incorporated into other courses without being explicitly labeled as such, or included in elective courses that are not regularly offered and are often not described in curricular documents, which may also be outdated or refer to other health-related undergraduate programs. To mitigate these limitations, calibration among researchers was performed, search terms were defined consistently with the literature and the researchers’ expertise, and a systematic double-review search strategy was applied to institutional websites, resulting in a robust sample for analysis.

Another limitation is related to the FPS curriculum, which presents broad learning objectives that include Pharmaceutical Services in Palliative Care, theoretical aspects of Health and Spirituality, practical aspects of Health and Spirituality, and Health Psychology. These objectives encompass relevant and necessary competencies for Palliative Care education; however, they are not explicitly described in the course syllabus, making it impossible to understand how the content is effectively delivered.

The lack of training in Palliative Care in undergraduate Pharmacy programs identified in this study is consistent with previous findings reported in Medicine and Nursing programs, suggesting that this gap may extend to other health-related undergraduate courses in Brazil [16, 19]. Therefore, the results indicate the need to develop national educational frameworks for Palliative Care teaching in undergraduate Pharmacy education that can align with international recommendations while simultaneously incorporating the specificities of the Brazilian context, such as the organization of the Unified Health System (SUS), the implementation of the National Palliative Care Policy, challenges related to access to and use of medications, and the provision of clinical pharmaceutical services aimed at the needs of patients with life-threatening illnesses and their families.

Conclusion

This study highlights the limited availability of Palliative Care education in undergraduate Pharmacy programs in Brazil, showing that only four institutions offer such courses. Given the increasing life expectancy and the consequent rise in chronic disease-related suffering in the coming years, it is both necessary and timely for health professionals to receive basic training in this field. Therefore, expanding the availability of Palliative Care courses in undergraduate health programs in Brazil is essential to better prepare future multidisciplinary teams, in addition to the need to provide education that reflects the Brazilian context.
