Temporally Flexible Transport Scheduling on Networks with Departure-Arrival Constriction and Nodal Capacity Limits
Anqi Dong, Karl H. Johansson, Johan Karlsson

Abstract
We investigate the optimal transport (OT) problem over networks, wherein supply and demand are conceptualized as temporal marginals governing departure rates of particles from source nodes and arrival rates at sink nodes. This setting extends the classical OT framework, where all mass is conventionally assumed to depart at t = 0 t=0 and arrive at t = t f t=t_{f} . Our generalization accommodates departures and arrivals at specified times, referred as departure–arrival (DA) constraints. In particular, we impose nodal-temporal flux constraints at source and sink nodes, characterizing two distinct scenarios: (i) Independent DA constraints, where departure and arrival rates are prescribed independently, and (ii) Coupled DA constraints, where each particle’s transportation time span is explicitly specified. We establish that OT with independent DA constraints admits a multi-marginal optimal transport formulation, while the coupled DA case aligns with the unequal-dimensional OT framework. For line graphs, we analyze the existence and uniqueness of the solution path. For general graphs, we use a constructive path-based reduction and optimize over a prescribed set of paths. From a computational perspective, we consider entropic regularization of the original problem to efficiently provide solutions based on multi-marginal Sinkhorn method, making use of the graphical structure of the cost to further improve scalability. Our numerical simulation further illustrates the linear convergence rate in terms of marginal violation.



Keywords:

Optimal transport, graphical transportation, departure–arrival constraint, temporal flexibility, multi–marginal Sinkhorn algorithm

Introduction

Network flow problems aim to optimize the movement of commodities across a network while satisfying flow capacity and conservation constraints. These models have broad applications in mathematics, computer science, and transportation optimization

In many networked logistics and service systems, however, time is the primary degree of freedom. Control here is, in essence, a schedule: a measured release of mass, orchestrated in time, that guides it through junctions and grants it passage downstream. In port drayage, cranes, yard blocks, and gates operate under tight time-varying capacity. The present work builds on this line of thought and brings it into a network setting where time is the control input. We tie optimization and control by taking the schedule as the decision. Path-wise couplings and crossing-time profiles are chosen to minimize transport cost under marginal and capacity constraints. A schedule is a distribution of crossing times along each path. Boundary time profiles act as release and acceptance laws at the source and the sink. At each interior node, a single crossing-time profile meters flow and enforces conservation, since the same time index labels both incoming and outgoing mass under the capacity limit. From a network optimization perspective, our formulation can be viewed as minimum-cost flow problem with time flexibility. Classical minimum-cost flow fixes edge capacities and costs and optimizes how much mass uses each arc, while time either does not appear or is treated as a static resource. Here, the network and total transported mass are fixed, and time itself becomes control variable. The decision is when mass is allowed to traverse each node and edge, under explicit departure–arrival profiles and nodal throughput limits. In this sense, we lift minimum-cost flow from a static allocation problem to a path-wise scheduling problem while remaining within an optimal transport framework.

Herein, we cast optimal transport on networks as a
scheduling-and-control
problem with time as the actuator. The framework is path-wise. Flows are represented by multi-dimensional couplings whose marginals encode source and sink distributions together with crossing-time profiles at interior nodes. In this way, release curves, delivery deadlines, and per-node throughput constraints are handled within a single optimal transport formulation rather than through ad hoc scheduling rules. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first framework that integrates departure–arrival specifications and time-varying nodal capacities into a path-wise optimal transport model on networks, provides structural conditions for well-posedness, and propose scalable
Schrödinger-Fortet-Sinkhorn

Problem setup and background

We start with a recap of optimal transport, focusing on its extension from bi-marginal to multi-marginal formulations, marginal- and coupling-wise relaxations, and the incorporation of physical constraints. We then specialize to network-based optimal transport, which will serve as the reference point for the temporally flexible formulations in Sections
3
and
4
.

2.1
Multi-marginal optimal transport

Multi-marginal optimal transport
(MMOT) generalizes the classical optimal transport framework to settings with more than two marginals, and seeks a joint distribution that minimizes a high-dimensional cost while matching given marginals. Key challenges include establishing the existence and uniqueness of solutions for general transport costs. See, for instance,
[
10
,
29
,
37
]
.

Given compactly supported non-negative measures
\mu^{0},\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{{\mathcal{T}}}
on spaces
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a continuous cost function
c(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{\mathcal{T}})
, the Monge formulation of MMOT seeks transport maps
T_{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
\ell=1,\dots,{\mathcal{T}}
that minimize

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}c\big(x_{0},T_{1}(x_{0}),\dots,T_{\mathcal{T}}(x_{0})\big)\,d\mu^{0}(x_{0}),

subject to
T_{\ell}{}_{\sharp}\mu^{0}=\mu^{\ell}
for
\ell=1,\dots,{\mathcal{T}}
. As usual, the push-forward notation
T_{\ell}{}_{\sharp}\mu^{0}=\mu^{\ell}
means that for any Borel set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mu^{0}\big(T_{\ell}^{-1}(S)\big)=\mu^{\ell}(S)
. As in the bi-marginal case, a Monge solution may fail to exist.

The Kantorovich formulation of MMOT replaces transport maps by couplings and minimizes

\displaystyle\langle c,\pi\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}c(x_{0},\dots,x_{\mathcal{T}})\,d\pi(x_{0},\dots,x_{\mathcal{T}}),

(3)

over the set of admissible couplings

\displaystyle\Pi(\mu^{0},\dots,\mu^{\mathcal{T}})=\Big\{\pi\;\Big|\;P_{\ell}(\pi)=\mu^{\ell},\ \ell=0,1,\dots,{\mathcal{T}}\Big\},

(4)

and
P_{\ell}(\pi)
denotes the projection of
\pi
onto its
\ell
-th marginal, i.e.,

\displaystyle P_{\ell}(\pi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}d\pi(x_{0},\dots,x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell},x_{\ell+1},\dots,x_{{\mathcal{T}}}),

where the iterated integral is taken over all variables
x_{k}
with
k\neq\ell
.

Conditions for uniqueness of the solution

We next recall classical conditions that guarantee the uniqueness of the optimal coupling in the bi-marginal setting, and then their multi-marginal analogue.

Definition 1

(
Monge condition

[
39
]
)
.

A cost function
c:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

[

\Delta_{(x,x^{\prime})}^{(y,y^{\prime})}(c):=c(x,y)+c(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-c(x,y^{\prime})-c(x^{\prime},y)

satisfies
\Delta_{(x,x^{\prime})}^{(y,y^{\prime})}(c)\leq 0

If

is quasi-antitone, in the sense that

, or quasi-monotone, in the sense that
\Delta_{(x,x^{\prime})}^{(y,y^{\prime})}(c)\geq 0
, then the minimizer of the transport problem with cost

is unique, see
[
5
]
and

. In matching theory, the cross-difference condition expresses that, for given matching pairs

[

7

10

and extended to higher dimensions in
[

Definition 2

(
Twist condition

[
47
]
)
.

Let
c(x,y)\in C^{1}
. The cost
c
is said to satisfy the twist condition if, for each fixed
x
, the map
y\mapsto\nabla_{x}c(x,y)
is injective, and for each fixed
y
, the map
x\mapsto\nabla_{y}c(x,y)
is injective. Equivalently, for all distinct pairs

and

, one has

D_{x}c(x,y)\neq D_{x}c(x,y^{\prime})
and

D_{y}c(x,y)\neq D_{y}c(x^{\prime},y)
.

In other words, the gradient
\nabla_{x}c(x,y)

\nabla_{y}c(x,y)

In the multi-marginal setting, an analogous structure is obtained by imposing a Monge-type condition on all two-marginal projections of the cost.

Definition 3

(
Generalized Monge condition

[
39
]
)
.

Consider a cost functional
c:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form
c(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, where
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
lie in the support of a measure on
{\mathcal{X}}^{n}
with
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}
. The cost

is said to satisfy the generalized Monge condition if, for any pair of indices
1\leq i<j\leq n
and all
x_{i}\leq x_{i}^{\prime}
and
x_{j}\leq x_{j}^{\prime}
, one has
c(x_{i},x_{j})+c(x_{i}^{\prime},x_{j}^{\prime})\leq c(x_{i},x_{j}^{\prime})+c(x_{i}^{\prime},x_{j})

with all other coordinates held fixed.

Under the generalized Monge condition, one can show that, for each marginal
\mu_{i}
, the optimal coupling takes the form

\displaystyle\pi=\big(T^{1},\dots,T^{i-1},\mathrm{Id},T^{i+1},\dots,T^{n}\big)_{\sharp}\mu_{i},

where each transport map
T^{j}
is unique and monotone for all
j=1,\dots,n
, and this representation holds for every
i=1,\dots,n
. Hence, the joint coupling
\pi
is uniquely determined across all marginals. This generalized Monge structure will reappear later when we establish uniqueness in our one-dimensional line-graph setting.

2.3
Problem: optimal transport on networks

We established departure–arrival constrained models over a graph based on the Kantorovich formulation. Let
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{W}})
be a directed graph, where
{\mathcal{V}}
is the node set,
{\mathcal{E}}
the directed edge set, and
{\mathcal{W}}
the collection of edge weights. Each edge
e\in{\mathcal{E}}
carries a weight
w(e)
that represents the cost (or, distance) of transporting one unit of mass along that edge. A subset
{\mathcal{V}}^{-}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
collects source nodes and a subset
{\mathcal{V}}^{+}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
collects sink nodes.

Discrete source and sink distributions are given by nonnegative functions
\mu
and
\nu
on
{\mathcal{V}}
, supported on
{\mathcal{V}}^{-}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{+}
respectively. We assume that total supply matches total demand, i.e.,

\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\mu(v)=\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\nu(v)
.
A transportation plan is a nonnegative function
\pi
on
{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}
, where
\pi(v_{i},v_{j})
specifies how much mass is sent from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
and consistency with marginals requires
\sum_{v_{i}\in{\mathcal{V}}}\pi(v_{i},v_{j})=\nu(v_{j})
and
\sum_{v_{j}\in{\mathcal{V}}}\pi(v_{i},v_{j})=\mu(v_{i})
.
The transportation cost is encoded by a function
c:{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{R}
, typically induced by the graph geometry, for instance by the length or travel time of the shortest path from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
in
{\mathcal{G}}
. The network-based optimal transport problem then minimizes

\sum_{v_{i},v_{j}\in{\mathcal{V}}}c(v_{i},v_{j})\,\pi(v_{i},v_{j})
,
over all plans
\pi
that satisfy the marginal constraints. This coincides with a minimum-cost flow problem
[
1
]
on
{\mathcal{G}}
with node supplies
\mu
and demands
\nu
. In this static setting, time does not appear explicitly. In the next sections, we enrich the model by attaching time coordinates to paths, so that departures, crossings, and arrivals become time profiles rather than instantaneous transfers, and by imposing capacity constraints at intermediate nodes together with departure–arrival specifications at the boundary.

3
Independent DA constraints: problem formulation and uniqueness

In this section, we specialize to a path graph and make time explicit. We consider a graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{W}})
whose vertices are ordered as

{\mathbf{p}}=\{v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{{\mathcal{T}}-1},v_{{\mathcal{T}}}\}
,
with
v_{0}
the unique source and
v_{{\mathcal{T}}}
the unique sink. Each edge
e=(v_{i-1},v_{i})\in{\mathcal{E}}
is assigned with a positive weight
w(v_{i-1},v_{i})>0
. We assume that one unit of mass is dispatched from
v_{0}
and must reach
v_{{\mathcal{T}}}
, so that in the static setting, transport takes place along the single path
{\mathbf{p}}
and reduces to a shortest-path problem. We consider a departure-arrival over a line graph, in which departures from
v_{0}
, crossings at the intermediate nodes
v_{1},\dots,v_{{\mathcal{T}}-1}
, and arrivals at
v_{{\mathcal{T}}}
are described by time profiles subject to departure–arrival specifications and nodal capacity bounds.

The mass flow is governed by departure and arrival rate constraints. The departure rate
\mu^{0}(t)
describes the mass density over time at which mass leaves the source node
v_{0}
, so that
\int_{t}^{t+dt}\mu^{0}(\tau)\,d\tau
is the amount departing in the interval
[t,t+dt]
. Similarly, the arrival rate
\mu^{\mathcal{T}}(t)
describes the mass density over time at which mass reaches the sink node
v_{\mathcal{T}}
, with
\mu^{\mathcal{T}}(t)\,dt
the amount arriving in
[t,t+dt]
. In addition to these boundary specifications, the flow is subject to rate bounds at each intermediate node along the path,

\sigma^{i}(t)\leq r(v_{i}),\qquad i=1,\dots,{\mathcal{T}}-1,

where
\sigma^{i}(t)
denotes the instantaneous rate of mass crossing node
v_{i}
. These constraints ensure that the throughput at each intermediate node does not exceed the prescribed flux limit, reflecting physical or logistical capacity. An illustration is given in Fig.
1
.

Mass flow along the line graph
{\mathbf{p}}=\{v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{{\mathcal{T}}-1},v_{\mathcal{T}}\}
. Each particle departs from
v_{0}
according to the departure rate
\mu^{0}
, travels sequentially through intermediate nodes
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{{\mathcal{T}}-1}
abides flow-rate constraints at each node, and arrives at the sink
v_{\mathcal{T}}
according to the arrival rate
\mu^{\mathcal{T}}
.

Independent DA constraints refer to the case where the departure distribution
\mu^{0}(t)
and the arrival distribution
\mu^{\mathcal{T}}(t)
are prescribed separately, without specifying how individual departures are paired with arrivals. Our first step is to formulate the scheduling problem on a single intermediate node. This will serve as the basic building block for the multi-node line graph. We consider a single toll located at
v_{1}
, which imposes a flow-rate constraint
\sigma^{1}(t)\leq r
with threshold
r>0
. The times at which mass departs from
v_{0}
, crosses
v_{1}
, and arrives at
v_{2}
are denoted by
t_{0}
,
t_{1}
, and
t_{2}
, respectively, and the cost of transporting mass along the two segments is modeled by

\displaystyle c(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{w(v_{0},v_{1})}{t_{1}-t_{0}}+\frac{w(v_{1},v_{2})}{t_{2}-t_{1}}.

(5)

The first term measures the transit cost from
v_{0}
to
v_{1}
over the time interval

, and the second term the cost from
v_{1}

.

(6b)

(6c)

\displaystyle\iint_{t_{0},t_{2}}\pi(dt_{0},dt_{1},dt_{2}):=\sigma^{1}(dt_{1})\leq rdt_{1}.

F_{0}^{-1}(u)=\inf\{t\mid F_{0}(t)\geq u\}

F_{{\mathcal{T}}}^{-1}(u)=\inf\{t\mid F_{{\mathcal{T}}}(t)\geq u\}
. Then

F_{{\mathcal{T}}}^{-1}(u)\ \geq\ F_{0}^{-1}(u)+\Delta

for all
u\in(0,1)
. Define
\pi
as the image of the Lebesgue measure on
(0,1)
under the map

u\ \mapsto\ \big(F_{0}^{-1}(u),\,F_{{\mathcal{T}}}^{-1}(u)\big)
.
By construction, the first marginal of
\pi
has CDF
F_{0}
and the second marginal has CDF
F_{{\mathcal{T}}}
. The pointwise inequality above gives
t_{{\mathcal{T}}}\geq t_{0}+\Delta
for
\pi
-almost every pair, hence
\pi
is feasible. The support bounds follow by evaluating
F_{0}(t)\geq F_{{\mathcal{T}}}(t+\Delta)
at
t=\inf\supp(\mu^{{\mathcal{T}}})-\Delta
and at
t=\sup\supp(\mu^{0})-\Delta
.
∎

The condition
F_{0}(t)\geq F_{{\mathcal{T}}}(t+\Delta)

is related to order-type comparisons between distributions
(see, e.g.,
[
27
,
42
]
),
but unlike in martingale optimal transport
[
2
]
we do not impose any
martingale or mean-preserving constraint here. We only use it as
a simple feasibility check for the DA pair.

3.2
Existence and uniqueness for single intermediate node

Before analyzing uniqueness, we first establish the existence of a minimizer for Problem
1
.

Proposition 1

(
Existence of solution
).

For sufficiently large
r
, Problem
1
admits a minimizing solution
\pi
.

Proof.

The space of admissible measures
\pi
, namely

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\Pi=\bigg\{\pi\in\mathcal{M}_{+}\big([0,t_{f}]^{3}\big)\Big|\;\iint_{t_{1},t_{2}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\pi=\mu^{0}(t_{0}),\ \iint_{t_{0},t_{1}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\pi=\mu^{\mathcal{T}}(t_{2}),\iint_{t_{0},t_{2}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\pi:=\sigma^{1}(t_{1})\leq r\,dt_{1}\bigg\},\end{aligned}

(7)

is tight and closed for narrow convergence. Hence it is weakly compact. Since the cost (
6a
) is lower semicontinuous, the direct method gives existence of a minimizer once we know that
\Pi
is non-empty.

We now show that
\Pi
is non-empty when
r
is large enough by an explicit construction.
By Lemma
1
, the minimum travel time condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle F_{{\mathcal{T}}}^{-1}(u)\geq F_{0}^{-1}(u)+\Delta\qquad\text{for all }u\in(0,1).

(8)

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\Delta)
and take sufficient large
r
so that
\varepsilon+1/r<\Delta
.
Let
u\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
and
s\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1/r)
be independent, and define

t_{0}=F_{0}^{-1}(u)
,
t_{2}=F_{{\mathcal{T}}}^{-1}(u)
, and
t_{1}=t_{0}+\varepsilon+s
. Let
\tilde{\pi}
be the law of
(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
.
Then
\tilde{\pi}
has
t_{0}
- and
t_{2}
-marginals
\mu^{0}
and
\mu^{{\mathcal{T}}}
. Moreover, (
8
) and
s\leq 1/r
imply that
t_{1}-t_{0}\geq\varepsilon
and
t_{2}-t_{1}\geq\Delta-\varepsilon-\frac{1}{r}>0
, so both traveling times are positive. The condition
\varepsilon+1/r<\Delta
means
\epsilon
plus the maximal delay
1/r
still fits inside the available travel-time budget
\Delta
. Here,
t_{1}
is the crossing time at intermediate node. The constant
\varepsilon
is a fixed transit delay after departure, before any mass can reach the node. The extra waiting time
s
, sampled uniformly on an interval of length
1/r
, spreads each departure cohort over a window of width
1/r
so that the flow respects the capacity bound
r
.

Finally, conditional on
t_{0}
, the variable
t_{1}
is uniform on an interval of length
1/r
. Hence, for any Borel set
S\subset[0,t_{f}]
, we have
\mathbb{P}(t_{1}\in S\mid t_{0})\leq r\,|S|
since a uniform law on an interval of length
1/r
has density
r
.
Taking expectations yields to
\sigma^{1}(S)=\mathbb{P}(t_{1}\in S)\leq r\,|S|
, which is equivalent to
\sigma^{1}\leq r\,dt_{1}
. Therefore, there always exists a
\tilde{\pi}
so that
\tilde{\pi}\in\Pi
, and
\Pi
is non-empty.
∎

We are now in a position to establish the uniqueness of the optimizer for Problem
1
. For simplicity, we omit the superscript and adopt the notation
\sigma^{1}\equiv\sigma
for the remainder of this section.

Lemma 2

(
Monotonicity
).

Consider the cost function

c_{t_{0},t_{1}}:=\frac{w(v_{0},v_{1})}{t_{1}-t_{0}},

where
(t_{0},t_{1})\in\mathcal{M}_{+}\big([0,t_{f}]^{2}\big)
, and let
\mu^{0}
and
\sigma
be measures supported on
[0,t_{f}]
. Assume that
\sigma(t)\,dt
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Then, the minimizer of the Kantorovich problem

\min_{\pi}\int_{t_{0}}\int_{t_{1}}c_{t_{0},t_{1}}\,\pi(dt_{0},dt_{1}),

where
\pi
represents a coupling of the marginals
\mu^{0}(dt_{0})
and
\sigma(t_{1})\,dt_{1}
, is unique and supported on the graph of a non-increasing function
T^{\mathcal{D}}(t_{1})
.

Proof.

We first observe that for any two pairs
(t_{1},t^{\prime}_{1})
and
(t_{0},t^{\prime}_{0})
satisfying

t_{f}>t_{1}>t^{\prime}_{1}>t_{0}>t^{\prime}_{0}>0
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{w(v_{0},v_{1})}{t_{1}-t_{0}}+\frac{w(v_{0},v_{1})}{t^{\prime}_{1}-t^{\prime}_{0}}>\frac{w(v_{0},v_{1})}{t^{\prime}_{1}-t_{0}}+\frac{w(v_{0},v_{1})}{t_{1}-t^{\prime}_{0}}.

The ordering in this inequality characterizes
c_{t_{0},t_{1}}
as being quasi-monotone, in the terminology of
[
5
]
, see also
[
39
, Section 3.1]
.

It follows from
[
46
, Theorem 2.18 and Remark 2.19]
that the optimal coupling
\pi
exists and is given by the monotone rearrangement of
\mu^{0}
and
\sigma
. That is, for a suitable function
T^{\mathcal{D}}(t_{1})
, the optimal transport plan satisfies
\int_{0}^{T^{\mathcal{D}}(t_{1})}\mu^{0}(dt_{0})=\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\sigma(s)\,ds
.
Since
T^{\mathcal{D}}(t_{1})<t_{1}
, it follows that
T^{\mathcal{D}}(t_{1})
is non-increasing (and remains constant over time intervals corresponding to Dirac components of
\mu^{0}
, and also, over intervals where
\sigma
carries no mass).
∎

In the same spirit, the cost
c_{t_{1},t_{2}}
with
(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{+}([0,t_{f}]^{2})
is quasi-monotone. Consequently, for a suitable function
T^{\mathcal{A}}(t_{1})
, the optimal transport plan satisfies

\int_{0}^{T^{\mathcal{A}}(t_{1})}\mu^{\mathcal{T}}(dt_{2})=\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\sigma(s)\,ds

for
T^{\mathcal{A}}(t_{1})>t_{1}
, so that
T^{\mathcal{A}}(t_{1})
is non-increasing.

With the monotonicity of the push-forward maps
T^{\mathcal{D}}(t_{1})
and
T^{\mathcal{A}}(t_{1})
established, we now present the following uniqueness result.

Theorem 1

(
Uniqueness
).

Under the assumptions of Problem
1
, the minimizer is unique. Moreover, there exist functions
T^{\mathcal{D}}(t_{1})
and
T^{\mathcal{A}}(t_{1})
that are monotonically non-increasing such that
\pi=\big(T^{\mathcal{D}},{\rm Id,T^{\mathcal{A}}}\big)_{\sharp}\sigma
,
where
{\rm Id}(t_{1})=t_{1}
is the identity map, and
\sigma(t_{1})\,dt_{1}
is an absolutely continuous measure on
[0,t_{f}]
satisfying
\sigma(t_{1})\leq r
.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be a minimizer as established in Proposition
1
, and define
\pi_{t_{0},t_{1}}:=\int_{t_{2}}\pi
,
\pi_{t_{1},t_{2}}:=\int_{t_{0}}\pi
, and
\sigma:=\iint_{t_{0},t_{2}}\pi
. Since the objective function satisfies

\displaystyle\iiint_{t_{0},t_{1},t_{2}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!c\,\pi=\iint_{t_{0},t_{1}}\!\!\!\!\!c_{t_{0},t_{1}}\pi_{t_{0},t_{1}}+\iint_{t_{1},t_{2}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!c_{t_{1},t_{2}}\pi_{t_{1},t_{2}},

it follows that both
\pi_{t_{0},t_{1}}
and
\pi_{t_{1},t_{2}}
must be minimizers of their respective problems. Otherwise, there would exist alternative couplings
\hat{\pi}_{t_{0},t_{1}}
and
\hat{\pi}_{t_{1},t_{2}}
achieving strictly lower costs

\displaystyle\iint_{t_{0},t_{1}}c_{t_{0},t_{1}}\hat{\pi}_{t_{0},t_{1}}<\iint_{t_{0},t_{1}}c_{t_{0},t_{1}}\pi_{t_{0},t_{1}},\ \iint_{t_{1},t_{2}}c_{t_{1},t_{2}}\hat{\pi}_{t_{1},t_{2}}<\iint_{t_{1},t_{2}}c_{t_{1},t_{2}}\pi_{t_{1},t_{2}}.

Since both couplings share the same marginal on the
t_{1}
-axis, we have

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}\hat{\pi}_{t_{0},t_{1}}(dt_{0},dt_{1})=\int_{t_{2}}\hat{\pi}_{t_{1},t_{2}}(dt_{1},dt_{2})=\sigma(t_{1})\,dt_{1}.

By the gluing lemma
[
47
, page 11]
, there exists a coupling
\hat{\pi}
on
\mathcal{M}_{+}([0,t_{f}]^{3})
that preserves these marginals while achieving a strictly lower cost, contradicting optimality. Thus, both
\pi_{t_{0},t_{1}}
and
\pi_{t_{1},t_{2}}
must be optimal.

Next, we show that the relaxed marginal
\sigma
is unique, which establishes the desired result via Lemma
2
. Suppose there exist two distinct minimizers,
\pi^{a}
and
\pi^{b}
. Each induces a density
\sigma^{a}(t_{1})
and
\sigma^{b}(t_{1})
, respectively, on the
t_{1}
-axis, along with corresponding transport maps
(T^{\mathcal{D},a},T^{\mathcal{A},a})
and
(T^{\mathcal{D},b},T^{\mathcal{A},b})
. Since both
\sigma^{a}(t_{1})
and
\sigma^{b}(t_{1})
satisfy the constraint
\sigma(t_{1})\leq r
, their convex combination
\bar{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{a}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{b}

also remains feasible. Similarly, the convex combination

\bar{\pi}=\frac{1}{2}\pi^{a}+\frac{1}{2}\pi^{b}

defines an optimal coupling. However, the bi-marginal coupling

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{xt}=(T^{\mathcal{D},a},{\rm Id})_{\sharp}\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{a}+(T^{\mathcal{D},b},{\rm Id})_{\sharp}\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{b}

fails to be supported on the graph of a function, which implies that there exists a
cheaper
coupling with strictly lower cost, obtained by monotone rearrangement of
\bar{\pi}_{xt}
. Since
\bar{\pi}_{xt}
satisfies the marginal constraints, meaning

T^{\mathcal{D},a}_{\sharp}\sigma^{a}+T^{\mathcal{D},b}_{\sharp}\sigma^{b}=\mu^{0}
, the only way for
\bar{\pi}_{xt}
to be supported on the graph of a function is if
T^{\mathcal{D}}
is unique, i.e.,
T^{\mathcal{D}}=T^{\mathcal{D},a}=T^{\mathcal{D},b}
.

A similar argument applies to
\bar{\pi}_{yt}
, ensuring that
T^{\mathcal{A}}
is also unique. If this were not the case, a more efficient coupling could be constructed through monotone rearrangement, contradicting optimality, and thus completes our proof.
∎

4
Coupled DA constraints: formulation and uniqueness

In this section, we turn to the second regime, where departures and arrivals are no longer specified independently but are tied together through a joint departure–arrival law. On the same line graph as in Section
3
, each particle now carries a prescribed pair
(t_{0},t_{{\mathcal{T}}})
describing its release and delivery times, so that the DA constraint fixes which departures must match which arrivals. The scheduling problem is to insert crossing times at the intermediate nodes that respect these joint DA specifications while satisfying nodal capacity limits. This leads naturally to an unequal-dimensional OT formulation, in which the two-dimensional DA distribution is mapped to one-dimensional crossing-time profiles along the path. We now formally define the coupled DA constraint as follows.

Definition 4

(
Coupled DA constraint
).

A coupled DA constraint is a probability measure
\mu^{0,{\mathcal{T}}}
defined on product space
\mathcal{M}_{+}([0,t_{f}]^{2})
. The measure
\mu^{0,{\mathcal{T}}}(dt_{0},dt_{\mathcal{T}})
specifies the joint probability that mass departing at time
t_{0}
arrives at time
t_{\mathcal{T}}
.

The coupled DA constraint captures the dependency between departure and arrival times, establishing a structured relationship that governs the temporal dynamics of the transport process, see Fig.
2
.

Figure 2:
Illustration of the coupled DA constraint.
(a) When the joint probability measure
\mu^{0,{\mathcal{T}}}
follows a Monge map, it is supported on the graph of a function, ensuring that each particle is assigned a unique DA time pair. This enforces the assumption that particles departing earlier also arrive earlier.
(b) When
\mu^{0,{\mathcal{T}}}
is not constrained to a function’s graph, the DA relationship is relaxed. Instead of a fixed pair, the arrival time can vary within an interval, introducing flexibility in transport dynamics.

4.1
Single intermediate node with coupled DA

Adopting the same assumptions as in Problem
1
, we consider the case of a single toll. Under this setting, the problem can be formulated as follows.

The generalized form of Problem

4.2
Unequal-dimensional OT for uniqueness

Before further analyzing the properties of Problem
2
, we formally introduce unequal-dimensional OT, where the optimal transportation plan extends to probability measures with different dimensional supports, i.e.,
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},
with
m\neq n.
Previous works
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
have investigated the existence, uniqueness, and smoothness of the optimal map in this setting. To establish the uniqueness of the optimal transport map in the unequal-dimensional setting, we introduce three key concepts that characterize the structure of optimal solutions.

The non-degeneracy condition ensures that the cost function is sufficiently regular to yield meaningful transport maps, preventing degenerate solutions.

Definition 5

(
Non-degeneracy condition

[
12
]
).

A cost function
c(x,y)
satisfies the non-degeneracy condition if

\text{rank}\left(D^{2}_{xy}c(x,y)\right)=\min\{m,n\}
,
where
n
and
m
denote the dimensions of
x
and
y
, respectively.

We need conditions that characterize transport plans concentrated on deterministic mappings.

Definition 6

(
Pure matching

[
10
]
).

A stable matching is said to be pure if the transport plan
T
is concentrated on the graph of a function
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
. This implies that almost all agents of type
x
are matched exclusively with a single agent of type
y=T(x)
.

A weaker form of the twist condition is also proposed for the unequal-dimensional case, ensuring injectivity from the higher-dimensional space to the lower one without full bijectivity.

Definition 7

(
x
-twist condition

[
12
]
).

The
x
-twist condition states that, for fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the gradient of the cost function w.r.t.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n>m
),
\nabla_{x}c(x,y)
, is injective in
x
, i.e.,
y\mapsto\nabla_{x}c(x,y)

is injective for fixed
y
.

We are now in a position to establish the uniqueness of the transport plan, which maps the coupled DA constraint to a unique crossing time.

Proposition 2

(
Purity and uniqueness
).

If the cost function
c(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})
satisfies both the non-degeneracy and twist condition, then the minimizer of Problem
2
is unique. Moreover, there exist unique functions
T
such that

\pi=({\rm Id},T)_{\sharp}\mu^{0,{\mathcal{T}}}
,
where
{\rm Id}_{\sharp}\mu^{0,{\mathcal{T}}}=\mu^{0,{\mathcal{T}}}
is the identity map, and
T_{\sharp}\mu^{0,{\mathcal{T}}}=\sigma
, with
\sigma(t_{1})
an absolutely continuous measure on
[0,t_{f}]
with
\sigma(t_{1})\leq r
.

Proof.

We begin by verifying the non-degeneracy condition of the cost functional (
11a
) in Problem
2
,
and introduce the notation
t_{da}
for departure–arrival time pair
t_{da}:=(t_{0},t_{2})
. The second-order mixed partial derivative matrix of the cost function is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{t_{da}t_{1}}c(t_{da},t_{1})=\begin{bmatrix}\partial^{2}c/\partial t_{0}\partial t_{1}\\[5.78172pt]
\partial^{2}c/\partial t_{2}\partial t_{1}\end{bmatrix}=2\begin{bmatrix}w(v_{0},v_{1})(t_{1}-t_{0})^{-3}\\[5.78172pt]
w(v_{1},v_{2})(t_{2}-t_{1})^{-3}\end{bmatrix}.

Since this matrix has rank 1, the non-degeneracy condition is satisfied.

Next, we establish that the
x
-twist condition holds. The gradient
\nabla_{t_{da}}c(t_{da},t_{1})
is

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}c(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\partial c/\partial t_{0}\\[5.78172pt]
\partial c/\partial t_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-w(v_{0},v_{1})(t_{1}-t_{0})^{-2}\\[5.78172pt]
\phantom{-}w(v_{1},v_{2})(t_{2}-t_{1})^{-2}\end{bmatrix}.

For any
t_{0}<t_{1}\neq t^{\prime}_{1}<t_{2}
, we compute the difference

\displaystyle\nabla_{t_{da}}c(t_{da},t_{1})-\nabla_{t_{da}}c(t_{da},t^{\prime}_{1})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{(t^{\prime}_{1}-t_{1})(t^{\prime}_{1}+t_{1}-2t_{0})}{(t_{1}-t_{0})^{2}(t_{1}-t_{0})^{2}}\\[5.78172pt]
\frac{(t^{\prime}_{1}-t_{1})(2t_{2}-t^{\prime}_{1}-t_{1})}{(t^{\prime}_{1}-t_{2})^{2}(t_{1}-t_{2})^{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
t_{0}<t_{1},t^{\prime}_{1}<t_{2}
, it follows that

\nabla_{t_{da}}c(t_{da},t_{1})\neq\nabla_{t_{da}}c(t_{da},t^{\prime}_{1})\quad\text{for every }t_{1}\neq t^{\prime}_{1}
.
Thus, the
x
-twist condition holds, ensuring that the mapping
t_{da}:=(t_{0},t_{2})\mapsto\nabla_{t_{da}}c(t_{da},t_{1})
is injective for each fixed
t_{1}
. By Definition
6
, the transport plan is supported on the graph of a function, implying uniqueness of the stable matching as in
[
10
, Corollary 4]
. The uniqueness of the relaxed marginal
\sigma(t_{1})
follows from Theorems
1
and
2
, and we omit repetition.
∎

(

When the

for further discussion.

4.3
Multi-node line graph with coupled DA

We now pass from the single-node setting of Problem

\mathbf{p}=\{v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{{\mathcal{T}}-1},v_{{\mathcal{T}}}\}

Problem
\mathbf{2}^{\prime}

Given a coupled departure–arrival law

Theorem 4

(
Purity and uniqueness

Assume that the path cost

such that

and the induced crossing-time marginals

Proof.

Introduce
t_{da}=(t_{0},t_{\mathcal{T}})
and
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{{\mathcal{T}}-1})

For the

\displaystyle\nabla_{t_{da}}c^{(\mathbf{p})}(t_{da},\mathbf{t})=\begin{bmatrix}-w(v_{0},v_{1})(t_{1}-t_{0})^{-2}\\[5.78172pt]

11

12

5
Multi-path generalization and optimization methodology

We recall that
{\mathcal{V}}^{-}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{+}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
denote the sets of source and sink nodes, and that
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{V}}^{-},{\mathcal{V}}^{+})
is a collection of admissible paths from sources to sinks.
For a given node
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
, we write
{\mathcal{P}}(v)
for the subsets of paths in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{V}}^{-},{\mathcal{V}}^{+})
that contain the node
v
.
The general form of Problem
1
is then expressed in terms of a path-wise transportation plan
\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}\big([0,t_{f}]^{n_{\mathbf{p}}}\big)
: for each admissible path (line graph)
{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{V}}^{-},{\mathcal{V}}^{+})
, the joint coupling measure
\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}
describes the evolution of mass across the
n_{\mathbf{p}}
vertices along
{\mathbf{p}}
, and the objective is to minimize the total transportation cost over all commodities and all paths.
The problem is thus formulated as follows.

Problem 3

(
Path-wise DA-constrained transport
).

Given probability measures
\mu^{0}(t_{0},v^{-}_{i})
for
v^{-}_{i}\in{\mathcal{V}}^{-}
and
\mu^{\mathcal{T}}(t_{\mathcal{T}},v^{+}_{j})
for
v^{+}_{j}\in{\mathcal{V}}^{+}
, and flow-rate bounds
r(t_{\ell},v_{\ell})>0
at every intermediate node
v_{\ell}
. We want to determine
\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{+}([0,t_{f}]^{n_{\mathbf{p}}})
for all
{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{V}}^{-},{\mathcal{V}}^{+})
that minimizes

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{V}}^{-},{\mathcal{V}}^{+})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\big\langle c^{({\mathbf{p}})},\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\big\rangle:=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{V}}^{-},{\mathcal{V}}^{+})}\int c^{({\mathbf{p}})}\,d\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})},

subject to the departure and arrival marginals

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(v^{-}_{i})}P_{v^{-}_{i}}\big(\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\big)=\mu^{0}(t_{0},v^{-}_{i}),\quad v^{-}_{i}\in{\mathcal{V}}^{-}

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(v^{+}_{j})}P_{v^{+}_{j}}\big(\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\big)=\mu^{\mathcal{T}}(t_{\mathcal{T}},v^{+}_{j})\quad v^{+}_{j}\in{\mathcal{V}}^{+},

and the flow rate capacity constraints

\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(v_{\ell})}P_{v_{\ell}}\big(\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\big)\;\leq\;r(t_{\ell},v_{\ell})\,dt_{\ell},\quad\mbox{ for }v_{\ell}\in{\mathcal{V}},

(13)

where
P_{v}:\mathcal{M}_{+}\big([0,t_{f}]^{n_{\mathbf{p}}}\big)\to\mathcal{M}_{+}([0,t_{f}])
denotes the projection onto the marginal associated with node
v
.

A motivation for this formulation is that given an initial and final destination, there are typically a limited number of effective paths. However, for a single path with
n
nodes, the size of the corresponding transportation map
\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}
grows exponentially as the number of temporal marginals at each node increases. To address this challenge, we develop methods based on multimarginal optimal transport. In Section
5.1
, we review multi-marginal entropic regularization and its Sinkhorn implementation. In Section
5.2
, we adapt this framework to a path-wise Sinkhorn algorithm tailored to DA-constrained transport on networks.

5.1
Multi-marginal Sinkhorn algorithm, recap

For the MMOT problem, the discretized formulation with objective functional in (
3
) and admissible set (
4
) yields a large-scale linear program. In the multi-marginal setting, the computational cost typically grows exponentially with the number of marginals, so direct solvers quickly become impractical. The situation improves substantially when the cost has a graphical structure, for instance, when it decomposes into pairwise terms,

c(x_{0},\dots,x_{\mathcal{T}})=c(x_{0},x_{1})+c(x_{1},x_{2})+\dotsb+c(x_{{\mathcal{T}}-1},x_{\mathcal{T}}).

In such cases, entropic regularization combined with Sinkhorn-type scaling has become a standard tool for large-scale MMOT. Recent work shows that tensor factorization can further accelerate these methods by sharing structure across marginals and removing redundant projections within each iteration, see e.g.
[
19
,
24
,
25
]
for details and applications.

By adding an entropic penalty to the original problem, the regularized version can be solved using an alternating minimization scheme
[
38
]
. The key idea of entropic regularized OT, is to introduce the entropic penalty term of
\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{0}\times\cdots\times n_{\mathcal{T}}}
defined as

\displaystyle D(\pi):=-\sum_{i_{0},\dots,i_{\mathcal{T}}}\pi_{i_{0},\dots,i_{\mathcal{T}}}\Big(\log\pi_{i_{0},\dots,i_{\mathcal{T}}}-1\Big),

(14)

with the convention
0\log(0)=0
. With a regularization parameter
\epsilon
to penalize the entropic term, the entropic regularization problem can be written as

\displaystyle\pi_{\epsilon}^{\star}:=\argmin_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu^{0},\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{\mathcal{T}})}\ \langle c,\pi\rangle-\epsilon D(\pi).

(15)

The unique solution
\pi^{\star}
of (
15
) converges to the optimal solution with maximal entropy within the set of all optimal solutions
\pi\in\Pi
, see
[
38
, Proposition 4.1]
.

To obtain an iterative updating scheme for finding the optimizer of
\pi^{\star}_{\epsilon}
, we derive the Lagrangian

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\pi,\lambda^{0,\dots,{\mathcal{T}}})=\langle c,\pi\rangle-\epsilon D(\pi)+\sum_{\ell=0}^{{\mathcal{T}}}\lambda^{\ell}\left(P_{\ell}(\pi)-\mu^{\ell}\right),

where, for each
\ell
, the marginal
\mu^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{\ell}}
and Lagrange dual variable
\lambda^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}}
for the discretized marginal constraint
P_{\ell}(\pi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{\ell}}
. Then, by taking the first-order optimality condition, we have

\displaystyle\partial{\mathcal{L}}(\pi,\lambda^{0},\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{\mathcal{T}})/\partial\pi=0,

\displaystyle\partial{\mathcal{L}}(\pi,\lambda^{0},\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{\mathcal{T}})/\partial\lambda^{\ell}=0,

which gives that the optimizer
\pi
has a closed-form expression

\pi_{i_{0},\dots,i_{\mathcal{T}}}=\exp(-c_{i_{0},\dots,i_{\mathcal{T}}}/\epsilon)\cdot\prod_{\ell=0}^{{\mathcal{T}}}\exp(-\lambda_{i_{\ell}}^{\ell}/\epsilon).

By introducing the notation
{\mathbf{K}}:=\exp(-c/\epsilon)
and
{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}:=\exp(-\lambda_{\ell}/\epsilon)
, the optimizer can be written in the more compact form
3
3

3

In the language of Schrödinger bridge problem, where only the entropic term is considered, taking the limit
\epsilon\to+\infty
reduces the problem to maximum likelihood estimation. In this setting, the reference measure
{\mathbf{K}}
acts as the prior, while the solution
\pi
corresponds to the posterior.

\displaystyle\pi={\mathbf{K}}\odot\left({\mathbf{u}}_{0}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\dots\otimes{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)

(16)

with
\odot
the element-wise product and
\otimes
as the outer product. The dual variables
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\mathcal{T}}
have to satisfy the marginal constraints, which follow after we derive the dual by substituting (
16
) into the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\argmax_{\lambda^{0},\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{\mathcal{T}}}-\epsilon{\mathbf{K}}\odot\left({\mathbf{u}}_{0}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\dots\otimes{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathcal{T}}\right)+\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell},\mu^{\ell}\rangle.

The updating strategy thus initializes
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}={\mathbbm{1}}_{n_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{0}}
,
and then for
\ell=0:{\mathcal{T}}
, we iteratively compute

{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}\longleftarrow\mu^{\ell}\oslash P_{\ell}(\pi)

(17)

until convergence, where
\oslash
denotes element-wise division. The above updating strategy is known as the Sinkhorn algorithm
[
14
,
38
,
43
]
, which also can be viewed as matrix scaling, by iteratively modifying
{\mathbf{u}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathcal{T}}
to satisfy the marginal constraint. In addition, for the relaxed marginal, i.e.,
P_{\ell}(\pi)\leq\mu^{\ell}
, the updates are

{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}\longleftarrow\max\Big\{\mu^{\ell}\oslash P_{\ell}(\pi),\;\mathbf{1}\Big\}.

This approach is analogous to the standard Sinkhorn method, and thus inherits a linear convergence rate
[
26
]
.

5.2
Path-wise Sinkhorn algorithm

We next derive a Sinkhorn-type scaling scheme for the path-wise formulation in Problem
3
. Each admissible path
{\mathbf{p}}
carries its own coupling
\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}
over the crossing times of the nodes it visits, while the source and sink marginals and interior-node capacities couple
across
paths through aggregated projections. Entropic regularization yields a multiplicative structure and simple alternating matrix rescaling, in the spirit of the multi-marginal case, with the difference that each interior node shares one multiplier wherever it appears.

In practice it is often unnecessary to work with the full all-path formulation. Instead, one may restrict attention to a prescribed family of admissible paths and enlarge it only when needed, in the spirit of column generation for large-scale linear programs
[
21
,
35
]
. In network flow models with path variables, such enrichment is typically guided by a shortest-path-type pricing subproblem under dual weights
[
20
,
34
]
.

For a discrete path plan
\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{0}\times\cdots\times n_{n_{\mathbf{p}}}\times n_{{\mathcal{T}}}}
, we consider the path-wise entropy
D(\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})})
defined in (
14
), and add it to the cost functional in Problem
3
, yielding its entropically regularized formulation. By introducing dual variables
\alpha_{v^{-}}(t_{0})
,
\beta_{v^{+}}(t_{{\mathcal{T}}})
, and
\gamma_{v}(t_{\ell})\geq 0
corresponding to the source node, sink node, and flow rate constraints, respectively, the Lagrangian can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\{\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\},

\displaystyle\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}}\Big\langle c^{({\mathbf{p}})}+\varepsilon\big(\log\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}-1\big),\,\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\Big\rangle\hskip-0.4pt-\sum_{v^{-}\in{\mathcal{V}}^{-}}\Big\langle\alpha_{v^{-}},\!\!\!\!\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(v^{-})}P_{v^{-}}\big(\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\big)-\mu^{0}(\cdot,v^{-})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle-\sum_{v^{+}\in{\mathcal{V}}^{+}}\Big\langle\beta_{v^{+}},\!\!\!\!\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(v^{+})}P_{v^{+}}\big(\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\big)-\mu^{{\mathcal{T}}}(\cdot,v^{+})\Big\rangle-\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\Big\langle\gamma_{v},\!\!\!\!\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(v)}P_{v}\big(\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\big)-r(\cdot,v)\Big\rangle,

where
d\mathbf{t}
denotes integration over
d\mathbf{t}:=(t_{0},\{t_{\ell}:\ v_{\ell}\in{\mathbf{p}}\},t_{{\mathcal{T}}})
and
P_{0}

P_{{\mathcal{T}}}

are the marginal operators extracting the
t_{0}

t_{{\mathcal{T}}}
,
t_{\ell}

\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}

\displaystyle 0=c^{({\mathbf{p}})}+\varepsilon\log\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\!-\!\alpha_{v^{-}}(t_{0})\!-\!\!\sum_{v_{\ell}\in{\mathbf{p}}}\gamma_{\ell}(t_{\ell})\!-\!\beta_{v^{+}}(t_{{\mathcal{T}}}).

Hence, the path-wise optimizer admits a closed-form solution in the form

\displaystyle\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}(t_{0},\{t_{\ell}\},t_{{\mathcal{T}}})=\exp\!\left(\frac{\alpha_{v^{-}({\mathbf{p}})}(t_{0})+\sum_{v_{\ell}\in{\mathbf{p}}}\gamma_{\ell}(t_{\ell})+\beta_{v^{+}({\mathbf{p}})}(t_{{\mathcal{T}}})-c^{({\mathbf{p}})}}{\varepsilon}\right).

Next, define the Gibbs kernel and multiplicative scalings

{\mathbf{K}}^{({\mathbf{p}})}(t_{0},\{t_{\ell}\},t_{{\mathcal{T}}}):=\exp\!\big(-c^{({\mathbf{p}})}(t_{0},\{t_{\ell}\},t_{{\mathcal{T}}})/\varepsilon\big)
, together with

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}_{v^{-}}\!\!:=\exp\!\big(\frac{\alpha_{v^{-}}}{\varepsilon}\big),{\mathbf{w}}_{v}:=\exp\!\big(\frac{\gamma_{v}}{\varepsilon}\big),\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{v}}_{v^{+}}:=\exp\!\big(\frac{\beta_{v^{+}}}{\varepsilon}\big).

Then the path-wise plan may be factorized as (cf.

[
19

\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}={\mathbf{K}}^{({\mathbf{p}})}\;{\mathbf{u}}_{v^{-}}(t_{0})\;\Big(\prod_{v\in{\mathbf{p}}}{\mathbf{w}}_{v}(t)\Big)\;{\mathbf{v}}_{v^{+}}(t_{{\mathcal{T}}}).

(18)

Fix a path
{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle A^{({\mathbf{p}})}_{v^{-}}(t_{0}):=\int_{\{t_{\ell}\},t_{\mathcal{T}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\mathbf{K}}^{({\mathbf{p}})}\Big(\prod_{v_{\ell}\in{\mathbf{p}}}{\mathbf{w}}_{v_{\ell}}(t_{\ell})\Big){\mathbf{v}}_{v^{+}}(t_{{\mathcal{T}}}),

\displaystyle A^{({\mathbf{p}})}_{v^{+}}(t_{{\mathcal{T}}}):=\int_{t_{0},\{t_{\ell}\}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\mathbf{K}}^{({\mathbf{p}})}{\mathbf{u}}_{v^{-}}(t_{0})\Big(\prod_{v_{\ell}\in{\mathbf{p}}}{\mathbf{w}}_{v_{\ell}}(t_{\ell})\Big),

\displaystyle m_{v^{-}}(t_{0})

Theorem 5

(
Linear convergence
).

Algorithm
1
converges to the unique optimal plan at a linear rate.

Proof.

The entropic formulation is strictly convex in
\{\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\}
, hence the optimizer is unique and strong duality holds. Each multiplicative update in Algorithm

enforces one set of marginals or capacity constraints in closed-form, which is the exact maximizer of the dual over that coordinate block. The algorithm is therefore exact block coordinate ascent

Remark 2

(
Stopping criteria on Algorithm

).

For the stopping condition of Algorithm
1
, we monitor marginal violations and capacity violations, i.e.,

E_{0}=\sum_{i}\|m^{0}_{i}-\mu^{0}(\cdot,v^{-}_{i})\|_{1}

E_{{\mathcal{T}}}=\sum_{j}\|m^{{\mathcal{T}}}_{j}-\mu^{{\mathcal{T}}}(\cdot,v^{+}_{j})\|_{1}
, and

Algorithm

being the precision tolerance.

Remark 3

For the coupled DA case, where the boundary marginal is prescribed jointly in

m^{0,\mathcal{T}}(t_{0},t_{\mathcal{T}}):=\sum_{{\mathbf{p}}}\int\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}(t_{0},\{t_{\ell}\},t_{\mathcal{T}})\,d\{t_{\ell}\},

and set the boundary ratio
\Lambda(t_{0},t_{\mathcal{T}}):=\mu^{0,\mathcal{T}}(t_{0},t_{\mathcal{T}})\oslash m^{0,\mathcal{T}}(t_{0},t_{\mathcal{T}})
. The direct projection step is then applied path-wise via

\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}\;\leftarrow\;\Lambda\odot\pi^{({\mathbf{p}})}

for all
{\mathbf{p}}
, while the interior capacity projections remain unchanged. Similar computations of projections in multimarginal problems with joint marginals constraints were also considered in
[
23
,
25
]
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a comprehensive framework for OT over networks subject to DA constraints, extending classical formulations by incorporating temporal specifications and nodal flux limitations at sources and sinks. In particular, two types of DA constraints were considered: independent constraints, where departure and arrival rates are prescribed separately, and coupled constraints, where each particle’s transportation timespan is explicitly specified. We established feasibility, existence, and uniqueness in both settings. The independent DA formulation aligns naturally with a multi-marginal OT structure, while the coupled case gives rise to an unequal-dimensional OT problem, reflecting joint timing constraints. To address the arising optimization challenges from our problem, we began by analyzing transportation over line graphs, where we derived analytical conditions for feasibility and uniqueness based on monotonicity and the generalized Monge condition. We also introduced a node aggregation strategy for path reduction. We proposed efficient numerical solving for the resulting high-dimensional problem by considering its entropic regularization, and develop customized Sinkhorn-type algorithms for both the independent and coupled departure–arrival constricted setting. The scalable algorithm improved applicability for large-scale network optimization. Future work may include incorporating origin–destination (OD) structure together with DA profiles directly at the path level. It may also explore refined graph reductions where large networks are approximated by DAG skeletons that preserve path-wise timing, and extend the framework to richer physical constraints such as speed limits, buffer sizes, and fundamental-diagram-type flow bounds, aiming toward deployable scheduling tools for specified systems.
