Introduction

Medical education operates within an apprenticeship model where senior clinicians serve as teachers, assessors and gatekeepers to professional progression, meaning that their behaviour significantly influences students’ learning, well-being and career development.1 2 Bullying in medical training is commonly defined as repeated, harmful behaviour occurring within a power-imbalanced relationship and may include verbal abuse, humiliation, intimidation, exclusion, discrimination and unfair assessment.1 Globally, bullying and mistreatment of medical students remain highly prevalent, with systematic reviews reporting that approximately 59%–63% of medical students experience some form of mistreatment or bullying during their training.3 These behaviours may be overt, such as shouting, insults and public humiliation, or covert, such as exclusion from learning opportunities and biased evaluation, and are often embedded within hierarchical clinical environments characterised by strong differentials.3,11 Regionally, studies from low-income and middle-income countries report similar patterns of mistreatment, suggesting that bullying is a widespread phenomenon across different health systems rather than being context-specific.7 8 The consequences of bullying are well documented and include psychological distress (such as anxiety and depression), reduced academic engagement, impaired professional identity formation, diminished clinical confidence and in some cases changes in specialty choice or intentions to leave the profession.1 9 12 13 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that bullying is a pervasive issue in medical education with significant implications for both student well-being and the quality of healthcare training.

In South Africa, empirical evidence on bullying of medical students by clinicians is emerging but remains limited. Available studies indicate that mistreatment of medical students is highly prevalent within some medical schools, with reported rates as high as approximately 80% of students experiencing some form of bullying or mistreatment, including being ignored or excluded, verbal abuse, discrimination and offensive behaviour during clinical training.14 These behaviours are commonly reported to occur in clinical learning environments such as wards and during teaching rounds, and are often perpetrated by senior clinicians, including registrars and consultants.15,17 Despite these findings, the South African literature remains sparse and largely limited to single-institution studies, which restricts generalisability across medical schools and training contexts. Furthermore, under-reporting is a major concern, as many students do not formally report bullying due to fear of victimisation, perceived power imbalances or lack of awareness of reporting systems.14 This suggests that the actual prevalence may be higher than reported figures.

Given the hierarchical nature of medical training and the variability in how bullying is defined and reported across studies, there is a need to systematically map the available evidence on bullying of medical students by clinicians. Although existing literature has explored academic mistreatment and workplace bullying broadly, there is limited synthesised evidence focusing specifically on clinician-to-medical student bullying, particularly within the South African context. This lack of consolidated evidence restricts understanding of the scope, nature and contextual drivers of the problem, thereby limiting the development of targeted institutional policies and interventions to promote safe clinical learning environments. A scoping review is therefore justified as it allows for comprehensive mapping of heterogeneous literature, identification of key concepts, and clarification of evidence gaps.

Therefore, this scoping review will characterise the types and prevalence of bullying by medical consultants towards medical students; synthesise reported outcomes for students; and map contextual drivers and reported institutional responses. The results will inform educators, regulators and health system leaders seeking to create safer, more supportive clinical learning environments.

Study objective

This study aims to review and map existing literature related to the bullying of medical students by clinicians, in South African medical training contexts, including the forms, prevalence, contributing factors, consequences and how institutions respond.

Methods

The Joanna Briggs Institute manual for evidence synthesis will be followed in conducting this scoping review, and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR) standards will be followed in reporting.18 19 The review is expected to take 10 months to complete, commencing on 10 November 2025 and concluding on 10 September 2026. PROSPERO has registered this protocol (CRD420251179182). Data from qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods investigations will be analysed by the authors using a mixed-methods methodology. Additionally, the socioecological model (SEM) will serve as the foundation for the mapping and analysis of data from the included studies.20 SEM considers multiple levels of influence on behaviour, which is particularly relevant for understanding bullying in clinical education, where hierarchical structures and organisational culture play a major role.

Identification of relevant literature

A systematic and comprehensive search will be conducted to identify studies examining bullying of medical students by clinicians in South Africa. The review will focus on studies published in English to ensure accessibility and accurate interpretation. Multiple electronic databases will be searched, including PubMed, Cumulative Index to Nursing & Allied Health Literature, Scopus, Web of Science, Psych-Info and African Journals Online (AJOL), covering biomedical, educational, psychological and region-specific literature. Both controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH terms) and free-text keywords will be used, such as “medical student*,” “bullying,” “harassment,” “mistreatment,” “intimidation,” “clinician*,” “consultant*,” “supervisor*” and “South Africa.” Boolean operators (AND, OR), truncation and proximity searches will be applied to refine the results. Furthermore, the university librarian will be invited to assist with the literature search. A full and detailed search strategy for all databases, including keywords, MeSH terms, Boolean operators and limits applied, is provided in online supplemental document 1.

Study selection

Following the identification of relevant studies, the screening and selection process will be conducted in two stages to ensure methodological rigour and transparency. First, all titles and abstracts will be independently screened by two reviewers against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria based on the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework illustrated in table 1. Studies that clearly do not meet the criteria will be excluded at this stage. Second, the full texts of potentially eligible studies will be retrieved and independently assessed by the same reviewers to determine final eligibility. Discrepancies between reviewers at both stages will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be documented. The PRISMA-ScR flow diagram will be used to illustrate the study selection process, including the number of studies identified, screened, excluded and included in the review19 (see figure 1).

Data extraction

Data from the studies included will be systematically extracted using a predesigned data extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel. The form will capture key information including author(s), year of publication, study location, study design and methodology, participant characteristics (eg, year of study, gender, medical school), type and forms of bullying, contributing factors, consequences for students and any interventions or institutional responses reported. The data extraction process will be piloted on a small sample of studies to ensure consistency and relevance, and adjustments will be made as necessary. Two independent reviewers will extract the data, and discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. Additionally, extracted data will be mapped onto the SEM illustrated in table 2 to categorise findings across individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy levels.20

Data analysis and reporting

Data from the included studies will be analysed using a narrative synthesis approach, which is appropriate for integrating diverse qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods evidence. Extracted data will first be organised and categorised according to the five domains of the SEM: individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy levels, to systematically identify factors contributing to bullying, its consequences, and existing interventions or responses.

Quantitative data (eg, prevalence rates or frequency of bullying) will be summarised using descriptive statistics, where applicable. For qualitative findings, a thematic analysis will be conducted following the six-phase approach outlined by Braun and Clarke.21 This process will involve:

familiarisation with the data through repeated reading of extracted findings;

familiarisation with the data through repeated reading of extracted findings;

defining and naming themes; and

producing a structured narrative synthesis of the findings.

producing a structured narrative synthesis of the findings.

The coding process will be conducted independently by at least two reviewers to enhance rigour and reduce bias. Any discrepancies in coding or theme development will be resolved through discussion, and where necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Qualitative data management and coding will be facilitated using software such as NVivo V.12, enabling systematic organisation, retrieval and comparison of coded data across studies.

The themes and subthemes generated from the thematic analysis, together with quantitative summaries (such as prevalence and frequency data) and their categorisation within the SEM framework, will be presented in tables, charts and figures to clearly illustrate patterns and trends across the included studies. Illustrative quotes from the qualitative data will also be included to enrich the interpretation of findings. The review will further identify gaps in the existing literature to inform future research priorities. All results will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and completeness in reporting.

Stakeholder consultation

While no direct stakeholder consultation will be conducted in this scoping review, the findings will be used to inform relevant stakeholders, including medical educators, clinicians, student representatives and policymakers.

Patient and public involvement

The development of this scoping review protocol did not involve input from patients or the public, and they will not be involved in its conduct.

Operational definition of outcomes

In this review protocol, the outcomes will refer to the reported forms of bullying experienced by medical students, the prevalence and patterns of bullying where available, the identified perpetrators (eg, clinicians, supervisors, consultants), the contexts or settings in which bullying occurs (eg, clinical wards or teaching hospitals) and the reported consequences on medical students, such as psychological distress, burnout and academic impact.
