Integrating interval jump rope exercise into a school setting improve body composition, cardiorespiratory fitness and glycolipid metabolism parameters in adolescents with overweight and obesity: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Shitong Shao, Meng Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood obesity is closely linked to the development of cardiovascular risk factors and other impairments. Jump rope exercise (JRE) is a simple and efficient mode of exercise that can be easily implemented in schools, effectively enhancing the physical fitness of adolescents. However, studies examining the effectiveness of JRE to improve the health of adolescents with obesity are scarce. This study examined the effects of a 12-week interval JRE integrating into physical education (PE) class on body composition, cardiorespiratory fitness, and glycolipid metabolism parameters in adolescents with overweight/obesity.MethodsIn this randomized control trial (RCT), adolescents aged 10–13 years with overweight/obesity (age = 12.4 ± 0.6 years, BMI = 24.7 ± 1.1 kg/m2) were recruited. Participants were randomized to JRE with short work time (JRE-1; n = 40), JRE with long work time (JRE-2; n = 40), or no-training control (CON; n = 40). JRE groups were performed three weekly exercise sessions over 12 weeks. All participants had indices of body composition, cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) and glycolipid metabolism parameters measured at pre- and post-intervention. Repeated measures of two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to evaluate interaction, between-group, and within-group differences, respectively.ResultsFollowing the school-based training program, significant improvements in body mass (BM), body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage (BFP), and CRF in both exercise training groups (P < 0.05). Also, the fasting blood glucose (FBG), fasting insulin (FINs), and the triglycerides (TG) improved significantly (P < 0.05). The low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) decreased only in JRE-2 group (− 2.20 mmol/L, P < 0.05). BM and BMI decreased more in the JRE-2 group when compared with the JRE-1 group (MD: −1.92 kg vs. −0.72 kg/m2, P < 0.01). No significant group differences were observed on cardiorespiratory fitness and glycolipid metabolism variables between intervention groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionsIntegrating interval jump rope exercise into PE classes has effectively improved health-related outcomes for adolescents with excessive body weight. Further research is needed to explore factors influencing the effectiveness of these interventions, the scalability of exercise programs for treating childhood obesity, and to inform health policy.Trial registrationThis trial was registered on the Chinese clinical trial registry (ChiCTR2100048737) on July 15, 2021. Introduction

Childhood obesity is a critical global health crisis requiring urgent attention [1]. Obesity incidence rate has been rising consistently, which is concerning as these years are crucial for establishing lifelong healthy habits. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), over 150 million children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 were living with obesity by 2020, and this number could exceed 250 million by 2030 without decisive action [2]. Childhood obesity negatively impacts growth and development (e.g., physical, emotional, psychosocial), increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) in adulthood and enhancing the social burden [3]. The primary method to reduce the incidence of obesity among adolescents is to increase the levels of physical activity (PA). Current recommendations recommend that children and adolescents should engage in an average of 60 min of moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) per day to enhance their health benefits [4]. Unfortunately, over 80% of youth do not meet the PA guideline [5]. Factors such as access to exercise facilities, available equipment, and limited time or space significantly impact the PA levels of adolescents. Therefore, it is important to explore and develop simple and practical exercise programs for youth to encourage routine PA and its associated health benefits.

Exercise training can enhance cardiovascular fitness and muscle strength, providing protective benefits for the heart [6]. Furthermore, exercise serves as an effective method for reducing chronic low-grade inflammation [7]. Among them, jump rope exercise (JRE) is recognized by the American Heart Association as an effective method to promote physical health for people of all ages [8]. JRE characterized by high engagement and broad accessibility, effectively enhances adolescents’ adherence to physical activity. As a low-cost, moderate-to-vigorous aerobic modality, it requires minimal space and time investment, rendering it a safe and equitable option across diverse socioeconomic contexts [9]. Given its negligible spatial and equipment requirements, JRE emerges as a particularly suitable intervention for improving exercise compliance—especially among obese adolescents. Several studies have reported the efficacy of JRE on various health outcomes. Kim et al. established that JRE was an effective intervention for improving the body composition and reducing adiponectin levels of obese male adolescents [10]. Another study by Eskandari et al. demonstrated that dark chocolate supplementation potentiated JRE-induced improvements in body composition and inflammatory factors in obese adolescent boys [11]. Two recent meta-analyses have also demonstrated the promoting effect of JRE on the physical fitness of healthy adolescents [8, 9]. Recently, the use of JRE in physical education (PE) classes and recess activities has been on the rise [12].

High-intensity interval training (HIIT) has been widely proven to be an effective exercise strategy and popularity globally for weight management in obese populations and consistently featured among the top fitness trends in 2025 [13]. The JRE program for children and adolescents currently involves long-term, moderate-intensity training (60–90 jump/min and more than 30 min). However, recent studies in obese adults showed that HIIT-based JRE (100–120 jump/min or ≥ 80% HRmax) is more effective for improving body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), while also being more time-efficient for adults with obesity [14, 15]. Schools provide an ideal environment for PA interventions, as adolescents spend much of their time there. Implementing positive activities over several weeks can significantly enhance children’s physical health, fitness, and metabolic function. Thus, integrating exercise programs into physical education classes is crucial. A JRE program can be readily implemented in schools, with a positive impact on students’ PA levels [16]. Additionally, JRE may be equally or more effective than other training methods (such as running, cycling, etc.) at enhancing CRF and aerobic capacity [17]. School-based intervention is required that trial low-cost, accessible JRE protocols over the long-term. In this context, the use of HIIT-based JRE integrating into PE class and more suitable for school is an appealing option.

This study aimed to examine the effects of 12 weeks of HIIT-based JRE intervention integrated into school physical education (PE) lesson on body composition, cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) and glycolipid metabolism parameters in adolescents with excessive body weight. The primary outcome was to examined the effects of JRE on body composition and CRF between exercise and control (CON) conditions. Secondary outcomes included glucose homeostasis and blood lipid profiles. Therefore, it is hypothesized that a 12-week JRE could positively impact both body composition and CRF in adolescents with overweight/obesity.

Methods

Sample size

The primary outcome variable was maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max). Sample size was calculated by considering a type I error rate of 5% and a power of 80% based on the results of Racil et al. [18], which led to a minimum same sample size of 12 subjects in each group, considering the 20% of dropout rate, a total of 45 participants were deemed necessary to ensure sufficient statistical power. Ultimately, we successfully recruited 120 participants for this study.

Study Design

This study employed a single-blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT) design. The study flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 120 overweight and obese adolescents according to the World Health Organization (WHO) growth standard to estimate BMI percentiles were used to categorize as either overweight (85th–95th percentile) or obesity (≥ 95th percentile) [19], were enrolled into this randomized controlled trial (ChiCTR2100048737, registered on 2021) from Experimental School in China, this trial was registered on 15/07/2021.

Participants and Recruitment

Convenience sampling was employed based on our school-based recruitment through local pediatrician consultations. The inclusion criteria were: (1) be aged between 11 and 13 years; (2) be between Tanner stage 2 to 3 (pubertal stage was evaluated according to the Tanner classification by a trained pediatrician) [20]; (3) present a BMI greater than or equal to the 95th percentile for their gender and age; (4) be free of any medication that could interact with the protocol (e.g., cardiac abnormalities, hypertension, diabetes, orthopedic, neuromuscular, or neurological disorders); (5) present no contraindication to physical activity. The exclusion criteria were: (1) participation in organized sports or exercise training except PE classes in the last 6 months; (2) medical contraindication to training, and/or cardiorespiratory or respiratory disease; (3) absence from more than one-third of the scheduled exercise intervention sessions.

After the enrollment, participants written consent and assent were provided by their legal guardians or parents, respectively, they all completed a set of the medical history questionnaires and passed a physical examination conducted by a pediatric practitioner. The discussion of the potential outcomes and the importance of this experiment with parents or legal guardians before they signed informed consent. Participants were also informed that they can withdraw from the study at any time based on their personal circumstances, willingness, or reasons. Prior to baseline assessments, participants were familiarized with all study procedures. Subsequently, participants were randomly assigned into the following three groups based on their BMI and gender: jump rope exercise with short work time (JRE-1; n = 40), jump rope exercise with long work time (JRE-2; n = 40), or no-training control (CON; n = 40). Allocation concealment was implemented in this study using a simple randomization method generated by SPSS software (v 22.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The person who created the random assignment table did not participate in subject selection. Copies of the random distribution were made for the project leader, school principal, and statistician. Both participants and data analysts remained blinded after allocation. During the intervention, participants in different exercise groups received training, while an independent researcher conducted statistical analysis without knowledge of the intervention distribution. The exercise instructors are solely responsible for guiding participants in training and are unaware of how they are grouped. Additionally, exercise instructors do not partake in result analysis or clinical evaluations. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Department of Medicine at Shenzhen University (Approval No: PN-202400005).

Anthropometry and body composition

Body mass (kg), body fat mass (kg), body fat percentage (%), and fat free mass (kg) were measured using the InBody770 device (Biospace, Seoul, Korea), stature was measured in centimeters without shoes, heels together, and the back of the subject parallel to the stadiometer (RGZ-120-RT, Shanghai, China). The participants’ BMIs were derived using the formula: BMI = weight/height × height in kg/m2. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 95th percentile for age and sex, according to guidelines for school-age children [21]. Body composition assessments will be conducted following a standardized one-hour instrument warm-up period. Participants are required to report for testing without metallic accessories, attired in lightweight clothing, and must remove footwear for accurate bioelectrical impedance analysis. Body composition assessments are scheduled to occur once during the week preceding the formal intervention and again during the week following the completion of the 12-week intervention period. To maintain measurement precision, all assessments will be performed between 8:00–10:00 AM, with participants required to observe an 8–10 h overnight fasting.

Cardiorespiratory fitness

Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) was the secondary outcome of this study. Considering the convenience and feasibility, we used a 20-meter shuttle run test (20-mSRT) to measure the CRF of the participants which has been validated as a predictor of maximal aerobic capacity in children [22]. Taking into account both operational feasibility and participant energy levels, the 20-meter shuttle run test (20-mSRT) for participants is scheduled on the school playground between 4:00–6:00 PM. The participants performed the test in an outdoor track between two lines that were separated by 20-m, while keeping pace with the audio signals that were emitted from an MP3 that was produced by the National Coaching Foundation. The test began at a speed of 8.0 km/h, with the pace increasing by 0.5 km/h every minute. The participants were instructed to complete as many shuttles as possible. The test was ended if the participants gave up or failed to run 20-m run within the allotted time on two consecutive attempts. After 20-mSRT, the maximum oxygen uptake (VO2max, mL/kg/min) was then calculated using the formula [23]: VO2max = 41.76799 + (0.49261 × laps) - (0.00290 × laps²) - (0.61613 × BMI) + (0.34787 × gender × age), where gender is coded as 1 for male, 0 for female, and age is expressed in years. A one-hour preparation period during which all Polar heart rate monitoring devices will undergo systematic verification to confirm adequate battery charge and functional integrity. Immediately prior to testing, trained personnel will assist participants with proper heart rate band placement to optimize measurement precision.

Blood pressure and heart rate

After relaxation in a seated position for approximately over 5 min, resting systolic and diastolic blood pressures (SBP and DBP) along with resting heart rate (HRrest) were measured using an automatic BP monitor (Omron BP652, Omron Healthcare Inc., Vernon Hills, IL, USA). No SBP/DBP were measured during exercise test or training session. During 20-mSRT the maximal heart rate (HRmax) was recorded with a Polar team pro system (Polar; Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland). The chest belt was positioned according to the manufacturer instructions and the heart beat signal was checked for quality without any interference. Collected HR data were analyzed and averaged by a computer after the exercise sessions. The HRmax response was averaged from 5-sec continuous highest HR near the end of the 20-mSRT sessions pre- and post-interventional tests.

Fasting blood samples

Fasting blood samples were processed in duplicate by a local Pediatric hospital, and were collected in the morning to analyze cardiometabolic risk factors (that is, blood glucose and lipid profile). The blood testing session for participants is arranged at the designated hospitals from 8:00 to 11:00 AM. Participants were instructed to fast for 10 h before these measurements. In addition, they were refrain from moderate exercise for 24-h and vigorous exercise for 48-h beforehand. Blood samples for glucose and lipids Total cholesterol (TC), high-density cholesterol (HDL) and triglyceride (TG) levels were measured by enzymatic methods, and low-density cholesterol (LDL) concentrations were derived using the Frielwald et al. formula. Blood glucose concentrations were measured using an automated device (AU2700, Olympus, France). Blood insulin was assayed by an IRMA Insulin kit (Immunotech, France). Insulin resistance was assessed using the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) which has been computed as follows:

HOMA-IR = [Fasting insulin (µU/mL)] × Fasting glucose (mmol/L)]/22.5.

Exercise training protocol

Participants in JRE groups performed three sessions of interval jump rope training per week for 12 weeks. Training data for the two training groups are showed in Table 1. Except for the exercise tests and anthropometric measures, participants kept regular PE and activity in school without additional exercise training. The 10-min JRE sessions were integrated into the PE classes (a PE class lasts 40 min, and JRE is conducted 15 min before the end of the class). Therefore, the warm-up activities have been omitted and conducted 3–5 min of stretching after training. Participants in the JRE group performed 1 set of jump rope in one session; JRE-1 group performed 10 × 30-s jump rope interspaced with 30-s rest, and JRE-2 group performed 5 × 60-s jump rope interspaced with 60-s rest. Jump rope intensity at 100 jump/min for 1–4 weeks progressed to 110 jump/min for 5–8 weeks and 120 jump/min for 9–12 weeks. The cadence of jumps was controlled by recording a rhythmic MP3. All training sessions were monitored by personal trainers who verified adherence to the training protocol. The selected JRE protocol refers to previous research in obese populations [24].

Dietary and exercise control

Daily energy intake was estimated with validated 24-h dietary recalls (3 weekdays and 1 weekend day) [25], during the pre- and post- of the training program. It was carried out by all participants with the help of their parents and/or the investigators. Daily energy intake based on the dietary records was calculated with software app (Boohee Health Software, Boohee Info Technology Co., Shanghai, China), averaged, and reported as kilocalories per day (kcal/day). Subjects were asked to maintain their current diet throughout the study.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to conduct all statistical analysis. Normality of the data was assessed by examining skewness and kurtosis values and results for the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics and Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the means of all between-group variables/results. A two-way analysis of ANOVA with repeated measures (JRE-1 vs. JRE-2 vs. CON ×2 times: pre- vs. post-intervention). Post-hoc test (with Bonferroni) was applied if the main factor was significant. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η²). Figures 2, 3 and 4 were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1, San Diego, CA, USA). P-values less than 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. Paired t-test were applied to compare the means of each variable in all groups pre- and post- of the intervention.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A CONSORT diagram (Fig. 1) summarizes participant flow through each stage of the trial and outlines the reasons for participant drop out. Between January and February of 2024, we screened 223 obese adolescents. Of these only 120 (53.8%) met the criteria for baseline evaluation, and were subsequently randomized to the JRE-1, JRE-2, or CON groups. The other 103 participants were not randomized because of not signing the informed consent or other reasons. During the 12-week intervention period, 1 participant withdrew due to injury, but not from the jump rope training, the other 18 participants withdrew due to no time (n = 5), no post-test (n = 10), or no reason (n = 1). Finally, 101 obese adolescents (50 females, 12.4 ± 0.6 years) fully completed the current study. The JRE intervention in this study was integrated into PE classes, the attendance rate of the participants was notably high (JRE-1: 91%, JRE-2: 88%, Table 1). A summary of the baseline characteristics for obese adolescents is presented in Table 2. There were no significant differences between the groups at baseline.

Primary outcome

Body composition

Participants’ body composition variables are presented in Tables 3 and 4; Fig. 2. When compared to baseline, BM (JRE-1 = − 3.6 ± 2.6 kg, P < 0.01, JRE-2 = − 5.5 ± 2.5 kg, P < 0.01), BMI (JRE-1 = − 1.4 ± 1.0 kg/m2, P < 0.01, JRE-2 = − 2.1 ± 0.9 kg/m2, P < 0.01), FM (JRE-1 = − 2.2 ± 1.8 kg, P < 0.01, JRE-2 = − 2.7 ± 2.4 kg, P < 0.01), BFP (JRE-1 = − 1.4 ± 2.9%, P < 0.05, JRE-2 = − 1.3 ± 3.3%, P < 0.05), and FFM (JRE-1 = − 1.4 ± 2.4 kg, P < 0.01, JRE-2 = − 2.7 ± 2.1 kg, P < 0.01) were decreased in all two interventions, and BM (0.7 ± 1.8 kg, P < 0.05) and FM (0.5 ± 1.4 kg, P < 0.05) were increased in CON group. BM and BMI decreased more in the JRE-2 group compared with the JRE-1 group (MD: −1.92 kg; 95% CI, − 0.56 to − 3.29; P < 0.01, and MD: −0.72 kg/m2; 95% CI, − 0.19 to − 1.25; P < 0.01; Table 3). No other significant group comparisons were observed (all P > 0.05; Table 4).

Changes in body composition, cardiorespiratory fitness, glycolipid profile and dietary intake in the JRE groups compared with the CON group

Cardiorespiratory fitness

Participants’ cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) variables are presented in Tables 3 and 4; Fig. 3. When compared to baseline, VO2max (JRE-1 = 3.4 ± 1.4 mL/kg/min, P < 0.01, JRE-2 = 4.0 ± 1.1 mL/kg/min, P < 0.01, CON = 1.6 ± 1.0 mL/kg/min, P < 0.01) increased in all groups. SBP (JRE-1 = − 2 ± 2 mmHg, P < 0.05, JRE = − 2 ± 3 mmHg, P < 0.05) and HRrest (JRE-1 = − 3 ± 2 b.p.m, P < 0.05, JRE = − 2 ± 2 b.p.m, P < 0.05) were decreased in both intervention groups. HRmax only increased in JRE-2 group (1 ± 1 b.p.m, P < 0.05). No significant group differences were observed on cardiorespiratory fitness variables between intervention groups (all P > 0.23; Table 4; Fig. 3).

Secondary outcome

Glycolipid metabolism

Participants’ glycolipid metabolism variables are presented in Tables 3 and 4, and Fig. 4. When compared to baseline, FBG (JRE-1 = − 0.29 ± 0.26 mmol/L, P < 0.05, JRE = − 0.26 ± 0.27 mmol/L, P < 0.05), FINs (JRE-1 = − 2.03 ± 2.36 µU/mL, P < 0.01, JRE = − 2.26 ± 2.24 µU/mL, P < 0.01), HOMA-IR (JRE-1 = − 0.58 ± 0.36, P < 0.01, JRE = − 0.62 ± 0.59, P < 0.01), and TG (JRE-1 = − 0.12 ± 0.19 mmol/L, P < 0.01, JRE = − 0.12 ± 0.17 mmol/L, P < 0.01) were decreased in all intervention groups. No significant group differences were observed on glycolipid metabolism variables between intervention groups (all P > 0.78; Table 4; Fig. 4).

Discussion

Our primary purpose was to examine the effects of HIIT-based JRE integrated into PE class on body composition, CRF, and glycolipid metabolism indicators in obese adolescents. Over 12-week both JRE interventions effectively improved body composition, CRF, and glycolipid metabolism in adolescents with obesity. Moreover, these beneficial effects were significantly greater following JRE-2 (60-s: 60-s) than JRE-1 (30-s: 30-s). In addition, the body weight and BMI of the untrained control group increased significantly. This indicates that adolescents with obesity who do not engage in additional exercise training may worsen their obesity issues. The increase in VO2max observed in the participants could be due to the health benefits associated with regular PE classes in school.

Our findings on the effects of JRE on body composition are consistent with recent meta-analyses involving preadolescents and youth [8, 9]. Singh et al. [9] conducted a meta-analysis of 21 studies with 1021 healthy participants under the age of 18, demonstrating that JRE significantly improve BMI and FM. Additionally, Kim et al. [26] reported a notable reduction on BMI and BFP among obese male adolescents after six weeks of moderate-intensity JRE, which consisted of 60 jumps/min at 40–70% of heart rate reserve, for 30 min per session. The results supported by another study of JRE in obese adolescents [10]. The meta-analysis by Zhao et al. suggests that JRE for 8–12 weeks, twice a week, with a duration >40 min, is beneficial for improving physical fitness in adolescents [8]. However, this study demonstrated that using HIIT-based JRE also has the effect of improving body composition and is more time efficient. This could be the result of differences in training intensity (< 90 jump/min vs. >100 jump/min). Singh et al.‘s meta-analysis suggests that JRE has no significant effect on FFM (SMD = 0.023, 95% CI = − 0.195, 0.394) [9], and Sung et al. found that FFM increased in adolescent girls with prehypertension after JRE [27]. While the results of this study are contrary, our results indicated that FFM decreases as body fat decreases in both JRE groups (− 1.4 kg in JRE-1 and − 2.7 kg in JRE-2). This consistent with the recent study of HIIT-based JRE for obese adults [24]. The reduction of FFM will lower the resting metabolic rate (RMR), making it more difficult to achieve a negative daily energy balance, which is not conducive to long-term weight management in obese adolescents. A study by Batrakoulis et al. demonstrated that HIIT enhanced endurance and musculoskeletal performance, resulting in a long-term negative energy balance and a gradual, sustained reduction in body and fat mass [28]. Therefore, it is recommended that obese adolescents participate in more resistance training or whole-body strength training while undergoing JRE to achieve better weight loss effects.

Adolescents with excessive body weight tend to have lower cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) when compared to their peers of normal weight, which increases the risk of developing CVD [29]. Even a modest improvement in CRF can greatly reduce the risk of CVD and premature death [30]. This study demonstrated that CRF (e.g., VO2max) in adolescents with obesity was significantly improved after 12-week JRE, which similar increased in JRE-1 (+ 3.4 mL/kg/min) and JRE-2 (+ 4.0 mL/kg/min). Our findings align with recent meta-analyses which reported the CRF enhancements in preadolescents and youth. Several studies on JRE in obese adolescents have not focused on improving CRF, and similar results were found in a recent study from Phongchin [31] and Yang [24], HIIT-based JRE intervention can significantly improve the CRF of health and obese adults. CRF level is affected by oxygen delivery and extraction capacity, including heart rate (HR) and stroke volume (SV), peripheral perfusion and diffusion capacity (i.e. vasodilation and capillary density), and skeletal muscle oxidative capacity. Previously, Helgerud et al. reported that SV was more significantly increased after HIIT [32], which could explain a greater improved VO2max. The present study indicated that the HRrest slightly but significantly decrease in obese adolescents reflects an increase in cardiac output [33], which could also be a contributable factor for a greater increase in CRF. Another notable finding of this study is that JRE reduced SBP by ~ 2 mmHg. This result aligns with the study by Sung et al., which showed that after 12 weeks, JRE reduced SBP by ~ 6 mmHg in girls with prehypertension [27]. A decrease in SBP is clinically significant, as even a small reduction of just 2 mmHg can lead to a nearly 6% decrease in the mortality rate from stroke and a 4% decrease in coronary heart disease mortality [34]. Therefore, the 2 mmHg reduction observed in this study may help prevent cardiovascular complications that could develop later in adulthood. JRE induced the increases in nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, resulting in a decrease in arterial stiffness levels and an increase in microvascular density, which may be a potential mechanism for blood pressure reduction [35, 36]. Currently, research on the JRE training response in obese adolescents is limited, and its specific effects on CRF require further investigation.

Common risk factors accompanied by obesity include glycolipid metabolism disorder (e.g., hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia) [37]. Reducing the incidence of obesity in adolescents and improving metabolic disorders caused by obesity, such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and insulin resistance, are effective methods for preventing and treating metabolic syndrome (MetS) [38]. Our research evidence suggested that JRE can improve the glycolipid status of adolescents with obesity. Our results were consistent with the previous studies, demonstrating the JRE interventions benefit blood lipids improving. For example. a RCT involving 142 young adults with excessive body weight found a reduction in TG, TC, and LDL [39]. Similarly, the JRE intervention also led to significant reduction in TG in obese children [10]. The intensity and duration of training significantly modulate in vivo lipid metabolism [19]. Preclinical investigations indicate that high-intensity exercise may optimize lipid metabolic processes via the regulation of mitochondrial biosynthesis [40]. Childhood obesity is frequently associated with dysregulated glucose and insulin homeostasis, which may progress to insulin resistance (IR) or MetS [41], elevated IR is well-established as a critical risk factor for the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [42]. Accordingly, the implementation of targeted interventions to ameliorate glucose metabolism in adolescents with obesity is imperative for preventing T2DM onset [43]. In the present study, body fat percentage (− 1.4% and − 1.3%), FBG (− 0.29 mmol/L and − 0.26 mmol/L), FINs (− 2.03 µU/mL and − 2.26 µU/mL) and HOMA-IR (− 0.58 and − 0.62) were both significantly reduced following the JRE-1 and JRE-2, respectively. The reduction in body fat mass may have played a role in the reduction of IR in the present study, which is consistent with previous findings [26]. However, it’s contrast to conflicting results on FBG, and FINs levels after JRE among adults [39]. Some studies have shown that upregulation of GLUT4, increased aerobic enzyme activity, and mitochondrial biogenesis may be a potential mechanism of HIIT promoting glucose uptake and improving insulin sensitivity [44, 45]. Due to the limited number of studies and inconsistent findings, it is challenging to draw confident conclusions about the effects of HIIT-based JRE on glucose regulation. Future research should include well-designed randomized controlled trials to address these gaps.

The JRE integrated into PE is more time-efficient, requiring a total training time of 10 min per week (100–120 jumps per minute, 3 times/week). Our data shows that reducing exercise time while achieving greater improvements makes JRE a more appealing and convenient intervention for combating adolescent obesity. The current review on HIIT-induced psychological adaptations demonstrates that this popular and demanding exercise mode can significantly enhance most mental health-related indices [46]. Additionally, for school-age adolescents, the shorter duration and incorporation into PE classes encourage better exercise adherence and wider applicability in schools, leading to more sustained intervention effects over time. Individual factors like diet habits, and sleep patterns significantly affect body weight. To combat rising obesity rates among adolescents, it’s essential to enhance physical education resources and sports facilities in schools, fostering healthier environments and encouraging lifelong physical activity [47].

The strength of this study lies in the integration of the JRE into PE classes, which requires minimal equipment and is more time-efficient for adolescents with obesity. Meanwhile, this study benefits from a relatively large sample size compared to similar previous studies, which is a notable strength. However, the number of eligible participants may still be insufficient. Therefore, future trials should be conducted simultaneously in multiple schools and include larger sample sizes to better understand the health benefits of JRE on adolescents with obesity. While this study presents several beneficial outcomes, it is important to acknowledge some limitations. First, we used BIA to measure body composition variables. Although dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is a more validated method for measuring body composition, the reliability and validity of BIA have been supported by numerous studies. Second, although we recorded the EI of the subjects both before and after the intervention through an app, we did not observe a significant change. Additionally, dietary intake and energy intake (EI) were not controlled during the intervention period, and the participants’ sleep was not monitored, which may have influenced the overall results. To enhance the quality of the study, we could utilize diaries or questionnaires alongside accelerometers to track each participant’s daily physical activity levels and sleep patterns. Providing dietary guidance and family activity recommendations could also contribute to improving the study quality.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study showed that interval jump rope exercise integrating into PE class is more effective in improving body composition, cardiorespiratory fitness, and glycolipid metabolism parameters in obese adolescents. Further research exploring factors influencing intervention effectiveness (e.g. optimal exercise intensity, total duration, and exercise frequency, etc.), and evaluate the long-term effects of intermittent rope skipping to understand how scalable exercise interventions can address childhood obesity and inform health policies.
