The (dis-)advantages of telehealth consultation for allied health services: a scoping review
Authors: Deniz Senyel, Elizabeth Boutros, Madison Frith, Feby Savira, Lin Tong, Bernard Kwadwo Yeboah Asiamah-Asare, Richard Norman, Suzanne Robinson, James H. Boyd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic resulted in an uptake of teleconsultations across the healthcare sector, including for allied health services. However, (dis-)advantages specific to allied health services are under-researched. The aim of this scoping review is the understand the (dis-)advantages patients and providers perceive when using teleconsultations for allied health services.MethodsThis scoping review was conducted using the JBI methodology for scoping reviews and reported according to the PRISMA-ScR statement. The final search was conducted in January 2024, through the databases MEDLINE Complete, EMBASE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, AMED Allied and Complementary Medicine. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported qualitative findings on the (dis-)advantages of teleconsultations for allied health services from a patient’s and/or provider’s perspective. The screening process was conducted by two independent researchers, while the data extraction and inductive analysis were performed by the first author.ResultsWe identified 116 eligible articles. Eight categories were identified from a patient’s perspective, including improved access to care, resource savings, convenience, comfort, reduced infection risk, effects on therapy, privacy, and patient-provider relationship. From the provider’s perspective, additional categories included telehealth as a trigger for change and improvement, the patients in their own home, disruptions, interpreters, efficiency, changes to workday, and safety and risks.ConclusionWhile there are clear advantages, such as improved access to care, and disadvantages, such as the lack of hands-on treatment, the impact of telehealth often depends on the individual context of both the provider and the patient. Although some aspects are broadly relevant across all allied health professions, there are notable differences in the suitability of telehealth for specific services. Understanding the disadvantages and advantages of teleconsultations is key to informing policies and identifying suitable applications of telehealth.

Background

The global onset of the COVID-19 pandemic acted as a significant catalyst for the widespread adoption of telehealth technology in the healthcare sector, as it reduced infection risks and therefore the spread of the virus [1–3]. Telehealth became not just preferable but often the only feasible option, ensuring continuity of care when in-person services were unattainable. There are multiple ways to provide telehealth, including synchronous and asynchronous modes, which both can be used to diagnose, treat, and remotely monitor patients [4]. Teleconsultations, using synchronous technologies such as phone or video calls, can be used to simulate in-person appointments [5], thereby increasing access by removing geographical, logistical, and financial barriers [6]. Telehealth is therefore particularly useful for patients living regionally or rurally who would typically travel lengthy distances to receive health care. However, challenges exist in the use of telehealth, including limited digital literacy among both practitioners and patients [7], particularly older adults and individuals with cognitive, hearing, or visual impairments [8]. Additionally, equity of access remains a concern, as socioeconomic factors influence the ability to utilise telehealth services. Rising living costs, along with the expense of internet access and the necessary communication technology, may make telehealth inaccessible for some [7].

As we navigate the post-pandemic era, it is crucial to reflect on the (dis-)advantages of telehealth and devise strategies for enhancing the telehealth system moving forward.

One important sector of healthcare where telehealth has been applied is allied health services. They form a substantial part of the healthcare system [9], catering to a vast variety of patient needs. The allied health landscape is diverse, including services that traditionally are considered very hands-on such as physiotherapy or exercise physiology through to services that are typically more verbal, including mental health and counselling services [9]. Despite differences in therapeutic approaches, allied health services share common opportunities and challenges when delivering care via telehealth. Increased access and convenience for patients are consistent benefits across services. The suitability and extent of telehealth adoption within allied health are contingent upon profession-specific factors. For instance, professions like psychology have readily embraced virtual consultations for therapy and support services [10, 11]. Conversely, disciplines such as physiotherapy which can involve hands-on assessment and manipulation face inherent barriers to fully virtual service delivery [12, 13]. Identifying key advantages, such as improved accessibility, reduced travel burden, and enhanced continuity of care, can inform strategies to maximise these benefits and support broader adoption. At the same time, recognising limitations, including reduced hands-on assessment, engagement barriers, and digital literacy gaps, can guide targeted improvements to ensure telehealth can complement in-person care.

This scoping review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of both provider and patient perspectives on the disadvantages and advantages of allied healthcare through telehealth.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews [14] and is reported according to the PRISMA-ScR statement [15]. A scoping review methodology was applied as the aim of the article is to summarise the current literature landscape while also analysing research gaps.

Participants

Studies were eligible for inclusion if participants, for both the service provider and patient cohorts, were aged over 18 years. Studies that featured child or adolescent participants were excluded as the needs and experiences of younger populations potentially differ substantially from those of adults. Given the already broad scope of this scoping review, including these studies could have introduced further heterogeneity that would limit the relevance and applicability of findings. However, studies were eligible for inclusion if a provider’s account of telehealth (dis)advantages included both adults and children, but only data related to adult recipients were extracted. No restrictions were made on religious affiliations or racial, ethnic, or cultural populations.

Concept

Studies needed to include the provision of allied healthcare via telehealth. We defined telehealth as the synchronous provision of healthcare through virtual means, telephone or video calls, such as teleconsultations. Asynchronous technology such as mobile phone applications or email correspondence was excluded for better comparability of the results.

Allied healthcare is a broad term, and this review looked at studies that refer to allied healthcare in general as well as singular allied healthcare professions. For the specific disciplines, the categories of the allied health professions in Australia were used as a guideline [16] (Table 2). Any allied health services that are unlikely or impossible to be provided via telehealth, such as radiologists, orthotics/prosthetics, perfusion, and sonography were excluded. Further social work was excluded. Search words used

Context

No restrictions were placed on the country of origin or the context in which the telehealth intervention was delivered; studies from any setting were eligible for inclusion.

Types of sources

This scoping review only included qualitative evidence. In the case of mixed methods studies, only the qualitative analysis was considered. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they used a cross-sectional or longitudinal design. Both experimental and observational methodologies were eligible. Commentaries, conference presentations, opinion pieces, reviews, case studies or case series were excluded. Articles in English, published since 2019 were eligible for inclusion, to focus on the rise of telehealth throughout the COVID-19 pandemic.

Search strategy

For the search string, synonyms for allied healthcare and teleconsultations were combined with search words describing (dis-)advantages. For all search terms, relevant MeSH terms were identified and included. The following databases were used: MEDLINE Complete, EMBASE, CINAHL, PsycINFO, AMED Allied and Complementary Medicine. Final searches were conducted on 30th of January 2024. No additional hand-searching or grey literature searches were conducted due to the high number of results retrieved from the databases, which were expected to provide a comprehensive overview of the research area. Table 2 presents the search words used.

Study selection

All identified studies were imported into Covidence where duplicates were automatically removed. All articles were independently screened by title and abstract by two reviewers, with a third reviewer resolving conflicts. The same process was repeated for the full text screening.

Data extraction, analysis and presentation

From the included studies the following data points were extracted by the first author: author, year of publication, country, study methods, allied health profession, and perspective (patient or provider). To answer the research questions, qualitative data on the perceived (dis-)advantages of allied health via telehealth from the patients’ and providers’ perspectives were extracted. Full texts of included studies were uploaded into NVIVO, a software designed for qualitative content analysis. Inspired by Braun and Clarkes thematic analysis approach an inductive analysis was applied to generate categories of (dis-)advantages of allied health via telehealth services [17]. Descriptions of advantages and disadvantages were extracted from the evidence and preliminarily coded. Once all relevant excerpts had been extracted, they were grouped by topic and subsequently organised into broader categories. For example, “cost for fuel” was grouped under the topic “cost,” which was then categorised as “resource savings.” The analysis was conducted by the first author. For the presentation of results, the categories were reported narratively, divided by perspective (patient vs. provider). To reduce unnecessary repetition, the provider section will focus on additional categories that emerged beyond those identified from the patient perspective.

Study characteristics

The results showed that interviews (n = 46) and surveys (n = 43) were the most frequently used methods. Focus groups were used in 8 studies. A combination of surveys and interviews were reported in 12 studies, while surveys and focus groups combined were used in 3 studies. One study combined surveys, interviews, and focus groups, and another included interviews and analysis of medical records. Additionally, one study utilised audio diary recordings of practitioners and one combined interviews and focus groups.

Studies focused on mental health therapists (n = 34) and physiotherapists (n = 26) most frequently. The use of multidisciplinary teams was reported in 18 studies. Other professions included speech therapists (n = 8), occupational therapists (n = 6), pharmacists (n = 4), substance use specialists (n = 4), music therapists (n = 4), and dieticians (n = 4). Audiologists (n = 2) and physiologists (n = 2) were each discussed in two studies. Chiropractors (n = 1), osteopaths (n = 1), genetic counsellors (n = 1), and art therapists (n = 1) were each mentioned once. Of all the included articles, 57 studied the providers’ perspective, 37 focused on patients’ perspectives, and 22 included both voices.

(Dis-)advantages from the patient perspective

Eight categories were identified regarding the (dis-)advantages of telehealth for allied health services from a patient’s perspective. A summary of all advantages and disadvantages including the patient and provider perspective can be seen in Table 4. The categories are: improved access to care, resource savings, convenience, comfort, reduced infection risk, effects on therapy, privacy, and patient-provider relationship. Improved access to care, reduced resource utilisation, convenience, comfort, and lower infection risk were clear advantages, while the patient-provider relationship was considered a disadvantage. The effects on therapy and privacy had both positive and negative aspects. Each category is explored in more detail below. Table 5 shows which categories were reported by which articles, with exemplary quotes to represent the categories.

Improved access to care

Telehealth was widely recognised across the studies for its role in improving access to care, especially for individuals facing geographical, financial or logistical barriers. This was particularly beneficial for those in rural or remote areas who previously had to travel long distances for healthcare services.

Telehealth improved access to care in cases where allied health services were otherwise unavailable, which increased the frequency of service contact, and ensured continuity of care in situations where treatment would otherwise be paused (e.g., during a pandemic). Overall, a clear advantage of telehealth was its increase in access to and continuity of care.

Resource savings

Another advantage of telehealth appointments is the reduction in resources needed to attend appointments. This mostly involved travel, time, and cost. All of these are interlinked. Firstly, the shift away from face-to-face consultations removed the need to commute, which not only reduced the time investment but also reduced the cost spent on public transport or parking and fuel. The reduced travel was particularly beneficial for those with mobility issues, transportation barriers, or work or family commitments. The flexibility of remote care was also considered to be positive from a financial perspective, as it allowed people to access services without taking time off work or arranging childcare.

For patients in regional and remote areas the amount of resources saved through telehealth was significantly higher and therefore more important.

Convenience

From the patient perspective, the convenience of telehealth was a prominent advantage, largely attributed to the decreased resources required for remote encounters. Convenience, encompassing ease of access through time and cost savings, as well as flexibility, was particularly valued. Participants reported that telehealth facilitated easier scheduling and attendance, seamlessly fitting into their daily routines, primarily due to the elimination of travel and associated expenses, alongside its inherent temporal flexibility.

Comfort

A clear advantage of telehealth was the ability to attend appointments from one’s own home. Patients often referred to the comfort of their own home, which offered a more relaxed atmosphere than an allied health professional’s office. A participant’s own home was often described as a more natural environment, offering emotional safety, especially for psychology appointments. Further, patients felt that they were more in control of their surroundings, fostering a sense of autonomy. Patients liked being able to choose where to have their appointments from and even having self-regulating strategies in place such as having their pets with them. Lastly, attending appointments remotely was linked to other categories such as resource saving, with decreased travel and time commitment which resulted in less physical and mental burden and thus higher comfort.

Reduced infection risk

Another clear advantage of remote consultations was the reduced infection risk. This was critical during the COVID-19 pandemic, where social distancing was necessary to reduce infection rates.

Effects on therapy

The sixth category concerned the relative (dis-)advantages telehealth appointments had when compared to in-person appointments for the therapy itself. Many patients highlighted the limitations of telehealth due to it not being suitable if hands-on therapy is required. A common concern was the inability to perform comprehensive physical assessments, including palpation and manual manipulation, which impacted diagnostic accuracy and treatment effectiveness.

While some participants acknowledged that telehealth could be useful for follow-up appointments or services not requiring physical contact, such as psychology, many expressed a preference for in-person consultations when physical examination or manual treatment was necessary. Physiotherapy was frequently cited as an example. The challenge of effectively demonstrating and learning exercises remotely was also noted, with concerns about incorrect technique. Some felt that in-person care was crucial for providers to guide movement and provide hands-on correction or physical cueing. The quality of exercise treatment was further reduced if special exercise equipment, that would have been used for in-person sessions, were lacking in a person’s home. Additionally, patients expressed concerns about the accuracy of teleconsultations, particularly regarding whether providers could effectively monitor and correct exercise techniques. Some worried that therapists might miss emotional or physical cues indicating discomfort or improper form.

While those with prior in-person experience felt more confident exercising virtually, they still questioned whether they were performing movements correctly. The lack of direct supervision was a significant concern, with some participants reporting injuries or increased pain due to inadequate guidance. Others felt that exercising without in-person oversight was unsafe, highlighting the perceived limitations of telehealth in ensuring proper technique and preventing harm.

A critical aspect of teleconsultations was the restricted view provided by a camera. Many found it difficult to position their devices in a way that allowed providers to see their full body or specific movements clearly. Some had to constantly adjust their camera angles, leading to frustration and concerns that their therapist was not getting a complete picture of their technique or areas of pain.

However, telehealth also provided advantages to allied health services. Firstly, patients often felt, that it was easier to open up emotionally via telehealth, which is a positive aspect for allied health services such as psychology. The familiar and private setting of their own space reduced feelings of self-consciousness and inhibition, allowing for more open discussions. Some noted that the absence of physical closeness in telehealth settings made it easier to disclose sensitive topics, while others appreciated the reduced anxiety that came with speaking from home rather than in a face-to-face environment.

For introverted individuals or those with social anxieties, virtual consultations felt particularly suitable, as they could express themselves more freely without the pressure of being in the same room as their therapist. Some also mentioned feeling a greater sense of control over the interaction, which contributed to their comfort. However, experiences varied, with some participants feeling less able to express themselves online compared to in-person sessions.

Furthermore, telehealth facilitated the accommodation of family and friends for emotional comfort, either during the appointment or afterwards.

Lastly, telehealth also enabled patients to take a more active role in their care. This shift in responsibilities could lead to an improvement in self-management and patient empowerment. Some found that keeping their own records of consultations made them feel more engaged and in control. The ability to take notes during online sessions, since the patient was sitting at their desk, was seen as an advantage, making it easier to retain information and stay focused on health goals. However, not everyone experienced telehealth in the same way - while some appreciated the increased independence, others missed the sense of being actively guided through their rehabilitation in person.

Privacy

Telehealth was seen to be both more and less private than face-to-face appointments and could therefore be an advantage or disadvantage based on the individual’s perspective. On one side telehealth was seen as more private as patients felt less judged by others, for example in waiting rooms. However, privacy at home could be compromised if no appropriate space was available. This included if other members of the household could overhear conversations. Patients sometimes tried to solve this issue by using public spaces such as libraries, which had similar issues regarding privacy and confidentiality. Overall, the sentiment that privacy was lacking at home overweighed the notion of improved privacy thus making the lack of privacy in telehealth appointments more often a disadvantage.

Patient-provider communication and relationship

Telehealth was generally considered to have a mostly negative impact on the patient-provider relationship and therefore was seen as a disadvantage. Reasons for diminished relationships were difficulties communicating, the lack of body language and non-verbal cues, and overall lack of personal connection. Patients found it harder to express their concerns and demonstrate physical issues effectively in telehealth settings, making communication less clear and comfortable. Some service users worried that providers might not fully grasp what they were trying to communicate, leaving them uncertain about whether they had been understood.

Language and cultural barriers were further exacerbated in virtual settings, as participants struggled to express themselves appropriately, particularly in phone-based consultations where non-verbal cues were absent. Some individuals also reported needing to put in extra effort to understand and be understood, adding to the cognitive burden of remote interactions. Additionally, difficulties with hearing comprehension were more pronounced in digital settings.

The restriction of non-verbal communication further impacted the patient-provider relationship. Patients noted that the inability to observe physical cues, such as facial expressions, body posture, and subtle movements, made it harder to communicate effectively. Phone calls were hereby even harder than video consultations. The absence of physical contact and shared physical space further weakened communication, with some patients noting that their experience of therapeutic connection was diminished. These limitations led some patients to perceive telehealth as less personal, less intense, and less effective than in-person consultations.

Lastly, telehealth could increase or decrease isolation, depending on the available alternatives, and therefore was seen as both an advantage and disadvantage. In the case of the COVID-19 pandemic where social engagement was limited, telehealth helped patients to feel less isolated and more connected with others. However, if in-person appointments are possible, telehealth can be more isolating in comparison.

Overall, patients felt that telehealth decreased the quality of their relationships with allied health practitioners in comparison to in-person engagement. While it can be an opportunity for social contact, telehealth was less appealing compared to standard face-to-face appointments. Therefore, worsened patient-provider relationships can be seen as a disadvantage for telehealth.

In other instances, providers identified the same categories but added their own perspectives to them. Trigger for change and improvement

One advantage of telehealth was that it could potentially serve as a catalyst for innovation and improvement. Since providers had to overhaul their workflows, therapies, and patient interactions, they were prompted to reflect on and refine these aspects of care. Some practitioners reported gaining new skills, such as enhanced communication techniques and the ability to deliver high-quality care in different ways where physical contact is generally utilised in standard practice care. For example, osteopaths noted improvements in patient satisfaction and their own communication abilities, while physical therapists found that telehealth encouraged a stronger focus on education and coaching. Beyond individual practice, telehealth facilitated greater collaboration among professionals, encouraging a more holistic and adaptive approach to care.

Patients in their own home

Another advantage of telehealth was the ability to see patients in their home environment, which provided valuable insights that varied across different allied health services. For exercise-based therapies, conducting sessions in the home allowed therapists to tailor treatment plans to the patient’s specific surroundings. This also enhanced the transferability of skills, as patients could practice exercises in the space where they would continue them. Similarly, occupational therapists found that working in the patient’s natural environment enabled more accurate goal setting and treatment planning, while also allowing for immediate guidance on home modifications, assistive devices, and functional tasks. In mental health services, being able to observe a patient’s living space provided a more holistic understanding of their context, daily challenges, and support systems. This deeper insight not only refined assessment and intervention strategies but also created opportunities to better advocate for patient well-being. Overall, telehealth facilitated a more personalised and contextually relevant approach to care by integrating real-world environments into treatment planning.

Disruptions

Telehealth introduced both increases and decreases in disruptions, but most providers observed that clients tended to be more distracted at home. Common distractions included clients checking their phones, engaging in other tasks during appointments, or being interrupted by people in their household. Some patients also joined sessions from unsuitable locations, such as supermarkets, while driving, or even from bed, which affected the professionalism and effectiveness of the interaction. Additionally, providers noted that disruptions in the home environment - such as background noise, lack of privacy, and competing demands - often interfered with engagement and therapeutic rapport.

Providers also faced challenges in maintaining their focus, as the virtual setting made it easier to check notifications or complete administrative tasks during sessions. While telehealth had the potential to facilitate undivided attention on the person on the screen, in practice, it was often reported as more distracting than in-person consultations due to these environmental factors.

Interpreters

The use of interpreters in telehealth was seen as both an advantage and a disadvantage. On one hand, telehealth expanded accessibility by allowing interpreters to join remotely, enabling connections with professionals outside the immediate community. Some providers even noted that virtual interpretation enhanced privacy for clients, as they felt more comfortable with a remote interpreter. However, challenges also emerged, including technological barriers such as poor audio or video quality, dropped calls, and platform limitations that prevented multiple participants from joining simultaneously. Additionally, some clients were less trusting of virtual interpreters, particularly if they’d had a negative experience in the past. In some cases, providers found that hosting an interpreter in-person was more effective, particularly when using only telephone-based interpretation, which was often seen as impractical for virtual consultations. Ultimately, the effectiveness of interpreter use in telehealth depends on the technological capabilities of the platform, the quality of the device, and the preferences of both providers and patients.

Efficiency

The effectiveness and efficiency of telehealth are not the primary focus of this paper and will not be discussed in detail, as they vary depending on the specific allied health service and patient group. However, it is worth noting that telehealth was reported to both enhance and hinder efficiency. Some providers found telehealth increased efficiency by reducing travel time in case of appointments that traditionally were held at the patient’s home, which enabled them to see more patients in a shorter period and streamlining workflows through electronic systems. Virtual consultations also facilitated more punctual appointments, reduced patient non-attendance, and allowed for more flexible scheduling.

Conversely, technological issues could extend appointment times, leading to scheduling disruptions and decreased efficiency. Some providers also felt that telehealth models increased their workload rather than reducing it, particularly when technical problems arose or when additional coordination was required. Ultimately, whether telehealth serves as an advantage or disadvantage in terms of efficiency depends on the specific healthcare setting, the technology in use, and the organisation of care delivery.

Changes to workday

Telehealth influenced providers’ workdays in both positive and negative ways. Some providers appreciated the increased flexibility, as telehealth allowed them to work from home, reducing commuting time and expenses while providing a better balance between work and personal life. However, others experienced challenges in maintaining work-life boundaries. Some providers reported that telehealth blurred the separation between work and personal time, with patients contacting them outside of scheduled hours. The shift to remote work also made it harder to enforce structured working hours, leading to increased workloads and, in some cases, dissatisfaction. Additionally, working from home introduced distractions, such as childcare responsibilities, which some providers found affected their ability to focus on clinical work. Ultimately, telehealth’s impact on a provider’s workday depends on personal circumstances, workplace policies, and individual preferences, making it both an advantage and a disadvantage depending on the context.

Safety and risk

Providers reported increased safety risks associated with telehealth. In physical rehabilitation services, there were concerns about patients attempting exercises alone at home without a support person, increasing the risk of falls or injuries. Additionally, providers found it more challenging to monitor and correct improper movements remotely. Similarly, in mental health services, there were psychological risks, particularly for patients at risk of self-harm. Some therapies may not be suitable for remote delivery without a mental health professional physically present. These safety concerns highlight a key disadvantage of telehealth.

Discussion

Summary of results

The aim of this scoping review was to identify (dis-)advantages of telehealth for allied health services from patient and provider perspectives. Based on the 116 included articles, several (dis-)advantages were noted. While some aspects were clearly identified as advantages or disadvantages, others were considered to be either positive or negative, dependent on context. For both patients and providers, the improved access to care was seen as a clear advantage, as was the reduction in resources needed, including time, travel, and cost. Furthermore, the convenience and comfort provided to both patients and providers were seen as advantages of telehealth. Notably during the COVID-19 pandemic telehealth offered a decrease in infection risk which was appreciated by all parties involved. On the other end of the spectrum, the patient-provider relationship was seen as an area where telehealth had a clear disadvantage, as telehealth reduced rapport building and mutual connection. This was mostly due to communication issues, which were increased due to less nonverbal cues. Some issues that were not clearly categorised as advantages or disadvantages were privacy during telehealth appointments, as it could be improved in some instances where there was a greater sense of anonymity from telehealth or worsened in others through the presence of other people in the patient’s home. Lastly, telehealth had mixed effects on the actual treatment provision. Both patients and providers noted the lack of hands-on care which limited treatment options and decreased the perceived value of treatment. On the other hand, for mental health services, telehealth could be an advantage as it is easier for some patients to open up at home. Additional categories that were only identified by providers included how telehealth could be a catalyst for change and improvement, as the necessary restructuring invited providers to rethink their workflow and therapeutic approach. It was further an advantage for many providers to see the patients in their own home, as this provided additional information and context, which could be used to improve treatment. However, some negative aspects were noted, such as an increase in disruption during appointments.

Arguments were made for and against the accommodation of interpreters. Some saw it as positive that interpreters regardless of the location could be used in virtual appointments. However, others stated that it was difficult to include interpreters in a virtual appointment due to technological restrictions and communication difficulties. Similarly, the efficiency of telehealth was both seen as improved and impaired as some saw the streamlining of workflows and the reduced travel time to patients as more efficient, while others saw technical disruptions and added workload as less efficient. This also influenced the provider’s workday. Some providers work-life balance improved as they could schedule appointments at their own convenience and reduce their travel time. Conversely, others saw a decrease in boundaries between work and personal life where appointments were offered after hours. Lastly, providers saw a clear disadvantage in the increased risk for patients either due to risk of injury while exercising without oversight or psychological risk in the case of unstable patients.

Understanding the advantages and disadvantages of telehealth for allied health services is crucial when deciding if and when telehealth should be offered to patients. It is also important to understand the barriers and enablers for the implementation and uptake of telehealth. This paper only focuses on the advantages and disadvantages; however, a significant amount of literature focuses on enablers and barriers, giving us good insight into how to increase the uptake of telehealth.

The most significant barrier involved the technology itself: lack of devices and internet access, accessibility and the required digital literacy can put people at a disadvantage when using telehealth [6, 133]. Firstly, patients require the necessary hardware such as smartphones, computers or webcams to access virtual appointments. Furthermore, stable internet connections and data plans are needed. For patients under financial stress acquiring these can be challenging [134]. For patients in rural and remote areas, telehealth is often highlighted as a way to improve access, however, internet access is not always provided in these areas, thus hindering the advantages telehealth should provide [135].

Even when internet and necessary devices are available, their use can still be challenging for certain patient groups. Firstly, technology can present accessibility barriers for patients with various disabilities [136–138]. For deaf and hard-of-hearing patients, the lack of high-quality captioning and the inability to lip-read effectively can make telehealth services inaccessible [139]. As observed in this scoping review, interpreters - including sign language interpreters - are not always available for virtual appointments. Additionally, disabilities such as vision impairment, limb differences, or difficulties with fine motor skills can further hinder access to telehealth [140].

The lack of accessibility and user-friendly design in technology reduces the likelihood of people utilising telehealth services. To address this, the World Health Organization and the International Telecommunication Union have developed guidelines to enhance telehealth accessibility for people with disabilities, including those with visual and hearing impairments, speech difficulties, physical disabilities, and developmental or intellectual disabilities [141]. Other guidelines have been developed by government bodies with the oversight of clinical safety, such as a Safer Care Victoria in Victoria, Australia [142]. Additionally, many allied health professional bodies have developed their own guidance for their members to assist in their use of telehealth [143].

Policy and practice recommendations

The findings highlight both the opportunities and challenges associated with telehealth, underscoring the need for policies and guidelines for telehealth use by allied health professionals that optimise its benefits while addressing its limitations. Limitations and strengths

This scoping review focuses on a highly diverse group of healthcare services. While the analysed articles do contain a wide spectrum of allied health services, there is a high representation of mental health services and physiotherapy. While this can be seen as a limitation in the representation of services, it does highlight two very different allied health modalities. The results offer a good baseline for understanding the perspectives of telehealth for allied health services. Further, the scoping review was limited to articles written in English, which might have excluded valuable evidence. The results do show a high representation of English-speaking and high-income countries, which limits the applicability to developing or low-income countries. Lastly, since the scoping review does not include critical appraisal of the included studies the findings and any conclusions drawn should be interpreted with caution.

Implications for future research

This scoping review highlighted the wealth of literature on mental health services and physiotherapy via telehealth. While speech therapy and occupational therapy are also often represented, other allied health professions such as pharmacists, substance use specialists, music therapists, and dietitians were less often mentioned. Future research should close the research gap on underrepresented allied health professions. Further, research should address how technology could be used to reduce the current disadvantages of telehealth. For example, an often-noted disadvantage is the limitation of the camera perspective. As technology improves and high-quality devices become more accessible to the wider population, it is of interest whether motion tracking would be a possible improvement to the current shortcomings of telehealth for physical therapies. Another technology of interest would be virtual reality telehealth as it would also reduce the above-mentioned shortcomings of traditional cameras, while also creating a virtual space shared by patient and provider. This could help improve the patient-provider relationship which currently falls short in traditional telehealth settings. Overall, while technology improves and opens up new avenues, research needs to identify which innovations are suitable to improve healthcare provision without unacceptable compromises in quality and safety.

Conclusion

Despite the wide variety of services provided by allied health professionals, common (dis-)advantages were noted by patients and providers regarding the usage of telehealth. Notably, while telehealth does increase access to healthcare and reduces time and money spent by patients, the lack of hands-on care and the negatively impacted patient-provider relationships do reduce its value. Nonetheless, telehealth offers benefits as an addition rather than a substitution for traditional face-to-face appointments in allied health care in certain circumstances.
