Sondre Tesdal Galtung, Katrin Grunert

Abstract
. Following conservative solutions of the nonlinear variational wave equation u t ​ t − c ⁡ ( u ) ​ ( c ⁡ ( u ) ​ u x ) x = 0 u_{tt}-c(u)(c(u)u_{x})_{x}=0 along forward and backward characteristics, we identify criteria, which guarantee that wave breaking either occurs in the nearby future or occurred recently. Thereafter, we apply the established criteria to show that not every traveling wave solution is a conservative solution. Furthermore, we show that conservative solutions can locally behave like solutions to the linear wave equation and hence energy that concentrates on sets of measure zero might remain concentrated instead of spreading out immediately.

1.
Introduction

We will here study the nonlinear variational wave equation, which for an unknown scalar
u=u(t,x)
reads

(1.1)

u_{tt}-c(u)(c(u)u_{x})_{x}=0,

where the real numbers
t
and
x
respectively represent time and position.
Equation (
1.1
) was derived in
[
19
]
as a simplified model for the director field of a nematic liquid crystal, where
u
represents the angle of director fields restricted to the unit circle.
The term
variational
comes from the fact that (
1.1
) is the Euler–Lagrange equation of the Lagrangian

\iint\left(u_{t}^{2}-(c(u)u_{x})^{2}\right)\,dx\,dt

associated with the Oseen–Frank theory of liquid crystals, see
[
9
, Section 2]
for a derivation.
On a related note, smooth solutions of (
1.1
) preserve the energy

(1.2)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(u_{t}^{2}+(c(u)u_{x})^{2}\right)\,dx.

In
[
15
]
the authors considered weakly nonlinear, unidirectional waves satisfying (
1.1
), and derived an asymptotic equation which turns out to be completely integrable.
This equation is now known as the Hunter–Saxton equation and has spawned its own vast literature, see e.g.
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
11
]
and the references therein.

1.1.
Singularity formation

The nonlinear variational wave equation (
1.1
) can be seen as a particular, in-between case in the one-parameter family of equations

(1.3)

u_{tt}=c(u)^{2-\alpha}(c(u)^{\alpha}u_{x})_{x},\qquad 0\leq\alpha\leq 2,

see
[
9
]
.
One extreme here is the nonlinear wave equation where
\alpha=0

(1.4)

u_{tt}-c(u)^{2}u_{xx}=0,

studied in
[
17
]
, which is expected to have globally smooth solutions.
The other extreme is the conservation law-like equation

(1.5)

u_{tt}-\left(c(u)^{2}u_{x}\right)_{x}=0.

In fact, (
1.5
) can be seen as a particular case of the so-called
p
-system
[
14
, Example 5.3]
, which reads
u_{t}=v_{x}
and
v_{t}=-(p(u))_{x}
for some pressure-like term
p
.
The choice
p^{\prime}(u)=-c(u)^{2}
yields (
1.5
) and ensures the system is hyperbolic with eigenvalues
\pm c(u)
, and, typical for such systems, we can expect shocks and discontinuous solutions.

If we write out the spatial derivative in the one-parameter family (
1.3
) for nonzero
\alpha
, we get a quadratic term in
u_{x}
, and the above discussion hints at this term being an obstacle for retaining smoothness of solutions.
Indeed, it drives singularity formation such as blow-up and oscillations, cf.
[
9
]
.
Our emphasis is on blow-up, and to this end we consider the quantities

(1.6)

R\coloneqq u_{t}+c(u)u_{x},\qquad S\coloneqq u_{t}-c(u)u_{x},

which are Riemann invariants for the linear wave equation.
That is, when
c(u)\equiv c>0
,
R
and
S
are constant along the backward and forward characteristics, respectively
x=-ct
and
x=ct
.
For smooth solutions of (
1.1
) we may rewrite the equation as a system of equations in
R
and
S
, namely

(1.7)

R_{t}-c(u)R_{x}=\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{4c(u)}\left(R^{2}-S^{2}\right),\qquad S_{t}+c(u)S_{x}=\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{4c(u)}\left(S^{2}-R^{2}\right).

Observe that we can add the two equations in (
1.7
) and divide by two to recover (
1.1
).
If we for simplicity replace the factors involving
c
and
c^{\prime}
in (
1.7
) with
1
, we obtain what is known as the Carleman system, for which the solutions
R
and
S
blow up along respectively
x=-t
and
x=t
for appropriate sign choices in the initial data. This blow-up mechanism is analogous to that of a Riccati equation.
The same idea is employed in
[
8
]
under additional assumptions on the sign of
c^{\prime}
.
It is much harder to predict blow-up when
c^{\prime}
is allowed to change sign, as this may delay or even prevent it from happening.

Clearly, one cannot expect (
1.1
) to have smooth solutions in general, and so a weaker notion of solution is needed beyond their breakdown.
Assuming
u
smooth enough, we have the pointwise identities

\left(R^{2}+S^{2}\right)_{t}-\left(c(u)(R^{2}-S^{2})\right)_{x}=0,\qquad\left(\frac{R^{2}-S^{2}}{c(u)}\right)_{t}-\left(R^{2}+S^{2}\right)_{x}=0,

where we recognize
\frac{1}{4}(R^{2}+S^{2})=\frac{1}{2}(u_{t}^{2}+(c(u)u_{x})^{2})
as the energy density from (
1.2
).
Let us then introduce the positive Radon measures
\mu
and
\nu
with their respective absolutely continuous parts
\frac{1}{4}R^{2}\,dx
and
\frac{1}{4}S^{2}\,dx
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
Based on the previous pointwise identities we then require these measures to satisfy the distributional identities

(1.8)

(\mu+\nu)_{t}-\left(c(u)(\mu-\nu)\right)_{x}=0,\qquad\left(\frac{\mu-\nu}{c(u)}\right)_{t}-(\mu+\nu)_{x}=0.

Together with a weak formulation of (
1.1
), this is how
conservative solutions
of the nonlinear variational wave equation were defined in
[
4
]
.
Here
(\mu+\nu)(t,\mathbb{R})
is the total energy, which is conserved in time for conservative solutions.
Recalling (
1.2
), this implies that
u_{x}^{2}
remains integrable, which in turn means that
u(t,\cdot)
is Hölder-continuous.
Hence, conservative solutions of (
1.1
) still have more regularity than we can expect from solutions of (
1.5
).

These conservative solutions are typically studied using characteristics, see
[
4
,
13
]
, which in this case consist of two families of curves with velocities
\pm c(u)
, as opposed to the straight lines of the linear wave equation.
The aforementioned Hunter–Saxton equation and the related Camassa–Holm equation share the conservative solution concept, and this is typically studied using characteristics, cf.
[
2
]
.
These equations model unidirectional wave propagation, and so there is only one family of characteristic curves traveling with velocity
u
, owing to the Burgers advection term
uu_{x}
featured in both equations.
This makes them considerably easier to study in comparison to (
1.1
) with its two families of characteristics.
For instance, with these characteristics one typically changes to so-called Lagrangian coordinates where one replaces the Eulerian coordinate
x
in a fixed reference frame with
\xi
by following a given characteristic, i.e.,
(t,x)\mapsto(t,\xi)
.
For the nonlinear variational wave equation, the change to Lagrangian coordinates involves two such labels and a coordinate change in both space and time,
(t,x)\mapsto(\xi,\eta)
, see
[
13
]
.
Furthermore, characteristics have been used to show uniqueness of conservative solutions in
[
3
]
.
On a similar note, we mention that (
1.1
) appears as an example in an application of so-called singular characteristics
[
18
, Section 8.4]
, see also
[
16
]
.

1.2.
Prediction of wave-breaking

For the Hunter–Saxton and Camassa–Holm equations, blow-up happens for the slope
u_{x}
.
Since their energy densities contain the term
u_{x}^{2}
,
u
will remain both bounded and Hölder-continuous while this happens, which leads to a vertical wave profile at these points.
This is called wave-breaking and leads to the breakdown of classical solutions.
An analogous mechanism drives wave-breaking for (
1.1
) where blow-up happens for one or both of
R
and
S
, while
u
remains bounded.
Indeed, one such blow-up result for (
1.1
) is found in
[
8
, Theorem 1]
where the initial data takes a special form.
That is, assuming
c^{\prime}(\bar{u})\neq 0
for some constant
\bar{u}\in\mathbb{R}
, one takes

u(0,x)=\bar{u}+\varepsilon\phi\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right),\qquad u_{t}(0,x)=-\sgn(c^{\prime}(\bar{u}))c(u(0,x))u_{x}(0,x)

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
and a nonzero, compactly supported
\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(0,1)
.
Moreover,
c\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is assumed strictly positive, and bounded from both below and above.
For such data, depending on the sign of
c^{\prime}(\bar{u})
, exactly one of
R
and
S
will initially be identically zero.
The idea is then to show that one of
R
and
S
will remain of order
\varepsilon
, while the other will blow up along a characteristic in finite time.

Let us mention some other (non-)existence results related to the nonlinear variational wave equation (
1.1
).
A local-in-time existence result for classical solutions is found in
[
22
]
, together with global-in-time existence results for what they call rarefactive solutions where one assumes
c^{\prime}(x)>0
and
R(0,x),S(0,x)\leq 0
.
In the latter case, we may observe that the combination of signs prohibits
R
and
S
in (
1.7
) from blowing up along their appropriate characteristics.
Another potential problem may occur when the wave speed
c
is not bounded away from zero; this is called finite-time degeneracy and is studied in
[
20
]
.The same author
[
21
]
extended the aforementioned blow-up result of
[
8
]
to (
1.3
) for
\alpha\in(0,1]
under similar assumptions.

The aim of this paper is to give more new, more general and precise estimates on when to expect wave-breaking for (
1.1
), in the spirit of earlier works on related equations, e.g.,
[
10
]
.
Before we summarize the content of these result, we will state our assumptions on the wave speed
c=c(u)
in the remainder of the paper.
We assume there exists
\kappa>1
such that

(1.9)

\frac{1}{\kappa}\leq c(x)\leq\kappa\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, there exists
\lambda\geq 0
and
\bar{\lambda}\geq 0
such that

(1.10)

|c^{\prime}(x)|\leq\lambda\quad\text{ and }\quad|c^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq\bar{\lambda}\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Next we give a summary of our main results, that is, sufficient conditions on the initial data for us to predict wave-breaking for (
1.1
), and to this end we denote by
u_{0}
,
R_{0}
, and
S_{0}
the initial values of
u
,
R
, and
S
.
Moreover, let us write
E_{0}
for the initial energy
(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R})
.
In a similar vein to previous results, we will leverage the Riccati-type blow-up mechanism, but here we shall do so in Lagrangian coordinates.
Since the blow-up of either
R
or
S
corresponds to wave-breaking, this enables us to give a lower bound
t_{l}
and an upper bound
t_{u}
for when this happens.

For instance, the quantity
R
may blow up along backward characteristics, that is, characteristics moving with velocity
-c(u)
.
If the initial condition
R_{0}
satisfies our condition for blow-up, then, depending on the sign of
c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))R_{0}(\bar{x})
,
R
will either have already have blown up in the past, or will blow up in the future.

To be more precise, consider a point
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}
:

•

Blow-up along backward characteristics
: If
\left|R_{0}(\bar{x})\right|
is large enough compared to
\lambda\kappa^{2}E_{0}
, and additionally satisfies an inequality which involves
\left|c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))\right|
, then
R
blows up along a backward characteristic.
This blow-up happens in the future if
c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))R_{0}(\bar{x})
is positive, otherwise it has happened in the past.

•

Blow-up along forward characteristic
: If
\left|S_{0}(\bar{x})\right|
is large enough compared to
\lambda\kappa^{2}E_{0}
, and additionally satisfies an inequality which involves
\left|c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))\right|
, then
S
blows up along a forward characteristic.
This blow-up happens in the future if
c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))S_{0}(\bar{x})
is positive, otherwise it has happened in the past.

In both cases we are able to determine a time interval
[t_{l},t_{u}]
where wave-breaking happens, and we refer to Theorems
3.1
–
3.4
in Section 3 for the details.

As an application of our results, we will in Section 4 use our wave-breaking criteria to prove that a traveling wave, described in
[
8
]
as “hut”-shaped, is not a conservative solution of the nonlinear variational wave equation.
This is done by showing that the conservative solution evolving from the initial traveling-wave profile must exhibit a form of wave-breaking which is incompatible with the evolution of the traveling wave.
As such, this is an alternative to verifying that the measure-equations (
1.8
) do not hold for this traveling wave.

We finish Section 4 with an example that highlights a major difference between (
1.1
) and the Hunter–Saxton and Camassa–Holm equations, namely that concentrated energy need not immediately spread out again, but may remain concentrated over time.
This can happen when the derivative of the wave speed
c=c(u)
is zero.
In our example, the left- and right-moving Radon measures
\mu
and
\nu
associated with the concentrated energy behave locally like a solution of the linear wave equation, that is, they move with constant velocities.

2.
Background

The prediction of wave breaking is based on a good understanding of how wave breaking can be described and identified. In the case of conservative solutions the unique weak solution to any admissible initial data can be derived via a generalized method of characteristics, see
[
13
]
and
[
3
]
. Thus, to predict wave breaking one needs to understand well the interplay between Eulerian and Lagrangian coordinates as well as the underlying system of partial differential equations in Lagrangian coordinates. Since the generalized method of characteristics for conservative solutions has been discussed in detail in
[
13
]
, we here focus, on the one hand, on sketching the method of characteristics from
[
13
]
and, on the other hand, on how wave breaking is characterized in both Eulerian and Lagrangian coordinates.

In the case of the linear wave equation, i.e.,
c(u)=c\in\mathbb{R}
, every classical solution is given by d’Alemberts formula, which implies

u(t,x)=f(x+ct)+g(x-ct).

That is, for
c>0
,
u(t,x)
consists of one part,
f
, traveling to the left and one part,
g
, traveling to the right with speed
c
. Therefore one can state the initial data at
t=0
in terms of
f
and
g
as

(u_{0}(x),(u_{0,t}+cu_{0,x})(x),(u_{0,t}-cu_{0,x})(x))=((f+g)(x),2cf^{\prime}(x),-2cg^{\prime}(x)),

instead of using
u_{0}
and
u_{0,t}
. Furthermore, every classical solution to the linear wave equation can be computed using the classical method of characteristics. This means that one introduces a change of variables
(t,x)\to(\xi,\eta)
, such that the backward and forward characteristics, given by
y(t,\xi)=\xi-ct
and
z(t,\eta)=\eta+ct
, are mapped to vertical and horizontal lines, respectively. This is achieved by implicitly defining
(\xi,\eta)\mapsto(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))
as

(2.1)

x(\xi,\eta)=y(t(\xi,\eta),\xi)=z(t(\xi,\eta),\eta).

Or, in other words,
(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))
denotes the unique intersection point of
y(t,\xi)
and
z(t,\eta)
. Moreover, (
2.1
) implies that

x_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)=ct_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)\quad\text{ and }\quad x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)=-ct_{\eta}(\xi,\eta),

which is the Lagrangian formulation of the forward and backward characteristics.

As a consequence, one has, in this case, that the line
\{(0,x)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for which the initial data is defined, is mapped to the curve
\bar{\mathcal{C}}=\{(\xi,\eta)\mid\xi=\eta\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, along which the initial data in Lagrangian coordinates is given by 3 triplets

(2.2a)

\displaystyle(t(\xi,\xi),x(\xi,\xi),U(\xi,\xi))

\displaystyle=(0,\xi,u_{0}(\xi)),

(2.2b)

\displaystyle(t_{\xi}(\xi,\xi),x_{\xi}(\xi,\xi),U_{\xi}(\xi,\xi))

\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{2c},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2c}(u_{0,t}+cu_{0,x})(\xi)),

(2.2c)

\displaystyle(t_{\eta}(\xi,\xi),x_{\eta}(\xi,\xi),U_{\eta}(\xi,\xi))

\displaystyle=(-\frac{1}{2c},\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2c}(u_{0,t}-cu_{0,x})(\xi)).

To compute
(t,x,U)
on all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, one solves the reformulation of the linear wave equation in Lagrangian coordinates, which is given by

(t_{\xi,\eta}(\xi,\eta),x_{\xi,\eta}(\xi,\eta),U_{\xi,\eta}(\xi,\eta))=(0,0,0)\quad\text{ for all }(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

for the initial data given by (
2.2
).

To finally extract the solution in Eulerian coordinates from the tuplets
((t,x,U),

(t_{\xi},x_{\xi},U_{\xi}),(t_{\eta},x_{\eta},U_{\eta}))
, one uses the following relations

(2.3a)

\displaystyle u(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))

\displaystyle=U(\xi,\eta),

(2.3b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c}(u_{t}+cu_{x})(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))x_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=U_{\xi}(\xi,\eta),

(2.3c)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{c}(u_{t}-cu_{x})(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=U_{\eta}(\xi,\eta).

In the case of the nonlinear variational wave equation, no analogue to d’Alembert’s formula is known, but a generalized method of characteristics, which mimics the approach for the linear wave equation, makes it possible to follow the solution along backward and forward characteristics, which are given by

(2.4)

y_{t}(t,\xi)=-c(u(t,y(t,\xi)))\quad\text{ and }\quad z_{t}(t,\eta)=c(u(t,z(t,\eta))),

respectively. Thus each initial data at
t=0
will contain triplets

(u_{0},R_{0},S_{0})=(u_{0},u_{0,t}+c(u_{0})u_{0,x},u_{0,t}-c(u_{0})u_{0,x}).

On the other hand, if wave breaking occurs in the future, which is not possible for the linear wave equation, either
(u_{t}+c(u)u_{x})(t,x)
or
(u_{t}-c(u)u_{x})(t,x)
become unbounded pointwise and energy may concentrate on sets of measure zero either along backward or forward characteristics, respectively. Thus the set of admissible initial data consists of tuplets
(u_{0},R_{0},S_{0},\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
, where
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
are positive finite Radon measures, which describe the concentration of energy and split the total energy
\mu_{0}+\nu_{0}
into two parts.

Definition 2.1

(Eulerian coordinates)
.

The set
\mathcal{D}
consists of all tuplets
(u,R,S,\mu,\nu)
such that

(u,R,S)=(u,u_{t}+c(u)u_{x},u_{t}-c(u)u_{x})\in[L^{2}(\mathbb{R})]^{3}

and
\mu
and
\nu
are positive, finite Radon measures whose absolutely continuous parts
\mu_{ac}
and
\nu_{ac}
satisfy

(2.5)

d\mu_{ac}=\frac{1}{4}R^{2}dx\quad\text{ and }\quad d\nu_{ac}=\frac{1}{4}S^{2}dx.

As for the linear wave equation, one needs to associate to any initial data
(u_{0},R_{0},S_{0},\mu_{0},\nu_{0})\in\mathcal{D}
a corresponding set of Lagrangian coordinates
(Z,Z_{\xi},Z_{\eta})
, which is given along a curve

\bar{\mathcal{C}}=\{(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s),\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid s\in\mathbb{R}\}.

The definition of this curve is based on the measures
(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})
, which contain all the information about initial energy concentration. In particular,
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
must guarantee that when energy concentrates in single point, then the spreading out of the energy afterwards mimics in a sense the behavior of a rarefaction wave. Therefore, introduce

(2.6a)

\displaystyle x_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid x+\mu_{0}((-\infty,x))<X\},

(2.6b)

\displaystyle x_{2}(Y)

\displaystyle=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid x+\nu_{0}((-\infty,x))<Y\},

which are increasing and Lipschitz continuous functions, then

\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s)=\sup\{X\in\mathbb{R}\mid x_{1}(X^{\prime})<x_{2}(2s-X^{\prime})\text{ for all }X^{\prime}<X\}

and
\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s)=2s-\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s)
. Note that both
\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s)
and
\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s)
are increasing, Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant at most
2
, and

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s)=\pm\infty=\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s),

which implies that to any given
(\xi,\eta)\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
, there exists a unique
s\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\xi+\eta=2s
and
(\xi,\eta)=(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s),\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s))
.

Next, introduce

(2.7)

\bar{x}(s)=x_{1}(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s))=x_{2}(\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s)),

then

(2.8)

\bar{x}(s)=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid 2x+(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})((-\infty,x))<2s\},

which implies that for
\mathcal{S}=\bar{x}(\mathcal{B})
, where

(2.9)

\mathcal{B}=\{s\mid\dot{\bar{x}}(s)=0\},

one has

(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})_{\mathrm{ac}}=(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})|_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\quad\text{ and }\quad(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})_{\mathrm{sing}}=(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})|_{\mathcal{S}}.

Furthermore, the points at which wave breaking occurs initially in Eulerian coordinates correspond to the set
\mathcal{B}
in Lagrangian coordinates. However,
\bar{J}(s)=2s-2\bar{x}(s)
in Lagrangian coordinates corresponds to
\mu_{0}+\nu_{0}
in Eulerian coordinates and hence does not allow to distinguish between
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
. On the other hand, we can use (
2.6
) to split
\bar{J}(s)
as follows

\bar{J}(s)=\bar{J}_{1}(s)+\bar{J}_{2}(s)=J_{1}(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s))+J_{2}(\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s))

where

(2.10)

J_{1}(X)=X-x_{1}(X)\quad\text{ and }\quad J_{2}(Y)=Y-x_{2}(Y),

which will be important later. Last, but not least we introduce

\bar{U}(s)=u_{0}(\bar{x}(s)).

With all these definitions in place we can define the initial Lagrangian coordinates along the curve
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
by

Z(\xi,\eta)=(t,x,U,J)(\xi,\eta)=(0,\bar{x}(s),\bar{U}(s),\bar{J}(s))\quad\text{ for }(\xi,\eta)=(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s),\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s))\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}.

In analogy to the linear wave equation, we also need to know
Z_{\xi}
and
Z_{\eta}
on the curve
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
before we can turn our attention towards the underlying system of partial differential equations. Here it is important, that we keep the properties, which we highlighted for the linear wave equation,
i.e., following vertical and horizonal lines in the
(\xi,\eta)
plane corresponds to following backward and forward characteristics, respectively, since

(2.11)

x(\xi,\eta)=y(t(\xi,\eta),\xi)=z(t(\xi,\eta),\eta),

and

(2.12)

x_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)=c(U)t_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)\quad\text{ and }\quad x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)=-c(U)t_{\eta}(\xi,\eta),

as well as add some additional properties, which are related to (
2.5
), namely

(2.13)

(c(U)U_{\xi})^{2}(\xi,\eta)=2x_{\xi}J_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)\quad\text{ and }\quad(c(U)U_{\eta})^{2}(\xi,\eta)=2x_{\eta}J_{\eta}(\xi,\eta),

for all
(\xi,\eta)\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
.

Inspired by (
2.2
), one defines
Z_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)
and
Z_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)
for
(\xi,\eta)=(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s),\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s))\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
as follows

\displaystyle Z_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=(t_{\xi},x_{\xi},U_{\xi},J_{\xi})(\xi,\eta)

(2.14a)

\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{2c(\bar{U}(s))}x_{1}^{\prime}(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s)),\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{\prime}(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s)),\frac{R(\bar{x}(s))}{2c(\bar{U}(s))}x_{1}^{\prime}(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s)),J_{1}^{\prime}(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s)))

\displaystyle Z_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=(t_{\eta},x_{\eta},U_{\eta},J_{\eta})(\xi,\eta)

(2.14b)

\displaystyle=(-\frac{1}{2c(\bar{U}(s))}x_{2}^{\prime}(\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s)),\frac{1}{2}x_{2}^{\prime}(\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s)),-\frac{S(\bar{x}(s))}{2c(\bar{U}(s))}x_{2}^{\prime}(\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s)),J_{2}^{\prime}(\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s))).

Here it is important to note that wave breaking occurs initially at all those points
(\xi,\eta)=(\bar{}\mathcal{X}(s),\bar{}\mathcal{Y}(s))\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
such that either
(x_{\xi},U_{\xi})(\xi,\eta)=(0,0)
or
(x_{\eta},U_{\eta})(\xi,\eta)=(0,0)
, since
\mathcal{B}
, given by (
2.9
), contains all points for which wave breaking occurs initially, and
\bar{x}(s)
is related to
x_{1}(X)
and
x_{2}(Y)
by (
2.7
).

To compute
(t,x,U,J)
on all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, in the next step, one uses a reformulation of the nonlinear variational wave equation in Lagrangian coordinates, which is given by

(2.15a)

\displaystyle t_{\xi,\eta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}(t_{\xi}U_{\eta}+t_{\eta}U_{\xi}),

(2.15b)

\displaystyle x_{\xi,\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}(x_{\xi}U_{\eta}+x_{\eta}U_{\xi}),

(2.15c)

\displaystyle U_{\xi,\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c^{3}(U)}(x_{\xi}J_{\eta}+x_{\eta}J_{\xi})-\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}U_{\xi}U_{\eta},

(2.15d)

\displaystyle J_{\xi,\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}(J_{\xi}U_{\eta}+J_{\eta}U_{\xi}).

In
[
13
]
it is shown that the above system has a unique solution in a rather complicated space. For us only some properties satisfied by the solutions are of particular interest. The triplet
(Z,Z_{\xi},Z_{\eta})
belongs to the following space,

Z\in[W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})]^{4},

for almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

Z_{\xi}(\xi,\cdot)=(t_{\xi},x_{\xi},U_{\xi},J_{\xi})(\xi,\cdot)\in[W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})]^{4},

and for almost every
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,

Z_{\eta}(\cdot,\eta)=(t_{\eta},x_{\eta},U_{\eta},J_{\eta})(\cdot,\eta)\in[W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})]^{4}.

Moreover, observe that by (
2.6
), (
2.10
), and (
2.14
)

(2.16)

(2x_{\xi}+J_{\xi})(\xi,\eta)=1\quad\text{and} \quad(2x_{\eta}+J_{\eta})(\xi,\eta)=1\quad\text{for all }(\xi,\eta)\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}.

Although these relations are not preserved by the system of differential equations (
2.15
), a slightly weaker result holds. Namely, given
L>0
, let

(2.17)

S_{L}=\{(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\mathrm{dist}((\xi,\eta),\bar{\mathcal{C}})<L\}.

Then, there exist
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, dependent on
L
, such that we have for all
(\xi,\eta)\in S_{L}

(2.18)

0<C_{1}<(2x_{\xi}+J_{\xi})(\xi,\eta)<C_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad 0<C_{1}<(2x_{\eta}+J_{\eta})(\xi,\eta)<C_{2}.

Furthermore, one has

(2.19)

J(\xi,\eta)\to 0\quad\text{ as }\quad\xi+\eta\to-\infty,

and

(2.20)

J(\xi,\eta)\to(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{ as }\quad\xi+\eta\to\infty,

Observe that (
2.18
) states that if
x_{\xi}(\bar{\xi},\eta)
tends to
0
as
(\bar{\xi},\eta)\to(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})
, then
J_{\xi}(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})
is strictly positive, and hence by (
2.13
), we have that

\left(c(U)\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}\right)^{2}(\bar{\xi},\eta)=2\frac{J_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}(\bar{\xi},\eta)\to\infty\quad\text{as }(\bar{\xi},\eta)\to(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta}).

Following the same lines of reasoning, we have that if
x_{\eta}(\xi,\bar{\eta})
tends to
0
as
(\xi,\bar{\eta})\to(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})
, then

\left(c(U)\frac{U_{\eta}}{x_{\eta}}\right)^{2}(\xi,\bar{\eta})=2\frac{J_{\eta}}{x_{\eta}}(\xi,\bar{\eta})\to\infty\quad\text{as }(\xi,\bar{\eta})\to(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta}).

Or in other words, if wave breaking occurs at a point
(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})
in Lagrangian coordinates, which happens if either
x_{\xi}(\bar{\xi},\eta)
or
x_{\eta}(\xi,\bar{\eta})
tend to
0
, then

(2.21)

\left(\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}\right)^{2}(\bar{\xi},\eta)\quad\text{ or }\quad\left(\frac{U_{\eta}}{x_{\eta}}\right)^{2}(\xi,\bar{\eta})\quad\text{tend to }\infty.

Furthermore, by (
2.12
)

(2.22)

t_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)\geq 0\quad\text{ and }\quad t_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)\leq 0,

which means that the connected set

E_{T}=\{(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid t(\xi,\eta)=T\}

need not be a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In fact, it may consist of the union of a curve with a countable number of rectangles. Furthermore, for
T>0
, the set
E_{T}
will lie below
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
. Thus, to go back from Lagrangian to Eulerian coordinates at time
T
, one needs to extract a curve
\mathcal{C}_{T}\subset E_{T}
. Since this curve is in general not unique, one makes a specific choice. Namely,

\mathcal{C}_{T}=\{(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))\mid s\in\mathbb{R}\},

where

\mathcal{X}(s)=\sup\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}\mid t(\xi^{\prime},2s-\xi^{\prime})<T\quad\text{ for all }\xi^{\prime}<\xi\},

and
\mathcal{Y}(s)=2s-\mathcal{X}(s)
.

Through the values of
U(\xi,\eta)
along
\mathcal{C}_{T}
, we can define the function
u(T,X)
as follows

u(T,X)=U(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))\quad\text{ if }x(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))=X.

For the functions
R
and
S
, we can use, in analogy to (
2.3
), that

\displaystyle R(T,X)x_{\xi}(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))

\displaystyle=c(u(T,X))U_{\xi}(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))

\displaystyle\quad\text{ for any }s\text{ such that }x(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))=X\text{ and }\dot{}\mathcal{X}(s)>0,

and

\displaystyle S(T,X)x_{\eta}(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))

\displaystyle=-c(u(T,X))U_{\eta}(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))

\displaystyle\quad\text{ for any }s\text{ such that }x(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))=X\text{ and }\dot{}\mathcal{Y}(s)>0.

In view of (
2.21
), one has that if
x_{\xi}(\bar{\xi},\eta)\to 0
as
(\bar{\xi},\eta)\to(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
, then

R^{2}(\bar{t},\bar{x})\to\infty\quad\text{ as }(\bar{t},\bar{x})=(t,x)(\bar{\xi},\eta)\to(t,x)(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})=(T,X).

If, on the other hand,
x_{\eta}(\xi,\bar{\eta})\to 0
as
(\xi,\bar{\eta})\to(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
, then

S^{2}(\bar{t},\bar{x})\to\infty\quad\text{ as }(\bar{t},\bar{x})=(t,x)(\bar{\xi},\eta)\to(t,x)(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})=(T,X).

This means that if wave breaking occurs in Lagrangian coordinates, which corresponds to
x_{\xi}=0
or
x_{\eta}=0
, then either
R^{2}
or
S^{2}
in Eulerian coordinates blow up.

To complete the tuplet
(u,R,S,\mu,\nu)
, it remains to extract the measures
\mu
and
\nu
. Therefore, introduce
x(s)=x(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\mu=x_{\#}(J_{\xi}(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))\dot{}\mathcal{X}(s)ds)

and

\nu=x_{\#}(J_{\eta}(\mathcal{X}(s),\mathcal{Y}(s))\dot{}\mathcal{Y}(s)ds).

Here we want to highlight that
\mu
and
\nu
are positive Radon measure, since
Z_{\xi}
and
Z_{\eta}
not only satisfy (
2.12
) and (
2.22
), but also

J_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)\geq 0\quad\text{ and }\quad J_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)\geq 0,

which are conserved in the following sense

(2.23)

(\mu+\nu)(T,\mathbb{R})=(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R}).

Furthermore, one has as earlier, for
\tilde{S}=x(\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\{s\mid\dot{x}(s)=0\}
, that

(\mu+\nu)(T)_{\mathrm{ac}}=(\mu+\nu)(T)|_{\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{c}}\quad\text{ and }\quad(\mu+\nu)(T)_{\mathrm{sing}}=(\mu+\nu)(T)|_{\tilde{\mathcal{S}}}.

Last but not least we want to highlight and prove one property of the solution
u(t,x)
, which is essential in the next sections.

Lemma 2.2
.

The function
u(t,x)
is globally Hölder continuous, i.e., there exists a constant
D
, dependent on
\kappa
and
(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R})
, such that

|u(t_{1},x_{1})-u(t_{2},x_{2})|\leq D\sqrt{|t_{2}-t_{1}|+|x_{2}-x_{1}|}\quad\text{ for all }(t_{1},x_{1}),(t_{2},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
(t_{1},x_{1})
,
(t_{2},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
t_{1}<t_{2}
. Then there exist
(\xi_{1},\eta_{1})
and
(\xi_{2},\eta_{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, not necessarily unique, such that

(t_{i},x_{i},u(t_{i},x_{i}))=(t,x,U)(\xi_{i},\eta_{i})\quad i\in\{1,2\}.

Furthermore, there exists a point
(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

(2.24)

\bar{\xi}+\bar{\eta}=B=\xi_{2}+\eta_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad(t,x,U)(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})=(t_{1},\bar{x},u(t_{1},\bar{x})),

which implies that

\displaystyle|x_{1}-\bar{x}|

\displaystyle\leq|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|x_{2}-\bar{x}|=|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|x(\xi_{2},\eta_{2})-x(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})|.

By (
1.9
), (
2.12
), (
2.22
), and (
2.24
), the last term on the right hand side satisfies

\displaystyle|x(\xi_{2},\eta_{2})-x(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})|

\displaystyle=|\int_{\bar{\xi}}^{\xi_{2}}(x_{\xi}-x_{\eta})(\tilde{\xi},B-\tilde{\xi})d\tilde{\xi}|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa|\int_{\bar{\xi}}^{\xi_{2}}(t_{\xi}-t_{\eta})(\tilde{\xi},B-\tilde{\xi})d\tilde{\xi}|

\displaystyle=\kappa|t(\xi_{2},\eta_{2})-t(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})|=\kappa |t_{2}-t_{1}|

and

|x_{1}-\bar{x}|\leq\kappa|t_{2}-t_{1}|+|x_{2}-x_{1}|.

To obtain the anticipated Hölder estimate, write

(2.25)

|u(t_{1},x_{1})-u(t_{2},x_{2})|\leq|u(t_{1},x_{1})-u(t_{1},\bar{x})|+|u(t_{1},\bar{x})-u(t_{2},x_{2})|.

For the first term on the right hand side, observe that by Definition
2.1

2c(u)u_{x}(t_{1},x)=(R-S)(t_{1},x)\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}

and hence

\displaystyle|u(t_{1},x_{1})-u(t_{1},\bar{x})|

\displaystyle=|\int_{\bar{x}}^{x_{1}}\frac{1}{2c(u)}(R-S)(t_{1},\tilde{x})d\tilde{x}|

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa\sqrt{(\mu+\nu)(t_{1},\mathbb{R})}\sqrt{|\bar{x}-x_{1}|}

(2.26)

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa\sqrt{(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R})}\sqrt{\kappa|t_{2}-t_{1}|+|x_{2}-x_{1}|},

where we also used (
2.23
).

For the second term on the right hand side of (
2.25
), note that since
t_{1}<t_{2}
, (
2.24
) and (
2.22
) imply that

\bar{\xi}<\xi_{2},\quad\bar{\eta}>\eta_{2},\quad\text{ and }\quad t_{1}\leq t(\bar{\xi},\eta_{2})\leq t_{2}.

Combining next the above relations with (
2.13
), (
2.12
), (
2.22
), (
2.19
), and (
2.20
) yields

\displaystyle|U(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})-U(\bar{\xi},\eta_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\kappa}\int_{\eta_{2}}^{\bar{\eta}}\sqrt{(-t_{\eta}J_{\eta})(\bar{\xi},\tilde{\eta})}d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\kappa}\sqrt{|J(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})-J(\bar{\xi},\eta_{2})|}\sqrt{|t(\bar{\xi},\eta_{2})-t(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})|}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\kappa}\sqrt{(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R})}\sqrt{|t_{2}-t_{1}|}

and following the same lines

|U(\bar{\xi},\eta_{2})-U(\xi_{2},\eta_{2})|\leq\sqrt{2\kappa}\sqrt{(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R})}\sqrt{|t_{2}-t_{1}|}.

Thus

\displaystyle|u(t_{1},\bar{x})-u(t_{2},x_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq|U(\bar{\xi},\bar{\eta})-U(\bar{\xi},\eta_{2})|+|U(\bar{\xi},\eta_{2})-U(\xi_{2},\eta_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{8\kappa}\sqrt{(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R})}\sqrt{|t_{2}-t_{1}|},

which combined with (
2.25
) and (
2.26
) finishes the proof.
∎

3.
Prediction of wave breaking

In the last section, we outlined that wave breaking occurs along backward characteristics, which correspond to vertical lines in the
(\xi,\eta)
plane, if
x_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)
tends to zero, while wave breaking occurs along forward characteristics, which correspond to horizontal lines in the
(\xi,\eta)
plane, if
x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)
tends to zero. In this section we will present some criteria, which allow to predict whether or not wave breaking either will take place in the near future or occurred recently. Again, we will distinguish between following backward and forward characteristics to differ between
R(t,x)=(u_{t}+c(u)u_{x})(t,x)
and
S(t,x)=(u_{t}-c(u)u_{x})(t,x)
becoming unbounded pointwise.

3.1.
Wave breaking along backward characteristics

Let
(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which corresponds to the point
(0,\bar{x})=(t(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}),x(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))
in Eulerian coordinates, such that
x_{\xi}(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})>0
. Then wave breaking occurs along the backward characteristic
y(t,\xi_{0})
if the function
R(t,y(t,\xi_{0}))=(u_{t}+c(u)u_{x})(t,y(t,\xi_{0}))
becomes unbounded, which, as highlighted in the last section, is equivalent to
x_{\xi}(\xi_{0},\eta)
tending to
0
along the vertical line
\{(\xi_{0},\eta)\mid\eta\in\mathbb{R}\}
in the
(\xi,\eta)
plane. A natural question that arises in this context is the following: Does there exist a point
(\xi_{0},\bar{\eta})
close to
(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})
such that

x_{\xi}(\xi_{0},\eta)\to 0
as
\eta\to\bar{\eta}
? If so, wave breaking either occurred recently or will take place in the near future and it then remains to estimate the actual breaking time.

As a starting point we consider the function

(3.1)

f(\xi_{0},\eta)=\sqrt{\frac{c(U(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))}{c(U(\xi_{0},\eta))}}x_{\xi}(\xi_{0},\eta).

Here two observations are important. First, due to (
1.9
),
f(\xi_{0},\eta)\to 0
as
\eta\to\bar{\eta}
is equivalent to
x_{\xi}(\xi_{0},\eta)\to 0
as
\eta\to\bar{\eta}
. Second,
f(\xi_{0},\eta)
satisfies, in contrast to
x_{\xi}(\xi_{0},\eta)
, cf. (
2.15
), a homogeneous linear differential equation. Namely,

f_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\eta)=\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}f(\xi_{0},\eta),

which implies that

f(\xi_{0},\eta)=f(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})e^{\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}}.

By (
3.1
)
f(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})>0
, since, by assumption,
x_{\xi}(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})>0
and
c(x)
satisfies (
1.9
), and hence
f(\xi_{0},\eta)
can only tend to zero as
\eta\to\bar{\eta}
if

(3.2)

\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\rightarrow-\infty.

Thus our goal is to establish a criterion, which guarantees that the above integral tends to
-\infty
for
\bar{\eta}
close to
\eta_{0}
, which can be formulated in Eulerian coordinates. The key element of our argument is the function

(3.3)

h(\xi_{0},\eta)=c(U)\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}(\xi_{0},\eta),

since (
3.2
) can only hold if
c^{\prime}(U)h(\xi_{0},\eta)
becomes unbounded, due to (
1.9
) and (
2.18
). Moreover, by (
1.10
)
c^{\prime}
is bounded but can attain the value
0
, so that

c^{\prime}(U)h(\xi_{0},\eta)
can only become unbounded if
h(\xi_{0},\eta)
becomes unbounded. In a next step, we will therefore analyse when
h(\xi_{0},\eta)
can become unbounded by having a closer look at the underlying first order differential equation, which is given by

(3.4)

h_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\eta)=\alpha(\xi_{0},\eta)+\gamma h^{2}(\xi_{0},\eta),

where

\alpha(\xi_{0},\eta)=\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c^{2}(U)}J_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\eta)

and

(3.5)

\gamma(\xi_{0},\eta)=-\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{4c^{2}(U)}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\eta).

Note that by (
2.22
), (
2.12
), and (
2.13
), the signs of
\alpha(\xi_{0},\eta)
and
-\gamma(\xi_{0},\eta)
coincide with the sign of
c^{\prime}(U)(\xi_{0},\eta)

Assume that
\eta_{0}\leq\eta
, which implies, using (
2.22
), that
t(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\geq t(\xi_{0},\eta)
and hence will yield a criterion for recent wave breaking. Then (
3.2
) requires

c^{\prime}(U)h(\xi_{0},\eta)\to-\infty\quad\text{ as }\quad\eta\to\bar{\eta},

for some
\bar{\eta}\geq\eta_{0}
. Therefore, we will distinguish two cases dependent on the signs of
c^{\prime}(U)(\xi_{0},\eta)
and
h(\xi_{0},\eta)
.

Case 1:

Assume there exists an interval
[\eta_{0},\eta_{1}]
such that

h(\xi_{0},\eta)<0\quad\text{ and }\quad c^{\prime}(U)(\xi_{0},\eta)>0\quad\text{ for all }\eta\in[\eta_{0},\eta_{1}],

and hence

(3.6)

\alpha(\xi_{0},\eta)\geq 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\gamma(\xi_{0},\eta)\leq 0\quad\text{ for all } \eta\in[\eta_{0},\eta_{1}].

Then (
3.4
) implies

h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})+\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\gamma h^{2}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\leq h(\xi_{0},\eta)\leq a+\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\gamma h^{2}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

for any real number
a
which satisfies

(3.7)

h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})+\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta_{1}}\alpha(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\leq a<0.

Furthermore, the above integral inequality fulfills all the assumptions from Proposition
A.1
due to (
3.6
) and we end up with

\frac{h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})}{1-h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\gamma(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}}\leq h(\xi_{0},\eta)\leq\frac{a}{1-a\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\gamma(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}},

where
a
can be any real number which satisfies (
3.7
).

Case 2:
Assume there exists an interval
[\eta_{0},\eta_{1}]
such that

h(\xi_{0},\eta)>0\quad\text{ and }\quad c^{\prime}(U)(\xi_{0},\eta)<0\quad\text{ for all }\eta\in[\eta_{0},\eta_{1}],

and hence

\alpha(\xi_{0},\eta)\leq 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\gamma(\xi_{0},\eta)\geq 0\quad\text{ for all } \eta\in[\eta_{0},\eta_{1}].

Then
\check{h}(\xi_{0},\eta)=-h(\xi_{0},\eta)
satisfies the differential equation

\check{h}_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\eta)=\check{\alpha}(\xi_{0},\eta)+\check{\gamma}\check{h}^{2}(\xi_{0},\eta),

where

\check{\alpha}(\xi_{0},\eta)=-\alpha(\xi_{0},\eta)\geq 0

and

\check{\gamma}(\xi_{0},\eta)=-\gamma(\xi_{0},\eta)\leq 0

for all
\eta\in[\eta_{0},\eta_{1}]
. This means
\check{h}(\xi_{0},\eta)
satisfies all the assumptions from Case 1 and
we obtain

\frac{\check{h}(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})}{1-\check{h}(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\check{\gamma}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}}\leq\check{h}(\xi_{0},\eta)\leq\frac{\check{a}}{1-\check{a}\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\check{\gamma}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}},

where
\check{a}
can be any real number which satisfies

\check{h}(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})+\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta_{1}}\check{\alpha}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\leq\check{a}<0.

Thus we end up with

\frac{h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})}{1-h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\gamma(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}}\geq h(\xi_{0},\eta)\geq\frac{a}{1-a\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\gamma(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}},

where
a
can be any real number such that

(3.8)

0<a\leq h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})+\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta_{1}}\alpha(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}.

To finally obtain an upper and a lower bound on (
3.2
), note that

\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\eta)=-2\gamma h(\xi_{0},\eta)\leq 0\quad\text{ for all }\eta \in[\eta_{0},\eta_{1}],

and
-\gamma(\xi_{0},\eta)
and
h(\xi_{0},\eta)
have opposite sign. Thus, combining Case 1 and Case 2 yields

\displaystyle\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\gamma h(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle\geq-2\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\gamma(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})}{1-h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\tilde{\eta}}\gamma(\xi_{0},\hat{\eta})d\hat{\eta}}d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=2\ln\left(1-h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\gamma(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\right)

\displaystyle=2\ln\left(1+h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{1}{4}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c^{2}(U)}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\right)

(3.9)

\displaystyle=2\ln\left(1-h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{1}{4}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\right),

where we used (
3.5
)
and, following the same lines,

(3.10)

\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\leq 2\ln\left(1-a\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{1}{4}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\right).

Comparing the above inequalities (
3.9
) and (

), we observe that they have nearly the same form. To ensure that wave breaking takes place and to obtain an upper and a lower bound on the wave breaking time, we will bound the constant

by a multiple of

. This will be done next.

A closer look at (

) and (

) reveals that

are admissible.
Furthermore, (

), (
1.10
), (

), and (

) imply that

\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta_{1}} |\alpha(\xi_{0},\eta)|d\eta\leq\frac{1}{2}\lambda\kappa^{2}(J(\xi_{0},\eta_{1})-J(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))\leq\frac{1}{2}\lambda\kappa^{2}(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R})

and (
3.11
) is satisfied for all constants
a
such that

(3.12)

0<|a|\leq|h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})|-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\kappa^{2}(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R}).

This means in particular, that if there exists a constant
A\geq 1
such that

(3.13)

|h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})|=A\lambda\kappa^{2}(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R}),

then the constant
a
given by

(3.14)

a=\left(1-\frac{1}{2A}\right)h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}),

will not only satisfy (
3.12
), but also (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), which require that
a
and
h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})
have the same sign, and (
3.10
) rewrites as

(3.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\leq 2\ln\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2A}\right)h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{1}{4}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\right).

Recalling that
\eta\geq\eta_{0}
and (
3.2
), which must be satisfied if wave breaking takes place along a backward characteristic, yields that wave breaking occurred for sure backward in time if there exists
\bar{\eta}>\eta_{0}
such that

1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2A}\right)h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{1}{4}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\to 0\quad\text{ as }\quad\eta\to\bar{\eta}

and (
3.15
) and (
3.9
) will provide a lower and an upper bound on the wave breaking time, respectively.

Theorem 3.1

(Wave breaking along backward characteristics - Part 1)
.

Given some initial data
(u_{0},R_{0},S_{0},\mu_{0},\nu_{0})\in\mathcal{D}
, denote by
(u(t),R(t),S(t),\mu(t),\nu(t))
the global conservative solution to the NVW equation at time
t
and by
D
the constant from Lemma
2.2
. If the following conditions are satisfied

•

c^{\prime}(u_{0})R_{0}(\bar{x})=c^{\prime}(u_{0})(u_{0,t}+c(u_{0})u_{0,x})(\bar{x})<0
,

•

there exists
A\geq 1
such that

|R_{0}|(\bar{x})=A\lambda\kappa^{2}(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{ and }

•

12\bar{\lambda}^{2}D^{2}(1+\kappa)<(1-\frac{1}{2A})\frac{1}{\kappa}|c^{\prime}(u_{0})|^{3}|R_{0}|(\bar{x})
,

then wave breaking occurred along the backward characteristic
y(t,\xi_{0})
with
y(0,\xi_{0})=\bar{x}
within the time interval

[t_{l},t_{u}]=[-\frac{3}{b},-\frac{3}{5\tilde{b}}],

where

b=\frac{1}{4\kappa} \left(1-\frac{1}{2A}\right)|c^{\prime}(u_{0})R_{0}|(\bar{x})<\frac{1}{4}\kappa|c^{\prime}(u_{0})R_{0}|(\bar{x})=\tilde{b}.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that

R_{0}(\bar{x})<0\quad\text{ and }\quad c^{\prime}(u_{0})(\bar{x})>0,

since the other case can be treated similarly. Then there exists
(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})
, not necessarily unique, such that

(0,\bar{x})=(0,y(0,\xi_{0}))=(t(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}),x(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))\quad\text{ and }\quad R_{0}(\bar{x})=h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}).

Furthermore, the integral turning up in both (
3.9
) and (
3.15
), can be rewrittes as

\displaystyle\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(u(t(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}),x(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})))}{c(u(t(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}),x(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})))}t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=-\int_{t(\xi_{0},\eta)}^{0}\frac{c^{\prime}(u(s,y(s,\xi_{0})))}{c(u(s,y(s,\xi_{0})))}ds,

since
x(\xi_{0},\eta)=y(t(\xi_{0},\eta),\xi_{0})
for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, we aim at estimating the integral on the right hand side from above as well as below. However, be aware that the estimates (
3.9
) and (
3.15
) are only valid as long as
c^{\prime}(u(s,y(s,\xi_{0})))>0
for all
s\in(t(\xi_{0},\eta),0]
. This is guaranteed locally as we will see next.

By (
1.10
), Lemma
2.2
and (
2.4
), we have that

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(u(s,y(s,\xi_{0})))

\displaystyle\geq c^{\prime}(u_{0}(y(0,\xi_{0})))-|c^{\prime}(u(s,y(s,\xi_{0})))-c^{\prime}(u(0,y(0,\xi_{0})))|

\displaystyle\geq c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))-\bar{\lambda}D\sqrt{|s|+|y(s,\xi_{0})-y(0,\xi_{0})|}

(3.16)

\displaystyle\geq c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))-\bar{\lambda}D\sqrt{1+\kappa}\sqrt{|s|}>0

for all
0\leq-s\leq-t(\xi_{0},\eta)<T
,
where
T
satisfies

(3.17)

|c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))|=\bar{\lambda}D\sqrt{1+\kappa}\sqrt{T}.

Furthermore, following the same lines we also obtain an upper bound

(3.18)

c^{\prime}(u(s,y(s,\xi_{0})))\leq c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))+\bar{\lambda}D\sqrt{1+\kappa}\sqrt{|s|}.

Assume from now on that
0\leq-t(\xi_{0},\eta)\leq T
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=-\int_{t(\xi_{0},\eta)}^{0}\frac{c^{\prime}(u(s,y(s,\xi)))}{c(u(s,y(s,\xi)))}ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{t(\xi_{0},\eta)}^{0}-c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))+\bar{\lambda}D\sqrt{1+\kappa}\sqrt{-s}ds

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))t(\xi_{0},\eta)+\frac{2}{3}\bar{\lambda}D\sqrt{1+\kappa}(-t(\xi_{0},\eta))^{3/2}\right).

To obtain a lower bound
t_{l}
for the exact wave breaking time
t(\xi_{0},\bar{\eta})
, observe that (
3.15
) combined with the above estimate implies that
t_{1}\leq t(\xi_{0},\bar{\eta})<0
, where
t_{1}
is implicitly given as the solution to

1-\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\frac{1}{2A}\right)R_{0}(\bar{x})\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))t_{1}+\frac{2}{3}\bar{\lambda}D\sqrt{1+\kappa}(-t_{1})^{3/2}\right)=0.

Note that those
(-t_{1})^{1/2}>0
, which satisfy the above equality, can bee seen as zeros (on the positive real line) of the third order polynomial

f(x)=-ax^{3}+bx^{2}-1,

where
a
and
b
are positive constants. As a closer look reveals
f
attains a minimum at
x=0
, where
f(0)=-1
, a maximum at
x=\frac{2b}{3a}=\sqrt{T}
, where
f(\frac{2b}{3a})=\frac{4b^{3}}{27a^{2}}-1
, and is strictly decreasing on the interval
[\frac{2b}{3a},\infty)
. Thus
f(x)
will either have
0
,
1
, or
2
positive zeros. In particular, one has that the change from
0
to
2
positive zeros
\bar{z}_{1}<\bar{z}_{2}
occurs when
4b^{3}=27a^{2}
. Or, in other words for
4b^{3}>27a^{2}
, which we assume from now on,
f(x)
has two positive zeros
\bar{z}_{1}<\frac{2b}{3a}=\sqrt{T}<\bar{z}_{2}
and wave breaking occurs before
c^{\prime}(u(s,y(s,\xi_{0})))
changes sign. Although we cannot compute
\bar{z}_{1}=(-t_{1})^{1/2}
explicitly, we can try to estimate it from above. Therefore, observe that

g(x)=\frac{1}{3}bx^{2}-1\leq f(x)\quad\text{ for all } x\in[0,\frac{2b}{3a}],

with equality for
x=0
and
x=\frac{2b}{3a}
. Thus there exists a unique
\hat{z}_{1}\in(0,\frac{2b}{3a}]
such that
g(\hat{z}_{1})=0
and
z_{1}\leq\hat{z}_{1}
. As a closer look reveals
\hat{z}_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{b}}
and

t_{l}=-\frac{3}{b}=-\hat{z}_{1}^{2}\leq-z_{1}^{2}=t_{1}.

To obtain an upper bound
t_{u}
for the exact wave breaking time
t(\xi_{0},\bar{\eta})
, we slightly modify the steps leading to (
3.19
) by using (
3.18
) to obtain the following lower bound

\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}t_{\eta}(\xi,\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\geq\kappa\left(c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))t(\xi_{0},\eta)-\frac{2}{3}\bar{\lambda}D\sqrt{1+\kappa}(-t(\xi_{0},\eta))^{3/2}\right).

Combined with (
3.9
) this estimate implies that
t(\xi_{0},\bar{\eta})\leq t_{2}<0
, where
t_{2}
is implicitly given as the solution to

1-\frac{1}{4}R_{0}(\bar{x})\kappa\left(c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))t_{2}-\frac{2}{3}\bar{\lambda}D\sqrt{1+\kappa}(-t_{2})^{3/2}\right)=0.

Note that those
(-t_{2})^{1/2}>0
, which satisfy the above equality, can be seen as zeros (on the positive real line) of the third order polynomial

\tilde{f}(x)=\tilde{a}x^{3}+\tilde{b}x^{2}-1,

where
\tilde{a}
and
\tilde{b}
are positive constants. As a closer look reveals
\tilde{f}
attains a minimum at
x=0
, where
\tilde{f}(0)=-1
, is strictly increasing on the interval
[0,\infty)
and crosses the positive
x
-axis in exactly one point
\bar{z}
, which we can try to estimate from below. Therefore, observe that

\tilde{a}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{2A}}\kappa^{2}a\geq a\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{b}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{2A}}\kappa^{2}b\geq b,

which implies
\frac{2b}{3a}=\frac{2\tilde{b}}{3\tilde{a}}=\sqrt{T}
and that

\tilde{g}(x)=\frac{5}{3}\tilde{b}x^{2}-1\geq\tilde{f}(x)\geq f(x)\quad\text{ for all } x\in[0,\frac{2\tilde{b}}{3\tilde{a}}]

with
\tilde{g}(x)=\tilde{f}(x)
for
x=0
and
x=\frac{2\tilde{b}}{3\tilde{a}}
. Thus there exists a unique
\hat{z}\in(0,\frac{2\tilde{b}}{3\tilde{a}})
such that
\tilde{g}(\hat{z})=0
and
\hat{z}\leq\bar{z}
. As a closer look reveals
\hat{z}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{5\tilde{b}}}
and

t_{2}=-\bar{z}^{2}\leq-\hat{z}^{2}=-\frac{3}{5\tilde{b}}=t_{u}.

This finishes the proof, since the wave breaking time
t(\xi_{0},\bar{\eta})
satisfies

-\frac{3}{b}=t_{l}\leq t_{1}\leq t(\xi_{0},\bar{\eta})\leq t_{2}\leq t_{u}=-\frac{3}{5\tilde{b}}.

∎

Next, assume that
\eta\leq\eta_{0}
, which implies, using (
2.22
), that
t(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\leq t(\xi_{0},\eta)
and hence will yield a criterion for wave breaking in the near future. In this case (
3.2
) requires

c^{\prime}(U)h(\xi_{0},\eta)\to\infty\quad\text{ as }\quad\eta\to\bar{\eta}

for some
\bar{\eta}\leq\eta_{0}
. Due to a symmetry argument, which we present next, the result is an immediate consequence of the estimates established until now.

Define the change of coordinates

(\xi,\eta)\to(2\xi_{0}-\xi,2\eta_{0}-\eta),

which corresponds to rotating
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

around the point
(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})
. In particular, one has that the half line through

(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})
given by

. Moreover, defining

\displaystyle\hat{Z}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=(\hat{t},\hat{x},\hat{U},\hat{J})(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=(t,x,U,J)(2\xi_{0}-\xi,2\eta_{0}-\eta)=Z(2\xi_{0}-\xi,2\eta_{0}-\eta),

which satisfies

\hat{Z}_{i}(\xi,\eta)=-Z_{i}(2\xi_{0}-\xi,2\eta_{0}-\eta) \quad\text{ for }i\in\{\xi,\eta\}

as well as (
2.15
), yields upon substitution

\displaystyle\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\eta_{0}}^{2\eta_{0}-\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},2\eta_{0}-\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta_{0}}^{2\eta_{0}-\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(\hat{U})}{2c(\hat{U})}\frac{\hat{U}_{\xi}}{\hat{x}_{\xi}}\hat{x}_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}.

By assumption

, which implies

where

\hat{h}(\xi_{0},\eta)=-h(\xi_{0},2\eta_{0}-\eta)=-c(\hat{U})\frac{\hat{U}_{\xi}}{\hat{x}_{\xi}}(\xi_{0},\eta)

and

In this new coordinates, (
3.20
) rewrites as

for some
\bar{\eta}\geq\eta_{0}
. Furthermore,
\hat{h}(\xi_{0},\eta)
satisfies the first order differential equation

\hat{h}_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\eta)=\hat{\alpha}(\xi_{0},\eta)+\hat{\gamma}\hat{h}^{2}(\xi_{0},\eta),

where

(3.25)

\hat{\alpha}(\xi_{0},\eta)=\alpha(\xi_{0},2\eta_{0}-\eta)=-\frac{c^{\prime}(\hat{U})}{2c^{2}(\hat{U})}\hat{J}_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\eta).

and
(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\gamma},\hat{h})
play the same role and have the same properties as

in the case
\eta\geq\eta_{0}
. As an immediate consequence we obtain that if

satisfies (
3.13
), then

(3.26)

\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\geq 2\ln\left(1+h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta}^{\eta_{0}}\frac{1}{4}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\right)

and

(3.27)

\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\leq 2\ln\left(1+\left(1-\frac{1}{2A}\right)h(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\int_{\eta}^{\eta_{0}}\frac{1}{4}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}\right).

From here on we follow the proof of Theorem
3.1
with slight modifications and we assume without less of generality that

R_{0}(\bar{x})=(u_{0,t}+c(u_{0})u_{0,x})(\bar{x})>0\quad\text{ and }\quad c^{\prime}(u_{0}(\bar{x}))>0.

t_{u}

, where

where

and

,

, where

3.2.
Wave breaking along forward characteristics

Again, let
(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which corresponds to the point
(0,\bar{x})=(t(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}),x(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))
in Eulerian coordinates, such that

tending to

along the horizontal line
\{(\xi,\eta_{0})\mid\xi\in\mathbb{R}\}
in the
(\xi,\eta)

(\bar{\xi},\eta_{0})
close to

as

If so, wave breaking either occurred recently or will take place in the near future and it then remains to estimate the actual breaking time.

To answer this question, we can follow the same line of reasoning as in Section
3.1
, since (
2.15
), where the right hand side can be viewed as
F(U,Z_{\xi},Z_{\eta})
, satisfies

(Z_{\eta})_{\xi}=F(U,Z_{\eta},Z_{\xi})=F(U,Z_{\xi},Z_{\eta})=(Z_{\xi})_{\eta}.

However, some small and straightforward adjustments are needed, due to the sign difference in (
2.12
). Thus, we here do not present all the details, but restrict ourselves to sketching the red line.

Define

\check{f}(\xi,\eta_{0})=\sqrt{\frac{c(U(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))}{c(U(\xi,\eta_{0}))}}x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta_{0}),

which satisfies that
\check{f}(\xi,\eta_{0})\to 0
as

if and only if
x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta_{0})\to 0
as
\xi\to\bar{\xi}
. Furthermore,

\check{f}(\xi,\eta_{0})=\check{f}(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})e^{\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\xi}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}\frac{U_{\eta}}{x_{\eta}}x_{\xi}(\tilde{\xi},\eta_{0})d\tilde{\xi}},

and hence our goal is to establish a criterion, which guarantees that

(3.28)

\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\xi}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}\frac{U_{\eta}}{x_{\eta}}x_{\xi}(\tilde{\xi},\eta_{0})d\tilde{\xi}\to-\infty\quad\text{ as }\quad\xi\to\bar{\xi}.

The function

\check{h}(\xi,\eta_{0})=c(U)\frac{U_{\eta}}{x_{\eta}}(\xi,\eta_{0}),

which plays the same role as

in Section
3.1
, satisfies the differential equation

\check{h}_{\xi}(\xi,\eta_{0})=\check{\alpha}(\xi,\eta_{0})+\check{\gamma}\check{h}^{2}(\xi,\eta_{0}),

where

\check{\alpha}(\xi,\eta_{0})=\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c^{2}(U)}J_{\xi}(\xi,\eta_{0})

and

\check{\gamma}(\xi,\eta_{0})=-\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{4c^{2}(U)}x_{\xi}(\xi,\eta_{0}).

Assume that
\xi_{0}\leq\xi
, which implies, using (
2.22
), that
t(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})\leq t(\xi,\eta_{0})
. Then (
3.28
) requires that

c^{\prime}(U)\check{h}(\xi,\eta_{0})\to-\infty\quad\text{ as }\quad\xi\to\bar{\xi}

for some
\xi_{0}\leq\bar{\xi}
. Following the same lines as in the case
\eta\geq\eta_{0}
in Section
3.1
and recalling (
2.12
) we obtain that if there exists a constant
A\geq 1
such that

|\check{h}(\xi_{0},\eta_{0})|=A\lambda\kappa^{2}(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle 2

3.3.
Remark

All the derived criteria for wave breaking have in common that either

or

become unbounded, which implies that either

or
u_{x}
or both become unbounded. However, it can be shown, and this will be done next, that wave breaking can only occur if

becomes unbounded, which rules out the possibility that

remains bounded while

blows up due to (

3.3.1.
Backward characteristics

When wave breaking occurs along a backward characteristic, which corresponds to the vertical line
\{(\xi_{0},\eta)\mid\eta\in\mathbb{R}\}
in the
(\xi,\eta)
plane, the integral

(3.31)

\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta},

tends to
-\infty
. Since

U(\xi,\eta)=u(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta)),

implies

\displaystyle U_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=u_{t}(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))t_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)+u_{x}(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))x_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)

(3.32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c(U(\xi,\eta))}(u_{t}+c(u)u_{x})(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))x_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)

and

\displaystyle U_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=u_{t}(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))t_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)+u_{x}(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)

(3.33)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c(U(\xi,\eta))}(u_{t}-c(u)u_{x})(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta),

the following identity holds

\displaystyle\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c(U(\xi,\eta))}(u_{t}+c(u)u_{x})(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=-U_{\eta}(\xi,\eta)+2u_{x}(t(\xi,\eta),x(\xi,\eta))x_{\eta}(\xi,\eta).

Therefore the integral (
3.31
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}\frac{U_{\xi}}{x_{\xi}}x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c(U)}U_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{c(u)}u_{x}(t(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}),x(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}))x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\ln(c(U(\xi_{0},\eta)))-\ln(c(U(\xi_{0},\eta_{0}))))

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{c(u)}u_{x}(t(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}),x(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}))x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta},

where the first term on the right hand side is always finite due to (
1.9
). As a consequence the integral on the left hand side can only tend to
-\infty
if and only if the second integral on the right hand side tends to
-\infty
. Moreover, (
2.12
) and (
2.11
) imply that

\displaystyle\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}\frac{c^{\prime}(u)}{c(u)}u_{x}(t(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}),x(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}))x_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\eta_{0}}^{\eta}c^{\prime}(u)u_{x}(t(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}),x(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}))t_{\eta}(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=-\int_{t(\xi_{0},\tilde{\eta}_{0})}^{t(\xi_{0},\eta)}c^{\prime}(u)u_{x}(l,y(l,\xi_{0}))dl,

and hence wave breaking can only occur in the future if
c^{\prime}(u)u_{x}(l,y(l,\xi_{0}))
tends to
\infty
and in the past if
c^{\prime}(u)u_{x}(l,y(l,\xi_{0}))
tends to
-\infty
.

3.3.2.
Forward characteristics

When wave breaking occurs along a forward characteristic, which corresponds to the horizontal line
\{(\xi,\eta_{0})\mid\xi\in\mathbb{R}\}
in the
(\xi,\eta)

c^{\prime}(u)u_{x}(l,z(l,\eta_{0}))

\infty

is very large, we cannot distinguish between possible wave breaking in the future along a backward characteristic and in the past along a forward characteristic. This is also the reason why our wave breaking criteria are formulated in terms of

c^{\prime}(u)S=c^{\prime}(u)(u_{t}-c(u)u_{x})

1

12

[

\mu

\nu

Second, we consider some initial data, where the measures

(u_{0},R_{0},S_{0})=(\gamma,0,0)

(u,R,S)\equiv(\gamma,0,0)

4.1.

.

To conclude the proof of our claim (
4.17
). Observe that (
2.15
) and (
2.13
) imply

\displaystyle U_{\xi,\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c^{3}(U)}(x_{\xi}J_{\eta}+x_{\eta}J_{\xi})-\frac{1}{2}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c(U)}U_{\xi}U_{\eta}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c^{3}(U)}(\sqrt{x_{\xi}J_{\eta}}-\sqrt{x_{\eta}J_{\xi}})^{2}\geq 0

which combined with (
4.18
) and
c(U)U_{\xi}(\xi,\eta)=0
for all

Therefore,

\displaystyle(c(U)U_{\xi})_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{c^{2}(U)}(x_{\xi}J_{\eta}+x_{\eta}J_{\xi})+\frac{1}{2}c^{\prime}(U)U_{\xi}U_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{\prime}(U)}{2c^{2}(U)}(\sqrt{x_{\xi}J_{\eta}}+\sqrt{x_{\eta}J_{\xi}})^{2}\geq 0,

and recalling (

for all

with

.
Thus, choosing

\varepsilon=\min(\frac{1}{8}\alpha,\frac{1}{2}(t(\xi_{2},\eta_{0})-t(\xi_{2},\eta_{1})))=\min(\frac{1}{8}\alpha,\frac{1}{2}(t(\xi_{2},\eta_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\alpha)),

Remark 4.3
.

In
[
3
]
the uniqueness of conservative solutions to the nonlinear variational wave equation has been established. One of the properties characterising this class of solutions is the following:
For almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the singular parts of
\mu(t)
and
\nu(t)
are concentrated on the set where
c^{\prime}(u)=0
.

As the above example illustrates the set where
c^{\prime}(u)=0
plays a key role, since it allows for conservative solutions such that there exists a set
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}
with
\mathrm{meas}(\mathcal{B})>0
such that for every
t\in\mathcal{B}
either the measure
\mu(t)
or
\nu(t)
is not purely absolutely continuous. The main underlying reason is the fact that in regions where
c^{\prime}(u)=0
the solution behaves like those for the linear wave equation and hence concentrated energy cannot be spread out.

For the conservative solutions of the Camassa–Holm and the Hunter–Saxton equation, on the other hand, the situation is quite different. One has to require that there exists a null set
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that for every
t\not\in\mathcal{N}
the measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous. Since
u
for those equations plays the same role as
c(u)
for the NVW equation, the imposed criteria is in alignment with the one in
[
3
]
if
c^{\prime}(x)\not=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Appendix A
Useful estimates

Proposition A.1
.

Assume
h(t)<0
and
\gamma(t)\leq 0
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and that

(A.1)

a+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\gamma h^{2}(s)ds\leq h(t)\leq\tilde{a}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\gamma h^{2}(s)ds<0,

then

\frac{a}{1-a\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\gamma(s)ds}\leq h(t)\leq\frac{\tilde{a}}{1-\tilde{a}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\gamma(s)ds}.

Proof.

We only establish the upper bound, since the lower bound can be established following the same lines.

Consider the function

z(t)=\tilde{a}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\gamma h^{2}(s)ds,

which satisfies
h(t)\leq z(t)<0
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
by (
A.1
),
z(t_{0})=\tilde{a}<0
, and

z^{\prime}(t)=\gamma h^{2}(t)\leq\gamma z^{2}(t).

Since the above differential inequality can be solved explicitly, we end up with

h(t)\leq z(t)\leq\frac{\tilde{a}}{1-\tilde{a}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\gamma(s)ds}.

∎
