Single-Cell Transcriptomics Identifies a Pivotal Role of SPHK1+ Macrophage-Driven Inflammation in Mechanism of Aortic Dissection and Highlights SPHK1 as a Therapeutic Target
Authors: Dianjun Tang, Yanshuo Han, Han Jiang, Jamol Uzokov, Fandong Li, Zhong Wang, Mario D’Oria, Philipp Erhart, Dittmar Boeckler, Yu Lun, Jian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMacrophages are a key heterogeneous cell population involved in the pathogenesis and progression of acute aortic dissection (AD), though their mechanisms remain unclear. This study aims to identify the gene driving inflammatory damage in acute AD within a specific macrophage subcluster, highlighting potential therapeutic targets. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was employed to analyze the cellular heterogeneity in acute AD and normal aortic tissues. Bioinformatic analysis was conducted to identify the key gene. The role of sphingosine kinase 1 (SPHK1) was further explored using human acute AD tissue samples, macrophage cell line experiments, and AD mouse models. scRNA-seq identified six macrophage subclusters in AD tissues, with subcluster a exhibiting a pronounced pro-inflammatory phenotype. SPHK1 was significantly upregulated in this subcluster and was identified as the key regulatory gene. In vitro, inhibition of SPHK1 reduced macrophage-mediated inflammatory damage via the S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA-ROCK1 signaling pathway. In vivo studies revealed a 40% reduction in the incidence of AD and significantly reduced severity in mice treated with PF-543 (a specific SPHK1 inhibitor). In this model, PF-543 reduced macrophage infiltration and the inflammatory damage caused by inflammatory factors and matrix metalloproteinases. This study identifies macrophage-driven inflammation, particularly via SPHK1, as a central mechanism in AD. Introduction

Acute aortic dissection (AD) is a highly lethal vascular surgical emergency and the most common disease of acute aortic syndrome. It is characterized by tears in the inner layer of the aorta, which allows blood to flow between the layers of the aortic wall, forcing the layers apart [1].The incidence of AD ranges from 2.6 to 3.5 cases per 100,000 person-years, with higher prevalence in males and older individuals [2]. The progression of the dissection can lead to arterial rupture, pericardial tamponade and other fatal conditions. Early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention are critical but remain challenging due to the complex pathophysiology of AD [3]. Recent research has shifted towards a deeper understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms that contribute to the initiation and progression of AD [4]. However, the pathogenesis of AD is still unclear, and there are no effective drugs.

Inflammatory processes can damage the aortic wall through inflammatory cell infiltration, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, and elastic fiber disruption [4]. In addition, cytokines and chemokines released by lymphocytes, monocytes, and macrophages may further weaken the aortic wall [5, 6]. In AD, circulating monocytes infiltrate the aortic wall and differentiate into macrophages, which constitute a major population of infiltrating immune cells [7]. Notably, these macrophages are functionally heterogeneous and accumulate at sites of intimal tearing and intramural hematoma, where they clear necrotic debris and secrete various cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), thereby promoting inflammatory injury during the acute phase of AD [8]. The mechanisms regulating macrophage inflammatory responses during AD progression require further investigation.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables detailed characterization of the cellular heterogeneity of AD and normal aortic tissues, providing a comprehensive map of the cellular landscape and its changes during AD development. The identification of specific macrophage subpopulations involved in inflammatory responses offers new avenues for therapeutic intervention, potentially allowing for the modulation of these cells to mitigate AD progression. These cells exhibit distinct gene expression profiles that are linked to pro-inflammatory and tissue remodeling processes. Notably, the study identifies sphingosine kinase 1 (SPHK1) as a key regulatory gene within a pro-inflammatory macrophage subcluster, suggesting its role in exacerbating the inflammatory milieu in AD.

SPHK1 is a key enzyme in the sphingolipid metabolism pathway. It phosphorylates sphingosine to generate the bioactive lipid mediator sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) [9]. Upon binding to cell-surface S1P receptors (S1PRs), S1P promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [10–13]. Recent studies of neuroinflammation following spinal cord injury and of liver fibrosis have shown that SPHK1 mediates macrophage migration to the lesion site [10, 14]. S1PRs are differentially expressed across cell types and mediate diverse functions [15]. In the cardiovascular system, S1PR1 supports endothelial barrier integrity and vasodilation, whereas S1PR2 and S1PR3 are generally associated with pro‑inflammatory responses. Within the immune system, S1PR1 regulates lymphocyte egress, S1PR2 enhances macrophage retention and confines B cells [16–19], and S1PR4/5 modulate T-cell and NK-cell functions, respectively [20, 21]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which SPHK1 regulates macrophage function in AD and thereby influences disease progression remain to be elucidated. This study aims to investigate the key genes and their mechanisms in the sphingolipid metabolic pathway that regulate the inflammatory response of macrophages, which causes vascular injury leading to the development of AD, providing a theoretical reference for subsequent drug target research.

Methods

The corresponding author will provide the data, analytical methods, and study materials to fellow researchers upon reasonable request. Detailed descriptions of the methods and sources of reagents are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Tables 1, 2, and 4.

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the European Parliament Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals used. Mice were anesthetized using 1% isoflurane (inhalation) delivered through a precision vaporizer. The dose was carefully adjusted to maintain a stable surgical plane of anesthesia while avoiding over-sedation Isoflurane was administered continuously throughout the imaging procedure, and vital signs were closely monitored to ensure animal well-being. Euthanasia was performed using a combination of isoflurane inhalation followed by an intra-peritoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg).

The human study was conducted in accordance with Guidelines of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (KYLL-2022P232). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study, ensuring that they were fully informed of the nature, purpose, and potential risks of the study.

Results

Monocyte Counts are Associated with the Progression of AD

Immune cells, including monocytes, play a critical role in the progression of AD. Compared to an age- and sex-matched control group, patients with AD exhibited significantly higher levels of peripheral blood leukocytes and monocytes. Of 108 patients with AD, 19 experienced a poor prognosis at discharge, including 10 fatalities. Poor prognosis was defined as the occurrence of at least one of the following: permanent loss of function in one or more organs requiring intensive or intermediate care, functional impairment resulting in severe disability, or death [22]. Monocyte levels were significantly elevated in patients who died and in those with a poor prognosis compared to survivors and patients with a favorable outcome, respectively (Fig. 1A), and correlated positively with Interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels in AD patients (Fig. 1B).

Heterogeneity Analysis of Cellular Components in AD Tissues

Our single-cell transcriptome analysis provided a comprehensive overview of the cellular heterogeneity in AD tissues compared to normal aortic tissues (Supplementary Table S3 and Fig. 1C). A total of 25,628 cells were analyzed, with 70.3% originating from AD tissues and 29.7% from normal tissues. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) revealed distinct cellular distributions between AD and control samples (Fig. 1D). Cells were clustered into four major types: immune cells, endothelial cells (ECs), fibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells (SMCs). Immune cells predominated in AD tissues, comprising 78.9% of profiled cells, whereas ECs and SMCs were substantially reduced compared with controls (Fig. 1D). The differential gene expression analysis within these cell types revealed distinct expression patterns (Supplementary Figure S2A). Gene ontology (GO) analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes in immune cells were enriched in pathways related to leukocyte-cell adhesion, upregulation of leukocyte activation and leukocyte proliferation regulation (Supplementary Figure S2B and S2C). To characterize cells in the AD tissue, we classified them into macrophages, T cells, classical dendritic cells, mast cells, B cells, ECs, SMCs and fibroblasts based on marker genes (Supplementary Figure S2D). The heatmap displayed the correlation between subclusters (Supplementary Figure S2E). The UMAP plot showed that, compared with the control group, the macrophage cluster was significantly increased in the AD group, while the T cell, classical dendritic cell, and B cell clusters were decreased (Fig. 1E). To further understand the cellular changes during AD progression, we subdivided major cells such as macrophages and fibroblasts into multiple subclusters with distinct molecular features (Fig. 1F). The relative proportions of each cell subcluster in the AD and control groups suggested that B cell subcluster, EC subcluster d, macrophage subclusters a, e, f, fibroblast subcluster d, and SMC subclusters c, d, f had higher proportions in AD tissues (Fig. 1G).

Macrophage Subpopulation Dynamics in AD

Our analysis revealed the significant changes in macrophage proportions, and we focused on macrophage subclusters to explore the characteristics of AD. A total of 18,041 macrophages were detected, and differences in cluster distribution between the AD and control groups were observed (Fig. 2A). In AD tissues, we identified six distinct macrophage subclusters, with clusters a, e, and f significantly elevated relative to controls, suggesting potential roles in disease progression (Fig. 2B-C). Among these, subclusters a and b showed strong associations with pro-inflammatory phenotypes (Fig. 2D). Functional attributes of macrophage subclusters

Additionally, our study delves into the temporal progression of macrophage subtypes during AD progression. Pseudotime analysis was used to model the developmental trajectories of macrophage subtypes within AD. In normal aortic and AD tissues, macrophage evolution was classified into five states: I-V (Supplementary Figure S3A). Along the trajectory, the expression levels of three gene modules increased, while those of the other three decreased (Supplementary Figure S3B). Cells from normal aortic tissues were predominantly located near pseudotime 10.0, indicating the trajectory’s starting point (Fig. 2E). As AD progresses, macrophage subclusters showed dynamic changes (Fig. 2F-G), with a, e, f increasing, c, d decreasing, and b remaining unchanged (Supplementary Figure S3C). Further analysis of the pseudotime distribution revealed that AD1 around pseudotime 2.5, and AD2 around 6.0 (Supplementary Figure S3D). Cell density analysis indicated that subcluster e increased around pseudotime 6.0, predominantly in AD2 samples; subcluster f increased around pseudotime 2.5, predominantly in AD1 samples. Subcluster a increased at both pseudotime 2.5 and 6.0, showing high abundance (Fig. 2H). This suggested that subcluster a likely played a more central role in AD progression, potentially by exacerbating the inflammatory microenvironment within AD tissues.

Gene Expression and Pathway Enrichment in Macrophage Subcluster a

To explore the genes associated with AD pathogenesis in macrophage subcluster a, we analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between AD and control groups in macrophage subcluster a, as well as between subcluster a and other subclusters (b-f, Fig. 2I). The Venn diagram showed the intersection of 134 upregulated DEGs in both comparisons (Fig. 2J). Further GO enrichment analysis of these DEGs highlighted pathways mainly involved in responses to chemokines, cytokine-cytokine interactions, and response to IL-1 (Fig. 3A). To identify hub genes, we used protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis on the 134 DEGs and selected genes with more than three interaction nodes as core candidates for further investigation of the key regulatory genes in macrophage subcluster a (Fig. 3B).

SPHK1 as a Key Regulatory Gene in Macrophage Subcluster a: Implications for Inflammatory Response Modulation in Acute AD

Then, we selected DEGs in AD and normal tissues from the human bulk RNA-seq datasets (Fig. 3C). The Venn diagram revealed an intersection among DEGs from the GSE52093, GSE153434, and GSE147028 datasets and the 134 DEGs from macrophage subcluster a, identifying three hub genes: SPHK1, ABCA1, and UPP1 (Fig. 3D). Validation across four mouse AD datasets (GSE138484, GSE138558, GSE215935, and GSE222382) showed that only SPHK1 was significantly upregulated in AD tissues in all four datasets (Fig. 3E). To further validate SPHK1 as a key gene in macrophage subcluster a, we analyzed its expression distribution across 18,041 macrophages and found that SPHK1 was highly expressed in AD samples, but barely expressed in normal aorta tissues (Fig. 3F). According to pseudotime analysis, SPHK1 expression increased with AD progression (Fig. 3G). To investigate the role of SPHK1 in macrophage subcluster a in AD, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed that higher SPHK1 expression was enriched in pathways including ECM–receptor interaction and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction (Supplementary Figure S4). These results suggest that SPHK1 may play an important role in the development of AD. Targeting SPHK1 and its associated pathways offers a potential therapeutic avenue for modulating inflammatory responses in AD.

Role of SPHK1 in Promoting Inflammatory Responses in Human AD

Histological examination revealed that, compared to normal aortic tissue, the aortic diameter in patients with acute AD was significantly enlarged, with intimal thickening and marked inflammatory infiltration. The medial SMCs lost their intact cellular morphology and regular arrangement, with local disruption and fragmentation of elastic and collagen fibers (Supplementary Figure S5A and S5B). This damage is likely related to the release of inflammatory mediators and the degradation of the ECM by MMPs. To investigate the role of SPHK1 in macrophage subcluster a of AD, we isolated primary cells from AD tissues. The SPHK1+ macrophages displayed the substantially elevated IL-6 expression using flow cytometry (Fig. 4A). We then analyzed the expression of SPHK1 and IL-6 in AD tissues with onset times of 3, 12, 24, and 96 h, respectively. The results showed that SPHK1 and IL-6 expression increased at 3–12 h post-onset, returned to the 3-hour level by 12–24 h, and declined between 24 and 96 h (Fig. 4B). This suggests a critical role for SPHK1 in the early inflammatory phase of AD. Analysis of human tissues revealed that IL-1β and IL-6 mRNA levels were significantly higher in AD than in normal aorta and were positively correlated with SPHK1 mRNA expression (Fig. 4C). In consecutive parallel sections from normal and AD aorta, the level of SPHK1 were significantly increased in AD tissues within CD68-positive regions, consistent with single‑cell data. The expression of IL6 and IL-1β increased in the CD68+SPHK1+ region of human AD tissue (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Figure S5C). Meanwhile, in human AD tissues, MMP2 and MMP9 mRNA levels were significantly elevated compared with normal aortic tissue and correlated positively with SPHK1 mRNA level (Fig. 4E and F). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence confirmed increased MMP2 and MMP9 expression within CD68⁺SPHK1⁺ regions of human AD tissue compared with normal aorta (Fig. 4G and H and Supplementary Figure S5D). These data indicated that SPHK1 might promote aortic inflammatory damage in AD patients, likely through inflammatory mediator release and MMP-driven ECM degradation.

Activated SPHK1 can catalyze sphingosine to S1P, which mainly exerts biological functions through S1PR binding. We therefore further examined the expression of all five S1PRs. The results showed that the expression levels of S1PR2 and S1PR3 in the AD group were significantly higher than those in the control group, while the expression levels of S1PR1, S1PR4, and S1PR5 showed no significant difference between groups (Fig. 5A). Clinically, we assessed whether the SPHK1/S1P/S1PR pathway was involved in the occurrence and progression of AD. Patients who experienced death or unfavorable prognosis exhibited significantly elevated expression of S1PR2 and S1PR3 (Fig. 5B). Both S1PR2 and S1PR3 showed a significant positive correlation with the maximum diameter of the aorta, maximum diameter of the false lumen, and levels of the inflammatory cytokine IL-6 (Fig. 5C). Further analysis revealed that SPHK1 mRNA levels were positively correlated with those of S1PR2 and S1PR3 in AD patients (Fig. 5D), suggesting that these downstream receptors may be involved in SPHK1⁺ macrophage-mediated progression of acute AD. In line with previous reports that, S1PRs promote macrophage migration and inflammatory damage via the ras homolog family member A (RhoA)/rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase 1 (ROCK1) pathway in inflammatory bowel disease [23]. At the protein level, we also confirmed that the expression levels of S1PR2, S1PR3, RhoA and ROCK1 were significantly increased in AD tissues (Fig. 5E). These results suggest that SPHK1 likely contributed to AD development through the S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA/ROCK1 signaling pathway.

Mechanistic Insights into SPHK1’s Role via S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA-ROCK1 Signaling in Vitro

To functionally characterize SPHK1 in vitro, we treated RAW264.7 macrophages with the SPHK1 inhibitor PF-543 or agonist MHP. As mentioned above, SPHK1 may participate in the development of AD through the S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA/ROCK1 signaling pathway (Fig. 6A). After 24h of cultivation, the expression levels of MMP2 and MMP9 were significantly decreased in the PF-543 group but upregulated in the MHP group (Fig. 6B). Immunofluorescence showed that PF-543 treatment reduced the co-localization of SPHK1 with MMP2 and MMP9 in RAW264.7 cells, whereas MHP treatment enhanced their co‑expression (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Figures S6A), suggesting that SPHK1 induces macrophages to secrete MMPs to degrade ECM. Correspondingly, PF-543 treatment significantly decreased intracellular S1P levels and the expression of downstream S1PR2/3, RhoA, and ROCK1. In contrast, MHP treatment upregulated these pathway components (Figs. 6D-F). These findings suggest that SPHK1 regulates macrophage inflammatory responses via the S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA/ROCK1​ pathway. In subsequent experiments, we designed three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), among which SPHK1-si2 and SPHK1-si3 exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency (Fig. 6G). Immunofluorescence co-localization studies demonstrated that SPHK1 knockdown resulted in reduced fluorescence signal overlap between SPHK1 and MMP2, and between SPHK1 and MMP9 (Fig. 6H and Supplementary Figures S6B). SPHK1 knockdown significantly reduced the mRNA levels of IL-6, IL-1β and S1PR2/3 (Fig. 6I). To rescue SPHK1 expression, cells transfected with SPHK1‑si3 were subsequently transfected with an SPHK1‑si3‑resistant pCDH‑SPHK1 expression vector. We found a significant recovery of expression of MMP9 and S1PR2 in the SPHK1-si3 + pCDH-SPHK1-mut group compared with the SPHK1-si3 group (Fig. 6J). To verify the involvement of the S1P-S1PRs-RhoA axis, exogenous S1P added to SPHK1‑silenced cells. The results showed that S1P supplementation significantly restored the protein levels of downstream S1PR2, RhoA, and MMP9 (Fig. 6K). In SPHK1-overexpressing cells, knockdown of S1PR2 using S1PR2-si2 reversed the upregulation of RhoA and MMP9 caused by SPHK1 overexpression (Fig. 6L-M). These findings suggest that SPHK1 might exert its biological effects through the S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA/ROCK1 signaling pathway.

SPHK1 Inhibition Reduces AD Progression by Modulating Inflammatory Pathways and S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA-ROCK1 Signaling in a Mouse Model

After confirming its safety in toxicity testing, we used PF‑543 to inhibit SPHK1 in a beta-aminopropionitrile (BAPN)‑induced AD mouse model (Supplementary Figure S7A). Vascular ultrasound images taken on day 21 after modeling showed that in the BAPN + vehicle group, partial mice developed AD, and the aortic dilation was more pronounced compared to the BAPN + PF-543 group (Supplementary Figure S7B). Survival analysis indicated that the BAPN + PF-543 group had a significantly prolonged survival time compared to the BAPN + vehicle group (Fig. 7A). PF-543 also reduced the cumulative range of AD (Fig. 7B). In the control group, no AD occurrence or deaths were observed. The BAPN + vehicle group exhibited a 75% AD incidence and a 50% mortality rate due to aortic rupture. In contrast, the BAPN + PF-543 group showed a reduced AD incidence rate of 40% and a mortality rate of 15%. These results indicated that PF-543 could significantly reduce the occurrence rate of AD and the mortality rate caused by aortic rupture in mice (Fig. 7C). To observe the pathological changes in mouse aorta, we performed hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E) staining and Verhoeff’s Van Gieson staining (EvG) staining. The results showed that, in mice from the BAPN + vehicle group, a massive false lumen, medial thickening, and significant inflammatory infiltration were observed in the aorta. In the BAPN + PF-543 group, the intramural hematoma was reduced, and the medial thickness was less than that in the BAPN + vehicle group (Fig. 7D). Elastic fiber disruption was more severe in the BAPN + vehicle group than in the BAPN + PF-543 group (Fig. 7E). Next, IHC showed that in the BAPN + vehicle group, MMP2 and MMP9 protein levels were markedly increased in CD68⁺ regions of aortic tissue and were positively correlated with SPHK1 expression. By contrast, PF-543 treatment significantly reduced MMP2⁺/MMP9⁺ cells accumulation in these regions (Fig. 7F). Correspondingly, immunofluorescence showed decreased MMP2/MMP9 expression within CD68⁺SPHK1⁺ regions of the aorta after PF-543 treatment relative to vehicle treatment (Fig. 7G and Supplementary Figure S7C). Similarly, aortic mRNA levels of MMP2, MMP9, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly lower in PF-543-treated than in vehicle-treated BAPN-induced mice. In the BAPN + vehicle group, SPHK1 expression was positively correlated with MMP2, MMP9, IL-1β, and IL-6 expression (Fig. 7H).

In vivo, Western blot analysis showed that PF-543 reduced the protein levels of MMP2 and MMP9 in mouse AD tissues (Fig. 8A). ELISA revealed that compared to mice in the BAPN + vehicle group, the levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-1β in the peripheral blood plasma in the BAPN + PF-543 group were significantly decreased (Fig. 8B), suggesting that SPHK1 inhibition might attenuate AD progression by suppressing inflammation. We further examined the mRNA expression of SPHK2 and S1P metabolic network-related genes. PF-543 treatment did not significantly alter the mRNA levels of the S1P-synthesizing kinase SPHK2, the S1P-degrading enzyme SGPL1, or the S1P phosphatases SGPP1 and SGPP2 (Fig. 8C), indicating that PF-543 exerted its effects specifically through SPHK1. After using PF-543, there was a reduction in S1P levels of mouse aortic tissues and plasma (Fig. 8D). Moreover, expression levels of S1PR2 and S1PR3 were significantly lower in the BAPN + PF‑543 group than in the BAPN + vehicle group, consistent with findings in human tissues. Correlation analysis in the BAPN + vehicle group showed that SPHK1 was positively correlated with both S1PR2 and S1PR3 (Fig. 8E-F). This indicates that SPHK1 is involved in the occurrence and development of AD through the S1P/S1PR2/3 pathway. Given prior evidence that the SphK1/S1PR3 axis promotes macrophage inflammation by glycolysis-related molecules including hypoxia‑inducible factor‑1α (HIF‑1α), hexokinase 2 (HK2), and phosphofructokinase‑fructose‑2,6‑bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) [11], we assessed these markers. HIF‑1α mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated in AD aortic tissues compared with controls, and this increase was attenuated by PF‑543 treatment. In contrast, Hk2 and Pfkfb3 mRNA levels showed no significant differences among groups (Fig. 8G-H). Furthermore, PF-543 administration significantly reduced RhoA and ROCK1 protein expression in aortic tissue of AD model (Fig. 8I). Together, these data suggested that SPHK1 contributed to AD pathogenesis and progression via the S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA-ROCK1 signaling pathway.

The macrophage subcluster a and its key gene SPHK1 are increased, enhancing macrophage inflammatory injury through the S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA-ROCK1 signaling pathway in AD. This upregulation increases IL-1β and IL-6 levels, as well as MMP2 and MMP9 expression, thereby damaging the aortic wall and promoting AD development. The SPHK1 inhibitor PF-543 can reduce inflammatory injury, thereby improving the incidence and progression of AD.

Discussion

AD is an aortic wall lesion characterized by tissue injury, repair, and remodeling, with inflammation as a critical driver of its initiation and progression [24]. Circulating monocytes have gained increasing attention in recent research on the inflammatory mechanisms of AD. The extant evidence suggests that following arterial inflammation, monocytes are recruited to the tissue and differentiate into macrophages, participating in local inflammatory responses in AD [25, 26]. Several studies employed scRNA-seq to analyze thousands of cells from both AD and normal aortic tissues, allowing for a detailed examination of cellular heterogeneity and the identification of distinct cellular subtypes. For instance, Liu et al., through scRNA-seq analysis found that pro-inflammatory macrophage subtypes expressed lower levels of tissue-resident macrophage markers such as F13A1, LYVE1, and GAS6, suggesting that the proliferating macrophages in AD were primarily derived from recruited and differentiated monocytes. Using scRNA-seq, we classified macrophages into six subclusters based on markers and functions. Subcluster a showed prominent M1 polarization and pro-inflammatory characteristics, which increased with disease progression in pseudotime analysis. This subcluster expressed genes such as THBS1, SERPINB2, and CCL20, suggesting its monocytic origin, consistent with literature reports [7]. In our study, peripheral blood monocyte levels in patients with AD were significantly elevated compared with healthy individuals, and higher monocyte levels predicted a poor prognosis. Elevated monocyte levels may provide a basis for macrophage recruitment and differentiation in aortic tissue.

In the early stages of AD, macrophages are the most abundant infiltrating immune cell population. Macrophage-derived inflammatory cytokines and MMPs directly contribute to ECM degradation and vascular wall injury in AD [26, 27]. Recent scRNA-seq studies have shown that macrophages, particularly pro-inflammatory subsets such as IL1RN⁺/TREM1⁺ macrophages, are increased in AD tissues and contribute to thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection progression. These cells increase with disease severity and are a major source of pathological factors, including MMP9 and IL-1β [28]. In addition, an imbalance between MMP9 and its tissue inhibitors is considered one of the pathological mechanisms underlying AD [29]. Inflammatory mediators also contribute to AD progression. Another study found that macrophages promote AD progression by regulating neutrophil extracellular traps via the CXCL3/CXCR2 axis [30]. Our results from scRNA-seq indicate that macrophage subcluster a mainly plays a primary role in AD-associated inflammatory responses. Notably, this subtype exhibits markedly elevated SPHK1 expression levels compared to other macrophage subclusters, suggesting that SPHK1+ macrophages may be involved in inflammatory injury processes during the acute phase of AD.

SPHK is the key enzyme that catalyzes the phosphorylation of sphingosine to produce S1P. It has two main subtypes: SPHK1 and SPHK2 [31]. Recent study suggests that SPHK1 enhances the pro-inflammatory function of macrophages by activating the ASK1-JNK/p38 signaling pathway [14]. Both SPHK1 and SPHK2 generate S1P and can activate NLRP3 inflammasomes via the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and HIF‑1α pathways [32, 33]. Our scRNA-seq data indicated that SPHK1 expression levels were significantly elevated within macrophage subcluster a, whereas SPHK2 expression showed no discernible change. We also found that in the CD68-positive areas of human AD, SPHK1 levels were significantly higher than in controls, suggesting that SPHK1 promote macrophage accumulation and local inflammatory injury. In vascular SMCs, SPHK1 drives pathological vascular remodeling by promoting proliferation and exerts an anti-calcific effect by maintaining intracellular S1P levels [34]. SPHK2 can be activated by endothelium-derived EMAP II, engaging the EMAP II/SPHK2/S1P/histone H3K9 acetylation axis to promote proliferation and phenotypic switching, ultimately exacerbating pulmonary arterial hypertension [34, 35]. In vascular ECs, SPHK1 activates the PI3K/Akt pathway via the S1P/S1PR1 axis, helping to maintain endothelial barrier integrity, regulate vascular tone, and suppress inflammatory responses. In contrast, S1P activates the Rho/ROCK and PTEN pathways via S1PR2, which inhibits Akt phosphorylation and disrupts intercellular junctions, thus antagonizing the protective effects mediated by S1PR1 [36, 37]. SPHK2 can be activated by high-density lipoprotein through the PKC/ERK pathway, thereby reducing apoptosis [38], and also promotes nuclear S1P generation, facilitating the synthesis and release of vasoprotective prostaglandin I₂ [39]. Elevated local S1P levels inhibit macrophage cholesterol efflux via S1PR3 signaling, thereby accelerating plaque progression and rupture, a process dependent on SphK2 but not SphK1 [40, 41]. Collectively, in cardiovascular system, SPHK isoforms mediate distinct biological functions, and their downstream effects depend on activation of specific signaling pathways. Our data indicate that SPHK1, rather than SPHK2, is highly expressed in macrophage subcluster a, where it modulates pro-inflammatory function by sphingolipid metabolism. SMCs, ECs, and other cellular components, which comprise a smaller proportion of AD tissue, exhibit lower SPHK1 expression. To explore the transcriptional basis for selective SPHK1 upregulation in macrophage subcluster a, we performed promoter analysis using the hTFtarget database and identified 280 transcription factors with potential binding sites in the SPHK1 promoter. By intersecting the 280 transcription factors with the 134 hub genes of subcluster a (identified from bulk mRNA‑seq and our scRNA‑seq data), NFKB1 is identified as a predicted regulator of SPHK1, and it shows no differential expression in ECs or SMCs in AD. This NFKB1 expression pattern may help explain the selective elevation of SPHK1 in macrophage subcluster a and suggests distinct regulatory programs across vascular cell types. Further studies are needed to experimentally validate NF-κB binding to the SPHK1 promoter and to characterize this pathway in detail.

Following phosphorylation of sphingosine to S1P by SPHK1, S1P binds primarily to S1PR1-5 to exert its biological functions. S1PR1-5 are expressed in resting macrophages [42]. Studies on liver inflammation and fibrosis show that SPHK1 activates macrophage inflammatory responses via the SPHK1/S1P/S1PR2 pathway [43]. Notably, S1PR2 has been implicated in M1 polarization through NF-κB activation in acute pancreatitis. Inhibition of S1PR2 with JTE-013 significantly alleviates pancreatitis [44]. Concurrently, S1PR3 also contributes to inflammatory macrophage regulation. Within inflammatory and atherosclerotic environments, S1P is involved in monocyte/macrophage chemotaxis and recruitment, primarily through S1PR3 [45]. Wang et al. have shown that inhibiting S1PR3 inhibition reduces NLRP3 and IL-1β in lipopolysaccharide-induced macrophages, thereby suppressing inflammation [46]. In a sepsis model, S1pr3−/− mice show increased mortality and bacterial load, likely due to reduced macrophage bactericidal activity in the absence of S1PR3 [47]. Recent studies shows that the SphK1/S1PR3 axis promotes M1 polarization through glycolytic signaling, a metabolic shift essential for pro-inflammatory macrophage activation [11, 48]. Our findings underscore the mechanistic role of SPHK1 in AD through its influence on the S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA-ROCK1 pathway. Additionally, we suggest that SPHK1 may be involved in macrophage glycolysis via the S1PR3-HIF-1α axis. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

The SPHK1 inhibitor PF-543 exerts therapeutic effects in multiple diseases. Animal experiments establish that bi-daily administration of 10 mg/kg of PF-543 achieves effective pharmacological inhibition of SPHK1 [49]. In murine models of sepsis and acute ethanol intoxication, PF-543 significantly reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine release, attenuates pulmonary injury, and demonstrates potent anti-inflammatory properties [50, 51]. In a mouse AD model, we utilized the selective SPHK1 inhibitor PF-543 to inhibit SPHK1 function. SPHK1 inhibition reduced macrophage infiltration in dissection tissue, diminished arterial wall disruption, and significantly reduced the rate of AD modeling and aortic rupture. We also assessed mRNA levels of SPHK2 and other S1P metabolic genes in aortas from PF‑543-treated mice, none of which changed significantly. These results support that PF‑543 modulates macrophage inflammatory phenotype and vascular injury via SPHK1. Although SPHK1 or S1PR2/3 inhibition may affect skeletal muscle metabolism, osteoblast differentiation, and immune homeostasis [52–54], available evidence indicates acceptable safety under disease conditions [55, 56]. In acute AD, SPHK1 is upregulated in pro‑inflammatory macrophages, supporting the feasibility of its therapeutic inhibition. Future translational studies could explore localized or targeted delivery approaches to enhance drug accumulation at the aortic lesion while limiting systemic exposure.

Due to the considerable challenges in obtaining human aortic tissue samples and stringent quality control requirements, we ultimately performed scRNA-seq on tissue from only 2 AD patients and 1 control, which could potentially limit the statistical power and representativeness of our analysis. We mitigated this limitation by analyzing relevant human gene expression datasets from the GEO database (GSE52093, AD, n = 7 and control, n = 5; GSE147028, AD, n = 4 and control, n = 4; GSE153434, AD, n = 10 and control, n = 10) and by validating the findings through multi-level experiments. Only male mice are included in this study, as AD is more prevalent in males, while estrogen-mediated vascular protection in females may complicate the investigation of vascular mechanisms. Future studies will include both sexes to further investigate the role of SPHK1 in macrophages during AD pathogenesis and to improve the generalizability of our findings.

In summary, our analysis of cellular heterogeneity in AD tissues and subsequent characterization of macrophages indicate that SPHK1+ macrophages are a primary source of inflammatory injury during the development and progression of acute AD. Inhibition of SPHK1 with PF-543 suppresses macrophage pro-inflammatory activity, effectively reducing the incidence and rupture rate of AD. The findings suggest that targeting SPHK1 and its associated pathways may represent a novel and promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate AD progression and to prevent aortic rupture. Furthermore, our study proposes and preliminarily validates that SPHK1 drives macrophage pro-inflammatory responses via the SPHK1/S1P-S1PR2/3-RhoA-ROCK1 axis, providing mechanistic insights and new directions for future research into AD pathogenesis and pharmacological intervention.
