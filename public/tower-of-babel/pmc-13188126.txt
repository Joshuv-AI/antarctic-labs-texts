Life Cycle Assessment of HoLEP: Quantifying Environmental Impact and Opportunities for Sustainable Urologic Surgery
Authors: Clément Klein, Véronique Humbert, Mathieu Meune, Françoise Tubiana-Dussin, Stéphanie Charon, Karin Lamouroux, Grégoire Capon, Eric Alezra, Vincent Estrade, Peggy Blanc, Gaelle Margue, Jean-Christophe Bernhard, Grégoire Robert, Franck Bladou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageLife cycle assessment reveals that HoLEP has a measurable environmental footprint; adoption of fluid management systems developed according to sustainable-by-design principles can significantly reduce its impact.Keywords: Environmental footprint; Life cycle assessment; HoLEP, Urology

What does this study add?

This study aimed to quantify through standardized life cycle assessment, the environmental impacts associated with a Holmium laser enucleation of the prostate procedure. The analysis identified fluid management as the principal contributor to the procedure’s environmental footprint. Systems designed according to sustainable-by-design principles (ISO 14006), such as the closed-circuit SUPER S.HO.W. aspirator, were associated with substantial reductions in carbon emissions and waste generation. These results provide a framework for integrating sustainability metrics into surgical practice. The methodology can be extended to other procedures to inform climate-conscious healthcare policy.

Clinical Relevance

HoLEP generates a median carbon footprint of 122 kg CO2-eq per procedure, with single-use fluid canisters accounting for 48% of the device-related environmental impact. Adoption of a sustainable-by-design fluid management system reduced the overall Product Environmental Footprint score by 23.6% and carbon emissions by 13.1%, while attenuating the incremental environmental cost of longer procedures. These findings support the incorporation of environmental performance alongside clinical and economic criteria in device procurement and surgical quality frameworks. Associate Editor: Véronique Phé.

Patient Summary

We evaluated the environmental impact of a commonly performed urologic procedure (Holmium laser enucleation of the prostate) using a standardized life cycle assessment and identified fluid management as the main contributor to its environmental footprint. The study shows that eco-efficient, sustainable-by-design equipment can substantially reduce emissions, waste generation, and resource consumption during the procedure.

Introduction

Climate change represents a major global challenge, requiring rapid and profound transformations across all sectors, including healthcare. The environmental impact of this sector is increasingly scrutinized due to its substantial resource consumption, biomedical waste production, and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions [1], [2]. Surgical activity is a major contributor, generating large volumes of plastic and electronic waste [3]. Reducing this impact requires a clear understanding of the life cycle of medical devices and procedures; however, life cycle assessment (LCA) remains underused in medicine despite its value for supporting evidence-based sustainability strategies [4].

LCA is a standardized method that quantifies the environmental footprint of a product or process by considering all stages from material extraction to end-of-life. In healthcare, it offers an essential framework for guiding environmentally responsible choices. To improve consistency and comparability, the European Commission introduced the Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) methodology, which provides harmonized guidelines for evaluating environmental impacts across 16 categories [5]. Integrating PEF within LCA strengthens transparency, robustness, and reproducibility.

In urology, surgery for benign prostatic obstruction (BPO) is among the most frequently performed interventions [6], [7]. Holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP) has become a reference technique due to its efficacy, safety profile, and applicability across all prostate sizes. However, despite extensive clinical evaluation, its environmental impact has not been comprehensively assessed.

This study aimed to quantify the environmental footprint of HoLEP using a full LCA, focusing on surgical processes and waste management. By identifying major environmental hotspots, we sought to provide evidence-based insights to support more sustainable surgical practice.

Materials and methods

Study population and surgical technique

Twenty patients underwent HoLEP under general anesthesia (GA) for lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) related to BPO. All provided written informed consent. Procedures were performed using a 100W Holmium:YAG laser (Lumenis, Yokneam, Israel), a reusable 550-µm Holmium laser fiber (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA), a 26Fr resectoscope, and a Drill-Cut morcellator (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germanny), following an en-bloc technique with early apical release. During enucleation and coagulation, the SUPER S.HO.W. system (INCAS, Villafranca di Verona, Italy) was used for fluid management. This device collects and disinfects liquid waste before automatic discharge into the wastewater system, eliminating manual handling and disposable plastic canisters usually routed to hazardous waste streams.

Patient and procedural characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Median age was 72.5 y (interquartile range [IQR]: 64.7–78.2) and median prostate volume was 82.5 g (IQR: 70–102.5). Median operative time was 83 min (IQR: 49.7–95.7), including 55 min for enucleation (35.7–67.7), 8 min for coagulation (6–12), and 13 min for morcellation (11.5–21). Median resected tissue weight was 50 g (42–70.5), delivered energy 103 kJ (93–109), and irrigation volume 25.5 L (20.2–33.7).

LCA and Bilan Carbone methodology

LCA is a standardized and internationally accepted method, governed by ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 standards (more information at: https://www.iso.org), that evaluates the environmental impacts of a product or service throughout its entire life cycle [8]. It considers all stages, from raw material extraction to end-of-life disposal, integrating indicators such as climate change, resource depletion, pollution, and ecotoxicity. The four required steps were followed: defining study goal and scope, compiling the life cycle inventory, assessing environmental impacts, and interpreting results. LCA is an iterative process requiring continuous adjustments to ensure a precise and useful analysis. It helps to identify environmental improvement opportunities and objectively compare different options to support sustainable decision-making.

The carbon footprint (CF) of the entire HoLEP procedure (all perioperative phases) was calculated using the Bilan Carbone methodology, encompassing direct (scope 1), energy-related indirect (scope 2), and supply chain (scope 3) emissions specific to the medical process. The Bilan Carbone methodology is consistent with ISO 14064-1:2018, which applies to GHG inventories “defined within a single geographical boundary, organizational unit, or production process”, thereby explicitly encompassing a production process such as a medical procedure.

The functional unit of HoLEP has been defined as one complete HoLEP for a patient with LUTS due to BPO under GA. The reference flows considered are the fluid management system (SUPER S.HO.W. or 10-L cans), the electrical energy consumed, and the solid waste generated (cans, packaging, consumables).

Goal and scope definition

The objective was to quantify the environmental impacts of HoLEP and identify the main contributors, with a focus on medical devices and procedure-related resource use. This study also provides quantitative data on the environmental footprint of HoLEP that may support hospital procurement teams, biomedical departments, and institutional decision-makers when evaluating equipment during tender processes, optimizing resource use in surgical practice, and informing the development of devices incorporating sustainable-by-design principles. Four stages were included: (1) Manufacturing (which encompassed raw material extraction, upstream transportation, and the production processes of medical devices and healthcare products); (2) Distribution (including transportation from the manufacturer to the hospital); (3) Use phase (electricity, water and consumables required for the procedure); (4) End-of-life management (waste treatment and disposal).

Patient and staff transportation were excluded as they are not directly modifiable within the procedural setting and fall outside the study’s system boundaries. Maintenance of biomedical equipment, hospital construction, and pharmaceuticals lacking reliable inventory data were also excluded.

To compensate for these limitations, several assumptions were made. Energy consumption was allocated based on floor area (operating room [OR] vs entire building), distinguishing between thermal and electrical energy, and considering the total duration of the procedure.

Environmental impacts were characterized with the EF 3.1 method (16 PEF categories), producing a single aggregated score in Points (Pt) facilitating comparisons between products or processes.

Life cycle inventory

A secured database was used to record all medical devices, medications, and consumables, including product references, material composition, and packaging weights. Devices were weighed with and without packaging. For multi-material items, components were either separated after use or estimated by percentage composition. Technical specifications (sterilization, materials, manufacturing) were retrieved from manufacturers.

Clinical workflow data were collected prospectively, including procedure timing, waste quantities by category, cleaning products, gases, and device usage. Data collection was structured according to different stages of the protocol: pre-intervention, intervention, and post-intervention (Fig. 1).

System boundary of the HoLEP life cycle assessment. The diagram depicts the three peri-operative phases (pre-intervention, intervention, and post-intervention) and the processes included within the system boundary. Each phase encompasses medical devices and consumables (production, packaging, and transport), energy consumption (electricity, heating), water use, and waste streams (infectious clinical waste, municipal waste). Hand washing includes water and soap consumption. Equipment preparation and verification cover electricity for device setup and testing (laser, morcellator, resectoscope, video tower). Anesthesia includes the anesthetic circuit and consumables; anesthetic gases were excluded. Patient and staff transportation, hospital infrastructure, equipment manufacturing amortization, and pharmaceuticals without reliable inventory data were excluded from the analysis.

LCA evaluation

Environmental modeling was performed using SimaPro v9.6 (Amersfoort, NL) with the Ecoinvent v3.5 database. PEF indicators (16 categories) were used for impact assessment (Supplementary Appendix Table 1). By applying PEF principles, this study aims to enhance the accuracy and comparability of environmental impact assessments, ensuring a more standardized and transparent evaluation of HoLEP.

Simulation of the scenario without SUPER S.HO.W.

To objectively compare both surgical approaches, and to satisfy LCA methodological principles, we reconstructed the environmental impact of the “without SUPER S.HO.W.” scenario for all patients who actually underwent surgery with the device.

This simulation was based on the exact material configuration historically used in our department before adoption of the SUPER S.HO.W. Specifically, we identified the type, number, and mass of plastic collection containers previously used per HoLEP procedure. Therefore, the comparison between techniques reflects two real-world, operationally implemented workflows, with the SUPER S.HO.W. scenario representing current practice, and the “without” scenario representing the validated historical standard in our institution.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; version 4.0.0). Continuous variables included age, body mass index, prostate volume, operative time, laser time, morcellation time, delivered energy, irrigation volume, PEF score, and CF. These variables were summarized as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables included American Society of Anesthesiologists score, antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation therapy, hospitalization type, and use of the SUPER S.HO.W. system, and were summarized as counts and percentages. Because environmental impacts were modeled for each procedure under two scenarios (with and without the closed-circuit system), comparisons were paired and performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Associations between operative duration and environmental outcomes were evaluated using univariable linear regression stratified by device use, with regression lines and 95% confidence bands. Two multivariable linear regression models (PEF and CF) were built, including prostate volume, laser time, delivered energy, and morcellation time. Regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, p values, and model performance metrics (R2, adjusted R2) were reported. Assumptions were checked via residual analysis. Multivariable analyses were exploratory and intended to identify potential procedural drivers of environmental impact.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Environmental Impact Analysis

LCA HoLEP without SUPER S.HO.W.

The functional unit was defined as one complete HoLEP performed for LUTS due to BPO under GA, without use of the closed-circuit fluid management system. The reference flows included fluid collection canisters (10-L containers), electricity consumption during the procedure, and solid waste generation (including canisters, packaging, and consumables).

The LCA estimates for HoLEP are presented in Table 2. The PEF score and CF for the entire procedure, from OR entry to exit (including all steps described in Fig. 1), were 7.5 (6.9–8.7) and 122 kg CO2-eq (IQR: 118–144), respectively. The surgical phase accounted for 50% of the total PEF score and 41% of the total CF.

Scenario analysis: LCA HoLEP with SUPER S.HO.W. (sustainable-by-design)

The functional unit was defined as one complete HoLEP performed for a patient with LUTS due to BPO under GA, using a closed-circuit fluid management system. The reference flows included the closed-circuit fluid management system (SUPER S.HO.W.), electricity consumption during the procedure, and solid waste generation (including fluid containers, packaging, and consumables).

With SUPER S.HO.W., the PEF score and CF decreased to 5.9 points (5.7–6.1) and 106 kg CO2-eq (102–118), respectively. The surgical phase represented 49% of the PEF score and 36% of the CF.

This scenario using the SUPER S.HO.W. was associated with reductions across all assessed impact categories, including a 23.6% decrease in overall PEF, a 36.5% reduction in the surgical phase contribution, and a 45.7% decrease in solid waste production. The system was associated with a small additional energy requirement (0.005 mPts), which remained marginal relative to the total procedural impact.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the LCA to key modeling assumptions. Variations in OR energy consumption (450–750 kWh/m2), OR surface area, and procedure duration produced only minor changes in the PEF score and CF, without altering the relative contribution of major impact drivers [9], [10].

Similarly, varying assumptions regarding consumable quantities and the lifespan of reusable textiles (40–80 use cycles, reference: 60 cycles) resulted in minimal variation in environmental indicators [11]. Across all tested scenarios, fluid management and single-use device consumption remained the dominant contributors to environmental impact.

Overall, these analyses indicate that the main findings were robust to plausible variations in modeling parameters.

Relationship between operative duration and both the PEF score and CF

Linear regression models showed a significant association between operative duration and both PEF and CF in procedures performed with and without SUPER S.HO.W. (Fig. 2). For the PEF score, the slope was steeper without the device (β = 0.027 ± 0.005) than with it (β = 0.010 ± 0.001), indicating a higher marginal environmental cost per operative minute. Model fit was strong in both groups but higher with SUPER S.HO.W. (R2 = 0.73 vs 0.65).

Comparison of PEF score as a function of operative duration with and without SUPER S.HO.W.

For the CF, operative duration was again positively associated with emissions. The slope was almost twice as steep without the device (β = 0.427 ± 0.073) compared to with it (β = 0.217 ± 0.035). Model performance remained good (R2 = 0.68 with SUPER S.HO.W. vs 0.65 without).

Overall, longer procedures yielded higher environmental impacts in all cases, but the magnitude of the increase was consistently attenuated when using the SUPER S.HO.W. device.

Multivariable analysis

To further examine the determinants of environmental impact during HoLEP, multivariable linear regression models were performed for both the PEF score and the CF (Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. S1), adjusting for prostate volume, laser time, delivered energy, and morcellation duration. Laser time, prostate volume, and morcellation duration were all independent predictors of higher environmental impact. Laser time was the strongest driver (PEF: β = 0.026, p = 0.0015; CF: β = 0.452, p = 0.0010). Prostate volume (PEF: β = 0.0128; CF: β = 0.202) and morcellation duration (PEF: β = 0.034; CF: β = 0.553) also contributed significantly, while delivered laser energy showed no association.

Discussion

Robust quantitative evaluations and evidence-based strategies are needed to reduce the environmental footprint of healthcare and support sustainable surgical practices [12].

Surgery for LUTS due to BPO is among the most common urological procedures, and the use of HoLEP has increased substantially [7]. Although its clinical benefits are well established, its environmental impact has remained insufficiently characterized.

This study provides a comprehensive LCA of HoLEP using the standardized PEF methodology. A previous study by Misrai et al assessed the CF of disposable devices used in transurethral prostate surgery, including the HoLEP laser fiber, but did not evaluate the full intra-operative pathway or the 16 PEF impact categories [12]. Our work extends these findings by analyzing the entire HoLEP procedure, including energy use and waste management associated with fluid disposal.

HoLEP contributes substantially to the environmental burden of surgical activity, with a median CF of 122 kg CO2-eq—comparable to other urological or minimally invasive procedures [13]. The adoption of the SUPER S.HO.W. system significantly reduced both the PEF score and CF (−23.6% and −13.1%), primarily by eliminating single-use plastic canisters. Importantly, these reductions were driven by structural changes in fluid waste handling rather than device-specific modeling assumptions, suggesting that similar closed-circuit systems could yield comparable environmental benefits. Operative duration was a key determinant of impact, but the closed-circuit fluid management system attenuated this increase, reducing the incremental environmental impact associated with longer procedures.

These results are consistent with previous LCAs comparing reusable versus single-use urological devices [11], [13], [14]. For example, Baboudjian et al reported that reprocessing reusable cystoscopes may generate a higher CF than single-use devices. Similarly, Pradere et al highlighted fluid management and waste segregation as important levers for climate-smart surgical practice [4]. Although our study focused on liquid waste, potential reductions in packaging waste associated with canister elimination merit further evaluation.

Medical waste disposal remains a critical but often neglected dimension of the surgical footprint. Although waste treatment contributes modestly to total GHG emissions, it represents an important public health and regulatory concern. The World Health Organization estimates that 15% of healthcare waste is hazardous. Recent sustainability frameworks identify waste management as a key driver of environmentally responsible hospital operations [15]. Integrated fluid management systems offer both environmental and occupational safety benefits.

All procedures in this cohort were performed under GA, which has its own environmental burden due to anesthetic gases—particularly desflurane and nitrous oxide [16], [17], [18]. Spinal anesthesia can substantially reduce emissions by avoiding volatile agents, while local anesthesia used in minimally invasive BPO treatments such as prostatic urethral lift (UroLift), water vapor thermal therapy (Rezūm), or temporary implantable nitinol device (iTIND), may further decrease upstream impacts. Although anesthesia was not our primary focus, adopting low-carbon anesthetic strategies represents an additional opportunity to reduce HoLEP’s footprint.

Contextualizing HoLEP among other BPO treatments is essential. Based on the partial LCA by Misrai et al, device-related emissions range from 76 g CO2-eq for temporary implantable nitinol devices to >2 kg CO2-eq for water vapor thermal therapy systems [12]. These values, however, exclude the substantial intra-operative footprint linked to energy use, irrigation, and waste. Our full-process analysis indicates that HoLEP’s environmental performance is comparable to other endoscopic techniques, while demonstrating clear benefits when combined with sustainable-by-design fluid management systems [18].

Recent studies have increasingly quantified the environmental footprint of urological diagnostics and interventions. Leapman et al evaluated the environmental impact of prostate magnetic resonance imaging and biopsy pathways, while Jahrreiss et al compared reusable and single-use cystoscopy systems [19], [20]. Additional work has estimated emissions associated with prostate-specific antigen testing and common laparoscopic or robotic procedures [21], [22]. Together, these studies highlight that reported CFs vary substantially depending on how system boundaries are defined—whether analyses focus on individual devices, anesthesia, or the entire perioperative pathway.

In this context, our cradle-to-grave procedural approach allows a more comprehensive assessment of HoLEP and facilitates comparison with other surgical LCAs. Although direct comparisons remain limited by methodological heterogeneity, the environmental footprint of HoLEP appears to be within the range reported for other endoscopic urological procedures. These findings emphasize the importance of transparent boundary definitions when interpreting and comparing environmental metrics across studies.

Healthcare accounts for approximately 4.4% of global GHG emissions—and up to 10% in high-income countries—when including scope 1–3 emissions across the value chain, driven in part by ORs with high energy and disposable consumption [1]. Integrating environmental performance indicators into surgical quality frameworks is increasingly necessary.

This study has several strengths, including prospective data collection, an ISO-compliant LCA, and the use of standardized PEF indicators. Limitations include system boundaries excluding infrastructure, equipment manufacturing, and staff transport, which may underestimate absolute impacts but do not affect comparative conclusions. The sample size was limited, and a full environmental analysis would ideally include the entire perioperative pathway. Accordingly, the multivariable analyses should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution.

Beyond its direct environmental impact, climate change may also influence surgical practice through supply chain disruptions. For instance, shortages of intravenous fluids following major hurricanes in the United States have led to postponement of elective procedures, including HoLEP. This illustrates the bidirectional relationship between healthcare and climate change: surgical activity contributes to environmental emissions, while climate-related events can in turn compromise the availability of essential medical resources. Incorporating resilience and resource efficiency into surgical systems may therefore provide both environmental and operational benefits [23], [24].

Ultimately, this study highlights the need for healthcare systems to align clinical excellence with environmental responsibility. Beyond technological innovation, sustainable surgery will require education, policy evolution, and procurement strategies that integrate environmental considerations into routine decision-making.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive LCA of the HoLEP procedure, identifying liquid waste management as a major contributor to its environmental impact. The SUPER S.HO.W. device offers meaningful reductions in both PEF score and CF, illustrating the value of technologies developed according to sustainable-by-design principles. However, lasting progress will also require broader efforts, including education, regulatory support, and the incorporation of environmental indicators into surgical quality metrics and procurement strategies.

Overall, this work reinforces that healthcare has both the opportunity and the responsibility to align clinical performance with environmental stewardship, making sustainability an essential component of modern urological practice.
