Epigenomic study of the lower airway reveals COPD-associated methylation patterns and potential microbiota links
Authors: Shuo Cao, Abhishek Shrivastav, Eugene R Bleecker, Siddharth Madapoosi, John Erb-Downward, Joe Zein, Xianfeng Chen, Timothy D Howard, Gregory A Hawkins, Igor Z Barjaktarevic, Russell Bowler, Robert Graham Barr, Alejandro Comellas, Christopher B Cooper, David Couper, Meilan Han, Nadia N Hansel, Annette T Hastie, Robert Kaner, Richard E Kanner, Victor Kim, Fernando J Martinez, Wendy C Moore, Wanda K O’Neal, Robert Paine, Benjamin Smith, Eric A Hoffman, Deborah A Meyers, Prescott G Woodruff, Stephanie Christenson, Jeffrey L Curtis, Yvonne Jean Huang, Victor E Ortega
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite the identification of multiple susceptibility loci by genome-wide association studies (GWAS), considerable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) heritability remains unexplained.AimTo identify interaction networks of airway epithelial cell DNA methylation in COPD and further explore potential correlations with airway bacterial composition, as potentially collective regulators of biological pathways influencing COPD severity.MethodsUsing DNA isolated from bronchial airway brushings of 67 ever-smokers (>20 pack-years) from the SubPopulations and InteRmediate Outcomes Measures in COPD Study (SPIROMICS), we assessed proportion of DNA methylation (β) by epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) and examined associations of differentially methylated CpG probes (DMPs) with risk for moderate-to-severe COPD (N=34) versus absent or mild COPD (N=33). We tested co-methylation modules generated by Weighted Correlation Network Analyses (WGCNA) for associations with moderate-to-severe COPD and with bacterial genus-level relative abundances (16S rRNA sequencing).ResultsEWAS-identified nominally significant DMPs enriched for lung function GWAS loci. Eigengenes in six WGCNA modules were associated with moderate-to-severe COPD (false discovery rate <0.05). Four of those modules were enriched for forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity GWAS loci, and five overlapped with DMPs from EWAS. Overlapping CpG loci in three COPD-associated modules were adjacent to mucin genes; one had 10 genes highly ranked by connectivity with MUC5B, including important pathway genes: B3GNT6, DGKI and ITGA8. CpGs in an independent COPD-associated module showed the most correlations with Rothia, with directionality suggestive of negative associations with moderate-severe COPD.ConclusionsBronchial epithelial DNA methylation modules enriched for lung function GWAS loci associate with COPD severity in SPIROMICS. Potential module relationships to bronchial bacterial composition require further validation.Keywords: Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive; Airway Epithelium

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a leading worldwide cause of death and morbidity, is a group of disorders defined by incompletely reversible airflow obstruction. To date, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of lung function have identified over 1000 loci of susceptibility to lung function impairment. Unsurprisingly, based on the definition of COPD, many such loci are shared with COPD risk. However, despite an observed heritability of COPD of approximately 40%, current polygenic risk scores account for only a small proportion of disease prevalence.1,8 Mechanistic explanations of this missing heritability to understand risk for COPD are crucial to developing interventional strategies.

Development of COPD is, of course, not purely genetic, but instead also depends crucially on environment factors, particularly exposure to tobacco smoke. In response to various environmental exposures, DNA undergoes epigenetic modification, best studied as DNA methylation. Studies in cohorts with significant smoking histories, without or with spirometrically defined COPD, have identified colocalisation of numerous differentially methylated CpG probes (DMPs) sites near regions previously identified as associated with COPD risk (‘COPD GWAS loci’).9,15 Yet even these findings also account for only a small proportion of COPD heritability. A potential explanation is limitations based on analysis of DNA isolated from blood, sputum cells or homogenised lung tissues containing a mixture of cell types. In contrast, studying airway epithelial cells from the disease site can enhance understanding of the local epigenome landscape and its impact on COPD severity.

One environmental factor increasingly recognised as driving lung disease development is the community structure of the lower respiratory tract microbiome.16,19 Microbiome studies in at-risk current and ex-smokers have demonstrated significant differences in airway microbiota composition related to smoking and COPD risk.1719,21 Although an earlier study examined relationships between upper airway microbiota and nasal epigenetic changes in childhood asthma,22 to our knowledge, no previous studies have integrated the airway epigenome, transcriptome and microbiome in COPD to understand their joint contributions to COPD severity. Such an approach has previously provided evidence for a link between dysbiosis of the gut microbiome and epigenetic alterations in inflammatory bowel disease,23 obesity,24 cancer25,28 and metabolic disorders.29

Here, we performed integrative multiomics studies to identify DNA methylation sites in bronchial epithelium associated with COPD severity, with a specific exploratory focus on potential relationships with bronchial bacterial composition. Such an approach could provide additional insights into the molecular underpinnings of COPD pathogenesis at the epithelial interface that may help explain missing COPD heritability.

Methods

Participants description

We performed an integrated analysis for bronchial air epithelial DNA methylation and microbiome datasets in 67 participants from the SubPopulations and InteRmediate Outcomes Measures in COPD Study (SPIROMICS) cohort who underwent bronchoscopy and had available protected epithelial DNA brushings collected for integrated epigenetic and Microbiota analysis (online supplemental figure E1 and E2A,B). Participant recruitment and exclusion criteria have been described in the SPIROMICS study design paper.

DNA methylation, microbiome and transcriptome data were generated from airway epithelial cell brushings from n=79 participants in the SPIROMICS cohort. For analyses in the current study, participant data were excluded if they had no history of smoking (N=6), missing information on COPD status (N=1), missing principal components (PCs) derived from ancestry data (N=3), or missing PRMfSAD data (N=1), or were identified as cluster outliers (N=1). The remaining current and ex-smoking participants (N=67) with a prior smoking history of >20 pack-years (having smoked the equivalent of one pack of cigarettes per day for 20 years or more) were divided based on those without (N=23) or with mild COPD (GOLD spirometric stage 1, N=10), for comparison to participants with moderate or severe COPD (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) spirometric stage 2 or greater, N=34).

Bronchoscopic collection and epigenome-wide methylation data generation

Together with negative extraction controls, genomic DNA was extracted from cell pellets of bronchoscopic protected specimen brushings of the bronchial mucosa, as previously described,30 and used for both DNA methylation and microbiota analysis.

The extracted DNA underwent bisulfite conversion using the EZ-96 DNA Methylation kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, California, USA). We detected DNA methylation using the Illumina Infinium Methylation EPIC BeadChip Array, covering 850 000 CpG sites across the genome and measured as a β proportion of methylation.

We conducted a comprehensive quality control process using the ChAMP R package,31 including comprehensive filtering and batch effect correction (slides and array), as detailed previously.32 Specifically, we applied the following filters, including: (a) removal of probes with detection p>0.01; (b) removal of probes with fewer than 3 beads in at least 5% of samples per probe; (c) exclusion of all non-CpG probes; (d) exclusion of probes overlapping with known SNPs; (e) exclusion of cross-reactive (multihit) probes and (f) exclusion of probes located on sex chromosomes (X and Y). Batch effects from slides and arrays were corrected using ComBat. Probes with β<0.2 in at least 20% of samples were removed to focus on highly variable probes and the top 400 000 probes with the highest variance among samples were selected for downstream analysis.

Microbiota data generation

16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq at the University of Michigan Microbiome Core as previously described.17 All samples were sequenced in a single batch. Sequence data were processed in QIIME2.33 Rarefaction curves showed that alpha-diversity plateaued at approximately 1000 reads, and the median number of sample reads was 5500. The 16S rRNA sequence files will be available under NCBI Sequence Read Archive Bioproject ID PRJNA1146081.34 Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were determined using DADA235 and matched to taxonomies using the SILVA database (V.138–99).36 The ASV-level data were collapsed to genus level, and centred log-ratio transformation applied to the relative abundance measures.

Epigenome-Wide Association Study analysis

Normalised methylation levels of 400 000 CpG sites were used to plot the hierarchical clustering dendrogram of samples based on their Euclidean distance and one outlier sample was removed. Subsequently, we performed Epigenome-Wide Association Study (EWAS) using linear mixed-effects models implemented in the Meffil R package37 to identify the correlation of EWAS DMPs with moderate-to-severe COPD status. EWAS regression models included age, sex, smoking pack-years, smoking status (current vs former, defined as ≥6 months of cessation), clinical site, inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) use, and the first five PCs of ancestry. Because no DMPs reached epigenome-wide or false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted significance, we reported our top associations and evaluated associated DMPs with an unadjusted p<0.05 for downstream enrichment analyses. The Manhattan plot was generated using the R package CMplot.38 Those genes corresponding to these DMPs were subjected to enrichment analysis in GWAS Catalog using Enrichr,39 as well as canonical pathway analysis using QIAGEN Ingenuity Pathway Analysis.40

DNA co-methylation and interaction network analyses

We performed co-methylation network analyses of DNA methylation data using the R package Weighted Correlation Network Analyses (WGCNA).41 To achieve a scale-free topology, an optimal soft thresholding power was determined. The ‘blockwiseModules’ function with specified parameters detected co-methylation modules as detailed in the online supplemental file 1. Module colours were assigned arbitrarily for identification purposes and do not reflect any biological significance. In WGCNA, modules represent groups of co-methylated CpG sites, and each subject contributes to all modules through continuous module eigengene (ME) values.

Linear multivariate regression models evaluated associations between each module (eigengene values) and traits of interest after covariate correction as described above and in the online supplemental file 1. Module membership (MM) was defined as the correlation between each gene’s methylation profile and the ME, reflecting how central a gene is to a given module. Gene Significance (GS) was defined as the absolute value of the correlation between each gene’s methylation and COPD status. Pearson correlations were calculated between MM and GS for each module to determine whether genes most representative of a module were also the genes most strongly associated with COPD status. The genes corresponding to CpG sites within each COPD-associated module were used for enrichment analysis in the GWAS Catalog and canonical pathway analysis. The top ten genes in the black module containing MUC5B were selected to construct mucin gene biological interaction networks through Cytoscape42 as detailed in the online supplemental file 1.

Identification of microbiota-associated modules and CpG sites

Genus values beyond 1.5 times the IQR of Q1 or Q3 were treated as outliers and removed. Linear regression models including the same covariates as above assessed the correlation between six WGCNA COPD-associated modules (eigengene values) and normalised genus abundance. For microbiota-associated modules, we evaluated individual CpG sites to identify those directly associated with microbiota genus abundance. The genes corresponding to CpG sites regulated by microbiota were also evaluated for GWAS Catalog enrichment and biological pathway analysis.

Correlation analysis between DNA methylation and gene expression

Transcriptomic data were generated as previously described. Detailed information on library preparation, sequencing and quality control can be found in the original publication.43 We replaced expression levels and DNA β methylation values with residuals obtained from regression analysis to control for the same covariates. Samples were stratified into high and low methylation groups and compared with differences in transcript expression levels between these two groups.44

Statistical analysis

We performed statistical analysis using R (V.4.2.2) or Python (V.3.10.13) programming language. We used the Student’s t-test or χ² test to compare the difference in clinical characteristics between two groups. To account for multiple comparisons, we used the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the FDR. Unless otherwise stated, all reported FDR values refer to this method. We performed multivariable linear regression analyses to evaluate the associations of interest, adjusting for potential confounders including study site, age, sex, inhaled steroid use, current smoking status, smoking pack-years and the top five genetic PCs. All models were implemented using the statsmodels package in Python.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Airway epithelial epigenetic associations with COPD status

After removing a single outlier (online supplemental figure E2C), we identified 15 349 EWAS DMPs (online supplemental table E1) as nominally associated with moderate-to-severe COPD (p≤0.05); 13 DMPs reached a suggestive (p<1.00×10−4) threshold (online supplemental figure E3A) but none attaining epigenome-wide significance. Among our top associations (online supplemental table E2) was a DMP adjacent to the NEIL2 gene (cg09768257, p=9.00×10−6), which contributes to safeguarding role in the airway against virus infection.46 Another was adjacent to the SSH1 gene (cg14032749, p=3.36×10−5). Previous research has shown that the mRNA levels of SSH1 correspond with DNA methylation differences in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension.47

The gene set corresponding to the nominally associated DMPs was enriched (PFDR<0.05) for terms in the GWAS Catalog for ‘postbronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/FEV ratio’ and ‘postbronchodilator FEV1 in COPD’ (online supplemental figure E3B). Pathway enrichment analysis identified five top biologic pathways, including protein kinase A signalling (online supplemental figure E3C), which regulates inflammation and tissue damage in COPD,48 beta-adrenergic receptor signalling that regulates airway smooth muscle tone and beta-agonist induced bronchodilation.49 Upstream Regulator analysis also identified TGFB150 and TNF-alpha which relate to fibrosis, repair and inflammation in airways cells in COPD (online supplemental figure E4).51

Identification of COPD-associated co-methylation modules

Hierarchical clustering was performed to group the samples, followed by outlier detection. A sample dendrogram was generated to all related traits (figure 1A). We identified 47 co-methylation modules, of which 6 (table 2; full results in online supplemental table E6) demonstrated FDR-adjusted significant, differential associations with moderate-to-severe COPD (online supplemental figure E6). Correlations were positive for the Salmon, Orange-red4 and Plum1 modules (r=0.54, p=1.15×10−6; r=0.46, p=5.19×10−5; r=0.47, p=3.04×10−5, respectively), and negative for the yellow, light-yellow and black modules (r=–0.56, p=3.65×10−7; r=–0.47, p=3.66×10−5; r=–0.49, p=1.64×10−5) (figure 1B, colour-labelled). MM and GS for COPD strongly correlated, as seen in the black (r=–0.50, p<1.00×10−200) and salmon module (r=0.53, p<1.00×10−200), respectively (figure 1C,D).

Pathway enrichment and overlap of co-methylation modules and EWAS loci

Of the six moderate-severe COPD-associated WGCNA modules, all but light-yellow were enriched (PFDR<0.05) for GWAS loci for lung function and COPD status based on the GWAS Catalog (online supplemental figure E5A). These modules were enriched for multiple biologic pathways relevant to COPD (online supplemental figure E5B). These included pathways known to regulate intercellular and intracellular signalling: (a) via neurotransmitters (Opioid signalling pathway and Serotonin Receptor Signalling); (b) by and distal to cell surface receptors (G-Protein Coupled Receptor Signalling, S100 Family signalling pathway, and ERK/MAPK Signalling); (c) within epithelia (Epithelial Adherens Junction Signalling and Regulation of the Epithelial Mesenchymal Transition by Growth Factors Pathway) and via cytokines (IL-15 Production, STAT3 Pathway and Oncostatin M signalling). The top five upstream regulators for each module are listed in the online supplemental figure E7A.

Five of the six moderate-severe COPD-associated WGCNA modules showed significant overlap (p<0.05) with nominally significant EWAS DMPs (online supplemental figures E5C,D and E7B–D). The black, salmon and yellow modules displayed overlapping DMPs adjacent to genes encoding mucin proteins, including MUC5B (online supplemental figures E5C,D and E7E). Within the black module, 166 genes had network connectivity with MUC5B (online supplemental table E3, top 10 connected genes illustrated in online supplemental figure E5E).

Exploratory analysis of DNA methylation-microbiota associations in COPD

Finally, to explore potential crosstalk between bronchial epithelial DNA methylation and bronchial microbiota composition, we correlated abundances of the 222 identified bacterial genera and the six COPD-associated co-methylation modules. This exploratory correlation analysis identified nine genera-module associated pairs at an unadjusted p<0.05 (table 3; full results in online supplemental table E7) encompassing seven bacterial genera (Neisseria, Sneathia, Rothia, F0058, Corynebacterium, Butyrivibrio and Anaeroglobus) spanning four WGCNA co-methylation modules (salmon, orange-red4, light-yellow and plum1) (figure 2A). By deconstructing these four co-methylation modules into individual CpG probes, we identified the following number of potential genera-DMP paired associations (p<0.001): salmon, 322; light-yellow, 7; orange-red4, 3; plum1, 3; (online supplemental table E4). In descending order, the most frequently DMP-associated genera included Rothia, F0058, Neisseria, Sneathia, Mogibacterium, Peptococcus, Corynebacterium and Kingella (figure 2B). The CpG probes within the Salmon module that associated with Rothia abundance (online supplemental table E5) were enriched for the GWAS Catalog term ‘lung function (FEV1/forced vital capacity)’ (figure 2C) and for the following biological pathways: Th1, Th1 and Th2 activation, and signalling via actin cytoskeleton, sonic hedgehog and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) (figure 2D).

Within the identified genera-module pairs with nominal significance, we observed a CpG probe (cg17238009) in the salmon module that exhibited pronounced modulation by most genera (151/182 or 83.0%); this methylation site was adjacent to the gene, C10orf11, also known as LRMDA (online supplemental figure E8A). To investigate the relationship between DNA methylation of this site and transcript expression of C10orf11, we divided the samples into high-methylation (top quartile) and low-methylation (bottom quartile) groups based on their DNA methylation values. This eQTM analysis revealed that the high-methylation group exhibited significantly lower transcript expression compared with the low-methylation group (online supplemental figure E8B). The median transcript expression level for the high-methylation versus low-methylation groups was −0.49 vs 0.49 for all samples group (p=0.035),–0.72 vs 0.53 for the without or mild COPD group (p=0.45), and −0.80 vs 0.66 for the moderate or severe COPD group (p=0.0019), respectively.

Discussion

Using bronchoscopically collected brush specimens from 67 individuals with smoking history in SPIROMICS, this study examined DNA methylation of bronchial airway epithelia and performed paired ‘omics analysis to further explore correlation of DNA methylation pattern with bronchial bacterial composition. Results indicate that: (1) specific CpG probes, genes and methylomic modules from bronchial epithelia enriched for lung function GWAS loci differentially associated with COPD severity and (2) epithelial-associated bacterial genera, in particular Rothia, displayed negative directional relationships with specific co-methylation modules that were associated with more advanced (moderate-to-severe) COPD. Despite the small sample size of this unique dataset, these novel findings provide preliminary evidence that epigenomic changes in bronchial epithelial cells are differentially associated with COPD severity. We also identified possible epigenome-microbiome interactions at the epithelial interface that could play a role in COPD pathogenesis, pending confirmation in independent, larger datasets.

Through WGCNA analyses, we identified several COPD-associated CpG probes and genes that were enriched not only for GWAS loci for impaired lung function and COPD risk, but also for multiple highly plausible signalling pathways. They included neurotransmitters52 53 and GPCRs,54 55 which by regulating airway smooth muscle tone and mucus production potentially contribute to airway constriction and inflammation in COPD. The dysregulation of multiple S100 family members in COPD has led them to be considered potential therapeutic targets.56 57 MAPK signalling cascade is involved in the inflammatory response of lung injury.58,60 Alterations in epithelial signalling pathways could impact lung function by affecting tissue remodelling and repair.61,63 In COPD, dysregulated cytokine signalling is common, including IL-15 signalling, which has been linked to emphysema pathogenesis.64 65

MUC5B has been extensively studied in the context of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), where a common promoter variant (rs35705950) is one of the strongest known genetic risk factors for IPF and other interstitial lung diseases.66,68 This variant causes MUC5B overexpression in distal airways, contributing to impaired mucociliary clearance and susceptibility to fibrosis.69 While these associations are well established, our study focuses on COPD, in which the role of MUC5B is distinct from its role in other respiratory diseases. MUC5B in COPD is primarily involved in mucus hypersecretion and impaired clearance, contributing to airway obstruction, chronic bronchitis and exacerbations.70,72 Our investigation revealed significant overlap between EWAS DMP genes and COPD-associated co-methylation module genes. MUC5B, MUC6 and MUC19, found in the black module, are primarily associated with airway mucosal defence and mucus regulation. Their presence within the overlap highlights the complexity of mucin subtypes and suggests that epigenetic mucin gene regulatory modification may contribute to the established role of mucin regulation in COPD pathophysiology. In the future, direct RNAscope or immunofluorescence in bronchial biopsies would be ideal to strengthen these findings. Furthermore, among the top 10 genes with the highest connectivity to MUC5B were DGKI, which influences airways smooth muscle contraction,73 74 B3GNT6, involved in glycosylation of mucins,75 and ITGA8, which mediates TGF-β activation.76

Our nascent observation of potential interactions between DNA methylation and bacterial microbiota composition in the lower airway epithelium invites further consideration of the potential role such interactions may have in modulating susceptibility to COPD and progression to more advanced disease. Within the salmon module (which was associated directionally with moderate-severe COPD), the most conspicuous correlation was observed with the Rothia genus. Notably, Rothia mucilaginosa has been shown to inhibit pathogen-induced inflammation in vitro and in vivo and correlated with reduced pro-inflammatory markers in bronchiectasis patients via NF-κB pathway inhibition.77 Consistent with these molecular mechanisms, the presence of Rothia correlated in a potentially protective direction, against moderate-severe COPD in our study, based on the negative correlation between Rothia abundance and CpG probes in the Salmon module that were positively associated with moderate-severe COPD, even if non-significant with FDR adjustment. This observation is also concordant with previous results from this same SPIROMICS bronchoscopy substudy, in which Rothia and other genera in bronchoalveolar lavage samples associated with higher lung function.78 In the current analysis, we note that other bacterial genera, such as Butyrivibrio and Anaeroglobus, displayed similarly suggestive protective associations. These genera belong to the Lachnospiraceae and Veillonellaceae families, members of which have also been associated with better clinical outcomes.78 These potential associations between DNA methylation and specific genera, particularly Rothia, warrant further investigation in future studies.

The strong and numerous microbiota associations with methylation at cg17238009, which corresponds to the C10orf11 (LRMDA) locus, are interesting, as this gene has been identified in a lung function GWAS of European whites.8 Significant differences between the control and COPD groups in methylation levels of CpG adjacent to C10orf11 were previously found in analysis of whole-blood DNA.11 Based on these associations and the eQTM association between DNA methylation and transcript expression of C10orf11 we describe, we can speculate that the nature of COPD risk related to C10orf11 could result from microbiome-epigenome interactions.

Although there are important insights revealed by this study, there are also limitations. First, the small size of our study sample reduced the power of statistical tests to identify significant differences. For this reason, to facilitate subsequent downstream analysis and reduce false negatives, we used less conservative thresholds but supported our findings with plausible GWAS locus and biologic pathway enrichment and functional eQTM analyses, helping to prevent potentially meaningful biological associations from being overlooked. Additionally, although the number of covariates included in our models was large relative to our small size, each covariate was necessary for the analysis. To address the limitation of the current study’s small sample size, further studies using larger sample sizes in sputum are currently underway, which we anticipate will provide more robust validation of our findings. Our epigenomic study, similar to previous GWAS of lung function and COPD status,2,68 is challenged in distinguishing general population lung function genes versus disease genes because they are present in both normal and disease samples with minimal differences. Although the reported associations do not necessarily imply causation, independent verification would provide further support and be ideal. However, to our knowledge, there are no comparable studies that include paired multiomics data (epigenome, microbiome and transcriptome) from lower airway epithelial samples. Additionally, the use of a β-value <0.2 cut-off for probe selection, while prioritising variability, may have excluded biologically relevant loci with low methylation. Future studies could explore different filtering thresholds to better balance variability with biological relevance.

In summary, this integrative analysis of lung epigenetics and microbiome identifies novel microbial-epigenetic interactions within the lung and their potential cooperative role in COPD development and progression. Although our findings require confirmation with functional in vitro studies and independent cohort replication, our findings may have potential clinical implications, such as COPD-associated co-methylation modules and microbiome-epigenomic interactions that could serve as biomarkers for disease risk, progression or therapeutic response and may ultimately guide the development of precision medicine strategies in COPD.
