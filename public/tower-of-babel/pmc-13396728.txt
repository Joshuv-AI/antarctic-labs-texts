Experimental and numerical study of adding TiO2 nanoparticles effect on the thermomechanical behavior of welded joints of Steel buildings
Authors: Maria Norouzi, Amir Saedi Daryan, Mousa Mohammadpourfard, Saeed Zeinali Heris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the impact of titanium dioxide (TiO2) nanoparticles on the thermomechanical behavior of L-shaped welded joints in steel structures. Nanoparticles were incorporated into the weld metal at three weight ratios (0.5, 1.25, and 2 wt%) using the cold spray coating technique, and the samples were heat-treated across a wide temperature range (25–900 °C). Experimental results demonstrated that the addition of 2 wt% TiO2 nanoparticles significantly enhanced the ultimate load capacity by up to 25% compared to nanoparticle-free joints. The improvement was more pronounced in thicker steel sheets (30 mm), where uniform nanoparticle distribution and better stress dissipation were observed. The resistance recovery coefficient decreased from 94 to 97% at lower temperatures (25–400 °C) to 60–80% at 900 °C, with nanoparticle-enhanced samples showing superior retention of mechanical properties. Microstructural analysis confirmed the anatase phase of TiO2 with an average crystallite size of 20 nm and spherical morphology (20–40 nm). Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations revealed that nanoparticle porosity and specific surface area critically influence heat transfer and pressure drop in coated welded joints, with optimal thermal performance achieved at higher specific surface areas. This work highlights the synergistic effects of TiO2 nanoparticles, sheet thickness, and thermal treatment on weld performance, offering a promising pathway for enhancing the durability and thermal management of steel structures in demanding applications.Keywords: Arc welding, Hardness, Nano-welding, Tensile strength, TiO2 nanoparticlesSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Welded joints are essential structural components in modern steel construction, forming the backbone of everything from high-rise buildings and industrial facilities to bridges and offshore platforms. Their primary role is to transfer loads between adjacent steel elements, such as beams, columns, braces, or plates, ensuring structural continuity, integrity, and overall stability under both static and dynamic loading conditions1. This continuity is particularly critical in seismic-prone regions, where welded connections must accommodate cyclic deformations without catastrophic failure, as shown in post-earthquake assessments of steel moment frames2. Beyond their structural function, welded joints offer architects and engineers significant design flexibility. They enable the creation of complex geometries and clean, aesthetic connections without the visual intrusion of bolts or rivets. When properly executed, welded joints also demonstrate excellent durability and corrosion resistance, especially when protected by advanced coatings. This is a key factor in extending the service life of infrastructure exposed to harsh environmental conditions. However, the welding process introduces several thermomechanical challenges. These include microstructural alterations in the heat-affected zone (HAZ), residual stresses from rapid heating and cooling, and potential defects like porosity, cracks, or lack of fusion3–5. Such issues can compromise joint performance, particularly under fatigue loading or in corrosive environments. This underscores the need for advanced materials and techniques to improve weld reliability. Consequently, ongoing research into innovative welding materials, including nanoparticle-reinforced weld metals, aims to address these limitations and expand the possibilities for steel construction6,7.

Recent advancements in welding technology have explored the integration of nanoparticles to mitigate these limitations and enhance joint performance. Nanoparticles such as TiO2, Al2O3, ZrB2, and SiC have shown promising results in refining microstructure, improving mechanical properties, and enhancing thermal and corrosion resistance8–11. Additionally, the use of nanoparticles in friction stir welding (FSW) of aluminum alloys has been explored, demonstrating improved joint strength and microstructural refinement12,13. Furthermore, the co-precipitation behavior of nanoparticles during welding processes has been studied, revealing their role in mitigating softening in heat-affected zones and enhancing tensile strength post-weld heat treatment9. Cao et al.11 studied the mechanical properties of FSW joints by incorporating ZrB2 nanoparticles into the 7N01 matrix composites. They reported that the presence of ZrB2 nanoparticles increases the recrystallization driving force, leading to a refinement of the grains in the stir zone (SZ). Specifically, the average grain size is reduced from 4.8 μm in the 7N01 matrix to 3.7 μm in the ZrB2/7N01 composite. Additionally, the recrystallization proportion improves from 75.1% to 85.4% due to the nanoparticles’ influence. Also, the ZrB2 nanoparticles exhibit superior coarsening resistance, maintaining their morphology and size even at high temperatures. This stability contributes positively to the strengthening of the FSW joints during the welding process. Sun et al.10 investigated the impact of ZrB2 and Al2O3 nanoparticles on the microstructure and mechanical properties of friction stir welded joints in 7085Al matrix composites. The study highlights that the presence of nanoparticles promotes dynamic recrystallization, resulting in a weld nugget (WN) with an equiaxed morphology and a grain size of 1.82 μm. In the thermo-mechanically affected zone (TMAZ), grains undergo plastic deformation, leading to elongation and the generation of numerous new dislocations within the grains. These findings underscore the potential of nanoparticles to revolutionize welding techniques and material properties, leading to more robust applications in various industries.

Among nanoparticles, TiO2 has garnered particular attention due to their unique combination of high thermal stability, excellent photocatalytic activity, corrosion resistance, and compatibility with ferrous matrices14,15. Studies have demonstrated that TiO2 additions can lead to significant grain refinement in the weld metal and HAZ, resulting in improved tensile strength, hardness, and fatigue resistance8,16,17. For instance, Shahedi et al.8 studied the effect of TiO₂ nanoparticles on FSW copper joints, finding they significantly increased stir zone hardness (up to 85.7%) and impact toughness (up to 94.5%) through grain refinement and dislocation pinning. However, tensile strength was unaffected as failure occurred in the thermally weakened HAZ. The research concluded that lower welding temperatures are more beneficial for joint integrity than increasing the number of weld passes. Alithari et al.17 studied the effect of TiO2 nanoparticles on the impact toughness of shielded metal arc welding (SMAW) joints in mild steel. They reported that incorporating TiO2 nanoparticles via a cold spray coating technique significantly enhanced the impact energy of the welded joint. The optimal improvement was achieved with a 1.5 wt% nanoparticle addition, which increased the average impact toughness from 162.4 J to 231.2 J, an improvement of 42.36%. This enhancement was attributed to the refinement of the weld microstructure, where the nanoparticles promoted the formation of fine intragranular acicular ferrite and reduced the presence of coarse Widmanstätten ferrite. However, they noted that exceeding the optimal nanoparticle concentration (beyond 1.5 wt%) led to a decline in toughness, likely due to an increased oxygen content. Mirjavadi et al.16 investigated the influence of TiO2 nanoparticles and multiple weld passes on FSW AA5083 aluminum alloy. They reported that the uniform dispersion of TiO2 nanoparticles in the stir zone led to significant grain refinement, reducing the average grain size from 45 μm in the base metal to 8.5 μm after four passes. This microstructural enhancement resulted in substantial improvements in mechanical and tribological properties. Specifically, the four-pass sample exhibited a 40% increase in microhardness and a 25% improvement in ultimate tensile strength compared to unreinforced welds. The study also found that wear resistance increased while the friction coefficient decreased by up to 50% with nanoparticle addition and multiple passes, attributed to grain boundary pinning and dislocation strengthening mechanisms.

Despite these advances, several research gaps remain. Most studies have focused on flat or butt-welded configurations, with limited attention given to complex geometries such as L-shaped corner joints, which are prevalent in structural steelwork. Furthermore, the combined effects of sheet thickness, heat treatment temperature, and nanoparticle concentration on the thermomechanical behavior of welded joints are not well understood. There is also a scarcity of integrated experimental and computational studies that correlate nanoparticle characteristics (e.g., porosity, specific surface area) with thermal and mechanical performance under realistic service conditions. Additionally, while cold spray coating has emerged as a promising method for depositing nanoparticles during welding, its application to TiO2-reinforced steel welds remains underexplored, particularly regarding coating uniformity, adhesion, and interaction with the weld pool.

To address these gaps, this study investigates the effect of TiO2 nanoparticles on the thermomechanical behavior of L-shaped welded joints in steel structures. Using a cold spray coating technique, TiO2 nanoparticles were incorporated at varying weight percentages (0.5, 1.25, and 2 wt%) into welds of different sheet thicknesses (6, 15, and 30 mm). The joints were subjected to heat treatment across a wide temperature range (25–900 °C) to evaluate their performance under thermal cycling. Mechanical properties were assessed via tensile testing, while computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were employed to analyze heat transfer characteristics and the role of nanoparticle porosity and specific surface area. The novelty of this work lies in its holistic approach, combining experimental analysis with numerical modeling to provide a comprehensive understanding of how TiO2 nanoparticles influence the strength, ductility, and thermal resilience of corner-welded joints, a configuration critical to structural steel design.

By bridging the gap between material science and structural engineering, this research aims to provide actionable insights for the development of high-performance, durable welded connections suitable for advanced steel construction, including applications in seismic-resistant and high-temperature environments.

Syntheses of TiO2 Nanoparticles

The sol-gel (SG) technique was utilized for the synthesis of TiO2 nanoparticles. To achieve this, 150 mL of ethanol was mixed with 10 mL of deionized water while stirring continuously. Additionally, 9 mL of TTIP was added to the previously mentioned solution under constant stirring, keeping the temperature at 85 °C using a paraffin oil bath for a period of 4 h. After the gel formation, it was dried in a hot air oven at 60 °C. Calcination was carried out for 3 h at 400 °C.

Experimental work

In this research, the influence of TiO2 on the performance of L-shaped joints with corner welding at various temperature levels and with differing dimensions of steel sheets has been examined. To achieve this objective, the samples were made from steel sheets with thicknesses of (6, 15, and 30) mm, having dimensions of 50*200 mm. The experimental standards and sample preparation for this study are outlined as follows. The mechanical properties of the base metal, including tensile and shear strength, were evaluated in accordance with the ASTM E8/E8M standard18. Developed by the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM), this standard specifies test methods to determine key metallic properties such as yield strength, tensile strength, elongation, and reduction of area. All welding procedures and weld metal characterization were conducted following the relevant AWS/ISO standards19, established by the American Welding Society (AWS). The joints were made using an E6013 electrode. The selected target temperature levels for the heat treatment of the samples were (25, 100, 250, 400, 500, 700, 900 °C). Three different weight ratios of TiO2 nanoparticles colloidal (0.5, 1.25, and 2 wt%) were incorporated into the weld metal using the cold spray coating technique provided by US Research, Inc. The overall count of samples was 12: three welded samples lacking nanoparticles with a thickness of 6 mm, 15 mm, and 30 mm, respectively (M, N, and P); three samples of 6 mm sheet welded with nanoparticles at 0.5 wt%, 1.25 wt%, and 2 wt% (M1, M2, and M3); three samples of 15 mm sheet welded with nanoparticles at 0.5 wt%, 1.25 wt%, and 2 wt% (N1, N2, and N3); and three samples of 30 mm sheet welded with nanoparticles at 0.5 wt%, 1.25 wt%, and 2 wt% (P1, P2, and P3).

The TiO2 nanoparticle colloidal was applied using the cold spray coating technique (US Research, Inc.). To ensure a uniform and consistent coating layer, the process was carefully controlled through optimized parameters including nozzle pressure (5–7 bar), stand-off distance (30 mm), traverse speed (50 mm/s), number of spray passes, and spray duration. Specifically, each weld zone received three consecutive spray passes, with each pass lasting 5 s at the constant traverse speed. These parameters are known to promote homogeneous nanoparticle deposition in metallic substrates. Furthermore, the absence of erratic mechanical behavior or premature failure in nanoparticle-enhanced samples suggests effective and uniform integration of TiO2 within the weld metal. Prior to the coating process, the welding area is treated with TiO2 nanoparticles after the removal of welding slag and the measurement of the welding area’s temperature. Experimental findings reveal that the best adhesion of TiO2 nanoparticles is achieved at a temperature of 300 °C. This is succeeded by an additional layer of welding. It has been observed that the temperature of the welding area before coating plays a vital role. Moreover, at higher temperatures surpassing 500 °C, the TiO2 nanoparticle colloid dries rapidly, leading to flaking and inadequate adhesion of TiO2 nanoparticles to the welding area. In contrast, at lower temperatures below 200 °C, the colloidal dries at a slower rate, resulting in its expulsion from the welding area due to air compressor pressure.

The heat treatment procedure

A manual temperature control system was utilized in an electric furnace for the heat treatment of the samples. The preheating process for the samples, as outlined in Fig. 1, commenced with a gradual increase in temperature at a rate of 10 °C per minute and continued until the furnace chamber reached the desired temperature. After this, the samples were held at the designated temperature for 30 min to ensure uniform temperature distribution across all areas. The furnace was then switched off, allowing the heat within the chamber to gradually escape through the built-in outlet until the temperatures of the samples equaled the surrounding ambient temperature.

Characteristics of nanoparticle

In this study, the nanoparticles were characterized using several analytical techniques. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was employed to analyze their chemical composition and identify surface functional groups. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) provided detailed insights into their morphology, size, and surface features. Additionally, X-ray diffraction (XRD) was utilized to determine the crystalline structure and phase composition, offering information on their crystallinity and atomic arrangement. Together, these techniques facilitated a comprehensive understanding of the nanoparticles’ properties and potential applications.

Tensile test

In contrast to standard tensile specimens that can be readily placed into the tensile testing apparatus, L-shaped specimens pose a challenge for uncomplicated loading. They require a custom adapter to efficiently transfer the machine’s net force to both ends of the specimens. This adapter must not only firmly secure the specimens but also allow for free rotation to guarantee that the tensile force is applied mainly along the length of the specimens, thus ensuring that the welded joint undergoes the tensile force. Furthermore, the material utilized for this adapter must be adequately rigid to ensure that the machine’s force causes deformation solely in the primary specimen, preventing any deformation of the adapter components from affecting the force-displacement curves. To ensure the tensile force was applied purely along the longitudinal axis of the specimen and through the welded joint, several design and procedural measures were implemented. The inclusion of a self-aligning rotational joint between the stationary grip and the gripping jaw allowed the specimen-holding assembly to pivot freely, ensuring the gripping jaw aligned with the specimen’s natural orientation as the load was applied and preventing parasitic bending stresses at the onset of loading. The specimen was secured using three clamping rods distributed symmetrically across the gripping jaw, providing balanced constraint and preventing tilting or eccentric loading during the test. All adapter components were manufactured from high-stiffness materials, CK45 hardened carbon steel for the grips and grade 12.9 alloy steel for the pins and rods, to minimize elastic deformation under load and ensure that measured displacement originated solely from the specimen. Figure 2(b) illustrates the mechanical properties of these materials. Prior to fabrication, the design was validated through linear-elastic finite element analysis under the maximum anticipated load (~ 10 kN), which confirmed that fixture stresses remained well below the yield strength and deformations were negligible. Additionally, a pre-test alignment protocol involved visual inspection of proper seating, verification of rotational joint freedom, and application of a small pre-load to confirm symmetric engagement and absence of initial bending. Force was applied by the machine’s moving crosshead to the top of the L-shaped specimen via a standard flat grip, while the bottom was held by the custom adapter, ensuring the welded joint experienced pure tension and enabling accurate recording of force-displacement curves for mechanical analysis.

Simulation and numerical analysis

The heat transfer behavior of welded joints coated with TiO2 nanoparticles was investigated using Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD). Due to the high thermal resistance of air, convective cooling of metal joints is often negligible under static conditions. However, the introduction of a nanoparticle coating significantly alters the thermal interface by modifying thermal conductivity, increasing effective surface area, and inducing localized fluid turbulence. Consequently, the heat transfer process becomes critically dependent on the physical and geometric properties of the nanoparticles, including porosity, pore density, and specific surface area.

Solver settings and validation

The simulations were performed using a finite volume method (FVM)-based solver in ANSYS Fluent (v. 2022 R1). The pressure-velocity coupling was handled using the SIMPLE algorithm. Second-order upwind schemes were employed for the discretization of momentum and energy equations to enhance accuracy. The solution was considered converged when the residuals for the continuity, momentum, and energy equations fell below 10− 4, 10− 4, and 10− 6, respectively.

The validity of the CFD model was further reinforced by comparing the general trends of simulated cooling behavior with the experimental thermal profiles observed during the post-weld heat treatment phase.

Result

As shown in Fig. 12(a), the XRD pattern of the TiO2 nanoparticles exhibited distinct peaks at 2θ = 25.3°, 37.3°, 37.7°, 38.1°, 48°, 53.9°, 55.1°, 62.2°, 68.7°, and 70.6°, which correspond to the (101), (103), (004), (112), (200), (105), (211), (204), (116), and (220) planes of the anatase phase of TiO2, as confirmed by comparison with the standard JCPDS card No. 21–1272. The presence of these peaks confirms the formation of the anatase phase, which is known for its photocatalytic properties. The intensity and sharpness of the peaks suggest that the synthesized TiO2 nanoparticles possess a high degree of crystallinity. The calculated average crystallite size was found to be 20 nm, indicating that the nanoparticles are in the nanoscale range.

The SEM image (Fig. 12(b)) reveals that the TiO2 nanoparticles exhibit a predominantly spherical morphology with an average diameter in the range of 20–40 nm. The particles are well-dispersed, indicating successful synthesis and minimal agglomeration. Notably, the surface of the nanoparticles appears smooth, which may enhance their reactivity and adsorption capabilities in various applications. Overall, the SEM analysis confirms the successful synthesis of TiO2 nanoparticles with desirable characteristics for their intended applications. The observed morphology is consistent with previous studies, reinforcing the reliability of the synthesis method employed in this research.

The FTIR spectrum of the TiO2 nanoparticles (Fig. 12(c)) displays several characteristic peaks. A prominent absorption band observed at approximately 1617 cm− 1 is attributed to the bending vibrations of hydroxyl (-OH) groups, indicating the presence of surface hydroxyls, which are crucial for photocatalytic activity. Additionally, the peak around 551 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the Ti-O stretching vibrations, confirming the formation of TiO2. Another notable peak is observed at 3415 cm⁻¹, which may be associated with C-H bending and stretching vibrations. These peaks suggest the possible presence of organic residues from the synthesis process.

The force-displacement diagrams for the welded parts were analyzed to evaluate the mechanical performance of the joints in the presence of TiO2 nanoparticles. The diagrams illustrate the relationship between the applied force and the resulting displacement during tensile testing, providing insights into the material’s stiffness, strength, and ductility. Figures 13(a-d) show the force-displacement diagrams for samples M, M1, M2, and M3, respectively. The initial linear region of the force-displacement curves indicates elastic behavior, where the force increases proportionally with displacement. Notably, samples containing nanoparticles exhibit a steeper slope in this region compared to the control sample without nanoparticles (M), suggesting enhanced stiffness and load-bearing capacity. The yield point, marking the transition from elastic to plastic deformation, is observed in all samples. However, samples with nanoparticles demonstrate a higher yield strength compared to their non-nanoparticle counterparts. This increase in yield strength can be attributed to the reinforcing effect of the nanoparticles, which improve the overall mechanical properties of the weld. Following the yield point, the force-displacement curves show a nonlinear increase in force with displacement, indicating plastic deformation16,21,22. Samples with higher concentrations of nanoparticles exhibit a more pronounced plastic region, suggesting improved ductility and energy absorption capabilities. This behavior is indicative of the nanoparticles’ role in enhancing the toughness of the welded joints. Each curve reaches a peak force, known as the ultimate load. Samples with nanoparticles consistently achieve higher ultimate loads compared to the control sample. For example, sample M reached a maximum peak force of 724 N at 100 °C, while samples M1, M2, and M3 show peak forces of 781, 831, and 883 N, respectively. This rise in peak force signifies an improvement in the mechanical properties of the welds due to the addition of nanoparticles and is associated with microstructural changes, such as grain refinement and enhanced homogeneity within the weld.

Force-displacement curves for 6 mm thick welded samples: (a) without nanoparticles (M); with TiO2 nanoparticles at (b) 0.5 wt% (M1), (c) 1.25 wt% (M2), and (d) 2 wt% (M3).

Figure 14 shows the variations in ultimate load of different samples at a constant displacement and at a temperature of 100 °C. A comparative analysis of the curves reveals that the performance of the welded samples improves with increasing nanoparticle concentration. For example, sample P1, which contains 0.5 wt% of nanoparticles, shows an approximately 8% increase in ultimate load compared to sample P, while this value reaches 25% in sample P3, which contains 2% wt% of nanoparticles. In addition, it can be seen that the effectiveness of nanoparticles in improving the mechanical properties also depends on the thickness of the samples. The stronger tensile improvements observed in thicker plates after TiO2 nanoparticle addition are attributed to several synergistic mechanisms related to plate geometry and thermal behavior. Thicker sections possess greater thermal mass, which leads to slower cooling rates and more uniform heat distribution during welding. This allows for improved dispersion and interfacial bonding of nanoparticles within the weld metal and heat-affected zone, reducing defects such as porosity and agglomeration. Additionally, the increased constraint in thicker plates promotes a triaxial stress state, enhancing the load-transfer efficiency of the nanoparticles and facilitating grain refinement through dislocation pinning. The reduced thermal gradient also minimizes residual stresses, contributing to a more homogeneous microstructure. Consequently, the combined effects of improved nanoparticle integration, lower residual stresses, and enhanced mechanical constraint in thicker plates result in more pronounced gains in tensile strength and ductility compared to thinner sections.

Ultimate load variation for different sample thicknesses and TiO2 concentrations at 100 °C and constant displacement.

Figure 15 (a-c) illustrate the variations in the resistance recovery coefficient for various samples. It is evident that, for all samples, the degree of resistance decrease at temperatures between 25 and 400 ℃ is nearly insignificant, and the resistance recovery coefficient of the samples falls within the range of 94% to 97%. Starting at a temperature of 500 ℃, the critical state and unstable conditions emerge in the samples, and as the temperature rises, this value correlates with an additional decline, such that at 700 ℃, the resistance recovery coefficient attains a range of 85 to 95%. Ultimately, at a temperature of 900 ℃, the residual resistance falls within the range of 60 to 80%. It is clear that the resistance recovery coefficient is affected by the thickness of the samples and the addition of nanoparticles. Thicker samples have a larger cross-sectional area to distribute stress, which can lead to better recovery after deformation. The incorporation of nanoparticles can enhance the mechanical properties of the welded joints, potentially improving the resistance recovery coefficient. Nanoparticles can help in refining the microstructure, leading to improved strength and ductility. This enhancement can result in better energy absorption and recovery characteristics after deformation.

The uniformity of TiO2 nanoparticle dispersion within the welded joints was indirectly evaluated based on the consistency of mechanical test results. All nanoparticle-enhanced samples exhibited repeatable improvements in ultimate load capacity, yield strength, and ductility across triplicate tests, with minimal data scatter (see Figs. 13, 14 and 15). This reproducibility suggests that the cold spray coating method produced a reasonably uniform distribution of nanoparticles, as non-uniform dispersion typically leads to inconsistent mechanical properties and early failure. Additionally, the progressive enhancement of mechanical performance with increasing nanoparticle concentration (0.5 to 2 wt%) further supports the effective integration of TiO2 into the weld matrix. While direct visualization via techniques such as SEM/EDS mapping is recommended for future validation, the mechanical data presented here provide strong indirect evidence of uniform nanoparticle reinforcement in the studied joints.

In welded connections coated with titanium dioxide nanoparticles with high porosity, where air is used as the working fluid, the characteristics of the nanoparticles (e.g., geometric parameters such as porosity and pore density, as well as the specific surface area of ​​nanoparticles), as well as the inlet air velocity, significantly affect the heat transfer process. In the present study, the effect of inlet air velocity for five different amounts of nanoparticle layers (MF 10,380; MF 10,462; MF 20,580; MF 20,720; and MF 20,860) has been investigated using two models: thermal equilibrium and non-equilibrium. It should be noted that in these layers, the numbers 10 and 20 represent the pore density (pores per unit area), while the numbers 380, 462, 580, 720, and 860 denote the porosity (percentage of void space) One of the most important results obtained through computational fluid dynamics simulation is the distribution of velocity, pressure, and temperature at various points within the computational domain. Understanding the distribution of velocity and temperature aids in better analyzing and interpreting events in various processes. Figure 4 shows the distribution of airflow velocity when using different layers of titanium dioxide nanoparticles. The results clearly indicate that in both groups of titanium dioxide nanoparticles (with pore densities of 10 and 20), as porosity decreases (and specific surface area increases), the airflow velocity becomes more uniform after passing through the titanium dioxide nanoparticles. The titanium dioxide nanoparticles with a pore density of 10, due to their larger pores, have also contributed to a less uniform distribution of fluid velocity. Changes in the inlet air velocity had little effect on the velocity distribution, with only the velocity values at different points varying.

Figure 5 also shows the distribution of air pressure at the welded joints when air flows over the welded connections and the area covered with nanoparticles. As can be seen, the majority of pressure changes and pressure drop at the welded joints is due to the thickness of the nanoparticles present against the fluid flow.

Figure 6 shows the two-dimensional temperature distribution contour for the airflow passing through connections coated with titanium dioxide nanoparticles. This particular figure effectively demonstrates how variations in the velocity of the incoming air impact the overall temperature distribution when utilizing titanium dioxide nanoparticles that possess a density of 20. It is evident from the illustration that as the velocity of the fluid increases, there is a noticeable reduction in the Perforation Per Inch (PPI), which results in a smaller fraction of the fluid being able to reach the peak temperature, which corresponds to the temperature of the surfaces of the pipes. Conversely, at lower fluid velocities, the temperature of the air exiting the system tends to rise to a significantly greater degree.

Figure 7 shows the effect of varying the layer of titanium dioxide nanoparticles used on the temperature distribution of the air passing over the joints at an approximate constant velocity of 3.2 m/s. It can be observed that with the increase in the specific surface area of the titanium dioxide nanoparticles, the temperature of the air passing over the surface of the joints significantly increases, and more heat transfer occurs at the joints with nanoparticles that have a larger specific surface area.

Figure 8 illustrates the temperature distribution for air flowing over welded joints using two models: thermal equilibrium and imbalance. The thermal equilibrium model assumes that the air and the solid structure (welded joints or nanoparticle layer) instantly reach the same temperature at every point, meaning they are in perfect thermal balance. This simplification implies that heat transfer between the phases is instantaneous, leading to a uniform temperature distribution in the exiting air. As a result, the model predicts a lower and more consistent bulk air temperature because energy is evenly distributed without localized hot spots. However, this approach becomes less accurate at higher airflow speeds, where real-world conditions prevent immediate thermal equilibrium due to insufficient contact time. The thermal imbalance model accounts for a temperature difference between the air and the solid, recognizing that heat transfer takes finite time and may not reach equilibrium. This more realistic approach allows for variations in temperature between phases, particularly at higher flow velocities where the air moves too quickly to fully absorb heat from the solid. Consequently, the exiting air temperature is higher and less uniform compared to the equilibrium model, as some regions retain more heat. The discrepancy between the two models grows with increasing airflow speed, as thermal equilibrium becomes harder to achieve under faster-moving conditions. It is noted that with identical air inlet velocity and a similar layer, the thermal equilibrium model results in a more uniform air bulk temperature exiting the nanoparticle layer area, and this temperature is lower than that from the thermal imbalance model. Additionally, Fig. 9 shows that at lower speeds, the heat transfer in the thermal balance model closely aligns with the thermal imbalance model’s prediction, with the difference between the models growing as the inlet speed increases. This growing difference is because, at higher air speeds, there isn’t enough time to achieve thermal equilibrium.

Figure 10 compares the pressure drop results obtained from simulating airflow over welded joints containing titanium dioxide nanoparticles for various types of layers and different inlet air velocities. The results indicate that regardless of the thickness and type of layer used, the pressure drop of the airflow increases exponentially with the increase in inlet air velocity. Additionally, with an increase in the specific surface area (reduction in porosity) of the titanium dioxide nanoparticles, the pressure drop of the airflow passing through also increases. The reason for this is the reduction in the airflow passage area with the decrease in the porosity of the titanium dioxide nanoparticles. Therefore, the use of layers with higher specific surface areas also imposes a greater pressure drop. Among these results, the pressure drop due to the interference of the cavity density and porosity effects for the MF10.462 nanoparticles is somewhat similar to that of MF20.580 nanoparticles.

Figure 11 compares the heat transfer results obtained from simulating airflow over welded joints containing titanium dioxide nanoparticles for various types of nanoparticle layers and different inlet air velocities. The increase in inlet air velocity has improved the heat transfer process, allowing more heat to be transferred from the hot joints to the air. This increasing trend is more pronounced initially and diminishes as the inlet air velocity increases further. At lower velocities, there is more time for heat exchange, whereas at higher velocities, this exchange time decreases. Therefore, the decreasing trend in heat transfer at higher velocities can be attributed to the dominance of the reduced heat exchange time due to improved transfer. Furthermore, in Fig. 11, it is observed that the heat transfer amount increases with an increase in the specific surface area of the nanoparticles. Just as observed in Fig. 9, the increase in the heat transfer surface area also leads to a greater pressure drop. Therefore, to select the optimal nanoparticle layer, both factors of pressure drop and heat transfer should be considered together. These results also show similarities in the behavior of the MF10.462 and MF20.580 nanoparticle layers.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive experimental and numerical analysis of the thermomechanical performance of L-shaped welded steel joints enhanced with TiO₂ nanoparticles, revealing significant and quantifiable improvements in both mechanical and thermal properties. The incorporation of 2 wt% TiO2 nanoparticles increased the ultimate load capacity by approximately 25%, with intermediate concentrations of 0.5 wt% and 1.25 wt% yielding gains of about 8% and 15%, respectively. Enhanced stiffness and yield strength were consistently observed in nanoparticle-treated samples, with thicker Sect. (30 mm) demonstrating the most pronounced benefits, achieving up to a 25% increase in ultimate load due to more uniform nanoparticle dispersion and improved stress distribution. Thermal performance assessments indicated strong resistance recovery, with coefficients remaining between 94 and 97% at temperatures up to 400 °C, retaining 85–95% at 700 °C, and 60–80% at 900 °C, outperforming untreated welds. Computational fluid dynamics simulations further showed that TiO2 layers with higher specific surface area could enhance heat transfer by 18–22%, albeit with a corresponding 12–30% increase in pressure drop, while optimal nanoparticle adhesion was achieved at a pre-coating temperature of 300 °C. These findings underscore the potential of TiO2 nanoparticles to significantly augment the strength, durability, and thermal management of welded joints, supporting their use in high-demand applications such as seismic-resistant structures and high-temperature industrial systems. To further advance the application of nanoparticle-enhanced welding in structural engineering, future research should focus on long-term durability assessments under cyclic and corrosive conditions, optimization of nanoparticle deposition techniques beyond cold spraying, and the exploration of alternative or hybrid nanomaterials (e.g., Al2O3, SiO2, or carbon-based nanoparticles) to tailor multifunctional performance. Additionally, integrating advanced computational modeling with machine learning could facilitate the predictive design of weld parameters and nanoparticle concentrations for specific service environments. Field trials in real structural applications, along with lifecycle and economic analyses, will be essential to validate practical feasibility and support the development of standardized guidelines for industrial adoption.
