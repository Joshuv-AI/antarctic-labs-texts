Assessment of changes in selected parameters of Arnett’s soft tissue cephalometric analysis (STCA) in patients undergoing SARME/SARPE
Authors: Rafał Nowak, Anna Olejnik, Ewa Zawiślak, Ada Kaczmarek-Niedzielska, Hanna Gerber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaxillary constriction in adults often requires surgically assisted maxillary expansion. While skeletal and dental changes are well described, soft-tissue effects remain less clear. This study evaluated soft-tissue changes using selected parameters of Arnett’s soft tissue cephalometric analysis (STCA) after bone-borne SARME.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort study included 42 adults (mean age 27.67 years) who underwent SARME with a bone-borne distractor. Lateral cephalograms were obtained preoperatively and 6–8 months post-expansion. Sixteen STCA parameters and angles (SNA, SNB, ANB) were measured by two calibrated observers. Statistical analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Spearman’s correlation.ResultsSARME was associated with in a mean clockwise rotation of the maxillary occlusal plane of + 1.52° and the mandibular occlusal plane of + 1.72°. Vertical soft-tissue changes included an increase in maxillary anterior height (+ 0.62 mm) and upper lip height (+ 0.56 mm). Sagittally, nasal tip projection decreased (–0.44 mm) and chin to forehead distance decreased (–3.11 mm), indicating posterior chin displacement. Airway analysis showed a significant increase only at the Pog level (PAS-Pog + 1.94 mm). SNB decreased and ANB increased significantly, consistent with mandibular clockwise rotation. No differences were found between skeletal Class II and III groups.ConclusionsBone-borne SARME was associated with measurable vertical and sagittal soft-tissue changes. Clockwise occlusal plane rotation was linked to increased facial height and posterior chin movement. STCA can support communication and planning in adult maxillary expansion.

Introduction

One of the primary diagnostic tools used in orthodontics and orthognathic surgery for assessing skeletal and dental parameters is cephalometric analysis.

Since the introduction of lateral cephalometry, numerous analyses—such as those proposed by Downs, Steiner, Ricketts, Björk, and others—have been developed to assess craniofacial morphology, treatment needs and outcomes [9, 35].

The concept of natural head position (NHP), introduced by Moorrees and Kean [40], provides a reproducible and clinically relevant reference for cephalometric assessment. Its reliability and reproducibility were subsequently confirmed by Cooke and Wei as well as by Lundström and Lundström, supporting its use in facial aesthetic evaluation [18, 37].

In 1999, William Arnett et al. [5] introduced cephalometric analysis (Fig. 1) based on the assessment of soft tissue positioning - soft tissue cephalometric analysis, which was a radiological complement to Arnett’s philosophy regarding treatment planning for malocclusion presented in two publications from 1993 [4, 6]. Similarly, the need for bite correction was recognised alongside the need to improve facial aesthetics.

Arnett’s STCA

STCA involves positioning the patient’s head in the NHP with the seated condyle and passive lips, and determining the true vertical line (TVL). The TVL serves as the reference plane for measurements. The TVL runs through the subnasale point and is perpendicular to the NHP. Arnett et al., originally proposed 46 measurements divided into 5 groups: dentoskeletal factors, soft tissue structure, facial length, projections to TVL and harmony. Years later, these measurements were expanded to include a sixth group, i.e. the airways, as well as additional measurements within each group and in many centres STCA became a basis for interdisciplinary treatment planning for malocclusion.

Maxillary transverse deficiency most often occurs as a component of a complex malocclusion or skeletal deformity, less often as an isolated defect [41]. It is estimated that in more than 30% of patients seeking orthodontic or orthodontic-surgical treatment, one of the problems to be solved is the correction of the transverse dimension of the maxilla [7].

Both orthodontic and surgical methods have become well-established in the treatment of maxillary constriction [41], and their correct choice is a major factor in determining therapeutic success. One of the key elements determining the choice of therapy is skeletal maturity, and thus the age of the patient [29, 48].

In recent years, the MARPE (miniimplants/miniscrews assisted rapid maxillary expansion) method has been gaining popularity [28]. However, recent studies demonstrate that the predictability of MARPE decreases with increasing skeletal maturity [43, 50].

During the surgical correction of a skeletal deformity, it is possible to increase the transverse dimension of the maxilla using the Le Fort I osteotomy with segmentation, either simultaneously or as a stand-alone SARME/SARPE (surgically assisted rapid maxillary/palatal expansion) procedure, with either a bone-borne or tooth-borne appliance [39]. The undoubted advantage of the first method is that the results can be achieved during one operation. However, this method has its limitations and the stability of treatment effects is debatable [46].

Although, it is not a problem to obtain the correct transverse dimension with the correct application of the surgical method of maxillary expansion using a palatal distractor, the effect of this method on changes in the position of the skeletal, dental and soft tissue structures of the face in the spatial dimension remains unclear.

STCA provides a standardized method to quantify facial soft-tissue projection and harmony, making it particularly suitable for assessing soft-tissue effects of maxillary expansion.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate soft-tissue changes after bone-borne SARME using selected parameters of Arnett’s soft tissue cephalometric analysis (STCA), providing a novel perspective on facial profile adaptation following maxillary expansion.

The aim of this study is to assess selected STCA parameters in patients treated for maxillary constriction using surgically assisted maxillary expansion with a bone-borne appliance.

Methods

This retrospective observational cohort study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Wrocław (KB-492/2013) and complies with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki on medical research protocols and ethics.

Patients

The retrospective study examined the medical records of 42 patients treated for maxillary constriction between 2013 and 2016 at the Department of Maxillofacial Surgery, Medical University of Wrocław.

The study group included 18 women and 24 men, aged between 17 and 45 years (mean 27.67).

In order to be included in the study they met the following criteria: completed bone growth, crossbite of skeletal etiology, moderate to severe maxillary constriction (> 5.0 mm) assessed for the anterior and posterior dental arch width, or failure of previous orthodontic treatment. In addition, the study group comprised patients for whom surgically assisted rapid maxillary expansion was the first surgical procedure for treating a complex skeletal deformity. These patients were also scheduled to undergo orthognathic surgery in the future.

The exclusion criteria for the study were as follows: past trauma in the craniofacial area, recognition of a craniofacial anomaly (e.g. cleft lip palate, or Pierre-Robin syndrome), incomplete medical records, having undergone additional facial surgery between examinations.

Eligibility for treatment was based on the patient’s medical history, clinical examination, analysis of plaster models, and medical imaging.

Medical imaging included pantomographic, lateral cephalometric and cephalometric x-rays in the AP projection.

The sample size was determined by the availability of complete pre- and post-treatment records that met the inclusion criteria. No a priori power calculation was performed due to the retrospective design of the study.

Each patient signed a formal consent for treatment, as well as for the use of medical records for scientific research.

Surgical procedure

All patients were operated on by one maxillofacial surgeon (RN) following the same protocol.

The surgical procedure was performed under general anaesthesia with standard perioperative management. An osteotomy was performed with a piezoelectric saw along the Le Fort I line. Then, in the zygomaticoalveolar buttress area, the osteotomy line was expanded with a carbide round bur to ensure that the expansion proceeded without colliding with the osteotomy fragments. The maxilla was separated at the palatal suture, with mobility of bone fragments being checked each time prior to installing the distractor. The UniSmile distractor (Titamed, Belgium) was used. The distractor was activated mid-operatively, with the aim of achieving a separation of 1.0 mm between the maxillary central incisors. The procedure was concluded with the locking of the distractor, a haemostasis check, and the closing of the wound with resorbable sutures.

The postoperative latency period ranged from 5 to 7 days. Then, at the first postoperative outpatient appointment, after radiographic examination (OPG), the distractor was activated and the active maxillary expansion phase began. The duration of the expansion phase depended individually and ranged from 12 to 32 days, with an average of 17 days. Once a day, the module was activated by 0.25 mm. Examinations were conducted at 7-day intervals during the distraction period. Once the desired width of the upper arch was achieved, the distractor was blocked.

On average, orthodontic treatment and diastema closure were started 6 weeks after the active phase of maxillary expansion had ended [1].

After the active phase, it was recommended that a NiTi 0.16 × 022 orthodontic arch be placed. Gradual closure of the diastema, stabilisation of the lateral sections and individual orthodontic treatment followed.

The consolidation period lasted for an average of six months following the conclusion of active distraction [1], after which the distractor was removed under local anaesthesia. At this stage, a lateral cephalometric radiograph and an AP position cephalometric radiograph were taken.

Clinical data and measurements

For each case, cephalometric radiographs taken before treatment (T0) and 6–8 months after surgically assisted maxillary expansion at the time of distractor removal (T1) were analysed.

In cases where the recorded head posture on the cephalogram deviated from the NHP, the image was realigned to this position following the method described by Lundström and Lundström. For this purpose, each cephalogram was superimposed on a standardized lateral facial photograph obtained at the same time point, positioned in NHP by an experienced clinician. The cephalogram was then adjusted to match the photographic NHP orientation, ensuring consistent and reproducible head positioning prior to cephalometric analysis [36].

The cephalometric images were imported into the NemoFab software (Nemotec, Madrid, Spain), and STCA analysis was performed. Sixteen parameters were selected for comparison (Table 1). To ensure measurement reliability, all cephalometric analyses were performed independently by two experienced observers (AKN, AO), each conducting duplicate assessments separated by a two-month interval. Inter- and intra-observer agreement was verified prior to the main analysis and demonstrated excellent reproducibility (intraclass correlation coefficients > 0.90 for all linear and angular parameters).

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from cephalogram analysis were entered into Numbers (Apple, California, USA) to create a database and then analysed using R version 4.4.2 (The R Foundation, USA) [47].

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the values of quantitative variables (i.e. those expressed in numbers) in the two repeated measurements.

The comparison of the values of quantitative variables in the two groups was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Correlations between quantitative variables were analysed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

The study used a significance level of 0.05, so all p values below 0.05 were interpreted as indicating statistically significant changes.

Study design, bias control, and sample size justification

This was a retrospective observational cohort study. The sample size was determined by the availability of complete pre- and post-treatment records that met the inclusion criteria. To minimize bias, all patients were operated on by the same surgeon following an identical protocol, and all cephalometric measurements were performed independently by two calibrated observers, demonstrating excellent reproducibility (ICC > 0.90). The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational cohort studies.

Overall, SARME produced several significant skeletal and soft-tissue changes. A clockwise rotation of both the maxillary and mandibular occlusal planes was observed, accompanied by a mild increase in vertical facial dimensions (maxillary anterior height and upper lip height). Sagittal soft-tissue changes included a slight posterior displacement of the chin and a small reduction in nasal tip projection. Airway evaluation demonstrated a significant widening only at the Pog level. These findings indicate that bone-borne SARME was associoated with measurable but clinically moderate soft-tissue adaptations, with vertical changes predominating over sagittal effects.

Although all parameters changed, not all of these changes were statistically significant.

Of the dentoskeletal factor measurements, the values for Mx and Md occlusal planes were statistically significantly higher for T1, indicating a clockwise change in the occlusal plane inclination.

The changes in soft tissue structure parameters, i.e. upper lip thickness and soft palate length, were found to be statistically insignificant.

The changes in both facial length (vertical) parameters were found to be statistically significant. Mx anterior height and upper lip height increased in the T1 study.

Of the parameters defining tissue projections to the TVL (sagittal parameters), the following changed significantly – nasal projection decreased in the T1 study, chin to forehead decreased in the T1 study and hyoid bone to Md increased in the T1 study. The change in the last two parameters indicates a change in the projection of the chin, specifically its displacement in the posterior direction.

Of the parameters defining the airways, only Airway PAS-Pg increased significantly in the T1 study. Airway PAS-A decreased in the T1 study. Airway PAS-Mx and Airway PAS-B increased, but these changes were not found to be statistically significant.

The SNA angle in the T1 study did not increase statistically significantly, while the SNB angle decreased statistically significantly and the ANB angle increased statistically significantly.

Discussion

Surgically assisted rapid maxillary expansion can be performed prior to a subsequent orthognathic procedure, or it may be planned as the only surgical intervention for treating malocclusion. Consequently, it is important to predict possible changes in relation to the skeletal system and soft tissue of the face. This is important for treatment planning and for informing patients of potential changes.

Changes that occur in the dental and skeletal systems and soft tissue of the face after SARME are measured using both cephalometric techniques [13, 16, 17, 21, 32, 34, 44] and analyses of 3D images taken using CBCT [21, 38, 51, 53].

Changes that occur in the airways as a result of SARME are also of interest to many researchers [12, 14, 30, 33, 38, 53].

Furthermore, three-dimensional photography, which is constantly improving, is used to determine the impact of surgical procedures on changes in soft tissue of the face [12].

There are plenty of publications comparing the changes resulting from treatment using the RME and SARME methods [8]. In orthodontic methods, the skeletal system remains intact. Therefore, the forces acting on the craniofacial skeleton, along with the accompanying stresses, are considerably greater [52]. Additionally, in contrast to surgical methods, orthodontic methods are most commonly used for patients of developmental age. Consequently, a comparison of the effects obtained with RME and SARME appears to be unjustified.

Finite element studies have further illustrated that different expander designs produce distinct stress and displacement patterns within craniofacial structures and anchorage units, potentially influencing skeletal and dental treatment outcomes. These biomechanical observations provide additional insight into how the mode of expansion and anchorage type may affect the overall craniofacial response [19].

In our study, we analysed changes in the skeletal system and in soft tissue of the face in patients after surgically assisted maxillary expansion using selected parameters of Arnett’s STCA.

Occlusal plane changes and rotational effects

One of the most significant findings of the present study is the change in the inclination of the occlusal plane of the maxilla (an increase of 1.52 degrees) and the mandible (an increase of 1.72 degrees).

This change corresponds with the clockwise rotation of the maxillomandibular complex, as found by other authors.

Parihiz et al. [44] also confirmed the clockwise direction of the occlusal plane rotation in patients after maxillary expansion, as evidenced by their findings regarding 50 cases. However, they were in relation to a longer period after surgery – the second study was performed on average 20 months after the maxillary expansion procedure.

In their study on a group of 78 patients, Xi et al. [51] also confirmed the clockwise rotation of the occlusal plane (the angle between the line between points S and N and the occlusal plane increased by 4.5 degrees after maxillary expansion). As with Parihiz et al., their findings also involved patients after, on average, 20 months following maxillary expansion surgery.

The statistically significant change in the SNB angle that was identified in this study is consistent with the findings reported by other researchers. Gunbay et al. [27] found statistically significant changes in the SNA and SNB angles as well as the ANS and SNGoGn parameters in a group of patients after surgically assisted maxillary expansion using a bone-borne appliance, which also indicates a clockwise rotation of the mandible and is, therefore, the same as rotation of the occlusal plane of the mandible, which our study also confirmed.

Similarly, Gilon et al. [25] found a decrease in the SNB angle and maxillary movement in the anterior direction with an increase in the SNA angle in a study group of patients after SARME using a tooth-borne appliance. The SNA angle increased in our study group, but this was not a statistically significant result.

In their comparison of patients after RME and SARME with a bone-borne appliance, Aras et al. [3] found maxillary movement in the anterior direction and clockwise rotation of the mandible (an increased SNGoGn angle and a decreased SNB angle) in patients after SARME, as well as an increase in the ANB and ANPog angles. This finding is consistent with our results regarding the clockwise rotation of the occlusal plane of the mandible and a decrease in the value of the SNB angle, as well as an increase in the value of the ANB angle. Furthermore, our study confirmed a statistically significant correlation (r=-0.379, p = 0.013) between changes in the mandibular occlusal plane inclination and the SNB angle value.

However, Dowgierd et al. in their study [21] found that the angle formed between the S, N and occlusal plane points did not change statistically significantly. Furthermore, other parameters that may indirectly indicate a change in the inclination of the maxillary occlusal plane, i.e. S-PNS and N-ANS, did not increase statistically significantly.

In the meta-analysis published by Sekertzi et al. [49] in 2023 on skeletal, dental and soft tissue changes after SARME, among the 27 publications selected for review, only 3 dealt with changes in the parameters described above. This finding suggests that the medical literature contains only a small number of publications on this issue.

Vertical and sagittal changes

The clockwise rotation of the maxilla and mandible may be accompanied by an increase in the anterior height of the maxilla and a change in the projection of the chin – a displacement of the chin in the posterior direction is to be expected. Lengthening of the subnasal region may be also associated with increased exposure of the upper incisors.

In our study, both parameters indicative of a change in the facial height, i.e. maxillary anterior height and upper lip height, increased statistically significantly in patients after SARME. This represented an average increase of 0.62 mm for the first parameter and 0.56 mm for the second.

Xi et al. [51] found an increase of 1.5 mm in the vertical maxilla position, as measured from point N to point A. Furthermore, the projection of the upper incisors exhibited an increase exceeding 2 mm. Additionally, an increase in the projection of the incisors and an increase in the vertical maxilla position were found to be associated with a change in the occlusal plane inclination. Our findings also confirmed a positive correlation between an increase in maxillary anterior height (Mx anterior height) and an increase in the maxillary occlusal plane inclination (r = 0.494, p = 0.001).

Similarly, Chung et al. [17] found that the N-A distance increased by 0.55 mm following maxillary expansion, primarily due to a 0.45 mm decrease in ANS. Kurt [31] also found an average increase of 1.21 mm in the N-ANS measurement in patients after SARME.

These researchers’ findings are consistent with our findings which indicate that the anterior height of the maxilla increases as a result of expansion.

However, it should be noted that alternative findings are also available. Farfel et al. [22] found a 0.4 mm decrease in the FH-ANS distance 4 months after surgery. After 10 months, this distance was found to be 0.1 mm. Dowgierd [21] found an increase in the N-ANS distance. However, this change was not statistically significant in his study group. On the other hand, Gungor et al. [24] found a decrease in the N-ANS measurement of 0.11 mm. This might be due to the fact that a variety of cephalometric assessments are employed, and because, in Arnett’s STCA, height parameters are measured over short distances (Sn – incisal edge of central incisor; Sn – oral fissure), which makes them more accurate at reflecting changes.

In our study group, we found changes in all sagittal parameters of STCA, with a statistically significant decrease in nasal projection, chin to forehead, and hyoid bone to mandible.

As we found no publications in the medical literature regarding changes in the nasal tip projection following maxillary expansion, it can be assumed that our finding is novel. The average retraction of the nasal tip by 0.44 mm may be explained by maxillary rotation, but further studies are certainly required. Unfortunately, our study confirmed no statistically significant correlation between changes in the maxillary occlusal plane inclination and the nasal tip projection (r = 0.136, p = 0.389). The available publications only indicate changes in the nose width and the subnasal region in a small group of 19 patients after SARME.

On the other hand, various researchers have found that the chin is displaced towards the posterior direction after SARME. They are in agreement that the clockwise rotation of the mandible is responsible for this change [16, 20, 24, 51].

Our study has also confirmed a negative correlation between changes in the mandibular and maxillary occlusal plane inclinations as well as changes in the chin to forehead parameter (r = 0.406, p = 0.008, r = 0.582, p < 0.001). The greater the inclination of the mandibular and maxillary planes, the greater the retraction of the Pg point.

Similarly, it seems logical that the decrease in the hyoid bone to mandible distance is related to the clockwise rotation of the mandible after SARME. A negative correlation between the parameters was also found in this instance.

Although several parameters reached statistical significance, the magnitude of most observed changes was small (generally < 1 mm). Such differences are often within the range of normal soft-tissue variability and may fall below the threshold of clinical detectability [2, 42]. Therefore, statistical significance in this context does not necessarily imply clinical relevance. The observed changes should be interpreted as subtle adaptations of the facial profile rather than clinically perceptible alterations.

From a clinical perspective, understanding these small but predictable tendencies may still support patient counselling and improve expectations regarding the aesthetic impact of SARME. From an orthognathic planning perspective these changes could modify facial relationships prior to definitive surgery and should be taken into account during treatment planning.

Airway changes

Of the respiratory parameters studied, Airway PAS-A decreased, while Airway PAS-Max, Airway PAS-B and Airway PAS-Pg increased. However, only the increase in Airway PAS-Pg was statistically significant.

We found no statistically significant correlation between the change in the occlusal planes of the maxilla and mandible and an increase in Airway PAS-Pg. Interestingly, the displacement of the chin in the posterior direction and therefore the decrease in the chin to forehead parameter also does not correlate with an increase in the Airway PAS-Pg parameter.

There is a consensus that maxillary expansion, irrespective of the method employed, exerts a beneficial effect on the airway parameters, resulting in an increase in the airway volume and cross-sectional area [10, 23]. However, these effects do not appear to be stable over a longer period of study.

Depending on the methods employed, an increase in the airway volume and cross-section of airways was found in the first period after maxillary expansion, but these changes decreased with time [11, 45]. In the context of literature, the change in a single cephalometric analysis parameter in our study does not appear significant enough, and certainly requires further investigation.

Comparison between skeletal class II and class III patients

The second novel finding is that no statistically significant differences were found in the changes of the STCA parameters in patients with skeletal class II and III after maxillary expansion. We found that the parameters in our study change similarly for both skeletal classes. The published literature [3, 10, 20–25, 27, 30, 31, 33, 38, 49, 51, 53] provides descriptions of groups of patients treated for maxillary constriction. However, none of these publications classifies patients by concomitant class II and class III skeletal deformities. The comparison should be regarded as exploratory, and the absence of statistically significant differences should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and single-center cohort may limit generalizability. The use of two-dimensional cephalometry does not fully capture three-dimensional soft-tissue and airway changes. The follow-up period (6–8 months) reflects early adaptation rather than long-term outcomes [15, 26]. Additionally, the study was not powered for subgroup analysis, and comparisons between skeletal Class II and III patients should be considered exploratory.
