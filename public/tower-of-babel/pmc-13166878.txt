Early planting and early maturing sunflower hybrids are neglected management tools to remedy outbreaks of the red sunflower seed weevil (Coleoptera: Curculionidae)
Authors: Jarrad R Prasifka, Sam Ireland, Adam J Varenhorst, Patrick Wagner, Jeffrey D Cluever, Kristin J Simons, Philip Rozeboom, Michael H Ostlie, Ryan Buetow, Tania Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe red sunflower seed weevil, Smicronyx fulvus L. (Coleoptera: Curculionidae), is a univoltine seed-feeding pest of sunflowers. Recent increases in S. fulvus damage and resistance to pyrethroids show a need for additional tools to complement insecticides. Cultural practices to advance sunflower bloom before S. fulvus emergence is complete were shown to be effective in the 1980s, but are not in use today. To address growers’ concerns, insecticide-free field trials were conducted to assess the efficacy, profitability, and sustainability of S. fulvus avoidance with early planting and early maturing hybrids. In central and western South Dakota, May plantings reduced S. fulvus damage by 60% or more relative to typical plantings from 15 to 20 June, and an early maturing hybrid may further reduce seed damage. However, in North Dakota, early planting did not provide a clear advantage, likely because of relatively low S. fulvus populations. Yields generally showed no negative effect from planting early, but yields were lower for the most common planting period (15–20 June) in central South Dakota. Adult S. fulvus emerged earlier where early planted sunflowers were grown in the previous year, but the benefits of weevil avoidance were not eliminated. The strategy of S. fulvus avoidance through early planting (before ≈ 15 May) and early maturing hybrids seems justified in South Dakota where pest populations are very high and insecticide efficacy is low because of resistance to pyrethroid active ingredients. For other key sunflower-growing areas including North Dakota, avoidance may become useful in the future if S. fulvus populations increase.Keywords: integrated pest management, cultural control, pest avoidance

Introduction

The red sunflower seed weevil, Smicronyx fulvus L. (Coleoptera: Curculionidae), is a native, univoltine pest of wild and cultivated sunflowers (Helianthus spp.) in North America. The geographic distribution of S. fulvus includes most of the United States east of California and extends northward into Canada (Anderson 1962). Adult weevils appear in early summer, female weevils oviposit through hulls onto developing seeds, and after feeding, mature larvae drop to overwinter in the soil starting in late summer or early fall (Oseto and Braness 1979). Unless S. fulvus populations are very high, most infested sunflower achenes contain a single larva, which consumes between one-quarter and one-third of the sunflower seed (Oseto and Braness 1980, Peng and Brewer 1995a).

According to Satterthwait (1946), Stephen A. Forbes noted severe damage to sunflowers by seed weevils as early as 1915, with the pest later determined to be S. fulvus. Furthermore, with more than 50% of seeds damaged in some fields in Missouri and Illinois during the 1930s, Satterthwait (1946) suggests S. fulvus resulted in sunflower being “almost entirely discontinued until war needs revived interest.” Much more recently, crop surveys across North American sunflower fields show several-fold increases in seed weevil damage in South Dakota, but relatively stable estimates in neighboring states (Cluever et al. 2025a). The recent S. fulvus outbreak in South Dakota likely has several causes (eg mild winter temperatures, limited soil disturbance [tillage], resistance to pyrethroid insecticides) and there is a real concern that one of the primary areas of sunflower production in the United States could be lost without changes to seed weevil management.

A combination of economic thresholds and aerially applied insecticides appears to be the primary means of S. fulvus management by sunflower growers (Cluever et al. 2025b). However, small-plot and field-scale research suggest several additional strategies that may complement or substitute for insecticides. For example, planting an early maturing sunflower hybrid to the perimeter of a large field (and applying insecticides along the perimeter) may limit damage and costs of management (Brewer and Schmidt 1995). Tillage appears to reduce emerging adult populations by one-third (Gednalske and Walgenbach 1984), likely because most S. fulvus larvae overwinter within ≈6 cm of the soil surface (Pantzke et al. 2023). Host plant resistance was suggested in various field trials (Brewer and Charlet 1995, Gao and Brewer 1998, Charlet et al. 2010) and eventually led to the release of an inbred line which receives about 70% less seed damage compared to susceptible lines under equal weevil populations (DeGreef et al. 2020). Pest avoidance may be the simplest and most effective strategy for S. fulvus management; trials in South Dakota (Gednalske 1983) and North Dakota (Oseto et al. 1987) during the 1980s show that planting early or planting early maturing hybrids can result in completion of anthesis (when the crop is attractive and susceptible to S. fulvus) before many adult weevils have emerged.

Despite demonstration that avoidance is effective for S. fulvus management, it appears that the strategy is not being used. For selection of early maturing hybrids, indirect evidence suggests that the use of early hybrids is uncommon. For example, of 39 industry submissions of hybrids to a 2022 yield trial in Fargo, North Dakota (https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/sites/default/files/2022-12/a652-22.pdf), the median time from planting to bloom was 69 d. Using a standard that an early maturing hybrid is 4 or more days earlier than the median days to bloom, only 2 of the 39 commercial hybrids could be considered early (though 1 USDA-developed hybrid bloomed at 58 d in the same trial). Grower data on planting practices submitted to USDA’s Risk Management Agency (RMA) for central South Dakota (Hyde, Hughes, Potter, and Sully Counties) provide a good look at the use of early planting. In 2022, less than 1% of insured units (ie groups of fields) were planted within the first 28 d of the crop insurance window (26 April to 20 June; Supplementary Material S1).

Recent discussions with sunflower industry leaders and growers have been used to investigate skepticism that pest avoidance is a viable strategy. Over several meetings, a series of concerns or complaints have been raised (Varenhorst and Prasifka, personal observations). The most straightforward worry is that data from the 1980s showing avoidance to be effective are likely no longer valid or that area growers have tried this strategy and not succeeded in reducing weevil damage. The second concern is (whether or not pest avoidance works to manage S. fulvus) that yields will be poor if sunflowers are planted early. A third type of objection is that pest avoidance is no more sustainable than insecticides and that any benefits of earlier planting will be countered by selection for earlier weevil emergence.

Although the most serious problem in managing S. fulvus appears concentrated in South Dakota, factors contributing to the outbreaks (ie warmer winters, insecticide resistance) are likely to expand to North Dakota and other sunflower-producing states. As a result, there is a need to address concerns about (i) the efficacy of early planting and early maturing hybrids in reducing S. fulvus damage, (ii) whether the strategy of pest avoidance limits sunflower yields and profitability, and (iii) whether early planting appears sustainable if adopted as a management tool. These issues were evaluated in South Dakota and North Dakota from 2022 to 2024 in insecticide-free field trials designed to address growers’ concerns regarding the viability of avoidance as a management strategy for S. fulvus.

Materials and Methods

To assess the effects of early planting and early maturing hybrids on S. fulvus and sunflower yields, experimental plantings were used in 9 trials (=location × year combinations) including 4 locations (Pierre and Sturgis, South Dakota, and Dickinson and Carrington, North Dakota). For each trial, 2 different sunflower hybrids were planted on 4 dates (or 3 for Pierre in 2022 and Carrington in 2024) within the period RMA shows as earliest and final planting dates for the county where the trial was located. Differences in local production practices, availability of plot space, and equipment available across 4 locations combined to create considerable variation in plot management. Generally, plots were rain-fed (no irrigation), no-till (except Carrington, North Dakota), with 0.76 m between rows. The smallest plots followed methods used in public-sector yield trials of industry hybrids (see https://extension.sdstate.edu/south-dakota-sunflower-hybrid-performance-trials and https://www.ag.ndsu.edu/varietytrials/sunflower), but plots in other locations were substantially larger (Table 1).

Trials conducted in 2022 were initially considered as proof-of-concept, without some of the elements (eg combine-estimated yields, adult emergence monitoring) included in 2023 to 2024. Other than routine weed management (manual removal or herbicides), the only trial activity in plots prior to crop maturity was collection of notes on bloom timing. Specifically, observations were made at each location in 2023 to 2024 to estimate when ≈10% of plants in each treatment had begun to shed pollen. Because oviposition by S. fulvus is generally limited to stage R5 (when plants are shedding pollen), bloom notes show how differences in planting date affect the timing of the crop’s susceptibility to the pest.

Seed Damage

Whether sunflowers were harvested mechanically or by hand, small threshed samples (≤0.4 kg) from each plot were packaged for further analysis. To assess seed damage by S. fulvus, a 100-seed (2022) or 200-seed (2023 to 2024) subsample from each plot was packaged for X-ray imaging. The X-ray unit used (UltraFocus 23 × 29, Faxitron Bioptics, LLC, Tucson, Arizona, United States), automates the voltage (kV), current (mA), and exposure times (s), though settings were typically close to 25 kV, 0.40 mA for 3 s. Digital images of the seed X-rays were examined in ImageJ (Schneider et al. 2012), which allowed marking of S. fulvus-damaged seeds in each image. The distinct appearance of feeding by weevil larvae (Peng and Brewer 1995b) also enabled separate counts of damage by lepidopteran larvae (either banded sunflower moth, Cochylis hospes Walsingham [Lepidoptera: Cochylidae], or sunflower moth, Homoeosoma electellum [Hulst] [Lepidoptera: Pyralidae]). Consistency in scoring of samples was addressed by using 1 experienced rater to evaluate all samples from every location × year combination.

Yield Components

When possible, yield measurements from trials were taken from combine readings for each plot. In cases where mechanical harvest was not possible, heads were harvested by hand (at least 5 heads from each of 3 locations per plot), threshed, cleaned, and weighed. In addition to overall yields, oil content (%) is important to profitability because of premiums paid to growers (eg 2% price premium for each 1% beyond 40% oil). Consequently, oil content was assessed using 80 ml subsamples from each plot using a benchtop nuclear magnetic resonance analyzer (Oxford MQC 5, Oxford Instruments, Concord, Massachusetts, United States). Separate calibration curves were established for hybrids with mid-oleic (55% to 75%) or high-oleic (>80%) oil profiles and standard check samples were run after every 20 field-collected samples.

Weevil Emergence

Prior experience suggested useful data on adult emergence are difficult to obtain without high overwintering populations. Consequently, emergence traps were only used at the Pierre-area location, where weevil populations were very high. As with prior research on adult emergence (Gednalske and Walgenbach 1984), a polyethylene bucket (RG503, 30.5 cm diameter, Argee Corportation, El Cajon, California, United States) was modified for use as a trap. A hole saw was used to make 2 circular openings (6.4 cm diameter) at the bottom of each bucket, which were later covered by melting the polyethylene to a circular piece of brass cloth over the openings. The brass cloth allowed for air and water exchange while preventing exit by weevils. The assembled traps were then inverted and pressed into soil where sunflowers were grown in the previous season to contain adults as they emerged from the soil. To further limit the chance that weevils could escape the buckets, surrounding soil was mounded around the base of the trap and a landscaping brick was placed on top of each trap.

In 2023, traps were placed into the ground used for planting date trials in the previous year, using the earliest (n = 4 traps, 27 May) and latest (n = 3 traps, 17 June) 2022 planting dates. Traps were checked twice each week from 2 June to 7 August 2023 for emerged adults, which were aspirated from traps for later counting. Because of low numbers of weevils emerging into traps in 2023, especially traps over early planted sunflowers, the numbers of traps were increased in 2024 for both earliest (n = 20 traps, 1 May) and latest (n = 10 traps, 15 June) 2023 planting dates. Traps were checked once each week from 3 July to 19 September 2024.

Data Analysis

Differences across locations and years (eg plot size, hybrids grown, weather) were common and unable to be accounted for analytically. Consequently, each location × year combination was analyzed separately. Dependent variables (weevil damage [%], yield [kg/ha], and oil content [%]) were tested for fixed effects of planting date, hybrid, and date × hybrid interaction (PROC MIXED, SAS Institute Inc. 2018) with residuals visually inspected for normal distribution and equal variance. When significant date effects were found, comparisons between dates were made using Tukey’s test. If a significant date × hybrid interaction was detected, comparisons between dates were made within each hybrid. For the locations with the most weevil feeding damage (Pierre and Sturgis), the relationship between the percentage of S. fulvus-damaged seed and oil content were analyzed using simple linear regression. Data on weevil emergence were analyzed in 2 ways. First, to test whether emergence of S. fulvus adults was influenced by the planting date in the prior year, Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests (PROC NPAR1WAY) were used to compare cumulative emergence of weevils into traps placed in the earliest- and latest-planted sunflowers for the previous year. Second, to test whether the earliest- and latest-planted sunflowers produced different numbers of weevils, total emerged weevils for each trap were used and compared via a 1-tailed t-test. Finally, to assess which variables might help growers determine when planting might achieve a desired reduction in damage by S. fulvus, seed damage for samples from Pierre (the location with the highest high weevil damage) were regressed against 4 potential predictors. Specifically, seed damage data used only the later-maturing hybrid for each year (2022 to 2024), and potential predictors included (i) day of year at planting, (ii) soil temperature (°C) at planting, (iii) mean soil temperature over 14 d prior to planting, and (iv) cumulative weevil degree-days at planting (using a lower developmental threshold of 5 °C after Pantzke et al. 2024). Soil temperature data were obtained for the Pierre location of the South Dakota State University Mesonet (https://climate.sdstate.edu/).

Results

The period between earliest and latest plantings across location-years was as small as 28 d (Carrington in 2023) and as large as 55 d (Pierre in 2024). However, because of faster development (=more degree-days per day) in later plantings, differences in the onset of bloom were reduced. In the most extreme instance (Pierre in 2024), the numbers of days between bloom for the earliest and latest plantings (24 to 27 d, depending on sunflower hybrid) were about half the number of days between those plantings (55 d). Weevil Emergence

At the Pierre location, the first adult weevils collected in 2023 were found in traps placed where early planted sunflowers were grown in the previous year (2022). Emergence into these “early” traps started on 14 July and appeared complete before August. However, the loss of seasonal help to check traps during August meant emergence traps over late-planted sunflower were last checked on 7 August, when weevils might still be actively emerging. Comparison of the emergence curves (distribution functions) into traps over early- and late-planted sunflowers from the previous year showed emergence in 2023 was affected by planting time in 2022 (Kolmorgorov–Smirnov D = 0.69, P = 0.010). Although the mean number of weevils collected per trap appeared to be distinct between traps over early (mean ± SD; 1.50 ± 1.29) and late-planted (20.67 ± 23.71) sunflowers, the 2023 data do not show a significant difference (t = 1.67, df = 5, P = 0.078).

For 2024, both the number of traps and duration of weevil collection were increased. The first adult weevils were collected in traps on 23 July and continued until 19 September, at which point traps were removed to allow for harvest of grain sorghum grown following 2023 sunflowers. Weevil emergence into traps over early and late-planted sunflowers from the previous year again differed (Kolmorgorov–Smirnov D = 0.43, P < 0.001). Inspection of cumulative emergence curves for 2023 and 2024 suggest that 50% emergence for weevils was delayed 10 to 14 d for late-planted sunflowers (Fig. 5). The mean number of weevils collected per trap in 2024 for early (2.35 ± 2.54) and late-planted (7.10 ± 5.70) sunflowers were significantly different (t = 3.18, df = 28, P = 0.002).

Predictors of Weevil Damage

When damage to a hybrid of normal (or late) maturity was regressed against potential predictors using data from Pierre (2022 to 2024), soil temperature (10 cm depth) at the time of planting (R2 = 0.53) and average soil temperature over 14 d prior to planting (R2 = 0.58) both helped explain observed weevil damage. However, both day of year and cumulative weevil degree-days at planting appeared more useful (Fig. 6) for predicting weevil damage in the absence of insecticides or other S. fulvus management.

Discussion

Field trials in South Dakota and North Dakota were conducted to determine whether early planting and early maturing hybrids are effective in reducing S. fulvus damage. The pest avoidance strategy was previously demonstrated as effective in research during the 1980s (Gednalske 1983, Oseto et al. 1987), but sunflower growers have consistently chosen not to use early planting or early maturing hybrids for pest management. In discussions with sunflower growers (and feedback from crop advisors; Cluever et al. 2025b), the first objection to using pest avoidance has been that it is not an effective tool to reduce weevil damage. Field trials in South Dakota from 2022 to 2024 largely refute the idea that avoidance does not work; results from a central location (Pierre) showed planting dates spanning 1 to 27 May reduced weevil damage by > 60% relative to typical dates of 15 to 20 June. For a western site (Sturgis), where overall S. fulvus populations are lower, trials also showed ≈60% less damage in plantings from 8 to 22 May versus 15 to 20 June. However, results for North Dakota were less clear. Early planting did not provide a clear advantage in North Dakota for either western (Dickinson) or central (Carrington) locations; high-seed damage by C. hospes larvae in the 2024 early planting at Carrington underscore that the utility of early planting is context dependent. The most obvious explanations for differences between states include (i) that weevil populations are far greater in South Dakota and (ii) that the earliest planting date compatible with crop insurance in North Dakota starts 15 days later than the South Dakota locations (ie there is less opportunity to reach bloom prior to weevil emergence). However, S. fulvus populations in North Dakota have previously reached ≈100 weevils per plant (Roseland et al. 1990), and the spread of pyrethroid resistance from South Dakota seems likely, as genetic data suggests S. fulvus across the Great Plains appear to be indistinct (N. Miller, unpublished data). Combined, prior periods of high S. fulvus abundance and apparent gene flow throughout the region suggest a need may develop for cultural practices to manage red sunflower seed weevils in North Dakota.

Reduced yield has also been repeatedly mentioned by growers as a reason not to use early planting as part of S. fulvus management. While planting date certainly influences yield in sunflower and other crops, outside of extremely early or late planting, the effects may be subtle or inconsistent (Alessi et al. 1977, Obopile et al. 2013). Within the range of dates prescribed for crop insurance, overall yields (kg/ha) generally showed no negative effect from planting early (Carrington and Sturgis) or showed that yields were greater in plantings earlier than the most common planting period (15 to 20 June) near Pierre. When percentage of oil is considered as a yield component, date effects were common, but usually in favor of early planting. For both crude yield at Pierre and percentage of oil for Pierre and Sturgis, the advantages of early planting appeared driven by moderate-to-high levels of S. fulvus damage (Fig. 4). Because price premiums for exceeding 40% oil appear very unlikely as S. fulvus-damaged seed exceeds 20% or 30% (depending on location and year), effective S. fulvus management including early planting provides benefits in excess of those to overall yields (kg/ha). Other potential benefits, like reduced costs through fewer aerial applications of insecticide (as much as $45 per hectare for 1 to 3 applications; McClure 2025), were not considered but may be important particularly when commodity prices are low.

A final reason given for sunflower growers’ skepticism of weevil avoidance is that the practice is not sustainable because weevils may adapt just as they have to frequent use of pyrethroid insecticides. Data on S. fulvus emergence from early and late-planted sunflowers in 2023 and 2024 provide 2 insights into what might happen if early planting was adopted by large numbers of growers in an area. First, it is clear that weevil emergence (eg 50% of cumulative emergence) from early planted sunflowers occurs before emergence from later plantings. However, the shifts in phenology do not appear to be even. From 2022 to 2024 data at Pierre, moving planting dates 30 to 45 d earlier appears to advance S. fulvus emergence by 10 to 14 d, but sunflower bloom was advanced by as much as 30 d, suggesting (at least in the near-term) early plantings would avoid exposure to much of the pest population. Second, reduced infestation of early plantings should produce lower overall S. fulvus populations in subsequent years. Emergence data from 2024 suggest that widespread early planting of sunflower could reduce weevil populations by two-thirds in a single year, though emergence trapping was limited to 1 location over 2 years. The emergence data cannot be used to estimate how long the use of early planting would be viable, but experience with other univoltine pests managed using cultural practices are worth considering. For example, the western corn rootworm (Diabrotica virgifera LeConte [Coleoptera: Chrysomelidae]), developed resistance to widespread use of crop rotation as a management strategy, though Levine et al. (2002) note that insect adaptation (a shift away to egg-laying outside of corn) seems to have taken more than 80 years. Similarly, the northern corn rootworm (Diabrotica barberi Smith and Lawrence) has overcome crop rotation using extended (2-year) diapause. Although a low (2%) incidence of delayed hatch was shown by Chiang (1965), an increase in extended diapause to about 40% seems to have been gradual (Krysan et al. 1984). Pest adaptations to cultural practices are not commonly documented (Levine et al. 2002), but the examples available suggest early planting could remain effective against S. fulvus for many years.

Sunflower grower decisions on when (or what varieties) to plant can be complex and are influenced by the other crops they grow, especially through availability of planting equipment. However, planting date changes for avoidance of insect pests are recommended in crops including cotton (Aghaee et al. 2019), corn (Kaur et al. 2024), soybean (McPherson et al. 2008), and grain sorghum (Uyi et al. 2023) when insect pests are severe, widespread, or otherwise difficult to manage with insecticides. Results from trials in 2023 to 2024 suggest S. fulvus avoidance through early planting and early maturing hybrids is justified for South Dakota (where pest populations are high and insecticide efficacy is low because of resistance to pyrethroid active ingredients), but not for North Dakota. Data for central South Dakota suggest planting between 7 and 15 May is a compromise that will reduce S. fulvus populations (both at bloom for the planted crop and for subsequent years, if early planting is common in the surrounding area) but also reduce the risk of cool, wet conditions that interfere with germination and establishment of the crop. For other key sunflower-growing areas like North Dakota, S. fulvus avoidance may be an option in the future, but may not be useful at present because of relatively low weevil populations and higher populations of other pests like banded sunflower moth or sunflower bud moth (Suleima helianthana [Riley] [Lepidoptera: Tortricidae]) for which early planting increases risks of economic losses (Oseto et al. 1989, Knodel et al. 2011).
