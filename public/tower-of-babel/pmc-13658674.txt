Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is recognized as a major cause of severe disease in adults, particularly those having cardiorespiratory comorbidities or immunosuppression [1, 2]. The persistence of long-term symptoms that follow the initial infection highlight the need to expand prevention strategies, primarily RSV vaccination [3]. IDSA Guidelines were recently published, providing strong recommendations for RSV vaccination of immunocompromised adults and adolescents, including those with hematological malignancies [4]. The recent deployment of monoclonal antibodies targeting the prefusion F protein, including nirsevimab, also represents a major advance in RSV prevention in infants [5, 6], and could be used in the future to prevent RSV-associated hospitalization and critical illness in immunocompromised adult patients. However, amino acid substitutions within the F protein may confer reduced susceptibility, raising concerns regarding resistance emergence and transmission [7]. We are reporting a case of RSV infection occurring in an adult patient who was immunocompromised and in whom we identified a primary resistance-associated RSV-B variant harboring the F:K68Q substitution without prior exposure to nirsevimab.

Case Presentation

Methods

This report is part of the ongoing ARF-RSV study (NCT06197152), a prospective multicenter observational cohort designed to characterize the virological determinants of RSV infection severity in hospitalized adults. The main objective of the study is to identify biomarkers associated with the severity of RSV infection in adults and validate their use in clinical practice. Patients with severe disease, defined as developing acute respiratory failure with a World Health Organization (WHO) 10-point scale [8] ≥ 6 at any time of hospital stay, and those with mild disease (WHO 10-point scale remaining < 6 during hospital stay) will be compared according to their inflammatory and immune responses, as assessed by transcriptomic analyses of peripheral blood and in respiratory samples (nasopharyngeal swabs and, when available, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or tracheal aspirates) obtained within 72 h of hospitalization. Adult patients hospitalized for acute respiratory infection with documented RSV infection by PCR were prospectively enrolled. The first patient was included on December 27, 2023. In June 2026, among the 12 participating centers (all located in France), 117 patients had been enrolled in the study, which has a target of inclusions of 233 patients. Sequential nasopharyngeal swabs were collected at inclusion and during follow-up (day 7). Demographic, clinical, microbiological, and laboratory data were prospectively recorded.

Whole-genome RSV sequencing was performed from nasopharyngeal samples using a whole-genome amplification strategy derived from the French National Influenza Reference Center protocol, as previously described [4]. Viral genomes were reconstructed by reference-based assembly, and intrahost viral diversity was assessed through deep sequencing analysis.

The study was approved by the CPP Sud-Méditerranée I ethics committee on February 3, 2023 (reference number: 2022-A02194-39). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives.

Clinical and Virological Findings

A 64-year-old man with a history of insulin-dependent type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and aggressive mantle cell lymphoma under active treatment was admitted to the intensive care unit for severe sepsis in the setting of febrile aplasia. He had received lymphodepletion chemotherapy 1 week earlier and was planned to receive CAR-T cells. He had not been vaccinated against RSV before entering into the CAR-T cell program.

At admission, no clear source of infection was identified apart from a respiratory syndrome evolving for 7 days. Microbiological investigations were notable for detection of RSV in a nasopharyngeal sample. A concomitant bacterial infection was suspected but remained undocumented, and empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy was initiated.

The patient initially required hemodynamic support, consistent with septic shock. Clinical evolution was rapidly favorable, with resolution of hemodynamic failure within a few days. Due to the infectious complication, the injection of CAR-T cells was suspended and eventually performed 1 month later.

Two nasopharyngeal samples collected 7 days apart were positive for RSV-B. Sequencing was performed as part of the ongoing ARF-RSV study, a prospective multicenter cohort designed to characterize viral genomic diversity and host responses in adults hospitalized with RSV infection. Both samples achieved complete genome coverage (100%) with high sequencing depth. Genetic analysis identified a strain belonging to lineage B.D.E.1, with both samples harboring a dominant F:K68Q substitution in the fusion protein. In the first sample, the mean depth of coverage was over 180× and the F:K68Q substitution was detected at an allele frequency of 99%. In the second sample, collected 7 days later, the mean depth of coverage was 170×, with the same substitution detected with an allele frequency of 97%, consistent with persistence as a dominant variant over time. This substitution has been previously associated with high-level resistance to nirsevimab [9, 10]. In addition to K68Q, the consensus F-protein sequence harbored R209Q, S190N and S211N. No other minority resistance-associated substitution was detected in the F protein. Notably, the patient had not received nirsevimab.

Discussion

This particular case highlights the value of integrating genomic surveillance into prospective clinical cohorts of RSV-infected patients. Such an approach is essential to better characterize the baseline diversity of RSV and to distinguish naturally occurring polymorphisms from mutations emerging under selective pressure. In this context, the detection of F:K68Q in a monoclonal antibody-naïve patient reinforces the hypothesis that this substitution may circulate independently of nirsevimab exposure. Historical data, including reports of several cluster cases in Kenya in 2017 [11], further support its classification as a naturally occurring polymorphism with episodic expansion. The re-emergence of such variants in the current era, coinciding with widespread implementation of nirsevimab prophylaxis, raises concerns about potential selection and amplification under immunoprophylactic pressure. Finally, patients who are immunocompromised may represent important reservoirs for prolonged viral replication and diversification [12], potentially facilitating the persistence or emergence of such variants.

This case report has several limitations. Firstly, the substitution was detected using whole genome sequencing, but no phenotypic assay was performed on the patient’s isolate. However, Wilkins et al. showed markedly reduced nirsevimab susceptibility of RSV-B carrying K68Q in phenotypic neutralization assays [10], and deep mutational scanning by Simonich et al. independently confirmed that K68Q strongly impairs nirsevimab neutralization [9]. Secondly, although the first nasopharyngeal sample was obtained upon admission to the intensive care unit, there was a 7-day time lag between the onset of symptoms and admission. This means that we cannot tell whether the infection is from a circulating strain with the F:K68Q substitution or whether the mutation emerged de novo within the host.

Our report also has its strengths. Firstly, this case was identified within an ongoing, multicenter, prospective study, which implies that virological samples were collected within predefined timeframes and that clinical data were collected prospectively. Secondly, the availability of sequential respiratory samples combined with whole-genome deep sequencing allowed us to demonstrate the persistence of F:K68Q as the dominant viral variant over time. To our knowledge, although rare, the longitudinal persistence of an RSV resistant variant has not previously been documented.

Conclusions

Primary resistance to nirsevimab has rarely been reported in the context of its recent implementation. The persistence of a resistant RSV variant in a patient who was immunocompromised without prior exposure suggests that such variants may circulate and be transmitted. In this context, RSV vaccination of patients who are immunocompromised may not only provide individual protection but also help limit the dissemination of resistant strains.
