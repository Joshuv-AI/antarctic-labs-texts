Prevalence and factors associated with sugar-sweetened beverage consumption among adolescents and women aged 10–49 years in Kenya
Authors: Sharonmercy Okemwa, Caleb Nyakundi, Romeo Warera Ngesa, Catherine Akoth, Peter Kibe, Loise Ng’ang’a, Elvis Omondi Achach Wambiya, James Odhiambo Oguta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) are major sources of free sugars in diet and have been linked to obesity, chronic diseases, and increased risk of premature deaths. Evidence suggests that the health impacts of SSBs may be greater among females than males; however, there is limited evidence on SSB consumption among women, especially in low-and middle-income countries. Therefore, we aimed to determine the prevalence and factors associated with SSB consumption among women aged 10–49 years in Kenya using secondary data from the first and second rounds of Kenya’s Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) nutrition surveys. We stratified the analysis by residence (rural vs. urban), and used univariable and multivariable logistic regression to assess the determinants of SSB consumption. The prevalence of SSB consumption among women was 52.4% (95% CI 47.9, 56.8). The prevalence of SSB consumption was higher in urban than rural areas (53.2% vs. 51.7%) and was high among women aged 20–29 years (54.1%), women in union (52.9%), women with tertiary level of education (59.1%), and women from the richest households (52.4%). Urban women had 1.29 times higher odds of SSB consumption compared to rural women. Moreover, education level was significantly associated with SSB consumption, with higher education linked to increased odds of consumption. Overall, more than half of women aged 10–49 years reported consuming SSBs. These findings underscore the need for targeted policy interventions to reduce SSB consumption, especially among urban residents and individuals with higher socioeconomic status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49411-4.

Introduction

Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) are the main contributors of free sugars in diets with very low nutritional benefit1. They include non-carbonated and carbonated soft drinks, flavored water, energy drinks, flavoured milk products, ready-to-drink tea, and coffee2. The health effects of SSB consumption are a significant public health issue, especially in low and middle-income countries (LMICs)3. This is largely attributable to the popularity of SSBs, alongside rapid urbanization, economic growth, and nutritional transition in LMICs4.

Globally, the burden of SSB consumption by 2010 contributed to 8.5 million disability-adjusted life years and 184,000 deaths, with the most significant burden being in the LMICs3. SSB consumption has been independently associated with non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as obesity5, type 2 diabetes6, cardiovascular disease7, dental caries and erosion8, and an increased risk of premature deaths9. Previous studies have associated high consumption of SSBs with an unhealthy lifestyle, such as a poor-quality diet, lower intake of fruits and vegetables, and inadequate household food supply10–13. In addition, gender, age, socioeconomic status, marital status, and educational level have been positively associated with SSB consumption14–16.

The worldwide intake of SSB increased by 16% from 2015 to 2018, with the largest intake increasing in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) from 1.93% to 41.4%17. In SSA, the rates of SSB consumption differ by country and sociodemographic characteristics, with consumption ranging from 16% among adults in South Africa20 to 34.9% among Ghanaian adolescents18 = and 87% among Nigerian adolescents19. The national sugar consumption in Kenya has gradually increased between 1969 and 2009 from 35.5 g per person per day to 60.8 g21. Data collected by the Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) surveys on the intake of SSB showed the proportion of adolescents and women (10–49 years) consuming SSBs increased from 47.7% in 2017 to 63.1% in 201822. The significant increase in sugar consumption could be attributed to growth in retail, industrial, and food services23, and increased consumption of tea and coffee24.

The burden of SSB consumption contributes significantly to the development of obesity and other nutrition-related NCDs, with higher economic implications for national and household income25. There is limited evidence on SSB consumption in Kenya, with an existing study focusing on a pooled analysis of Kenya and Burkina Faso with no specific prevalence rates and insights tailored to the Kenyan context26. Evidence has shown women who consume SSBs have a significantly greater risk of ischaemic stroke compared to men, and SSB consumption is associated with cardio-metabolic risk factors which may disproportionately affect women of reproductive age27. We therefore aimed to assess the prevalence of SSB consumption and identify associated factors among adolescents and women aged 10–49 years in Kenya.

Methods

Study setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Kenya, a lower-and middle-income country (LMIC) with a population of over 54 million and over 12.1 million women aged 10–49 years living across 47 counties. More than two-thirds (69%) of Kenyans live in rural areas with an overall national poverty headcount rate of 38.6%28.

Data source and sample size

We used secondary data from the first22 and second29 rounds of Kenya’s PMA nutrition surveys conducted in 2017 and 2018, respectively. The surveys used a multi-stage stratified cluster design, with stratification based on administrative regions (11 counties) and residence (rural–urban). The primary sampling units for the survey were the enumeration areas created during the 2009 Kenya Population and Housing Census. The 151 enumeration areas were selected systematically with probability proportional to size and based on urban/rural stratification in the 11 counties. Prior to data collection, all key landmarks, health service delivery points (SDPs), and households in each enumeration area (EA) were listed and mapped to create a sampling frame for the second stage of the sampling process. In the first survey round (2017), 140 households were randomly selected from each EA22, while in the second round (2018), 56 households were randomly selected per EA29. A female-child questionnaire was administered to all women 10–49 years of age in the selected households in Round 122, while in Round 2, it was administered to women aged 10–49 years in 25% of the households29.

Data collection

The PMA nutrition surveys were administered through face-to-face household interviews conducted by trained female resident enumerators (REs) using standardized, Open Data Kit (ODK) structured questionnaires programmed on mobile data-collection devices. The PMA surveys use standardized household and female questionnaires designed to ensure cross-country comparability with nationally representative surveys. Questionnaires were reviewed and adapted for the Kenyan context, with country-specific questions added as required to reflect country priorities30,31. Enumerators were responsible for distributing and administering the surveys at the household level, while field supervisors oversaw implementation and ensured adherence to study protocols. Prior to each survey round, REs and supervisors participated in refresher training designed to reinforce interviewing techniques, consent administration, and research ethics. Training included paired interview practice sessions and evaluation through written and phone-based assessments. Trainings were conducted primarily in English, with selected sessions held in Kiswahili. RE and supervisors were also evaluated based on their performance before the data collection process31.

Verbal informed consent was obtained from all eligible women after explaining the study objectives, with participants informed of voluntary participation, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any time. Round one included 7,260 females aged 10–49 years, yielding a response rate of 99.2%. In round two, a total of 7,034 complete female–child questionnaires were collected (99.5% response rate), of which 2,471 were from females aged 10–49 years. Data were collected from June to August 2017 for Round 1 and from May to August 2018 for Round 2.

The PMA surveys protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health and the Kenyatta National Hospital-University of Nairobi Ethics and Research Committee (P801/09/2019). The data for this secondary analysis were obtained upon request from PMA and were used in accordance with the PMA Data Use Policy.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The main outcome variable, SSB consumption, was measured by asking the participant, “Ate/drank yesterday: Any sugar-sweetened beverages like sweet fruit drinks, fizzy drinks, sweet tea”, with an optional response of Yes, No, or No response. We created a binary outcome variable based on the questions and equated it to 1 if the respondents consumed any SSB and 0 otherwise22,29.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was stratified by residence (rural or urban) and adjusted for cluster sampling using survey weights available in the dataset. We described the participants’ characteristics and the prevalence of SSB using frequencies and weighted percentages. We used univariable and multivariable logistic regression to assess the determinants of SSB consumption. Explanatory variables that were clinically significant and those with p < 0.25 in the univariable analysis were retained for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. We reported crude and adjusted odds ratios and evaluated statistical significance at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software version 15 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas)33.

Prevalence of SSB consumption

Table 3 presents the prevalence of SSB consumption across demographic characteristics. The prevalence of SSB consumption was 52.4% (95% CI 47.9, 56.8) with a higher prevalence in urban areas than in rural areas (53.2% vs. 51.7%). The prevalence was highest in Kericho county (65.3%), among those aged 20–29 years (54.1%), in union (52.9%), with tertiary level of education (59.1%), the richest (52.4%), and employed (57.0%). Among participants from rural areas, the highest prevalence of SSB consumption was observed among women aged 40–49 years (54.4%), those not in union (52.5%), women from richer households (55.7%), pregnant women (52.0%), and those with a normal BMI (54.7%). Discussion

The study assessed the prevalence of SSB consumption among women aged 10–49 years in Kenya while identifying the demographic, socioeconomic, and health behavior factors associated with it. The estimated prevalence of SSB consumption was 52.4%, with a notable increase observed from 48.3% in 2017 to 63.6% in 2018. The observed prevalence is comparable to that of adolescents in Western Kenya (53.8%)34, and adolescents across 53 LMICs (54.3%)35, but higher than that of Ghanaian adolescents (34.9%)18, adults in South Africa (16.0%)20, and pregnant women in Nigeria (25%)14. The high prevalence of SSB consumption in LMICs, including Kenya, could be partly attributed to their affordability and accessibility, driven by the rising consumer incomes and insufficient action on policies to regulate the pricing of SSBs36. While the Government of Kenya imposes an excise tax on soft drinks, including those with and without sugar, this tax is not specific to SSBs, limiting the effectiveness of this policy on reducing consumption. These findings highlight the need for targeted policies that aim to reduce the consumption of SSBs, including introducing SSB-specific taxation .

Urban areas in LMICs are increasingly undergoing a nutritional transition, shifting away from traditional diets toward consumption of processed foods and beverages with high sugar levels37. Consistent with a study assessing the frequency of SSB consumption and its association with social and behavioral factors in South Africa20, our findings indicated that SSB consumption was higher among urban residents, with 29% higher odds of consumption compared to rural residents. However, this rural-urban difference was lower than that reported by the Global Alliance for Improved Nutrition, which found SSB consumption to be twice as high in urban areas than in rural areas4. This pattern is potentially driven by the widespread advertising of unhealthy foods and beverages in urban settings. A study conducted in deprived urban neighbourhoods in Ghana and Kenya found that SSBs are the most advertised items in urban areas, accounting for nearly half (48.3%) of all food and beverage advertisements38. This concentrated marketing, coupled with increased product accessibility, including sales among street vendors, increases SSB accessibility among urban residents.

A non-significant inverse association was observed between age and SSB consumption. This pattern is consistent with findings from a study examining SSB consumption among pregnant women in Nigeria14, which showed a decreasing pattern of consumption with increase in age. Stratification by residence revealed that rural women aged 40–49 years had the lowest odds of SSB consumption. The higher consumption of SSBs observed among younger respondents in the present study may be attributable to higher exposure to SSB advertising, stronger taste preferences for sweetened beverages, and the popularity of these products within younger age groups. Furthermore, younger people tend to eat outside the home more frequently, including at fast-food and chain restaurants, which is associated with an increased likelihood of SSB consumption.

A landscape analysis for SSB taxation in SSA identified socioeconomic status as a determinant of food choices, with employment increasing the purchasing power and the affordability of SSBs39. Consistent with this landscape analysis, our findings indicate that the prevalence of SSB consumption was higher among employed women and those in the richest wealth quintile. Employed individuals may be more likely to engage in out-of-home eating, thereby increasing their exposure to and consumption of SSBs14. Additionally, the trend of high SSB consumption among wealthier women may reflect lifestyle choices wherein high-income individuals opt for less healthy dietary options despite affording healthier options. Implementing workplace wellness programs that promote healthy beverage alternatives could reduce SSB intake.

The prevalence and odds of SSB consumption increased significantly with higher educational attainment among women in both rural and urban areas. This pattern is consistent with findings from a multi-country study examining SSB intake among adults across 185 countries, which similarly found higher intakes among more educated populations in SSA. However, an inverse relationship was observed in the Middle East and North Africa17. The study findings challenge the assumption that higher education attainment translates into healthier dietary choices, suggesting that literacy alone does not protect against SSB consumption. Therefore, interventions need to be tailored to specific educational levels and demographic groups, with targeted messaging on the consequences of excessive SSB intake.

Food cravings and hormone changes associated with pregnancy may contribute to increased SSB consumption among women40. This study found the consumption of SSBs higher among pregnant women in rural areas compared to their non-pregnant counterparts. This finding is in contrast to a study comparing SSB intake between pregnant and non-pregnant women of reproductive age, which found that non-pregnant women reported higher frequencies of SSB consumption41. While increased caloric intake is an expected physiological response in pregnancy, the high consumption observed among pregnant women in this study is of concern, and may reflect limited awareness of the harmful effects of SSBs during pregnancy. Interventions aimed at reducing the consumption of SSBs should incorporate health education targeting women of reproductive age prior to conception, while also integrating nutritional counselling into routine antenatal care services14.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate SSB consumption among women and adolescent girls in Kenya using a nationally representative sample, thus addressing a gap in the existing literature. By examining the different associated factors with a substantially larger sample, the findings of this study could inform public health policy regarding sugar consumption in Kenya. This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The study included only women and adolescent girls, as the PMA nutrition surveys collect individual-level dietary data exclusively from female respondents. As a result, our findings may not be generalizable to the male population in Kenya. Nevertheless, existing evidence indicates that women disproportionately bear the health consequences of high SSB consumption, reproductive years; focusing on women provides important public health insights42. Future studies should seek to incorporate male populations to enable sex-comparative analyses of SSB consumption patterns. In addition, we did not evaluate the types of SSB consumed, the frequency of SSB consumption, or the servings of sugar consumed in SSBs. Moreover, SSB intake was assessed based on recall, which may not be representative of usual SSB intake.

Conclusion

Overall, more than half of Kenyan adolescents and women aged 10–49 years reported consuming sugar-sweetened beverages, with an increasing trend in consumption over the survey period. Urban residence, higher educational attainment, wealth status, employment, and pregnancy status were significantly associated with SSB consumption. The cross-sectional design of the study limits causal inference; however, the associations identified highlight groups with a higher likelihood of SSB intake and provide evidence to inform public health interventions. Longitudinal studies, including both sexes, are needed to better understand determinants of SSB consumption and their associated health outcomes.
