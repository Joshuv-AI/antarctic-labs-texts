Ten-year trend of dental emergency visits in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Kexuan Li, Ziqi Chen, Lin Zhang, Yining He, Rong Du, Yanyi Tang, Linjun Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDemand for dental emergency services has increased in recent years, creating an imbalance between service capacity and patient needs. To assess the status of dental emergency resources and provide insights for optimizing service allocation, this study examined the characteristics of visits to the largest dental emergency center in China.Materials and methodsThis retrospective, repeated, cross-sectional study analyzed 396,383 dental emergency visits across all age groups at Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, from 2011 to 2020. Medical records, including age, sex, time, day, and disease type, were collected and analyzed.ResultsA total of 206,006 male and 190,377 female patients were included in the study, with a sex ratio of 1.082:1. The most common age group was 19–39 years (35.25%), and infections and swelling were the most frequent emergencies (36.12%). Trauma was more prevalent in males (61.3%), whereas temporomandibular joint disease was more common in females (61.5%). The peak time period for significant differences in patient numbers was observed during the Chinese National Day Holiday compared to other holidays (p = 0.002), with similar trends observed for the Chinese Spring Festival (p = 0.049).ConclusionThis study provides a comprehensive overview of dental emergency visits to China’s largest center and highlights a substantial increase in cases during holidays, particularly the National Day Holiday. These findings support the need for optimizing emergency service allocation, including strategic dentist scheduling during peak periods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07219-0.

Introduction

Dental emergency services are essential components of the oral healthcare system, providing care for a wide range of acute oral conditions and injuries. With the advancement of the economy and the public awareness of oral health growing, the number of dental emergency visits has increased annually, placing greater demand on resource allocation and the workload of emergency practitioners. Research has shown that factors such as patient age, sex, time of visit, and disease type significantly influence patterns of dental emergency visits [1].

Efficient allocation of clinical resources is crucial for the smooth operation of dental emergency services and for delivering substantial benefits to both hospital management and patient care. Research indicates that optimized resource allocation enhances care quality, reduces patient waiting times, and improves overall satisfaction while alleviating operational costs for healthcare facilities [2]. These improvements are particularly critical in high-demand settings, where poor resource management can result in service bottlenecks, compromised patient outcomes, and reduced institutional efficiency.

Therefore, a systematic analysis of dental emergency data not only helps in understanding patient visit characteristics and addressing their chief complaints more effectively but also provides valuable insights for hospital management and the optimization of emergency services. The [institution name hidden] manages a substantial number of dental emergencies in Shanghai and the Yangtze River Delta region, with nearly 400,000 visits recorded over the past decade. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the ten-year trends in dental emergency visits at a major emergency center in Shanghai, China. The specific objectives were to (1) analyze temporal changes in patient demographics and disease categories (2), assess visit patterns during holidays and off-peak hours, and (3) explore the implications of these patterns for resource allocation and service planning.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a retrospective, repeated cross-sectional study analyzing emergency dental visits over a ten-year period (January 1, 2011, to December 31, 2020) at the Department of General Dentistry, [Institution name hidden], one of the largest 24-hour dental emergency centers in eastern China.

Data were collected independently for each calendar year without individual patient follow-ups, consistent with a repeated cross-sectional rather than a longitudinal or time-series design. Although temporal trends were analyzed descriptively and inferentially, the primary focus was on the cross-sectional characteristics observed each year rather than on modeling continuous time-based outcomes.

All electronic visit records were extracted from the hospital’s health information system (HIS) using structured database queries. The raw data were exported in Excel format and pre-processed using Microsoft Excel 2019 and IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A total of 396,384 dental emergency visits were identified. Each record was treated as an independent encounter, and repeat visits by the same individual could not be linked because of deidentification. Therefore, the unit of analysis was per visit, not per patient.

All 396,384 records extracted from the HIS database contained complete entries for key variables—visit date, age, sex, and diagnosis—and were retained for analysis without further exclusions. The inclusion criteria were: (1) emergency visits between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2020; (2) treatment provided in the Department of General Dentistry; and (3) a documented diagnosis by an emergency dentist. The participant flow process is summarized in a STROBE-compliant diagram (Appendix, Fig. 7).

Variable definitions

Age was categorized into six groups: two for minors (0–6 years for preschool children and 7–18 years for school-aged children) and four for adults (19–39, 40–59, 60–79, and ≥ 80 years).

Visit timing was classified according to calendar day and hour. Calendar days were grouped into three categories: weekdays, weekends, and public holidays. Public holidays were defined according to the official Chinese national holiday calendar published by the State Council and did not include half-day holidays. Long holidays refer to the Spring Festival and National Day holiday periods. Visit dates were matched against year-specific holiday schedules (2011–2020) to assign an appropriate calendar category for analysis.

The time of visit was based on the triage time (i.e., the time the patient was first registered in the emergency system), as it most accurately reflects actual patient demand. Time periods were divided according to the department’s operational scheduling and physician shift patterns. Time of day was categorized into four 6-hour bins (00:00–05:59, 06:00–11:59, 12:00–17:59, and 18:00–23:59) to align with physician shift rotations and to capture variations in patient flow across typical work, rest, and off-duty periods.

Diagnoses were grouped into nine major disease categories based on patients’ primary complaints and clinical diagnoses recorded in the electronic system: (1) hemorrhage (2), infection and swelling (3), trauma (4), toothache (5), temporomandibular joint disease (6), oral mucosal disease (7), salivary gland disease (8), periodontal disease, and (9) other conditions.

This classification scheme was adapted from previous literature and tailored to the clinical characteristics commonly observed in our emergency dental service. Although formal ICD-10 coding was not used, the classification was developed using consistent clinical definitions aligned with routine diagnostic patterns and supported by prior research.

Statistical analysis

RStudio (version 3.5.2, Posit, PBC, Boston, MA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) were used for the statistical analysis. Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) was used for descriptive summaries and percentage calculations. Patient records, including age, sex, time, day, and diagnosis, were analyzed to examine trends over time and across subgroups.

Categorical variables (e.g., disease type by sex or age group) were compared using the chi-square test. When expected cell counts were low, Fisher’s exact test was used as appropriate. Visit volumes across different holiday types or time periods were compared using the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Kruskal–Wallis test, depending on group structure and data distribution. For comparisons between two dependent groups (e.g., Spring Festival vs. National Day across matched years), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. The non-normality of the data was confirmed prior to the application of nonparametric methods. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, for selected binary outcomes, such as the prevalence of trauma or temporomandibular joint disorders by sex, odds ratios (ORs) and risk ratios (RRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated using 2 × 2 contingency tables. These analyses quantified the magnitude of association between sex and specific disease categories.

To control for potential confounding factors, stratified analyses were conducted based on patient characteristics such as age, sex, and visit timing (e.g., weekday vs. weekend, daytime vs. nighttime). The consistency of clinical workflows throughout the decade and the large sample size helped mitigate potential measurement bias. However, because this was a retrospective study using de-identified data, unmeasured confounders such as socioeconomic status and comorbidities could not be adjusted for, which remained a limitation of the study.

Results

Patients demographics

The patients included 206,006 males and 190,377 females. The sex ratio was 1.082:1. The most common age group was 19–39 years, including 139,714 (35.25%) patients, followed by 40–59 years, with 95,038 (23.98%) patients, and 60–79 years, with 77,268 (19.49%) patients. Based on disease type, the majority of emergencies were due to infection and swelling (143,178 patients, 36.12%), followed by toothache (89,825 patients, 22.66%) and trauma (81,776 patients, 20.63%) (Table 1).

Gender distribution of visits

No significant difference was found between the sexes in the total number of visits over the ten-year period. Among the different disease types, trauma was more common in males (50,096, 61.3%), and temporomandibular joint disease was more prevalent in females (3,602, 61.5%). Compared to females, males had significantly higher odds of presenting with trauma (OR = 1.61; 95% CI: 1.58–1.64) and a 46% higher risk (RR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.44–1.48). Conversely, females were more likely to present with temporomandibular joint disorders than males (OR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.54–0.61; RR = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.55–0.61). Among the different age groups, the majority of patients under 18 years of age were male (43,123, 61.0%), and 56.8% of patients over 80 years of age were female (see Appendix, Figure 1).

There was an overall upward trend in the number of visits across age groups during the ten years. Temporal trends

The number of visits at different times also showed a steady upward trend over the ten years, while visits in the 18:00–23:59 time group decreased significantly in 2017, which may have been related to the opening of a craniomaxillofacial specialist emergency department that redirected trauma cases during the night hours (Appendix Figure 2). To further quantify this overall temporal trend, Poisson regression models were fitted to the total annual visit counts. As shown in Appendix Figure 3, the full-sample Poisson model indicated a significant annual increase in emergency visits (p < 0.001). Additionally, a segmented Poisson regression, excluding 2020 data due to pandemic-related confounding, suggested a marked change in slope after 2017, consistent with the structural shifts in service delivery introduced by the new specialty department. This pattern was also observed in nighttime visits (18:00–23:59). Segmented linear regression showed a significant upward trend before 2017 (slope = + 1,723 visits/year, p = 0.001), followed by a non-significant level drop in 2017 (− 1,807 visits, p = 0.36) and a significant decline in slope thereafter (− 1,602 visits/year, p = 0.049), highlighting the temporal shift in evening patient volume due to departmental reorganization (Appendix Figure 4).

Holiday trends

During 7-day or longer holidays, there were significant differences in the number of patients between the National Day holiday and other holidays, excluding the National Day Holiday (p = 0.002), as well as between the National Day Holiday and non-holiday days (p = 0.002) (Fig. 2). The Spring Festival and National Day Holiday were similar in volume, but significant differences were found between the Spring Festival and holidays excluding the Spring Festival (p = 0.049), and between the Spring Festival and non-holiday days (p = 0.004)(Fig. 3). The number of visits between the National Day Holiday and the Spring Festival was also significantly different, with a p-value of 0.002 (Fig. 4). To address the risk of Type I error inflation due to multiple comparisons among holiday periods, the Bonferroni correction was applied to five pre-specified pairwise tests. While the reference groups for “other holidays” differed slightly across comparisons (e.g., excluding National Day vs. excluding Spring Festival), a unified correction was conservatively applied across all p-values. Disease composition

There was a noticeable upward trend in the total number of visits from 2011 to 2020, with peaks in 2016 and 2019 associated with the opening of craniomaxillofacial specialist emergency departments. The number of patients with infections, swelling, and hemorrhage increased annually, highlighting their growing contribution to the overall composition of emergency cases. Conversely, the number of trauma patients significantly declined in 2017, closely related to the diversion of craniofacial emergencies (Appendix Fig. 5). A segmented regression analysis was conducted to further quantify this trend. From 2011 to 2016, trauma-related visits increased steadily by approximately 1,365 cases per year (p < 0.001). However, after the opening of the craniomaxillofacial emergency department in 2017, there was a sharp decline of 9,631 visits at the breakpoint (p < 0.001), with a continued annual decrease of approximately 1,386 cases thereafter (p < 0.001). This model (adjusted R² = 0.98) highlighted a significant shift in trauma visit patterns, consistent with service-level structural changes. Appendix Fig. 6 shows the segmented regression model.

Discussion

This study demonstrated a gradual increase in the total number of dental emergency visits over the past decade, correlating with the growing permanent population of Shanghai and increased awareness of oral health issues. Notably, significant declines were observed in 2017 and 2020, which were closely linked to the establishment of a craniomaxillofacial emergency department at [institution name hidden] and the global pandemic. Craniomaxillofacial emergency departments primarily treat patients with facial and neck injuries, thereby reducing the burden on dental emergency services, particularly during evening hours.

The age group with the highest number of DE visits was 19–39 years, accounting for 35.25%. This finding aligns with a report by Kim et al. from South Korea, which identified two leading age groups as 20–29 and 30–39 years [3]. In addition, Verma et al. showed that the peak age of patients with dental presentations was the 26–30 years group [4]. This trend may be related to the prevalence of caries and pericoronitis in this demographic [5], which also had a higher proportion of females (53.1%), possibly due to hormonal changes during pregnancy that predispose them to oral diseases [6]. Patients aged 19–39 often have limited opportunities to seek dental care during working hours. Of the total number of visits, males constituted a larger proportion (52.0%), consistent with other research findings [7, 8]. Among minors (ages 0–6 and 7–18), males constituted over 61.0% of visits, with trauma accounting for 58.3%, likely reflecting sex-related behavioral differences in contact sports and adventure activities [9].

The peak time for emergency visits was between 18:00 and 23:59, representing 50.77% of total visits, coinciding with the closure of outpatient services during this period. The average peak visit time throughout the year was from 19:00 to 23:00, whereas visits were minimal between 04:00 and 07:00. Huang et al. noted that the peak visit time in Taiwan ranged from 22:00 to 24:00, with a low point from 04:00 to 07:00, which aligns with our findings [10]. During holidays, the peak visit time shifted to 09:00–11:00, likely due to the closure of outpatient services throughout the day, leading the public to seek emergency care after waking up. In 2017, there was a significant decline in patient visits during the 18:00–23:59 time slot. This decrease can be attributed to the establishment of craniomaxillofacial emergency departments, which divert patients presenting with trauma. A substantial proportion of these patients (47.68%) sought care between 18:00 and 23:59. Hospital management should consider increasing staff during nighttime and holiday hours to better accommodate peak emergency demand.

In our study, the leading causes of dental emergency visits were infections and swelling (36.12%), trauma (20.63%), and toothache (22.66%), while hemorrhage, temporomandibular joint disease, oral mucosal disease, salivary gland disease, and periodontal disease collectively accounted for 11.09%. Bae et al. reported that up to 66% of patients with DE in South Korea presented with dental trauma [11]. In Taiwan, Huang et al. found that 22.2% of DE cases were caused by trauma [10], which was the second most common type of disease in our study. The relatively low proportion of trauma cases in our data may be attributed to the establishment of a craniomaxillofacial emergency department at the study site. Optimizing the scheduling system to increase the proportion of trauma-trained clinicians could further enhance care delivery and ensure timely, specialized management of such injuries. Infections and swelling, the most prevalent disease categories, are associated not only with postoperative complications from dental procedures, such as tooth extractions or implants, but also with prolonged appointment intervals for oral treatments. Some patients with pulpitis, apical periodontitis, or pericoronitis develop swelling in the oral and maxillofacial regions due to delays in receiving timely and comprehensive care [12].

The results indicate a significant increase in visit volume during holidays compared with weekdays (p < 0.05), particularly during extended holidays such as National Day (p = 0.002) and the Spring Festival (p = 0.004). Significant differences were also observed between National Day and the Spring Festival (p = 0.002) and between National Day and other holidays (p = 0.002). The disparity between these two holidays may be influenced by traditional Chinese cultural practices, in which people tend to complete dental checkups and treatments before the Spring Festival to avoid seeking care during the holiday period. The seven-day National Day holiday may provide working individuals with more opportunities to address oral health issues, thereby increasing their likelihood of seeking care.

This study was conducted at a major tertiary referral center, Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, which may introduce referral bias, as the patient population likely includes more complex or severe cases referred from other institutions. Although the hospital is not the only 24-hour dental emergency provider in the region, it receives the overwhelming majority of emergency visits owing to its clinical capacity, strong reputation, and central location. As such, the data reflect dominant regional patterns but may not be fully generalizable to smaller or community-based emergency centers.

In addition to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, service-level changes, such as the opening of a dedicated craniomaxillofacial emergency department in 2017, likely contributed to the observed decline in trauma-related visits. Notably, this department assumed responsibility only for head and neck trauma cases, which did not affect the triage or management of other dental emergency conditions. Therefore, the decline in the trauma volume likely reflects referral diversion, whereas trends in other disease categories remained stable. These targeted changes represent potential confounding factors for specific diagnostic groups and should be considered when interpreting temporal patterns.

Future studies should consider applying stratified analyses or interrupted time-series models to better isolate the effects of such interventions. Moreover, broader demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors such as comorbidities and treatment outcomes were not captured in this study. Expanding data sources and leveraging artificial intelligence [13] based decision support tools or remote triage systems [14] may help address these limitations and enhance emergency service planning.

Building on these findings, future efforts could focus on reducing unnecessary emergency visits through improved care continuity. Some dental emergencies may stem from delayed treatment, inadequate post-procedural monitoring, or limited access to timely outpatient services. Encouraging regular dental check-ups, implementing simple follow-up reminders, enhancing post-treatment follow-up and monitoring treatment completion, and expanding access to tele-dental consultations could help patients address minor problems before they become urgent [15, 16]. In addition, promoting preventive oral health education within community-based dental services may relieve pressure on emergency departments and contribute to more efficient utilization of healthcare resources.

Conclusion

The results of this study offer valuable insights into the patterns of dental emergency visits in Shanghai’s largest dental emergency center, highlighting peak visit times, seasonal trends, and the distribution of disease types. These findings emphasize the importance of optimizing resource allocation, particularly during peak hours and holidays, to improve the overall quality and efficiency of dental emergency services.
