Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR)-verified autoxidation and hydrolysis induce lipid degradation in pan-fried hand-grasp pancakes
Authors: Pengyuan Wei, Jingjing Lun, Yihang Ding, Jinying Wang, Linshang Zhang, Cong Sun, Kaizhou Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of pan-frying on fat quality in hand-grasp pancakes. The results demonstrated that fats exhibited significant quality deterioration: acid value increased by 50%–85%, peroxide value rose by 175%–490%, and p-anisidine value elevated by 65%–430%. The increments of conjugated diene value and conjugated triene value ranged from 20% to 50% and 35% to 73%, respectively. Meanwhile, the DPPH radical scavenging capacity decreased by 3%–30%, indicating a remarkable decline in antioxidant activity. Statistical analysis revealed consistent compositional changes in post-frying fats: the average free fatty acid (FFA) content in fats of the six brands of hand-grasp pancakes increased by 113.04%, whereas the average contents of unsaturated fatty acid (UFA) and unsaturated triacylglycerol (UUU) decreased by 1.81% and 4.82%, respectively. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements confirmed the presence of alkyl radicals (R•) in pancake fats verifying that autooxidation served as the primary oxidation pathway of fats in hand-grasp pancakes. This study provides theoretical foundations for the development of dedicated fats and quality improvement of hand-grasp pancake products.Keywords: Hand-grasp pancakes, Pan-frying, Basic physicochemical indicators, Fat oxidation, Free radicals, EPR

Introduction

Hand-grasp pancakes are a characteristic, traditional snack in China. Featuring crispy, layered texture and simple cooking procedures, they can be served together with fried eggs, milk, and other ingredients as a nutritious breakfast, making them one of the popular fast foods favored by consumers amid fast-paced lifestyles. The global frozen pancake market was valued at 1.133 billion US dollars in 2025, reflecting a broad consumer market. Hand-grasp pancakes constitute a complex food matrix, mixed with fats, starch, protein, and water. Fats account for 10%–20% of the total composition (Wang et al., 2025) and serve as the key component responsible for the golden color, attractive flavor, and crispy texture of hand-grasp pancakes (Renzetti et al., 2016).

Pan-frying is the common cooking method for hand-grasp pancakes, at home and in catering establishments. During pan-frying, the shortening in hand-grasp pancakes melts and the internal moisture is released into fats, which may trigger fat hydrolysis. Oxidation reactions also take place in fats throughout pan-frying; meanwhile, Maillard reactions occur via the interaction between fats, starch, and proteins. Accordingly, the pan-frying system of hand-grasp pancakes is a complex, multiphase system featuring phase transformation between solid and liquid fats, coexistence of water and fats, rapid temperature rise, and uneven heating. As one of the dominant reactions occurring in this system, fat oxidation is affected by multiple factors and generates distinctive, characteristic products. The types of reactions undergone by fats and the corresponding variations in fat compositions during the pan-frying of hand-grasp pancakes are scientific questions worthy of in-depth investigation, and the relevant research can provide guiding implications for residents' health.

At present, studies on fat oxidation behaviors mainly focus on pure fat systems, food frying systems, and the influence of antioxidant addition on frying systems. In particular, numerous investigations have explored the quality variations of frying oil induced by different fried food materials, as such research is more consistent with the practical scenarios of fat utilization in daily life. For instance, Hu et al. studied the effects of frying French fries on soybean oil quality. Their results indicated that the triacylglycerol content of soybean oil decreased to 75% of that in fresh oil after 60 h of frying, and the content of total polar compounds reached 27% after 56 h of frying (Hu et al., 2025). In another study concerning the impact of fried fish cakes on the quality of high-oleic sunflower oil, Luo et al. found that the peroxide value declined within the initial 3 h and then remained stable, maintaining a dynamic equilibrium from 3 h to 24 h, while the p-anisidine value rose continuously with the extension of frying time (Luo et al., 2024). Nevertheless, few reports have addressed the fat variations within the complex food matrix of hand-grasp pancakes. In this study, commercially available hand-grasp pancakes were purchased, and a household pan-frying process was simulated to treat the samples. Fats were extracted from hand-grasp pancakes before and after pan-frying, and the basic physicochemical indicators and compositions of these fats were analyzed to evaluate the influence of fat oxidation on the quality of hand-grasp pancakes during pan-frying.

EPR is a method for determining free radicals in a system by detecting unpaired electrons. Short-lived free-radical intermediates formed at the early oxidation stage can be captured via spin-trapping technology (Barba et al., 2020). EPR enables early-stage evaluation of oxidation extent, and has been applied in fields including oxidation stability and shelf-life assessment of edible oils (Jiang et al., 2020), in-situ monitoring of free-radical dynamics during frying processes (Chen et al., 2017), and efficacy evaluation of natural and synthetic antioxidants (Cui et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2026). Compared with the determination of oxidation products at the late oxidation stage by conventional physicochemical indicators, EPR can assess the early-stage fat oxidation and reveal the oxidation mechanism by monitoring variations in free radicals (Joaqu et al., 2020). Based on the determination of physicochemical indicators and combined with EPR measurement, this study detects the presence of free radicals generated during fat oxidation, and explores the formation rule of free radical chain reactions of fats in hand-grasp pancakes throughout pan-frying, so as to assess the effect of pan-frying on fat oxidation in hand-grasp pancakes. This research provides theoretical foundations for the development of more nutritious and healthy dedicated fats for hand-grasp pancakes.

Materials and methods

Materials

Six commercial brands of hand-grasp pancakes were all purchased from official online stores. N-Hexane and isopropanol were supplied by Fuyu Chemical Co., Ltd., Glacial acetic acid, potassium iodide (KI), potassium hydroxide (KOH), and anhydrous sodium sulfate (anhydrous Na₂SO₄) were obtained from Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Chloroform, diethyl ether, and toluene were bought from Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. Sodium methoxide, DPPH, and Trolox were acquired from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and Aladdin (Shanghai, China), respectively.

Extraction of fats from hand-grasp pancakes

For pan-frying treatment, raw, hand-grasp pancake doughs were baked in a SUPOR electric pancake griddle for 4–5 min. The fat extraction procedure for pancakes, before and after pan-frying, was performed according to Zhang Kaibo (Kaibo, 2023). Briefly, raw pancake doughs were pre-frozen for 24 h and subsequently lyophilized at −55 °C for another 24 h in a freeze dryer. The dried samples were crushed with a pulverizer, and the resulting powder was wrapped in qualitative filter paper for Soxhlet extraction, using n-hexane as the extraction solvent. After extraction, n-hexane was removed via rotary evaporation to obtain crude fats.

Physicochemical indicators

Acid value and peroxide value were determined according to the official AOCS methods Cd 3d-63 and Cd 8-53, respectively.

p-Anisidine value: The determination was performed following the method reported by Holler (Martina et al., 2023). The sample was heated to 10 °C above its melting point for dissolution. A 1–2 g aliquot was weighed and diluted to 25 mL with isooctane. Reaction and measurement: Unreacted solution: 5 mL of sample solution mixed with 1 mL of glacial acetic acid; Reaction solution: 5 mL of sample solution mixed with 1 mL of p-anisidine reagent; Blank solution: 5 mL of isooctane mixed with 1 mL of p-anisidine reagent. All three solutions were kept in the dark at 23 °C ± 3 °C for 8 min, and their absorbances A₀, A₁ and A₂ were measured at 350 nm, respectively. The p-anisidine value was calculated as shown in Eq. (1), where m is the mass of the sample, and 1.2 is the correction factor for the test solution diluted with 1 mL of p-anisidine reagent or glacial acetic acid.

Total oxidation value: The calculation was slightly modified according to Ahmadi(Ahmadi, Ghavami, Rashidi, Gharachorloo and Nateghi, 2024) and ISO 6885:2016, as shown in Eq. (2).

Conjugated diene value and conjugated triene value: The determination was carried out following the method described by Chen (Jia et al., 2022). The sample was heated to 10 °C above its melting point for dissolution. A 0.05 g aliquot was weighed and diluted to 25 mL with isooctane. Using isooctane as blank, the absorbance was measured at 232 nm and 268 nm with a 1 cm path-length cuvette. The conjugated diene value and conjugated triene value were calculated as shown in Eq. (3), where Aλ refers to the absorbance of the sample at 232 nm and 268 nm, and c is the mass concentration of the sample in g/100 mL.

Determination of DPPH radical scavenging capacity

Sample Preparation: Weigh 0.2–0.5 g of oil sample, dissolve it with ethyl acetate, and fix the volume to 10 mL in a volumetric flask. The measurement method for DPPH radical scavenging capacity was referenced from Xie Jiarong (She et al., 2024), with minor modifications, and detailed procedures are provided in the supplementary materials.

Determination of glyceride compositions

A Shimadzu GC-2010 Pro gas chromatograph was employed to determine the glyceride compositions of oil samples. The detailed parameters were as follows: chromatographic column, DB-1HT (30.0 m × 0.25 mm × 0.10 μm); injector temperature, 350 °C; detector temperature, 350 °C; split ratio, 10:1; temperature programming: initial temperature held at 50 °C, raised to 180 °C at a rate of 25 °C/min, then increased to 230 °C at 7 °C/min, further elevated to 350 °C at 45 °C/min, and maintained for 14 min; hydrogen flow rate, 40 mL/min; injection volume, 1.0 μL(Yufei et al., 2025).

Determination of fatty acid compositions

An Agilent 8860 gas chromatograph was used to analyze fatty acid compositions. Simple methyl esterification was carried out according to AOCS Ce 2-66 (2017), Preparation of Methyl Esters of Fatty Acids. The chromatographic column adopted was SGE HP-88 (100.0 m × 0.25 mm × 0.2 μm); injector temperature: 260 °C; detector temperature: 260 °C; split ratio: 50:1; temperature programming: initial temperature maintained at 140 °C for 5 min, then ramped up to 240 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min, and held for 25 min; injection volume: 1 μL (Wang et al., 2025).

Determination of triacylglycerol compositions

An Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph was utilized to analyze the triacylglycerol compositions of oil samples, and the detailed analytical procedures were conducted according to the method reported by Zhang, Linshang (Linshang et al., 2023). A high-temperature gas chromatograph, model GC-7890B, manufactured by Agilent Technologies (USA), was adopted. The gas chromatographic parameters were referenced from Reference (Zhang et al., 2025), with a CP-TAP CB chromatographic column (25 m × 0.25 mm × 0.1 μm); injector temperature: 340 °C; detector temperature: 350 °C; split ratio: 1:10; temperature programming: the initial temperature was set at 50 °C, increased to 280 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C/min, and held for 2.5 min, then ramped up to the final temperature of 350 °C at 10 °C/min; injection volume: 1.0 μL.

Determination of alkyl radicals in fats of hand-grasp pancakes via EPR

EPR tests for free radicals were carried out using an EPR spectrometer (Model EMXplus-6/1, Bruker, Germany). The oil sample was melted in a water bath at 50–60 °C, and 200 μL of the molten oil was transferred into an NMR tube, followed by the addition of 20 μL DMPO solution (dissolved in toluene, with a final concentration of 500 mmol/L). After thorough mixing, the NMR tube was placed into the resonator cavity of the EPR instrument, preheated to the target temperature for testing. The measurement procedure was referenced from Zhang, Qing (Zhang et al., 2026). The instrumental detection parameters of the EPR spectrometer were set as follows: central magnetic field of 3510.00 G, sweep width of 100.00 G, sweep time of 30.00 s, microwave power of 6.325 mW, modulation amplitude of 1.000 G, conversion time of 10.00 ms, heating temperature of 373.15 K, and high-purity nitrogen flow protection. The Xenon software suite (Bruker BioSpin) was adopted for EPR spectrum acquisition, processing, and simulation, and spectral fitting was performed via its integrated SpinFit module.

Statistical analysis

All determinations were performed in triplicate, and the experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was adopted to calculate significance levels at p < 0.05, and comparative tests were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 software for statistical analysis. Origin 2024 was used for graph plotting.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical indicators

The acid value is an indicator used to evaluate the amount of free fatty acids in fats. Free fatty acids are one of the primary products formed during the hydrolytic rancidity of fats. Fig. 1(A) shows that the acid values of fats extracted from six brands of hand-grasp pancakes increased by 50%–85% after pan-frying. This phenomenon may be attributed to the hydrolysis reaction that occurs when water molecules attack the ester chains of triacylglycerols in fats during pan-frying. This reaction generates free fatty acids (Dana et al., 2003; Kuek et al., 2020). Additionally, since hand-grasp pancakes are flour-based products, internal moisture seeps out of the dough during pan-frying and comes into contact with the fats. The hydrolysis reaction intensifies under high-temperature pan-frying conditions, accelerating the rate at which free fatty acids are cleaved from the glycerol backbone. This leads to a remarkable rise in free fatty acid content and acid value (Peri & Saguy, 2015). Fats with high levels of free fatty acids have adverse effects on human metabolism (Guan et al., 2026).

(A) acid value; (B) peroxide value; (C) p-anisidine value; (D) total oxidation value; (E) conjugated diene value; (F) conjugated triene value. BF: before pan-frying; AF: after pan-frying.

Peroxide value is an indicator for evaluating the primary oxidation degree of fats, which mainly reflects the content of monohydroperoxides (ROOH). Fat oxidation occurs in sequential stages: ROOH and polyperoxy compounds are generated in the primary stage, whereas hydroperoxides decompose to form secondary oxidation products, including aldehydes and ketones, in the secondary stage. After pan-frying, the peroxide values of fats from six brands of hand-grasp pancakes all increased to different extents, with the growth rate ranging from 175% to 490%, demonstrating that primary oxidation took place and a large amount of ROOH accumulated in fats of hand-grasp pancakes during pan-frying. This phenomenon may result from the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids in fats under high temperature, and the intrinsic high moisture content in hand-grasp pancakes accelerates the oxidation process (Chen et al., 2017). Fig. 1(B) shows that Brand 6 had the highest growth rate of peroxide value, at 488%, meaning the maximum accumulation of ROOH. It is speculated that this may be related to the high content of unsaturated fatty acids in its raw fats. Unsaturated fatty acids contain unsaturated double bonds or triple bonds, and are more prone to oxidation compared with saturated fatty acids (Eraci et al., 2022). The peroxide value of Brand 3 only increased by 175%. It is presumed that suitable antioxidants were added to its shortening. Antioxidants can serve as hydrogen atom or electron donors, or have the ability to trap free radicals, which generates more stable phenolic radical intermediates, blocks the free radical chain reaction of fat oxidation, and thus inhibits the oxidation of fats in hand-grasp pancakes during high-temperature pan-frying (Zhang et al., 2024).

The p-anisidine value (p-AnV) is an indicator for quantifying the yield of secondary oxidative decomposition products generated during fat oxidation. Compared with hydroperoxides produced in primary oxidation, small-molecule substances such as aldehydes and ketones among secondary oxidative decomposition products are not readily degraded under high temperature (Jia et al., 2022; Ya et al., 2022). Therefore, p-AnV can accurately reflect the degree of advanced fat oxidation. Fig. 1(C) shows that the p-AnV values of the six varieties of hand-grasp pancakes increased by 50%–450% after pan-frying, which indicated the formation of aldehyde and ketone compounds throughout pan-frying. Among all samples, Brand 5 presented the maximum increase of p-AnV, at 430.5%. It is inferred that the proportion of PUFA in the fatty acid composition of the base fats of its shortening was higher than the average PUFA content in other brands of hand-grasp pancakes. More unsaturated bond structures and abundant active hydrogen atoms adjacent to double bonds existed in the fat system, making the fats more vulnerable to attack by free radicals and oxidation, thereby leading to a sharp rise in p-Anisidine Value (Kusumoto et al., 2024).

Total oxidation value can comprehensively reflect the accumulation of primary oxidation products and secondary oxidation products and evaluate the oxidation degree of fats in a more holistic manner. Fig. 1(D) shows that the total oxidation values of fats extracted from the six brands of hand-grip pancakes increased by 130%–370% after pan-frying. Brand 6 exhibited the maximum increment of total oxidation value, at 366%, while Brand 4 had the minimum increment, of merely 133%. Such discrepancies may stem from the distinct triacylglycerol compositions of fats adopted by different brands of hand-grasp pancakes (Rudzińska et al., 2018). Fats with a higher proportion of unsaturated triacylglycerols are prone to severer oxidation under high-temperature pan-frying conditions compared with those rich in fully saturated triacylglycerols.

Conjugated diene value and conjugated triene value are indicators used to assess fat oxidation degree by measuring the variation of conjugated double bonds formed during fat oxidation. During oxidation, the free radical intermediates of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) contained in fats undergo rearrangement to produce ROOH structures containing conjugated dienes and conjugated trienes. Monounsaturated fatty acids with two double bonds (such as linoleic acid) and polyunsaturated fatty acids with three or more double bonds (such as linolenic acid) in fats are key substances for generating conjugated double bond structures and conjugated triene structures, respectively (Dahle, 1962; Takajo et al., 2007). Fig. 1(E) and (F) show that both the conjugated diene value (K232) and conjugated triene value (K268) of fats from six brands of hand-grasp pancakes increased remarkably after pan-frying, with growth rates ranging from 20% to 50% and 29% to 70%, respectively. This reveals that the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids in fats of hand-grasp pancakes generated ROOH with conjugated double bond structures during pan-frying. The growth rates of K232 and K268 of Brand 4 were only 22% and 35%, respectively, indicating a relatively mild oxidation degree of its unsaturated fatty acids.

DPPH radical scavenging rate

Antioxidants in fats possess certain scavenging capacity against DPPH•. Fats inherently contain trace natural antioxidants, such as vitamin E and sterols. In addition, exogenous antioxidants, including tert-butyl hydroquinone (TBHQ), butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA), and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), may be incorporated during processing. These antioxidants are capable of donating hydrogen protons, which enables them to bind to free radicals generated in oxidative chain reactions during fat oxidation, block the chain reactions, and restrain fat oxidation (Fedko et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Fig. 2 shows that the DPPH radical scavenging rates of fats extracted from hand-grasp cakes declined to varying degrees after pan-frying, which can be explained by two factors. On the one hand, high temperature during pan-frying accelerates fat oxidation, leading to the rapid consumption of antioxidants. On the other hand, natural and exogenous antioxidants decompose under high-temperature conditions (Wu et al., 2019). The combined effect of these two factors reduces the antioxidant content in fats of pan-fried, hand-grasp pancakes, weakening the antioxidant capacity and DPPH radical scavenging capacity accordingly.

Glyceride compositions

Table 1 shows that the initial free fatty acid (FFA) content of fats from six brands of hand-grasp pancakes differed after pan-frying. Fig. 3 shows that the average FFA content in fats of hand-grasp pancakes increased significantly after pan-frying, with an increase of 113.04%. This result indicates that triacylglycerols (TAG) underwent hydrolysis to produce FFAs under high-temperature pan-frying conditions, which accounts for the remarkable growth in acid value of fats from all brands of hand-grasp pancakes after pan-frying (Chandran & Manickam, 2026). The average monoglyceride (MG) content in fats of hand-grasp pancakes increased by 7.35%, while the average diglyceride (DG) and TAG content in fats of hand-grasp pancakes decreased by 2.72% and 0.12%, respectively, with small magnitudes of change. Previous studies have reported that the contents of MG and DG are not constant under high-temperature heating, but maintain a dynamic equilibrium. During TAG hydrolysis, the concentrations of MG and DG increase slightly. Nevertheless, continuous oxidation reactions cause DG to decompose into aldehydes and ketones, and MG to break down into short-chain aldehydes (such as hexanal), ketones, and other compounds, ultimately triggering variations in oxidative indicators (Hu et al., 2023).

Fatty acid compositions

Fats undergo oxidation reactions during heating and pan-frying. At the micro-component level, oxidation mainly takes place in fatty acids (Bazina et al., 2025). Therefore, measuring the fatty acid compositions of fats extracted from hand-grasp pancakes before and after pan-frying can reflect the influence of fat oxidation on the quality of hand-grasp pancakes during pan-frying. Fig. 4 shows that the average unsaturated fatty acid (UFA) content in fats of the six brands of hand-grasp pancakes decreased by 1.81% after pan-frying. Table 2 shows that Brands 5 and 6 exhibited relatively large reductions in UFA content, which were mainly caused by the oxidative consumption of linoleic acid (C18:2) and linolenic acid (C18:3). Overall, the average linoleic acid and linolenic acid contents in fats of the six brands of hand-grasp pancakes decreased by 9.94% and 25.23%, respectively, after pan-frying. This observation agrees with the findings reported by Luo (Luo et al., 2024), who found that the linoleic acid content in palm fats dropped from 15.96% to 11.30% when frying fish cakes. Unsaturated fatty acids carry one or more double bonds, and hydrogen atoms next to double bonds are highly active and easily trigger oxidative chain reactions. Thus, UFAs are oxidized and consumed prior to saturated fatty acids(Chen et al., 2025). It can be inferred that differences in the UFA contents of fats from different brands of hand-grasp pancakes affect the extent of fat oxidation during pan-frying. Considering the nutritional and health values of unsaturated fatty acids (Yao et al., 2023), fats with smaller reductions in UFA contents after pan-frying better satisfy consumers' requirements for nutritional health.

Triacylglycerol compositions

The structures of TAG are closely related to the oxidative stabilities of fats, the generation of oxidation products, and general physicochemical properties (Zeb, 2012). The positional distributions of different fatty acids on glycerol backbones affect the degree of oxidation when triacylglycerols are exposed to oxygen and high temperatures. Triacylglycerols with high unsaturation levels exhibit faster oxidation rates during pan-frying, compared with fully saturated triacylglycerols.Comprehensive analysis of Table 3 and Fig. 5 shows that, overall, the average UUU content in fats of the six brands of hand-grasp pancakes declined by 4.82% after pan-frying. The variations in the types and contents of triacylglycerols among different brands may stem from different base fats adopted and whether modification treatments via interesterification were applied during processing (Yang et al., 2025). Intramolecular and intermolecular interesterification can alter the compositions and structures of triacylglycerols, enrich the types of triacylglycerols in fats, and adjust the contents of triacylglycerols (Sivakanthan & Madhujith, 2020), thereby regulating the oxidation behaviors of fats.

Verification of alkyl radicals

Autoxidation of fats proceeds via a free radical chain reaction. Lipid oxidation initiates when free radicals abstract labile hydrogen atoms from UFAs to form alkyl radicals (R·). Subsequently, R· reacts with oxygen to generate peroxyl radicals (ROO·), and ROO· reacts with unsaturated fat substrates to produce ROOH and new R· for chain propagation. ROOH is unstable; cleavage of its −O − O− bond decomposes it into alkyl radicals (R·) and hydroxyl radicals (·OH), sustaining the cycle of free radical chain reactions (Choe & Min, 2006). Ultimately, β-scission of RO· generates volatile secondary products, including aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols, which raises the p-anisidine values of fats (Aladedunye & Dellaporta, 2025). Therefore, fats extracted from Brand 5 hand-grasp pancakes, which exhibited the largest difference in p-anisidine values before and after pan-frying, were selected for EPR measurements to verify the presence of free radicals during fat oxidation.

Fig. 6 shows that free radical signals were detected in fats of Brand 5 hand-grasp pancakes, both before and after pan-frying. Spectral fitting analysis confirmed that the radical signal peak corresponded to alkyl radicals (R·). This indicates that autoxidative free radical chain reactions proceeded continuously within the fat systems of hand-grasp pancakes, regardless of pan-frying treatment. Continuous generation of ROOH via free radical chain reactions elevated peroxide values, while the decomposition of ROOH into aldehydes, ketones, and other compounds increased p-anisidine values. This trend was consistent with the growth of conjugated diene and triene values after pan-frying. Neither RO· nor ROO· signals were detected in the systems. A plausible explanation is that the chain reactions of fat oxidation proceed extremely rapidly under high-temperature pan-frying. As intermediate free radical species, RO· and ROO· are quickly consumed to drive subsequent chain reactions. Their extremely short lifetimes and low steady-state concentrations make them difficult to be effectively trapped by spin traps (Zhang et al., 2026).

Conclusion

Simulating household pan-frying conditions, basic physicochemical indicators and fat compositions of fats extracted from six commercially available brands of hand-grasp pancakes formulated with palm-based shortenings as the main ingredient were determined and analyzed before and after pan-frying. The results revealed that pan-frying exerted profound, adverse effects on the quality of fats in hand-grasp pancakes. Cross-verified trends among acid values and glyceride compositions, peroxide values and fatty acid profiles, as well as peroxide values and triacylglycerol compositions, demonstrated that the relative contents of free fatty acids in fats increased remarkably under high-temperature pan-frying, leading to elevated acid values. Oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids brought about higher peroxide values and a reduction in the relative content of unsaturated triacylglycerols. The increases in p-anisidine values, conjugated diene values, and conjugated triene values after pan-frying further illustrated that the molecular structures of unsaturated fatty acids in fats were altered by oxidation during high-temperature pan-frying, generating conjugated olefin structures. Meanwhile, oxidation triggered the accumulation of secondary oxidation products, such as aldehydes and ketones. EPR measurements confirmed the existence of alkyl radicals (·R) in the fats of hand-grasp pancakes, proving that the oxidation of fats during pan-frying followed the general mechanism of fat autoxidation, namely, the free radical chain reactions consisting of chain initiation, chain propagation, and chain termination. The differences in fat quality among different brands of hand-grasp pancakes essentially stem from the varied blending ratios of base oils within palm-based shortenings. Some products may incorporate auxiliary fats such as lard and soybean oil to further modify the initial fatty acid and triacylglycerol compositions of fats, which in turn affects the degree of oxidative deterioration of fats during high-temperature pan-frying. The variation patterns of fat quality in hand-grasp pancakes during pan-frying revealed by this study can provide references for the formula improvement of fats used for quick-frozen hand-grasp pancakes: when palm-based fats are selected as base oils, the proportions of UFA and unsaturated triacylglycerols in the system can be reasonably controlled by adjusting the blending ratio of palm stearin and palm olein. Meanwhile, antioxidant adjuvants can be supplemented to enhance the antioxidant capacity, thereby mitigating the oxidative deterioration of fats under household pan-frying conditions, possess certain practical reference significance.
