Untangling Sample and Population Level Estimands in Bayesian Causal Inference
\name Arman Oganisian \email arman_oganisian@brown.edu

Abstract
Bayesian inference for causal estimands has been growing in popularity, however many misconceptions and implementation errors arise from conflating sample and population-level estimands and inference. We have anecdotally witnessed these at conference talks, in the course of peer review service, and even in published and arXiv-ed papers. Our goal here is to elucidate the crucial differences between sample and population-level quantities when it comes to identification, modeling, Bayesian computation, and interpretation. For example, common sample-level estimands require cross-world assumptions for identification, whereas common population-level estimands do not. Similarly, the former requires explicit imputation of counterfactuals from their joint posterior, whereas the latter typically only requires a posterior distribution over parameters. We start by defining some examples of both types of estimands, then discuss the full joint posterior over all unknowns (both missing counterfactuals and population distribution parameters). We continue to outline how inference for different estimands are derived from different marginals of this joint posterior. Because the differences are conceptually subtle but can be practically substantial, we provide an illustration of using synthetic data in Stan 1 1 1 https://github.com/stablemarkets/untangle . We also provide a detailed appendix with derivations and computational tips along with a discussion of common implementation errors. The overarching message here is to always engage in first-principles thinking about which marginal of the joint posterior is of interest in a particular application, then follow the strict logic of Bayes’ theorem and probability to avoid common implementation errors.



Keywords:

Bayesian inference, causal inference, missing data

1
Data Structure and notation

Throughout, we assume familiarity with basic causal inference concepts in the potential outcomes framework and Bayesian probability calculations in the point-treatment setting. We consider a setting with an observed continuous, real-valued outcome,
Y_{i}
, for subject
i=1,2,\dots,n
, a continuous real-valued confounder
L_{i}
, and a binary treatment indicator
A_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. We let
Y_{i}(a)
denote the potential outcome for subject
i
under treatment
a\in\{0,1\}
. Throughout we use capital letters to denote random variables and lowercase to denote realized values. We assume the following: stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA),
Y=(1-A)Y(0)+AY(1)
; joint conditional exchangeability,
Y(1),Y(0)\perp A\mid L
; and positivity,
P(A=1\mid L=l)>0
for all
l
for which
f_{L}(l)>0
. Under SUTVA, each subject’s observed outcome is their
factual
potential outcome
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(a_{i})
. Similarly, their
counterfactual
potential outcome is missing, which we denote as
Y_{i}^{M}=Y_{i}(1-a_{i})
. We let
\bm{Y}^{M}=(Y_{1}^{M},Y_{2}^{M},\dots,Y_{n}^{M})
denote the full set of counterfactuals. The observed data is denoted by
D^{O}=\{y_{i}(a_{i}),a_{i},l_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, which contains the
factual
potential outcome,
y_{i}(a_{i})
, under observed treatment assignment
a_{i}
. Equivalently,
D^{O}=\{y_{i}(1),a_{i}=1,l_{i}\}_{i\mid a_{i}=1}\cup\{y_{i}(0),a_{i}=0,l_{i}\}_{i\mid a_{i}=0}
.

2
Sample vs. Population-level Causal Estimands

In the potential outcomes framework, causal effects are defined as comparisons of potential outcomes within the same population (e.g. perhaps the same unit, some defined sub-population, or an overall target population). We will consider the following estimands throughout which are quite common in point-treatment settings in the literature:

1.

ITE:
The individual treatment effect (ITE) for subject
i\in\{1,2\dots,n\}
,

\theta_{i}(Y_{i}^{M})=Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)

we write it as a function of
Y_{i}^{M}
to emphasize that uncertainty about the ITE is due to uncertainty in subject
i
’s (unobserved) counterfactual - if we only knew the counterfactual, then we could compute the difference using the factual we have to get
\theta_{i}(Y_{i}^{M})
. That is, uncertainty is due to lack of knowledge about the counterfactual. We will suppress this notational dependence and write
\theta_{i}
when this emphasis is unwarranted.

2.

SATE:
The finite-sample average treatment effect (SATE) is simply the sample average of the ITEs:

\theta(\bm{Y}^{M})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)

again, we write
\theta(\bm{Y}^{M})
to emphasize that uncertainty in the SATE comes from the full set of missing counterfactuals, but will generally suppress this notation.

3.

CATE:
The conditional average treatment effect (CATE)

\psi(l,\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0})=E[Y(1)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y1}]-E[Y(0)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y0}]

This is the difference in potential outcomes averaged in the
subpopulation
of the target with
L=l
. It contrasts the average effect had everyone in this subpopulation got treatment 1 versus 0. Here
\phi_{Ya}
is the parameter vector governing he conditional outcome distribution under treatment
a
. We write this as a function of
(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0})
to emphasize that all uncertainty about the CATE comes from lack of knowledge about these parameters, but will generally suppress this and simply write
\psi(l)
.

4.

PATE:
The related population-level average treatment effect (PATE)
\Psi=E[Y(1)]-E[Y(0)]
- the difference in average outcome across the entire target population had everyone taken treatment
A=1
versus
A=0
. This is obtained by averaging the CATEs over the population distribution of the confounder:

\Psi(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0},\phi_{L})=\int\Big(E[Y(1)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y1}]-E[Y(0)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y0}]\Big)f_{L}(l;\phi_{L})dl

(1)

Here,
\phi_{L}
is a parameter vector governing the marginal covariate distribution. Again, the notation
\Psi(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0},\phi_{L})
emphasizes that uncertainty in this quantity stems from uncertainty about the three parameter vectors, but we will generally suppress and simply write
\Psi
. Note that under the causal identification assumptions, each of the two expectations is
E[Y(a)\mid L=l;\phi_{Ya}]=E[Y\mid A=a,L=l;\phi_{Ya}]
for
a\in\{0,1\}
. Substituting this equivalence into
\Psi
is known as the standardization formula. It is a special case of the g-formula
(
Robins 1986
)
.

The first two estimands are not functions of unknown population-level parameters, but rather functions of unknown counterfactuals for specific units in our sample. Hence, we call these finite-sample estimands. The latter two, on the other hand, are purely functions of the unknown parameters governing the population distribution of the data, hence we call these population-level estimands.

3
The Joint Posterior Distribution

Making Bayesian inferences for any of these estimands follows from the same procedure we use to make Bayesian inferences for all other problems: we condition on quantities we know, and find a distribution over the quantities we want to know but do not (i.e. the posterior). That is, a Bayesian statistician is one who “analyzes data by calculating, via Bayes’ theorem, the conditional distribution of unknowns given knowns”
(
Rubin 1978
)
.

What we know is the observed data,
D^{O}
. What do we
not
know? First, we do not know each subject’s counterfactual. To borrow terminology from the missing data literature, the “complete data” we ideally would observe is the union of the missing data and the observed data:
D^{C}=\bm{Y}^{M}\cup D^{O}=\{y_{i}(1),y_{i}(0),a_{i},l_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Together, the complete data for all
n
row-exchangeable subjects arises from the joint distribution
f(D^{C};\omega)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(y_{i}(1),y_{i}(0),a_{i},l_{i};\omega)
. This is governed by some parameter vector
\omega
, which is the second quantity unknown to us. Bayesian inference for all causal estimands is done using the joint posterior distribution which, up to a proportionality constant, is given by Bayes’ rule as

f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})\propto f(D^{C};\omega)f(\omega)

(2)

The first factor is the complete-data distribution while the second is the joint prior distribution. As is commonly done in Bayesian analysis, we assume
\omega=(\phi_{A},\phi_{Y},\phi_{L})
is comprised of distinct component parameter blocks and factorize the joint distribution of the complete data as follows using the chain rule,

\begin{split}f(D^{C};\omega)&=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(A=a_{i}\mid y_{i}(1),y_{i}(0),l_{i};\phi_{A})\cdot f(y_{i}(1),y_{i}(0)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})\cdot f(l_{i};\phi_{L})\end{split}

(3)

Note the presence of the bivariate distribution of a given subject’s potential outcomes, which is not identifiable even under the usual causal assumptions. Suppose, generally, it is parameterized in terms of
\phi_{Y}=(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0},\rho)
, where the first two (identifiable) components govern the induced marginal distribution of each potential outcome whereas the (non-identifiable) third component,
\rho
, governs the dependence between the two potential outcomes. In the Rubin Causal Model
(
Rubin 1972
;
Rubin 1976
;
Rubin 1978
)
, the first factor of (
3
) is referred to as “the design” and the product
f(y(1),y(0)\mid l;\phi_{Y})f(l;\phi_{L})
is referred to as “the science.”

We typically use an
a priori
independent family of priors which means the joint prior is
f(\omega)=f(\phi_{A})f(\phi_{Y})f(\phi_{L})
with, similarly,
f(\phi_{Y})=f(\phi_{Y1})f(\phi_{Y0})f(\rho)
. This states, for instance, that prior knowledge about the distinct parameters governing the propensity score, would not change our prior beliefs about the outcome model. Recent research suggests that, although this assumption has become a convenient default, it leads to poor frequentist operating characteristics in high-dimensional models (See
Linero 2023
,
Oganisian and Linero 2025
, and Section 4.b of
Li et al. 2023
). Taken together, the joint posterior distribution of all unknowns is proportional to

\begin{split}f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})\propto&f(\omega)\prod_{i|a_{i}=1}P(A=1\mid y_{i}(1),y_{i}(0),l_{i};\phi_{A})f(y_{i}(0),y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})f(l_{i}\mid\phi_{L})\\
&\ \ \ \times\prod_{i|a_{i}=0}P(A=0\mid y_{i}(1),y_{i}(0),l_{i};\phi_{A})f(y_{i}(0),y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})f(l_{i}\mid\phi_{L})\\
\end{split}

Now, if
A\perp Y(1),Y(0)\mid L
, then the treatment mechanism becomes
P(A=a_{i}\mid y_{i}(1),y_{i}(0),l_{i};\phi_{A})=P(A=a_{i}\mid l_{i};\phi_{A})
so that the joint posterior is further simplified to

\begin{split}f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})\propto&f(\omega)\prod_{i|a_{i}=1}P(A=1\mid l_{i};\phi_{A})f(y_{i}(0),y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})f(l_{i}\mid\phi_{L})\\
&\ \ \ \times\prod_{i|a_{i}=0}P(A=0\mid l_{i};\phi_{A})f(y_{i}(0),y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})f(l_{i}\mid\phi_{L})\\
\end{split}

(4)

From this general result for the joint posterior, paths diverge depending on which causal estimand is of interest. This is because inference for different estimands are done with different marginals of this joint.

4
The Many Marginals of the Joint Posterior

Bayesian inference for different causal estimands correspond to different integrals over the joint posterior
f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})
derived in the previous section. For example, since the ITE,
\theta_{i}
, is only a function of unit
i
’s unknown counterfactual, inference is based on

f(y_{i}(1-a_{i})\mid D^{O})=\int\int f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})\ d\omega\ d\bm{y}^{M}_{-i}

Where
\bm{y}^{M}_{-i}=(y_{1}(1-a_{1}),y_{2}(1-a_{2}),\dots,y_{i-1}(1-a_{i-1}),y_{i+1}(1-a_{i+1}),\dots,y_{n}(1-a_{n}))
is the set of missing counterfactuals for all subjects except subject
i
, which are integrated out of the joint. Since the SATE,
\theta
, is a function of
all
units’ unknown potential outcomes, inference is based on

f(\bm{y}^{M}\mid D^{O})=\int f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})d\omega

Since the CATE at any specified
l
,
\psi(l)
, is a function of unknown parameters governing the marginal distributions of the two potential outcomes (not the joint), inference is based on

f(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0}\mid D^{O})=\int\int\int\int f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})\ d\bm{y}^{M}\ d\phi_{A}\ d\rho\ d\phi_{L}

As identified by the g-formula, the PATE,
\Psi
, is a function of unknown the parameters governing the marginal potential outcome distributions
and
the covariate distribution. So, inference is based on the marginal posterior

f(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0},\phi_{L}\mid D^{O})=\int\int\int f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})\ d\bm{y}^{M}\ d\phi_{A}\ d\rho

The key points are that these are 1) distinct estimands with distinct interpretations, 2) rely on different identification assumptions (as we will discuss subsequently), and 3) have uncertainty stemming from distinct marginals of the joint posterior distribution. However, they are often conflated in practice despite some particularly important practical differences:

1.

Interpretation
: sample and population-level estimands have different interpretations. The ITE,
\theta_{i}
, is not the same as the CATE evaluated at
l_{i}
,
\psi(l_{i})
. The former is the treatment effect for subject
i
in
D^{O}
. The latter is the average causal effect within the sub-population of patients who have the same covariate profile as subject
i
.

2.

Cross-world identification assumptions
: finite-sample estimands such as the ITE and the SATE require cross-world assumptions as the marginal posterior of interest is proportional to the
joint
distribution of a given subjects potential outcome
pair
. For example, some invoke a “no contamination across imputations” assumption
(
Rubin 2007
)
. This asserts conditional independence of the potential outcomes, implying that
f(y_{i}(0),y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})=f(y_{i}(0)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})f(y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})
in Equation (
4
). In contrast, the PATE and the CATE only require assumptions about the marginals of each potential outcome, since the missing counterfactual is integrated out of the joint posterior. Thus, no cross-world assumptions are needed on top of the usual causal assumptions in this setting. Reliance on cross-world assumptions is highly controversial. While assumptions such as ignorability can be forced to hold by designing and controlling the treatment assignment mechanism in a hypothetical trial, there is no trial that can force cross-world assumptions to hold even in principle.

3.

Uncertainty estimation
: Because different estimands are functionals of different unknowns, the uncertainty in each can be dramatically different. For example, posterior uncertainty about the ITE may be much larger than the CATE. Thus, in addition to interpretational differences, conflating ITEs and CATEs can lead to incorrect inferences.

4.

Modeling requirements
: Different estimands require different models. For example, while the CATE does not require a model for the covariate distribution (its parameters are integrated out), the PATE
does
require a model for the this distribution. This means that targeting different estimands will involve different degrees of model specification burden and, as in the previous bullet, different levels of uncertainty. For the PATE in particular, differing models used in practice for the confounder distribution (including the empirical distribution, the Bayesian bootstrap, or parametric models) can lead to drastically different posterior uncertainty.

5.

Computation
: Sample-level Bayesian inference for estimands such as
\theta
and
\theta_{i}
requires an “imputation” approach where there missing counterfactual for each subject is imputed from its posterior. In contrast, Bayesian inference for estimands such as
\psi
and
\Psi
only require a posterior for the parameters and do not require imputing missing counterfactuals. While computing the integral in the PATE is often done via Monte Carlo (MC) simulation of potential outcomes, MC simulations are distinct from proper posterior “imputations”.

In the following section, we provide an implementation example of these differences using a synthetic data set. In order to expound on the mathematical details underlying these differences points without cluttering the main text, we have written a detailed supplement touching on each of the above points. Supplement Sections
A.1
and
A.2
compute the integrals above for the SATE and PATE, respectively, to illustrate clearly the differences in identification, modeling, and computation underlying both types of estimands.

Supplement Section
A.3
discusses common errors due to conflating population versus sample estimands. Readers who are not familiar with these details are highly encouraged to read these sections before proceeding to the next section.

As we mentioned earlier in the document, a crucial differences between the PATE and the CATE is that the former requires a model for the covariate distribution. The desire for flexibility may tempt one to keep the covariate distribution fixed at the empirical distribution and ignore posterior uncertainty when computing the PATE. On the other hand, the desire for uncertainty propagation may tempt one to parametrically model the joint covariate distribution, then compute the integral over this distribution via Monte Carlo simulations. However, this risks model mispecification. The synthesis of these two is the Bayesian Bootstrap model for the covariate distribution, which accounts for uncertainty while also retaining flexibility. See Supplement Section

6
Discussion

There are many other estimands (e.g. principal strata estimands, quantile causal effects, etc.) and it is impossible to discuss them all in one manuscript or make fully generalizable statements. For example, while the PATE and CATE do not require cross-world assumptions, other population-level estimands do. Consider, as one example, comparisons of complier-average potential outcomes,
E[Y(a)\mid C(1)=C(0)=1]
, where
C(a)\in\{0,1\}
is a binary post-treatment indicator of whether someone would have complied with assigned treatment under assignment
a
. This is a population-level quantity - it is the average potential outcome under treatment
a
among the sub-population that would have complied with treatment under either assignment,
C(1)=C(0)=1
. However, identification is often accomplished via cross-world assumptions such as monotonicity because we never observe
C(1)
and
C(0)
together for a given subject.

Similarly, in some settings contrasts are inherently finite-sample and so require explicit imputation of missing counterfactuals. For example, suppose we city-level data on
i=1,2,\dots,n
United States cities over time, pre and post implementation of some public policy (e.g. a consumption tax increase). If we are interested in estimating the causal effect of this policy in New York City specifically, then the estimand in question is the ITE. That is, we do not care about average outcomes across repeated hypothetical resamples of cities from some infinite superpopulation of cities. We care about New York City specifically.

Thus, when doing Bayesian causal inference, our main recommendation is to - as have done here - engage in first-principles thinking about the joint posterior distribution of all unknowns and which marginal posterior is actually of interest. Following the strict logic of Bayes’ rule and probability calculus will prevent many of the errors of the sort we discussed in Appendix
A.3
.

A.1
Posterior Inference for Sample-Level Estimands

In this supplement, we take the SATE,
\theta
, as an example and work through the derivation, identification, modeling, and computation to illustrate differences from population-level inference. As discussed, inference for the SATE requires marginalizing out the unknown parameters from the joint posterior in (
4
) to get,

\begin{split}f(\bm{y}^{M}\mid D^{O})=&\int f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})d\omega\\
\propto&\int f(\phi_{Y})\prod_{i|a_{i}=1}f(y_{i}(0),y_{i}\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})\prod_{i|a_{i}=0}f(y_{i},y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})d\phi_{Y}\\
\end{split}

(5)

The second line invokes SUTVA to replace the factual potential outcomes with the observed outcomes - just to make clear which of the two outcomes is a component of the unknowns
\bm{Y}^{M}
and which is observed in
D^{O}
. The components of
\omega
governing the treatment and covariate models are all absorbed into a proportionality constant,
C
, as they do not involve
\bm{y}^{M}
. Specifically,

C=\int\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(A=a_{i}\mid l_{i},\phi_{A})f(l_{i};\phi_{L})f(\phi_{A})f(\phi_{L})\ d\phi_{A}\ d\phi_{L}

Thus, the combination of
A\perp Y(1),Y(0)\mid L
and imposing prior independence renders the propensity score model “ignorable” in the sense of being absorbed into the proportinality constant. This is analogous to Bayesian missing data methods, in which the combination of the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption along the the “separability conditions” (i.e. distinct, a priori independent parameters governing the missingness mechanism and data model) is known as “Bayesian ignorability” (see Chapter 5 of
Molenberghs et al. 2014
).

Note that because
\theta
is a sum of unit-level treatment effects, we must model the full set of parameters governing the entire joint,
\phi_{Y}=(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0},\rho)
, which includes the non-identifiable dependence parameter,
\rho
. Because of the non-identifiability, inference for
\theta
with a joint model for both potential outcomes is heavily influenced by the prior on
\rho

(
Ding and Li 2018
;
Li et al. 2023
)
.

Others discuss an assumption of “no contamination across imputations”
(
Rubin 2007
)
which asserts independence:
f(y(0),y(1)\mid l)=f(y(0)\mid l)f(y(1)\mid l)
. Under this assumption, it suffices to model the marginals. Note that this assumption that
Y(0)\perp Y(1)\mid L
is distinct from exchangeability,
A\perp Y(1),Y(0)\mid L
. The latter is an assumption about how the treatment mechanism is related to both potential outcomes and can be satisfied by design. The former is an assumption about how the two potential outcomes are related to each other, and cannot be satisfied be design even in principle. Such modeling assumptions are known as “cross-world” assumptions as they impose structure on the relationship between potential outcomes across two hypothetical “worlds” in which a subject got treatment
a=1
and
a=0
. The reliance of identification on such assumptions makes inference for sample-level estimands such as
\theta
and
\theta_{i}
uniquely challenging relative to population-level estimands. This reliance is also quite controversial because cross-world assumptions cannot be forced to hold by any hypothetical experiment. Said another way, assertion of cross-world assumptions cannot be experimentally falsified and is therefore problematic to those who view falsifiability as central to the scientific method.

We will typically not be able to compute the integral over
\phi_{Y}
in (
5
) analytically. Rather, we could proceed by sampling from the posterior using, say, a two-step Metropolis-in-Gibbs Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling algorithm. At iteration
t
in the sampler,

1.

Given previous parameter draws,
\phi_{Y}^{(t)}
, update each subject’s missing potential outcome. For a treated unit,

y_{i}^{(t)}(0)\mid\phi_{Y}^{(t)},y_{i},l_{i}\sim\ \propto f(y_{i}(0),y_{i}\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y}^{(t)})

and for an untreated unit,

y_{i}^{(t)}(1)\mid\phi_{Y}^{(t)},y_{i},l_{i}\sim\ \propto f(y_{i},y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y}^{(t)})

These updates may or may not require a Metropolis step depending on the form of the joint potential outcome model. For example, following the usual Metropolis steps, we would propose a candidate value of the counterfactual
y_{i}^{(t)*}(1)
from some proposal distribution (which for simplicity we take to be symmetric), then accept or reject this proposal according to how consistent it is with the factual outcome and model parameters via the ratio evaluation

\frac{f(y_{i},y_{i}^{(t)*}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y}^{(t)})}{f(y_{i},y_{i}^{(t-1)}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y}^{(t)})}

After cycling through all units, we have

\bm{Y}^{M,(t)}=\big(Y_{i}^{(t)}(1-a_{i}),Y_{2}^{(t)}(1-a_{2}),\dots,Y_{n}^{(t)}(1-a_{n})\big)

2.

Combining the imputation of the missing counteractuals,
\bm{Y}^{M,(t)}
, with the observed data
D^{O}
, update the unknownparameters

\phi^{(t)}_{Y}\mid\bm{Y}^{M,(t)}\sim\propto f(\phi_{Y})\prod_{i|a_{i}=1}f(y_{i}^{(t)}(0),y_{i}\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})\prod_{i|a_{i}=0}f(y_{i},y_{i}^{(t)}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})

Again, in general this may require a MH update as the distribution may only be known up to a proportionality constant.

3.

Compute a posterior draw of the SATE

\theta^{(t)}=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{i:a_{i}=1}y_{i}-y_{i}^{(t)}(0)+\sum_{i:a_{i}=0}y_{i}^{(t)}(1)-y_{i}\Big)

Note that we need not simulate the factual potential outcome - this is observed and fixed
a posteriori
since it is in
D^{O}
. Only the counterfactual is simulated since it is unknown and not in
D^{O}
. Across repeated simulations
t=1,2,\dots,T
, this yields
T
posterior draws of the SATE which can be summarized via the posterior mean and, say, percentile-based credible intervals. For example, the posterior expectation is approximated via the mean of these draws,

\operatorname*{E}[\theta\mid D^{O}]=\int\theta(\bm{y}^{M})f(\bm{y}^{M}\mid D^{O})d\bm{y}^{M}\approx\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\theta^{(t)}

Similarly, since we had to impute each subject’s counterfactual in the process, we get inference for the ITE of each subject,
\theta_{i}
, for free. For example, a point estimate can be formed for the ITE of a treated patient via the posterior mean:

E[\theta_{i}\mid D^{O}]=\int\theta_{i}\big(y_{i}^{M}\big)\cdot f(y_{i}^{M}\mid D^{O})dy_{i}^{M}\approx\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{i}-y_{i}^{(t)}(0)

The connections with multiple imputation used in missing-data problems is immediate. The fact that the counterfactual are not in
D^{O}
is exactly “the fundamental problem of causal inference”
(
Holland 1986
)
. In this sense, “we can view causal inference entirely as a missing data problem, where we multiply impute the missing potential outcomes”
(
Rubin 2007
)
as in Step 1 of the algorithm above. Specifically, missing data imputation methods such as multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE) iterate between an “analysis step” which updates parameters conditional on the complete data and an “imputation step” which imputes missing data conditional on those parameters. These steps are analogous to step 2 and step 1, respectively, of the algorithm described.

A.2
Posterior Inference for Population-Level Estimands

Now let us contrast the finite-sample inference in the previous appendix section with population-level inference for
\Psi
. Again, we will work through derivation, identification, modeling, and computation to highlight differences. As discussed, inference for this quantity is done by integrating the joint in (
4
) over the unneeded parameters and the missing counterfactuals

\begin{split}f(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0},\phi_{L}\mid D^{O})&=\int\int\int f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})\ d\bm{y}^{M}\ d\phi_{A}\ d\rho\\
&\propto f(\phi_{Y1})f(\phi_{Y0})f(\phi_{L})\\
&\ \ \ \ \times\prod_{i:a_{i}=1}f(y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y1})f(l_{i};\phi_{L})\prod_{i:a_{i}=0}f(y_{i}(0)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y0})f(l_{i};\phi_{L})\end{split}

note that, in the above, the counterfactual for each subject is integrated out of the joint distribution of the potential outcome, leaving just the marginal distribution of the factual outcome of each subject,
f(y_{i}(a_{i})\mid l_{i};\phi_{Ya_{i}})
. This dodges the cross-world assumptions/modeling that was present for the SATE and the ITE.

A constant term
C=\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(A=a_{i}\mid l_{i};\phi_{A})f(\phi_{A})d\phi_{A}

does not involve the parameters of interest, and so was absorbed into the proportionality constant. See
Oganisian and Linero 2025
for a discussion of when the propensity score plays a role in Bayesian causal inference. Since
\Psi
is also a function of the covariate distribution parameters
\phi_{L}
, the confounder model
cannot
be absorbed into the proportionality constant. This is in contrast to posterior inference for the SATE, which relied on a posterior that both required assumptions about the joint distribution of the potential outcomes and did
not
require modeling the covariate distribution.

Models must be specified for the potential outcome under treatment,
f(y(1)\mid l;\phi_{Y1})
, under no treatment
f(y(0)\mid l;\phi_{Y0})
, the confounder model
f(l;\phi_{L})
, as well as models for our prior beliefs:
f(\phi_{Y1})
,
f(\phi_{Y0})
, and
f(\phi_{L})
. Typically, the posterior above will not have a known form and instead we must use, as before, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to obtain
T
draws from it, denoted
\{\phi_{Y1}^{(t)},\phi_{Y0}^{(t)},\phi_{L}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.

Given these draws, the
t^{th}
draw of the PATE is obtained by plugging in each draw into the g-formula in (
1
)

\Psi^{(t)}=\int\Big(E[Y(1)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y1}^{(t)}]-E[Y(0)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y0}^{(t)}]\Big)f(l;\phi_{L}^{(t)})dl

(6)

Now we must compute the integral, a process known as g-computation.
4
4

4

We often call this “Bayesian g-computation”, but strictly speaking there are no separate “frequentist” and “Bayesian” g-formulas. These terms refer to paradigms of statistical inference, while the g-formula is true as a consequence of the tower property and the causal assumptions. It simply maps functionals of the potential outcome distribution to functionals of the observed data distribution. It does not prescribe
how
or within what paradigm we should estimate these observed data functionals.

If the integral is computable analytically, as may be the case with very simple linear/additive regression specifications, we are done. But in complex models deployed in practice, the integral over
L
cannot be done analytically. Therefore, Monte Carlo (MC) integration is done by simulating,
L^{(1)},L^{(2)},\dots,L^{(S)}\sim f(l;\phi_{L}^{(t)})
and approximating the integral via an average over these draws as

\Psi^{(t)}\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Big(E[Y(1)\mid L=L^{(s)};\phi_{Y1}^{(t)}]-E[Y(0)\mid L=L^{(s)};\phi_{Y0}^{(t)}]\Big)

(7)

This covers many cases in which standard regression models (e.g. a generalized linear model (GLM)) are used to model each outcome distribution. However, in many other cases,
E[Y(1)\mid L=L^{(s)};\phi_{Y1}^{(t)}]
itself may not have a known form into which we can plug in
\phi_{Ya}^{(t)}
. In these cases, we need a second round of MC integration to approximate the conditional expectation at each
L^{(s)}
. That is, we simulate
B
values
Y^{(1)}(a),Y^{(2)}(a),\dots,Y^{(B)}(a)\sim f(y(a)\mid L=L^{(s)};\phi_{Ya}^{(t)})
for each treatment option
a\in\{0,1\}
. Each conditional expectation in (
7
) above is then approximated via an average of these simulations

E[Y(a)\mid L=L^{(s)};\phi_{Ya}^{(t)}]\approx\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}Y^{(b)}(a)

(8)

Note that unlike for the SATE, when targeting the PATE there is no imputation of counterfactuals required since all posterior uncertainty about PATE comes from lack of knowledge about parameters. The simulated
\{Y^{(1)}(a),Y^{(2)}(a),\dots,Y^{(B)}(a)\}
and
\{L^{(1)},L^{(1)},,\dots,L^{(S)}\}
are
not
imputations as in Step 1 of the algorithm in Section
A.1
. They are just independent draws from the data distribution models done for the purposes of Monte Carlo approximation of expectations. Thus, we should set
B
and
S
to be large so that the Monte Carlo approximation error is close to zero. Fast MC integration techniques specially tailored for these causal settings is an active area of research in its own right - see, for example, work by
Linero 2021
. For another discussion of this approach to g-computation in a Bayesian framework see Chapter 3.6
Daniels et al. 2023
and Section 3.3 of
Oganisian and Roy 2021
.

Now that we have posterior draws
\Psi^{(t)}
, we often summarize them via point and interval quantities. For example, a posterior mean for
\Psi
can be approximated as

E[\Psi\mid D^{O}]=\int\int\int\Psi(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0},\phi_{L})f(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0},\phi_{L}\mid D^{O})d\phi_{Y1}d\phi_{Y0}d\phi_{L}\approx\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Psi^{(t)}

A 95% credible interval can be formed by taking percentiles of the
T
draws.

Finally, note that posterior inference for the CATE is an automatic by-product of the procedure above - just as the ITE was when inferring the SATE. Specifically, a posterior draw of the CATE at covariate value
l
is

\psi^{(t)}(l,\phi_{Y1}^{(t)},\phi_{Y0}^{(t)})=E[Y(1)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y1}^{(t)}]-E[Y(0)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y0}^{(t)}]

(9)

These are the differences we averaged in (
7
) over MC draws of the covariate distribution. The key takeaway here is the Bayesian implementation of the g-formula does not require posterior predictive simulations or additional cross-world assumptions about the joint distribution of the potential outcomes.

A.2.1
Marginal versus Joint Ignorability

The previous section identified the PATE under the assumption that
Y(1),Y(0)\perp A\mid L
. However, this is actually stronger than required and identification can instead be done by assuming each potential outcome is marginally independent of treatment at each covariate level. That is,
Y(a)\perp A\mid L
for each
a\in\{0,1\}
. Since
Y(1),Y(0)\perp A\mid L
implies
Y(a)\perp A\mid L
for each
a\in\{0,1\}
, we often simply write the joint version anyway even if marginal independence is all that is needed.

To see this note, all we have to do is integrate out the counterfactuals
before
invoking ignorability.

\begin{split}f(\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0},\phi_{L}\mid D^{O})&=\int\int\int f(\bm{y}^{M},\omega\mid D^{O})\ d\bm{y}^{M}\ d\phi_{A}\ d\rho\\
&\propto\int\int\int f(a_{i},l_{i},y_{i}(1),y_{i}(0)\mid\omega)f(\omega)\ d\bm{y}^{M}\ d\phi_{A}\ d\rho\\
&\propto\int f(a_{i},l_{i},y_{i}(a_{i})\mid\omega)f(\phi_{A})f(\phi_{Y})f(\phi_{L})\ d\phi_{A}\\
&\propto\int f(\phi_{A})f(\phi_{Y})f(\phi_{L})\prod_{i|a_{i}=1}f(A=1\mid l_{i},y_{i}(1);\phi_{A})f(y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y1})f(l_{i};\phi_{L})\\
&\ \ \ \ \times\prod_{i|a_{i}=0}f(A=0\mid l_{i},y_{i}(0);\phi_{A})f(y_{i}(0)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y0})f(l_{i};\phi_{L})\ d\phi_{A}\end{split}

Now if we assume
Y(a)\perp A\mid L
for each
a\in\{0,1\}
, then
f(A=a_{i}\mid l_{i},y_{i}(a_{i});\phi_{A})=f(A=a_{i}\mid l_{i};\phi_{A})
and so the constant
C=\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(A=a_{i}\mid l_{i};\phi_{A})f(\phi_{A})d\phi_{A}
can be dropped while maintaining proportionality, leaving the same result as in the last section.

A.3
Common Implementations and Errors

Implementations of Bayesian causal inference sometimes conflate the PATE and the SATE in ways that can lead to incorrect uncertainty estimation and interpretations. This is perhaps because Bayesian causal inference, as popularized initially within the Rubin Causal Model framework, is popularly understood to involve “imputing the missing counterfactual” from the posterior predictive. While this is the case for the SATE, this is not the case for the PATE. In this section we walk through some common pitfalls in implementation.

A.3.1
Targeting the PATE, but doing “posterior predictive imputation”

When attempting to make posterior inferences about the PATE, the following intuitive, yet generally incorrect, computational strategy is sometimes employed. Given posterior draws of the outcome model parameters
\{\phi_{Y1}^{(t)},\phi_{Y0}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, a posterior draw of the “causal effect” is computed as follows. Given draw
t
,

1.

For each subject
i
in the observed data, simulate potential outcome under treatment
a=1
and
a=0
,

y_{i}^{(t)}(1)\sim f(y(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y1}^{(t)})

y_{i}^{(t)}(0)\sim f(y(0)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y0}^{(t)})

these are sometimes referred to as “posterior predictive draws.”

2.

Average the differences to get

\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{(t)}(1)-y_{i}^{(t)}(0)

\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}
is often taken to be a posterior draw of
\Psi
, but this would be incorrect. It departs from the procedure described in Section
A.2
in two important ways.

1.

First, rather than simulating covariates
L^{(1)},L^{(2)},\dots,L^{(S)}\sim f(l;\phi_{L}^{(t)})
, it evaluates the causal effect at each
observed
covariate value. That is, implicitly, it assumes the covariate distribution is the probability mass function that places mass
\phi_{Li}^{(t)}
on observed covariate value
i
:

f(l;\phi_{L}^{(t)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{Li}^{(t)}\delta_{l_{i}}(l)

where the vector of probability masses
(\phi_{Li}^{(t)},\phi_{L2}^{(t)},\dots,\phi_{Ln}^{(t)})
lives in the simplex (i.e. has non-negative elements which sum to 1). The above procedure sets
\phi_{Li}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{n}
, for all
t
- thus reducing it to the empirical distribution and ignoring posterior variability.

2.

Second, rather than simulating large
B
values
Y^{(1)}(a),Y^{(2)}(a),\dots,Y^{(B)}(a)\sim f(y(a)\mid L=L_{i};\phi_{Ya}^{(t)})
for each
L_{i}
, this approach essentially sets
B=1
when approximating the CATE at each
l_{i}
.

The first departure means that posterior uncertainty (reflected as variation across
t
in
\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}
) does not account for the unknown covariate distribution. A flexible Bayesian alternative instead places a Dirichlet prior on the probability vector
(\phi_{L1}^{(t)},\phi_{L2}^{(t)},\dots,\phi_{Ln}^{(t)})
, which has a conjugate posterior. This is known as the Bayesian bootstrap
(
Rubin 1981
)
and is the improper limit of a Dirichlet Process prior on the unknown confounder measure. It has been implemented in Bayesian g-computation to capture uncertainty in the confounder distribution
(
Wang et al. 2012
;
Oganisian and Roy 2021
;
Daniels et al. 2023
)
- which is an important source of uncertainty that differentiates the PATE from the SATE. We detail implemenetation and comparison of the empirical distribution versus the Bayesian bootstrap in Supplement Section
A.5
.

The second departure is also concerning as
B=1
will not in general eliminate Monte Carlo error. Thus variability in
\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}
could be too large as it will capture both posterior variability
and
MC error. Credible intervals based on draws of
\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}
may be much too wide, which may lead to over-coverage in repeated samples. However, the average (across
t
) of
\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}
will still be unbiased for the PATE.

The misunderstanding underlying the implementation above is that making inferences about the PATE is not a posterior predictive exercise. The PATE is not a function of the subjects’ unknown counterfactuals, but rather a function of unknown parameters. Thus, if in our model the conditional expectations
E[Y(a)\mid L=l;\phi_{Ya}]
are available in closed form, then we do not need to approximate it via MC by simulating outcomes at all. If we choose to do so anyway, we should at least do it for
B>>1
so that the MC approximation is negligible.

A.3.2
Conflating ITEs and CATEs

In other settings, analysts target “the causal effect” for a given subject - but often fail to specify whether they are referring to the ITE,
\theta_{i}(Y_{i}^{M})
or the CATE,
\psi(l,\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0})
, evaluated at
l_{i}
. As discussed before, these are different quantities with uncertainty stemming from different marginals of the joint posterior, which can lead to different inferences. The ITE is the causal effect for a specific unit in the sample. On the other hand,
\psi(l_{i},\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0})
is average causal effect within the sub-population of units in the target population who have the covariate profile
L=l_{i}
.

As in the previous sub-section, given posterior draws of the outcome model parameters
\{\phi_{Y1}^{(t)},\phi_{Y0}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, it may be tempting to compute the posterior draw of the “causal effect for subject
i
” as follows: Given draw
t^{th}
parameter draw,

1.

Simulate potential outcome under treatment
a=1
and
a=0
,

y_{i}^{(t)}(1)\sim f(y(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y1}^{(t)})

y_{i}^{(t)}(0)\sim f(y(0)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y0}^{(t)})

2.

Compute the difference

\tilde{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}=y_{i}^{(t)}(1)-y_{i}^{(t)}(0)

Then,
\tilde{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}
is taken to be a draw of the causal effect “for subject
i
”. Across draws
t=1,2,\dots,T
, this is believed to yield a set of posterior draws of this effect. If by “for subject
i
”, the analyst means that
\tilde{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}
is the
t^{th}
draw of the ITE, the procedure above is not valid in general. A requirement for validity is the “no contamination across imputations” assumption,
Y(1)\perp Y(0)\mid L
, which would justify simulating the two potential outcomes for subject
i
independently from their marginals. As noted earlier, this is a strong, cross-world assumption, but is seldom mentioned when doing the procedure above. Even under this assumption, however, it is unnecessary to simulate
both
potential outcomes for subject
i
- one of them is already observed and, therefore, known with certainty. Only
Y_{i}(1-a_{i})
must be simulated - simulating
Y_{i}(a_{i})
will lead to extra uncertainty. Thus, this departs from the procedure in Section
A.1
, which both includes a joint model for the potential outcomes explicitly and imputes only the missing counterfactual.

On the other hand, sometimes the procedure above is done and
\tilde{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}
is mistakenly taken to be the
t^{th}
draw of the CATE
\psi(l_{i},\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0})
. But this falls into the same trap described in the previous section. CATEs are contrasts of
expected
conditional outcomes. We can indeed view
\tilde{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}
as a Monte Carlo approximation of the conditional expectations in
\psi
,
\tilde{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}\approx\psi^{(t)}(l_{i},\phi_{Y1}^{(t)},\phi_{Y0}^{(t)})
as in equation (
9
). That is, the simulated draws are taken to be
E[Y(a)\mid L=l_{i};\phi_{Ya}^{(t)}]\approx y_{i}^{(t)}(a)
with
B=1
MC iteration for approximation of the expectation. Thus, while the average (across draws
t
) of
\tilde{\theta}_{i}^{(t)}
will be unbiased for
\psi(l_{i},\phi_{Y1},\phi_{Y0})
, the uncertainty will be too large as it will include the Monte Carlo error. If the CATE is truly of interest, then we should take
B>>1
.

A common thread between the previous two examples is conflation of “posterior predictive simulation” with Monte Carlo approximations of integrals.

A.3.3
The Bayesian g-formula of Keil et al. (2018)

In their manuscript entitled “A Bayesian approach to the g-formula”,
Keil et al. 2018
develop “a Bayesian version of the potential outcome distribution, the posterior predictive distribution of the potential outcome”, denoted as
f(\tilde{y}(a)\mid D^{O})
in the notation of our paper. Their goal is to do inference for the PATE,
\Psi=E[Y(1)]-E[Y(0)]
. However, the posterior predictive inference they present is instead for

E[\tilde{Y}(1)\mid D^{O}]-E[\tilde{Y}(0)\mid D^{O}]=\int f(\tilde{y}(1)\mid D^{O})d\tilde{y}(1)-\int f(\tilde{y}(0)\mid D^{O})d\tilde{y}(0)

Note that
E[\tilde{Y}(1)\mid D^{O}]-E[\tilde{Y}(0)\mid D^{O}]
is distinct from
\Psi=E[Y(1)]-E[Y(0)]
because the expectations are with respect to different distributions. The expectations in
\Psi
are with respect to the marginal
population distributions
of the potential outcomes. On the other hand, the expectations in
E[\tilde{Y}(1)\mid D^{O}]-E[\tilde{Y}(0)\mid D^{O}]
are with respect to the posterior distributions of the potential outcomes. The meaning and interpretations are, therefore, qualitatively different.

Here, the tilde in
\tilde{Y}(a)
indicates the potential outcome of an arbitrary new subject in the target population - not one of the
n
subjects in
D^{O}
. Then
E[\tilde{Y}(1)-\tilde{Y}(0)\mid D^{O}]
has the interpretation of “the difference in potential outcomes
for an arbitrary subject in the target population
that we would expect
a posteriori
.”, where
a posteriori
means “after having considered/conditioned on the data
D^{O}
.” That is, the interpretation is inherently finite-sample, similar to the ITE and SATE. This is in contrast to the PATE, where
\Psi
has the interpretation of “the difference in average outcomes
among everyone in the target population
had they
all
received treatment
a=1
versus had they
all
received
a=0
” Since the g-formula, as first defined by
Robins 1986
, is an identification result for the PATE, not a sample-level quantity, calling this approach a “g-formula” is misleading. Recall from Section
A.2
that Bayesian g-computation does not require posterior predictive simulations as the PATE is solely a functional of unknown population-level parameters.

Importantly, this approach also differs from the finite-sample Bayesian approach associated with the Rubin Causal Model (RCM)
(
Rubin 1976
;
Rubin 1978
)
, described in Section
A.1
. The RCM is concerned with inferring the missing counterfactual of one (in the case of the ITE) or all (in the case of the SATE) of the
n
subjects in the observed data,
D^{O}
. In contrast,
E[\tilde{Y}(1)-\tilde{Y}(0)\mid D^{O}]
is not the posterior expectation of the ITE for a subject in the data. Rather, it is the posterior expectation of an ITE for an arbitrary subject in the target population. Consequently, both potential outcomes are simulated, since even the factual is not observed. That is,
E[\tilde{Y}(1)\mid D^{O}]-E[\tilde{Y}(0)\mid D^{O}]
is neither the ITE, SATE, PATE, or CATE.

The formula presented by
Keil et al. 2018
(See Equation 5, Appendix I, Section 7.2) for each posterior predictive expectation in
E[\tilde{Y}(1)\mid D^{O}]-E[\tilde{Y}(0)\mid D^{O}]
is

E[\tilde{Y}(a)\mid D^{O}]=\int\int\int E[\tilde{Y}(a)\mid\tilde{L}=\tilde{l};\phi_{Ya}]f_{\tilde{L}}(\tilde{l};\phi_{L})f(\phi_{Ya},\phi_{L}\mid D^{O})\ d\tilde{l}\ d\phi_{Ya}\ d\phi_{L}

which, they call the Bayesian g-formula but, again, this is not an expression for a population-level quantity and so calling it a g-formula can lead to confusion. The posterior expectation is approximated by evaluating the integral over
\tilde{l}
given the
t^{th}
posterior draw of
\phi_{Ya}^{(t)}
and
\phi_{L}^{(t)}
.

They propose the following computational strategy for evaluating the integral, which we glean from page 5 of the paper and associated code in the appendix. We note that the appendix only provides code in the time-varying treatment setting, not for the point-treatment setting discussed in their paper.

•

First, simulate a covariate value from
f_{\tilde{L}}(\tilde{l};\phi_{L}^{(t)})
. Rather than modeling this distribution, they say it is “often identical to the population by the observed sample, and…can be sampled by taking the empirical distribution of the baseline covariates in the data.” That is, they randomly choose a covariate vector from the observed set of vectors uniformly with probability
1/n
, with replacement. In their code, this is done
n
times. Denote these by
\tilde{l}^{(t)}_{1},\tilde{l}^{(t)}_{2},\dots,\tilde{l}^{(t)}_{n}
.

•

Then for each posterior predictive draw
l^{(t)}_{i}
, draw a predictive potential outcome under each treatment
a\in\{0,1\}

\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}(a)\sim f(\tilde{y}(a)\mid\tilde{L}=\tilde{l}_{i}^{(t)};\phi_{Y}{a}^{(t)})

•

Then, average the difference in outcomes

\bar{E}^{(t)}[\tilde{Y}(1)-\tilde{Y}(0)\mid D^{O}]:\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{y}_{i}^{(1)}(a)-\tilde{y}_{i}^{(0)}(0)

They take this quantity
\bar{E}^{(t)}[\tilde{Y}(1)-\tilde{Y}(0)\mid D^{O}]
to be a “posterior draw” of the PATE, what they denote at times as
rd^{(1,0)}
. A few points are worth noting about this computational procedure:

1.

The average of the
n
simulations
\tilde{l}^{(t)}
is, it seems, meant to be a Monte Carlo approximation of the integral with respect to the posterior predictive density
f_{\tilde{L}}(\tilde{l};\phi_{L}^{(t)})
. However, there is no reason to cap the number of MC simulations at the sample size
n
. In small sample settings, this may not be sufficient to eliminate MC error. In time-varying treatment settings, with complicated outcome-covariate feedback, it is even more dubious to cap the number of MC iterations to
n
. This may potentially be a factor why, in their Table 2, the Bayesian credible interval is slightly over-covering in small sample settings, but then gets closer to nominal in larger sample sizes.

2.

This approach does not account for posterior uncertainty in the unknown confounder distribution because it keeps it fixed at the empirical distribution.

This procedure thus seems to mix and match computational steps for the PATE with computational steps used for the SATE and, in doing so, blurs the distinction between the two. Regarding point 1 above, it seems the authors subsequently realize this as the GitHub repository has a commit on May 20, 2020 (commit 9235816) which adds the following comments to the code not present in the appendix of their published paper:

// note: ...the sample size of the simulated data need not equal N and
should often be larger in complex problems;

In a later commit (commit 3d1ed52), they re-code to do
M=1000

>n
simulations per posterior draw. In the same commit, they add the following comment to a code file seeming to acknowledge that their approach has a finite-sample interpretation rather than the population-level interpretation of the PATE identified by Robins’ g-formula:

// Here, the baseline covariates are fixed across samples,
making this a ’conditional’ or finite-sample causal effect

However, as we’ve seen, the posterior interpretation, uncertainty, and implementation for finite and population-level estimands is quite different and so it is worth being clear about what particular estimand is of interest from the beginning when doing Bayesian causal inference.

A.4
Stan
code - Joint Posterior Sampling

Here we provide details of implementing the example discussed in Section
5
in
Stan
. Specifically, we program a
Stan
model to sample from the following joint posterior

\begin{split}f(\bm{y}^{M},\phi_{Y},\phi_{L}\mid D^{O})\propto\ &f(\phi_{Y},\phi_{L})\prod_{i|a_{i}=1}MVN_{2}(y_{i}(0),y_{i}\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})N(l_{i}\mid\phi_{L})\\
&\ \ \ \times\prod_{i|a_{i}=0}MVN_{2}(y_{i},y_{i}(1)\mid l_{i};\phi_{Y})N(l_{i};\phi_{L})\\
\end{split}

Importantly, since
\bm{y}^{M}
is another unknown along with the parameters, posterior inference for functionals of these values (such as the ITEs and SATE) requires declaring them in the
parameters
block of the
Stan
file as follows:

...

parameters {
// outcome mean vector
real beta01;
real beta11;
real beta00;
real beta10;

// Full covariance matrix
real<lower=0> sigma1;
real<lower=0> sigma0;
real<lower=-1,upper=1> rho;

// unobserved potential outcomes
vector[n] y_m;

...

}
...

The “
\dots
” represents skipped lines for compactness. In other words, the missing counterfactuals are treated just like parameters. We use the transformed parameters block to compute the bivariate normal mean vector and covariance matrix:

transformed parameters {
// outcome mean vector
vector[n] mu1 = beta01 + beta11 * l;
vector[n] mu0 = beta00 + beta10 * l;

// Full covariance matrix
matrix[2,2] Sigma;
Sigma[1,1] = square(sigma1);
Sigma[2,2] = square(sigma0);
Sigma[1,2] = rho * sigma1 * sigma0;
Sigma[2,1] = Sigma[1,2];
}

Finally, the complete-data likelihood is specified in the
model
block of the
Stan
file:

model {
rho ~ uniform(-.9, .9);

// covariate model contribution
l ~ normal(eta, tau);

// outcome model contribution
for (i in 1:n) {
vector[2] mu_i = [ mu1[i] , mu0[i] ]’ ;
vector[2] y_c; // complete potential outcome - observed with missing

if (a[i] == 1) {
y_c = [ y[i] , y_m[i] ]’ ;
} else {
y_c = [ y_m[i] , y[i] ]’ ;
}

y_c ~ multi_normal(mu_i, Sigma);
}
}

Now that the prior and likelihood have been specified, the
generated quantities
block of the
Stan
file can be used to compute draws of the various causal quantities of interest. For example, the ITE and SATE can be computed as

generated quantities{
...
real ITEs[n];
real SATE;
...

for( i in 1:n){
if (a[i] == 1) {
ITEs[i] = y[i] - y_m[i];
} else {
ITEs[i] = y_m[i] - y[i];
}
}

SATE = mean(ITEs);
}

Similarly, the CATE and the PATE can be computed in this block as well. For the PATE, we compute the integral over
L
using
S=1000
MC simulations from the parametric Normal model we specified, as dictated by Equation (
7
)

generated quantities{
int S=1000;
...
vector[n] CATE;
real PATE;
...
CATE = (beta01 + beta11 * l ) - ( beta00 + beta10 * l );

PATE = 0;
for (s in 1:S){
real l_sim = normal_rng(eta, tau);
PATE += ( (beta01 + beta11 * l_sim ) - ( beta00 + beta10 * l_sim ) );
}
PATE = PATE/S;
}

The results are visualized in Figure
1
.

A.5
Stan
code - Bayesian Bootstrap versus Empirical Distribution

Here we provide a more technical version presentation of integrating CATEs over a Bayesian bootstrap draw of the confounder distribution. To do this we need to be more precise with our notation. We begin by rewriting Equation (
6
) as

\Psi^{(t)}=\int\Big(E[Y(1)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y1}^{(t)}]-E[Y(0)\mid L=l;\phi_{Y0}^{(t)}]\Big)dF(l;\phi_{L}^{(t)})

This is the Reimann-Stieltjes integral where
F(l;\phi_{L}^{(t)})
represents the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the confounder distribution, governed by parameters
\phi_{L}
, evaluated at a given draw of those parameters,
\phi_{L}^{(t)}
, plugged in. The notation
dF(l;\phi_{L}^{(t)})
for discrete
l
denotes the jump in the CDF at point
l
while
dF(l;\phi_{L}^{(t)})=f(l;\phi_{L}^{(t)})dl
for continuous
l
. This notation is necessary as it unifies integration between discrete and continuous random variables and the Bayesian bootstrap assumes discreteness.

The Bayesian bootstrap models the CDF as

F(l;\phi_{L})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{Li}\cdot I(l\leq l_{i})

here
\phi_{L}=(\phi_{L1},\phi_{L2},\dots,\phi_{Ln})
is a vector of non-negative entries that sum to 1 across
n
- i.e. it’s a probability distribution over the
n
unique points,
\{l_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Note this CDF is a step function with jump size
dF(l;\phi_{L})=\phi_{Li}
at point
l_{i}
and jump
dF(l;\phi_{L})=0
at any other value of
l
. It’s a very flexible model and considered nonparametric, where “nonparametric” means it has a parameter space growing with
n
. The empirical distribution is the special case that plugs in
\phi_{Li}=\frac{1}{n}
,

\hat{F}(l)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(l\leq l_{i})

A Bayesian, however, would like to account for uncertainty by placing a
Dir(0_{n})
prior on
\phi_{L}
, where
0_{n}
is the
n
-dimensional vector of zeroes. The resulting posterior is
\phi_{L}\mid D^{O}\sim Dir(1_{n})
, where
1_{n}
is the
n
-dimensional vector of ones. Now given a posterior draw
\phi_{L}^{(t)}
, the integral above simply becomes

\Psi^{(t)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(E[Y(1)\mid L=l_{i};\phi_{Y1}^{(t)}]-E[Y(0)\mid L=l_{i};\phi_{Y0}^{(t)}]\Big)\phi_{Li}^{(t)}

This is because the Reimann-Stieltjes integral adds up the jumps in the CDF and, given the discreteness of the model, there are only
n
jumps - one at each observed covariate value. The integrand is zero elsewhere, thus allowing us to replace the integral with a sum over the
n
values.

A key point here is that, unlike in the setting described in the main manuscript, there is no Monte Carlo integration required and so there is no need to simulate
\{L^{(1)},L^{(2)},\dots,L^{(S)}\}
. When using the Bayesian Bootstrap, we simply evaluate the CATE at each observed
l_{i}
, then take a weighted sum where the weights are the
\phi_{L}\mid D^{O}\sim Dir(1_{n})
draws. When using the empirical distribution, this reduces to an empirical average of the CATEs:

\Psi^{(t)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(E[Y(1)\mid L=l_{i};\phi_{Y1}^{(t)}]-E[Y(0)\mid L=l_{i};\phi_{Y0}^{(t)}]\Big)

since we set each
\phi_{Li}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{n}
.

The Bayesian bootstrap is only a reasonable model if we have a large enough sample size. Although it is flexible and inherits the robust frequentist properties of the empirical distribution, it does make a very important assumption: values of
l
not in your data are assumed to have zero probability. To take an extreme case, suppose we just have one covariate, baseline age, and
n=2
. If the subjects are 35 and 55, the empirical distribution and Bayesian bootstrap assume there is no one in the target population between 35 and 55, there is no one older than 55, and no one younger than 35. This makes inference based on the Bayesian bootstrap very sensitive to the tails of the covariate distribution. For example, it could be that there are patients
l>55
and that
\psi(l)
is very large at these values of
l
. See
Oganisian et al. 2022
for modifications that try to fix this behavior.

All three of these approaches can be done in
Stan
within the
generated quantities
block. The Monte Carlo simulation approach was already demonstrated in Section
A.4
. Below is the code for the Bayesian bootstrap:

generated quantities{
...
real PATE_bb;
...
simplex[n] phi_L = dirichlet_rng(rep_vector(1, n));
...
// PATE - Bayesian Bootstrap
PATE_bb = 0;
for (i in 1:n){
PATE_bb += ( (beta01 + beta11 * l[i] ) - ( beta00 + beta10 * l[i] ) )*phi_L[i];
}
...
}

The
+=
syntax is a sum increment. I.e.
x += y
is the same as
x = x + y
. Here is the code for the empirical distribution approach:

generated quantities{
...
real PATE_ecdf;
...

// PATE - Empirical distribution
PATE_ecdf = 0;
for (i in 1:n){
PATE_ecdf += ( (beta01 + beta11 * l[i] ) - ( beta00 + beta10 * l[i] ) )*(1.0/n);
}

}

Note the decimal format in
1.0/n
is needed to prevent conversion to integer arithmetic in
Stan
.
