Evaluation and treatment of refractory chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy
Authors: Lynette Kiers, Belinda Cruse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is an inflammatory, immune-mediated neuropathy of the peripheral nerves and nerve roots. CIDP is unlikely to be a discrete disease entity, but rather a spectrum of related conditions, in which cell-mediated and humoral mechanisms act synergistically to cause damage to peripheral nerves. The 2021 guideline of the European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society on the diagnosis and treatment of CIDP has modified the CIDP spectrum to include typical CIDP and four well-defined CIDP variants. Patients with CIDP usually respond well to immunoglobulin therapy, steroids or plasmapheresis; however, 20–30% do not respond well, and approximately 15% remain refractory to all treatment modalities. Rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil and cyclophosphamide are of therapeutic benefit for some of these patients. Patients with some CIDP variants respond less well to immunotherapy, suggesting a difference in the pathogenic mechanisms underlying these variants. Potential novel treatments trialled in CIDP, targeting functionally relevant disease mechanisms, include neonatal Fc receptor blockers and complement inhibitors. These new treatment approaches are needed to optimise disease outcomes in refractory patients, and as an alternative for patients with suboptimal response requiring high doses or experiencing side effects from first-line therapies. Increasing the therapeutic options for patients with CIDP, particularly for refractory patients, highlights the need for more accurate diagnosis of typical CIDP and CIDP variants, objective evidence of treatment response and the need for reliable clinical biomarkers.

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is an acquired, immune-mediated neuropathy, affecting both the peripheral nerve and nerve roots, with variable clinical presentations. In 2021, the European Academy of Neurology (EAN) and the Peripheral Nerve Society (PNS) published revised guidelines on the diagnosis and treatment of CIDP1 which provided simplification and clarification of clinical categories with separation of the typical subtype from what are now recognised to be four principal variant subtypes. The significant phenotypic variability in the clinical spectrum of CIDP suggests that there are differing immunopathological mechanisms at play.2 There remains no blood-based biomarker to aid diagnosis of CIDP nor to separate patients into subgroups. Despite the recent identification of pathogenic autoantibodies in the autoimmune nodopathies, and an association with anti-ganglioside antibodies in Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) and CIDP,3 autoantigens and well-characterised antibodies remain unknown in most patients with CIDP. The diagnosis of CIDP remains clinical, supported by nerve conduction studies (NCSs) and potentially imaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination and rarely nerve biopsy. The 2021 EAN/PNS guidelines simplified the electrodiagnostic criteria as well as introducing variant-specific electrodiagnostic criteria.1 Patients with CIDP usually respond well to immunomodulatory therapies; up to 81% of patients show improvement from immunoglobulin therapy, corticosteroids or plasmapheresis, which are considered first-line treatments.4 However, approximately 20–30% of patients do not respond adequately to these first-line therapies, and 15% remain treatment refractory.5 In large randomised controlled trials of CIDP treatments, the majority of participants had typical CIDP,6–8 providing limited data on the treatment responses of patients with CIDP variants. This is relevant given that patients with multifocal acquired demyelinating sensory and motor neuropathy (MADSAM) and distal acquired demyelinating symmetric (DADS) variants of CIDP respond less well to immunotherapy.9 10

A subset of previously considered refractory CIDP patients has been demonstrated to harbour antibodies against nodal-paranodal cell-adhesion molecules.1 Since the 2021 EAN/PNS revision, patients with detectable antibodies to these molecules would now be classified as having an autoimmune nodopathy1; a distinct entity from CIDP.

There remains a subgroup of idiopathic CIDP patients, refractory to first line therapies, with significant unmet treatment needs. There are no randomised clinical trials to guide the appropriate course of treatment when first-line therapies fail. In addition to second-line immunosuppressive agents, novel therapeutic approaches are becoming available, including molecules or monoclonal antibodies targeting the neonatal Fc receptors (FcRn) and complement.

This review will provide an overview of the 2021 EAN/PNS updated guidelines on the diagnosis and treatment of CIDP, current therapeutic options for patients with CIDP refractory to first-line therapies and potential future therapies.

Diagnosis of typical CIDP and its variants

The diagnosis of CIDP is complicated by heterogeneity of the variant forms, which can lead to both overdiagnosis and underdiagnosis and result in delay to the initiation of appropriate treatment. In 2021, the EAN/PNS second revision consensus guidelines on the diagnosis and management of CIDP were published with the aim of optimising diagnostic accuracy and improving patient outcomes.1 The updated guidelines have modified the CIDP spectrum to include typical CIDP and four well-characterised CIDP variants. The latter includes distal CIDP (DADS), sensory CIDP, multifocal/focal CIDP (MADSAM) and motor CIDP. Acute-onset CIDP (A-CIDP) remains a subset of typical CIDP (up to 13% patients) in which symptoms present acutely and rapidly progress within 4 weeks from onset.1 Many of these patients will be initially diagnosed as GBS but will later be reclassified as A-CIDP following deterioration for more than 8 weeks from symptom onset or after three or more relapses after initial improvement (treatment-related fluctuations).1 Notable exclusions from the CIDP variant classification are chronic immune sensory polyradiculopathy (CISP), distal neuropathy with an IgM paraprotein and antibodies against myelin-associated glycoprotein (IgM-MAG positive neuropathies) and the autoimmune nodopathies11 (figure 1). CISP is currently not included as a CIDP variant as there was considered to be insufficient evidence to determine if CISP is demyelinating or a subgroup of sensory CIDP rather than a distinct entity.1 Autoimmune nodopathies (ie, antibodies against contactin-associated protein 1 (Caspr 1), contactin 1 (CNTN1), neurofascin (NF)-186 and NF155) and anti-MAG neuropathy are not included as part of the CIDP syndrome as they have distinct clinical, laboratory and pathologic features and respond poorly to CIDP treatment, particularly IVIg.1 11

In the 2021 EAN/PNS guidelines, specific electrodiagnostic criteria (including both motor and sensory conduction) are provided for typical CIDP and each of the CIDP variants. More precise definitions of features strongly suggestive of demyelination, including sensory nerve conduction criteria and distal compound motor action potential duration prolongation, are provided, and the levels of diagnostic certainty have been changed to CIDP and possible CIDP with removal of the terms ‘probable’ and ‘definite’ CIDP. It is beyond the scope of this review to discuss these criteria in more detail, however, careful application of the criteria is essential in the accurate assessment and diagnosis of CIDP.

Immunopathogenesis of CIDP

Although the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying CIDP are not fully elucidated, both humoral and cell-mediated mechanisms have been demonstrated to play a role2 12 (figure 2). The trigger for the aberrant immune response and target antigen(s) remains undefined in most patients.2 Diverse immune mechanisms encompassing cellular and humoral pathways are implicated, with the complement system playing a role in promoting macrophage-mediated demyelination.13 The abnormal cytokine profile in the CSF of CIDP patients reflects the active intrathecal inflammation, suggesting that the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines may be a crucial link in this process.14 A study using multiparameter flow cytometry to examine the phenotype and function of peripheral blood mononuclear cells in 25 CIDP patients, clinically stable on treatment, found evidence of a proinflammatory state with impaired regulatory T cell (Treg) function suggesting inadequate suppression of ongoing inflammation despite apparent clinical stability on standard of care therapies.15 Based on their findings, the authors proposed that targeting T cells, especially inhibiting Th1 and polyfunctional CD8+ T cells or improving Treg cell function, could be potential targets for future therapeutic research.

Potential therapeutic strategies can be inferred from the proposed immunopathogenesis of CIDP and provide rationale for alternate and novel therapies for patients with refractory CIDP and those with suboptimal response to first-line therapies.

Refractory CIDP

About 25% of patients with CIDP respond poorly or are refractory to first line therapies,3 16 including patients with typical CIDP with high disease activity and axonal degeneration, as well as some patients with DADS and MADSAM variants. In all patients with poor or no response to first-line therapies, it is essential to re-evaluate the diagnosis. Consideration of mimic disorders is essential, with the differential diagnosis and appropriate investigations varying depending on the clinical phenotype. The 2021 EAN/PNS guidelines strongly advise electrodiagnosis, serum and urine monoclonal protein immunofixation, fasting blood glucose, complete blood count, renal function and liver function in both typical and variant presentations.1 A retrospective study by Allen and Lewis17 reported that almost half of patients referred for a second opinion regarding possible CIDP received alternate diagnoses. Common reasons for diagnostic error in this cohort were overreliance on equivocal electrophysiologic features as evidence for segmental demyelination and overinterpretation of mild or moderate albuminocytologic dissociation.17 Investigation for nodal/paranodal antibodies is also required to exclude an autoimmune nodopathy, particularly if there are atypical clinical features such as ataxia, tremor, cranial nerve involvement, neuropathic pain, severe phenotype and poor response to IVIg,11 or non-neurologic features including nephrotic syndrome in patients with anti-CNTN118 and anti-NF140/186 antibodies.19

There are no established clinical or electrophysiological features of CIDP at or early after symptom onset that predict refractoriness to first line therapy; however, insidious onset and axon loss have been described as risk factors.5 In a recent retrospective single-centre study of 58 patients with CIDP (25 of whom were treatment refractory), refractory patients exhibited a longer disease duration from symptom onset to diagnosis and worse functional impairment compared with non-refractory CIDP patients.20 In this cohort, electrophysiological studies revealed greater axonal impairment and more severe demyelination in the refractory patients20; however, it is not known if this reflects the delay in diagnosis and treatment initiation in these patients or an inherent characteristic of their neuropathy.

Characterising a patient as refractory to first-line therapies requires the use of objective, well-validated, treatment-response outcome scales. Objective response to treatment requires improvement on at least one disability and one impairment scale.1 The Inflammatory Neuropathy Cause and Treatment (INCAT) disability scale and the Medical Research Council (MRC) sum score are commonly used, with ≥1 point and ≥2–4 points considered the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) respectively.21

If intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) is used first, at an induction dose of 2 g/kg followed by 1 g/kg every 3 weeks, data from the ProCID study22 demonstrate that 62% of IVIg responders can be expected to show improvement of at least 1 adjusted INCAT score point after the induction dose alone (by week 3). In this study and the ICE trial,23 nearly all patients who will respond to IVIg do so following a single induction and 2 maintenance doses (within 6–8 weeks). If there has been no objective improvement at this time, IVIg should be ceased and steroids commenced. The EAN/PNS 2021 guidelines state that the best corticosteroid regimen is not known,1 but that pulsed high-dose corticosteroid treatment (intravenous methylprednisolone or oral dexamethasone) may be considered as an alternative to daily oral prednisolone, both for induction and maintenance treatment.1 Plasma exchange can be used as an alternative if there is profound motor weakness and a rapid response is required.1

Management of CIDP Variants

CIDP variants differ not just in their clinical, pathological and electrophysiological characteristics, but also in their variable response to first-line therapies effective for typical CIDP.24 High-quality evidence is lacking regarding the best management options for each variant. A multicentre, retrospective analysis of 460 patients with CIDP registered with an Italian web-based database highlighted the better treatment response overall in patients with typical (87%), sensory (90%) and motor CIDP (88%), with poorer responses in patients with MADSAM (67%) and DADS (64%).10 A distinction was not drawn between different DADS subtypes (ie, idiopathic DADS versus DADS with monoclonal paraproteinaemia). In this analysis, there was a better response to IVIg vs corticosteroids for pure motor CIDP (82% vs 43% respectively), supporting reports of previous studies suggesting unresponsiveness or worsening with steroids for this clinical phenotype.25 Numerous studies have shown that in MADSAM, the treatment response to first-line agents, long-term outcomes and remission rates are inferior compared with typical CIDP.10 26 27 Studies to date differ in their findings with respect to the most effective treatment approach; some studies report a better treatment response to corticosteroids in patients with MADSAM10 26 while others report a better response to IVIg.27

Potential therapeutic strategies

Herein, we summarise CIDP treatments with evidence available for refractory disease (table 1), the therapeutic target(s) of each, and outline newer therapies or therapeutic targets being evaluated for CIDP in general (table 2). There are no randomised trials comparing treatment options in refractory CIDP. Furthermore, because of the heterogeneous pathophysiology of CIDP, it is not possible, based on the available evidence, to predict the most effective therapeutic class/target for individual patients. This poses a considerable challenge for treating clinicians and highlights the need for biomarkers to identify disease mechanisms in individual patients with CIDP, especially as treatments are developed with increasingly specific immune targets.

T and B cell targeted therapies

Mycophenolate mofetil

Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) is an immunosuppressant that inhibits the enzyme inosine 5'-monophosphate dehydrogenase, which is necessary for the synthesis of purines. MMF suppresses the immune system, specifically targeting the proliferation of activated T and B lymphocytes. A retrospective case–control study evaluated the efficacy of MMF in refractory CIDP.28 In this study, patients were classified as refractory if they had a suboptimal response to IVIg or steroids, requiring either ultrahigh doses of IVIg (>2 g/kg/month)±corticosteroids or with an inadequate reduction in disability or duration of response. MMF had been added to IVIg treatment regimens as part of normal care, either due to unstable disease or increasing IVIg/other drug requirements. The patients were rapidly uptitrated to therapeutic doses (1–1.5 g orally two times per day). 12 months following initiation of MMF, 87.5% patients had an improvement in MRC sum score and 77.8% in Inflammatory Rasch-built Overall Disability Scale (I-RODS) score meeting MCID thresholds. There was no significant change in median 10 m walk time. There was no significant change in median IVIg dose, although 50% patients were subsequently maintained on lower doses of IVIg with associated cost savings. There have been no other studies examining the role of MMF in treatment-resistant CIDP.

Rituximab

Rituximab is a monoclonal anti-CD20 antibody that targets the CD20 antigen on mature B cells and modulates B cell activation. When administered for autoimmune disorders, rituximab is typically administered as 1 g on day 1 and another 1 g on day 15,29 with repeat treatment at 6 months considered, but not always essential, in patients for whom complete, or near-complete remission has occurred.30 Varying dosing schedules, however, have been evaluated in CIDP.

Rituximab has been reported to be effective in refractory CIDP in retrospective case series and open-label studies.31–33 Many of these reports were written prior to the 2021 guidelines and included patients with IgG4 nodal/paranodal antibodies, with what would now be defined as an autoimmune nodopathy, and in whom very high response rates to rituximab have been reported.14 34–36 The inclusion of these patients is therefore likely to have overestimated the response rate of idiopathic CIDP to rituximab. A systematic review of twenty-three studies found that rituximab was effective in 63% of CIDP patients (paranodal antibody negative, idiopathic), and that rituximab therapy led to reduced IVIg requirements in 50% patients.35 In contrast, in a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study,37 CIDP patients (paranodal antibody negative) receiving immunoglobulin therapy were assigned to receive either rituximab (1 g on days 1, 15 and 180±7) or placebo. Both groups continued their regular immunoglobulin doses for 6 months following the first dose of rituximab/placebo. In this study, rituximab was not more effective than placebo in preventing clinical deterioration following the discontinuation of immunoglobulin therapy. Possible reasons for the negative result in this study include the longer disease duration (6.1 years vs 3.4 years) and lower baseline MRC score (median 54 vs 57) in the rituximab group, insufficient rituximab doses (suggested by non-zero CD19 levels at months 12 and 18), or reduced immune reactivity in the patient population due to long duration of prior treatment, resulting in a plasma cell pool less responsive to rituximab.37 It is also possible that the discrepancy between prior positive studies on refractory CIDP patients might reflect different pathogenic mechanisms in immunoglobulin responsive patients.37

Three studies have evaluated rituximab specifically in patients with refractory CIDP.

The efficacy and safety of rituximab in refractory CIDP was explored in the ‘RECIPE’ trial.38 This multicentre study included an open-label trial cohort of 10 patients with antibody-negative CIDP. Patients received 375 mg/m2 rituximab weekly for 4 weeks, of whom 66.7% showed an improvement (decrease of >1 point on adjusted INCAT) from baseline up to 52 weeks post-treatment.39

A prospective, open-label trial of rituximab in 16 patients with antibody-negative CIDP (and 1 additional patient with NF-155 antibodies), not responding to conventional immune therapies, demonstrated clinical improvement in 76.5% patients at 6 months and 88.2% patients at 12 months after treatment.40 Patients received two doses of 1 g intravenous rituximab on days 1 and 15. The clinical improvement was supported by improvement in NCSs at 6 and 12 months.

In an observational single-centre study, long-term low-dose rituximab was administered to 15 idiopathic CIDP patients, 10 with typical CIDP and 5 with idiopathic-DADS. The rationale for transitioning to rituximab therapy included refractoriness to previous therapies in seven patients, an attempt to arrest the relapsing course in another seven patients and IVIg sparing in one patient.41 Patients were administered 600 mg of rituximab intravenously every 6 months. Nine of 15 (60%) patients after the first infusion and three of six (50%) patients after the second infusion exhibited significant clinical improvement compared with baseline evaluation. Additionally, rituximab facilitated a reduction or cessation of other medications in 73% of patients at last visit.41

Cyclophosphamide

Cyclophosphamide is a potent suppressor of B-cell activity and antibody formation and in high doses causes T-cell lymphopenia and suppression of effector function.42 Cyclophosphamide has been reported to be effective in refractory CIDP in numerous case series,43–45 most convincingly by Good et al,42 in a cohort of 15 subjects refractory to all 3 first-line therapies, where the drug was used monthly in association with high-dose steroids. Amelioration was achieved within a mean of 4 months, complete remission in 11/15 (73.3%). Cyclophosphamide for the treatment of refractory CIDP was evaluated in a systematic review and meta-analysis including 13 studies (5 case reports) with 83 patients.45 All 13 studies were included in the systematic review, 7 in the meta-analysis. The pooled estimate of the response rate was 68% (95% CI 45% to 90%). The pooled estimate of the adverse event rate was 8% (95% CI 0% to 15%) and included haemorrhagic cystitis and alopecia.

For refractory CIDP, infusion protocols generally consist of 1 g/m2 intravenous pulse cyclophosphamide, administered monthly for a maximum of 6 months unless sustained improvement is obtained after three treatments. In some studies, cyclophosphamide was administered with concurrent high-dose corticosteroids, although this has not been shown to confer additional benefit when all studies were examined by meta-analysis.42 45

Bortezomib

A novel treatment reported to be of benefit in refractory CIDP is bortezomib, a proteasome inhibitor targeting highly metabolically active cell types such as plasma cells.46 In a case series of 10 treatment-refractory CIDP patients (including first-line therapies and rituximab or cyclophosphamide), one or two cycles of bortezomib stabilised six patients during treatment, with improved clinical and electrophysiological parameters of four patients up to 1 year later. No relevant side effects were reported. Each bortezomib cycle consisted of 1.3 mg/m2 subcutaneously (SC) on days 1, 4, 8 and 11. In a retrospective cohort study, bortezomib was effective in chronic inflammatory neuropathies and was suggested as a therapy option in patients refractory to rituximab.47 The EAN/PNS guidelines on the diagnosis and treatment of CIDP1 state, however, that the available evidence on effectiveness is too limited, and potential harms too great, to support the use of bortezomib and have advised against its use for CIDP.

Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation

Haematopoietic autologous stem cell transplantation (HSCT) has been widely used in autoimmune diseases with the goal of reconstructing the immune system.14 Small case series were reported as showing benefit of HSCT in patients with refractory CIDP, on disability level and electrophysiologic parameters.48 An open-label prospective phase I/II exploratory trial evaluated the results of HSCT on 60 CIDP patients with dependence on, or failure of at least 2 of 3 first-line therapies.49 HSCT was demonstrated to reverse disabilities and offer long-term immune therapy independence. The rate of treatment-free remission was 80% at 6 months post-HSCT and remained stable at 5 years and the rate of assistance-free ambulation increased from 32% pre-HSCT to >80% after 1 year.30 49 There was a 5% rate of severe toxicity but no treatment-related deaths. A meta-analysis of published series up to December 2022, evaluating 11 studies and 89 treated cases, found a response rate of 86%, remission rate of 85% and a rate of treatment-freedom post-HSCT of 81%.50 It is notable that the mean age of patients was 42.1 years (younger than most CIDP cohorts) and less than 50% patients had trialled rituximab or cyclophosphamide prior to HSCT.

The EAN/PNS guidelines on the diagnosis and treatment of CIDP1 state that there is insufficient evidence of a positive effect of HSCT and that the treatment should only be considered as a last resort option in specialised CIDP centres.

Anti-B cell maturation antigen CAR-T cell therapy

Recent advances in chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy have demonstrated promising efficacy in treating refractory autoimmune disorders. Two publications to date report on the use of this therapy in refractory CIDP. The first describes two patients with refractory CIDP, one with motor-predominant CIDP and one with typical CIDP, treated with anti-B cell maturation antigen CAR-T cells.51 Both patients demonstrated manageable safety profiles, with one patient initially responding well but suffering a relapse 12 months postinfusion and the other achieving sustained clinical remission for over 2 years. The second publication52 reports on two patients, one with a refractory motor variant of CIDP with positive anti-GM1 IgM antibodies and the other, a refractory paranodopathy, with detectable antibodies to NF-155. Safety profiles were again manageable. In both cases, a marked clinical improvement was observed. Clinical progress up to 300 days is described with the INCAT-ODSS dropping from 4 to 5 pretreatment to 0 by day 200 in the patient classified as motor CIDP. Given the potential pathogenic roles of B cells and autoantibodies in CIDP, this approach warrants further research, with particular consideration for highly refractory, disabled patients and patients with autoimmune nodopathies.

Complement targeted therapies

Activation of the complement system is the terminal effect of several immune pathways and directly results in tissue damage.14 In CIDP, the complement system appears to play a role in promoting macrophage-mediated demyelination.13 Complement involvement in CIDP pathogenesis is further suggested by complement deposition in sural nerve biopsies and signs of increased complement activation in serum and CSF of patients with CIDP.13

Several complement inhibitors, each targeting a specific step in the complement pathway, are under development or in clinical trials to explore their potential clinical benefits in CIDP.

Eculizumab

Eculizumab is a humanised monoclonal antibody that binds to complement factor C5 and prevents its cleavage to C5a and C5b, thereby inhibiting membrane attack complex formation.53 Eculizumab has yielded disappointing results as add-on therapy in phase 2/3 clinical trials in GBS.54 No clinical trial has investigated eculizumab’s role in CIDP. A report of a single case demonstrated significant clinical and neurophysiologic benefit for up to 7 months follow-up in a patient with progressively worsening disability from CIDP, refractory to immunoglobulin, corticosteroids and rituximab.53 Postvaccination for Haemophilus Influenzae B, Pneumococcus and Neisseria Meningitidis, eculizumab was infused, 900 mg every week for 5 weeks followed by 1200 mg biweekly. Antibiotic prophylaxis was given for the first 10 weeks of treatment. A dramatic improvement in limb strength was noted 1 month after eculizumab initiation with reduced disability (INCAT scale 8 pretreatment reduced to 4 at 1 month and to 2 at 7 months).

Riliprubart

Riliprubart is an anti-C1s humanised monoclonal antibody acting in the proximal portion of the classical complement system. In selectively inhibiting the C1 complex, riliprubart suppresses downstream activation of complement and has the potential to inhibit key inflammatory mechanisms that drive demyelination and axonal damage in CIDP.30 This selective complement pathway inhibition proximal to C3 may offer a better safety profile than C5 inhibitors, especially with regard to infectious complications, as the alternative and lectin pathways remain functionally intact for the purpose of host defence.55

Preliminary results from a phase 2 open-label study, which included 18 patients with CIDP refractory to standard-of-care (SOC) treatment, demonstrated efficacy and safety at 12 months after treatment initiation. Riliprubart was administered by SC route for 24 weeks (part A) followed by an optional 52-week treatment extension (part B). 89% (16/18) of SOC-refractory patients improved or remained stable with riliprubart after 24 weeks, with 50% (9/18) showing improvement. 89% (8/9) sustained their response after approximately 1 year of riliprubart treatment. Riliprubart reduced neurofilament light chain levels by 35% up to 48 weeks of treatment, indicating that riliprubart may reduce disease activity and the underlying nerve damage.56 Treatment-emergent adverse events occurred in 88.9% of SOC-refractory patients consisting mainly of headache, nasopharyngitis and fatigue. A global phase 3 study evaluating the safety and efficacy of riliprubart in adults with refractory CIDP (failure or inadequate response to SOC treatment) is currently underway (MOBILIZE, NCT06290128).

Neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) targeted therapies

The mechanistic concept of FcRn-targeting therapeutics is to accelerate IgG catabolism by blocking the FcRn-mediated intracellular IgG recycling pathway, thereby reducing overall plasma IgG levels, including the levels of pathogenic autoantibodies.57

Efgartigimod

Efgartigimod, a humanised IgG1 Fc fragment blocking the FcRn, has been the subject of a randomised controlled trial in CIDP, the ADHERE study.8 ADHERE was a multistage, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of weekly, SC efgartigimod, coformulated with recombinant human hyaluronidase PH 20, in patients with CIDP. Eligible patients included those receiving IVIg or corticosteroid treatment who were willing to discontinue that treatment, as well as treatment-naïve patients. 87% of participants included in the ADHERE study had typical CIDP. During a run-in period, previous treatment was ceased, with mandatory evidence of clinically meaningful deterioration prior to inclusion in stage A. In stage A (322 participants), participants received efgartigimod on an open-label basis for a maximum of 12 weeks. Participants with confirmed evidence of clinical improvement (ECI) advanced to stage B (221 participants), which assessed the efficacy of efgartigimod versus placebo. There was ECI in 66% (95%CI 61.0% to 71.6%) of participants in stage A. SC efgartigimod PH20 significantly reduced the risk of relapse versus placebo by 61% (HR 0.39 (95%CI 0.25 to 0.61); p<0.0001) in stage B.8 Methodological uncertainties regarding the study design have been raised, including the variable timing of efgartigimod therapy initiation after the last IVIg dose, different outcome scales used in stage A and stage B and high placebo response rate in previously off-treatment participants in stage B.58 Furthermore, the reported 90% relapse rate during the run-in phase suggests a possible perceived incentive to transition to stage A.59 Treatment-related adverse events were similar in stage A and B of the study, observed in 63% and 64% of participants, respectively. When compared with placebo in stage B, injection site reactions occurred in 14% (vs 6% in the placebo group), headache in 4% (vs 2%) and infections, mostly COVID-19, occurred in 32% (vs 34% in the placebo group). There were no opportunistic infections.59

Whether efgartigimod will have a role in refractory CIDP remains to be established. While the ADHERE trial was successful, there was indication from the open label phase that not all patients with CIDP respond to FcRn therapy. By the end of stage A, 53 of 165 participants who had been on immunoglobulin therapy (32%) dropped out.59 Levine and Muley reported their experience with 9 CIDP patients who were stable on IVIg and transitioned to SC efgartigimod.60 Four patients experienced rapid and significant deterioration following transition from IVIg to efgartigimod, with worsening of symptoms within 2–9 days after first efgartigimod administration. Three of the patients had transitioned from IVIg to efgartigimod hoping for additional improvement due to residual weakness on IVIg. This suggests that in some patients with CIDP, the driver of the disease may include mechanisms other than IgG autoantibodies.60

Rozanolixizumab

Rozanolixizumab, a humanised, high-affinity, IgG4 anti-FcRn monoclonal antibody,56 has been the subject of a randomised, placebo-controlled, phase 2a trial in 34 patients with CIDP.61 Adults with CIDP receiving SC or intravenous maintenance therapy were randomised to once weekly SC infusions of rozanolixizumab 10 mg/kg or placebo for 12 weeks. The primary outcome was change from baseline (CFB) in iRODS centile score at week 13. The study was negative with no significant difference in CFB in iRODS for patients receiving rozanolixizumab versus placebo, despite an IgG reduction >80% in the treatment arm. Two-thirds of patients on placebo remained stable, suggesting that not all patients were Ig-dependent, which may have skewed results. The occurrence of treatment-emergent side effects (TEAEs) was similar between the rozanolixizumab and placebo. Infections occurred in three participants (17%) in each group, and headaches in ~30% of each group. Serious TEAEs were limited to CIDP worsening in the rozanolixizumab group, and one severe TEAE occurred in this group deemed not related to drug administration.61

Conclusions

First-line therapies are effective for the majority of patients with CIDP; however, a significant minority have a limited or no response to first-line treatments and are defined as ‘refractory’. For all patients apparently refractory to first-line therapies, the diagnosis should be re-evaluated and alternative diagnoses excluded. CIDP is a heterogeneous condition both in its clinical features and its response to therapy, and there remains insufficient evidence to guide clinicians about the most suitable next-line therapy for individual patients. In addition to second-line immunotherapies, newer therapies are being developed that target specific immune pathways (eg, complement inhibitors, FcRn inhibitors) and new therapeutic targets are being discovered (eg. Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors). It is hoped that these therapies better meet the needs of treatment refractory patients, but also serve as an alternative for patients with a suboptimal response or side effects from first-line therapies. Further research is needed to determine the exact place and value of these newer therapeutic classes, with comparison of speed of action, degree of benefit and remission rates. The hope for the future is that key underlying disease mechanisms in individual patients can be identified and directly targeted to achieve better outcomes for patients with CIDP.
