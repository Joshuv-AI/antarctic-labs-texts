Risk factors for perioperative inspiratory muscle weakness in cardiac surgery patients: a prospective observational study
Authors: Qiaoying Wang, Jiayi Shao, Yanchun Peng, Sailan Li, Xizhen Huang, Liangwan Chen, Yanjuan Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInspiratory muscle influences postoperative outcomes following cardiac surgery. Most studies have focused on preoperative inspiratory muscle weakness (IMW). However, the prevalence, risk factors and postoperative outcomes of perioperative persistent IMW or newly developed IMW postoperatively remain inadequately understood.MethodsA prospective observational study enrolled 210 patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) was measured preoperatively (admission, one day before surgery) and at discharge. Patients were categorised into three groups by preoperative and discharge MIP: Group A (probable persistent IMW), Group B (newly developed IMW), and Group C (normal function). Demographic, cardiovascular and comorbid conditions, clinical data were collected for analysis.ResultsThere were 84 (40.0%) patients in Group A, 78 (37.1%) patients belonged to Group B and 48 (22.9%) patients in Group C. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that, relative to Group C, male (OR = 4.743, 95% CI 1.196–18.804), lower body mass index (BMI) (OR = 0.823, 95% CI 0.701–0.967), lower handgrip strength (HGS) (OR = 0.897, 95% CI 0.815–0.987) and peak expiratory flow (PEF) (OR = 0.142, 95% CI 0.066–0.304), and prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation (MV) (OR = 1.036, 95% CI 1.009–1.064) were risk factors associated with Group A. Conversely, relative to Group C, male (OR = 6.732, 95% CI 1.867–24.279), lower HGS (OR = 0.911, 95% CI 0.839–0.990) and PEF (OR = 0.477, 95% CI 0.274–0.832), and prolonged MV duration (OR = 1.027, 95% CI 1.001–1.054) were identified as independent risk factors for Group B. Patients in Group A had a higher incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) (69.0%), intensive care unit (ICU) stay of 46.42 (39.52–92.09) (hours) and hospital stay of 20.00 (15.00–23.75) (days), and lower quality of life at discharge (69.39 ± 11.22).ConclusionsThis study identified a high prevalence of probable persistent IMW and newly developed IMW in cardiac surgery patients, explored their risk factors and the worst postoperative outcomes. Classifying perioperative IMW informs targeted respiratory muscle rehabilitation and graded screening protocols.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03390-w.Keywords: Cardiac surgical procedures, Perioperative period, Respiratory muscles, Risk factors, Postoperative complications

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) affects an estimated 621 million individuals worldwide [1], and the global total of deaths due to CVD is expected to reach 35.6 million in 2025 [2]. The intervention and care of CVD populations present considerable challenges to global public health, and cardiac surgery is recognised as a vital and effective intervention for reversing the progression of CVD [3]. However, various traumatic injuries associated with surgical procedures along with invasive interventions, such as postoperative intubation, can elevate the risk of adverse outcomes, including postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), prolonged hospitalisation, and increased mortality [4]. Inspiratory muscle weakness (IMW) is a key factor associate with adverse outcomes [5], particularly PPCs [6]. Reduced inspiratory muscle strength due to IMW directly impacts patients’ capacity to perform both ventilatory and non-ventilatory activities, such as coughing, speaking, and swallowing, thereby compromising respiratory protection [7] and significantly increasing pulmonary dysfunction. Additionally, IMW can elevate the risk of various complications including PPCs [5, 6, 8], which may further damage the respiratory muscles [9, 10].

The literature indicates that up to 50.6% of elective cardiac surgery patients exhibit preoperative IMW [11], and a postoperative decline in inspiratory muscle strength ranging from 17 to 36% relative to the preoperative state, a condition that is difficult to recover in a short time [6]. Matsuo’s work reported that although cardiac rehabilitation has been implemented, two-thirds of patients continued to experience respiratory muscle weakness at discharge [12]. Given the critical role of inspiratory muscles in PPCs and respiratory dysfunction following cardiac surgery [6], as well as inspiratory muscles’ significant association with patients’ postoperative exercise capacity and quality of life [13], further research is warranted. But most studies primarily focus on diaphragmatic dysfunction in cardiac surgery patients, with relatively few addressing the overall function of the inspiratory muscles. The diaphragm is the primary inspiratory muscle; however, the contributions of other inspiratory muscles, along with a thorough evaluation of inspiratory muscle function in rehabilitating respiratory capacity, should not be disregarded [14].

Preoperative IMW has been found to be frequent, but data are controversial, with reports ranging from 25.0 to 50.6%. This variability may stem from differences in IMW definition [6, 11] or may reflect a limitation in assessing only the preoperative IMW, as cardiac surgery patients can undergo significant changes by the time of discharge, regardless of whether they had preoperative IMW. More specifically, patients without preoperative IMW are still prone to fail in reaching normal respiratory muscle strength at discharge, despite completing respiratory and cardiac rehabilitation postoperatively [12]. Notably, studies on risk factors for IMW have mostly included heart failure and coronary artery disease, with still controversial conclusions [15–17]. In contrast, fewer studies have focused on cardiac surgery patients, where the risk factors appear to be more homogeneous, including body mass index (BMI), anxiety, and depression [18, 19]. But IMW in cardiac surgery patients is influenced by perioperative factors such as low physical activity levels, impaired cardiac function, surgical trauma, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and intubation [9, 20–22].

Thus, cardiac surgery patients were categorised into three groups, including probable persistent IMW, newly developed IMW, and normal function. This study aims to clarify the incidence rates, risk factors, and postoperative outcomes associated with these groups, with PPCs as the primary outcome, and secondary outcomes including new postoperative pneumonia, reintubation rate, secondary transfer to intensive care unit (ICU), length of ICU and hospital stay, and quality of life at discharge. Elucidating these associations is clinically meaningful, as more scientific IMW grouping and risk factors identification can provide a reference for implementing graded interventions for cardiac surgery patients.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective observational study adheres to the STROBE guidelines (Supplemental Document 2) and was carried out between April 2023 and September 2023 at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (China). The study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional ethics committee on 7 April 2023 under document number 2023KY175. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Included patients were aged ≥ 18 years, and underwent elective cardiac surgery, including coronary artery bypass graft (CABG), cardiac valve surgery or a combination of both. And all enrolled patients should be conscious and able to communicate and cooperate. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) emergency operation; (2) active pneumonia before surgery caused by all pathogens, including COVID-19; (3) chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; (4) neuromuscular diseases, such as myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, etc.; (5) left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 30%; (6) pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) > 70 mmHg; (7) history of pulmonary or cardiac surgery.

Measurement and definition

Maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP) measurements were obtained using POWERbreathe® K5 (POWERbreathe International Ltd., Warwickshire, UK). The test was performed by specifically-trained nurses according to the methods recommended by the European Respiratory Society [23]. Subsequently, we calculated the predicted values using the sex and age [24]. IMW was defined as MIP < 70% of the predicted value (%MIP < 70%) [6]. MIP was performed on admission, the day before surgery, and at hospital discharge. For preoperative inspiratory muscle strength, adopt day-before-surgery data when inspiratory muscle function is normal on admission, yet IMW preoperatively, and take the higher of the two test values in all other situations. Patients were categorized into three groups based on the presence or absence of IMW preoperatively and at discharge: probable persistent IMW (Group A, %MIP < 70% both preoperatively and at discharge), newly developed IMW (Group B, %MIP ≥ 70% preoperatively but < 70% at discharge), or normal function (Group C, %MIP ≥ 70% both preoperatively and at discharge or preoperative %MIP < 70% that normalized to ≥ 70% at discharge).

Following a standard protocol from ATS/ERS [25], Lung function was assessed using a spirometer (HOMED A1; Guangzhou Red Elephant Medical Technology Co., Ltd, Guangzhou, China) on the day of admission. Lung function testing involved three dynamic lung function indices, peak expiratory flow (PEF), forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) and were obtained from a minimum of three repeatable and acceptable trials [25]. Dominant hand grip strength (HGS) was measured using the electronic grip tester (SENSSUN EH101, Guangdong Senssun Weighing Apparatus Group Co., Ltd, Zhongshan, China). HGS was measured with three trials of 6-s maximal contraction, each followed by a 15-s rest [26]. The highest value on admission was recorded.

Frailty index score, anxiety, depression and self-efficacy for exercise were also assessed on the day of hospital admission. The modified 5-item frailty index (mFI-5) score is a simplified version of a frail assessment tool used to quickly assess a patient's physical status and frailty level [27]. The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used to evaluate depression and anxiety [28]. Self-efficacy for exercise (SEE) scale was used to assess self-efficacy for exercise [29]. Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation.

Other data collected from medical records and laboratory analysis, which included sex, age, BMI, smoking history, drinking history, operation type, hypertension, diabetes, previous myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, previous cerebral infarction, LVEF, PASP, haemoglobin (HB), glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c), operation time, CPB duration, aortic clamp time and mechanical ventilation (MV) duration. Postoperative outcomes included PPCs, new postoperative pneumonia, reintubation, secondary transfer to ICU, length of ICU stay, length of hospital stay, and quality of life assessed using the China questionnaire of quality of life in patients with cardiovascular diseases (CQQC) at discharge [30]. According to the European Perioperative Clinical Outcome [31] defined PPCs, the diagnostic criteria for postoperative pneumonia defined by the National Healthcare Safety Network [32].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 29.0. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as means ± SD, while non-normally distributed data were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (P25, P75). Categorical data are expressed in terms of frequency and composition ratios. For categorical variables, group differences were analysed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test; post-hoc tests with Bonferroni correction followed significant results. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, overall group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with significant findings further evaluated via Kruskal–Wallis post-hoc pairwise tests and Bonferroni correction. For normally distributed continuous variables with homogeneous variance, one-way ANOVA was applied, and significant results were analysed using the LSD post-hoc test Fig. 1.

Recruitment flow diagram of the current study.

Initially, univariate multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed on all candidate variables. Subsequently, variables with a p < 0.05 and covariates (sex and BMI) from the univariate logistic regression were included in a multivariable multinomial logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for probable persistent IMW and newly developed IMW. The significance threshold adopted was p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristic

The flow chart of patient recruitment is shown in Fig. 1. During the study period, we included 343 patients, 210 of whom were included in the final analysis.

In the two preoperative tests, none of the patients with normal inspiratory muscle function at admission developed IMW in the test on the day before surgery. Furthermore, no enrolled patient had IMW preoperatively but achieved normalised inspiratory muscle function at discharge. Thus, all patients in Group C maintained normal inspiratory muscle strength both preoperatively and at discharge. 84 (40.0%) patients were assigned to Group A, 78 (37.1%) patients to Group B, and 48 (22.9%) patients to Group C. The results showed significant differences between groups: sex, age, BMI, previous cerebral infarction, frailty index score, PASP, HB, HGS, PEF, FEV1, FVC, anxiety, depression and MV duration (p < 0.05).

The characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. Of the enrolled patients, 108 (51.4%) were men, and 86 (41.0%) were aged over 60 years; the mean BMI was 23.45 ± 3.24 kg/m2. Valve surgery was the most common operation type, performed in 138 (41.3%) patients, whereas only 15 (4.5%) patients underwent combined valve surgery and CABG. The mean operation time was 266.00 ± 79.42 min, and the mean MV duration was 19.49 (13.77–37.72) hours.

Predictors for probable persistent IMW and newly developed IMW

Univariate multinomial logistic regression was performed to identify potential factors of Group A and Group B compared to Group C (Supplemental Table 1). Models indicated that Group A was associated with male (OR = 0.310, 95% CI 0.148–0.649), BMI (OR = 0.860, 95% CI 0.768–0.964), frailty index score (OR = 8.682, 95% CI 1.257–59.943), PASP (OR = 1.039, 95% CI 1.012–1.066), HB (OR = 0.971, 95% CI 0.952–0.992), HGS (OR = 0.850, 95% CI 0. 803–0.899), PEF (OR = 0.980, 95% CI 0.974–0.986), FEV1 (OR = 0.074, 95% CI 0.034–0.165), FVC (OR = 0.096, 95% CI 0.047–0.199), anxiety (OR = 1.165, 95% CI 1.028–1.319), depression (OR = 1.238, 95% CI 1.046–1.465), and MV duration (OR = 1.041, 95% CI 1.017–1.066) compared to Group C. Group B was associated with HGS (OR = 0.951, 95% CI 0.908–0.996), PEF (OR = 0.994, 95% CI 0.990–0.998), and MV duration (OR = 1.031, 95% CI 1.007–1.056).

Multivariate multinomial logistic regression was conducted with p < 0.05 variables in univariate logistic regression, using Group C as the reference. Notably, FEV1 and FVC with VIF > 10 were removed to address multicollinearity.

Figure 2 illustrates that in the analysis, compared to Group C, male (OR = 4.743, 95% CI 1.196–18.804) and MV duration (OR = 1.036, 95% CI 1.009–1.064) were associated with an increased risk of Group A, while BMI (OR = 0.823, 95% CI 0.701–0.967), HGS (OR = 0.897, 95% CI 0.815–0.987), and PEF (OR = 0.142, 95% CI 0.066–0.304) were correlated with a reduced risk, serving as protective factors. Risk factors for Group B included male (OR = 6.732, 95% CI 1.867–24.279) and MV duration (OR = 1.027, 95% CI 1.001–1.054), and the protective factor was HGS (OR = 0.911, 95% CI 0.839–0.990), PEF (OR = 0.477, 95% CI 0.274–0.832), compared to Group C.

Predictors for probable persistent IMW and newly developed IMW

Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was performed on the multinomial logistic regression model to verify the robustness of the results. Specifically, given that the covariance of the independent variables in the model (VIF < 10), two extended models were reconstructed. One incorporates operation time and aortic clamp time, and the other incorporates operation time and CPB duration (Supplemental Document 1). Sensitivity analysis results demonstrated that the conclusions of the original model remained robust.

Postoperative outcomes among the three groups

Group A, with an incidence of PPCs of 69.0%, had a notably higher rate than either Group B (46.2%) or Group C (43.8%). Compared with Group C, Group A had a longer length of ICU stay [46.42 (39.52–92.09) (h)], longer length of hospital stay[20.00 (15.00–23.75) (d)], and worse quality of life at discharge (69.39 ± 11.22); Group B showed a longer length of hospital stay [18.50 (15.00–26.00) (d)] and worse quality of life at discharge (71.44 ± 11.69), with no significant difference in ICU stay. Discussion

Our study provides a more scientific classification of IMW in cardiac surgery patients and reports its incidence data, based on perioperative IMW status. We observed that all patients with preoperative IMW remained in a state of IMW at discharge (Group A). This subgroup exhibited the worst prognosis and require more intensified respiratory muscle intervention. Overall, 77.1% of patients exhibited IMW at discharge, regardless of their preoperative inspiratory muscle status. This finding is similar to the result of previous study [12]. This indicates that despite in-hospital cardiac rehabilitation, still a considerable number of patients had insufficient inspiratory muscle strength to resist perioperative detrimental factors. Notably, 37.1% of patients with normal preoperative inspiratory muscle strength developed IMW by discharge, suggesting that this population require close monitoring.

Male, lower HGS, lower PEF, and longer MV duration were independent risk factors associated with probable persistent IMW and newly developed IMW. While lower BMI was an independent risk factor for probable persistent IMW. Research indicates that the male diaphragm may be more prone to fatigue due to variations in muscle fiber-type and substrate utilization [33]. Additionally, men generally require a higher work of breathing to sustain the same exercise intensity as women, and their greater respiratory muscle mass necessitates enhanced ventilation to ensure adequate oxygenation [33]. The overloading of respiratory muscles that have not yet recovered may further impede the recuperation of inspiratory muscles. Recent studies have highlighted gender differences in the pathology of the pectoralis major muscle among heart failure with reduced LVEF, males exhibit more significant abnormalities, which correlate with transcriptomic, fiber phenotypes, capillary, and circulating factors [34]. Such findings remain contentious [33], given the close interplay between peripheral and respiratory muscles [35, 36]. Collectively, future clinical studies are warranted to explore the mechanisms underlying gender-related respiratory muscle function in CVD, particularly cardiac surgery patients, to inform more targeted nursing interventions.

In addition, we found an interesting phenomenon that the univariate model showed male were at a reduced risk for probable persistent IMW. PEF and HGS may have adjusted for the risk effect of gender in the multifactorial model. Specifically, sex differences in physiological structure and function are reflected in significantly higher HGS [37] and PEF [38] in men compared to women. HGS and PEF reflect skeletal muscle strength and are significantly correlated with respiratory muscle strength [39–41]. It has even been suggested that respiratory muscle strength can be directly measured by PEF [42]. Moreover, PEF directly reflects airway patency and lung secretion clearance [43], which are strongly associated with postoperative pneumonia [44]. Higher HGS and PEF have a strong protective effect on the inspiratory muscles, and our findings confirm this conclusion. However, the relatively wide confidence intervals for gender in Group A (OR: 1.196–18.804) may indicate small sample size or unmeasured variables (e.g., hormonal status). Thus, the role of gender requires further clarification with larger samples and more comprehensive controls.

Coinciding with previous studies, longer MV duration is a significant risk factor for both probable persistent IMW and newly developed IMW. Notably, the relationship between MV duration and IMW is undoubtedly bidirectional. For one thing, prolonged MV reduces diaphragmatic strength and endurance rapidly via oxidative stress, enhanced protein catabolism, mitochondrial damage, and autophagy activation—processes closely linked to MV-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction, which further impairs inspiratory muscle function and increases weaning difficulty [45, 46]. For another, preoperative IMW significantly prolongs MV duration in cardiac surgery patients [5, 8, 47]. This bidirectional association is particularly prominent in the probable persistent IMW group. However, the complexity of their mutual interaction also limits, to a certain extent, the identification of risk factors in patients with newly developed IMW.

Lower BMI is a specific risk factor for the probable persistence IMW. A large sample size study stressed that BMI exhibits a quadratic non-linear relationship with MIP [48]. Specifically, malnutrition along with diminished muscle mass in patients increases the potential of IMW when BMI drops below the threshold [48, 49]. This is in good agreement with Iida et al., who reported that CABG patients with higher BMI may protect inspiratory muscle function by mitigating the inflammatory response through the anti-inflammatory effects of adipose tissue [18]. It is noteworthy that most participants included in this study had a normal BMI, implying that the observation of a lower BMI should be interpreted as a normal low value, and our findings may not extrapolate to obese or malnourished cohorts. However, lower BMI does not appear to be an independent risk factor for newly developed IMW. We speculate that the inflammatory stress response triggered by perioperative therapeutic interventions may have a more significant impact than that of a lower BMI. Future studies are needed to clarify the BMI thresholds associated with IMW for more precise clinical management.

The present study has several limitations to acknowledge. First, the respiratory assessment was restricted to MIP, failing to capture holistic respiratory muscle function, and lacked data on diaphragmatic ultrasound or electrophysiology, limiting differentiation between central and peripheral mechanisms of weakness. Methodologically, our single-centre, valve-dominant case mix may restrict generalizability. At the same time, unmeasured confounders—including pain, sedation depth, analgesia use, and intraoperative factors (e.g., phrenic nerve injury)—could represent unaccounted drivers of IMW. Additionally, for safety in pulmonary function testing, we excluded high-risk subgroups (LVEF ≤ 30%, PASP > 70 mmHg, COPD), limiting applicability to complex cardiac cases. Besides, we lacked a sample size basis for statistical efficacy calculations, and the sample sizes for the subgroups were somewhat small. We were unable to conduct a sensitivity analysis where IMW was defined as %MIP < 80% due to limitations arising from insufficient sample size. Finally, the outcome was limited to the hospital, and longitudinal follow-up beyond hospital discharge is needed in the future.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that all cardiac surgery patients with preoperative IMW continued to exhibit IMW at discharge. These patients demonstrated the worst postoperative outcomes. Although cardiac rehabilitation has been implemented, 37.1% of patients with newly developed IMW were unable to restore their inspiratory muscle strength to preoperative levels by discharge, significantly impacting their quality of life at discharge and hospital length of stay. This study provides a scientific basis for implementing a hierarchical screening and respiratory muscle rehabilitation program within the cardiac surgery population by comparing the identification of risk factors and postoperative outcomes across different IMW incidence groups. Future research should establish HGS/PEF thresholds for clinical screening and develop risk group-specific intervention protocols for newly developed IMW and preoperative IMW.
