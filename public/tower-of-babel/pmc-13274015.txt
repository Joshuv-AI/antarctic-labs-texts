Dynamic wetting of co-assembled supramolecular herbicides on young hydrophobic leaves enables sustainable weed management
Authors: Yuntian Xiao, Chuanhua Wu, Changzhen Zhang, Qiuxiang Yin, Jiawang Wang, Quan Gao, Min Liao, Haiqun Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reducing liquid surface tension is widely employed to enhance droplet retention on broad, rigid crop leaves such as cabbage and cucumber; however, its effectiveness on young hydrophobic weeds with lightweight, flexible foliage remains poorly understood. On such leaves, droplet impact readily induces leaf motion, which intensifies fragmentation and splashing, thereby reducing deposition and increasing leaching risks. Here, we report a supramolecular nano-formulation in which the herbicide 2,4-D and the biobased surfactant rhamnolipid (RHL) co-assemble into uniform nanoparticles (mean diameter 134.33 nm) via hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions. Compared with two commercial salt formulations, this design reduces liquid surface tension by more than 55.5%, enabling efficient spreading and interfacial wetting on hydrophobic polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) surfaces. On three small hydrophobic weed species, droplets from commercial formulations detached rapidly upon impact, whereas nanoparticle-laden droplets adhered strongly and moved synchronously with the leaves. Beyond enhanced retention, the nano-formulation exhibits temperature-responsive release, suppresses herbicide leaching, and achieves superior control of both broadleaf and grass weeds in pot trials, while markedly reducing toxicity to zebrafish, earthworms, and human cells. Together, these findings establish a supramolecular co-assembly strategy that integrates high herbicidal efficacy with improved environmental safety, offering a promising platform for sustainable weed management.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04446-7.

Introduction

Pesticides remain indispensable to global food security and the pursuit of sustainable agriculture [1, 2]. Among them, herbicides constitute the primary tool in modern weed management, owing to their high efficiency, operational simplicity, and compatibility with large-scale agricultural systems [3, 4]. However, their performance is often limited by the waxy cuticular layer that coats many weed leaves [5]. This hydrophobic barrier restricts droplet spreading and adhesion, thereby reducing spray deposition and limiting the bioavailability of active ingredients [6].

To overcome this interfacial resistance, three main strategies have been developed. The first focuses on reducing liquid surface tension to suppress rebound, splashing, and fragmentation, most commonly through the addition of highly efficient surfactants [7–9]. The second strategy increases droplet extensional viscosity to slow retraction and rebound, typically by incorporating flexible polymers such as poly(ethylene oxide) [10], nanocrystalline cellulose [11], or organogel carriers [12], thereby strengthening droplet-leaf interactions. The third approach imparts electrical charge to droplets, altering their trajectories within the boundary layer and enhancing deposition on target surfaces [13, 14]. Despite demonstrated effectiveness, each strategy suffers from inherent limitations. Surfactant-based formulations can improve deposition but rarely address encapsulation, controlled release, leaching suppression, or toxicity. Nanocarrier systems enable sustained release and targeted delivery, yet often rely on complex fabrication processes, incur higher costs, and raise concerns regarding environmental persistence and ecological safety [15]. Electrically charged sprays require specialized equipment and frequently exhibit variable performance under field conditions. There is therefore an urgent need for herbicide formulations that integrate simplicity, affordability, high deposition efficiency, and environmental compatibility.

Supramolecular self-assembly offers a versatile and increasingly attractive route to advanced pesticide formulations [16, 17]. It refers to the spontaneous organization of molecular building blocks into ordered and functional architectures through noncovalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and electrostatic attraction [18–20]. The resulting assemblies span a wide range of structural forms, such as self-assembled nanoparticles [21], supramolecular hydrogels [22], crystalline frameworks [23] and host–guest systems. [24] Recent studies indicate that supramolecular self-assembly combines operational simplicity and low cost with a distinctive capacity to modulate interfacial properties, thereby improving droplet wettability on leaf surfaces. For example, Wei et al. reported glycyrrhizic acid-spinosad nanoparticles that reduced surface tension, promoted droplet spreading on cabbage leaves, and enhanced insecticidal activity [25]. An et al. further designed surfactant-emamectin benzoate nanoparticles that improved droplet wettability on crops including cucumber and pepper [26]. Likewise, Wang et al. demonstrated that cucurbituril-based host–guest assemblies facilitated droplet deposition on tomato leaves while disrupting bacterial biofilms [27]. Although these studies underscore the promise of supramolecular self-assembly for improving agrochemical performance, they have largely focused on crops with broad, rigid leaves that remain nearly static upon droplet impact. Whether such assemblies can maintain their wetting advantages on young hydrophobic weeds with small, flexible leaves, where dynamic leaf motion strongly influences droplet impact and retention, remains an open and critical question.

Motivated by this challenge, we designed a nanoparticle system in which the herbicide 2,4-D co-assembles with the natural biosurfactant rhamnolipid (2,4-D NPs), with the aim of achieving high deposition efficiency on hydrophobic, dynamically moving leaves (Fig. 1). Compared with commercial sodium (2,4-D Na) salt and dimethylamine (2,4-D DMA) salt formulations, the co-assembled nanoparticles markedly enhanced dynamic wettability on the leaves of multiple hydrophobic broadleaf weed species. This improvement arises from a pronounced reduction in liquid surface tension, exceeding 55.5% relative to both commercial formulations, together with strengthened dynamic adhesion during droplet impact. These interfacial effects translated into improved weed control efficacy. In addition, the co-assembled system exhibited temperature-responsive release behavior and significantly reduced the leaching potential of the active ingredient, thereby mitigating the risk of groundwater contamination. Because rhamnolipid is produced via microbial fermentation, the formulation also confers intrinsic biodegradability and strong environmental compatibility. Consistent with this profile, safety assessments revealed reduced adverse effects on non-target organisms, including zebrafish, earthworms, and human cells, compared with commercial formulations. Together, these results establish a practical supramolecular self-assembly strategy for water-based nano-herbicides that enhances dynamic wettability and efficacy against young hydrophobic weeds while enabling controllable release and reduced non-target toxicity, offering a blueprint for the rational design of environmentally benign nano-pesticide systems based on natural biosurfactants.Fig. 1Conceptual framework illustrating the design of co-assembled 2,4-D NPs and wetting behaviors on hydrophobic leaf surfaces. Results and discussion

Co-assembly and characterization of 2,4-D NPs

The preparation of co-assembled nanoparticles composed of 2,4-D and rhamnolipid (RHL) is illustrated in Fig. 2a. Briefly, 2,4-D was dissolved in ethanol and RHL in water, after which the ethanolic solution was added dropwise to the aqueous phase at predefined mass ratios. Vigorous stirring combined with ultrasonication facilitated supramolecular self-assembly. Systematic variation of the RHL-to-2,4-D mass ratio revealed a pronounced influence on nanoparticle characteristics (Fig. 2b). As the ratio decreased, both the hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential increased, whereas the polydispersity index (PDI) exhibited a U-shaped dependence, initially decreasing and subsequently increasing. These trends were also reflected at the macroscopic level: suspensions became progressively more turbid at lower ratios, and at a ratio of 1:0.160, large aggregates formed and rapidly precipitated, accompanied by a marked reduction in zeta potential to 12.4 mV (Fig. 2c). Considering colloidal stability, particle size distribution, and surface charge collectively, a mass ratio of 1:0.080 was identified as optimal and is hereafter denoted as 2,4-D NPs. At this composition, the suspension exhibited a pronounced Tyndall effect (Fig. 2b and d), with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 134.33 nm, a narrow size distribution (PDI = 0.02), and a zeta potential of 33.7 mV. High-performance liquid chromatography further determined a loading capacity of 7.36% and an encapsulation efficiency of 71.19%. To elucidate the noncovalent assembly mechanism between 2,4-D and RHL, proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy was performed (Fig. 2e and h). The 1H NMR spectra revealed clear distinctions between the co-assembled nanoparticles and the corresponding parent molecules. Notably, the carboxyl proton signal of 2,4-D at 13.15 ppm disappeared upon nanoparticle formation, accompanied by a notable chemical shift of the adjacent methylene protons (Δδ = 0.12 ppm; Table S3). In contrast, proton signals located progressively farther from the carboxyl group exhibited smaller shifts (Fig. 2e), indicating that the carboxyl moiety of 2,4-D serves as the primary interaction site with RHL. In parallel, RHL displayed its largest chemical shift (Δδ = 0.11 ppm; Table S4) for the methylene protons adjacent to the carbonyl group, suggesting that both the carbonyl and carboxyl functionalities of RHL participate in intermolecular interactions with 2,4-D. Additional structural insight was obtained from two-dimensional nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (2D NOESY; Fig. 2h), which revealed distinct cross-peaks between the methylene protons adjacent to the 2,4-D carboxyl group and the methyl protons of the β-hydroxy fatty acid residues of RHL [28]. These correlations confirm that weak intermolecular interactions drive the co-assembly process by bringing the two molecules into close spatial proximity.

Molecular dynamics simulations of co-assembly

The NMR analyses indicated that 2,4-D and RHL interact through weak noncovalent forces, prompting molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to further elucidate the assembly pathway and underlying intermolecular interactions [29]. Representative snapshots of the co-assembly process are shown in Fig. 3a, illustrating how initially dispersed molecules progressively converge into a stable nanostructure over a 100 ns simulation. During the initial 0–40 ns, the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of both 2,4-D and RHL exhibited substantial fluctuations, reflecting continuous conformational rearrangements as the molecules explored energetically favorable interaction modes (Figure S3). Beyond 40 ns, the RMSD stabilized at approximately 3.6 nm, indicating that the system had reached a dynamically equilibrated state. Consistent with this evolution, the radius of gyration (Rg) decreased from higher initial values to approximately 2.15 nm (Figure S4), while the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) steadily declined and plateaued at around 136.0 nm2 (Figure S5). The temporal evolution of hydrogen bonding further supports the proposed assembly pathway (Fig. 3c). The number of heteromolecular hydrogen bonds between 2,4-D and RHL increased from zero to approximately ten during the simulation and remained stable thereafter, indicating persistent intermolecular interactions at equilibrium. In parallel, homomolecular hydrogen bonds stabilized at around 80 for RHL and at approximately one for 2,4-D, providing additional structural stabilization within the assembled nanostructure. Energetic analyses revealed a concomitant strengthening of both electrostatic and van der Waals interactions throughout the assembly process, which stabilized at approximately -2000 kJ mol−1 and -500 kJ mol−1, respectively (Fig. 3d). These trends underscore the thermodynamic favorability of co-assembly and highlight the dominant contribution of electrostatic interactions. Consistent with this interpretation, the binding free energy converged to approximately -330 kcal mol−1 (Figure S6), in agreement with the interaction energy profile. At the molecular level, the assembled nanoparticles are stabilized by a combination of noncovalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and π-alkyl contacts (Fig. 3b).

Deposition effects and mechanism on hydrophobic PTFE surfaces

Following spray application, the initial interaction between droplets and leaf surfaces governs deposition, spreading, and retention, thereby determining the fraction of active ingredient available for plant uptake [30, 31]. Weed leaves are typically covered by hydrophobic cuticles and possess complex surface microstructures, which impede wetting, weaken adhesion, and promote rapid runoff [32]. Consequently, a substantial proportion of applied herbicides fails to reach the biological target and instead contributes to environmental loading. Enhancing droplet wettability and interfacial retention across diverse weed species under realistic conditions is therefore critical for improving herbicide bioavailability and persistence.

We next evaluated the interfacial wettability of 2,4-D NPs in comparison with two commercial formulations and examined the underlying physicochemical basis. At an identical active ingredient concentration of 1000 mg L−1, the density, viscosity, and surface tension of each formulation were measured. The density and viscosity of 2,4-D NPs were approximately 1.00 g cm−3 and 0.89 mPa s, respectively (Figure S7), values essentially indistinguishable from those of pure water. This indicates that suppression of droplet rebound is unlikely to arise from enhanced viscous dissipation of kinetic energy. In contrast, pronounced differences were observed in surface tension. Pure water exhibited a surface tension of 72.2 mN m−1, while 2,4-D sodium salt and dimethylamine salt formulations reduced this value by less than 3%. By comparison, 2,4-D NPs decreased the surface tension to 31.3 mN m−1, highlighting the substantial capacity of supramolecular assemblies formed via rhamnolipid self-assembly to modulate interfacial properties and promote droplet wettability (Fig. 4b).Fig. 4Wetting behavior and mechanism of 2,4-D NPs droplets on hydrophobic surfaces. This advantage was clearly manifested in both static and dynamic wetting behaviors. Using smooth hydrophobic polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) films as a model substrate (Fig. 4a), the static contact angle of 2,4-D NPs was 56.19°, nearly half that of pure water (110.95°). By contrast, the two commercial formulations produced only modest reductions in contact angle, consistent with their limited ability to reduce surface tension. Because spray droplets impact leaf surfaces at finite velocities, dynamic liquid–solid interactions are equally critical for determining deposition and retention. To capture this process, tensile adhesion experiments were conducted in the vertical direction, revealing a characteristic sequence of droplet approach, contact, elongation, retraction, and eventual separation (Fig. 4d). Relative to pure water, all three 2,4-D formulations exhibited enhanced liquid-bridge elongation and delayed filament rupture, with 2,4-D NPs displaying the most pronounced effect. Quantitative analysis corroborated these observations. The residual mass of 2,4-D NPs reached 6.00 mg, markedly exceeding that of pure water (0.84 mg), 2,4-D Na (1.28 mg), and 2,4-D DMA (2.00 mg). Likewise, the rupture distance for 2,4-D NPs was 2.19 mm, substantially larger than those measured for pure water (1.60 mm), 2,4-D Na (1.62 mm), and 2,4-D DMA (1.63 mm) (Fig. 4c). Collectively, these results demonstrate that 2,4-D NPs substantially enhance liquid–solid interactions by reducing surface tension, leading to improved static wettability, as reflected by smaller contact angles, and strengthened dynamic adhesion, as evidenced by increased rupture distances and residual masses.

Dynamic impact behavior on hydrophobic weed leaves

Contact angle and tensile adhesion experiments on PTFE indicated a clear enhancement of dynamic adhesion. To elucidate how this advantage manifests during transient interfacial dynamics, droplet impact events were captured using high-speed imaging. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, an impacting droplet undergoes a characteristic sequence of free fall, substrate contact, rapid spreading within ~ 5 ms, retraction between ~ 5 and 10 ms, and rebound between ~ 15 and 25 ms. Pure water, 2,4-D Na, and 2,4-D DMA droplets all exhibited pronounced rebound behavior, whereas droplets containing 2,4-D NPs wetted the PTFE surface almost instantaneously and displayed only minimal rebound. This distinction is clearly reflected in the temporal profiles of the normalized spreading diameter and rebound height (Fig. 5b and c). Although the maximum spreading diameter appeared comparable among the formulations, this apparent similarity likely arises from the substantially reduced surface tension of 2,4-D NPs, which promotes early necking and partial breakup during the pinning stage, thereby reducing the effective initial droplet diameter and volume. Despite this initial reduction, the 2,4-D NP droplets maintained a markedly larger spreading diameter from ~ 9 ms onward (Fig. 5b). Rebound was strongly suppressed, with the normalized rebound height (Ht/D0) reaching only 0.427 at 14 ms before decaying, compared with values of 1.351 for pure water, 1.209 for 2,4-D Na, and 1.030 for 2,4-D DMA (Fig. 5c), all of which continued to increase in amplitude over the same time window.Fig. 5Dynamic impact behavior of droplets on a hydrophobic surface. To elucidate the momentum pathways underlying these behaviors, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were performed using a volume-of-fluid (VOF) approach, and the resulting velocity vector fields were analyzed [33, 34]. The positional maps and velocity distributions shown in Fig. 5d closely reproduce the experimentally observed impact stages and reveal a tendency of 2,4-D NPs to sustain strong horizontal velocity components over the time window from -0.6 to 60 ms. Decomposition of the velocity fields into horizontal and vertical components (Figures S8-S11) highlights clear divergences among the formulations. Upon impact, the downward momentum imparted by gravity is redistributed into lateral motion along the substrate. For pure water, 2,4-D Na, and 2,4-D DMA, a rapid development of reversed vertical velocity occurs, driving obvious rebound. By contrast, droplets containing 2,4-D NPs preserve and progressively amplify lateral motion, such that horizontal kinetic energy dominates the impact dynamics at later times. These simulation results are consistent with the normalized metrics shown in Figures S12-S13 and quantitatively capture the practical advantage of the nanoparticle formulation. Relative to the commercial formulations, 2,4-D NPs increased the spreading diameter by more than 57% and reduced rebound height by at least 35%, redirecting impact energy from vertical rebound into surface coverage and interfacial adhesion.

Dynamic impact behavior on hydrophobic weed leaves

Compared with the broad, rigid hydrophobic leaves of crops such as cabbage and cucumber, weed control at the juvenile stage is often more effective but also more challenging in terms of spray deposition. During spraying, the kinetic energy of impacting droplets readily induces leaf motion, particularly for young leaves that are small, soft, and lightweight. This dynamic response degrades interfacial wettability and complicates the attainment of effective wetting and retention [35].

To evaluate interfacial performance on realistic weed substrates, we examined two representative broadleaf species, Medicago falcata L. and Trifolium pratense L., together with barnyardgrass (Echinochloa crus-galli) as a representative grass weed. Static contact angle measurements confirmed strong hydrophobicity across all three species, with pure water exhibiting contact angles of approximately 134–135°. The two commercial formulations, 2,4-D Na and 2,4-D DMA, produced only marginal reductions in wettability. Even for the better-performing 2,4-D DMA, contact angles remained high at 129.36°, 132.45°, and 126.68° on the three leaf types, respectively, consistent with trends observed on PTFE substrates. By contrast, 2,4-D NPs markedly decreased the contact angles to 55.71°, 70.06°, and 68.47° on the three species, respectively, nearly halving the values relative to pure water (Fig. 6a). We next quantified the liquid holding capacity (LHC) per unit leaf area to further assess droplet retention on weed surfaces. As shown in Fig. 6b, both commercial formulations exhibited consistently poor performance, with LHC values remaining below 2.5 mg·cm−2 across all three weed species. Measurements of static contact angles and liquid holding capacity, however, provide only limited insight into the transient dynamics that ultimately govern droplet behavior under realistic spray conditions. To resolve these processes, high-speed imaging was employed to capture droplet deposition and spreading following free fall from a height of 6 cm (Figure S14).

On the surfaces of three hydrophobic weed species, droplets containing 2,4-D NPs reached their maximum spreading diameter more rapidly; however, their normalized maximum spreading diameter (D/D₀) was consistently slightly lower than that of the commercial formulations (Fig. 6c). For example, on Echinochloa crus-galli leaves, droplets of 2,4-D Na and 2,4-D DMA attained maximum spreading diameters of D/D0(max) = 2.11 and 2.40, respectively, at 7 ms, whereas 2,4-D NP droplets reached their peak spreading earlier, at 5 ms, with a lower D/D₀(max) of 1.966 (Fig. 6d, f, and h). This behavior can be attributed to the multiscale surface roughness and intrinsic curvature of hydrophobic weed leaves. During the early stages of droplet impact, these surface characteristics facilitate the formation of numerous pinning sites, which hinder lateral spreading and induce repeated pinning-depinning cycles [36, 37]. As a result, the wetted area expands more gradually despite the rapid initial spreading. Similar dynamic spreading behaviors of surfactant-laden droplets impacting cucumber leaves have been reported by He et al.[38], supporting this interpretation. Interestingly, droplets containing 2,4-D Na and 2,4-D DMA induced only minor blade vibrations, whereas impacts by 2,4-D nanoparticle droplets generated substantially stronger elastic rebound, occasionally causing the weed leaves to momentarily lift off the supporting surface (Fig. 6e, g, and i).

From an energy-conservation perspective, the post-impact dynamics of droplets on leaf surfaces can be described by the redistribution of momentum during impact. Droplets from the commercial formulations detached rapidly after impact, carrying away most of their upward momentum, which limited the kinetic energy transferred to the leaf and resulted in a relatively low rebound height. In contrast, 2,4-D nanoparticle droplets remained adherent and moved synchronously with the leaf, enabling a larger fraction of longitudinal momentum to be transmitted to the substrate. This enhanced dynamic coupling led to a more pronounced elastic rebound, while simultaneously reducing the energy available for lateral spreading, revealing an intrinsic trade-off between rebound height and spreading extent.

These results indicate that, in contrast to the broad and rigid leaves of typical crops, juvenile weed leaves are small, flexible, and highly hydrophobic, allowing them to actively interact with the impacting droplets and undergo significant deformation. Their compliance and intrinsic curvature fundamentally alter the energy distribution between the droplet and the substrate, leading to a coupled dynamic behavior where spreading and rebound evolve in a coordinated manner. In plant-protection UAV spraying scenarios, droplets are smaller yet possess much higher impact momentum, causing more pronounced leaf deformation and displacement. More importantly, the elastic recovery of the leaf can expel adhered droplets, further compromising dynamic wettability.

To emulate this process, comparative experiments were conducted using a laboratory spray tower (Figure S15), where potted weeds were fixed in place and the spray nozzle was translated to simulate a passing spray. For all three weed species, the leaves exhibited a characteristic sequence: initial droplet contact, impact-induced deformation, elastic recovery, and eventual stabilization. The results show that neither pure water (Figure S16) nor the commercial formulations 2,4-D Na and 2,4-D DMA achieved effective wetting (Fig. 7); by the end of the experiment, almost no droplets remained on the leaf surfaces. Notably, although 2,4-D DMA temporarily adhered to Medicago falcata L. leaves, nearly all droplets were expelled upon leaf recoil. In contrast, 2,4-D NPs consistently maintained stable wetting during the recovery phase, with numerous small droplets remaining on the leaf surface (Fig. 7). These findings suggest that future spraying strategies for hydrophobic weeds should consider not only the physicochemical properties of droplets, but also their mechanical interactions with deformable leaf surfaces, as this coupling governs dynamic wetting efficiency and pesticide retention.

Stimuli-responsive release and leaching behaviors

The release behavior of herbicide nanoparticles is crucial to their efficacy [39, 40]. To evaluate the effects of temperature and pH on release properties, we investigated the release kinetics of 2,4-D NPs under varying conditions. As shown in Fig. 8a, cumulative release in pure water increased gradually with rising temperature. After 48 h, the cumulative release reached 83.12% at 35 °C, slightly higher than the values observed at 15 °C (79.73%) and 25 °C (80.79%). Further analysis of the early release phase, within the first three hours, revealed intrinsic dissolution rates (IDR) of 0.4501 mg/h at 15 °C, 0.5226 mg/h at 25 °C, and 0.5525 mg/h at 35 °C (Fig. 8b). These results indicate that higher temperatures promote the release of 2,4-D, which may enhance its herbicidal effectiveness in warmer environments. In addition to temperature, pH also influenced the release behavior. At 25 °C, we examined the release profiles of 2,4-D NPs at pH 5.4, 6.8, and 8.0 (Fig. 8d). Although the variation was less pronounced than with temperature, the cumulative release after 48 h was higher under alkaline conditions, reaching 81.40% at pH 8.0 compared to 77.50% at pH 5.4 and 79.55% at pH 6.8. The intrinsic dissolution rate (IDR) followed a different trend (Fig. 8e). The lowest IDR was observed at pH 5.4 (0.6497 mg/h), while pH 6.8 and 8.0 resulted in similar rates of 0.6792 mg/h and 0.6709 mg/h, respectively. The enhanced release under alkaline conditions can be attributed to two synergistic effects. First, the increased surface charge density of RHL at higher pH promotes hydrolysis and accelerates the cleavage of aliphatic ester bonds, relaxing the nanoparticle structure and facilitating 2,4-D diffusion. Second, at high pH, 2,4-D undergoes deprotonation (pKa ≈ 2.8), which significantly improves its aqueous solubility and further accelerates the release [42, 43]. To further clarify the release mechanism, the data were fitted to four kinetic models: zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Ritger-Peppas. As presented in Fig. 8c, f, and Table S5, the first-order model consistently provided the best fit (R2 > 0.93), suggesting that the release of 2,4-D follows a coupled dissolution-diffusion process, characterized by non-Fickian diffusion, where the release rate decreases as the herbicide concentration declines [44].

It is well established that 2,4-D Na and 2,4-D DMA exhibit exceptionally high water solubility, making them prone to leaching into groundwater during irrigation or rainfall, thus posing risks to aquatic ecosystems [45, 46]. To assess the leaching behavior of the nano-formulation, we developed a simulated soil leaching system (Fig. 8g) and compared its performance with that of two commercial formulations. As shown in Fig. 8h-i, the daily and cumulative leaching profiles over fifteen days clearly demonstrate the controlled-release characteristics of 2,4-D NPs. Throughout the experiment, the maximum daily leaching of 2,4-D NPs remained below 22% and decreased to nearly undetectable levels after day 14, indicating significantly delayed release. In contrast, 2,4-D Na reached its peak leaching as early as day 5 (38.97%). Both 2,4-D DMA and 2,4-D NPs exhibited peak leaching on day 6, but the release from 2,4-D DMA (32.52%) was substantially higher than that of 2,4-D NPs (21.31%), a difference attributed to the distinct solubility of the active ingredients. The cumulative leaching data further highlight these differences: 2,4-D Na was nearly completely released by day 9, and 2,4-D DMA by day 10, whereas 2,4-D NPs showed continued release up to day 14. These findings underscore the ability of 2,4-D NPs to retard pesticide migration and mitigate groundwater contamination. The controlled-release performance of this nano-formulation is therefore crucial for reducing herbicide impacts on aquatic environments and protecting aquatic ecosystems.

Greenhouse herbicidal efficacy

2,4-D is a synthetic auxin herbicide that mimics the endogenous phytohormone indole-3-acetic acid [47]. By perturbing auxin signaling, 2,4-D induces abnormal cell division, disrupts protein synthesis, and stimulates the accumulation of stress hormones such as ethylene and abscisic acid, leading to tissue malformation, chlorosis, wilting, and plant death [48, 49]. In this study, all three weeds mentioned above were selected as model species. Visual inspection, along with plant height and fresh weight measurements, were used for assessment. As shown in Fig. 9a, d, and g, untreated controls exhibited vigorous growth with dark green leaves. Treatment with 2,4-D formulations produced characteristic auxinic symptoms, including leaf curling, wrinkling, and chlorosis, which were most pronounced in the broadleaf species. In the controls, both species reached an average height of ~ 5.2 cm. Across all dosages, height inhibition by 2,4-D Na and 2,4-D DMA remained below 30% (Fig. 9b and e). In contrast, at the highest dosage of 1200 g a.i. ha−1, 2,4-D NPs suppressed plant height by 41.06% in Medicago falcata L. and 19.42% in Trifolium pratense L. Fresh weight inhibition further underscored the superiority of 2,4-D NPs. In Medicago falcata L., inhibition by 2,4-D NPs and 2,4-D DMA was comparable at 400 and 800 g a.i. ha−1, both significantly exceeding that of 2,4-D Na. At the highest dosage, 2,4-D NPs reduced fresh weight by 78.21% (Fig. 9c). Broadleaf weeds possess large, exposed leaf areas and a well-developed cambium with continuous vascular bundles, features that facilitate foliar uptake and phloem-mediated transport of herbicide to meristematic tissues, accelerating necrosis [50, 51]. In contrast, grasses such as Echinochloa crus-galli have scattered vascular bundles and limited cambial development, making them less responsive to auxin perturbation and reducing their sensitivity to 2,4-D [51]. In our Echinochloa crus-galli assays, plants treated with 2,4-D Na or 2,4-D DMA maintained heights of 17–19 cm, and fresh-weight inhibition did not exceed 31% (Fig. 9h). By contrast, 2,4-D NPs exhibited significantly higher efficacy, even at lower doses. At 1200 g a.i. ha−1, treated leaves exhibited pronounced twisting and chlorosis, with nearly complete de-greening and fresh-weight inhibition of 61.54%, more than double that of the commercial formulations (Fig. 9i, Table S8 and S11). These results demonstrate that 2,4-D NPs outperform commercially available sodium and dimethylamine salts against both highly sensitive broadleaf species and auxin-insensitive grasses in a dose-dependent manner, establishing them as promising candidates for next-generation broad-spectrum herbicides.

Toxicity toward soil earthworms was assessed in a 48-h filter paper assay (Fig. 10b) [54]. LC50 values followed the trend: 2,4-D Na (36.225 mg·L−1) < 2,4-D DMA (169.796 mg·L−1) < < 2,4-D NPs (561.650 mg·L−1). Survival data corroborated this ranking: 2,4-D NPs maintained 100% survival below 12.5 mg·L−1, over 90% survival below 100 mg·L−1, and 33.3% survival even at 1000 mg·L−1. In contrast, 2,4-D DMA reduced survival to less than 30% above 100 mg·L−1 and caused complete mortality at 1000 mg·L−1. 2,4-D Na showed only marginally better performance, with survival dropping to 3.3% at 1000 mg·L−1. These results demonstrate that, compared to the commercial salts, 2,4-D NPs markedly reduce toxicity to soil invertebrates. Together with zebrafish assays, these findings highlight the superior environmental compatibility of 2,4-D NPs.

With the continued global use of 2,4-D in agriculture, concerns regarding its potential risks to human health have intensified [55]. Evidence suggests that its toxicological spectrum extends beyond established hepatotoxicity and neurotoxicity to include disruption of fundamental cellular processes, perturbation of cell cycle progression, impairment of immune function, and altered expression of DNA repair genes [56–58]. To assess the cytotoxicity of different 2,4-D formulations, human cardiomyocytes (AC16) and hepatocytes (MIHA) were exposed to varying concentrations of 2,4-D Na, 2,4-D DMA, and 2,4-D NPs. Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay, and live/dead staining was employed to evaluate cell fate. Cytotoxicity was both dose- (Fig. 10c and e) and time-dependent (Fig. 10d, f); however, at equivalent concentrations and extended exposures, 2,4-D NPs consistently exhibited lower toxicity compared to the commercial salts (Fig. 10i).

To assess whether cytotoxicity was linked to oxidative stress, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were quantified using the DCFH-DA probe. In MIHA cells at 400 μM, relative fluorescence intensities were 2.29 for 2,4-D DMA, 2.04 for 2,4-D Na, and 1.73 for 2,4-D NPs. In AC16 cells at 200 μM, the respective values were 3.32, 2.52, and 2.18. Using the NP group as reference, ROS levels induced by 2,4-D DMA increased 1.32-fold in MIHA and 1.52-fold in AC16, while 2,4-D Na increased ROS 1.18- and 1.16-fold, respectively (Fig. 10g-h). Fluorescence imaging revealed stronger intracellular green signals in the salt-treated groups, and three-dimensional intensity plots confirmed higher peaks for DMA and Na, whereas NPs displayed a smoother profile (Fig. 10j). The ROS patterns closely paralleled the CCK-8 cytotoxicity results, indicating that 2,4-D toxicity is largely mediated by oxidative stress. Mechanistically, salt-based formulations generate transiently high free-drug concentrations that disrupt mitochondrial electron transport, induce excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, overwhelm antioxidant defenses, and ultimately trigger lipid peroxidation and programmed cell death. By contrast, the self-assembled system moderates free-drug concentration peaks and enables sustained release of the active ingredient, thereby attenuating ROS maxima, preserving mitochondrial integrity, and reducing cell death [59]. Beyond release control, RHL has been reported to exhibit intrinsic antioxidant and lipid peroxidation-inhibitory activity, potentially through free radical scavenging and modulation of membrane lipid interactions, which may further contribute to lowering intracellular ROS levels [60]. Taken together, these results indicate that 2,4-D NPs markedly alleviate toxicity in both MIHA and AC16 cells by regulating ROS dynamics, reinforcing the potential of nanoparticle platforms to mitigate herbicide cytotoxicity and support the development of safer agrochemical formulations.

Conclusions

In summary, we report a supramolecular co-assembled system composed of the herbicide 2,4-D and the surfactant RHL that effectively overcomes the long-standing challenge of dynamic wetting on young weed leaves. This system enables spray droplets to firmly adhere to, and move synchronously with, small, flexible, and hydrophobic leaf surfaces upon impact, markedly suppressing the rebound behavior characteristic of commercially available formulations. These findings underscore the critical role of mechano-interfacial coupling between droplets and deformable leaf surfaces in governing herbicide wetting, particularly given the fundamentally different wetting dynamics of juvenile weed leaves compared with mature, rigid crop leaves. Beyond interfacial behavior, the nano-delivery system exhibits stimulus-responsive release, significantly suppresses soil leaching, and mitigates oxidative stress, collectively reducing toxicity toward aquatic organisms, soil invertebrates, and human cardiac and hepatic cells. By integrating a scalable supramolecular assembly strategy with a green, bio-based surfactant, this work establishes a multifunctional nano-herbicide platform that combines high deposition efficiency, enhanced safety, and sustained bioactivity. More importantly, this supramolecular self-assembly strategy provides a promising framework for green pesticide formulation and more sustainable weed management.
