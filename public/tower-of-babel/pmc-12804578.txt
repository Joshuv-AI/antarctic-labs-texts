Advances in Bionic Therapies for Targeting Neural Circuit Reconstruction and Integration for Spinal Cord Injury
Authors: Tao Li, Zhi-Hong Zhao, Hai-Bin Tang, Zhe Chen, Zi-Wei Lu, Xue-Ling Yang, Li-Li Zhao, Ye Li, Mei-Juan Dang, Zi-Yi Chen, Gui-Lian Zhang, Ling Liu, Hong Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Spinal cord injury (SCI) is one of the most common critical illnesses, which can cause neurological deficits and disabilities of motor, sensory and autonomic nervous system in mild cases, and lead to paralysis or even death following severe trauma. Although there are currently no effective and satisfactory clinical treatments, the efforts for repair SCI never stop. Besides the traditional strategies such as drugs, surgical interventions and rehabilitative care, the bionic therapies have attracted significant attention due to its considerable promise. The bionic therapies for SCI mainly included engineered biomaterials-based approaches aiming for reconstruction of internal neural circuit and brain machine interfaces (BMI)-based technologies to integrate extrinsic control and intrinsic circuit. This review provides an extensive overview of SCI research and bionic therapies, with focus on reconstruction and integration of neural circuit, which might provide promising insights on clinical treatment.Graphical Abstract

Background

Spinal cord injury (SCI) was described as “ailment not to be treated” in Egyptian In 1700BC. Almost 4 millennia later, now there is limited progress in functional improvement in severe SCI treatment, only in 20th century did physician help keep the injured people alive. The number of individuals paralyzed by SCI was estimated over 250,000 in the United States alone and approximately 2 million worldwide(Singh et al. 2014). The incidence rate and prevalence of SCI have gradually increased due to car accidents, mining accidents, falls, sports injuries, and the global trend towards an aging population, despite the lack of precise figures for SCI patients in China(Lu et al. 2024). Not only the trauma causes physical and psychological impacts to people suffered from severe SCI, but also it imposes substantial burdens to their family and society(Cao & Krause, 2020). Given that the life expectancy of individuals paralyzed by SCI is almost same as that of healthy people(Ragnarsson 2012), it is urgent to develop effective therapeutic strategies.

Traditional strategies, including pharmacological interventions, surgery and rehabilitative measures, have demonstrated limited efficacy in cases of severe animal SCI (Shang et al. 2022). Fortunately, ongoing advances in tissue engineering and Brain Machine Interfaces (BMI) technology make bionic therapies more curative for severe SCI(Jackson & Zimmermann, 2012,Lebedev, & Nicolelis, 2017), Slutzky 2019; Cajigas & Vedantam et al. 2021, Anderson et al. 2022). The bionic therapies for SCI contain structural bionic and functional bionic treatments. The former includes engineered biomaterials-based approaches aiming for reconstruction of internal neural circuit, while the latter contains BMI-based, Brain-Machine-Spine Interfaces (BMSIs)-based, Brain-Machine-Muscle Interfaces (BMMIs)-based, epidural electrical stimulation (EES)-based therapies to integrating extrinsic control and intrinsic circuit.

This review provides an extensive overview of SCI research and treatment, highlighted in bionic therapies based on reconstruction and integration of neural circuit, which might provide promising future directions in SCI treatment.

Review

Correlation of Pathological Regions with Clinical Manifestations

The pathophysiology of spinal cord injury (SCI) includes primary and secondary mechanisms of injury, which determines the final extent of damage(Venkatesh et al. 2019; Anjum et al. 2020). Primary injury is the compression, tearing and shearing of spinal cord and blood vessels at the time of injury, which can directly cause cell death and breakdown of blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB)(Alizadeh et al. 2019; Jin et al. 2021). Secondary injury refers to a series of cellular, molecular and biochemical cascade that occur within minutes to months after injury, which can lead to further tissue damage, including neuronal death, axonal injury, ischemia and hypoxia, spinal cord edema and inflammatory reactions (Fig. 1)(Oyinbo 2011; Hilton et al. 2017; Orr & Gensel, 2018). The key pathological mechanism underlying neurological deficits after SCI is neuronal death and axonal injury, which directly affects the neural conduction (Fig. 2)(Beattie et al. 2002; Tai & Zhang, 2023). Thus, the strategies for SCI treatment are aimed to inhibit neuronal death and axonal injury, replace the dead neurons and promote axonal regeneration.

Spinal cord injury is commonly categorized into several syndromes according to clinical presentation, functional anatomy and the mechanism of injury(Venkatesh et al. 2019). The most common reported syndromes is central cord syndrome (CSS), which accounts for approximately 9% of all traumatic SCI. It typically stems from a cervical injury and presents with a greater weakness in the upper limbs than the lower limbs(Epstein & Hollingsworth, 2015). Anterior cord syndrome (ACS) results from injury to the anterior horn of the cord, is characterized by loss of motor function, as well as the loss of pain and temperature sensation (Diaz & Morales, 2016). Brown-Sequard syndrome (BSS) is typically caused by hemisection (incomplete damage to one lateral half) of the spinal cord, which is characterized by ipsilateral loss of motor function and proprioception and contralateral loss of sensitivity to pain and temperature (Rekand 2013).

Anatomic Differences of Nerve Tract Between Human and Rat

Patients with SCI typically present with more severe symptoms than those with brain injuries of comparable size, because nearly all of its regions are functionally critical (Russ et al. 2013; Callaghan et al. 2018; Nakamura et al. 2021). Thus, a deep understanding of anatomical organization of neural tract in spinal cord is crucial for developing effective bionic therapy.

The anatomical differences of neural tracks in spinal cord between human and rat must be considered, particularly when evaluating axonal damage and regeneration in animal models (Silva et al. 2014). A key difference in ascending tracts between humans and rats lies in the range of distribution of gracile nucleus and cuneate nucleus in dorsal column (Fig. 3). Within the descending tracts, there is a significant difference in the anatomical location of the corticospinal tract between human and rats. Except in anterior funiculus, the corticospinal tracts (CST) are mainly located in lateral column in human, while it travels predominantly in the ventral part of the dorsal column in rats (Fig. 3). Understanding this anatomical difference is essential for interpreting the diverse symptoms after SCI and evaluating therapeutic efficacy, particularly when assessing motor recovery in rats, as evaluated by corticospinal tract (CST) regeneration.

Bionic Therapy for Reconstruction and Integration of Neural Circuits

The symptoms following SCI are directly caused by disruption of neural impulse transmission owing to damage to spinal nerve tracts and neurons. Thus, all strategies for treating SCI, including the biomimetic therapy, are aimed at structural reconstruction or functional replacement of the damaged neural circuits(Nomura et al. 2006; Courtine & Sofroniew et al., 2019, Yang et al. 2020). The strategies of structural reconstruction are aimed to “repair the damaged pathways” by combing biomaterial with cells, cell derivatives and molecules, while strategies for functional replacement are designed to “build bypassing communication” to connect circuits through neural interface technologies.

Bionic Treatment for Structural Reconstruction and Integration

Poor microenvironment following SCI limits the structural reconstruction and integration, while integrated strategy is widely studied for its multifaceted capabilities in structural support, anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective property. Biomaterials-based combination therapy is recommended for repairing and reconstructing nerve tracts following SCI, because physical gap formation is a common pathological feature in all types of SCI (Potter et al. 2008; Tian et al. 2015). Combinatorial strategies, including biomaterial-based cell bionic, cell derivatives bionic, molecules bionic, and spinal cord organoid, have been attempted to facilitate maximal functional recovery following SCI. A summarized table outlines the mechanism, advantages, and challenges of these technologies (Table 1). Designed to mimic native tissue, biomaterial scaffolds fill the anatomical defects to overcome biological barriers to provide targeted and long-lasting repairing effects. These combinatorial bionic biomaterial systems enable the spatiotemporally controlled delivery of therapeutics directly to the injury site. This capability enhances regenerative processes like nerve regeneration and vascularization while inhibiting pathological events such as edema and pro-inflammatory responses, ultimately achieving repair efficacy unattainable by conventional means.

Bionic treatment for structural reconstruction and integration

Cell Bionic Therapy

Considering the complexity of pathological mechanism of SCI, it is difficult to utilize cells or biomimetic materials alone to address the challenges of repair. Mimicking the extracellular matrix (ECM) of spinal cord, biomaterials can support regeneration, and their combination with cells represents a promising strategy to promote repair through multiple mechanisms.

It has been reported that stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cell, Schwann cells, olfactory ensheathing cells are commonly utilized transplanted cells in SCI treatment(Gomez et al. 2018; Song et al. 2018; Yao et al. 2018; Ramotowski et al. 2019; Trawczynski et al. 2019). Three primary mechanisms are involved in SCI repair facilitated by cell transplantation: (i) the differentiation of transplanted stem cells into functional neurons; (ii) the secretion of supportive nutrients that promote neuronal survival and axonal regeneration; (iii) the regulation of the local environmental(Assinck et al. 2017; Vismara et al. 2017; Ramalho et al. 2021). When combined with biomaterials, it is important to note that different cell types may have specific compatibility requirements with different materials. Previous study has reported that a tendency for oligodendrocyte differentiation of transplanted neural progenitor cells (NPCs) within biomaterial composed of collagen, hyaluronic acid and laminin(Geissler et al. 2018). Although stem cells possess the capacity to differentiate into glial cells, enhancing its potential for neuronal differentiation represents a critical direction for repairing SCI.

The design of biomimetic scaffolds is critical for directed differentiation of loaded cells and therapy efficacy. For instance, a linearly ordered collagen aggregates (LOCAS) scaffold loaded with neural stem cells (NSCs) has been demonstrated to enhance motor function recovery in rats of SCI by promoting neuronal differentiation and nerve fiber regeneration(Xu et al. 2024). To further optimize performance, fabricated aligned collagen-fibrin hydrogels with excellent stretchability and strong adhesion, which enables the spatiotemporal release of SDF1α and paclitaxel, effectively recruited endogenous neural stem/progenitor cells and promoted their differentiation into neurons, ultimately leading to improved hindlimb locomotion after SCI in rat (Chen et al. 2022). In addition, dual-network porous collagen fiber scaffolds significantly promoted the oriented alignment and neuronal differentiation of transplanted NSCs in rat model of SCI(Zhao et al. 2024). Moreover, Koffler et al. demonstrated that µCPP 3D-printed scaffolds loaded with NPCs significantly promoted functional recovery by supporting axonal regeneration and forming new ‘neural relays’ across the injury site (Koffler et al. 2019).

Optimizing the composition of scaffolds is another key determinant for survival and functional differentiation of the transplanted cells, as well as the regeneration efficiency. The development of a composite bioink incorporating chitosan, hyaluronic acid derivatives and matrix gel promoted the survival and differentiation of NSCs, thereby significantly improving rat motor function recovery after SCI (Liu et al., 2021b). In addition, scaffolds incorporating conductive polymers into a gelatin/polyethylene glycol system promoted neurite outgrowth and neuronal differentiation of NSCs in rats after SCI (Song et al. 2023). Moreover, in rat SCI models, a dual biomimetic scaffold combining decellularized spinal cord extracellular matrix gel with gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) provided an optimized microenvironment for neuronal differentiation of menstrual blood-derived mesenchymal stem cells(He et al. 2022).

The cell-based bionic strategy aims to integrate the supporting function of biomaterials and the cellular capability in neural tissue replacement, trophic factor secretion and microenvironment modulation to promote regeneration. The cornerstone for therapeutic efficacy improvement is optimizing cell-material compatibility to ensure the long-term survival and functional differentiation of the transplanted cells. Despite the promise of transplantation of cell-loaded biomaterials for treating SCI, concerns regarding immune responses, oncogenicity and ethical issues may impede its clinical translation.

Cell Derivatives Bionic Therapy

Cellular-derivatives have attracted growing attention from researchers because they serve as effective nutrient carriers while avoiding the problems of cell-loaded biomaterials(Huang et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2019). The integration of bionic biomaterials with cell derivatives, particularly exosomes and other extracellular vesicles, represents a promising strategy for SCI treatment(An et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2025; Liao et al. 2025). Regardless of the delivery method, direct injection or loading onto biomimetic materials, exosome from diverse sources exhibited beneficial effects, including immuno-modulation, promoting axonal regeneration and remodeling of vessels(Ren et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2021a, ). The use of biomaterial-loaded exosomes enables localized and sustained release, thereby enhancing their therapeutic benefits.

The therapeutic efficacy of cell derivatives-based bionic therapy is largely determined by the compatibility of scaffold and cell derivatives. For example, incorporating CD271+CD56+ BMSC-derived exosomes into a hydrogel created a sustained release system that enhanced axonal regeneration and synaptic formation in mouse model via the miR-431-3p/RGMA axis(Sun et al. 2024a, b). Similarly, an injectable decellularized extracellular matrix gel demonstrated excellent stability and compatibility for delivering cortical neuron-derived exosomes, and promoted endogenous NSC activation and axonal regeneration in rat model of SCI(Wang et al. 2024). To enhance the targeting effect, engineered exosomes embedded into collagen-I scaffolds showed sustained release of miR-21, thereby promoting neuronal survival and reducing glial scarring in rat after SCI(Liu et al. 2022). In addition, gelatin sponges loaded with human umbilical cord MSC-derived exosomes improved motor function and reduced pain in SCI of rat (Poongodi et al. 2024).

The physicochemical properties and the responsiveness of scaffold to microenvironment are also key determinants for therapeutic efficacy. An injectable thermos-responsive hydrogel system encapsulated engineered exosomes attenuated inflammation and oxidative damage in rat model of SCI(Xiao et al. 2023). In addition, Professor Dai reported that a matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-responsive hydrogel with bionic mechanical and conductive properties loaded with the recombinant protein GST-TIMP-bFGF could promote axonal regeneration, angiogenesis and improve motor function in SCI of rat(Fan et al. 2022).

Biomaterial-supported cell derivatives therapy represents a paradigm shift in SCI treatment, a strategy which integrates the neuroregenerative and immunomodulatory potential of cell derivatives with protective support of scaffold. This cell-free strategy circumvents the practical challenges of transplanting live cells, such as poor survival, uncontrolled differentiation, and ethical concerns, by utilizing their encapsulated factors within a scaffold. As a sustained-release reservoir, optimizing the specific properties of biomaterial such as biodegradation rate, porosity, and affinity for the derivatives is pivotal for ensuring spatiotemporally controlled release and maximal functional integration with the host tissue. Despite avoiding the challenges of cell transplantation and retaining therapeutic benefits, the biomaterial-supported cell derivatives face challenges such as variability in secretome composition and unpredictable biomaterial degradation that require improved quality control.

Molecules Bionic Therapy

Spinal cord injury is a complex condition characterized by limited regenerative capacity, primarily due to a cascade of pathological processes including inflammation, oxidative stress, neurotoxic substances and glial scar formation. Biomaterial-supported molecular therapy aims to mimic and potentiate repair processes by integrating molecules or molecular drugs and microenvironment-modulating scaffolds. By enabling the local, controlled and sustained release of molecules, biomaterial-supported bionic therapy offers enhanced safety and reduced side effects(Xu et al. 2019). The combination of the structural support of the biomaterial and the bioactivity of the released molecules creates a favorable niche for repair that neither component could achieve alone.

Molecules-based bionic therapy primarily aims to promote axonal regeneration through enhancing axonal growth factors or neutralizing growth inhibitors. Nerve growth factors are commonly applied molecules promoting neuronal survival and axonal regeneration, which include brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF), glial-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), nerve growth factor (NGF), Neurotrophin-3 (NT-3) and Neurotrophin-4/5 (NT-4/5)(Anderson et al. 2018). Furthermore, a coaxial electrospun fiber membrane with nafamostat mesylate in the shell and neurotrophin-3 (NT3) in the core promoted neuronal differentiation of NSCs and improved motor and sensory recovery in mice model of SCI(Zhang et al. 2025).

An alternative therapeutic approach has focused on neutralizing substances that inhibit axonal regeneration, including Nogo, chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs)(Chambel & Cruz, 2023, Yang et al. 2024) For example, a functional self-assembling peptide hydrogel targeted at perturbing CSPG effectively supported robust axon regrowth and synaptic integration, culminating in functional recovery in rat after SCI(Hosseini et al. 2022). Furthermore, a longitudinally multi-tubular hyaluronic acid hydrogel was co-functionalized to neutralize the Nogo receptor and deliver neurotrophic factors, which mitigated inflammation and ultimately promoted functional recovery in rat model of SCI(Wen et al. 2016).

Molecules-based bionic therapy is advancing toward dynamic, multifunctional system that emulates intrinsic repair mechanisms. This platform enables the sustained and spatiotemporally controlled release of therapeutic molecules-such as neurotrophic factors, anti-inflammatory cytokines, or axon-guidance cues. The principal advantage and challenge of this strategy lies in maintaining a therapeutically concentration of molecules over a critical period. For instance, an engineered scaffold can be designed for sequential release: an initial bolus to inhibit inflammation, followed by a sustained release of molecules that promote axonal regeneration. The future challenges lie in optimizing the proportion, integration and duration of the molecules within scaffold.

Spinal Cord organoid-Based Bionic Therapy

Although significant progress has been made, treatments for neural regeneration following SCI are still unsatisfactory, as disorganized axonal growth impedes the restoration of neural connections.Spinal cord organoid-based bionic therapy aims to address these limitations by recreating a biomimetic structure and cellular diversity of spinal cord, providing region-specific neurons and glial cells that supports guided axonal regeneration, myelination, and neural circuit reconstruction(Huang et al. 2025).

Recent studies have developed various biomimetic therapeutic strategies by combining spinal cord organoids with functional biomaterials. For instance, decellularized spinal cord matrix hydrogels have been used as carriers to significantly enhance the survival and differentiation of organoids, leading to functional recovery after SCI in rat(Sun et al. 2024a, 2024b). Further advancements employ channel-patterned collagen-Matrigel scaffolds to guide organoid growth and enhance axonal regeneration post-transplantation(Ma et al. 2025). In terms of cell sources, human iPSC-derived spinal cord organoids encapsulated in GelMA hydrogel demonstrated enhanced neural integration and anti-inflammatory effects in rat SCI(Li et al. 2025). Another approach involved direct reprogramming of human astrocytes into spinal cord organoids, which differentiated into neurons and formed synaptic connections with host tissue in mice with complete SCI(Xu et al. 2023).

Although Spinal cord organoids can partially recapitulate the anatomy and cellular diversity of the spinal cord and show significant potential in SCI repair, it continues to face substantial hurdles such as the lack of vascularization, insufficient neuronal maturity, incomplete cell types, meaningless synaptic reconnection. Furthermore, manufacturing complexities and high costs pose significant challenges for translational research.

Bionic Treatment for Functional Reconstruction and Integration

For spinal cord contusion, lots of effort have been made to repair the damage by rescuing more functional cells or promoting axonal regeneration(Ahuja et al. 2017; Courtine & Sofroniew, 2019). However, biological therapies alone have demonstrated limited efficacy in cases with complete transection (Heimburger 2005; Lin 2019). Beyond replacement and rescue strategies, function below the injury level could be restored by establishing bypassing pathway to connect circuits.

Restoring limb motor function takes precedence over addressing sensory and autonomic deficits as the primary objective in treating SCI. From an engineering perspective, although nerve tracts are damaged by SCI, which results in disruption of the brain-spinal cord connection, the cell body of upper motor neurons remain intact and capable of generating motor command. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have reported that the brain continues to generate movement-intention-related neural activity even after SCI (Freund et al. 2011; Alam et al. 2016). It reminds and inspired us whether we could transmit signal through bypass circuit through application of external equipment to interface with the second-order neurons. In recent years, neural interface technologies are designed to create a bypass circuit crossing the injury site (Lobel & Lee, 2014). With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, computing and engineering, the brain-machine interfaces (BMIs)- and epidural electrical stimulation (EES)-based bionic therapy have become a new trend in bionic therapy for SCI(Lazarou et al. 2018; Cajigas & Vedantam et al. 2021; Squair 2023; Squair et al., 2021a). A summarized figure outlines the rationale, implementation strategy and the primary functional restoration of these technologies (Fig. 4). Notably, BMIs-based bionic therapies are primarily designed to engage with voluntary movement or artificial devices control, whereas EES-based strategy is aimed at restoring autonomic function.

Brain Machine Interfaces (BMIs)-Based Bionic Therapy

Movement disorder or paralysis after SCI attributed to the disrupted neural circuits between brain and spinal cord. To bypass the injured motor nerve tracts, the brain-machine interfaces (BMIs) technology establish a direct link between cerebral cortex and an designed output device help paralyzed person to perform desired movements(Shih et al. 2012).

A BMIs system that enables volitional control of an external device comprises three sequential components: signal acquisition, information extraction and translation, and device output (Fig. 5). Brain signals are first acquired by electrodes located on the scalp, cortical surface, or within the brain. Noninvasive electroencephalogram (EEG) is the most widely used approach for signal recording, owing to its minimal risk and relative convenience (Machado et al. 2010; Lin et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2019). It has been reported that the sensorimotor rhythms recorded from the scalp have been employed to control various devices, including computor cursors(Wolpaw & McFarland, 2004, McFarland et al. 2008; McFarland et al. 2010), spelling systems(Cincotti et al. 2008), hand orthoses(Pfurtscheller et al. 2000), robotic prostheses(Muller-Putz et al. 2005) and wheelchairs(Tanaka et al. 2005). As an important neurophysiologic technique, electrocorticography(ECoG) enables decoding of neural signals to control finger(Miller et al. 2009; Scherer et al. 2009; Acharya et al. 2010), hand (Gunduz et al. 2009), and arm movements(Pistohl et al. 2008). In the second step, information extraction and translation comprises analyzing the digital signals by feature extraction algorithms algorithm and converting them into the appropriate commands for the output device(Dangi et al. 2013; Shenoy & Carmena, 2014). In the final step, the output device is actuated based on decoded signals for tasks including cursor control, robotic arm operation, and other commands(Shih et al., 2012). Feedback system enables the dynamic modification of the brain signals processing to optimize device performance(Zimmermann & Jackson et al., 2014, Cajigas et al. 2021). The most significant application of BMI-based bionic therapy lies in restoring motor control of patients with artificial devices (Schwartz et al. 2006; Ohnishi et al. 2007). Researchers have developed a bionic arm for patients that provides near-natural motor control, allowing users to perform tasks with intuitive thought(Fatima et al. 2020). It has been reported that long-term ECoG recordings were stable for motor-imagery classification and motor control in patients with SCI (Davis et al. 2025).

This strategy circumvents the biologically challenging aim of regenerating severed axons by instead decoding intention-related signals from the motor cortex for direct control external assistive devices. Although the development of BMIs-based bionic therapy has generated considerable enthusiasm among scientists, engineers, clinicians and the SCI patients, a long way remains toward achieving full technological maturity(Rupp 2014). To ensure the future validation and reliability of BMI-based bionic therapy, priority must be given to improving the long-term stability and fidelity of neural recordings, developing more robust and adaptive decoding algorithms that can generalize across various tasks and environments.

Brain-Machine-Spine Interfaces (BMSIs)-Based Bionic Therapy

For individuals with quadriplegia, BMIs-based bionic therapy represents a potential solution to replace the function of upper-limb with external device (Fatima et al. 2020; Nsugbe 2021). However, the primary challenge in treating patients with paraplegia is restoring their lower limb function. Although injury prevents cortical signals from reaching the anterior horn of the cord, the neurons below the injury sites, which are responsible for volitional movements of the lower limb, are functional. Thus, it is theoretically possible to restore lower limb function by stimulating the neurons in anterior horn of cord below the SCI lesion from through decoded motor signals from the brain (Rattay et al., 2000; Yakovenko et al., 2002).

The development of brain-machine-spine interfaces (BMSIs) technology enables the creation of a bypass between the brain and spinal cord by interfacing leg motor cortex activity with an intraspinal electrical stimulator (Fig. 6). It has been reported that BMSIs-based bionic therapy successfully alleviated gait deficits in primates after SCI (Capogrosso et al., 2016), indicating its potential for treating paralyzed patients. The core technology of BMSIs is a stimulation system comprising a spatially selective epidural implant and a real-time triggered pulse generator (Capogrosso et al. 2016). In eight patients with motor-complete SCI (C5-C6), non-invasive BMSIs enabled the real-time, task-dependent regulation of spinal motor neuron activity, which was monitored via wearable muscle sensors that confirmed the voluntary control(Oliveira et al. 2024). The wireless design of the implemented systems enables unrestricted movement for the user (Liu et al., 2015).

The profound impact of BMSIs-based bionic therapy lies not only in motor recovery but also in its potential to regulate neuroplasticity. In view of the bidirectional closed-loop, this system could reinforce remaining neural pathways and promote functional reorganization within the targeted circuits. Furthermore, the ultimate goal of this therapy hinges on the integration of the BMSIs with sensory feedback of the patients to realize functional bidirectional interfaces. Future research should focus on creating closed-loop systems that not only precisely decode motor commands but also convey nociceptive and proprioceptive sensations back to the brain, creating a truly immersive and natural bionic integration. Another major challenge lies in optimizing the efficacy of BMSIs-based bionic therapy without compromising its coordination for paralyzed patient rehabilitation(van den Brand et al. 2012; Nishimura et al. 2013).

Brain-Machine-Muscle Interfaces (BMMIs)-Based Bionic Therapy

Signal interruption of impulses from the brain to targeted muscles resulted in paralysis by SCI(Xu et al. 2011). Based on deep understanding of the efferent signal and anatomy of the motor circuits, brain-machine-muscle interface (BMMI) technology offers the possibility of restoring voluntary movement in individuals with tetraplegia. Unlike BMIs that control external devices like robotic arms, BMMIs-based bionic strategy employs a bypass system to directly connect the first-order neurons in cerebral cortex and targeted muscles(Giszter et al. 2010; Alam et al. 2014; Ajiboye et al. 2017).

The BMMIs technology integrates the electroencephalogram (EEG)-recording with functional electrical stimulator (FES) to elicit contractions in paralyzed muscles (Pfurtscheller et al. 2003; Chadwick et al. 2011). The process involves the decoding of movement intentions from brain activity, followed by algorithmic processing and translation into commands for electrical stimulation devices. The interface completes the loop by providing sensory feedback from the limbs back to the brain, enhancing movement accuracy and promoting neural plasticity. Zhou et al. developed a dual electrical stimulation system that combines epidural electrical with muscle stimulation to emulate feedforward and feedback electrical control in spinal sensory motor circuits, which not only promotes the expression of axonal regeneration-related genes in motor neurons, but also increases the excitability of spinal neurons in mice with complete SCI (Zhou et al. 2024).

Despite its promising potential, it is of paramount importance to address some challenges, including ensuring the structural and functional longevity of implanted devices, defining stimulation parameters that prevent muscle fatigue and injury. More importantly, it is a technological challenge to replicating the complex sequence of individual muscle activation patterns underlying natural and adaptive locomotor movements.

Biomimetic Epidural Electrical Stimulation (EES)

Spinal cord injury not only leads to motor and sensory dysfunction, but also induces severe autonomic nervous system disorders, such as hemodynamic instability, which increases the risk of life-threatening hypotension, hinders neurological recovery(Squair et al. 2017), even affects survival of patients(Inoue et al. 2014). Interruption of supraspinal efferent signals to sympathetic circuits typically resulted in autonomic dysfunction, and injury of thoracic spinal segments were identified as underlying cause of haemodynamic instability. In 2021, Squair et al. presented a sophisticated neuroprosthetic system, a biomimetic, closed-loop stimulator, that restored haemodynamic stability after SCI through epidural electrical stimulation (Squair et al. 2021, 2021a). The translation from preclinical models to human with chronic SCI demonstrates its significant clinical potential. Furthermore, recently, to lighten burden of chronic hypotensive complications in SCI patients, the same team developed a purpose-built implantable system based on biomimetic EES and demonstrated that it decreased the need for pharmacological interventions and improved quality of life(Phillips et al. 2025).

The implantable biomimetic EES not only establish a path forward to manage treatment-resistant hypotensive complications due to SCI, but also pave the way for future clinical trials. Considering that the interplay between stimulation parameters for cardiovascular control and functional recovery remains largely unexplored, the translational application demands the development of closed-loop platform that can adapt stimulation depending on real-time arterial pressure-based feedback.

Conclusions

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe trauma to patients and remains devastating problem for society. Despite promising results in animal experiments, these therapeutic approaches have encountered significant obstacles in clinical trials. The development of innovative strategies relies on a deep understanding of underlying mechanisms responsible for neurological disorders after SCI.

The primary cause of neurological deficits after SCI is the disruption of neural impulse transmission due to injury of the spinal cord tracts. Thus, the novel bionic strategies that integrate biology and engineering show promising potential for SCI treatment by focusing on structural reconstruction and functional restoration of the damaged neural circuits.

The bionic strategies for structural reconstruction integrate tissue-compatible biomaterials with therapeutic agents such as cells, cell-derived factors or neurotrophic factors. In addition to replacing severely damaged cord and bridging the lesion gap, engineered spinal cord materials can also be fabricated to support transplanted cell survival and provide controlled release of neurotrophic factors, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes. In addition to biological bionic strategies, the latest artificial intelligence methods such as BMI technology can decode cortical information and directly connect with the prosthesis or the lower neuromuscular system bypass the injury segment, which in turn help patients execute the movement intention. It has been achieved promising results in both rodents and primate/human experiments. Perhaps there is still a long road to realize the goal of “making paraplegic patients stand up”, we believe that it would be more worth expecting to combine the advantages of these therapies for effective treatment of SCI. While the goal of enabling paraplegic patients to stand remains challenging, we believe that integrating various therapeutic approaches holds greater promise for effectively treating SCI.
