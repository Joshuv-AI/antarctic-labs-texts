Abstract
. We study positive energy solutions of the anisotropic Kepler problem with homogeneous potential. First some asymptotic property of positive energy solutions is obtained, as time goes to infinity. Afterwards, we prove the existence of hyperbolic solutions with given initial configuration and asymptotic behavior, when time goes to positive or negative infinity, and in the planar case, the existence of bi-hyperbolic solutions with given asymptotic behaviors, when time goes to both positive and negative infinities, under various conditions.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study the following second order differential equation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 2

(1)

\ddot{x}=\nabla U(x),

under the following condition

(U0)

\begin{cases}U(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}U(\frac{x}{|x|}),\;&\text{ with }\alpha\in(0,2);\\
U|_{\mathbb{S}}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S},\;\mathbb{R}^{+}),\;&\text{ with }\mathbb{S}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|=1\}.\end{cases}

Recall that the energy of the system, given as below, is constant along a solution

(2)

h=\frac{1}{2}|\dot{x}|^{2}-U(x).

When
\alpha=1
and
U|_{\mathbb{S}}
is a constant, it is the well-known Kepler problem, which is an integrable system. More we have explicit expressions for all solutions, which are classified as elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic solutions corresponding to negative, zero and positive energies.

In this paper, we are mainly interested in the case that
U|_{\mathbb{S}}
is not a constant, which we refer as
the anisotropic Kepler problem (with
\alpha
-homogeneous potential)
. The cases that we are mostly interested in are

(U1)

U(x)=\langle x,Mx\rangle^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}},\text{ where }M=\text{diag}(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})\text{ with each }m_{i}>0.

When
\alpha=1
and
m_{i}
’s are not identical to each other, it was first investigated by physicist Martin Gutzwiller, as it can describe the motion of an electron inside a semi-conductor with donor impurity. The problem also played an important role in the development of quantum chaos.

In
[
14
]
Gutzwiller found the famous
Gutzwiller trace formula
and applied it to this problem, when
d=2
and conditions (
U1
) and (
U4
) (given below) are satisfied. Later under the same conditions, in
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
, he further investigated periodic solutions and chaotic behaviors in this problem. Rigorous proofs were given by Devaney in
[
9
]
using McGehee’s blow-up method developed in the study of triple collision for the three body problem
[
24
]
.

For more backgrounds, we refer the readers to Gutzwiller’s book
[
17
]
. To distinguish it from the general case, when condition (
U1
) holds, we call it
Gutzwiller’s anisotropic Kepler problem
(with homogeneous
\alpha
-potential).

The homogeneous condition in (
U0
) implies the energy of bounded solutions must be negative and solutions with non-negative energies must be unbounded. Zero energy solutions have recently been studied by Barutello, Terracini and Verzini in
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
using McGehee’s blow-up method and variational methods. In
[
18
]
, Hu and the author studied the Morse indices of zero energy solutions of the planar anisotropic Kepler problem using McGehee’s blow-up method and the Maslov-type index theory
[
21
]
. More recently Barutello, Canneori and Terracini
[
1
]
explored symbolic dynamics of the planar
N
-center problem with anisotropic forces, when energies are negative but close to zero.

Meanwhile for positive energy solutions, very few results seem to be available, and it will be the main focus of our paper.

To explain our results, first let’s rewrite (
1
) in polar coordinates:
(r,s)=(|x|,\frac{x}{|x|})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S},

(3)

\begin{cases}&\ddot{r}=r|\dot{s}|^{2}+r^{-(\alpha+1)}\langle\nabla U(s),s\rangle;\\
&r\ddot{s}+2\dot{r}\dot{s}+r|\dot{s}|^{2}s=r^{-(\alpha+1)}\tilde{\nabla}U(s).\end{cases}

Here
\tilde{\nabla}U(s)
represents the gradient of
U
restricted on
\mathbb{S}

(4)

\tilde{\nabla}U(s)=\nabla U(s)-\langle\nabla U(s),s\rangle s.

Denote the set of critical points of
U
on
\mathbb{S}
as

Cr(U):=\{s\in\mathbb{S}:\;\tilde{\nabla}U(s)=0\}.

The energy identity in polar coordinates can be written as

(5)

h=\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}r^{2}|\dot{s}|^{2}-r^{-\alpha}U(s).

The problem has a singularity at the origin, where the potential is infinite. When a solution approaches the origin, it satisfies the following asymptotic estimates.

Proposition 1.1
.

[
2
, Section 4]

Given a solution
x(t)=r(t)s(t)
with
r(t)\to 0
, when
t\to t_{0}^{\pm}
, then there exist positive constants
\kappa_{\pm}
, such that the following asymptotic results hold, as
t\to t_{0}^{\pm}
,

(a).

r(t)\simeq(\kappa_{\pm}|t-t_{0}|)^{\frac{2}{2+\alpha}}
,
\dot{r}(t)\simeq\pm\frac{2\kappa_{\pm}}{2+\alpha}\big(\kappa_{\pm}|t-t_{0}|\big)^{\frac{-\alpha}{2+\alpha}}
;

(b).

U(s(t))\to\beta_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{2+\alpha}\kappa_{\pm}\right)^{2}
;

(c).

\text{dist}\big(s(t),\text{Cr}(U;\beta_{\pm})\big)\to 0
, where
\text{Cr}(U;\beta_{\pm})=\{s\in\text{Cr}(U):U(s)=\beta_{\pm}\}
.

The above type of asymptotic estimates are well-known for collision solutions in celestial mechanics (
[
29
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
12
]
). In
[
2
]
, it was generalized to a large class of quasi-homogeneous potentials with singularity including the case we are considering here.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
x(t)
be a positive
h
-energy solution of (
1
) with maximal domain
(T^{-},T^{+})
. Then either
|T^{\pm}|<\infty
or
|T^{\pm}|=\infty
. In particular,

(a).

if
T^{\pm}
is finite, then
x(t)\to 0
, when
t\to T^{\pm}
;

(b).

if
T^{\pm}=\pm\infty
, then there exists an
s^{\pm}\in\mathbb{S}
, such that

(6)

x(t)\simeq\sqrt{2h}|t|s^{\pm}+o(|t|),\;\text{ as }t\to\pm\infty.

By the above result, a positive energy solution either approaches the singularity at the origin at a finite time (forwardly or backwardly), or become unbounded as time goes to positive or negative infinity. Moreover in the later it must satisfies the asymptotic expression given in (
6
).

By Lemma
2.1
,
T^{\pm}
in the above theorem can not both be finite. Following notations from celestial mechanics, we will also refer positive energy solutions as
hyperbolic solutions
. In particular, a positive energy solution will be called
bi-hyperbolic
, if its maximal domain is
\mathbb{R}
.

With the above result, we are interested in the following two questions:

(Q1):

for any
h>0
, starting from a given initial configuration, is there an
h
energy solution with the prescribed asymptotic expression as time goes to negative or positive infinity?

(Q2):

for any
h>0
, is there an
h
-energy solution with given asymptotic expressions, when time approaches both positive and negative infinities?

For the
n
-body problem, a question similar to (Q1) was completely answered first in
[
22
]
, and later in
[
20
]
,
[
30
]
and
[
26
]
with different approaches but all variational in nature, meanwhile a question similar to (Q2) is far from being answered, up to our knowledge only partial results are available in
[
10
]
.

As we can see (Q2) is closely related with the scattering theory, and since Rutherfold the major part of our knowledge about molecules, atoms and elementary particles is obtained by scattering experiment. For more details see
[
19
, Chapter 12]
.

Our approach in this paper is variational as well. Recall that solutions of (
1
) correspond to critical points of the following action functional

(7)

\mathcal{A}(\gamma;t_{1},t_{2})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}L(\gamma,\dot{\gamma})\,dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}|^{2}+U(\gamma)\,dt,\;\gamma\in H^{1}_{t_{1},t_{2}}(p,q).

For any
p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t_{1}<t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
H^{1}_{t_{1},t_{2}}(p,q)
denotes the set of all Sobolev paths going from
p
to
q
, as time goes from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
.

Definition 1.1
.

We say
\gamma\in H^{1}_{t_{1},t_{2}}(p,q)
is
a fix-time minimizer
of the action functional
\mathcal{A}
, if

\mathcal{A}(\gamma;t_{1},t_{2})\leq\mathcal{A}(\xi;t_{1},t_{2}),\;\forall\xi\in H^{1}_{t_{1},t_{2}}(p,q),

and
a fix-time local minimizer
, if the above
\xi
’s further satisfy

\|\xi-\gamma\|_{H^{1}}<\delta,\text{ for some }\delta>0\text{ small enough.}

A minimizer, or more general a critical point of
\mathcal{A}
, is a classical solution of (
1
), only if it does not contain any singularity. We call such an minimizer
collision-free
, following notations from celestial mechanics. To apply variational methods to our problem, or any problem with singularity, it is crucial to rule out singularities in the corresponding critical points.

When
\alpha\geq 2
, it is well-known any critical point of
\mathcal{A}
with finite action value can not contain any singularity. However for
\alpha\in(0,2)
, critical points with finite action value could still contain singularities. In particular, if only condition (
U0
) is satisfied, a local minimizer may not always be collision-free.

For the
n
-body problem, a local minimizer must be collision-free. This was proven in
[
8
]
for Newtonian potential and in
[
12
]
for
\alpha\in(0,2)
using Marchal’s averaging method
[
23
]
. Such a method can be applied to the anisotropic problem, if the potential is invariant under certain rotational symmetry, and the following result was proven in
[
2
]
.

Proposition 1.2
.

[
2
, Theorem 6.1]

A fix-time local minimizer of
\mathcal{A}
is collision-free, if

(U2)

\text{there exists a two-dim linear subspace }W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ with }U|_{W\cap\mathbb{S}}=\text{Constant}.

Here we prove for Gutzwiller’s anisotropic Kepler problem, a local minimizer must be collision-free.

Proposition 1.3
.

Under condition (
U1
), a fix-time local minimizer of
\mathcal{A}
is collision-free.

Remark 1.1
.

Notice that for Gutzwiller’s anisotropic Kepler problem, condition (
U2
) is satisfied, if
m_{i}=m_{j}
, for some
i\neq j
, but not, if
m_{i}\neq m_{j}
, for any
i\neq j
. In particular, for the plane case condition (
U2
) is satisfied only when it is the Kepler problem.

Using the above propositions, we can prove the following result regarding (Q1).

Theorem 1.2
.

Under condition (
U1
) or (
U2
), for any
h>0
,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s^{\pm}\in\mathbb{S}
, there is an
h
-energy hyperbolic solution
x:[t_{0},\pm\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
x(t_{0})=x_{0}
and

x(t)\simeq\sqrt{2h}|t|s^{\pm}+o(|t|),\;\text{as }t\to\pm\infty.

Remark 1.2
.

When
x_{0}=0
, by
x(t_{0})=x_{0}
, we mean
\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}x(t)=0
.

Remark 1.3
.

From our proof of the theorem in Section
3
, one can see the above result holds, as long as the fix-time minimizers of
\mathcal{A}
are collision-free.

For (Q2), the main difficulty is the lack of coercivity, when we approach it with variational methods. In the planar case, one way to force coercivity is to impose proper topological constraint. The problem then is minimizers are not always collision-free as shown in
[
13
]
and
[
3
]
. Despite of this, we are able to give some partial answer to (Q2). To explain it, set
\mathbb{R}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{C}
and define

\tilde{U}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R};\;\theta\mapsto U(e^{i\theta}).

Denote the set of minimizers of
\tilde{U}
as

(8)

\mathcal{M}(\tilde{U}):=\{\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}:\tilde{U}(\theta_{0})\leq U(\theta),\;\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Theorem 1.3
.

When
d=2
, under the non-degenerate condition

(U3)

\tilde{U}^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{0})\neq 0,\;\forall\theta_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\tilde{U}),

for any
\theta_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
|\theta_{+}-\theta_{-}|>\pi
, there is a constant
\bar{\alpha}=\bar{\alpha}(U,\theta_{\pm})\in(0,2)
, such that for any
\alpha\in(\bar{\alpha},2)
, there is an
h
-energy bi-hyperbolic solution
x:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying

x(t)\simeq\sqrt{2h}|t|e^{i\theta_{\pm}}+o(|t|),\;\text{ as }t\to\pm\infty.

Remark 1.4
.

The constant
\bar{\alpha}
was found in
[
3
]
, for the detail see Proposition
4.1
.

While condition (
U3
) is relatively weak, the constant
\bar{\alpha}
could be larger than 1, which rules out the most important Newtonian potential. On the other hand, for Gutzwiller’s anisotropic Kepler problem, we are able to obtain a result, which holds for all
\alpha\in(0,2)
.

Theorem 1.4
.

When
d=2
, under the conditions (
U1
) and

(U4)

\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}>1+\frac{(2-\alpha)^{2}}{8\alpha}

for any pair of
s^{\pm}=s^{\pm}_{1}+is^{\pm}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|s^{\pm}|=1
, if one of the following conditions holds,

(i).

s^{+}\neq-s^{-}
,
s^{-}_{1}\neq\pm 1
and
s^{-}_{2}s^{+}_{2}\leq 0
;

(ii).

s^{+}\neq-s^{-}
,
s^{-}_{1}=\pm 1
,

there exists at least one
h
-energy bi-hyperbolic solution
x:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying

x(t)\simeq\sqrt{2h}|t|s^{\pm}+o(|t|),\;\text{as }t\to\pm\infty.

2.
Asymptotic analysis of positive energy solutions

Throughout this section, let
x(t)=r(t)s(t)
be a positive
h
-energy solution with maximal domain
(T^{-},T^{+})
. We introduce two auxiliary functions that will be needed in our proofs.

(9)

I(t)=I(x(t))=r^{2}(t);\;\;\Gamma(t)=\Gamma(x(t))=\frac{1}{2}r^{\alpha}(t)(2h-\dot{r}^{2}(t)).

Lemma 2.1

(Lagrange-Jacobi identity)
.

\forall t\in(T^{-},T^{+})
,
\ddot{I}(t)=4h+2(2-\alpha)U(x(t))>4h
.

Proof.

By a direct computation,
\dot{I}=2\langle\dot{x},x\rangle
. Then for
\alpha\in(0,2)
,

\displaystyle\ddot{I}/2

\displaystyle=|\dot{x}|^{2}+\langle x,\ddot{x}\rangle=|\dot{x}|^{2}+\langle x,\nabla U(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=2(h+U(x))-\alpha U(x)=2h+(2-\alpha)U(x)>2h.

∎

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
T^{-}<t_{0}<t_{1}<T^{+}
,

(10)

I(t_{1})\geq 2h(t_{1}-t_{0})^{2}+\dot{I}(t_{0})(t_{1}-t_{0})+I(t_{0}).

Proof.

By the above lemma,
\ddot{I}\geq 4h
, as a result for any
t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}
,

(11)

\dot{I}(t)\geq 4ht-4ht_{0}+\dot{I}(t_{0}).

Integrating both sides of the above inequality from
t_{0}
to
t_{1}
gives us the desired result.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
t\in(T^{-},T^{+})
,
\dot{\Gamma}(t)=-\frac{2-\alpha}{2}r^{\alpha+1}\dot{r}|\dot{s}|^{2}
.

Proof.

By the energy identity (
5
),

(12)

\Gamma(t)=\frac{1}{2}r^{2+\alpha}|\dot{s}|^{2}-U(s)

Then a direct computation shows

\dot{\Gamma}=\frac{2+\alpha}{2}r^{\alpha+1}\dot{r}|\dot{s}|^{2}+r^{2+\alpha}\langle\dot{s},\ddot{s}\rangle-\langle\nabla U(s),\dot{s}\rangle.

Meanwhile by taking an inner product of the second equation in (
3
) with
\dot{s}
, we get

r^{2+\alpha}\langle\dot{s},\ddot{s}\rangle=\langle\nabla U(s),s\rangle-2r^{\alpha+1}\dot{r}|\dot{s}|^{2}.

Plugging this into the previous equation gives us the desired result.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

If
\dot{I}(t_{0})\geq 0
for some
t_{0}\in(T^{-},T^{+})
, then for any
t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<T^{-}
,

(13)

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|\dot{s}|\,dt\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}\frac{\sqrt{2hr^{\alpha}(t_{0})+2U_{max}}}{(2h)^{\frac{2+\alpha}{4}}}(t_{1}-t_{0})^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

Proof.

Notice that
\dot{r}(t)>0
,
\forall t>t_{0}
, as the assumption and (
11
) imply

\dot{I}(t)=2r(t)\dot{r}(t)\geq 4h(t-t_{0})+\dot{I}(t_{0})>0,\;\forall t>t_{0}.

Then Lemma
2.3
implies
\dot{\Gamma}(t)\leq 0
,
\forall t>t_{0}
. As a result, when
r_{0}=r(t_{0})
,

\Gamma(t)\leq\Gamma(t_{0})\leq hr^{\alpha}_{0},\;\forall t>t_{0}.

Let
C_{1}=2U_{max}
, then the above inequality and (
12
) imply

r^{2+\alpha}(t)|\dot{s}(t)|^{2}\leq 2\big(hr_{0}^{\alpha}+U(s(t))\big)\leq 2hr_{0}^{\alpha}+C_{1}.

Meanwhile according to (
10
),

I(t)=r^{2}(t)\geq 2h(t-t_{0})^{2},\;\forall t>t_{0}.

Combining these estimates, we get

|\dot{s}(t)|\leq\left(\frac{2hr_{0}^{\alpha}+C_{1}}{r^{2+\alpha}(t)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2hr_{0}^{\alpha}+C_{1}}}{(2h(t-t_{0})^{2})^{\frac{2+\alpha}{4}}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2hr_{0}^{\alpha}+C_{1}}}{(2h)^{\frac{2+\alpha}{4}}}(t-t_{0})^{-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

The result now follows from a direct integration.
∎

With the above lemmas, we can prove the following asymptotic estimates.

Proposition 2.1
.

When
T^{\pm}=\pm\infty
, the following asymptotic estimates hold.

(a).

r(t)=\sqrt{2h}|t|+o(|t|)
, as
t\to\pm\infty
.

(b).

there is a
s^{\pm}\in\mathbb{S}
, such that
\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}s(t)=s^{\pm}
.

Proof.

We only give the details for
T^{+}=\infty
.

(a). By Lemma
2.1
and
2.2
,
r^{2}(t)=I(t)\to\infty
and

(14)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{I}{t^{2}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\dot{I}}{2t}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\ddot{I}}{2}=2h+\lim_{t\to\infty}2(2-\alpha)\frac{U(s)}{r^{\alpha}}=2h,

As
\dot{I}=2r\dot{r}
, plugging this into the above identities, we get

2h=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{r\dot{r}}{t}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(t^{-2}I\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\dot{r}=\sqrt{2h}\lim_{t\to\infty}\dot{r}.

Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}\sqrt{I/t^{2}}=\sqrt{2h}
, we get

\lim_{t\to\infty}\dot{r}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{r}{t}=\sqrt{2h}.

This gives us the desired property.

(b). By Lemma
2.3
,
\Gamma(t)
is non-increasing for
t
large enough, this implies

(15)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\Gamma(t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}r^{2+\alpha}|\dot{s}|^{2}-U(s)=C_{1},

and
\frac{1}{2}r^{2+\alpha}|\dot{s}|^{2}\leq C_{2}
, for some constant
C_{2}>0
. Then for
t
large enough

(16)

|\dot{s}(t)|\leq\sqrt{2C_{2}}r(t)^{-\frac{2+\alpha}{2}}\leq C_{3}t^{-\frac{2+\alpha}{2}}.

This means for
t_{1}>t_{0}
large enough,

\lim_{t_{2}\to\infty}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|\dot{s}(t)|\,dt\leq\lim_{t_{2}\to\infty}\frac{\alpha}{2}C_{2}(t_{1}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}-t_{2}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}})\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}C_{2}t_{1}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}<\infty,

which implies the convergence of
s(t)
, when
t\to\infty
.
∎

Theorem
1.1
now follows directly from the above results.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

(a). By the existence theorem of ordinary differential equations, if
T^{\pm}
is finite, when
t\to T^{\pm}
,
x(t)
must approach to the set of singularities of
U
, which in our case is a single point corresponding to the origin.

(b). This follows from Proposition
2.1
, as

\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\frac{x(t)}{|t|}=\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\frac{r(t)}{|t|}s(t)=\sqrt{2h}s^{\pm}.

∎

3.
Hyperbolic solutions with given initial configuration

A proof of Theorem
1.2
will be given in this section. To obtain solutions with prescribed energy, instead of fix-time minimizers, we will look for free-time minimizers defined as below.

Definition 3.1
.

For any
h>0
, define
the action potential

\phi_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\phi_{h}(p,q)=\inf\left\{\mathcal{A}_{h}(\xi;\tau_{1},\tau_{2}):=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}L(\xi,\dot{\xi})+h\,dt:\;\xi\in\cup_{-\infty<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\infty}H^{1}_{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}(p,q)\right\}.

We say
\gamma\in H^{1}_{t_{1},t_{2}}(p,q)
is
a
h
-free-time minimizer
, if

\mathcal{A}_{h}(\gamma;t_{1},t_{2})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}L(\gamma,\dot{\gamma})+h\,dt=\phi_{h}(p,q).

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
h>0
and
p\neq q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists at least one
\gamma\in H^{1}_{0,T}(p,q)
satisfying

\mathcal{A}_{h}(\gamma;0,T)=\phi_{h}(p,q).

Moreover if
\gamma
is collision-free, it is an
h
-energy solution of (
1
).

Proof.

First let’s show the existence of such a
\gamma
. For this, define a function
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
as

f(T)=\inf\{\mathcal{A}_{h}(\xi;0,T):\;\xi\in H^{1}_{0,T}(p,q)\}.

First we claim the above infimum is in fact a minimum. As
\mathcal{A}_{h}
is lower semi-continuous, we just need to show it is coercive in
H^{1}_{0,T}(p,q)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}_{h}(\xi;0,T)\to\infty
, if
\|\xi\|_{H^{1}}\to\infty
.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for any
t\in[0,T]

(17)

|\xi(t)|\leq|\xi(t)-p|+|p|\leq\|\xi\|_{L^{1}}+|p|\leq\sqrt{T}\|\dot{\xi}\|_{L^{2}}+|p|.

Hence

\|\xi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq(\sqrt{T}\|\dot{\xi}\|_{L^{2}}+|p|)^{2}T.

As a result,
\|\dot{\xi}\|_{L^{2}}\to\infty
, if
\|\xi\|_{H^{1}}\to\infty
. This implies coercivity, as
\mathcal{A}_{h}(\xi;0,T)\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\xi}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
.

Then for any
T>0
, we can find a
\gamma_{T}\in H^{1}_{0,T}(p,q)
with
\mathcal{A}_{h}(\gamma_{T};0,T)=f(T)
. By (
17
),

f(T)=\mathcal{A}_{h}(\gamma_{T};0,T)\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\dot{\gamma}_{T}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\geq\frac{|p-q|^{2}}{2\sqrt{T}}\to\infty,\text{ if }T\to 0.

Meanwhile
f(T)\to\infty
, if
T\to\infty
, as
\mathcal{A}_{h}(\gamma_{T};0,T)\geq hT
.

As a result, it is enough to show
f(T)
is continuous. For any
\delta>0
, define

and (
47
)

\mathcal{A}(y_{n};-\delta,\delta)-\mathcal{A}(x;-\delta,\delta)=\lambda_{n}^{\frac{2-\alpha}{2+\alpha}}\left(\mathcal{A}(y^{\lambda_{n}};-\delta/\lambda_{n},\delta/\lambda_{n})-\mathcal{A}(x^{\lambda_{n}};-\delta/\lambda_{n},\delta/\lambda_{n})\right)<0,

which is absurd.
∎

6.
Bi-hyperbolic solution for Gutzwiller’s anisotropic Kepler problem

The main purpose of this section is to give a proof of Theorem
1.4
, so we will only consider the planar case.

Lemma 6.1
.

Under condition (
U4
), if
x:[T,0]\to\mathbb{C}
is a collision solution with
x(0)=0
and

(48)

x(t)/|x(t)|\to\pm 1,\text{ as }t\to 0^{+},

it is not a local minimizer. A similar result holds for an ejection solution
x:[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
with
x(0)=0
.

Proof.

In polar coordinates, condition (
U4
) is equivalent to

\tilde{U}^{\prime\prime}(k\pi)<-\frac{(2-\alpha)^{2}}{8}\tilde{U}(k\pi),\;\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Meanwhile under the above condition and (
48
), Theorem 1.2 in
[
18
]
shows the Morse index of the collision solution
x
is infinite, so it can not be a local minimizer.

∎

With this lemma, we can prove the following proposition, which rules out collision in minimizers under certain topological constraints.

Proposition 6.1
.

Under conditions (
U1
) and (
U4
), if
x=re^{i\theta}\in\Lambda_{T^{\pm}}(r_{1}e^{i\theta_{-}},r_{2}e^{i\theta_{+}})
satisfies

\mathcal{A}_{h}(x;T^{-},T^{+})=\inf\{\mathcal{A}_{h}(\xi):\;\forall\xi\in\Lambda(r_{1}e^{i\theta_{-}},r_{2}e^{i\theta_{+}})\},

then it is collision-free, when
\theta^{\pm}
satisfy one of the following three conditions:

(i).

\theta_{-}\in[0,\pi]
and
\theta_{+}\in[-\pi,2\pi];

(ii).

\theta_{-}=0
and
\theta_{+}\in[-2\pi,2\pi]
;

(iii).

\theta_{-}=\pi
and
\theta_{+}\in[-\pi,3\pi]
.

Proof.

By a contradiction argument, let’s assume
x
is not collision-free. Without loss of generality, we may further assume
0\in(T^{-},T^{+})
and
x(0)=0
. The fact that
x
is an
h
-free-time minimizer under topological constraint implies zero must be the only collision moment, as if there is another collision moment
t_{0}>0
(the argument for
t_{0}<0
is the same), by deleting the sub-path
x|_{(0,t_{0})}
, we get a new path still belonging to
\Lambda_{T^{\pm}}(r_{1}e^{i\theta_{-}},r_{2}e^{i\theta_{+}})
, but with strictly smaller
\mathcal{A}_{h}
action value, which is absurd.

For
i=1,\dots,4
, let
\Pi_{i}
be the
i
-th quadrant of the complex plane. Here each
\Pi_{i}
should be seen as a close set including its boundary. Notice that under condition (
U1
), the potential
U
is symmetric with respect to both the real and complex axes. This means we may assume the sub-path
x|_{[T^{-},0]}
(resp.
x|_{[0,T^{+}]}
) is entirely contained in the quadrant containing
x(T^{-})
(resp.
x(T^{+})
).

We will divide our proof into several different cases.

Case 1
:
\theta_{-}\in[0,\pi]
and
\theta_{+}\in[\pi,2\pi]
.
By the above argument, we may assume
x([T^{-},0])
is entirely contained in
\Pi_{1}
or
\Pi_{2}
depending on the value
\theta_{-}
. By Proposition
1.1
,

\lim_{t\to 0^{-}}\theta(t)\in\{0,\pi/2,\pi\}.

Lemma
6.1
then implies the limit must be
\pi/2
, as otherwise
x|_{[T^{-},0]}
is not a local minimizer, which is absurd.

Meanwhile
\theta_{+}\in[\pi,2\pi]
implies
x([0,T^{+}])
is entirely contained in
\Pi_{3}
or
\Pi_{4}
and a similar argument as above shows

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\theta(t)=3\pi/2.

Let
\bar{x}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be the zero energy homothetic solution given as below

\bar{x}=\begin{cases}(\kappa_{+}t)^{\frac{2}{2+\alpha}}e^{i\frac{3\pi}{2}},&\text{ if }t\geq 0;\\
(-\kappa_{-}t)^{\frac{2}{2+\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}},&\text{ if }t\leq 0.\end{cases}

For any
\varepsilon>0
small enough, define a local deformation of
\bar{x}
near the origin as

(49)

\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)=\bar{x}(t)+f_{\varepsilon}(t)\sigma,\text{ where }\sigma=e^{i\pi}.

Here
f_{\varepsilon}(t)
is an even function defined as in (
39
).

For
\bar{x}
and
\sigma
given as above, by
Case 2
in the proof of Lemma
5.5
,

\mathcal{A}(\bar{x}^{\varepsilon};-T,T)<\mathcal{A}(\bar{x};-T,T),\;\forall T>0.

Meanwhile for a sequence of positive numbers
\{\lambda_{n}\searrow 0\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, let
x^{\lambda_{n}}
be a
\lambda_{n}
-blow-up of
x
,

x^{\lambda_{n}}(t)=\lambda_{n}^{-\frac{2}{2+\alpha}}x(\lambda_{n}t),\;t\in[T^{-}/\lambda_{n},T^{+}/\lambda_{n}].

Fix a
T>0
, for
n
large enough, we define a deformation of
x^{\lambda_{n}}
as

y^{\lambda_{n}}(t)=\begin{cases}x^{\lambda_{n}}(t),&\text{ when }t\in[T^{-}/\lambda_{n},T^{+}/\lambda_{n}]\setminus[-T,T];\\
x^{\lambda_{n}}(t)+\phi(t)+\psi_{n}(t),&\text{ when }t\in[-T,T],\end{cases}

with
\psi_{n}(t)
as in (
36
) and

\phi(t)=\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}(t)-\bar{x}(t),\;\text{when }t\in[-T,T].

The above definitions imply
y^{\lambda_{n}}\in\Lambda_{T^{\pm}/\lambda_{n}}(\lambda_{n}^{-\frac{2}{2+\alpha}}r_{1}e^{i\theta_{-}},\lambda_{n}^{-\frac{2}{2+\alpha}}r_{2}e^{i\theta_{+}})
. As a result,

y_{n}(t)=\lambda^{\frac{2}{2+\alpha}}y^{\lambda_{n}}(\lambda_{n}^{-1}t)\Lambda_{T^{\pm}}(r_{1}e^{i\theta_{-}},r_{2}e^{i\theta_{+}}).

Then the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
1.3
implies

\mathcal{A}(y_{n};T^{-},T^{+})<\mathcal{A}(x;T^{-},T^{+}),

which is absurd.

The proofs for the remaining cases are almost the same, the only difference is to choose the correct
\sigma
in (
49
) corresponding to different choices of
\theta_{\pm}
, which are listed below.

Case 2
:
\theta_{-}\in[0,\pi]
and
\theta_{+}\in[0,\pi]
.

\sigma=e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}}.

Case 3
:
\theta_{-}\in[0,\pi]
and
\theta_{+}\in[-\pi,0]
.

\sigma=e^{i0}.

Case 4
:
\theta_{-}=0
and
\theta_{+}\in[-2\pi,-\pi]
.

\sigma=e^{-i\pi}.

Case 5
:
\theta_{-}=\pi
and
\theta_{+}\in[2\pi,3\pi]

\sigma=e^{i2\pi}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

First let’s consider the case that
s^{\pm}
satisfy condition (i). As the system is symmetric with respect to the real axis, we may further assume
s^{-}_{2}>0
. Condition (i) then implies
s^{+}_{2}\leq 0
. As a result, we can find
\theta_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
s^{\pm}=e^{i\theta_{\pm}}
and

\theta_{-}\in(0,\pi),\;\theta_{+}\in[-\pi,2\pi]\;\text{ and }\;|\theta_{+}-\theta_{-}|>\pi.

Next let’s consider the case that
s^{\pm}
satisfy condition (ii). As the system is also symmetric with respect to the imaginary axis, we may assume
s^{\pm}=1
. Then we can find
\theta_{\pm}
, such that

\theta_{-}=0,\;\theta_{+}\in[-2\pi,2\pi]\;\text{ and }\;|\theta_{+}-\theta_{-}|>\pi.

In both cases, such a pair of
\theta_{\pm}
must satisfy one the conditions given in Proposition
6.1
, then the desired bi-hyperbolic solution can be obtained by the same proof of Theorem
1.3
.

∎
