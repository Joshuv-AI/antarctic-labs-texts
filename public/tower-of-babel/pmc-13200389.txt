Association between dental implantation and dementia: a nationwide population-based study
Authors: Yong Sung Kim, Yun-Jin Lee, Eun-Hye Lee, Sojeong Park, Dongwoo Kang, Wonjae Sung, Hee-Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDementia is a growing global health concern with an increasing focus on identifying modifiable risk factors. Emerging evidence suggests a potential link between oral health and cognitive function in older adults. This study aimed to investigate the association between dental implants and the risk of dementia in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) using nationwide data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service.MethodsWe conducted a large-scale population-based cohort study using data from 2010 to 2020. Patients aged ≥ 65 years who were diagnosed with MCI were included and categorized based on their dental implant status. Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the incidence of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). The models were adjusted for age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index scores, income level, and residential area.ResultsAmong 63,901 patients with MCI, those who received dental implants (n = 9,203) showed a significantly lower risk of developing AD than those without implants (n = 54,698) (adjusted HR = 0.37, 95% CI = [0.346–0.388], p < 0.001). For DLB, the association was not statistically significant after adjusting for confounders (adjusted HR = 0.57, 95% CI [0.326–1.004], p = 0.05). Patients with MCI who received dental implants were generally younger, with a higher proportion of males, urban residents, and individuals in higher income brackets.ConclusionsAlthough additional residual confounding factors cannot be excluded, dental implantation in patients with MCI was consistently associated with a lower risk of progression to AD after adjustment for measured covariates. Further prospective and interventional studies are warranted to explore the underlying mechanisms and establish a more apparent causal relationship between oral health and dementia risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07461-3.

Background

Dementia is a common condition characterized by difficulties with memory, language, problem-solving, spatial and temporal orientation, and other cognitive skills that impair a person’s ability to carry out daily activities [1]. Despite a decline in age-specific incidence rates in high-income countries, the overall number of individuals living with dementia continues to rise as global life expectancy increases [2]. Currently, an estimated 55 million people are affected by dementia worldwide, and this number is expected to rise to 153 million by 2050 [3, 4]. Identifying modifiable risk factors for dementia has become increasingly important, as advancements in both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions now offer greater opportunities to prevent, detect, and manage dementia, ultimately enhancing the quality of life of individuals, their families, and society [2]. Within the continuum of cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is clinically recognized as an intermediate stage between normal cognitive aging and dementia [5, 6]. Epidemiological data suggest that MCI affects approximately 20% of community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 71 years, with prevalence estimates rising to nearly 40% among individuals receiving care in specialized memory clinics [7–9]. The annual conversion rates to dementia have been reported to range from 6% to 15% in population-based studies and cohorts from dementia centers [10, 11]. Given the potentially modifiable nature of MCI, increasing attention has been directed toward identifying both modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors that may influence the likelihood of progression to dementia.

Emerging evidence supports the potentially significant association between oral health and cognitive function. Oral health and cognitive function exhibit a complex bidirectional relationship, wherein poor oral conditions may contribute to cognitive decline through mechanisms such as systemic inflammation and neurodegeneration, whereas cognitive impairment can exacerbate oral health deterioration by impairing an individual’s ability to maintain adequate oral hygiene and self-care [12, 13]. Among various oral conditions, tooth loss is particularly prevalent in older adults. It is affected by multiple factors, including aging, smoking, poor socioeconomic status, malnutrition, and underlying oral pathologies [14].

Dental implants are a reliable treatment option for partial and complete edentulism, demonstrating high long-term success rates and advantages, such as reduced risk to adjacent teeth and improved bone preservation [15]. In addition to their functional and structural benefits, dental implants may contribute to cognitive health, as individuals with implants have been shown to exhibit better cognitive performance than those without tooth replacements [16]. However, the current body of evidence remains extremely limited, particularly in population-based studies that examine the association between dental implants and the risk of dementia. Despite the growing interest in the relationship between oral health and cognition, evidence regarding the potential protective role of dental implants against dementia remains scarce. With growing awareness of the need for long-term monitoring of dementia and its associated socioeconomic burden, the exploration of modifiable risk factors—such as oral health status—has become increasingly warranted [17]. To address this gap, we conducted a large-scale population-based cohort study using data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) to examine whether dental implant placement in individuals with MCI is associated with a reduced risk of subsequent dementia. In addition, we compared the clinical and demographic characteristics of the patients with dementia according to the presence or absence of prior dental implant treatment.

Methods

Data acquisition

The data used in this study were sourced from the NHIS database, which comprises de-identified health claims data for nearly the entire Korean population. The NHIS reviews the medical claims data submitted by healthcare institutions. The NHIS database provides baseline patient information, socioeconomic variables, International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10), and comprehensive medical data, including diagnostic tests and treatment methods. This study analyzed data from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2020, extracted from the NHIS database, to assess the demographics and comorbidities, as indicated by the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) scores of patients with dementia with or without dental implants. The views expressed herein are those of the authors, and do not necessarily represent those of the NHIS.

Study population

This study included individuals aged ≥ 65 years who were diagnosed with either dementia or MCI between 2010 and 2020. Dementia was defined on the basis of claims containing ICD-10 diagnostic codes for Alzheimer’s disease (F00, G30), vascular dementia (F01), dementia in Parkinson’s disease (F02.3), unspecified dementia (F03), or dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) (G31.82), recorded as either the primary or secondary diagnosis in the NHIS database [18]. A diagnosis of dementia was defined as the presence of at least one recorded diagnostic code and at least one prescription for anti-dementia medication within the same claim. Anti-dementia medications included donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, and memantine.

MCI was identified based on claims containing the ICD-10 diagnostic code for mild cognitive impairment (F06.7). Individuals were classified as having MCI if they had at least two outpatient diagnoses or one inpatient diagnosis.

To construct the study cohort, a 10% random sampling of eligible individuals was conducted using the NHIS database, yielding an initial sample of 212,642 participants. A washout period was applied to exclude individuals with a prior diagnosis of dementia or MCI prior to 2009 (n = 24,036), ensuring that only newly diagnosed cases were included. Additionally, individuals with missing data for key covariates were excluded (n = 3,914), resulting in a final study population of 184,692 (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of study population selection

Dental implant procedures were identified based on the NHIS-classified procedure codes. The implantation process consists of three stages: initial diagnosis and treatment planning (UB11), fixture placement (UB12), and prosthetic restoration (UB13) (Journal of Korean Academy of Oral Health, 2022). The UB12 code, which represents the surgical placement of the implant fixture, was used to determine whether an individual had undergone a dental implant procedure.

CCI

In this study, we used the CCI not as a direct assessment tool for dementia risk factors but as a validated measure to capture the overall burden of patients’ baseline comorbid conditions. The ICD-10 diagnostic codes for CCI, encompassing 17 conditions such as acute myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebral vascular accident, dementia, pulmonary disease, connective tissue disorder, peptic ulcer, liver disease, diabetes, diabetes complications, paraplegia, renal disease, cancer, metastatic cancer, severe liver disease, and human immunodeficiency virus infection, were employed [19]. The CCI score was calculated based on weighted values for each condition. The corresponding ICD-10 diagnostic codes are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analyses were used to assess the participants’ demographic profiles. For group comparisons, continuous variables, such as mean age, were analyzed using independent samples t-tests, while categorical variables, including nominal and ordinal scales, were evaluated using chi-square tests. These methods were selected to appropriately analyze the respective data types and ensure robust comparisons between groups. Individuals were categorized based on sex, residential area, income level, and CCI score into three groups: 0, 1–2, and ≥ 3. We used Cox proportional hazard regression models to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to evaluate the difference in dementia rates between individuals with MCI who received dental implants and those who did not. The analysis focused on the time until the event with dementia (either AD or DLB) as the outcome of interest. Three adjustment models were used: Model 1 accounted for age and sex, Model 2 included additional adjustments for CCI scores, and Model 3 further incorporated socioeconomic factors, such as income level and residential area. Both crude and adjusted HRs were calculated to evaluate the association between dental implant placement and the risk of dementia. Incidence rates (IRs) were determined per 100,000 person-years (PY) to compare dementia rates between the groups. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression models was assessed using Schoenfeld residual plots. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using the SAS software (version 9.3; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hanyang University Seoul Hospital (2020-09-024). The need for informed patient consent was waived because the study used publicly accessible and anonymized patient data.

Results

Among the 184,692 patients selected through 10% sampling from the cohort, 72,218 were diagnosed with MCI before dementia (Fig. 1). We excluded 6,995 patients who had undergone dental implantation before their MCI diagnosis and 1,322 patients who were diagnosed with dementia before dental implants. Finally, 63,901 patients were included in the analysis. Of these, 54,698 patients did not undergo dental implant procedures following their diagnosis of MCI, whereas 9,203 patients received dental implants after being diagnosed with MCI, thereby clearly indicating that the MCI diagnosis preceded the dental implant procedure in temporal order. The demographic characteristics of the patients with MCI, categorized by the presence or absence of dental implantation, are presented in Table 1. Individuals with MCI who underwent dental implantation were diagnosed at a younger age than those without implants (74.61 ± 4.30 vs. 78.04 ± 5.47, p < 0.001). Additionally, a higher proportion of males was found in the implant group (35.92%) than in the non-implant group (31.32%) (p < 0.001). There were also differences in residential areas and income levels between the two groups. Urban residents constituted 42.39% of the implant group and 39.12% of the non-implant group, and those in the fifth income quintile represented 43.62% and 37.60%, respectively (p < 0.001 for both). While there was no significant difference in the distribution of CCI categories between patients with MCI with and without dental implants (p = 0.2909), the mean CCI score was significantly higher in those without dental implants (p < 0.0001).

To investigate the incidence of dementia among patients with MCI who received dental implants compared to those who did not, a Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conducted (Table 2; Fig. 2). Incidence rates, including age-specific and cumulative incidence rates, differed between groups with and without dental implants (supplementary Table 2, supplementary Figure). The results demonstrated that patients with dental implants had a significantly lower risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease compared to those without implants. This association was evident in both the crude model (Hazard Ratio [HR] = 0.30, 95% CI = [0.283–0.317], p < 0.001) and the model adjusted for age, sex, CCIs, income level, and residential area (HR = 0.37, 95% CI = [0.346–0.388], p < 0.001). For DLB, the crude model indicated a lower risk in the dental implant group (HR = 0.56, 95% CI = [0.324–0.974], p = 0.04). However, this association was no longer statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, CCIs, income level, and residential area (HR = 0.57, 95% CI [0.326–1.004], p = 0.05).

From the cohort, 112,474 individuals diagnosed with dementia were selected for analysis. We excluded 4,661 patients who underwent dental implantation after the diagnosis of dementia. The remaining sample comprised 103,553 dementia patients without a history of dental implants, and 4,260 dementia patients who had received dental implants before their diagnosis. Table 3 shows the demographic profile of the patients with dementia, categorized according to whether they had undergone dental implantation. The mean age of patients with dementia without a history of dental implants was significantly higher than that of patients who had undergone dental implant procedures before their dementia diagnosis (81.39 ± 6.45 vs. 80.28 ± 4.46, p < 0.001). The proportion of male patients was also significantly higher in the group with a history of dental implants (41.60% vs. 31.15%, p < 0.001). Additionally, individuals diagnosed with dementia after receiving dental implants were more likely to reside in urban areas than those without an implant history (64.91% vs. 62.91%, p = 0.007). The income level of patients with dementia who had undergone dental implants was significantly higher than that of patients without an implant history. There was a significant difference in the distribution of CCI categories between the patients with dementia with and without a history of dental implants (p < 0.0001). The proportion of patients with a CCI score of ≥ 3 was higher in those with a prior implant history (66.15%) than in those without implants (56.12%). Additionally, the mean CCI score was significantly higher in patients with a history of dental implants than those without (4.09 ± 2.94 vs. 3.42 ± 2.75, p < 0.0001). With the exception of moderate or severe liver disease and acquired immune deficiency syndrome/human immunodeficiency virus, patients with dementia and no history of dental implants exhibited a higher prevalence of comorbidities.

Discussion

This study examined the association between dental implantation and the risk of dementia in patients with MCI by analyzing nationwide data from the NHIS between 2010 and 2020. Additionally, we explored the differences in demographic and clinical characteristics between dementia patients with a history of dental implantation and those without. Patients with MCI who had undergone dental implantation were generally younger, with a higher proportion of males, urban residents, and individuals in higher-income brackets. Furthermore, these patients exhibited a significantly lower risk of progressing to Alzheimer’s disease than those without implants, even after adjusting for potential confounding factors. Among patients already diagnosed with dementia, those with a history of dental implantation before their diagnosis were also younger, more likely to be male, and had higher income levels than those without a history of implants.

Patients who underwent dental implant placement after being diagnosed with MCI had an earlier age at diagnosis than those with MCI who had no history of dental implants. Although detailed information on the participants’ dental health status was unavailable, those who received dental implants may have had a history of chronic dental caries or periodontitis, the leading cause of tooth loss [20]. Chronic oral inflammation associated with poor oral health has been identified as a potential risk factor for cognitive impairment and dementia [21, 22]. Therefore, a pre-existing poor oral health condition that ultimately necessitated dental implant placement may have contributed to the earlier onset of MCI. While patients with MCI who received dental implants after diagnosis may have had poor oral health necessitating the procedure, they were likely to have relatively better general health, as evidenced by their lower mean CCI scores. This result is plausible, given that implant surgery typically requires a minimum threshold of systemic health. Previous studies have noted that a patient’s systemic condition and overall disease control are critical determinants in selecting candidates for implant therapy [23]. Patients with multiple comorbidities may be less likely to undergo elective and invasive dental procedures. These findings likely reflect patient selection practices in real-world clinical settings, in which those with sufficient systemic health to tolerate surgical procedures generally receive implant therapy.

Patients who received dental implants exhibited a significantly lower risk of developing AD than those who did not receive implants. This outcome may be attributed to the restoration of lost teeth and resolution of periodontal disease, a primary cause of tooth loss [22]. Previous epidemiological studies have found a link between tooth loss and heightened risk of AD and dementia [24, 25]. Dental implants enhance chewing ability by replacing lost teeth, which is directly linked to cognitive health [26]. Notably, a causal relationship has been identified between mastication and cognitive function [27], with mastication playing a crucial role in enhancing the cognitive processing speed [28]. Moreover, a study comparing masticatory ability in older women with dementia to their cognitively healthy counterparts demonstrated that the dementia group exhibited a marked decline in their ability to chew specific foods [29]. These findings suggest that dental restoration using implants may mitigate cognitive decline by preserving or enhancing masticatory function. Furthermore, tooth loss is an outcome of periodontal disease, a chronic inflammatory condition with potential systemic implications [30, 31]. Given that chronic inflammation has been implicated in AD pathogenesis, the systemic inflammatory burden associated with periodontal disease may contribute to cognitive decline [22, 32]. Thus, in addition to improving mastication, dental implants may help to reduce the risk of AD by alleviating the underlying inflammatory processes linked to tooth loss. However, the observed association between dental implants and dementia risk should be interpreted with caution because of potential residual confounding. Although adjustments were made for income and residential area, these variables may not entirely account for selection bias, whereby healthier individuals with greater health-care utilization are more likely to receive implants. Consequently, the possibility that this association is driven by unmeasured confounding rather than a direct protective effect cannot be excluded. Future studies incorporating more comprehensive clinical and behavioral variables are warranted to better control for confounding factors and validate these findings. In DLB, the hazard ratio for subjects with MCI who received dental implants ranged from 0.56 to 0.6 across all models, indicating a lower prevalence than that in patients with MCI without dental implants. However, statistical significance was observed only in the crude model, whereas the adjusted models merely demonstrated a trend toward a reduced risk of DLB onset. DLB is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder leading to dementia [33]. These findings suggest that dental implants may be associated with a reduced risk of dementia overall, but the protective effect may not apply to specific subtypes, such as DLB, after adjustment.

Individuals with dementia who had dental implants were diagnosed at a younger age, likely to live in urban areas and belong to the high-income quintile. Notably, the relationship between comorbidity burden and dental implant status differed depending on the timing of implant placement and cognitive decline. Among patients with MCI who received implants after the diagnosis of MCI, lower mean CCI scores were observed, likely reflecting a selection tendency in which healthier individuals were eligible for implant procedures. In contrast, among patients with dementia with a history of implants prior to diagnosis, CCI scores were higher, possibly reflecting the role of chronic systemic diseases in tooth loss and implant therapy before cognitive decline. This group also showed higher urban residency and income levels, suggesting greater access to healthcare, which may have facilitated implant treatment and earlier detection of dementia. Together, these findings emphasize the importance of interpreting implant status in the context of health status and timing relative to cognitive trajectory.

The present study has some limitations. First, although multiple adjustment models were applied to minimize potential bias, the NHIS database lacks several important clinical and behavioral variables, including cognitive test scores, MCI severity, neurodegenerative biomarkers, smoking status, alcohol use, and oral hygiene practices. These unmeasured factors may have influenced dental implant placement and dementia risk, but were not fully accounted for in our analysis. The following modifiable dementia risk factors were not measured in this study: less education, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, excessive alcohol consumption, social isolation, air pollution, and untreated vision loss [2]. Furthermore, it is thought that implants could serve as a proxy indicator for such unmeasured factors as health status, oral health history, or socioeconomic advantage. As a result, residual confounding is likely to persist despite statistical adjustments. Future studies incorporating a broader range of clinical and behavioral variables are needed to better control for confounding and validate these findings. In addition, the decision to undergo dental implantation itself may reflect differences in healthcare access, socioeconomic resources, and health-related behaviors that were not fully captured in the available data. Individuals who received dental implants may have had greater engagement with healthcare services, higher health awareness, and overall better health status, factors that are also associated with cognitive outcomes. Although adjustments were made for income level and residential area, such factors may not have been fully accounted for, raising the possibility of selection bias in which healthier individuals or those with better healthcare utilization were more likely to receive implants. This bias may have contributed to the observed association between dental implants and dementia risk. Second, due to this study’s observational nature, we could identify associations but could not establish a causal relationship between dental implantation and dementia risk. Although we hypothesized that improvements in mastication and reductions in periodontal inflammation may contribute to cognitive health, the absence of longitudinal clinical assessments, randomized interventions, and relevant laboratory values to support the systemic inflammation hypothesis limits the ability to confirm a direct causal effect. Third, this study focused on AD and dementia with Lewy bodies as primary outcomes because of the availability and reliability of diagnostic data within the Korean NHIS database. Detailed and validated diagnostic information for other common dementia subtypes, such as vascular and mixed dementias, was not accessible in this dataset, precluding their inclusion in the analysis. Given the established associations between oral health and vascular risk factors, the exclusion of vascular and mixed dementia represents an important limitation that may affect the generalizability of our findings to the general population. Future research incorporating a broader spectrum of dementia types, when appropriate data become available, is warranted to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationships examined in this study. Fourth, the binary classification of dental implant status may not adequately reflect the overall condition of oral health. The presence or absence of dental implants alone does not capture key factors such as masticatory function, total number of remaining teeth, or the severity of periodontal disease. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the observed association reflects an actual protective effect of dental implants on cognitive function or merely serves as a surrogate marker for better oral health. Future studies should incorporate more detailed oral health indicators—including denture use, chewing efficiency, periodontal status, and total tooth count—to strengthen the interpretation of findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between oral health and dementia risk.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the potential association between dental implantation and the risk of dementia in individuals with MCI. By leveraging a large nationwide dataset, we comprehensively analyzed real-world health outcomes. Our findings suggest that individuals with MCI who received dental implants exhibited a lower risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease, even after adjusting for multiple confounding factors. Additionally, our study highlights essential demographic and socioeconomic differences between individuals with and without dental implants, emphasizing the need to consider oral health as a potential modifiable factor in dementia prevention. Further prospective and interventional studies are warranted to explore the underlying mechanisms and to establish a more apparent causal relationship between dental health and dementia.

Conclusions

This large-scale population-based cohort study provides evidence of a significant association between dental implants and a reduced risk of Alzheimer’s disease progression in patients with MCI. The findings indicate that individuals with MCI who received dental implants exhibited a 63% lower risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease than those without implants. However, these results should be interpreted with caution because of the high potential for substantial residual confounding. Further research incorporating more comprehensive clinical and behavioral data is warranted to validate these findings and to clarify the nature of this relationship. This association suggests a potential role for oral health interventions in dementia prevention; however, it is important to consider that this relationship may also reflect confounding by overall health status, whereby individuals with better general health and healthcare access are more likely to receive dental care and simultaneously have a lower risk of dementia. Therefore, while maintaining oral health may contribute to cognitive outcomes, further research is needed to disentangle these effects and confirm causality.

The results of this study underscore the complex interplay between oral health and cognitive function. Although our findings suggest that dental implants may serve as protective factors could be attributed to improved mastication, enhanced nutritional intake, and reduced systemic inflammation associated with periodontal diseases against cognitive decline, this observational study cannot establish a direct causal relationship. It is important to consider that this association may partly reflect residual confounding or reverse causation. Therefore, while maintaining oral health through dental implants may play a role in dementia prevention, further research, including longitudinal clinical assessments and randomized interventions, is necessary to confirm the protective effects and clarify the underlying mechanisms.

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest a potential association between maintaining oral health through dental implants and a reduced risk of developing dementia. While these results highlight the possible contribution of dental interventions within broader strategies addressing cognitive aging, caution is warranted due to the study’s observational nature and the potential for residual confounding. Further research incorporating more comprehensive clinical and behavioral data is required to clarify the role of oral health in dementia prevention and substantiate these preliminary observations.
