Structure characterization assists rational design of polyethyleneglycol diglycidyl ether (PEGDE) substituted hyaluronic acid
Authors: Yiyang Wu, Yujuan Chen, Siran Zhao, Jinyu Wan, Jiangwei Su, Ying Kan, Hongmei Li, Jin-Ping Li, Tianji Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyethyleneglycol diglycidyl ether (PEGDE) is a new generation of cross-linker with the advantages of low toxicity and improved elasticity. The physicochemical properties of PEGDE-HA are closely related to its degree of modification (MoD), effective cross-linker ratio (CrR), as well as modification types/positions. In this study, we developed a structural analysis method for comprehensive characterization of PEGDE cross-linked HA. Combined HPLC-MS/MS analysis accurately measures the parameters of MoD and CrR, and ambiguously locates modification sites/residues. Using the established method, we have, for the first time, identified the existence of intra-chain cross-linked structures in the hydrogel and revealed the difference in structures and rheological properties of hydrogels synthesized under different conditions. The insights obtained from this analytical method can be applied to guide the manufacturing process to generate HA hydrogel of “tailored” structures for different application scenarios.Keywords: Hyaluronic acid, PEGDE cross-linking, HPLC, Tandem mass spectrometry

Introduction

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a linear, high-molecular-weight polysaccharide ubiquitously distributed in connective tissues and body fluids [1]. The primary structure of HA is composed of repeating disaccharides units of d-glucuronic acid (GlcA) and N-acetyl-d-glucosamine (GlcNAc) linked by β-(1→3) glycosidic bonds, and the disaccharides building blocks are connected by β-(1→4) glycosidic bonds (Fig. 1a) [2]. Despite its simple chemical composition, HA has a flexible and higher ordered structure that endows it unique rheological and biological properties. Owing to its good biocompatibility, HA based materials have been widely used in the medical and cosmetic fields [3,4].

Structures of HA (a) and PEGDE (b). The asterisks indicate chiral carbon atoms.

However, native HA has poor residence time in vivo, as it is rapidly degraded by endogenous hyaluronidases. To prolong its half-life, cross-linking technologies have been developed. Physical cross-linking relies exclusively on intra- or inter-molecular noncovalent interactions to aggregate HA chains and form a stable three-dimensional network structure. It is characterized by reversibility, mild reaction conditions, and the absence of chemically toxic residues [5]. The HA molecular chain contains numerous hydrophilic groups (hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amide groups), which lay the foundation for physical cross-linking through intra- or inter-molecular hydrogen bonding interactions [6]. Additionally, HA is an anionic polysaccharide that can form polyionic complexes with cationic polymers (such as chitosan, polylysine) via electrostatic interactions, rapidly constructing a gel structure [7]. Hydrophobic moieties present on the HA molecules could also undergo hydrophobic interactions in aqueous solutions, forming stable physical cross-links that enhance gel stability [8]. In contrast, chemical cross-linking involves multifunctional cross-linking agents to undergo covalent chemical reactions with the HA molecules, connecting HA chains into a network via stable covalent bonds. This process markedly reinforces the mechanical properties and stability of the hydrogels. Common cross-linking agents include 1,4-butanediol diglycidal ether (BDDE) [[9], [10], [11]], polyethyleneglycol diglycidyl ether (PEGDE) [12], divinyl sulfone (DVS) [13,14], methacrylic anhydride [15] and biscarbodiimide (BCDI) [12,16], among which BDDE is currently the most frequently used [12].

PEGDE is a new generation of the cross-linkers developed on the basis of BDDE, offering improved gel elasticity and reduced cytotoxicity. Recent studies indicate that compared to BDDE-HA, PEGDE-HA filler extracts significantly reduce cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses in human skin cells [17,18]. BDDE and PEGDG cross-link with HA through the same mechanism. Under alkaline conditions, the hydroxyl groups on the HA sugar chain act as nucleophilic reagents to undergo etherification reactions with the epoxide groups of PEGDE [19]. One or both ends of the agent can be linked to HA, forming mono- or cross-linked structures. Distinct from conventional cross-linkers, PEGDE is a polymer with variable (CH2–CH2–O)- repeating units (Mn = 500) (Fig. 1b), which leads to more complex molecular structure of the resultant hydrogels. The higher molecular weight of PEGDE (compared with BDDE, Mn = 202) results in distinct mechanical properties of the hydrogels. A recent study demonstrated that HA fillers cross-linked with PEGDE exhibited similar cohesivity to those cross-linked with BDDE, while displaying higher G′, tan δ, G∗ and complex viscosity [18]. This is likely achieved through the formation of a fibrous, interconnected “spiderweb-like” matrix, wherein the long, flexible PEGDE chains promote extensive chain entanglement and physical interactions [20]. The increased chain length of PEGDE has also been shown to modulate the internal hydrogen bonding within the polymer network, which may subtly influence surface hydration and interaction with the extracellular matrix [21].

The physical and biochemical properties of a hydrogel are closely correlated to the degree of modification (MoD), effective cross-linker ratio (CrR), the linkage patterns and the modification sites/residues. Hence, methods for accurately determining these parameters are highly demanded. Several different methods have been proposed for the structural characterization of cross-linked HA. Due to their low solubility, enzymatic degradation of the polymers into oligosaccharides is routinely required prior to instrumental analysis. Subsequently, LC-MS and NMR based methods are generally applied [9,10,17,[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. Chromatographic separations like size exclusion chromatography [9] and amide chromatography [26] allow sufficient resolution of the oligos of varying molecular weights derived from enzyme degradation, while the porous graphitic carbon (PGC) column enable efficient separation of isomers modified at distinct residues/positions. NMR has been applied to determine the MoD and CrR of cross-linked HA in combination with enzyme degradation [17,25,27], but cannot distinguish the linkage patterns [10,26].

In this work, an HPLC-MS/MS method combination with enzyme degradation was developed to characterize the molecular structure of the PEGDE-HA hydrogel. Besides MoD and CrR, this approach enables inambiguous identification of modification sites and/or linkage patterns of various oligosaccharide species derived from distinctive regions of the PEGDE-HA hydrogel. Particularly, through gradient enzymatic digestion, we successfully distinguished inter- and intra-chain linkages for the first time. This robust method allows finger-printing analysis of the PEGDE-HA and can be further applied to establish quality control standards of related products. Using the established method, we explored different cross-linking conditions to synthesize PEGDE-HA with desired structural and rheological properties.

Material and methods

Materials

HA (Mw 1.483 × 106 Da, Fig. S1) and Hyaluronidase B (HAase-B, from Bacillus sp. A50) were purified in our own lab. The cross-linking agent (PEGDE, average Mn 500) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. LC grade acetonitrile, water, ammonium acetate and ammonium formate were obtained from Fisher Scientific (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA).

Cross-linking reactions

PEGDE and HA were dissolved in 2% NaOH solution. The reactions were performed under conditions indicated in the text and legends to figures. Reactions were stopped by dilution with an aqueous solution. Resultant hydrogels were washed with de-ionized water to remove excessive amounts of cross-linkers.

Enzymatic digestion of PEGDE cross-linked HA hydrogels and NaBH4 reduction

The cross-linked HA hydrogel (0.4 g; approximately 20 mg HA/1 g hydrogel) prepared as above was digested with a gradient amount of HAase-B (10 kU, 30 kU, 50 kU or 80 kU, referred as digests 1–4, respectively). The hydrogel and enzyme were dissolved in 8 mL buffer of sodium dihydrogen phosphate and disodium hydrogen phosphate, pH 6. The reactions were performed at 42 °C for 48 h and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove insoluble fragments. The filtrate was directly analyzed by SEC-MS for the determination of MoD and CrR.

For NaBH4 reduction, the digestion products were transferred to a 10 mL glass vial, and 0.6 mL of freshly prepared 0.5 M NaBH4 solution was slowly added. The reaction was incubated in a 42 °C water bath for 2 h (with gentle shake every 20 min) and neutralized with 0.6 mL of 0.5 M acetic acid. After passing through a 0.22 μm membrane, the filtrate was lyophilized and re-dissolved in H2O to a final oligosaccharide concentration of 16 mg/mL). The reduced oligos were analyzed by PGC-MS for elucidating the modification sites and residues.

Liquid chromatography separation

For separation of the enzymatic digests without reduction, the samples (approximately 1 mg/mL) were separated on an ultra-high performance ACQUITY SEC column (1.7 μm, 300 × 4.6 mm, Waters) that was coupled to an Vanquish Ultra-High Performance LC (UPLC) system. The column was eluted with isocratic solution of 10 mM ammonium acetate in 50% H2O and 50% methanol at a flowrate of 0.15 mL/min.

For separation of the reduced enzymatic digests, the samples were separated on a PGC column (5 μm, 150 × 4.6 mm, Thermo) coupled to a Vanquish UPLC system and eluted with solvent A (5 mM ammonium formate in water, pH = 3) and B (5 mM ammonium formate in 90% aqueous acetonitrile, pH = 3). The stepwise gradient was 95% to 78% solvent A in 3 min, 78%–45% solvent A in 100 min, 45%–0% solvent A in 105 min, 0% A in 105–115 min, 95% A in 115–140 min. The flow rate was 0.2 mL/min. Eluates from 0 to 17.5 min were introduced to waste for precluding the salt peak.

Electrospray ionization mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS) and tandem MS analysis

Eluates from the columns (2.4) were directly introduced to the ESI-MS interface of a ORBITRAP ECLIPSE mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). Negative ion mode was applied with sheath gas flow rate at 40 arb; aux gas flow rate at 10 arb; I spray voltage at 3.0 KV; capillary temp at 275 °C, and S-Lens RF Level at 70% V. For MS/MS or MS/MS/MS analysis, the fragmentation parameters were set as following: Iso width (m/z), 3.0; normalized collision energy, 28 for CID, 60 for HCD. m/z of each oligo was extracted, and peaks were integrated using the Xcalibur software with predefined signal-to-noise and mass tolerance parameters (10 ppm).

Rheology analysis

To characterize the rheological properties of the hydrogel, changes in the storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli with oscillation frequency were recorded by a Rotational Rheometer (Thermo Scientific HAAKE RS6000, with a UTM controller). Measurements were performed at 25 °C using a parallel-plate configuration (20 mm diameter and 1 mm gap) in the oscillatory mode, with a frequency range of 0.01∼1 Hz.

Results and discussion

Determination of MoD and CrR by UPSEC-HRMS

The macromolecular PEGDE substituted HA hydrogel was first subjected to degradation with Bacillus sp. A50 derived hyaluronidase B [23]. Theoretically, resulted oligosaccharides with varied number of sugar units can be divided into four different types: unmodified, mono-linked, cross-linked, and intra-chain linked. The oligosaccharides are described as x-P-y (n = z) in the following text, where x and y indicate the number of sugar units, P indicates the PEGDE linker, and z indicates the number of repeating units in the PEGDE cross-linker (Fig. 1b–S2a). Representative structures of the oligos and their originated regions on the hydrogel are depicted in Fig. 2. To optimize the enzyme digestion conditions, the hydrogel was incubated with different amounts of HAase-B from 10 to 80 kU under the incubation condition described in section 2.3. As shown in Table S1, the proportion of 2-P structure elevated from 43% to 82% with increasing the enzyme concentration. Since further increasing enzyme amount did not significantly change oligosaccharide distribution in the digest, 80 kU of HAase-B (digest 4) could represent an end degradation of the hydrogel, and was thereby adopted for exhaustive digestion in the following experiments. Considering the extreme structural complexity of PEGDE conjugated HA, it was expected that the enzymatic degradation would generate oligos of varied sugar units and n values. Separation of the digests was achieved on an ultra-high performance size exclusion column coupled to an HPLC system. Subsequent high-resolution MS analysis revealed oligosaccharides of 2–6 sugar units with n values ranging from 1 to 25 (Table S2). Interestingly, we found that n = 8 was the most abundantly detected linker in majority of the oligosaccharides (Fig. S2b–d). This is highly comparable to the cross-linker itself, with n = 8 being the most abundant species (Fig. S2a). However, according to the corresponding distribution profiles, we found that the n value of PEGDE correlated with its reactivity. Generally, PEGDE with smaller n values exhibited a higher reactivity with HA. Distinctively, the cross-linker with n = 3 was found to be preferable to form the intra-chain 2-P structure (Fig. S2e), suggesting a possible steric hindrance of the cross-linker with higher n numbers.

Major PEGDE oligosaccharides resulted from enzymatic degradation. Note that PEGDE substitution could occur at other residues/positions in representative structures.

To accurately determine the relative abundance of each oligosaccharide, m/z corresponding to different charges of the same species were extracted (Table S2), peak area obtained from each extracted ion chromatogram (EIC) was then added up for quantification. As each oligosaccharide has distinct isotope distribution, the monoisotopic peak was first integrated, followed by conversion to the peak area of total isotopes according to proportion of the monoisotopic peak (Table S2) [26]. To check whether the MS response of each degraded product was within the linear range, a serial dilution of the enzymatic digest was analyzed. The results showed that all detected oligosaccharides exhibited linearity of R2 > 0.99 from concentrations of 0–5 mg/mL (Fig. S3). As expected, unsaturated 2-mer without substitution was the most abundant species. Assessment of the linkage network in the hydrogel

To elucidate the substitution position and pattern of the PEGDE-linked hydrogel, oligosaccharides were reduced with NaBH4 to eliminate the α and β anomers (Fig. S4). The reduction efficiency was estimated to be over 99.99% based on the ratio of reduced and non-reduced unsaturated 2-mer. The reduced samples were then resolved on a PGC column with chromatographic conditions described in section 2.4. The reduction step greatly simplified the chromatograms in comparison with the non-reduced samples by converting the α and β anomers into a single peak (Fig. S4). As shown in the EIC (Fig. 3), multiple peaks were detected in each oligo product, suggesting the existence of isomers resulted from different substitution positions, as well as absolute conformations of the PEGDE molecule due to the presence of chiral carbons (Fig. 1b).

LC-MS analysis of PEGDE-HA. Extracted ion chromatogram of a) 2-P; b) 4-P; c) inter-chain 2-P-2; d) intra-chain 2-P after NaBH4 reduction.

Despite the fact that all the oligosaccharides resulted from the enzymatic digest, including the mono-linked, cross-linked, and intra-chain linked oligos, contained species of PEGDE with different n values, the same oligo with different n values exhibited basically identical peak patterns with only a backward shift in retention time (Fig. 3). The extension of retention time was presumably due to the interaction between the stationary phase and the cross-linker with longer chains (i.e., higher n-numbers), while similar peak profiles indicated the same modification patterns of the PEGDE-oligos with different n values. Indeed, the peaks of 2-P n = 2 were proved to be modified at identical positions as the corresponding peaks of 2-P n = 8, respectively (peaks 1-5, Fig. 3). This is an indication that the n value of the cross-linker does not essentially affect the crosslinking patten in a given hydrogel. Thus, to reduce workload, only PEGDE-oligos with n = 8 (Fig. 4) were selected for further analysis as they generally displayed the highest signal intensity (Fig. S2).

LC-MS analysis of PEGDE-HA. Extracted ion chromatogram of 2-P, 4-P, inter-chain 2-P-2 and intra-chain 2-P (n = 8) after NaBH4 reduction.

Structural identification of mono-linked oligosaccharides

First, we analyzed the 2-P and 4-P oligosaccharides originated from the mono-linked regions in the hydrogel (Fig. 2). Five peaks were detected in the 2-P species and the substitution positions were located by the online LC-MS/MS analysis (Fig. 4). The CID mode (using helium as collision gas) or HCD mode (using nitrogen as collision gas) was applied to obtain structurally informative daughter ions. Peaks 1–3 of 2-P exhibited diagnostic ion of Y1 (722.38) in the CID mode, indicating that the substitution occurred at the GlcNAc residue (Fig. 5a). The first three peaks shown in Fig. 4 displayed very similar MS/MS spectrum (Fig. 5a), despite that the ratio of Y1 (722.38) to Z1 (704.37) was much higher in peak 3 (approximately 10:1) than in peaks 1 and 2 (approximately 2:1). As modification at -OH4 potentially prevented adjacent glycosidic bond rupture, it has been reported that the ratio of Y1/Z1 can be used for distinguishing -OH4 and -OH6 substitution [26]. Thus, the higher ratio of Y1/Z1 in peak 3 suggested an -OH6 substitution in the GlcNAc. Additionally, the diagnostic ion of 0,2A1 (115.00) in peaks 4 and 5 revealed that the GlcA residues were modified at the -OH2 position instead of -OH3 (Fig. 5b).

Structural identification of the 2-P oligosaccharides (with the GlcNAc residue reduced). 2-P peaks 1-5 were fragmented with either CID or HCD mode as indicated in each panel. Key diagnostic ions were labeled in bold.

Four peaks were detected in the 4-P species (Fig. 4). The PEGDE molecules were located with the CID mode through online LC-MS/MS analysis. Fragmentation of peaks 1 and 2 led to basically identical MS/MS spectrum, where Y1 (722.38) indicated PEGDE substitution at the reducing end GlcNAc (Fig. 6a). Peaks 3 and 4 exhibited distinct MS/MS spectrum compared with peaks 1 and 2. Z2 (880.40), indicating the modification site at the reducing end GlcNAc or at the GlcA in the middle, while Z2/B3 (657.30) confirmed PEGDE at the GlcA residue (Fig. 6a). To figure out the exact modification site on each residue, the daughter ion Z2 (880.40) was further fragmented with the CID (for peaks 1 and 2) or HCD mode (for peaks 3 and 4). As shown in Fig. 6b, MS3 spectrum of 4-P peaks 1 and 2 (MS3@1259.51@880.40) were very similar to the MS2 spectrum of 2-P peaks 1 and 2 (MS2@880.40) (Fig. 5a), including the ratio of Y1 (722.38) to Z1 (704.37). The substitution position of 4-P peaks 1 and 2 was then determined to be at -OH4 of the reducing end GlcNAc. On the other hand, MS3 spectrum of 4-P peaks 3 and 4 (MS3@1259.51@880.40) led to distinct daughter ions (Fig. 6b) compared with the MS2 spectrum of 2-P peaks 4 and 5 (MS2@880.40) (Fig. 5b), while the diagnostic ion 115.00 for GlcA-OH2 was absent (Fig. 6b). Hence, substitution position of 4-P peaks 3 and 4 were determined to be -OH3 of the GlcA residue in the middle.

Structural identification of the 4-P oligosaccharides (with the reducing end GlcNAc residue reduced). a) 4-P peaks 1–4 were fragmented with the CID mode, key diagnostic ions were labeled in bold. b) 880.40 resulted from a) was further fragmented with the CID or HCD mode as indicated.

Structural identification of oligosaccharides originated from the cross- or intra-chain linked regions

Cross-linked regions in the hydrogel mainly lead to the formation of 2-P-2 oligosaccharides in the exhaustively digested samples (Fig. 2). Two types of 2-P-2 were detected, with m/z being 1243.5202 (z = 1, n = 8) and 1241.5041 (z = 1, n = 8) after reduction (Fig. 7).

Representative structures of 2-P-2 after NaBH4 reduction, with m/z of 1243.5202 (z = 1, n = 8) and 1241.5041 (z = 1, n = 8): a) two GlcNAc residues were reduced; b) one GlcNAc was modified by PEGDE at the hydroxyl group linked to C1 (-OH1); c) intra-chain 4-P. Note that the representative structures could be substituted at other residues/positions.

The m/z of 1243.5202 corresponded to the structure in which both GlcNAc residues were reduced by NaBH4 (Fig. 7a), indicating that these fragments originated from the inter-chain cross-linked regions of the PEGDE-HA hydrogel (Fig. 2). Fifteen major peaks in the 2-P-2 species had a m/z of 1243.52 (n = 8, z = 1) (Fig. 8). Using the online LC-MS/MS analysis with CID mode, a diagnostic ion Y1/Y3 (927.47) was detected, which corresponded to the fragment in which both GlcA residues were ruptured. As Y1/Y3 (927.47) was present in peaks 1–5 and 9 (Fig. 8), GlcNAc residues in these peaks were bridged by the PEGDE linker. The simultaneous presence of the Y3 (1085.49) and B3 (1020.41) daughter ions in peaks 6–8 and 10–12 (Fig. 8) represented the structures in which GlcA was linked to GlcNAc, linkage pattern of these peaks was thus determined to be GlcNAc-P-GlcA. The presence of B3 (1020.41) but not Y3 (1085.49) in peaks 13–15 inferred the linkage pattern of GlcA-P-GlcA. MS/MS spectra of peaks 1–15 were presented in Fig. S5.

Structural identification of the 2-P-2 oligosaccharides. 2-P-2 (with both GlcNAc residues reduced, m/z = 1243.5202, n = 8) were fragmented with CID mode, diagnostic ions were extracted for structural identification. Note that PEGDE substitution could occur at other positions in representative structures.

In addition to the oligosaccharides originated from the inter-chain cross-linked regions of the PEGDE-HA, we also detected 2-P-2 oligos with m/z of 1241.5041, in which one GlcNAc residue was resistant to NaBH4 reduction. As the reduction efficiency was close to 100%, the m/z of 1241.5041 could represent two types of structures: PEGDE modified at the -OH1 of one GlcNAc (-OH1 linkage) (Fig. 7b), or both ends of PEGDE connected to a tetra-saccharide (Fig. 7c). Based on the overall structure of the hydrogel (Fig. 2), it can be deduced that the -OH1 modified 2-P-2 oligos originated from the reducing end of the polysaccharide, while the other type of oligosaccharide (intra-chain 4-P) originated from intra-chain linkage region of the hydrogel.

Further analysis and comparison of the enzymatic digests 1–4 (section 2.3) facilitated the distinguishment of these two types of structures. Peaks in group B were absent in the exhaustively digested sample, while peaks in group A were barely affected (Fig. 9b). This result was an indication that the glycosidic bonds of the intra-chain 4-P oligo could be disrupted with excessive amount of enzyme (Fig. 9a). Thus, it was deducted that group A peaks were -OH1 modified, while group B peaks were intra-chain linked. To further identify the linkage patterns, MS/MS experiments were performed with the CID mode. Diagnostic ions presented in peaks A1 and A2 indicated that the -OH1 of GlcNAc was linked with the GlcA residue of the other 2-mer by PEGDE, while the diagnostic ions of peaks B1 and B2 suggested a structure of PEGDE cross-linked GlcA and the GlcNAc residue at the reducing end (Fig. 9b–S6).

Note that PEGDE substitution could occur at other positions in representative structures.

In addition to the intra-chain 4-P oligosaccharides, we detected 8 major peaks of intra-chain 2-P oligosaccharides (Fig. 4). Online LC-MS/MS analysis with CID mode detected a diagnostic ion of 722.37 in peaks 1–3 (Fig. 10b), which represented the structure of PEGDE substituted at both GlcNAc and GlcA residues (Fig. 10a). The presence of the daughter ion 704.36 but not 722.37 in peaks 4, 5, 7, and 8 (Fig. 10b) suggested both ends of PEGDE connected with the GlcNAc residue (Fig. 10a). The fragment of m/z = 639.28 was found in the MS/MS spectrum of peaks 4 and 6 (Fig. 10b), which represented an oligosaccharide structure with both ends of GlcA residue substituted with PEGDE (Fig. 10a). Diagnostic ions of m/z = 704.36 and 639.28 were simultaneously detected in peak 4, indicating that peak 4 was likely a hybrid peak with mixed populations. The presence of intra-chain linkages (Fig. 2, purple panel) is a previously unreported discovery, representing a novel structural feature of PEGDE cross-linked HA hydrogels. It is so far unclear whether the intra-chain and -OH1 cross-linking has an impact on the physiochemical properties of the hydrogel and should be of interest to investigate. From a polymer network perspective, different cross-linking patterns may influence macroscopic material behavior in distinct manners. Inter-chain cross-links establish connectivity between polysaccharide chains and contribute directly to enhanced elastic modulus, reduced swelling ratio, and enhanced resistance to deformation. Such features are generally associated with increased mechanical strength and potentially prolonged in-vivo residence due to reduced enzyme accessibility. In contrast, intra-chain linkages introduce looped structures within a single HA chain. Whether these loops contribute to physicochemical properties in the same manner as inter-chain cross-links remains unexplored. At the current stage, selectively synthesizing a specific linkage motif is not yet achievable. Therefore, we cannot decouple each motif's contribution to mechanical performance, degradation kinetics, or bioavailability of the hydrogel. Nevertheless, the high-resolution analytical approach capable of distinguishing different linkage types provides the prerequisites for future systematic investigations. Comparative analysis of HA hydrogels

Correlation of synthetic conditions with the hydrogel structures

Based on the established method, we compared the structures of the hydrogels synthesized under different conditions. Three conditions (conditions 1–3) with varied ratios between HA and PEGDE were investigated. In each set of conditions, samples were collected at three incubation time points (t1–t3), thus, a total of nine samples were analyzed. All samples were exhaustively digested with HAase and analyzed in parallel using the approaches described above. The results of MoD and CrR are shown in Table S4. As expected, the MoD and CrR were elevated with longer reaction time under all synthetic conditions. Comparison of conditions 1 and 2 suggested that both MoD and CrR exhibited correlation with temperature, while comparison of conditions 2 and 3 revealed that the PEGDE/HA ratio had a significant impact on MoD, but negligible impact on CrR. This could be due to the kinetics of the etherification reactions.

It is known that the degree of MoD and CrR endows the hydrogels half-life and mechanical properties that are critical for their applications in diverse scenarios. Improving the ‘effective cross-linking ratio’, i.e., the proportion of inter-chain cross-linking has been considered a feasible way to enhance viscoelasticity/cohesivity and extended residential time of the hydrogel in the tissues. Meanwhile, cytotoxicity risk is minimized by avoiding excessive contents of the cross-linking reagents. Our experimental results indicated that combining cross-linker/HA ratio with temperature settings enabled the synthesis of hydrogel with desired MoD and CrR (Table S4).

In addition to MoD and CrR, we also evaluated the structures of oligosaccharides obtained by enzymatic digestion. Albeit that the overall MoD and CrR varied in the hydrogels synthesized under different conditions, the distribution of oligosaccharides generated by HAase appeared highly similar, as reflected by the chromatographic spectra (Figs. S8–S11). Since the mono-linked residues were the major population in the modified oligosaccharides (Table S5), we approximated the modification sites of the hydrogel by quantitating the relative abundance of GlcA-OH2, GlcA-OH3, GlcNAc-OH4 and GlcNAc-OH6 peaks in the 2-P and 4-P oligos. Only minor differences were observed among the nine samples, with -OH4 being the dominant modification site, and -OH3 at the lowest percentage. This finding indicated that the synthetic conditions and cross-linking agent/HA ratio had neglectable impact on the substitution sites of the hydrogel. Whether there is a structural preference of the cross-linker on the macromolecular HA chains or whether the conformational and molecular property of HA ‘defines’ the crosslinking sites is of interest to investigate. This will require further rigorous experimental settings to testify HA hydrogels resulted from more synthetic conditions.

Rheological characteristics of the hydrogels

The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) are important rheological parameters representing the mechanical properties of hydrogels. The G′ indicates the amount of energy stored by the gel during deformation, while the G″ determines the energy loss through internal friction during shear deformation. To explore the correlations of synthetic conditions, structures and rheological characteristics of the hydrogels, G′ and G″ values were determined at the range of 0.01∼1Hz (Fig. 11). G′ of all the samples elevated and displayed higher value than G″, indicating elastic behavior of the hydrogels. G″ exhibited distinct trends that were related to the structure and manufacture process of each sample. For the highly cross-linked hydrogels (hydrogels 5, 6, 8 and 9, CrR >20%), G″ decreased significantly with the increase of oscillation frequency, while G″ of the low cross-linked samples (hydrogels 1–4, and 7, CrR <20%) increased slightly with elevated oscillation frequency. Conversely, the G′ of all the nine samples showed similar ascending trend with frequency. G′ of hydrogels 4-9 were positively related with MoD and CrR, while no obvious correlation was observed in hydrogels 1–3. Hydrogel 1 (Condition 1, t1) had comparable MoD and CrR, but significantly lower G′ than that of Hydrogel 7 (Condition 3, t1). It is speculated that Condition 1 led to mild cleavage of the HA chains, which reduced elasticity of the resulting hydrogel network.

Conclusions

In summary, we have established a powerful and highly sensitive analytical method that can depict basically all structural parameters of the PEGDE-HA hydrogel. Through gradient digestion experiments, the coexistence of both inter- and intra-chain structures was discovered and confirmed for the first time, breaking the cognitive limitations of traditional studies that focused only on the MoD and CrR. Based on this method, different synthesis conditions were investigated for exploring their correlations with molecular structures and rheological parameters of the products. We anticipate that these findings could be applied as guidance for the design of hydrogels with desired MoD, CrR and mechanical properties, advancing the customization of hydrogel properties for specific applications. Moreover, the methodological strategy provides a toolkit that can be used in the quality control of cross-linked HA hydrogels.
