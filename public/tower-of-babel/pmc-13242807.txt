Effect of Propofol Combined with Oliceridine versus Fentanyl on Desaturation During Gastrointestinal Endoscopy: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Fangsheng Xu, Yuanyuan Cui, Qian Wang, Jian Tang, Yuan Xu, Rongguang Liu, Zhengle Wang, Wei Zhang, Feng Wu, Shuyu Ma, Mincong Wang, Mengqi Liu, Jianfeng Pu, Meifang Wang, Ye Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPropofol combined with fentanyl is widely used for gastrointestinal endoscopy sedation but may be associated with oxygen desaturation. Oliceridine, a G protein-biased μ-opioid receptor agonist, may have fewer opioid-related respiratory effects. We compared propofol-oliceridine with propofol-fentanyl for desaturation during gastrointestinal endoscopy.MethodsThis single-center, double-blind, randomized clinical trial was conducted at Changshu No. 2 People’s Hospital, China. Adults aged 18–65 years with ASA physical status I–II undergoing bidirectional gastrointestinal endoscopy were randomized 1:1 to receive propofol combined with either oliceridine 20 μg/kg or fentanyl 1 μg/kg. The primary outcome was mild desaturation, defined as SpO2 <95%. Exploratory secondary outcomes included severe desaturation, SpO2 <90%, airway interventions, hypotension, bradycardia, recovery-related adverse events, and satisfaction scores.ResultsAmong 228 randomized patients, 224 were included in the modified intention-to-treat analysis. Mild desaturation occurred in 18 of 112 patients in the oliceridine group and 30 of 112 patients in the fentanyl group (16.1% vs 26.8%; unadjusted OR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.27–1.01; P=0.053; adjusted OR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.24–0.997; P=0.049). A full intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis including all randomized patients yielded consistent adjusted results (adjusted OR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.23–0.95; P=0.035). Severe desaturation and airway intervention did not differ significantly between groups. Compared with the fentanyl group, the oliceridine group had lower incidences of SBP <90 mmHg, SBP <80 mmHg, and vasopressor use. Initial sedation success and intra-procedural MOAA/S sedation depth were comparable between groups.ConclusionIn this randomized clinical trial of patients undergoing bidirectional gastrointestinal endoscopy, propofol-oliceridine may reduce mild desaturation and hypotension compared with propofol-fentanyl while maintaining comparable intra-procedural sedation depth.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2500096869.Keywords: gastrointestinal endoscopy, oliceridine, fentanyl, sedation, desaturation

Introduction

Gastrointestinal endoscopy is an essential diagnostic and therapeutic modality in contemporary clinical practice.1 Procedural sedation is commonly used to improve patient comfort, facilitate endoscope insertion, and optimize procedural conditions.2 Propofol remains one of the most widely used sedatives for gastrointestinal endoscopy because of its rapid onset and predictable recovery profile; however, dose-dependent respiratory depression, upper airway obstruction, and hypotension remain important concerns during propofol-based deep sedation.3,4

To improve tolerance to endoscopic stimulation and reduce propofol requirements, propofol is frequently combined with short-acting opioids such as fentanyl.5 Although this strategy may improve analgesia and procedural conditions, the combined respiratory depressant effects of propofol and opioids can narrow the respiratory safety margin and increase the risk of oxygen desaturation and airway intervention.6 Notably, previous studies have reported that mild desaturation (SpO2 <95%), occurs in up to 26.1% of patients receiving propofol-fentanyl sedation during gastrointestinal endoscopy.7 This high incidence highlights the need to optimize propofol‑based sedation strategies that maintain adequate procedural conditions while minimizing respiratory and hemodynamic instability.

Oliceridine is an intravenous μ-opioid receptor agonist that preferentially activates G-protein signaling with reduced β-arrestin recruitment, a pathway implicated in several opioid-related adverse effects.8–10 Clinical and pharmacodynamic studies suggest that oliceridine can provide analgesia with a potentially lower burden of respiratory adverse effects than conventional opioids in selected settings.11–13 These pharmacologic characteristics make oliceridine a plausible alternative opioid adjunct to propofol for procedural sedation. However, prospective comparative evidence directly evaluating propofol‑oliceridine versus propofol‑fentanyl during gastrointestinal endoscopy remains limited, and it is unclear whether oliceridine can reduce oxygen desaturation without compromising sedation efficacy.

Although SpO2 <90% is commonly used to define clinically more severe hypoxemia and serves as an indicator for airway intervention,14 mild desaturation may reflect a reduced respiratory safety margin and prompt closer airway assessment, increased oxygen flow, airway repositioning, jaw thrust, or other stepwise airway support before progression to more severe hypoxemia.15 Therefore, we hypothesized that, compared with fentanyl, oliceridine as an adjunct to propofol would reduce mild oxygen desaturation and hemodynamic instability while maintaining comparable sedation efficacy in adults undergoing bidirectional gastrointestinal endoscopy.

Methods

Study Design

This single-center, double-blind, randomized clinical trial was conducted at Changshu No. 2 People’s Hospital (Changshu, China). The study protocol was approved by the hospital Ethics Committee (No. 2024-KY-Y05) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500096869). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The trial was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki and is reported in compliance with the CONSORT statement.

Participants

Adults scheduled for sedated bidirectional gastrointestinal endoscopy, defined as esophagogastroduodenoscopy followed by colonoscopy, between February 2025 and September 2025 were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were age 18–65 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I–II, body mass index 18–30 kg/m2, and planned procedural sedation. Exclusion criteria included chronic use of sedative-hypnotics, opioids, gabapentinoids, or other centrally acting analgesics for ≥2 weeks within 1 month before enrollment; a history of gastrointestinal surgery; suspected difficult airway, including Mallampati class III or IV; diagnosed or suspected obstructive sleep apnea; clinically significant arrhythmia; known hypersensitivity or contraindication to any study medication; and planned therapeutic endoscopic procedures, including polypectomy, endoscopic mucosal resection, endoscopic submucosal dissection, hemostasis, or dilation.

For the modified intention-to-treat analysis, participants were excluded after randomization if the planned sedated procedure was converted to an unsedated endoscopy, the procedure was canceled, consent was withdrawn, or the procedure duration exceeded 30 minutes. The 30-minute cutoff was prespecified to reduce heterogeneity in sedation exposure, procedural stimulation, and propofol requirements, all of which may influence the risk of desaturation and hemodynamic events.

Randomization and Blinding

A research coordinator not involved in enrollment, intra-procedural management, or outcome assessment generated the randomization sequence using a web-based randomization tool. Block randomization with variable block sizes (4 and 8) was used, and participants were allocated in a 1:1 ratio to the oliceridine group (Group O) or fentanyl group (Group F). Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes.

An independent anesthesia nurse opened the envelope immediately before the procedure, prepared the assigned opioid in an identical syringe with a total volume of 2 mL, and labeled the syringe with the participant’s randomization number only. Patients, anesthesiologists, endoscopists, post-anesthesia care staff, and data analysts remained blinded to group assignment throughout the trial.

Study Interventions

All gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures were performed according to standardized institutional protocols. Patients fasted for at least 8 hours and abstained from clear liquids for at least 4 hours prior to the procedure. Upon arrival in the anesthesia preparation area, a peripheral intravenous catheter was inserted, and 0.9% sodium chloride solution was administered at a minimal maintenance rate. After transfer to the endoscopy suite, standard monitoring was applied, including electrocardiography (ECG), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), heart rate (HR), and pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2). Patients were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position and received supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula at 5 L/min throughout the procedure.

According to randomization, participants received either oliceridine 20 μg/kg (2 mL: 2 mg; Jiangsu Nhwa Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) or fentanyl 1 μg/kg (2 mL:0.1mg, Yichang Humanwell Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) intravenously. One minute later, propofol 1.5 mg/kg was administered intravenously at approximately 0.5 mL/s. Sedation depth was assessed using the Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) scale, which ranges from 5 (fully alert) to 0 (no response to painful stimuli), with lower scores indicating deeper sedation.16,17 Endoscope insertion was permitted only after a MOAA/S score ≤1 was achieved. If MOAA/S remained >1 after induction, an additional propofol bolus of 0.5 mg/kg was administered.

During the maintenance phase, sedation depth was assessed using the MOAA/S scale every 2 minutes and whenever clinical signs suggested inadequate sedation. The target maintenance depth was MOAA/S <3, together with the absence of purposeful movement, coughing, gagging, grimacing, or procedural interference. If MOAA/S was ≥3 or clinically inadequate sedation occurred, additional propofol boluses of 0.2–0.5 mg/kg were administered according to the patient’s clinical response and hemodynamic status. The same assessment interval, target sedation depth, and propofol titration algorithm were applied in both groups.

Endoscopy timing was categorized a priori as procedures performed before 12:00 or after 13:00 to account for the routine scheduling interval in our endoscopy unit. All procedures were performed by a single experienced endoscopist following a standardized sequence: esophagogastroduodenoscopy followed by colonoscopy. After completion, patients were transferred to the recovery area and assessed every 2 minutes using verbal and tactile stimulation. Discharge readiness was determined using the Modified Aldrete Score; discharge criteria required three consecutive scores ≥9.

During induction, NIBP was measured every 1 minute; during the endoscopy procedure, measurements were obtained every 2 minutes. Hypotension was treated with intravenous ephedrine (3–9 mg) when systolic blood pressure (SBP) was <80 mmHg or mean arterial pressure (MAP) was <60 mmHg. Bradycardia (heart rate <50 beats/min) was treated with intravenous atropine (0.3–0.5 mg). When SpO2 was <95% for more than 30 seconds or <90% at any time, oxygen flow was increased to 5–10 L/min and airway support was provided in a stepwise manner (jaw thrust and airway repositioning, insertion of an oral or nasopharyngeal airway, assisted mask ventilation as needed, and endotracheal intubation if refractory). All adverse events and interventions were recorded according to prespecified definitions.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of mild desaturation, defined as any episode of SpO2 <95% during the procedure.

Secondary outcomes included the incidence of severe desaturation (SpO2 <90%); airway interventions, defined as any airway support initiated in response to desaturation (jaw thrust, airway repositioning, insertion of an oral or nasopharyngeal airway, assisted mask ventilation, or endotracheal intubation); Initial sedation success, defined as achieving a MOAA/S score ≤1 within 1 minute after administration of propofol 1.5 mg/kg without movement, coughing, hiccup, or grimacing during endoscope insertion; Intra-procedural MOAA/S sedation-depth data were additionally summarized to assess the comparability of maintenance sedation depth between groups; hemodynamic events, including hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg), severe hypotension (SBP <80 mmHg), hypotension requiring pharmacologic intervention (SBP <80 mmHg or MAP <60 mmHg treated with ephedrine), and bradycardia (HR <50 beats/min); Hemodynamic trends, assessed by changes in MAP, SBP, and HR at predefined time points:T0 baseline (before opioid administration), T1: at MOAA/S ≤ 1, T2: start of gastroscopy, T3: start of colonoscopy, and T4: recovery (eye opening on verbal command). Post-procedure adverse events, including nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and fatigue. Fatigue was assessed at discharge using a 0–10 numerical rating scale (NRS), with scores ≥4 considered clinically significant. Satisfaction scores (0–10 NRS) reported independently by the anesthesiologist, endoscopist, and patient.

Additional procedural variables included total propofol consumption, endoscopy duration (from scope insertion to removal), and anesthesia duration (defined as the time from the start of anesthetic drug administration to eye opening in response to verbal command).

Sample Size

Based on prior literature, the incidence of mild desaturation (SpO2 <95%) during propofol-fentanyl sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy was approximately 26.1%.7 According to our preliminary data, the use of oliceridine in combination with propofol was associated with an absolute reduction of approximately 15 percentage points in the incidence of mild desaturation compared with fentanyl. Therefore, we assumed an incidence of 11% in the oliceridine group for sample size estimation. With a two-sided α level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, 206 patients were required to detect this difference. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of 10%, the final sample size was set at 228 participants.

Statistical Analysis

The primary analysis was conducted in the modified intention-to-treat population, which included randomized participants who received the assigned study medication and completed the sedated endoscopic procedure according to the prespecified protocol. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using graphical methods and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean (SD) and were compared using independent-sample t tests. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are summarized as counts (percentages) and were compared using χ2-tests or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate.

For binary outcomes, effect estimates were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) derived from logistic regression models. For continuous outcomes, linear regression models were used and results are reported as regression coefficients (B) with 95% CIs. The covariate-adjusted analysis of the primary outcome was prespecified and was used as the principal analysis to improve precision by adjusting for clinically relevant baseline factors, including age, sex, BMI, history of hypertension, and endoscopy timing (before 12:00 vs after 13:00). Unadjusted results are also reported for transparency. To assess the potential influence of post-randomization exclusions, a full intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis of the primary outcome was performed, including all randomized participants according to their assigned treatment groups.

Repeated-measures analysis of variance was used to assess changes in hemodynamic variables over time, including time effects, group effects, and group-by-time interactions. Prespecified subgroup analyses for the primary outcome were conducted according to age (≥60 vs <60 years), sex, BMI (<25 vs ≥25 kg/m2), hypertension status, endoscopy timing, endoscopy duration (>15 minutes vs ≤15 minutes), and initial sedation success. Treatment-by-subgroup interaction terms were tested to assess whether the treatment effect differed across subgroups.

No interim analyses were performed. Secondary outcomes were considered exploratory, and no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp) and R version 3.6.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

In the full intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis including all 228 randomized participants, mild desaturation occurred in 18 of 114 patients in Group O and 31 of 114 patients in Group F (15.8% vs 27.2%). The findings were consistent with the modified intention-to-treat analysis. After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, history of hypertension, and endoscopy timing, propofol-oliceridine remained associated with a lower incidence of mild desaturation compared with propofol-fentanyl (adjusted OR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.23–0.95; P=0.035).

Secondary Outcome

The incidence of severe desaturation (SpO2 <90%) did not differ significantly between groups (6.3% vs 8.0%; adjusted OR 0.67; 95% CI 0.23–1.95; P = 0.461). Similarly, airway intervention was required in 9.8% of patients in the oliceridine group and 15.2% in the fentanyl group (adjusted OR 0.58; 95% CI 0.24–1.38; P = 0.219) (Table 2).

Hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg) occurred less frequently in the oliceridine group than in the fentanyl group (43.8% vs 63.4%; adjusted OR 0.44; 95% CI 0.25–0.77; P = 0.004). Severe hypotension (SBP <80 mmHg) was also less common with oliceridine (17.9% vs 35.7%; adjusted OR 0.40; 95% CI 0.21–0.75; P = 0.004). The need for vasopressor intervention was significantly lower in the oliceridine group (22.3% vs 39.3%; adjusted OR 0.45; 95% CI 0.25–0.80; P = 0.007). Bradycardia (HR <50 beats/min) did not differ significantly between groups (Table 2).

Initial sedation success rates were similar between groups (84.8% vs 83.9%; adjusted OR 0.87; 95% CI 0.41–1.83; P = 0.709). There were no significant differences in postoperative nausea and vomiting, dizziness, or fatigue scores at discharge. Satisfaction scores reported by anesthesiologists (adjusted B 0.71; 95% CI 0.31–1.11; P < 0.001) and endoscopists (adjusted B 0.36; 95% CI 0.02–0.71; P = 0.041) were higher in the oliceridine group, whereas patient satisfaction did not differ significantly between groups (Table 2).

Intra-Procedural MOAA/S Sedation Depth

Intra-procedural MOAA/S sedation-depth data are summarized in Table 3. The number of MOAA/S assessments per patient was similar between groups [8.0 (IQR, 7.0–9.0) vs 8.0 (IQR, 6.75–9.0); P=0.666]. The mean intra-procedural MOAA/S score per patient did not differ significantly between groups (0.708 ± 0.191 vs 0.689 ± 0.181; P=0.447). Most recorded MOAA/S assessments were within the prespecified maintenance target of MOAA/S <3 in both groups [865/895 (96.6%) vs 873/898 (97.2%)]. Lighter-than-target sedation, defined as MOAA/S ≥3, occurred infrequently and similarly between groups [30/895 (3.4%) vs 25/898 (2.8%)]. The proportion of patients with any MOAA/S ≥3 during maintenance was also comparable [29/112 (25.9%) vs 25/112 (22.3%); P=0.640]. These findings suggest that maintenance sedation depth was broadly comparable between groups according to the prespecified target.

Hemodynamic Fluctuations

Mauchly’s test indicated violation of the sphericity assumption for both mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR) (both P < 0.001); therefore, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. No significant group-by-time interaction was detected for MAP (F = 0.615; P = 0.633) or HR (F = 0.095; P = 0.970), and main effects were analyzed.

There was a significant effect of time for both MAP (F = 388.346; P < 0.001) and HR (F = 162.589; P < 0.001), characterized by an initial decrease followed by recovery. A significant group effect was observed for MAP (F = 9.233; P = 0.003), with lower values in the fentanyl group than in the oliceridine group at T1-T3 (all P < 0.05). In contrast, HR did not differ between groups (F = 0.185; P = 0.668) (Figure 2).

Subgroup Analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses showed no significant interaction between treatment assignment and age, sex, BMI, hypertension status, initial sedation success, or endoscopy duration (all P for interaction >0.05). A greater reduction in desaturation was observed in procedures performed before 12:00 (P = 0.022), whereas no significant difference was noted for procedures performed after 13:00 (P = 0.824). However, the interaction test was not statistically significant, suggesting overall consistency of treatment effect across subgroups (Figure 3).

Discussion

This randomized clinical trial found that, compared with propofol-fentanyl, propofol-oliceridine was associated with a lower incidence of mild desaturation during bidirectional gastrointestinal endoscopy. The oliceridine regimen was also associated with fewer hypotensive events and reduced vasopressor use, while initial sedation success, recovery-related adverse events, and patient satisfaction were similar between groups. These findings support further evaluation of propofol-oliceridine as a potential alternative opioid adjunct to propofol-based sedation in selected patients undergoing bidirectional gastrointestinal endoscopy.

Respiratory depression remains one of the most clinically relevant adverse events during propofol-based gastrointestinal endoscopy sedation. Compared with severe desaturation or hypoxemia, mild desaturation (SpO2 <95%) occurs more frequently in clinical practice and may serve as an early signal of compromised respiratory function. Such episodes often prompt closer airway assessment, increased oxygen flow, airway repositioning, or jaw thrust before progression to more severe hypoxemia.18,19 Therefore, this threshold may serve as a sensitive measure of early respiratory instability during deep sedation. In the present study, mild desaturation occurred in 26.8% of patients receiving propofol-fentanyl, which is consistent with prior reports in comparable endoscopy settings.7 The absolute risk reduction with propofol-oliceridine was 10.7 percentage points, corresponding to approximately one fewer mild desaturation event for every ten patients treated. Although a universally accepted minimal clinically important difference for SpO2 <95% has not been established in this setting, the observed reduction suggests a potentially meaningful decrease in early respiratory instability. These findings are concordant with prior procedural sedation data; Ma et al20 similarly reported reduced mild respiratory depression and fewer airway interventions with oliceridine compared with sufentanil during gastrointestinal endoscopy.

Notably, under the same MOAA/S-guided protocol, both initial sedation success and intra-procedural sedation-depth data were comparable between groups. This supports the interpretation that differences in opioid pharmacology may have contributed to the observed respiratory profile. Pharmacodynamic studies have suggested that oliceridine can provide μ-opioid receptor-mediated analgesia with less respiratory depression than conventional opioids, and its rapid intravenous onset, relatively short terminal half-life, and metabolism into largely inactive metabolites may be compatible with brief monitored sedation procedures.21 From an analgesic potency perspective, oliceridine has been estimated to be approximately 5-fold as potent as morphine, whereas fentanyl is approximately 100-fold as potent as morphine.22–24 This approximate potency relationship provided a pharmacologic rationale for selecting oliceridine 20 μg/kg and fentanyl 1 μg/kg as clinically comparable opioid adjunct doses. However, a validated equianalgesic conversion ratio between oliceridine and fentanyl has not been established for gastrointestinal endoscopy sedation. Therefore, residual pharmacodynamic or dose-related influences cannot be fully excluded, although similar initial sedation success and the use of the same MOAA/S-guided titration protocol support the practical comparability of the two regimens under the study protocol.

Hemodynamic instability is a well-recognized concern during propofol-based sedation, particularly in patients undergoing bowel preparation and prolonged fasting.25 In both groups, mean arterial pressure declined after induction and gradually recovered during the post-procedural phase, consistent with the vasodilatory and myocardial depressant effects of propofol.26,27 However, patients receiving oliceridine exhibited consistently higher MAP values during induction and early procedural stages, along with significantly lower incidences of hypotension and vasopressor use. These findings align with those of Chen et al,28 who observed improved hemodynamic stability with oliceridine versus fentanyl during anesthetic induction in elderly patients. Similarly, Ma et al20 reported a lower incidence of hypotension with oliceridine compared with sufentanil during gastrointestinal endoscopy under sedation. Collectively, these findings suggest that propofol-oliceridine may be associated with a more stable hemodynamic profile than propofol-fentanyl in selected patients.

Although clinician-reported satisfaction scores were higher in the oliceridine group, the absolute between-group differences were small and may not represent clinically meaningful improvements. Moreover, because satisfaction was assessed using a numerical rating scale that has not been formally validated for this specific purpose, these outcomes should be considered exploratory.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this single-center study enrolled selected adults with ASA physical status I–II and BMI 18–30 kg/m2 undergoing non-therapeutic bidirectional gastrointestinal endoscopy, which may limit generalizability. Second, because oxygen supplementation and airway support were initiated early, the effect of propofol-oliceridine on more severe respiratory outcomes, including SpO2 <90% and airway intervention, remains uncertain. Third, although the prespecified adjusted analysis and full intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis yielded consistent findings, the unadjusted comparison did not reach conventional statistical significance; larger multicenter trials are needed to confirm the magnitude and clinical relevance of the observed effect. Fourth, the selected opioid doses represented clinically comparable regimens rather than a validated equianalgesic conversion ratio; therefore, subtle pharmacodynamic or dose-related differences cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In adults undergoing bidirectional gastrointestinal endoscopy, propofol-oliceridine may reduce mild desaturation and hypotension compared with propofol-fentanyl while maintaining comparable intra-procedural sedation depth. Further studies are needed to determine whether this regimen reduces clinically more severe respiratory events and to confirm its generalizability in broader patient populations.
