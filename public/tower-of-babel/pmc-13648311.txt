Rouhani et al. ‘Formaldehyde Emissions From 2‐Octyl Cyanoacrylate: Quantified Risk for Allergic Contact Dermatitis’
Authors: Jörg Tomaszewski, Monica Posgai, Julian Quintero
Published: 2026

We read with interest the article by Rouhani et al. [1] reporting formaldehyde emissions from 2‐octyl‐cyanoacrylate mesh based adhesive systems. While this work presents initial quantitative data, we believe that several methodological and interpretive limitations materially limit the relevance of the findings for clinical medical device safety assessment.

From a medical device toxicological perspective, the study design does not align with the principles of ISO 10993‐1 risk based biological evaluation. The authors apply a microchamber method (ASTM D7706) as a surrogate for patient and healthcare provider exposure. However, this method is intended for comparative screening of volatile organic compounds from indoor products, not for quantifying clinically relevant dermal or inhalation exposure from externally applied medical devices. Although this distinction is critical, it remains insufficiently addressed when extrapolating laboratory outputs to clinical risk.

Formaldehyde emission estimates are derived from only two small area samples, without statistical characterization of variability or reproducibility, limiting confidence in both absolute values and downstream exposure modelling. Furthermore, hydrolysis was artificially initiated by a humidified chamber environment rather than the natural hydrolysis that may occur on human skin, introducing uncertainty regarding polymerization kinetics and degradation pathways that are not addressed in the manuscript.

The microchamber was operated at 1 air change per hour, a condition that does not reflect clinical environments such as operating rooms or recovery areas, where substantially higher ventilation rates are standard. No effort was made to model clinically relevant ventilation conditions. Consequently, the reported airborne concentrations cannot be considered representative of realistic patient or provider exposure. Nevertheless, these values are compared directly with occupational or health protective benchmarks, without adequate discussion of the disconnect between static laboratory conditions and dynamic clinical and post‐clinical settings.

Equally important, the dermal exposure evaluation relies on benchmarks that are not intended as device safety limits. The EPA 1 μg/cm2 ‘health‐protective’ value used as a comparator is a conservative screening benchmark derived from occluded patch‐test data in formaldehyde‐sensitized individuals, incorporating precautionary uncertainty factors. Comparisons to occupational exposure limits are made without time‐weighting, breathing‐zone modelling, or adjustment for clinical ventilation, potentially overstating inhalation relevance. These are not regulatory acceptance criteria for medical devices and are not used by the FDA as stand‐alone determinants of safety. Under ISO 10993‐17 and FDA toxicological risk‐assessment frameworks, such screening values must be interpreted in the context of dose magnitude, duration, route of exposure, population susceptibility and overall clinical risk, rather than treated as exceedance thresholds—principles that are not clearly articulated in the manuscript.

The exposure modelling further increases uncertainty. Two‐hour microchamber measurements are extrapolated to 7–14 days under constant‐flux or semi‐constant assumptions. Given the chemistry of long‐chain cyanoacrylates and the variability of clinical wear conditions, these assumptions may overestimate cumulative dose in the absence of empirical validation under clinically relevant conditions.

Notably, the manuscript provides limited regulatory context. Laboratory emission data generated under highly controlled conditions should not be regarded as a surrogate for regulatory toxicological risk assessment. Medical‐device safety evaluations rely on a totality‐of‐evidence approach, integrating material characterization, biological testing, toxicological risk assessment and post‐market surveillance under conditions relevant to clinical use, in a risk management quality system. In the absence of this framework, these bench‐top findings should be interpreted with caution.

In summary, the study's testing paradigm, benchmark selection, modelling assumptions and constrained consideration of clinical and regulatory reality substantially limit its significance for medical device toxicological risk assessment under FDA and ISO 10993 principles.
