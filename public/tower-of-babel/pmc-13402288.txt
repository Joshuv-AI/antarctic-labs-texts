Using an RNA binding protein to detect mRNA on lipid nanoparticles
Authors: Meagan McMahon, Hareth Al-Wassiti, Kirsten Vandenberg, Karen L. Laurie, Steven Rockman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of lipid nanoparticles (LNP) to deliver nucleic acid has emerged as a promising technology for vaccines and gene therapy. The efficiency of delivery relies on the ability of lipid chemistry to encapsulate the nucleic acid within its shell rather than exposing the nucleic acid on the surface of the LNP. To develop an encapsulation assay to detect the amount of mRNA on the surface or internalised in a LNP, we utilised a viral RNA binding protein, a monoclonal antibody to this RNA binding protein and a magnetic bead arrangement to determine the encapsulation efficiency of the RNA in a standard RNA LNP formulation. By manipulating the components, we could determine the efficiency of RNA incorporation within the LNP. We suggest that this approach will be useful in optimising the delivery of nucleic acids by LNPs and their applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43554-0.

Introduction

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) vaccines have emerged as a promising approach for the treatment and prevention of various diseases, particularly against the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) virus responsible for causing the worldwide pandemic of the severely infectious coronavirus 2019 disease (COVID-19). mRNA vaccines rely on the delivery of the mRNA into the cytoplasm of host cells, where it is transcribed into antigenic proteins to trigger an antibody response. However, the large size and negative charge of mRNA prevent efficient cellular uptake1. In addition, vaccination of naked mRNA in vivo leads to degradation of the mRNA prior to cellular uptake2. Therefore, lipid delivery vehicles, such as lipid emulsions, liposomes or lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are used to encapsulate the mRNA, blocking degradation of the mRNA in plasma, whilst also promoting cellular uptake for efficient delivery of the mRNA in vivo. Lipid delivery vehicles are commonly formed using cationic lipids, such as DOTAP, or ionisable lipids, such as D-Lin-MC3 (PMID: 35787388). Other neutral lipids, like cholesterol, PEGylated lipids and phospholipids, are used to help stabilise the LNP and increase LNP efficacy, tolerability and biodistribution3,4. LNPs also help nucleic acids to pass through the plasma membrane of the cell and enter the cytoplasm more readily2. However, several adverse cytotoxic effects of LNPs are known, including the production of reactive oxygen species5,6 and accumulation of LNPs in the liver7.

Ongoing efforts focus on identifying novel lipids or lipid compositions that can protect mRNA from degradation and elimination, as well as provide effective intracellular delivery and/or expression/translation of mRNA. Current mRNA-LNP formulations approved for human use contain a ratio of ~ 50:1.5 (mole: mole) ionizable lipid to pegylated lipid8. However, this high ratio of ionizable lipid to pegylated lipid may be the cause of reactogenicity5,6. Encapsulation of mRNA into LNPs is typically measured with fluorescently labelled RNA dyes9. If mRNA is encapsulated efficiently, the signal from the fluorescently labelled RNA dye is weak as Ionisable lipids encapsulate RNA within a lipophilic and compact particle shielding it from Fluorescence dye binding that drives fluorescence signal, indicating that the mRNA has been successfully incorporated into the LNP10. However, novel LNP formulations that utilise proteins for targeted LNP delivery (e.g. antibody targeted LNPs recently described by Breda et al. for hemopoietic stem cell modification11 may affect the ability for the fluorescently labelled RNA dye to bind mRNA, affecting the ability to assess encapsulation efficiency. To circumvent this, we developed an immunodetection assay to determine the amount of encapsulated mRNA when the mRNA is bound to a recombinant protein. Our immunodetection assay utilises an anti-nucleoprotein (NuP) monoclonal antibody (mAb) bound to an influenza NuP, a known RNA binding protein, to detect any unencapsulated mRNA (Fig. 1). The anti-NuP mAb is able to bind mRNA that has been bound to an influenza NuP allowing effective measurement of encapsulation. Indeed, adding the LNPs to the magnetic alone could also determine whether the cationic lipid was on the surface of the LNP rather than internalised. This was demonstrated when the LNPs were formulated with DOTAP known to externalise this cationic lipid12. Thus, this assay enables the determination of free RNA, incorporated RNA, surface RNA and surface cationic lipid.

Methods

mRNA synthesis

Modified mRNA expressing nanoluciferase was synthesized using the Hiscribe mRNA IVT kit as recommended by the manufacturer (New England BioLabs, USA). Cap1 (TriLink, USA) and N1-MethylPseudoUridine (MedChem Express Catalogue # HY-112582) RNA reactions were run for 3 h followed by DNase digestion. mRNA was precipitated by Lithium chloride (8 M) and then further purified by cellulose purification as described13. RNA concentration was adjusted with water and measured using a Nanodrop (Thermofisher, USA). The nanoluciferase mRNA was tested for integrity using denaturing formaldehyde agarose gel electrophoresis as previously described14. Purified mRNA was stored at -80 °C until use.

Preparation of LNPs that contain an ionizable lipid

LNPs were prepared as described previously PMID: 39,687,732. Briefly, LNPs formulated with an ionizable lipid were prepared with D-Lin-MC3-DMA (MedChem Express, Catalogue #HY-112251), DSPC (Avanti Polar Lipids, Catalogue #850365 C), Cholesterol (Sigma, Catalogue #C1231) and DMG-PEG 2000 (Avanti Polar Lipids, Catalogue #880151P), with the ratio of lipids prescribed in Table 1. Lipids were prepared in 200 proof ethanol (Sigma, Catalogue #E7023) to 10 mM and lipids were then mixed at different ratios of ionizable lipid (Dlin-MC3-DMA) to DMG-PEG. mRNA encoding luciferase were prepared for LNP formulation with (NuP+mRNA) or without recombinant NuP (mRNA) derived from B/Florida/04/2006 (ATUM). The ratio of recombinant NuP to mRNA was a 2:1 mass ratio. The mRNA or NuP+mRNA and lipid mix were then encapsulated into a LNP using a NanoAssemblr Ignite (Precision NanoSystems) and dialysed against 10 mM Tris-HCL, 8.8% Sucrose (w/v) overnight. Samples were stored at 4 °C until use. Following dialysis, LNP sizes were measured with dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Malvern Zetasizer instrument.

Preparation of LNPs that contain a cationic lipid

LNPs formulated with a cationic lipid were prepared with DOTAP (Avanti Polar Lipids, Catalogue #890890P), DSPC, Cholesterol and DMG-PEG 2000. Lipids were prepared in ethanol to 10 mM. mRNA encoding luciferase were prepared for LNP formulation with (NuP+mRNA) or without recombinant NuP (mRNA) derived from B/Florida/04/2006. The ratio of recombinant NuP to mRNA was 2:1 (weight: weight). The mRNA or NuP+mRNA and lipid mix were then encapsulated into a LNP using a NanoAssemblr Ignite (Precision NanoSystems) and dialysed against Tris-sucrose overnight. Samples were stored at 4 °C until use. Particle sizes were measured with DLS as above.

mRNA immunodetection assay with a recombinant NuP

PBS (Gibco, Catalogue #10010023) washed magnetic beads (Dynabeads Protein G, Invitrogen, Catalogue #10003D) were coated with 0–10 µg of an anti-NuP mAb (prepared in house) and incubated for 20 min at room temperature with rotation. The magnetic beads were then placed on a magnet and the supernatant containing any unbound anti-NuP mAb was removed. The magnetic beads were washed in PBS to remove any residual unbound anti-NuP mAb and the magnetic beads were coated with 20 ug of NuP recombinant protein and incubated for 20 min at room temperature with shaking rotation. The magnetic beads were then placed on a magnet and the supernatant containing any unbound recombinant NuP was removed. The magnetic beads were washed in PBS to remove any further unbound recombinant NuP protein. The magnetic beads were incubated with different concentrations of luciferase mRNA (500, 100, 50, 10 or 0 ng) in 100 µL PBS for 20 min at room temperature with shaking rotation. The magnetic beads were then placed on a magnet and the supernatant collected (termed “Unbound from Magnet”) and the magnetic beads were resuspended in PBS (termed “Bound to Magnet”). The “Unbound from Magnet” and “Bound to Magnet” supernatant were then diluted 1:10 in 0.05% Triton-X 100 (Sigma-Aldrich, Catalogue #X100) and incubated at 95 °C for 10 min. The supernatants were allowed to cool to room temperature and RNA concentration was measured using the Ribogreen RNA assay kit (Invitrogen, Catalogue #R11490). These assays were performed twice.

Immunodetection of mRNA on LNP surface with recombinant protein

PBS washed magnetic beads (Dynabeads Protein G) were coated with 0–10 µg of an anti-NuP mAb and incubated for 20 min at room temperature with shaking rotation. The magnetic beads were then placed on a magnet and the supernatant containing any unbound anti-NuP mAb was removed. The magnetic beads were then washed in PBS to remove any residual unbound anti-NuP mAb and the magnetic beads were then coated with 20 µg of recombinant protein (influenza NuP or influenza hemagglutinin (Creative Biomart, Catalogue #HA-002) and incubated for 20 min at room temperature with shaking rotation. The magnetic beads were then placed on a magnet and the supernatant containing any unbound recombinant protein was removed. The magnetic beads were washed in PBS to further remove any unbound recombinant protein. The magnetic beads were then coated with mRNA-LNPs or (NuP+mRNA)-LNPs were incubated for 20 min at room temperature with shaking rotation. The magnetic beads were then placed on a magnet and the supernatant collected (Unbound from Magnet) and the magnetic beads were resuspended in PBS (Bound to Magnet). The Unbound from Magnet and Bound to Magnet supernatant were then diluted 1:10 in 0.05% Triton-X100 and incubated at 95 °C for 10 min. The supernatants were allowed to cool to room temperature and RNA concentration was measured using the Ribogreen RNA assay kit. These assays were performed 2–3 times. Encapsulation efficiency was calculated by the average replicates then dividing the sum of eluted mRNA from the total mRNA.

Results

Recombinant NuP binds to mRNA in an immunodetection assay

The influenza NuP is a known RNA binding protein15. To determine if an influenza NuP could be used in an immunodetection assay for mRNA on the surface of a LNP, different concentrations of luciferase mRNA (500, 100, 50, 10 or 0 ng) were added to Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb. When recombinant NuP was added to the Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb, we observed that the amount of mRNA bound to the magnet corresponded to the amount of input mRNA and no mRNA was eluted from the magnet (Fig. 2A). When recombinant NuP was not added to the Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb, we observed that the amount of mRNA eluted from the magnet corresponded to the amount of input mRNA and no mRNA was bound to the magnet (Fig. 2B). We also tested if a non-RNA binding protein, haemagglutinin (HA) and an anti-influenza HA mAb, would cause any background binding of mRNA in the immunodetection assay (Figure S1). Here we found that the amount of mRNA eluted from the magnet corresponded to the amount of input mRNA and no mRNA was bound to the magnet. These results indicate that a RNA binding protein (NuP) can bind to mRNA in an antibody immunodetection assay and this feature could be used to detect mRNA on the surface of a LNP.

mRNA on the surface of a LNP can be detected in an immunodetection assay using recombinant NuP

mRNA-LNPs formulated with decreasing amounts of ionizable lipid may be beneficial to circumvent observed reactogenicity following mRNA-LNP vaccination5. However, decreasing the amount of ionizable lipid may affect the ability for the mRNA to be efficiently encapsulated. To determine if decreasing the amount of ionizable lipid affected mRNA encapsulation we used our immunodetection assay with mRNA-LNPs formulated with decreasing amounts of ionizable lipid (mole ratio − 50:1.5, 20:1.5, 15:1.5, 10:1.5 5:1.5 and 0:1.5). We first assessed mRNA-LNP size and found the size decreased with decreasing amounts of ionizable lipid (Fig. 3A). No notable differences in polydispersity were observed (Fig. 3B). To assess mRNA encapsulation, we used our immunodetection assay to determine if mRNA was found outside of the LNP. To assess immunodetection of mRNA in the presence of recombinant NuP, mRNA-LNPs with decreasing amounts of ionizable lipid (50:1.5, 20:1.5, 15:1.5, 10:1.5 5:1.5 and 0:1.5 ionizable lipid to pegylated lipid) were added to Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb. When recombinant NuP was added to the Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb, we observed that decreasing the amount of ionizable lipid in an mRNA-LNP lead to more mRNAs being bound to the magnet, indicating that some mRNA was on the outside of the LNP (Fig. 3C). There was a trend for a dose response, with 4 times more mRNA being detected on the outside of the LNP at a ratio of 5:1.5 as compared to 50:1.5. However, most of the detected mRNA was eluted from the magnet, indicating that the majority of mRNA was on the inside of an mRNA-LNP, even when the amount of ionizable lipid used in the mRNA-LNP is decreased.

When recombinant NuP was not added to the Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb, we observed that no mRNA-LNPs were bound to the magnet (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that using recombinant NuP in an antibody immunodetection assay allows the immunodetection of mRNA on the surface of a LNP and that decreasing the amount of ionizable lipid in a mRNA-LNP does lead to increased amounts of exogenous mRNA, though this is not substantial.

Using NuP bound mRNA in a LNP leads to more mRNA on the surface

Given that mRNA could be detected on the surface of mRNA-LNPs that contain decreasing ratios of ionizable lipid, we wanted to investigate if the addition of a mRNA protectant could help prevent mRNA degradation. Here, we bound mRNA to an influenza NuP, a known RNA binding protein, and then formulated LNPs with the expectation that the mRNA/NuP would be enclosed in the LNP (NuP+mRNA-LNPs). LNPs were formulated with decreasing amounts of ionizable lipid. It is likely that adding a protein to the mRNA and decreasing the amount of ionizable lipid will lead to changes in LNP size, polydispersity and NuP+mRNA encapsulation into the LNP. We first assessed NuP+mRNA-LNP size and found that size of the LNPs increased with decreasing amounts of ionizable lipid (Fig. 4A). The size of NuP+mRNA-LNPs were also larger than the mRNA-LNP counterparts (Fig. 3A). In particular, the 0:1.5 NuP+mRNA-LNPs increased when compared to the 0:1.5 mRNA-LNPs. NuP+mRNA-LNPs were also more disperse (Fig. 4B) when compared to the mRNA-LNP counterparts (Fig. 3B). To assess mRNA encapsulation, we used our immunodetection assay to determine if mRNA was found outside of the LNP interior. To assess immunodetection of mRNA in the presence of recombinant NuP, NuP+mRNA-LNPs with decreasing amounts of ionizable lipid (50:1.5, 20:1.5, 15:1.5, 10:1.5 5:1.5 and 0:1.5) were added to Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb. When recombinant NuP was added to the Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb, we observed similar levels of mRNA being bound to the magnet (Fig. 4C). However, most of the detected mRNA was eluted from the magnet, indicating that the majority of mRNA is on the inside of a NuP-mRNA-LNP, even when the amount of ionizable lipid used in the NuP+mRNA-LNP is decreased. When recombinant NuP was not added to the Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb, we observed that no NuP+mRNA-LNPs were bound to the magnet (Fig. 4D). These results indicate the anti-NuP mAb could not detect any NuP on the surface of the LNP.

LNPs that contain a surface cationic lipid bind to the magnetic beads

Lastly, we wanted to determine if the mRNA encapsulation assay described here could be used with mRNA-LNPs that are formulated with a cationic lipid, a common lipid used in LNP formulation, but not currently approved for human use. Here, mRNA or NuP+mRNAs were formulated with a DOTAP cationic lipid at a 50:1.5 ratio of ionizable lipid to PEG to generate mRNA-LNPs and NuP+mRNA-LNPs. We first assessed LNP size and again found that binding mRNA to NuP lead a larger LNP size (Fig. 5A). Although mRNA-LNP and NuP+mRNA-LNPs formulated with cationic lipids showed similar dispersity (Fig. 5B). To assess mRNA encapsulation, we used our immunodetection assay to determine if mRNA was found on the surface of the LNP as opposed to the interior when the LNP contained a cationic lipid. mRNA-LNPs or NuP+mRNA-LNPs formulated with cationic lipids were added to Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb. When recombinant NuP was added to the Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb, we found that both LNP formulations were bound to the magnet (Fig. 5C). When recombinant NuP was not added to the Protein G dynabeads bound to an anti-NuP mAb, we again observed that both LNP formulations bound to the magnet (Fig. 5D). Lastly, we performed the immunodetection assay in the absence of the anti-NuP mAb and found that both LNP formulations were bound to the magnet (Fig. 5E), an occurrence we did not observe with mRNA-LNPs or NuP+mRNA-LNPs (Figure S2). These results indicate that mRNA encapsulation into a LNP with a magnetic bead-based immunodetection assay cannot be assessed when LNPs are formulated with cationic lipids.

Discussion

The presence of a RNA binding protein in mRNA-LNP formulations presents a challenge for measuring encapsulation efficiency. Because of this, we developed an immunodetection assay where an anti-NuP mAb bound to recombinant NuP was used to bind any mRNA that may be present in solution. This assay worked effectively for unencapsulated mRNA, where we observed that if recombinant NuP was present then the mRNA was able to be immunoprecipitated onto a magnet bead. When assessing if we could immunoprecipitate mRNA from mRNA-LNPs or NuP+mRNA-LNPs that contained decreasing ratios of ionizable lipid to pegylated lipid we found that majority of the mRNA was encapsulated, but more mRNA was detected in mRNA-LNP formulations as the ratio decreased.

A titration of mRNA on the surface of NuP+mRNA-LNP formulations was not observed when the ratio of ionizable lipid to pegylated lipid decreased. Rather, the amount of mRNA bound to the magnet when recombinant NuP was used in the assay remained at similar levels across all formulations (50:1.5, 20:1.5, 15:1.5, 10:1.5 5:1.5 and 0:1.5). It is likely that the use of recombinant NuP in the NuP+mRNA-LNP formulations lead to changes in LNP structure which may lead to partial mRNA exposure on the LNP surface detectable when the immunodetection assay utilised recombinant NuP. When the immunodetection assay was performed in the absence of recombinant NuP, no mRNA was bound to the magnet, suggesting that the NuP was not exposed on the surface of the LNP, only mRNA.

Although this assay worked effectively for LNPs formulated with an ionizable lipid, it did not work with LNPs formulated with a cationic lipid. Cationic lipids have a head group with a permanent positive charge16. When assessing mRNA-LNPs and NuP+mRNA-LNPs formulated with cationic lipids we found that the positive charge led to LNP binding to the magnetic beads in the absence of both recombinant NuP and the anti-NuP mAb. Blakney et al. (2019), used cationic lipids to bind mRNA to the surface of a LNP, instead of encapsulating mRNA within the interior of LNP. We intended to create similar formulations for our body of work to definitively prove that our immunodetection assay could distinguish between mRNA on the surface versus the interior. Future iterations of this assay may look to develop formulations with mRNA on the surface that are not subject to issues with the cationic lipid.

In addition, in this work we bound a standard amount of an anti-NuP mAb to Protein G dynabeads. A standard amount of recombinant NuP was then added, followed by the addition of mRNA-LNPs or NuP+mRNA-LNPs. Subsequent iterations of this assay may wish to investigate different concentrations of recombinant NuP or anti-NuP mA, incubation times or use alternative methods for measuring the amount of mRNA bound to the magnetic beads or eluted from the magnetic beads. In addition, it may be beneficial to investigate alternative ways to bind the recombinant NuP to mRNA-LNP. For example, by encasing the mRNA-LNP with exogenous NuP prior to adding to the anti-NuP mAb. Further iterations may also test if there are limitations with the length of the mRNA sequence.

The development of novel LNP formulations that are formulated with recombinant proteins or antibodies16,17 may limit the use of fluorescent dye-based encapsulation assays, due to the protein blocking the fluorescent dye access to the mRNA. Current assays, such as the Ribogreen assay, do not discriminate between free and encapsulated RNA. Ribogreen is an assay that is strongly dependant on many assumptions including that the Lipid nanoparticles are fully intact and do not let Ribogreen access This assay intends to bridge this gap by allowing the quantification of free and encapsulated RNA. This assay could also help decipher formulations with surface RNA versus LNP formulations that are fully encapsulated. In addition, LNP formulations with leaky or entry permissible may not be measurable with Ribogreen dye as Ribogreen cannot differentiate between internal/external mRNA, adding to the repertoire of available techniques to the researchers to investigate encapsulation. The assay described here is a simple and cost-effective approach to determine the efficiency of incorporation prior to expression or biodistribution assays. In conclusion, our work provides a novel and simple approach manipulating materials to measure the encapsulation efficiency of mRNA, the location of the mRNA in LNP formulations and whether any cationic lipid was being expressed on the surface of the LNPs using a magnetic-based immunodetection assay to determine the level of free RNA.
