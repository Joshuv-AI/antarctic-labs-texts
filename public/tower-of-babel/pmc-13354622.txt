Short-Term and Long-Term Safety Analyses of Brexpiprazole for Agitation Associated with Dementia due to Alzheimer’s Disease: Timing and Duration of Adverse Events
Authors: Anton P. Porsteinsson, Sanjeda R. Chumki, David Wang, Pedro Such, Anton M. Palma, Zhen Zhang, Alpesh Shah, Uwa Kalu, C. Brendan Montano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAgitation symptoms are a common and burdensome aspect of Alzheimer’s dementia. Historically, agitation has been managed using off-label treatments such as atypical antipsychotics, but this approach is associated with safety concerns in older, more vulnerable patients. Brexpiprazole is an atypical antipsychotic that has been recently approved in several countries for the treatment of agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease. Previous analyses show that brexpiprazole was efficacious and generally well tolerated for up to 24 weeks. Building upon previous work, this post hoc analysis aimed to evaluate the timing and duration of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) during brexpiprazole treatment.MethodsIn a 12-week analysis, data were pooled from three phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials of brexpiprazole in participants with agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease. In a separate 24-week analysis, brexpiprazole data were combined from a 12-week randomized trial and a 12-week active-treatment extension trial. The median time from starting treatment to first reporting a TEAE and the median duration of all TEAEs were determined.ResultsA total of 1043 participants received at least one dose of trial medication. Over 12 weeks, brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day (the approved therapeutic dosages in the United States, N = 366) compared to placebo (N = 388) had similar time to first TEAE (32 days and 28 days, respectively), similar duration of all TEAEs (6 days and 4 days), and longer time to discontinuation due to adverse events (47 days and 30 days). Over 24 weeks (N = 163), the time to first TEAE on brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day was 52 days, and the duration of all TEAEs was 3 days. Among participants who did not report a TEAE in the 12-week parent trial, TEAEs were rare throughout the 12-week extension trial.ConclusionsThese exploratory analyses reinforce that brexpiprazole is generally well tolerated over 12 weeks, and also over 24 weeks among patients who tolerated the first 12 weeks of treatment. The results provide a practical clinical insight into the safety of brexpiprazole over time in patients with agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease.Trial RegistrationPost hoc analysis of NCT01862640, NCT01922258, NCT03548584, NCT03594123 (ClinicalTrials.gov).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40264-026-01670-w.

Key Points

In 12-week clinical trials in participants with agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease, adverse events occurred at similar times after starting treatment, and had similar durations, with brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day and placebo. Participants who stopped treatment because of an adverse event did so earlier with placebo compared with brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day.Among participants who tolerated the first 12 weeks of treatment, brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day remained well tolerated in an extension trial over a total of up to 24 weeks.These analyses provide further support for the tolerability of brexpiprazole for agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s dementia is a neurodegenerative disorder associated with cognitive decline usually involving memory loss [1]. Agitation symptoms are a burdensome aspect of Alzheimer’s dementia that are experienced by approximately half of patients [2, 3]. Agitation symptoms may be non-aggressive or aggressive, and can be characterized as excessive motor activity, verbal aggression, or physical aggression [4]. For patients, agitation symptoms reduce quality of life, reduce the ability to function and live at home, and are associated with faster deterioration and earlier death [5–8]. For caregivers, agitation symptoms are associated with increased burden and psychological distress [9].

When pharmacological intervention is warranted for agitation, patients are commonly prescribed off-label medications such as atypical antipsychotics [10, 11]. However, off-label atypical antipsychotic treatment has limited evidence for clinical efficacy [12]. A recent review of randomized controlled trials of agitation as a primary endpoint in people with dementia (Alzheimer’s or mixed) identified just seven trials that showed a benefit for pharmacological treatment [13]. Three of these trials provided support for brexpiprazole (leading to its approval—see below), with the other trials providing unreplicated support for off-label risperidone and quetiapine (atypical antipsychotics), citalopram (an antidepressant), and nabilone (a cannabinoid) [13].

Off-label atypical antipsychotic treatment has multiple safety concerns [12]. Safety is of particular importance when treating older people, especially those with dementia, who may be more susceptible to medication side effects [14]. In the United States, atypical antipsychotics carry a class boxed warning stating that older patients with dementia-related psychosis treated with these drugs are at increased risk of death [15, 16], hence the limited number of studies in this area. In Alzheimer’s dementia, some antipsychotics are associated with an increased risk of cerebrovascular events (such as stroke), cardiovascular events (such as heart failure), extrapyramidal symptoms, orthostatic hypotension and dizziness, falls and fractures (in some studies), and somnolence (including sedation) [11, 14, 17–20]. Adverse events can lead to early discontinuation of antipsychotic treatment [21]. These accumulated risks contribute to recommendations that antipsychotics, when used, should be prescribed at the lowest effective dose, and with caution over the treatment duration [14, 18, 22, 23].

Brexpiprazole is an atypical antipsychotic with regulatory approval in several countries for the treatment of agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease [24, 25]. In the United States, brexpiprazole’s boxed warning for mortality was amended in 2023 to clarify its agitation indication and to state that is ‘not approved for the treatment of patients with dementia-related psychosis without agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease’ [24]. In clinical trials, brexpiprazole dosed at 2 or 3 mg/day significantly and meaningfully reduced agitation symptoms compared with placebo [26–30]. A safety analysis of pooled brexpiprazole clinical trial data identified four adverse events that were reported by ≥ 2% of people taking brexpiprazole and greater than placebo over 12 weeks: insomnia (brexpiprazole, 3.7%; placebo, 2.8%), somnolence (3.4%; 1.8%), nasopharyngitis (2.7%; 2.6%), and urinary tract infection (2.6%; 1.5%) [31]. The analysis also showed a low (and generally similar to placebo) incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) in categories of interest: cerebrovascular events (brexpiprazole, 0.5%; placebo, 0.3%), cardiovascular events (3.7%; 2.3%), extrapyramidal symptoms (5.3%; 3.1%), orthostatic hypotension/dizziness (4.6%; 4.9%), accidents including falls and fractures (2.3%; 4.1%), and somnolence (including sedation) (3.7%; 1.8%) over 12 weeks [31]. Longer-term data from an extension study indicated that brexpiprazole was generally well tolerated for up to 24 weeks, with no worsening of cognition [31, 32]. Few deaths were reported in the randomized trials (brexpiprazole, 0.9%; placebo, 0.3%), and the deaths were considered by investigators to be unrelated to brexpiprazole (intracranial hemorrhage, meningoencephalitis, pneumonia, choking, end-stage Alzheimer’s dementia, and heart failure) [31].

Building upon previous work [31], the aim of this pooled analysis was to evaluate the safety of brexpiprazole in patients with agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease, through analysis of the timing and duration of TEAEs throughout 12 and 24 weeks of treatment. This information will provide practical insight to clinicians, patients, and caregivers on when (and if) TEAEs may be expected to occur, and for how long.

Methods

Trial Design and Participants

This post hoc analysis included data from three phase 3 randomized trials and one extension trial. The trials were carried out at multiple centers in Europe, Russia, the United States, and Canada between 2013 and 2022. The trials were conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice Guideline and local regulatory requirements. Ethics approval is not required for post hoc analyses; however, the original trial protocols were approved by relevant institutional review boards and independent ethics committees. All participants and/or their legal representatives provided written or electronic informed consent to participate.

Randomized Trials

The three randomized trials were registered at ClinicalTrials.gov and published previously, as follows: ‘Trial 283’ (NCT01862640) [26], ‘Trial 284’ (NCT01922258) [26], and ‘Trial 213’ (NCT03548584) [27].

The trials enrolled older people (55–90 years) with agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease. Participants could be living at home or in a residential facility. ‘Probable Alzheimer’s disease’ was diagnosed using the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS-ADRDA) criteria [1], supported by a previous magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography scan. The severity of dementia was assessed using the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) tool [33]. People with an MMSE score of 5–22 points (which included mild-to-severe cognitive dysfunction) were eligible.

In each trial, agitation was confirmed by a score ≥ 4 points on the agitation/aggression domain of the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (either the standard version or the Nursing Home version) [34, 35]. Investigators must have checked that each participant’s agitation was not caused by reversible factors such as pain, infection, or polypharmacy. A nonpharmacological intervention for agitation must have been attempted. In Trial 213, participants were also required to meet the International Psychogeriatric Association agitation criteria and to show positivity on the aggressive behaviors factor of the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory (CMAI) [4, 36, 37]. These criteria led to a sample with overall increased agitation severity (including on physically non-aggressive behaviors and verbally agitated behaviors), thereby providing greater scope to explore treatment benefits [27]. All participants had a named caregiver who could describe the participant’s symptoms.

People with non-Alzheimer’s forms of dementia were excluded, including people whose scans indicated vascular changes (e.g., cortical stroke, multiple infarcts), space-occupying lesions (e.g., tumors), or other major structural brain disease. People with clinically significant or uncontrolled medical conditions (other than Alzheimer’s dementia) were not eligible. People with stable, controlled comorbidities (such as hypertension, diabetes, and depression) could be enrolled.

Each trial had a similar design. The trials began with a screening period of up to 42 days in which prohibited medications were washed out. Next, participants entered a 12-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled treatment period. Finally, participants were re-assessed for safety after 30 days. In Trial 283, participants were randomized (1:1:1:1) to fixed dosages of brexpiprazole 0.5 mg/day, brexpiprazole 1 mg/day, brexpiprazole 2 mg/day, or placebo. The 0.5-mg/day arm was later removed from the protocol in response to new information indicating this dosage was non-efficacious, after 20 participants had been randomized to this arm. In Trial 284, participants were randomized (1:1) to flexible dosages of brexpiprazole 0.5–2 mg/day (with a target dosage of 1 mg/day) or placebo. In both trials, brexpiprazole was started at 0.25 mg/day (days 1–3), increased to 0.5 mg/day (days 4–14), and then increased to 1 mg/day (days 15–28). Participants eligible for the 2-mg/day dosage received it from day 29 onwards. In Trial 213, participants were randomized (2:1) to fixed dosages of brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day or placebo. Within the brexpiprazole arm, participants were further randomized (1:2) to 2 mg/day or 3 mg/day. Titration in Trial 213 was faster than in Trials 283 and 284. In Trial 213, brexpiprazole was started at 0.5 mg/day (days 1–7), increased to 1 mg/day (days 8–14), and then increased to 2 mg/day (days 15–28). Participants randomized to 3 mg/day received this from day 29 onwards.

Brexpiprazole was provided as an oral tablet. Treatments were blinded to participants, caregivers, and investigators. Participants were allowed to continue taking stable Alzheimer’s disease medications (e.g., acetylcholinesterase inhibitors or memantine) and antidepressants that they were taking at screening. Use of benzodiazepines for agitation or insomnia was limited to the first 4 weeks. Antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, and anticonvulsants were prohibited.

Extension Trial

The extension trial, ‘Trial 182’, was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03594123) and published previously [32].

Trial 182 enrolled people who completed Trial 213, hereafter referred to as the ‘parent trial.’ Trial 182 had a 12-week, single-arm design. Participants were re-assessed for safety after 30 days. All participants in Trial 182 received brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day. Dosing was blinded to participants, caregivers, and investigators. One dose decrease (to a minimum of 2 mg) and one dose increase (to a maximum of 3 mg) were permitted.

Assessments

Participant characteristics were collected at screening. These included age, sex, race, severity of cognitive dysfunction (MMSE score) [33], frequency of occurrence of agitation symptoms (CMAI total score) [37, 38], and severity of agitation symptoms (Clinical Global Impression – Severity of illness [CGI-S] score) [39].

The focus of this pooled analysis is adverse events, which were collected as specified in the trial protocols during visits at weeks 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 of the randomized trials (also at day 3 for Trials 283 and 284), and at weeks 13, 14, 18, and 24 of the extension trial (relative to the start of the parent trial). Adverse events were recorded with the start date and end date and coded using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) version 25.0 preferred terms. TEAEs were defined as adverse events that started after trial medication was initiated, or were continuous from baseline and worsened after trial medication was initiated. Serious TEAEs were life-threatening, incapacitating, or required hospitalization.

Statistical Analysis

Brexpiprazole was investigated over 12 weeks and over 24 weeks. The 12-week dataset was created by pooling data from the three randomized trials (Trials 283, 284, and 213). The focus of the analysis was on participants receiving brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day fixed (participants from Trials 283 and 213), where 2 mg/day is the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-recommended target dosage and 3 mg/day is the FDA-approved maximum dosage [24]. Two lower-dosage groups were also investigated: brexpiprazole 0.5 or 1 mg/day fixed (participants from Trial 283) and brexpiprazole 0.5–2 mg/day flexible (participants from Trial 284). A pooled placebo group was created using participant data from Trials 283, 284, and 213.

The 24-week dataset was created by combining data from the parent randomized trial (Trial 213) and the extension trial (Trial 182). Specifically, the sample comprised participants who received brexpiprazole in Trial 213 and also continued into Trial 182. This allowed brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day to be investigated over 24 weeks.

Safety analyses were performed in the safety sample, which consisted of all randomized participants who received at least one dose of trial medication. In all analyses, baseline was defined as the baseline visit of the randomized trials, prior to the first dose of trial medication.

The timing of TEAEs was determined by two methods:The proportion of participants with a TEAE was calculated at 2-week intervals, relating to events in the past 2 weeks. Proportions were calculated for participants who reported a new TEAE (‘incidence’), and for participants who reported a new or ongoing TEAE (‘prevalence’). The proportion of participants with a TEAE (‘incidence’) was also determined over the full analysis period (12 and 24 weeks).The time from starting treatment to first reporting a TEAE was calculated based on reported medication start dates and TEAE start dates. This is known as a ‘time-to-event’ analysis. Median and interquartile range (IQR) times, in days, were calculated from observed data (i.e., descriptive statistics). In addition, curves were plotted to show the proportion of participants who reported a TEAE over time (time to first TEAE). These curves used ‘Kaplan–Meier’ methodology, which is a technique that can be employed in time-to-event analyses to account for censored observations, such as participants who have not yet experienced the event or were lost to follow-up [41]. A ‘Cox proportional hazards model’ was used to generate hazard ratios for brexpiprazole groups versus placebo [42], with p values corrected for multiple comparisons using Hochberg adjustment. A hazard ratio greater than 1 indicates shorter time to first TEAE for brexpiprazole versus placebo, and a hazard ratio less than 1 indicates longer time to first TEAE for brexpiprazole versus placebo.

Analyses used SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc; Cary, NC) software.

Results

Participants

12-Week Analysis

In the 12-week analysis, 1047 participants were randomized and 1043 received at least one dose of trial medication (the safety sample). By treatment group, 366 participants received brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg fixed, 157 received brexpiprazole 0.5 or 1 mg fixed, 132 received brexpiprazole 0.5–2 mg flexible, and 388 received placebo (Fig. 1A). Most participants (941 [90.2%]) received ≥ 10 weeks of treatment. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally similar across treatment groups, and were published previously [31]. The mean (SD) age of participants was 73.9 (8.0) years [31]. The sex distribution was: female, 604 (57.7%) and male, 443 (42.3%) [31]. The race distribution was: Asian, 11 (1.1%); Black or African American, 33 (3.2%); White, 1002 (95.7%); and other race, 1 (0.1%) [31]. The mean (SD) severity of cognitive dysfunction (MMSE score) at baseline was 14.6 (4.0) points [31]. The mean frequency of agitation symptoms (CMAI total score) at baseline was 73.7 points (range 35–140) [31]. The mean severity of agitation symptoms (CGI-S score) at baseline was 4.6 points (range 2–7), indicating moderate-to-marked illness [31, 39].

24-Week Analysis

The extension trial (Trial 182) enrolled 259 participants, of whom 163 had received brexpiprazole in the parent trial (Trial 213) [32]. These 163 participants formed the safety sample for the 24-week analysis (Fig. 1B). The number of participants exposed to 24 weeks of brexpiprazole was 142 (87.1%). Disposition details for the extension trial were published previously [32].

Baseline demographic characteristics (where baseline was the start of the parent trial) were as follows: mean (SD) age, 74.5 (7.9) years; female, 99 (60.7%); male, 64 (39.3%); Asian, 2 (1.2%); Black or African American, 7 (4.3%); and White, 154 (94.5%). The mean (SD) severity of cognitive dysfunction (MMSE score) at baseline was 16.0 (3.5) points. The mean (SD) frequency of agitation symptoms (CMAI total score) at baseline was 80.4 (15.6) points. The mean (SD) severity of agitation symptoms (CGI-S score) at baseline was 4.7 (0.6) points, indicating moderate-to-marked illness. At week 12 (the start of the extension trial), the mean (SD) CMAI total score was 57.3 (17.2) points and CGI-S score was 3.5 (0.9) points, indicating mild-to-moderate illness [32].

Incidence and Prevalence of TEAEs Over Time

12-Week Analysis

Throughout 12 weeks (at any point), TEAEs were reported (‘incidence’) in 183 participants (50.0%) on brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg fixed, 77 (49.0%) on brexpiprazole 0.5 or 1 mg fixed, 75 (56.8%) on brexpiprazole 0.5–2 mg flexible, and 178 (45.9%) on placebo [31]. During the first 2 weeks of treatment (i.e., during initial titration), the incidence of TEAEs was 8.7% for brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg fixed, 15.9% for brexpiprazole 0.5 or 1 mg fixed, 12.9% for brexpiprazole 0.5–2 mg flexible, and 11.3% for placebo (Fig. 2). 24-Week Analysis

Throughout 24 weeks (at any point), TEAEs were reported (‘incidence’) in 79 participants (48.5%) on brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg. During the first 2 weeks of treatment (i.e., during initial titration), the incidence of TEAEs was 6.1% (Fig. 3). Following titration, the incidence of TEAEs at 2-week intervals fluctuated in the range 1.3–12.9%, and did not increase with longer-term treatment (Fig. 3). 12-Week Analysis

In the 12-week analysis, the median (IQR) time from starting treatment to first TEAE was 32 (19–56) days with brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day fixed, 24 (10–52) days with brexpiprazole 0.5 or 1 mg/day fixed, 28 (15–54) days with brexpiprazole 0.5–2 mg/day flexible, and 28 (13–54) days with placebo (Table 1). The median (IQR) time from starting treatment to an adverse event that led to discontinuation was 47 (35–54) days with brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day fixed, 45 (32–56) days with brexpiprazole 0.5 or 1 mg/day fixed, 30 (7–45) days with brexpiprazole 0.5–2 mg/day flexible, and 30 (23–53) days with placebo (Table 1).

24-Week Analysis

In the 24-week analysis, the median (IQR) time from starting treatment to first TEAE was 52 (21–84) days with brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day (Table 1). This was longer than in the 12-week analysis. It was rare for participants to report their first TEAE during the extension trial (Fig. 4B).

The median times from starting treatment to first reporting a serious TEAE and each TEAE of specific interest were longer in the 24-week analysis compared with the 12-week analysis (Table 1).

24-Week Analysis

In the 24-week analysis, the median (IQR) duration of all TEAEs was 3 (1–8) days (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Table 1 in Online Resource 1; see the electronic supplementary material). The median (IQR) duration of serious TEAEs was similar, at 4 (1–22) days (Fig. 5B). An unbiased value could not be determined for the median duration of adverse events leading to discontinuation, as (by definition) no participants in this sample discontinued during the parent trial.

Discussion

This analysis builds upon a large body of evidence from clinical trials showing that brexpiprazole is a generally well-tolerated treatment for people with agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease [26, 27, 31, 32]. In the present exploratory work, patients who reported a TEAE (approximately half of all treated patients) were evaluated over time to determine when the TEAEs occurred, and for how long they lasted. In 12-week randomized trials, the brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day group (the FDA-approved therapeutic doses) had a similar pattern of TEAEs to placebo across 2-week intervals, and a similar median time to first TEAE (32 days and 28 days, respectively, with a non-significant hazard ratio). These findings suggest that brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day is not associated with an earlier onset of adverse events compared to placebo. While this analysis did not identify any specific safety concerns with brexpiprazole, all patients receiving treatment for agitation in Alzheimer’s dementia should be continuously monitored for efficacy and potential side effects [13].

Among patients who discontinued the randomized trials because of an adverse event (4.9% of patients on brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day), the median time to the adverse event was longer for brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day compared with placebo (47 days and 30 days, respectively). This suggests that brexpiprazole was similarly tolerated to placebo. A prior analysis showed significantly shorter time to discontinuation due to adverse events for olanzapine, quetiapine, and risperidone compared with placebo in patients with dementia who were treated for psychosis, agitation, or aggression [21].

The median duration of all TEAEs in the 12-week randomized trials was similar between brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day and placebo (6 days and 4 days, respectively). This indicates that adverse events were likely to resolve over similar time periods with brexpiprazole and placebo. However, the duration of TEAEs could be calculated only for events that had resolved during the trials, which means that the durations are likely to be underestimates.

Certain TEAEs are of particular concern to older patients with dementia taking antipsychotics [11, 14, 17–20]. In this analysis, the timing and duration of cardiovascular events, extrapyramidal symptoms, akathisia, orthostatic hypotension/dizziness, falls, insomnia, and somnolence (including sedation) were generally similar between brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day and placebo over 12 weeks. This finding, together with previous evidence, may provide reassurance regarding the tolerability of brexpiprazole for these specific events among medically vulnerable patients with Alzheimer’s dementia [31]. Cerebrovascular TEAEs are also of specific concern in this population [18]. However, cerebrovascular TEAEs were uncommon in the brexpiprazole randomized trials (0.5% for brexpiprazole overall and 0.3% for placebo) [31] and did not occur at all in the extension trial [32], indicating a low risk over 12 and 24 weeks. Similarly, few deaths were reported in the brexpiprazole group (0.9% in the randomized trials, 0% in the extension trial), and the deaths were considered unrelated to brexpiprazole (intracranial hemorrhage, meningoencephalitis, pneumonia, choking, end-stage Alzheimer’s dementia, and heart failure) [31]. A previous analysis found that five of the six deaths occurred over a week after the participant’s last dose of brexpiprazole [31].

The proportion of participants with a new TEAE was similar during brexpiprazole titration compared to the remainder of the treatment period. With regard to dosing, there were no additional safety concerns with brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg fixed compared to lower doses. The hazard ratio for brexpiprazole 0.5–2 mg flexible (target brexpiprazole dose, 1 mg) versus placebo suggested that TEAEs might occur earlier in this group, but the difference was non-significant. The incidence of extrapyramidal symptoms and somnolence/sedation were also numerically higher in the brexpiprazole flexible-dose group compared with fixed doses or placebo. Rather than reflecting an effect of dose, it could be hypothesized that TEAEs were more likely to be reported in the flexible-dose trial, since patients, caregivers, and doctors were aware that the dose could be adjusted to deal with such events.

Over the longer term (24 weeks), the median time to first TEAE (52 days) and the duration of all TEAEs (3 days) were favorable compared to the 12-week data. The proportion of patients reporting a new TEAE in the 24-week analysis did not increase over time. Among patients who had not reported a TEAE in the 12-week parent trial, TEAEs were rare throughout the 12-week extension trial. As a caveat, the 24-week analysis included only those patients who completed the parent trial. This means that no patients in this sample discontinued over the first 12 weeks, which may have resulted in a sample better able to tolerate brexpiprazole. Nonetheless, the longer-term data suggest that brexpiprazole remained well tolerated over 24 weeks among patients who tolerated the first 12 weeks of treatment.

Brexpiprazole acts as a partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT1A and dopamine D2 receptors and an antagonist at 5-HT2A and noradrenergic (norepinephrinergic) α1B and α2C receptors, all with subnanomolar affinity [43]. Whereas 5-HT1A receptor agonism has been associated with gastrointestinal adverse events [44], this was not observed for brexpiprazole [31]. D2 receptor antagonism is linked to extrapyramidal symptoms and hyperprolactinemia, whereas D2 receptor agonism is linked to akathisia, insomnia, and nausea; it is hypothesized that brexpiprazole’s partial agonism with low intrinsic activity at D2 receptors may strike a balance between these extremes [43]. Serotonin 5-HT2A receptor antagonism may also reduce the risk of akathisia [43]. Whereas blockade of noradrenergic and cholinergic receptors is linked to orthostatic hypotension, dizziness, and cardiovascular events [45], this was not observed for brexpiprazole. Finally, activity at histamine H1 receptors is linked to sedation [46]; brexpiprazole has moderate antagonistic affinity at these receptors [43].

Strengths of this analysis include its large sample, allowing for rigorous assessment of safety. The 12-week analysis included a placebo group to enable comparisons. Both the 12- and 24-week analyses assessed brexpiprazole doses that are clinically relevant in the United States. The 24-week data provided insights into long-term safety, despite the smaller sample size. The timing of TEAEs was assessed by two methods (incidence/prevalence and time-to-event analyses), increasing confidence in the findings. Finally, the trials allowed the enrollment of patients in different care settings, at different stages of cognitive impairment, with stable comorbidities (such as hypertension, diabetes, and depression), and patients on concomitant Alzheimer’s disease medications and antidepressants. Inclusion of patients with different profiles (some of which have been analyzed elsewhere [47]) reflects real-world situations and increases the applicability of results to clinical practice.

Limitations of the clinical trials include that TEAEs were reported by the patients and their caregivers. This is a subjective situation with the potential for recall bias, particularly with regard to the timing and duration of events. This limitation may be more applicable to the extension trial (in which there were no visits from week 18 until week 24) than to the randomized trials (which had regular 2-week visits). The trial enrollment criteria did not include biomarker confirmation for Alzheimer’s disease (e.g., amyloid cerebrospinal fluid or blood tests), which may have resulted in a biologically heterogeneous sample. The enrollment criteria aimed to exclude individuals with cerebrovascular changes (based on prior magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography scans), but some patients may have had small vessel disease, such as cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Brexpiprazole has not been studied in patients with clinically significant comorbidities such as cardiovascular conditions (though people with stable comorbidities such as hypertension could be enrolled). Additional limitations of the post hoc analysis are its exploratory, hypothesis-generating nature, which should be repeated in a trial with a prespecified time-to-event analysis plan. The TEAE duration analyses may be underestimates, as described above. The duration analyses were also limited by the low number of reported TEAEs in specific categories of interest. There were some differences in trial design, such as flexible dosing in one trial, which may have facilitated the resolution of TEAEs and thereby impacted TEAE duration estimates. The most recent of the randomized trials enriched for the aggressive behaviors factor, which is thought to have enhanced signal detection [27]. As discussed earlier, there was potential bias towards increased tolerability in the 24-week analysis sample. There was no comparator group in the 24-week analysis, which limits meaningful interpretation of long-term safety. Finally, data over longer than 24 weeks are needed to assess the cumulative burden of TEAEs over time.

Conclusions

In a large sample of patients with agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease, the time to first TEAE in 12-week trials was similar between brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day (the FDA-approved therapeutic doses) and placebo. The time to discontinuation due to an adverse event was longer with brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day compared with placebo, suggesting that brexpiprazole was similarly tolerated to placebo. TEAEs had similar durations in both treatment groups. Among patients who did not report a TEAE in the 12-week parent trial, TEAEs were rare throughout the 12-week extension trial. Thus, in a sample that tolerated brexpiprazole over the first 12 weeks, longer-term (24 weeks in total) treatment with brexpiprazole was not associated with an increased risk of TEAEs. Together with previous studies [26, 27, 31, 32], the present results reinforce that brexpiprazole 2 or 3 mg/day is a generally well-tolerated treatment for people with agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease. Beyond aggregate safety data, these exploratory analyses provide a practical clinical insight into the safety of brexpiprazole over time. Thus, these TEAE time profiles may provide insights to help patients, their caregivers, and physicians to better understand the short- and longer-term safety of brexpiprazole treatment.
