Percutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion versus oral anticoagulation in atrial fibrillation: A pragmatic meta-analysis of randomized trials within the DOAC era
Authors: Gabriel Moraes Kuniyoshi, Rafael Zoppi Campane, Nikita Patil, Ana Clara Vitorino de Oliveira, Beatriz Araújo, Ricardo Ryoshim Kuniyoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) is an established alternative to oral anticoagulation (OAC). However, its safety and efficacy boundaries remain incompletely defined in dedicated comparisons against direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs).ObjectiveThis study applied a pragmatic framework to evaluate the comparative efficacy and safety of LAAO versus OAC in a DOAC-predominant setting.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library were searched for randomized controlled trials comparing LAAO with OAC, restricted to a DOAC-predominant comparator. Risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated via a Mantel-Haenszel random-effects model.ResultsFour trials comprising 5890 patients were included (96.4% of controls assigned to DOACs). Overall stroke (RR 1.18, 95% CI 0.87–1.60; p = 0.28), systemic embolism (RR 1.09, 95% CI 0.29–4.08; p = 0.89), major bleeding (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.81–1.19; p = 0.85), hemorrhagic stroke (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.45–1.51; p = 0.53), and all-cause mortality (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.85–1.17; p = 0.99) did not differ significantly. Ischemic stroke presented numerical asymmetry favoring OAC that approached statistical significance (RR 1.36, 95% CI 0.99–1.89; p = 0.06). Conversely, within available protocol-defined datasets, LAAO significantly reduced nonprocedural clinically relevant bleeding (RR 0.50, 95% CI 0.43–0.60; p < 0.01).ConclusionsIn a pragmatic analysis of the DOAC era, randomized data show no statistically significant differences for hard individual endpoints, though broad confidence intervals reflect precision limits. A numerical asymmetry for ischemic stroke favored OAC, while LAAO reduced nonprocedural clinically relevant bleeding. Current evidence remains non-definitive, warranting shared clinical decision-making to balance individual thromboembolic risks against long-term bleeding susceptibility.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Left atrial appendage occlusion, Direct oral anticoagulants, Ischemic stroke, Hemorrhagic stroke, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Percutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) is an established nonpharmacological alternative for thromboembolic prevention in patients with atrial fibrillation, particularly those at high risk for major bleeding [1]. The PROTECT-AF and PREVAIL trials demonstrated that this procedure provides stroke prevention comparable to warfarin [2]. In a 5-year patient-level meta-analysis, LAAO achieved a significant 80% reduction in hemorrhagic stroke and a 27% decrease in all-cause mortality compared with warfarin therapy. However, those studies presented specific statistical variations. The PREVAIL control arm ischemic stroke rate was unexpectedly low (0.73%) [2]. In the pooled data from the PROTECT-AF and PREVAIL trials, 48% of major bleeding events in the LAAO arm occurred within 7 days postrandomization [3]. Beyond 6 months of follow-up, this analysis also demonstrated that warfarin therapy was associated with a 5.5% incidence of major gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, which was significantly reduced to 1.4% in the LAAO arm.

The therapeutic landscape has since evolved, with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) emerging as the standard of care. DOACs reduced the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke by approximately 50% compared with warfarin [4]. However, they increased the risk of GI bleeding by 25%, resulting in a 2.56% incidence in the DOAC arm compared to 2.02% with warfarin. In parallel, second-generation occluders currently define modern LAAO technology. The combination of design iterations and expanding operator experience has enhanced procedural safety [5]. Real-world data show an implant success rate of 97.5% with these newer devices [6]. Additionally, the 45-day rate of pericardial effusion requiring intervention decreased to 0.5%. Randomized data through 3 years confirm that modern closure systems maintain comparable long-term safety profiles and procedure-related complication rates [7]. Concurrently, post-LAAO antithrombotic protocols have transitioned from mandatory warfarin regimens toward tailored DOAC or dual antiplatelet strategies [8].

A previous network meta-analysis published by Abdelfattah et al. [9] assessed this shifting therapeutic paradigm. Leveraging both direct and indirect evidence, the investigators indicated lower odds of mortality associated with LAAO compared with both DOACs and warfarin. Statistical uncertainty remained concerning relative bleeding and stroke risks. Because their direct evidence was restricted to the PRAGUE-17 trial [10], [11], the application of those findings was constrained by a limited sample size, concluding that subsequent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) directly comparing LAAO with DOACs were warranted.

Recently, this clinical and statistical landscape has been significantly expanded by the publication of new randomized trials. The larger datasets by Turagam et al. [12], Khan et al. [13] and Karakasis et al. [14] integrated historical vitamin K antagonist (VKA) cohorts or concomitant complex procedures. Concurrently, a recent analysis by Chan et al. [15] restricted to the DOAC era provided robust safety data on isolated long-term bleeding parameters. However, those late hemorrhages were reported through a series of separate sub-analyses, which hinders the integrated clinical rationale required for routine decision-making.

The clinical adoption of LAAO ultimately depends on whether its long-term safety advantages translate into a lower overall rate of major bleeding [1]. Evaluating nonprocedural bleeding is critical to establishing LAAO's biologic plausibility and safety profile. Indeed, the invasive approach inherently carries periprocedural hazards not shared by a pharmacological alternative. Beyond severe hemorrhages, an evaluation incorporating clinically relevant non-major bleeding is warranted, given its direct implications for patient quality of life, treatment adherence, and healthcare utilization. By isolating long-term hemorrhagic drivers from early procedural risk, this approach yields an assessment that better reflects contemporary clinical practice. Current management is shaped by growing operator experience and second-generation device technologies. It also incorporates de-escalated postprocedural antithrombotic regimens and direct comparisons against oral anticoagulants.

Accordingly, the objective of this pairwise meta-analysis was to pragmatically evaluate the comparative efficacy and safety of percutaneous LAAO versus oral anticoagulation (OAC) in a contemporary, DOAC-predominant setting.

Methods

This meta-analysis adhered to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [16] and the PRISMA guidelines [17]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261357102). PRISMA checklists are presented in Supplementary Methods 1 and 2.

Data source and search strategy

PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) were searched from inception through March 2026, supplemented by a manual screening of the reference lists of retrieved studies. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings and keywords. These terms included ‘atrial fibrillation’, ‘left atrial appendage occlusion’, ‘left atrial appendage closure’, and individual direct oral anticoagulant names. Comprehensive search strings are available in the Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were RCTs comparing percutaneous LAAO versus DOAC-based therapy in patients with atrial fibrillation. Trials were included only if they reported at least one prespecified outcome. Furthermore, the control cohort must have predominantly received DOAC therapy. Studies involving concomitant interventions for non-arrhythmic cardiac conditions were excluded.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two authors (G.K. and R.C.) independently extracted study data. Extracted variables included author names, trial registration, publication year, sample size, follow-up duration, and baseline characteristics. Endpoint data were independently extracted by G.K. and N.P., resolving disagreements via consensus. Methodological risk of bias was systematically assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [18].

Because overall major bleeding serves as one of the primary drivers for clinical decision-making when contrasting an invasive intervention against pharmacological therapy, a prespecified leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was structured for this safety outcome. This model evaluated the stability of primary safety estimates against potential variations in trial-specific bleeding classification systems and data availability.

Outcomes

Prespecified endpoints were (1) overall stroke, (2) ischemic stroke, (3) systemic embolism, (4) hemorrhagic stroke, (5) major bleeding, (6) nonprocedural clinically relevant bleeding, and (7) all-cause mortality, defined per individual trial protocols. Nonprocedural clinically relevant bleeding comprised major and clinically relevant non-major bleeding events not attributable to the procedure and was analyzed only in trials reporting this endpoint. Trial-specific bleeding outcome definitions are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

To address the baseline trial discrepancies in mathematical reporting models, raw patient and event counts and sample sizes were utilized to calculate standardized pooled risk metrics. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a Mantel–Haenszel random-effects model, with tau2 estimated via restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Between-study heterogeneity was systematically assessed using Cochran's Q test and quantified by the I2 statistic. All statistical analyses were performed using the metabin function using the meta package in R (version 4.5.2). Formal statistical assessment for publication bias via funnel plot asymmetry testing or Egger's test was not performed due to the limited number of trials, in accordance with Cochrane guidelines [16].

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 515 records were identified, from which five publications corresponding to four distinct RCTs [10], [11], [19], [20], [21] comprising 5890 patients were included (Fig. 1). In total, 2949 patients (50.1%) were randomized to LAAO and 2941 (49.9%) to OAC. Among the control group, 2835 patients (96.4%) actively received DOACs. In the pooled population, the mean CHA₂DS₂-VASc score was 3.8 and the mean HAS-BLED score was 1.6. Follow-up periods ranged from 36 to 48 months. The pooled procedural success rate for LAAO was 98.6% (96.8% in PRAGUE-17, 98.8% in OPTION, 98.3% in CLOSURE-AF, and 98.8% in CHAMPION-AF). Baseline characteristics of the included studies are reported in Table 1. Modalities for LAAO post-implantation imaging evaluation are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Outcomes and pooled estimates

Pooled analyses revealed no statistically significant differences between LAAO and OAC regarding all-cause mortality (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.85–1.17; p = 0.99; I2 = 0.9%), overall stroke (RR 1.18, 95% CI 0.87–1.60; p = 0.28; I2 = 0%), systemic embolism (RR 1.09, 95% CI 0.29–4.08; p = 0.89; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 2), and overall major bleeding (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.81–1.19; p = 0.85; I2 = 0%; Fig. 3). Hemorrhagic stroke rates also did not differ significantly between groups (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.45–1.51; p = 0.53; I2 = 0%; Fig. 4A). Conversely, ischemic stroke rates presented a numerical asymmetry favoring anticoagulation that approached statistical significance (2.9% in the LAAO arm versus 2.1% in the OAC arm; RR 1.36, 95% CI 0.99–1.89; p = 0.06; I2 = 0%; Fig. 4B).

Regarding secondary safety metrics, based on pooled data from the PRAGUE-17, OPTION, and CHAMPION-AF trials [10], [11], [19], [20], LAAO significantly reduced the risk of nonprocedural clinically relevant bleeding by 50% (RR 0.50, 95% CI 0.43–0.60; p < 0.01; I2 = 0%; Fig. 5). This analysis was restricted to trials explicitly capturing these granular data, as the CLOSURE-AF trial [21] was omitted due to the unavailability of the corresponding endpoint. To ensure methodological robustness, a sequential leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for major bleeding was performed by omitting each trial in turn. The resulting risk estimates remained consistently non-significant across all permutations (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

This pairwise meta-analysis demonstrates that no statistically significant differences were observed between LAAO and DOAC-based therapies for most hard endpoints, although wide confidence intervals prevent definitive conclusions. Evaluated outcomes included all-cause mortality, overall stroke, systemic embolism, hemorrhagic stroke, and overall major bleeding. Ischemic stroke rates also demonstrated no significant difference, despite a numerical asymmetry favoring OAC. Conversely, LAAO yielded a significant 50% risk reduction in nonprocedural clinically relevant bleeding.

Our study complements recently published meta-analyses by providing a pragmatic evaluation focused exclusively on LAAO vs. OAC trials within the DOAC era. While existing systematically pooled overviews examine data across historical anticoagulation regimens [12], [13], [14], our protocol restricts the investigation to a cohort reflecting contemporary clinical practice. Consequently, these findings contextualize available evidence within modern practice, which is increasingly shaped by second-generation device technologies and de-escalated postprocedural antithrombotic regimens.

Thromboembolic protection and ischemic risks

Historically, the clinical advantages of LAAO over warfarin were driven by a reduction in hemorrhagic stroke, offsetting a numerically higher, non-significant ischemic stroke rate (HR 1.71, 95% CI 0.94–3.11) [2]. In the contemporary era, DOACs have reduced hemorrhagic stroke risks by approximately 50% compared with VKAs [4]. Consequently, the relative weight of these endpoints shifts, drawing attention to the imprecise risk estimate favoring OAC for ischemic stroke prevention observed in our data. Importantly, the large CHAMPION-AF trial strongly drives this pooled estimate, accounting for 47.2% of the meta-analysis weight and demonstrating a significant increase in ischemic risk for LAAO (RR 1.67, 95% CI 1.04–2.67) (Fig. 4B). However, this trend lacks uniformity at the individual trial level. While PRAGUE-17 (RR 1.30, 95% CI 0.58–2.90) and CLOSURE-AF (RR 1.19, 95% CI 0.61–2.33) also numerically favor OAC, the OPTION trial does not (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.36–2.19). This statistical signal toward a higher ischemic stroke risk with LAAO matches a consistent historical pattern observed since early VKA trials [2]. Recent registries combining both VKA and DOAC cohorts similarly noted this unfavorable thromboembolic signal [12], [13]. Our findings directly parallel the randomized data on DOAC-based therapies summarized by Chan et al. [15]. Notably, Karakasis et al. recently demonstrated statistical significance for this hard endpoint in a broader dataset (RR 1.41; 95% CI 1.07–1.86; p = 0.015) [14]. By synthesizing the composite of ischemic stroke and systemic embolism under the longest available follow-up durations, their analysis overcame the statistical imprecision of individual inconclusive reports. However, it is critical to note that their study pooled data from both VKA and contemporary DOAC trials. Hence, this combined signal may not definitively extrapolate to modern, DOAC-based clinical practice.

Within our pooled cohort, the absolute difference in ischemic stroke between the LAAO and OAC arms was 0.8% (2.9% vs. 2.1%). Given these low event rates, the analysis remains susceptible to statistical imprecision. This data sparsity broadens the confidence intervals, precluding definitive conclusions regarding the comparative efficacy of these strategies for individual endpoints. Therefore, ongoing contemporary RCTs are required to provide the statistical power necessary to clarify these comparative efficacy boundaries in modern clinical practice. This residual numerical asymmetry warrants careful interpretation. LAAO targets a single anatomical source of thromboembolism, leaving patients exposed to risks stemming from diffuse atrial cardiomyopathy. Atrial fibrillation serves as a marker of an underlying diseased atrial substrate that drives cardioembolic risk independently of the appendage [23]. Concomitant risk factors and systemic comorbidities interact synergistically to magnify this risk [23], [24]. Additionally, device-specific complications such as device-related thrombosis (DRT) and residual peridevice leaks (PDL) can compromise long-term protection. DRT is associated with a four- to fivefold increase in ischemic stroke risk [25]. Similarly, PDL raises thromboembolic rates [26]. Whether these complications represent a unique embolic source or reflect a higher baseline systemic thromboembolic profile remains challenging to determine [27]. This assessment is further complicated by heterogeneous postprocedural antithrombotic protocols (Table 1) and variable imaging surveillance timelines across trials (Supplementary Table 3).

Translating these findings into routine clinical practice requires evaluating this risk profile in absolute terms. Across our pooled cohort, LAAO was associated with approximately 8 additional ischemic strokes per 1000 patients compared with OAC (28.8 vs. 21.1 events per 1000). Conversely, LAAO achieved a substantial absolute reduction of approximately 73 nonprocedural clinically relevant bleeding events per 1000 patients (74.7 vs. 148.1 events per 1000). However, these pooled absolute estimates must be interpreted with caution. Because they represent an average across a clinically heterogeneous population, spanning from younger post-ablation patients to elderly cohorts with a higher risk of hemorrhages, these average numbers may not directly extrapolate to individual patient phenotypes. Furthermore, although our dataset evaluated the composite nonprocedural bleeding endpoint without decomposing its individual elements, our analysis demonstrates no significant difference in the incidence of global major bleeding between the groups (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.81–1.19). Indeed, contemporary data from Chan et al. indicate that the absolute reduction in late bleeding is predominantly driven by non-major events [15].

Hemorrhagic outcomes and patient frailty

The cumulative data suggest that LAAO's risk-benefit balance is not uniform across the atrial fibrillation population. Our statistical findings of low heterogeneity (I2 < 1%) can mask critical baseline clinical discrepancies between contemporary trials.

Conceptually, integrating second-generation devices and de-escalated antithrombotic regimens would be expected to lower major bleeding. However, while expanded operator experience and hardware iterations have successfully minimized acute periprocedural complications [28], reducing this late hard safety endpoint remains a persistent challenge. These findings suggest that long-term bleeding risks may be largely driven by baseline systemic comorbidities and patient frailty, rendering them independent of the chosen preventive strategy.

This interaction between clinical risk and safety outcomes is demonstrated by the divergent results of contemporary trials. The CHAMPION-AF trial enrolled a comparatively intermediate-risk population with a mean CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 3.5 and a HAS-BLED score of 1.3 [20]. In this cohort, LAAO demonstrated composite nonprocedural bleeding superiority alongside primary efficacy noninferiority. However, this safety benefit was driven by the reduction in clinically relevant non-major events. Notably, the incidence of non-procedure-related major bleeding did not differ significantly between groups. In contrast, the CLOSURE-AF trial evaluated a higher-risk, vulnerable population with a mean CHA₂DS₂-VASc of 5.2 and a HAS-BLED of 3 [21]. In those frail individuals, LAAO failed to achieve noninferiority for its primary net clinical endpoint (stroke, systemic embolism, major bleeding, or cardiovascular or unexplained death), a result driven in part by the lack of reduction in major bleeding events with the device compared to contemporary medical therapy, compounded by early periprocedural complications. This divergent trial-level behavior provides a practical framework for risk-stratified decision-making. In intermediate-risk patients, device therapy offers robust protection against minor hemorrhages. However, in vulnerable individuals, the burden of baseline comorbidities can counterbalance these safety advantages. This interaction underscores the risk of procedural futility in specific cohorts.

Real-world data from the contemporary NCDR LAAO Registry reinforce these observations, reporting a one-year major bleeding rate of 6.4% [6]. Furthermore, the EWOLUTION [29] registry demonstrated a 16.4% all-cause mortality rate at two years. Major hemorrhage occurred in 20.6% of those non-survivors. These unselected population data confirm that long-term hemorrhagic events remain a major clinical challenge. Moreover, in clinical scenarios involving patients with catastrophic bleeding, complete discontinuation of all antithrombotic therapies often emerges as a compelling clinical choice. Currently, a critical gap in medical literature persists, as completed randomized trials directly evaluating LAAO against a strategy of complete antithrombotic cessation are lacking [22]. While ongoing three-arm trials, such as the French A3ICH (NCT03243175), seek to address this dilemma, contemporary management must rely on optimizing available strategies to mitigate these residual clinical uncertainties.

Consequently, clinical practice should combine postprocedural imaging surveillance for device complications [30] with systemic risk factor control [1]. Future investigations focusing on imaging-guided surveillance and less intensive postprocedural antithrombotic regimens remain valuable. Recent data suggest that reduced-dose DOAC therapy mitigates bleeding events without increasing device thrombosis [31]. However, larger randomized clinical trials are required to establish a definitive balance between ischemic protection and safety.

Strengths

This analysis clarifies the contemporary status of LAAO through a strictly pragmatic approach. Our protocol eliminates the statistical noise of historical VKA cohorts and concomitant interventions. By isolating the DOAC era, the findings demonstrate that cumulative randomized evidence presents wide confidence intervals and remains insufficient for definitive conclusions on hard endpoints. Ultimately, this pragmatic framework establishes composite non-procedure-related bleeding reduction as LAAO's primary safety advantage. Crucially, this restrictive design unmasks a numerical asymmetry in ischemic stroke favoring OAC. This asymmetry defines an area of residual clinical uncertainty that broader or fragmented overviews fail to isolate.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to trial-level meta-analyses. First, all included trials utilized an open-label design. This lack of blinding may introduce ascertainment bias, particularly for nonprocedural clinically relevant non-major bleeding. Patient and clinician reporting and ascertainment may differ between an ongoing medication strategy and a one-time mechanical intervention. Second, the specific focus on the contemporary DOAC era constrains the final selection to available randomized platforms. Third, small sample sizes and low absolute event rates restrict analytical precision for low-incidence endpoints. This sparsity broadens the confidence intervals for specific ischemic and hemorrhagic outcomes. Fourth, heterogeneous bleeding definitions across protocols introduce potential classification bias. Additionally, postprocedural antithrombotic regimens lacked standardization across the included cohorts. Fifth, follow-up durations ranging from 36 to 48 months preclude the evaluation of mechanical closure over a patient's lifetime exposure. Finally, the aggregate nature of trial-level data prevents personalized patient-stratified assessments.

Conclusions

In a pragmatic analysis of the DOAC era, randomized data show no statistically significant differences between LAAO and OAC for overall stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality. LAAO significantly reduces nonprocedural clinically relevant bleeding. For ischemic stroke, a numerical asymmetry favors OAC, though analytical precision for this and other hard endpoints remain limited by data sparsity. Consequently, clinical management requires individualized patient selection and shared decision-making to balance thromboembolic protection against long-term bleeding susceptibility.
