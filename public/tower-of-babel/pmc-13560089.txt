Evolution of virulence of a plant RNA virus in developmental stage-structured host populations
Authors: José L Carrasco, Christina Toft, Santiago F Elena, Adi Stern
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural host populations are age-structured, and developmental stages differ in susceptibility and within-host pathogen dynamics, potentially imposing distinct selective pressures on viruses. However, the evolutionary consequences of host age structure remain poorly understood. We experimentally evolved turnip mosaic potyvirus for 5 passages in Arabidopsis thaliana populations spanning 7 demographic regimes, from juvenile- to mature-dominated cohorts. We quantified disease progression, symptom severity, and viral load, cross-inoculated evolved lineages across host stages to construct infection matrices, and performed whole-population sequencing at passages 1 and 5. Disease traits changed markedly with passage, demography, and their interaction. Disease progression evolved faster in older populations, whereas symptom severity was independent of median age, indicating demographic reweighting of virulence components. Viral load increased across passages and positively correlated with severity, linking within-host fitness to symptoms. Cross-inoculation assays revealed a modular infection network: juvenile-evolved lineages specialized on juvenile hosts, whereas lineages from intermediate and older populations were more generalist. Genomically, we detected both parallel and demography-specific adaptations, including recurrent changes in the viral protein VPg (involved in translation, replication, and host interactions) as well as synonymous variants showing consistent or opposing selection across host population stage structures. Overall, host age structure emerges as a major ecological driver of virulence evolution, shaping tradeoffs between disease progression and severity and determining specialization versus generalism. These results integrate phenotypic and genomic responses and suggest that manipulating crop age structure could steer virus evolution toward less damaging outcomes.Keywords: Arabidopsis thaliana, experimental evolution, host aging, turnip mosaic virus, virus diversity, virus evolution

Introduction

Host homogeneity is rare in natural populations, meaning that pathogens typically encounter host populations whose individuals are genetically diverse and differ in their susceptibility to infection (Pfennig 2001; González et al. 2019; Elena et al. 2025). Interactions between pathogens and hosts that vary genetically in susceptibility have been well studied (Chang et al. 1992; Schmid-Hempel and Koella 1994; Brown and Tellier 2011; Hillung et al. 2014; Navarro et al. 2022), as has the influence of developmental stage on host resistance (Chang et al. 1992; Heath 1993; Rupe and Gbur 1995; Izhar and Ben-Ami 2015; Melero et al. 2023). Variation in disease susceptibility across ages or developmental stages is a key component of host heterogeneity, with the potential to affect host–pathogen outcomes and epidemiological dynamics (Ben-Ami 2019). These differences may imply that a virus can be adapted to a particular host developmental stage yet fail to infect others effectively. In nature, interactions between host developmental stage and susceptibility have been observed in many animal and plant systems across a wide range of pathogens. In animals, young individuals are generally more susceptible to bacterial and viral infections than older ones (Krakowka and Koestner 1976; Klinge et al. 2009; Cleton et al. 2014). However, for some pathogens such as fungi, older animals may show increased susceptibility (Bradley et al. 2019). In plants, hosts commonly become increasingly resistant to bacterial and fungal pathogens as they mature (García-Ruiz and Murphy 2001; Kus et al. 2002; Shibata et al. 2010), a pattern associated with age-related resistance (ARR) (Hu and Yang 2019). ARR, however, is not universal and depends on the specific pathosystem: some plants become more susceptible to particular fungal pathogens (Miller 1983) and RNA viruses (Huang et al. 2020; Melero et al. 2023) as they develop.

Plants finely coordinate growth and development to optimize fitness by mounting rapid, appropriate responses to diverse stresses. The life cycle of flowering plants can be viewed as a succession of distinct phases, including the transition from a juvenile vegetative stage to a mature reproductive stage. The transition to flowering is governed by a complex genetic network that integrates endogenous and environmental cues (Amasino 2010). Although flowering itself is not the developmental transition required for increased pathogen resistance (Wilson et al. 2013), several studies have described links between flowering and defense (Giolai and Laine 2024). Depending on the pathogen and host genotype, viral infection can delay reproduction (Pagán et al. 2008; Shukla et al. 2018) or accelerate flowering (Korves and Bergelson 2003; Winter et al. 2011). These patterns are consistent with resource-allocation theory: plants have a limited pool of resources that must be distributed among functions throughout the life cycle, creating tradeoffs (Weiner 2004; Boege and Marquis 2005; Giolai and Laine 2024). As plants develop, they eventually shift resources from biomass accumulation and structural growth to reproduction. A similar reallocation occurs during pathogen attack, when plants must deploy resources strategically to overcome infection. A particularly well-studied example is the tradeoff between defense and reproductive development, especially consequential for monocarpic species. Here, we use this term to refer primarily to costs in flowering time and reproductive output, such as delayed flowering and/or reduced production of flowers, fruits, or seeds, rather than specifically to changes in flower size. These costs follow from resource-allocation theory and have been documented in plant defense and virus-infection contexts (Weiner 2004; Boege and Marquis 2005; Pagán et al. 2008; Shukla et al. 2018). Moreover, pathogens encounter different host defense responses depending on plant developmental stage (Kus et al. 2002; Korves and Bergelson 2003). These developmental stage-specific differences can elicit different pathogen strategies to counter host defenses. Developmental stage-specific physiological changes, such as shifts in reactive oxygen species or the reprogramming of hormone crosstalk pathways (Khan et al. 2014; Mhamdi and van Breusegem 2018; Melero et al. 2024), influence host susceptibility and thus present a changing environment encountered by the pathogen.

Several molecular mechanisms may underlie these developmental-stage effects on infection. In Arabidopsis thaliana and other plants, ARR is associated with developmental changes in salicylic acid (SA)-dependent defense, accumulation of antimicrobial activity in the apoplast, and altered expression or deployment of immune signaling pathways (Kus et al. 2002; Carella et al. 2015; Wilson et al. 2017; Hu and Yang 2019). More recently, the heterochronic miR156-SPL module has been directly connected to immune maturation: declining miR156 levels during vegetative phase change permit accumulation of SPL transcription factors, and SPL2, SPL10 and SPL11 promote ARR in adult arabidopsis leaves, with SPL10 enhancing SA-pathway activity partly through direct activation of PAD4 (Zheng et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2023). Thus, developmental timing can modulate defense not only through broad physiological changes but also through genetically defined regulatory modules that couple phase change with immune output. In the A. thaliana-turnip mosaic virus (TuMV) pathosystem, developmental stage also modifies host transcriptional and hormonal responses to infection: later-stage plants show altered expression of cell-wall-related genes and a tradeoff between SA-mediated resistance and reproductive output, consistent with development-dependent tolerance and susceptibility to viral infection (Melero et al. 2024). These stage-specific differences in defense, resource allocation, and tolerance may expose viral populations to distinct selective environments as host populations shift from juvenile to reproductive composition.

Despite the attention given to developmental stage during infection, we still lack information on how different host stages impose distinct selective environments for viruses, thereby modulating viral evolution. Most eco-evolutionary theory assumes a link between virulence and transmission (Anderson and May 1982), positing a compromise between infection duration (i.e. the time during which a pathogen can propagate and infect other hosts) and the damage inflicted on the host. Notably, the virulence-transmission tradeoff appears to be affected by host's life history (Ebert and Herre 1996; Perlman 2008; Izhar and Ben-Ami 2015). Although parasite-induced host mortality is the most commonly used measure of virulence for horizontally transmitted parasites (Alizon et al. 2009), some pathogens employ alternative strategies that ensure reproduction and transmission without prematurely killing the host. For plant viruses, such alternatives may include increasing within-host accumulation, accelerating systemic movement, altering symptom timing, or modifying host development in ways that enhance the availability or quality of infectious tissue without necessarily causing rapid host death (Pagán et al. 2007, 2008; Shukla et al. 2018). Because development entails substantial reallocation of host resources (Harper and Ogden 1970), viruses infecting the same host genotype may nonetheless face different selective pressures depending on host age. Consequently, adaptive mutations that enhance viral fitness at one developmental stage may be neutral, or even disadvantageous, at another.

Here, we sought to better understand how variation in host developmental stage within populations influences virus evolution. We used populations of A. thaliana that differed in demographic composition (Fig. 1) to evaluate the evolutionary dynamics of their natural parasite, TuMV (species Potyvirus rapae, genus Potyvirus, family Potyviridae). Experimental plant populations differed in the proportion of individuals at 3 developmental stages: (i) juvenile vegetative, characterized by resource allocation to growth; (ii) bolting, which marks the developmental transition (Pouteau and Albertini 2009); and (iii) flowering, during which mature plants allocate resources to reproduction. These stages have already been shown to affect the severity of TuMV infection (Melero et al. 2023) and involve age-specific reprogramming of defense responses (Melero et al. 2024). We evaluated how host population stage structure, summarized in part by median ordinal host age, influences the evolution of TuMV virulence and viral genetic diversity. In addition, we identified candidate beneficial mutations and tested whether viral adaptation was specific to the host developmental stage structure experienced during experimental evolution.

Schematic of the 7 host population stage structures used for experimental evolution. Numbers in parentheses show the median ordinal age categories (Ã) for each population. The upper row shows “expansive” populations dominated by juveniles (Ã = 1 or 1.5), the middle row shows “stationary” populations with balanced age classes (Ã = 2 in all 3 cases), and the lower row shows “constrictive” populations dominated by older plants (Ã = 2.5 or 3). Each population comprised 24 A. thaliana plants allocated to prebolting (juvenile), bolting (transition), or postbolting (mature) stages in the indicated proportions.

We expected host populations dominated by juvenile plants to favor viral genotypes that rapidly exploit highly susceptible hosts, potentially leading to faster disease onset and stage-specific adaptation. By contrast, populations containing balanced or older developmental stages were expected to impose more heterogeneous physiological and defense environments, favoring either broader stage generalism or mutations whose fitness effects depend strongly on demographic context. We further predicted that such demographic selection would leave genomic signatures of both parallel evolution, when similar age structures impose similar constraints, and demography-specific adaptation, when host population stage structure changes the local fitness landscape.

Results

Host population stage structure was analyzed in 2 complementary ways (Table S1). First, the 7 demographic treatments were treated as categorical experimental conditions in generalized linear mixed models (GLMM)/multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVA) analyses, which test whether evolutionary outcomes differ among specific population structures. Second, the median ordinal age category, Ã, was used only as a continuous summary variable to test whether estimated rates of evolution showed a monotonic trend along the juvenile-to-mature demographic gradient. These analyses address different questions and need not yield identical conclusions (Table S1).

The evolution of disease progression and symptom severity depends on host population stage structure

Across 5 serial passages, all disease-related traits showed pronounced changes whose magnitude and direction depended on the host population stage structure. Univariate GLMM analyses for each trait consistently detected significant effects of passage, host population stage structure, and their interaction, with mostly large effect sizes (ηP2): incubation period (Figure S1), area under the disease progress stairs (AUDPS; Figure S2), area under symptom intensity progress stairs (AUSIPS; Figure S3), mean symptom severity (Figure S4), and maximum symptom severity at 14 days postinoculation (dpi; Figure S5). For example, incubation period and symptom traits each exhibited highly significant between-subjects effects of passage and host population stage structure, as well as significant passage × host population stage structure interactions, indicating that the tempo and intensity of disease evolution depended on the host age structure experienced during evolution.

A MANOVA on the 5 disease traits corroborated these patterns, revealing highly significant and large-magnitude effects of passage, host population stage structure, and their interaction (Table S2; Wilks’ Λ, all P < 0.001, ηP2 ≥ 0.235).

Traits were not independent. Controlling for Ã, AUDPS was negatively correlated with the incubation period (rp = −0.999, 4 d.f., P < 0.001), whereas AUSIPS correlated positively with mean symptom severity (rp = 1, 4 d.f., P < 0.001) and with maximum symptom severity at 14 dpi (rp = 0.940, 4 d.f., P = 0.005).

Given that MANOVA detected strong multivariate effects of passage, host population stage structure, and their interaction across the set of disease traits, the outcomes are best interpreted in a multivariate space rather than trait-by-trait. Moreover, the observed correlations among traits indicate redundancy and potential collinearity; therefore, we used PCA to reduce dimensionality and extract orthogonal composite axes that capture shared variance while enabling clearer inference and visualization with fewer multiple comparisons. This analysis summarized disease phenotypes into 2 axes (Fig. 2a and b). PC1 (63.1% variance) primarily captured symptom severity [large positive loadings for AUSIPS (0.928), mean severity (0.948), and maximum severity at 14 dpi (0.955); near-zero weight for incubation period (−0.112) and AUDPS (0.119)], whereas PC2 (35.1%) represented disease progression timing, loading strongly and oppositely on incubation period (−0.991) and AUDPS (0.345). Thus, PC1 reflects how severe disease becomes, and PC2 how fast disease develops, largely independent of severity (Fig. 2a and b).

Fit of PC1 to the GLMM used above for each individual traits shows a significant passage effect of large magnitude (F4, 21.340 = 3.084, P = 0.038, ηP2 = 0.366), although no net effect of host population age was detected (F6, 20.694 = 2.249, P = 0.079, ηP2 = 0.395). Likewise, the fit of PC2 to the same GLMM identified significant effects of large magnitude for host population stage structure (F6, 20.877 = 20.674, P < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.856), evolutionary passage (F4, 21.695 = 25.826, P < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.826) and the interaction of both factors (F20, 114 = 1.889, P = 0.020, ηP2 = 0.249).

Within-host viral fitness in infected individuals depends on the host age pyramid structure

Viral load per infected host (RT-qPCR) increased during experimental evolution but did so heterogeneously across host population stage structures (Fig. 3a). A GLMM including passage, host population stage structure, and their interaction as fixed effects, with lineage nested within host population stage structure as random effect, detected highly significant, large effects of passage (F1,99 = 73.223, P < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.425), host population stage structure (F6,99 = 26.128, P < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.613), and their interaction (F6,99 = 18.808, P < 0.001, ηP2 = 0.533) on log-viral load. Lineage and passage × lineage terms were also significant (Table S3).

Consistent with this pathosystem, viral load correlated positively with symptom severity metrics when controlling for Ã: mean severity (rp = 0.830, 4 d.f., P = 0.041), maximum severity at 14 dpi (rp = 0.952, 4 d.f., P = 0.003), AUSIPS (rp = 0.815, 4 d.f., P = 0.048), and PC1 (rp = 0.965, 4 d.f., P = 0.002). These associations support the conclusion that higher within-host virus accumulation underlies stronger symptom expression in A. thaliana—TuMV.

Thus, viral load evolved during serial passage in a demography-dependent manner and was tightly associated with the severity axis of disease evolution.

Host population stage structure imposes variable constraints on the rates of evolution for different traits

Here, rate of evolution refers to the lineage-specific temporal slope or directional change inferred from the 5-passage phenotypic time series, rather than to the raw trait values shown in Figs. 2a, b and 3a. Rates of phenotypic evolution estimated from time series (ARIMA) revealed distinct dependencies on host demography (Fig. 2c and d, and Figures S1b to S5b, and Fig. 3b). After controlling for independent lineages, the rate of change in PC1 (severity) did not vary detectably with Ã (Fig. 2c; rp = −0.133, 31 d.f., P = 0.462), whereas the rate of change in PC2 (timing) increased with Ã (Fig. 2d; rp = 0.369, 31 d.f., P = 0.034), indicating faster evolution of disease timing in older host populations. By contrast, the rate of viral load evolution showed no measurable association with Ã (Fig. 3b; rp = −0.219, 22 d.f., P = 0.305).

Specificity of virus adaptation to host population stage structure

Cross-infection assays using evolved lineages on host populations containing different proportions of individuals at the 3 plant developmental stages produced a quantitative infection matrix that was non-nested (WNODF = 7.591, P = 1) but strongly modular (Q = 0.097, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). TuMV lineages evolved in expansive host populations (Ã = 1 or 1.5) achieved high viral loads only in prebolting hosts, consistent with specialization. In contrast, viral lineages evolved in stationary or constrictive populations (2 ≤ Ã ≤ 3) performed comparably across all developmental stages, suggesting broader host-stage generalism.

Rows correspond to lineages evolved under each host population stage structure. These results indicate that juvenile-dominated host populations favored stage-specialized viral performance, whereas more balanced or older host populations favored broader performance across developmental stages.

Genetic variability and parallel evolution across demographic treatments

Figure 5 illustrates the genome-wide changes in genetic variability observed during experimental evolution of TuMV across host populations differing in age structure. Specifically, the figure shows the per-site change in heterozygosity (Δh) between passages t = 1 and t = 5 for all experimental lineages. The distribution of Δh values reveals substantial heterogeneity among genomic sites, with some positions showing sharp increases or decreases in variability, while others remain largely unchanged. These differences are not uniform across demographic treatments: distinct age-structured host populations show unique signatures of heterozygosity change, demonstrating that the host's demographic composition shapes the evolutionary trajectories of individual viral genomic sites. To statistically evaluate these patterns, only SNVs with allele frequency (p) > 5% and coverage > 100 reads were retained (Kofler et al. 2011; Spitzer et al. 2020). We performed an ART ANOVA, which revealed strong and highly significant effects of passage, host population stage structure, and their interaction on Δh (Figure S6a; P < 0.001 in all cases). Thus, both temporal evolutionary processes and demographic differences in host populations influence viral genetic variability. By contrast, the rate of heterozygosity evolution showed no measurable association with the mean host age Ã (Figure S6b; P = 0.120), indicating that although age structure affects patterns of variability, it does not systematically accelerate or decelerate the rate of heterozygosity change across treatments.

Changes in genetic variability. Differences in per site heterozygosity along TuMV genome between passages t = 1 and t = 5 (Δh). Different cistrons are indicated with different colors in the abscissae. Lineages are indicated with colored circles.

To assess whether replicate lineages evolving under the same demographic conditions followed similar genetic trajectories, we performed CMH tests of parallel evolution (Fig. 6). Several demographic treatments exhibited significant parallelism: multiple replicates acquired the same mutation and showed allele-frequency shifts in the same direction, either increases or purging (see File S2 for the complete set of cases). The convergent mutation with the largest beneficial effect was 9140A > C (CP/K3004N; basic by polar), observed in all replicates evolved under 0:0:24 (s = 0.627 ± 0.448; Δp = 0.041). The second-largest beneficial effect corresponded to 3366U > C (synonymous), detected only in 4:8:12 lineages (s = 0.460 ± 0.567; Δp = 0.048). Remarkably, in 12:8:4, mutations 6243A > G (VPg/N2039D; polar by acid) and 6252C > U (VPg/R2042H; basic by polar) consistently behaved as beneficial across replicates, with large mean selection coefficients per passage (s = 0.316 ± 0.108 and 0.276 ± 0.129, respectively) and substantial increases in frequency (Δp = 0.221 and 0.070), consistent with strong positive selection. Both amino acid replacements occur within a short VPg segment and, taken together, produce a net increase in negative charge. Parallelism was also pervasive among deleterious variants present in the standing genetic variation (File S2). The demographic-specific mutation with the largest deleterious effect was 6198G > A (VPg/G2024S; nonpolar by polar), found only in 24:0:0 (s = −0.641 ± 0.309; Δp = −0.033). In 8:8:8, all lineages shared 6258G > U (VPg/N2044Y; conservative polar) and 9131G > A (synonymous), both strongly deleterious (s = −0.994 ± 0.341 and −1.094 ± 0.067) and undergoing pronounced purifying selection (Δp = −0.194 and −0.124).

Test of parallelism within and across experimental populations with different demographic compositions. Asterisks indicate statistically significant cases.

Several mutations showed treatment-independent deleterious effects (Fig. 6; File S2). For example, 6258A > G (VPg/N2044G; polar by nonpolar) was deleterious in all 7 treatments, with selection coefficients ranging from −1.069 ± 0.134 (Δp = −0.132) in 4:16:4 to −0.510 ± 0.502 (Δp = −0.096) in 0:0:24. Mutation 6247A > G (VPg/E2040G; acid by nonpolar) occurred in 5 demographic conditions (absent in 12:8:4 and 4:16:4), with the strongest negative effect in 4:8:12 (s = −0.793 ± 0.286; Δp = −0.066) and the weakest in 24:0:0 (s = −0.092 ± 0.330; Δp = −0.021).

Among synonymous mutations, 9131G > A was deleterious in all demographies except 24:0:0, with selection coefficients ranging from the aforementioned −1.094 in 8:8:8 to −0.410 ± 0.384 in 0:24:0 (Δp = −0.054). Likewise, 6261A > G (synonymous) appeared in lineages from 5 treatments (absent in 24:0:0 and 4:8:12), with s ranging from −0.599 ± 0.221 (0:24:0; Δp = −0.022) to −0.287 ± 0.430 (0:0:24; Δp = −0.056). In contrast, 6243A > G was consistently beneficial across all treatments, with s from 0.112 ± 0.648 (24:0:0; Δp = 0.226) to 0.456 ± 0.094 (0:0:24; Δp = 0.389), indicating a robust advantage irrespective of demography.

A subset of mutations displayed selection coefficients that depended strongly on host age structure, revealing demographic-specific fitness tradeoffs. For instance, 6252C > U (VPg/R2042C; basic by nonpolar) was deleterious in constrictive 0:0:24 (s = −0.310 ± 0.035; Δp = −0.058) and 4:8:12 (s = −0.021 ± 0.556; Δp = −0.101) demographies, but beneficial in all others, with s from 0.009 ± 0.518 (0:24:0; Δp = 0.183) to 0.379 ± 0.242 (8:8:8; Δp = 0.219). Two additional notable cases were 6253G > A (VPg/R2042L; basic by nonpolar), which was beneficial in 0:0:24 (s = 0.321 ± 0.364; Δp = 0.067) and 12:8:4 (s = 0.098 ± 0.415; Δp = 0.064) but deleterious in 0:24:0 (s = −0.491 ± 0.408; Δp = −0.045); and 7067C > U (synonymous), beneficial in 0:0:24 (s = 0.321 ± 0.268; Δp = 0.072) and 8:8:8 (s = 0.162 ± 0.201; Δp = 0.027) but mildly deleterious in 4:16:4 (s = −0.043 ± 0.300; Δp = −0.046). These sign reversals indicate an adaptive landscape strongly modulated by host age.

Remarkably, among all the parallel mutations discussed, only VPg/R2042C and VPg/R2042H were de novo beneficial mutations that were not detected in standing genetic variation at passage t = 1, and both affected the same amino acid residue in VPg.

Taken together, Figs. 5 and 6 show that: (i) patterns of genetic variability (Δh) are strongly shaped by both evolutionary time and host demographic composition; (ii) parallel evolution is common within demographic treatments, with several mutations, including synonymous ones, showing consistent beneficial or deleterious effects across replicates; and (iii) across treatments, convergent evolution comprises a mixture of universally beneficial, universally deleterious, and demography-dependent effect mutations, underscoring the complex, multifactorial nature of TuMV adaptation.

Discussion

Demography reshapes selection and the virulence-transmission landscape

Our experimental evolution shows that the age composition of host populations imposes distinct and repeatable selective pressures on TuMV, reshaping both the spatial pattern and tempo of genetic change across the viral genome. In particular, we detected strong effects of passage, host population stage structure, and their interaction on the per-site change in heterozygosity (Δh), whereas the rate of heterozygosity evolution did not scale with the median age Ã (Figure S6). These findings align with classic eco-evolutionary theory linking virulence and transmission to host life history (Anderson and May 1982; Ebert and Herre 1996; Ben-Ami 2019): heterogeneity in host demography shifts the local balance among replication, damage to the host, and onward transmission, thereby moving the location of the virulence-transmission optimum in genotype space. Conceptually, this is consistent with tradeoff frameworks for virulence evolution and with the prediction that host life stage can shift the selective gradient experienced by pathogens (e.g., via differences in exposure, within-host resources, or immune regulation) (Alizon et al. 2009; Ben-Ami 2019). Our time-series of disease traits shows that host age structure not only affects whether virulence-related traits evolve but also which components of disease phenotype respond. The PCA separated disease phenotypes into 2 largely independent axes: symptom severity (PC1; driven by AUSIPS, mean severity and maximum severity at 14 dpi) and disease timing (PC2; dominated by incubation period and AUDPS). Across passages, both axes changed, but their evolutionary rates showed different relationships with host demography. Disease timing evolved faster in older host populations, whereas symptom severity did not show a detectable monotonic relationship with median host age. This suggests that host population stage structure can reweight distinct components of virulence, separating the evolution of how quickly disease develops from how severe symptoms become. Such decoupling is consistent with virulence-evolution theory, which predicts that different host life-history states can shift the balance among replication, host damage and opportunities for onward transmission (Anderson and May 1982; Ebert and Herre 1996; Alizon et al. 2009; Ben-Ami 2019). Within this framework (Fig. 7), host population stage structure acts upstream of both viral phenotypic evolution and genomic change, with host physiological state and within-host replication providing plausible mediating steps.

Viral load provides a possible mechanistic link between within-host adaptation and symptom expression. Viral accumulation increased during experimental evolution and was positively associated with severity-related traits, indicating that higher within-host replication contributes to stronger disease expression in this pathosystem. In addition, the infection matrix suggests that demographic context influenced the breadth of host-stage performance: lineages evolved in juvenile-dominated populations performed best on prebolting plants, whereas lineages from stationary or constrictive populations showed broader performance across developmental stages. We therefore interpret host population stage structure as a factor that can alter the selective balance among replication, disease timing, symptom severity, and host-stage specificity. However, because our experiment did not directly measure between-host transmission or epidemic spread, these results should be viewed as hypotheses about possible epidemiological consequences rather than direct evidence for field-scale dynamics. Future experiments that manipulate host developmental structure while measuring transmission would be needed to test whether these within-host and serial-passage patterns translate into differences in epidemic tempo or evolutionary outcomes in natural or agricultural populations (Alizon et al. 2009; Ben-Ami 2019; Melero et al. 2023, 2024).

A limitation of our demographic descriptor is that the median ordinal age category collapses biologically distinct host populations into the same value. For example, homogeneous bolting populations and heterogeneous mixtures can share Ã = 2 while differing in age variance, developmental heterogeneity, and physiological diversity. Thus, trends with Ã should be interpreted as tests of a simplified juvenile-to-mature gradient, not as complete descriptions of host demographic complexity. Future experiments that explicitly manipulate both mean developmental stage and age variance would be needed to disentangle the contributions of average host age, developmental heterogeneity, and coexistence among host stages.

Phenotypic signatures of virulence under age structure and applied implications

The sign reversals we document (e.g. VPg/N2039S, VPg/R2042C or VPg/R2042L) highlight clear evolutionary tradeoffs: a mutation improving performance in expansive populations can be neutral or deleterious in constrictive populations. This maps naturally onto the idea that host age changes the effective fitness components (replication rate, movement, within-host carrying capacity, and transmission opportunity) that selection sees, shifting the set of optimal genotypes for TuMV in each demographic context. From an applied perspective, these results suggest that age structure may be an important ecological variable to consider when forecasting pathogen evolution or designing management strategies, such as cultivar deployment across developmental windows or staggered planting.

Host developmental programs as the ecological driver of selection

From the host side, plants display ARR (Kus et al. 2002; Hu and Yang 2019) and ontogenic reprogramming (Carella et al. 2015; Hu and Yang 2019; Melero et al. 2024) of defense, prominently involving SA signaling, phase-change regulators such as the miR156-SPL module, hormone crosstalk, RNA silencing, and transcriptional immune networks. ARR and ontogenic SA upregulation have been demonstrated in A. thaliana and other plants, mechanistically linking development with immune maturation. Recent syntheses highlight that, although general trends exist, ARR is pathosystem-specific and reflects multilevel regulation (RNA silencing, hormones, and transcriptional networks) that can generate stage-dependent selection on viruses, precisely the pattern our data reveal (e.g. sign changes in selection coefficients across demographic treatments) (Wilson et al. 2013, 2017; Melero et al. 2023). Together, our results and this literature suggest that age structure does not merely scale infection risk; it remodels the adaptive landscape encountered by TuMV, shifting which mutations are beneficial, neutral, or deleterious in a demography-dependent manner (e.g. VPg/R2042C beneficial in most treatments but deleterious in constrictive population 0:0:24; Fig. 6).

Repeatability and context dependence: parallelism within and across demographies

Multiple mutations rose or declined in parallel within the same host demography (e.g. beneficials CP/K3004N in 0:0:24 and VPg/N2039 and VPg/R2042H in 12:8:4; deleterious VPg/G2024S in 24:0:0 and VPg/N2044Y in 8:8:8), while several others displayed convergent behavior across different demographic treatments (e.g. VPg/N2044G consistently deleterious in all demographies). These outcomes echo extensive evidence that RNA viruses frequently use repeatable genomic routes to adapt to recurring challenges. Parallelism has been observed in experimental systems (e.g. vesicular stomatitis virus parallel/convergent mutations across demographic regimes; HIV-1 lineages passaged for ∼1 year) and when viruses adapt to new hosts (e.g. Drosophila C virus evolving in 19 species, with stronger parallelism among closely related hosts) (Cuevas et al. 2002; Longdon et al. 2018; Bertels et al. 2019; Bons et al. 2020; Navarro et al. 2022; Ambrós et al. 2024). Clinically and epidemiologically, SARS-CoV-2 provides a recent illustration: independent lineages repeatedly acquire similar spike mutations under host immune pressure and within immunosuppressed patients under antiviral therapy, clear signatures of convergent evolution under shared selection (immune escape, receptor binding) (Roemer et al. 2023). In plant viruses, field and population genomic syntheses emphasize that host ecology and genetic context shape emergence and adaptation, and that repeated solutions can appear in geographically or ecologically distinct settings, patterns mirrored here at laboratory timescales under controlled demography (Elena et al. 2014; Lefeuvre et al. 2019).

Mechanistically, such predictability arises when (i) the environment presents a narrow set of functional challenges (Remold 2012), e.g. access to translation initiation factors such as eIF4E/eIF(iso)4E for potyviruses, (ii) the viral genome contains structurally or functionally constrained hotspots that house large-effect mutations (e.g. VPg in potyviruses), and (iii) epistasis canalizes trajectories onto a limited number of fit mutational pathways (Sanjuán et al. 2005; Cervera et al. 2016). All 3 factors have been repeatedly implicated in viral experimental evolution and outbreak analyses. We have shown that some sites are globally selected against (e.g. VPg/N2044G), whereas others flip sign depending on demography (e.g. VPg/R2042C), underscoring that predictability is conditional on the host context.

Although several mutations showed parallel behavior within demographic treatments, replicate lineages also diverged substantially. This divergence is biologically informative rather than merely noise. It may reflect stochastic sampling during serial passage, founder effects during transmission bottlenecks, differences in the subset of infected plants contributing to pooled inocula, and unmeasured physiological heterogeneity among nominally equivalent hosts. Such effects are expected to be especially important in RNA virus populations, where standing variation, high mutation supply, clonal interference, and epistasis can generate alternative adaptive routes even under shared selection (Sanjuán et al. 2005; Cervera et al. 2016).

VPg as an evolutionary hinge in potyvirus adaptation

A central outcome of our study is that the majority of recurrent changes map to VPg, and many of them cluster between residues L2003 and N2044. This region has been independently identified as a repeated target of selection in TuMV experimental evolution, with D2037G and R2042H serving as canonical examples of convergent or background-specific solutions in earlier work (Ambrós et al. 2024). The functional salience of this segment is well grounded: VPg mediates key interactions with plant eIF4E/eIF(iso)4E that underlie potyvirus translation, replication, and host specificity, and small amino acid changes within VPg are known to tune or disrupt these contacts (and related interactions), thereby altering fitness in a host- and context-dependent fashion. Beyond protein-factor binding, recent work also shows that evolution in VPg can rewire broader VPg-host proteome interactions, with adaptive substitutions in this very interval modifying the interaction landscape and the balance between growth and defense during infection (Carrasco et al. 2024). The recurrence of VPg hits in our data, especially at positions corresponding to ∼L2003—N2044, therefore dovetails with independent evidence that this short tract behaves as an evolutionary “hinge” enabling rapid improvement under diverse selective scenarios.

Because several recurrent substitutions occur within the L2003—N2044 region, their effects may not be independent; local epistasis among residues in this region could influence which combinations are accessible or beneficial in each demographic context.

Synonymous mutations and compositional constraints are visible to selection

A notable feature of our data is the presence of parallelism at synonymous sites, including a uniformly beneficial (6243A > G) and deleterious (6261A > G) synonymous changes and demography-dependent synonymous variants (e.g. 7067C > U and 9131G > A). That synonymous mutations can be targets of selection is now well established across RNA viruses (Jack et al. 2017; Canale et al. 2018; Le Nouën et al. 2019; Kustin and Stern 2021). Multiple mechanisms can impart strong fitness effects to synonymous changes: (i) Synonymous variants modulate initiation/elongation rates via tRNA availability or codon bias, thereby changing protein yield, stoichiometry across polyproteins, or co-translational folding. Synthetic recoding studies demonstrate that codon optimization and codon deoptimization reproducibly attenuate viruses (poliovirus, influenza A, bacteriophage T7), with measured reductions in mRNA stability and translation efficiency—and importantly, these effects are strictly synonymous. General reviews emphasize that “synonymous is not silent” due to widespread codon-bias-mediated impacts on expression and fitness (Novella et al. 2004). (ii) Synonymous changes can remodel local or long-range RNA secondary structures required for replication, translation, or packaging; time-series fitness landscapes show many synonymous effects in RNA viruses and their enrichment in structured regions. For positive-sense plant RNA viruses, densely packed genomes impose multiple overlapping constraints (cis-acting motifs, frameshifts, RNA-protein interaction sites), magnifying the chance that a synonymous change is functionally visible. (iii) In vertebrate systems, elevating CpG/UpA frequencies strongly attenuates viruses via ZAP and OAS3/RNase L pathways; in influenza, engineered CpG-enrichment produces attenuation but robust immunogenicity (Odon et al. 2019). Although plants lack ZAP, plant RNA viruses also show low CpG/UpA and compositional biases shaped by selection and mutation, implying that synonymous changes that shift dinucleotide content or codon usage could influence replication, RNA stability, or recognition by plant defenses (e.g. RNA silencing), with context-dependent fitness effects (González de Prádena et al. 2020; He et al. 2022). These mechanisms provide plausible routes for the recurrent selection we observe at synonymous sites, linked to expression tuning of polyprotein cleavage products (e.g. VPg, NIa-Pro, NIb), maintenance-required RNA structures, or modulating engagement with plant defense layers whose activity varies with host age and tissue ontogeny. The fact that one synonymous variant was beneficial across all demographics (6243A > G), whereas others changed sign with host demography, is consistent with multimechanism control: some sites may tune universal functions (e.g. robust RNA fold stabilization), while others couple to age-dependent immunity or resource landscapes (e.g. shifting tRNA pools or translational capacity across developmental stages).

Methodological considerations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered. Selection was inferred from 2 pooled time points, passages t = 1 and t = 5. This design identifies candidate variants showing large and repeatable frequency changes, but denser longitudinal sampling would be needed to resolve transient polymorphisms, short-term allele-frequency fluctuations, and epistatic interactions.

Our design also has limited power for formal genotype–phenotype association. The number of independent evolved lineages is small relative to the number of candidate variants, and many mutations are partly associated with specific demographic treatments. We therefore interpret links between recurrent mutations and phenotypic trajectories as hypotheses for future testing, rather than as evidence of direct causality. Reverse-genetics reconstruction of candidate VPg and synonymous variants will be needed to determine their individual and epistatic effects.

The mechanisms underlying synonymous variants also remain unresolved. These mutations may affect viral fitness through RNA structure, codon usage, dinucleotide composition, or other plant-specific processes. Testing these alternatives will require engineered viral genotypes and measurements of host traits that vary with developmental stage, including hormone profiles, small-RNA landscapes, ribosome occupancy, tRNA pools, and VPg interactions with eIF4E/eIF(iso)4E.

Finally, our conclusions are based on a single A. thaliana genotype and a single TuMV genetic background. Although this design isolates host population stage structure as the main experimental variable, other host accessions or viral isolates could shift the balance between specialization, generalism, and demography-dependent tradeoffs.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that host age structure is not a passive backdrop but an active driver of viral evolution that alters which mutations are favored, the extent of parallelism, and whether selection is consistent or demography-dependent. The repeated emergence of both nonsynonymous and synonymous changes underscores that multiple layers of constraint—protein function, RNA structure, and translational/compositional biases—shape predictable paths of adaptation in RNA viruses. By integrating host demography into evolutionary inference, we gain sharper, mechanistically grounded forecasts of how plant viruses will adapt under realistic, age-structured host populations.

Materials and methods

Plants and viruses

A. thaliana (L.) Heyhn, ecotype Col-0, was used in this study. Plants were grown in a BSL2 climatic chamber under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod (LED tubes, PAR 90 to 100 μmol·m−2·s−1), at 24 °C during light and 20 °C during dark, with 40% relative humidity. This day/night regime was used to approximate standard controlled growth conditions for A. thaliana and to maintain reproducible plant development and TuMV infection. Because all treatments were grown under the same regime, temperature was not confounded with host population stage structure. The substrate consisted of 50% DSM WNR1 R73454 (Kekkilä Professional, Vantaa, Finland), 25% grade 3 vermiculite, and 25% perlite (3 to 6 mm). Biological pest control was implemented using Stratiolaelaps scimitus and Steinernema feltiae (Koppert Co., Málaga, Spain).

The infectious plasmid p35STunos, containing a cDNA of the YC5 TuMV isolate from calla lily (Zantedeschia sp.; GenBank accession AF530055.2) under the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter and nos terminator (Chen et al. 2003), was used to infect Nicotiana benthamiana Domin.

For inoculation of A. thaliana, infected N. benthamiana leaf tissue was ground and suspended at 100 mg/mL in inoculation buffer (50 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7, 3% PEG 6000), supplemented with 10% carborundum suspension (100 mg/mL in the same buffer). Two leaves of 3-week-old plants were mechanically inoculated with 5 μL of this suspension. Mock-inoculated plants received buffer plus Carborundum only.

Experimental evolution design

Ancestral TuMV was experimentally evolved in A. thaliana in populations with different age compositions through 5 serial passages. For passage t = 1, plants were inoculated with sap from infected N. benthamiana. For subsequent passages, symptomatic plants were pooled at 14 dpi and used as inoculum for the next passage.

Host populations consisted of varying proportions of plants at 3 developmental stages (Fig. 1): prebolting (juvenile, ordinal age category 1), bolting (transition to reproductive growth, ordinal age category 2) and postbolting (mature, ordinal age category 3). Four independent lineages evolved under mixed demographic conditions (prebolting:bolting:postbolting): expansive 12:8:4 (median ordinal age category Ã = 1.5), stationary 4:16:4 (Ã = 2) and 8:8:8 (Ã = 2), and constrictive 4:8:12 (Ã = 2.5). For homogeneous populations (expansive 24:0:0, Ã = 1; stationary 0:24:0, Ã = 2; and constrictive 0:0:24, Ã = 3), 3 new lineages were evolved plus 3 previously reported by Melero et al. (2023). These ratios were chosen to represent homogeneous, juvenile-biased, balanced, mature-biased, and fully mature population structures while keeping total population size constant at 24 plants. The median ordinal age category was used as a simple descriptor of the juvenile-to-mature gradient, whereas the full treatment identity was retained as a categorical factor in the main analyses.

Phenotyping disease

Symptoms were scored daily for each plant using a semi-quantitative scale (0 = no symptoms, 5 = systemic necrosis; Butković et al. 2021), except for passage 2. These data were used to estimate: (i) incubation period (mean time to first symptoms) via Kaplan–Meier regression, (ii) mean symptoms severity over 14 days, (iii) maximum symptoms severity at 14 dpi, (iv) AUDPS (Simko and Piepho 2012), and (v) AUSIPS (Kone et al. 2017). AUDPS summarizes the cumulative progression of disease through time, whereas AUSIPS summarizes the cumulative intensity of symptoms through time. Thus, AUDPS captures how rapidly disease develops in the population, whereas AUSIPS captures the accumulated burden of symptom expression. AUDPS and AUSIPS were computed using the R package “agricolae” version 1.3-2 (de Mendiburu 2020).

The 14 dpi time point was chosen because it captures systemic infection and symptom expression in this TuMV-A. thaliana pathosystem while preceding extensive tissue senescence in older plants, thereby allowing comparisons among developmental stages at a common infection endpoint.

Statistical analyses of phenotypic data

Symptom severity was scored daily for each plant as an ordinal variable using the 0 to 5 scale described above. These raw daily symptom scores were analyzed as the ordinal response in a cumulative-logit mixed model. In this model, dpi was treated as a repeated-measures factor because symptom scores were recorded repeatedly through time for the same plants. Evolutionary passage and hot population stage structure were included as fixed categorical predictors, and independent viral lineages were modeled as a random factor nested within host population stage structure. Tests involving the repeated-measures factor were corrected for violations of sphericity using the Greenhouse-Geisser adjustment.

For the mean symptom severity, maximum symptom severity at 14 dpi, AUDPS, AUSIPS, and log10-viral load, we fitted GLMM with normal error distribution and identity link function. Evolutionary passage, demographic condition, and their interaction were included as fixed factors, and lineage was included as a random factor nested within demographic condition. In addition to univariate analyses, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was performed on the 5 disease-related traits using Wilks’ Λ statistic and the same model structure. To explore trait correlations, we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation and Kaiser normalization. Component importance was assessed based on eigenvalues and their contribution to explained variance.

To estimate rates of phenotypic evolution for each disease-related trait (and inferred PCs) and viral load (see the section RNA extraction and estimation of viral load by RT-qPCR for estimation), time-series data were modeled using ARIMA(p, d, q) models following Elena et al. (2025). Model selection was performed with the R package “forecast” version 8.23.0, using automatic estimation of the Box–Cox transformation parameter λ. For each lineage and trait, the resulting rate estimate summarizes the direction and magnitude of temporal change across passages and was subsequently used as the response variable in analyses testing whether evolutionary rates varied with median ordinal host age.

In GLMM, the ηP2 statistic was used to quantify effect sizes (how much of the variance in a dependent variable is explained by a specific factor, after accounting for other factors in the model). It was calculated as SSeffect/(SSeffect + SSerror) from the Type III sums of squares. We used conventional benchmarks for interpretation, treating ηP2 < 0.05 as small effects, 0.05 ≤ ηP2 < 0.15 as medium effects and ηP2 ≥ 0.15 as large effects.

All graphs were generated using the “ggplot2” package version 4.0.2 (Wickham 2009). R-based analyses indicated above were done under R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team 2025) within RStudio version 2025.01.1 + 403 (Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA). When not otherwise specified, analyses were done with SPSS version 30.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RNA extraction and estimation of viral load by RT-qPCR

Viral accumulation was quantified after passages t = 1 and t = 5. Twenty-four symptomatic plants were pooled, frozen, and homogenized under liquid nitrogen. RNA was extracted using the NZY Plant/Fungi RNA isolation kit (NZYtech, Lisbon, Portugal) following the manufacturer's recommendations.

Viral load was measured by absolute real-time quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) using a TuMV standard curve and the primers TuMV F117 forward (5′-CAATACGTGCGAGAGAAGCACAC-3′) and F118 reverse (5′-TAACCCCTTAACGCCAAGTAAG-3′), amplifying a 173 bp fragment of the CP cistron (Corrêa et al. 2020). Briefly, the standard curve consisted of 8 serial dilutions of the in vitro synthesized TuMV genome prepared in total plant RNA purified from healthy A. thaliana plants.

Reactions were run in 10 μL using the qPCRBIO SyGreen 1-step Go Hi-ROX System (PCRBiosystems, London, UK) on an ABI StepOne Plus Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Cycling: RT 45 °C (10 min), denaturation at 95 °C (2 min), then 40 cycles of 95 °C (5 s) and 60 °C (25 s), followed by melting curve analysis. Negative controls included RNA from healthy plants and water. Each sample was run in triplicate. Results were analyzed using the StepOne software 2.2.2 (Applied Biosystems).

Infection matrix

Evolved lineages were used to inoculate A. thaliana plants at the 3 aforementioned developmental stages. Infection progress was monitored on a daily basis; viral load was quantified at 14 dpi. Trait values and log-viral load were transformed into integers (0 to 7) (Moury et al. 2021) for nestedness and modularity analysis using R packages “bipartite” version 2.18 and “igraph” version 1.5.0.9008. Nestedness was estimated with WNODF (Almeida-Neto and Ulrich 2011) and modularity with the spinglass algorithm (Newman and Girvan 2004). Significance was tested against 10,000 null matrices generated under the B model using the Patefield (1981) algorithm.

In this context, nestedness tests whether specialist viral lineages that perform well on only a few host developmental stages are restricted to stages that are also suitable for more generalist viral lineages. Modularity tests whether groups of viral lineages perform preferentially on particular host developmental stages, indicating stage-specific adaptation or specialization.

Library preparation and RNA-seq sequencing

Total RNA extractions described above (section RNA extraction and estimation of viral load by RT-qPCR for estimation) were used for library preparation, and Illumina sequencing was performed by Novogene (Cambridge, UK) using a NovaSeq X Plus Series (PE150) platform and an Lnc-stranded mRNA-seq library method, rRNA depletion, directional library preparation, 150 bp paired-end, and approximately 6 Gb raw data per sample. Novogene did a quality check of the libraries using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), qPCR for quantification, and a bioanalyzer for size distribution detection.

Bioinformatics methods

Fastq quality was assessed with FASTQC (Andrews 2010) and MultiQC (Ewels et al. 2016). Reads were trimmed with BBDuk (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/) to remove adapters, the first 10 nucleotides, and low-quality bases (Q < 10), discarding reads < 80 nucleotides long. Parameters were ktrim = r, k = 31, mink = 11, qtrim = r, trimq = 10, maq = 5, forcetrimleft = 10, and minlength = 80. Reads were mapped to the TuMV YC5 genome using BWA-MEM (Li, 2013). SAM files were processed with SAMtools (Danecek et al. 2021), and duplicates marked with GATK MarkDuplicates version 4.6.1.0 (McKenna et al. 2010). A summary of sequencing output, mapping statistics, and mean genome coverage across samples is provided in Table S4.

Base composition and per-site variant metrics were additionally extracted using pysamstats (version 1.1.2). Per-site heterozygosity (h) was computed using custom R scripts. It was analyzed using the aligned rank transform (ART) method for nonparametric factorial ANOVA, implemented in the R package “ARTool” version 0.11.2.

Inferences of selection coefficients and tests of parallelism

For each genomic position × treatment × replicate, allele counts were aggregated for the ancestral (t = 1) and final (t = 5) time points. Alternative-allele frequency at site i and at each timepoint t, pi(t), was defined as the sum of all nonreference allele counts divided by the total coverage on site i. Thus, pi(1) and pi(5) refer to alternative-allele frequencies at the 2 sampled time points, respectively, and allele–frequency changes were calculated as Δpi = pi(5) − pi(1).

To quantify consistent, parallel allele-frequency changes across replicates, we applied the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel (CMH) test (Kofler et al. 2011) to each genomic position, identifying variants that rose repeatedly in multiple replicates within the same demographic conditions (Cuevas et al. 2002; Hillung et al. 2014; Longdon et al. 2018; Bertels et al. 2019; Navarro et al. 2022). For each site, 2 × 2 contingency tables (alternative vs reference at t = 5 vs t = 1) were constructed for each replicate, and CMH statistics and P-values were computed across replicates (Kofler et al. 2011; Schlöterer et al. 2015; Spitzer et al. 2020). Direction of allele–frequency change was taken from the CMH output, which is derived from the sign of the odds ratio (OR). CMH P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) procedure.

A site was classified as exhibiting parallel evolution within a demographic condition if it met 3 criteria: (i) an FDR-corrected CMH P ≤ 0.05, (ii) at least two-thirds of replicates showing allele–frequency change in the same direction, and (iii) an absolute mean allele–frequency change was |ΔP| ≥ 0.02.

Finally, selection rate constants for the dominant allele at locus i (si) were estimated for each site and replicate using the logit-slope approximation si ≈ [logit pi(5) − logit pi(1)]/Δt (Ewens 2004). Sampling variances were estimated using the delta method applied to Binomial variances under read depth at t = 1 and t = 5. Replicate-specific estimates were combined using inverse–variance meta-analysis to obtain treatment-level s^i (Paris et al. 2019; He et al. 2020). This approach is closely related to estimators used in other approaches but adapted for two-timepoint data [e.g. WFABC (Foll et al. 2014), HMM-based diffusion methods (Bollback et al. 2008; Mathieson and McVean 2013) or CLUES2 (Vaughn and Nielsen 2024)].
