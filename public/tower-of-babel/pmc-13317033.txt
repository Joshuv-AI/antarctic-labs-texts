Long-term efficacy of combined phacoemulsification and Hydrus microstent in open-angle glaucoma versus combined-mechanism/primary angle closure glaucoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kelly C. Nguyen, Eden Ng, Sunee Chansangpetch, Sunita Radhakrishnan, Ngoc Nguyen, Andrew G. Iwach, Shan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompare the efficacy of combined Hydrus-cataract surgery in open angle glaucoma (OAG) to combined mechanism glaucoma (CMG) and primary angle closure glaucoma (PACG) over 36 months.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of patients with CMG, PACG, or OAG who were treated with 1 or more glaucoma medications prior to surgery. Retrospective review of OAG, CMG, or PACG eyes which underwent combined Hydrus-cataract surgery between Jul 2020-Dec 2021. Regression models were used to evaluate IOP, medication use, success rate (IOP ≤ 18 mmHg without medication) and proportion of medication-free eyes.ResultsThe CMG/PACG group (n = 39) had significantly higher IOP reduction from baseline to 36-months than the OAG group (n = 34) (OAG, 15.03 ± 2.90 mmHg to 13.44 ± 3.85 mmHg, p = 0.016; CMG/PACG, 15.62 ± 3.10 to 13.21 ± 2.61 mmHg, p = 0.002) (p = 0.021 for difference). Number of medications was significantly reduced in both groups (OAG, 2.44 ± 1.33 to 1.18 ± 1.40, p < 0.001; CMG/PACG, 2.29 ± 1.27 to 0.87 ± 1.08, p < 0.001) (p = 0.882 for difference). CMG/PACG had higher success rate (49% vs. 29%) and more medication-free eyes (49% vs. 35%) than OAG but these were not statistically significant (p = 0.09 and 0.25, respectively).ConclusionsThis retrospective study supports the long-term efficacy of combined Hydrus-cataract surgery for CMG/PACG and OAG.

Introduction

Glaucoma is one of the leading causes of irreversible blindness worldwide [1]. There are no current treatments to restore vision in patients with glaucoma, but several modalities are used to manage and reduce the intraocular pressure (IOP), the only modifiable risk factor that has been shown to delay or prevent the progression of the disease [1]. Standard therapies to lower IOP include medical therapies, laser treatments, and incisional surgeries [1].

Over the past two decades, minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) has been an increasingly used approach [2], commonly combined with cataract extraction, to lower IOP in patients, often through a device implant [1] MIGS typically has modest IOP-lowering effects, but has a better safety profile, less invasiveness, and faster procedural time than other penetrating surgical therapies used for glaucoma [1]. MIGS procedures act to lower IOP in several ways. In the case of the iStent and Hydrus, the devices are implanted into the Schlemm canal to bypass the trabecular meshwork and facilitate aqueous drainage [1].

The Hydrus microstent (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX) is an 8 mm curved scaffold approved by the FDA in 2018 [3]. Functionally, the Hydrus microstent aids the flow of aqueous humor to the collector channel ostia without disruption of tissue integrity and serves as a scaffold over multiple clock-hours of Schlemm canal [3]. Several studies have shown efficacy of the Hydrus microstent in lowering IOP and the burden of glaucoma medications in patients with OAG [4]. However, studies on the efficacy of the Hydrus microstent have been conducted on predominantly White populations [5], limited to 12 months [5], and/or only included open angle forms of glaucoma [4, 5].

Glaucoma can be categorized into multiple types, including open angle and angle closure forms [1]. Primary open angle glaucoma (POAG), the only form approved for use of MIGS, is the most prevalent form of glaucoma globally. However, angle closure glaucoma types could potentially benefit from MIGS procedures. Angle closure glaucoma can be subdivided into primary angle closure glaucoma (PACG) and combined mechanism glaucoma (CMG). In PACG, angle closure is the primary cause of elevated IOP [6]. CMG cases can be defined as having characteristics of both OAG and PACG and after treatment with laser peripheral iridotomy [7].

Angle closure glaucoma is more common in Asians than in other ethnic groups, particularly in Chinese and South Asian populations [1]. However, few studies have investigated MIGS procedures in ethnically diverse populations with PACG/CMG. Presently, there is a single case report of the Hydrus microstent implantation in angle closure glaucoma, with IOP reduction from 18 mmHg to 12 mmHg without medications at 6 months [8]. Other MIGS procedures, including the iStent implant, have shown efficacy in angle closure glaucoma, with IOP reduction and decreased burden of medication [9]. The aim of this study is to assess the efficacy of the Hydrus microstent in OAG compared to PACG/CMG over an extended time frame.

Materials and methods

This retrospective chart review of patients with combined cataract-Hydrus surgery was conducted at the ophthalmology clinic of a single glaucoma specialist (N.N.). The need for ethical approval was exempted by the institutional review board (IRB) [Advarra, Columbia, MD] due to the retrospective study design and inability of subjects to provide informed consent. The Hydrus implant has received FDA approval for glaucomas such as POAG and ocular hypertension (OHTN), but not for CMG and PACG. However, existing MIGS have been used for the treatment of CMG and PACG and secondary glaucomas. This study only used the Hydrus implant in eyes in which the nasal quadrant was open and visible for adequate insertion, similar to other MIGS which have been used in CMG and PACG cases [10]. Medical charts of patients who received cataract surgery with Hydrus implantation by N.N. between July 2020-December 2021 were reviewed. The following inclusion criteria were used to screen eligible patients: (1) greater than 40 years of age; (2) had age-related cataract with best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/40 or worse with or without glare test; (3) findings of glaucomatous optic neuropathy with mild-moderate severity of visual field loss; (4) complete eye examination with gonioscopy; (5) underwent uncomplicated cataract surgery and successful Hydrus implant surgery; and (6) completed 36-month follow-up. Glaucoma severity was defined using the Hodapp-Anderson Parrish criteria [11].

Patients were classified into open angle or angle closure forms of glaucoma. Open angle was defined as eyes that had Shaffer angle grading of at least 2 for more than 180 degrees. Angle closure glaucoma forms in this study were sub-divided into PACG and CMG. PACG was defined in eyes that met the glaucoma criteria and had Shaffer angle grade 1 or less for 180 degrees or greater on gonioscopy. CMG was defined as eyes with initially closed angles (Shaffer angle grading 1 or less for 180 degrees or more), and the angle was subsequently deepened and met the open angle definition after laser iridotomy, but the IOP remained elevated and/or required glaucoma medications.

The exclusion criteria included: (1) any ocular or systemic condition that could affect vision and/or visual field interpretation; (2) any ocular or systemic condition that could affect IOP or preclude accurate tonometry (e.g., corneal pathology, previous refractive surgery, concurrent use of systemic steroid); (3) previous intraocular surgery including filtering surgery, glaucoma drainage device implantation, and retina surgery; (4) history of ocular trauma; and (5) complicated cataract surgery including posterior capsule rupture, vitreous loss, intraoperative zonular instability, and dropped nucleus. The presence of PAS was not used as exclusion criteria for CMG or PACG groups but eyes with significant PAS at the nasal angle were excluded due to inadequate access to the Schlemm canal.

Surgical technique

Patients received standard phacoemulsification using the divide-and-conquer technique through a temporal clear corneal incision. All patients underwent monitored anesthesia care (MAC) during the procedure and were given preservative-free 0.5% Xylocaine in the anterior chamber. The Hydrus microstent was implanted after an acrylic foldable IOL was successfully implanted. The anterior chamber was filled with viscoelastic, and then a paracentesis was made in the left eye at the 1 o’clock position and in the right eye at the 7 o’clock position. Both the patient’s head and microscope were repositioned before the Hill surgical gonioprism (Ocular Instrument Inc, Bellevue, WA, USA) was placed over the cornea to visualize the nasal angle. Once the TM was identified, the Hydrus inserter was directed through the paracentesis site and the Hydrus was implanted in the infero-nasal quadrant of the left eye or super-nasal quadrant of the right eye by sliding the leading edge through the TM into the Schlemm canal. The Lester hook was used to push on the trailing end of the Hydrus edge to ensure two-thirds of the distal end (between the last ‘window’ and the trailing tip) of the Hydrus was covered by the TM. The viscoelastic (Viscoat) (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX) was not removed, in order to act as a tamponade for bleeding from the angle. The wound was closed by irrigation with balanced salt solution except in cases of a floppy iris in which case the wound was secured with a suture (10 − 0 nylon). All patients were given acetazolamide 500 mg sequels BID post-operation for same day use.

Postoperative follow up

Following the surgery, all patients were advised to use a fluorometholone drop 4 times daily for a 5-week period, and adjusted to a lower dose depending on the anterior chamber inflammation and IOP; a 0.3% nepafenac drop once a day for a 5-week period; and a fluoroquinolone antibiotic drop 4 times a day for a 1-week period and then 2 times a day for 1 week. Patients were seen at day 1, week 1 (7–10 days post-operation), week 5–6, and every 3 months consecutively until 3-years post-operation. The glaucoma specialist (N.N.) discontinued, restarted, or adjusted glaucoma medications at each follow up visit as needed.

Data collection and outcomes

The following preoperative data were collected: central corneal thickness (CCT), gonioscopy, cup-to-disc (C/D) ratio, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), Humphrey visual field (HVF) automated perimetry (24 − 2 Swedish interactive threshold algorithm [SITA] standard on Humphrey Field Analyzer-2; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA), IOP measured by Goldmann applanation tonometry, number of glaucoma medications, and average retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) using optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Cirrus OCT, CarlZeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA). The IOP and number of medications were recorded at week 1 and then every 3 months until 36 months. BCVA was recorded at baseline and week 5–6 post-operation. Any prior laser or glaucoma surgery such as selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT) and transscleral cyclophotocoagulation were noted. Any procedural adverse events or complications such as hyphema, infections, or IOP spike (IOP > 35 mmHg) were noted. The outcome measures were: (1) success rate, which was defined as eyes that had IOP ≤ 18 mmHg [12, 13] without glaucoma medications; (2) postoperative IOP, (3) number of glaucoma medications at 36 months, and (4) proportion of medication-free eyes at 36 months.

Statistical analyses

For continuous variables, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon tests were used to compare baseline and follow-up measurements for both groups. The data was summarized using mean and median with standard deviations. Student’s t-test was used to compare between both groups. For categorical variables, chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used. Repeated-measure models were used to analyze longitudinal data while accounting for correlation of repeated measurements. Multilevel mixed-effects linear regression models were used for continuous outcomes, such as IOP, number of medications and visual acuity. Mixed-effects logistic regression models were employed for binary outcomes, such as success rate and the percentage of medication-free patients. Models were designed to include interactions with time to evaluate changes in each outcome that occurred across follow up duration. All analyses were conducted using STATA 16.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX) with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

Of the total eyes (n = 73) included in the study, 34 eyes comprised the OAG group and 39 eyes comprised the CMG/PACG group (33 CMG and 6 PACG). Patient eyes can be further classified as having early (OAG, n = 24; CMG/PACG, n = 29), moderate (OAG, n = 7; CMG/PACG, n = 10), or severe (OAG, n = 3; CMG/PACG, n = 0) glaucoma. The majority of patients self-identified as Chinese or Vietnamese (95%) (Table 1). There were more males in the OAG group (n = 18, 67%) compared to the CMG/PACG group (n = 13, 36%), and thus, more females in the CMG/PACG group (n = 23, 64%) compared to the OAG group (n = 9, 33%) (p = 0.023) (Table 1).

Regarding the IOP outcome, IOP of the entire study population decreased from baseline to 36-months (from 15.34 ± 3.00 mmHg to 13.32 ± 3.22 mmHg, p < 0.001 for change). There was significant reduction in IOP in each of the groups from baseline to 36-months (OAG, 15.03 ± 2.90 mmHg to 13.44 ± 3.85 mmHg, p = 0.02; CMG/PACG, 15.62 ± 3.10 to 13.21 ± 2.61 mmHg, p = 0.002). Repeated measures analysis showed a significant difference in IOP reduction between the OAG and CMG/PACG groups, with a mean difference of -2.06 mmHg (95% CI -3.81 to -0.31, p = 0.021), with greater reduction observed in the CMG/PACG group.

Medication use and visual acuity

For both groups, there was a significant reduction in the number of medications from baseline to 36-months (OAG, baseline 2.44 ± 1.33 to 36-months 1.18 ± 1.40, p < 0.001; CMG/PACG, baseline 2.29 ± 1.27 to 36 months 0.87 ± 1.08, p < 0.001). For both groups, best corrected visual acuity (measured in decimal unit) did not improve or improved slightly (OAG, baseline 0.3 ± 0.1 to 36 months 0.4 ± 0.1, p = 0.06; CMG/PACG, baseline 0.3 ± 0.1 to 36 months 0.3 ± 0.1, p = 0.72), although findings were not significant. The difference between OAG and CMG/PACG groups were not significant for reduction in the number of medications or change in visual acuity (all p > 0.05).

Success rates

After cataract surgery with Hydrus implantation, the CMG/PACG group showed a higher success rate (IOP ≤ 18 mmHg with no medications) at 36-months than the OAG group, but the results were not statistically significant (Table 2). The overall success rate in the CMG/PACG group was 49% compared to 29% in the OAG group (p = 0.09) (Table 2). There were 42% (n = 31) of patients who were medication-free at 36-months (Table 2). Furthermore, there was a greater number of patients who were medication-free in the CMG/PACG group (n = 19, 49%) compared to the OAG group (n = 12, 35%) (p = 0.25) but this was not statistically significant.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we found that both OAG and CMG/PACG groups showed significant reductions in IOP and medication use after combined Hydrus implantation with cataract surgery. Although the reductions in the number of medications and IOP were greater in the CMG/PACG group, this was statistically significant for IOP only. Post-procedure complications were negligible and did not significantly differ between the groups.

The CMG/PACG group showed a greater IOP reduction and number of medication-free patients at 36-months compared to the OAG group although only the IOP decrease was statistically significant (Figs. 1 and 2). There are possible explanations for the improved clinical outcomes seen in angle closure glaucoma. Cataract surgery alone in cases with angle closure glaucoma has been shown to result in substantial IOP reduction [14]. PACG is characterized by the progressive thickening of the lens which causes narrowing of the anterior chamber angle. Several studies have provided evidence of the effectiveness of cataract surgery in treating PACG, as lens extraction helps to widen the narrow angle in part by moving the lens-iris diaphragm posteriorly [14, 15]. Additionally, cataract surgery alleviates pupillary block, a common cause of PACG that is a result of resistance between the lens surface and pupillary margin of the iris [15]. Furthermore, there is evidence that cataract surgery combined with MIGS devices has been more effective in reducing IOP and medication burden than in cataract surgery alone [9]. For instance, Clement et al. found that the iStent inject showed an IOP reduction of 20.8% and medication reduction of 88.9% over 36-month follow up in primary angle closure eyes [16]. Another study of single iStent implantation performed at the time of phacoemulsification reported significant reductions in IOP in both POAG and CMG groups [10].

The Hydrus microstent has been found to be effective in angle closure glaucoma through restoring outflow via the trabecular meshwork and collector channels [17]. The Hydrus effectively provides another route for aqueous humor to bypass resistance at the juxtacanalicular segment of the trabecular meshwork and drain into the Schlemm canal and collector channels [3]. Additionally, removal of the cataract expands the Schlemm canal and thus helps to restore aqueous outflow, leading to IOP reduction [17]. Laroche et al. found that the risk of angle closure was eliminated in a mixed mechanism glaucoma patient, together with other benefits of Hydrus-cataract combined surgery including scaffolding of the Schlemm canal, elimination of lens-iris rubbing, and improvement of aqueous outflow [17].

Previously, studies have supported the efficacy of the Hydrus microstent over short- and medium-term follow up [18–20]. For instance, our prior study found significant reductions in postoperative IOP and medication use for OAG and CMG/PACG groups after one year follow up [20]. However, our study’s findings suggest that the efficacy of cataract-Hydrus combined surgery can be sustained over a longer time period. Furthermore, the CMG/PACG group had higher IOP reduction and number of medication-free eyes from 18 months to 36 months compared to the OAG group (Figs. 1 and 2). As overall support of our long-term findings, the HORIZON randomized trial reported a greater proportion of eyes with IOP of 18 mmHg or less without medications (49.5% vs. 33.8%; p = 0.003) and medication-free eyes (66% vs. 46%; p < 0.001) in the cataract-Hydrus combined surgery group compared to the group with cataract surgery alone over 5-year follow up [21]. These studies encourage further investigation on long-term effects of MIGS devices, as the risk of further incisional surgeries and medication dependence has been found to be lower in patients after Hydrus-cataract combined surgery [21].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to report intermediate-term outcomes of phacoemulsification combined with the Hydrus microstent in glaucoma eyes within the angle-closure spectrum and to compare these outcomes with those in open-angle glaucoma. However, this study is not without limitations. The data was collected from a single clinic and glaucoma specialist, so results may differ from other clinical settings and cannot be generalized to an entire population. Additionally, the study population was largely comprised of Chinese and Vietnamese patients due to the clinic’s demographics, so findings may not apply to other ethnically diverse populations. The retrospective study design can lead to selection bias, or inconsistent reporting of pre-existing data, and thus may not be representative of the population. Due to the small sample size, there may be greater variability in the data and potential bias in the results that limit generalizability to the larger population. Although there was a significant reduction in IOP for both groups when comparing baseline to follow-up, the absence of a control group with patients who underwent cataract surgery alone may limit the determined efficacy and observed changes of the Hydrus microstent in our study. The Hydrus implant only has FDA approval for POAG and OHTN. However, it was used for the CMG/PACG cases due to its demonstrated efficacy for IOP and medication reduction and overall safety. Use of medications and procedures not approved for those indications can raise ethical concerns, but this has become standard practice of care since most glaucoma medications and procedures are approved only for OAG and OHTN. Importantly, angle safety was optimized as the Hydrus was only used in eyes that had open angles in the nasal quadrant for adequate insertion. Additionally, the superior postoperative outcomes in the CMG/PACG group compared to the OAG group may be overestimated without accounting for postoperative gonioscopic findings since angle opening may partially account for the IOP reduction in the CMG/PACG group. Also, issues of potential development of PAS, device obstruction, or malposition are not known, which may affect IOP outcomes as well, although it is a positive sign that IOP was significantly reduced in the CMG/PACG group.

Conclusion

This retrospective study supports the efficacy of Hydrus-cataract combined surgery in eyes with CMG/PACG compared to eyes with OAG. Studies have shown the effectiveness of the Hydrus in OAG, but few studies have followed ocular outcomes beyond 12-months or investigated different types of glaucoma including angle closure glaucoma. Our study found that the CMG/PACG and OAG groups had significant reductions in IOP and medication use after 3-year follow up, suggesting the benefits of the Hydrus implant can be sustained over time for mild to moderate glaucoma. Future studies should assess the efficacy of the Hydrus implant with varying severity of glaucoma over extended follow up with control groups that underwent cataract surgery alone.
