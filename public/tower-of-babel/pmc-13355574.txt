Combined Wide Resection and Bioabsorbable Spacer Placement for High-Dose Postoperative Radiation Therapy in Retroperitoneal Dedifferentiated Liposarcoma: Considerations Regarding Metallic Coil Artifacts
Authors: Yusei Katsuyama, Ryu Terauchi, Norihiro Aibe, Toshiharu Shirai, Yuki Mori, Seiji Shimomura, Ryosuke Nishi, Kenji Takahashi
Published: 2026

Introduction

Dedifferentiated liposarcoma (DDLPS) is a high-grade malignant tumor for which complete surgical resection remains the only curative treatment.1 However, in retroperitoneal DDLPS, achieving negative margins is often difficult because of complex regional anatomy.

Postoperative radiation therapy (PORT) may improve local control, but safe dose escalation is limited by the tolerance of the adjacent bowel and other radiosensitive organs.2,3 Bioabsorbable spacers have recently been introduced to increase the distance between the target volume and surrounding organs during radiation therapy.

Here, we report a case of retroperitoneal DDLPS treated with wide surgical resection and intraoperative placement of a bioabsorbable tissue spacer followed by PORT, highlighting the importance of multidisciplinary treatment planning.

Case Presentation

A 72-year-old Japanese man with a history of malignant melanoma was referred to our institution after an incidental retroperitoneal mass was detected during evaluation for renal dysfunction. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed hydronephrosis of the left kidney and a 11 × 10 × 9 cm left retroperitoneal mass invading the ureter, testicular artery, and psoas major muscle, while abutting the left common iliac artery and kidney (Fig. 1a-c). No metastases were identified. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated slightly low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and heterogeneously high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, with contrast enhancement. The portion adjacent to the psoas major muscle showed poor contrast enhancement, low signal intensity on T1-weighted images, and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images (Fig. 2a-c). Because the tumor was located deep in the pelvis and surrounded by multiple feeding arteries, the risk of intraoperative bleeding was expected to be high. Therefore, preoperative selective arterial embolization was performed 1 day before surgery. At our institution, metallic microcoils are routinely used for preoperative embolization because of their reliable hemostatic efficacy, and nonmetallic embolic materials were not considered at that time. The left testicular, lumbar, and iliolumbar arteries were selectively occluded using a total of 34 metallic microcoils, including C-STOPPER Coils (Piolax Medical Devices), Tornado Embolization Coils (Cook Medical), and Target XL Detachable Coils (Stryker Neurovascular) (Fig. 4).Figure 4Findings immediately after preoperative transarterial embolization. Through a midline laparotomy, wide resection of the tumor was performed (Fig. 5a). The lesion extended near the inferior mesenteric artery, which was divided. The renal artery and vein were transected, and the left kidney was mobilized and removed en bloc with the tumor and psoas major muscle (Fig. 5b). Because the tumor was in close contact with the common, internal, and external iliac arteries, marginal resection was unavoidable in this area. Given the anticipated risk of microscopically positive margins near the iliac vessels, the surgical strategy included planned intraoperative placement of a bioabsorbable tissue spacer (Neskeep, Alfresa Pharma Corporation) composed of polyglycolic acid to facilitate subsequent PORT (Fig. 5c). The spacer was fixed to the adjacent peritoneum and fascia using absorbable sutures. This configuration created an approximately 15 to 20 mm safety distance from the bowel. The total operative time was 657 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 1074 mL; the intraoperative transfusion volume was 560 mL.

Pathology confirmed DDLPS with positive surgical margins. The postoperative course was uneventful. Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered 3 weeks later. Postoperative CT demonstrated that the spacer was located in an ideal position for PORT (Fig. 6a). However, severe metallic artifacts from the embolization coils were present adjacent to the target volume for PORT (Fig. 6b). The location of the metal artifacts interfered with the proton beam angles that would most effectively reduce the gastrointestinal dose, making it difficult to generate a high-quality proton therapy plan without beam paths traversing the metal artifacts. Furthermore, at our institution, treatment planning using Monte Carlo simulation, which enables a more accurate assessment of metal-related effects, was not available. Although postoperative proton therapy had been planned before surgery, multidirectional photon intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) was ultimately chosen because metallic artifacts were considered less likely to cause pronounced dose perturbation and degrade the quality of treatment planning in photon IMRT than in proton therapy.Figure 6Dose distribution achieved by spacer placement. Target delineation for the IMRT plan was performed carefully using postoperative contrast-enhanced CT and treatment-planning CT with single-energy metal artifact reduction, which was applied to reduce metal artifacts and improve anatomical visualization. The IMRT plan was created using Precision version 3.3.1.3 (Accuray Inc), in which the metal artifact regions were replaced with water-equivalent densities. Simultaneous integrated boost irradiation at 3 dose levels (51.2 Gy, 64 Gy, and 70.4 Gy in 32 fractions) was delivered using a D50% prescription approach, in which the prescribed dose covered 50% of the target volume, according to the estimated risk of recurrence as assessed by the radiation oncologist (Fig. 6c).

The spacer placement enabled adequate target dose coverage while reducing the dose to the gastrointestinal tract. For the prescribed planning target volumes of 70.4 Gy, 64 Gy, and 51.2 Gy, the D50% values were maintained at 70.4 Gy, 64.0 Gy, and 56.7 Gy, respectively, whereas the D95% values were 65.3 Gy, 57.1 Gy, and 47.3 Gy, respectively. Meanwhile, the Dmax and D0.1 cm3 of the intraperitoneal space, including the gastrointestinal tract, were limited to 54.3 Gy and 50.1 Gy, respectively. Dx% indicates the dose covering x% of the structure volume. Dmax represents the maximum dose within the structure, and D0.1 cm3 represents the minimum dose delivered to the hottest 0.1 cm³ volume.

Postoperative follow-up included radiological assessment using serial magnetic resonance imaging. Gastrointestinal toxicity was assessed according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. At 24 months after surgery, the patient remained free from local or distant recurrence and no radiation-induced gastrointestinal or urinary complications, including grade ≥2 adverse events.

Discussion

This case demonstrates 2 important clinical messages. First, in cases where microscopically positive surgical margins are anticipated preoperatively, a planned combination of tumor resection and intraoperative placement of a bioabsorbable spacer can be an effective strategy to enable safe PORT. Second, although metallic coil embolization is effective for hemorrhage control, coil-related artifacts can impair PORT planning and restrict radiation therapy options. Earlier multidisciplinary discussion might have avoided this limitation, underscoring the importance of coordination among the surgical, interventional, and radiation oncology teams.

Complete resection is the cornerstone of DDLPS treatment; however, in retroperitoneal disease, achieving R0 margins is often difficult because of anatomical constraints.1 In soft tissue sarcoma, positive margins are a major risk factor for local recurrence,4 highlighting the need for a multidisciplinary, multimodal approach that balances oncologic control with organ preservation.

Radiation therapy improves local control in soft tissue sarcoma. In extremity sarcomas, perioperative radiation therapy reduces local recurrence, whereas in retroperitoneal sarcomas, safe dose escalation is limited by the proximity of the bowel and the risk of gastrointestinal toxicity.2,3 Although postoperative doses of 60 to 66 Gy are recommended,5 delivering such doses safely remains challenging. Proton beam therapy may reduce radiation exposure to organs at risk compared with photon radiation therapy and is therefore considered promising for safe high-dose delivery.6,7

Spacer placement has been used in radiation therapy to reduce radiation exposure to adjacent organs. Historically, nonabsorbable materials such as silicone sheets, expanded polytetrafluoroethylene, and saline-filled tissue expanders have been used to displace radiosensitive structures.8 Although spacer placement has traditionally been performed as a standalone procedure, several reports have described intraoperative spacer placement combined with tumor resection followed by PORT for retroperitoneal sarcomas.

Several reports have described intraoperative spacer placement combined with tumor resection to facilitate postoperative radiation therapy for retroperitoneal sarcomas.9,10 By physically separating the bowel from the high-risk target volume, this strategy may enable safer delivery of high-dose radiation therapy in situations where microscopically positive margins are anticipated. However, these previous reports primarily used nonabsorbable materials, such as tissue expanders, which often require secondary removal surgery and may cause chronic foreign-body complications.8, 9, 10, 11

Recently, various bioabsorbable spacers have been developed to reduce radiation exposure to adjacent normal tissues. Gel-based spacers are usually injected percutaneously and are mainly used in prostate radiation therapy to temporarily separate the rectum from the prostate during treatment.12 In contrast, Neskeep is a surgically placed, nonwoven polyglycolic acid sheet spacer that is hydrolytically degraded and absorbed while maintaining its thickness during the treatment period. This intraoperative sheet-type spacer can create a larger and more stable separation in complex anatomical spaces, and previous reports have mainly described its use in space-making particle therapy for abdominal, pelvic, or bone and soft-tissue tumors adjacent to critical organs.13,14 In the present case, without spacer placement, the target volume and the intraperitoneal space would have been directly abutting each other, and it would likely have been difficult to achieve such a steep dose gradient while delivering a sufficiently high dose to the target and limiting Dmax and D0.1 cm3 of the intraperitoneal space to 54.3 Gy and 50.1 Gy, respectively. Planned concomitant wide resection and bioabsorbable spacer placement made it possible to safely deliver postoperative photon IMRT at 70.4 Gy without spacer-related complications or gastrointestinal toxicity.

This strategy may be particularly applicable to retroperitoneal sarcomas adjacent to the bowel, major vessels, or critical nerves, for which microscopically positive margins are anticipated, and high-dose postoperative radiation therapy may improve local control. It may be especially valuable in high-risk histologies with a strong tendency for local recurrence. However, routine use across all retroperitoneal sarcomas is not justified, and careful patient selection is necessary to balance the dosimetric benefit against the additional operative complexity.

Preoperative transarterial embolization is useful in the management of retroperitoneal sarcomas because it can reduce intraoperative blood loss and facilitate safe resection of hypervascular tumors.15 Metallic coils are widely used owing to their reliable and durable hemostatic effect.16 However, metallic embolic materials can generate substantial CT artifacts, potentially impairing target delineation and dose calculation during PORT.17,18 In the present case, these artifacts were considered more problematic for proton therapy than for photon IMRT, particularly because proton treatment planning relied on a pencil beam algorithm rather than Monte Carlo simulation.19 As a result, proton therapy was abandoned, and IMRT was selected.

Importantly, although proton therapy had been considered preoperatively by the orthopedic oncology and radiation oncology teams, this treatment strategy was not sufficiently shared with the interventional radiology team before embolization. Consequently, metallic coils were selected according to routine practice. Earlier multidisciplinary planning involving orthopedic oncology, radiation oncology, and interventional radiology might have enabled the use of nonmetallic embolic agents and preserved the option of particle therapy. This case therefore highlights the importance of multidisciplinary communication during the initial treatment planning stage for complex retroperitoneal sarcomas.

In summary, concomitant wide tumor resection and placement of a bioabsorbable spacer enabled safe delivery of high-dose PORT in a case of retroperitoneal DDLPS for which R0 resection was anticipated to be difficult. However, metallic coil-induced artifacts limited radiation therapy planning options, underscoring the need for early multidisciplinary coordination to optimize treatment and material selection.
