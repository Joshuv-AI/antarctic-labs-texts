An NLR family member X1 mutation (p.Arg707Cys) suppresses hepatitis B virus infection in hepatocytes and favors the interaction of retinoic acid-inducible gene 1 with mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein
Authors: Qian Jiao, Shu Zhu, Baolin Liao, Huiyuan Liu, Xiaoyan Guo, Lina Wu, Chuming Chen, Liang Peng, Chan Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNLR family member X1 (NLRX1) is an important member of the NOD-like receptor (NLR) family and plays unique roles in immune system regulation. Patients with hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection are more likely to have the NLRX1 mutation p.Arg707Cys than healthy individuals. It has been reported that NLRX1 increases the infection rate of HBV in HepG2 cells expressing sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP). However, the role of NLRX1 mutation (p.Arg707Cys) in hepatitis remains unclear. We constructed Huh7 cells that stably overexpressed NTCP, using LV003 lentivirus. First, wild-type (WT) and mutant (MT) NLRX1 overexpression plasmids were constructed. The MT plasmid contained a point mutation at position 707 of the WT overexpression plasmid. Then, Huh7-NTCP cells were transfected with the WT or MT NLRX1 overexpression plasmid, and subsequent NLRX1 expression was analyzed using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and western blot. HBV RNA levels were determined using RT-qPCR. HBsAg and HBcAg levels were confirmed immunohistochemically. Interferon alpha (IFN-α), interleukin 6 (IL-6), and type I interferon beta (IFN-β) levels were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits. p-p65, p-interferon regulatory factor (IRF) 3, and p-IRF7 expression levels were examined using western blot. The interaction of NLRX1 and retinoic acid-inducible gene (RIG)-1/mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) protein was confirmed by coimmunoprecipitation. The interaction of NLRX1 with IFN-α, IL-6, or IFN-β was analyzed by dual luciferase reporter gene assay. The levels of HBV RNA, HBsAg, and HBcAg in infected cells transfected with the WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1 expression plasmid were higher than those in the untransfected control group; and these levels were lower in the cells transfected with MT NLRX1 than in those transfected with WT NLRX1. The levels of IFN-α, IFN-β, IL-6, p-p65, p-IRF3, and p-IRF7 were lower in cells transfected with WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1 than in control cells. The levels of IFN-β, p-p65, p-IRF3, and p-IRF7 were higher in cells transfected with MT NLRX1 than in those transfected with WT NLRX1. Moreover, NLRX1 competitively inhibited RIG1 binding to MAVS, but the mutation in MT NLRX1 reduced this inhibitory effect. In addition, NLRX1 decreased the promoter activity of IFN-α, IFN-β, and IL-6. Our findings revealed that NLRX1 is a regulatory factor that inhibits the anti-HBV ability of hepatocytes and that the mutation p.Arg707Cys in NLRX1 suppresses HBV infection and activates the IFN/nuclear factor κB pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00705-024-06133-0.Keywords: Hepatitis B virus, Infection, NLR family member X1, Mutation, Type I interferon, Nuclear factor κB

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a hepatotropic virus that establishes acute or chronic infection in humans through immune anergy [1, 2]. Chronic HBV infection can lead to chronic liver inflammation, liver fibrosis, and cirrhosis [3]. It has been shown that 25–40% of HBV carriers progress to decompensated liver disease or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [4]. In addition, integration of the HBV genome into the host genome increases the oncogenic potential of the cell, and this is another pathway that contributes to HCC [5].

Subviral particles negatively modulate intrinsic immune pathways such as the mitogen‑activated protein kinase and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathways and restrain inflammatory cytokines and the transcription of interferon-stimulated genes that are regulated by Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling [6–9]. Retinoic acid-inducible gene (RIG) 1-like receptors (RLRs), which are sensors of cytoplasmic viral dsRNA, play a vital role in controlling RNA virus infection [10, 11]. Upon recognition of a virus, melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA5) and RIG1 can interact with mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) protein, which induces the activation of interferon regulatory factor (IRF) 3, IRF 7, and NF-κB. This process ultimately results in antiviral gene expression, including interferon (IFN) and IFN-stimulated genes and multiple proinflammatory cytokines, including NF-κB, which inhibit viral replication and transmission [12].

NLR family member X1 (NLRX1) is a member of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptor (NLR) family that is localized in mitochondria [13] and is expressed in eukaryotes, including mammals and plants. In humans, NLRX1 is expressed mostly in highly metabolic tissues, such as the heart and muscles [14]. The N-terminus of NLRX1 contains a mitochondrial localization sequence that plays a key role in the transport of NLRX1 from the mitochondrial membrane to the mitochondrial matrix [15]. The central domain of NLRX1 is the NOD [16], which typically binds to molecules of the innate immune signaling pathway. The C-terminal region of NLEX1 is a leucine-rich repeat that binds to viral RNA [17]. NLRX1 has complex functions that can trigger multiple signaling pathways, including immune inflammatory response, mitochondrial damage, apoptosis, and autophagy [14, 16]. In mitochondria, NLRX1 interacts and combines with ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase core protein 2 (UQCRC2), which is a component of the ubiquitin-cytochrome c reductase complex, which functions in the mitochondrial respiratory chain (MRC) [15]. The MRC generates electrochemical signals to induce the production of ATP and reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress and tissue damage [18]. In the presence of TNFα and double-stranded RNA, NLRX1 can induce cellular production of ROS and activate inflammatory pathways [19]. NLRX1 can also act as a negative regulator of the TLR-mediated NF-κB pathway [20]. In resting cells, NLRX1 interacts with tumor necrosis factor receptor-activated factor 6 (TRAF6), but when cells are stimulated by lipopolysaccharide, NLRX1 rapidly detaches from TRAF6 and binds to IκB kinase (IKK), leading to inhibition of IKKα/β phosphorylation and NF-κB activation [21]. A previous study showed that NLRX1 inhibits the production of IFN-I [22], and another study demonstrated that NLRX1 overexpression decreases MAVS-RLR signaling and suppresses the production of cytokines, thereby aggravating HBV infection [23]. Exome sequencing revealed a significant difference in the expression of p.Arg707Cys, a genetic variant of NLRX1, between patients with hepatitis B and normal subjects [24], suggesting that NLRX1 gene mutations can affect an individual’s susceptibility to infection with HBV. In this study, we investigated the role of an NLRX1 mutation in HBV infection and provide a theoretical basis for further exploration of antiviral drugs and application immunotherapy for hepatitis B infection.

Materials and methods

Construction of NLRX1 and mutant plasmids

NLRX1 sequences were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; NM_001282143.1, 2928 nt), and primers were synthesized by Invitrogen (Shanghai, China). The NLRX1 overexpression plasmid was constructed with a His-tag (Orgenbiotech, Guangzhou, China) between the XbaI/NotI sites of the LV003 vector (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). WT and mutant-type (MT) NLRX1 overexpression plasmids were constructed as described previously [24]. The MT overexpression plasmid contained a point mutation leading to an amino acid change at position 707 (p.Arg707Cys) in NLRX1. Primers for molecular cloning and qPCR

Construction of Huh7 cells overexpressing sodium taurocholate transporting polypeptide

The sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP) gene sequence (human CHODL NM_001204174.1, NTCP cDNA) was obtained from NCBI. NTCP cDNA was amplified by polymerase chain reaction and inserted into the LV003 vector. 293T cells (Cellcook, Guangzhou, China) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biological Industries, Israel) at 37°C in an incubator with 5% CO2. Transfection was performed when the cell density reached 80%, using FuGENE 6 Transfection Reagent (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The ratio of LV003-NTCP:pMDLg to pRRE:pRSV-REV:pCMV-VSVG was 10:5:2.5:2.5. The medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium after 4 h. Virus-containing medium was collected and fresh medium was added after another 48 h. Virus-containing medium was again collected after 12 h. The lentivirus was recovered by passing the virus-containing medium through a 0.45-µM low-protein-binding filter. Subsequently, Huh7 cells (5 × 104 cells; Cellcook) were seeded into 24-well plates and infected when the cell density reached 60%. The Huh7 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS. Before infection, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 5% FBS. Then, lentivirus (multiplicity of infection = 10) and polybutadiene (final concentration, 8 µg/mL) were added to the well. After 48 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium supplemented with 2 µg of puromycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat. No. A11138) per mL. Stable cell lines were formed after 10–15 days.

Hepatitis B virus infection

HBV infection was performed as described by Aliabadi et al. [25]. Briefly, 24-well plates were coated with 500 µL (50 µg/mL) of type I collagen for 30 min. Huh7-NTCP cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were then seeded in the plates and grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 24 h. Subsequently, the medium was changed to DMEM containing 4% polyethylene glycol 8000 (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA Cat. No. P2139), 2% dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No: 6164), and 10% HBV serum (3 × 108 copies/well) and incubated for an additional 48 h.

Cell transfection

Huh7-NTCP cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were uniformly seeded in six-well plates to reach a density of approximately 70% the next day. The cells were then transfected with the WT and MT NLRX1 plasmids, using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot assay

Transfected Huh7-NTCP cells were lysed by radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA; cat. no. P0013B; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) with 100 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride. After quantification using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (cat. No. KGB2101; KeyGEN BioTECH), 40 µg of protein was separated by 10% SDS-PAGE (cat. no. E-IR-R306, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). After blocking for 1 h using 5% skimmed milk, the membrane was treated with the primary antibody at 4°C overnight, washed three times for 6 min with Tris-buffered saline wash buffer with 5% Tween-20 (TBST), and treated with a horseradish-peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (cat. no. ab6789 or ab97051; Abcam; 1:2000) for 1 h. The membrane was washed three times for 8 min with TBST, and the bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate kit (Amersham Biosciences Inc., Piscataway, New Jersey, USA). The primary antibodies were as follows: HBcAg (Santa Cruz, #sc-23947; 1:1000); HBsAg (Santa Cruz, #sc-53299; 1:1000); NLRX1 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China, #17215-1-AP; 1:2000); RIG1 (CST, #3743T; 1:2000); MAVS (Abcam, ab31334; 1:2000); p65 (Abcam, ab7970; 1:500); p-p65 (CST, #3033S; 1:1000); IRF7 (CST, #13014; 1:1000); p-IRF7 (CST, #12390; 1:1000); IRF3 (CST, #4302; 1:1000); p-IRF3 (CST, #29047; 1:1000); and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, 1:8000).

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was used to determine the levels of NLRX1 and HBV RNA. After infection with HBV, the Huh7-NTCP cells were collected and total RNA was extracted using TRNzol Universal Reagent (TianGen, China). Total RNA was converted to cDNA by reverse transcription, using a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Otsu, Japan), and cDNA was amplified with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Otsu, Japan). The specific primers are listed in Table 1. The internal control for the relative quantification of each gene was GAPDH.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cell supernatants were collected from each group, and the levels of interferon alpha (IFN-α), interferon beta (IFN-β), and interleukin 6 (IL-6) were determined using ELISA kits (Abcam) as per the manufacturers’ instructions [26].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

WT NLRX1- and MT NLRX1-transfected Huh7-NTCP cells were infected with HBV. HBsAg and HBcAg expression was detected using an IHC assay. The treated cells on the microscope slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Santa Cruz, CA, USA) for 30 min and immersed in a mixture of 30% H2O2 and pure methanol (1:50) for 30 min. After blocking with goat serum (cat. no. 16210, Gibco, Grand Island, USA), HBV antigens were stained with the corresponding primary antibody (HBs, 1:50; HBc 1:50, Cambridge, UK) at 4°C overnight and with goat mouse/rabbit IgG antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 1 h. Instead of the antibody, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco, Grand Island, USA) was used as a negative control. Subsequently, the cells were applied to a streptavidin–biotin complex (Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd. cat. no. SA1054) at 20°C-37°C for 20 min and after washing with PBS (Gibco, Grand Island, USA), stained using a diaminobenzidine (DAB) kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA), followed by hematoxylin, dehydration, hyalinization, and sealing with neutral gum (cat. no. N116470, Aladdin, Shanghai, China). The preparations were then visualized under a light microscope (CCD TP510, Optec, Inc., Lowell, MI, USA).

Coimmunoprecipitation

Coimmunoprecipitation (CO-IP) was used to investigate the effect of the NLRX1 mutation on the binding of MAVS to RIG1 in Huh7-NTCP cells. The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and centrifuged (13,400 × g, 15 min at 4°C). Total protein was harvested and diluted in PBS (Gibco, Grand Island, USA) to a final concentration of 1 µg/µL; 500 µL of total protein was treated with 1 g of rabbit IgG Isotype (Boster, BM4020) or rabbit MAVS (Abcam, ab31334) for 2 h and 100 µL of protein A agarose for 1 h. After centrifugation, the antigen–antibody complexes bound to the agarose beads were harvested, washed three times with 800 µL of RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and boiled twice in 60 µL of SDS for 10 min before performing western blot experiments.

Luciferase reporter gene experiments

Using the predicted IFN-α, IL-6, and IFN-β promoter sequences on the UCSC website (https://genome.ucsc.edu/), IFN-α, IL-6, and IFN-β plasmids were constructed using the psiCHECK-2 vector. Huh7-NTCP cells were placed in 24-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and cotransfected with the plasmid constructs and WT NLRX1 (or MUT) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 48 h. Luciferase activity was measured using a Double-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (TransGen, Beijing, China, #FR201).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times independently. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the results were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad, CA, USA). P < 0.05 was used as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Effect of the NLRX1 mutation on HBV infection in Huh7-NTCP cells

Supplementary Figure S1 shows the sequences of WT NLRX1 and MT NLRX1, which are 2,928 nt in length. The only difference between WT and MT NLRX1 was that the C at position 2,119 nt in WT NLRX1 was changed to T in MT NLRX1 (Supplementary Fig. S1A. The base change in the MT NLRX1 plasmid construct was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Huh7-NTCP cells were transfected with the overexpression plasmids, and RT-qPCR was performed using the NLRX1(WT) or NLRX1(MT) primer pairs, as appropriate. The RT-qPCR results revealed that the level of NLRX1 expression was higher in cells transfected with the WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1 construct than in cells transfected with the vector (Fig. 1A), which confirmed the successful construction of WT NLRX1 and MT NLRX1. Because only one amino acid had changed (arginine to cysteine), commercial antibodies were able to detect the expression of both WT and MT NLRX1. Figure 1B shows the expression of NLRX1 protein in transfected cells, as detected by western blot, which shows higher NLRX1 expression in cells transfected with WT NLRX1 or MT than in those transfected with the vector (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, overexpression of MT NLRX1 increased the level of HBV RNA to a lower extent than overexpression of WT NLRX1 (Fig. 1C). An IHC analysis showed that the levels of HBsAg and HBcAg were higher in cells transfected with WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1 than in those transfected with the vector. The HBsAg and HBcAg levels were lower in cells with MT NLRX1 than in those with WT NLRX1 (Fig. 1D). Taken together, these results imply that the NLRX1 mutation reduces the efficiency of HBV infection of Huh7-NTCP cells.

Activation of the IFN/NF-κB pathway in Huh7-NTCP cells by the NLRX1 mutation

During a viral infection, RIG1 and MDA5 can interact with MAVS to induce activation of the transcription factors IRF3/IRF7 and NF-κB [27]. Using specific ELISA kits, we observed lower levels of IFN-α, IFN-β, and IL-6 in HBV-infected cells overexpressing WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1 than in the untransfected HBV-infected control. The levels of IFN-β were higher in cells with MT NLRX1 than in cells with WT NLRX1 (Fig. 2A). We also found that the levels of the main transcription factors of the IFN/NF-κB pathway (i.e., p-p65/p65, p-IRF3/IRF3, and p-IRF7/IRF7) were lower in HBV-infected cells overexpressing WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1 than in the infected controls and that these levels were higher in infected cells overexpressing MT NLRX1 than in those overexpressing WT NLRX1 (Fig. 2B). These results suggest that the NLRX1 mutation favors activation of the IFN/NF-κB pathway in Huh7-NTCP cells.

Mutation of NLRX1 results in activation of the IFN/NF-κB pathway. (A) Measurement of IFN-α, IL-6, and IFN-β levels by ELISA after overexpression of WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1 in Huh7-NTCP cells. (B) Western blot analysis of p-p65, p-IRF3, and p-IRF7 expression in Huh7-NTCP cells overexpressing WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1. GAPDH was used as a loading control. All experiments were repeated three times independently. **, P < 0.01 vs. NC + HBV; +, P < 0.05 vs. WT NLRX1 + HBV

The NLRX1 mutation promotes MAVS/RIG1 interaction and targets IFN-α, IL-6, and IFN-β

To investigate the possible mechanism of NLRX1 action, immunoprecipitation was used to analyze the interaction between MAVS and WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1 in Huh7-NTCP cells. The results showed that cells transfected with the WT NLRX1 or MT NLRX1 construct had increased levels of NLRX1 compared with the negative control and that the levels of MT NLRX1 were lower than in cells expressing WT NLRX1. Moreover, WT NLRX1 caused a decrease in the level of RIG compared with the control, whereas MT NLRX1 did not significantly affect NLRX1 expression compared with the control. Neither WT NLRX1 nor MT NLRX1 altered MAVS levels compared with the control (Fig. 3A). These results suggested that NLRX1 competitively inhibits RIG1 binding to MAVS and that MT NLRX1 has a reduced inhibitory effect on the binding between RIG1 and MAVS. Moreover, the promoter activity of IFN-α, IL-6, and IFN-β was found to be significantly lower in the NLRX1 WT + HBV and NLRX1 MT + HBV cells than in the NC + HBV cells (Fig. 3B). Luciferase activity from the IL-6 promoter construct was higher in NLRX1 MT + HBV cells than in NLRX1 WT + HBV cells (Fig. 3B). No significant change in the luciferase activity from the IFN-α and IFN-β promoter construct was observed, but a higher level was seen in NLRX1 MT + HBV cells than in NLRX1 WT + HBV cells (Fig. 3B). Thus, the NLRX1 mutation has a relevant effect on the MAVS/RIG1 interaction as well as IFN-α, IL-6, and IFN-β levels.

Discussion

HBV infection can lead to various clinical symptoms [2, 28]. Studies have demonstrated that the human immune status plays a vital role in disease progression, clinical regression, and therapeutic efficacy [29, 30]. To explore the mechanisms of HBV clinical regression, investigating the mechanisms that regulate the intrinsic immune response of hepatocytes to antivirals after HBV infection is another crucial direction.

In recent years, immunotherapy has become a new focus of attention [31]. For example, the immune negative regulators PD-1 and CTLA-4 have become the focus of new treatment approaches for infections and cancers, with the goal of modulating T cell immunity [32]. Immunotherapy has been shown to improve clinical antiviral response rates [33]. Therefore, it is crucial to explore new targets for anti-HBV immunomodulatory therapy. Virus infections usually trigger a rapid and robust intrinsic host immune response in which antiviral molecules are produced to limit viral replication [34]. Among these, IFN-α, and IFN-β are representative cytokines that are capable of mediating an intrinsic host immune response to viral infection [35]. Recent studies on the intrinsic immune responses to viruses have focused on TLRs and RLRs [36, 37], but the NLR protein family also plays a crucial role in inflammasome-mediated inflammatory responses [38]. Several NLR proteins can negatively regulate innate immunity by associating type I interferon responses with NF-κB-induced proinflammatory cytokines [21, 39–41]. NLRX1 is the only member of the family of intracellular NLRs that had been identified to be localized to mitochondria [14]. A previous study showed that overexpression of NLRX1 promoted HBV infection [23], and another study found that the NLRX1 mutation p.Arg707Cys was more frequently found in patients with HBV than in healthy people [24]. However, there have been no studies on the role of NLRX1 p.Arg707Cys in HBV infection. In this study, the Huh7 cell line, in which NLRX1 is expressed at a very low level, was selected to make Huh7-NTCP cells overexpressing WT and MT NLRX1. Both WT NLRX1 and mutant NLRX1 with the p.Arg707Cys mutation promoted increased HBV RNA, HBsAg, and HBcAg expression in HBV-infected Huh7-NTCP cells, suggesting that overexpression of either WT or MT NLRX1 is favorable for HBV infection. In addition, HBV infection was suppressed to a higher degree in cells expressing MT NLRX1 than in those expressing WT NLRX1.

Mutations in NLRX1 have different functions in HBV infection and have been hypothesized to alter the signaling pathways that are activated during HBV infection. It has been shown that NLRX1 interacts with MAVS through its unique nucleotide-binding oligomerization and amino-terminal X domains, sequesters MAVS, and inhibits virus-induced interferon responses that are mediated by the pathogen sensor RIG1 [42]. Similarly, in the present study, we found that NLRX1 overexpression decreased the binding between RIG1 and MAVS, but the mutation in NLRX1 resulted in increased binding between RIG1 and MAVS to activate the IFN/NF-κB pathway. IFN is a well-known antiviral cytokine that can activate the JAK/STAT pathway in an autocrine or paracrine manner, resulting in the expression of hundreds of downstream antiviral host effector proteins that can regulate viral infection in the affected cells and confer resistance to infection in neighboring cells [43]. The NF-κB pathway regulates the expression of inflammatory factors and recruits immune cells to eliminate pathogens and viruses [44, 45]. Therefore, it was speculated that mutations in NLRX1 can affect the RIG1/MAVS and TRAF6-NF-κB pathways.

RIG1 and MDA5, which are ligands of MAVS, are specialized in sensing viral replication intermediates in the cytoplasm [46, 47]. During MAVS activation, RLR signaling is indispensable for antiviral immunity [48]. Upon recognition of a viral infection, RIG1 and MDA5 can interact with MAVS and induce the activation of NF-κB and IRF3/7. In a previous study, NLRX1 was shown to competitively inhibit RIG1 binding to MAVS, thereby inhibiting the activation of MAVS-RLR-mediated downstream signaling pathways such as NF-κB, IRF3, and IRF7 [42]. NLRX1 overexpression promotes HBV infection [23] because it inhibits the binding between RIG1 and MAVS, thereby reducing the activation of the MAVS-RLR-mediated downstream pathways. Similarly, our study confirmed that WT NLRX1 overexpression reduced the expression of IFN-α, IFN-β, IL-6, p-p65/p65, p-IRF3/IRF3, and p-IRF7/IRF7. In addition, compared with WT NLRX1, a mutation in NLRX1 was found to promote MAVS/RIG1 interaction, reduce NLRX1/MAVS interaction, and activate IRF3/IRF7 and NF-κB. These results suggest that the mutation in MT NLRX1 enhances the ability of hepatocytes to resist HBV infection when compared with WT NLRX1.

Our study demonstrated that NLRX1 overexpression weakened the promoter activity of IFN-α, IFN-β, and IL-6 and decreased their expression. Compared with WT NLRX1, MT NLRX1 significantly increased the promoter activity of IL-6 but did not significantly affect the promoter activity of IFN-α and IFN-β, although an increasing trend was noticed. In contrast, MT NLRX1 significantly increased IFN-β expression, while IFN-α and IL-6 expression showed no significant change, although there was an increasing trend. This inconsistency may be due to post-translational modifications; however, further research is required to confirm this.

Because the pathogenesis of hepatitis B is complex and there can be important differences between in vitro and in vivo microenvironments, the roles of NLRX1 mutations still need to be further explored in a mouse model to determine their relevance for treatment of hepatitis B. Moreover, the effect of NLRX1 (p.Arg707Cys) on the ability of hepatocytes to resist HBV infection by regulating the RIG1/MAVS and TRAF6-NF-κB pathways was not clarified in this study and needs to be examined in a large number of rescue experiments in future studies.

Conclusions

NLRX1 can promote HBV infectivity by inhibiting the intrinsic immune response of hepatocytes and increasing the production of the antiviral cytokines IFN-α, IL-6, and IFN-β. In addition, NLRX1 (p.Arg707Cys) might be a novel target of immunotherapy for chronic HBV, with RIG1/MAVS and TRAF6-NF-κB as the possible regulatory pathways. Therefore, this study is of great significance for exploring new targets of anti-HBV immunomodulatory therapy that could potentially improve the clinical antiviral response rate and prognosis of patients.
