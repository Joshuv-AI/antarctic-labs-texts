Exploring the correlation between academic achievement and self-regulated learning on critical thinking in undergraduate medical students
Authors: Muhammad Alif Farhan, Feda Anisah Makkiyah, Yanti Harjono Hadiwiardjo, Agneta Irmarahayu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examines the relationship among medical students’ academic achievement, self-regulated learning, and critical thinking ability.MethodsThis study employed a cross-sectional analytical design involving 120 medical students. The Critical Thinking Tools (CriTT) questionnaire, validated instrument for measuring critical thinking skills, was used. Academic achievement was assessed using Grade Point Average (GPA), and self-regulated learning was evaluated using the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ). Data analysis included Spearman’s rank correlation and multiple linear regression using SPSS version 24 to examine association between variables with statistical rigor.ResultsThis study shows that the respondents demonstrated an average critical thinking score of 198.13 ± 25.14. The average GPA of the respondents was 3.60 ± 0.16. The respondents exhibited an average self-regulated learning score of 184.12 ± 19.68. Bivariate analysis showed significant relationships between critical thinking ability and self-regulated learning (p = 0.000).ConclusionThe findings highlight the significant role of self-regulated learning in fostering critical thinking, whereas academic achievement, as measured by GPA, was not a significant predictor. These insights advocate for pedagogical reforms in medical education to nurture reflective and analytical learning environments.Keywords: Academic achievement, Critical thinking, Self-regulated learning, Medical student

Background

Critical thinking is essential for developing adaptive learners in medical education, enabling students to interpret, analyze, and evaluate information effectively [1, 2]. As medical knowledge expands, students must cultivate strong critical thinking skills to navigate complex clinical decision-making and lifelong learning [3].

Despite its significance, critical thinking remains underdeveloped in medical education, including in Indonesia. A lack of critical thinking skills can hinder academic success, reduce job prospects, and limit engagement in professional practice [4]. Studies show that healthcare professionals trained in traditional medical education models—such as nurses and doctors—often score low to moderate on standardized critical thinking assessments [5].

Studies have demonstrated that Asian students, including those in Indonesia, tend to have lower critical thinking scores than their Western counterparts due to limited evaluation and lack of structured assessment [4]. A study in Central Java, Indonesia, found that only 50% of medical students demonstrated strong disposition toward critical thinking, and just 2.1% scored at a high level based on the California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CCTDI) [3].

The low emphasis on critical thinking in Indonesian medical education can be attributed to (1) Traditional Lecture-Based Teaching: The curriculum is content-heavy and teacher-centered, prioritizing knowledge transmission over student-led inquiry [6]; (2) Memorization-Focused Assessments: Students focus on high-stakes exams, such as UKMPPD and OSCE, which emphasize recall and procedural competency over analytical reasoning [7]; (3) Limited Implementation of Problem-Based Learning (PBL): While PBL and Case-Based Learning (CBL) are used in some faculties, they often become fact-finding exercises rather than true problem-solving discussions [8]; (4) Cultural Emphasis on Authority: Indonesian students may hesitate to question instructors or challenge established knowledge, limiting opportunities for critical discourse [9].

While critical thinking is often linked to academic success, research—including studies in Indonesia—has found no significant correlation between GPA and critical thinking [10, 11].

This discrepancy may stem from: Assessment Bias Toward Memorization: (1) High GPAs in medical education primarily reflect memorization skills, rather than deep analytical reasoning [6]; (2) Hierarchical Learning Structures: A passive learning culture limits opportunities for discussion and reflective inquiry [7]; (3) Professional Certification Priorities: Medical students focus on passing exams (UKMPPD) rather than developing broader intellectual skills, making GPA a weak predictor of critical thinking ability [8].

A study at Universitas Pembangunan Nasional Veteran Jakarta confirmed that students with high GPAs do not necessarily perform well in problem-based tasks that require critical thinking, suggesting that academic performance does not fully capture a student’s ability to think critically [12].

Self-regulated learning (SRL) is a cyclical process where students plan, monitor, and adjust their learning strategies [13]. Zimmerman (2002) outlined three SRL phases: (1) Forethought: Goal-setting and task analysis, (2) Performance: Self-control and strategic learning, (3) Reflection: Self-evaluation and adaptation.

Studies indicate that students with strong SRL skills tend to develop better critical thinking abilities, as they engage in reflective and strategic learning [9]. Research at Freiburg University found that students with higher SRL scores had significantly stronger critical thinking skills, suggesting that self-directed learning enhances analytical reasoning [14].

However, in Indonesia’s medical education system, SRL remains underdeveloped–-students often rely on structured syllabi, passive learning, and teacher-directed instruction [15, 16]. This educational approach may explain why SRL correlates with critical thinking development in our study, whereas academic achievement (GPA) does not directly predict critical thinking ability.

While past research suggests positive correlations between academic achievement, SRL, and critical thinking, their interaction in medical education remains complex [17]. Unlike other academic disciplines, medical education is characterized by high-stakes assessments that shape students’ learning behavirs, intensive clinical exposure that influences the development of cognivite skills, and a rigid curriculum that may limit opportunities for independent inquiry. These factors collectively impact how critical thinking skills are cultivated during medical training.

Previous research has primarily explored SRL and GPA’s impact on critical thinking [18, 19]. However, few studies have examined whether the pressures of medical education—including certification demands and memorization-heavy curricula—may disrupt expected relationships between these factors [20, 21].

This study aims to analyze the interaction between academic achievement, SRL, and critical thinking within Indonesia’s medical education context. Specifically, it investigates (1) whether self-regulated learning is significantly associated with critical thinking ability among medical students, and (2) whether critical thinking ability correlates with academic achievement as measured by Grade Point Average (GPA). It hypothesizes that an overemphasis on GPA and licensing exams may reduce the role of critical thinking in academic success. By exploring these relationships in a sample of medical students at one Indonesian University, this study aims to provide insights that may support future curriculum development.

Methods

This study employed a cross-sectional analytical design to examine the relationship among academic achievement, self-regulated learning, and critical thinking in medical students at the Faculty of Medicine, UPN Veteran Jakarta. A cross-sectional approach allows for the collection and analysis of data from different participants at a single point in time, making it suitable for identifying correlations among variables without inferring causality. This methos is efficient in terms of time and resources, as it does not require follow-up over extended periods [22]. Approval to carry out the study was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the UPN Veteran Jakarta with approval No. 26/I/2023/KEPK.

Sampling and participant techniques

The participants for this study were selected using a proportional stratified random sampling technique. The student population was divided into three strata based on their semester (3rd, 5th, and 7th semesters) and gender. Students in these three semesters were chosen because they represent distinct stages of medical educations: semester 3 students are in the early stages of their studies, semester 5 students are in the intermediate stages, and semester 7 students are nearing the end of their formal academic straining. By including students from different semesters, the study aimed to capture a range of experiences and perspectives regarding self-regulated learning, academic achievement, and critical thinking.

The survey was distributed to all students enrolled in the 3rd, 5th, and 7th semesters of the 2022/2023 academic year. A minimum sample size of 120 was required. This target was determined using a rule-of-thumb for multiple linear regression – specifically, by requiring 10–15 participants per predictor variable – to ensure sufficient power for the planned analyses. With an adjustment for a 10% potential dropout rate, the final target sample size remained at 120 students. The surveys were administered electronically, and responses were collected until the minimum sample size was reached.

A sample of 145 (minimum 120) met the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria for participants were active students enrolled at the Faculty of Medicine UPN Veteran Jakarta in the 2022/2023 academic year. This study excluded students who did not meet criteria such as being inactive students in the current academic year, being in a semester outside the specified range, namely the third, fifth, or third semester of the regulated academic year, being unable to provide a valid cumulative grade point average (GPA) as an indicator of academic performance, and being unwilling to participate or not filling out the questionnaire. This study excluded students who were inactive in the current academic year, were in semesters outside the specified range (third, fifth, or seventh semester), were unable to provide a valid cumulative GPA as an indicator of academic performance, or were unwilling to participate or did not complete the questionnaire. These exclusions ensured that the sample accurately represented students at distinct stages of medical education and allowed for reliable comparisons of self-regulated learning, academic achievement, and critical thinking. Additionally, excluding students without a valid GPA ensured that academic performance data were consistent and reliable across all participants.

Instruments

Data were collected using three standardized instruments. First, the critical thinking ability was assessed using The Critical Thinking Tools (CriTT) questionnaire, which consists of three factors: self-confidence in critical thinking, assessment of critical thinking, and misconceptions. The reliability of this questionnaire has been tested, and factors 1 and 2 have shown high reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.9 for factor 1, Cronbach’s α = 0.79 for factor 2), while factor 3 demonstrates moderate reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.60). Validity testing has also been conducted on this questionnaire, and it has been proven to be valid. The CriTT questionnaire is a tool to assess individual critical thinking skills, which focuses on evaluating how effectively people use reasoning, analysis, evaluation, and problem-solving techniques in everyday life or in specific situations. It offers a structure and standardized way to assess critical thinking skills, making it easier for educators and researchers to measure and compare individual abilities over time or among different groups [23]. These steps together ensured that the CriTT questionnaire was a reliable and valid tool for assessing critical thinking in the population of medical students. The process of testing for reliability and validity contribute to ensuring that the collected data would be robust, consistent, and useful for analysis.

Second, Academic achievement was measured using the GPA. GPA was obtained through questionnaires administered to respondents, who were required to provide evidence in the form of screenshots from the university’s education learning management system (SIAKAD). Academic records were used to measure students’ cumulative grade point average (GPA) as an indicator of academic achievement.

Third, the self-regulated learning within the sample was measured using The Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ), is a widely used self-report assessment tool designed to measure students’ motivation and learning strategies. The MSLQ is often used in educational research to explore the relationship between students’ motivation, their use of different learning strategies, and academic performance [24].

The questionnaire employed a scale comprising intrinsic goal orientation, extrinsic goal orientation, task value, learning belief control, cognition, metacognition, and resource management [25]. Figure 1 shows the research workflow.

Data collection

The instruments were distributed electronically via an online survey platform, ensuring ease of access and convenience or participants. The survey consisted of structured questions, including both closed-ended and Likert-scale items, designed to assess critical thinking ability, self-regulated learning, and academic achievement. Participants were instructed to answer the question based on their personal experiences and current academic performance.

To facilitate understanding and minimize errors, clear instructions were provided at the beginning of the survey. In addition, participants were given a one-week window to complete the surveys, allowing sufficient time for thoughtful responses. To ensure consistency, the survey was administered by the research team, who were available to clarify any questions participants may have had about the process. Once the survey period ended, data were retrieved for analysis, ensuring that all responses were collected within the designated timeframe.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS version 24. Descriptive statistics were first used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and the scores for academic achievement, self-regulated learning, and critical thinking. Spearman’s rank correlation test was performed to assess the strength and direction of associations between these variables, given the normality assumption were not met. Subsequently, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the extent to which self-regulated learning predicted critical thinking, while controlling for academic achievement. Before conducting multiple linear regression, the assumptions of linearity, normality of residuals, multicollinearity, and homoscedasticity were tested and met. Spearman’s correlation was selected due to the non-normal distribution of some variables, as confirmed by the Shapiro-Wilk test.

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that there may be no significant association between GPA and critical thinking abilities. This result align with a study conducted in Iran involving 360 nursing students at Islamic Azad University, which also found no clear correlation between academic achievement and critical thinking skills [6]. One possible explanation could be the traditional evaluation methods in higher education, which often emphasize memorization over the development of analytical and critical thinking skills. Mirzaie and Mirzaie’s revealed a noteworthy correlation between various components of the curriculum and the development of students’ critical thinking disposition, highlighting how educational context may influence students’ willingness or inclination to engage in critical thinking. Specifically, improvements in curriculum content, objectives, teaching methods, and assessment were associated with enhanced critical thinking [10]. These findings suggest that efforts to strengthen teaching and evaluation methods should account for multiple curricular elements. Recognizing the value of cultivating critical thinking, some educators argue that developing such skills should be a core goal of higher education [6].

Higher GPAs can also be influenced by psychological factors, whether they are in college or University students. A person’s academic performance is significantly influenced by psychological elements such as motivation, stress levels, self-esteem, and perceptions. Meanwhile, a review of the study by Meepradit e al. claims that psychological elements have an impact on academic achievement. Since motivation is one of the psychological aspects of humans, it has been discovered that it can serve as a standard for academic performance [26].

Despite the lack of a statistically significant correlation between GPA and critical thinking in this study, this finding remains informative. GPA, often used as a proxy for academic success, may not fully reflect the cognitive skills associated with critical thinking. This suggests a possible disconnect between grading practices that emphasize memorization and the complex reasoning processes involved in critical thinking. Rather than dismissing this null result, it should prompt reflection on how medical education assesses student outcomes. Incorporating more authentic, analytical, and reflective assessments could better align with the objectives of critical thinking development. Future research should also consider contextual and mediating variables, such as learning environments and psychological resilience, that may influence this relationship.

Notably, although both GPA and SRL data were drawn from the same sample, only SRL was significantly associated with critical thinking. This difference may stem from what each variables represents: GPA reflects performance on assessments that often reward memorization, while SRL includes cognitive, metacognitive, and motivational strategies closely tied to analytical thinking. Thus, SRL may serve as a more direct indicator of students; engagement in reflective learning processes that underpin critical thinking.

While GPA remains a standard metric of academic performance, its lack of association with critical thinking highlights the need for assessment approaches that better capture the higher-order cognitive skills essential in clinical reasoning.

Furthermore, SRL, academic achievement, and critical thinking are interconnected and should not be viewed as entirely independent constructs. Critical thinking depends on a strong knowledge base; without sufficient understanding, complex reasoning cannot occur. However, in our study context, GPA may not adequately represent conceptual knowledge due to its emphasis on factual recall and procedural evaluation. This mismatch may partially explain the absence of a significant GPA–critical thinking correlation. Future studies could benefit from using more performance–based measures of academic achievement that align with critical thinking processes.

Our results showed a significant positive relationship between SRL and critical thinking, consistent with previous research on medical students at Freiburg University [9]. Theoretically, SRL supports critical thinking through its metacognitive dimension, including self-evaluation. A lack of metacognitive regulation may limit critical thinking, as it reduces students’ ability to assess and refine their reasoning [10]. SRL also includes cognitive dimensions such as practice, development, and organization, which further support higher–order thinking [27].

Interestingly, although SRL correlated with critical thinking, it did not show a significant relationship with GPA in this study. This may reflect a disconnect between the nature of STL and current academic assessment structures. SRL promotes deep, strategic learning, whereas GPA may primarily reflect outcomes of assessments focused on content retention. This finding echoes prior studies suggesting that academic achievement metrics may not fully capture the benefits of self–regulated learning, especially in educational settings where assessments do not measure metacognitive or analytical skills.

Our findings indicate that academic achievement and SRL collectively accounted for 41.9% of the variance in critical thinking ability. However, other factors–such as ethnicity, health status, and curriculum design–likely contribute to the remaining unexplained variance [7, 28]. Future research should investigate these variables to gain a more comprehensive understanding of critical thinking development in medical students.

Limitation and recommendation

The primary limitation of this study pertains to its limited sample size, which exclusively consists of medical students from Universitas Pembangunan Nasional Veteran Jakarta. Therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing the findings to other institutions or educational contexts. Additionally, several contextual variables that could act as potential confounders were not fully controlled or explored. These include variations in curriculum design, instructional methods, and assessment strategies that may influence the development of critical thinking skills. Differences in the year of study may also reflect disparities in learning experiences, academic demands, or exposure to critical thinking opportunities that were not evenly distributed across semesters. Furthermore, the reliance on GPA as a proxy for academic achievement may not fully capture the depth or breadth of students’ intellectual engagement or learning outcomes. Future research is encouraged to incorporate these contextual factors through a more diverse multi-center sample and consider longitudinal design to better capture developmental trends in critical thinking overtime.

Conclusions

To enhance students’ critical thinking skills, institutions like UPNVJ should integrate authentic learning assessments, promote metacognitive strategies, and implement training modules that foster self-regulated learning. Curriculum reform must prioritize analytical competencies alongside content mastery to better prepare students for complex clinical challenges.
