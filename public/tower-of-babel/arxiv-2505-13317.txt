Unlabeled Data or Pre-trained Model: Rethinking Semi-Supervised Learning and Pretrain-Finetuning
Song-Lin Lv, Rui Zhu, Yu-Feng Li, Lan-Zhe Guo

Abstract
Semi-supervised learning (SSL) alleviates the cost of data labeling process by exploiting unlabeled data, and has achieved promising results on various tasks such as image classification. Meanwhile, the Pretrain-Finetuning paradigm has garnered significant attention in recent years, and exploiting pre-trained models could also reduce the requirement of labeled data in downstream tasks. Therefore, a question naturally occurs: When the labeled data is scarce in the target tasks, should we exploit unlabeled data or pre-trained models? To answer this question, we select pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) as representative pretrain-finetuning instances and propose Few-shot SSL —a framework that enables fair comparison between these two paradigms by controlling the amount of labeled data used. Extensive experiments across various settings demonstrate that pre-trained VLMs generally outperform SSL methods in nearly all cases, except when the data has low resolution or lacks clear semantic structure. Therefore, we encourage future SSL research to compare with pre-trained models and explore deeper integration, such as using pre-trained knowledge to enhance pseudo-labeling. To support future research, we release our unified reproduction and evaluation framework. Codes are available here .



1
Introduction

Deep neural networks have demonstrated performance comparable to that of humans in certain supervised learning tasks, such as image classification
[
13
,
20
,
28
]
. However, these impressive results rely heavily on large amounts of labeled training data. In many real-world applications, obtaining such labeled data often demands substantial human and financial resources
[
54
]
. To reduce the dependence on labeled data and thereby cut costs, researchers have proposed
Semi-Supervised Learning (SSL)
—an effective approach that leverages unlabeled data to enhance model performance.

Recently, the
Pretrain-Finetuning
paradigm has gained significant attention, as models pre-trained on large-scale, readily available web data exhibit a strong ability to rapidly adapt to downstream tasks through fine-tuning. In image classification, for example, the semantic alignment acquired during pre-training enables models—such as CLIP
[
43
]
, a pre-trained vision-language model—to perform zero-shot classification without task-specific supervision. Furthermore, building upon the rich knowledge gained during pre-training, various fine-tuning strategies (e.g., prompt tuning) have been proposed to boost task-specific performance with minimal labeled data
[
24
,
25
,
56
,
57
]
.

Both SSL and pretrain-finetuning paradigms are well-suited for downstream tasks with limited labeled data, leveraging either unlabeled data or pre-trained knowledge, respectively. This makes us come up with a question naturally:

When labeled data is scarce in the target task, should we rely on additional unlabeled data or pre-trained models? What are the respective advantages and disadvantages of each paradigm?

To answer this question, we propose
Few-shot SSL
, an evaluation framework designed to provide a fair comparison between SSL methods and pre-trained models under consistent conditions. This framework ensures a straightforward and intuitive comparison by aligning the number of labeled samples per class used for fine-tuning with the minimum number observed across various SSL settings. We adopt Vision-Language Models (VLMs), such as CLIP and its enhanced variants, as representative pre-trained models due to their strong generalization ability and proven effectiveness in zero- and few-shot learning across diverse domains. Both paradigms are then evaluated across four widely adopted semi-supervised settings within the unified
Few-shot SSL
framework, providing a comprehensive analysis of their respective advantages and limitations.

Experimental results indicate that SSL methods are well-suited for handling low-resolution datasets with limited semantic information. In contrast, pretrain-finetuning paradigm, without relying on unlabeled data, achieves competitive performance across various datasets and open-environment settings. The key takeaways under different settings are as follows:

•

Pre-trained models demonstrate strong generalization capabilities and high data efficiency, enabling them to outperform traditional SSL methods with only a small amount of labeled data. However, achieving optimal performance with pre-trained VLMs requires higher-resolution input images, typically exceeding 96
\times
96.

•

When the unlabeled data contains out-of-distribution classes, pre-trained VLMs can be leveraged to avoid unlabeled training, capitalizing on their extensive pre-trained knowledge and rapid adaptation capabilities. Additionally, pre-trained VLMs offer advantages such as robust result stability and high performance.

•

In the open-world setting, SSL methods perform better on low-resolution datasets, while pre-trained VLMs demonstrate balanced performance on both base and new classes in high-resolution datasets. Combined with our open-set analysis, we conclude that the pretrain-finetuning paradigm is better suited for scenarios where unlabeled data may contain previously unseen classes.

•

The low data requirements of pre-trained models enable them to effectively handle long-tailed distributions, offering a promising approach to addressing the inherent imbalance in many real-world datasets. Meanwhile, SSL methods remain well-suited for tasks characterized by less distinct semantic boundaries between categories, where learning directly from data representations proves advantageous.

In summary, the pretrain-finetune paradigm demonstrates strong performance across most tasks, except in scenarios with limited image resolutions or insufficient semantic information.
To further advance research in related areas, we propose the following directions:
(I) Incorporate comparisons with pre-trained models (e.g., VLMs) in SSL studies focused on image classification task. (II) Enhance the robustness of pre-trained models to low-resolution visual inputs, thereby improving their versatility across diverse real-world scenarios. (III) Explore methods for integrating semi-supervised learning with pre-trained model adaptation to further boost performance under limited supervision.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
, we review the related work on the two paradigms, followed by a detailed explanation of their underlying mechanisms and our proposed
Few-shot SSL
framework in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we present extensive experiments to thoroughly investigate the performance of both paradigms under various settings. In Section
5
, we provide an in-depth discussion on potential future directions, including the integration of these two paradigms and the impact of long-tailed data distributions on pretrain-finetuning approaches. Finally, in Section
6
, we conclude our paper and offer recommendations for future research.

2
Related Work

Semi-Supervised Learning.
The goal of Semi-Supervised Learning is to reduce the cost of data annotation by leveraging unlabeled data, under the assumption that both labeled and unlabeled samples belong to a predefined set of known classes. The primary objective is to assign accurate ground-truth labels to unlabeled instances. Existing SSL methods can be broadly categorized into three main approaches: Entropy Minimization
[
14
,
15
]
, Consistency Regularization
[
30
,
38
,
46
]
, and Hybrid or Holistic Methods
[
4
,
5
]
. Specifically, EntMin
[
14
]
promotes confident (i.e., low-entropy) predictions on unlabeled samples by introducing an additional loss term that minimizes prediction uncertainty, regardless of class. Pseudo-Labeling
[
32
]
assigns pseudo-labels to unlabeled data based on the model’s own predictions throughout training. FixMatch
[
48
]
combines consistency regularization and confidence-based filtering by enforcing consistency between weakly and strongly augmented samples, while only using high-confidence pseudo-labels for training, resulting in significant performance gains with limited labeled data.

Pretrain-Finetuning.
The recent progress in pre-trained models has garnered significant attention, particularly with the emergence of Large Language Models like GPT
[
1
]
and Vision Language Models like CLIP
[
43
]
. These models, pre-trained on vast amounts of data, exhibit remarkable adaptability to downstream tasks, leading to the widespread adoption of the pretrain-finetuning paradigm. In image classification tasks, VLMs
[
2
,
43
,
55
]
are pre-trained on large-scale text-image pairs, leveraging shared embeddings of textual and visual representations to enable zero-shot classification.
While these pre-trained VLMs have captured broad, generalized representations of images and text, efficiently adapting them to downstream tasks remains a significant challenge. Inspired by the success of prompt tuning in NLP
[
21
,
33
]
, many researchers have proposed to adapt VLMs by learning the prompts in end-to-end training. As the pioneering work, CoOp
[
57
]
for the first time introduces learnable prompts to transfer the task-specific knowledge to VLMs. Building on this idea, MaPLe
[
24
]
and PromptSRC
[
25
]
extend prompt tuning to both visual and textual encoders, enabling joint vision-language adaptation.
In
Few-shot SSL
, we select CoOp and PromptSRC as the primary baselines.

Preliminary

Pipelines of semi-supervised learning and pretrain-finetuning.

In this section, we first present an overview of the working principles behind SSL and the pretrain–finetuning paradigms, as illustrated in Figure
1
, along with their respective strategies for adapting to downstream tasks.
We then describe the four semi-supervised settings used in our experiments, both in their original form and as adapted within our
Few-shot SSL

3.1
Semi-Supervised Learning

SSL leverages both a small set of labeled examples
\mathcal{D}_{l}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{l}}
and a larger pool of unlabeled examples
\mathcal{D}_{u}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{u}}
during training stage (see Figure
1
(a)). Let
f_{\theta}
denote the model with parameters
\theta
. SSL jointly minimizes a supervised loss on
\mathcal{D}_{l}
and an unsupervised loss on
\mathcal{D}_{u}
:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{l}}\ell\bigl(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),y_{i}\bigr)\;+\;\lambda\frac{1}{N_{u}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{u}}\mathcal{L}_{u}\bigl(f_{\theta},x_{j}\bigr),

(1)

Here,
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the cross-entropy loss. The first term represents the supervised loss, while the second term captures the unsupervised loss, with
\lambda>0
serving as a balancing factor between them. Although SSL reduces the dependence on labeled data, this advantage often comes with significant computational overhead during training, owing to reliance on large-scale use of unlabeled data.

3.2
Pretrain-Fintuning

Pre-trained models typically involve two stages before downstream evaluation (see Figure
1
(b)). Taking CLIP
[
43
]
as an example, it employs an image encoder
f
and a text encoder
g
to extract feature representations
f(I)
and
g(T)
from an input image
I
and its corresponding text caption
T
, respectively. During pre-training, the model minimizes a contrastive loss over
M
image-text pairs:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{pre-train}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Bigl[-\log\frac{\exp(s_{ii})}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp(s_{ij})}\Bigr]\;+\;\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\Bigl[-\log\frac{\exp(s_{jj})}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\exp(s_{ij})}\Bigr]\,.

(2)

where
s_{ij}
is the similarity score between
f(I_{i})
and
g(T_{j})
, and The first and second terms represent the image-to-text and text-to-image contrastive losses (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{img}\to\mathrm{text}}\ and\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{text}\to\mathrm{img}}
). After pre-training, CLIP exhibits strong zero-shot performance by virtue of its large-scale domain knowledge and cross-modal alignment.

To enable rapid adaptation to downstream tasks, VLMs
[
56
,
57
]
fine-tune a set of learnable prompts
P=[t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{b}]
, while keeping both the image and text encoders frozen. Given class name embeddings
[c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{N}]
, each class representation is formed by concatenating
P
with
c_{i}
, and the prediction is computed as:

p(y=i\mid I)=\frac{\exp\left(\cos\left(f(I),g([P,c_{i}])\right)/\tau\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\left(\cos\left(f(I),g([P,c_{j}])\right)/\tau\right)},

(3)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes concatenation and
\tau
is a temperature parameter. During fine-tuning, only
P
is updated using cross-entropy loss, enabling task-specific adaptation.

3.3
Few-Shot SSL

This section introduces four common semi-supervised settings and explains how they can be reformulated as a unified few-shot setting for pre-trained VLMs, referred to as
Few-shot SSL
. The dataset
\mathcal{D}
used in this setting consists of a small labeled subset
\mathcal{D}_{l}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{l}}
and a larger unlabeled subset
\mathcal{D}_{u}=\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{u}}
, where the number of labeled samples is significantly smaller than the unlabeled samples
(N_{l}\ll N_{u})
. The following provides a overview of each setting, and more details are shown in Appendix
A
.

•

Standard Semi-Supervised Setting
[
48
]
.
In this setting, the labels for both subsets belong to the same known class set
\mathcal{Y}^{l}
. For VLMs, which do not rely on unlabeled data for training, the
Few-shots’ SSL
framework reduces this setting to a standard few-shot scenario, provided the number of labeled samples remains the same.

•

Open-Set Semi-Supervised Setting
[
41
]
.
In this setting, the unlabeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{u}
may include instances from unseen classes, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}^{l}\subset\mathcal{Y}^{u}
with
\mathcal{Y}^{\text{new}}=\mathcal{Y}^{u}\setminus\mathcal{Y}^{l}
. Since VLMs do not utilize unlabeled data, the open-set setting in
Few-shots’ SSL
reduces to the semi-supervised setting.

•

Open-World Semi-Supervised Setting
[
39
]
.
This setting builds upon the open-set setting, aiming to classify both known and new classes. For VLMs, pre-training endows them with zero-shot classification capability, allowing for direct classification of both known and new classes. Therefore, the Open-world semi-supervised setting is analogous to the commonly used Base-to-Novel setting
[
56
]
for VLMs.

•

Long-Tailed Semi-Supervised Setting
[
29
]
.
This setting features a highly imbalanced class distribution, where head classes have many samples and tail classes have few. The imbalance ratio (IR) is defined as the sample count ratio between the largest and smallest classes. Since VLMs use a fixed number of samples per class in few-shot settings, the head and tail classes become consistent due to the same number of samples. Thus, the
Few-shot SSL
framework reduces this setting to a standard few-shot scenario by matching the number of shots to that of the tail classes, ensuring fair comparison.

Datasets and Experimental Setup.

We evaluate both SSL and pre-trained VLMs on CIFAR-10/100
[
27
]
, STL-10
[
9
]
, and ImageNet
[
10
]
, under varying annotation levels and augmentation strategies. For SSL methods (e.g., UDA
[
53
]
, MixMatch
[
5
]
), we report the standard results as presented in FixMatch
[
48
]
. For VLMs, we simulate few-shot learning by training on the same labeled data only, without accessing unlabeled samples. All experiments on VLMs are conducted using the ViT-B/16 backbone and evaluated over three random seeds. Full implementation details are provided in Appendix

.

Experiment Results.

As shown in Table
1
, SSL methods generally perform well on lower-resolution datasets such as CIFAR-10/100 (32
\times
32), surpassing zero-shot CLIP and its fine-tuning variants. This advantage stems from the difficulty pre-trained VLMs face in extracting meaningful features from lower-resolution inputs.
In contrast, pre-trained VLMs excels on higher-resolution datasets like STL-10 (96
\times
96), where even zero-shot CLIP surpasses the best-performing SSL baselines. Moreover, as shown in Table
2(a)
, on the ImageNet dataset, FixMatch requires a significantly larger proportion of labeled data (around 10%) but still lags behind in accuracy. By comparison, VLMs achieve over 73% accuracy using only 2% of labeled samples, with performance improving as more labeled data becomes available.
In addition to accuracy, SSL methods typically involve much longer training times. For instance, training SSL models on CIFAR datasets takes several hours
[
4
,
48
]
, whereas prompt tuning approaches for VLMs
[
24
,
25
,
57
]
can be completed in just a few minutes.
These observations highlight the complementary strengths of SSL and pretrain-finetuning paradigms, with the latter generally offering a higher performance ceiling and faster training speed.

Analysis.
To investigate how input resolution affects pre-trained models’ performance, we evaluate several methods on the STL-10 dataset at varying resolutions (see Table
2(b)
). As resolution increases, all VLMs exhibit substantial accuracy gains, highlighting their sensitivity to image resolution. We also experiment with different visual encoder backbones (presented in Appendix
B
) and find that ViT-based architectures consistently outperform ResNet-50 across all resolutions, indicating that the performance degradation is not due to an intrinsic limitation of Vision Transformer
[
11
]
. In fact, it stems from the fixed input size requirement (e.g., 224
\times
224) of VLMs: low-resolution images must be upsampled via interpolation to satisfy this requirement, a process that inevitably introduces distortions and impairs the models’ ability to extract reliable visual features.

Takeaway 1:
Pre-trained models demonstrate strong generalization capabilities and high data efficiency, enabling them to outperform traditional SSL methods with only a small amount of labeled data. However, achieving optimal performance with pre-trained VLMs requires higher-resolution input images, typically exceeding 96
\times
96.

Datasets and Experimental Setup.
We evaluate both paradigms on standard open-set benchmarks, including CIFAR-10/100
[
27
]
, ImageNet-30
[
22
]
, and ImageNet-100
[
6
]
. For CIFAR-10/100, we use 100 labeled images per class from CIFAR-10 as the supervised data and the entire CIFAR-100 dataset as the unsupervised data. For ImageNet-30 and ImageNet-100, we adopt a 50-shot setting, using 50 labeled samples per class from the selected in-distribution (ID) classes.
It is important to note that the test set in this setting contains only those classes present in the training data. For the pretrain–finetuning paradigm, the experimental setup and VLM performance remain consistent with those described in the semi-supervised setting. Further details can be found in Appendix
A.3
.

Experiment Results.
As shown in Table
3
, zero-shot CLIP and its fine-tuned variants consistently outperform other methods across all datasets, with particularly strong results on ImageNet-30 and ImageNet-100. This advantage stems from their data efficiency—without reliance on noisy unlabeled data, VLMs are less impacted by out-of-distribution (OOD) samples in low-quality datasets.
In contrast, SSL methods are more sensitive to such data quality issues, as they require specialized mechanisms to identify and filter out OOD samples. This reliance on additional OOD handling procedures inevitably affects both training efficiency and final performance, often leading to suboptimal results.
Moreover, unlike SSL paradigms, pretrain–finetuning approaches benefit from extensive large-scale pretraining followed by targeted fine-tuning, resulting in consistently lower performance variance and a higher ceiling that scales with the amount of labeled data.
Based on the above analysis, we conclude that the pretrain–finetuning paradigm is more suitable in scenarios where unlabeled data contains previously unseen classes.

Takeaway 2:
When the unlabeled data contains out-of-distribution classes, pre-trained VLMs can be leveraged to avoid unlabeled training, capitalizing on their extensive pre-trained knowledge and rapid adaptation capabilities. Additionally, pre-trained VLMs offer advantages such as robust result stability and high performance.

As shown in Table

Zero-shot CLIP evaluations reveal that this hierarchical coarsening injects semantic ambiguity, making it challenging for pretrained VLMs to confidently associate a fine-grained concept with a specific image.
Although downstream fine-tuning can partially mitigate this issue, the underlying semantic constraints remain. Detailed analysis and visualization are shown in Appendix

Takeaway 4:
The low data requirements of pre-trained models enable them to effectively handle long-tailed distributions, offering a promising approach to addressing the inherent imbalance in many real-world datasets. Meanwhile, SSL methods remain well-suited for tasks characterized by less distinct semantic boundaries between categories, where learning directly from data representations proves advantageous.

5
Discussion

Potential for combining SSL and pre-trained models.

Integrating SSL with pre-trained models offers new opportunities for semi-supervised classification. While SSL leverages unlabeled data to reduce labeling costs, pre-trained models excel in zero-shot learning and generalization. Their combination can effectively address challenges such as few-shot learning, class imbalance, and other specialized scenarios.
For instance,
PromptKD

[
34
]
introduces a two-stage, prompt-driven knowledge distillation framework that aligns teacher and student models through learnable prompts, achieving effective knowledge transfer using a diverse mix of ID and OOD unlabeled images, resulting in significant improvements across various datasets. Similarly,
UPL

[
23
]
automatically generates high-quality prompts from unlabeled data, reducing the dependence on large-scale labeled datasets and improving overall model fit.
GRIP

[
36
]
represents another promising direction, proposing a pseudo-label-based fine-tuning method that effectively combines SSL principles with VLMs.
Clearly, leveraging pseudo-labeled data, semi-supervised learning concepts, and the generalization strengths of VLMs offers a promising path for enhancing performance across a wide range of downstream tasks, prompting a fresh perspective on hybrid learning strategies.

Effects of training with long-tailed data on pre-trained models.

When training data follows a long-tailed distribution, excessive fine-tuning of pre-trained models can be detrimental, particularly to the performance on tail classes. As highlighted in
[
47
]
, aggressive fine-tuning can disrupt the class-conditional structure learned during pre-training, resulting in poor representation for underrepresented classes.
To address this issue, they proposed
LIFT
, a lightweight fine-tuning approach that modifies only a small portion of the model (e.g., prompts or adapter layers), effectively adapting to long-tailed distributions while preserving the semantic consistency of the pre-trained model. This demonstrates that leveraging pre-trained models can help mitigate the adverse effects of long-tailed distributions.
However, current approaches primarily focus on reducing fine-tuning intensity, often at the cost of head class performance. This highlights the need for strategies that can guide models to fully adapt to imbalanced data while minimizing learning bias.

6
Conclusion and Recommendation

Our experiments provide a comprehensive and fair analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of SSL and pretrain-finetuning paradigms. When should each approach be preferred in real-world applications, and what are their advantages and limitations? Our key insights are as follows:

•

The SSL Paradigm: Best suited for scenarios with low-resolution images or ambiguous class semantics. However, it struggles with noisy, unlabeled data, often requiring more training time and a larger number of training parameters.

•

The Pretrain-Finetuning Paradigm: Ideal for handling imbalanced data distributions and noisy, unlabeled datasets, but tends to underperform on low-resolution images due to its heavy reliance on high-resolution visual inputs and clear semantic information.

Our study also points to promising directions for future research, including but not limited to designing hybrid approaches that effectively combine the strengths of both paradigms:

•

Datasets: Higher-resolution and more challenging datasets should be included to better evaluate the performance of semi-supervised algorithms and pre-trained models, in line with the continued advancement of image resolution.

•

The SSL Paradigm: Future work on semi-supervised learning for image classification should include comparisons with pre-trained models (e.g., VLMs) under matched labeled data conditions.

•

The Pretrain-Finetuning Paradigm: Pre-trained models should be made more robust to low-resolution images to enhance their generalization across a wider range of real-world scenarios.

•

General Directions: Explore methods that integrate semi-supervised learning with pre-trained model adaptation to further improve their performance in labeled data-scarce environments.

One limitation of our study is that we focused solely on evaluating CLIP-based pre-trained models for image classification. In the future, we will conduct more extensive comparisons, hoping that our results and findings will inspire further advancements in SSL and Pretrain-Finetuning paradigms.
