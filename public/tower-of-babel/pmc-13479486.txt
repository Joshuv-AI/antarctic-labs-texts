Understanding language barriers at the registration desk of an after-hours clinic: a qualitative observational study
Authors: Mohamed Irfan, Antoon Cox, Heidi Salaets, Marc Sabbe, Birgitte Schoenmakers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients often seek after-hours medical care due to urgent concerns, convenience or limited access to their regular providers. In Belgium, such care is primarily delivered through general practitioner cooperatives (GPCs), where regional general practitioners collaborate and rotate shifts. Patients may present at GPCs either directly or after calling the national helpline (1733). While doctor–patient communication has been widely studied, interactions between patients and reception staff have received little attention. These initial encounters can strongly shape patients’ comfort and perceptions of care.MethodsThis ethnographic study used a dual-method approach to examine communication during initial interactions between patients and reception staff at an after-hours clinic. The methods included: (1) interactive observations of the registration process at the reception desk, supported by detailed field notes and (2) semistructured interviews with on-duty reception staff, focusing on their perceived ability to assist patients, particularly when facing language barriers or communication challenges.ResultsOver a 4-week period, more than 60 hours of observational data were collected, offering valuable insights into patient flow, the languages spoken in the practice and the frequency of home visits. The observations also highlighted various strategies used to overcome communication challenges, such as involving accompanying persons, relying on non-verbal gestures and using technological aids. In addition, thematic analyses of the interviews conducted with five staff members revealed three key themes: professional experience, communication and self-reflection. Among these, communication emerged as a central concern, with language barrier cases consistently identified as a daily challenge in practice.ConclusionThis study highlights the critical yet often overlooked role of reception staff in ensuring equitable access to healthcare in linguistically diverse settings. The findings underscore the need to equip front-desk personnel with appropriate tools, training and institutional support to enable culturally and linguistically competent service delivery at the first point of contact.

Introduction

After-hours medical care, a critical component of healthcare systems worldwide, is defined as care delivered outside of ‘normal working hours’ —typically on weekdays between 18:00 and 08:00, and throughout weekends and public holidays.1 Patients often seek after-hours care for several reasons, including perceived urgency, convenience and the unavailability of their usual healthcare providers. Understanding the reasons behind patients’ choices to seek after-hours medical care can inform healthcare planning and policy, ensuring that services are appropriately aligned with patient needs and reducing unnecessary emergency department visits.23 The models of care vary widely, from practice-based care in the UK to deputising services by private companies in Australia, and integrated primary care centres with emergency departments in the Netherlands.4 Similarly, in Belgium, legislation obliges medical practitioners to provide and manage out-of-hours (OOH) medical care.5 Through a retrospective observational study based on medical records from OOH primary care services in Belgium, Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, Slovenia, Spain and Switzerland; it was noticed that patients reported often with infectious diseases, such as respiratory and viral infections or with acute pain-related syndromes.6

OOH or after-hours primary care provides medical services beyond the standard daytime schedules of general practitioners (GPs). In Belgium, this care is predominantly delivered through general practitioner cooperatives (GPCs), which are centralised organisations where GPs from a specific region collaborate and rotate mandatory responsibilities for OOH duties.7 These centralised centres rotate GPs—ranging from trainees to experienced practitioners—for mandatory after-hours duties. This system improves accessibility, coordination and supports ongoing improvements in primary care. While patients can walk in during operating hours, they are encouraged to call the national OOH number, 1733, beforehand. As of December 2024, there were 55 after-hours clinics (GP on-call stations) in Flanders.8

On reporting to these facilities, their first interaction is with the reception staff. The initial interaction between patients and reception staff plays a pivotal role in shaping patient satisfaction in medical clinics. Receptionists play a pivotal role within general practice and family practice settings, providing an interface between the practice and the patient.9 Work undertaken by the reception staff includes patient registration, collecting contact information and document language preferences.10 While front desk communicative strategies matter, patients may consider them to be short, too officious and maybe even negative.911 This can be attributed if correct cultural and linguistic competent practices are not employed.10

Communication difficulties in healthcare communication have been shown to result in miscommunication or complete communication breakdown between the involved participants.12 A reason for communication difficulty is rooted in language barriers. Language differences due to language discordance can hinder effective communication between patients and healthcare providers.4 Through research studies, it has been shown that perceived language barriers can lead to negative outcomes, like: (1) inhibit understanding, dosage and use of medications as advised by the physician or the pharmacist13; (2) cause longer duration of hospital stays (both inpatient care and emergency)14; (3) increase the risk of hospital readmission15; (4) result in critical communication inaccuracies and omissions16 and (5) increase the risk of serious medical events.17 Language challenges also lead to misunderstandings and misinterpretations that cannot be understood at the moment of occurrence as the situation may be instantaneous and thus passes quickly. Existing health service research on language barriers and communication challenges has predominantly focused on disparities related to health outcomes and patient–provider interactions.18

However, there is comparatively less understanding of non-provider experiences, such as those involving patient registration systems and interactions with clinic administrative staff (referred to here as ‘front office staff’).10 Indeed, these interactions frequently occur prior to the patient’s actual encounter with healthcare providers, significantly influencing their overall comfort and perceptions of the service experience.19 Patients engage with front office staff to schedule and check in for appointments, might also inquire about insurance coverage, provide necessary information during each visit and be escorted to their appointments with healthcare providers.19

The study was guided by the research question: ‘How do healthcare reception staff interact, how do they perceive their ability to assist patients who speak different languages, and what are the primary challenges they face in this context?’. This study aimed to observe communication practices at a walk-in clinic reception, explore reception staff’s perceptions of their multilingual abilities and identify the key challenges they encounter when assisting linguistically diverse patients.

Methods

Study design

The study employed an ethnographic research design comprising two main components: direct interactive observations and semistructured interviews. Observations were conducted at the reception desk during the patient registration process, with detailed field notes documenting these interactions.2021 These were complemented by semistructured interviews with reception staff to explore their self-perceived ability to assist patients facing language barriers.22

Direct observations

In this study, we observed the initial interactions and first responses exchanged between patients and reception staff at an after-hours clinic. The observations concentrated on the communicative strategies employed by reception staff in three distinct situations: (1) instances in which the interaction displayed indications of language discordance, (2) cases where patients relied on an accompanying individual to mediate communication and (3) encounters in which patients used gestures to supplement their verbal communication.

To carry out these observations, the researcher (MI) was positioned in proximity to the reception area but remained outside the patients’ direct line of sight during the registration process. This placement enabled him to closely monitor the reception staff’s communicative behaviours with patients while minimising any potential influence on the interaction.

Interviews with reception staff

In line with the researcher’s direct observations of interactions between reception staff and patients, semistructured interviews were conducted with the reception staff on duty. These were carried out after consulting the reception staff, informing them about the interview technique and obtaining their informed consent. A semistructured interview guide was used for the interviews (online supplemental annex I). The guide included questions specifically designed to elicit participants’ experiences with patients, including registration processes, requests for medical appointments and care coordination. The interviews also assessed the respondents’ self-perceived ability to manage these tasks. The interview recordings were made in MP4A format using an audio recorder (PHILIPS DVT4110/00—DICTAFOON).

Participants

The participants for the interview were the reception staff who welcomed and interacted with patients at the reception desk while registering them into the system. The shifts of the reception staff were organised such that one staff member worked during the evening from 18:00 until midnight, and another staff member worked from midnight until 08:00 the following day. During weekends (Saturday and Sunday), two reception staff members were present from 08:00 until 18:00, and one staff member was on duty from 18:00 until 08:00 the next day (Saturday and Sunday evenings).

Setting

The study was conducted at the after-hours clinic of Oost Vlaams—Brabant, Khobra. The after-hours Khobra clinic timings are during the weekdays (Monday to Thursday) from 18.00 hours till next day 08.00 hours; while on the weekends, it is open from 18.00 hours on Friday till Monday morning 08.00 hours. The centre operates as an after-hours walk-in clinic where patients can report for a non-surgical medical emergency. While they can walk in during the operational hours without appointments, patients are asked to tele-register by calling the emergency number 1733, which is mandatory during the weekend operational hours. The staff working at the after-hours clinic during its operational hours are the non-medical staff at the reception and the driver for home visits; and the medical staff comprising GPs or training doctors. Medical staff shifts are scheduled on a rotational basis, with all regional GPs—members of the local GP cooperative—sharing on-call duties as part of their clinical responsibilities.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval of the study was received in line for project Managing Language Barriers in Unplanned Care (MaLBUC) by the ethical committee of UZ Leuven, Gasthuisberg (study number S-67908). All the reception staff were informed about the study and the researchers' schedule through emails that were communicated by the researcher and through the study information pamphlet displayed in the meeting room.

Data collection

Observations

The researcher was present at the registration desk of the after-hours clinic to collect data through non-participant observations for 4 weeks from 28 January to 29 February 2024. He was present on weekdays from opening until the end of the first reception shift (18:00–00:00), and on weekends during either the 12:00–18:00 or 18:00–00:00 slot. Observation notes included the number of patients reporting to the clinic, the division between patients who spoke Dutch and those who used another language to speak with the reception staff (non-Dutch speaking) were recorded in a logbook. For the non-Dutch-speaking patients, notes were made on which language was used for communicating with the reception staff. Attention was also paid to any non-verbal body gestures that the reception staff used to communicate with due to the language deficit. To maintain clarity, field notes were labelled according to the serial number of the reporting patient, in accordance with when the researcher was present.

Interviews

Semistructured interviews were conducted by the researcher (MI) with the reception staff present on duty, after receiving their consent (online supplemental annex II). The participants were approached at a moment convenient to them and were provided with the informed consent form in Dutch. They were given sufficient time to read the document carefully, which outlined the study’s aims, objectives, procedures and ethical considerations, including voluntary participation and the right to withdraw. Only after written informed consent had been obtained did the interviews proceed. For the interview, the recorder was placed equidistant from the researcher and the reception staff member to allow clear and precise recording of the interview. Interviews were conducted during periods when reception staff were not engaged with patient registration or payment. If a patient arrived during an ongoing interview, the audio recording was paused and the interview resumed once the staff member was available. The length of the interviews averaged between 5 and 8 mins.

Data analysis

Observations

The analysis of the observed interactions was based on data systematically collected on a daily basis during the periods when the researcher was present in the field. Within the field notes, particular attention was given to identifying specific or noteworthy observations that emerged during the patient registration process. Anything specific or which stood out was marked differently on the logbook, by numbering or labelling the following transcription.

Interviews

Data analysis of the recorded interviews involved three main stages: transcription, verification and thematic coding. Transcription was conducted using the Microsoft Word transcription tool, which generated an initial automated transcript for each audio recording. The researcher (MI) subsequently reviewed each transcript manually while listening to the corresponding audio to ensure accuracy, completeness and data integrity. During this stage, all personally identifying information was replaced with pseudonyms to maintain confidentiality.

The interview data were analysed using an inductive, reflexive thematic analysis approach, supported by NVivo V.1523 as a data-management tool. The analytic process followed the iterative phases described by Braun and Clarke including familiarisation with the data, systematic coding, development of candidate themes and the review and refinement of themes.24

The overall analytic workflow unfolded across eight iterative steps (table 1). MI, a PhD researcher, first collected the observational and interview data, transcribed the interviews and verified any missing information. The verified transcripts were then discussed within the full research team (MI, AC, HS, MS, BS), which included two professors with expertise in communication, languages and linguistics (AC, HS) and two professors with backgrounds in medicine and healthcare (MS, BS). MI conducted the initial round of NVivo coding, after which theme refinement and final coding were carried out collaboratively by MI and BS. MI drafted the results, which were subsequently reviewed and revised by the multidisciplinary team (MI, AC, HS, MS, BS) to ensure analytical consistency, methodological rigour and the integration of both linguistic-communication and clinical perspectives.

Data collection and analysis

In the next stage of analysis, MI conducted line-by-line coding to identify early concepts emerging from participants’ accounts (eg, work experiences, patient experiences, alternative communication strategies, self-reflection). These initial codes were continuously compared across transcripts to examine similarities, variations and patterns within the dataset. Through iterative comparison and refinement, related codes were clustered into broader analytical categories, such as communication experiences, case frequency and professional challenges (MI, BS). As the analysis progressed, these categories were further developed into overarching themes. Communication emerged as the central organising concept, providing the structural foundation for integrating the related subthemes and shaping the study’s conceptual framework.2526

The reporting of the data from the semistructured interviews followed the Consolidated criteria for reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines, developed by Tong, Sainsbury and Craig.27 The 32-item checklist enabled reporting of all elements of the findings from semistructured interviews (online supplemental annex III).

Data saturation

Given the small and defined group of reception staff working at the after-hours clinic, interviews were conducted with staff present during the observation period. Of the total number of six employed staff, interviews were conducted with five reception staff who were on duty during the data collection period. Thematic analysis showed repetition of themes related to professional experience, communication and self-reflection, suggesting that sufficient thematic depth had been reached.

Field notes

The field notes documented several recurring interaction patterns that were evident during real-time observation of reception encounters. Specifically, they showed that reception staff frequently relied on non-verbal strategies to support communication with patients, especially when language barriers arose. The physical environment further shaped these interactions: a transparent plastic barrier at the reception desk restricted clear verbal exchange, prompting the installation of microphones on both sides—one positioned at a small opening for staff, and the other placed to the left side of the patient window.

Within this setup, the field notes highlighted that non-verbal actions played an essential role in managing communication. Staff routinely gestured towards the microphones to indicate where patients should speak and pointed to the card reader to guide the correct placement of identification cards. When ID cards were inserted incorrectly, receptionists combined brief verbal explanations with hand gestures to prompt correction. Subtle nods and body movements were also used to confirm demographic details such as address, phone number and the name of the patient’s regular GP. In situations where patients lacked an ID card, staff directed them—again using pointing gestures—to a displayed list of acceptable alternative documents, such as a passport.

These field-note observations enrich the results by illustrating how staff adapted their communicative practices to compensate for structural constraints and language differences.

Of these, three staff members were on weekday shifts, while the other two staff members were on weekend duty: one during the day shift and the other during the night shift. Four of the interviewees were women. Their work experience at the current position varied from a minimum of 1 year to a maximum of 9 years. Professional experience

Eight staff members were employed at the after-hours clinic reception desk. During the observation period, one was on leave, and another was a backup replacement member. Staff backgrounds provided insights into their prior experience and current roles, summarised in two subthemes: (1) previous employment and (2) current responsibilities.

Previous work profiles

Of the five interviewed staff, three had worked in non-healthcare fields—aviation, administration and marketing—all involving public interaction. Two had prior healthcare experience, one as a lab assistant and the other as a diagnostic clinic receptionist. A shared feature across all roles was direct contact with people.

This is not my first job in direct contact with the people, so I've always done that. I was at the administrative section so would meet people all day. (Respondent 1)

I've worked in Brussels as an accountant. So that’s a new system for me….new new people and of course in the beginning it’s more difficult than as it is now. (Respondent 4)

Current profile at the after-hours clinic

Reception duties included patient registration, handling house call requests, transferring emergency calls to on-call doctors, managing payments and guiding patients to pharmacies. Communication

Since the clinic operates without appointments, receptionists were typically the first point of contact, establishing communication with patients on arrival. This initial point of contact served as the foundation for establishing communication and facilitating further interaction with patients presenting at the clinic.

Communication was a central theme, encompassing all interactions between reception staff and patients—both native and non-native—across in-person and telephone encounters, and involving both verbal and non-verbal forms. The perception of communication effectiveness was influenced by individual experiences at the clinic and was further shaped by their prior professional work experience. Under the umbrella concept of communication, identified subconcepts included (1) experiences with a language barrier and its frequency; (2) replacement languages encountered for communication and (3) alternative techniques utilised to facilitate communication.

Experience of communication and frequency with a language barrier

All interviewees acknowledged having encountered communication with patients where a language barrier was evident. Given that the setting is an after-hours clinic, reception staff reported that non-native-speaking patients presented daily. The frequency of such encounters varied depending on the time and date. Staff working weekend or holiday shifts noted a higher incidence of patients who were non-native speakers or communicated in languages other than French, Dutch or German.

There are more and more people who report as we are the waiting post, allez the clinic is open till late (respondent 1).

In every shift that I have, let’s say 80%. Yeah, yeah, yeah. Sometimes more, I think, yeah (respondent 4).

Non-native languages and their frequency

The total languages that were spoken by the complete pool of reception staff were Dutch, English, French, German, Italian, Portuguese and Spanish. Dutch and French were the primary languages used by nationals and long-term residents to communicate with the staff. A significant finding in this context was the reliance on an alternative language by non-native speakers, with English emerging as the primary medium for facilitating understanding and communication. Other languages were also spoken, though to a lesser extent, including Dutch interspersed with Turkish or Arabic words, Spanish and Ukrainian. The increased presence of Ukrainian-speaking patients was attributed to recent migration trends, resulting from the ongoing conflict between Ukraine and Russia.

It’s relatively easy actually. So when it’s in English or in French or in German, then I can handle it… because, I know these languages and it’s an interaction, but it’s a very it’s a short interaction and only very. (respondent 1).

Yes, there are. All, every, every day there are. There are. There are people who don't speak Dutch, so they speak English or French, but mostly they try to, to speak English. (respondent 4).

Alternatives used for communication

All reception staff described various alternative modalities they employed to facilitate effective communication with patients. These included the use of gestures, active listening, communication through the accompanying person; and also use of translation through translation applications. Gestures were frequently used to indicate the need for an identification or residence card, required for patient registration. Staff commonly conveyed this by forming a card-like shape with their hands or tracing its outline on paper. Alternatively, reception staff would display their own identification cards to help patients understand the requirements. This practice persisted despite the presence of visual prompts on the reception window instructing patients to have their identification card ready.

And then when I show them umm… my ID cards, ehh… but sometimes they have a very little notion of English like ID card is very international. Or I just show them mine and then I'll just show them how they have to give it to me. (respondent 1).

Yes, but sometimes they even don't understand when I ask for an ID. I make the same symbol ID card like this or show my own. (respondent 2).

Nummer and yeah hand gebaren…And so. So you you you use what you what you have in that moment. What is necessary to help the people to. (respondent 3).

The personnel similarly emphasised that attentively and patiently listening to patients was essential. Given that the staff members are seated behind a desk with a window, and the microphone designated for patients is positioned at a considerable distance, patients’ speech was often observed to be incomprehensible when communicated in a non-native language. The different dialects also of the spoken language (native or non-native) would make it necessary for the reception staff to listen intently to accurately understand what the patients were saying.

I speak Dutch from my native language is Dutch, so English, English is not my mother language. So I have also I also have to search for more to for words. (respondent 3).

There are also people who speak English, but they speak English in their own language, like in their Indian English is other other another. Yeah, that it it sounds very different than normal English. English. Yeah. So it’s it’s. Yeah. You have to listen good. (respondent 4).

Reception staff also identified the use of an accompanying person—either an adult or a child—as an alternative communication strategy. This approach was employed in situations where adults were accompanied by young children who communicated on their behalf, or when relatives or friends served as intermediaries to facilitate communication. While effective in the moment, they did express ethical and logistical questions, particularly in terms of accuracy, patient confidentiality and the burden placed on child interpreters.

They bring a friend or a neighbor that they come with someone else. Yeah. Yeah, it happens. They also bring their children to speak for them; which I feel sorry for the child as he should do the talking. (respondent 2).

The end of the semistructured interview was with an open question on any final thoughts or views that the personnel would want to mention. The personnel stated aspects like repetition of words, gathering information in a correct and rightful manner would be beneficial. Privacy was also an aspect that the personnel said they found very important when interacting with patients. Data triangulation

Triangulation of the observational and interview data revealed consistent patterns in communication practices at the reception desk. Observations conducted over approximately 60 hours showed that reception staff frequently adapted their communication strategies during patient registration, particularly in the presence of language barriers, often relying on gestures, visual cues and guidance through the registration process. Similar experiences were described in the interviews, where reception staff reported regularly encountering language barriers and using strategies such as non-verbal communication, translation applications and accompanying persons to facilitate understanding. The convergence of these findings across both data sources highlights the central role of adaptive communication strategies in patient–reception interactions.

Discussion

This ethnographic field work investigated the first interactions at an after-hours walk-in medical clinic between patients reporting there and the reception staff on duty. Simultaneously, semistructured interviews with the on-duty reception staff gave a glimpse into their routine practices that they undertook during patient interactions. The analysis of the views and opinions of the reception staff reflected their self-methods of customisations to form a base for communication. The findings revealed several interconnected themes such as work profiles past and current, the role of communication as a foundational element and the strategies employed to overcome language and infrastructural barriers.

The clinic’s after-hours walk-in nature meant that interactions with patients were often brief and occurred without any prior relationship. The experience of the interaction during the registration process acted as the gatekeeping experience for the patients. The previous professional background of the staff was diverse and only a few had previous healthcare-related experience; but all had substantial exposure to public-facing roles, which appeared to contribute positively to their ability to adapt and function effectively in their current positions. Organisational support through staff training in cultural and linguistic competence is a crucial measure, as such competence begins at the initial point of contact—typically with front desk personnel.20

Communication emerged as a key term that amalgamated all aspects of the staff duties. Whether dealing with local residents or newcomers, in person or over the phone, in Dutch, French, English or other languages, effective communication formed the foundation of a successful interaction. The staff’s perception of communication effectiveness was shaped not only by the immediate patient encounter but also by their cumulative experiences and previous professional experiences. As presented by Hewitt and colleagues in their study, the communicative strategies employed by the receptionists, along with their bureaucratic work, helped build the rapport in oiling the wheels of communication with reporting patients.9

For staff, language barriers were a daily reality, particularly during weekends or holidays when a higher number of patients who did not speak Dutch or French sought care. English emerged as the most commonly used bridge language, despite it not being the native language for many staff or patients. This reliance on English—and in some cases other languages like Spanish, Arabic or Ukrainian—highlighted both the importance of multilingualism and the limitations it imposes when proficiency is inconsistent on both sides of the interaction. The use of English as highlighted by the interview respondents brings back its importance of a communication tool in healthcare, enabling language concordance.12 This language concordance enabled positivity for patients to access care and present their complaints to the healthcare staff, medical and allied.28

Faced with these linguistic challenges, staff demonstrated alternative strategies that extended well beyond basic language use. Non-verbal communication techniques—such as gestures, showing ID cards, drawing shapes or mimicking actions—were employed frequently to overcome misunderstandings. The importance of gestures comes from the understanding that gestures and speech are integrated.2829 But if the speech component of an utterance is not supplying information for gesture interpretation, the gesture speech may become disintegrated messages, thereby implying that contexts are often provided by accompanying verbal utterances.2930

The findings from the final open-ended question of the semistructured interviews highlight several critical themes related to communication practices, privacy and linguistic adaptation among reception staff in a multilingual healthcare setting. One of the most salient points raised by the staff was the importance of clear communication through repetition and confirmation of information. Repeating key information and verifying patients’ understanding was perceived as instrumental in ensuring accuracy and reducing misunderstandings, especially when dealing with non-native speakers. This worked in their pivotal role in service delivery, more so in the first encounter was an aspect overlooked or unrecognised.911

Overall, these insights underline the multifaceted role of reception staff not only as administrative staff but also as front-line communicators and facilitators of care. Their linguistic and behavioural adaptability played a key role in promoting a respectful, efficient and inclusive healthcare experience for a diverse patient population.19

Strengths

This study offers several important strengths. Most notably, it represents the first known investigation in Belgium to explore first response communication experiences between patients and reception staff at an after-hours primary care walk-in clinic, employing both direct observations and staff interviews. The integration of these qualitative methods provides a multifaceted understanding of communication dynamics in this unique healthcare setting. Furthermore, the study achieved a high level of staff engagement, with five of the eight receptionists on duty participating in interviews. This substantial participation rate enhances the credibility and representativeness of the findings. In addition, observational data were collected across various shifts throughout the week, thereby capturing a wide range of patient interactions and clinic workflows. This approach allowed for the inclusion of a diverse patient population and a broad range of communication scenarios, thereby enriching the depth and contextual relevance of the data collected.

Limitations

While this study has several notable strengths, a number of limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted at a single after-hours primary care clinic in Belgium. Although this clinic is the largest after-hours services in Flanders and serves 12 major municipalities, each comprising several subcommunities, the findings may not be fully representative of other after-hours walk-in clinics operating in different regions of Belgium.

Second, because the clinic functions on a walk-in basis, patient flow could not be predicted or controlled in advance. Nevertheless, observations indicated that patient attendance was particularly high during weekends, especially between 09:00 and 18:00. As a result, the data collected may disproportionately reflect interactions occurring during periods of increased patient demand and workload.

Third, although patient flow was documented based on walk-in registrations, weekend observations during the late shifts often commenced when patients were already waiting to be seen. Consequently, some interactions preceding the observation period may not have been captured. Furthermore, registration desk encounters were typically brief and transient in nature. While considerable effort was made to record and document distinctive features of each interaction, the short duration of these encounters may have limited the completeness of the observational data, and some interactional nuances may have been missed. Also, patient–provider communication occurring during the subsequent medical consultation was not included in the present analysis. Accordingly, the current study focused exclusively on communication occurring at the registration desk, and the findings should be interpreted within the context of this specific phase of the patient journey.

Finally, several interviews were subjected to interruptions due to the operational nature of the clinic. These interruptions occurred when new patients arrived for registration, when postconsultation patients required payment processing or pharmacy information, or when staff were called to manage house-call visits.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the vital yet often overlooked role of reception staff in facilitating patient access to healthcare in linguistically diverse settings. As the first to observe front office interactions in a Belgian clinic, it reveals—through observations and semiformal interviews—the need for proper tools, training and institutional support for these frontline workers. Reception staff frequently serve as the first point of contact for patients, shaping their initial experience of care and helping to navigate communication challenges, including language barriers. As healthcare settings become increasingly multilingual, consideration could be given to providing reception staff with communication training, practical resources and organisational support. Such measures may help staff address communication challenges and contribute to a more inclusive and accessible experience for patients.
