The Andersen–Kashaev volume conjecture for FAMED geometric triangulations
Fathi Ben Aribi and Ka Ho Wong

Abstract
. We investigate the Andersen–Kashaev volume conjecture by introducing the notion of FAMED triangulations, a class of ideal triangulations of 3 3 -manifolds satisfying certain specific combinatorial properties. For any FAMED triangulation of a one-cusped hyperbolic 3 3 -manifold M M with trivial second homology, we prove the existence of the Jones function in the Teichmüller TQFT of M M . For FAMED geometric triangulations of M M , we establish an asymptotic expansion of the Jones function in terms of the Neumann–Zagier potential function and the 1-loop invariant of Dimofte–Garoufalidis. As a consequence, we prove the Andersen–Kashaev volume conjecture for M M and provide new insights for the AJ conjecture for the Teichmüller TQFT developed by Andersen–Malusa. We further discover a new phenomenon: for FAMED geometric triangulations, the partition function in Teichmüller TQFT decays exponentially with decrease rate the hyperbolic volume of a cone structure determined by the prescribed angle structure. This perspective provides a potential application to the Casson conjecture on angle structures. Expanding the previous result of Guéritaud, Piguet-Nakazawa and the first author and complementing a parallel result of Guilloux and both authors, we prove all the above generalizations of the Andersen–Kashaev volume conjecture for every hyperbolic twist knot and for the first 42,000 hyperbolic knots in 𝕊 3 \mathbb{S}^{3} .

1.
Introduction

Volume conjectures expect that certain asymptotics of quantum invariants of hyperbolic knots reveal their hyperbolic volume. The ways to prove such conjectures lie at the interface of topology, combinatorics, hyperbolic geometry, quantum algebra and asymptotic analysis.

For a one-cusped hyperbolic
3
-manifold
M
with trivial second homology, endowed with a triangulation
X
with an angle structure
\alpha
, the partition function
\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)
of the Andersen–Kashaev TQFT (or Teichmüller TQFT, see Section
2.7
and
[
AK
]
) is expected in the Andersen–Kashaev volume conjecture to manifest an exponential decrease with rate the hyperbolic volume of
M
when
\hbar\to 0^{+}
.
In this paper, we generalize the Andersen–Kashaev volume conjecture and we prove it for all triangulations of hyperbolic knot complements which satisfy certain combinatorial and geometric properties (namely FAMED geometric triangulations, see Section
1.1
).

In particular, the subtle algebraic simplifications in the partition function of the TQFT and the technical "saddle point" constraints of asymptotic analysis now directly follow from the fact that a geometric triangulation of the knot complement satisfies
combinatorial
properties… which are easy to check! As evidence of this, we mention in Section
1.6
that such triangulations were found in
[
BAGW
]
for more than 42,000 knots via the computer, which provided as many new examples of knots satisfying the Andersen–Kashaev volume conjecture.

We hope parts of this work can be extended to the study of volume conjectures for other quantum invariants, for which the analytic parts of the proofs are notoriously difficult.

1.2.

Consider a one-cusped hyperbolic

Conjecture 1.5

.

(where

(1)

\left|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}+i\frac{\mu_{X}(\alpha)}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}}J_{X}(\hbar,x)e^{\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}}x\lambda_{X}(\alpha)}dx\right|,

(2)

\lim_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}2\pi\hbar\log|J_{X}(\hbar,0)|=-\operatorname{Vol}(\mathscr{N}\backslash K).

(3)

(a)

1.3.
Jones functions and the AJ-conjecture

Let us digress about the AJ-conjecture before going back to asymptotics of the Andersen–Kashaev TQFT in the next section.

Based on Corollary
1.9
, we study the connection between the asymptotic expansion conjecture of the Jones functions, the annihilating polynomial of the Jones function and the classical A-polynomial of knots.
It would be interesting to compare this result
with Andersen–Malusa’s version of the AJ conjecture on the Teichmüller TQFT stated in
[
AM

, which studies a general level
\mathfrak{N}
Jones function (in this paper we restrict to the case
\mathfrak{N}=1

To be precise, let
K
be an hyperbolic knot in
{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
.
We consider the set of functions

\mathscr{F}=\{f\mid f:{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\times{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}\}.

Let
\hat{M},\hat{L}:\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{F}
be two operators defined by

\hat{M}(f(\hbar,\mathrm{x}))=e^{\mathrm{x}}f(\hbar,\mathrm{x}),\quad\hat{L}(f(\hbar,\mathrm{x}))=f(\hbar,\mathrm{x}+4\pi i\hbar).

Let

. Note that
\hat{L}\hat{M}=q\hat{M}\hat{L}
. In particular,
\hat{M},\hat{L}
define a quantum torus

\mathscr{T}=\langle\hat{M},\hat{L}\mid\hat{L}\hat{M}=q\hat{M}\hat{L}\rangle.

Let

be the
PSL(2;{\mathbb{C}})

(see Section

Our second main result (Theorem

is a multiple of the geometric component of the A-polynomial of the knot. A similar connection between the asymptotics of the relative Reshetikhin-Turaev invariants and their annihilators will be discussed in an another preprint by G. Belletti and the second author.

1.5.
Approaching the Casson conjecture via FAMED triangulations

Next, we explore the connection between the exponential decay rate of the invariant and the Casson conjecture (Conjectures
1.18
and
1.19
). Let
M
be a 3-manifold with toroidal boundary whose interior admits a complete hyperbolic metric. Let
X
be an ideal triangulation of the interior of
M
with
\mathscr{A}_{X}\neq\emptyset
. Let
\gamma
be a simple closed curve on the boundary torus
\partial M
and let

\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,\gamma}(\alpha)

be the angular holonomy of
\gamma
at the angle structure
\alpha
.
For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\mathscr{A}^{\gamma}_{X}(\theta):=\{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}\mid\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,\gamma}(\alpha)=\theta\}
.
The space of angle structures can thus be decomposed into the disjoint union

\mathscr{A}_{X}=\bigsqcup_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}\mathscr{A}^{\gamma}_{X}(\theta)=\bigsqcup_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}\{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}\mid\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,\gamma}(\alpha)=\theta\}.

The existence of hyperbolic cone structures with angle
\theta
is closely related to the maximization problem of the volume functional on the subspace
\mathscr{A}_{X}^{\gamma}(\theta)
. More precisely, the volume functional has a maximum point on
\mathscr{A}_{X}^{\gamma}(\theta)
if and only if there exist shape parameters
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z_{\gamma,\theta}}
with positive imaginary parts satisfying
\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\gamma}(\textbf{z})=i\theta
. In this case, we have

\sup\{\operatorname{Vol}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}^{\gamma}(\theta)\}=\max\{\operatorname{Vol}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}^{\gamma}(\theta)\}=\operatorname{Vol}(M;\gamma,\theta),

where
\operatorname{Vol}(M;\gamma,\theta)=\operatorname{Vol}(\mathbf{z_{\gamma,\theta}})
is the volume of
M
with the hyperbolic cone structure with
\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\gamma}(\textbf{z})=i\theta
.

If the volume functional does not attain its maximum, the Casson conjecture (so called by Luo in
[
Luo
]
, see also the last paragraph in
[
FG
, Section 6.4]
) predicts that the volume functional is bounded above by the hyperbolic volume of the manifold.

Conjecture 1.18

(Casson conjecture, Problem 5 in
[
Luo
]
)
.

Let
M
be a 3-manifold with toroidal boundary whose interior admits a complete hyperbolic metric. Let
X
be an ideal triangulation of the interior of
M
. Suppose the space of angle structures
\mathscr{A}_{X}
of
X
is non-empty. Then we have

\sup\{\operatorname{Vol}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}\}\leq\operatorname{Vol}(M).

It is natural to ask the same question on each subspace
\mathscr{A}_{X}^{\gamma}(\theta)
, which lead us to state a "level Casson conjecture":

Conjecture 1.19
.

Let
M
be a 3-manifold with toroidal boundary whose interior admits a complete hyperbolic metric. Let
X
be an ideal triangulation of the interior of
M
.
Let
\gamma
be a simple closed curve as before.
Let
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
\mathscr{A}_{X}^{\gamma}(\theta)\neq\emptyset
. Suppose
M
has a hyperbolic cone structure with
\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\gamma}(\textbf{z})=i\theta
. Then we have

\sup\{\operatorname{Vol}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}^{\gamma}(\theta)\}\leq\operatorname{Vol}(M;\gamma,\theta).

The following corollary to Theorem
1.12
provides the following partial results on Conjecture

1.6.
Computational results about FAMED triangulations

In
[
BAGW
]
, A. Guilloux and the authors checked the FAMED conditions for the
59,937
triangulations of knot complements in the Snappy census HTLinkExteriors. We have the following observations at the time of writing.

(1)

For 42,117 knots of the census, including all knots with at most 12 crossings and all but six knots whose census triangulation has at most 23 tetrahedra, we could find a FAMED geometric triangulation of the knot complement, up to retriangulation if need be. This yields certified proofs of the Andersen–Kashaev volume conjecture for these 42,117 knots.

(2)

We observed that conditions (2) and (3) in Definition
1.1
were always satisfied at the same time, and when they were satisfied, so was condition (4).

(3)

When the triangulation of a knot complement is FAMED, we always observe we have
\operatorname{det}\mathscr{A}=\pm 1
and
\operatorname{det}\mathbf{B}=\pm 2
.

(4)

The FAMED condition is not equivalent to geometricity. There exists a triangulation of a knot complement which is geometric but not FAMED. Similarly, there exists a triangulation of a knot complement which is FAMED but not geometric.

(5)

There exists a knot complement with a FAMED triangulation and a non-FAMED triangulation.

k=0,\ldots,3
) the set of
k
-cells of
X
after identification by
\sim
. In this paper we always assume that
no face is left unpaired by
\sim
, thus
X^{2}
is always of cardinality
2N
. By a slight abuse of notation we also call
T_{j}
the
3
-cell inside the tetrahedron
T_{j}
, so that
X^{3}=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{N}\}
. Elements of
X^{1}
are usually represented by distinct types of arrows, which are drawn on the corresponding preimage edges, see Figure

for an example.

An
ideal triangulation

X
contains ideal tetrahedra, and in this case the quotient space minus its vertices
M_{X}{\smallsetminus}X^{0}
is an open manifold. In this case we will denote
M=M_{X}{\smallsetminus}X^{0}
and say that the open manifold
M
admits the ideal triangulation
X
.

Finally, for
X
a triangulation and
k=0,1,2,3,
we define
x_{k}\colon X^{3}\to X^{2}
the function such that
x_{k}(T)
is the equivalence class of the face of

opposed to its vertex

.

.

2.2.
Angle structures

For a given triangulation
X=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{N},\sim)
we denote
\mathscr{S}_{X}
the set of
shape structures on
X
, defined as

\mathscr{S}_{X}=\left\{\alpha=\left(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{N},c_{N}\right)\in(0,\pi)^{3N}\ \big|\ \forall k\in\{1,\ldots,N\},\ a_{k}+b_{k}+c_{k}=\pi\right\}.

An angle
a_{k}
(respectively
b_{k},c_{k}
) represents the value of a dihedral angle on the edge
\overrightarrow{01}
(respectively
\overrightarrow{02}
,
\overrightarrow{03}
) and its opposite edge in the tetrahedron
T_{k}
. If a particular shape structure
\alpha=(a_{1},\ldots,c_{N})\in\mathscr{S}_{X}
is fixed, we define three associated functions
\alpha_{j}\colon X^{3}\to(0,\pi)
(for
j=1,2,3
) that send
T_{k}
to the
j
-th element of
\{a_{k},b_{k},c_{k}\}
for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Let
(X,\alpha)
be a triangulation with a shape structure as before. We denote
\omega_{X,\alpha}\colon X^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
the associated
weight function
, which sends an edge
e\in X^{1}
to the sum of angles
\alpha_{j}(T_{k})
corresponding to tetrahedral edges that are preimages of
e
by
\sim
.
For example, if we denote

\alpha=(a_{+},b_{+},c_{+},a_{-},b_{-},c_{-})
a shape structure on the triangulation
X
of Figure
1
, then
\omega_{X,\alpha}(\,\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\rightarrow$}})=2a_{+}+c_{+}+2b_{-}+c_{-}.

We finally define
\mathscr{A}_{X}:=\left\{\alpha\in\mathscr{S}_{X}\ \big|\ \forall e\in X^{1},\ \omega_{X,\alpha}(e)=2\pi\right\}

the set of
balanced shape structures on
X
, or
angle structures on
X
.

2.3.
Thurston’s complex gluing equations

To a shape structure
(a,b,c)
on an ordered tetrahedron
T
(i.e. an element of
(0,\pi)^{3}
of coordinate sum
\pi
) we can associate bijectively a
complex shape structure

z\in\mathbb{R}+i\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, as well as two companion complex numbers of positive imaginary part

z^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{1-z}\text{\ and \ }z^{\prime\prime}:=\frac{z-1}{z}.

Each of the
z,z^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime}
is associated to an edge, in a slightly different way according to
\varepsilon(T)
:

—

In all cases,
z
corresponds to the same two edges as the angle
a
.

—

If
\varepsilon(T)=1
, then
z^{\prime}
corresponds to
c
and
z^{\prime\prime}
to
b
.

—

If
\varepsilon(T)=-1
, then
z^{\prime}
corresponds to
b
and
z^{\prime\prime}
to
c
.

Another way of phrasing it is that
z,z^{\prime},z^{\prime\prime}
are always in a counterclockwise order around a vertex, whereas
a,b,c
need to follow the specific vertex ordering of
T
.

In this article we will use the following definition of the complex logarithm:

\operatorname{Log}(z):=\log|z|+i\operatorname{arg}(z)\ \textrm{for}\ z\in\mathbb{C}^{*},

where
\operatorname{arg}(z)\in(-\pi,\pi]
.

We now introduce a third way of describing the shape associated to a tetrahedron
T
, by the complex number

y:=-\operatorname{Log}(z)+i\pi\in\mathbb{R}+i(0,\pi),

which lives in a horizontal strip of the complex plane. In particular, we have

z=-e^{-y},\ \ \operatorname{Log}(z)=-y+i\pi,\ \ \operatorname{Log}(z^{\prime})=-\operatorname{Log}(1+e^{-y})\ \ \text{and}\ \ \operatorname{Log}(z^{\prime\prime})=-\operatorname{Log}(1+e^{y}).

For clarity, we have the diffeomorphism

\psi_{T}\colon\mathbb{R}+i\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}+i(0,\pi),\ z\mapsto-\operatorname{Log}(z)+i\pi,

and its inverse

\psi^{-1}_{T}\colon\mathbb{R}+i(0,\pi)\to\mathbb{R}+i\mathbb{R}_{>0},\ y\mapsto-e^{-y}.

We can now define the
complex weight function

\omega^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\alpha}\colon X^{1}\to\mathbb{C}
associated to a triangulation
X
and an angle structure
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
, which sends an edge
e\in X^{1}
to the sum of logarithms of complex shapes associated to preimages of
e
by
\sim
. For example, for the triangulation
X
of Figure

1
and an angle structure
\alpha=(a_{+},b_{+},c_{+},a_{-},b_{-},c_{-})
, we have:

\displaystyle\omega^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\alpha}(\,\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\rightarrow$}})

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Log}(z_{+})+\operatorname{Log}(z^{\prime}_{+})+2\operatorname{Log}(z^{\prime}_{-})+\operatorname{Log}(z^{\prime\prime}_{-})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\dfrac{\sin(c_{+})^{2}\sin(b_{+})\sin(c_{-})^{2}\sin(a_{-})}{\sin(b_{+})^{2}\sin(a_{+})\sin(a_{-})^{2}\sin(b_{-})}\right)+i\omega_{X,\alpha}(\,\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin={c}]{90.0}{$\rightarrow$}}).

Let
\mathbb{T}
denote one toroidal boundary component of a
3
-manifold
M
ideally triangulated by
X=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{N},\sim)
, and
\sigma
an oriented normal closed curve in
S
.
Truncating the tetrahedra
T_{j}
at each vertex yields a triangulation of
S
by triangles coming from vertices of
X
(called the
cusp triangulation
, see Figure
2
for an example).
If the curve
\sigma
intersects these triangles transversely (without back-tracking), then
\sigma
cuts off corners of each such encountered triangle. Let us then denote
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{l})
the sequence of (abstract) complex shape variables associated to these corners (each such
z_{k}
is of the form
z_{T_{j_{k}}},z^{\prime}_{T_{j_{k}}}
or
z^{\prime\prime}_{T_{j_{k}}}
).
Following
[
FG
]
, we define the

complex (or logarithmic)
holonomy
\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\sigma}(\mathbf{z})
as

\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\sigma}(\mathbf{z}):=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\epsilon_{k}\operatorname{Log}(z_{k}),

where
\epsilon_{k}
is
1
if the
k
-th cut corner lies on the left of
\sigma
and
-1
if it lies on the right. The
angular holonomy

\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,\sigma}(\alpha)
of
\sigma
is similarly defined, replacing the term
\operatorname{Log}(z_{k})
by the (abstract) angle
d_{k}=\operatorname{arg}(z_{k})=\Im(\operatorname{Log}(z_{k}))
(which is of the form
a_{T_{j_{k}}}
,
b_{T_{j_{k}}}
or
c_{T_{j_{k}}}
) lying in the
i
-th corner. The
complex gluing edge equations
associated to
X
consist in asking that the complex holonomies of each closed curve in
\partial M
circling a vertex of the induced boundary triangulation are all equal to
2i\pi
, or in other words that

\forall e\in X^{1},\omega_{X,\alpha}^{\mathbb{C}}(e)=2i\pi.

The
complex completeness equations
require that the complex holonomies of all curves generating the first homology
H_{1}(\partial M)
vanish (when
M
is of toroidal boundary).
Compare with Section
1.1
and Figure
2
.

If
M
is an orientable
3
-manifold with boundary consisting of tori, and ideally triangulated by
X
, then an angle structure
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
corresponds to the complete hyperbolic metric on the interior of
M
(which is unique) if and only if
\alpha
satisfies the complex gluing edge equations and the complex completeness equations.
We say that the triangulation
X
is
geometric
if such a unique
\alpha
exists.

More generally, let
\partial M=\mathbb{T}_{1}\coprod\dots\coprod\mathbb{T}_{k}
and let
\gamma_{j}
be an oriented normal closed curve in
\mathbb{T}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,k
. An angle structure
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
corresponds to the (possibly incomplete) hyperbolic metric on the interior of
M
with
\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\gamma_{j}}(\mathbf{z})=i\theta_{j}
for
j=1,\dots k
if and only if
\alpha
satisfies the complex gluing edge equations and the
(complex) holonomy equations

\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\gamma_{j}}(\mathbf{z})=i\theta_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,k
. It is proved in
[
Ko
]
that if
\theta_{j}<\pi
for all
j
, then such a hyperbolic structure is unique if it exists.

2.4.
The classical dilogarithm

For the dilogarithm function, we will use the definition:

\operatorname{Li_{2}}(z):=-\int_{0}^{z}\operatorname{Log}(1-u)\frac{du}{u}\ \ \ \textrm{for}\ z\in\mathbb{C}{\smallsetminus}[1,\infty)

(see for example
[
Za
]
).
For
z
in the unit disk,
\operatorname{Li_{2}}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{-2}z^{n}
.
We will use the following properties of the dilogarithm function, referring for example to
[
AH
, Appendix A]
for the proofs.

Proposition 2.2

(Some properties of
\operatorname{Li_{2}}
)
.

(1)

(inversion relation)

\forall z\in\mathbb{C}{\smallsetminus}[1,\infty),\ \operatorname{Li_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)=-\operatorname{Li_{2}}(z)-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Log}(-z)^{2}.

(2)

(integral form) For all
y\in\mathbb{R}+i(-\pi,\pi)
,

\frac{-i}{2\pi}\operatorname{Li_{2}}(-e^{y})=\int_{v\in\mathbb{R}+i0^{+}}\dfrac{\exp\left(-i\frac{yv}{\pi}\right)}{4v^{2}\sinh(v)}\,dv.

In the previous formula and in the remainder of the paper,
\mathbb{R}+i0^{+}
denotes a contour in
\mathbb{C}
that is deformed from the horizontal line
\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}
by avoiding
0
via the upper half-plane (with a small half-circle for example).

2.5.
The Bloch–Wigner function

The
Bloch–Wigner function

D:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

D(z):=\Im(\operatorname{Li_{2}}(z))+\operatorname{arg}(1-z)\log|z|\quad\text{ if $z\in\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\mathbb{R}$, and $0$ otherwise}

is continuous on
\mathbb{C}
, and real-analytic on
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0,1\}
(see
[
Za
, Section 3]
for details).
The Bloch-Wigner function plays a central role in hyperbolic geometry.
The following result will be important for us (for a proof, see
[
NZ
]
).

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
T
be an ideal tetrahedron in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with complex shape structure
z
. Then, its volume is given by

\operatorname{Vol}(T)=D(z)=D\left(\frac{z-1}{z}\right)=D\left(\frac{1}{1-z}\right).

2.6.
Faddeev’s quantum dilogarithm

Recall
[
AK
]
that for
\hbar>0
and
\mathsf{b}>0
such that

(\mathsf{b}+\mathsf{b}^{-1})\sqrt{\hbar}=1,

Faddeev’s quantum dilogarithm

\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}
is the holomorphic function on
\mathbb{R}+i\left(\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)
given by

\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(z)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{4}\int_{w\in\mathbb{R}+i0^{+}}\dfrac{e^{-2izw}dw}{\sinh(\mathsf{b}w)\sinh({\mathsf{b}}^{-1}w)w}\right)\ \ \ \ \text{for}\ z\in\mathbb{R}+i\left(\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}}\right),

and extended to a meromorphic function for
z\in\mathbb{C}
via the functional equation

\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(z-i\frac{\mathsf{b}^{\pm 1}}{2}\right)=\left(1+e^{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{\pm 1}z}\right)\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(z+i\frac{\mathsf{b}^{\pm 1}}{2}\right).

Recall that
\mathbb{R}+i0^{+}
denotes a contour in
\mathbb{C}
that is deformed from the horizontal line
\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}
by avoiding
0
by above.

Note that
\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}
depends only on
\hbar=\frac{1}{(\mathsf{b}+\mathsf{b}^{-1})^{2}}
. Furthermore, as a consquence of the functional equation, the poles of
\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}
lie on
i\left[\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}},\infty\right)
and the zeroes lie symmetrically on
i\left(-\infty,\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}}\right]
. We stress the fact that in this paper we always assume that
\mathsf{b}
is a real positive number, which simplifies several formulas in
[
AK
, Appendix A]
; notably the poles and zeroes live in the imaginary line instead of in sectors.

We now list several useful properties of Faddeev’s quantum dilogarithm. We refer to
[
AK
, Appendix A]
for these properties (and several more), and to
[
AH
, Lemma 3]
for an alternate proof of the semi-classical limit property.

Proposition 2.4

(Some properties of
\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}
)
.

(1)

(inversion relation) For any
\mathsf{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and any
z\in\mathbb{R}+i\left(\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)
,

\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(z)\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(-z)=e^{i\frac{\pi}{12}(\mathsf{b}^{2}+\mathsf{b}^{-2})}e^{i\pi z^{2}}.

(2)

(unitarity) For any
\mathsf{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and any
z\in\mathbb{R}+i\left(\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)
,

\overline{\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(z)}=\frac{1}{\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(\overline{z})}.

(3)

(semi-classical limit in
\mathsf{b}
) For any
z\in\mathbb{R}+i\left(-\pi,\pi\right)
,

\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{z}{2\pi\mathsf{b}}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{-i}{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{2}}\operatorname{Li_{2}}(-e^{z})\right)\left(1+O_{\mathsf{b}\to 0^{+}}(\mathsf{b}^{2})\right).

(4)

(behavior at infinity) For any
\mathsf{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(z)\ \ \underset{\operatorname{Re}(z)\to-\infty}{\sim}

\displaystyle\ \ 1,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(z)\ \ \underset{\operatorname{Re}(z)\to\infty}{\sim}

\displaystyle\ \ e^{i\frac{\pi}{12}(\mathsf{b}^{2}+\mathsf{b}^{-2})}e^{i\pi z^{2}}.

In particular, for any
\mathsf{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and any
d\in\left(\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)
,

\displaystyle|\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(x+id)|\ \ \underset{\mathbb{R}\ni x\to-\infty}{\sim}

\displaystyle\ \ 1,

\displaystyle|\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(x+id)|\ \ \underset{\mathbb{R}\ni x\to+\infty}{\sim}

\displaystyle\ \ e^{-2\pi xd}.

Let us expand on Proposition
2.4
(3), via the following
uniform
estimate in the semi-classical limit.

Proposition 2.5
.

There exists constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
such that for all
\delta\in(0,\pi)
, for all
\mathsf{b}\in(0,\sqrt{\delta})
, for all

y\in\mathbb{R}+i[-(\pi-\delta),\pi-\delta]
, we have:

(1)

(uniform semi-classical limit with
\mathsf{b}
)

e^{-\left(\frac{C}{\delta}+C^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{b}^{2}}\leqslant\left|\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{y}{2\pi\mathsf{b}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{i}{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{2}}\operatorname{Li_{2}}(-e^{y})\right)\right|\leqslant e^{\left(\frac{C}{\delta}+C^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{b}^{2}},

(2)

similar inequalities hold if one replaces
y
with
y(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})
:

e^{-\left(\frac{C}{\delta}+C^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{b}^{2}}\leqslant\left|\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{y(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})}{2\pi\mathsf{b}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{i}{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{2}}\operatorname{Li_{2}}(-e^{y(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})})\right)\right|\leqslant e^{\left(\frac{C}{\delta}+C^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{b}^{2}},

(3)

(comparison of classical dilogarithms with
\mathsf{b}
and
\hbar
)

e^{-C_{\delta}}\leqslant\left|\exp\left(\frac{-i}{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{2}}(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})^{2}\operatorname{Li_{2}}(-e^{y})\right)\exp\left(\frac{i}{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{2}}\operatorname{Li_{2}}(-e^{y(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})})\right)\right|\leqslant e^{C_{\delta}},

where
C_{\delta}>0
is a constant independant of
\mathsf{b}
(see
[
BAGPN
, Lemma 7.17]
).

(4)

(uniform semi-classical limit with
\hbar
)

e^{-\left(\frac{C}{\delta}+C^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{b}^{2}-C_{\delta}}\leqslant\left|\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{y}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{i}{2\pi\hbar}\operatorname{Li_{2}}(-e^{y})\right)\right|\leqslant e^{\left(\frac{C}{\delta}+C^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{b}^{2}+C_{\delta}},

Moreover, the constants
C,C^{\prime}
can be computed explicitly (see
[
BAGPN
, Section 7]
).

Proof.

The inequality (1) follows directly from
[
BAGPN
, Section 7]
. Indeed, Lemmas 7.13 and 7.14 in
[
BAGPN
]
deal with
\textstyle\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(\frac{y}{2\pi\mathsf{b}})
, with the assumptions
\Im(y)\in[\delta,\pi-\delta]
and
\Im(y)\in[-\pi+\delta,-\delta]
respectively. It is easy to see that these assumptions, keeping the same conclusions, can be extended to
\Im(y)\in[0,\pi-\delta]
and
\Im(y)\in[-\pi+\delta,0]
.

As for (2), let us prove it follows from (1). Inequality (1) holds in particular for all

y\in\mathbb{R}+i[-\frac{(\pi-\delta)}{1+\mathsf{b}^{2}},\frac{(\pi-\delta)}{1+\mathsf{b}^{2}}]
, and for those
y
we obtain:

e^{-\left(\frac{C}{\pi-\frac{(\pi-\delta)}{1+\mathsf{b}^{2}}}+C^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{b}^{2}}\leqslant\left|\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{y(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})}{2\pi\mathsf{b}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{i}{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{2}}\operatorname{Li_{2}}(-e^{y(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})})\right)\right|\leqslant e^{\left(\frac{C}{\pi-\frac{(\pi-\delta)}{1+\mathsf{b}^{2}}}+C^{\prime}\right)\mathsf{b}^{2}}.

We conclude by remarking that
\pi-\frac{(\pi-\delta)}{1+\mathsf{b}^{2}}>\pi-(\pi-\delta)=\delta
, and thus
(2) follows.

Inequalities of (3) follow almost directly from
[
BAGPN
, Lemma 7.17]
, except that the proof of
[
BAGPN
, Lemma 7.17]
can be immediately generalized to
\Im(y)\in[0,\pi-\delta]
and
\Im(y)\in[-\pi+\delta,0]
(like in (1)). Moreover, the upper bound
c_{\delta}
on
\mathsf{b}
in the proof of
[
BAGPN
, Lemma 7.17]
is smaller than
\sqrt{\delta}
, so we use this bound instead to avoid introducing yet another notation.

Finally, (4) is a direct application of (2) and (3), using that
\hbar^{-1/2}=\mathsf{b}+\mathsf{b}^{-1}
.
∎

To study the asymptotics of partition functions in
\hbar
, we will also need the following result about the semi-classical limit of the quantum dilogarithm function in
\hbar
, going one step further into the Taylor expansion than Proposition
2.5
but losing an uniform bound on the error term over non-compact contours. The result has also been used in
[
AK
, Lemma 3]
by Andersen and Kashaev when they prove their conjecture for
4_{1}
and
5_{2}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

(semi-classical limit in
\hbar
) For any
z\in\mathbb{R}+i\left(-\pi,\pi\right)
,

\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{z}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{i}{2\pi}z\log(1+e^{z})+\frac{i}{\pi}\operatorname{Li_{2}}\left(-e^{z}\right)-\frac{i}{2\pi\hbar}\operatorname{Li_{2}}\left(-e^{z}\right)\right)\left(1+O_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}(\hbar)\right).

Proof.

For any
z\in\mathbb{R}+i\left(-\pi,\pi\right)
, by Proposition
2.4
, we have

\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{z}{2\pi\mathsf{b}}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{-i}{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{2}}\operatorname{Li_{2}}(-e^{z})\right)\left(1+O_{\mathsf{b}\to 0^{+}}(\mathsf{b}^{2})\right).

In particular, since
(\sqrt{\hbar})^{-1}=\mathsf{b}+\mathsf{b}^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{z}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)=\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{z(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})}{2\pi\mathsf{b}}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{-i}{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{2}}\operatorname{Li_{2}}\left(-e^{(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})z}\right)\right)\left(1+O_{\mathsf{b}\to 0^{+}}(\mathsf{b}^{2})\right).

By Taylor’s theorem, we have

\operatorname{Li_{2}}\left(-e^{(1+\mathsf{b}^{2})z}\right)=\operatorname{Li_{2}}\left(-e^{z}\right)-\mathsf{b}^{2}z\log(1+e^{z})+O_{\mathsf{b}\to 0^{+}}(\mathsf{b}^{4}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{z}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)=

\displaystyle\ \exp\left(\frac{i}{2\pi}z\log(1+e^{z})-\frac{i}{2\pi\mathsf{b}^{2}}\operatorname{Li_{2}}\left(-e^{z}\right)\right)\left(1+O_{\mathsf{b}\to 0^{+}}(\mathsf{b}^{2})\right).

Next, since

\frac{1}{\hbar}=\mathsf{b}^{2}+2+\frac{1}{\mathsf{b}^{2}},

we can write

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{z}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)=

\displaystyle\ \exp\left(\frac{i}{2\pi}z\log(1+e^{z})+\frac{i}{\pi}\operatorname{Li_{2}}\left(-e^{z}\right)-\frac{i}{2\pi\hbar}\operatorname{Li_{2}}\left(-e^{z}\right)\right)\left(1+O_{\mathsf{b}\to 0^{+}}(\mathsf{b}^{2})\right).

Finally, since

\lim_{\mathsf{b}\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{b}^{2}}{\hbar}=\lim_{\mathsf{b}\to 0}(\mathsf{b}^{4}+2\mathsf{b}^{2}+1)=1,

we can replace
O_{\mathsf{b}\to 0^{+}}(\mathsf{b}^{2})
by
O_{\hbar\to 0^{+}}(\hbar)
. This completes the proof.
∎

2.7.
The Teichmüller TQFT of Andersen–Kashaev

In this section we follow
[
AK
,
KaWB
,
Kan
]
. Let
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the Schwartz space of smooth
functions from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{C}
that are rapidly decaying (in the sense that any derivative decays faster than any negative power of the norm of the input).
Its continuous dual
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of tempered distributions.

Recall that the
Dirac delta function
is the tempered distribution
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}
denoted by
\delta(x)
or
\delta
and defined by

\delta(x)\cdot f:=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\delta(x)f(x)dx=f(0)
for all
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
(where
x\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the argument of
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
).
Furthermore, we have the equality of tempered distributions

\delta(x)=\int_{w\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-2i\pi xw}\,dw,

in the sense that for all
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
,

\left(\int_{w\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-2i\pi xw}\,dw\right)(f)=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{w\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-2i\pi xw}f(x)\,dw\,dx\ =f(0)=\delta(x)\cdot f.

The second equality follows from applying the Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}
twice and using the fact that
\mathscr{F}(\mathscr{F}(f))(x)=f(-x)
for
f\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}),x\in\mathbb{R}
. Recall also that the definition of the Dirac delta function and the previous argument have multi-dimensional analogues (see for example
[
Kan
]
for details).

Given a triangulation
X
,
writing
X^{k}
for its collection of
k
-cells (
k\in\{0,1,2,3\}
), we assign to the tetrahedra
T_{1},\ldots,T_{N}\in X^{3}
formal real variables
t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}
.
We name
\mathsf{t}\colon T_{j}\mapsto t_{j}
the corresponding bijection, and
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})
the corresponding
formal vector in
\mathbb{R}^{X^{3}}
.

Recall the notation
x_{i}(T)\in X^{2}
for the
i
-th face (
i\in\{0,1,2,3\}
) of the tetrahedron
T\in X^{3}
.

We now define the kinematical kernel of
X
, which is a tempered distribution. In many cases of interest, a distribution-free formula holds (Lemma
2.9
below) and might be used as an alternate definition.
Note that our notation is related to the notation in
[
BAGPN
]
by the change of variables
(t_{1},\dots,t_{N})\to(\epsilon(T_{1})t_{1},\dots,\epsilon(T_{N})t_{N})
, which only induces a sign change and does not change the modulus of the partition function.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
X
be a triangulation such that
H_{2}(M_{X}\smallsetminus X^{0},\mathbb{Z})=0
. The kinematical kernel of
X
is a tempered distribution
\mathscr{K}_{X}\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}\left(\mathbb{R}^{X^{3}}\right)
defined by the integral

\displaystyle\ \mathscr{K}_{X}(\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{X^{2}}}d\mathbf{x}\prod_{T\in X^{3}}e^{2i\pi x_{0}(T)\mathsf{t}(T)}\delta\left(x_{0}(T)-x_{1}(T)+x_{2}(T)\right)\delta\left(x_{2}(T)-x_{3}(T)+\varepsilon(T)\mathsf{t}(T)\right)

where, with a slight abuse of notation,
x_{i}(T)

refers to the
x_{i}(T)
-th component of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{X^{2}}
.
(This convention, of denoting by
x_{i}(T)
both a
2
-cell and the formal variable associated to it, is taken from
[
AK
]
: it will help keep our formulas short.)

Essentially, if
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{X^{2}\cup X^{3}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{X^{3}}
denotes the canonical projection, then

\mathscr{K}_{X}(\mathbf{t})
associates to a Schwartz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{X^{3}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the (normalized) integral,
over the affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{X^{2}\cup X^{3}}
where the arguments of the
\delta
’s vanish,
of the product
(f\circ\pi)\cdot g
, where
g:\mathbb{R}^{X^{2}\cup X^{3}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the exponential of a certain quadratic form.

More formally, one
should understand the integral of the previous formula as the following equality of tempered distributions, similarly as above (
{\!\top}
denoting the transpose):

\mathscr{K}_{X}(\mathbf{t})=\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{X^{2}}}d\mathbf{x}\int_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}}d\mathbf{w}\ e^{2i\pi\mathbf{t}^{\!\top}\mathscr{R}}\mathbf{x}e^{-2i\pi\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathscr{A}}\mathbf{x}e^{-2i\pi\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathscr{B}}\mathbf{t}\ \in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}\left(\mathbb{R}^{X^{3}}\right),

where

\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{N},w^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,w^{\prime}_{N})
is a vector of
2N
new real variables, such that
w_{j},w^{\prime}_{j}
are associated to

\delta\left(x_{0}(T_{j})-x_{1}(T_{j})+x_{2}(T_{j})\right)
and

\delta\left(x_{2}(T_{j})-x_{3}(T_{j})+\mathsf{t}(T_{j})\right)
, and where

\mathscr{R,A,B}
are matrices with integer coefficients depending on the values
x_{k}(T_{j})
, i.e. on the combinatorics of the face gluings. More precisely, the rows (resp. columns) of
\mathscr{R}
are indexed by the vector of tetrahedron variables
\mathbf{t}
(resp. of face variables
\mathbf{x}
) and
\mathscr{R}
has a coefficient
1
at coordinate
(t_{j},x_{0}(T_{j}))
and zero everywhere else;
\mathscr{B}
is indexed by
\mathbf{w}
(rows) and
\mathbf{t}
(columns) and has a
1
at the coordinate
(w^{\prime}_{j},t_{j})
; finally,
\mathscr{A}
is such that
\mathscr{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathscr{B}\mathbf{t}
is a column vector indexed by
\mathbf{w}
containing the values

\left(x_{0}(T_{j})-x_{1}(T_{j})+x_{2}(T_{j})\right)_{1\leq j\leq N}
followed by
\left(x_{2}(T_{j})-x_{3}(T_{j})+\varepsilon(T_{j})t_{j}\right)_{1\leq j\leq N}
.

Remark 2.8
.

The matrices
\mathscr{R,A,B}

can also be defined directly from the boundary maps
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
, as in Section
1.1
: we have
\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{X}_{0}
,
\mathscr{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{X}_{0}-\mathscr{X}_{1}+\mathscr{X}_{2}\\
\mathscr{X}_{2}-\mathscr{X}_{3}\end{pmatrix}
and

\mathscr{B}:=\begin{pmatrix}0_{N}\\
\mathscr{E}\end{pmatrix}
. See also Figure
1
for the values of these matrices for the figure-eight knot complement.

Lemma 2.9
.

[
BAGPN
]

If the
2N\times 2N
matrix
\mathscr{A}
in the previous formula is invertible, then the kinematical kernel is simply a bounded function given by:

\mathscr{K}_{X}(\mathbf{t})=\frac{1}{|\operatorname{det}(\mathscr{A})|}e^{2i\pi\mathbf{t}^{\!\top}Q\mathbf{t}},

where
Q=\frac{-1}{2}\big(\mathscr{X}_{0}\mathscr{A}^{-1}\mathscr{B}+(\mathscr{X}_{0}\mathscr{A}^{-1}\mathscr{B})^{\!\top}\big)
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
BAGPN
, Lemma 2.9]
and the change of variables

(t_{1},\dots,t_{N})\to(\epsilon(T_{1})t_{1},\dots,\epsilon(T_{N})t_{N}).

∎

The product of several Dirac delta functions might not be a tempered distribution in general. However the kinematical kernels in this paper will always be, thanks to the assumption that
H_{2}(M_{X}{\smallsetminus}X^{0},\mathbb{Z})=0
(satisfied by any knot complement). See
[
AK
]
for more details, via the theory of wave fronts. The key property to notice is the linear independence of the terms
x_{0}(T_{j})-x_{1}(T_{j})+x_{2}(T_{j}),\ x_{2}(T_{j})-x_{3}(T_{j})+\varepsilon(T_{j})t_{j}
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
X
be a triangulation. Its dynamical content associated to
\hbar>0
is a function
\mathscr{D}_{\hbar,X}\colon\mathscr{A}_{X}\to\mathscr{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^{X^{3}}\right)
defined on each set of angles
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
by

\mathscr{D}_{\hbar,X}(\mathbf{t},\alpha)=\prod_{T\in X^{3}}\dfrac{\exp\left(\hbar^{-1/2}\alpha_{3}(T)\varepsilon(T)\mathsf{t}(T)\right)}{\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\varepsilon(T)\left(\mathsf{t}(T)-\dfrac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(\pi-\alpha_{1}(T))\right)\right)^{\varepsilon(T)}}.

Note that
\mathscr{D}_{\hbar,X}(\cdot,\alpha)
is in
\mathscr{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^{X^{3}}\right)
thanks to the properties of
\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}
and the positivity of the dihedral angles in
\alpha
(see
[
AK
]
for details).

More precisely, each term in the dynamical content has exponential decrease as described in the following lemma, which immediately follows from Proposition
2.4
(4).

Lemma 2.11
.

[
BAGPN
, Lemma 2.11]

Let
\mathsf{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
a,b,c\in(0,\pi)
such that
a+b+c=\pi
. Then

\left|\dfrac{e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar}}cx}}{\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(x-\frac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(b+c)\right)}\right|\underset{\mathbb{R}\ni x\to\pm\infty}{\sim}\left|e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar}}cx}\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(x+\frac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(b+c)\right)\right|\ \ \left\{\begin{matrix}\underset{\mathbb{R}\ni x\to-\infty}{\sim}e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar}}cx}.\\
\ \\
\underset{\mathbb{R}\ni x\to+\infty}{\sim}e^{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar}}bx}.\end{matrix}\right.

Lemma
2.11
illustrates why we need the three angles
a,b,c
to be in
(0,\pi)
:
b
and
c
must be positive in order to have exponential decrease in both directions, and
a
must be positive as well so that
b+c<\pi
and
\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(x\pm\frac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(b+c)\right)
is always defined.
Alternatively, using the inversion formula of the quantum dilogarithm function (Lemma
2.4
(1)), the dynamical content can be written in the following form.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
X
be a triangulation. Up to a multiplicative constant with norm 1, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\hbar,X}(\mathbf{t},\alpha)=

\displaystyle\ \ \prod_{T\in X^{3}}\exp\left(\hbar^{-1/2}\alpha_{3}(T)\epsilon(T)\mathsf{t}(T)-i\pi\left(\frac{\epsilon(T)+1}{2}\right)\left(\mathsf{t}(T)-\dfrac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(\pi-\alpha_{1}(T))\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \times\prod_{T\in X^{3}}\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(-\mathsf{t}(T)+\dfrac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(\pi-\alpha_{1}(T))\right).

Proof.

The result follows immediately by using Lemma
2.4
(1) and the fact that
|e^{i\frac{\pi}{12}(\mathsf{b}^{2}+\mathsf{b}^{-2})}|=1
for all
\mathsf{b}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
.
∎

Now, for
X
a triangulation such that
H_{2}(M_{X}{\smallsetminus}X_{0},\mathbb{Z})=0
,
\hbar>0
and
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
an angle structure, the associated
partition function of the Teichmüller TQFT
is the complex number:

\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)=\int_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{X^{3}}}\mathscr{K}_{X}(\mathbf{t})\mathscr{D}_{\hbar,X}(\mathbf{t},\alpha)d\mathbf{t}\ \ \ \in\mathbb{C}.

Andersen and Kashaev proved in
[
AK
]
that the modulus
\left|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)\right|\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is invariant under Pachner moves with positive angles, and then studied a generalization of this property to a larger class of moves and triangulations with angles, using analytic continuation in complex-valued
\alpha

[
AKicm
]
.

One natural question is :
for an hyperbolic structure on
M
and any two corresponding angle structures
(X,\alpha),(X^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
, do we have

\left|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)\right|=\left|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\right|
?
This question is still open at the time of writing.
The previously mentioned analytic continuation in complex-valued
\alpha
may be a useful tool for proving a positive answer to this question.

Let
\alpha=(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},\dots,a_{N},b_{N},c_{N})\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
. Define

C(\alpha)=(\varepsilon(T_{1})c_{1},\dots,\varepsilon(T_{N})c_{N})^{\!\top}
and
\mathscr{W}(\alpha)=2Q(\bm{\pi-a})-C(\alpha),
where the first term in
\mathscr{W}(\alpha)
is the product of the matrix
Q\in M_{N}({\mathbb{Q}})
and the vector
\bm{\pi-a}=(\pi-a_{1},\dots,\pi-a_{N})^{\!\top}
.
Following
[
BAGPN
]
, under the assumption that
\operatorname{det}\mathscr{A}\neq 0
, we have the following expression of the partition function.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let

\tilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\hbar,\alpha}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left({\mathbb{R}}+i(\pi-a_{k})\right).

Assume that
\operatorname{det}\mathscr{A}\neq 0
. We have

\displaystyle\ |\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)|=\frac{1}{|\operatorname{det}\mathscr{A}|}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\Big)^{N}

\displaystyle\ \times\left|\int_{\mathscr{Y}_{\alpha}}e^{\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\left(i\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}Q\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}\mathscr{W}(\alpha)-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(T_{k})+1}{4}\right)y_{k}^{2}\right)}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{y_{k}}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)d\mathbf{y}\right|.

Proof.

Denote

\tilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\hbar,\alpha}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left({\mathbb{R}}+\frac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(\pi-a_{k})\right).

By applying the change of variables
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=-\mathbf{t}+\frac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(\bm{\pi-a})
, we have

\displaystyle\ \left|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \left|\int_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{X^{3}}}\mathscr{K}_{X}(\mathbf{t})\mathscr{D}_{\hbar,X}(\mathbf{t},\alpha)d\mathbf{t}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \left|\int_{\mathbf{y}\in\tilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\hbar,\alpha}}\mathscr{K}_{X}\left(-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(\bm{\pi-a})\right)\mathscr{D}_{\hbar,X}\left(-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\bm{\pi-a}\right)d\mathbf{t}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \left|\frac{1}{\operatorname{det}\mathscr{A}}\int_{\mathbf{y}\in\tilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\hbar,\alpha}}e^{2i\pi\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\!\top}Q\tilde{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\hbar}}(\bm{\pi-a})Q\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\frac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}(\bm{\pi-a})Q(\bm{\pi-a})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar}}C(\alpha)^{\!\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{i}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}C(\alpha)^{\!\top}(\bm{\pi-a})}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad e^{-\sum_{k=1}^{N}i\pi\left(\frac{\varepsilon(T_{k})+1}{2}\right)\tilde{y}_{j}^{2}}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(\tilde{y}_{k})d\mathbf{\tilde{y}}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \left|\frac{1}{\operatorname{det}\mathscr{A}}\int_{\mathbf{y}\in\tilde{\mathscr{Y}}_{\hbar,\alpha}}e^{2i\pi\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\!\top}Q\tilde{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar}}\mathscr{W}(\alpha)^{\!\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\sum_{k=1}^{N}i\pi\left(\frac{\varepsilon(T_{k})+1}{2}\right)\tilde{y}_{j}^{2}}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}(\tilde{y}_{k})d\mathbf{\tilde{y}}\right|,

where
\mathscr{W}(\alpha)=2Q^{\!\top}(\bm{\pi-a})-C(\alpha)=2Q(\bm{\pi-a})-C(\alpha)
.
Applying the change of variables
\mathbf{y}=2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
, we have the first result.
∎

2.8.
\mathbf{PSL(2;\mathbb{C})}
A-polynomial

In this section, we follow the setup in
[
P20
]
to define the
PSL(2;\mathbb{C})
A-polynomial. Given a hyperbolic knot
K\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
, let
m
and
l
be a meridian and corresponding preferred longitude respectively. Let
R(\pi_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K)))
be the
PSL(2;\mathbb{C})
representation variety of the fundamental group of
{\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K
. Let
R^{U}(\pi_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K))\subset R(\pi_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K))
be the affine algebraic set consisting of
\rho
with the property that
\rho(m)
and
\rho(l)
are upper triangular, i.e.
\rho(m)
and
\rho(l)
are of the form

\rho(m)=\pm\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{w_{m}^{loc}}{2}}&*\\
0&e^{-\frac{w_{m}^{loc}}{2}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho(l)=\pm\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{w_{l}^{loc}}{2}}&*\\
0&e^{-\frac{w_{l}^{loc}}{2}}\end{pmatrix}

for some complex numbers
w_{m}^{loc}
and
w_{l}^{loc}
.
Define a map
\zeta:R^{U}(\pi_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K))\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
by

\zeta(\rho)=\left(\left(\pm e^{\frac{w_{m}^{loc}}{2}}\right)^{2},\left(\pm e^{\frac{w_{l}^{loc}}{2}}\right)^{2}\right)=\left(e^{w_{m}^{loc}},e^{w_{l}^{loc}}\right).

We let
(M,L)
be the coordinates of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. For each irreducible component
C
of the algebraic set
R^{U}(\pi_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K))
, if the Zariski closure of
\zeta(C)\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
is one-dimensional, we let
F_{C}(M,L)
be the defining polynomial. Otherwise, we let
F_{C}(M,L)=1
.

Definition 2.14
.

The
PSL(2;\mathbb{C})
A-polynomial of
K
is defined by

A_{K}(M,L)=\prod_{C}F_{C}(M,L),

where the product is over all irreducible components
C
of
R^{U}(\pi_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K))
.

Let
\rho_{0}:\pi_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K)\to PSL(2;C)
be the discrete faithful representation associated to the complete hyperbolic structure of
{\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K
. Up to conjugation we assume that
\rho_{0}\in R^{U}(\pi_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}K))
. Since
\rho_{0}(m)
and
\rho_{0}(l)
are parabolic elements, we have

\rho_{0}(m)=\pm\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{0}{2}}&*\\
0&e^{-\frac{0}{2}}\end{pmatrix}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho_{0}(l)=\pm\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{0}{2}}&*\\
0&e^{-\frac{0}{2}}\end{pmatrix}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&*\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Let
C_{0}
be the irreducible component containing
\rho_{0}
.

Definition 2.15
.

The geometric component of the
PSL(2;\mathbb{C})
A-polynomial of
K
is defined by

A_{K}^{0}(M,L)=F_{C_{0}}(M,L).

Note that generically,
\rho(m)
and
\rho(l)
are loxodromy elements of the form

\rho(m)=\pm\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{w_{m}^{loc}}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{-\frac{w_{m}^{loc}}{2}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho(l)=\pm\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{w_{l}^{loc}}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{-\frac{w_{l}^{loc}}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

In particular, we have

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\rho(m)\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\frac{w_{m}^{loc}}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{\frac{w_{m}^{loc}}{2}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\rho(l)\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\frac{w_{l}^{loc}}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{\frac{w_{l}^{loc}}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

As a result, these two conjugated representations correspond to two distinct points in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

We conclude the discussion by giving several remarks. First, for any
\rho
sufficiently near
\rho_{0}
, we define the complex logarithmic holonomies of
m
and
l
to be
w_{m}^{loc}
and
w_{l}^{loc}
in such a way that
w_{m}^{loc}=w_{l}^{loc}=0
at
\rho_{0}
. Besides, it is known that for hyperbolic knots,
A_{K}^{0}(M,L)\neq 1
and
(1,1)
is a smooth point. Especially, near the complete hyperbolic structure,
w_{m}^{loc}
implicitly determines
w_{l}^{loc}
through the defining equation
A_{K}^{0}(M,L)=0
. Finally, the A-polynomial can be defined similarly with respect to any pair of simple closed curves on
\partial({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}\nu(K))
that generate
\pi_{1}(\partial({\mathbb{S}}^{3}{\smallsetminus}\nu(K)))
.

2.9.
Neumann-Zagier potential function

Let

\mathscr{N}

be a 3-manifold and let
K\subset\mathscr{N}
be a hyperbolic knot in
\mathscr{N}
, i.e. the complement
M=\mathscr{N}{\smallsetminus}K
admits a complete hyperbolic structure with finite volume. Recall from
[
NZ
]
that for any simple closed curve
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\partial(\mathscr{N}{\smallsetminus}\nu(K)))
, locally near the complete hyperbolic structure, the deformation space of hyperbolic structure of
M
can be parametrized in a generically 2:1 way with one complex variable
w^{loc}_{\gamma}

such that, for any complex number
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
close enough to
0
,
the point
w^{loc}_{\gamma}=\xi
corresponds to the hyperbolic structure where the complex logarithmic holonomy of
\gamma
is equal to
\xi
(if
X
is an ideal triangulation of
\mathscr{N}{\smallsetminus}K
, this means the structure such that

\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\gamma}(\mathbf{z})=\xi
).

Furthermore, given a pair of simple closed curves
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
that generates
\pi_{1}(\partial(\mathscr{N}{\smallsetminus}\nu(K)))
, the transition map

\Psi^{loc}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}

from
w^{loc}_{\gamma_{1}}
to

w^{loc}_{\gamma_{2}}=\Psi^{loc}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(w^{loc}_{\gamma_{1}})

is a locally biholomorphic map around 0 that sends 0 to 0.
For example, when
\mathbf{z}
is a shape structure very close to the complete one, we have
\Psi^{loc}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\gamma_{1}}(\mathbf{z}))=\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\gamma_{2}}(\mathbf{z})
.

In particular, one can consider the holomorphic function
\phi_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}
defined locally on a simply connected neighborhood around 0 by

\phi_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(w^{loc}_{\gamma_{1}})=i\Big(\operatorname{Vol}(M)+i\operatorname{CS}(M)\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{w^{loc}_{\gamma_{1}}}\Psi^{loc}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(t)dt,

where
w^{loc}_{\gamma_{2}}=\Psi^{loc}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(t)
is regarded as a function in a complex variable
t
, the integral is along any contour from
0
to
w^{loc}_{\gamma_{1}}
, and
\operatorname{Vol}(M)
and
\operatorname{CS}(M)
are the hyperbolic volume and the Chern-Simons invariant of
M=N{\smallsetminus}K
respectively.
Note that the holomorphic function
\phi_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}
satisfies the properties that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(0)=i\Big(\operatorname{Vol}(M)+i\operatorname{CS}(M)\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{d\phi_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}\big(w^{loc}_{\gamma_{1}}\big)}{d\big(w^{loc}_{\gamma_{1}}\big)}=\frac{\Psi^{loc}_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(w^{loc}_{\gamma_{1}})}{2},

which uniquely characterize the function.

Moreover, by
[
WY2
, Equation 2.4]
, in the special case where
w^{loc}_{\gamma_{1}}=i\theta
for some
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\phi_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(i\theta)=\operatorname{Vol}\Big(M;\gamma_{1},\theta\Big),

where
\operatorname{Vol}(M;\gamma_{1},\theta)
is the hyperbolic volume of
M
with (possibly incomplete) hyperbolic structure satisfying
\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,\gamma_{1}}(\mathbf{z})=i\theta
.

2.10.
Neumann-Zagier datum and gluing equations

Let
L
be a hyperbolic link in a closed oriented 3-manifold
\mathscr{N}
and let
\partial(\mathscr{N}{\smallsetminus}\nu(L))=\mathbb{T}_{1}\coprod\dots\coprod\mathbb{T}_{k}
. On each
\mathbb{T}_{i}
, we choose a simple closed curve
\gamma_{i}\in\pi_{1}(\mathbb{T}_{i})
and let

\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k})\in\pi_{1}(\partial(\mathscr{N}{\smallsetminus}\nu(L)))

\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k})
, the
i
-th entry of the vector

\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{f}^{\!\top}+\mathbf{G^{\prime}}\cdot{\mathbf{f}^{\prime}}^{\!\top}+\mathbf{G^{\prime\prime}}\cdot{\mathbf{f}^{\prime\prime}}^{\!\top}

, a priori the integration multi-contour depends on
\alpha
. We are going to show that we can change the integration multi-contour from
\mathscr{Y}_{\alpha_{1}}
to
\mathscr{Y}_{\alpha_{2}}
without changing the integral.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
with same angular holonomy
\theta:=\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha_{1})=\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha_{2})
along the longitude. Consider the subspace of angle structure

\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta):=\{\alpha\in\mathscr{A}_{X}\mid\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)=\theta\}
. Since
\mathscr{A}_{X}
is convex,
\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta)
, which is a restriction of a convex set on a linear subspace, is also convex. Consider the projection map
\mathrm{proj}_{a}:\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
defined by
\mathrm{proj}_{a}(\alpha)=(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
. Since
\mathrm{proj}_{a}
is an affine map and
\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta)
is convex, the image
\mathrm{proj}_{a}(\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta))
is also convex.

Moreover, since
\mathbf{B}^{-1}
is invertible, for any
(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
sufficiently close to
\mathrm{proj}_{a}(\alpha_{1})
, by using (
2.4
) and the equations
a_{k}+b_{k}+c_{k}=\pi
for
k=1,\dots,N
, we can find the corresponding
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},\dots,a_{N},b_{N},c_{N})
that depends continuously on
(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
and satisfies
(\ref{thurstoneqs2})
. In particular, by continuity, since
\alpha_{1}\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
, for any
(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
sufficiently close to
\mathrm{proj}_{a}(\alpha_{1})
, we have
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},\dots,a_{N},b_{N},c_{N})\in(0,\pi)^{N}
. Since
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},\dots,a_{N},b_{N},c_{N})
satisfies (
2.4
), we have
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},\dots,a_{N},b_{N},c_{N})\in\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta)
. Therefore, for any
(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
sufficiently close to
\mathrm{proj}_{a}(\alpha_{1})
, we can find an angle structure
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta)
such that
\mathrm{proj}_{a}(\alpha)=(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})
.

This shows that the image
\mathrm{proj}_{a}(\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta))
has dimension
N
. Altogether, for any
\alpha^{\prime}
such that
\mathrm{proj}_{a}(\alpha^{\prime})=(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{N}^{\prime})
satisfies
a_{k}^{\prime}\in[\min\{a^{1}_{k},a^{2}_{k}\},\max\{a^{1}_{k},a^{2}_{k}\}]
, the formula for
|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha^{\prime})|
holds. In particular, this implies the exponential decay properties of the integrand at infinity and the absolute convergence of the integral for all such
(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{N}^{\prime})
. As a result, by Bochner-Martinelli formula (see
[
Kr
]
), we have

|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha_{1})|=|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha_{2})|

for all
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
with
\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha_{1})=\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha_{2})=\theta
. This proves the second result.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Given
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, for all
\hbar>0
, the integrand in (
3.1
) does not depends on the choices of
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta)
. Moreover, the integration multi-contour can be chosen to be any
\mathscr{Y}_{\alpha}
as long as
\alpha\in\mathscr{A}^{l}_{X}(\theta)
. In this sense, we say that
|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)|
depends only on
\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)
.

Consider the potential function

The functions

In particular, in this subsection, every results on

when we study the asymptotics of the Jones function. We relate the critical point equations of

Note that since

We temporarily focus specifically on the property of

\xi=i\mathrm{H}^{R}_{X,l}(\alpha)

Proposition 3.5
.

Write
y_{l}=h_{l}+id_{l}\in{\mathbb{C}}
for

.
Under the assumption and the correspondence described in Proposition
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}S\left(\mathbf{y};\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)\right)=-\sum_{l}^{N}D(z_{l})+\sum_{l=1}^{N}h_{l}\frac{\partial}{\partial h_{l}}\mathrm{Re}S\left(\mathbf{y};\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)\right),

where
D(z)
is the Bloch-Wigner dilogarithm function given by

D(z)=\mathrm{Im}\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z)+\log|z|\mathrm{Arg}(1-z).

In particular, for every angle structure
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathscr{A}_{X}^{l}(\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha))
and the corresponding
\mathbf{y}_{c}(\alpha^{\prime})
defined in Proposition
3.4

\mathrm{Re}S\left(\mathbf{y}_{c}(\alpha^{\prime});\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)\right)=-\operatorname{Vol}(\alpha^{\prime}),

where
\operatorname{Vol}(\alpha^{\prime})
is the volume of the angle structure
\alpha^{\prime}
.

In particular, if we let
\mathbf{y}_{c}(\alpha)
be the critical point of
S
described in Proposition
3.4
, then we have

\mathrm{Re}S\left(\mathbf{y}_{c}(\alpha);\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)\right)=-\operatorname{Vol}\Big(M;l,\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)\Big),

is the volume of
M
with the (possibly incomplete) hyperbolic cone structure satisfying
\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{X,l}(\mathbf{z})=i\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)
.

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle S\left(\mathbf{y};\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)\right)=i\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}Q\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\bm{\nu}+\bm{u})-\mathscr{G}\bm{\pi})-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(T_{k})+1}{4}\right)y_{k}^{2}-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathrm{Li}_{2}\left(-e^{y_{k}}\right).

Write
y=h+id
. Note that

\frac{d}{dy}\Big(i\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-e^{y})\Big)=-i\operatorname{Log}(1+e^{y}).

By using the Cauchy-Riemann equation, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial h}\mathrm{Re}\Big(i\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-e^{y})\Big)=\mathrm{Re}\Bigg(\frac{d}{dy}\Big(i\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-e^{y})\Big)\Bigg)=\mathrm{Arg}(1+e^{-y}).

As a result, by the definition of the Bloch-Wigner dilogarithm function,

\mathrm{Re}\Big(i\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-e^{y})\Big)=-\mathrm{Im}\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-e^{y})=-D(-e^{y})+h\mathrm{Arg}(1+e^{y})=-D(-e^{y})+h\frac{\partial}{\partial h}\mathrm{Re}\Big(i\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-e^{y})\Big).

Thus, we have

\mathrm{Re}\Big(-i\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-e^{y_{l}})\Big)=D(-e^{y_{l}})+h_{l}\frac{\partial}{\partial h_{l}}\mathrm{Re}\Big(-i\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-e^{y_{l}})\Big)=-D(z_{l})+h_{l}\frac{\partial}{\partial h_{l}}\mathrm{Re}\Big(-i\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-e^{y_{l}})\Big).

Finally, note that the real part of all the remaining terms (the non-dilogarithm terms) are linear in
\{h_{l}\mid l=1,\dots,N\}
, thus

\displaystyle\ i\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}Q\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\bm{\nu}+\bm{u})-\mathscr{G}\bm{\pi})-i\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(T_{k})+1}{4}\right)y_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{l=1}^{N}h_{l}\frac{\partial}{\partial h_{l}}\mathrm{Re}\left(i(\mathbf{y_{+}},\mathbf{y_{-}})Q\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y_{+}}\\
\mathbf{y_{-}}\end{pmatrix}+(-\mathbf{y_{+}},\mathbf{y_{-}})(\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\bm{\nu}+\bm{u})-\mathscr{G}\bm{\pi})-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(T_{k})+1}{4}\right)y_{k}^{2}\right).

The result then follows from summing up the equations.
∎

Proposition 3.6

For any

3.3.
Asymptotic expansion formula of the Teichmüller TQFT partition function

We can now prove the second main theorem, Theorem
1.12
. For the sake of clarity, we recall that with the assumptions and notations used in Theorem
1.12
,

—

Theorem
1.12
(1) states that if
X
is FAMED then

\limsup_{{\hbar}\to 0}2\pi{\hbar}\log|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)|\leq-\sup\{\operatorname{Vol}(\alpha^{\prime})\mid\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathscr{A}_{X},\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha^{\prime})=\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)\},

—

Theorem
1.12
(2) states that if
X
is FAMED and admits a precise hyperbolic cone structure then

|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)|=\left|\frac{\exp\left(\frac{i}{\pi}R(\mathbf{z})\right)}{\operatorname{det}(\mathscr{A})\sqrt{2\operatorname{det}\mathbf{B}^{-1}}}\cdot\frac{\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2\pi{\hbar}}\operatorname{Vol}\left(M;l,\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)\right)\Big)}{\sqrt{\pm\tau(M,l,X,\mathbf{z})}}\Big(1+O({\hbar})\Big)\right|.

Proof of Theorem
1.12
.

First we remark that (3) follows directly from (2). Let us prove (1) and (2).

We assume
X
is FAMED for the curve
l
.

For any
\alpha=(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},\dots,a_{N},b_{N},c_{N})\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
, let
\mathscr{Y}_{\alpha}
be a multi-contour given by

\mathscr{Y}_{\alpha}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Big({\mathbb{R}}+i(\pi-a_{k})\Big).

Step 1: Re-writing the partition function

We start by re-writing the partition function with a convenient form, thanks to the FAMED property.

By Proposition
3.2
, since X is FAMED, then for all
\hbar>0
, we have

\displaystyle\ |\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)|=\frac{1}{|\operatorname{det}\mathscr{A}|}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\Big)^{N}\times

\displaystyle\ \left|\int_{\mathscr{Y}_{\alpha}}e^{\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\left(i\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}Q\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\bm{\nu}+\bm{u})-\mathscr{G}\bm{\pi})-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(T_{k})+1}{4}\right)y_{k}^{2}\right)}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{y_{k}}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)d\mathbf{y}\right|.

Recall that

\displaystyle S\left(\mathbf{y};\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha)\right)=i\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}Q\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{\!\top}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\bm{\nu}+\bm{u})-\mathscr{G}\bm{\pi})-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(T_{k})+1}{4}\right)y_{k}^{2}-i\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathrm{Li}_{2}\left(-e^{y_{k}}\right),

where
\bm{u}=(0,\dots,0,\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha))^{\!\top}
.

Thus we have

|\mathscr{Z}_{\hbar}(X,\alpha)|=\left|\frac{1}{\operatorname{det}\mathscr{A}}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\Big)^{N}\int_{\mathscr{Y}_{\alpha}}e^{\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}S(\mathbf{y};\mathrm{H}^{\mathbb{R}}_{X,l}(\alpha))}e^{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\operatorname{Log}\left(\Phi_{\mathsf{b}}\left(\frac{y_{k}}{2\pi\sqrt{\hbar}}\right)\right)-\frac{-i}{2\pi\hbar}\mathrm{Li}_{2}\left(-e^{y_{k}}\right)}d\mathbf{y}\right|.

Recall that
