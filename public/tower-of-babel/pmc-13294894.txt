XPS Analysis of Co–Ni Alloy Passive Layers: Computational Peak-on-Satellite Analysis of 2p Core Levels Reveals the Influence of the Co/Ni Ratio on Oxide Chemistry and Structure
Authors: Barbara Laïk, Morgane Richet, Nicolas Emery, Loïc Perrière, Yvan Cotrebil, Vincent Russier, Ivan Guillot, Pierre Dubot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thin native oxide films spontaneously formed on a series of cobalt–nickel alloys with varying cobalt concentrations were investigated using X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS). Combining XPS with sequential ion abrasion allowed identification of the various oxides and hydroxides present within the passive layers, depending on their depth in the films, which do not exceed 5 nm. A recent study of the Co20Ni80 alloy showed that the surface layer is heterogeneous both laterally and in depth, with some regions covered by oxidized cobalt compounds and others by oxidized nickel compounds. Different oxide thicknesses were observed on the Co20Ni80 alloy compared to pure Ni and Co metals. In the present study, we investigate the thicknesses and compositions of cobalt and nickel oxide films in binary alloys Co x Ni100–x , with x = 0, 20, 50, 70, 90, and 100. Building on the previous study of Co20Ni80, where a surface galvanic effect induced by alloying was proposed, we show that variations in Co concentration suggest that the magnetic properties of the substrate influence the composition of the passivation layers formed on the cobalt–nickel alloys.

Introduction

Co–Ni alloys exhibit particularly attractive properties, such as good electrocatalytic activity, high strength, high toughness, high temperature resistance, and corrosion resistance. These properties make them suitable for a wide range of applications, from the automotive industry to aerospace and various other industrial sectors, including energy development and the chemical industry. In the form of thin films, they are specifically used in various magnetic devices, particularly in microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), manufacturing sensors, actuators, and magnetic information storage devices (MRAM). −

It is well-known and widely reported in the literature that the properties of Co–Ni alloys depend on their structure and morphology, which in turn depend on the preparation method and the Co/Ni ratio. Different preparation methods have been recently summarized and compared by Yang et al. In the case of pulse electrodeposition, the pulse frequency can be adjusted to improve wear resistance and corrosion resistance, resulting in higher corrosion protection than coatings obtained by DC electrodeposition. These differences in behavior are linked to the single FCC structure or the coexistence of both FCC and HCP phases as well as the grain size and grain boundary constitution of the plated layer. ,

Indeed, a native oxide film spontaneously formed on Co–Ni alloys in the atmosphere is dense, adherent, and insoluble in the application medium. However, the characteristics of this native oxide film that confer anticorrosive properties depend on the structure of the substrate on which it grows. This statement prompted Dorenlor to conduct an in-depth study of the Co20Ni80 alloy, in which she highlighted the impact of microstructural parameters on corrosion resistance and passivation mechanisms.

Continuing this work on the Co20Ni80 alloy, it is crucial to obtain information about the chemical composition and structure (topology) of the thin native oxide film, regardless of the alloy composition. This includes both the first atomic layers at the surface (oxide film–vacuum interface) and the in-depth distribution of the different chemical species that can coexist in the thin oxide film (up to the oxide film–bulk interface). A detailed assessment of these two limiting domains of the thin layer is critical for both surface reactivity in relation to corrosion properties in a humid environment and film–substrate adhesion.

Recently, we presented an original methodology based on X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) coupled with sequential low-energy ion sputtering (depth profiling and spectra modeling) to study both the chemical composition and topological structure of oxide-hydroxide thin films. The Co20Ni80 alloy was chosen as a model. We demonstrated that modeling the XPS 2p spectral region of Ni and Co provides a detailed description of the thin film’s composition and structure. Given the complexity of the XPS 2p spectra of nickel and cobalt oxides, oxyhydroxides, and hydroxides, especially when multiple compounds are present, a homemade code was developed to analyze the complex 2p photoelectron spectra. This code is based on reference compounds spectra (Figures S1–S3) and the use of satellite peaks that depend on the chemical structure. It enables characterization of the Co20Ni80 alloy passive layer composition and allows us to accurately identify the in-depth evolution of oxides, hydroxides, and oxyhydroxides percentages in the passive layer. Moreover, exploiting the 2p XPS intensities of the metallic and oxide components can provide valuable information about the oxide layer structure. In particular, measuring the ratio of metallic to oxide intensity values at t = 0 (no sputtering), noted ROxOH, as well as the metallic intensity ratio at t = 0 to t = ∞, noted by RMet in this work, can help us to calculate various parameters of a heterogeneous layer structure. This heterogeneous layer consists of a homogeneous oxide layer of thickness d 1, with a portion θ of thicker islands of thickness d 2. This description applies to both nickel and cobalt areas.

This simple model allows for the theoretical calculation of ROxOH and RMet using the signal exponential attenuation law depending on the attenuation length and layer thickness. The layer structure estimation is obtained using a homemade code based on a Monte Carlo algorithm, in which the layer structure parameters (thicknesses and island coverages) are randomly varied until the calculated ROxOH and RMet values match the experimental values. Thus, the thickness and density of the islands can be obtained by measuring two XPS observables: ROxOH and RMet. A complete description of the equations used in the code is provided in the previous paper.

We now propose to apply this methodology to different binary alloy Co x Ni100–x compositions, with x values equal to 0, 20, 50, 70, 90, and 100. We aim in a first step to highlight the influence of the Co/Ni ratio in the alloy on the structure and composition of passive layers. The further step will aim to correlate structure and composition of the passive layers to their anticorrosive properties. In this study, we report the evolution of the in-depth composition of the native oxide surface layers by coupling the XPS analysis with low-energy ion sputtering. Different thicknesses and compound coverings are determined. Finally, the constitution of the passive layer as a function of the Co/Ni ratio will be discussed in relation to the magnetic properties of the alloy, which seems to depend on the FCC or HCP structure.

Materials and Methods

All of the Co x Ni100–x alloys were prepared using the same procedure. The mixture of cobalt and nickel raw metals used was in the form of slugs and wires and had a purity exceeding 99.9 wt %. To remove traces of oxides, mechanical polishing was performed on raw metals before the melting process. These alloys were produced by high-frequency electromagnetic induction melting in a water-cooled copper crucible under a He atmosphere. Then gravity casting was performed to shape the ingots. The cooling rate was estimated to be about 103 K·s–1 and rods with a diameter of 13 mm and a length of around 80 mm were obtained. After homogenization at 850 °C for 1 h, the rods were cold-rolled at room temperature to a final thickness of 1 mm. Samples were prepared by mechanical grinding using 1200 to 4000 SiC papers. The samples were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a PANalytical XPert Pro diffractometer using the Cu Kα radiation at a wavelength of 0.154056 nm.

Figure gathers XRD patterns for the Co x Ni100–x , for x ∈{0, 20, 50, 70, 90, 100} samples. XRD patterns collected on the nickel richest samples (i.e. Ni, Co20Ni80, Co50Ni50) exhibit peaks at 44.5°, 51.9°, 76.5°, 93° and 98.5°. They are indexed with an FCC cell (Fm3̅m cubic space group). Increasing the cobalt content up to Co70Ni30 leads to small additional peaks observed at 41.5°, 47.5°, 62.5°, 76°, 84°, and 92.5°. In addition to the diffraction peaks of the FCC phase, these reflections attributed to an HCP phase (P63/mmc space group) indicate that we are dealing here with a two-phase FCC-HCP alloy. A further increase in Co content to Co90Ni10 and up to pure cobalt confirms the presence of the HCP phase, which is now the main phase in the sample.

XRD patterns of Co x Ni100–x , for x ∈ {0, 20, 50, 70, 90, 100} different samples.

Surface analyses were performed by a combination of X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) and Ar Ions Sputtering. The XPS analysis used a Thermo Electron Kalpha spectrometer with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (hυ = 1486.6 eV) operating at a pressure of approximately 10–9 mbar. The analyzer pass energy was 200 eV for survey spectra and 50 eV for high-resolution spectra. The energy resolution of our spectrometer is determined by measuring the peak of metallic silver 3d5/2, located around 368.2 eV. Measuring the full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of a Voigt-type peak yields a value of approximately 0.57 eV. The spectrometer was calibrated using Au 4f7/2 at 84.1 eV and when necessary, the energy scale was corrected by reference to the C1s of aliphatic compounds at 285.0 eV. The temperature during spectral acquisition was T = 298 K, and the base analyzer chamber pressure was approximately 3 × 10–9 mbar. The acquisition time at each depth profiling step was nearly 120 s. The spectra were recorded and analyzed using Thermo Avantage software and nonmetal peaks were modelized with a Lorentzian/Gaussian peak shape with a fixed ratio of 30/70. Peaks corresponding to the metallic state were modelized with an asymmetric peak that mimic the Doniach–Sunjic line shape. Advantage software allows an asymmetric line shape that depends on three parameters: tail mix, tail height, and tail exponent. In the applied fitting process, these three parameters were set, respectively, to (50.0, 0.05, and 0.06) and allowed to vary in a range of 5%. Before beginning the fitting process, a Shirley-type background subtraction was realized. During the fitting process for adjusting the peaks to best reproduce the total experimental spectrum, energy and full width at half-maximum were constrained to vary in a small interval (±0.1 eV) from values of some reference spectra.

Passive layer composition analyses were carried out by coupling XPS analysis with low-energy ion profilometry. To avoid significant spectral modifications of the 2p region related to irradiation damages (ion beam-induced reduction) and the preferential sputtering of one of the elements, ion sputtering was carried out with low-energy argon ions (200 eV).

We tested the influence of the primary ion beam energy on a spinel reference sample (NiCo2O4) and a powder mixture of CoO and Co­(OH)2 under these sputtering conditions.

We observed the evolution of the Ni/Co ratio during the XPS sputtering process in both cases. Sputtering the NiCo2O4 compound surface with 200 eV argon ions for 50 s did not result in any variation in the Co/Ni ratio, which remained constant at around 1.90; the O/Ni ratio remained at approximately 4.0.

Regarding the oxide layer grown on the Co x Ni100–x alloys, which contains mixed oxidized and hydroxylated compounds, preferential sputtering must be tested on the different oxidized compounds. The effects of sputtering on an X­(OH)2/XO, with X = Ni or Co, powder mixture (50:50) have been studied. The O1s ratio between OH (532.5 eV) and O (531.0 eV) remained constant until 50 s of sputtering, with a ratio at around 1.95. These results suggest that preferential sputtering, if it existed, was negligible on the spinel structure and on different oxidized compounds.

The ion gun that equips our XPS device (Thermo EX06) allows us to work with such a low primary energy. The surface density of the ions used was the lowest possible, corresponding to approximately less than 1 μA for a swept surface of about 3 × 2 mm2, which gives a current of approximately 0.5 mA, corresponding to a current density of about 0.1 mA·mm–2, and a sputtering speed of about 0.06 nm·s–1 on the Thermo Fisher Ta2O5 scale. For all the samples, 14 sputtering steps of 2 s duration were performed, i.e., 28 s of total ion sputtering and therefore an analyzed depth of the order of 2 nm.

Results

As previously shown, it is possible to characterize the mixture of the different oxides, oxyhydroxides, and hydroxides present at the Co–Ni alloy surface using the peak binding energy of the 2p core levels and the intensity of the satellites specific to the oxidized compounds of each of the two metals.

In this paper, the native oxide layer spontaneously formed at the surface of the Co x Ni100–x , for x ∈ {0, 20, 50, 70, 90, 100}, is analyzed by XPS profilometry, using the same methodology mentioned above, to point out the influence of the Co/Ni ratio on the passivation layer structures and compositions. At each sputtering step, a survey and the Ni2p, Co2p, O1s, and C1s regions have been recorded. The C1s peak is an indicator of unavoidable surface contamination. Three main components are observed (Figure a). They correspond to oxidized forms of carbon-like alcohol functions (C–OH, 286–287 eV), ketone functions (CO, 288 eV), or hydrogen-carbonates functions (COOH, 289–290 eV). The O1s spectral region is also interesting since it clearly shows four components (Figure b), one related to O2– at the lowest energies (structural anions of oxides, about 531 eV), another to OH at intermediate energies (hydroxyls, at ca. 532.5 eV), and two others to water at the highest energies (ca. 533.5 and 534.8 eV). The disappearance of this signal upon sputtering confirms the complete removing of the oxidized film at the alloy surface during the profilometry measurement. To avoid erroneously estimating the chemical composition of the oxide layer, we did not use the O1s peak for cobalt or nickel compounds due to possible interference between the O1s peaks of the oxidized species in the layer and the O1s peaks of organic compounds contaminating the surface. Finally, only the Ni2p and Co2p spectral regions, which can be decomposed into different components, are analyzed. For each alloy composition, the spectra collected at different sputtering times are plotted in Figure S4. Oxidized Layers Composition

For each of the two pure metals, oxides (NiO/CoO and Co3O4), oxyhydroxides (NiO­(OH)/CoO­(OH)), and hydroxides (Ni­(OH)2/Co­(OH)2) are found in the spontaneous films. The composition of the outer surface of the passivated films (film/vacuum interface) depends on the metal.

In the case of pure cobalt (Figure ), the film is a mixture of 50% oxides/50 % oxyhydroxides-hydroxides. The oxides part contents 40% of CoO and 10% of Co3O4, while the hydroxide Co­(OH)2 and the oxyhydroxide CoO­(OH) are in quite the same proportions. This repartition highlights that the film is about twice as rich in divalent compounds as in trivalent ones. This surface composition is different in the case of pure nickel where Ni­(OH)2 is quite negligible with a 5% rate, and the proportions of divalent species NiO and trivalent ones NiO­(OH) are almost the same (respectively, 40 and 55%). So, for pure metals, the oxidized Co surface is richer than the Ni surface in divalent compounds.

With sputtering time, in both pure metal cases, the oxyhydroxide proportions decrease, more rapidly in the case of pure cobalt than that for pure nickel. Concomitantly, the hydroxide and oxide proportions increased. In the case of pure cobalt, after the quite complete removal of oxyhydroxide (after 6 sputtering steps, i.e., after about 12 s of sputtering time), the hydroxides begin to be removed in turn, leading to an increase of the oxide, CoO and Co3O4, proportions. In the case of nickel, the same trend is observed but with a transition at about 12 sputtering steps. It has been previously shown, by computational analysis, that these behaviors agree with a layer structure containing islands. The observed decrease in oxyhydroxylated species, concomitant with an increase in hydroxylated compounds, suggests that the former is deposited over the latter. Furthermore, the CoO­(OH) overlayer is observed to be thinner than that of the NiO­(OH).

In the case of alloys, whatever the composition, the presence of the spinel NiCo2O4 has not been highlighted like it has been already shown for the particular composition Co20Ni80. The mathematical treatment of the homemade code yielded the same conclusion as that reached for the Co20Ni80 alloy, namely, that some areas are covered with cobalt-oxidized compounds and others with nickel-oxidized compounds.

For low nickel content in the alloy (Co90Ni10), though the Co­(OH)2 content is as low as 10%, the film–vacuum interface external part composition presents the same trend as that described for pure cobalt at the beginning of the abrasion. Indeed, during approximately the first 8 abrasion steps (16 s), CoO and Co3O4 proportions remain constant and CoO­(OH) is removed to reveal underlying Co­(OH)2. For longer sputtering times, the four oxidized compound proportions keep constant values. The main difference with pure cobalt is the high CoO­(OH) percentage, about 20% vs 4%.

Concerning nickel species, the behavior is comparable to that of pure nickel but with a slight increase of Ni­(OH)2 at the oxide film surface. The NiO­(OH) removal takes place but is faster (4 abrasion steps versus 12); after the partial abrasion of Ni­(OH)2, the composition is very close to that of pure nickel.

In the cases of Co70Ni30 and Co50Ni50, parts covered by oxidized forms of nickel have compositions similar to those described for pure nickel. In parts covered by oxidized forms of cobalt, Co­(OH)2 and CoO percentages are very low; the film is mainly made of Co3O4 and CoO­(OH). As for nickel, the oxyhydroxide percentage decreases with abrasion time, concomitantly with an increase in the oxide contentthough not the CoO divalent oxide as in the case of nickel (NiO) but Co3O4 mixed-valence oxide.

The case of Co20Ni80 is different. The proportions of the oxidized compounds of nickel, like those of cobalt, do not show any notable variation during sputtering. It can be concluded that the thin film formed spontaneously at its surface is rather homogeneous in its thickness, regardless of the part considered. It is worth noting, however, that in this particular alloy, the relative proportions of oxides and hydroxides are inverted: compared to alloys with lower nickel content, the proportion of Ni­(OH)2 has increased significantly, while that of the oxyhydroxide has markedly decreased. In contrast, for cobalt, the trend is reversed: the proportion of Co­(OH)2 has increased, whereas the oxyhydroxide content has decreased. It ceases to apply once the nickel content falls below a critical threshold, leading to a phase transformation from FCC to HCP.

Oxidized Layers Structure

To evidence the influence of the Co/Ni ratio on the thicknesses of the different parts of the oxidized film, containing either nickel compounds or cobalt ones, it is also convenient to look at the evolution of the ratio IsurfX,t=0IbulkX,t=0 (noted ROxOH,X) as shown in our previous work. In this ratio, I bulk X,t=0 and I surf X,t=0 stand for, respectively, the peak intensity of the metal X (with X = Ni or Co) and for the sum of the peak intensities of all oxidized forms of X contained in the oxide layer. These intensities are determined by peak fitting of the spectra recorded before sputtering (t = 0). For t = 0, the alloy is covered by the thin layer of the oxidized compounds previously mentioned. Consequently, the signal emitted by the metal in the alloy is attenuated through this layer, and it is all the more attenuated as it is thick. Therefore, the higher the ROxOH,X, the thicker the film.

In Figure are reported the ROxOH,X ratio values as a function of the cobalt content in the Co x Ni100–x alloy. With an ROxOH,X value 40 times higher for pure cobalt than for pure nickel (8 vs 0.2), it is evident that the passivation layer on cobalt metal is much thicker than that of nickel. Concerning the alloys, regardless of the Co/Ni ratio, ROxOH,Co is always greater than ROxOH,Ni. That implies that the parts covered with cobalt-oxidized species are thicker than those covered with nickel ones. The thickness difference between nickel and cobalt zones is the lowest for Co70Ni30 with very close ROxOH,X values (0.60 vs 0.40). For atomic concentrations of cobalt lower than 70%, the oxide layer thicknesses do not seem to depend notably on the concentration of the alloy (ROxOH,X value is quite constant), whereas for concentrations higher than 70%, the ROxOH,X values increase rapidly with the concentration of cobalt in the alloy, meaning a decrease (increase) of their thicknesses with the nickel (cobalt) content increase in the alloy.

Evolution of the ROxOH,X ratio as a function of cobalt content in the Co–Ni alloy.

To address this question, an estimation can be made based on the calculations and approximations outlined in ref . Using peak intensities as a first approximation, the surface fraction of Co (or Ni) species can be approximated as being proportional to the cumulative intensity of all peaks corresponding to oxidized cobalt (or nickel) compounds. In Figure , we can observe that Co90Ni10 behaves differently than the other alloys. The repartition between nickel and cobalt species is close to that of the alloy. A slight enrichment in cobalt compounds, within the margin of error of measurements and processing, may be noticed. Moreover, with the sputtering time, the cobalt and nickel percentages are relatively constant.

For the other compositions (Co20Ni80, Co50Ni50, and Co70Ni30), before the sputtering process (vacuum–solid interface), the composition of the film is close to that of the alloy, with, respectively, 33%, 59%, and 77% compared to 20%, 50%, and 70%. During sputtering, a clear enrichment of cobalt compounds is observed (Figure ). For these alloy compositions, the relative atomic percentage of cobalt compounds increases with abrasion time, which may be correlated with the fact that the oxide layer of cobalt compounds is thicker than that of nickel compounds (nickel-oxidized compounds layer disappears more rapidly than that of cobalt-oxidized compounds layer), as shown in Figure .

Combining the facts that (i) the film formed spontaneously on the surface of the different alloys is made up of zones containing oxidized species of nickel and others of oxidized species of cobalt, (ii) the proportion of each of these zones is dependent on the alloy and varies with the abrasion time, and (iii) the layer is not homogeneous in thickness, it is possible to propose a structure for this oxidation layer formed spontaneously on the surface of the alloys.

The exploitation of the experimental intensities ratios IsurfX,t=0IbulkX,t=0 (noted ROxOH,X) already described above, and IbulkX,t=0IbulkX,t=∞ , (noted RMet) with I bulk X,t=∞ standing for the peak intensities of the bulk compound after complete abrasion, leads to different values of the oxides layer thickness (noted d X). This result implies the heterogeneity in thickness of the covering thin film and led us to consider a thickness inhomogeneous simple model consisting of different zones for each element, some thicker than the others.

The topological structure of the passive layer that spontaneously forms on the surface of the Co20Ni80 alloy has been modeled and described in a previous article. The methodology relies on theoretical calculations of ROxOH,X and RMet using the exponential attenuation law of XPS signals, which depends on the electron attenuation length (EAL) and layer thickness, to compare the calculated values with the experimental ones. The modelization of the heterogeneity of the oxidized layer for both cobalt and nickel is schematized in the inset of Figure a. In this model, a portion of the analyzed surface (SCo, for example, as well as SNi for nickel) is covered with oxidized cobalt or nickel compounds. This is made up of zones of different thicknesses (two in the proposed model). These thicker parts could correspond to islands that cover a θCo fraction of the SCo surface. These islands have a thickness of d 2_Co and are embedded in a thin film of homogeneous thickness d 1_Co. Carbon contamination is also considered in the thinner part of the layer. This contamination is expected to have a thickness of d C_Co and covers a (1 – αCo) fraction of the thinner layer. The same description applies to the nickel oxide areas, with parameters θNi, αNi, d1_Ni, d2_Ni, and dC_Ni. In the following, X stands for either Co or Ni.

A homemade code (Wolfram Mathematica) was used to estimate the layer structure parameters. These equations link αX, θX, d 1_X, d 2_X, and d C_X to the ROxOH,X and RMet parameters. In these equations, λX (λbulk in the case of a pure metal), λox (λsurf in the case of an oxidized layer), and λC (for carbon contamination) are the electron attenuation lengths, which are set to 1.1, 1.4, and 1.6 nm, respectively. To reduce computation time, we worked with a fixed EAL. We assessed the adjustment of the various parameters relating to nickel (αNi, θNi, d 1_Ni, d 2_Ni, and d C_Ni) and cobalt (αCo, θCo, d 1_Co, d 2_Co, and d C_Co) when the calculation results fit the experimental values of ROxOH and RMet. In the Mathematica code, the αX, θX, d 1_X, d 2_X, and d C_X parameters are randomly scanned within constrained intervals using a Monte Carlo algorithm. The parameter values that best fit the experimental values of ROxOH and RMet are stored and averaged at the end of the calculation. Results for αX, θX, d 1_X, and d 2_X using this simple topological model are shown in Figure .

In Figure a, the thicknesses d 1_X and d 2_X are represented for X = Co and Ni as a function of the Co x Ni100–x alloy Co contents. In all cases, the thicknesses of layers containing cobalt compounds (red lines) are thicker than those containing nickel compounds (blue lines), in agreement with the conclusion drawn from Figure . This behavior is valid for either the thin parts (layer of uniform thickness) of the film or the thicker parts (islands). When x < 70, decreasing the nickel concentration in the alloy correlates with thinner oxide layers, regardless of whether the oxide phase (layer or island) comprises nickel or cobalt.

For nickel content greater than 30%, the thicknesses of parts covered by cobalt compounds seem relatively constant, about 1 nm for the homogeneous film and 2 nm for the islands. For these cases compared to pure nickel, we observe that adding cobalt in pure nickel entails an increase of thickness for both the homogeneous film and islands containing nickel. This figure emphasizes the particular composition Co70Ni30, where x = 70. Indeed, behaviors are really different between higher and lower x values. This result matches with the different crystallographic structures: FCC for x < 70, HCP phase apparition for x > 70.

Figure b highlights the same behavior for the evolution of the island percentage coverage. Indeed, there is only about 5–10% of islands as long as x < 70. For higher x values than 70, when the HC phase is present in the alloy, the percentage of islands increases up to 25% in the case of cobalt-oxidized compounds.

To complete the characterization of the passive layer as a function of the Co/Ni rate in the alloy, the global covering rate (islands + homogeneous layer) percentages of the cobalt species and nickel species, contained in the film, were calculated as a function of the cobalt content in the alloy. The results reveal a small covering increase of cobalt oxides, oxyhydroxides, and hydroxides compared to the nominal concentration in the alloy (reverse for nickel) in the whole range of composition.

Discussion

Decomposing the nickel and cobalt 2p spectra based on peak-to-satellite ratios allowed us to identify the chemical compounds present in the thin films as well as estimate the average thickness of these layers according to the cobalt content of the alloys. The evolution of the metallic and oxidized components over sputtering time suggests that the oxidized layer is chemically and topologically heterogeneous, with areas covered by nickel and cobalt compounds containing some thicker discrete islands, as previously demonstrated for Co20Ni80. This distribution is not surprising, as this type of chemical heterogeneity has previously been observed in the structures and compositions of passive films on certain pure metals. Notably, the work of Barr , shows that the compounds CoO, Co3O4, CoO­(OH), and Co­(OH)2 are present on the surface of cobalt in the form of CoO islands. In the case of nickel, the oxidized layer is shown to be mainly composed of NiO, NiO­(OH), and Ni­(OH)2, with some regions of Ni2O3 that could be cation-defective NiO. However, the existence of domains covered with nickel compounds and others with cobalt compounds is difficult to explain for a single-phase substrate (substitutional alloy). Nevertheless, similar behavior has been observed for Co–Cu alloys used as catalysts.

Due to the heterogeneous surface topology, the depth-resolved chemical distribution of the various compounds cannot be interpreted unambiguously. Indeed, several phenomena can be responsible for an increase in compound concentration with sputtering time. It may indicate that the signal of a compound becomes less attenuated as the sputtering progresses. Thus, this compound might be buried within the oxide layer. Another possibility is that the compound is the main component of thicker islands. Consequently, its signal intensity is less affected by sputtering than the intensity emitted by compounds contained in thinner films that progressively disappear under the sputtering process. However, with regard to the zones covered by the oxidized nickel compounds, it is proposed that NiO is present at the oxide film–bulk interface or in the islands, while the hydroxylated compounds are located at the oxide film–vacuum interface (Figure ). This agrees with the observations of Hoppe and Strehblow on pure nickel. In regions covered by oxidized cobalt species, however, no consistent trend is observed across the full range of alloy compositions studied here.

In addition to topological heterogeneity, our computations reveal thickness heterogeneity in islands (of thickness d 2) buried into a homogeneous layer (of thickness d 1). Figures and a demonstrate that regions coated with cobalt oxide are thicker than regions coated with nickel oxide, regardless of the alloy composition. This is true for both homogeneous films and discrete islands. This behavior could be linked to the preferential oxidation of cobalt versus nickel, which has already been discussed in the literature based on oxidizability properties , or to the easier diffusion path of cobalt than nickel toward the surface of the oxide film–vacuum interface. On the other hand, the thickness of the passive layer, as well as the density of the islands, clearly depends on the cobalt content (Figure a,b).

The influence of cobalt concentration in Co–Ni alloys on the quantity of oxidized nickel present at the solid surface was first observed in the context of high-temperature oxidation. Indeed, as early as 1955, Frederick and Cornet demonstrated that the oxidation rate increased with increasing cobalt content, which was correlated with a decrease in the activation energy for nickel oxidation. A few years later, Smeltzer, who was working with Co–Ni alloys containing low levels of cobalt (10.5% and 25.1% Co) at temperatures ranging from 400 to 800 °C, proposed that the magnetic and electronic properties of the alloys influence the diffusion rate of metallic cations through the oxide layer and thereby affect the oxidation rate. However, despite his intuition, Smeltzer acknowledged that distinguishing between purely electronic, diffusional, and magnetic effects was challenging. More recently, Zhou et al. confirmed the influence of cobalt content in Co–Ni alloys on the oxidation rate in a study of the oxidation behaviors of electrodeposited nickel–cobalt coatings with different cobalt contents.

In the case of aqueous and atmospheric corrosion of cobalt–nickel alloys under atmospheric conditions, Kuhn and Wellwood concluded that most of the reported data suggest that the corrosion resistance of Co–Ni alloys decreases with the progressive addition of cobalt.

In addition to the influence of the Co/Ni ratio on the alloy, the present study shows that the evolution of the oxide film thickness and island density evidently exhibits a transition at x = 70 in Ni100–x Co x . For cobalt content lower than 70%, the oxyhydroxide layers (for both Co and Ni) appear to be relatively independent of cobalt concentration, whereas thicknesses appear to increase with cobalt content for concentrations above 70%. As was the case for the composition profiles in depth (Figure ) and the evolution of the ROxOH,X ratio (Figure ), as well as the evolution of the Co/Ni proportions in the layer (Figure ), this change in thickness behavior with Co concentration corresponds to the crystallographic structure transition observed in the phase diagram of the Co–Ni-substitution alloy (FCC to HCP). In accordance with the Ni100–x Co x phase diagram, as well as recent DFT calculations on such systems, the XRD analysis presented for the prepared alloys (Figure ) shows that the HCP phase is dominant for cobalt contents greater than 70%, while the FCC phase is dominant for cobalt contents lower than 70%. Therefore, the transition observed in our work with respect to layer thickness, composition, and structural variations with cobalt content is consistent with the change in the crystal structure of the alloys.

We may then ask why the composition, and consequently the crystallographic structure, of the alloys exerts such a pronounced influence on the characteristics of the spontaneously formed oxide film across the compositions studied. One possible approach would be to consider the electronic properties evolution with the alloy composition. The nature of the bonds between the alloy atoms plays a critical role in determining reactivity toward oxygen, thus impacting the resulting formation of oxide phases. While an almost free distribution of electrons is observed in pure Ni and pure Co, typical of metal bonds, these bonds between the atoms are weakened in the Co–Ni alloy. This phenomenon becomes increasingly important as the cobalt content of the alloy increases and affects the various possible bonds differently. The Co–Co bond becomes significantly more covalent than the Co–Ni bond, which in turn is more covalent than the Ni–Ni bond. This partially covalent character in the alloy imparts significant electronic cohesion and tends to slow down electron extraction during the initial stages of oxidation. However, this trend contradicts our observations and therefore cannot justify the influence of the Co content in the alloy on the characteristics of the formed oxidized film.

A second physical phenomenon can be considered to explain the influence of alloy composition on the oxide layer growth and thickness as both the alloy substrate and the oxidized layer compounds are magnetic. The magnetic properties of Co–Ni alloys are worth discussing, since the phases formed during metal oxidation depend on the magnetic properties of the substrate. In the case of Co–Ni alloy, an increase in the Co/Ni ratio is accompanied by a change in the crystallographic structure and magnetic properties, since FCC and HCP phases are ferromagnetic, albeit with different magnetic hardness. Bensouilah et al. measured magnetization as a function of the applied magnetic field for various Ni(100–x)Co x compositions. Values of saturation magnetization (Ms) and coercivity (Hc) were deduced. The Ms values are 54.4 emu·g–1 and 162 emu·g–1 for pure Ni and pure Co, respectively. Introducing increasing amounts of Co into Ni leads to a similar evolution of both Ms and Hc: lower (higher) Co contents lead to lower (higher) saturation magnetization and coercivity. For low cobalt contents (below 60–70%), the Ms and Hc values remain almost constant, whereas they significantly increase for richer Co compositions in which cobalt is in the HCP phase. This HCP phase is known to have strong magneto-crystalline anisotropy, which considerably increases the value of the coercive field Hc. This trend was also observed by Qin, who studied the structure and magnetic properties of Co–Ni patterned nanowire arrays.

In the light of the evolution of film thickness shown in Figure (almost constant before 70%, then almost linear growth after 70%), we could think that magnetism interactions related to the alloy structure transition can influence this behavior and a link can be supposed between the thin oxy-hydroxide films grown on Co–Ni alloys and the ferromagnetic hardness of the underlying alloy. One might ask whether the ferromagnetism of the Co–Ni alloy influences the growth of certain oxide layer compounds due to their intrinsic magnetic properties. In ferro–antiferromagnetic heterostructures, the intrinsic strength of the antiferromagnetic (AFM) phase is critical in governing the magnitude of interfacial exchange coupling, exchange bias, and coercivity enhancement. Notably, when the ferromagnetic substrate is an alloy whose magnetization increases approximately linearly with the fraction of a constituent element, the interfacial exchange energy also increases linearly. In the ultrathin limit, this increased exchange coupling stabilizes AFM order over a larger thickness. Therefore, increasing the ferromagnetic moment of the substrate increases the effective critical thickness below which the AFM layer remains magnetically rigid.

Although thermodynamics and kinetics still govern overall layer formation, interfacial magnetic energy can promote the stabilization of a more robust AFM phase near the metal-oxide boundary.

The previous section, which concerned the characterization of passive layer compositions, highlights the presence of some antiferromagnetic phases, such as CoO, NiO, Co­(OH)2, and Ni­(OH)2, in the thin passive layers. Therefore, we can conclude that the growth of the passive layer, in terms of both thickness and chemical composition, is influenced by ferro–antiferromagnetic coupling between the oxy-hydroxide layer and the metallic substrate. Earlier work by Meiklejohn and Bean led to the discovery of exchange coupling between ferromagnets and antiferromagnets in oxidized fine cobalt powders, known as Co/CoO core–shell structures. These authors interpreted this phenomenon as exchange coupling between ferromagnets and antiferromagnets via their interface as depicted previously. This type of effect has been widely studied and applied, particularly since the late 1960s. For reference articles on magnetic interactions in low-dimensional systems, see the work of Néel or Cortie’s more recent work on interface effects in magnetic metal/metal-oxide thin films.

Though magnetism effects could explain the transition evolution of the oxidized film structure with the alloy composition, their expected magnitude suggests that they cannot be the dominant effect. Figure shows that an increase in cobalt content within the HCP phase is accompanied by a rise in the nickel compound Ni­(OH)2 and a decline in NiO­(OH) and NiO. Simultaneously, the concentrations of Co­(OH)2 and particularly the most AFM CoO increase, while the concentrations of CoO­(OH) and Co3O4 decrease. Concomitantly, Figure highlights a clear correlation between the oxide/hydroxide thickness and the crystallographic structure linked to cobalt content in the alloy. In the FCC regime domain (Co100Ni0 → Co70Ni30), the oxide thickness remains almost constant at approximately 2–3 nm despite an increase in cobalt content. Conversely, in the HCP regime (Co70Ni30 → Co100Ni0), the oxide thickness increases quasi-linearly up to approximately 5 nm.

The enhancement of cation transport in Co-rich HCP alloys is reported in the literature. It is attributed to the intrinsic characteristics of the HCP lattice and its structural defect. In HCP structures, the combination of anisotropic atomic planes and a defect-rich microstructure provides energetically favorable pathways for cation migration toward the metal/oxide interface, facilitating oxidized compounds growth. In contrast, FCC Ni-rich alloys exhibit a more isotropic lattice and lower intrinsic defect mobility, which limits cation transport and contributes to the formation of thinner, self-passivating oxide layers. First-principles calculations have shown that the formation energies and configurations of point defects, including vacancies and vacancy–oxygen complexes, differ significantly between HCP and FCC structures, affecting the availability of pathways for cation transport in the two structures.

Additionally, DFT studies of diffusion in HCP metals highlight strong directional anisotropy, where certain crystallographic directions provide lower-energy migration routes for atomic species, while diffusion in FCC Co or FCC Ni is largely governed by isotropic vacancy-mediated mechanisms, with fewer preferential pathways. Together, these structural and defect-related factors explain the facilitated cation transport in HCP Co-rich alloys relative to that in FCC Ni-rich alloys. This cation diffusion pathway difference justifies both the oxidized film thickness difference between FCC and HCP Co–Ni alloys and its increase with cobalt in Co-rich HCP alloys.

Conclusions

In this work, we investigated the thickness and composition of cobalt and nickel oxide films spontaneously formed on binary Co x Ni100–x , for x ∈ {0, 20, 50, 70, 90, 100}, as a function of cobalt content. Our results demonstrate that the structural transition from the FCC phase to the HCP phase, occurring near 70% Co, strongly affects both the composition and the thickness of the passivation layers. In particular, the transition to the HCP structure is accompanied by a pronounced increase in the CoO fraction within the passive film influenced by ferro/antiferromagnetic interactions with the substrate, as well as a significant thickening of the oxidized layer. While the oxide thickness remains nearly independent of composition within the FCC domain, it increases progressively with cobalt concentration in the HCP domain.

These observations indicate that the oxidation behavior of Co–Ni alloys is governed not only by alloy composition but also by the crystallographic structure. Although changes in magnetic properties and their influence on oxide nucleation may contribute to the observed trends, the enhanced cation diffusion associated with the HCP structure appears to be the primary factor controlling oxide growth. The presence of anisotropic diffusion pathways in the HCP lattice likely facilitates ionic transport, thereby promoting the formation of thicker oxidized layers on Co-rich alloys.

Overall, this study highlights the critical role of crystallographic structure in determining the growth and composition of passive oxide films on Co–Ni alloys and provides new insight into the mechanisms governing their oxidation behavior.
