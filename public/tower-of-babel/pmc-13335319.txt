A retrospective analysis of the correlation between vaginal microecology and high-risk HPV infection among 5727 women
Authors: Zhenghua Dong, Qin Jia, Ning Jin, Xixi Yuan, Xiaofei Zhang, Jiaqi Han, Ji Yang, Lingyun Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the association between vaginal microecological status and high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection, providing large-sample evidence for HR-HPV prevention/control and vaginal microecological imbalance intervention.MethodsClinical data of 5,727 women who underwent both HR-HPV and vaginal microecology testing in the Clinical Laboratory of the South Campus of Shanghai East Hospital (January 2023–November 2025) were retrospectively analyzed. Participants were divided into HR-HPV-positive (n = 503, 8.78%) and negative (n = 5,224, 91.22%) groups. Core microecological indicators (Lactobacillus abundance, pathogenic microorganisms, epithelial cells, etc.) were compared by chi-square test. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were performed with HR-HPV positivity, HPV16 positivity, and HPV18/45 positivity as outcomes, incorporating 10 independent variables.ResultsCompared with the HR-HPV-negative group, the positive group had significantly higher proportions of vaginal cleanliness grades Ⅲ (22.47%) and Ⅳ (20.87%) (χ²=22.25, P < 0.001), intermediate bacterial vaginosis (BV, 42.94%) and BV (10.74%) (χ²=32.87, P < 0.001), as well as mild aerobic vaginitis (AV, 28.63%) and severe AV (0.99%) (χ²=8.314, P < 0.05). Significant differences were also observed in miscellaneous bacteria positive rate (89.07%), clue cell positive rate (8.15%), and epithelial cell (2 + and 4+) proportion (28.23%) (χ²=45.82, 11.09, 11.34; all P < 0.05). Univariate analysis identified 7 significant variables for HR-HPV infection, and multivariate analysis confirmed 5 independent factors (age, Lactobacilli, epithelial cells, miscellaneous bacteria, white blood cells). Age and miscellaneous bacteria were associated with HPV16 infection, while Lactobacillus levels were significantly associated with HPV18/45 infection.ConclusionLarge-sample analysis confirms that HR-HPV infection is closely associated with vaginal microecological imbalance, mainly manifested by abnormal bacterial and fungal proliferation. Vaginal microecological disorder may be a risk factor for HR-HPV infection, and its regulation can assist in HR-HPV prevention and treatment.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13347-x.

Introduction

Vaginal microecology, HPV infection, and cervical lesions are closely interrelated. A balanced vaginal microenvironment enhances mucosal barrier function and local immunity, facilitating HPV clearance, whereas dysbiosis compromises these defenses and increases infection susceptibility [1]. In China, HR-HPV infection has been confirmed to be closely associated with approximately 90% of cervical squamous intraepithelial lesions (SIL) and 99% of cervical cancers [2]. Emerging evidence indicates that vaginal microbiota dysbiosis not only promotes various infections but also contributes to HR-HPV persistence and cervical lesion progression, potentially driving carcinogenesis through chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation [3]. Despite these advances, region-specific research remains limited—a gap addressed by a 2018 Kazakhstan study establishing the HPV research protocol for Central Asia [4]. Conventional vaginal microecology assays used in most previous studies offer poor sensitivity and resolution for quantifying specific microbial components, and large-sample clinical data based on the more precise double-fluorescence (DF) staining assay remain scarce in this field [5, 6]. Here, we retrospectively analyzed vaginal microecological indicators detected by DF staining assay in a large cohort, to identify specific markers associated with HR-HPV infection, aiming to inform clinical diagnostic and management strategies.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

We retrospectively enrolled 5,727 women who underwent concurrent HR-HPV and vaginal microecology testing at Shanghai East Hospital (South Campus) between January 2023 and November 2025. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) same-day sampling for both tests; (2) complete microecology data and definitive HR-HPV results; and (3) traceable medical records with complete demographic information. Exclusion criteria were incomplete core testing data or unverifiable key clinical information. This single-center retrospective cohort included 503 h-HPV-positive cases (age 17–74 years, mean 39.1 ± 12.5 years) and 5,224 h-HPV-negative controls (age 17–86 years, mean 41.4 ± 11.3 years). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Shanghai East Hospital (2023 Research Pre-Examination No. 021; 2024 Research Review No. 031; 2025 Research Review No. 064). Detection methods

HR-HPV detection

Cervical exfoliated cells were collected from the squamocolumnar junction and analyzed using the Panther System (HOLOGIC Gen-Probe, USA) with the Aptima HPV assay, which detects 14 h-HPV genotypes (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68). Testing was performed strictly according to manufacturer protocols. The positive group was defined as having one or more positive genotypes; the negative group comprised samples negative for all genotypes.

Results were automatically interpreted by system software based on Internal Control (IC) validation and the Sample/Cut-Off (S/CO) ratio—the ratio of sample signal to the cut-off value. Results were classified as negative, positive, or invalid. Invalid results (e.g., abnormal kinetic curves) required retesting.

DF staining

Vaginal secretion smears were analyzed using a DF staining system comprising staining, optical imaging, and image acquisition modules integrated with AI-powered software. Images were captured via digital scanning microscopy, and microbial and cellular components—including epithelial cells, white blood cells, Lactobacillus, fungi, miscellaneous bacteria, clue cells and Trichomonas—were identified using AI-assisted classification. Vaginal cleanliness was comprehensively assessed microscopically, with all results reviewed and confirmed by specialists.

Nugent and Donders scoring criteria

Nugent scoring [7]: Each item was scored based on the average number of each bacterial morphotype observed per 10 oil immersion fields: 1–3 indicated normal; 4–6, intermediate bacterial vaginosis (BV); and 7–10, definitive BV.

Donders scoring [8] (for aerobic vaginitis, AV): The Donders scoring system, which sums five parameters from vaginal wet mounts—Lactobacillus grading, leukocytes per high-power field (leukocytes/hpf), toxic granulated leukocytes, background flora, and parabasal cells—enables both accurate diagnosis of aerobic vaginitis (AV) and disease severity stratification. Scores of < 3 indicated no AV; 3–4, mild AV; 5–6, moderate AV; and > 6, severe AV.

Vaginal cleanliness criteria

It is important to note that Nugent scoring is the gold standard for BV, and Donders scoring is a specific method for diagnosing AV. The DF staining method used in this study to evaluate vaginal microecology represents one of the most widely accepted and routinely used approaches in gynecological practice, rather than a universally validated gold standard for vaginal microecological assessment.

Statistical methods

All detection results were analyzed using R statistical software. Intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, univariate logistic regression was performed to evaluate the crude associations between each variable and HR-HPV infection. Multivariate logistic regression that included all variables simultaneously was conducted to adjust for confounding effects and interactions among variables, and to identify independent risk factors for HR-HPV infection.

Results

Vaginal microecological status between groups

The HR-HPV-positive group exhibited significantly poorer vaginal cleanliness, with grades III and IV accounting for 22.47% and 20.87% of cases, respectively, compared with 19.39% and 14.53% in the negative group. By Nugent criteria, intermediate bacterial vaginosis (BV) and definitive BV were more prevalent in the HR-HPV-positive group (42.94% and 10.74%) than in controls (37.42% and 5.16%; P < 0.001). Similarly, donders scoring revealed higher rates of mild and severe aerobic vaginitis (AV) in the positive group (28.63% and 0.99% vs. 25.33% and 0.33%; P < 0.05). These findings indicate a compromised vaginal microenvironment in HR-HPV-infected women. Detailed results are presented in Fig. 2; Table 2.

Comparison of specific vaginal microecological indicators

The HR-HPV-positive group showed significantly higher rates of miscellaneous bacteria (89.07% vs. 83.33%), clue cells (8.15% vs. 4.75%), and epithelial cell proportions (2 + and 4+: 28.23% vs. 22.51%) compared with the negative group (all P < 0.05). Conversely, Lactobacillus abundance (≥ 2+) was lower in the positive group. Although the positive group exhibited higher Trichomonas positivity and white blood cell counts (2+–3+), these differences did not reach statistical significance. Comparison of fungal indicators

Significant differences in fungal indicators were observed between groups. Spores, budding spores, and hyphae represent distinct reproductive stages of Candida; thus, all three were compared. While the HR-HPV-positive group showed higher proportions of all morphotypes, only spores and hyphae reached statistical significance (P < 0.05). See Fig. 4; Table 4.

Univariate and multivariate analyses

Binary logistic regression was performed with HR-HPV status as the outcome (positive vs. negative). Among ten candidate variables, univariate analysis identified seven significantly associated with HR-HPV infection: age, Lactobacillus, epithelial cells, miscellaneous bacteria, clue cells, spores, and hyphae. Multivariate analysis subsequently confirmed five independent predictors: age, Lactobacillus, epithelial cells, miscellaneous bacteria, and white blood cells (Table 5). Stratified analyses revealed genotype-specific associations. For HPV16, both univariate and multivariate models identified age and miscellaneous bacteria as significant predictors (Supplementary Table 1). For HPV18/45, Lactobacillus was the sole significant factor in both analyses (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

In our previous study, we reported an association between vaginal microecology and HR-HPV infection using a novel DF staining assay, though the sample size was limited (n = 376) [6]. The present study expands this cohort to 5,727 cases, confirming our prior findings while identifying additional significant associations. Consistent with the earlier analysis, hyphae and epithelial cells differed significantly between HR-HPV-positive and negative patients. This larger cohort further revealed significant differences in vaginal cleanliness, clue cells, and spores, reinforcing the close relationship between vaginal microecological imbalance and HR-HPV infection.

Lactobacillus depletion induces vaginal microbial community (VMB) dysbiosis and immune dysregulation, promoting pathogenic anaerobe overgrowth. These bacteria secrete polyamines and bioactive molecules that upregulate proinflammatory cytokines and facilitate immune evasion, increasing susceptibility to sexually transmitted infections including HIV [9, 10]. VMB imbalance is also linked to elevated HPV risk, with meta-analytic evidence showing that non-Lactobacillus- or L. iners-dominated community state types correlate with higher HPV prevalence than L. crispatus-dominated types [11]. Specific bacterial taxa—including BVAB1, BVAB2, Sneathia, Prevotella, and Megasphaera—are more prevalent in HPV-positive women [12–14], while sialidase genes of Atopobium and Gardnerella vaginalis may serve as biomarkers for persistent HR-HPV infection [15]. Few studies have examined HR-HPV infection in relation to Nugent and Donders scores in large cohorts. Addressing this gap, we demonstrate that higher scores on both scales significantly associate with HR-HPV positivity, reinforcing clinical links between vaginal dysbiosis—specifically bacterial vaginosis and aerobic vaginitis—and HR-HPV infection.

A previous study of Hispanic women in Puerto Rico first simultaneously analyzed associations between cervicovaginal bacterial and fungal communities, HR-HPV infection, and cervical intraepithelial neoplasia [16]. It revealed that fungal community structure and diversity correlated significantly with HPV risk status and lesion severity, with distinct fungal biomarkers identified: Malassezia was significantly associated with HR-HPV infection, while Sporidiobolaceae and Saccharomyces correlated with atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASCUS). Consistent with these findings, our results demonstrated higher proportions of fungal spores, budding spores, and hyphae in the HR-HPV-positive group, with spores and hyphae reaching statistical significance (P < 0.05). This confirms a positive association between vaginal fungal colonization and HR-HPV infection, suggesting that microecological dysbiosis characterized by fungal overgrowth may constitute a risk factor for HR-HPV infection.

Trichomonas vaginalis (TV) is the most prevalent non-viral sexually transmitted infection worldwide, with an estimated 275 million annual infections [17]. It causes vaginitis and pelvic inflammatory disease and is associated with increased risks of HIV infection and cervical cancer [17, 18]. TV induces local inflammation through direct host epithelial cell interaction [17, 19]. However, we found no significant association between TV and HR-HPV infection. TV prevalence was 0.8% (4/503) in the HR-HPV-positive group versus 0.46% (24/5,224) in the negative group (χ²=0.49, P = 0.486), consistent with prior reports. Other study also showed that trichomonas infection is not statistically associated with HR-HPV infection [20]. Clinical consensus similarly emphasizes that TV lacks a direct causal relationship with HPV, and routine TV screening is not recommended for HPV-positive women without specific symptoms [21]. The precise relationship between TV and HPV thus warrants further investigation.

We found marginally higher Lactobacillus abundance in the HR-HPV-negative group, though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.153). Multivariate analysis nonetheless identified Lactobacillus as a protective factor against HR-HPV and HPV18/45 infection, supporting the consensus that reduced Lactobacillus abundance—the dominant vaginal flora—impairs mucosal barrier function and increases HR-HPV susceptibility. Three factors may explain this apparent discrepancy. First, we measured total Lactobacillus abundance without distinguishing functionally distinct subtypes; L. crispatus exhibits potent antiviral effects, whereas L. iners provides weaker protection, and such variation may obscure true associations. Second, limited statistical power may have precluded detection of subtle differences, warranting validation in larger cohorts. Third, HR-HPV positivity may relate more to Lactobacillus dysfunction (e.g., inflammation-impaired lactic acid production) than to reduced abundance per se, suggesting that abundance alone inadequately reflects its role in vaginal homeostasis [10, 22, 23].

We incorporated age as an independent variable in regression analyses. Age emerged as a significant risk factor for HR-HPV and HPV16 infection, but not for HPV18/45. Aging reduces estrogen levels, potentially decreasing Lactobacillus abundance and disrupting vaginal microecology, thereby theoretically increasing HR-HPV susceptibility. However, our older HR-HPV-negative participants exhibited lower infection rates, suggesting that age effects are confounded by sexual activity, lifestyle, and other factors with complex interactions. Younger women experience higher HR-HPV exposure due to more active sexual behavior and less stable vaginal microecology, which may amplify the association between dysbiosis and HR-HPV infection.

Commonly used vaginal microecological detection methods include traditional wet smear microscopy, dry chemical enzymatic assay, Gram staining, and automated fluorescence staining. The wet smear is simple and rapid for outpatient use but highly subjective, prone to misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis due to manual observation and visual fatigue, and lacks specimen traceability [24, 25]. Dry chemical enzymatic assay is easy to operate but requires incubation, is susceptible to interference, and shows unsatisfactory timeliness and accuracy [26]. Gram staining is relatively reliable but involves complex, time-consuming procedures and high operator dependence, making it unsuitable for outpatient screening [25, 27]. In contrast, DF staining combined with AI intelligent recognition enables distinct color visualization of cellular and pathogenic components, minimizes subjectivity, improves detection sensitivity especially for inactive trichomonads, resists interference from contaminated specimens, and significantly enhances overall accuracy and positivity rate for clinical application. Consequently, our results reflect tendential rather than exact abundance changes, and precise quantification with species-specific analysis requires validation using complementary molecular methods.

This study has several limitations. We did not systematically collect key covariates—including sexual history, contraceptive use, menstrual history, hygiene practices, smoking, antibiotic use, and parity—that are established determinants of vaginal microecology and HR-HPV risk [1, 28, 29]. Recent antibiotic use disrupts vaginal microecology, elevating HPV infection risk. Conversely, HPV vaccination provides active protection, with earlier administration conferring superior efficacy. Systemic infections—whether acute or chronic—impair HPV clearance through immune suppression and dysregulation. HIV exacerbates HPV persistence and carcinogenic risk via CD4 + T-cell depletion, though antiretroviral therapy enables partial immune reconstitution [30]. Long COVID similarly compromises HPV clearance and vaccine immunogenicity through sustained immune dysregulation [31]. These factors operate synergistically, compounding HPV-related risks when present in combination. Their omission precluded confounding adjustment and interaction analysis, limiting interpretation and generalizability. Nonetheless, this large retrospective study confirms a close association between HR-HPV infection and vaginal dysbiosis, driven predominantly by pathogenic bacterial and fungal overgrowth. These findings suggest that vaginal microecological modulation may serve as a complementary prevention strategy. Longitudinal studies and mechanistic investigations are warranted to elucidate the vaginal microbiota’s role in HR-HPV susceptibility and cervical cancer control.
