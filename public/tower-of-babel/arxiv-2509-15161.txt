Discrete measured groupoid von Neumann algebras via the Gaussian deformation
Felipe Flores & James Harbour

Abstract
Given a discrete measured groupoid 𝒢 \mathcal{G} , we study properties of the corresponding von Neumann algebra L ⁡ ( 𝒢 ) L(\mathcal{G}) using the techniques of Popa’s deformation/rigidity theory. More specifically, we define and study the Gaussian deformation associated with any 1 1 -cocycle of 𝒢 \mathcal{G} and use it to prove primeness and fullness under appropriate assumptions. We also characterize the maximal rigid subalgebras of L ⁡ ( 𝒢 ) L(\mathcal{G}) and produce unique prime factorization results for algebras of the form L ⁡ ( 𝒢 1 × … × 𝒢 n ) L(\mathcal{G}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{G}_{n}) .

Introduction

Since its birth, Popa’s deformation/rigidity theory has been extremely useful for the endeavor of obtaining structural properties in von Neumann algebras, especially for group von Neumann algebras. These properties include (but are not limited to)
W^{*}
-superrigidity
[
27
,
9
]
, primeness
[
42
,
47
,
24
]
, product rigidity, non-existence/existence and uniqueness of Cartan subalgebras
[
53
]
and many more. The idea behind this article is to extend this approach -by means of the Gaussian deformation- to the far wider context of groupoid von Neumann algebras, in the hopes of extending older results and possibly finding new phenomena.

One of the structural properties that one often studies using deformation/rigidity is that of primeness. A type
\mathrm{II}
von Neumann algebra is said to be prime if one cannot write it as the tensor product of two type
\mathrm{II}
von Neumann algebras. The first primeness result was obtained by Popa
[
48
]
, where he showed that the group von Neumann algebra of an uncountably-generated free group is prime. Later, in
[
23
]
using Voiculescu’s work on free entropy dimension, Ge proves that all group factors coming from finitely generated free groups are prime. Then Ozawa, using
C^{*}
-algebra techniques, greatly generalizes this to show that all i.c.c. hyperbolic groups give rise to prime factors
[
38
]
. Using deformation/rigidity, Popa showed that all
\mathrm{II}_{1}
factors arising from Bernoulli actions of nonamenable groups are prime
[
47
]
. Peterson used his derivation approach to deformation/rigidity
[
42
]
to prove that any
\mathrm{II}_{1}
factor coming from a countable group with positive first
\ell^{2}
-Betti number is also prime. More recent results on primeness can be found in the work of Hoff
[
24
]
on equivalence relations, or in the works of Marrakchi
[
35
]
and Patchell
[
40
]
on generalized wreath products.

After primeness, the natural property to study seems to be that of decomposition into prime factors. The idea is to find a decomposition
M=P_{1}\overline{\otimes}P_{2}\overline{\otimes}\ldots\overline{\otimes}P_{n}
, which is unique in the sense that if we have any other decomposition
M={Q_{1}}\overline{\otimes}{Q_{2}}\overline{\otimes}\ldots\overline{\otimes}{Q_{m}}
, then
n=m
and
P_{i}
has to be unitarily conjugated to
Q_{i}
, modulo permutation of indices and amplifications. A first result like this was obtained by Ozawa and Popa in
[
39
]
, where a combination of Ozawa’s solidity results
[
38
]
and Popa’s intertwining techniques
[
49
]
is used to show that any
\mathrm{II}_{1}
factor arising from a tensor product of hyperbolic group factors has such a unique tensor product decomposition. After this landmark discovery, several other unique prime factorization results have appeared, for example, in the works of Sizemore and Winchester
[
52
]
and Houdayer and Isono
[
26
]
. It has also led to the study of product rigidity (see
[
17
]
and the references therein).

Besides our results on primeness and prime factorizations, we also prove fullness of a fairly large class of factors. Fullness of von Neumann algebras was introduced by Connes in
[
11
]
. We recall that a von Neumann algebra
M
is called
full
if the inner automorphism group
{\rm Inn}(M)
is closed in the automorphism group
{\rm Aut}(M)
with respect to the
u
-topology. For any factor
M
with separable predual,
M
is full if and only if every norm-bounded asymptotically central sequence of
M
is asymptotically trivial. Effros showed that the group von Neumann algebra of a non-inner amenable countably infinite group is a full factor
[
18
]
. A little later, Choda showed that the crossed products
L^{\infty}(X)\rtimes G
, associated with the same class of groups, are full factors as soon as the action
G\curvearrowright X
is strongly ergodic, essentially free and probability measure preserving
[
10
]
. Some other notable results about fullness can be found in the work of Houdayer and Isono on non-singular actions of bi-exact groups
[
25
]
, which Ozawa later generalized to a bigger class of actions
[
37
]
, Hoff’s paper on equivalence relation algebras
[
24
]
or the paper of Marrakchi on crossed products with factors
[
34
]
.

Our main tool in this article is the Gaussian deformation, which we defined for discrete measured groupoids, directly extending previous approaches of Sinclair
[
51
]
, Peterson and Sinclair
[
43
]
and Hoff
[
24
]
. The Gaussian deformation has also appeared in work by Vaes
[
53
]
, de Santiago, Hayes, Hoff, Sinclair
[
14
]
and de Santiago, DeBonis, and Khan
[
13
]
in slightly different fashions. Hoff’s work
[
24
]
is of particular relevance to us, as the study of equivalence relations naturally precedes the study of general groupoids. The study of general groupoids, however, is a little more involved, and many tools become unavailable. Particular but notable examples include the structural results obtained by Feldman and Moore
[
19
]
and by Connes, Feldman, and Weiss
[
12
]
. The reader will notice that the unavailability of these results or appropriate analogs was an obstacle that needed to be circumvented (see, for example, the discussion preceding Lemma
2.27
or Theorem
6.6
and its proof).

In any case, we applied this deformation to obtain our first two main theorems, which are the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a nonamenable ergodic discrete measured groupoid that admits a strongly unbounded 1-cocycle into a mixing orthogonal representation weakly contained in the regular representation. Then
L(\mathcal{G})
is prime. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{G}
is strongly ergodic and
L(\mathcal{G})
is a factor, then it is also a full factor.

Theorem 1.2

(Corollary
7.2
)
.

For
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
, let
\mathcal{G}_{i}
be a nonamenable strongly ergodic discrete measured groupoid that admits a strongly unbounded
1
-cocycle into a mixing orthogonal representation weakly contained in the regular representation. Further assume that
L(\mathcal{G}_{i})
is a factor. Then
M=L(\mathcal{G}_{1}{\times}\mathcal{G}_{2}{\times}\dots{\times}\mathcal{G}_{k})
satisfies the following.

(i)

If
M=N\overline{\otimes}Q
for tracial factors
N,Q
, there must be a partition
I_{N}\cup I_{Q}=\{1,\dots,k\}
and
t>0
such that
N^{t}=\bigotimes_{i\in I_{N}}L(\mathcal{G}_{i})
and
Q^{1/t}=\bigotimes_{i\in I_{Q}}L(\mathcal{G}_{i})
modulo unitary conjugacy in
M
.

(ii)

If
M=P_{1}\overline{\otimes}P_{2}\overline{\otimes}\cdots\overline{\otimes}P_{m}
for
\rm{II}_{1}
factors
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
and
m\geq k
, then
m=k
, each
P_{i}
is prime, and there are
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}>0
with
t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{k}=1
such that after reordering indices and conjugating by a unitary in
M
we have
L(\mathcal{G}_{i})=P_{i}^{t_{i}}
for all
i
.

(iii)

In (ii), the assumption
m\geq k
can be omitted if each
P_{i}
is assumed to be prime.

As the reader can tell, our theorems are proven for the class of groupoids that admit “strongly unbounded”
1
-cocycles, whose definition is a technical strengthening of the (regular) definition of unbounded
1
-cocycles. Let us mention that these two notions coincide in many situations. In the case of transformation groupoids, every unbounded
1
-cocycle induced by the group is strongly unbounded. Moreover, every unbounded
1
-cocycle is strongly unbounded if the groupoid is a group bundle, an equivalence relation or, more generally, a semidirect product groupoid. A discrete measured groupoid is automatically a semidirect product groupoid when the equivalence relation associated with the groupoid is treeable (which includes the case of hyperfinite equivalence relations). This property makes the class of semidirect product groupoids very desirable and it is one of the reasons why they have been gaining some popularity in recent times (see
[
50
,
6
]
). Let us also mention that the study of treeability is of central importance in measured group theory (see
[
21
]
) and percolation theory (see
[
8
, Section 4.2]
).

Our next result makes use of the framework of maximal rigid subalgebras developed in
[
14
]
to produce a von Neumann algebra-based generalization of a famous result of Peterson and Thom
[
44
, Theorem 5.6, Corollary 5.8]
. This result was obtained for group algebras by de Santiago, Hayes, Hoff, and Sinclair
[
14
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
8.5
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ergodic discrete measured groupoid and
\pi
a weak mixing, orthogonal representation of
\mathcal{G}
on a real Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{G}
admits an unbounded 1-cocycle into
\pi
and that
L(\mathcal{G})
is diffuse. Then, for any pair
P,Q\subset L(\mathcal{G})
of subalgebras with property (T) such that
P\cap Q
is diffuse, we have that
P\vee Q\not=L(\mathcal{G})
.

Restricting ourselves to the setting of transformation groupoids yields results about crossed products (also called ‘group measure space von Neumann algebras’), which are interesting by themselves. In such a case, we require that the acting group admits an unbounded
1
-cocycle into a mixing orthogonal representation weakly contained in the regular representation, as this induces a strongly unbounded
1
-cocycle on the transformation groupoid. The main examples of such groups are those groups with positive first
\ell^{2}
-Betti number. Peterson and Thom noted that the groups with positive first
\ell^{2}
-Betti number are exactly those that admit an unbounded
1
-cocycle into the (real) left regular representation
[
44
]
. Examples of such groups contain finitely generated free groups, groups which are measure equivalent to them, some one-relator groups (see
[
15
]
) and surface groups. For these groups and their crossed products, we obtained the following corollaries, which are a direct application of the previously stated theorems.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
G
be a countable group that admits an unbounded
1
-cocycle into a mixing orthogonal representation weakly contained in the regular representation, and let
G\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
be an ergodic, nonamenable probability measure preserving action. Then
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes G
is prime. Furthermore, if the action is strongly ergodic and
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes G
is a factor (immediate if
G
is i.c.c. or if the action is free), then
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes G
is also a full factor.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
G
be a countable group that admits an unbounded
1
-cocycle into a weak mixing orthogonal representation weakly contained in the regular representation, and let
G\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
be an ergodic probability measure preserving action. Suppose that
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes G
is diffuse. Then, for any pair
P,Q\subset L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes G
of subalgebras with property (T) such that
P\cap Q
is diffuse, we have that
P\vee Q\not=L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes G
.

It is worth noting that a crossed product
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes G
is diffuse in most cases of interest. For example, and because of the ergodicity assumption, this is the case if the action is ergodic,
G
is infinite and
\mu
is not supported in a finite set.

As a more concrete example, we found the following primeness result for generalized Bernoulli shifts. For the groups that fit our setting, it generalizes the results of Patchell
[
40
, Theorem 1.3]
and of Marrakchi
[
35
]
.

Corollary 1.6

(Corollary
5.4
)
.

Let
G
be a countable group which admits an unbounded
1
-cocycle into a mixing orthogonal representation weakly contained in the regular representation, and let
G\curvearrowright(Y^{I},\nu^{\otimes I})
be a generalized Bernoulli shift. Further suppose that
G\curvearrowright I
is nonamenable. Then
L^{\infty}(Y,\mu)^{I}\rtimes G
is a prime factor.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
contains preliminaries that include the general definitions involving discrete measured groupoids and their von Neumann algebras, unitary/orthogonal representations,

5
is devoted to obtaining our primeness result. Section

1.1

7

[
24
]
and its application which, due to the previous work, immediately yields Theorem
1.2
. Finally, in Section
8

1.3
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1 Discrete measured groupoids

We will work with groupoids
\mathcal{G}
over a unit space
X\equiv\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, identified as small categories in which all the morphisms (arrows) are invertible.
The domain and range maps are denoted by
{\rm d,r}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and the family of composable pairs by
\,\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\!\subset\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
. For
x\in\mathcal{G}\,,\,A,B\subset\mathcal{G}
we use the notations

AB\coloneqq\{ab\,:\,a\in A,b\in B,{\rm d}(a)={\rm r}(b)\},

(2.1)

Ax\coloneqq A\{x\}\quad\textup{and}\quad xA\coloneqq\{x\}A

These sets could be void in non-trivial situations. A subset of the unit space
Y\subset\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is called
invariant
if
Y={\rm r}(\mathcal{G}Y)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{G}
is a groupoid equipped with the structure of a standard Borel space
such that the composition and the inverse map are Borel and

{\rm d}^{-1}(\{x\})
is countable for all
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Then the domain and range maps are measurable,
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\subset\mathcal{G}
is
a Borel subset, and
{\rm d}^{-1}(\{x\})
is countable.

Now let
\mu
be a probability measure on
the set of units
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Then,
for any measurable subset
A\subset\mathcal{G}
, the function

\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\ni x\mapsto\#\bigl({\rm d}^{-1}(x)\cap A\bigr)

is measurable, and the measure
\mu_{\rm d}
on
\mathcal{G}
defined by

\mu_{\rm d}(A)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\#\bigl({\rm d}^{-1}(x)\cap A\bigr)d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\#\bigl(Ax)d\mu(x)

is
\sigma
-finite. The measure
\mu_{\rm r}
is defined in an analogous manner, replacing
{\rm d}
by
{\rm r}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
i:x\to x^{-1}
be the inversion map in
\mathcal{G}
. The following conditions on
\mu
are equivalent.

1.

\mu_{\rm d}=\mu_{\rm r}
,

2.

i_{*}\mu_{\rm d}=\mu_{\rm d}
,

3.

for every Borel subset
E\subset\mathcal{G}
such that

{\rm d}|_{E}
and
{\rm r}|_{E}
are injective we have

\mu({\rm d}(E))=\mu({\rm r}(E))
.

Such a probability measure is called
invariant
and we denote
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}=\mu_{\rm r}=\mu_{\rm d}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A discrete, measurable groupoid
\mathcal{G}
together with an invariant
probability measure
on
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is
called a
discrete measured groupoid
.

For
A\subset\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
one uses the standard notations

\mathcal{G}_{A}\!\coloneqq{\rm d}^{-1}(A)\,,\quad\mathcal{G}^{A}\!\coloneqq{\rm r}^{-1}(A)\,,\quad\mathcal{G}_{A}^{A}\coloneqq\mathcal{G}_{A}\cap\mathcal{G}^{A}.

If
A
is Borel, then we equip
\mathcal{G}_{A}^{A}
with the normalized measure
\frac{1}{\mu(A)}\mu|_{A}
, so it becomes
a discrete measured groupoid, called the
restriction
of

\mathcal{G}
to
A
and is denoted by
\mathcal{G}|_{A}
. Note that every unit
x\in X
gives rise to a subgroup
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{x}
, called an
isotropy subgroup
. The union of all isotropy subgroups is naturally a group bundle, we denote it by
{\rm Iso}(\mathcal{G})
and call it the
isotropy subgroupoid
of
\mathcal{G}
.

As usual, the set of complex-valued, measurable, essentially bounded functions (modulo almost everywhere null functions) on
\mathcal{G}
with respect to
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}
is denoted by
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G},\mu_{\mathcal{G}})
. For a function
\phi:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we put

\displaystyle D(\phi)(x)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{g\in\mathcal{G}:\phi(g)\neq 0,{\rm d}(g)=x\right\},

\displaystyle R(\phi)(x)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{g\in\mathcal{G}:\phi(g)\neq 0,{\rm r}(g)=x\right\}.

The
groupoid ring

\mathbb{C}\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{G}
is defined as

\mathbb{C}\mathcal{G}=\left\{\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G},\mu_{\mathcal{G}}):\text{$D(\phi)$ and $R(\phi)$ are essentially bounded on $\mathcal{G}^{(0)}$}\right\}.

\mathbb{C}\mathcal{G}
is a
∗
-algebra containing
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\equiv L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{(0)},\mu)
as a
∗
-subalgebra. Multiplication is given by the convolution product

\phi*\eta(x)=\sum_{yz=x}\phi(y)\eta(z)

and the involution is defined by

\phi^{*}(x)=\overline{\phi(x^{-1})}.

The groupoid ring
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{G}
of discrete measured groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is a weakly dense
∗
-subalgebra in the
von Neumann algebra

L(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{G}
. In fact,
L(\mathcal{G})
is defined as the WOT-closure of
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{G}
, when the latter is
∗
-represented as convolution operators

L_{\psi}(\xi)(x)=\psi*\xi(x)=\sum_{yz=x}\psi(y)\xi(z),\quad\textup{ for }\psi\in\mathbb{C}\mathcal{G},\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mu_{\mathcal{G}}).

The von Neumann algebra
L(\mathcal{G})
has a canonical tracial state
{\sf tr}_{L(\mathcal{G})}
, which is induced by the invariant measure
\mu
. For
\phi\in\mathbb{C}\mathcal{G}\subset L(\mathcal{G})
, it is given by the formula

{\sf tr}_{L(\mathcal{G})}(\phi)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\phi(x)d\mu(x).

Definition 2.3
.

A (Borel)
bisection
of
\mathcal{G}
is a Borel subset
\sigma\subseteq\mathcal{G}
such that the sets
\sigma x
and
x\sigma
have at most
1
element for every
x\in G^{0}
. Borel bisections form an inverse semigroup with respect to the operation on sets described in (
2.1
).

The
full pseudogroup

[[\mathcal{G}]]
of
\mathcal{G}
is the inverse semigroup consisting of Borel bisections modulo the relation of being equal almost everywhere. The
full group

[\mathcal{G}]
is the subset of
[[\mathcal{G}]]
consisting of the Borel bisections
\sigma
such that
\sigma\sigma^{-1}=\sigma^{-1}\sigma=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. When
\sigma\in[\mathcal{G}]
,
\sigma x
and
x\sigma
have exactly one element, so we identify them with said element.

For any pseudogroup element
\sigma\in[[\mathcal{G}]]
, we define the partial isometry
u_{\sigma}:=L_{1_{\sigma}}\in L(\mathcal{G})
as left convolution by the indicator function of
\sigma\subseteq\mathcal{G}
. When
\sigma
is a full group element,
u_{\sigma}
is a unitary in
L(\mathcal{G})
.

Definition 2.4

(
[
3
]
)
.

The groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is called
amenable
if there exists a norm one projection
M:L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G},\mu_{\mathcal{G}})\to L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
with
M(\phi*\psi)=\phi*M(\psi)
for all
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{G},\mu_{\mathcal{G}})
, and all Borel functions
\phi
on
\mathcal{G}
such that
\sup_{x\in X}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}^{x}}|\phi(g)|<\infty
.

It is known that a discrete measured groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is amenable if and only if its von Neumann algebra
L(\mathcal{G})
is amenable
[
3
, Corollary 6.2.12]
. Moreover, we say that
\mathcal{G}
has an amenable direct summand if there is a measurable subset
Y\subset X
such that
\mu(Y)>0
,
\mathcal{G}|_{Y}
is amenable, and
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}|_{Y}\cup\mathcal{G}|_{Y^{c}}
. In this case,
L(\mathcal{G})
has an amenable direct summand as well.

We now turn our attention to some of the main examples of discrete measured groupoids and their von Neumann algebras. Subsection
2.2
will introduce another important class of examples. For more examples, we recommend looking at
[
7
]
or at the last section of
[
5
]
.

Example 2.5
.

Suppose
G
is a countable group acting (in a measure-preserving way) on the standard probability space
(X,\mu)
, denoted by
g\cdot x\equiv g\cdot_{\theta}x
, for
g\in G,x\in X
. The
transformation groupoid

\mathcal{G}\coloneqq X\rtimes_{\theta}G
has
G\times X
as the underlying set. The multiplication is

(g,x)(h,g^{-1}\cdot x)\coloneqq(gh,x)

and inversion reads

(g,x)^{-1}\coloneqq\big(g^{-1}\!,g^{-1}\cdot x\big).

So
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}=\{1_{G}\}\times X
gets identified with
X
, so
{\rm r}(g,x)=x
and
{\rm d}(g,x)=g^{-1}\cdot x
. In this case,
L(\mathcal{G})\cong L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes_{\theta}G
with its usual trace. In particular, if
X=\{x_{0}\}
is a singleton, this construction gives the group algebra
L(G)
again with its usual trace.

Definition 2.6
.

We will say that the action
G\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
is
amenable
, or even
Zimmer amenable
if the associated transformation groupoid
X\rtimes G
is amenable.

Example 2.7
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a standard probability space and
\mathcal{R}\subset X\times X
be an equivalence relation which is a measurable subset.
\mathcal{R}
becomes a discrete measured groupoid with the operations

{\rm d}(x,y)=(y,y)\,,\quad{\rm r}(x,y)=(x,x)\,,\quad(x,y)(y,z)=(x,z)\,,\quad(x,y)^{-1}\!=(y,x)\,.

The unit space is
\mathcal{R}^{(0)}={\sf Diag}(X)
and we identify it with
X
, via the map
(x,x)\mapsto x
. We say that
\mathcal{R}
is measure preserving if the resulting groupoid is a discrete measured groupoid. The algebra
L(\mathcal{R})
introduced here coincides with the usual equivalence relation von Neumann algebra (typically introduced using the full pseudogroup). For example, see
[
24
, Section 2.2]
.

Remark 2.8
.

A particular way to obtain equivalence relations is via group actions. Namely, if
G
is a countable group acting in a measure-preserving way on the probability space
(X,\mu)
, we can form the equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}_{G,X}
given by the orbits of the action, more explicitly

\mathcal{R}_{G,X}=\{(x,y)\in X\times X:y=g\cdot x,\textup{ for some }g\in G\},

with the inherited Borel structure of
X\times X
. It is a famous result of Feldman and Moore that every discrete measured equivalence relation can be realized as
\mathcal{R}_{G,X}
for some probability measure-preserving group action
[
19
, Theorem 1]
.

It is worth noting that, when the action is essentially free (meaning that for almost all
x\in X
, the stabilizer of
x
is trivial), the groupoids
\mathcal{R}_{G,X}
and
X\rtimes_{\theta}G
are isomorphic up to a measure zero set and so they generate the same von Neumann algebra (cf.
[
20
]
).

Definition 2.9
.

A discrete measured groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is called
ergodic
if
\mu(Y)\in\{0,1\}
for every Borel invariant subset
Y\subset\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
.

Let
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)^{\mathcal{G}}
denote the algebra of
L^{\infty}
functions fixed by
\mathcal{G}
. That is, every
a\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)^{\mathcal{G}}
satisfies

a(\sigma^{-1}x)=a(x)\text{ for almost all }x\in X\text{ and all }\sigma\in[\mathcal{G}].

It is clear that
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)^{\mathcal{G}}=\mathbb{C}1
if and only if
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic. We also note that
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)^{\mathcal{G}}
lies in the center
\mathcal{Z}(L(\mathcal{G}))=L(\mathcal{G})^{\prime}\cap L(\mathcal{G})
of
L(\mathcal{G})
. Therefore, the ergodicity of
\mathcal{G}
is necessary for
L(\mathcal{G})
to be a factor. In a recent preprint, Berendschot, Chakraborty, Donvil and Kim characterize the groupoids that give a factor
[
5
]
by introducing and studying the notion of
i.c.c.
groupoids.

We will deal mostly with ergodic groupoids, so it seems convenient to examine when our examples satisfy this condition.

Remark 2.10
.

The transformation groupoid introduced in Example
2.5
is ergodic precisely when the action
\theta
is ergodic. Moreover, note that, for
(g,x)\in\mathcal{G}
and
y\in Y
with
{\rm d}(g,x)=y
, we have

{\rm r}\big((g,x)(1_{G},y)\big)={\rm r}\big((g,x)\big)=x=g\cdot_{\theta}y,

so
{\rm r}(\mathcal{G}Y)=G\cdot_{\theta}Y
and
X\rtimes_{\theta}G
is ergodic if and only if
\mu(G\cdot_{\theta}Y)\in\{0,1\}
for every Borel subset
Y\subset X
. In particular, every group is an ergodic groupoid.

Remark 2.11
.

In the case of equivalence relation groupoids
\mathcal{R}
(Example
2.7
), we have

{\rm r}\big((x,y)(y,y)\big)={\rm r}(x,y)=x,

so
{\rm r}(\mathcal{G}Y)=\{x\in X:x\sim_{\mathcal{R}}y,\textup{ for some }y\in Y\}=:[Y]_{\mathcal{R}}
and
\mathcal{R}
is ergodic if
\mu([Y]_{\mathcal{R}})\in\{0,1\}
, for every Borel subset
Y\subset X
.

2.2 Semidirect product groupoids

A discrete measured groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\mu)
comes with an associated discrete Borel equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}
, defined on
(X,\mu)
with
X=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. It is, in fact, given by

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq\{({\rm r}(g),{\rm d}(g)):g\in\mathcal{G}\}.

It also comes with a canonical quotient map

q:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}},\quad\text{given by}\quad q(g)=\big({\rm r}(g),{\rm d}(g)\big).

The purpose of this section is to introduce a special class of groupoids, called semidirect product groupoids. These could be characterized as the groupoids for which the above-mentioned quotient map admits a right-inverse measurable map
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathcal{G}
which is also a homomorphism.

In what follows, we understand a group bundle
\Gamma=(G_{x})_{x\in X}
simply as a Borel groupoid where the maps
{\rm d}
and
{\rm r}
coincide. In this case, the group
G_{x}
is precisely the isotropy subgroup associated to the unit
x\in X
.

Now consider any discrete Borel equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}
on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
, and a group bundle of discrete countable groups
\Gamma\coloneqq(G_{x})_{x\in X}
. An action of
\mathcal{R}
on
\Gamma
is given by a family of group isomorphisms
\delta_{(y,x)}:G_{x}\rightarrow G_{y}
for all
(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\delta_{(z,y)}\circ\delta_{(y,x)}=\delta_{(z,x)}
,
\delta_{(y,x)}^{-1}=\delta_{(x,y)}
, and
\delta{(x,x)}=\text{id}_{G_{x}}
hold
\mu
-almost everywhere.

In the above setting, one defines the
semidirect product groupoid

\mathcal{G}=\Gamma\rtimes_{\delta}\mathcal{R}
as the set

\{((y,x),g)\in\mathcal{R}\times\Gamma\mid g\in G_{x}\text{ and }(y,x)\in\mathcal{R}\},

equipped with the natural measurable structure and the operations

((z,y),h)\circ((y,x),g)=((z,x),\delta_{(x,y)}(h)\cdot g)

and

((y,x),g)^{-1}=((x,y),\delta_{(y,x)}(g^{-1}))\,.

It then follows that the domain and range maps can be described by the projections
{\rm d}((y,x),g)=x
and
{\rm r}((y,x),g)=y
, after the -natural- identification of the unit space
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}=\{((x,x),1_{G_{x}})\in\mathcal{R}\times\Gamma:x\in X\}
with
X
.

Recall now that a discrete Borel equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}
on
(X,\mu)
is called
treeable
if there exists a Borel graph
T
on
(X,\mu)
such that
T\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and if for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
the connected component of
x
in
T
is a tree with vertex set
T\cdot x
. Hyperfinite equivalence relations provide the easiest example of treeable equivalence relations (cf.
[
12
]
). In
[
50
, Proposition 6.5]
, Popa-Shlyakhtenko-Vaes proved the following proposition, of great utility for us.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a discrete measured equivalence relation on the standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. The following two statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{R}
is treeable.

(ii)

Any discrete measured groupoid
\mathcal{G}
with
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}\cong\mathcal{R}
is isomorphic to a semidirect product groupoid.

2.3 Unitary representations and
1
-cohomology

Given a collection of Hilbert spaces
\{\mathcal{H}_{x}\}_{x\in X}
, the Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
is the set of pairs
X\ast\mathcal{H}=\{(x,\xi_{x}):x\in X,\xi_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{x}\}
.
A section
\xi
of
X\ast\mathcal{H}
is a map
x\mapsto\xi_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{x}
.

A
measurable Hilbert bundle
is a Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
endowed with a
\sigma
-algebra generated by the maps
\{(x,\xi_{x})\mapsto\langle\xi_{x},\xi^{n}_{x}\rangle\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
for a
fundamental sequence of sections

\{\xi^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfying

(i)

\mathcal{H}_{x}=\overline{{\rm span}\{\xi^{n}_{x}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}}
for each
x\in X
, and

(ii)
the maps
\{x\mapsto\|\xi^{n}_{x}\|\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
are measurable.

It is a useful fact that the
\sigma
-algebra of any measurable Hilbert bundle can be generated by an
orthonormal fundamental sequence of sections
, i.e. sections which moreover satisfy

(iii)

\{\xi^{n}_{x}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
for
x\in X
with
\dim\mathcal{H}_{x}=\infty
, and if
\dim\mathcal{H}_{x}<\infty
, the sequence
\{\xi^{n}_{x}\}_{n=1}^{\dim\mathcal{H}_{x}}
is an orthonormal basis and
\xi^{n}_{x}=0
for
n>\dim\mathcal{H}_{x}
.

A
measurable section
of
X\ast\mathcal{H}
is a section
\xi
such that
x\mapsto(x,\xi_{x})\in X\ast\mathcal{H}
is a measurable map, or equivalently, such that the maps
\{x\mapsto\langle\xi_{x},\xi^{n}_{x}\rangle\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
are measurable for the fundamental sequence of sections
\{\xi^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. We let
S(X\ast\mathcal{H})
denote the vector space of measurable sections, identifying
\mu
-a.e. equal sections. It is also useful to reserve some notation for the sections with constant norm:

S_{1}(X\ast\mathcal{H})=\{\xi\in S(X\ast\mathcal{H}):\norm{\xi_x}_{\mathcal{H}_{x}}=1\textup{ a.e.}\}.

The elements in
S_{1}(X\ast\mathcal{H})
are called
normalized
sections. As hinted, we will often abuse the notation and confuse the map
x\mapsto(x,\xi_{x})
with
\xi
. We then consider the
direct integral

\displaystyle\int_{X}^{\oplus}\mathcal{H}_{x}d\mu(x)=\{\xi\in S(X\ast\mathcal{H}):\int_{X}\|\xi(x)\|^{2}d\mu(x)<\infty\}

which is a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\xi,\eta\rangle=\int_{X}\langle\xi_{x},\eta_{x}\rangle d\mu(x)
. If
a\in L^{\infty}(X)
and
\xi\in\int_{X}^{\oplus}\mathcal{H}_{x}d\mu(x)
we denote by
a\xi
or
\xi a
the element of
\int_{X}^{\oplus}\mathcal{H}_{x}d\mu(x)
given by
[a\xi](x)=[\xi a](x)=a(x)\xi_{x}
. If
\{\xi^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal fundamental sequence of sections, any
\xi\in\int_{X}^{\oplus}\mathcal{H}_{x}d\mu(x)
with
\esssup_{x\in X}\norm{\xi_x}<+\infty
has an expansion
\xi=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\xi^{n}
where
a_{n}\in L^{\infty}(X)
is given by
a_{n}(x)=\langle\xi_{x},\xi^{n}_{x}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{x}}
.

Definition 2.13
.

A
unitary (resp. orthogonal) representation
of
\mathcal{G}
on a complex (real) measurable Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
, with
X=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and a map
\mathcal{G}\ni g\mapsto\pi(g)\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{{\rm d}(g)},\mathcal{H}_{{\rm r}(g)})
(in the real case,
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
denotes the set of orthogonal maps from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{K}
) such that

\pi(gh)=\pi(g)\pi(h),\quad\textup{ for almost all }(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}

and such that
\mathcal{G}\ni g\mapsto\langle\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)},\eta_{{\rm r}(g)}\rangle
is a measurable map for all
\xi,\eta\in S(X\ast\mathcal{H})
.

Example 2.14
.

Given a measurable Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{K}
with orthonormal fundamental sequence
\Xi=\{\xi^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, one can always define the
identity representation

{\rm id}_{\Xi}
by the formula

{\rm id}_{\Xi}(g)\xi^{n}_{{\rm d}(g)}=\xi^{n}_{{\rm r}(g)},

for each
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Example 2.15
.

The
(left) regular representation

\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}
of
\mathcal{G}
is obtained by taking
\mathcal{H}_{x}=\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}^{x})
for each
x\in X=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, forming the measurable Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
with any fundamental sequence such that
S(X\ast\mathcal{H})=\{\xi\textup{ is a measurable function}:\xi_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{{\rm r}(x)}\}
, and considering the identity representation with respect to this fundamental sequence. An example of such a fundamental sequence is achieved by fixing a topology in
\mathcal{G}
that generates the Borel structure and taking any sup-norm dense sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{C}_{\rm c}(\mathcal{G})
to define
\xi_{x}^{n}=f_{n}|_{\mathcal{G}^{x}}
.

In this case, one has in a natural way that

\int_{X}^{\oplus}\mathcal{H}_{x}\differential\mu(x)\cong L^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mu_{\mathcal{G}})

and the action of
\mathcal{G}
is given by

\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}(g)\xi(h)=\xi(g^{-1}h),\quad\text{ for }(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\text{ and }\xi\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}^{x}).

More suggestively, for
g\in\mathcal{G}
let
\delta_{g}\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}^{{\rm r}(g)})
be the indicator function of
\{g\}\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{{\rm r}(g)}
. Then
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}(g)\delta_{h}=\delta_{gh}
for all
(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
. This induces a representation
[[\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}]]
of
[[\mathcal{G}]]
on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
. Then
L(\mathcal{G})
may also be defined as the von Neumann algebra generated by the partial isometries
v_{\sigma}\coloneqq[[\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}]](\sigma)\in\mathbb{B}(L^{2}(\mathcal{G},\mu_{\mathcal{G}}))
.

Example 2.16
.

Given two representations
\pi_{i}
on
X\ast\mathcal{H}^{i}
(
i=1,2
), one may form their tensor product
\pi_{1}\otimes\pi_{2}
by constructing the Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
, with fibers
\mathcal{H}_{x}=\mathcal{H}_{x}^{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{x}^{2}
and a fundamental sequence given by
\xi^{i,j}=\xi^{i}_{1}\otimes\xi_{2}^{j}
, in terms of the fundamental sequences
\{\xi_{i}^{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of
X\ast\mathcal{H}^{i}
. The formula for
\pi_{1}\otimes\pi_{2}
on simple tensors is

\pi_{1}\otimes\pi_{2}(g)(\xi_{1}\otimes\xi_{2})=\pi_{1}(g)\xi_{1}\otimes\pi_{2}(g)\xi_{2}.

Definition 2.17
.

Given representations
\pi
on
X\ast\mathcal{H}
and
\rho
on
X\ast\mathcal{K}
, we say that
\pi
and
\rho
are
unitarily equivalent
if there is a family of unitaries
\{U_{x}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{x},\mathcal{K}_{x})\}_{x\in X}
with

U_{{\rm r}(g)}\pi(g)=\rho(g)U_{{\rm d}(g)}\quad\text{for all }g\in\mathcal{G},

and such that
x\mapsto U_{x}\xi_{x}
is in
S(X\ast\mathcal{K})
for each
\xi\in S(X\ast\mathcal{H})
.

Unitary equivalence allows us to identify two representations as the same. An example of a natural unitary equivalence is presented below.

Lemma 2.18

(Fell’s Absorption Principle for Groupoids)
.

Let
\pi
be a representation of
\mathcal{G}
on
X\ast\mathcal{H}
and
\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}
the left regular representation of
\mathcal{G}
. Then for any orthonormal fundamental sequence of sections
\Xi=\{\xi^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
for the bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
, we have that
\pi\otimes\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}
is unitarily equivalent to
{\rm id}_{\Xi}\otimes\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}

Proof.

Let
\Xi=\{\xi^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be an orthonormal fundamental sequence of sections for
X\ast\mathcal{H}
. For
g,h\in\mathcal{G}
and
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we compute

\displaystyle\langle\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n}\otimes\delta_{g},\pi(h)\xi_{{\rm d}(h)}^{m}\otimes\delta_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n},\pi(h)\xi_{{\rm d}(h)}^{m}\rangle\cdot\langle\delta_{g},\delta_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n},\pi(h)\xi_{{\rm d}(h)}^{m}\rangle\cdot\delta_{g=h}

\displaystyle=\langle\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n},\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{m}\rangle\cdot\delta_{g=h}

\displaystyle=\delta_{g=h}\cdot\delta_{n=m}

\displaystyle=\langle\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}^{n}\otimes\delta_{g},\xi_{{\rm r}(h)}^{m}\otimes\delta_{h}\rangle

So, for every
x\in X
, setting
U_{x}(\xi_{x}^{n}\otimes\delta_{g})=\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n}\otimes\delta_{g}
defines a unitary on
\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathcal{G}^{x})
. Now let
(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we see that

\displaystyle U_{{\rm r}(g)}\big({\rm id}_{\Xi}\otimes\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\big)(g)[\xi_{{\rm r}(h)}^{n}\otimes\delta_{h}]

\displaystyle=U_{{\rm r}(g)}(\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}^{n}\otimes\delta_{gh})

\displaystyle=\pi(gh)\xi_{{\rm d}(h)}^{n}\otimes\delta_{gh}

\displaystyle=\big(\pi\otimes\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\big)(g)[\pi(h)\xi_{{\rm d}(h)}^{n}\otimes\delta_{h}]

\displaystyle=\big(\pi\otimes\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\big)(g)U_{{\rm d}(g)}[\xi_{{\rm r}(h)}^{n}\otimes\delta_{h}].

The computation above implies that
U_{{\rm r}(g)}\big({\rm id}_{\Xi}\otimes\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\big)(g)=\big(\pi\otimes\lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\big)(g)U_{{\rm d}(g)}
, for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. The fact that
\{U_{x}\}_{x\in X}
maps measurable sections into measurable sections is obvious. ∎

Definition 2.19
.

We say that
\pi
is
weakly contained
in
\rho
, denoted
\pi\prec\rho
, if for any
\varepsilon>0
,
\xi\in S(X\ast\mathcal{H})
, and
E\subset\mathcal{G}
with
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}(E)<\infty

\{\eta^{1},\dots,\eta^{m}\}\subset S(X\ast\mathcal{K})
with

\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\Big(\{g\in E:|\langle\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)},\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\rho(g)\eta^{i}_{{\rm d}(g)},\eta^{i}_{{\rm r}(g)}\rangle|\geq\varepsilon\}\Big)<\varepsilon.

Definition 2.20
.

Let
\pi
be a representation of the discrete measured groupoid
\mathcal{G}
on a Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
. We say that a measurable section
\xi\in S(X\ast\mathcal{H})
is
invariant
if

\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}=\xi_{{\rm r}(g)},\text{ for almost all }g\in\mathcal{G}.

We say that
\pi

Now we introduce the definitions of weakly mixing and mixing representations. They were taken from
[

and

Definition 2.21
.

Let
\pi
be a representation of a discrete measured groupoid
\mathcal{G}
on a Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
. Then
\pi
will be called

(i)

weak mixing

and every

mixing

Definition 2.22
.

A
1
-cocycle
for a representation
\pi
on
X\ast\mathcal{H}
is a measurable map
\mathcal{G}\ni g\mapsto b(g)\in\mathcal{H}_{{\rm r}(g)}\subset X\ast\mathcal{H}
such that

b(gh)=b(g)+\pi(g)b(h)\quad\text{for all }(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}.

The
1
-cocycle
b
is a
1
-coboundary
if there is a measurable section
\xi
of
X\ast\mathcal{H}
such that

b(g)=\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}-\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}\quad\textup{for $\mu_{\mathcal{G}}$-a.e. $g\in\mathcal{G}$}.

A pair of
1
-cocycles
b
and
b^{\prime}
are
cohomologous
if
b-b^{\prime}
is a
1
-coboundary. The set of
1
-cocycles of
\pi
is denoted
Z^{1}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
and the set of
1
-coboundaries by
B^{1}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
. The quotient

H^{1}(\mathcal{G},\pi)=Z^{1}(\mathcal{G},\pi)/B^{1}(\mathcal{G},\pi)

is called the
1
-cohomology group of the representation
\pi
and it is typically endowed with the quotient topology after giving
Z^{1}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
the topology of convergence in the measure
\mu
.

The following result is due to
[
1
, Theorem 3.19, Lemma 3.20]
.

Lemma 2.23

(Anatharaman-Delaroche)
.

Let
b
be a 1-coboundary associated with the representation
\pi
of
\mathcal{G}
in
X\ast\mathcal{H}
, then there exists a Borel subset
E\subset X
of positive measure, such that for every
x\in E
,
\sup\{\norm{b(g)}:g\in\mathcal{G}_{E}^{x}\}<\infty
. If
\mathcal{G}
is ergodic, then this last condition is equivalent to
b
being a 1-coboundary and, moreover,
E
can be chosen to have measure
1
.

However, we are interested in a particular class of
1
-cocycles that we will introduce now.

Definition 2.24
.

Let
b
be a
1
-cocycle associated with the representation
\pi
of
\mathcal{G}
in
X\ast\mathcal{H}
. Then
b
is said to be
bounded
if there exists a sequence of measurable subsets
\{E_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of
X
with
\mu(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n})=1
and
\sup\{\|b(g)\|:g\in\mathcal{G}|_{E_{n}}\}<\infty
for each
n\geq 1
. On the contrary,
b
is called
unbounded
if it is not bounded. Moreover,
b
is called
strongly unbounded
if there exists a
\delta>0
such that for all
R>0
there is a
\sigma\in[\mathcal{G}]
such that

\mu(\{x\in X:\norm{b(x\sigma)}\geq R\})>\delta.

In the equivalence relation setting, Hoff characterized coboundaries as unbounded
1
-cocycles
[
24
, Lemma 2.1]
. His proof also works here.

Lemma 2.25
.

A
1
-cocycle
b
for a representation
\pi
of
\mathcal{G}
on
X\ast\mathcal{H}
is a coboundary if and only if it is bounded.

Proof.

If
b
is a coboundary, then there is a
\xi\in S(X\ast\mathcal{H})
such that
b(g)=\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}-\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}
for
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}
-almost every
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Then for
n\geq 1
, we can set
E_{n}=\{x\in X:\norm{\xi_x}\leq n\}
and that makes
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}=X
true. Furthermore, we check that for
g\in\mathcal{G}|_{E_{n}}
we have

\norm{b(g)}\leq\norm{\xi_{\r(g)}}+\norm{\xi_{\d(g)}}\leq 2n<\infty.

For the only if part, we assume the existence of the sequence of measurable subsets
\{E_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
that almost everywhere exhausts
X
and
\sup\{\norm{b(g)}:g\in\mathcal{G}|_{E_{n}}\}<\infty
for each
n\geq 1
. Then by
[
1
, Lemma 3.21]
, we know that for each
n
we know that
b
restricted to
\mathcal{G}|_{E_{n}}
is a coboundary and therefore we can find a section
\xi^{n}\in S(E_{n}\ast\mathcal{H})
with

b(g)=\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}^{n}-\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n},

(2.2)

for
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}
-almost every
g\in\mathcal{G}|_{E_{n}}
. But we notice that the formula (
2.2
) also defines
\xi^{n}
in the saturation
F_{n}=\bigcup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}{\rm r}(g(E_{n}))
, when reinterpreted as
\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}^{n}=b(g)+\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n}
. This definition does not depend on the choice of
g
; if
g,h\in\mathcal{G}|_{E_{n}}
and
x={\rm r}(g)={\rm r}(h)
, then
(g^{-1},h)
is a composable pair,
g^{-1}h
lies in
\mathcal{G}|_{E_{n}}
and

\displaystyle[b(g)+\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n}]-[b(h)+\pi(h)\xi_{{\rm d}(h)}^{n}]

\displaystyle=-\pi(g)b(g^{-1}h)+\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n}-\pi(h)\xi_{{\rm d}(h)}^{n}

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\big(-b(g^{-1}h)+\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n}-\pi(g^{-1}h)\xi_{{\rm d}(h)}^{n}\big)

\displaystyle=0.

Thus we have a sequence of sections
\{\xi^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfying (
2.2
) but for
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}
-a.e.
g\in\mathcal{G}|_{F_{n}}
. Now for
x\in F=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}F_{n}
, define

\displaystyle\xi_{x}=\xi^{n_{x}}_{x},\quad\text{where}\quad n_{x}=\min\{n\geq 1:x\in F_{n}\},

and let
\xi_{x}=0
for
x\not\in F
. Note that if
x,y\in X
lie in the same orbit, then
n_{x}=n_{y}
since each
F_{n}
is invariant.
Thus

b(g)=\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}^{n_{{\rm r}(g)}}-\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{n_{{\rm d}(g)}}=\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}-\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)},

for all
g\in\mathcal{G}|_{F}
.
∎

Example 2.26
.

Suppose
G
is a countable group acting in a measure-preserving way on the standard probability space

Lemma 2.27

If

Proof.

Proposition 2.28
.

Let

be a 1-coboundary associated with the representation
\pi
of
\mathcal{G}
in
X\ast\mathcal{H}
. If
b
is strongly unbounded, then it is unbounded. For semidirect product groupoids, the two notions coincide.

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
as in the definition of strong unboundedness and suppose that
b
is bounded. Then there exists a measurable subset
E\subset X
such that
\mu(E)>1-\frac{\delta}{2}
and
R=\sup\{\|b(g)\|:g\in\mathcal{G}|_{E}\}<\infty.
. But by assumption, there must be a
\sigma\in[\mathcal{G}]
such that
F=\{x\in X:\|b(x\sigma)\|>R\}
has measure at least
\delta
. Then we would have
{\rm d}\big((E\cap F)\sigma\big)\subset E^{c}
, which implies

\mu({\rm d}\big((E\cap F)\sigma\big))=\mu({\rm r}\big((E\cap F)\sigma\big))=\mu(E\cap F)\leq\mu(E^{c}).

In consequence,

\mu(E\cup F)\geq\mu(E)+\mu(F)-\mu(E^{c})=2\mu(E)+\mu(F)-1>1.

Now, if
b
is unbounded and
\mathcal{G}
is isomorphic with a semidirect product groupoid, we can, without loss of generality, assume that
\mathcal{G}=\Gamma\rtimes_{\delta}\mathcal{R}
, with
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\Gamma={\rm Iso}(\mathcal{G})
. Since
\mathcal{G}
contains copies of both
\Gamma
and
\mathcal{R}
, the

-cocycle

restricts to
1
-cocycles

and
b|_{\mathcal{R}}
, for the representations
\pi|_{\Gamma}
and
\pi|_{\mathcal{R}}
, respectively. If any of these is strongly unbounded, the proof is finished since
[\Gamma],[\mathcal{R}]\subset[\mathcal{G}]
in a natural manner. Hence we assume that it is not the case. But then Lemma
2.27
and
[
24
, Lemma 2.1]
imply that both

and

are bounded. Then, by definition, there are sequences of measurable subsets

of

with
\mu(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n})=\mu(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}G_{n})=1
and the quantities
s_{1}=\sup\{\|b(g)\|:g\in{\mathcal{R}}|_{E_{n}}\}
,
s_{2}=\sup\{\|b(g)\|:g\in{\Gamma}|_{G_{n}}\}
are finite for each

. Define the double-indexed sequence of measurable sets

and note that

\mu\big(\cup_{n,m=1}^{\infty}A_{n,m}\big)\geq\mu\big((\cup_{n}^{\infty}E_{n})\cap(\cup_{m=1}^{\infty}G_{m})\big)=1,

while, for

In particular, it follows that all non-trivial

2.4 Malleable deformations

In the following, we survey the basics of Sorin Popa’s deformation rigidity theory as well as various relevant approaches/results from
[
14
]
which motivate this paper’s main results.

The intuitive idea behind deformation/rigidity theory is to study rigidity results for a von Neumann algebra
M
which can be deformed inside another algebra
\widetilde{M}\supseteq M
by an action
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to{\rm Aut}(\widetilde{M})
whilst containing subalgebras which are
rigid
with respect to the deformation.

Let
(M,\tau)
be a tracial von Neumann algebra and
{\rm Aut}(M)
the group of trace-preserving
∗
-automorphisms of
M
. Then we have the following fundamental definition due to Popa.

Definition 2.29

(Popa)
.

Let
\widetilde{M}\supseteq M
be a trace-preserving inclusion of tracial von Neumann algebras.

1.

A
malleable deformation
\alpha
of
M
inside
\widetilde{M}
is a strongly-continuous action
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to{\rm Aut}(\widetilde{M})
such that
\alpha_{t}(x)\xrightarrow{\norm{\cdot}_{2}}x
as
t\to 0
for every
x\in\widetilde{M}
.

2.

An
s-malleable deformation
(\alpha,\beta)
of
M
inside
\widetilde{M}

is a malleable deformation
\alpha
combined with a distinguished involution
\beta\in{\rm Aut}(\widetilde{M})
such that
\beta|_{M}=id
and
\beta\alpha_{t}=\alpha_{-t}\beta
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

On its own, deformations do not give much information about the algebra itself; however, they do provide one with a quantitative way to locate subalgebras with prescribed properties that force them to be
rigid
with respect to the deformation. Explicitly, given a malleable deformation
\alpha
of
M
inside
\widetilde{M}
, a subalgebra

is

if the deformation converges uniformly on the unit ball of

\lim_{t\to 0}\sup_{x\in(Q)_{1}}\norm{\alpha_{t}(x)-x}_{2}=0.

Locating
\alpha
-rigid subalgebras is a fundamental step to applying deformation/rigidity theory to a problem. An attempt can be made to directly prove rigidity by harnessing the structural properties of the subalgebra, such as the relative property (
T
). The recent notion of maximal rigidity for subalgebras studied by de Santiago, Hayes,
Hoff, and Sinclair in
[
14
]
has had relative success in providing a more indirect way of proving rigidity.

Lemma 2.35

(Popa’s transversality inequality)
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to{\rm Aut}(\widetilde{M})
,
\beta\in{\rm Aut}(\widetilde{M})

\norm{\delta_{2t}(x)}_{2}\leq 2\norm{\alpha_{2t}(x) - x}_{2}\leq 4\norm{\delta_t(x)}_{2}

\norm{[\delta_t(x), y]}_{2}\leq 2\norm{x}\norm{\alpha_t(y) - y}_{2}+\norm{[x, y]}_{2}.

For (i), we see that

\displaystyle\norm{\delta_t(x)}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\alpha_t(x) - x}_{2}+\norm{x - \E_M(\alpha_t(x))}_{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\alpha_t(x) - x}_{2}+\norm{\E_M(x - \alpha_t(x))}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\norm{\alpha_t(x) - x}_{2},

and since

\displaystyle\norm{\alpha_{2t}(x) - x}_{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\alpha_t(x) - \alpha_{-t}(x)}_{2}\leq\norm{\alpha_t(x) - \E_M(\alpha_t(x))}_{2}+\norm{\alpha_{-t}(x) - \E_M(\alpha_t(x))}_{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\delta_t(x)}_{2}+\norm{\beta(\alpha_t(x) - \E_M(\alpha_t(x)))}_{2}=2\norm{\delta_t(x)}_{2}.

\displaystyle\norm{[\delta_t(x), y]}_{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{(1-E_M)([\alpha_t(x), y])}_{2}\leq\norm{[\alpha_t(x), y]}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\alpha_t(x)y - \alpha_t(x)\alpha_t(y)}_{2}+\norm{[\alpha_t(x), \alpha_t(y)]}_{2}+\norm{\alpha_t(y)\alpha_t(x) - y\alpha_t(x)}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\norm{x}\norm{\alpha_t(y) - y}_{2}+\norm{[x, y]}_{2},

Proof.

Most of the details of this proof are contained in

Lemma 3.1

For

A key tool in studying unitary representations of groups is the notion of positive-definite functions and multipliers. We have a corresponding definition for groupoids.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose

(b)

\norm{\sum_{i=1}^{s} \Phi_{\xi_{i}}(1)}=\norm{\sum_{i=1}^{s} \inp{\xi_{i}}{\xi_{i}}_{A}}_{\infty}

(c)

\tau\circ\sum_{i=1}^{s}\Phi_{\xi_{i}}\leq\norm{\sum_{i=1}^{s} \inp{\xi_{i}}{\xi_{i}}_{A}}_{\infty}\cdot\tau

(d)

The map
\sum_{i=1}^{s}\Phi_{\xi_{i}}
is

-bounded, i.e. for all

\norm{\sum_{i=1}^{s}\Phi_{\xi_i}(x)}_{2}\leq\norm{\sum_{i=1}^{s} \inp{\xi_{i}}{\xi_{i}}_{A}}_{\infty}\cdot\norm{x}_{2}

Proof.

Items (a) and (b) follow directly from the definitions of the Connes-Fusion tensor product and of
\Phi_{\xi}
, so we only show item (c). Suppose

and

\displaystyle\tau(\Phi_{\xi}(au_{\sigma}))

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\mathbb{E}_{A}(\langle\pi(\cdot\sigma)\xi_{{\rm d}(\cdot\sigma)},\xi_{\cdot}\rangle au_{\sigma})\differential{\mu(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\langle\pi(x\sigma)\xi_{{\rm d}(x\sigma)},\xi_{x}\rangle a(x)1_{\{z\in X:z\sigma=z\}}(x)\differential{\mu(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\langle\xi,\xi\rangle_{A}(x)a(x)1_{\{z\in X:z\sigma=z\}}(x)\differential{\mu(x)}=\tau(\langle\xi,\xi\rangle_{A}au_{\sigma})

By linearity and normality, it follows that

for all

\displaystyle=\tau(x^{1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{i}\rangle_{A}x^{1/2})=\langle x^{1/2},\sum_{i=1}^{s}\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{i}\rangle x^{1/2}\rangle

6
Fullness

As mentioned before, our purpose for this section is to provide conditions that imply that the groupoid von Neumann algebra
L(\mathcal{G})
is a full factor. Let us recall the definition of fullness, which was introduced by Connes
[
11
]
as the negation of Murray-von Neumann’s property Gamma
[
36
]
.

Definition 6.1
.

A factor with separable predual
M
will be called
full
if for any uniformly bounded sequence
\{\psi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset M
with
\norm{\psi_n\phi-\phi\psi_n}_{2}\to 0
for any
\phi\in M
, must satisfy
\norm{\psi_n-\tau(\psi_n)1}_{2}\to 0
.

In particular, it will be a standing assumption of the section that our groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is such that
L(\mathcal{G})
is a factor. Since
L(\mathcal{G})
admits a faithful tracial state, it is forced to be a
{\rm II}_{1}
-factor. The idea now is to provide conditions that ensure that
L(\mathcal{G})
is a full factor. Let us note that a necessary condition for fullness is that of ‘strong ergodicity’, which is defined as follows.

Definition 6.2
.

A discrete measured groupoid
\mathcal{G}
is called
strongly ergodic
if for any sequence of measurable subsets
Y_{n}\subset X
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(\sigma Y_{n}\sigma^{-1}\Delta Y_{n})=0\text{ for every }\sigma\in[\mathcal{G}],

we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(Y_{n})\big(1-\mu(Y_{n})\big)=0
.

Before diving into our fullness result, let us state two lemmas that will be useful for its proof. The first one is a very simple statement coming from ergodic theory.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a probability space and
f\in{\rm Aut}(X,\mu)
, with
f^{2}={\rm id}_{X}
such that
F={\rm Fix}_{f}^{c}=\{x\in X:f(x)\not=x\}
has positive measure. Then there is a subset
E\subset F
of positive measure such that
F\setminus\big(E\sqcup f(E)\big)
is null.

Proof.

Consider the equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}\subset F\times F
given by the orbits of
f
and let
\sigma\in[\mathcal{R}]
be any non-trivial full group element. Projecting
\sigma\setminus{\rm Diag}(F)
onto the first coordinate gives a set of positive measure
E\subset X
that has to satisfy
\mu\big(f(E)\cap E\big)=0
.

Now, by Zorn’s lemma, one can find a maximal family of subsets
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
with positive measure and such that
\mu\big(f(E_{i})\cap E_{j}\big)=0
for all
i,j\in I
and
E_{i}\cap E_{j}=\emptyset
, for
i\not=j
. Such a family must necessarily be countable, and their union
E=\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}
is the required subset.
∎

\displaystyle\langle x\widehat{a}y,\widehat{a}\rangle

\displaystyle=\tau(a^{*}xay)=\tau(a^{*}xayz)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(a^{*}xayv_{i}v_{i}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(a^{*}xav_{i}yv_{i}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(a^{*}xayv_{i}v_{i}^{*}v_{i}v_{i}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(a^{*}xayv_{i}\widetilde{S}T\widetilde{S}v_{i}^{*})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau(\widetilde{S}v_{i}^{*}a^{*}xayv_{i}\widetilde{S}T_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\widetilde{S}v_{i}^{*}a^{*}xayv_{i}\widetilde{S}\xi_{n},\xi_{n}\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle xav_{i}\widetilde{S}\xi_{n}y,av_{i}\widetilde{S}\xi_{n}\rangle.

To show weak containment, suppose
F_{1}\subseteq M
,
F_{2}\subseteq Pz
are finite subsets,
\epsilon>0
, and
a\in M
. Set
\eta_{n}^{i}\coloneqq av_{i}\widetilde{S}\xi_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq i\leq m
. Since these subsets are finite, we may choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left|\langle x\widehat{a}y,\widehat{a}\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle x\eta_{k}^{i}y,\eta_{k}^{i}\rangle\right|<\epsilon\quad\text{for all }x\in F_{1},\,y\in F_{2}.

By density of
M
inside
L^{2}(M)
, this shows that we can in fact approximate any vector state in
L^{2}(M)
by vector states from
\mathcal{H}
, whence we conclude that
\prescript{}{M}{L^{2}(M)}_{Pq}\prec\prescript{}{M}{\mathcal{H}}_{Pq}
.
∎

The preceding lemma presents a certain relative amenability condition for direct summands. In the opposite case of strong nonamenability, one can use the contrapositive of the previous lemma to find unitaries in
P
for which commutation with these unitaries detects closeness to
M
.

We will now put everything together to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a strongly ergodic discrete measured groupoid which is nonamenable and admits a strongly unbounded
1
-cocycle into a mixing orthogonal representation weakly contained in the regular representation. If
L(\mathcal{G})
is a factor, then it is a full factor.

Proof.

Let
M,\widetilde{M}
and
\{\alpha_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\subset{\rm Aut}(\tilde{M})
as constructed in Section
3
and suppose that
M
has property Gamma. Then there is a sequence
\{u_{n}\}\in\mathcal{U}(M)
with
\tau(u_{n})=0
for all
n
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\norm{u_nx-xu_n}_{2}=0,

for each
x\in M
.
Then for any
u\in\mathcal{N}_{M}(A)
we have

\displaystyle\norm{u\E_A(u_n)u^*-\E_A(u_n)}_{2}=\norm{\E_A(uu_nu^*)-\E_A(u_n)}_{2}\leq\norm{uu_nu^* - u_n}_{2},

which goes to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Since the sequence
\mathbb{E}_{A}(u_{n})
is bounded in norm and
M=\mathcal{N}_{M}(A)^{\prime\prime}
, it follows that
\norm{x \E_A(u_n) - \E_A(u_n)x}_{2}\to 0
for each
x\in M
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is strongly ergodic, it follows that
\norm{\E_A(u_n)}_{2}=\norm{\E_A(u_n)-\tau(\E_A(u_n))1}_{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Consider the quotient map

q:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}},\quad\text{given by}\quad q(g)=\big({\rm r}(g),{\rm d}(g)\big).

Noting that
q
is countable-to-one, by Lusin-Novikov
[
29
, Theorem 18.10]
there is a Borel right inverse to
q
, we denote it by
f:\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathcal{G}
. By replacing
f
with
f^{\prime}(x,y):=f(x,y)f(y,y)^{-1}
if necessary, we can assume that
f(x,x)=x
, for all
x\in X
. Note that
\sigma=f(\widetilde{\sigma})\in[\mathcal{G}]
as soon as
\widetilde{\sigma}\in[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}]
and therefore we can write every element in
[\mathcal{G}]
as a multiplication
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
, with
\sigma_{1}\in[{\rm Iso}(\mathcal{G})]
and
\sigma_{2}=f(\widetilde{\sigma_{2}})\in f\big([\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}]\big)
.

Hence the full group
[\mathcal{G}]
fits into a short exact sequence (of groups) in the following manner

1\to[{\rm Iso}(\mathcal{G})]\to[\mathcal{G}]\xrightarrow{q}[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}]\to 1,

where the map
q:[\mathcal{G}]\to[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}]
is induced by the original quotient map.

Now fix any
\widetilde{\sigma}\in[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}]
with
{\widetilde{\sigma}}^{2}=X
and let
\sigma=f(\widetilde{\sigma})
. Note that
z_{\sigma}=\mathbb{E}_{A}(u_{\sigma})
is a projection given by
z_{\sigma}=z_{\sigma^{-1}}=1_{Y}
, where
Y={\{x\in X:{\sigma}x{\sigma}^{-1}=x\}}={\{x\in X:\widetilde{\sigma}x\widetilde{\sigma}^{-1}=x\}}={\rm Fix}_{g}
, where
g(x)={\sigma}x{\sigma}^{-1}
, for all
x\in X
. We now observe that

u_{\sigma}^{*}z_{\sigma}=\mathbb{E}_{A}(u_{\sigma}^{*}z_{\sigma})=\mathbb{E}_{A}(u_{\sigma}^{*})z_{\sigma}=z_{\sigma}.

Hence

\displaystyle\norm{\E_A(u_nu_\si^*)z_\si}_{2}=\norm{\E_A(u_nu_\si^*z_\si)}_{2}=\norm{\E_A(u_n)z_\si}_{2}\leq\norm{\E_A(u_n)}_{2}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0

(6.1)

Moreover, since
1-z_{\sigma}=1_{Y^{c}}
and
Y={\rm Fix}_{g}
, lemma
6.3
allows us to find a projection
z\in A
such that
1-z_{\sigma}=z+u_{\sigma}zu_{\sigma}^{*}
and
z\perp u_{\sigma}zu_{\sigma}^{*}
. It now follows that

\displaystyle\norm{\E_A(u_nu_\si^*)(1-z_\si)}^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\E_A(u_nu_\si^*)(z + u_\si zu_\si^*)}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\E_A(u_nu_\si^*)z}_{2}^{2}+\norm{\E_A(u_nu_\si^*)u_\si zu_\si^*}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\E_A(u_nu_\si^*)(z-u_\si zu_\si^*)}_{2}^{2}=\norm{z\E_A(u_nu_\si^*)-\E_A(u_nu_\si^*u_\si zu_\si^*)}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\E_A(zu_nu_\si^*-u_nzu_\si^*)}_{2}^{2}\leq\norm{zu_n-u_nz}_{2}^{2}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

(6.2)

Combining (
6.1
) and (
6
) we see that
\norm{\E_A(u_nu_\si^*)}_{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for each
\sigma=f(\widetilde{\sigma})
, where
\widetilde{\sigma}\in[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}]
with
{\widetilde{\sigma}}^{2}=X
. By Feldman and Moore
[
19
]
, the involutions
\widetilde{\sigma}
from before cover
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}
and so
\{au_{\sigma_{1}}u_{f(\widetilde{\sigma_{2}})}:a\in A,\sigma_{1}\in[{\rm Iso}(\mathcal{G})],\sigma_{2}\in[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}],\widetilde{\sigma_{2}}^{2}=X\}^{\prime\prime}
contains all the unitaries
u_{\sigma}
coming from
\sigma\in f\big([\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}]\big)
. In consequence, it has to coincide with
L(\mathcal{G})
and we obtain that
\norm{\E_A(xu_ny)}_{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for any pair
x,y\in M
.

Recall that, in the setting of Lemma
2.35
, we defined
\delta_{t}(x)=\alpha_{t}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{M}(\alpha_{t}(x))
. By Lemma
5.1
, we know that
{}_{M}L^{2}(\tilde{M})\ominus L^{2}(M)_{M}
is mixing relative to
A
, so this implies that

\langle u_{n}\delta_{t}(x),\delta_{t}(x)u_{n}\rangle\to 0,\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for every
x\in M
. From Lemma
5.1
, we obtain that the convergence
\alpha_{t}\to{\rm id}
is not uniform on
(M)_{1}
, and hence by part (i) of Lemma
2.35
there is an
\varepsilon>0
and sequences
\{x_{k}\}\subset(M)_{1}
,
\{t_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
t_{k}\to 0
, such that
\norm{\delta_{t_k}(x_k)}_{2}\geq\varepsilon
for all
k
. Then, using Popa’s transversality inequality again, we get that for any
k
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\delta_{t_k}(x_k)}_{2}^{2}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left[\frac{1}{2}\norm{[u_n, \delta_{t_k}(x_k)]}_{2}^{2}+\text{Re}\langle u_{n}\delta_{t_{k}}(x_{k}),\delta_{t_{k}}(x_{k})u_{n}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left[2\norm{\alpha_{t_k}(u_n) - u_n}_{2}+\norm{[u_n, x_k]}_{2}\right]^{2}\leq 8\liminf_{n\to\infty}\norm{\delta_{t_k/2}(u_n)}_{2}^{2}

Thus setting
s_{k}=\frac{t_{k}}{2}
for each
k
we can find
n_{k}\geq k
such that
\norm{\delta_{s_k}(u_{n_k})}_{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
. Finally, we see that, for any
x\in M
,

\norm{[\delta_{s_k}(u_{n_k}), x]}_{2}\leq 2\norm{\alpha_{s_k}(x) - x}_{2}+\norm{[u_{n_k}, x]}_{2}\to 0\text{ as }k\to\infty.

Since we also have
\norm{x\delta_{t_k/2}(u_{n_k})}_{2}\leq 2\norm{x}_{2}
for all
k
, we apply Lemma
6.5
to find that
{}_{M}L^{2}(M)_{M}\prec{}_{M}L^{2}(\tilde{M})\ominus L^{2}(M)_{M}
. On the other hand, Lemma
5.1
shows that
{}_{M}L^{2}(\tilde{M})\ominus L^{2}(M)_{M}\prec{}_{M}L^{2}(M)\otimes L^{2}(M)_{M}
. This implies that
M
is amenable, which is a contradiction.
∎

The following corollary is now immediate from the Theorem and previous considerations.

Corollary 6.7
.

Let
G
be a countable group which admits an unbounded
1
-cocycle into a mixing orthogonal representation weakly contained in the regular representation and let
G\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
a strongly ergodic, nonamenable probability measure preserving action such that
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes G
is a factor (immediate if
G
is i.c.c. or if the action is free). Then
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes G
is a full factor.

Unique prime factorization

Let us recall the following Theorem due to Hoff
[
24
, Theorem 6.4]
, which he used to obtain a result about product rigidity for equivalence relations. It will do the heavy lifting for our result on unique prime factorizations.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
M_{1},\dots,M_{k}
be
\rm{II}_{1}
full factors, each with an
s
-malleable deformation
\{\alpha^{i}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\subset{\rm Aut}(\tilde{M}_{i})
for some tracial von Neumann algebras
\tilde{M}_{i}\supset M_{i}
. Suppose that for each
i
, the
M_{i}
-
M_{i}
bimodule
{}_{M_{i}}L^{2}(\tilde{M}_{i})\ominus L^{2}(M_{i})_{M_{i}}
is weakly contained in the coarse
M_{i}
-
M_{i}
bimodule and mixing relative to some abelian subalgebra
A_{i}\subset M_{i}
. Assume that the convergence
\alpha^{i}_{t}\to{\rm id}
is not uniform in
\norm{\cdot}_{2}
on
(M_{i})_{1}
for any
i
. Then
M_{i}
is prime for each
i
, and

(i)

If
M=M_{1}\overline{\otimes}M_{2}\overline{\otimes}\dots\overline{\otimes}M_{k}=N\overline{\otimes}Q
for tracial factors
N,Q
, there must be a partition
I_{N}\cup I_{Q}=\{1,\dots,k\}
and
t>0
such that

N^{t}=\bigotimes_{i\in I_{N}}M_{i}
and
Q^{1/t}=\bigotimes_{i\in I_{Q}}M_{i}
modulo unitary conjugacy in
M
.

(ii)

If
M=M_{1}\overline{\otimes}M_{2}\overline{\otimes}\dots\overline{\otimes}M_{k}=P_{1}\overline{\otimes}P_{2}\overline{\otimes}\cdots\overline{\otimes}P_{m}
for
\rm{II}_{1}
factors
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
and
m\geq k
, then
m=k
, each
P_{i}
is prime, and there are
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}>0
with
t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{k}=1
such that after reordering indices and conjugating by a unitary in
M
we have
M_{i}=P_{i}^{t_{i}}
for all
i
.

(iii)

In (ii), the assumption
m\geq k
can be omitted if each
P_{i}
is assumed to be prime.

In fact, the above-mentioned theorem can be applied directly in our setting. Its application in conjunction with Theorem
5.2
and Theorem
6.6
yields the following result.

(Theorem

)
.

For

, let

8
Maximal rigid subalgebras

In order to finish the article, let us describe the maximal rigid subalgebras of
L(\mathcal{G})
. We will achieve that in the following two propositions.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ergodic discrete measured groupoid and
\pi
an orthogonal representation of
\mathcal{G}
on a real Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
. Let
b\in Z^{1}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
be a
1
-cocycle and set

\mathcal{S}=\{g\in\mathcal{G}:b(g)=0\}.

Then
\mathcal{S}
is a wide (i.e
\mathcal{S}^{(0)}=\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
) discrete measured subgroupoid of
\mathcal{G}
. Moreover, if
L(\mathcal{S})
is diffuse and
\pi|_{\mathcal{S}}
is weak mixing, then
L(\mathcal{S})
is a maximal rigid subalgebra for
\alpha_{b}
.

Proof.

We will follow the proof of
[
14
, Proposition 4.3]
.
\mathcal{S}
is easily seen to be a wide subgroupoid; every unit
x\in X
satisfies
x^{2}=x
and hence the
1
-cocycle identity implies
x\in\mathcal{S}
. Showing closedness for multiplication and inverses is even easier. We also note that
\alpha_{b,t}|_{L(\mathcal{S})}={\rm id}_{L(\mathcal{S})}
.

We now want to show that
L(\mathcal{S})^{\prime}\cap\widetilde{M}\subset M
. Note that, for
\tau\in[[\mathcal{G}]]
and
\psi=\sum_{\sigma\in[[\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}]]}a_{\sigma}u_{\sigma}\in L(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})
, one has

u_{\tau}\psi u_{\tau}^{*}=u_{\tau}\big(\sum_{\sigma\in[[\mathcal{G}]]}a_{\sigma}u_{\sigma}\big)u_{\tau}^{*}=\sum_{\sigma\in[[\mathcal{G}]]}\theta_{\tau}(a_{\sigma})u_{\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}}=\kappa\otimes\lambda_{c}(\tau)\psi,

where
\kappa:[[\mathcal{G}]]\to L^{2}(X\ast\Omega)
is the Koopman representation (cf.
[
22
, Section 3.8]
) and
\lambda_{c}:[[\mathcal{G}]]\to L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
is the conjugation representation, that is,
\lambda_{c}(\sigma)\phi(x)=\phi(\sigma^{-1}x\sigma)
for all
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
and
\sigma\in[[\mathcal{G}]]
. It is therefore enough to argue that the representation
(\kappa\otimes\lambda_{c})|_{[\mathcal{S}]}
on
L^{2}(\widetilde{M})\ominus L^{2}(M)
has no nonzero invariant vectors. But this is the case as
(\kappa\otimes\lambda_{c})|_{[\mathcal{S}]}=\kappa|_{[\mathcal{S}]}\otimes\lambda_{c}|_{[\mathcal{S}]}
and
\kappa|_{[\mathcal{S}]}
is weakly mixing because of Remark
3.2
and the assumption on
\pi|_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Finally, we let
P
be the rigid envelope of
L(\mathcal{S})
under the deformation
\alpha_{b,t}
. By
[
14
, Theorem 3.5]
, we have that

\norm{(\alpha_{b,t}-{\rm id})|_{P}}_{\infty,2}\leq 24\norm{(\alpha_{b,t}-{\rm id})|_{L(\S)}}_{\infty,2}=0

for all small enough
t>0
. Since
t\mapsto\alpha_{c,t}
is a homomorphism, we have that
\alpha_{c,t}\big|_{P}
is the identity for all
t
. But then it follows that
P\leq L(\mathcal{S})
, and therefore
P=L(\mathcal{S})
.
∎

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ergodic discrete measured groupoid and
\pi
an orthogonal representation of
\mathcal{G}
on a real Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
. Let
b\in Z^{1}(\mathcal{G},\pi)
be a
1
-cocycle and suppose
\mathcal{S}\leq\mathcal{G}
is a discrete measured subgroupoid with
L(\mathcal{S})
diffuse and such that
\pi|_{\mathcal{S}}
is weakly mixing and
b|_{\mathcal{S}}
is bounded. Let
P
be the rigid envelope of
L(\mathcal{S})
. Then
P=L(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{S}\leq\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is a maximal subgroupoid satisfying that
b|_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}
is bounded.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{S}
is ergodic, Lemma
2.23
gives a measurable section
\xi:X\to X\ast\mathcal{H}
, such that
b(g)=\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}-\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}
, for all
g\in\mathcal{S}
. Now define
\tilde{b}(g)\coloneqq b(g)-\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}+\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}
and its associated subgroupoid

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}={\rm Ker}\,\tilde{b}=\{g\in\mathcal{G}:\tilde{b}(g)=0\}.

It follows that
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
contains
\mathcal{S}
and is maximal under the condition that
b|_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}
is bounded: if
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
and
b(g)=\xi_{{\rm r}(g)}^{\prime}-\pi(g)\xi_{{\rm d}(g)}^{\prime}
for
g\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
, then
\xi^{\prime}-\xi
is invariant under
\pi|_{\mathcal{S}}
and since
\pi|_{\mathcal{S}}
is weakly mixing,
\xi^{\prime}=\xi
, from where
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
.

Now note that
\tilde{b}-b
is a coboundary, so by Lemma
2.23
, there is
E\subset X
of measure 1 such that

\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{E}^{x}}\norm{\tilde b(g)-b(g)}<\infty.

Now let
\alpha_{\tilde{b}},\alpha_{b}
be the associated
s
-malleable deformations inside
\widetilde{M}
. We observe

\displaystyle\norm{(\alpha_{\tilde b,t}-\alpha_{\tilde b,t})\big{|}_M}_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(\widetilde{M})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\sigma\in[\mathcal{G}]}2-\tau(\overline{f_{\tilde{c}_{t},\sigma}}f_{{c}_{t},\sigma}+\overline{f_{{c}_{t},\sigma}}f_{\tilde{c}_{t},\sigma})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\sigma\in[\mathcal{G}]}\left(1-\int_{X}e^{-t^{2}\norm{\tilde b(x\sigma)-b(x\sigma)}^{2}}\differential{\mu(x)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2-2\int_{X}e^{-t^{2}\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{E}^{x}}\norm{\tilde b(g)-b(g)}^{2}}\differential{\mu(x)}

thus

\lim_{t\to 0}\norm{(\alpha_{\tilde b,t}-\alpha_{\tilde b,t})\big{|}_M}_{\infty,2}^{2}=0.

So a diffuse subalgebra
Q\leq M
is
\alpha_{b}
-rigid if and only if it is
\alpha_{\tilde{b}}
-rigid and by Proposition
8.1
,
L(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
is maximal rigid for
\alpha_{b}
and equals
P
(Theorem
2.32
).
∎

In particular, when the groupoid is a transformation groupoid
\mathcal{G}=X\rtimes_{\theta}G
and the
1
-cocycle comes from the acting group
G
(that is, the setting of Example
2.26
), we note that
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is of the form
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=X\rtimes_{\theta}H
, for some subgroup
H\leq G
and therefore the rigid envelope of
L(\mathcal{S})
is a crossed product
L^{\infty}(X,\mu)\rtimes H
(even when
\mathcal{S}
is not assumed to be a transformation subgroupoid).

Before stating the last theorem of the paper, let us recall the definition of property (T), as taken from
[
45
]
.

Definition 8.3
.

A tracial von Neumann algebra
(M,\tau)
is said to have
property (T)
if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a finite subset
F\subset M
and a
\delta>0
such that for every bimodule
{}_{M}\mathcal{H}_{M}
and any tracial vector
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\max_{x\in F}\norm{x\xi-\xi x}\leq\delta
, there exists a central vector
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\norm{\eta-\xi}\leq\varepsilon.

Example 8.4
.

A group von Neumann algebra has property (T) exactly when the group has Kazhdan’s property (T). In particular
L(\rm{SL}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}))
has property (T) when
k\geq 3.

Theorem 8.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ergodic discrete measured groupoid and
\pi
a weak mixing, orthogonal representation of
\mathcal{G}
on a real Hilbert bundle
X\ast\mathcal{H}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{G}
admits an unbounded 1-cocycle into
\pi
and that
L(\mathcal{G})
is diffuse. Then, for any pair
P,Q\subset L(\mathcal{G})
of subalgebras with property (T) such that
P\cap Q
is diffuse, we have that
P\vee Q\not=L(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

Since
P
and
Q
have property (T), they are rigid for any deformation
[
45
, Proposition 4.1]
. It follows from
[
14
, Theorem 1.3]
that
P\vee Q
is rigid, and therefore it is contained in a maximal rigid subalgebra
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
. But Theorem
8.2
prevents this algebra from being the entirety of
L(\mathcal{G})
.
∎
