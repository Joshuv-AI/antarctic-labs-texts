Abstract
. A base for a permutation group G G acting on a set Ω \Omega is a subset ℬ \mathcal{B} of Ω \Omega whose pointwise stabiliser G ( ℬ ) G_{(\mathcal{B})} is trivial. There is a natural greedy algorithm for constructing a base of relatively small size. We write 𝒢 ⁡ ( G ) \mathcal{G}(G) the maximum size of a base it produces, and b ⁡ ( G ) b(G) for the size of the smallest base for G G . In 1999, Peter Cameron conjectured that there exists an absolute constant c c such that every finite primitive group G G satisfies 𝒢 ⁡ ( G ) ≤ c ​ b ​ ( G ) \mathcal{G}(G)\leq cb(G) . We show that if G G is S n \mathrm{S}_{n} or A n \mathrm{A}_{n} acting primitively then either Cameron’s Greedy Conjecture holds for G G , or G G falls into one class of possible exceptions.

1.
Introduction

A
base
for a permutation group
G
acting on a finite set
\Omega
is a subset
\mathcal{B}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{k}\}\subseteq\Omega
whose pointwise stabiliser
G_{(\mathcal{B})}
is trivial. The size
b(G)
of a smallest base for
G
is the
base size
of
G
. Bases rose to prominence in the 1970s through the study of permutation group algorithms (see e.g.
[
16
]
); for many algorithms the complexity is a function of the base size. Blaha
[
1
]
shows that the problem of determining the base size of an arbitrary finite permutation group is NP-hard.

We can derive elementary upper and lower bounds as follows. If
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a base for
G
then each
g\in G
is uniquely determined by the tuple
(\alpha_{i}^{g})_{i=1}^{k}
, so
|G|\leq|\Omega|^{k}
. On the other hand, if
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is
irredundant
— that is
G_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{i}}>G_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{i+1}}
for all
i
— then each index is at least two, so
|G|\geq 2^{k}
, and hence
(\log|G|)/(\log|\Omega|)\leq k\leq\log|G|
. (All of our logarithms are to the base two, unless otherwise specified.)

One can compute a relatively small base using a greedy algorithm, as analysed by Blaha
[
1
]
. Any point of
\Omega
with a smallest stabiliser lies in a largest orbit. Hence, if we let
\mathcal{B}_{0}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i-1}\cup\{\beta_{i}\}
,
i>0
, where
\beta_{i}\in\Omega
is a point in any largest orbit of
G_{(\mathcal{B}_{i-1})}
, then there is an
n\geq 0
such that
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is an irredundant base — called a
greedy base
— for
G
which produces the greatest reduction in group size at each stage.

Let
\mathcal{G}(G)
denote the maximum size of a greedy base for
G
. In
[
1
]
, Blaha shows that the greedy approximation is nearly sharp, and in particular that there is some absolute constant
d
such that

\mathcal{G}(G)\leq db(G)\log\log|\Omega|.

Moreover, for any
k\geq 2
and
n
sufficiently large, Blaha constructs (intransitive) groups of degree
n
satisfying
b(G)=k
and
\mathcal{G}(G)\geq\frac{1}{5}k\log\log n
.

For primitive groups the situation appears different, as illustrated by this 1999 conjecture of Peter Cameron.

Conjecture 1.1

(Cameron’s Greedy Conjecture
[
5
]
)
.

There is some absolute constant
c
such that if
G
is a finite primitive permutation group then
\mathcal{G}(G)\leq cb(G)
.

Let
I(G)
be the maximum size of an irredundant base for
G
. A greedy base is irredundant, and so
b(G)\leq\mathcal{G}(G)\leq I(G)
. However, the gap between
b(G)
and
I(G)
can be arbitrarily large, even for primitive groups. For example, in
[
15
]
it is shown that for a primitive action of the symmetric group
\mathrm{S}_{n}
with point stabiliser
H\neq\mathrm{A}_{n}
primitive on
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, an upper bound of
I(\mathrm{S}_{n})\leq O(\sqrt{n})
is best possible, and yet
[
3
]
shows that in this case
b(\mathrm{S}_{n})=2
for
n\geq 13
. Therefore Cameron’s Greedy Conjecture suggests that
\mathcal{G}(G)
should fall much closer to
b(G)
than to
I(G)
. This can be seen explicitly in recent work of the first author
[
8
]
, which shows that for
G
an almost simple primitive sporadic group,
\mathcal{G}(G)=b(G)
, whereas work of Lee
[
11
]
shows that in the vast majority of such cases
I(G)>b(G)
. Similarly, recent work of Brenner and the authors
[
2
]
has shown that if
G
is primitive of odd order then
\mathcal{G}(G)=b(G)\leq 3
.

In this paper we consider the almost simple primitive groups
G
with alternating socle. Burness, Guralnick, and Saxl
[
3
]
show that for such
G
one of the following holds:

(i)

G
is acting on
\binom{[n]}{r}
, the collection of
r
-subsets of
[n]
with
2r<n
;

(ii)

G
is acting on partitions of
[kl]
into
k
parts of size
l
with
kl=n
;

(iii)

b(G)=2
; or

(iv)

G
falls into a list of 18 exceptions: see
[
3
, Corollaries 1.4 and 1.5 and Remark 1.7]
.

In 2021, Morris and Spiga
[
14
]
building on
[
4
]
gave explicit formulae for
b(G)
for all
(k,l)
pairs with partition action, and the base size of the actions on
[n]\choose r
has recently been completely determined by the authors
[
9
]
and independently for
\mathrm{S}_{n}
in
[
13
]
.

In this paper we make progress towards a proof of Conjecture
1.1
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of finite permutation groups up to permutation isomorphism. We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
ravenous
if there exists some constant
c>0
such that
\mathcal{G}(G)\leq cb(G)
for all
G\in\mathcal{F}
; if we are given an explicit constant
c
we say that
\mathcal{F}
is
c
-ravenous
. Restated, Cameron’s Greedy Conjecture is that the family of finite primitive permutation groups is ravenous.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of almost simple primitive groups with alternating socle, excluding those acting on
{[n]\choose r}
with
4r^{2}>n>2r
. Then
\mathcal{F}
is an
11
-ravenous family.

No significant effort has been made to optimise the value 11 — it seems likely that it should be less than
2
.

In Section
2
we prove Theorem
2.9
, which states that
\mathrm{S}_{n}
and
\mathrm{A}_{n}
acting on
{[n]\choose r}
with
n\geq 4r^{2}
are 17/10-ravenous. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
3.18
, which states that
\mathrm{S}_{n}
and
\mathrm{A}_{n}
acting on uniform partitions are 11-ravenous. Theorem
1.2
then follows from the observation that if
G
is transitive and
b(G)=2
then
\mathcal{G}(G)=2
, and explicit computations with the 18 groups in (iv) above.

2.
\mathrm{S}_{n}
and
\mathrm{A}_{n}
on
r
-subsets

Let
n>2r\geq 4
. Let
\mathrm{S}_{n,r}
and
\mathrm{A}_{n,r}
be
\mathrm{S}_{n}
and
\mathrm{A}_{n}
, respectively, in their actions on
\Omega:={[n]\choose r}
. For the majority of this section we analyse
\mathrm{S}_{n,r}
, before deducing an analogous result for
\mathrm{A}_{n,r}
. We shall show that
\mathcal{F}_{1}:=\{\mathrm{S}_{n,r},\mathrm{A}_{n,r}:n\geq 4r^{2}\}
is
17/10
-ravenous: see Theorem
2.9
.

We start with a condition on sets of
r
-sets which is equivalent to being a base. Let
\mathcal{B}
be a set of
r
-subsets of
[n]
. For
u\in[n]
, define the
neighbourhood
of
u
to be
N_{\mathcal{B}}(u):=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}:u\in\alpha\}
. Then
\mathcal{B}
is a base for
\mathrm{S}_{n,r}
if and only if
N_{\mathcal{B}}(u)\neq N_{\mathcal{B}}(v)
for all distinct
u,v\in[n]
.

Fix
G=\mathrm{S}_{n,r}
, let
\alpha_{i}\in\Omega
be the
i
th
r
-set chosen by the greedy algorithm, let
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{i}\}
, and let
G_{i}=G_{(\mathcal{B}_{i})}
. Note that in its action on
[n]
the group
G_{i}
is a direct product of symmetric groups on its orbits and so, up to
G
-conjugacy,
G_{i}
is uniquely determined by the sizes of these orbits.
The
G_{i}
-orbit size of each
\alpha\in\Omega
is a product of binomial coefficients given by the size of the intersection of
\alpha
with each
G_{i}
-orbit on
[n]
. Therefore, we can think of the greedy algorithm at step
i+1
as making a sequence of
r
choices of points via the following meta-greedy algorithm.

Algorithm 2.1

(
MetaGreedy
)
.

Input:
A collection
\mathcal{B}_{i}:=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{i}\}
of
r
-subsets of
[n]
.

Step
0
:
Set
M_{0}:=\emptyset
.

Step
1\leq j\leq r
:
Let
m_{j}\in[n]\setminus M_{j-1}
be any point from any
G_{i}
-orbit
\Delta\subseteq[n]
that maximises

(1)

\frac{|\Delta\setminus M_{j-1}|}{|\Delta\cap M_{j-1}|+1},

and let
M_{j}=M_{j-1}\cup\{m_{j}\}
. If (
1
) has maximum value 1 then in addition, choose
m_{j}
to be a point with smallest
\mathcal{B}_{i}
-neighbourhood.

Output:

\alpha_{i+1}:=M_{r}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\alpha
be an
r
-set. Then
|\alpha^{G_{i}}|
is maximal amongst all elements of
\Omega
if and only if there is a possible output
\alpha_{i+1}
of
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
such that
(G_{i})_{\alpha}\cong(G_{i})_{\alpha_{i+1}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be the set of
G_{i}
-orbits on
[n]
. First, suppose that
|\alpha^{G_{i}}|
is maximal so that

\left|\alpha^{G_{i}}\right|=\prod_{\Delta\in\mathcal{O}}{|\Delta|\choose|\Delta\cap\alpha|}\geq\prod_{\Delta\in\mathcal{O}}{|\Delta|\choose|\Delta\cap\beta|},

for all
\beta\in\Omega
. We expand the binomial coefficients to obtain the inequality

\left|\alpha^{G_{i}}\right|=\prod_{\Delta\in\mathcal{O}}\prod_{l=0}^{|\Delta\cap\alpha|-1}\frac{|\Delta|-l}{l+1}\geq\prod_{\Delta\in\mathcal{O}}\prod_{l=0}^{|\Delta\cap\beta|-1}\frac{|\Delta|-l}{l+1}.

Consider
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
. The set
\alpha
contains a point
m_{1}
from a largest
G_{i}
-orbit on
[n]
, so
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
can choose
M_{1}\subseteq\alpha
. Suppose inductively that
M_{j}\subseteq\alpha
for some
j\geq 1
. Note

|M_{j}^{G_{i}}|=\prod_{\Delta\in\mathcal{O}}\prod_{l=0}^{|\Delta\cap M_{j}|-1}\frac{|\Delta|-l}{l+1},

so that

|\alpha^{G_{i}}|=|M_{j}^{G_{i}}|\cdot\prod_{\Delta\in\mathcal{O}}\prod_{l=|\Delta\cap M_{j}|}^{|\Delta\cap\alpha|-1}\frac{|\Delta|-l}{l+1}.

Since
|\alpha^{G_{i}}|
is maximal,
\alpha\setminus M_{j}
contains a point from at least one orbit
\Delta
that maximises (
1
). Therefore if
\frac{|\Delta|-|\Delta\cap M_{j}|}{|\Delta\cap M_{j}|+1}\neq 1
then
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
can pick
m_{j+1}\in\Delta\setminus M_{j}
. If this holds for all
j
then by induction
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
can return
\alpha_{i+1}=M_{r}=\alpha
.
Otherwise there exists a
j
for which (
1
) has maximum value
1
. Since this maximum value is non-increasing in
j
, and
n>2r
, the maximum value of (
1
) is 1 for all
s\geq j
. It follows that
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
chooses each
m_{s}\in[n]
to have minimum neighbourhood size. Since each stabiliser is a product of symmetric groups on its orbits we deduce that
(G_{i})_{M_{j-1}}\cong(G_{i})_{M_{j}}\cong(G_{i})_{\alpha}
. Hence in both cases there is a permissible output
{\alpha_{i+1}}
of
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
such that
(G_{i})_{\alpha}\cong(G_{i})_{\alpha_{i+1}}
.

For the other direction it suffices to show that all outputs of
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
yield
G_{i}
-orbits of the same size. Whenever (
1
) has maximum value
o
attained by multiple orbits,
MetaGreedy
will successively choose a point from each such orbit, and
|M_{j}^{G_{i}}|=o\cdot|M_{j-1}^{G_{i}}|
, so the result follows.
∎

Thus without loss of generality, we may assume each
\alpha_{i}
in a greedy base is chosen by
MetaGreedy
. Comparisons of the value of (
1
) are therefore useful. It will be most helpful in the form given now.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
be any two
G_{i}
-orbits on
[n]
. Then

\frac{|\Delta_{1}\setminus\alpha_{i+1}|+1}{|\Delta_{1}\cap\alpha_{i+1}|}\geq\frac{|\Delta_{2}\setminus\alpha_{i+1}|}{|\Delta_{2}\cap\alpha_{i+1}|+1}.

Proof.

Consider
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
. Let
j
be maximal subject to
m_{j}\in\Delta_{1}
. Then

\frac{|\Delta_{1}\setminus M_{j-1}|}{|\Delta_{1}\cap M_{j-1}|+1}\geq\frac{|\Delta_{2}\setminus M_{j-1}|}{|\Delta_{2}\cap M_{j-1}|+1}\geq\frac{|\Delta_{2}\setminus\alpha_{i+1}|}{|\Delta_{2}\cap\alpha_{i+1}|+1}.

The maximality of
j
implies
\Delta_{1}\cap\alpha_{i+1}=(\Delta_{1}\cap M_{j-1})\cup\{m_{j}\}
, and so
|\Delta_{1}\setminus M_{j-1}|=|\Delta_{1}\setminus\alpha_{i+1}|+1
, and
|\Delta_{1}\cap M_{j-1}|+1=|\Delta_{1}\cap\alpha_{i+1}|
, thus the result follows.
∎

Let
s
be maximal subject to
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{s}
being disjoint, let
E_{i}=[n]\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{i}\alpha_{j}\right)
be the points in no set in
\mathcal{B}_{i}
, and let
e_{i}=|E_{i}|
.
Conversely, let
F_{i}=\{m\in[n]:|N_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(m)|>1\}
be the set of points in more than one set in
\mathcal{B}_{i}
. If two elements of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
intersect in a single point
m\in[n]
, then
m
is fixed by
G_{j}
for all
j\geq i
; we shall prove in Lemma
2.3

that in many cases the pairwise intersections of elements of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
have size at most 1, and hence for most
i\in\{1,\ldots,\mathcal{G}(G)\}
the set
F_{i}
consists of all points fixed by
G_{i}
with neighbourhoods of
size at least two.
Eventually we shall prove that in fact all points in
F_{i}
are fixed by
G_{j}
for
j\geq i
.

We shall use Lemma
2.2
to show that for
n\geq 4r^{2}
and any
i
, there is a large collection of
G_{i}
-orbits on
[n]
all of size differing by at most one.
For
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
let
\mathcal{U}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i}
. For
i\geq s+1
, recursively define
\mathcal{U}_{i}:=\{A\setminus F_{i}\,:\,A\in\mathcal{U}_{i-1}\},
so that for
i\geq s+1
the set
\mathcal{U}_{i}
consists of the subsets of each of
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}
containing all points belonging to a unique element of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
. Thus,
\mathcal{U}_{i}
is a collection of
G_{i}
-orbits in its natural action on
\alpha_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\alpha_{s}\subseteq[n]
. For
u\in\{0,\ldots,r\}

and
i\in\{1,2,\dots,\mathcal{G}(G)\}
, let

\mathcal{O}_{u,i}=\{\alpha_{j}\setminus F_{i}\,:\,j\leq i,\ |\alpha_{j}\setminus F_{i}|\in\{u,u-1\}\},

which we shall view as a multiset, just to allow for multiple occurrences of the empty set.
Then
\mathcal{O}_{u,i}
is the multiset of all
G_{i}
-orbits of size
u
or
u-1
consisting of points chosen exactly once by step
i
. Now for
i>1
let
u_{i}
be the largest
u\in\{0,\ldots,r\}
satisfying

(i)

\mathcal{U}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{u,i}
; and

(ii)

\mathcal{O}_{u,i}
contains at least one
u
-set other than
\alpha_{i}\setminus F_{i}
,

or undefined if no such
u
exists.
The next result shows that
u_{i}
exists for all
i\geq 2
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
n\geq 4r^{2}\geq 16
. Then the following all hold.

(i)

s\geq 3r
.

(ii)

u_{2}=r
, and
u_{i}\in\{u_{i-1},u_{i-1}-1\}
for all
i>2
, so
u_{i}
exists for all
i
. Furthermore,
Metagreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
never chooses more than one point from any set in
\mathcal{O}_{u_{i},i}
.

(iii)

|\mathcal{O}_{u_{i},i}|\geq 3r
for all
i\geq s
.

(iv)

Suppose
u_{i}\geq 2
and
|\alpha\setminus F_{i}|\leq u_{i}
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
. If every element of
F_{i}
is fixed by
G_{i}
and has a neighbourhood of size exactly two, then the same is true for
F_{i+1}
and
G_{i+1}
.

Proof.

By definition of
s
, there exists an
i\leq s
such that

\alpha_{i}\cap\alpha_{s+1}\neq\emptyset
, so Lemma
2.2
with
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})=(\alpha_{i},E_{s})
shows that

\frac{r-1+1}{1}\geq\frac{(n-sr)-(r-1)}{(r-1)+1}.

Therefore
s\geq(n-r^{2}-(r-1))/r\geq(3r^{2}-(r-1))/r>3r-1
, but
s
is an integer hence (i) holds.

We show (ii) inductively. For
2\leq i\leq s
by definition
\mathcal{U}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathcal{O}_{r,i}
so
u_{i}=r
and
F_{i}
is empty. Let
i>s
. By induction, there is some
u_{i-1}
such that all
s
sets in
\mathcal{U}_{i-1}
have size in
\{u_{i-1},u_{i-1}-1\}
, so by (i) the group
G_{i-1}
has at least
3r
orbits on
[n]
of size either
u_{i-1}
or
u_{i-1}-1
. Now,
(u_{i-1}-1)/2\leq(u_{i-1}-1)/1
with equality if and only if
u_{i-1}=1
so from (
1
) we deduce the “furthermore” statement. Moreover,
MetaGreedy
(\mathcal{B}_{i})
only chooses a point from an orbit of size
u_{i-1}-1
if it has already chosen a point from every orbit of size
u_{i-1}
, so the result follows.

Parts (iii) and (iv) now follow from Parts (i) and (ii).
∎

Since Lemma
2.3
(ii) shows that
u_{i}
is uniquely determined by
i
, we shall write
\mathcal{O}_{i}
instead of
\mathcal{O}_{u_{i},i}
. We say that an
\alpha\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
is
excessive
if
|\alpha\setminus F_{i}|>u_{i}
, and a collection of

-sets is

if at least one member is excessive.
If we never encounter excessive sets, then our greedy bases are well-behaved.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose
n\geq 4r^{2}\geq 16
, and let
\mathcal{B}
be a greedy base for
\mathrm{S}_{n,r}
. If no
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is excessive, then
|\mathcal{B}|\leq\frac{2n}{r}
.

Proof.

Let
i
be maximal such that
u_{i}=2
. It follows inductively from Lemma
2.3
(iv) that every element of
F_{i+1}
is fixed by
G_{i+1}
and has a neighbourhood of size two. Since
u_{i+1}=1
, by Lemma
2.3
(ii) and (iii) there are at least
2r
singleton sets in
\mathcal{O}_{i+1}
, and at most
r
copies of the empty set. Since
u_{i+1}=1
, and
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
is not excessive, (
1
) implies that
e_{i}=|E_{i}|\leq 3
, and hence
e_{i+1}\leq 2
. We deduce that
\mathcal{B}_{i+2}
is a base with no neighbourhoods of size greater than two.

Thus double counting pairs
(\alpha,a)
such that
a\in\alpha\in\mathcal{B}
shows that

|\mathcal{B}|r=\sum_{a\in[n]}|N_{\mathcal{B}}(a)|\leq 2n,

hence the result.
∎

We now consider the possibility that some
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is excessive.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
n\geq 4r^{2}\geq 16
. Suppose that
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is not excessive, but
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
is excessive.
Then
\alpha_{i+1}
is the unique excessive set in
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
.
Let
f=|\alpha_{i+1}\cap E_{i}|
be the number of points chosen for the first time in
\alpha_{i+1}
. Then either

(i)

u_{i+1}=u_{i}=f-1
,
\mathcal{O}_{i+1}
contains at least
r
sets of size
u_{i}
, and
e_{i+1}\leq u_{i}^{2}+u_{i}
; or

(ii)

u_{i+1}+1=u_{i}\leq f\leq u_{i}+1
and
e_{i+1}\leq u_{i}^{2}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
(ii) for
j\leq i
we can bound
|\alpha_{j}\cap F_{i}|\leq|\alpha_{j}\cap F_{i+1}|+1
, and if
|\alpha_{j}\setminus F_{i}|=u_{i}-1
then
\alpha_{i+1}\cap\alpha_{j}\neq\emptyset
only if
u_{i+1}=u_{i}-1
, whence
\alpha_{i+1}
is the unique excessive set.

Since
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is not excessive,
|\alpha_{i}\setminus F_{i}|\leq u_{i}<r
. Thus
there exists a
G_{i-1}
-orbit
\Delta
of size
u_{i-1}
such that
|\Delta\cap\alpha_{i}|\geq 1
. Now Lemma
2.2
with
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})=(\Delta,E_{i-1})
shows that

(2)

u_{i-1}\geq\frac{e_{i}}{e_{i-1}-e_{i}+1}\geq\frac{e_{i-1}-u_{i}}{u_{i}+1}

Suppose that
u_{i+1}=u_{i}
. Lemma
2.3
(ii) states that
u_{i-1}\leq u_{i}+1
, so working with the first and last expression in (

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose

. Then

Proof.

If

,

contains at least

-neighbourhood of size one, thus — since

Since any pointwise stabiliser for the action of
\mathrm{S}_{n,r}
is a direct product of symmetric groups, and any non-trivial symmetric group contains a transposition, we immediately get the following corollary.

Corollary 2.8
.

Suppose

. Then
\mathcal{G}(\mathrm{A}_{n,r})\leq\frac{2n}{r}+1
.

Proof.

For
\mathrm{S}_{n,r}
there is a transposition in each stabiliser until we reach a base; the orbits for
\mathrm{A}_{n,r}
therefore have the same size as the corresponding orbits for
\mathrm{S}_{n,r}
and so the greedy algorithm will make the same choices for
\mathrm{A}_{n,r}
as it does for
\mathrm{S}_{n,r}
, but may stop one step earlier.
∎

With that we deduce that the subset actions with

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

We first consider

[

. By the proof of
[
6

, without loss of generality we may let

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\dots,\{2k-1,2k\}\}\mbox{ and }\mathcal{P}_{2}=\{\{2k,1\},\{2,3\},\dots,\{2k-2,2k-1\}\}

so that
G_{\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}}=\mathrm{D}_{2k}.
Let

\mathcal{P}_{3}=\{\{1,2\},\{3,5\},\{4,2k\}\}\cup\{\{2i,2i+1\}:3\leq i\leq k-1\},

which exists since

, and let

. Then
\{1,2\}
is the only set common to
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{3}
so

. If

\mathcal{P}_{2}^{\tau}=\mathcal{P}_{2}

Now let

3.1.
Intersection arrays and their properties

In this subsection we recall some key definitions from
[
6
]
, and generalise the concept of intersection matrices.

For the following to be well-defined we will usually think of each partition in
\Pi
as being ordered by the smallest element in each part. Let
t\in\{1,2,3\}
, and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote by
\mathcal{M}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{t}}
the set of all

intersection array

M(\mathcal{P}_{1},\dots,P_{t})
of
\mathcal{P}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{P}_{t}\in\Pi
is the array
M=(m_{i_{1}\cdots i_{t}})\in\mathcal{M}_{k,\dots,k}
with
m_{i_{1}\cdots i_{t}}=\left|\bigcap_{j=1}^{t}P_{ji_{j}}\right|
. We also say that
M
is a
(k,l)
-
intersection array
or a
(k,l)
-intersection
t
-array.

The next result is a 3-dimensional analogue of
[
6
, Lemma 2.1]

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
W=(w_{ijs})\in\mathcal{M}_{k,k,k}
and let
N=M(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})=(n_{ij})
for some
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}\in\Pi
. Then there is a
\mathcal{T}\in\Pi
such that
W=M(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{T})
if and only if
\sum_{s=1}^{k}w_{ijs}=n_{ij}
for all
i,j
and
\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq k}w_{ijs}=l
for all
s
.

Proof.

First suppose that
\mathcal{T}\in\Pi
satisfies
W=M(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{T})
. For all
i,j

\sum_{s=1}^{k}w_{ijs}=|P_{i}\cap Q_{j}|,

hence
N=M(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
satisfies
n_{ij}=\sum_{s=1}^{k}w_{ijs}
. Additionally, for each
s

\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}w_{ijs}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}|P_{i}\cap Q_{j}\cap T_{s}|=\sum_{j=1}^{k}|Q_{j}\cap T_{s}|=|T_{s}|=l.

On the other hand, suppose instead
\sum_{s=1}^{k}w_{ijs}=n_{ij}
for all
i,j
and
\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq k}w_{ijs}=l
for all
s
. For
1\leq s\leq k
, let
T_{s}=\bigsqcup_{1\leq i,j\leq k}X_{ijs}
where
X_{ijs}
is any set of size
w_{ijs}
such that

X_{ijs}\subseteq(P_{i}\cap Q_{j})\setminus\left(\bigcup_{r<s}T_{r}\right).

This is possible since
\sum_{s=1}^{k}w_{ijs}=n_{ij}=|P_{i}\cap Q_{j}|
. Then

|T_{s}|=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq k}|X_{ijs}|=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq k}w_{ijs}=l,

and so
\mathcal{T}:=\{T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{k}\}
is a
(k,l)
-partition. Moreover, since
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are partitions,
P_{i}\cap Q_{j}
is disjoint from
P_{i^{\prime}}\cap Q_{j^{\prime}}
for any
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\neq(i,j)
, so in particular
|P_{i}\cap Q_{j}\cap T_{s}|=|P_{i}\cap Q_{j}\cap X_{ijs}|=|X_{ijs}|=w_{ijs}
, as desired.
∎

We shall say that the
(k,l)
-intersection array
W=(w_{ijs})

arises
from

if
\sum_{s=1}^{k}w_{ijs}=n_{ij}
for all

.

(and

H\leq G_{\mathcal{P}}
for some

is
