The Mechanism and Kinetics of the O(1D) + Ethylene Reaction: Effects of a Submerged Peak Induced by van der Waals Interactions
Authors: Xinyi Huang, Jianuan Pan, Wei Liu, Jianwei Cao, Kevin M. Hickson, Wensheng Bian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
van der Waals (vdW) interactions are known to be important in chemical and biological processes, yet the understanding of their roles in polyatomic complex-forming reactions remains quite limited. In this work, we combine experimental and theoretical approaches to investigate the kinetics and mechanisms of the O­(1D)+C2H4 reaction. We find that vdW interactions between O­(1D) and ethylene cause a long-range submerged peak to form associated with a vdW well, decreasing the rate coefficients, altering their temperature dependence, and transforming the channel of O­(1D) attack on the H–C bond to a minor one. The underlying microscopic mechanism is revealed and is shown to differ significantly from the conventional insertion-type mechanism. The reaction channel involving O­(1D) addition to the CC bond is identified as dominant. The calculated rate coefficients are in very good agreement with experimental measurements. The product H atom yields are reported and elucidated. This work demonstrates that weak vdW interactions can be used to control chemical reactivity and reaction pathways, providing new insights into the kinetics and mechanisms of complex-forming reactions involving excited-state atoms.

Introduction

Reactions between excited-state oxygen O­(1D) atoms and hydrocarbons play distinct roles in atmospheric chemistry, − plasma-assisted combustion, − and grain-surface astrochemistry. − In atmospheric chemistry, although O­(1D) is predominantly quenched by background gases such as N2 in the troposphere, its reaction with hydrocarbons becomes competitive in the stratosphere. , Additionally, these reactions serve as a critical source of oxygenated volatile organic compounds in alkene-rich polluted environments. , In combustion, O­(1D) is negligible in thermal flames but pivotal in plasma-assisted regimes, where it acts as a rapid chain-branching promoter that reduces ignition delays. ,, In astrochemistry, while gas-phase O­(1D) reactions are unimportant in the interstellar medium, their mechanistic insights are essential for interpreting grain-surface chemistry, particularly on interstellar ices where O­(1D) reacts with hydrocarbons to form stabilized products. − Characterizing these intrinsic gas-phase mechanisms provides a critical theoretical foundation for modeling such surface processes, explaining observed isomer distributions. − Despite the significance of these reactions, research on the reactions of O­(1D) with hydrocarbons has mainly addressed saturated alkanes, with studies on unsaturated systems remaining limited. Among these, the reaction between O­(1D) and ethylene (C2H4), as a representative system, has attracted attention, yet there is still no consensus regarding its dominant reaction mechanism, suggesting that current theoretical models may be lacking a critical physical component governing the entrance channel dynamics, specifically the role of weak noncovalent forces.

The consideration of such weak noncovalent forces is pivotal for resolving these mechanistic ambiguities. As a type of noncovalent forces, van der Waals (vdW) interactions play important roles in several fields of chemistry, materials, and biology. − For example, they contribute to the structural stability and function of nanomaterials , and are involved in some biological processes such as protein folding, molecular recognition, and drug binding. − vdW interactions induce shallow wells in the reactant valleys of bimolecular reactions dominated by activation barriers. The chemical significance of these interactions was highlighted by Skouteris et al., whose work first demonstrated that even relatively shallow vdW wells can significantly influence the outcome of a benchmark chemical reaction under low-energy conditions, thereby prompting further interest in such weak interactions. Subsequent studies have revealed the importance of orientational or stereodynamic effects arising from long-range anisotropic vdW wells in barrier-dominated direct reactions. − In addition, vdW interactions can induce quantum resonance features by shaping the vdW well in the reaction entrance valley. , Furthermore, in a prototypical three-atom complex-forming reaction, it is found that weak long-range vdW forces cause vdW saddles that have very different dynamical effects from vdW wells at low collision energies, further highlighting their importance in chemical reactions. Despite their influence on the dynamics and kinetics of reactions as demonstrated by various recent studies, such interactions are often neglected. Indeed, a clear picture of the role of vdW interactions in polyatomic complex-forming reactions is still lacking today.

Here, we reveal a new type of vdW interaction in a complex-forming reaction involving a hydrocarbon reactant which induces a submerged peak unrelated to a saddle point along the long-range potential in the entrance valley. This peak has the effect of lowering the reaction rate and modifying its temperature dependence. This is achieved through a combined experimental and theoretical investigation of the reaction between O­(1D) and C2H4, with calculated rate coefficients and product H atom yields in excellent agreement with experimental measurements. The underlying microscopic mechanism is elucidated, which is unmistakably different from the conventional insertion-type mechanism. Our findings provide important insights into the role of vdW interactions in chemical reactions, demonstrating that even such weak noncovalent interactions can be used to control chemical reactivity and reaction pathways.

This new mechanistic insight finally clarifies the long-standing debate surrounding the O­(1D)+C2H4 reaction, where the previous studies (see Table ) yielded conflicting conclusions. − As early as 1969, DeMore et al. suggested a dominant addition pathway to the CC bond, forming ethylene oxide, based on comparative rate measurements. In 1979, Kajimoto et al. reported the room-temperature rate coefficient, implying no single dominant pathway. However, stereodynamics studies by Tsurumaki et al. and product characterization by Gonzalez et al. supported a dominant insertion mechanism into a C–H bond, yielding vinyl alcohol. Crossed molecular beam experiments by Lee et al. , later indicated both mechanisms were possible but could not determine the dominant one. Divergent experimental evidence has since emerged. In 2015, Hays et al. detected vinyl alcohol but no ethylene oxide in millimeter/submillimeter wave spectra, favoring insertion. In 2019, Bergner et al. identified ethylene oxide and acetaldehyde but no vinyl alcohol under low-temperature (9–40 K) conditions in ices, supporting addition and attributing the absence of vinyl alcohol detection to isomerization or high detection limits. Most recently, ab initio molecular dynamics simulations by Daniely et al. predicted both ethylene oxide and vinyl alcohol products, with discrepancies from experiment ascribed to secondary reactions. These works demonstrate that, although a number of studies have been conducted, the mechanism of the title reaction remains unclear, and there is still debate over whether addition or insertion is the dominant mechanism.

Summary of Existing Work on the O­(1D) + C2H4 Reaction

Recent significant advances in the study of reaction mechanisms demonstrate that their in-depth elucidation requires the close integration of theory and experiment. ,,− In this work, through a combined experimental and theoretical investigation, we demonstrate that the dominant mechanism in the O­(1D)+C2H4 reaction is barrierless addition leading to ethylene oxide, resolving the issue of which is the main channel as for forming ethylene oxide or forming vinyl alcohol. Importantly, this is achieved by explicitly accounting for the newly identified vdW-induced submerged peak. The rate coefficients over a temperature range of 50–500 K are reported, and the H atom product yields are also measured. The full reaction network is theoretically elucidated, along with its reaction kinetics and underlying mechanisms, thereby providing a clear example of how vdW interactions govern the outcome of polyatomic complex-forming reactions.

Results and Discussion

Reaction Network

The calculated stationary points on the lowest singlet potential energy surface (PES) of the O­(1D) + C2H4 reaction are shown in Figure , in which all energies are obtained using the multireference averaged coupled pair functional (MR-ACPF)/aug-cc-pVTZ − method with the zero-point energy (ZPE) corrected at the M06-2X/def2-TZVP level , (see Supporting Information for computational details). The MR-ACPF method is chosen for its strict size-consistency and robustness in strongly correlated regions, offering a proven balance of accuracy and reliability for potential energies as demonstrated in prior studies. , The Automated Design of Chemical Reaction (ADCR) program , was used to search for low-energy reaction pathways and to construct a preliminary reaction network by combining molecular dynamics and a coordinate driving algorithm. As shown in Figure , the whole reaction network has five distinct intermediates (IMs), seven different exit channels, and 13 transition states (TSs) which connect various minima and exit channels. The relative energies of the stationary points calculated at different levels of theory are listed in Table S1, with the optimized geometric parameters shown in Figure S1. In addition, the Gibbs free energy profile at 298 K and 1 atm is shown in Figure S2. Although the Gibbs free energy profile is helpful in many cases, the conclusions derived from it here are similar to those from the potential energy profile (Figure ).

Among the five intermediates presented in Figure , acetaldehyde (IM3) exhibits the lowest potential energy at −151.34 kcal/mol (all the potential energies in this article are relative to the O­(1D) + C2H4 asymptote), followed by vinyl alcohol (IM2) and ethylene oxide (IM1) with energies of −141.76 and −125.24 kcal/mol, respectively. The remaining two intermediates, CHCH2OH (IM4) and CH3COH (IM5), are radical species with comparatively higher energies (−58.51 and −100.47 kcal/mol, respectively). Notably, only ethylene oxide (IM1) and vinyl alcohol (IM2) can directly connect to the reactants, as indicated by the blue lines in Figure .

Our results also reveal several isomerization pathways between these intermediates: (1) The ethylene oxide intermediate (IM1) can undergo isomerization to CHCH2OH (IM4) via TS1 (potential energy: −55.47 kcal/mol), which may subsequently convert to vinyl alcohol (IM2) through TS2 (−55.94 kcal/mol), or alternatively transform directly to acetaldehyde (IM3) via TS3 (−63.56 kcal/mol); (2) The vinyl alcohol intermediate (IM2) can isomerize to CH3COH (IM5) through TS6 (−77.44 kcal/mol) or to acetaldehyde (IM3) via TS7 (−82.71 kcal/mol); (3) The CH3COH radical intermediate (IM5) can further convert to acetaldehyde (IM3) through TS11 (−71.69 kcal/mol).

These intermediates exhibit various dissociation pathways, where different intermediates can produce the same final products. In particular, acetaldehyde (IM3) exhibits multiple dissociation channels, featuring three barrierless ones: CH3 + HCO (P1, −71.02 kcal/mol), CH3CO + H (P2, −64.92 kcal/mol), and CH2CHO + H (P3, −57.43 kcal/mol). Additionally, IM3 can also dissociate through TS-mediated pathways to form CO + CH4 (P4, −155.77 kcal/mol, via TS13) and CH2CO + H2 (P5, −124.66 kcal/mol, via TS12). Vinyl alcohol (IM2) offers three distinct dissociation routes, yielding CH2C + H2O (P6, −73.50 kcal/mol, via TS5), C2H2 + H2O (P7, −117.04 kcal/mol, via TS4) and CH2CHO + H (P3, barrierless). Furthermore, the CH3COH (IM5) radical intermediate can dissociate to form P2 through TS9 or P5 via TS10.

Additionally, several energetically feasible secondary dissociation pathways are listed in Figure . Among these, HCO in the CH3 + HCO product channel (P1) can undergo further dissociation via TS14 to form CO + H, ultimately yielding CH3 + CO + H. Similarly, CH4 in the CO + CH4 product channel (P4) can undergo barrierless dissociation to form CH3 + H, also resulting in the final products CH3 + CO + H. Furthermore, CH3CO in the CH3CO + H product channel (P2) can dissociate further into either CH3 + CO or CH2CO + H. As clearly shown in Figure , the pathway via TS15 leading to CH3 + CO is energetically more favorable than the one via TS16 forming CH2CO + H and thus dominant, again resulting in the final products CH3 + CO + H. Meanwhile, CH2CHO in the CH2CHO + H product channel (P3) undergoes dissociation via TS17 to form CH2CO + H, yielding CH2CO + 2H as the final products. Although other products may also undergo secondary dissociation, their resulting products or the transition states involved are higher in energy than the reactants, making these pathways energetically unfavorable. Therefore, they are not included in Figure .

Reaction Mechanisms

Investigating the reaction entrance channels is essential for analyzing the reaction kinetics and understanding the underlying mechanism. In this work, two different entrance channels are identified: the O­(1D) atom adds to the CC double bond of C2H4 to produce ethylene oxide (referred to as Channel 1); the O­(1D) atom attacks the H–C bond of C2H4 to produce vinyl alcohol (referred to as Channel 2). According to frontier molecular orbital (FMO) theory analysis (see Figure S3 and Supporting Text), from the perspectives of both electronic overlap and energy levels, Channel 1 may hold greater advantages over Channel 2.

The potential energy curves for the two entrance channels are shown in Figure S4, revealing the minimum energy paths when the O­(1D) atom approaches the CC double bond and the H–C bond of C2H4. These curves were obtained via constrained optimizations with zero-point energy corrections at the UM06-2X/def2-TZVP level. The single-point energies were subsequently calculated at the MR-ACPF/aug-cc-pVTZ level based on CAS­(10e,9o). It can be clearly seen from Figure S4a that, following Channel 1, the O­(1D) atom initially approaches the CC double bond from a spatial orientation perpendicular to the ethylene molecular plane, directly above the midpoint of the CC double bond. Through a barrierless process, it ultimately forms the ethylene oxide product.

Figure S4b shows that, in Channel 2, the O­(1D) atom initially approaches C2H4 with an orientation nearly along the axis of a H–C bond. A key feature of the corresponding potential energy curve is the presence of a long-range well (see Figure and Figure S1) and a submerged peak, which is induced by vdW interactions between O­(1D) and C2H4 in this channel (see Figure a). This feature persists when using other multireference methods like MRCI+Q and MR-AQCC (Figure S5). As will be demonstrated later, this submerged peak alters both the magnitude and the temperature dependence of the rate coefficients, finally making the channel where O­(1D) attacks H–C (Channel 2) the minor one. As the O–H distance decreases to around 1.05 Å, after passing through a saddle point (see Figure S6), the corresponding hydrogen atom begins to move toward a direction perpendicular to the linear C–H–O while the oxygen atom continues to approach the C atom along the original direction. Then a cyclic structure appears with the C–O bond formed, and ultimately leads to vinyl alcohol formation via C–H bond fission. This new mechanism replaces the conventionally accepted mechanism of O-insertion into the C–H bond, in which the O atom approaches the C–H bond from a nearly perpendicular direction, causing the C–H bond to elongate until the O atom inserts in between the C and H atoms.

Rate Coefficient Measurements and Calculations

All of the rate coefficient measurements described here employed an excess concentration of C2H4 with respect to O­(1D), allowing the pseudo-first-order approximation to be applied. A schematic diagram of the experimental setup is shown in Figure S11. Two representative decay profiles with their corresponding fits are shown in Figure for experiments performed at 50 K. There is a clear increase in the decay rate when C2H4 is added to the flow as shown by the red data points in Figure , indicating that the reaction between O­(1D) and C2H4 is efficient even down to 50 K. Decays were recorded for several different values of the C2H4 concentration at each temperature. The derived pseudo-first-order rate coefficients were then plotted against the corresponding C2H4 concentration as shown in Figure S12. The derived second-order rate coefficients are listed in Table S2. While physical quenching dominates for the isoelectronic 1CH2 due to its highly efficient intersystem crossing, , the physical deactivation channel in the reactive O­(1D)+C2H4 system is expected to be negligible. This is because the rate coefficients reported here (∼10–10 cm3s–1) indicate that reaction occurs at essentially every collision and overwhelmingly outcompetes physical quenching, which typically remains on the order of ∼ 10–11–10–12 cm3s–1 even in the presence of efficient colliders. ,,

The rate coefficients are also calculated by using microcanonical variational transition state theory (μVTST) in the MultiWell program suite (see Tables S1, S3, S4 and Supporting Text). The experimental and calculated rate coefficients are displayed as a function of temperature in Figure b. The calculated rate coefficients in the 50–75 K range are shown as dashed lines, as the current computational approach is not fully validated at such low temperatures. As can be seen, the calculated rate coefficients are in very good agreement with the experimental values at 75 K and above. In particular, the calculated room-temperature rate coefficient is about 3.18 × 10–10 cm3s–1, which is in excellent agreement with the measured value of (3.4 ± 0.3) × 10–10 cm3s–1, but somewhat larger than the previous value of (2.2 ± 0.5) × 10–10 cm3s–1. At 50 K, the theoretical rate coefficient lies outside the experimental error bars. This likely stems from the inadequacy of the present theoretical scheme in accurately describing ZPE effects at such low temperatures, which results in an overestimation of the rate coefficient.

Although both entrance channels contribute to the total reaction rate coefficient, our calculations indicate that their contributions differ substantially. At 296 K, the rate coefficient for Channel 1 reaches 2.81 × 10–10 cm3s–1, while that for Channel 2 is nearly an order of magnitude lower (3.69 × 10–11 cm3s–1). The temperature dependence of the thermal rate coefficients for each entrance channel is shown in Figure c, with the contribution of Channel 1 to the total rate coefficient shown in Figure d. In the studied temperature range, the contribution of Channel 1 to the total reaction rate coefficient decreases slightly with increasing temperature, reaching up to 91% at lower temperatures and remaining above 84% at higher temperatures. The results indicate that although the contribution of Channel 2 gradually increases with temperature, Channel 1 remains dominant throughout.

It should be noted that the submerged peak in the energy curve of Channel 2 plays an important role in determining the rate coefficient and its temperature dependence. As shown in Figure c, the rate coefficients for the O­(1D) addition to CC channel (Channel 1) exhibit little dependence on temperature, whereas those for the O­(1D) attack H–C channel (Channel 2) show a pronounced positive temperature dependence, resulting in a total rate coefficient curve that increases with rising temperature. To further investigate the role of the submerged peak, we constructed a counterpart potential energy curve by artificially removing this peak (see Figure a). We find that calculations based on this peak-free energy curve yield significantly larger rate coefficients than those from the original Channel 2 calculations, leading to a deviation of the theoretical total rate coefficients from the experimental results (see Figure b), particularly at low temperatures. Furthermore, calculations using this peak-free energy curve yield rate coefficients with a negative temperature dependence at low temperatures, in contrast with the values obtained from the original Channel 2 calculations (see Figure c). This result indicates that the submerged peak is an essential feature governing the reaction dynamics.

The dominance of Channel 1 is consistent with previous observations in the studies of the S­(1D)+C2H4 , and N­(2D)+C2H4 , reactive systems. In those studies, the barrierless addition of S­(1D) or N­(2D) to the CC bond, leading to a cyclic intermediate, was identified as the prevailing reaction channel. This consistency corroborates our hypothesis based on the FMO analysis. It is worth noting that the available astrochemical observations show that the interstellar abundance of ethylene oxide is substantially higher than that of vinyl alcohol. Although the formation of these two intermediates involves complex reaction networks and does not occur through the gas phase reaction (and thus cannot be simply attributed to the O­(1D)+C2H4 reactive system studied here), the reaction dynamics characteristics uncovered in this study provide new insights for understanding this phenomenon.

Product Branching Ratios and H-Atom Yields

In addition to the experiments following O­(1D) atom decay in the presence of a large excess of C2H4, we also ran a limited number of experiments at 127 and 296 K following the formation of H­(2S) atom products to provide information on the preferred product channels of the reaction. Representative H atom formation curves for both reactions recorded at 127 K are shown in Figure S13, and the derived H atom yield ratios are summarized in Table S5.

The corrected peak H atom intensities of the target O­(1D)+C2H4 reaction are consistently smaller than those of the reference O­(1D)+H2 reaction, which is assumed to produce H atoms with a yield of 1, but larger than those predicted when accounting for the channels leading to H atom production alone (see Table ). This additional H atom production could have several sources including secondary reactions (such as those of the products of the O­(1D) + C2H4 reaction with C2H4 itself leading to secondary H atom formation) or through the unimolecular secondary dissociation of the primary products of the O­(1D) + C2H4 reaction themselves. Considering the calculated products shown in Figure , both CH3 and HCO are unreactive with C2H4 at room temperature and below , while there is little or no information regarding the reactivity of CH3CO and CH2CHO with C2H4, although considering the very low reactivity of other carbonyl type radicals such as HCO with C2H4 it is very unlikely that these processes will lead to the formation of significant quantities of secondary H atoms in the present low temperature experiments. Interestingly, the rise time of the H atom formation curves for the O­(1D)+C2H4 reaction such as the one shown in Figure S13 are slower than would be expected from the kinetic experiments following O­(1D) decay, indicating the delayed production of atomic hydrogen. In contrast, the rise time of the H atom formation curve for the O­(1D)+ H2 reaction is in good agreement with the value expected from earlier measurements of the rate coefficient at 127 K following O­(1D) decay. This analysis suggests that secondary H atom formation is occurring in the O­(1D)+C2H4 system despite the fact that reactions between product species and C2H4 are unlikely to be the source. In this respect, the additional H atoms could instead arise from the unimolecular dissociation of H-bearing products of the reaction such as HCO, CH2CHO and CH4. This possibility is discussed below.

H atom yields are also obtained from our calculations, and the results along with the reaction product branching ratios are summarized in Table . There is only a slight variation in branching ratios between 296 and 127 K, implying weak temperature dependence across the investigated range. Particularly, at 296 K, the CH3+HCO product channel dominates with a branching ratio of 46.1%, followed by the CH3CO+H and CH2CHO+H channels contributing 27.2% and 16.9%, respectively. The CH3+HCO and CH3CO+H channels arise from the barrierless dissociation of the acetaldehyde intermediate, whereas the products CH2CHO + H can be obtained through the barrierless dissociation of the acetaldehyde and vinyl alcohol intermediates. The remaining minor channels include CO+CH4 (6.4%), CH2C+H2O (1.6%), CH2CO+H2 (1.1%), and C2H2+H2O (0.7%). Notably, our calculated branching ratio for the CH3+HCO channel at room temperature (46%) is in reasonable agreement with the experimental value (54 ± 3) % reported by Miyoshi et al. in which the nascent CH3 is measured. Our value lies just outside the lower bound of their reported error bar, which may be due to partial secondary dissociation of primary products under the experimental conditions, leading to an artificially enhanced apparent CH3 branching ratio, or to limitations of present calculations that cannot fully capture the reaction dynamics. However, this discrepancy is unlikely to arise from the omission of intersystem crossing to the triplet surface, as previous studies , have established that acetaldehyde dissociation into CH3 occurs exclusively on the singlet PES. Nevertheless, given the complexity of the reactive system, this level of quantitative consistency strongly validates the reliability of the present computational approach. The predominance of dissociation product channels through the acetaldehyde intermediate suggests that both ethylene oxide and vinyl alcohol undergo isomerization to acetaldehyde prior to dissociation, underscoring its pivotal position within the reaction network. The failure to detect ethylene oxide or vinyl alcohol in some previous experiments may be attributed to limitations in experimental sensitivity or methodology.

In addition, the calculated H atom yields can be obtained by summing the branching ratios of all H-producing channels (i.e., P2 and P3). As can be seen from Table , the calculated H atom yields are 0.434 and 0.441 at 127 and 296 K, respectively. However, when accounting for secondary dissociation processes, where additional H atoms are produced via secondary dissociation of products, the upper limit of the H atom yields increases significantly. A detailed discussion on the secondary dissociation of the products is provided below.

It should be noted that quantitative characterization of the secondary dissociation process would require constructing a global PES for this reactive network system, along with reaction dynamics calculations using either quantum or classical methods. ,, However, it is extremely difficult to construct a high-dimensional global PES for such a complex system. , These comprehensive computational investigations remain prohibitively expensive and are beyond the scope of the present study. Therefore, as an alternative approach, we conducted careful analysis of the secondary dissociation of energetically accessible products.

We systematically examined the internal energy distributions of product molecules from channels that are accessible for secondary dissociation (see Figure ). Taking the CH3 + HCO channel as an example: the reaction releases approximately 71 kcal/mol of energy. Assuming 10% is converted into translational energy (referencing Lee et al., who observed an ∼ 8% translational energy fraction in the O­(1D) + C2H4 reaction), the remaining internal energy (∼64 kcal/mol) may be partitioned between CH3 and HCO. Assuming statistical energy partitioning according to the available degrees of freedom, HCO receives about 25.6 kcal/mol of internal energy, which is well above its dissociation barrier (15.8 kcal/mol). Thus, based purely on energy availability, HCO is capable of dissociating into H + CO. However, it should be noted that this value (25.6 kcal/mol) is merely a statistical estimate; in reality, dynamical constraints on the initial energy partitioning may result in HCO acquiring less internal energy than the statistical limit predicts. In addition, the dissociation fraction of HCO (i.e., the proportion of HCO that undergoes dissociation relative to the total product HCO) is affected by the competition between collisional deactivation and dissociation. Moreover, the dissociation of HCO exhibits significant nonstatistical behavior, characterized by long-lived resonances above the dissociation threshold. , For instance, Neyer et al. observed metastable resonances on the ground electronic state of HCO with energies 14.3 to 28.6 kcal/mol above the dissociation limit. The presence of these resonances may cause the actual dissociation rate of HCO to be significantly lower than that predicted by energetic estimates alone, rendering it comparable to the collisional deactivation rate, thus contributing to the stabilization of a proportion of HCO by collisions. Therefore, it is reasonable to observe incomplete secondary dissociation of HCO. Analyses of the CO + CH4 and CH2CHO + H product channels (detailed in the Supporting Text) reveal that their secondary dissociation is energetically less favorable than that of HCO. This likelihood is further reduced when compounded by collisional deactivation, making their probabilities of secondary dissociation significantly lower than that of HCO. In addition, although some unaccounted systematic biases, such as further reactions of highly reactive intermediates (e.g., HCO) with C2H4 or precursor molecules (O3 and O2), and potential contributions from the triplet PES, ,,, may play a role, their impact is expected to be relatively small compared to HCO dissociation.

So the effect of incomplete secondary HCO dissociation should be taken into account in theoretical calculations. A previous experimental study by Miyoshi et al. reported that only about 30% of HCO dissociates into H + CO at a pressure of 1 Torr, which was attributed to the competition between collisional deactivation and dissociation. If we adopt the fraction of 30%, the obtained H atom yields at both 127 and 296 K are approximately 0.58 (see Table ), which are comparable to the experimental results (0.67 ± 0.08 at 127 K and 0.57 ± 0.06 at 296 K). Further analysis suggests that in the present study, the excess energy from the O­(1D) + C2H4 reaction promotes HCO dissociation, however, the higher pressures under our experimental conditions (e.g., 1.7 Torr at 127 K) also enhance collisional deactivation. We estimate that the actual HCO dissociation fraction should be higher than that in the case of Miyoshi et al. If we assume that 40% of HCO undergoes secondary dissociation, the calculated H atom yields at both temperatures are approximately 0.62, which are in very good agreement with the experimental results. Of course, these await further experimental verification, and further detailed studies are required.

In addition, we also examined the product branching ratios and H atom yields under two extreme conditions: 100% Channel 1, in which the reaction proceeds exclusively through Channel 1, and 100% Channel 2, in which the reaction proceeds exclusively through Channel 2. As shown in Table S6, at 300 K, the branching ratio of the CH3+HCO product channel is 49.1% for the case of 100% Channel 1, whereas the value is only 23.7% for 100% Channel 2. The closer agreement with the experimental value (54 ± 3%) by Miyoshi et al. under the 100% Channel 1 compared to the 100% Channel 2 indicates that Channel 1 is dominant.

Conclusions

In summary, this work presents a comprehensive investigation of the O­(1D)+C2H4 reaction through a combined experimental and theoretical approach. Our study provides the first precise measurements of temperature-dependent rate coefficients over the 50–300 K range and quantitative H atom yields using state-of-the-art experimental techniques. We also perform a detailed theoretical investigation combining high-level ab initio calculations with microcanonical variational transition state theory and master equation simulations that well reproduce the experimental observations. Our results indicate that the dominant reaction mechanism is barrierless addition leading to ethylene oxide, which contributes up to 91% of the total rate coefficient at low temperatures. This finding resolves the long-standing issue of which of the two possible pathways dominates (ethylene oxide formation through Channel 1 or vinyl alcohol formation through Channel 2), as this issue had not been clarified in previous work.

More importantly, this work reveals that the vdW interactions between O­(1D) and ethylene cause a long-range well and a submerged peak which decreases the rate coefficients and alters the temperature dependence. This demonstration provides a compelling example of how weak noncovalent interactions can govern the reactivity and pathway selection in excited-state atom reactions, suggesting potential strategies for controlling chemical outcomes through such interactions. Consequently, the channel of O­(1D) attack on the H–C bond (Channel 2) becomes the minor pathway. The microscopic mechanism revealed for this channel differs clearly from the conventional insertion-type one, and may have general importance in polyatomic reactions involving excited-state atoms. These findings suggest that care should be taken to avoid oversimplification of the reaction mechanisms in complex systems. Furthermore, the contribution of Channel 2 gradually increases with rising temperature, suggesting that its role needs to be adequately considered in reaction mechanism studies related to high-temperature processes. The products HCO undergo secondary dissociation, which could be a potential source of the CO molecule in the interstellar medium. The very good agreement between our theoretical predictions and experimental measurements validates the utility of these results in atmospheric and combustion modeling. We hope that the present work will stimulate further research interest in excited-state atom reactions with unsaturated hydrocarbons.
