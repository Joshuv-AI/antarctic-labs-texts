Fourier optimization and pair correlation problems
Mithun Kumar Das, Tolibjon Ismoilov, Antonio Pedro Ramos

Abstract
. We introduce a generic framework to provide bounds related to the pair correlation of sequences belonging to a wide class. We consider analogues of Montgomery’s form factor for zeros of the Riemann zeta function in the case of arbitrary sequences satisfying some basic assumptions, and connect their estimation to two extremal problems in Fourier analysis, which are promptly studied. As applications, we provide average bounds of form factors related to some sequences of number theoretic interest, such as the zeros of primitive elements of the Selberg class, Dedekind zeta functions, and the real and imaginary parts of the Riemann zeta function. In the last case, our results bear an implication to a conjecture of Gonek and Ki (2018), showing it cannot hold in some situations.

41A30, 46E22, 11M06, 11M26

1.
Introduction

The celebrated work of Montgomery
[
18
]
initiated the study of how non-trivial zeros
\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma
of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
are distributed along the critical line by studying their pair correlation. His approach consisted of introducing the function
F(\alpha)
, a Fourier transform of the distribution of the gaps between zeros
\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}
. In
[
18
]
, Montgomery proposed a conjecture on the limiting distribution of the pair correlation of zeros of zeta, which came to be known as the pair correlation conjecture (PCC).
Goldston
[
12
]
subsequently proved an equivalence between PCC and certain asymptotics for average values of Montgomery’s function
F(\alpha)
, motivating the study of average estimates of
F(\alpha)
, which was done, for example, in
[
3
,
5
,
11
,
13
]
.

The approach of
[
18
]
was extended to many other settings. There are several instances in the literature where analogues of Montgomery’s function were used to study the pair correlation of zeros of
L
-functions, Dirichlet series, and similar objects. One can see
[
20
,
9
,
2
,
17
,
15
,
6
]
for a few examples. Naturally, there are corresponding conjectures for their asymptotic behavior. In this work, we will look at the problem of average estimates from a much broader perspective, introducing an axiomatic approach which allows us to bound the long-term averages of analogues of Montgomery’s function in a wide variety of settings. We do this by connecting these average bounds to two Fourier optimization problems, which constitute another major object of study of this paper. The investigation of these problems uses techniques from Fourier analysis and the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

As a consequence of the bounds we obtain, we address a conjecture of Gonek and Ki
[
15
]
related to the pair correlation of zeros of
\operatorname{Re}\zeta
,
\operatorname{Im}\zeta
, and other similar functions along vertical lines in the left-half of the critical strip. We show that the relevant form factor cannot have the conjectured expression in certain regimes.

1.0.1.
Notation

Throughout this text, we use the following definition of the Fourier transform for a given function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
:

\mathscr{F}[f](\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi i\xi x}f(x)\,\text{\rm d}x.

We also adopt this normalization for the Fourier transform of a finite Borel measure
\mu
, setting

\widehat{\mu}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi i\xi x}\,\text{\rm d}\mu(x).

For a real number
x
, we denote
x_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. Finally, the quantity

s_{0}:=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\sin{x}}{x}=-0.217233\ldots

will be useful to state many of our results.

1.1.
Montgomery’s pair correlation conjecture

Assuming the Riemann hypothesis (RH), let
\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma
denote a non-trivial zero of
\zeta(s)
.
Montgomery conjectured that, assuming RH, the pair correlation of zeros of zeta has the following limiting distribution: for any fixed
\beta>0
,

(1.1)

\bigg(\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\,{T}\bigg)^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T\\
0<\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\leq\frac{2\pi\beta}{\log\,{T}}\end{subarray}}1\sim\int_{0}^{\beta}\bigg(1-\bigg(\frac{\sin\,{\pi u}}{\pi u}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)\text{\rm d}u,\quad\mbox{as }T\rightarrow\infty.

This is now known as Montgomery’s pair correlation conjecture (PCC). In order to study the quantity on the left-hand side of (
1.1
), Montgomery
[
18
]
introduced the form factor

(1.2)

F(\alpha)=F(\alpha,T):=\bigg(\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\,{T}\bigg)^{-1}\sum_{0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T}T^{i\alpha(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})}w(\gamma\!-\!\gamma^{\prime}),

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
T\geq 2
,
w(u)=\frac{4}{4+u^{2}}
, and the sums are taken over ordinates of non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
, counting multiplicity.

For any function
r\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\widehat{r}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, the Fourier inversion formula gives that

\bigg(\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\,{T}\bigg)^{-1}\sum_{0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T}r\bigg((\gamma\!-\!\gamma^{\prime})\frac{\log\,{T}}{2\pi}\bigg)w(\gamma\!-\!\gamma^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{r}(\alpha)F(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.

One can see the connection between (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) from this identity.
Indeed, considering an appropriate sequence of functions
r
that approximate the characteristic function of the interval
(0,\beta]
in the above identity, we can obtain the asymptotic of the left-hand side of (
1.1
). Therefore, if we know the asymptotic behavior of the function
F(\alpha,T)
as
T\to\infty
, we know the asymptotic behavior of the pair correlation of zeros.

Montgomery
[
18
]
, then Goldston and Montgomery
[
14
, Lemma 8]
, obtained partial progress on understanding
F(\alpha,T)
conditionally on RH. They proved that

(1.3)

F(\alpha,T)=\left(T^{-2|\alpha|}\log{T}+|\alpha|\right)\left(1+O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\log\,\log{T}}{\log{T}}}\bigg)\right),\quad\text{ as }\;\;T\to\infty,

uniformly for
0\leq|\alpha|\leq 1
, improving the original work of Montgomery. Not only this, in
[
18
]
Montgomery also conjectured that for
|\alpha|\geq 1
and uniformly for any bounded interval,

(1.4)

F(\alpha,T)\sim 1,\quad\mbox{ as }T\rightarrow\infty,

which, together with (
1.3
), provides a complete description of the asymptotic behavior of
F(\alpha,T)
. This statement is what became known as the strong pair correlation conjecture, which, by the reasoning above, implies (
1.1
).

In 1988, Goldston
[
12
]
proved the equivalence of PCC and the following asymptotic for the averages of
F(\alpha,T)
:

(1.5)

\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{b}^{b+\ell}F(\alpha,T)\text{\rm d}\alpha\sim 1,\mbox{ as }T\rightarrow\infty,

for any
b\geq 1
and
\ell>0
.
Recently, Carneiro, Milinovich, and Ramos
[
5
]
proved, assuming RH, that

\displaystyle 0.9303+o(1)<\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{b}^{b+\ell}F(\alpha,T)\text{\rm d}\alpha<1.3208+o(1),\quad\mbox{ as }T\rightarrow\infty,

uniformly on
b\geq 1
for the upper bound and for
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(b)
for the lower bound, improving the previous results in
[
3
]
. They obtained this result by introducing new averaging mechanisms and making full use of the class of test functions considered in
[
7
]
for the sphere packing problem.

We now proceed to the axiomatic formulation which allows the generalization of the work of
[
5
]
to address multiple different objects.

1.2.
Axiomatic formulation

Our main purpose is to obtain a unified approach for various pair correlation functions, capturing the behavior of a wide variety of examples.
Our objects of interest will be sequences
\Gamma(T)=(\gamma_{n}(T))
of real numbers depending on a parameter
T\geq T_{0}
. Note that the elements of the sequence are not necessarily distinct.
For most of the number-theoretic instances we have in mind, such as when
\Gamma
consists of the ordinates of non-trivial zeros of a typical
L
-function, the sequence is independent of
T
. We introduce the dependence on the parameter because we are also interested in cases such as the ones Gonek and Ki studied in
[
15
]
, where, among other things, they considered the pair correlation of zeros of the real part of
\zeta(s)
along the lines
\operatorname{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{c}{\log T}
for a fixed
c>0
, which naturally vary with
T
. We discuss this and a few other examples in the dedicated Section
1.6
below.

Now, sequences of non-trivial zeros of a typical
L
-function share the behavior that the number of elements with height at most
T>0
is asymptotically
\frac{\lambda T}{2\pi}\log T
and the average spacing of elements up to that height is
\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\log T
for a given
\lambda>0
. Motivated by this, we are led to our first axiom.

Assumption 1
(A1). There is a
\lambda>0
such that

(1.6)

\liminf_{T\to\infty}\,\frac{\#\{n:\gamma_{n}(T)\in(0,T]\}}{\frac{\lambda T}{2\pi}\log T}=1.

In view of this assumption, we associate a form factor to
\Gamma
in analogy to Montgomery’s function (
1.2
):

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T):=\bigg(\frac{\lambda T}{2\pi}\log\,{T}\bigg)^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in(0,T]\cap\Gamma(T)}\end{subarray}}T^{i\lambda\alpha(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})}w(\gamma\!-\!\gamma^{\prime}),

where, as before,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
T\geq T_{0}
,
w(u)=\frac{4}{4+u^{2}}
, and elements in the sum are counted with multiplicity. The choice that the
\gamma
’s lie in the interval
(0,T]
in (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) is made for simplicity and is not particularly important. The interval
(0,T]

can be replaced by, say,
(T,2T]
or
[-T/2,T/2]
with no change in the arguments presented here.
From the definition it also follows that
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha)
is real-valued and even, while the identity

\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in(0,T]\cap\Gamma(T)}T^{i\lambda\alpha(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma)}\,w(\gamma^{\prime}\!-\!\gamma)=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi|u|}\Bigg|\sum_{\gamma\in(0,T]\cap\Gamma(T)}T^{i\lambda\alpha\gamma}e^{2\pi i\gamma u}\Bigg|^{2}\text{\rm d}u\,,

which holds for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, implies that it is non-negative.

Below we will give effective bounds for the long-term averages

\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{b}^{b+\ell}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha,\,\mbox{ as }T\rightarrow\infty,

in this very general situation, assuming some information is known about the asymptotic behavior of
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
as
T\to\infty
. This information will come in the form of a convergence of measures. To properly state this, we introduce a definition. We say a Borel measure
\nu
is a
limiting measure
for
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
if, for every function
\varphi\in C_{c}((-\Delta,\Delta))
,

(1.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\alpha)\,\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha).

In the language of measure theory, this means that, as
T\to\infty
, the absolutely continuous measures with Radon–Nikodym derivative
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
converge in the weak
∗
sense to
\nu
as measures on
[-\Delta,\Delta]
. Such a convergence assumption will constitute the basis of our second axiom on
\Gamma
.

Assumption 2
(A2). There is a
\Delta>0
and a finite Borel measure
\nu
such that
\nu
is a limiting measure for
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
.

Remark
.

There may be multiple Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}
for which (
1.8
) holds for all
\varphi\in C_{c}((-\Delta,\Delta))
. What is relevant here is that, if they exist, they must agree when restricted to the interval
[-\Delta,\Delta]
.

As an example, when
\Gamma
is the set of ordinates of non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
, we have that
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
is Montgomery’s function
F(\alpha,T)
and equation (
1.3
) implies that, under RH, the measure
\nu
given by

(1.9)

\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha)=\boldsymbol{\delta}(\alpha)+|\alpha|\,\text{\rm d}\alpha

is the limiting measure of
F(\alpha,T)
in
[-1,1]
, where
\boldsymbol{\delta}
is the Dirac measure concentrated at the origin. Other examples of form factors and their associated limiting measures can be found in Section
1.6
.

1.3.
Bounds via Fourier optimization

We obtain lower and upper bounds for the long-term averages of form factors through a Fourier optimization approach. There are two relevant extremal problems which we study, and these will be the key to obtain the desired bounds.

The first problem we consider makes use of the class of functions introduced by Cohn and Elkies in
[
7
]
to tackle sphere packing problems. For a
\Delta>0
, this is the class
\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}
of continuous and even functions
g
such that

(1)

g
and
\widehat{g}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
;

(2)

\widehat{g}(\alpha)\leq 0
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\Delta,\Delta]
;

(3)

g(\alpha)\geq 0
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now let
\nu
be a finite Borel measure supported in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
and define the functional

(1.10)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\nu}(g):=\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\widehat{g}(\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha).

Our first problem is the following.

Extremal Problem 1
(EP1): Given
\Delta>0
and a finite Borel measure
\nu
on
[-\Delta,\Delta]
. Find the quantity

(1.11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\nu}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{g\in\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}}\\
{g(0)>0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\Phi_{\nu}(g)}{g(0)}.

If a sequence
\Gamma
satisfies assumptions (A1) and (A2), we will show that we can provide upper and lower bounds for averages of
\mathfrak{F}(\alpha,T)
in terms of the optimization constant
\mathbf{C}_{\nu}
by applying the averaging mechanism of Carneiro, Milinovich, and Ramos
[
5
]
in our generic setting, leading to our first main result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a sequence of real numbers satisfying (A1) and (A2).
Let
\nu
be the limiting measure for
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
in the interval
[-\Delta,\Delta]
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, for any
b\geq 1
and sufficiently large
\ell
, one has

\displaystyle 1+s_{0}(\mathbf{C}_{\nu}-1)-\varepsilon+o(1)<\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{b}^{b+\ell}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha<\mathbf{C}_{\nu}+\varepsilon+o(1),

as
T\to\infty
, with
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(\varepsilon)
for the upper bound and
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(b,\varepsilon)
for the lower bound.

Remark
.

By the inequalities above, we have a necessary condition for the limiting measure
\nu
of
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
, since the solution of the problem (EP1) must then satisfy
\mathbf{C}_{\nu}\geq 1
.

In the next subsections, we discuss how to estimate the optimization constant
\mathbf{C}_{\nu}
in order to obtain effective bounds out of Theorem
1
.

We can strengthen the lower bound with the aid of another extremal problem. Given
\beta>0
, we introduce the class
\mathcal{R}_{\beta}
, consisting of continuous functions
g
such that

(1)

g
and
\widehat{g}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
;

(2)

\widehat{g}(\alpha)\leq\chi_{[-\beta,\beta]}(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
;

(3)

g(\alpha)\geq 0
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

This definition allows us to introduce the second problem.

Extremal Problem 2
(EP2): Given
\beta>0
, determine the value of

\mathbf{D}_{\beta}:=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{R}_{\beta}}g(0)=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{R}_{\beta}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{g}(\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.

We solve this problem completely in Section
2.2
, showing that
\mathbf{D}_{\beta}=\beta
(see Proposition
12
). Assuming (A1) and (A2), we obtain a lower bound for (symmetric) averages of
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}
in terms of the constant
\mathbf{D}_{\beta}
. Notably, due to the value
\mathbf{D}_{\beta}
, this yields a lower bound of
\frac{1}{2}
for averages of
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}
, which is independent of the size of the interval
[-\Delta,\Delta]
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a sequence of real numbers satisfying (A1) and (A2). Given
\varepsilon>0
, for any
b\geq 1
and
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(b,\varepsilon)
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{b}^{b+\ell}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha>\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon+o(1),

as
T\to\infty
.

Remark
.

Our proof of the above theorem shows that one does not need to assume (A2) to obtain an analogous result for averages over symmetric intervals (see Lemma
11
). Not only that, one can also see that Theorem
2
is valid under the weaker assumption that there is a
\Delta>0
for which
\limsup\limits_{T\to\infty}\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha
is finite.

1.4.
Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

For a given measure
\nu
, finding the exact value of the constant
\mathbf{C}_{\nu}
is not an easy task. We will use reproducing kernel Hilbert space theory to obtain a bound for
\mathbf{C}_{\nu}
by working over a subclass of
\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}
.
We introduce the relevant background briefly.

A Hilbert space of entire functions
\mathcal{H}
for which the functionals

f\mapsto f(w)\in\mathbb{C}

are continuous for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
is said to be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space because, as a consequence of the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a function
K:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
K(w,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
and

f(w)=\langle f,K(w,\cdot)\rangle,

for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
and
w\in\mathbb{C}
. This
K
is called the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
.

A classical example of such a space is the Paley–Wiener space
PW(\pi\Delta)
for a
\Delta>0
. We recall that a function
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be of exponential type at most
\pi\Delta
if for a given
\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
such that

|f(z)|\leq C_{\varepsilon}e^{(\pi\Delta+\varepsilon)|z|}

holds for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. The space
PW(\pi\Delta)
consists of all entire functions
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
of exponential type at most
\pi\Delta
such that
f|_{\mathbb{R}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. This forms a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with the usual
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-inner product. We note that the Paley–Wiener theorem establishes that the set of functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
which can be extended to a function in
PW(\pi\Delta)
coincides with the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-functions whose Fourier transforms are supported in
[-\frac{\Delta}{2},\frac{\Delta}{2}]
.

Here we will be interested in weighted versions of
PW(\pi\Delta)
. Given a finite Borel measure
\nu
supported in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
, we consider the set
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}
consisting of entire functions of exponential type at most
\pi\Delta
satisfying

\|f\|_{\nu}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}\,\widehat{\nu}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x<\infty.

It is not clear a priori that the functional above is a norm and that
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}
is even a vector space. One must have specific information about the measure
\nu
to be able to say anything more concrete. Therefore, we restrict our attention to a class of measures which addresses many interesting cases relevant from a number theory standpoint, namely, the measures
\nu
supported in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
given by

(1.12)

\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha)=c_{1}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\alpha)+c_{2}|\alpha|e^{-c_{3}|\alpha|}\text{\rm d}\alpha,

for
\alpha\in[-\Delta,\Delta]
, where
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2},c_{3}\geq 0
are constants.
In these cases, we determine that the sets
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}
satisfy the following.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\nu
be a measure in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
given by
\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha)=c_{1}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\alpha)+c_{2}|\alpha|e^{-c_{3}|\alpha|}\text{\rm d}\alpha
. For
\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\Delta^{2}\leq 5/3
and
c_{3}\geq 0
, we have that
\|\cdot\|_{\nu}
is a norm which makes the set
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}
a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Moreover, we have that
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}=PW(\pi\Delta)
as sets, and we have the equivalence of norms

(1.13)

a\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|f\|_{\nu}\leq b\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

for constants
a,b>0
depending only on the parameters
c_{1},c_{2}
, and
\Delta
.

To establish this theorem, we find bounds from above and below to the function
\widehat{\nu}(x)
, which introduces the need for the constraint that
\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\Delta^{2}\leq 5/3
. This is a sufficient condition on the parameters to obtain this result, but it is (almost) the best that can be done using this method. We can slightly extend the range by increasing the technicality of the proof, but opt for the value
5/3
for simplicity and clarity of exposition.

By working over the subclass of
\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}
consisting of functions
f
with
{\rm supp}\widehat{f}\subseteq[-\Delta,\Delta]
, we can take advantage of the reproducing kernel structure of
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}
to obtain an upper bound for
\mathbf{C}_{\nu}
. This restricted version of (EP1) was first solved by Montgomery and Taylor
[
19
]
in the case of the measure given by (
1.9
), i.e., the limiting measure associated with zeros of the Riemann zeta function. They used a variational method, while Carnerio, Chandee, Milinovich, and Littmann
[
4
]
used reproducing kernel Hilbert space theory to arrive at the same result. We adapt the proof of
[
4
, Corollary 14]
to our context, establishing the lemma below.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\nu
be a finite Borel measure supported in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
such that the inequalities (
1.13
) hold. Then
(\mathcal{H}_{\nu},\|\cdot\|_{\nu})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space such that
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}=PW(\pi\Delta)
as sets and

\mathbf{C}_{\nu}\leq\frac{1}{K_{\nu}(0,0)},

where
K_{\nu}
is its reproducing kernel.

This is the link between explicit bounds and reproducing kernels which underpins our strategy. We include a short proof of this lemma in Section
3
.

1.5.
Explicit bounds for averages

As we have just seen, one can produce an effective bound for
\mathbf{C}_{\nu}
in the case that
(\mathcal{H}_{\nu},\|\cdot\|_{\nu})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space if one knows the value of the reproducing kernel
K_{\nu}(w,z)
at
(w,z)=(0,0)
. Accordingly, the next results concern the value of
K_{\nu}(0,0)
, separated in two cases because of the significant increase in computational difficulty when
c_{3}\neq 0
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\Delta>0,\,c_{1}>0,\,c_{2}\geq 0
be such that
\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\Delta^{2}\leq 5/3
. For the measure
\nu
supported in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
given by
\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha)=c_{1}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\alpha)+c_{2}|\alpha|\,\text{\rm d}\alpha
, we have

K_{\nu}(0,0)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{c_{1}c_{2}}}\frac{\sin{\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_{2}}{2c_{1}}}\Delta\right)}}{\cos{\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_{2}}{2c_{1}}}\Delta\right)}+\sqrt{\frac{c_{2}}{2c_{1}}}\Delta\sin{\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_{2}}{2c_{1}}}\Delta\right)}}.

Now, to state the result when
c_{3}\neq 0
, we define the auxiliary functions of
\eta\in\mathbb{C}

(1.14)

\displaystyle A(\eta)

\displaystyle:=1+\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2}\cosh{(\eta\alpha)}\lvert\alpha\rvert e^{-c_{3}\lvert\alpha\rvert}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha;

(1.15)

\displaystyle B(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\eta^{2}+2\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}-c_{3}^{2}+2\frac{c_{2}c_{3}}{c_{1}}\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2}\cosh{(\eta\alpha)}e^{-c_{3}\lvert\alpha\rvert}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\Delta,c_{1},c_{2},\text{ and }c_{3}
be positive numbers such that
\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\Delta^{2}\leq 5/3
. For the measure
\nu
supported in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
given by
\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha)=c_{1}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\alpha)+c_{2}\lvert\alpha\rvert e^{-c_{3}\lvert\alpha\rvert}\text{\rm d}\alpha
, we have

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(0,0)=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{A(\eta_{1})B(\eta_{2})-B(\eta_{1})A(\eta_{2})}\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}-\frac{A(0)c_{3}^{2}}{2c_{2}+c_{3}^{2}c_{1}}\right)\left(\frac{\sinh{\frac{\Delta\eta_{1}}{2}}}{\eta_{1}}B(\eta_{2})-\frac{\sinh{\frac{\Delta\eta_{2}}{2}}}{\eta_{2}}B(\eta_{1})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta c_{3}^{2}}{2c_{2}+c_{3}^{2}c_{1}},

Remark

1.6.
Applications

Letting
\Gamma(T)
be a sequence coming from zeros of a Dirichlet series or similar object, possibly varying with
T
, we can obtain upper and lower bounds for averages of
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
as a consequence of our results. We treat three main classes of examples:

(1)

Primitive elements of the Selberg class;

(2)

Dedekind zeta functions of number fields;

(3)

Real and imaginary parts of the Riemann zeta function (and suitable generalizations of these).

1.6.1.
Primitive
L
-functions in
\mathcal{S}
of degree
m

The Selberg class
\mathcal{S}
, introduced by Selberg in 1989
[
21
]
, is the set of meromorphic functions
L(s)
satisfying five fundamental axioms: Dirichlet series representation, analytic continuation, functional equation, Ramanujan hypothesis, and Euler product. Associated with each
L
-function in
\mathcal{S}
is its degree
d_{L}
, which is determined by the parameters in its functional equation. Although not assumed in the axioms, it is conjectured that the degree of any element in the Selberg class is a non-negative integer, which is supported by all known examples. Some examples of elements in
\mathcal{S}
include:

•

The Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
(degree 1);

•

Dirichlet
L
-functions
L(s,\chi)
and their imaginary shifts
L(s+i\theta,\chi)
, where
\chi
is a primitive character (degree 1);

•

Dedekind zeta functions
\zeta_{K}(s)
of number fields
K
(degree
[K:\mathbb{Q}]
);

•

L
-functions associated to irreducible cuspidal automorphic representations of
GL_{m}
over
\mathbb{Q}
(degree
m
).

Murty and Perelli
[
20
]
studied the pair correlation of zeros of primitive functions in
\mathcal{S}
, which we recall are elements of this class that cannot be further factored into non-trivial elements of
\mathcal{S}
. Here, we will be considering sequences
\Gamma
consisting of ordinates of non-trivial zeros of primitive elements
L
of
\mathcal{S}
, i.e.,
\Gamma=\{\gamma:\rho=\beta+i\gamma,\,L(\rho)=0,\,0\leq\beta\leq 1\}
. An application of the argument principle shows that for such a typical element
L\in\mathcal{S}
, the zero counting function is given by

N_{L}(T)=\#\{\rho=\beta+i\gamma:L(\rho)=0,0\leq\beta\leq 1,-T/2\leq\gamma\leq T/2\}=\frac{d_{L}T}{2\pi}\log T+O(T),

proving
\Gamma
satisfies axiom (A1) with the interval
(0,T]
replaced by
[-T/2,T/2]
in (
1.6
), and with
\lambda=d_{L}
. Accordingly, we define the form factor
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
with a slight change in the definition in (
1.7
), replacing the intervals.
As we have remarked previously, this change does not create any issues and all of our results still apply. We make this choice for easy comparison with the set-up of
[
20
]
, where the authors studied the asymptotic behavior of form factors defined similarly to above under an assumption on the Dirichlet series coefficients of
\log L(s).

Recall that the Euler product axiom in the definition of the Selberg class states that, for any
L\in\mathcal{S}
,

\displaystyle\log{L(s)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{L}(n)}{n^{s}},

where
b_{L}(n)=0
unless
n=p^{k}
,
k\geq 1
and
b_{L}(n)\leq n^{\theta}
for some
\theta<1/2
.
Murty and Perelli proposed the following hypothesis about the coefficients
b_{L}(n)
.

Hypothesis MP.
Let
v_{L}(n):=b_{L}(n)\log{n}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}v_{L}(n)\overline{v_{L}(n)}=(1+o(1))x\log{x},\mbox{ as }x\rightarrow\infty.

They showed that this hypothesis together with GRH implies

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\sim d_{L}T^{-2\lvert\alpha\rvert d_{L}}\log{T}+\lvert\alpha\rvert,

\displaystyle\mbox{ as }T\rightarrow\infty,

uniformly for
0\leq\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq\frac{1}{d_{L}}(1-\delta)
, for any
\delta>0
. Therefore,
\Gamma
satisfies (A2) with the limiting measure
\nu
of
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)
in the interval
\left[-\frac{1}{d_{L}},\frac{1}{d_{L}}\right]
given by

\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha)=\boldsymbol{\delta}(\alpha)+\lvert\alpha\rvert\text{\rm d}\alpha.

As consequence of the above, by Corollary
7
we obtain the following.

Corollary 8
.

Assume GRH and Hypothesis MP, if
\Gamma
is the sequence of ordinates of the non-trivial zeros of a primitive element of
\mathcal{S}
of degree
m
, we have that, for all
\varepsilon>0
and
b\geq 1
,

\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+s_{0}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cot{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}m}}-\frac{2m-1}{2m}\bigg)\!\!\bigg)_{\!\!+}+\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon+o(1)<\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{b}^{b+\ell}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\text{\rm d}\alpha<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cot{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}m}}+\frac{1}{2m}+\varepsilon+o(1),

as
T\to\infty
, with
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(\varepsilon)
for the upper bound and
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(b,\varepsilon)
for the lower bound.

1.6.2.
Dedekind zeta functions of number fields

The Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{K}(s)
over a number field
K/\mathbb{Q}
of degree
n
with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is defined as

\displaystyle\zeta_{K}(s)=\sum_{I\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}}\frac{1}{N(I)^{s}},

where the sum is over the ideals
I
in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
N(I)
is the norm of
I
. It is known that for an abelian extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
,

\displaystyle\zeta_{K}(s)=\zeta(s)\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}L(s,\chi_{j}),

for some Dirichlet characters
\chi_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots n
, is a non-primitive
L
-function of degree
n
. In a recent preprint
[
8
]
, de Laat, Rolen, Tripp, and Wagner studied the pair correlation of zeros of
\zeta_{K}(s)
under the Grand Riemann Hypothesis (GRH). If
\Gamma
consists of the ordinates of the zeros of
\zeta_{K}
in the critical strip, standard results confirm it satisfies assumption (A1). In
[
8
]
, the authors studied the form factor
\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}
(defined as in (
1.7
)) and established that

\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\sim nT^{-2n|\alpha|}\log{T}+n|\alpha|,

as
T\to\infty
, uniformly for
\lvert\alpha\rvert\leq\frac{1}{n}-\delta
, with
\delta>0
.
This gives that axiom (A2) also holds for
\Gamma
, with limiting measure
\nu
in the interval
[-\frac{1}{n},\frac{1}{n}]
given by

\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha)=\boldsymbol{\delta}(\alpha)+n|\alpha|\,\text{\rm d}\alpha,

whence we get the following corollary of Theorem
7
.

Corollary 9
.

Assuming GRH, if
\Gamma
is the sequence of zeros of the Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{K}(s)
of a degree
n
abelian extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
, we have that, for all
\varepsilon>0
and
b\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+s_{0}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\cot{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\!\!\bigg)_{\!\!\!+}+\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon+o(1)<\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{b}^{b+\ell}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\text{\rm d}\alpha<\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\cot{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}}+\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon+o(1),

as
T\to\infty
, with
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(\varepsilon)
for the upper bound and
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(b,\varepsilon)
for the lower bound.

1.6.3.
The real and imaginary parts of the Riemann zeta function

Another application of our results is about the behavior of the form factor for the zeros of the real and imaginary parts of the Riemann zeta function and similar functions.
For

2.
Fourier Optimization Bounds

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1

Fix
g\in\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}
with
g(0)>0
and let
\phi
be a Schwartz function satisfying
\phi,\widehat{\phi}\geq 0
and
\|\phi\|_{1}=1
with
{\rm supp}{(\widehat{\phi})}\subset[-1,1]
. Define
\phi^{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\phi(x/\varepsilon)
and set
g_{\varepsilon}:=g*\phi^{\varepsilon}
. We find that
g_{\varepsilon}
occurs in
\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}^{BL}
, the collection of functions
f\in\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}
such that
\operatorname{supp}{(\widehat{f})}
is compact, and
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\Phi_{\nu}(g_{\varepsilon})}{g_{\varepsilon}(0)}=\frac{\Phi_{\nu}(g)}{g(0)}
. Thus, given a
\delta>0
, there is a
g\in\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}
and a suitably small
\varepsilon
for which
\frac{\Phi_{\nu}(g_{\varepsilon})}{g_{\varepsilon}(0)}\leq\mathbf{C}_{\nu}+\delta
.

Observing this fact, for a given
\varepsilon>0
there is a function
h\in\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}^{BL}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}
, that satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\Phi_{\nu}(h)\leq\mathbf{C}_{\nu}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Without loss of generality, we normalize
h
to have
h(0)=1
. Now, pick
g\in\mathcal{A}_{(1-\delta)\Delta}^{BL}
defined on the Fourier transform side by
\widehat{g}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{1-\delta}\widehat{h}\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\alpha\right)
for a
0<\delta<\frac{1}{2}
small enough such that

\int_{-(1-\delta)\Delta}^{(1-\delta)\Delta}\widehat{g}(\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha)\leq\Phi_{\nu}(h)+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

and
\operatorname{supp}{(\widehat{g})}\subset[-M,M]
, with
M
a parameter uniform in
\delta
.

The core of the proof is an inequality regarding a convenient approximation of the characteristic functions of intervals (established by Carneiro, Milinovich, and Ramos in
[
5
, eq: (2.6)]
and reproduced here as (
2.1
)). For
L>M
, define the function

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{L}(x)=(\widehat{g}*\chi_{[-L,L]})(x)=\int_{x-L}^{x+L}\widehat{g}(t)\text{\rm d}t.

We first address the upper bound. Setting
L=M+\frac{\ell}{2}
, it follows that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{L}\bigg(x-b-\frac{\ell}{2}\bigg)\geq\chi_{[b,b+\ell]}(x)-\|\widehat{h}\|_{1}\cdot\chi_{b+\frac{\ell}{2}+I_{L}}(x),

where
I_{L}:=[-L-M,-L+M]\bigcup[L-M,L+M]
. The above inequality implies that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{b}^{b+\ell}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{L}\bigg(\alpha-b-\frac{\ell}{2}\bigg)\,\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha+\|\widehat{h}\|_{1}\int_{b+\frac{\ell}{2}+I_{L}}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.

By the definiton of
\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}
and assumption (A2), we also get

(2.3)

\begin{split}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{g}(\alpha)\,\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha&\leq\int_{-(1-\delta)\Delta}^{(1-\delta)\Delta}\widehat{g}(\alpha)\,\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\\
&=\int_{-(1-\delta)\Delta}^{(1-\delta)\Delta}\widehat{g}(\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha)+o(1)\leq\Phi_{\nu}(h)+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+o(1),\end{split}

as
T\to\infty
. So that, by Fourier inversion,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{L}\bigg(\alpha-b-\frac{l}{2}\bigg)\,\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\lambda T}{2\pi}\log\,{T}\bigg)^{-1}\operatorname{Re}\sum_{0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T}T^{i\lambda(b+\frac{l}{2})(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})}(\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\chi_{[-L,L]}]\cdot g)\bigg((\gamma\!-\!\gamma^{\prime})\frac{\lambda\log\,{T}}{2\pi}\bigg)w(\gamma\!-\!\gamma^{\prime})

(2.4)

\displaystyle\leq 2L\bigg(\frac{\lambda T}{2\pi}\log\,{T}\bigg)^{-1}\sum_{0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T}g\bigg((\gamma\!-\!\gamma^{\prime})\frac{\lambda\log\,{T}}{2\pi}\bigg)w(\gamma\!-\!\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=2L\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{g}(\alpha)\,\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha

\displaystyle\leq 2L\left\{\Phi_{\nu}(h)+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+o(1)\right\},

which is almost enough to establish the upper bound. It remains to show that the second integral in the right-hand side of (
2.2
) is bounded. But this follows since
|I_{L}|=4M
, which is a finite length, and hence,
\int_{b+\frac{\ell}{2}+I_{L}}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\ll 4M
, by the argument of Goldston
[
12
, eq: (2.6)]
. Thus, for
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(\varepsilon)
, we get

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{b}^{b+\ell}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\leq\frac{2L}{\ell}\left\{\Phi_{\nu}(h)+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+o(1)\right\}\leq\mathbf{C}_{\nu}+\varepsilon+o(1).

Lemma 11
.

Let
\Gamma
be a sequence of real numbers satisfying (A1). The following bound holds as

\displaystyle\int_{-\beta}^{\beta}\mathfrak{F}_{\Gamma}(\alpha,T)\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\geq\mathbf{D}_{\beta}+o(1).

We know how to solve problem (EP2) exactly. This is the next step towards the lower bound independent of

Proposition 12
.

For all
\beta>0
, we have the equality
\mathbf{D}_{\beta}=\beta
.

Proof.

With no loss of generality, we can assume
\beta=1
. This is because for any

, one can rescale

to get an

. This goes in both directions, plainly yielding
\mathbf{D}_{\beta}=\beta\,\mathbf{D}_{1}
.

If we pick
g_{0}(x)=\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\right)^{2}
, we have
\widehat{g_{0}}(\alpha)=\left(1-|\alpha|\right)_{+}
, it is clear that
g_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
, so that
\mathbf{D}_{1}\geq 1
. Now we prove that we can do no better than that. Let

and
\phi
be a non-negative smooth function of compact support such that
\widehat{\phi}\geq 0
and

. If
\phi^{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\phi(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})

\widehat{g_{\varepsilon}}(\alpha)\leq\chi_{[-1,1]}(\alpha)
.
Note that

has bounded variation, which can be seen through another application of Young’s convolution inequality. We can therefore apply the Poisson summation formula to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{\varepsilon}(n)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g_{\varepsilon}}(k),

but since
g_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
\widehat{g_{\varepsilon}}\leq 0

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(0)\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{\varepsilon}(n)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g_{\varepsilon}}(k)\leq\widehat{g_{\varepsilon}}(0)=\widehat{g}(0)\,\widehat{\phi}(0)=\widehat{g}(0).

Since

is a continuous function, we have that

converges uniformly in compact sets as

∎

3.
The Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\nu}

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
3

Taking the Fourier transform of the measure
\nu
that is compactly supported in
[-\Delta,\Delta]
, we obtain

(3.1)

\begin{split}\widehat{\nu}(x)=\,&c_{1}+\frac{c_{2}\,e^{-c_{3}\Delta}}{(c_{3}^{2}+4\pi^{2}x^{2})^{2}}\bigg\{2e^{c_{3}\Delta}(c_{3}^{2}-4\pi^{2}x^{2})\\
&-2(c_{3}^{2}-4\pi^{2}x^{2}+c_{3}^{3}\Delta+4c_{3}\pi^{2}x^{2}\Delta)\cos(2\pi\Delta x)+4\pi x(2c_{3}+c_{3}^{2}\Delta+4\pi^{2}x^{2}\Delta)\sin(2\pi\Delta x)\bigg\}.\end{split}

If we prove that this function is bounded above and below away from zero, i.e., that there exists
a,b>0
such that
a^{2}\leq\widehat{\nu}(x)\leq b^{2}
, we are done, because then
\widehat{\nu}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x
is a positive measure equivalent to Lebesgue, and we have the inequalities

a^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)\,\text{\rm d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)\,\widehat{\nu}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x\leq b^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)\,\text{\rm d}x

for all Lebesgue measurable
g\geq 0
, whence we also have (
1.13
) by taking
g=|f|^{2}
for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, this means
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}
is a normed linear space, and its completeness follows from the completeness of
PW(\pi\Delta)
. This likewise applies to the reproducing kernel property.

Now, we can trivially estimate it from above

|\widehat{\nu}(x)|\leq c_{1}+c_{2}\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\lvert\alpha\rvert\ e^{-c_{3}\lvert\alpha\rvert}d\alpha\leq c_{1}+c_{2}\Delta^{2},

and to find a lower bound, set
t:=2\pi\Delta x
and
\sigma:=c_{3}\Delta
and write

\widehat{\nu}(x)=c_{2}\Delta^{2}\left\{\frac{c_{1}}{\Delta^{2}c_{2}}-G(\sigma,t)\right\},

where

\displaystyle G(\sigma,t)=-\frac{e^{-\sigma}}{(\sigma^{2}+t^{2})^{2}}\bigg\{2e^{\sigma}(\sigma^{2}-t^{2})-2(\sigma^{2}-t^{2}+\sigma^{3}+t^{2}\sigma)\cos(t)+2t(2\sigma+\sigma^{2}+t^{2})\sin(t)\bigg\}.

We will now show that
G(\sigma,t)
cannot be too large.

Observe first that this is a harmonic function. In fact, it is the real part of a holomorphic function of the variable
z=-\sigma+it
. Explicitly,

G(\sigma,t)=\operatorname{Re}\left\{\frac{e^{z}(1-z)}{z^{2}}-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right\}.

Hence, if we let
\Omega_{R}
be the region
\{-\sigma+it:\sigma>0,t>0,|z|<R\}
, we have by the maximum principle that

\max_{z\in\overline{\Omega}_{R}}G(\sigma,t)\leq\max_{z\in\partial\Omega_{R}}\operatorname{Re}\left\{\frac{e^{z}(1-z)}{z^{2}}-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right\},

but, when
|z|=R
, we have that

\left|\operatorname{Re}\left\{\frac{e^{z}(1-z)}{z^{2}}-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right\}\right|\leq\frac{e^{-\sigma}(1+R)+1}{R^{2}},

which becomes very small when
R\to\infty
. Moreover, when
t=0
, we have

G(\sigma,0)=-\frac{e^{-\sigma}}{\sigma^{4}}\bigg\{2e^{\sigma}(\sigma^{2})-2(\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{3})\bigg\}=\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\left\{-1+e^{-\sigma}\left(1+\sigma\right)\right\}\leq 0

by the elementary inequality
1+\sigma\leq e^{\sigma}
for
\sigma\geq 0
. So that to bound
G
in the first quadrant, it is enough to consider it on the half-line
\sigma=0
,
t\geq 0
. That is,

\sup_{\sigma\geq 0,t\geq 0}G(\sigma,t)=\sup_{t>0}\frac{2-2\cos(t)-2t\sin(t)}{t^{2}}=0.586\ldots.

Thus, choosing
\frac{c_{1}}{\Delta^{2}c_{2}}\geq\frac{3}{5}>0.586\ldots
, we obtain that
\widehat{\nu}
is bounded below uniformly away from zero, concluding the proof.

Remark
.

By a standard procedure involving a Fourier uncertainty principle (as used in
[
4
, proof of Lemma 12]
), we can extend the range of admissible values of

and
\Delta
all the way up to
\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\Delta^{2}\leq\frac{1}{0.586\ldots}
to guarantee that
(\mathcal{H}_{\nu},\|\cdot\|_{\nu})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space.

3.2.
Proof of Lemma

It is immediate from the inequalities in (

is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space which is equal to
PW(\pi\Delta)
as a set.

Returning to (EP1), we recall that, to find

, we want to minimize

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi_{\nu}(g)}{g(0)}=\frac{1}{g(0)}\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\widehat{g}(\alpha)\,\text{\rm d}\nu(\alpha),

over
g
in the class
\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}
. We restrict ourselves to a subclass of bandlimited functions to obtain an upper bound for
\mathbf{C}_{\nu}
. This is an adaptation of the proof of
[
4

to our setting.

Denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}:=\{g\in\mathcal{A}_{\Delta}:{\rm supp}{(\widehat{g})}\subseteq[-\Delta,\Delta]\}
and let

. Since
{\rm supp}{(\widehat{g})}\subseteq[-\Delta,\Delta]

has a unique extension to an entire function of exponential type at most

, which we also denote by
g

g(x)\geq 0
and

by Krein’s decomposition theorem
[
1

there exists an entire function

for all

On the other hand, by the reproducing kernel property and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

Now, equality can be attained in the above inequality if and only if

, which occurs in
\mathcal{B}_{\Delta}
since

by (

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}=-2\sqrt{\lambda}\Lambda(\lambda,c_{3})&+\lambda^{2}\big(I_{1}(\eta_{1})-I_{1}(\eta_{2})\big)-\lambda\sqrt{\lambda}\Lambda(\lambda,c_{3})\big(I_{1}(\eta_{1})+I_{1}(\eta_{2})\big)\\
&+2\lambda c_{3}\big(I_{0}(\eta_{1})-I_{0}(\eta_{2})\big)-2\lambda^{2}c_{3}\big(I_{0}(\eta_{2})I_{1}(\eta_{1})-I_{0}(\eta_{1})I_{1}(\eta_{2})\big)\\
=L_{1}+L_{2},\end{split}

where

\displaystyle L_{1}:=-\sqrt{\lambda}\Lambda(\lambda,c_{3})\big(2+\lambda\big(I_{1}(\eta_{1})+I_{1}(\eta_{2})\big)\big)

and

\displaystyle L_{2}:=\lambda^{2}\big(I_{1}(\eta_{1})-I_{1}(\eta_{2})\big)+2\lambda c_{3}\big(I_{0}(\eta_{1})-I_{0}(\eta_{2})\big)-2\lambda^{2}c_{3}\big(I_{0}(\eta_{2})I_{1}(\eta_{1})-I_{0}(\eta_{1})I_{1}(\eta_{2})\big).

Let
\sigma:=\lambda\Delta^{2}
, which by hypothesis satisfies
\sigma\leq 2.9
.
We can treat the problem in two disjoint cases:
(I)
c_{3}<\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}
and (II)
c_{3}>\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}
.

We proceed with case (I). Since the solutions of (
4.7
) are purely imaginary we can take
\eta_{1}=iy_{1},\text{ and }\eta_{2}=iy_{2},\text{where }0<y_{1}<y_{2}
. This choice of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
implies no loss of generality because
A
and
B
are even functions and the non-vanishing of
\mathcal{L}
is independent of this choice.

For
k\in\{0,1\}
, by using a trigonometric identity for
\cos{x}-\cos{y}
, we can compute

\displaystyle\left|I_{k}(\eta_{1})\right.-

\displaystyle\left.I_{k}(\eta_{2})\right|=4\left|\int_{0}^{\Delta/2}\sin\left(\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\;\alpha\right)\sin\left(\frac{y_{1}-y_{2}}{2}\;\alpha\right)\alpha^{k}e^{-c_{3}\alpha}\text{\rm d}\alpha\right|

\displaystyle\leq 4\frac{\left|y_{1}-y_{2}\right|}{2}\cdot\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\Delta/2}\alpha^{2+k}\;\text{\rm d}\alpha=(\eta_{1}^{2}-\eta_{2}^{2})\frac{\Delta^{k+3}}{(k+3)2^{k+3}}=\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\lambda-4c_{3}^{2}}\frac{\Delta^{k+3}}{(k+3)2^{k+2}}.

On the other hand, by employing the identity

\begin{split}\cos(y_{2}\alpha)\cos(y_{1}\beta)-\cos(y_{1}\alpha)\cos(y_{2}\beta)=&\sin\left(\frac{(y_{1}+y_{2})(\alpha+\beta)}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{(y_{1}-y_{2})(\alpha-\beta)}{2}\right)\\
&\qquad\quad+\sin\left(\frac{(y_{1}+y_{2})(\alpha-\beta)}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{(y_{1}-y_{2})(\alpha+\beta)}{2}\right)\end{split}

and the inequality
\sin{x}\leq x
, we derive that

\displaystyle\left\lvert I_{0}(\eta_{2})I_{1}(\eta_{1})\right.

\displaystyle-\left.I_{0}(\eta_{1})I_{1}(\eta_{2})\right\rvert\leq 2(y_{2}^{2}-y_{1}^{2})\int\limits_{0}^{\Delta/2}\int\limits_{0}^{\Delta/2}\left\lvert\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\right\rvert\beta\;\text{\rm d}\alpha\text{\rm d}\beta=\frac{11}{480}\Delta^{5}\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\lambda-4c_{3}^{2}}.

Combining the two upper bounds above we get

|L_{2}|\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\lambda-4c_{3}^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{4}}{32}+\frac{\lambda^{3/2}\Delta^{3}}{12}+\frac{11\lambda^{2}\Delta^{5}\sqrt{\lambda}}{480}\bigg)=\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\lambda-4c_{3}^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{32}+\frac{\sigma^{3/2}}{12}+\frac{11\sigma^{5/2}}{480}\bigg).

Since we know
0<y_{1}<y_{2}=\sqrt{\lambda-c_{3}^{2}+\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\lambda-4c_{3}^{2}}}<\sqrt{2\sigma}/{\Delta}
and
\sigma\leq 2.9<\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}
, this implies that
y_{1}\alpha,y_{2}\alpha\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right],
for all
\alpha\in\left[0,\frac{\Delta}{2}\right]
. Therefore,
\cos(y_{1}\alpha)\geq\cos(y_{2}\alpha)\geq 0
for all
\alpha\in\left[0,\frac{\Delta}{2}\right]
and hence
I_{1}(\eta_{1})+I_{1}(\eta_{2})>0
. Thus,
L_{1}<-2\lambda\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}
holds.

Finally, by our assumption
\sigma\leq 2.9
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\leq L_{1}+|L_{2}|<\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{\lambda-4c_{3}^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{32}+\frac{\sigma^{3/2}}{12}+\frac{11\sigma^{5/2}}{480}-2\bigg)<0.

Now, we treat case (II). Here, the solutions to (
4.7
) are of the form
\eta_{1},-\eta_{1},\overline{\eta_{1}},-\overline{\eta_{1}}
. We make the choice that
\eta_{1}
should be in the first quadrant of the plane, and select
\eta_{2}=\overline{\eta_{1}}
. Again, this selection implies no loss of generality because
A
and
B
are even functions and the non-vanishing of
\mathcal{L}
is independent of this choice. Given this, we can see
\mathcal{L}
is a purely imaginary number.

Let
\eta_{1}=x_{1}+iy_{1}
, where
x_{1}>0
and
y_{1}>0
, then
\eta_{2}=x_{1}-iy_{1}
and
\eta_{1}^{2}=c_{3}^{2}-\lambda+i\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}
which gives

\displaystyle x_{1}=c_{3}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2c_{3}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\lambda}{c_{3}^{2}}}}\quad\text{ and }\quad y_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}}{2c_{3}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2c_{3}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\lambda}{c_{3}^{2}}}}}.

Notice that
\sigma\leq 2.9<\pi^{2}
, so we can identify the signs of the terms involving trigonometric factors in
I_{1}(\eta_{1})
and
I_{1}(\eta_{2})
, because
0<y_{1}<\sqrt{\lambda}
, and we have
y_{1}\alpha\in[0,\pi/2]
for
0<\alpha<\Delta/2
. Hence,
I_{1}(\eta_{1})+I_{1}(\eta_{2})>0
and, consequently,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(L_{1})<-2\lambda\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}.

For
k\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\bigg\{I_{k}(\eta_{1})-I_{k}(\eta_{2})\bigg\}

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Im}\left\{I_{k}(x_{1}+iy_{1})\right\}=4\int_{0}^{\Delta/2}\sinh(x_{1}\alpha)\sin(y_{1}\alpha)\alpha^{k}e^{-c_{3}\alpha}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha.

Also, by using a trigonometric identity for
\cosh(x+y)
and a few simplifications, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\bigg\{

\displaystyle I_{0}(\eta_{2})I_{1}(\eta_{1})-I_{0}(\eta_{1})I_{1}(\eta_{2})\bigg\}=2\operatorname{Im}\left\{I_{0}(x_{1}-iy_{1})I_{1}(x_{1}+iy_{1})\right\}

\displaystyle=4\int\limits_{0}^{\Delta/2}\int\limits_{0}^{\Delta/2}[\sinh(x_{1}(\alpha+\beta))\sin(y_{1}(\alpha-\beta))+\sinh(x_{1}(\alpha-\beta))\sin(y_{1}(\alpha+\beta))]\alpha e^{-c_{3}(\alpha+\beta)}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\,\text{\rm d}\beta.

At this point, we have to consider two sub-cases: (a)
c_{3}\in\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2};\sqrt{\lambda}\right]
and (b)
c_{3}>\sqrt{\lambda}
.

For subcase (a), notice that we always have
0<x_{1}<c_{3}
and so, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

|\sinh(x_{1}t)|=\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert e^{x_{1}t}-e^{-x_{1}t}\right\rvert=\left|x_{1}t\right|e^{\xi t},

for some
\xi\in[-x_{1},x_{1}]\subseteq[-c_{3},c_{3}]
. Therefore, we can say

\left\lvert\sinh(x_{1}(\alpha+\beta))\sin(y_{1}(\alpha-\beta))\right\rvert\leq x_{1}y_{1}\lvert\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\rvert\cdot e^{c_{3}(\alpha+\beta)}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}\,\lvert\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\rvert\cdot e^{c_{3}(\alpha+\beta)}

for any
\alpha,\beta>0
.
Using this estimate, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\operatorname{Im}\bigg\{I_{0}(\eta_{2})I_{1}(\eta_{1})-I_{0}(\eta_{1})I_{1}(\eta_{2})\bigg\}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}\int\limits_{0}^{\Delta/2}\int\limits_{0}^{\Delta/2}\lvert\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\rvert\alpha\,\text{\rm d}\alpha\,\text{\rm d}\beta=\frac{11\Delta^{5}}{480}\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}.

Also, for
k\in\{0,1\}
we obtain using
x_{1}y_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}
that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\operatorname{Im}\bigg\{I_{k}(\eta_{1})-I_{k}(\eta_{2})\bigg\}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 4x_{1}y_{1}\int_{0}^{\Delta/2}\alpha^{2+k}\,\text{\rm d}\alpha=\frac{\Delta^{3+k}}{(3+k)2^{k+2}}\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}.

Hence, we get

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Im}(L_{2})|\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}\left(\frac{11\lambda^{5/2}\Delta^{5}}{240}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{4}}{32}+\frac{\lambda^{3/2}\Delta^{3}}{6}\right)=\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}\left(\frac{11}{240}\sigma^{5/2}+\frac{1}{32}\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\sigma^{3/2}\right).

Whenever
\sigma\leq 3
, combining the bound of
|\operatorname{Im}(L_{2})|
with (
5.3
) we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\big\{\mathcal{L}\big\}\leq\operatorname{Im}(L_{1})+|\operatorname{Im}(L_{2})|<\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{4c_{3}^{2}-\lambda}\left(\frac{11}{240}\sigma^{5/2}+\frac{1}{32}\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\sigma^{3/2}-2\right)<0.

In subcase (b), we have
c_{3}>\sqrt{\lambda}
and

|\sinh(x_{1}(\alpha\pm\beta))e^{-c_{3}(\alpha+\beta)}|\leq\frac{1}{2},

for any
\alpha,\beta>0
. Using this estimate, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\operatorname{Im}\bigg\{I_{0}(\eta_{2})I_{1}(\eta_{1})\right.-\left.I_{0}(\eta_{1})I_{1}(\eta_{2})\bigg\}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2y_{1}\int\limits_{0}^{\Delta/2}\int\limits_{0}^{\Delta/2}\bigg(\lvert\alpha-\beta\rvert+|\alpha+\beta|\bigg)\alpha\,d\alpha\,d\beta=\frac{3\Delta^{4}y_{1}}{32},

and since
y_{1}<\sqrt{\lambda}
, for
k\in\{0,1\}
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\operatorname{Im}\bigg\{I_{k}(\eta_{1})-I_{k}(\eta_{2})\bigg\}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2y_{1}\int_{0}^{\Delta/2}\alpha^{k+1}\,d\alpha=\frac{y_{1}\Delta^{k+2}}{(k+2)2^{k+1}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}\Delta^{k+2}}{(k+2)2^{k+1}}.

The two upper bounds above give

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Im}(L_{2})|\leq c_{3}\sqrt{\lambda}\left(\frac{3}{16}\lambda^{2}{\Delta^{4}}+\frac{\lambda^{3/2}\Delta^{3}}{12}+\frac{\lambda\Delta^{2}}{2}\right)=c_{3}\sqrt{\lambda}\left(\frac{3}{16}\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{12}\sigma^{3/2}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma\right).

Since
\lambda/c_{3}^{2}<1
, combining the upper bound of
\operatorname{Im}(L_{2})
with (
5.3
) we obtain that

\operatorname{Im}\big\{\mathcal{L}\big\}\leq\operatorname{Im}(L_{1})+|\operatorname{Im}(L_{2})|<c_{3}\sqrt{\lambda}\left(\frac{3}{16}\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{12}\sigma^{3/2}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma-2\sqrt{3}\right)<0,

whenever
\sigma\leq 2.9
.
∎
