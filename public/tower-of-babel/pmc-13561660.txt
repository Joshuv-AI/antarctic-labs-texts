Early Enhancement of Mitral Annular e' Following Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation: The Role of Early Diastolic Filling
Authors: Takahide Ito, Kanako Akamatsu, Shimpei Fujioka, Kensuke Kinoshita, Yohei Yamauchi, Daisuke Shishikura, Yumiko Kanzaki, Hideki Ozawa, Masahiro Daimon, Takahiro Katsumata, Hideaki Morita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeEarly improvements in left ventricular (LV) diastolic function have been documented following transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), but it remains unclear whether the increase in early diastolic mitral annular velocity (e′) reflects intrinsic improvement in LV relaxation or altered hemodynamic conditions. This study investigated early changes in e' after TAVI, with a particular focus on the hemodynamic determinants of the increase in e′.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted in 129 consecutive patients with severe aortic stenosis who underwent successful TAVI and echocardiographic evaluation before and within one week after the procedure. Clinical and echocardiographic parameters were compared before and after TAVI, and factors associated with changes in e′ were evaluated.ResultsTAVI significantly increased septal, lateral, and mean e′, transmitral early diastolic (E) velocity, and LV ejection fraction (LVEF) while reducing the transvalvular pressure gradient. In multivariate linear regression analysis, with changes in LVEF, E velocity, mean transvalvular pressure gradient, stroke volume, baseline mean e′, and atrial fibrillation entered as covariates, baseline mean e′ (β = −0.590, p < 0.001), atrial fibrillation (β = 0.523, p = 0.006), and changes in E velocity (β = 0.019, p = 0.018) were independently associated with changes in mean e′.Conclusione' increased rapidly after TAVI. Changes in E velocity were independently associated with changes in mean e′, even after adjustment for changes in stroke volume, suggesting that early changes in e′ reflect not only intrinsic improvement in LV relaxation but also load‐related mechanisms during early diastolic filling.Keywords: aortic stenosis, diastolic function, tissue Doppler imaging, transcatheter aortic valve implantation

Background

Severe aortic stenosis (AS) imposes chronic pressure overload on the left ventricle (LV), resulting in myocardial hypertrophy, impaired LV relaxation, and diastolic dysfunction [1]. Relief of valvular obstruction by transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has been shown to improve LV diastolic function [2, 3, 4], although the mechanisms underlying the early improvement in LV diastolic function remain incompletely understood.

Mitral annular early diastolic velocity (e′) is widely used as a noninvasive marker of LV relaxation and plays a central role in the echocardiographic assessment of diastolic function [5]. Experimental studies, however, have demonstrated that e′ is determined by myocardial relaxation, restoring forces arising from ventricular elastic recoil, and lengthening load (Figure 1) [6]. Lengthening load refers to the force exerted on the mitral annulus by early diastolic transmitral inflow and is represented by the transmitral E velocity [7]. Accordingly, e′ should not be regarded as a purely load‐independent index of LV relaxation, particularly under conditions associated with substantial changes in transmitral inflow.

Physiological determinants of mitral annular e′. Mitral annular early diastolic velocity (e′) is determined by three principal mechanisms: LV relaxation, restoring forces, and lengthening load generated by early diastolic transmitral inflow. The illustration is a conceptual representation based on the physiological framework described by Opdahl et al. [6].

Given these observations, TAVI provides a unique clinical model in which LV afterload is abruptly reduced, leading to early hemodynamic changes, including changes in stroke volume (SV) and E velocity [3, 4], before substantial structural reverse remodeling occurs. This setting offers an opportunity to examine whether changes in e′ after TAVI primarily reflect improved intrinsic LV relaxation or are also influenced by changes in early diastolic filling.

Therefore, the present study aimed to identify factors associated with the early increase in e′ following TAVI, with particular emphasis on whether changes in E velocity were associated with this increase.

Methods

Study Population

This single‐center observational study included 133 consecutive patients with severe AS who underwent transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) at our institution between October 2017 and March 2022. Eligibility required comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography to be performed before and within one week after TAVI. Four patients were excluded due to unsuccessful TAVI (n = 2) or incomplete echocardiographic data (n = 2), resulting in a final cohort of 129 patients.

The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (No. 1542–7). Written informed consent was waived due to the observational nature of the study; however, all patients were provided with the option to opt out.

Echocardiographic Assessment

Comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography was performed before and within one week after TAVI, following current guidelines. All examinations were performed by experienced sonographers.

Measurements included left atrial (LA) diameter, LV end‐diastolic dimension, interventricular septal thickness, and posterior wall thickness, using two‐dimensional echocardiography. LA volume was calculated via the biplane Simpson's method and indexed to the body surface area. LV ejection fraction (LVEF) was determined using the modified Simpson's method.

Transmitral early (E) and late (A) diastolic inflow velocities, E/A ratio, and deceleration time were measured using pulsed‐wave Doppler recordings. Early diastolic mitral annular velocity (e′) was assessed at the septal and lateral mitral annulus using tissue Doppler imaging, with the average of these measurements serving as an index of LV relaxation. The E to mean e′ ratio (E/mean e′) was used as an estimate of the LV filling pressure.

SV was calculated by multiplying the LV outflow tract (LVOT) velocity–time integral (VTI) by the LVOT cross‐sectional area, and was indexed by body surface area to draw SV index (SVI). Aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated using the continuity equation.The aortic valve area index (AVAI) was calculated by indexing AVA to body surface area. LV mass was calculated using the Devereux formula and indexed to the body surface area [8]. Relative wall thickness was calculated as twice the posterior wall thickness divided by the LV end‐diastolic diameter [8].

Biochemical Measurements

Plasma B‐type natriuretic peptide (BNP) concentrations were measured before and after TAVI.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons of pre‐ and post‐TAVI measurements were conducted using paired t‐tests or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests, as applicable. Changes in the number of patients with an SVI < 35 mL/m2 before and after TAVI were assessed using the McNamar's test. Multivariate linear regression analysis was employed to identify factors independently associated with changes in mean e′. The baseline value was incorporated as a key covariate to adjust for pre‐existing differences.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by replacing changes in SV with changes in LVOT‐VTI or baseline flow status in the multivariate model and by repeating the analysis after excluding patients with atrial fibrillation.

Statistical analyses were performed using JMP statistical software (version 17.0; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The cohort was elderly (84 ± 5 years) and predominantly female (62%), with a high prevalence of hypertension (60%), followed by dyslipidemia (38%) and diabetes mellitus (26%). In sensitivity analyses, similar results were obtained when changes in SV were replaced by changes in LVOT‐VTI or baseline flow status (low flow vs. normal flow) (Table S1). Correlations between changes in E velocity and changes in SV and between changes in E velocity and changes in LVOT‐VTI were weak (r = 0.26 and r = 0.32, respectively), whereas changes in SV and changes in LVOT‐VTI were strongly correlated (r = 0.84).

Another sensitivity analysis excluding 15 patients with atrial fibrillation showed that changes in E velocity was significantly associated with changes in mean e′ in univariate analysis (β = 0.029, p = 0.002), while the corresponding association in multivariate analysis did not reach statistical significance (β = 0.013, p = 0.14) (Table S2).

Discussion

Main Findings

Mitral annular e′ velocity increased rapidly within one week after TAVI. This early change was independently associated with an increase in E velocity, even after accounting for changes in SV. These findings suggest that the early enhancement of e′ after TAVI is multifactorial and may reflect both improved myocardial relaxation following afterload reduction [9] and the loading‐related influence of increased early diastolic inflow. Furthermore, e′ increased significantly regardless of LVEF improvement, suggesting that the early increase in e′ was not simply attributable to systolic recovery.

Mechanisms Underlying the Early Increase in e' after TAVI

LV diastolic dysfunction is a hallmark of severe AS, resulting from chronic pressure overload, myocardial hypertrophy, and impaired myocardial relaxation [1]. In this study, e′ increased significantly within one week after TAVI. This finding is consistent with an early improvement in LV relaxation following relief of valvular obstruction, although concomitant changes in loading conditions may also have contributed. Because LV mass and chamber geometry remained unchanged over this short period, the increase in e′ was unlikely to reflect structural reverse remodeling.

In light of these observations and the physiological determinants illustrated in Figure 1, two mechanisms appear particularly relevant to the early increase in e′ after TAVI: (1) afterload reduction facilitating myocardial relaxation and (2) increased E velocity augmenting lengthening load (Figure 3).

Proposed mechanisms underlying the early increase in mitral annular e′ after TAVI. TAVI may enhance mitral annular e′ velocity through two complementary pathways. Relief of afterload improves LV relaxation, whereas hemodynamic changes increase transmitral E velocity, thereby augmenting lengthening load. The latter pathway may reflect the combined effects of several physiological factors influencing early diastolic transmitral inflow. The present study demonstrates an independent association between increased E velocity and enhanced e′ but does not address the mechanisms responsible for the increase in E velocity itself.

First, Gaasch et al. demonstrated that afterload reduction enhances LV relaxation by shortening the time constant of isovolumic pressure decay (tau), underscoring the beneficial effect of pressure unloading on active relaxation [9]. Given the marked reduction in transvalvular pressure gradient after TAVI, improved active relaxation likely contributed, at least in part, to the early increase in e′.

Second, although the increase in E velocity may partly reflect improved LV relaxation, increased early diastolic inflow may itself augment e′ through lengthening load. Experimental studies, as reviewed by Opdahl et al., have demonstrated that e′ increases with augmented lengthening load even when myocardial relaxation remains unchanged [6]. Supporting this concept, Rivas‐Gotz et al. and Sohn et al. reported that transmitral inflow preceded or coincided with mitral annular lengthening in patients with elevated LV filling pressures [10, 11]. This mechanism may be particularly relevant in our cohort, in which E/e′ remained elevated after TAVI, suggesting persistently elevated LV filling pressures.

Importantly, E velocity and SV are not physiologically interchangeable. E velocity reflects the instantaneous hemodynamic conditions governing early diastolic inflow, whereas SV represents the integrated volume transported over the cardiac cycle. Indeed, in the present study, changes in E velocity were only weakly correlated with changes in SV and LVOT‐VTI (r = 0.26 and r = 0.32, respectively). A recent study using simultaneous echocardiographic and catheterization measurements during acute preload augmentation showed that E velocity normalized to SV identified elevated LV filling pressure more accurately than E/e′ [12]. In the present study, the association between changes in E velocity and e′ remained significant after adjustment for changes in SV, and E velocity increased after TAVI despite unchanged SV in the overall cohort. These findings suggest that the increase in E velocity cannot be explained solely by greater forward flow. The unchanged E/e′ further suggests that persistent pressure‐related influences on early diastolic filling may have contributed to the increase in e′.

Third, diastolic suction is an important determinant of transmitral E velocity [13]. Although late systolic blood flow inertia can promote diastolic suction by maintaining forward aortic flow during late systole [14], this mechanism may be attenuated in patients with severe AS because chronic LV hypertrophy, impaired relaxation, and reduced ventricular compliance may limit the generation of effective restoring forces [15]. Therefore, the post‐TAVI increase in E velocity does not necessarily indicate enhanced diastolic suction but may instead reflect altered transmitral driving conditions.

Previous Studies

Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the early effects of TAVI on LV diastolic function. Although echocardiographic studies have consistently demonstrated an early increase in e′ [2, 3, 4], invasive studies have not consistently shown an immediate improvement in indices of active relaxation [16, 17]. This discrepancy may partly reflect differences in the timing of assessment: invasive measurements were performed during or immediately after valve implantation, whereas the present study evaluated patients within one week after TAVI, when acute procedural influences may have partially subsided.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single‐center retrospective study with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In particular, the independent association between atrial fibrillation and changes in e′ should be interpreted with caution given the small number of patients with atrial fibrillation. Second, only short‐term echocardiographic changes within one week after TAVI were evaluated, and the long‐term implications of the observed increase in e′ remain unknown. Third, invasive hemodynamic measurements were unavailable, limiting direct assessment of the physiological changes underlying the increase in e′, particularly those related to active LV relaxation and filling pressures. Finally, although changes in E velocity were independently associated with changes in e′, the observational nature of the study precludes definitive conclusions regarding the directionality or causality of this relationship.

Conclusion

Mitral annular e′ velocity increased significantly within one week after TAVI. Changes in e′ were independently associated with changes in transmitral E velocity, whereas changes in SV and mean transvalvular pressure gradient were not. These findings suggest that the early post‐TAVI increase in e′ should be interpreted in relation to both myocardial relaxation and loading conditions, particularly lengthening load associated with E velocity. Whether this early increase in e′ translates into improved long‐term clinical outcomes remains uncertain.
