IQ Test for LLMs: An Evaluation Framework for Uncovering Core Skills in LLMs
Aviya Maimon 1 Amir DN Cohen 2 Gal Vishne 3 Shauli Ravfogel 4 Reut Tsarfaty 1 1 Bar-Ilan University 3 Data Science Institute, Columbia University 4 Center for Data Science, New York University 2 OriginAI {aviyamn, Amirdnc, gal.vishne, shauli.ravfogel, reut.tsarfaty}@gmail.com

Abstract
Current evaluations of large language models (LLMs) rely on benchmark scores, but it is difficult to interpret what these individual scores reveal about a model’s overall skills. Specifically, as a community we lack understanding of how tasks relate to one another, what they measure in common, how they differ, or which ones are redundant. As a result, models are often assessed via a single score averaged across benchmarks, an approach that fails to capture the models’ wholistic strengths and limitations. Here, we propose a new evaluation paradigm that uses factor analysis to identify latent skills driving performance across benchmarks. We apply this method to a comprehensive new leaderboard showcasing the performance of 60 LLMs on 44 tasks, and identify a small set of latent skills that largely explain performance. Finally, we turn these insights into practical tools that identify redundant tasks, aid in model selection, and profile models along each latent skill.

Paradigms in LLM Evaluation

As LLMs become increasingly central, the need to evaluate them effectively critically increases. This section surveys dominant LLM evaluation paradigms, highlighting their strengths, limitations, and motivating the rethinking of their assessment.

Challenges and limitations in current LLM evaluation methodologies.

LLMs are now deployed across diverse real-world applications, yet we still lack a principled way to compare two systems across use-cases.
While datasets such as SQuAD
Rajpurkar et al. 2016
and GSM8K
Cobbe et al. 2021
are designed to test specific underlying abilities like
reading comprehension
and
mathematical reasoning
,
they do so through limited and often inconsistent sets of instances. These instances serve as proxies for the intended skills, but differ in style, complexity, and assumptions. As a result, it remains unclear how to meaningfully compare models across tasks — giving rise to three interrelated problems:

First, because of task heterogeneity and inconsistent metrics, the relationships among existing datasets remain poorly understood, and their evaluation metrics are far from uniform, making it difficult to compare performance meaningfully across tasks.
Items within a
single
task category (e.g., question answering) can tap into different skills — e.g. GPQA
Rein et al. 2023
versus TriviaQA
Joshi et al. 2017
— whereas ostensibly distinct tasks can measure overlapping competencies (e.g., SQuAD
Rajpurkar et al. 2016
and XSum
Narayan et al. 2018
both rely heavily on reading comprehension and contextual understanding).

Second, because of this heterogeneity, researchers commonly report a simple average over all examined tasks. However, this score aggregation is misleading. We show that such aggregation obscures important performance differences and fails to capture a model’s true skill profile.

Finally, datasets coverage is itself imbalanced: domains such as textual entailment are over-represented
Sanh et al. 2022
, whereas critical areas like ethical and social judgment are notably under-represented
Hendrycks et al. 2023
. This uneven skill coverage clutters the view we can get from aggregate scores.

To address these limitations, we propose to identify a concise set of core skills that span most existing datasets and assess models performance directly along these latent dimensions. This skill-oriented framework yields a more nuanced and interpretable assessment, enabling fine-grained model comparisons and informing the design of future tasks.

Historical and contemporary inspiration.

Our framework is inspired by a rich tradition in psychometrics, a field concerned with quantifying unobservable psychological traits from observable test or questionnaire data. FA, a statistical method used to infer latent constructs from observed patterns in behavioral data,

Rather than designing new datasets, we apply the psychometric lens to analyze performance variation across existing datasets. Just as IQ tests use FA to uncover latent cognitive abilities, we treat the resulting factors as core

Modeling Latent Skills

In this section, we provide a formal overview of the process that goes from raw task scores to interpretable
latent skills
.

We begin with the
performance matrix

\bm{\Pi}
(§
3.1
), a
\textit{models}\times\textit{tasks}
matrix that contains the scores of all tasks on all LLMs.
We then use
Principal Axis Factoring
(PAF;
Harman 1976
) (§
3.2
), where each task (column) is treated as a variable and each model (row) as an observation. PAF takes the performance matrix
\bm{\Pi}
and extracts factors that account for
shared
variance across tasks, while discarding task-specific noise. Focusing on shared variance, PAF yields three benefits:
(i) the resulting factors
generalize
more reliably to unseen tasks because task-specific variance is removed;
(ii) the solution is more
robust to outliers
as the variance that appears in only one task is treated as noise and cannot distort the factors; and
(iii) the extracted factors are more
interpretable
, reflecting broad abilities rather than fragmented patterns.

2
2

2

At first glance Principal Component Analysis (PCA) seems equally suited for the task of deriving a low–dimensional representation, yet PCA optimizes a different objective: it selects orthogonal directions that maximize
total
variance, indiscriminately mixing variance shared by several variables with variance unique to a single one. A high
-
variance but essentially idiosyncratic feature can therefore dominate a principal component and mask the common structure we wish to uncover. PAF, that only models the covariance that variables share, is more suitable here.

Finally, we present how we name the extracted latent factors as skills, and conceptualize a more intuitive and interpretable
skill-based leaderboard
(§
3.3
).

5.1.1
Analysis

The upshot of the proposed analysis, is that the latent factors our method uncovers reflect deeper behavioral demands — e.g., reasoning complexity or fidelity — rather than superficial traits like task format or domain.
A closer inspection of the factor structure shows that the task clusters do not align with commonly used task categories (e.g., question answering versus classification). Instead, the latent factors partition the task space along finer-grained functional axes — for instance, whether the solution hinges on domain-specific knowledge or on token-level fidelity.
This suggests that organizing tasks by surface features such as format or domain can be misleading: tasks that appear similar on the surface often pull apart in factor space, and vice versa. In this Section, we provide illustrative examples of this trend.

Surface-similar tasks diverge.

First, let us consider pairs of tasks that share a format yet fall on different factors. For example, GPQA and TriviaQA are both defined as question-answering tasks, but GPQA loads on Factor 8.
Grad.-Level Reasoning
, because it stresses physics derivations, whereas TriviaQA lands on Factor 3.
Long Document
, wherein evidence extraction dominates; it appears that the
skills
needed for question-answering differ between these datasets. A similar split appears inside GLUE: MRPC (paraphrase detection) sits with Factor 1’s
General NLU
, but its sibling QNLI (question–sentence entailment) migrates to Factor 2, reflecting the finer-grained inference needed for entailment.

Generative tasks diverge as well: CoQA (conversational, document-grounded QA) again joins Factor 3.
Long Document
, while TruthfulQA-Generation, whose main challenge is hallucination avoidance, aligns with Factor 4’s
Truth and Inst.-Following
. Even two entailment datasets can separate — GLUE’s MNLI stays in Factor 1, but QNLI heads to Factor 2 — highlighting how an increases in reasoning granularity reshape the latent map.

Surface-dissimilar tasks converge.

The reverse pattern also appears: tasks with different formats or domains can load on the same factor due to shared underlying demands. For example, SQuALity (faithful-summary scoring) and Math (symbolic arithmetic) both rely on token-level exactness; unsurprisingly, they co-load on Factor 7, our
Precision & Fidelity
axis.
SQuAD and XSum — span-based QA versus abstractive summarization — meet in Factor 3 because both demand document comprehension and selective extraction. Finally, news-domain MRPC and legal Contract-NLI share Factor 1
Genrarl NLU

Evaluation of the Uncovered Skill Set

We now evaluate the validity and interpretability of the uncovered skill dimensions. This section addresses two key questions:

(i)

Does the factor structure reflect a stable and internally coherent latent space?

(ii)

Do the derived skill scores meaningfully align with external judgments of model behavior?

To answer (i), Section
6.1
draws on standard metrics from the FA literature

To address (ii), Section

7
Applications: Skill-Based Evaluation

Our FA assigns to each task and model coordinates in the shared latent
skill
space. Using this space, we propose to develop targeted, skill-aware applications that go beyond ad hoc leaderboards. Specifically, we propose to use this space to (i) diagnose whether a new dataset adds genuine signal or merely re-measures existing skills; (ii) profile an unseen model from a a small, diverse task subset; and (iii) identify the best model for a new task with minimal compute.
Each application is validated on held-out portions of our data.

Related and Future Work

Approaches that aim to uncover the skills that drive model behavior, rather than assume fixed benchmark scores, have recently begun to be emerge.

In parallel to our work,
Burnell et al. 2023
apply FA to the performance, revealing three latent factors.
Independently,
Ilić and Gignac 2024
performs a leaderboard-scale analysis and uncovers a dominant general-ability (
g
) factor.
Both studies showcase the promise of psychometric tools for LLM evaluation, yet each relies on more limited or homogeneous task collections than those examined here.
In addition, Sloth
(
Polo et al. 2025
)
used latent skills to model scaling laws.
Their work highlighted the potential of using latent constructs to predict model behavior, though it relied on a simplifying linear assumption about how skill-gains transfer to task performance.

Our approach goes beyond these efforts and complements them in multiple ways. First, we apply PAF to a far broader model-task matrix, and without assuming normality or uncorrelated residuals.
Second, beyond the mathematical rigor of our proposed method we proceeded to empirically validate the detected-skills robustness under model and task perturbations. Finally, on top of the identifying and naming latent skills we also develop open applications for skill-aware dataset vetting and model selection, showcasing the practical utility of our methodology alongside their explanatory purposes.

Looking ahead, we aim to reduce evaluation overhead by identifying small, representative subsets of tasks that are sufficient for reconstructing full skill profiles. We also plan to expand the framework to incorporate non-English and multimodal tasks, which will allow for broader analysis of cross-domain generalization.

9
Conclusion

In this study, we introduce a psychometric framework for evaluating LLMs based on FA of model performance across diverse tasks (Fig.
2
). By uncovering latent structure in task performance, our approach identifies eight distinct skills that underlie model behavior—ranging from general NLI to instruction-following and domain-specific reasoning. This moves beyond single-score metrics, offering a more interpretable and multifaceted view of LLM abilities.

These skills remain stable across model and task perturbations, capturing a robust structure that supports consistent evaluation and generalization to new tasks and models.
Based on this research, we develop suite of applications to help researchers assess the novelty of new evaluation tasks, project new models into the established skill space, and efficiently select the best model for a given use case. To support the adoption of this methodology, we release all data and code publicly
12
12

12

https://github.com/AviyaMn/llm_evaluation_using_skills
.
Our ultimate goal is to encourage a shift from single-score leaderboards to a more transparent, and practical, skill-based approach to LLM evaluation.

Limitations

While our skills-based framework offers a detailed map of current open-source LLMs, it is bounded by several deliberate design choices.

The first challenge is skill interpretability. While FA identifies statistically robust patterns, assigning a meaning or label to each skill is inherently subjective. We use LLM-generated labels based on task loadings with minimal human adjustment; these are meant to be intuitive, not formally defined or systematically validated.
With more time or domain-specific resources, a more rigorous validation process—such as expert annotation or user studies—could improve label quality and consistency.

A second limitation concerns the range of models analyzed—both present and future. Our current analysis focuses on open-source models in the 2–30B parameter range, offering fine-grained resolution over this segment but under-representing today’s largest frontier models. To mitigate this, the framework is designed to be dynamic: it supports the addition of new models over time and includes outlier diagnostics to flag when the skill space no longer generalizes well, prompting timely updates.

A third limitation lies in the scope of task coverage. While our analysis draws on a broad suite of datasets that span most established tasks for LLM evaluation, it cannot capture all possible tasks—particularly those from emerging or underrepresented domains. To address this, the framework includes a built-in task novelty diagnostic, enabling researchers to identify out-of-distribution tasks and systematically expand the evaluation space as new needs arise.

Finally, our method focuses on what makes models different, not what they have in common. Because FA looks for patterns of variation, it highlights skills where models perform differently—while skills that all models do equally well on (or equally poorly) often go unnoticed.

In PAF, we assume the latent
-
variable factorization

\bm{\Pi}\;=\;\bm{\Theta}\bm{\Lambda}^{\!\top}+\bm{\epsilon},

so that each row satisfies

\mathbf{p}_{i}=\bm{\theta}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}^{\!\top}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{i}
.

We further assume the following statistical conditions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{\theta}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{C}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{i})

\displaystyle=\bm{\Psi}

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{B})

\displaystyle\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\theta}_{i},\bm{\varepsilon}_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

The population covariance (or correlation) matrix of task scores is
decomposed as

\mathbf{R}\;=\;\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\Lambda}^{\!\top}+\bm{\Psi},

where
\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\Lambda}^{\!\top}
captures the
shared

\bm{\Psi}=\operatorname{diag}(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{B})
collects the

Relation to principal–component analysis (PCA)

PCA factorizes the same matrix as
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}^{\!\top}
with orthonormal loadings
\mathbf{U}
and diagonal eigenvalues
\mathbf{D}
, seeking directions that maximize total variance. Unlike FA, PCA
(i)
does not separate shared from unique variance,
(ii)
requires orthogonality of the components, and
(iii)
identifies a unique loading matrix up to sign.
FA, by contrast, models only the off
-

\mathbf{T}
with
\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{T}
spans the same latent subspace.

In FA the loading matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
is determined only up to

non
-

(\bm{\Lambda},\mathbf{f})\!\to\!(\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{T},\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{f})

\bm{\Lambda}^{\!\top}\bm{\Lambda}=\mathbf{I}_{m}
would merely freeze one arbitrary rotation without improving model fit. PAF aims to determine the
subspace
spanned by the latent loadings. PCA identifies each loading with a concrete direction that maximizes total variance, and rotating that direction will violate optimality. Orthogonality in the ordinary dot

\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle_{\Psi^{-1}}=\mathbf{a}^{\!\top}\bm{\Psi}^{-1}\mathbf{b}

-

Iterative solution algorithm for PAF

PAF estimates the loading matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
and uniquenesses
\bm{\Psi}
by minimizing
\|\mathbf{R}-\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\Lambda}^{\!\top}-\bm{\Psi}\|_{F}^{2}

j

\bm{\Lambda}
from a reduced correlation matrix whose diagonal equals the current communalities, and (ii) updating the communalities from the new loadings, until successive updates differ by less than a tolerance
\varepsilon

1.

Initial communalities

Set
h_{j}^{2,(0)}
to the squared multiple correlation of task

2.

Reduced correlation matrix

Replace the diagonal of
\mathbf{R}
with the current communalities

3.

Compute the truncated eigen

\mathbf{R}^{(t)}=\mathbf{Q}_{c}\,\bm{\Delta}_{c}\,\mathbf{Q}_{c}^{\!\top},

\bm{\Delta}_{c}=\operatorname{diag}(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{C})

The updated loading matrix is

\bm{\Lambda}^{(t+1)}\;=\;\mathbf{Q}_{c}\,\bm{\Delta}_{c}^{1/2},

4.

Update communalities

h_{j}^{2,(t+1)}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\lambda_{jc}^{2,(t+1)}

Appendix C
Instruction per Task

In this section, we elaborate the algorithm for constructing the instructions that we used for each one of the tasks on all models.

We adopted a systematic, iterative method for instruction creation.
First, a base instruction was generated using Gemini-pro-1.5
(
Gemini-Team 2024
)
and evaluated on a subset of 50 labeled examples across 10 diverse models. An instruction was accepted for a model if at least 70% of outputs were valid. If not, we refined the instruction using targeted prompts to rephrase it for better clarity, and re-evaluated it across all models to maintain broad applicability. This iterative refinement continued until the instruction achieved at least 70% valid outputs for all 10 models. Subsequently, the instruction was further validated on additional models, retaining only those models with valid outputs for at least 50% of the examples. Appendix F
Implementation Details

Factor Analysis.

We fit a PAF model using the
factor_analyzer
Python package, extracting
C=8
factors with
rotation=‘‘varimax’’
. The number of factors was selected based on eigenvalue thresholds and explained variance inspection.

Factor Scores.

Model-level factor scores were computed using the regression method from
factor_analyzer
. These scores represent each model’s strength on every latent skill and are used throughout all downstream applications (e.g., novelty detection, model profiling, skill-aware evaluation).

Factor Labeling.

To label each factor as a latent skill, we passed the high-loading task sets through two LLMs—
deepseek-R1
and
gpt-o3-pro
—prompted to propose intuitive skill names based on shared task properties. Labels were selected via majority agreement and manual refinement.

Appendix H
Emergent Generalizations in Factor Structure

Our FA reveals five structural regularities that clarify how latent skills organize and relate to model behavior:

H.1
Task Output Format Influence Factor Membership.

For most factors, salient tasks (
|\lambda|\geq 0.50
) cluster by output format: Classification tasks (e.g., GLUE, BBQ, BLiMP) load on Factors 1, 2, and 6, while generation tasks (e.g., SQuAD, XSum, Math) cluster in Factors 3, 4, and 7. Factors 5 and 8 mix formats, suggesting that content and difficulty sometimes override format.

H.2
Non-Saturated Tasks Shape the Factor Space.

Tasks on which models show wide performance gaps—that is, those with large score variance—contribute most to factor formation.
Tasks with wide model score variance shape factor structure most strongly. Easy, saturated tasks (e.g., SST-2, MNLI) compress into general Factor 1, while harder tasks (e.g., MedQA, LegalBench) form distinct axes.
Model diversity—across size, architecture, and training—is crucial to reveal them.

H.3
Task Difficulty Overrides Domain.

Task complexity—not domain—often dictates factor placement. Simple domain-specific tasks (e.g., Legal
-
Support, GSM8K) fall under general NLU (Factor 1), while harder variants (e.g., LegalBench, SQuALity, BoolQ) define specialized dimensions (Factors 5 and 7). Behavioral demands, not surface labels, drive separation.

H.4
Instruction-Following Forms a Distinct Skill Axis.

Factor 4 captures models’ ability to follow detailed instructions, with high loadings from
TruthfulQA
and
SQuALity
. Models like
Flan-T5
and
OpenChat-3.5
excel here, reflecting benefits from alignment tuning.

H.5
Token-Level Precision Forms a Cross-Domain Factor.

Factor 7 captures tasks where small errors break performance—e.g.,
Math
,
SQuality
—regardless of domain. These tasks demand token-level
exactness
in reasoning or generation. Top models (e.g.,
command-r
,
bloomz-3b
) are all instruction-tuned, linking this token-level fidelity skill to alignment.

Together
, Factors 4 and 7 reveal distinct outcomes of instruction tuning: it builds a dedicated instruction-following skill (Factor 4) and separately boosts token-level precision on exacting tasks (Factor 7). These aligned skills emerge beyond what traditional NLU datasets capture.

J.2
Naming MTEB Skills

Based on the Figure
8
the different skills are:

1.

Factual Knowledge & Information Retrieval: Dominated by tasks involving factual verification (e.g., FEVER), question answering (HotpotQA) and document retrieval (MSMROC). Represents the ability to retrieve, verify, and reason over factual or domain-specific knowledge.

2.

Sentiment & Intent Understanding: High loadings on tasks requiring nuanced interpretation of emotion detection (e.g., EmotionClassification), sentiment analysis (TonicConversionClassification), and intent recognition (MassiveIntentClassification).

3.

Semantic similarity and detection: based on STS12-STS16, STSBenchmark, SICK-R data sets it reflects understanding of textual equivalence, paraphrasing, and similarity scoring.

4.

Clustering & Topic Classification: Strong association with clustering tasks (e.g., topic modeling, semantic grouping). Reflects a model’s ability to identify latent thematic structures in unstructured data based on ArxivClusteringP2, MedrxivClusteringP2 etc.

5.

Summarization & Coherence Evaluation: based on summeval with secondary loadings in tasks requiring coherence assessment.

6.

Residual Skills: Most tasks load weakly on Factor 6 (e.g., SummEval: 0.075, Banking7Classification: -0.08, STS22: -0.17), with no clear dominant theme. This suggests it explains minimal shared variance compared to other factors. Some tasks (e.g., STS22, SystemDuplexekQuestions) show small negative correlations with Factor 6. This could indicate minor trade-offs or inverse relationships (e.g., tasks requiring specialized skills vs. general ones), but the lack of strong loadings makes these patterns hard to interpret meaningfully.

In FA, after extracting major factors, later factors often account for residual covariance or noise. Factor 6 might reflect idiosyncratic variance unique to specific tasks (e.g., SummEval’s focus on summarization evaluation) that doesn’t align with broader skills.

To validate its significance, we compared the eigenvalue of Factor 6 to those derived from random data. Although it does not fall below the random threshold—indicating that it explains more variance than expected by chance—its weak loadings and lack of thematic coherence continue to pose interpretative challenges.

J.6
Robustness of Latent Skills

As before, we analyze the stability of the factor structure under two perturbations: varying the number of factors and removing individual models. In both cases, principal angle analysis quantifies subspace alignment, with lower angles indicating greater stability.

Varying Number of Factors

For MTEB, where we use 6-factor solution, we compare models with 3, 6, and 10 extracted factors. As shown in the 3-factor model captures a coarse but stable approximation of the 6-factor solution, with moderate angular deviations indicating partial alignment. In contrast, increasing to 10 factors retains strong alignment for the core structure (angles
<5^{\circ}
for most shared factors) but introduces additional dimensions with high angular drift (>
70^{\circ}
), suggesting overfitting or modeling of noise. These results demonstrate that the six-factor solution captures a stable and interpretable core while avoiding the instability introduced by unnecessary complexity.

Robustness to Model Subset Variation

We test the sensitivity of the latent space by performing 5-fold cross-validation (splitting the models into training (80%) and test (20%) subsets). For each fold, we independently recomputed the PAF model on both subsets and quantified the alignment between factor spaces using principal angle analysis.

Low angles along the diagonal indicate strong agreement between factors learned from different model subsets. The first three factors exhibit high stability, while Factors 5 and 6—show high angular deviation (
>70^{\circ}
), suggesting they capture more task-specific or fragile structure.

Together, these analyses provide strong evidence that the skills identified by the PAF model represent stable and generalizable LLM abilities.

Robustness Across Models

To evaluate whether new models fall within the representational scope of the learned skill space, we test each model for outlier behavior. We computed the Mahalanobis distance
(
Mahalanobis 1936
)
between the model’s performance vector
\bm{\phi}
and the distribution of existing models.
In 5-fold MTEB validation, no model exceeded this threshold, suggesting that the existing factor space robustly accommodates current architectures.
