Symplectic coherence: a measure of position-momentum correlations in quantum states
Varun Upreti, Ulysse Chabaud

Abstract
The interdependence of position and momentum, as highlighted by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, is a cornerstone of quantum physics. Yet, position-momentum correlations have received little systematic attention. Motivated by recent developments in bosonic quantum physics that underscore their relevance in quantum thermodynamics, metrology, and computing, we establish a general framework to study and quantify position-momentum correlations in quantum states. We introduce symplectic coherence , a faithful and easily computable measure defined as the Frobenius norm of the block of the covariance matrix encoding position-momentum correlations, and demonstrate that symplectic coherence is monotone under relevant operations and robust under small perturbations. Furthermore, using a recent mapping by Barthe et al. Barthe et al. 2025 which relates the covariance matrix of a bosonic state to the density matrix of a finite-dimensional system, we show that position-momentum correlations correspond to beyond-classical correlations in a virtual finite-dimensional quantum state, with symplectic coherence mapping naturally to geometric quantum discord. Taking energy constraints into account, we determine the maximal position-momentum correlations achievable at fixed energy, revealing structural insights about the corresponding optimal states. Finally, we illustrate the operational relevance of symplectic coherence through several examples in quantum information tasks and quantum thermodynamics. In the process, we establish new technical results on matrix norms and quantum covariance matrices, and demonstrate the conceptual significance of viewing covariance matrices as density matrices of virtual quantum states.



I
Introduction

Despite its century-long history and a recent surge of interest driven by computational applications, quantum physics continues to pose profound conceptual challenges. A central question concerns the features that fundamentally distinguish quantum systems from classical ones. Within the framework of quantum information theory, these distinguishing features—commonly referred to as quantum resources
Howard and Campbell 2017
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
;
Albarelli et al. 2018
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
;
Amaral 2019
;
Thomas et al. 2024
—are what enable the long-sought quantum advantage
Shor 1994
;
Aaronson and Arkhipov 2013
;
Rønnow et al. 2014
;
Harrow and Montanaro 2017
;
Boixo et al. 2018
in computation, communication, metrology, and simulation. From a foundational physics perspective, elucidating these features is equally crucial for advancing our understanding of the fundamental principles that govern our world.

The Heisenberg uncertainty principle
Heisenberg 1927
lies at the foundation of quantum mechanics, imposing a bound on the product of position and momentum variances, and thus capturing the inherent trade-off in how “spread out” a wavefunction can be along these two conjugate variables. As such, it highlights the interdependence of position and momentum of a quantum system.

Position–momentum correlations also play a significant role in the physics of bosonic quantum systems. For instance, in
Serafini and Adesso 2007
, the authors gave numerical evidence that micro-canonical ensembles of quantum states with non-zero position–momentum correlations are systematically more entangled than those with no position–momentum correlations. Recent theoretical and experimental work in bosonic systems has also highlighted the importance of such correlations: they can serve as a potent resource in quantum metrology, leading to precision surpassing what is achievable with uncorrelated Gaussian probes
Porto et al. 2024
; moreover, these correlations markedly influence the open-system dynamics of bosonic quantum states, affecting decoherence rates and the purity of these states
da Silva et al. 2024
.

From a computational perspective, bosonic systems have recently gained attention as promising platforms for quantum computing, supported by experimental advances such as the deterministic generation of large-scale entangled states
Yokoyama et al. 2013
, robust quantum error correction schemes
Sivak et al. 2023
, and the potential of these bosonic quantum computers to possibly outperform discrete-variable quantum computing architectures
Chabaud et al. 2024
;
Brenner et al. 2024
;
Upreti and Chabaud 2025a
. Recent findings also indicate that universal bosonic computations are classically simulable when the quantum gates involved do not produce position–momentum correlations, regardless of the circuit’s other physical features
Upreti and Chabaud 2025b
.

In the context of quantum information processing, the uncertainty principle alone does not provide a complete picture. While it reveals the interdependence between position and momentum in a quantum system, it leaves open the question of “how much” position–momentum correlations are present in the system, and the quantification of this correlation between conjugate observables—specifically, position
q
and momentum
p
—has received relatively little systematic attention.

In light of these developments, this work is the first attempt, to the best of our knowledge, to quantify position-momentum correlations in a quantum state. While for a certain class of bosonic quantum states (Gaussian states), the covariance matrix fully captures position–momentum correlations, the characterization of some other bosonic quantum states (non-Gaussian states) also require higher-order position-momentum correlations. Here we focus on correlations described by the covariance matrix, as these are the easiest to measure in experiments. More concretely, we ask the question: Looking at the covariance matrix of a bosonic quantum system, how to rigorously quantify its position-momentum correlations?

To answer this question, we provide a new measure termed
symplectic coherence
, which possesses a clear operational interpretation in terms of distance to the set of states with no position-momentum correlations. Moreover, using a recent mapping introduced in
Barthe et al. 2025
, which allows one to map a covariance matrix to the density matrix of a (virtual) finite-dimensional quantum state, we show that position-momentum correlations in a bosonic quantum state can be understood as a form of beyond-classical correlations known as quantum discord
Ollivier and Zurek 2001
in this virtual state. Through this mapping, symplectic coherence is found to naturally correspond to geometric quantum discord
Dakić et al. 2010
.

Further, we perform a resource-theoretic treatment of position-momentum correlations, establishing the class of free states, free and non-free operations, and proving the faithfulness of symplectic coherence, and its monotonicity under free operations.
Taking into account the experimental constraint of finite available energy, we determine the maximum symplectic coherence that a quantum state can achieve under a fixed energy budget. This reveals that, to maximise position–momentum correlations, all available energy should be concentrated into a single mode, with the remaining modes in the vacuum state, followed by appropriate passive linear unitaries. As an application of this result, we further analyse the robustness of symplectic coherence, demonstrating its stability under small perturbations.

We conclude by demonstrating how symplectic coherence provides a measure for assessing diverse quantum information processing tasks and physical phenomena: it enhances the quantum Fisher information in displacement amplitude estimation, governs efficiency in channel discrimination protocols, and influences the behavior of entanglement within thermodynamic ensembles. These examples collectively highlight the role of position–momentum correlations, as captured by symplectic coherence, in various facets of quantum physics.

II.2
Quantum discord

For a bipartite quantum state
\varrho_{AB}
, quantum discord
Ollivier and Zurek 2001
is a measure of the non-classical correlations between the subsystems
A
and
B
, and is defined as

D(\varrho_{AB})_{\Pi_{A}}=\min_{\Pi_{A}}(\mathcal{I}(B:A)-\mathcal{J}(B:A)_{\{\Pi_{A}\}}),

(26)

where
\mathcal{I}(B:A)
is the mutual information between subsystems
A
and
B
, whereas
\mathcal{J}(B:A)_{\{\Pi_{A}\}}
can be informally defined as a measure of information gained about
B
by making measurements on
A
using the set of POVMs
\{\Pi_{A}\}
. In classical information theory, the notions of
\mathcal{I}(B:A)
and
\mathcal{J}(B:A)_{\{\Pi_{A}\}}
are equivalent, but this is not the case in quantum information theory, with even some non-entangled states showing non-zero discord
Ollivier and Zurek 2001
. Quantum discord has since found various applications

Streltsov 2015
.

The evaluation of quantum discord from Eq.
26
is highly non-trivial except for the simplest cases. This has led to the introduction of geometric quantum discord, defined as
Roga et al. 2016

D_{G}(\varrho_{AB})=\min_{\varsigma_{AB}\in\mathcal{CQ}}d^{2}(\varrho_{AB},\varsigma_{AB}),

(27)

where
\mathcal{CQ}
is the set of “classical-quantum” states (states with zero quantum discord with respect to measurements in the subsystem
A
), and
d
is a distance measure. The common distance measures used are the Hilbert–Schmidt distance, the trace distance and the Hellinger distance.

Note that, while the standard definition of quantum discord includes minimization over all possible measurement bases on
A
, we use hereafter a restricted version of quantum discord where the measurement basis is fixed to be in the computational basis. In appendix
A
, we formally define this restricted version of quantum discord, and derive some additional properties of geometric quantum discord for this definition.

II.4
Quantum Resource Theories

Quantum resources are physical properties that govern the performance of specific quantum information tasks and are thus regarded as valuable within these contexts. Quantum resource theories
Howard and Campbell 2017
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
;
Albarelli et al. 2018
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
;
Amaral 2019
;
Thomas et al. 2024
offer a rigorous mathematical framework for the characterization, quantification, and manipulation of these resourceful properties.

A central idea in quantum resource theories is the separation of quantum states into two categories:
free states
, which lack the physical feature (resource) of interest, and
resourceful states
, which possess it. When defining a resource measure—a non-negative function quantifying this property—an important requirement is that the measure should be zero if and only if the state is free. This property is known as the
faithfulness
of the measure. Another desirable property is (sub-)additivity under tensor product
Chitambar and Gour 2019
;
Albarelli et al. 2018
.

In the study of resource manipulation, quantum operations are also classified as
free operations
and
resourceful operations
. Free operations are those that do not increase the resource content of a quantum state, mapping free states to free states, while resourceful operations may potentially enhance it. In this context,
monotonicity
under free operations is another important criterion for a resource measure: it states that the measure’s value should not increase when free operations are applied to a quantum state.

With the necessary preliminaries detailed, we introduce a measure of position-momentum correlations in the next section.

III
Measure of position-momentum correlations

To define a measure of position-momentum correlations, we first define the set of free states, denoted by
\mathcal{C}
:

Definition 1

(Free states for position-momentum correlations)
.

The set of free states with respect to position-momentum correlations,
\mathcal{C}
is defined as

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\{\sigma:V_{xp}^{\sigma}=0\}.

(38)

This set consists in the states without position-momentum correlations as captured by their covariance matrix.

III.1
Symplectic coherence

Given the covariance matrix of a quantum state
\rho
,

V^{\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{\rho}&&V_{xp}^{\rho}\\
(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{T}&&V_{p}^{\rho}\end{bmatrix},

(39)

since the position-momentum correlations are encoded in
V_{xp}^{\rho}
, the measure of position-momentum correlations should be a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
of
V_{xp}^{\rho}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is the set of real
m\times m
matrices. Further
f
should be faithful, that is,

f(A)=0\text{ if and only if }A=\bm{0}_{m},

(40)

where
\bm{0}_{m}
is the
m\times m
zero matrix. One natural way to construct faithful measures of correlation is via matrix norms (see Section
II
). Among these, the Frobenius norm stands out for its simplicity: it is simple to compute (Eq.
31
), since it only requires element‐wise squaring and summation, whereas other norms would require singular value decomposition routines to find the singular values. Its simple definition also makes derivations of its properties more straightforward. Finally, since one can directly measure the entries of a covariance matrix in the lab,
||V_{xp}||_{F}
is an immediately computable quantity.

Motivated by these advantages, we define a measure of position–momentum correlations—termed
symplectic coherence
—by the squared Frobenius norm of
V_{xp}^{\rho}
:

Definition 2

(Symplectic coherence)
.

Given a quantum state
\rho
with covariance matrix

V^{\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{\rho}&&V_{xp}^{\rho}\\
(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{T}&&V_{p}^{\rho}\end{bmatrix},

(41)

the symplectic coherence of
\rho
, denoted by
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}
, is defined as:

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}\coloneqq||V_{xp}^{\rho}||_{F}^{2}.

(42)

Note that symplectic coherence of quantum states defined here is the different from the notion of symplectic coherence of quantum gates introduced in
Upreti and Chabaud 2025b
. Whereas symplectic coherence of quantum gates is defined as their ability to mix position and momentum quadratures, symplectic coherence of quantum states, defined in Eq.
42
is a quantifier of the amount of position-momentum correlations in them.

Moreover, although we focus on the Frobenius norm, standard inequalities between matrix norms (Section
II.3
) and properties followed by the commonly studied matrix norms in the literature (unitary invariance and triangle inequality) allow us to make statements about alternative matrix norm choices.

III.2
Operational interpretation of symplectic coherence

The following result gives an operation interpretation of symplectic coherence:

Theorem 1

(Operational interpretation of symplectic coherence)
.

Given a quantum state
\rho
with covariance matrix

V^{\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}&&V_{xp}\\
V_{xp}^{T}&&V_{p}\end{bmatrix},

(43)

its symplectic coherence is equal to the minimal Hilbert–Schmidt distance from the set of states with the set of free states
\mathcal{C}
, namely

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{2}||V^{\rho}-V^{\sigma}||_{F}^{2}=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{2}\Tr[(V^{\rho}-V^{\sigma})^{2}].

(44)

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in appendix
B.1
and consists of two steps: first, we find
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
whose covariance matrix is closest in Hilbert–Schmidt distance to
V^{\rho}
and then we calculate the distance of
V^{\rho}
to the said state to obtain the final expression. In this proof step, we also prove the rather non-trivial result:

Lemma 1
.

Given the covariance matrix of a quantum state

V^{\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{\rho}&&V_{xp}^{\rho}\\
(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{T}&&V_{p}^{\rho}\end{bmatrix},

(45)

the matrix obtained by removing the position-momentum correlations,

\tilde{V}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{\rho}&&0\\
0&&V_{p}^{\rho}\end{bmatrix},

(46)

represents a valid covariance matrix of a quantum state.

III.3
Properties of symplectic coherence

As explained in the preliminaries, desirable properties of a measure of a physical resource are: additivity, faithfulness and monotonicity. In this section, we demonstrate that symplectic coherence satisfies these properties.

From
2
the symplectic coherence is additive under tensor product, since the covariance matrix of a tensor product of quantum states is the direct sum of the covariance matrices of the individual quantum states, i.e.

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho\otimes\sigma}=\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}+\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}.

(47)

Moreover, we obtain the following result:

Theorem 2

(Faithfulness and monotonicity of symplectic coherence)
.

Given a quantum state
\rho
, its symplectic coherence
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=0
if and only if
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
(Definition
1
). Further, the symplectic coherence is non-increasing under the following operations:

•

Block-diagonal orthogonal unitary gates
\hat{O}
.

•

Displacement unitary gate.

•

Tensor product with free states.

•

Partial traces.

•

Classical mixing of zero first moment states.

While the faithfulness of symplectic coherence directly follows from the property of matrix norms, the proof of monotinicity is given in appendix
B.2
, and utilizes properties of covariance matrices and Frobenius norms. It is natural to expect that these operations should be free for any measure of position-momentum correlations, as we will explain in what follows: block-diagonal orthogonal transformations and displacement operations should qualify as free operations because matrix norms are invariant under unitary (and hence orthogonal) transformations. Specifically, block-diagonal orthogonal gates transform
V_{xp}^{\rho}
as
OV_{xp}^{\rho}O^{T}
for some orthogonal matrix
O
, while displacement operations leave
V_{xp}^{\rho}
unchanged. Given that any quantifier of position-momentum correlations is expected to be a matrix norm of
V_{xp}^{\rho}
, it follows directly that block-diagonal orthogonal gates and displacement operations do not alter this quantifier, and hence are indeed free operations.

Since making a tensor product of states does not generate additional position-momentum correlations than the ones already present in the individual states, a measure of position-momentum correlations should simply be additive under tensor product, which is what we obtain with symplectic coherence.

Taking partial traces removes some of the elements of the covariance matrix, so the value of any quantifier of position-momentum correlations should decrease, which is what we observe. Finally, covariance matrix obtained by classical mixing of states with zero first moment of quadratures is simply a linear combination of the covariance matrices. The triangle inequality of matrix norms indicates that gives that no position-momentum correlation quantifier should increase under classical mixing of such zero first moment states.

Since phase shifters can mix position and momenturm quadratures, they can possibly increase position-momentum correlations. In appendix
B.3
, we also give examples of cases where post-selecting on heterodyne measurements, homodyne measurements and classical mixing of states with non-zero first moments can increase the symplectic coherence.

An important class of non-free operations that we would like to highlight here are the block-diagonal active Gaussian unitary gates, whose symplectic matrices are of the form

\begin{bmatrix}A&&0\\
0&&(A^{T})^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

(48)

with
A
being any real, invertible and non-orthogonal
m\times m
matrix. These gates do not mix position-momentum quadratures and hence from the point of view of gate-based symplectic coherence defined in
Upreti and Chabaud 2025b
, they do not possess symplectic coherence. However, they can, in fact, increase the symplectic coherence of a quantum state, as formalized in the following Theorem:

Theorem 3

(Position-momentum correlation monotonicity no-go)
.

There is no faithful measure of position-momentum correlations which is non-increasing under all block-diagonal Gaussian symplectic operations.

The proof of Theorem
3
is given in appendix
B.4
, and follows from looking at how the block-diagonal Gaussians change the covariance matrices, and using properties of matrices. Therefore, even though these gates do not mix position and mometum quadratures, they can in fact increase the amount of position-momentum correlations in the state.

Inspired by the class of free operations defined by Theorem
2
, we define orthogonal Stinespring dilations:

Definition 3

(Orthogonal Stinespring dilation)
.

Given a quantum state
\rho
, an orthogonal Stinespring dilation of
\rho
is defined as

\Tr_{B}[\hat{D}\hat{O}(\rho\otimes\sigma^{(E)})\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{D}^{\dagger}],

(49)

where
\hat{O}
are block-diagonal orthogonal gates,
\hat{D}
is the displacement operator, and
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
.

Examples of orthogonal Stinespring dilation include pure photon loss and thermal loss channels. The following is then a standard resource-theoretic statement
Albarelli et al. 2018
:

Lemma 2

(Impossibility of deterministic conversion with orthogonal Stinespring dilations)
.

Given two quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
with
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}<\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}
it is impossible to deterministically convert
\rho
to
\sigma
through orthogonal Stinespring dilations.

In the following section, we describe a mapping originally proposed in
Barthe et al. 2025
, which represents covariance matrices as density matrices of virtual qubit–qudit systems. We then show how this mapping enables us to establish connections between symplectic coherence and quantum discord.

IV
Symplectic coherence and quantum discord

In this section, we establish a connection between symplectic coherence and quantum discord
Ollivier and Zurek 2001
. We start by connecting the set of free states
\mathcal{C}
with the set of classical-quantum states (with zero quantum discord
Piani et al. 2008
) of a qubit-qudit system with respect to computational basis measurements of the qubit. To do so, we rely on a mapping introduced in
Barthe et al. 2025
, which allows us to identify a covariance matrix of size
2m\times 2m
with the density matrix of a
2m
-dimensional qubit-qudit state.

Definition 4

(Covariance matrix to density matrix mapping
Barthe et al. 2025
)
.

Given a covariance matrix

V=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}&&V_{xp}\\
V_{xp}^{T}&&V_{p}\end{bmatrix},

(50)

we define a mapping
\mathcal{M}:V\mapsto\varrho
which maps
V
onto the density matrix
\varrho
in the
2m
-dimensional qubit-qudit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m}
where the state of the qubit
\ket{0}
or
\ket{1}
denotes the position or momentum quadratures and the state of the qudit corresponds to the mode number (Figure
1
). Including the normalization of the obtained virtual state, this mapping gives

\mathcal{M}\left(V=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}&&V_{xp}\\
V_{xp}^{T}&&V_{p}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\varrho=\frac{1}{\Tr[V]}\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}&&V_{xp}\\
V_{xp}^{T}&&V_{p}\end{bmatrix},

(51)

such that
V_{x}
denotes the block for
\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j}
,
V_{xp}
for
\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j}
, etc., where
\ket{\bm i}
are the qudit computational basis states.

Under this mapping, we obtain the following result:

Theorem 4

(Position-momentum correlations and quantum discord)
.

Under the mapping
\mathcal{M}
given in
4
, a covariance matrix
V^{\rho}
with
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
maps to a classical-quantum virtual qubit-qudit state
\varrho=\mathcal{M}(V^{\rho})
with zero quantum discord with respect to computational basis measurements of the qubit register. Furthermore, the symplectic coherence of
\rho
can be related to geometric quantum discord (
Eq.

27
),with Hilbert-Schmidt distance as the distance measure, with respect to computational basis measurements in the qubit subsystem of
\varrho
as:

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=\frac{\Tr[V^{\rho}]^{2}}{2}D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho)=8\left(\Tr[\rho\hat{E}]-\bm{d}^{T}\bm{d}\right)^{2}D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho).

(52)

The proof of Theorem
4
is given in appendix
B.5
, and consists in proving that the virtual qubit-qudit states obtained from covariance matrices with zero position-momentum correlations through the mapping given in
4
have zero quantum discord with respect to computational basis measurements of the qubit
(
Streltsov et al. 2017
, Section I)
. The connection between symplectic coherence
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}
and geometric quantum discord
D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho)
can further established by expressing
D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho)
in terms of the off-block-diagonal matrix of
\varrho
, and using the mapping
\mathcal{M}
to identify the corresponding block of
V^{\rho}
that maps to this off-block-diagonal part.

It must be emphasized, however, that
4
does not imply that position-momentum correlations are equivalent to quantum discord in CV systems, unlike Gaussian quantum discord
Giorda and Paris 2010
. Indeed, while Gaussian quantum discord aims to quantify the non-classical correlation between subsystems in Gaussian states, we are considering correlations between the position and momentum quadratures of a single quantum system. Theorem
4
instead connects position-momentum correlations in CV quantum states to quantum discord in a virtual (qubit-qudit) state over two subsystems.

Importantly, in defining our set
\mathcal{CQ}
we restrict to classical–quantum states with respect to measurements in the computational basis of the qubit, rather than allowing all possible measurement bases. This deliberate choice ensures a one‐to‐one correspondence between non-zero off‐diagonal entries
\varrho_{01}
(which map to
V_{xp}^{\rho}
under
\mathcal{M}
) and non-zero quantum discord. Concretely, if
\varrho_{01}\neq 0
, then position–momentum correlations are present and our restricted discord measure will detect them.

This choice also explains why symplectic coherence avoids the problem one encounters with geometric quantum discord with Hilbert–Schmidt distance as the distance measure.
Indeed, as pointed out in
Piani 2012
, geometric quantum discord with Hilbert–Schmidt distance as the distance measure suffers from logical inconsistencies. Specifically, while the quantum discord of a DV quantum state
\varrho
with respect to measurements in the subsystem of
\varrho
should not change when taking its tensor product with another state
\varsigma
, the authors exhibit in
Piani 2012
an example where the geometric quantum discord with respect to Hilbert–Schmidt distance actually increases. However, Theorem
2
demonstrates that we do not encounter any such logical inconsistencies with symplectic coherence, as symplectic coherence is additive under tensor product.

Finally, we would like to elucidate the differences between symplectic coherence in CV quantum states and quantum discord in virtual qubit-qudit states, as this will be important later when we discuss maximal symplectic coherence in the following section. While the density matrix of a quantum state may have complex entries and has to satisfy two conditions

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle 0,

(53)

the virtual density matrix obtained from the inverse of the mapping
\mathcal{M}
(Definition
4
) has only real matrix elements and has to satisfy an additional condition:

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{T}

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\succ

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\rho+i\Omega/c

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle 0\text{ for some constant }c\geq 2m.

(54)

The last condition comes from the uncertainty principle. This difference manifests itself in the value of maximal symplectic coherence (see
5
), which is different from what one would expect when looking purely from a quantum discord point of view. Another difference is that not all Gaussian unitary gates can be mapped to valid unitary gates acting on the virtual qubit-qudit system, a point that was also mentioned in the original paper defining the mapping
Barthe et al. 2025

V
Maximal symplectic coherence

In this section, we characterise the maximum amount of position-momentum correlations in states of bounded energy. According to Eq.
21
,

\Tr[\rho\hat{E}]=\frac{\Tr[V^{\rho}]+\bm{d}^{T}\bm{d}}{4},

(55)

where
\bm{d}
is the first moment vector given by Eq.
19
. Therefore, sticking to states with zero first moments, i.e. states with
\bm{d}
being a zero vector, fixing the trace of the covariance matrix is equivalent to fixing the average energy of the state. In what follows, we consider the set of states with covariance matrices
V^{\rho}
such that
\Tr[V^{\rho}]=E
, and investigate the maximal symplectic coherence obtainable by such states. Note that for states with non-zero first moments, fixing the trace of
V^{\rho}
still puts an upper bound on the average energy of the system, since
\bm{d}^{T}\bm{d}\geq 0
in Eq.
21
.
We first define a class of pure Gaussian states
\mathcal{S}^{\max}(E)
:

Definition 5
.

A pure Gaussian state
\ket{\psi}_{G}
belongs to the set
\mathcal{S}^{\max}(E)
if and only if it can be written in the form

\ket{\psi}_{G}=\hat{O}_{2}\hat{R}(\vec{\theta})\hat{O}_{1}\ket{r}\otimes\ket{0}^{m-1},

(56)

up to arbitrary displacements at the end, where
\ket{r}
is a squeezed state with squeezing parameter
r
such that

e^{2r}+e^{-2r}=E-2(m-1),

(57)

and where the phase shift angles
\vec{\theta}
and the block-diagonal orthogonal unitary gate
\hat{O}_{1}
with the associated symplectic matrix

\begin{bmatrix}O&&0\\
0&&O\end{bmatrix}

(58)

with orthogonal matrix
O
are defined such that for all
j=1\dots m
,

2|A_{1j}|=\delta_{1j},

(59)

for
A\in\{O^{T}CO,O^{T}SO,O^{T}(CS)O\}
, where
C\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(\cos\theta_{1},\dots,\cos\theta_{m})
and
S\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(\sin\theta_{1},\dots,\sin\theta_{m})
. The block-diagonal orthogonal unitary gate
\hat{O}_{2}
can be arbitrary.

We then have the following result for maximal symplectic coherence:

Theorem 5

(Maximal symplectic coherence of quantum states)
.

Given an
m
-mode quantum state
\rho
with covariance matrix
V^{\rho}
such that
\Tr[V^{\rho}]=E
, the symplectic coherence of
\rho
is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(E-2m)^{2}}{4}+(E-2m)

(60)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{max}}(E,m).

Moreover, the set of pure Gaussian states of maximal symplectic coherence is given by
\mathcal{S}^{\max}(E)
(Definition
5
), while the covariance matrix of set of all states with maximal symplectic coherence is contained in the convex hull of covariance matrices of states in
\mathcal{S}^{\max}(E)
.

The proof of Theorem
5
is given in appendix
B.6
, and consists of two steps: firstly, we prove that the maximal symplectic coherence of pure Gaussian states is given by
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{max}}(E,m)
and find the structure of maximally symplectic coherent pure Gaussian states. This step of the proof is rather technical and relies on mathematical results regarding matrix decompositions and matrix norms. Secondly, we prove that the maximal symplectic coherence of mixed state is less than equal to
\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{max}}(E,m)
and find the conditions on the the covariance matrix of such maximally symplectic coherent mixed states, by using Jensen’s inequality and a result from
(
Parthasarathy 2013
, Theorem 3)
stating that any mixed Gaussian state can decomposed as an equally weighted mixture of two pure Gaussian states.

Note that the maximal value achievable by the geometric quantum discord
D_{G}
of a qubit-qudit density matrix with respect to computational basis measurements of the qubit is equal to
1/2
(see Appendix
A
). With Eq.
52
we have

D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho)=\frac{2}{E^{2}}\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}\leq\frac{1}{2},

(61)

leading to
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}\leq E^{2}/4
. Theorem
5
proves that this is not a tight bound and underlines the lack of complete equivalence between symplectic coherence and geometric quantum discord: not all qubit-qudit density matrices are valid covariance matrices, and in particular those with maximal geometric discord are not.

The structure of maximally
5
not only allows one to engineer states of maximal symplectic coherence in the lab, such as

\ket{MSC}=\hat{R}_{1}(\pi/4)\ket{r}\otimes\ket{0}^{m-1},

(62)

with
r
satisfying Eq.
57
, it also gives an insight on the structure of the states maximizing position-momentum correlations. The composition of block‑diagonal orthogonal unitary gate
\hat{O}_{1}
with phase‑shift angles behaves as expected: the phase shifters, after conjugation with the block-diagonal unitary gate
\hat{O}_{1}
superposes the state’s information–encoded in the first mode, where all the energy resides–equally in the position and momentum quadrature. What appears to be novel, however, is the realization that one can only achieve maximal symplectic coherence by first concentrating all the energy into the first mode (with the remaining modes in the vacuum state) and then applying an appropriate passive linear unitary. To our knowledge, no previous work has explicitly identified this construction.

Note that when using other matrix norms in the definition of the measure of position-momentum correlations, the inequalities given in Eq.
34
and Eq.
37
allow us to bound their maximal values as well. However, whether the bound is tight and whether the set of states classified by
\mathcal{S}^{\max}
also maximizes these other measures remains an open question.

Finally, a remarkable consequence of
5
is the robustness of symplectic coherence under small perturbations:

Theorem 6

(Symplectic coherence under perturbations)
.

Given two quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
with symplectic coherences
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}
such that
\Tr[\hat{E}^{2}\rho],\Tr[\hat{E}^{2}\sigma]\leq E^{2}
, and
||\rho-\sigma||_{1}\leq\epsilon/m
, then

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}-\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(E^{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}).

(63)

The proof of Theorem
6
is given in appendix
B.7
together with an explicit bound, and follows from bounding the Hilbert–Schmidt distance between two covariance matrices in terms of their trace distance, combining results from
(
Mele et al. 2025
, Theorem 62)
with standard matrix norm inequalities given in
II.3
.

Theorem
6
implies that symplectic coherence is robust under small perturbations in the quantum state.
Additionally, we show that under the action of a photon loss channel with parameter
\eta
, the covariance matrix of a quantum state
V^{\rho}
changes as (Appendix
B.8
)

V^{\rho}\rightarrow\eta V^{\rho}+(1-\eta)\I_{2m},

(64)

and therefore the symplectic coherence of a quantum state
\rho
under that photon loss channel evolves as

\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}=\eta^{2}\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}.

(65)

VI
Symplectic coherence in quantum information processing

Having developed a framework for quantifying position-momentum correlations, we now consider a variety of quantum information processing tasks where position-momentum correlations, as quantified by symplectic coherence, plays a role. Note that we consider specific examples in which position-momentum correlations are expected to be resourceful, and demonstrate that symplectic coherence appropriately captures these correlations. Once again, we restrict to quantum states with zero first moments for brevity—analogous results may be obtained when including non-zero first moments.

VI.1
Symplectic coherence and entanglement

We first highlight a result that was numerically observed by the authors in
(
Serafini and Adesso 2007
, Figure 2)
, i.e. that, while looking at micro-canonical ensembles of pure Gaussian states
(
Serafini et al. 2007
, Section 3.1)
, states with zero position-momentum correlations are typically less entangled that states with non-zero position-momentum correlations, at fixed energy. We formalise this observation by the following Theorem (Informal version of Theorem
16
):

Theorem 7

(Position-momentum correlations enhance entanglement, informal version of Theorem
16
)
.

At fixed energy, the entanglement of pure Gaussian states, as measured by the symplectic eigenvalue squared of the reduced covariance matrix of the first mode, is higher on average for pure Gaussian states with non-zero position-momentum correlations.

A formal version along with the detailed proof of Theorem
7
is given in appendix
B.9
, and relies on proving that on average, the symplectic eigenvalue squared of the reduced covariance matrix of the first mode, is higher for pure Gaussian states with non-zero symplectic coherence using properties of Haar-random unitary and orthogonal matrices.

Since the covariance matrix of a pure Gaussian state is given by (Eq.
25
):

V^{\rho}=S_{U}\begin{bmatrix}Z^{2}&&0\\
0&&Z^{-2}\end{bmatrix}S_{U}^{T},

(66)

fixing the energy of pure Gaussian states is equivalent to fixing
\Tr[V^{\rho}]
through Eq.
21
(since we are restricting our study to zero first moment states) or equivalently fixing
Z^{2}
(
Z^{-2}
), since
S_{U}
is orthogonal. Then, we are Haar-randomly sampling the
2m\times 2m
symplectic orthogonal matrix
S_{U}
(isomorphic to an
m\times m
unitary), as it was done in
Serafini and Adesso 2007
and taking the average of symplectic eigenvalue squared of the reduced covariance matrix of the first mode over these samples. Note that while in
(
Serafini and Adesso 2007
, Figure 2)
, the entanglement is quantified by the entanglement entropy
h
, the symplectic eigenvalue squared of the reduced covariance matrix of the first mode
\nu^{2}
is also a valid measure of entanglement for pure Gaussian states
(
Serafini et al. 2007
, Section 5)
, with the two being related by

h=\frac{(\nu+1)}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)-\frac{(\nu-1)}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\nu-1}{2}\right).

(67)

This result may seem counterintuitive, since at first sight the (squared) symplectic eigenvalue of the reduced covariance matrix

\nu^{2}=(V_{x})_{1}(V_{p})_{1}-(V_{xp})_{1}^{2},

(68)

should decrease for non-zero
(V_{xp})_{1}
(non-zero symplectic coherence), where
(V_{x})_{1},(V_{p})_{1}
and
(V_{xp})_{1}
are elements of the reduced covariance matrix of the first mode. However, the effect of gates generating non-zero symplectic coherence in pure Gaussian states is such that they also focus the energy on the diagonal elements
(V_{x})_{1}(V_{p})_{1}
in such a way that on average, the symplectic eigenvalue squared of these states, and as a consequence the entanglement, increases.

Now, we discuss the role of symplectic coherence in two quantum information processing tasks: quantum metrology and quantum channel discrimination.

VI.2
Symplectic coherence and quantum metrology

Quantum metrology aims to utilize quantum states to probe quantum systems and measure physical parameters with a precision beyond the limits of classical measurement techniques, and is one of the central research themes in quantum information
Giovannetti et al. 2011
. The ultimate precision of estimation of a given parameter
r
with an input probe quantum state is given by the single-shot quantum Cramér–Rao bound:

\Delta r^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}_{Q}},

(69)

where
\Delta r
is the variance of
r
, which is distributed according to some probability distribution that depends on the estimation process, and
\mathcal{F}_{Q}
is the quantum Fisher information—the higher the quantum Fisher information, the higher the ultimate precision of estimation.

Here, we discuss the quantum metrology task of single-mode displacement amplitude single-shot estimation: Consider a scenario where a quantum system is acted upon by the displacement operator

\hat{D}(r)=\exp\left(ir\left(\hat{q}/\sqrt{2}+\hat{p}/\sqrt{2}\right)\right),

(70)

and we wish to probe the amplitude parameter
r
(Figure
2
). We show the following result:

Figure 2:
(Top) We are considering the metrological scenario where the input probe state
\rho
goes through a physical medium implementing a displacment operator
\hat{D}(r)
and we wish to estimate
r
by making measurements at the output. (Bottom) We consider a channel discimination scenario where using an input probe Gaussian state
\rho_{G}
and making appropriate measurements at the output, we want to determine which of the give two orthgonal Stinespring dilation (Definition
3
)
\mathcal{O}_{1}
or
\mathcal{O}_{2}
is the black box acting on the input state implementing.

Theorem 8

(Position-momentum correlations enhance metrology)
.

Given a pure single-mode input probe state
\rho
with covariance matrix
V^{\rho}
and zero first moments going through a single-mode displacement operator
\hat{D}(r)
, the Fisher quantum information with respect to
r
is given by

\mathcal{F}_{Q}\leq 2E+4V_{xp}^{\rho}=2E+4\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}},

(71)

where
E=V_{x}^{\rho}+V_{p}^{\rho}=4\Tr[\rho\hat{E}]
. Further, the inequality becomes an equality for pure input probe states.

The proof of Theorem
8
is given in appendix
C.1
and is based on the definition of Fisher quantum information of quantum states in terms of the generating operator
\hat{H}
of the parameter
r

(
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
, Eqs. 60,61)
(here,
\hat{H}=\hat{q}/\sqrt{2}+\hat{p}/\sqrt{2}
).

Therefore, for states with fixed energy
E
, increasing the symplectic coherence
V_{xp}^{\rho}
of the input probe state always increases the quantum Fisher information and as a consequence, the ultimate precision of estimation. Note that
V_{xp}^{\rho}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}}
holds only as long as
V_{xp}^{\rho}\geq 0
. This is always possible without loss of generality, since given a single-mode quantum state
\rho
with covariance matrix

V^{\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{\rho}&&V_{xp}^{\rho}\\
V_{xp}^{\rho}&&V_{p}^{\rho}\end{bmatrix},

(72)

with
V_{xp}^{\rho}<0
, we can always apply the Fourier gate
\hat{F}=e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}
to
\rho
to obtain

V^{\hat{F}\rho\hat{F}^{\dagger}}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{p}^{\rho}&&-V_{xp}^{\rho}\\
-V_{xp}^{\rho}&&V_{x}^{\rho}\end{bmatrix},

(73)

whose off-diagonal elements are now positive, without changing the symplectic coherence.

Note that this discussion also reveals that Fourier gate is another example of a gate which mixes position and momentum quadratures and thus has gate-based symplectic coherence
Upreti and Chabaud 2025b
, but does not change the symplectic coherence of a quantum state.

VI.3
Symplectic coherence and quantum channel discrimination

The task of quantum channel discrimination—distinguishing between a given set of quantum channels—is a fundamental problem in quantum information theory
Childs et al. 2000
;
Weedbrook et al. 2012
, with wide-ranging applications such as quantum reading
Pirandola 2011
and quantum state detection via quantum illumination
Lloyd 2008
;
Tan et al. 2008
.

To illustrate the use of symplectic coherence in quantum channel discrimination, we consider a scenario where the aim is to find out which of two orthogonal Stinespring dilations (Definition
3
) is being implemented on an input probe Gaussian state with zero first moments by making appropriate measurements at the output (Figure
2
). We are provided either single (single-shot discrimination) or multiple (multi-shot discrimination) copies of the probe state.

In this section, we focus on zero-mean Gaussian probe states to isolate the contribution of symplectic coherence to channel distinguishability. Non-zero displacements add linear terms but do not affect symplectic coherence, as displacement operations are free. Including them is simple but would obscure the clear link to the discrimination bounds, so we restrict to the mean-zero case for clarity.

VI.3.1
Single-shot discrimination

In this section, we take one of the two channels to be a pure photon loss channel with parameter
\eta
and the other channel to be any orthogonal Stinespring dilation channel (Definition
3
). An important example of this channel discrimination task would be a scenario in which we want to discern whether quantum states are passing through a given physical medium with or without experiencing photon loss. For such a channel discrimination task, we have the following result:

Lemma 3

(Symplectic coherence lower bounds optimal channel discrimination success probability)
.

The optimal success probability of successful channel discrimination between a multi-mode photon loss channel
\Lambda_{\eta}
and an arbitrary orthogonal Stinespring dilation
\mathcal{O}_{0}
for a given input probe state
\rho
is lower bounded in terms of its symplectic coherence
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}
:

p_{\mathrm{opt}}\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{400}\min\left(1,\frac{\sqrt{\frac{(1-\eta)^{2}(E-2m)^{2}}{2m}+2\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}(1-\eta)^{2}}}{E+1}\right),

(74)

where
E/4=\Tr[\rho\hat{E}]
.

The proof of Lemma
3
is given in
C.2
, and combines the Helstrom bound
Helstrom 1969
with a technical result which lower bounds the trace distance between two quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
in terms of the difference between their symplectic coherence:

Lemma 4

(Symplectic coherence lower bounds trace distance between two states)
.

Let
\rho
and
\sigma
be
m
-mode states with zero first moments, covariance matrices
V^{\rho},V^{\sigma}
with
\Tr[V^{\rho}]=4\Tr[\rho\hat{E}]=E_{1}
,

and

\rho

\frac{1}{2}||\rho-\sigma||_{1}\geq\!\frac{1}{200}\min\!\!\left(\!1,\frac{\sqrt{\frac{(E_{1}-E_{2})^{2}}{2m}+2(\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}}-\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}})^{2}}}{E+1}\right)\!.

(75)

If
\rho
and
\sigma
are general (Gaussian or non-Gaussian) states, we have

\frac{1}{2}||\rho-\sigma||_{1}\geq\frac{\frac{(E_{1}-E_{2})^{2}}{2m}+2(\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}}-\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}})^{2}}{3200\tilde{E}^{2}m},

(76)

where
\tilde{E}^{2}
is such that
\Tr[\hat{E^{2}}\rho]=\Tr[\hat{E^{2}}\sigma]\leq\tilde{E}^{2}
.

The proof of Lemma
4
is given in appendix

, and combines results from
(

and
(

with a bound on the Hilbert–Schmidt distance between two covariance matrices in terms of their trace and their symplectic coherence.

This result shows that higher symplectic coherence guarantees higher single-shot discrimination success between a photon loss channel (parameterized by
\eta

Multi-shot discrimination

In this section, we consider a different scenario in which we want to discriminate between two block-diagonal orthogonal Stinespring dilations
\mathcal{O}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}

Lemma 5

(Symplectic coherence underlines the efficiency of multi-shot discrimination protocols)

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have provided a rigorous framework for assessing the amount of position-momentum correlations in quantum states. To do this, we have introduced a geometric measure of position-momentum correlations, which we call
symplectic coherence
, that can be defined informally as a quantifier of how much “quantumness” of the state is encoded in its position-momentum correlations, in the sense of geometric quantum discord.

As a measure of position-momentum correlations, symplectic coherence is additive under tensor product, faithful, monotone under a large class of quantum operations, and robust under small perturbations. The search for maximal symplectic coherence reveals an important fact: to obtain states with the highest position-momentum correlations (as measured by symplectic coherence) at a fixed energy, one must start with all the energy concentrated in a single mode. Only then can the appropriate passive linear unitary transformations produce the states with maximal position-momentum correlations. To our knowledge, no prior results indicate that such a state should have this particular structure. Finally, we demonstrate the role of symplectic coherence in various quantum thermodynamic phenomena and quantum information tasks, highlighting the practical significance of our framework in addition to its conceptual relevance.

In deriving the different results of the paper, we also obtained several technical results about covariance matrices of quantum states and matrix norms, which may be of independent interest. In summary, this work establishes symplectic coherence as a rigorous and operationally meaningful quantifier of position-momentum correlations, highlighting their fundamental importance in quantum physics and establishing the framework for their use as a quantum resource in future applications.

Our work paves the way for several future research directions. An immediate next step is to investigate additional quantum information tasks where symplectic coherence serves as a resource quantifier. Another promising avenue is to explore whether mapping covariance matrices onto virtual quantum-state density matrices can yield further physical insights, just as it demonstrated the link between symplectic coherence and quantum discord. Additionally, developing a physical understanding of why concentrating all energy into a single mode maximizes position–momentum correlations of quantum states is an intriguing problem. Finally, extending the current framework to include higher‑order position–momentum correlations beyond covariance matrices–essential for non‑Gaussian states–is a compelling research direction.

Quantum discord for computational basis measurements

In this paper, we consider quantum discord for bipartite quantum states
\varrho_{AB}
, where:

•

the subsystem
A
is a qubit;

•

instead of minimizing over all possible measurements on

, we restrict the measurement of the qubit register to be in the computational basis.

The expression for quantum discord in this setting is given by (see
Section

II.2
)

D(\varrho_{AB})_{\{\ket{0}\bra{0},\ket{1},\bra{1}\}}=\mathcal{I}(B:A)-\mathcal{J}(B:A)_{\{\ket{0}\bra{0},\ket{1},\bra{1}\}}.

(88)

For this definition of quantum discord, the set of classical-quantum states (states with zero quantum discord
Piani et al. 2008
), denoted by
\mathcal{CQ}
, is given by
(
Ollivier and Zurek 2001
, Proposition 3)

\varsigma\in\mathcal{CQ}\iff\varsigma=\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\varrho_{0}+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\varrho_{1},

(89)

where
\varrho_{0}
and
\varrho_{1}
are positive semi-definite matrices in subsystem
B
(not necessarily trace one).

For this set of classical-quantum states, we define the geometric quantum discord using the Hilbert–Schmidt distance:

D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho_{AB})=\min_{\varsigma\in\mathcal{CQ}}\Tr[(\rho_{AB}-\varsigma)^{2}].

(90)

Then, we obtain the following result:

Lemma 8
.

Given a bipartite state with a qubit subsystem
A
, and a density matrix given by

\varrho_{AB}=\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{0}&&\varrho_{01}\\
\varrho_{01}^{\dagger}&&\varrho_{1}\end{bmatrix},

(91)

where
\varrho_{0},\varrho_{01},\varrho_{01}^{\dagger},\varrho_{1}
are matrices associated to
\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j},\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j},\ket{1}\bra{0}\otimes\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j}
and
\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j}
respectively, where
\ket{\bm i}
represents the orthonormal basis of subsystem
B
, its geometric quantum discord, defined in Eq.
90
, is given by

D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho_{AB})=2||\varrho_{01}||_{F}^{2}.

(92)

Proof.

First, we prove that the state
\varsigma\in\mathcal{CQ}
closest in Hilbert–Schmidt distance to
\varrho_{AB}
is given by

\varsigma=\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{0}&&0\\
0&&\varrho_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(93)

We prove this by contradiction. Suppose the state
\tilde{\varsigma}\in\mathcal{CQ}
closest in Hilbert–Schmidt distance to
\varrho_{AB}
is given by

\tilde{\varsigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\varsigma}_{0}&&0\\
0&&\tilde{\varsigma}_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(94)

This implies

\Tr[(\varrho_{AB}-\varsigma)^{2}]-\Tr[(\varrho_{AB}-\tilde{\varsigma})^{2}]\geq 0.

(95)

Now,

\Tr[\varrho_{AB}-\varsigma)^{2}]=\Tr[\varrho_{AB}^{2}]+\Tr[\varsigma^{2}]-2\Tr[\varrho_{AB}\varsigma]=\Tr[\varrho_{AB}^{2}]+\Tr[\varsigma^{2}]-2\Tr[\varsigma^{2}]=\Tr[\varrho_{AB}^{2}]-\Tr[\varsigma^{2}].

(96)

Since

\Tr[\varrho_{AB}\varsigma]=\Tr[\varrho_{0}^{2}+\varrho_{1}^{2}]=\Tr[\varsigma^{2}].

(97)

Similarly,
since

\Tr[\varrho_{AB}\tilde{\varsigma}]=\Tr[\varrho_{0}\tilde{\varsigma}_{0}+\varrho_{1}\tilde{\varsigma}_{1}]=\Tr[\varsigma\tilde{\varsigma}].

(98)

Therefore

\Tr[(\varrho_{AB}-\tilde{\varsigma})^{2}]=\Tr[\varrho_{AB}^{2}]+\Tr[\tilde{\varsigma}^{2}]-2\Tr[\varsigma\tilde{\varsigma}],

(99)

And finally

\Tr[\varrho_{AB}-\varsigma)^{2}]-\Tr[(\varrho_{AB}-\tilde{\varsigma})^{2}]=-\Tr[\varsigma^{2}]-\Tr[\tilde{\varsigma}^{2}]+2\Tr[\varsigma\tilde{\varsigma}]=-\Tr[(\varsigma-\tilde{\varsigma})^{2}]=-||\varsigma-\tilde{\varsigma}||_{F}^{2},

(100)

so the Eq.
95
gives

||\varsigma-\tilde{\varsigma}||_{F}^{2}\leq 0,

(101)

with an equality if
\tilde{\varsigma}=\varsigma
. Since the Frobenius norm cannot be negative, this shows that the covariance matrix closest in Hilbert–Schmidt distance to
\varrho_{AB}
is
\varsigma
. This gives the geometric quantum discord as

D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho_{AB})=\Tr[(\varrho_{AB}-\varsigma)^{2}]=\Tr[\varrho_{\mathrm{rem}}^{2}],

(102)

where

\varrho_{\mathrm{rem}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}0&&\varrho_{01}\\
\varrho_{01}^{\dagger}&&0\end{bmatrix}.

(103)

Therefore,

D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho_{AB})=\Tr[\varrho_{01}^{\dagger}\varrho_{01}+\varrho_{01}\varrho_{01}^{\dagger}]=2\Tr[\varrho_{01}^{\dagger}\varrho_{01}]=2||\varrho_{01}||_{F}^{2}.

(104)

∎

Our next result is an upper bound on geometric quantum discord:

Lemma 9
.

Given a bipartite state
\varrho_{AB}
with the subsystem
A
being a qubit and the density matrix given by

\varrho_{AB}=\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{0}&&\varrho_{01}\\
\varrho_{01}^{\dagger}&&\varrho_{1}\end{bmatrix},

(105)

its maximal geometric quantum discord is given by

D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho_{AB})=2||\varrho_{01}||_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(106)

Proof.

Since
\varrho_{AB}
is a density matrix,

\varrho_{AB}\succeq 0.

(107)

Therefore, using the property of semi-definite matrices,
\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, the principal submatrix formed by eliminating all rows and columns except the
i
th and the
m+j
th one satisfies

\begin{bmatrix}(\varrho_{0})_{ii}&&(\varrho_{01})_{ij}\\
(\varrho_{01})_{ij}^{*}&&(\varrho_{11})_{jj}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

(108)

This implies

\displaystyle|\varrho_{01}|_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\varrho_{0})_{ii}(\varrho_{11})_{jj},

(109)

and therefore

2||\varrho_{01}||_{F}^{2}=2\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}|\varrho_{01}|_{i,j}^{2}\leq 2\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(\varrho_{0})_{ii}(\varrho_{1})_{jj}=2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\varrho_{0})_{ii}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\varrho_{1})_{jj}\right).

(110)

Denote

(111)

Then since
\varrho_{AB}
has trace 1,

\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\varrho_{1})_{jj}=1-a.

(112)

This gives

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\varrho_{0})_{ii}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\varrho_{1})_{jj}\right)=a(1-a).

(113)

Now, the maximum value of

is equal to

for

. Putting all of this together,

D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho_{AB})=2||\varrho_{01}||_{F}^{2}\leq 2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\varrho_{0})_{ii}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\varrho_{1})_{jj}\right)=2a(1-a)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(114)

∎

(146)

Therefore

\Tr[(V-\tilde{V})^{2}]=\Tr[V^{2}]+\Tr[\tilde{V}^{2}]-2\Tr[V^{\sigma}\tilde{V}],

(147)

And finally

\Tr[(V-V^{\sigma})^{2}]-\Tr[(V-\tilde{V})^{2}]=-\Tr[(V^{\sigma})^{2}]-\Tr[(\tilde{V})^{2}]+2\Tr[V^{\sigma}\tilde{V}]=-\Tr[(V^{\sigma}-\tilde{V})^{2}]=-||V^{\sigma}-\tilde{V}||_{F}^{2},

(148)

so the earlier inequality (Eq.
143
) gives

||V^{\sigma}-\tilde{V}||_{F}^{2}\leq 0

(149)

With an equality if
\tilde{V}=V^{\sigma}
. Since a Frobenius norm is always non-negative and equal to zero only for the zero matrix, this proves that the covariance matrix closest in Hilbert–Schmidt distance to
V
is
V^{\sigma}
.
∎

With these results, the proof of Theorem
1
becomes straightforward.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

From Lemma
11
,

\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{2}||V^{\rho}-V^{\sigma}||_{F}^{2}=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{2}\Tr[(V^{\rho}-V^{\sigma})^{2}]=\frac{1}{2}\Tr[V_{\mathrm{rem}}^{2}],

(150)

where

V_{\mathrm{rem}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&&V_{xp}\\
V_{xp}^{T}&&0\end{bmatrix}.

(151)

Since,

V_{\mathrm{rem}}^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{xp}V_{xp}^{T}&&0\\
0&&V_{xp}^{T}V_{xp}\end{bmatrix}.

(152)

This gives

\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{2}||V^{\rho}-V^{\sigma}||_{F}^{2}=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{2}\Tr[(V^{\rho}-V^{\sigma})^{2}]=\frac{1}{2}\times 2\Tr[V_{xp}V_{xp}^{T}]=||V_{xp}||_{F}^{2},

(153)

which is the exactly the expression of symplectic coherence (Defintion
2
).
∎

B.2
Faithfulness and monotonicity of symplectic coherence

We restate Theorem
2
:

Theorem 10

(Faithfulness and monotonicity of symplectic coherence)
.

Given a quantum state
\rho
, its symplectic coherence
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=0
if and only if
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
(Definition
1
). Further, the symplectic coherence is non-increasing under the following operations:

•

Block-diagonal orthogonal unitary gates
\hat{O}
.

•

Displacement unitary gate.

•

Tensor product with free states.

•

Partial traces.

•

Classical mixing of zero first moment states.

Proof.

Faithfulness of symplectic coherence:
The faithfulness of symplectic coherence simply follows from the property of Frobenius norm that for a given
m\times m
matrix
A
:

||A||_{F}\iff A=\bm{0}_{m},

(154)

where
\bm{0}_{m}
represents the
m\times m
zero matrix. Therefore if
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=0
for an
m
-mode state
\rho
, this implies that its covariance matrix
V^{\rho}
is such that

||V_{xp}^{\rho}||_{F}^{2}=0.

(155)

Eq.
154
then implies
V_{xp}^{\rho}=\bm{0}_{m}
and hence
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
.

Block-diagonal orthogonal unitary gates:
Given the covariance matrix of the state
\rho
:

V^{\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{\rho}&&V_{xp}^{\rho}\\
(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{T}&&V_{p}^{\rho}\end{bmatrix},

(156)

under the effect of a block-diagonal orthogonal unitary gate
\hat{O}
,

V^{\rho}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}O&&0\\
0&&O\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{\rho}&&V_{xp}^{\rho}\\
(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{T}&&V_{p}^{\rho}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}O^{T}&&0\\
0&&O^{T}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}OV_{x}^{\rho}O^{T}&&OV_{xp}^{\rho}O^{T}\\
O(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{T}O^{T}&&OV_{p}^{\rho}O^{T}\end{bmatrix}.

(157)

Therefore,

\mathfrak{c}_{\hat{O}\rho\hat{O}^{\dagger}}=||OV_{xp}^{\rho}O^{T}||_{F}^{2}=||V_{xp}^{\rho}||_{F}^{2}=\mathfrak{c}_{\rho},

(158)

since the Frobenius norm is invariant under orthogonal matrices.

We note that the symplectic matrix of
\hat{O}
maps to a local unitary through the mapping
\mathcal{M}
(Definition
4
). Indeed, the symplectic matrix of
\hat{O}
is given by

S_{O}=\begin{bmatrix}O&&0\\
0&&O\end{bmatrix},

(159)

and it acts on the covariance matrix
V^{\rho}
as

V^{\rho}\mapsto S_{O}V^{\rho}S_{O}^{T}.

(160)

Through the mapping
\mathcal{M}
, the action of
\hat{O}
can be seen as the action of a unitary

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{loc}}=\begin{bmatrix}O&&0\\
0&&O\end{bmatrix}

(161)

on the virtual state
\rho=\frac{1}{\Tr[V^{\rho}]}V^{\rho}
, since

\hat{U}_{\mathrm{loc}}=\I_{2}\otimes\hat{O},

(162)

where
\I_{2}
is the identity operation on the Hilbert space of the qubit, whereas
\hat{O}
is a local unitary gate on the qudit. Local unitary gates do not increase discord, similar to how block-diagonal orthogonal unitary gates do not change symplectic coherence. This further strengthens the conceptual bridge between symplectic coherence and quantum discord discussed in the main text.

Displacement gates:
The symplectic matrix corresponding to the displacement gate is the identity
\I
. Therefore,

V^{\hat{D}\rho\hat{D}^{\dagger}}=V^{\rho},

(163)

and hence

\mathfrak{c}_{\hat{D}\rho\hat{D}^{\dagger}}=\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}.

(164)

Tensor product with free states:
Given an
m
-mode state
\rho
and an
n
-mode free state
\sigma_{B}
,

V_{xp}^{\rho\otimes\sigma_{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{xp}^{\rho}&&0\\
0&&0\end{bmatrix},

(165)

and therefore,

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho\otimes\sigma_{B}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m+n}(V_{xp}^{\rho\otimes\sigma_{B}})_{ij}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(V_{xp}^{\rho})_{ij}^{2}=\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}.

(166)

Partial traces:
Given a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
, where
A
is an
m
-mode system and
B
is an
n
-mode system,

V^{\rho_{AB}}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{xp}^{A}&&V_{xp}^{AB}\\
V_{xp}^{BA}&&V_{xp}^{B}\end{bmatrix},

(167)

we have that

V_{xp}^{\Tr_{B}[\rho_{AB}]}=V_{xp}^{A}.

(168)

Therefore,

\mathfrak{c}(\rho_{AB})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(V_{xp}^{A})_{ij}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(V_{xp}^{AB})_{ij}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(V_{xp}^{BA})_{ij}^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(V_{xp}^{B})_{ij}^{2}\geq\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(V_{xp}^{A})_{ij}^{2}=\mathfrak{c}_{\Tr_{B}[\rho_{AB}]}.

(169)

Classical mixing for zero first moments:
If we are only mixing states with zero first moments, i.e.

\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}\rho_{i},

(170)

where
\rho_{i}
has zero first moments of quadratures. Then

V^{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}V^{\rho_{i}},

(171)

and hence

||V_{xp}^{\rho}||_{F}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}||V_{xp}^{\rho_{i}}||_{F}\leq\max_{i}||V^{\rho_{i}}||_{F},

(172)

and therefore,

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=||V_{xp}^{\rho}||_{F}^{2}\leq\max_{i}\mathfrak{c}_{\rho_{i}}.

(173)

∎

B.3
Some important non-free operations for symplectic coherence

In this section, we highlight some experimentally relevant operations which are not free.

Heterodyne measurement:
Post-selected heterodyne measurements are not free for symplectic coherence. To prove this, we give an example where symplectic coherenct increases after measurement. Consider the state

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\ket{ \xi}\bra{\xi}+\frac{1}{2}\ket{-r}\bra{-r}\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0},

(174)

where
\ket{ \xi}\bra{\xi}
is a rotated squeezed vacuum state with
\xi=re^{i\theta}
with
r>0
, whereas
\ket{-r}
is a single-mode squeezed state, squeezed along the momentum direction. The off-block-diagonal matrix of its covariance matrix is given by

V_{xp}^{\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}0&&0\\
0&&\frac{1}{2}\sinh(2r)\sin(2\theta)\end{bmatrix},

(175)

and its symplectic coherence is given by

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=\frac{1}{4}\sinh^{2}(2r)\sin^{2}(2\theta).

(176)

Now, if we post select on the vacuum state
\ket{0}\bra{0}
on the first mode, corresponding to a
0
outcome for heterodyne measurement, the post measurement state is

\sigma=\frac{\bra{0}\rho\ket{0}}{\Tr[\bra{0}\rho\ket{0}]}=\frac{\cosh(r)}{1+\cosh(r)}\ket{\xi}\bra{\xi}+\frac{1}{1+\cosh(r)}\ket{0}\bra{0}.

(177)

This follows from the fact that
|\langle 0|-r\rangle|^{2}=1/\cosh(r).
Its off-diagonal covariance matrix element is

V_{xp}^{\sigma}=\frac{\cosh(r)}{1+\cosh(r)}\sinh(2r)\sin(2\theta),

(178)

and therefore its symplectic coherence is given by

\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}=\frac{\cosh^{2}(r)}{(1+\cosh(r))^{2}}\sinh^{2}(2r)\sin^{2}(2\theta).

(179)

Since
\frac{\cosh^{2}(r)}{(1+\cosh(r))^{2}}>\frac{1}{4}
for all
r\neq 0
. Therefore, for all
\theta\neq n\times\pi/2
, where
n\in\N
,
\mathfrak{c}
increases after the measurement.

Homodyne measurements:
Post-selected homodyne measurements are not free for symplectic coherence. Consider again the state

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\ket{ \xi}\bra{\xi}+\frac{1}{2}\ket{-r}\bra{-r}\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0},

(180)

with symplectic coherence is given by

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=\frac{1}{4}\sinh^{2}(2r)\sin^{2}(2\theta).

(181)

Denoting position eigenstates as
\ket{q}_{x}
, we have

|\langle-r|q\rangle_{x}|^{2}=\frac{e^{-r}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{q^{2}}{2}e^{-2r}\right).

(182)

Therefore, if we post select on the homodyne measurement
\ket{0}\bra{0}_{x}
, the post-selected state is

\sigma=\frac{\bra{0}\rho\ket{0}_{x}}{\Tr[\bra{0}\rho\ket{0}_{x}]}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-r}}\ket{\xi}\bra{\xi}+\frac{e^{-r}}{1+e^{-r}}\ket{0}\bra{0}.

(183)

Therefore its off-diagonal covariance matrix element is given by

V_{xp}^{\sigma}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-r}}\sinh(2r)\sin(2\theta),

(184)

and its symplectic coherence is given by

\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{(1+e^{-r})^{2}}\sinh^{2}(2r)\sin^{2}(2\theta).

(185)

Since
\frac{1}{(1+e^{-r})^{2}}>\frac{1}{4}
for all
r>0
. Therefore, for all
\theta\neq 2\pi
, symplectic coherence increases after the measurement.

Classical mixing for non-zero first moments:
To see this, consider the classical mixing

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{2}.

(186)

For such
\rho
,

\displaystyle V_{x_{i},p_{j}}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr[\{\hat{x}_{i}\hat{p}_{j}\}\rho]-\Tr[\hat{x}_{i}\rho]\Tr[\hat{p}_{j}\rho]

(187)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\Tr[\{\hat{x}_{i}\hat{p}_{j}\}\rho_{1}]}{2}-\Tr[\hat{x}_{i}\rho_{1}]\Tr[\hat{p}_{j}\rho_{1}]\right)+\left(\frac{\Tr[\{\hat{x}_{i}\hat{p}_{j}\}\rho_{2}]}{2}-\Tr[\hat{x}_{i}\rho_{2}]\Tr[\hat{p}_{j}\rho_{2}]\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Tr[\hat{x}_{i}\rho_{1}]\Tr[\hat{p}_{j}\rho_{1}]}{4}+\frac{\Tr[\hat{x}_{i}\rho_{2}]\Tr[\hat{p}_{j}\rho_{2}]}{4}-\frac{\Tr[\hat{x}_{i}\rho_{1}]\Tr[\hat{p}_{j}\rho_{2}]}{4}-\frac{\Tr[\hat{x}_{i}\rho_{2}]\Tr[\hat{p}_{j}\rho_{1}]}{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}V_{x_{i},p_{j}}^{\rho_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}V_{x_{i},p_{j}}^{\rho_{2}}+\frac{1}{4}(\langle x_{i}\rangle_{1}-\langle x_{i}\rangle_{2})(\langle p_{j}\rangle_{1}-\langle p_{j}\rangle_{2}).

Therefore,

V_{xp}^{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}V_{xp}^{\rho_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}V_{xp}^{\rho_{2}}+\frac{1}{4}((\bm{d}_{x})_{1}-(\bm{d}_{x})_{2})((\bm{d}_{p})_{1}-(\bm{d}_{p})_{2})^{T},

(188)

where
\bm{d}_{x}^{1}
(
\bm{d}_{p}^{1}
) is the first moment vector of position (momentum) quadratures in
\rho_{1}
, and similarly for
\rho_{2}
. Therefore, the covariance matrix is not simply a convex combination of covariance matrices of the individual states and therefore we cannot guarantee that the symplectic coherence is non-increasing under classical mixing.

As an example, consider the convex combination of the single-mode vacuum state and the coherent state
\ket{\alpha}
with first moment of position and momentum quadrature being
\alpha_{R}
and
\alpha_{I}
respectively, with
\alpha_{R},\alpha_{I}\neq 0
. Now,
\mathfrak{c}_{\ket{0}\bra{0}}=0
and
\mathfrak{c}_{\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}}=0
, but for

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\ket{0}\bra{0}+\frac{1}{2}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha},

(189)

we have

V_{xp}^{\rho}=\frac{1}{4}\alpha_{R}\alpha_{I},

(190)

and hence

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=\frac{1}{16}\alpha_{R}^{2}\alpha_{I}^{2}.

(191)

This provides a concrete example of increasing symplectic coherence by classical mixing.

B.4
A no-go result for the monotonicity of faithful measures of position-momentum correlations

We restate Theorem
3
:

Theorem 11

(Position-momentum correlation monotonicity no-go)
.

There is no faithful measure of position-momentum correlations which is non-increasing under all block-diagonal Gaussian symplectic operations.

Proof.

The symplectic matrix corresponding to a block-diagonal symplectic Gaussian
\hat{G}_{BD}
is given by

S_{BD}=\begin{bmatrix}A&&0\\
0&&(A^{T})^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

(192)

where
A
is any real and invertible matrix. Its action on a covariance matrix
V^{\rho}
is given by

V^{\rho}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}A&&0\\
0&&(A^{T})^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{\rho}&&V_{xp}^{\rho}\\
(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{T}&&V_{p}^{\rho}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A^{T}&&0\\
0&&A^{-1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}AV_{x}^{\rho}A^{T}&&AV_{xp}^{\rho}A^{-1}\\
(A^{T})^{-1}(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{T}A^{T}&&(A^{T})^{-1}V_{p}^{\rho}A^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(193)

Therefore, any faithful monotone (let us denote it by
||.||_{\mathcal{FM}}
) of position-momentum correlations which is non-increasing under all block-diagonal Gaussian symplectic operations should satisfy

\displaystyle||M||_{\mathcal{FM}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\text{ if and only if }M=\bm{0}_{m},

\displaystyle||AMA^{-1}||_{\mathcal{FM}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle||M||_{\mathcal{FM}},

(194)

for any real matrix
M
and real, invertible, non-orthogonal matrix
A
. This implies

||M||_{\mathcal{FM}}=||A^{-1}(AMA^{-1})A||_{\mathcal{FM}}\leq||AMA^{-1}||_{\mathcal{FM}}.

(195)

The only way both inequalities are satisfied is when

||AMA^{-1}||_{\mathcal{FM}}=||M||_{\mathcal{FM}},

(196)

for any real matrix
M
and real invertible matrix
A
.
This implies that
M\mapsto||M||_{\mathcal{FM}}
is a function of the eigenvalues of
M
only
Grossmann 2021
. However, in that case, the monotone
||M||_{\mathcal{FM}}
will give the amount of position-momentum correlations for a quantum state with off block-diagonal matrix

V_{xp}=\begin{bmatrix}0&&1\\
0&&0\end{bmatrix}

(197)

and for a quantum state with off block-diagonal matrix

V_{xp}=\begin{bmatrix}0&&0\\
0&&0\end{bmatrix}

(198)

to be the same and equal to zero, even though the quantum state with
V_{xp}
given by
Eq.

197
is not a free state (it has non-zero position-momentum correlations). Therefore
||M||_{\mathcal{FM}}
will not be faithful and we can conclude that we cannot build a faithful monotone which is non-increasing under all block-diagonal Gaussian operations.
∎

B.5
Symplectic coherence and quantum discord

To recap, we define a mapping
\mathcal{M}
that allows us to map covariance matrix of a quantum state as density matrix of a virtual
2m
dimensional qubit-qudit state:

Definition 8

(Covariance matrix to density matrix mapping
Barthe et al. 2025
)
.

Given a covariance matrix

V=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}&&V_{xp}\\
V_{xp}^{T}&&V_{p}\end{bmatrix},

(199)

we define a mapping
\mathcal{M}:V\mapsto\varrho
which maps
V
onto the density matrix
\varrho
in the
2m
-dimensional qubit-qudit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m}
where the state of the qubit
\ket{0}
or
\ket{1}
denotes the position or momentum quadratures and the state of the qudit is the mode number we are dealing with. Including the normalization of the obtained virtual state, this mapping gives

\mathcal{M}\left(V=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}&&V_{xp}\\
V_{xp}^{T}&&V_{p}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\varrho=\frac{1}{\Tr[V]}\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}&&V_{xp}\\
V_{xp}^{T}&&V_{p}\end{bmatrix},

(200)

such that
V_{x}
denotes the block for
\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j}
,
V_{xp}
for
\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j}
, etc., where
\ket{\bm i}
are the qudit computational basis states (Figure
1
).

Then we have the following result:

Theorem 12

(Position-momentum correlations and quantum discord)
.

Under the mapping
\mathcal{M}
given in
8
, a covariance matrix
V^{\rho}
with
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
maps to a classical-quantum virtual qubit-qudit state
\varrho=\mathcal{M}(V^{\rho})
with zero quantum discord with respect to computational basis measurements of the qubit register. Furthermore, the symplectic coherence of
\rho
can be related to geometric quantum discord (
Eq.

27
),with Hilbert-Schmidt distance as the distance measure, with respect to computational basis measurements in the qubit subsystem of
\varrho

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=\frac{\Tr[V^{\rho}]^{2}}{2}D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho)=8\left(\Tr[\rho\hat{E}]-\bm{d}^{T}\bm{d}\right)^{2}D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho).

(201)

Proof.

If
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
,

V_{xp}^{\rho}=(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{T}=0.

(202)

Then,
\varrho=\mathcal{M}(V^{\rho})
can be written as

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Tr[V^{\rho}]}\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\sum_{\bm{ij}}(V_{x}^{\rho})_{\bm{ij}}\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j}+\frac{1}{\Tr[V^{\rho}]}\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\sum_{\bm{ij}}(V_{p}^{\rho})_{\bm{ij}}\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j}

\displaystyle p_{x}\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\varrho_{x}+p_{p}\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\varrho_{p},

where
\ket{\bm i}
denotes the computational basis of the qudit register, and where we have defined

\displaystyle\varrho_{p}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Tr[V_{p}^{\rho}]}\sum_{\bm{ij}}(V_{p}^{\rho})_{\bm{ij}}\ket{\bm i}\bra{\bm j}.

(204)

is exactly the condition for zero quantum discord with respect to computational basis measurements of the qubit register
(
Streltsov et al. 2017
, Section I)
.

To prove the second part, recall that the geometric quantum discord of a qubit-qudit quantum state
\varrho
with Hilbert-Schmidt distance as the distance measure with respect to computational basis measurements in the qubit can be expressed in terms of the off-block diagonal matrix of

\varrho=\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{00}&&\varrho_{01}\\
\varrho_{01}^{T}&&\varrho_{11}\end{bmatrix}

(205)

as (

)

(206)

\mathcal{M}

(207)

Finally, remembering that

\Tr[\rho\hat{E}]=\frac{\Tr[V^{\rho}]+\bm{d}^{T}\bm{d}}{4}

(208)

we get that

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=\frac{\Tr[V^{\rho}]^{2}}{2}D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho)=8\left(\Tr[\rho\hat{E}]-\bm{d}^{T}\bm{d}\right)^{2}D_{G}^{HS}(\varrho).

(209)

∎

Note that we may also prove a converse result: let us look at the class of qubit-qudit states forming the image of the mapping

\varrho

\varrho=\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{0}&&\varrho_{01}\\
\varrho_{01}^{T}&&\varrho_{1}\end{bmatrix}

(210)

satisfying

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle\succ

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\varrho+\frac{i\Omega}{c}

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle 0,\text{ for some constant }c\geq 2m.

\displaystyle\varrho_{0}

\displaystyle\succ

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\varrho_{1}

\displaystyle\succ

\displaystyle 0.

Then, zero discord with respect to computational basis measurements of the qubit would mean that

B.6.1
Maximal symplectic coherence of pure Gaussian states

In this section, we prove the following result:

Lemma 12

\rho

Before proving Lemma
12

Lemma 13
.

Given a real, positive definite matrix A and a real-symmetric matrix M,

||A^{1/2}MA^{-1/2}||_{F}^{2}\geq||M||_{F}^{2}.

(215)

Proof.

Since A is positive definite, we can define

A^{1/2}=OD^{1/2}O^{T}

(216)

for some orthogonal matrix
O
and a diagonal matrix
D
with diagonal entries
d_{i}
such that
d_{i}>0\forall i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Similarly,

A^{-1/2}=OD^{-1/2}O^{T}.

(217)

Therefore,

||A^{1/2}MA^{-1/2}||_{F}^{2}=||OD^{1/2}O^{T}MOD^{-1/2}O^{T}||_{F}^{2}=||D^{1/2}\tilde{M}D^{-1/2}||_{F}^{2},

(218)

where we have used the fact that Frobenius norm is invariant under orthogonal matrix, and where we have defined

\tilde{M}\coloneqq O^{T}MO.

(219)

Now,

\displaystyle||D^{1/2}\tilde{M}D^{-1/2}||_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(D^{1/2}\tilde{M}D^{-1/2})_{i,j}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\frac{d_{i}}{d_{j}}\tilde{M}_{i,j}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{d_{i}}{d_{j}}+\frac{d_{j}}{d_{i}}\right)\tilde{M}_{i,j}^{2}.

The last equality follows from the fact that
\tilde{M}
is a symmetric matrix. Using the fact that

with equality if and only if

, we have,

(221)

∎

Lemma 14
.

Given a
m\times m
real positive definite matrix
A
,

and

||CAS-CA^{-1}S||_{F}^{2}\geq||CAS-SA^{-1}C||_{F}^{2}.

(222)

Proof.

We expand:

||CAS-CA^{-1}S||_{F}^{2}-||CAS-SA^{-1}C||_{F}^{2}=2\Tr[ACSA^{-1}CS]-\Tr[AC^{2}A^{-1}S^{2}]-\Tr[AS^{2}A^{-1}C^{2}].

We have

\Tr[AS^{2}A^{-1}C^{2}]=\Tr[(C^{2}A^{-1}S^{2}A)^{T}]=\Tr[C^{2}A^{-1}S^{2}A]=\Tr[AC^{2}A^{-1}S^{2}]

(224)

since the trace of a matrix and its transpose is the same. Therefore,

||CAS-CA^{-1}S||_{F}^{2}-||CAS-SA^{-1}C||_{F}^{2}=2(\Tr[ACSA^{-1}CS]-\Tr[AC^{2}A^{-1}S^{2}]).

(225)

And

\displaystyle\Tr[ACSA^{-1}CS]-\Tr[AC^{2}A^{-1}S^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr[ACSA^{-1}CS]+\Tr[AC^{2}A^{-1}C^{2}]-\Tr[C^{2}]

(226)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle||A^{1/2}CSA^{-1/2}||_{F}^{2}+||A^{1/2}C^{2}A^{-1/2}||_{F}^{2}-\Tr[C^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle||CS||_{F}^{2}+||C^{2}||_{F}^{2}-\Tr[C^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr[C^{2}S^{2}]+\Tr[C^{4}]-\Tr[C^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

where the third line follows from Lemma
13
and where we used
CS=SC
and
C^{2}+S^{2}=\I_{m}
.

Lemma 15

B.6.2

B.6.3
Maximal symplectic coherence of mixed Gaussian states

In this section, we prove the following result:

Theorem 14

(Maximal symplectic coherence of mixed Gaussian states)
.

Given the covariance matrix
V^{\sigma}
of a (possibly) mixed or non-Gaussian
m
-mode state
\sigma
with the property that
\Tr[V^{\sigma}]=E
, it holds that

\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}\leq c_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{(E-2m)^{2}}{4}+(E-2m)

(281)

with equality if and only if

V^{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}V^{G_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}V^{G_{2}}

(282)

such that
V^{G_{1}}
and
V^{G_{2}}
are covariance matrices corresponding to pure Gaussian states
\ket{G_1}
and
\ket{G_2}
respectively, such that
\ket{G_1},\ket{G_2}\in\mathcal{S}^{\max}(E)
(Definition
9
) and such that
V_{xp}^{G_{1}}=V_{xp}^{G_{2}}
.

Proof.

From
(
Parthasarathy 2013
, Theorem 3)
, any valid covariance matrix can be written as an equally weighted mixture of covariance matrices of two pure Gaussian states, i.e.,

V^{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}V^{G_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}V^{G_{2}}.

(283)

Note that this also implies

\Tr[V^{\sigma}]=E=\frac{1}{2}\Tr[V^{G_{1}}]+\frac{1}{2}\Tr[V^{G_{2}}]=\frac{1}{2}E_{1}+\frac{1}{2}E_{2}.

(284)

Therefore,

\displaystyle||V_{xp}^{\sigma}||_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left|\frac{1}{2}V_{xp}^{G_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}V_{xp}^{G_{2}}\right|\right|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{2}||V_{xp}^{G_{1}}||_{F}+\frac{1}{2}||V_{xp}^{G_{2}}||_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(E_{1}-2m)^{2}}{4}+(E_{1}-2m)}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(E_{2}-2m)^{2}}{4}+(E_{2}-2m)}.

The first inequality used is the triangle inequality whereas the second inequality follows from Lemma
12
, i.e.,
||V^{G_{i}}||_{F}\leq\sqrt{\frac{(E_{i}-2m)^{2}}{4}+(E_{i}-2m)}
for
\Tr[V^{G_{i}}]=E_{i}
. Call

x_{1}\coloneqq E_{1}-2m,\hskip 14.22636ptx_{2}\coloneqq E_{2}-2m,

(286)

such that

\frac{1}{2}x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}x_{2}=E-2m,

(287)

we have

\displaystyle||V_{xp}^{\rho}||_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{4}+x_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{4}+x_{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}f(x_{1})+\frac{1}{2}f(x_{2}).

(288)

Now,

f(x)\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{x^{2}}{4}+x}

(289)

is a strictly concave function of
x
, since

f^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{2}{(x^{2}+4x)^{3/2}}<0,

(290)

for
x\in(0,\infty)
. Therefore, using Jensen’s inequality for the concave function
f(x)
, we get

\displaystyle||V_{xp}^{\rho}||_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}f(x_{1})+\frac{1}{2}f(x_{2})\leq f\left(\frac{1}{2}x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}x_{2}\right)

(291)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{(\frac{1}{2}x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}x_{2})^{2}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}x_{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{(E-2m)^{2}}{4}+(E-2m)}.

Finally,

\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}=||V_{xp}^{\rho}||_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{(E-2m)^{2}}{4}+(E-2m).

(292)

To see when this inequality is saturated, note that

\displaystyle||A+B||_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(a_{ij}+b_{ij})^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}a_{ij}^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}b_{ij}^{2}+2\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}a_{ij}b_{ij}.

(293)

Now, by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}a_{ij}b_{ij}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}a_{ij}^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}b_{ij}^{2}}

(294)

with equality if and only if
b_{ij}=ca_{ij}
for some non-negative constant
c

\forall i,j

Axler 2015
. This would give

B=cA,

(295)

in which case

and

(297)

Applying this analysis to Eq.

\left|\left|\frac{1}{2}V_{xp}^{G_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}V_{xp}^{G_{2}}\right|\right|_{F}=\frac{1}{2}||V_{xp}^{G_{1}}||_{F}+\frac{1}{2}||V_{xp}^{G_{2}}||_{F}

(Eq.

(300)

if and only if

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{2},

\displaystyle E_{1}-2m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{2}-2m,

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{2}.

(301)

(302)

Finally, we want both

and

to attain maximal values (Eq.

\displaystyle||V_{xp}^{G_{2}}||_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{xp}^{G_{2}},

(307)

∎

have maximal symplectic coherence.

B.7
Behavior of symplectic coherence under perturbations of the state

We restate a more explicit version of Theorem
6
in the main text:

Theorem 15

(Symplectic coherence under perturbations)
.

Given two quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
with symplectic coherence
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}
, and second moments of energies satisfying
\Tr[\hat{E}^{2}\rho],\Tr[\hat{E}^{2}\sigma]\leq E^{2}

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}-\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 800E^{2}\epsilon+40\sqrt{2}E\max(\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}},\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}})\sqrt{\epsilon}

(308)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 800E^{2}\epsilon+40\sqrt{2}E\sqrt{(2E-m)^{2}+2(2E-m)}\sqrt{\epsilon}.

Proof.

We first give an upper bound on Hilbert–Schmidt distance between the covariance matrices of two states in terms of their trace distance:

Lemma 17
.

Given two quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
with covariance matrices
V^{\rho}
and
V^{\sigma}

\Tr[\hat{E}^{2}\rho],\Tr[\hat{E}^{2}\sigma]\leq E^{2}

||V^{\rho}-V^{\sigma}||_{F}\leq\sqrt{m}||V^{\rho}-V^{\sigma}||_{\infty}\leq 40E\sqrt{m||\rho-\sigma||_{1}}.

(309)

Proof.

The right hand side follows from
(
Mele et al. 2025
, Theorem 62)
, while the left hand side follows from the standard matrix norm inequality
||A||_{F}\leq\sqrt{m}||A||_{\infty}
for any
m\times m
matrix A.
∎

Therefore, if
||\rho-\sigma||_{1}\leq\epsilon/m
, then

||V^{\rho}-V^{\sigma}||_{F}\leq 40E\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(310)

Now if
\tilde{V}^{\rho}
is the covariance matrix with zero position-momentum correlations closest in Hilbert–Schmidt distance to
V^{\rho}
, then (Theorem
118
)

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}||V^{\rho}-\tilde{V}^{\rho}||_{F}^{2}.

(311)

Similarly,

\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}||V^{\sigma}-\tilde{V}^{\sigma}||_{F}^{2}.

(312)

Since
\tilde{V}^{\rho}
is the covariance matrix with zero position-momentum correlations closest to
V^{\rho}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}||V^{\rho}-\tilde{V}^{\rho}||_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}||V^{\rho}-\tilde{V}^{\sigma}||_{F}^{2}

(313)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(||V_{\rho}-V_{\sigma}||_{F}+||V_{\sigma}-\tilde{V}_{\sigma}||_{F})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}||V_{\rho}-V_{\sigma}||_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}||V_{\sigma}-\tilde{V}_{\sigma}||_{F}^{2}+||V_{\rho}-V_{\sigma}||_{F}||V_{\sigma}-\tilde{V}_{\sigma}||_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}||V_{\rho}-V_{\sigma}||_{F}^{2}+\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}}||V_{\rho}-V_{\sigma}||_{F},

(314)

so that, using Eq.
310
,

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}-\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}\leq 800E^{2}\epsilon+40\sqrt{2}E\max(\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}},\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}})\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(315)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}-\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 800E^{2}\epsilon+40\sqrt{2}E\max(\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}},\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}})\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(316)

Combining these two results,

|\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}-\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}|\leq 800E^{2}\epsilon+40\sqrt{2}E\max(\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}},\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}})\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(317)

Now, since

\Tr[\rho\hat{E}]^{2}\leq\Tr[\rho\hat{E}^{2}]\leq E^{2},

(318)

and

\Tr[\hat{E}\rho]=\frac{\Tr[V^{\rho}]}{4}+\frac{\bm{d}\bm{d}^{T}}{4}\leq E,

(319)

we have

(320)

since

. Similarly,

(321)

Therefore, from Theorem
5
,

\mathfrak{c}_{\rho},\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{max}}(4E,m),

(322)

where

is given by Eq.
213
. This gives

|\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}-\mathfrak{c}_{\sigma}|\leq 800E^{2}\epsilon+40\sqrt{2}E\sqrt{(2E-m)^{2}+2(2E-m)}\sqrt{\epsilon}

(323)

∎

B.8
Symplectic coherence under photon loss

In this sction, we show the following result on the evolution of the covariance matrix of a quantum state under photon loss:

Lemma 18
.

Under pure photon loss
\Lambda_{\eta}(.)
the covariance matrix of a quantum state
V^{\rho}
evolves as

V^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}=\eta V^{\rho}+(1-\eta)\I_{2m}.

(324)

Therefore, the symplectic coherence of a quantum state
\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}
transforms as

\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}=\eta^{2}\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}.

(325)

Proof.

Denoting the covariance matrix of a bipartite quantum state
\sigma_{AB}
as

\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{\sigma_{AB}}&&V_{xp}^{\sigma_{AB}}\\
(V_{xp}^{\sigma_{AB}})^{T}&&V_{p}^{\sigma_{AB}}\end{bmatrix},

(326)

with

V_{x}^{\sigma_{AB}}=\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{A}&&V_{x}^{AB}\\
V_{x}^{AB}&&V_{x}^{B}\end{bmatrix},

(327)

and similarly for
V_{p}
and
V_{xp}
. Then, the covariance matrix of
\sigma_{A}=\Tr_{B}[\sigma_{AB}]
is given by

\begin{bmatrix}V_{x}^{{A}}&&V_{xp}^{A}\\
(V_{xp}^{A})^{T}&&V_{p}^{A}\end{bmatrix}.

(328)

Now, the state obtained by the application of a multimode photon loss channel of a state
\rho
is given by

\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)=\Tr_{B}[U_{BS}(\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m})U_{BS}^{\dagger}],

(329)

that is, the first mode of the state is coupled to the first environmental mode (subsystem
B
) through a beam-splitter of efficiency
\eta
, the second mode of the state is coupled to the second environmental mode and so on.

Firstly, to analyze
\Tr[V^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}]
, we find
\forall i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,

\displaystyle(V_{x}^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)})_{i,i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr[U_{BS}^{\dagger}\hat{q}_{i}^{2}U_{BS}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{m-1}]-\Tr[U_{BS}^{\dagger}\hat{q}_{i}U_{BS}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{m-1}]^{2}

(330)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr[(\sqrt{\eta}\hat{q}_{i}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{q}_{i}^{B})^{2}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]-\Tr[(\sqrt{\eta}\hat{q}_{i}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{q}_{i}^{B})\rho]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(\Tr[\hat{q}_{i}^{2}\rho]-\Tr[\hat{q}_{i}\rho]^{2})+(1-\eta)\Tr[(\hat{q}_{i}^{B})^{2}\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(V_{x}^{\rho})_{i,i}+(1-\eta).

For
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle(V_{x}^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr[U_{BS}^{\dagger}\{\hat{q}_{i},\hat{q}_{j}\}U_{BS}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]-\Tr[U_{BS}^{\dagger}\hat{q}_{i}U_{BS}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]\Tr[U_{BS}^{\dagger}\hat{q}_{j}U_{BS}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]

(331)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr[\{\sqrt{\eta}\hat{q}_{i}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{q}_{i}^{B},\sqrt{\eta}\hat{q}_{j}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{q}_{j}^{B}\}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]

\displaystyle-\Tr[(\sqrt{\eta}\hat{q}_{i}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{q}_{i}^{B})\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]\Tr[(\hat{q}_{j}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{q}_{j}^{B})\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\left(\frac{1}{2}\Tr[\{\hat{q}_{i},\hat{q}_{j}\}\rho]-\Tr[\hat{q}_{i}\rho]\Tr[\hat{q}_{j}\rho]\right)=\eta(V_{x}^{\rho})_{ij}.

A similar result holds for
V_{p}^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}
. Finally, to analyze the symplectic coherence of
\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)
, we consider the elements of
V_{xp}^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}
:

\displaystyle(V_{xp}^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr[U_{BS}^{\dagger}\{\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{j}\}U_{BS}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]-\Tr[U_{BS}^{\dagger}\hat{q}_{i}U_{BS}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]\Tr[U_{BS}^{\dagger}\hat{p}_{j}U_{BS}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]

(332)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr[\{\sqrt{\eta}\hat{q}_{i}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{q}_{i}^{B},\sqrt{\eta}\hat{p}_{j}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{p}_{j}^{B}\}\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]

\displaystyle-\Tr[(\sqrt{\eta}\hat{q}_{i}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{q}_{i}^{B})\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]\Tr[(\hat{p}_{j}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{p}_{j}^{B})\rho\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}^{\otimes m}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\left(\frac{1}{2}\Tr[\{\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{j}\}\rho]-\Tr[\hat{q}_{i}\rho]\Tr[\hat{p}_{j}\rho]\right)=\eta(V_{xp}^{\rho})_{ij}.

Combining these results for
V_{x}^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)},V_{p}^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}
and
V_{xp}^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}
, we get

V^{\rho}=\eta V^{\rho}+(1-\eta)\I_{2m},

(333)

and therefore,

\mathfrak{c}_{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(V_{xp}^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)})_{i,j}^{2}=\eta^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(V_{xp}^{\rho})_{i,j}^{2}=\eta^{2}\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}.

(334)

∎

(416)

This gives

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}(\rho)}(\hat{M})=1+(\nu_{1}^{(1)})^{2}+2\mu_{1}^{2},

(417)

and

N_{1}=272\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\frac{1+(\nu_{1}^{(1)})^{2}+2\mu_{1}^{2}}{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{2}}.

(418)

Similarly, using the median of means estimator
X_{2}
we have

\Pr[|X_{2}-\mu_{2}|\geq d/2]\leq\delta,

(419)

as long as number of samples is greater than

N_{2}\coloneqq 272\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\frac{1+(\nu_{1}^{(2)})^{2}+2\mu_{2}^{2}}{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{2}}.

(420)

Therefore,

p_{\mathrm{err}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\Pr[|X_{1}-\mu_{1}|\geq d/2]+\frac{1}{2}\Pr[|X_{2}-\mu_{2}|\geq d/2]\leq\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}=\delta,

(421)

as long as the number of samples satisfies

N\geq N_{\mathrm{thres}}=272\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\max\left(\frac{1+(\nu_{1}^{(1)})^{2}+2\mu_{2}^{2}}{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{2}},\frac{1+(\nu_{1}^{(2)})^{2}+2\mu_{2}^{2}}{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{2}}\right).

(422)

Further, the reduced covariance matrix of the first mode can be written as

V_{\mathrm{red}}^{1}=\nu_{1}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}e^{2r}&&0\\
0&&e^{-2r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},

(423)

with
r\geq 0
. We also have

\Tr[V_{\mathrm{red}}^{1}]=\nu_{1}(e^{2r}+e^{-2r})\leq E-2(m-1),

(424)

where the last inequality comes from the fact that each of the rest of the individual modes have the trace of their reduced covariance matrix at least two (see Eq.
20
). Finally, since
e^{2r}+e^{-2r}\geq 2
for all
r
, this gives

\nu_{1}\leq E/2\leq(E-2(m-1))/2,

(425)

and thus

N_{\mathrm{thres}}\leq 272\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\max\left(\frac{1+(E-2(m-1))^{2}/4+2\mu_{1}^{2}}{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{2}},\frac{1+(E-2(m-1))^{2}/4+2\mu_{2}^{2}}{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{2}}\right).

(426)

We now simplify this upper bound on the number of sufficient samples, given that

\mu_{2}^{2}=(V_{xp}^{\mathcal{O}_{2}(\rho)})_{11}^{2}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}(V_{xp}^{\mathcal{O}_{2}(\rho)})_{ij}^{2}=||V^{\mathcal{O}_{2}(\rho)}||_{F}^{2}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{\rho},

(427)

and hence
|\mu_{2}|\leq\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}}
and similarly,
|\mu_{2}|\leq\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}}
. We are left with finding the minimal value of the function

\frac{f(m,E)+\max(\mu_{1}^{2},\mu_{2}^{2})}{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{2}}

(428)

given that
\mu_{2}\geq\mu_{1}
and
|\mu_{2}|,|\mu_{1}|\leq\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}}
. Writing

d=\mu_{2}-\mu_{1},

(429)

Eq.
428
can be written as

\frac{f(m,E)+\max(\mu_{1}^{2},(d+\mu_{1}))^{2})}{d^{2}}.

(430)

The minimal value of
\max(\mu_{1}^{2},(d+\mu_{1}))^{2})
is obtained for
\mu_{1}=-d/2
(to see this, note that for
\mu_{1}<-d/2
, we have
\mu_{1}^{2}>d^{2}/4>(\mu_{1}+d)^{2}
, while for
\mu_{1}>-d/2
, we have
(\mu_{1}+d)^{2}>d^{2}/4>\mu_{1}^{2}
) and is equal to

\frac{f(m,E)}{d^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}.

(431)

Here,
d\leq 2\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}}
with the upper bound saturated by
\mu_{2}=-\mu_{1}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}}
, therefore the upper bound on the sufficient number of samples may be refined as

N_{\mathrm{thres}}\leq 272\log(2/\delta)\left(\frac{1+(E-2(m-1))^{2}/4}{4\mathfrak{c}_{\rho}}+\frac{1}{4}\right).

(432)

∎

C.4
Symplectic coherence for loss channels discrimination

We restate Lemma
6
:

Lemma 22

(Symplectic coherence for loss channels discrimination)
.

Given a Gaussian input probe state
\rho
with
\Tr[V^{\rho}]=E
,
\Tr[\rho\{\hat{q}_{1},\hat{p}_{1}\}/2]=\mu
, and with
\nu
the symplectic eigenvalue and
E_{1}
the trace of the reduced covariance matrix of its first mode, two photon loss channels with transmissivities
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
can be distinguised with success probability
1-\delta
by measuring
\hat{M}=\{\hat{q}_{1},\hat{p}_{1}\}/2
, provided the number of samples of
\rho
is greater than

N_{\mathrm{thres}}=\frac{272\log(2/\delta)}{(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}}\max\big(g(\nu,\mu,E,\eta_{1}),\,g(\nu,\mu,E,\eta_{2})\big),

(433)

where

g(\nu,\mu,E,\eta)\coloneqq\frac{\eta^{2}\nu^{2}+(1-\eta)^{2}+\eta(1-\eta)E+2\eta^{2}\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}}.

(434)

For general
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
, this is lower bounded by

N_{\mathrm{thres}}\geq\frac{272\log(2/\delta)}{(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}}\max\big(\tilde{g}(m,E,\eta_{1}),\,\tilde{g}(m,E,\eta_{2})\big),

(435)

with

\tilde{g}(m,E,\eta)\coloneqq\frac{\eta^{2}+(1-\eta)^{2}}{\mathfrak{c}_{\max}(m,E)}+2\eta^{2},

(436)

where
\mathfrak{c}_{\max}(m,E)
is given by Eq.
213
.
This bound is saturated by a state of maximal symplectic coherence

\ket{MSC}=\hat{R}(\pi/4)\ket{r}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes(m-1)},

(437)

where
\ket{r}
is a momentum-squeezed state (
r>0
), with
r
satisfying Eq.
250
.

Proof.

Under the effect of a photon loss channel
\Lambda_{\eta}(.)
, the covariance matrix transforms as (
Section

B.8
)

V^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}=\eta V^{\rho}+(1-\eta)\I.

(438)

Therefore,

\Tr[\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)\hat{M}]=\eta\mu,

(439)

and we denote

d\coloneqq\mu(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}),

(440)

assuming
\eta_{2}>\eta_{1}
without loss of generality. We again use protocol
1
for discrimination. Following the proof steps of Lemma
5
, the probability of error using Protocol
1
satisfies

p_{\mathrm{err}}\leq\delta,

(441)

as long as the number of samples is greater than

N\geq 272\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\max\left(\frac{1+(\nu_{1}^{(1)})^{2}+2\eta_{1}^{2}\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}},\frac{1+(\nu_{1}^{(2)})^{2}+2\eta_{2}^{2}\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}}\right).

(442)

Now, if the reduced first mode covariance matrix of the first mode of the input probe state
\rho
is given by

\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{x}&&\mu\\
\mu&&\sigma_{p}\end{bmatrix},

(443)

then the symplectic eigenvalue of the reduced covariance matrix of the first mode of
\Lambda_{\eta_{1}}(\rho)
is given by

(\nu_{1}^{(1)})^{2}=(\eta_{1}\sigma_{x}+(1-\eta_{1}))(\eta_{1}\sigma_{p}+(1-\eta_{1}))-\eta_{1}^{2}\mu^{2}=\eta_{1}^{2}(\sigma_{x}\sigma_{p}-\mu^{2})+(1-\eta_{1})^{2}+\eta_{1}(1-\eta_{1})(\sigma_{x}+\sigma_{p})=\eta_{1}^{2}\nu^{2}+(1-\eta_{1})^{2}+\eta_{1}(1-\eta_{1})E_{1}.

(444)

where we used
V^{\Lambda_{\eta}(\rho)}=\eta V^{\rho}+(1-\eta)\I
and we denoted
E_{1}\coloneqq\sigma_{x}+\sigma_{p}
. With a similar analysis for
\nu_{1}^{(2)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle N\geq N_{\mathrm{thres}}\coloneqq 272\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\max\bigg(

\displaystyle\frac{1+\eta_{1}^{2}\nu^{2}+(1-\eta_{1})^{2}+\eta_{1}(1-\eta_{1})E_{1}+2\eta_{1}^{2}\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}},

(445)

\displaystyle\frac{1+\eta_{2}^{2}\nu^{2}+(1-\eta_{2})^{2}+\eta_{2}(1-\eta_{2})E_{1}+2\eta_{2}^{2}\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}}\bigg).

Since
\nu\geq 1
and for fixed
E_{1}
, both the functions inside the max function are decreasing in
\mu
. Since
\mu^{2}\leq E_{1}^{2}/4-1
, we have

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{thres}}\geq\frac{272}{(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\max\bigg(\frac{1+\eta_{1}^{2}\nu^{2}+(1-\eta_{1})^{2}+\eta_{1}(1-\eta_{1})E_{1}}{E_{1}^{2}/4-1}+2\eta_{1}^{2},

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\frac{1+\eta_{2}^{2}\nu^{2}+(1-\eta_{2})^{2}+\eta_{2}(1-\eta_{2})E_{1}+2\eta_{2}^{2}\mu^{2}}{E_{1}^{2}/4-1}+2\eta_{2}^{2}\bigg).

Since now both the functions inside the max function are decreasing in
E_{1}
we would want to set
E_{1}
to its maximal possible value, i.e.
E_{1}=E-2(m-1)
(the reduced covariance matrices of the rest of the modes should have traces at least
2
by Eq.
20
). The state which saturates all these lower bounds
\nu=1
,
\mu=E_{1}^{2}/4-1
and
E_{1}=E-2(m-1)
and hence gives the optimal number of sufficient samples is

\ket{\psi}=\hat{R}(\pi/4)\ket{r}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes(m-1)},

(447)

with the
\ket{r}
being a squeezed state (squeezed in the
p
direction, that is, with
r>0
), with the squeezing parameter
r
such that

e^{r}+e^{-r}=(E-2(m-1)).

(448)

With this input probe state, the number of sufficient samples is given by

\displaystyle\frac{272\log(2/\delta)}{(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}}\max\bigg(\frac{\eta_{1}^{2}+(1-\eta_{1})^{2}}{(E-2m)^{2}/4+(E-2m)}+2\eta_{1}^{2}+

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{1}(1-\eta_{1})(E-2m)}{(E-2m)^{2}/4+(E-2m)^{2}},

(449)

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\frac{\eta_{2}^{2}+(1-\eta_{2})^{2}}{(E-2m)^{2}/4+(E-2m)}+2\eta_{2}^{2}+\frac{\eta_{2}(1-\eta_{2})(E-2m)}{(E-2m)^{2}/4+(E-2m)}\bigg).

∎

C.5
Position-momentum correlations determine the distinguishability of certain Gaussian channels

To recap, we define
PP-MM
equivalent Gaussian channels:

Definition 12

(
PP-MM
equivalence between Gaussian channels)
.

Two
m
-mode Gaussian channels
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
are said to be
PP-MM
equivalent if given an arbitrary
m
-mode quantum state
\rho
with covariance matrix
V^{\rho}
, they change the diagonal block matrices of
V^{\rho}
the same, but the off-block diagonal matrices are changed differently.

Then we have the following Lemma:

Lemma 23

(Position-momentum correlations determines distinguishability for
PP-MM
equivalent channels)
.

Given an input single-mode probe state
\rho
with covariance matrix
V^{\rho}
and two
PP-MM
equivalent Gaussian channels
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
(Definition
12
), the total variation distance between probability distributions generated by rotated quadrature measurement
\hat{q}_{\theta}

\hat{q}_{\theta}=\hat{q}\cos\theta+\hat{p}\sin\theta

(450)

is upper bounded by

\min(1,h_{\theta}(\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)),h_{\theta}(\mathcal{G}_{2}(\rho))),\\

where

h_{\theta}(\mathcal{G}(\rho))\coloneqq\frac{|\sin(2\theta)||\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)}-\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{2}(\rho)}|}{|\sigma_{x}\cos^{2}\theta+\sigma_{p}\sin^{2}\theta+\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}(\rho)}\sin 2\theta|},

(451)

and
\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)}
is off-diagonal element of the covariance matrix of
\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)
, and similarly for
\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)}
.

Proof.

The rotated quadrature measurement
\hat{q}_{\theta}
on
\mathcal{G}(\rho)
can be seen as rotating the quantum state
\mathcal{G}(\rho)
by
\theta
and performing a measurement of the position quadrature.

If the covariance matrix of
\mathcal{G}(\rho)
is given by

V^{\mathcal{G}(\rho)}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{x}&&\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}(\rho)}\\
\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}(\rho)}&&\sigma_{p}\end{bmatrix},

(452)

then the covariance matrix of
\hat{R}(\theta)\mathcal{G}(\rho)\hat{R}^{\dagger}(\theta)
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{x}&&\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}(\rho)}\\
\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}(\rho)}&&\sigma_{p}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{x}\cos^{2}\theta+\sigma_{p}\sin^{2}\theta+\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}(\rho)}\sin 2\theta&&-\sigma_{x}\sin\theta\cos\theta+\sigma_{p}\cos\theta\sin\theta+\sigma_{xp}(\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta)\\
-\sigma_{x}\sin\theta\cos\theta+\sigma_{p}\cos\theta\sin\theta+\sigma_{xp}(\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta)&&\sigma_{x}\sin^{2}\theta+\sigma_{p}\cos^{2}\theta-\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}(\rho)}\sin 2\theta\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, the measurement of
\hat{q}
on
\hat{R}(\theta)\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)\hat{R}^{\dagger}(\theta)
samples a random variable from a normal distribution with zero mean and variance

\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{x}\cos^{2}\theta+\sigma_{p}\sin^{2}\theta+\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)}\sin 2\theta,

(454)

and similarly, the measurement of
\hat{q}
on
\hat{R}(\theta)\mathcal{G}_{2}(\rho)\hat{R}^{\dagger}(\theta)
samples a random variable from a normal distribution with zero mean and variance

\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{x}\cos^{2}\theta+\sigma_{p}\sin^{2}\theta+\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{2}(\rho)}\sin 2\theta.

(455)

From
(
Devroye et al. 2023
, Theorem 1.1)
, the total variation distance between two single-variate normal distributions
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}
of variances
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
satisfies

\mathrm{TVD}(\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{N}_{2})\leq\frac{3}{2}\min(\min\left(1,|\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{2}-1|\right),\min\left(1,|\sigma_{2}/\sigma_{1}-1|\right)).

(456)

Now,

\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{2}}-1=\frac{\sin 2\theta(\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)}-\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{2}(\rho)})}{\sigma_{x}\cos^{2}\theta+\sigma_{p}\sin^{2}\theta+\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{2}(\rho)}\sin 2\theta},

(457)

and similarly for
\sigma_{2}/\sigma_{1}-1
. Therefore, the total variation distance generated by rotated quadrature measurements
\hat{q}_{\theta}
on
\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}(\rho)
is given by

\mathrm{TVD}_{\hat{q}_{\theta}}\leq\min(\min(1,h_{\theta}(\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho))),\min(1,h_{\theta}(\mathcal{G}_{2}(\rho))),\\

where

h_{\theta}(\mathcal{G}(\rho))\coloneqq\frac{|\sin(2\theta)||\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{1}(\rho)}-\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}_{2}(\rho)}|}{|\sigma_{x}\cos^{2}\theta+\sigma_{p}\sin^{2}\theta+\sigma_{xp}^{\mathcal{G}(\rho)}\sin 2\theta|}.

(458)

∎
