Cycle Cancellation for Submodular Fractional Allocations and Applications
Chandra Chekuri, Pooja Kulkarni, Ruta Mehta, Jan Vondrák

Abstract
We consider discrete allocation problems where m m indivisible goods need to be allocated among n n agents. When agents’ valuation functions are additive, the well-known cycle canceling lemma [ 37 , 45 , 43 ] plays a key role in the design and analysis of rounding algorithms. In this paper, we prove an analogous lemma for the case of submodular valuations. Our algorithm removes cycles in the support graph of a fractional allocation while guaranteeing that each agent’s value, measured using the multilinear extension, does not decrease. We demonstrate applications of the cycle-canceling algorithm, along with other ideas, to obtain new algorithms and results for three well-studied allocation objectives: max-min (Santa Claus problem), Nash social welfare ( 𝖭𝖲𝖶 {\sf NSW} ), and maximin-share ( 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} ). For the submodular 𝖭𝖲𝖶 {\sf NSW} problem, we obtain a 1 5 \frac{1}{5} -approximation; for the 𝖬𝖬𝖲 {\sf{MMS}} problem, we obtain a 1 2 ​ ( 1 − 1 / e ) \frac{1}{2}(1-1/e) -approximation through new simple algorithms. For various special cases where the goods are “small” valued or the number of agents is constant, we obtain tight/best-known approximation algorithms. All our results are in the value-oracle model.

Introduction

Allocation problems are fundamental to both theory and practice. They arise naturally in diverse applications from optimization to fair division, and have been an influential source of techniques in discrete (combinatorial) optimization. In this paper we give a new tool for addressing allocation problems with submodular valuations.

Relax-and-round is a natural approach for most of these problems, where first a good

The purpose of this paper is to develop a widely applicable technical tool that helps round a fractional allocation effectively under submodular valuations. We further highlight the utility of this tool by instantiating it on the above mentioned three objectives.
Our tool is inspired by a corresponding result in the additive setting: A fractional allocation is an
m\cdot n

{\mathbf{x}}
which we interpret as consisting of values
x_{i,j}
for

{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
denote the
m

x_{i,j}
,
j\in[m]

{\mathbf{x}}
defines an allocation graph
G_{{\mathbf{x}}}
which is a bipartite graph with agents on one side and items on the other side and an edge between
i

x_{i,j}

x_{i,j}>0

{\mathbf{x}}

{\mathbf{x}}
the value of agent
i

v_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})

\sum_{j}v_{i}(\{j\})x_{i,j}

Lemma 1

(
[
37

45
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}
be a fractional allocation and
v_{i}
additive functions. Then there is another allocation

in the support of
{\mathbf{x}}
such that (i) the allocation graph

v_{i}({\mathbf{y}}_{i})\geq v_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})

{\mathbf{y}}
can be rounded to an integral allocation
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
such that
for all agents
i

v_{i}(A_{i})\geq V_{i}({\mathbf{y}}_{i})-\alpha_{i}
where
\alpha_{i}=\max_{j:y_{i,j}>0}v_{i}(\{j\})

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{x}}
is a fractional allocation such that
\max_{j:x_{i,j}>0}v_{i}(\{j\})\leq{\epsilon}v_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})
for each agent
i

{\mathbf{z}}
such that
v_{i}({\mathbf{z}}_{i})\geq(1-{\epsilon})v_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})
.

The corollary immediately allows one to find a good approximation in the so-called “small” items scenario. One can view the lemma and the corollary as interpolating between the fully fractional setting (which can be solved optimally via LP) and the discrete setting in which no single item is too important to an agent. The utility of the lemma can be understood by considering an alternative rounding strategy: randomly allocate each item independently to an agent based on fractional solution. The parameters obtained by such a rounding are much weaker — a logarithmic factor in the number of agents is typically lost since one has to rely on concentration inequalities followed by a union bound.

Here we generalize the preceding lemma to the
submodular setting. In order to do this we need to work with a continuous extension of submodular functions. As mentioned above, for maximization problems the multilinear extension
[
14
]

has played a natural role.

Definition 1

(Multilinear Extension of
f
)
.

{F}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{S\subseteq V}f(S)\prod_{j\in S}x_{j}\prod_{j\in V\setminus S}(1-x_{j})

(1)

It inherits some useful properties of submodularity and moreover, its value and partial derivatives can be evaluated for a given point
{\mathbf{x}}

Our first contribution is to formulate and prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Given an instance of the allocation problem
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
with submodular valuations
v_{i}
, and a fractional allocation
\mathbf{x}
of the goods to the agents, there is a fractional allocation
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
such that (i) the allocation graph
G_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}
is acyclic, and (ii)
V_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\geq V_{i}(\mathbf{x})
for all agents
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
; where
V_{i}
is the multilinear extension of
v_{i}
.

We state the preceding theorem in a non-constructive way. A polynomial-time algorithm requires one to use approximations due to the sampling aspects of the multilinear extension. We defer formal details of this to a full version of the paper since they are somewhat standard. An efficient version of the lemma yields an allocation
that loses a
(1-o(1))
-factor in the value for each agent which we ignore in this version. See the end of Section
2
for more details.

1.1.1
Santa Claus (Max-min)

Cycle-Cancellation under Multilinear Extension

In this section we present the main technical contribution of this work – Theorem
1
. This theorem states that given an instance
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
of the allocation problem and any fractional allocation
\mathbf{x}
, we can convert it to an allocation with at most
|{\mathcal{N}}|-1
fractional variables whose allocation graph forms a forest, while retaining the value received by any agent
under the multilinear relaxation
. It is well-known that when the function is submodular, the multilinear extension is convex in any
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}-{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
direction
[
14
]
. We first generalize this to show that the multilinear extension is convex in any direction given by
t_{i}{\mathbf{e}}_{i}-t_{j}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
, where
t_{i},t_{j}\geq 0
. This property has been recently observed independently in
[
39
]
. This allows us to argue how the function value changes when we move goods along a cycle allowing us to eventually cancel cycles in the allocation graph.

See
1

We remark that the theorem applies to all submodular functions (possibly non-monotone).
Before we prove the theorem, we see what happens to the value of the multilinear extension when for two goods
i
,
j
with
i\neq j
, we increase the allocation of good
i
by
t_{i}
and decrease the amount of good
j
by
t_{j}

Given a submodular function
f

F

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})

F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F({\mathbf{x}})\geq t_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}}\Big|_{{\mathbf{x}}}-t_{j}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{j}}\Big|_{{\mathbf{x}}}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})

\bar{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}+t_{i},\ldots,x_{j}-t_{j},\ldots,x_{m})

\displaystyle F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\sum_{R}(P_{\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}}(R)-P_{{\mathbf{x}}}(R))f(R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{R:\tilde{i},\tilde{j}}P_{\downarrow i,j}(R)(t_{j}(1-x_{i})-t_{i}(1-x_{j})-t_{i}t_{j})f(R)

\displaystyle+\sum_{R:i,\tilde{j}}P_{\downarrow i,j}(R)(t_{i}t_{j}+t_{i}(1-x_{j})+x_{i}t_{j})f(R)

\displaystyle+\sum_{R:\tilde{i},j}P_{\downarrow i,j}(R)(t_{i}t_{j}-(1-x_{i})t_{j}-t_{i}x_{j})f(R)

\displaystyle+\sum_{R:i,j}P_{\downarrow i,j}(R)(t_{i}x_{j}-x_{i}t_{j}-t_{i}t_{j})f(R).

We rewrite the above equations by combining the terms in
t_{i}t_{j}
,
t_{i}
and
t_{j}
together and we get the following equation

\displaystyle F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle t_{i}t_{j}\sum_{R:\tilde{i},\tilde{j}}P_{\downarrow i,j}(R)(-f(R)+f(R+i)+f(R+j)-f(R+i+j))

\displaystyle+t_{i}\sum_{R:\tilde{i},\tilde{j}}P_{\downarrow i,j}(R)(-(1-x_{j})f(R)+(1-x_{j})f(R+i)-x_{j}f(R+j)+x_{j}f(R+i+j))

\displaystyle+t_{j}\sum_{R:\tilde{i},\tilde{j}}P_{\downarrow i,j}(R)((1-x_{i})f(R)-x_{i}f(R+i)-x_{j}f(R+j)-x_{i}f(R+i+j))

f(R+i)+f(R+j)-f(R)-f(R+i+j)=\alpha

\displaystyle F(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-F(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\alpha t_{i}t_{j}+t_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}}\Big|_{{\mathbf{x}}}-t_{j}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{j}}\Big|_{{\mathbf{x}}}\geq t_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}}\Big|_{{\mathbf{x}}}-t_{j}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{j}}\Big|_{{\mathbf{x}}}

Proof of Theorem

The main idea is that if there is a cycle with fractional values

\delta_{i}
on one edge and
-\delta_{i}
on the other. Therefore, a reallocation strategy looks as follows:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{a_{i}g_{i}}

\displaystyle=x_{a_{i}g_{i}}+\delta_{i}

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{a_{i+1}g_{i}}

\displaystyle=x_{a_{i+1}g_{i}}-\delta_{i}

with index operations modulo
\ell
.
By Lemma

a_{i}
not to lose value, it is sufficient to satisfy:

\displaystyle\delta_{i}V^{\prime}_{a_{i},g_{i}}-\delta_{i-1}V^{\prime}_{a_{i},g_{i-1}}\geq 0

(2)

where, the index operations are modulo
\ell

\ell

\ell

V^{\prime}_{a_{i},g_{i}}
,
V^{\prime}_{a_{i},g_{i-1}}
.

First, if
V^{\prime}_{a_{i},g_{i}}=0
for some agent

\delta_{i^{\prime}}=0
for all

\delta_{i}\neq 0
in order to make the gain of agent

V^{\prime}_{a_{i},g_{i-1}}=0

\delta_{i^{\prime}}=0
for all

\delta_{i-1}\neq 0
in order to make the gain of agent

Hence, we can assume that

V^{\prime}_{a_{i},g_{i-1}}\neq 0

\delta_{0}

\delta_{i}V^{\prime}_{a_{i},g_{i}}-\delta_{i-1}V^{\prime}_{a_{i},g_{i-1}}=0

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell-1}=\delta_{0}\frac{V^{\prime}_{a_{1},g_{0}}}{V^{\prime}_{a_{1},g_{1}}}\frac{V^{\prime}_{a_{2},g_{1}}}{V^{\prime}_{a_{2},g_{2}}}\cdots\frac{V^{\prime}_{a_{\ell-1},g_{\ell-2}}}{V^{\prime}_{a_{\ell-1},g_{\ell-1}}}.

\displaystyle\delta_{0}V^{\prime}_{a_{0},g_{0}}-\delta_{\ell-1}V^{\prime}_{a_{0},g_{\ell-1}}\geq 0

\displaystyle\delta_{0}V^{\prime}_{a_{0},g_{0}}-\delta_{0}\frac{V^{\prime}_{a_{1},g_{0}}}{V^{\prime}_{a_{1},g_{1}}}\frac{V^{\prime}_{a_{2},g_{1}}}{V^{\prime}_{a_{2},g_{2}}}\cdots\frac{V^{\prime}_{a_{\ell-1},g_{\ell-2}}}{V^{\prime}_{a_{\ell-1},g_{\ell-1}}}V^{\prime}_{a_{0},g_{\ell-1}}\geq 0.

We want to choose the magnitude of

Notes on computational aspects of the procedure:

In the discussion above, we ignore the computational issues of computing or estimating the values of
V_{i}({\mathbf{x}})
and

and its partial derivatives are expectations over a certain product distribution and hence can be estimated by random sampling. The sampling error can be made smaller than
\frac{\alpha_{i}}{poly(m,n)}
where

might be off by

compared to our estimates. The number of rounding steps is polynomial in

(at least one new variable becomes an integer in each step), and hence we can choose the sampling parameters so that the loss for each agent is less than

at the end. In many applications, this additive error is negligible compared to the loss of
\alpha_{i}
which we shall incur in any case due to the rounding on the remaining forest. For completeness, we give our computational result:

Theorem 2
.

Given an instance of the allocation problem
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})

\alpha_{i}=\max_{j\in{\mathcal{M}}:x_{ij}>0}v_{i}(\{j\})

3.1

The max-min problem also known as the Santa Claus problem is defined as: given an instance of the allocation problem,
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})

Here we consider the setting with a fixed number of agents. In the additive setting there is a

For any fixed

Proof Sketch.

We sketch the ideas since the essence of this has already been captured in Theorem

Remark 2
.

One can use a different rounding approach for the second step after the guessing the large items and solving the relaxation. This was the approach that was outlined in
[
20

W_{i}=\max\{v_{i}(S_{i}),v_{i}(\ell(i)),v_{i}(\pi(i))\}
. From the pipage rounding procedure, we get
v_{i}(S_{i})\geq V_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})-v_{i}(\ell(i))
. Hence,

W_{i}\geq\max\{V_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})-v_{i}(\ell(i)),v_{i}(\ell(i)),v_{i}(\pi(i)\}\geq\max\{\frac{1}{2}V_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}),v_{i}(\pi(i))\}.

Finally, we estimate by AM-GM

\displaystyle\frac{{\sf OPT}}{{\sf ALG}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\frac{v_{i}(H_{i}\cup O_{i})}{W_{i}}\right)^{1/n}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\frac{v_{i}(H_{i}\cup O_{i})}{W_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\frac{V_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{i})+|H_{i}|v_{i}(\pi(i))}{W_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\left(\frac{V_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{*})}{\frac{1}{2}V_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})}+|H_{i}|\right).

Using Lemma
5
and the fact that
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}|H_{i}|\leq n
, we conclude that
\frac{{\sf OPT}}{{\sf ALG}}\leq 5
.
∎

3.2.2
Constant number of agents

We can prove the following theorem for
{\sf NSW}
with a constant number of agents This theorem also works for weighted
{\sf NSW}
. We note that a similar result was known previously
[
30
]
. See Remark
3
for a comparison.

Theorem 7
.

Given an instance of the fair allocation problem,
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
, we can compute in randomized polynomial time an allocation
A_{i}\in\Pi_{n}([m])
such that the value of
{\sf NSW}
of the allocation is
(1-1/e-\epsilon){\sf OPT}
where
{\sf OPT}
is the value of the optimal Nash social welfare.

Proof Sketch.

The proof is similar to proof of constantly many agents for the max-min problem (see
5
) and we only sketch the idea here. First, we predict the value agent
i
receives in an optimal allocation, say
{\sf OPT}_{i}
. For a particular agent, we can guess this to a
(1+\epsilon)
multiplicative approximation using a grid search in polynomial time. Therefore, for constantly many agents, we can guess the valuation profile for an optimal
{\sf NSW}
solution in polynomial time. Now, consider the corresponding optimal allocation,
(S_{1}^{*},\ldots,S_{n}^{*})
. From each
S_{i}^{*}
, we guess a subset
A_{i}\subseteq S_{i}^{*}
such that for each
g\in S_{i}^{*}\setminus A_{i}
,
v_{i}(g\mid A_{i})\leq\epsilon{\sf OPT}_{i}
. It is clear that we can have an
A_{i}
such that
|A_{i}|\leq(1/\epsilon)
otherwise, we have a value of more than
{\sf OPT}_{i}
for agent
i
. Therefore, together for all
n
agents, this
A_{i}
can be guessed in
m^{O(\nicefrac{{n}}{{\epsilon}})}
time. This is clearly polynomial when
n
and
\epsilon
are constants. Now, we find a fractional solution for the
{\sf NSW}
problem – for each agent, we define it’s new valuation function to be
v_{i\mid A_{i}}
which is the function that gives marginal values of sets on
A_{i}
. It is well-known and easy to see that this function is also submodular. For this function, we aim to allocate agent
i
value
{\sf OPT}_{i}-v_{i}(A_{i})
. Again, following the Theorem
3
from previous section, if such an allocation exists, we can compute a fractional allocation
\mathbf{x}
in time polynomial in
m
,
n
and
\epsilon^{\prime}
such that
v_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\geq(1-1/e-\epsilon^{\prime})({\sf OPT}_{i}-v_{i}(A_{i}))
. Further, we can also ensure that no agent is allocated a good of value more than
\epsilon\cdot{\sf OPT}
by making the value of any such good for the agent zero (this also maintains submodularity). Then we run the non-uniform pipage rounding algorithm and obtain an allocation that allocates each agent a value of
(1-1/e-\epsilon^{\prime}-\epsilon){\sf OPT}_{i}
. Therefore, in time polynomial in
m
,
n
and
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=\epsilon^{\prime}+\epsilon
, we can compute an allocation that maximizes
{\sf NSW}
with constant number of agent up to a factor of
(1-1/e-\epsilon^{\prime\prime})
i.e., we have a PTAS for this problem with constantly many agents. Note that this algorithm also works with asymmetric agents.
∎

Remark 3
.

A
(1-1/e-{\epsilon})
-approximation for constant
n
was achieved previously
[
30
]
. That work relied on an integral version of Theorem
3
which in turn was based on the same ideas as in max-min allocation from
[
20
]
. Therefore, to obtain an approximation guarantee of
(1-1/e-\epsilon)
for any fixed
\epsilon>0
,
[
30
]
requires guessing from an optimal allocation all goods allocated to an agent
i
of value at least
\epsilon\cdot{\sf OPT}_{i}/\log n
. In the preceding theorem, for a fixed
\epsilon>0
, we need to guess the “large” goods of value at least
\epsilon{\sf OPT}_{i}
. Therefore the size of the set to be guessed is reduced.

3.3
Approximating
{\sf{MMS}}
for Submodular Valuations

{\sf{MMS}}
is another fairness objective frequently studied in allocation problems. It is a share-based guarantee, i.e., each agent demands a certain value a.k.a. share. An allocation is considered to be fair if all agents receive their share. In case of
{\sf{MMS}}
, this value for a particular agent
i
depends only on the valuation function of the agent,
v_{i}
and the number of agents
|{\mathcal{N}}|
. It is defined as follows:

{\sf{MMS}}^{|{\mathcal{N}}|}_{i}({\mathcal{M}})\coloneqq\max_{A\in\Pi_{|{\mathcal{N}}|}{({\mathcal{M}})}}\min_{i\in[n]}v_{i}(A_{i}).

Essentially, the
{\sf{MMS}}
value of an agent is the maximum value she can ensure for herself when she divides the goods into
|{\mathcal{N}}|
parts and chooses the worst part for herself. When
{\mathcal{M}}
and
{\mathcal{N}}
are clear from the context, we denote
{\sf{MMS}}^{|{\mathcal{N}}|}_{i}({\mathcal{M}})
as simply
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}
. As mentioned in Section
1.1.3
, since
{\sf{MMS}}
-allocations do not exist, we study
\alpha
-
{\sf{MMS}}
allocations for
\alpha(\leq 1)
as large as possible. Here, we give an algorithm that achieves a value of
\approx 1/2(1-1/e)\approx 0.316
based on a simple application of the
NonUniformPipageRounding
. Our approach is again to round a fractional allocation. While in the previous two sections we solved some fractional program to find the fractional allocation, in this section, we give a direct solution using two known results. To state the results, we need to define another extension of submodular valuation functions called the concave extension. Given a submodular function,
f
the concave extension,
f^{+}
of
f
is defined as follows.

Definition 2

(Concave extension of
f
)
.

f^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\max_{(\alpha_{S})_{S\in 2^{{\mathcal{M}}}}}\{\sum_{S}f(S)\alpha_{S}:\sum_{S}\alpha_{S}=1\text{\penalty\ and\penalty\ }\sum_{S\ni j}\alpha_{S}=x_{j}\quad\forall j\in{\mathcal{M}}\text{\penalty\ and\penalty\ }\alpha_{S}\geq 0\quad\forall S\in 2^{{\mathcal{M}}}\}.

Now, we state the aforementioned results. The first one, from
[
19
]
gives an upper bound on
{\sf{MMS}}
in terms of the concave extension. The second one, from
[
14
]
, is called the correlation gap and connects the value of the concave extension at any fractional point with value of the multilinear extension at the same point.

Lemma 7

(
[
19
]
)
.

Given an instance of the fair division problem,
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
with submodular valuations,
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}\leq v_{i}^{+}(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{N}}|}({\mathbf{1}}^{|{\mathcal{M}}|}))
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, where
v_{i}^{+}
is the concave extension of the function
v_{i}
.

Claim 1

(
[
14
]
)
.

For any monotone submodular function,
f
with concave extension
f^{+}
and multilinear extension
F
,

F(\mathbf{x})\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{e}\right)f^{+}(\mathbf{x}).

Combining the above two results, we get,

Claim 2
.

Given an instance of the fair division problem,
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
with submodular valuations,
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}\leq\left(\frac{e}{e-1}\right)\cdot V_{i}(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{N}}|}({\mathbf{1}}^{|{\mathcal{M}}|}))
.

We can now use
NonUniformPipageRounding
to obtain:

Theorem 8
.

Given a fair division instance,
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
with submodular valuations, if
\max_{i,j}v_{i}(\{j\})\leq\epsilon\cdot{\sf{MMS}}_{i}
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}},j\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we can compute an allocation in randomized polynomial time where each agent receives a value of
\left(1-\frac{1}{e}-\epsilon\right){\sf{MMS}}_{i}
.

Proof.

We run
NonUniformPipageRounding
on the allocation
\mathbf{x}=(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{N}}|}{\mathbf{1}}^{|{\mathcal{M}}|})
. Therefore, we get an integral allocation,
\mathcal{A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{|{\mathcal{N}}|})
such that
v_{i}(A_{i})\geq V_{i}(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{N}}|}({\mathbf{1}}^{|{\mathcal{M}}|}))-v_{i}(\ell_{i})
where
\ell_{i}\coloneqq\argmax_{j}v_{i}(\{j\})
. Since by assumption
v_{i}(\ell_{i})\leq\epsilon{\sf{MMS}}_{i}
by assumption, and from Claims
1
and
2
,
V_{i}(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{N}}|}({\mathbf{1}}^{|{\mathcal{M}}|}))\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{e}\right){\sf{MMS}}_{i}
, the theorem follows.
∎

\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{e}-o(1))

Algorithm

gives a

{\sf{MMS}}

\sf NonUniformPipageRounding

4

{\sf{MMS}}
.

Claim 3
.

Given a fair division instance
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
, the
{\sf{MMS}}
value of any agent is retained if we remove any single agent and any single good. That is,

{\sf{MMS}}_{i}^{|{\mathcal{N}}|}({\mathcal{M}})\leq{\sf{MMS}}_{i}^{|{\mathcal{N}}|-1}({\mathcal{M}}\setminus\{g\})\qquad\text{ for all }g\in{\mathcal{M}}

We can now prove the following result for Algorithm
4
.

Theorem 9
.

Given a fair division instance,
({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{M}},(v_{i})_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}})
with submodular valuations, Algorithm
4
outputs an allocation where each agent receives a value of
\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{e}-o(1)\right){\sf{MMS}}_{i}
.

Proof.

First, let us assume that we can compute
V_{i}({\mathbf{y}})
exactly for any
{\mathbf{y}}\in[0,1]^{|{\mathcal{N}}|\times|{\mathcal{M}}|}
. Note that as per Algorithm
4
, an agent can receive an allocation either in the while loop of Steps
4

4
or in Step
4

NonUniformPipageRounding
. In every iteration of the while loop, we remove one agent and one good. Therefore, by Claim
3
, the
{\sf{MMS}}

4
, we always have
{\sf{MMS}}_{i}\leq(\frac{e}{e-1})V_{i}(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}|}{\mathbf{1}}^{|{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}|})
. Therefore, any agent who gets a good in the while loop receives a good of value at least
\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{e}){\sf{MMS}}_{i}
