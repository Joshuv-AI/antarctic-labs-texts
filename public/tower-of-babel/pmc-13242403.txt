The pseudokinase domain PK1 of UNC-89/obscurin is required for mitochondrial morphology and function in C. elegans
Authors: Yohei Matsunaga, Nasab Ghazal, Andreas Heim, Till Dorendorf, Hiroshi Qadota, Yuchu Wang, Thomas U. Mayer, Olga Mayans, Jennifer Q. Kwong, Guy M. Benian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
UNC-89 is a giant modular protein located at the sarcomeric M-line of C. elegans striated muscle and is required for sarcomere organization and function. UNC-89 contains two protein kinase domains, PK1 and PK2, separated by 850 residues, that includes a 645-residue long intrinsically disordered sequence that acts like an elastic spring. Bioinformatic analysis suggests that PK2 is an active kinase whereas PK1 is a pseudokinase. We recently reported that a genome-edited worm, unc-89(sf22), that expresses UNC-89 carrying a kinase-inactivating point mutation in PK2 has an unusual phenotype with normally organized sarcomeres and SR, normal muscle function and yet fragmented mitochondria, increased ATP levels, increased glycolysis and alterations in electron transport chain complexes and respiration. Here, we show that a genome-edited worm unc-89(sf23), that expresses UNC-89 with an in-frame deletion of the C-lobe of PK1 has approximately the same phenotype as the PK2 catalytically dead mutant. The fact that mutations in two different regions of UNC-89 result in a mitochondrial phenotype is further evidence of communication between the sarcomere and mitochondria. We further demonstrate that in vitro PK2 interacts with full length PK1 and the C-lobe of PK1. The protein kinase domains of giant sarcomeric proteins are autoinhibited by parts of their own sequence, and this is also likely for PK2, but the mechanism by which PK2 would be activated is unknown. Our data is compatible with a model in which PK1 interacts with PK2 and thereby stimulates PK2 kinase activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10974-026-09733-2.

Introduction

Throughout the animal kingdom, muscle sarcomeres contain extraordinarily large proteins, with molecular masses ranging from 750 kDa to 4,200 kDa (Kontrogianni-Konstantopoulos et al. 2009). Examples include twitchin, UNC-89, and TTN-1 in nematodes; titin and obscurin in humans; and projectin, stretchin and obscurin in insects. These giant molecules fold into multiple immunoglobulin (Ig) and fibronectin type III domains (Fn), one or two protein kinase domains, and often contain highly elastic intrinsically disordered regions. The functions of these proteins include sarcomere assembly and signaling, acting as mechanosensors, among other roles.

Loss of function mutations in the nematode gene unc-89 result in slow moving worms with disorganized muscle sarcomeres, including loss of M-lines (Waterston et al. 1980; Benian et al. 1999; Small et al. 2004; Qadota et al. 2020). unc-89 produces multiple protein isoforms using 3 promoters and extensive alternative splicing, ranging from 156,000 to 900,000 Da (Benian et al. 1996; Small et al. 2004; Ferrara et al. 2005). UNC-89 is localized to the sarcomeric M-line (Benian et al. 1996; Small et al. 2004), region in which myosin-containing thick filaments are crosslinked. The largest UNC-89 isoform consists of 53 Ig domains, two Fn domains, a triplet of SH3, DH and PH domains, and two protein kinase domains (PK1 and PK2) separated by a 645-aa long intrinsically disordered sequence (Qadota et al. 2020). Based on sequence analysis data, we predicted that PK1 was inactive and PK2 would be an active kinase (Small et al. 2004; Mayans et al. 2013). The vertebrate homolog of UNC-89 is obscurin (Bang et al. 2001; Young et al. 2001). Whereas obscurin contains all the same domains as UNC-89, the SH3, DH, and PH domains are located near the C-terminus rather than near the N-terminus as they are in UNC-89. Whereas UNC-89 is located at the M-line, different obscurin isoforms are located at either the M-line, or the Z-disk (Young et al. 2001; Bowman et al. 2007).

Recent bioinformatic analysis of UNC-89 PK2 indicate that PK2 is an active kinase (Matsunaga et al. 2024), but the physiological substrates of PK2 are not yet known. Recently, our laboratory generated a CRISPR/Cas9 genome-edited worm, unc-89(sf22), that expresses UNC-89 carrying a kinase-inactivating mutation in PK2 (Matsunaga et al. 2024). In contrast to all other known loss-of-function unc-89 mutants that display smaller sarcomeres, a lack of M-lines, disorganized sarcoplasmic reticulum and reduced locomotion, this UNC-89-PK2 mutated strain has normally organized sarcomeres and sarcoplasmic reticulum, increased locomotion, but fragmented mitochondria (Matsunaga et al. 2024). The increased mitochondrial fragmentation is likely due to the greater association of the fission protein DRP-1 with the mitochondria. Usually, mitochondrial fragmentation causes decreased function. However, the worm strain unc-89(sf22) has higher than normal ATP levels, increased glycolysis and altered levels of electron transport chain complexes. Its muscle mitochondria have increased complex I and decreased complex II basal respiration, each of which cannot be uncoupled further. This suggests that the mitochondria in the mutated worm are maximally uncoupled, possibly resulting from the observed increase in the uncoupling protein UCP-4. These results led us to deduce that the phosphotransfer activity of UNC-89-PK2 is involved in the communication between mitochondria and sarcomeres, possibly facilitating the matching of energy demands with energy production in the myofibril (Matsunaga et al. 2024).

The protein kinase domains of giant sarcomeric proteins are known to be autoinhibited by parts of their own sequence, and this is also likely for PK2. Crystal structures of twitchin kinase from C. elegans and A. californica as well as titin kinase from human and Medaka (Mayans et al. 2013; Bogomolovas et al 2021; Dorendorf et al 2025) show that there are two kinase extensions, a 47 aa sequence N-terminal of the catalytic core (NL), and a 60 aa sequence C-terminal of the catalytic core (CRD) that interact with key residues involved in phosphotransfer (von Catelmur et al. 2012; Dorendorf et al. 2025). In vitro kinase assays show both NL and CRD extensions are inhibitory of kinase activity (von Castelmur et al. 2012; Gravenhorst et al. 2026). How these kinases become activated is unknown. However, based on atomic force microscopy pulling experiments and steered molecular dynamics simulations, the prevalent hypothesis is that they are activated by small mechanical forces, occurring during normal muscle contraction/relaxation, that pull the autoinhibitory sequence(s) out of the catalytic pockets without compromising the structure of the kinase catalytic core, thereby exposing the active site that can now be accessed by the substrates (Grater et al. 2005; Puchner et al. 2008; Puchner and Gaub 2010; von Castelmur et al. 2012; Bogomolovas et al 2021). By placing FRET moieties on either side of the kinase domain of C. elegans twitchin kinase, we demonstrated that as the worm swims freely in the medium there is a periodic change in FRET signal with a maximum during contraction and a minimum during relaxation, supporting the view that the kinase tail extensions undergo stretch-induced conformational changes during muscle function, as predicted (Porto et al. 2021).

UNC-89 PK2 has a predicted C-terminal autoinhibitory sequence (CRD) and, thus, PK2 can also be expected to be autoinhibited (Mayans et al. 2013). Stretch-activation as postulated for titin and twitchin kinase requires the kinases to be held and restrained N- and C-terminally in the sarcomere so that stretch can ensue. In titin and twitchin, the kinase domains are flanked N- and C-terminally by large fractions of the protein, both sides allegedly being embedded in the sarcomere. UNC-89 PK2 does not meet this requirement as the CRD sequence is also the C-terminus of the UNC-89 protein and thus, it is unclear how PK2 could be subjected to an equivalent stretch. Instead, a recent study on obscurin/UNC-89 from Drosophila has proposed an alternative mechanosensory mechanism of PK2 regulation (Zacharchenko et al. 2023). In obscurin/UNC-89 proteins, there are 2 protein kinase domains, PK1 and PK2, separated by a long intrinsically disordered sequence (> 600 aa in length; IK). In Drosophila, PK1 is a demonstrated inactive pseudokinase and PK2 is predicted to be an active kinase. Expression of PK2 in fly thoraces (primarily indirect flight muscle) followed by IP and subsequent incubation of the beads with bacterially expressed PK1 revealed a PK1-PK2 interaction (Zacharchenko et al. 2023). It was then proposed that PK1 and PK2 form a dual pseudokinase/kinase complex, where PK1 predictably acts as regulator of PK2, with the interaction of the two kinases being regulated mechanically by the stretch of the intrinsically disordered interkinase sequence (Zacharchenko et al. 2023). This, integrated model where PK1-IK-PK2 elements act as a functional unit, is supported by the fact that these elements are evolutionarily conserved across obscurin proteins from both vertebrates and invertebrates.

C. elegans UNC-89 also contains a PK1 pseudokinase and a PK2 kinase spaced by a 647 residue long disordered interkinase sequence, which when tested by single molecule force spectroscopy in vitro displays the properties of an elastic spring (Qadota et al. 2020). In previous work we found that SCPL-1 (CTD small phosphatase-like protein 1) is a binding partner of both PK1 and PK2 (Qadota et al. 2008). Interaction with SCPL-1 required that PK2 include its CRD and upstream Ig and Fn domains (i.e. Ig-Fn-PK2-CRD), and that PK1 include its upstream Fn and Ig domains (ie. Fn-Ig-PK1) (Qadota et al. 2008). Then, using yeast hybrid assays we demonstrated formation of a ternary complex between Fn-Ig-PK1, SCPL-1 and Ig-Fn-PK2, while Fn-Ig-PK1 and Ig-Fn-PK2 did not appear to interact in the absence of SCPL-1 (Xiong et al. 2009). These interactions, however, are yet to be demonstrated in solution in vitro.

Despite accumulating data hinting at an interrelation between PK1 and PK2 domains in Obscurin/UNC-89 proteins, proof of the functional integration of these kinases is currently missing. Here, we report a worm strain created using CRISPR/Cas9 that expresses UNC-89 with an in-frame deletion of the C-terminal lobe of the PK1 kinase, unc-89(sf23). Intriguingly, these animals have roughly the same phenotype as the PK2 catalytically dead mutant: normal sarcomere structure, fragmented mitochondria with fewer cristae, and higher ATP levels. However, mitochondria isolated from sf23 muscle have respiration defects opposite to those of sf22: decreased complex I and increased complex II basal respiration, each of which can be uncoupled. The phenotypic relation of the PK1 and PK2 kinases is here further supported with data on their interaction. We show that when ectopically expressed in Xenopus oocytes, PK2 interacts with PK1 and this interaction seems to be primarily mediated by the C-lobe of PK1, so that the interaction between PK1 and PK2 can be expected to be abolished in the mutant sf23 worm strain. We speculate that our results are compatible with a model in which PK1 interacts with PK2, acting as an activator that stimulates PK2 activity.

To delete a portion of the unc-89 PK1 encoding region, we chose 2 PAM sequences: GTTTCACGGTTGACTCCTTTTGG (GN19NGG) and GTTGAGCTTGTGCCGTTGCCGG (GN18NGG). To construct Plasmid for unc-89 PK1 deletion site-directed mutagenesis with the Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit (New England Biolabs) was used.

For microinjection, the total DNA concentration was 100 ng/μl in TE, with the following ratio of DNAs: pDD162 with target sequence: template DNA: transformation marker DNA = 1: 2: 1, with about 40 wild type worms being injected. The transformation marker DNA was pTG96 which expresses sur-5-GFP in most somatic cells (Yochem et al. 1998). GFP positive worms were picked up and placed onto NGM plates (one worm per plate). After laying eggs, the desired mutation was detected by single worm PCR, restriction enzyme digestion, and finally, DNA sequencing. Upon screening 100 F1 worms one worm with the desired deletion was detected, and its progeny was screened for a homozygous deletion, which we designated unc-89(sf23).

Western blots

The method of Hannak et al. (2002) was used to prepare total protein lysates from wild type, unc-89(sf23), ucp-4(syb6586), and unc-89(sf23); ucp-4(syb6586). To detect UNC-89 large isoforms, we separated protein lysates on a 3% stacking and 5% separating Laemmli SDS-PAGE gel, transferred to nitrocellulose membrane for 2 h, and reacted with anti-UNC-89 EU30 affinity-purified rabbit polyclonal antibodies at 1:200 dilution (Benian et al. 1996), followed by reaction with anti-rabbit Ig-horseradish peroxidase and ECL. To compare the levels of total DRP-1 and mitochondrially associated DRP-1, total worm protein lysates, or lysates from purified mitochondria (described below) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to membrane and reacted with affinity-purified rabbit polyclonal antibodies to DRP-1 (Matsunaga et al. 2024) at 1:200 dilution.

Western blots were used to compare the levels of representative components of electron chain complexes from total protein lysates, using the following antibodies: complex I, mouse monoclonal to NDUFS3 (Abcam cat. no. ab14711) at 1:2,500 dilution; complex II, rabbit anti-SDHA (Cell Signaling, cat. no. 11998P) at 1:500 dilution; complex IV, mouse monoclonal to MTCO1 (Abcam cat. no. ab14705) at 1:2,000 dilution; complex V, mouse monoclonal to ATP5A (Abcam cat. no. ab147481) at 1:5,000 dilution; and mouse monoclonal to cytochrome C (Abcam cat. no. ab110325) at 1:1,000 dilution. Finally, we performed western blots to measure the level of UCP-4-HA in wild type, unc-89(sf23) backgrounds using anti-HA (rabbit monoclonal cat. no. C29F4, Cell Signaling Technology) at 1:1,000 dilution. Protein bands were quantitated by normalization to total protein in the corresponding lane detected by Ponceau S staining of the blot.

Immunostaining and confocal microscopy of body wall muscle myofilament lattice and mitochondria

Day 1 adult worms were fixed and immunostained using the method described by Nonet et al. (1993), with details described in Wilson et al. (2012). The following antibodies were used to image parts of the sarcomere, all at 1:200 dilution: anti-UNC-95 (rabbit polyclonal Benian-13)(Qadota et al. 2007), anti-MHC A (mouse monoclonal 5–6; purchased from Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, created by the NICHD of the NIH and maintained at The University of Iowa)(Miller et al. 1983), and anti-UNC-89(rabbit polyclonal EU30)(Benian et al. 1996). Phalloidin-rhodamine staining of thin filaments was conducted as described by Waterston et al. (1984). To image mitochondria, we used mouse monoclonal antibody (15H4C4) to ATP5A (Abcam cat. no. ab14748), also at 1:200 dilution. Secondary antibodies, used at 1:200 dilution, were anti-rabbit Alexa 488, and anti-mouse Alexa 594, purchased from Invitrogen. Confocal images were captured with a Zeiss confocal system (LSM510) equipped with an Axiovert 100 M microscope and an Apochromat × 63/1.4 numerical aperture oil immersion objective, in 1 × and 2.5 × zoom mode. The color balances were adjusted by using Adobe Photoshop (Adobe, San Jose, CA).

Mitochondrial length measurement

Using a Leica SP8 multiphoton upright microscope with a HC PL APO × 63/1.4 numerical aperture oil immersion objective images were taken of wild type and unc-89(sf23) animals co-stained with antibodies to ATP5A and UNC-95. UNC-95 staining was used to identify the body wall muscle cells. For each strain, ten body wall muscle cells were randomly selected from 10 individual worms. The mitochondria from each of these cells was identified, and then long axis length measurements were made using Adobe Illustrator. Each mitochondrion was outlined manually by drawing a rectangular box around it, and the length of the box was measured with reference to a scale bar. An example of how this was done is given for each strain in Supplementary Fig. 1. For each cell, approximately 11–33 mitochondria were measured from wild type, and 39–117 mitochondria were measured from unc-89(sf23), and the raw data are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Mean mitochondrial lengths were calculated for each cell, and these were plotted and shown on Fig. 4B.

Electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy of body wall muscle was performed as described in Matsunaga et al. (2017; 2024). Approximately 170 each of wild type, unc-89(sf22) and unc-89(sf23) day 2 adults were fixed and processed, and approximately 10 randomly selected ultrathin sections were examined, from which approximately 30 images of body wall muscle were taken, of which about half had mitochondria.

Swimming and crawling assays

Swimming and crawling assays on Day 1 adults were carried out as described in Moody et al. (2024).

ATP levels in whole worms

We followed the procedure described in Matsunaga et al. (2024). For wild type and for unc-89(sf23), 3 sets of 100 day 1 adults were hand-picked into M9 buffer, washed free from bacteria, and then processed. For each of these 6 samples, the total protein content was determined by a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, cat. no. 23225), and the total ATP content determined using a CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit (Promega Corporation, cat. no. G7570). Results are indicated as nmoles of ATP per milligram of protein.

Purification of muscle mitochondria and respirometry

Mitochondria from body wall muscle cells were purified from strains expressing tomm20-mKate2-HA from the promoter of the nearly muscle-specific gene myo-3, as described in Matsunaga et al. (2024) which is an adaptation of the method originally reported by Ahier et al. (2018). Briefly, after making a crude preparation mitochondria from whole nematodes the muscle-specific mitochondria were pulled out using anti-HA magnetic beads (Pierce, cat. no. 88836). These mitochondria were then used to measure respiration with a Clark oxygen electrode, again as described in Matsunaga et al. (2024).

Glycolysis assay

A 6-Phosphofructokinase (PFK) Activity Colorimetric Assay Kit (Biorbyt LLC) was used essentially according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 50 mg of packed worms were homogenized in 200 μl of Extraction Buffer, using a Pellet Pestle Motor (DWK Life Sciences Kimble Kontes). After determining the protein concentrations using a Bradford Assay, approximately 1 μg of total protein extract was used per assay. PFK activity was recorded as mU/μg of total protein.

Preparation of Xenopus laevis stage-VI oocytes

Xenopus laevis frogs were bred and maintained at the animal research facility, University of Konstanz, according to the regulations by the Regional Commission, Freiburg, Germany (Az. 35–9185.81/G-22/080). Ovaries surgically removed from mature frogs were incubated in 1xMBS (5 mM HEPES pH = 7,8; 88 mM NaCl; 1 mM KCl; 1 mM MgSO4; 2,5 mM NaHCO3; 0,7 mM CaCl2) supplemented with 50 ng/µl Liberase™ (Roche) at 23 °C for 90 min. After extensive washing in 1xMBS, stage-VI oocytes were collected and kept at 19 °C until further treatment.

PK2 Pull-down experiment

Polyadenylated mRNA encoding 3xFlag-PK2, ALFA-PK1, ALFA-PK1 N-lobe or ALFA-PK1 C-lobe was produced with the mMESSAGE mMACHINE™ T7 Ultra Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher). Needles for microinjection were pulled from glass capillaries (WPI #504,949) with a P-97 Micropipette Puller (Sutter Instrument) and cut manually to the desired length. Oocytes were injected as indicated with 2,7 ng 3xFlag-PK2 mRNA, 7,4 ng ALFA-PK1 mRNA, 14,8 ng ALFA-PK1 N-lobe mRNA or 7,4 ng ALFA-PK1 C-lobe mRNA using a Nanoliter 2010 Microinjection Pump (WPI). Oocytes were incubated at 19 °C in 1xMBS for 42 h. Per condition, 10 oocytes were lysed by mechanical shearing in 50 µl Lysis Buffer (137 mM NaCl; 2,7 mM KCl; 10 mM Na2HPO4; 2 mM KH2PO4; 5 mM β-glycerophosphate; 2 mM NaF; 1 × cOmplete™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich); pH = 7,4). Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C and the clear supernatant was retrieved. 5 µl of the clear supernatant were added to 5 µl 3 × Laemmli sample buffer (LSB) as input sample for immunoblotting and the rest was added to 2,5 µg α-Flag antibody (Sigma-Aldrich F1804) coupled to Dynabeads™ Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer´s instructions. After 1,5 h incubation at 6 °C, the Dynabeads were washed three times with 1xPBS + 0,1% Tween20 and resuspended in 10 µl 1,5 × LSB. 5 µl of all input and 10 µl of all Bead samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by immunoblotting. Flag-tag antibody for immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (F1804). Plasmid for ALFA-tag nanobody was a gift from Motoyuki Hattori (Addgene plasmid #159,986). The ALFA nanobody was expressed and purified as fusion protein with the Fc region of human antibodies.

However, because the PK1 coding sequence is spread out over a large region of the genome, it was more practical to delete in-frame just the coding sequence for the large C-terminal lobe (C-lobe) of PK1 using CRISPR/Cas9 (Fig. 1A). Inactivation of UNC-89’s PK2 domain was reported to result in worms that could crawl at a faster than wild type rate (Matsunaga et al. 2024). However, unc-89(sf23) worms crawl at rates similar to wild type (Fig. 3A). Yet, they swim at a slightly faster rate than wild type worms (Fig. 3B). We do not understand why inactivation of PK2, and to some extent deletion of part of PK1, results in faster moving worms. Inactivation of the protein kinase domain of twitchin results in an even greater increase in locomotion (Matsunaga et al. 2017). In the nematode, mitochondrial fusion and fission are primarily regulated by 4 proteins—fusion is mediated by FZO-1 (Mfn1 and Mfn2), and EAT-3 (OPA1), and fission is regulated by DRP-1(Drp1) (Van der Bliek et al. 2017). Drp1 shuttles between the cytosol and mitochondria and this localization is regulated by phosphorylation in a complex way. Upon association with the outer membrane of mitochondria as a polymeric spiral, fission is initiated. Using an antibody to DRP-1 (Matsunaga et al. 2024), we compared the levels of total and mitochondrial-associated DRP-1 by western blot. As shown in Fig. 5A, the total level of DRP-1 is not different comparing wild type with unc-89(sf23), but the level of DRP-1 associated with mitochondria is diminished (Fig. 5B). Because in unc-89(sf23) mitochondria are more fragmented, we expected that there would be more DRP-1 associated with mitochondria, not less. Although the increase in complex II-based respiration might partially account for the increased level of ATP in sf23 mutants, we explored the additional possibility that glycolysis was increased, as we observed for PK2 inactivation (Matsunaga et al. 2024). The rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis, 6-phosphofructose kinase, was measured. As indicated in Fig. 5D, this activity was increased in sf23 as compared to wild type worms. Thus, increased glycolysis at least partially explains how there is an increase in total ATP levels in unc-89(sf23) mutant worms.

As PK1 is an inactive kinase that lacks the strictly conserved lysine residue required for phosphotransfer (Small et al. 2004; Mayans et al. 2013), it is unlikely that the nematode phenotype observed in this study is the result of the direct disturbance of a PK1-mediated phosphotransfer cascade. Instead, the approximately similar phenotypes of PK2 catalytically dead mutant animals and the deletion of the C-lobe of PK1 suggest an interaction of PK1 and PK2 that predictably stimulates PK2’s catalytic activity. As a first step to test this hypothesis we examined the binding of the two kinase domains in cellulo using recombinant samples. We co-expressed in Xenopus oocytes, PK2, together with either full-length PK1, or PK1 N-lobe or PK1 C-lobe. PK2 was expressed with a Flag tag, and PK1 and its fragments were expressed with ALFA tags. Oocyte extracts were made, and 3xFlag-PK2 was immunoprecipitated with anti-Flag magnetic beads, and the material was separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to membrane and probed with nanobodies to ALFA to detect ALFA tagged PK1 and its 2 lobes. Results shown in Fig. 9, reveal interaction of UNC-89-PK2 with full-length PK1 and this seems to be mediated primarily by the C-lobe (the segment of PK1 deleted in-frame in unc-89(sf23)). Nevertheless, the N-lobe seems to interact weakly with Flag-PK2 and we therefore cannot rule out that it also contributes to the interaction between PK1 and PK2. Overall, these data support the formation of a PK1/PK2 complex in C. elegans, similar to that reported for the distantly related homologues PK1/PK2 from Drosophila obscurin/UNC-89 (Zacharchenko et al. 2023), suggesting that also in nematodes the PK1 pseudokinase acts as a regulator of PK2. These findings permit rationalizing the observed phenotype of worms carrying a truncated PK1 as being closely related to that of worms carrying an inactivated PK2, further leading us to deduce that PK1 must be an activating regulator of PK2. In this study, however, we could not establish if the interaction involves the direct binding of the two kinases or if it is mediated by SCPL-1, through its mutual binding to both kinases. Discussion

Table 1 shows a summary of our results on the PK1 deletion and comparison to our previously reported inactivation of PK2. As compared to wild type, many aspects of the PK1 deletion phenotype are similar to the PK2 inactivation phenotype: normal sarcomere organization with fragmented mitochondria that have fewer, less organized cristae, some increase in whole animal locomotion, increased total ATP levels, increased UCP-4 levels, similar changes in the levels of representative proteins in most ETC complexes with decreases in II and IV, no change in V, and increased cytochrome C, and an increase in glycolysis.

Several key aspects show opposite results: PK1 deletion shows a decrease in mitochondrial associated DRP-1, whereas PK2 inactivation shows an increase in mitochondrial associated DRP-1. The level of a complex I protein was decreased in PK1 deletion but showed no change in PK2 inactivation. Most intriguingly, we observed opposite effects on muscle mitochondrial function: complex I respiration is decreased in PK1 deletion, but increased in PK2 inactivation, whereas complex II respiration is increased in PK1 deletion but decreased in PK2 inactivation; finally, we found that PK1 deletion results in both complex I and II respiration being capable of being uncoupled, whereas PK2 inactivation results in neither complex I nor II respiration capable of being further uncoupled. The differences in phenotype between the mutants indicate that the phenotypic alterations due to deletion of PK1 are not the mere result of a disruption of PK2 signalling. It is plausible that the function of other potential binding partners, such as SCPL-1 and LIM-9 (Qadota et al. 2008; Xiong et al. 2009), of these kinases might have also become altered.

Our results, especially including the effects of unc-89(sf23) on muscle mitochondrial function, and the observed direct binding between PK2 and PK1, are compatible with the speculation that PK1 binds to and stimulates the catalytic activity of PK2. In support of this hypothesis, we show that PK2 can bind to full-length PK1 or the C-lobe of PK1 in a pulldown experiment. At this point we cannot test for PK2 activity, as we have not yet established a phosphotransfer assay for PK2, including the identification of a suitable substrate.

Here we have provided evidence for direct binding between PK1 and PK2. We previously reported that both PK2 and PK1 interact with SCPL-1 (Qadota et al. 2008), and we demonstrated formation of a ternary complex between Fn-Ig-PK1, SCPL-1, and Ig-Fn-PK2 (Xiong et al. 2009). We also reported that an additional protein, LIM-9 (FHL2) interacts with both the PK1 and PK2 regions of UNC-89 (Xiong et al. 2009). Thus, our current model is that full length PK1 or the PK1 C-lobe bind to PK2 directly and thus activates PK2 phosphotransfer activity (Fig. 10A), and that this interaction might be stabilized by subsequent binding to SCPL-1 and LIM-9 (Fig. 10B). Such interactions could occur with two oppositely oriented UNC-89 proteins at the M-line (top of Fig. 10A and B), or given that the interkinase region is a highly elastic spring (Qadota et al. 2020), these interactions could also occur intramolecularly, i.e. with a single “bent” UNC-89 polypeptide (bottom of Fig. 10A and B). Our model for interaction of PK1 and PK2 of nematode UNC-89, is essentially the same as the model put forth for interaction of PK1 and PK2 in Drosophila obscurin/UNC-89, including the possibilities that this can occur inter- or intramolecularly (Zacharchenko et al. 2023). In our study, we additionally provide genetic evidence for this interaction, and provide evidence that PK2 interacts primarily with the PK1 C-lobe. The organization of an inactive pseudokinase regulating an active kinase within the same protein is reminiscent of the well-studied Janus Kinases (JAKs; Babon et al. 2014). However, in the case of JAKs the pseudokinase and the kinase are separated by only 40 residues, whereas, in UNC-89, they are separated by 850 residues.
