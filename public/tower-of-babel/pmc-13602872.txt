Neurotransmitters as regulators of autoimmunity: mechanisms and translational targeting
Authors: Taruna Lamba, George C Tsokos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune diseases arise from sustained breakdown of immune tolerance, chronic activation of innate and adaptive immune responses, and progressive tissue damage. Although cytokines, antigen receptor signaling, and genetic susceptibility have traditionally been considered the principal drivers, increasing evidence demonstrates that neurotransmitters have an underappreciated role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. The neuroimmune interactions link neural activity, psychological stress, and tissue innervation to autoimmune disease development, progression, and relapse. In this review, we integrate current mechanistic studies demonstrating how neurotransmitters regulate innate and adaptive immune responses and discuss their role in autoimmune diseases.Subject terms: Immunology, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Autoimmune diseases develop when immune tolerance breaks down, leading to persistent inflammation, progressive tissue damage, and loss of organ function. Much of the earlier work in this field has focused on genetic susceptibility, antigen recognition, and cytokine-driven immune activation as the main drivers of disease1,2. While these mechanisms are clearly important, they do not fully explain key features of autoimmunity. Disease activity is often marked by unpredictable flares, frequently triggered by psychological or physiological stress, and tissue damage tends to remain localized even in the presence of systemic immune activation3–8. Indeed, conditions such as type 1 diabetes, systemic lupus erythematosus, atopic dermatitis, and Graves’ disease show clear associations with stress, pointing to additional layers of regulation beyond classical immune pathways.

In this context, the nervous system has gained attention as an important contributor to immune regulation9. Lymphoid organs and peripheral tissues are richly innervated, allowing neural signals to directly influence immune responses. Neurotransmitters, in turn, act as key messengers linking these two systems. Although traditionally viewed as mediators of neuronal communication, it is now well established that immune cells themselves can synthesize neurotransmitters and express their receptors, supporting local signaling networks, particularly in inflammatory settings9–14.

Growing experimental and clinical evidence indicates that neurotransmitter signaling influences multiple processes central to autoimmune pathology5,11,15–19. For example, dopaminergic pathways shape T cell differentiation and B cell function; adrenergic signaling influences immune cell trafficking and sets the inflammatory tone, and cholinergic pathways regulate cytokine production as well as immune cell metabolism. Serotonergic signaling intersects with tryptophan metabolism and pathways of immune activation and exhaustion, while neuropeptides released from sensory nerves can act early to initiate and amplify tissue-specific inflammation.

In this review, we integrate current mechanistic and disease-focused evidence to propose that neurotransmitters function as context-dependent immune checkpoints, coordinating neural activity with immune signaling, metabolism, and tissue-specific cues.

Neurotransmitters and their receptors in the immune system

Neurotransmitters are chemically diverse molecules that include monoamines, amino acids, and neuropeptides. Immune cells have well-defined machinery to synthesize neurotransmitters and express a broad range of receptors for these mediators, enabling direct modulation of immune function (Table 1).

Neurotransmitters control immune cell function through distinct cognate receptors

Dopamine

Dopamine signals through five G protein-coupled receptors, divided into two categories: D1-like (DRD1 and DRD5) and D2-like (DRD2, DRD3, DRD4). DRD3 has the highest binding affinity among these receptors, and these receptors differ in their coupling to adenylyl cyclase and downstream signaling pathways. Dopamine receptors are expressed on lymphocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, microglia, neutrophils, and natural killer cells, where they regulate cytokine production, cell differentiation, and migratory behavior9,11,20–23.

Catecholamines

Tyrosine hydroxylase is the rate-limiting enzyme in the synthesis of catecholamines, followed by the downstream production of dopamine, norepinephrine (NE), and epinephrine. Norepinephrine and epinephrine signal through α- and β-adrenergic receptors (AR), which include 3 α1-AR (α1a, α1b, and α1d), 3 α2-AR (α2a, α2b, and α2c), and 3 β-AR (β1, β2, and β3). These receptors couple to distinct G protein subtypes, with α1-AR engaging Gq to activate phospholipase C and Ca²⁺-dependent signaling, α2-AR coupling to Gi to suppress adenylyl cyclase and reduce cAMP, and β-AR signaling through Gs to enhance cAMP generation. Among these, β2-adrenergic receptors are widely expressed on lymphocytes and myeloid cells and represent a key interface between sympathetic nervous system activity and immune regulation9,12,21,24,25. Adrenergic signaling modulates cAMP levels, Ca2+ flux, metabolic programs, and transcriptional responses in immune cells and further shapes immune cell activation, migration, and effector function26.

Acetylcholine

Acetylcholine signals through nicotinic and muscarinic receptors, which are widely expressed on immune cells including B and T cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells, neutrophils, NK cells, and eosinophils9,27–29. Although classically linked to neuronal communication, cholinergic signaling within immune cells also plays a role in regulating inflammatory responses and maintaining immune homeostasis, forming an important neuroimmune link30. Among these, the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR) is a key component of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. However, α7nAChR signaling can influence adaptive immune responses and cellular metabolism, depending on the context.

Serotonin

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) acts through multiple receptor subtypes expressed on immune and stromal cells. Immune cells express several receptor classes, including 5-HT1, 5-HT2, 5-HT3, 5-HT4, and 5-HT7, along with the serotonin transporter (SERT) and enzymes involved in its synthesis and breakdown, such as TPH and monoamine oxidase9,31–34. Serotonin signaling inhibits secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-17 and IFN-γ) while enhancing IL-10 production, promoting anti-inflammatory regulatory T cell responses35. Serotonin also affects the activation and production of cytokines in antigen-presenting cells, further influencing the downstream adaptive immune responses36. Alterations in serotonergic pathways have been reported across autoimmune conditions including multiple sclerosis, type 1 diabetes, and rheumatoid arthritis, where changes in serotonin levels or receptor expression often correlate with disease activity33–35,37–39. Altogether, these studies report serotonin as a context-dependent regulator that links metabolic cues with inflammatory and neuroimmune signaling to shape disease progression.

Neuropeptides

Calcitonin-gene related peptide (CGRP) is a 37-amino acid neuropeptide released predominantly by nociceptive sensory neurons. Its receptor complex, composed of CALCRL and receptor activity-modifying proteins (RAMP1-3), is expressed on dendritic cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, and other stromal cells, enabling sensory neurons to directly shape immune responses in peripheral tissues9,14,40,41. Neuropeptide Y (NPY) is a 36-amino acid peptide that signals through its interaction with NPY receptors (NPY1-6R), which are expressed on different immune cells42,43. Substance P is an 11-amino acid peptide and exerts its effect through neurokinin receptors (NK1R, NK2R, and NK3R) and modulates immune cell function44,45.

Glutamate and γ-aminobutyric acid

Glutamate and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), the primary excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters, respectively, also function as key regulators of immune responses. Glutamatergic signaling through N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDA) and α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors promotes Ca²⁺ influx and pro-inflammatory activation, whereas GABAergic signaling via GABAA and GABAB receptors suppresses immune cell activation and cytokine production. Immune cells can both respond to and produce these neurotransmitters, enabling local autocrine and paracrine regulation46–49. Together, the balance between glutamate and GABA signaling impacts the immune activation and inflammatory status in autoimmune disease.

Neurotransmitters and innate immune cells

Innate immune cells are often the first immune targets of neurotransmitter signaling and play a critical role in shaping downstream adaptive responses. Dopamine and NE modulate macrophage polarization and inflammasome activation. Dopamine signaling through DRD1 increases intracellular cAMP and promotes ubiquitination and degradation of inflammasome components, thereby limiting excessive inflammatory responses. In contrast, signaling through other dopamine receptors can enhance pro-inflammatory cytokine production depending on context. CGRP promotes macrophage anti-inflammatory state via thrombospondin-1, which further leads to tissue repair in a diabetic mouse model50. These findings show the role of sensory neurons in the regulation of innate immune responses beyond nociception.

Dendritic cells integrate neurotransmitter signals to modulate antigen presentation and cytokine production. Dopamine receptor signaling in dendritic cells enhances IL-23 production, thereby promoting Th17 responses51. Dopamine engages DRD5 expressed on dendritic cells, which stimulates Th17-mediated immunity51. In bone-marrow-derived macrophages, DRD1 signaling inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome through the cAMP-E3 ligase MARCH 7 signaling to promote NLRP3 ubiquitination and ameliorate systemic inflammation52. CGRP promotes IL-23 secretion from CD301b+ dermal dendritic cells, which further stimulates γδ T cells to secrete IL-17A and alleviate cutaneous candidiasis and regulate psoriasiform skin inflammation16,53. GABAergic signaling through GABAA receptors promotes dendritic cell motility by engaging Cav1.3-mediated Ca²⁺ influx54. Treatment of macrophages with GABA during their maturation leads to inhibition of IL-1β and promotes an anti-inflammatory phenotype55,56. Glutamate secreted by dendritic cells increases the proliferation of T cells and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines57. Conversely, another study reported that metabotropic glutamate receptor 4 (mGluR4) deficient dendritic cells showed enhanced migration, antigen-presenting ability, and maturation resulting in activated T cells and NK cells responses and decreased tumor growth58.

Emerging evidence suggests that neurotransmitters also regulate neutrophil recruitment, innate lymphoid cell function, and stromal cell-immune interactions9. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that neurotransmitters act upstream of classical cytokine networks to shape innate immune activation state and inflammatory outcomes. Furthermore, these studies indicate that neurotransmitters act upstream of classical cytokine networks to regulate innate immune activation states, thereby shaping downstream adaptive immune polarization and tissue inflammation.

Neurotransmitters and adaptive immune cells

Lymphocytes express a broad repertoire of functional neurotransmitter receptors, enabling them to integrate neural cues with immunological signals during priming, tissue infiltration, and chronic inflammation9. Interestingly, neurotransmitters often act as a double-edged sword in adaptive immunity as they can either suppress or stimulate the adaptive immune response depending on the cellular context, receptor engagement, and stage of disease. The same neurotransmitter can promote either pathogenic or regulatory immune programs depending on receptor subtype, intracellular signaling bias, and tissue environment.

Regulation of CD8⁺ T cell effector function and persistence

Neurotransmitters exert profound effects on CD8⁺ T cell activation, cytotoxic function, metabolic programming, and exhaustion. Several reports have suggested that dopamine regulates the differentiation of immune cells. Chen et al. 22 showed that dopamine increased the antitumor immunity in colon cancer by enhancing the tissue-resident differentiation of CD8+ T cells via DRD5 signaling. In another study, dopamine was shown to enhance the antitumor response by increasing IL-2 secretion, favoring the differentiation of CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ production in a melanoma mouse model59.

NE negatively impacts the CD8+ T cell function and infiltration in lung adenocarcinoma by increasing the expression of CD39/CD73 on tumor cells and decreasing CXCL9 via the WNT7A/β-catenin signaling pathway and increasing resistance to anti-PD1 therapy60. The β-AR agonist isoproterenol reduced the expression of glucose transporter 1 and glycolysis in CD8+ T cells, impairing activation, while the effect was inhibited by propranolol and in Adrb2−/− CD8+ T cells25. NE, through ADRB1 signaling, inhibited CD8 T cell function, pushing them towards an exhausted stage. Blocking ADRB1 signaling along with immune checkpoint therapy improves CD8 T cell function61. CGRP was found to inhibit Th1 and CD8 T cells' function and suppress anti-tumor activity in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and sensory denervation resulted in the delay of tumor growth and increased Th1 and CD8 activity in the tumor microenvironment40. Glutamate results in exhaustion of CD8+ T cells along with memory T cell impairment62. Another study reported that glutamate receptor (mGluR1) colocalizes with TCRVβ8.1 and promotes phosphorylation of TCR signaling intermediate, resulting in activation and enhanced cytotoxicity63. Together, these findings establish neurotransmitters as key regulators of CD8⁺ T cell persistence, exhaustion, and tissue-specific effector capacity.

Neurotransmitter control of CD4⁺ T cell lineage commitment

Neurotransmitters influence CD4⁺ T cell differentiation and function, further regulating the balance between inflammatory and regulatory subsets. Signaling through DRD5 enhances the differentiation of Tfh cells from naïve CD4+ T cells through the PI3K/AKT/FOXO1/Klf2 axis17. Stimulation with a DRD3 agonist is associated with Th1 differentiation from naïve CD4 T cells by decreasing cAMP level and ERK phosphorylation, while stimulation with a DRD5 agonist resulted in increased CD4 T cell activation through the MEK1/2-ERK1/2-pathway64. CGRP signaling promotes Th1 differentiation through the RAMP3–CALCRL–CREB–ATF3 axis and inhibits Th2 differentiation41. Propranolol (β-AR-inhibitor) increased the anti-tumor activity of CD4 T cells and synergized with anti-CTLA therapy65. GABAergic signaling directly shapes immune function in human PBMCs and CD4⁺ T cells by modulating the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Activation of GABA receptors attenuates inflammatory responses, reinforcing the role of GABA as an immunoregulatory mediator beyond its classical neuronal functions66.

B-cell responses and humoral immunity

B lymphocytes are highly responsive to neurotransmitter signals. Dopamine and norepinephrine modulate B cell activation, antigen presentation, and migratory behavior. Prado et al.67 demonstrated a dual role for DRD3 signaling in B cells: while it increased the expression of CD49d on B cells involved in CNS-homing, it delivered anti-inflammatory properties in a pMOG-induced EAE murine model. Further, it increased CNS tropism of pro-inflammatory B cells due to increased expression of CXCR3 in the EAE model. These effects have direct implications for autoantibody production and tissue inflammation. Adrenergic signaling influences T cell trafficking, cytokine production, and exhaustion. Norepinephrine has been found to attenuate EAE by enhancing B cell cytotoxic activity along with increased expression of MHC I. These B cells kill the effector CD8 T cells through the Fas-FasL pathway18. NE acts on B cells via β-AR and increases IgE secretion in a p38-MAPK-soluble CD23-dependent manner68. GABA derived from B cells promotes IL-10+ anti-inflammatory macrophages and further inhibits CD8+ T cell69. Zhang et al. also reported that B220+ B cells secrete GABA, which negatively affects cytotoxicity of T cells, skewing macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory type and promoting tumor growth70. This finding highlights a novel stromal–immune neurochemical axis in adaptive immunity.

Neurotransmitters and systemic lupus erythematosus

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a prototypical systemic autoimmune disease characterized by autoantibody production, immune complex deposition, autoreactive and proinflammatory T cells leading to multi-organ involvement71. Dysregulation of neurotransmitter signaling plays a key role in SLE pathogenesis, including adaptive immune differentiation, immunometabolic programming, and tissue-specific inflammation72 (Table 2, Fig. 1).

Serotonergic control of immunometabolism and disease activity

Alterations in serotonergic signaling have been directly linked to disease activity in SLE. Lood et al.73 reported reduced serotonin levels in patients, driven by increased indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) activity, particularly in those with nephritis and elevated anti-dsDNA antibodies. These findings connect tryptophan metabolism with serotonergic pathways in shaping immune dysregulation and renal pathology. Beyond this, serotonin influences immune responses by regulating cytokine production, cellular metabolism, and pathways associated with immune exhaustion. Reduced serotonergic signaling may result in enhanced pathogenic immune activation while simultaneously weakening regulatory and tissue-repair mechanisms. Notably, serotonergic pathways intersect with key metabolic checkpoints that determine immune cell persistence and effector function, linking chronic inflammation with immune exhaustion and tissue injury in lupus.

Dopaminergic regulation of innate and adaptive immune differentiation

Dopaminergic signaling also plays a prominent role in modulating pathogenic innate and adaptive immune responses in SLE. Wu et al.17 showed that the dopamine receptor DRD5 correlates with inflammatory markers and is highly expressed in CD4⁺ T cells from patients with active disease. This increased expression promotes differentiation toward T follicular helper (Tfh) cells through upregulation of CXCR5, thereby supporting germinal center responses and potentially enhancing autoantibody production. This promotes germinal center reactions and pathogenic autoantibody production. In B cells, dopamine signaling regulates migratory behavior and tissue homing, influencing whether B cell responses adopt pathogenic or regulatory phenotypes. D1-like receptors are found to be highly present on B cells isolated from SLE patients compared to healthy controls, and D1-like receptors are found to promote B cell differentiation through the PTGS2-PRDGM axis. Further, D1-like receptor agonists increase disease severity in the pristane-induced SLE mouse model11. These findings demonstrate a critical role for dopamine in coordinating T–B cell interactions and shaping humoral autoimmunity in lupus.

Cholinergic pathways regulate immune metabolic reprogramming

Cholinergic signaling plays a complex role in SLE and acts as an additional layer of metabolic control in SLE. Acetylcholine produced by splenic fibroblast reticular cells reprograms metabolism in B cells. In B cells, signaling through α7nAChR leads to increased lipid uptake by the lipid transporter CD36, promoting mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and differentiation to plasma cells, suggesting its therapeutic efficacy in SLE28.

Neuropeptides and organ-specific pathology

Neuropeptide signaling further contributes to lupus disease severity; however, neurotransmitter dysregulation in SLE is not uniform across tissues. In lupus-prone mouse models, sensory neuron-derived neuropeptides, including CGRP, substance P, and NPY, accumulate in inflamed kidneys. Local neuropeptide signaling influences macrophage polarization, vascular permeability, and immune cell recruitment, thereby shaping organ-specific injury. Neuropeptide Y has also been known to associate with SLE disease severity. In a different study, a positive correlation between NPY levels and SLEDAI score was observed74. Along with that, administration of NPY antagonist in mice with pristane-induced SLE led to decreased disease severity, decreased IgG deposition, MMP-1, and MMP-8 expressions, along with decreased proinflammatory immune cell populations. Furthermore, NPY SNP polymorphisms were found to be associated with SLE. Local neuropeptide accumulation also contributes to organ-specific lupus pathology. In NZB/W lupus-prone female mice, substance P, CGRP, and NPY concentrations were found to be higher in the inflamed kidneys75.

Autoantibody-driven modulation of GABA and NMDA receptor signaling

Autoantibodies to gamma-aminobutyric acid type B receptors (GABABR) associate positively with SLE disease activity index (SLEDAI) in patients with neuropsychiatric SLE76. Further, gamma-aminobutyric acid receptor subunit pi (GABAPR) SNPs correlate positively with SLE susceptibility77. Anti-NR2 glutamate receptor antibodies lead to endothelial cell activation in the CNS of SLE patients, resulting in inflammation and disruption of the blood–brain barrier and the development of neuropsychiatric SLE78. Chan et al. observed that DNRAbs act as positive allosteric modulators of NMDARs with preferential potentiation of GluN2A-containing receptors, indicating that the GluN2A subunit, as a key mediator of antibody-induced neurotoxicity, can be a potential therapeutic target for SLE with brain dysfunction79.

Altogether, these findings position SLE as a disease in which dopaminergic, cholinergic, serotonergic, and neuropeptide pathways converge to regulate adaptive immune differentiation, immunometabolism, and organ-specific tissue injury. This convergence features neurotransmitter signaling as a crucial layer of immune regulation in lupus pathogenesis.

Neurotransmitters and multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the central nervous system characterized by immune-mediated demyelination and progressive neurodegeneration80. A defining feature of MS is the selective accumulation of immune cells within the CNS, and accumulating evidence indicates that neurotransmitters play a central role in shaping disease pathogenesis by regulating immune cell trafficking, effector function, and interactions with neural tissue (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Dopaminergic regulation of CNS immune cell tropism

Dopaminergic signaling has emerged as an important regulator of B-cell behavior in CNS autoimmunity. Dopaminergic signaling through DRD3 affects B cell tropism and homing in CNS autoimmunity according to data obtained in the EAE model67.

Adrenergic control of immune cell trafficking and disease relapse

Sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity is a key determinant of multiple sclerosis (MS) severity. Norepinephrine (NE) regulates immune trafficking, cytokine production, and B- and T-cell responses. Disruption of splenic sympathetic signaling with 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) exacerbates EAE, indicating that splenic SNS input restrains disease, partly through B cell cytotoxic activity18. Conversely, acupuncture at ST36-enhanced splenic sympathetic activity and β2-AR-cAMP signaling in B and T cells, promoting anti-inflammatory responses and reducing EAE pathology81. NE levels are reduced in MS patients during relapse compared to controls82. β2-AR blockade combined with anti-IL-17A antibodies attenuates EAE symptoms12, and NE directly inhibits IL-17A and IFNγ production by T cells from MS patients24. In contrast, NE promoted Th17 differentiation via α1-AR in EAE rats, whereas α1-AR blockade enhanced Treg responses and reduced Th17 polarization83. Serotonin and NE also suppress IL-17 and IFNγ/IL-17 secretion in PBMC cultures82. Collectively, adrenergic signaling links stress, neural tone, and immune activation, acting as a metabolic and immunologic checkpoint in MS relapse.

Association of serotonergic pathways and immune restraint in the CNS

Serotonin has been demonstrated to be a strong modulator of autoimmune diseases15. Serotonergic signaling has been implicated in MS pathogenesis and therapeutic responses. 5-HT7 receptor is highly expressed on T cells isolated from natalizumab-treated MS patients, demonstrating its protective role and therapeutic target in autoimmune diseases33. Sacramento et al.35 also observed that serotonin impaired IL-17A/IFNγ secretion and increased IL-10 secretion in in vitro-cultured T lymphocytes isolated from MS patients. Fluoxetine, a serotonin reuptake inhibitor, decreased disease severity in murine EAE by inducing Fas-dependent apoptosis in effector CD4 T cells and inhibiting antigen-presenting cells38. In obese mice subjected to EAE, serotonin was found to correlate positively with disease severity, MMP-9, and IL4 expression37.

Role of cholinergic pathway in regulating CNS demyelination

The anti-inflammatory cHAT+ NK cells, when transferred to the cerebral ventricles of CX3CR1 global knockout mice, resulted in decreased CCR2+Ly6Chi monocyte population in the CNS along with decreased demyelination and delayed onset of EAE. This was due to the accumulation of α7nAChRs on CCR2+Ly6Chi monocytes during immune synapse formation, resulting in decreased Qa-1 expression in monocytes29.

GABAergic and glutamatergic control of immune trafficking and excitotoxicity

Altered GABAergic/glutamatergic signaling is associated with central fatigue in MS84. The GABA agonist progabide reduces spasticity in multiple sclerosis without impairing motor strength, although its clinical utility is limited by frequent adverse effects85. Bhat et al. observed that administering GABAergic agents ameliorates paralysis in mice subjected to EAE by inhibiting inflammation by acting on antigen-presenting cells and subsequently diminishing the adaptive immune response48. Glutamate-mediated AMPAR signaling in T cells engages a noncanonical Gi protein-coupled pathway that shapes T cell migration and egress from lymphoid tissues. Inhibiting this glutamatergic signaling axis retains T cells within lymph nodes, limits their infiltration into the CNS, and markedly attenuates disease severity in EAE49. Th17 cells release glutamate through a glutamine-dependent vesicular mechanism involving β1-integrin and Kv1.3 channel signaling during direct interactions with neurons. Glutamate released from Th17 cells disrupts neuronal Ca²⁺ homeostasis and induces excitotoxic damage, establishing the neuroimmune axis and potential role in worsening neurodegenerative processes in MS47. Moreover, Larochelle et al. showed that Th17 cells interact with oligodendrocytes and release glutamate via CD29-associated pathways, leading to cellular stress, altered lipid metabolism, and impaired myelination, ultimately contributing to demyelination86. Clinically, elevated serum glutamate levels have been observed during relapse phases in MS patients87. Consistent with its pathogenic role, glutamate-driven excitotoxicity through AMPA/kainate receptors can be mitigated by receptor antagonists, which reduce disease severity and preserve neurofilament integrity in experimental models88.

These studies illustrate how neurotransmitters shape immune cell localization, effector function, and inflammatory balance within the CNS, thereby contributing to both disease initiation and progression in MS.

Neurotransmitter dysregulation in inflammatory bowel diseases

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) are chronic inflammatory disorders that are caused by loss of mucosal tolerance, disrupted immune responses, and altered distribution of commensal microbiota89. The gastrointestinal tract has been found to be highly innervated, resulting in a strong link between the gut, the immune system, and the neuronal axis. In IBD, neurotransmitter signaling emerges as a key regulator of immune tolerance, gut homing, and neuro-immune–microbiota interactions (Table 2, Fig 3).

Dopaminergic control of Treg stability and gut homing

Dopaminergic signaling critically regulates Treg function and intestinal inflammation. DRD3 signaling limits IL-10 production and promotes proinflammatory T cell responses, while its genetic or pharmacologic inhibition attenuates colitis. Ugalde et al.23 showed that DRD3 expression correlates with disease severity in acute and chronic colitis models. DRD3 is highly expressed in the lamina propria and mesenteric lymph nodes, where it reduces IL-10 and CCR9 expression, impairing Treg gut homing. DRD3-deficient Treg cells display enhanced suppressive function and ameliorate colitis after adoptive transfer. DRD3 signaling also promotes Th1 and Th17 expansion in chronic colitis90.

The A2A2 DRD2 variant is associated with reduced risk of refractory Crohn’s disease91. The DRD2–DRD3 heteromer acts as a dopamine sensor supporting Treg suppressive activity and colonic recruitment; disruption impairs Treg function in DSS colitis92. Additionally, a dopamine β-hydroxylase polymorphism (rs6271 C>T) is enriched in IBD patients; heterozygotes (Arg549Cys) exhibit reduced protein expression but increased enzymatic activity93.

Sympathetic regulation of vascular and immune barriers

Adrenergic signaling modulates intestinal inflammation by controlling extravasation of immune cells and endothelial activation. Local sympathetic fibers attenuated colitis in a DSS-induced model by decreasing the expression of MAdCAM-1 on endothelial cells, resulting in reduced extravasation of immune cells from the blood to the gastrointestinal tract19. During colitis, gut inflammation dampens sympathetic nerve signaling by reducing N-type Ca²⁺ channel-dependent NE release, revealing how inflammation directly disrupts neuroimmune communication in the intestine94.

Neuropeptides, pain, and microbiota interactions

Neuron-derived neuropeptides play key roles in IBD pathogenesis by regulating immune polarization, macrophage activation, epithelial integrity, and gut–brain communication. Substance P, CGRP, NPY, and neuropeptide S also influence pain and anxiety-like behavior, linking intestinal inflammation to neurological symptoms. Substance P alleviated anxiety-like behavior and improved microbiota composition via GABAergic/Ca²⁺ signaling in astrocytes in DSS colitis95. Ablation of TRPV1⁺ nociceptors induced dysbiosis and reduced nociceptor expression in IBD biopsies96. Substance P also suppressed Th17 responses, promoted Treg cells and M2 macrophage polarization, and reduced DSS-induced inflammation45.

Neuropeptide S exerted proinflammatory effects in chronic colitis; NPSR1 was highly expressed on CD4⁺ T cells and correlated with disease severity. NPSR1 knockdown reduced CD4⁺ T cell proliferation, Th1 chemotaxis, CCL2-dependent migration, and alleviated colitis97. β-CGRP levels were found to decrease in IBD patients, suggesting its potential role in the pathology of the disease98. NPY expression was reduced in colitis and associated with anxiety- and depressive-like behavior99. NPY1R inhibition reduced IL-6, TNFα, IFNγ, and colonic inflammation in experimental and human ulcerative colitis, while NPY2R blockade primarily improved pain symptoms, highlighting receptor-specific therapeutic potential43,100. Additionally, extracellular vesicles from Akkermansia muciniphila improved cognition and gut inflammation by restoring serotonin metabolism, underscoring microbiota-based therapeutic strategies101.

Context-dependent effects of GABAergic signaling in gut inflammation

Increased expression of GABAA receptors and glutamic acid decarboxylase was observed in the colon mucosa of patients with ulcerative colitis and the DSS-induced mouse model. GABAA receptor activation exacerbates symptoms of DSS-induced colitis but does not alter the production of proinflammatory cytokines102. Gold and Alcocer reported that treating DSS-induced colitis in mice with GABA alleviates the disease, decreases colon permeability, and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines103.

Collectively, these findings indicate that dopaminergic, adrenergic, and neuropeptide signaling pathways converge to regulate immune tolerance, inflammation, neural communication, and behavior in IBD. The observations further reinforce the concept that neurotransmitters act as tissue-specific regulators of intestinal immunity.

Neurotransmitters and rheumatoid arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is characterized by chronic synovial inflammation, joint destruction, and dysregulated interactions between the innate and adaptive immune compartments104. While cytokine networks and autoreactive lymphocytes are central to RA pathogenesis, emerging evidence indicates that neurotransmitter signaling also modulates immune activation, cellular proliferation, and inflammatory balance in RA in a context-dependent manner (Table 2, Fig. 4).

Cholinergic signaling is associated with compartment-specific immune regulation

Reports on cholinergic signaling in RA display the complexity of the effect of neurotransmitters. Lack of α7nAChR resulted in decreased T cell numbers in draining lymph nodes along with decreased disease severity in collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) and impaired T cell proliferation in response to concanavalin A105. Conversely, α7nAChR signaling results in promoting anti-inflammatory response by inhibiting cytokine production in collagen-induced arthritis106. Altogether, these observations show the dual and compartment-specific nature of cholinergic signaling, in which acetylcholine can simultaneously restrain innate inflammation while facilitating adaptive immune responses.

In a randomized, sham-controlled trial involving patients with active rheumatoid arthritis (RESET-RA), reduced disease activity was observed following vagus nerve stimulation. Patients receiving active neuromodulation had higher clinical response rates compared to sham controls, and these improvements were sustained over time without noticeable side effects. Mechanistically, vagal stimulation activates the inflammatory reflex, increasing acetylcholine release and engaging α7nAChR on immune cells, thereby dampening pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Together, these findings provide direct clinical evidence that neural signaling can be harnessed to regulate immune function in autoimmune disease13.

Serotonergic pathways as biomarkers of disease activity

Serotonergic signaling has emerged as a potential biomarker in RA. Epigenetic modification of the serotonin receptor gene HTR2A (cg15692052 methylation) was found to be higher in RA patients and to associate positively with erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein levels, and inflammation in RA, suggesting serotonergic signaling may serve as a diagnostic biomarker in patients with rheumatoid arthritis107. Serum serotonin levels associate positively with at-risk and early rheumatoid arthritis39.

Dopaminergic signaling regulates B-cell activation

Weiber et al observed high expression of DRD1 on B cells isolated from PBMCs of female RA patients108. This finding indicates the potential role of dopamine signaling in B-cell activation or function in RA, although its mechanistic implications remain to be fully elucidated.

GABAergic signaling as a modulator of T cell activation and inflammation

GABAergic signaling exerts anti-inflammatory effects in RA by regulating the immune response, the secretion of cytokines, and peripheral receptor activity, indicating its potential as a therapeutic target109. Engagement of peripheral GABA receptors alleviates autoimmune inflammation by inhibiting T cell proliferation and impairing antigen-presenting cell-mediated activation. In the CIA-induced arthritis mouse model, GABA treatment reduces disease incidence and severity, alongside decreased Th1 polarization and lower levels of pathogenic autoantibodies110.

Taken together, these findings conclude that multiple neurotransmitter pathways shape immune responses in RA, with their effects varying depending on the specific cellular and disease context. Understanding how these pathways integrate across innate and adaptive immune compartments may inform the development of more selective neuroimmune-targeted therapeutic strategies for RA.

Neurotransmitters and other autoimmune diseases

In psoriasis and vitiligo, local neuroimmune interactions play prominent roles. CGRP promotes imiquimod-induced psoriasiform skin inflammation by stimulating IL-23 production from dermal dendritic cells, which further drives the production of IL-17A and IL-22 from IL-23+ γδ T cells16. In another study, inhibition of CGRP signaling by lidocaine resulted in decreased IL-23 secretion by dendritic cells, suppressed psoriasis, and epidural administration of lidocaine improved PASI scores in patients with psoriasis patients111.

Nociceptor-derived CGRP is also known to play a critical role in vitiligo. CGRP has been demonstrated to promote the function of cD1 through CALCRL-RAMP1 and enhanced interaction between cDC1 and CD8+ T cells, resulting in the killing of melanocytes. Abrogation of neuronal CGRP ameliorated vitiligo, and administration of CGRP antagonist alleviated vitiligo in mice and decreased lesions with minimal side effects in a pilot human study14. Norepinephrine, derived from the sympathetic nervous system, promotes autoimmune inflammation in vitiligo by activating adrenergic signaling in fibroblasts and keratinocytes, leading to CXCL9/10 and IL-6/15 secretion and subsequent CD8⁺ T cell recruitment112. Norepinephrine release in secondary lymphoid organs is dynamically regulated by α2-adrenergic receptors in a tissue-specific manner, reflecting differences in sympathetic innervation and architecture, while simultaneously shaping immune cell positioning and local neuroimmune interactions113.

Stress has been associated with autoimmune disease flares and disease severity by modulating neurotransmitter functions4,114. In alopecia areata, psychological stress activates sympathetic nerves in the skin, driving NE-dependent hair follicle damage that exposes self-antigens and triggers pathogenic CD8⁺ T-cell-mediated autoimmunity5.

The serotonin reuptake inhibitor fluvoxamine inhibited PI3K-AKT, impaired glycolysis and effector T cell function in NOD mice, and delayed the onset and decreased incidence of TID34. CGRP has been claimed to serve as a CD4 T cell autoantigen and to induce disease in T1D NOD mice and probably in humans as well115. The CGRP SNP CALCB_rs3829220 T (c.224G+846) and rs382922 C (c.224G+848) variants have been reported to associate with T1D, and βCGRP levels were found to be higher in the serum and in the infiltrating T cells in the pancreas of T1D patients116. GABAergic signaling modulates immune responses by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production in human PBMCs and CD4⁺ T cells, supporting its potential role in limiting autoimmune inflammation in type 1 diabetes66. Together, these studies illustrate how sensory neuron-derived neurotransmitters can initiate and sustain localized autoimmune responses across distinct tissue environments.

Future direction and perspective

The studies discussed in this review demonstrate that neurotransmitters are core regulators of immune function in autoimmune disease, rather than peripheral modifiers of inflammation. Importantly, neurotransmitter signaling does not fit neatly into a binary pro- or anti-inflammatory framework. Instead, neurotransmitters exert their effects in a highly context-dependent manner, shaping immune cell behavior according to receptor expression and availability, cellular identity, tissue microenvironment, and disease stage. Conceptualizing neurotransmitters as dynamic immune regulators provides a useful way to understand why the same signals can lead to distinct, and sometimes opposing, outcomes across different autoimmune settings.

A major challenge in the field lies in achieving cell-type- and receptor-specific resolution of neuroimmune interactions. Immune cells commonly express multiple neurotransmitter receptors, and receptor expression is dynamically influenced by activation state, differentiation, and tissue residency. As a result, genetic deletion or systemic pharmacological manipulation often produces complex phenotypes that complicate cell-intrinsic mechanisms. Recent advances in single-cell and spatially resolved technologies now offer powerful approaches to map neurotransmitter receptor expression and downstream signaling within intact tissues. Applying these tools across disease stages will be essential for defining how neuroimmune circuits change during disease initiation, remission, and relapse.

Another important priority is to clarify the relative contributions of neuronal versus immune-derived neurotransmitters. Many immune, stromal, and tissue-resident cells possess the enzymatic machinery required for neurotransmitter synthesis and express functional neurotransmitter receptors, enabling local autocrine and paracrine signaling within inflamed tissues. Understanding how these locally generated signals interact with neuron-derived neurotransmitters will be critical for accurately defining neuroimmune pathways and identifying mechanisms amenable to selective therapeutic targeting.

Increasing evidence also places immune cell metabolism at the center of neurotransmitter-mediated immune regulation. Dopaminergic, adrenergic, cholinergic, and serotonergic pathways directly influence glycolysis, mitochondrial respiration, and lipid metabolism, thereby shaping effector and regulatory immune cell fates. From a therapeutic perspective, these insights support the development of precision strategies including receptor-selective targeting, biased agonism, tissue-restricted delivery, and neuromodulation to suppress pathogenic immune responses while preserving immune homeostasis. It is well understood that modulation of the neurotransmitter pathways may have unwanted side effects. In future clinical trials, the risk vs. benefit ratio of each new approach will need to be determined.
