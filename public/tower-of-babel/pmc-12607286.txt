GLM-DM: language model boosted neural networks for HbA1c trend prediction in diabetes mellitus
Authors: Yikun Ban, Xinrui He, Patricia M Verona, Curtiss B Cook, Jingrui He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimPredict Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) trends, a key metric in diabetes mellitus (DM) management, using readily available patient variables and language models (LMs).MethodsWe propose GLM (Language Model Boosted Neural Network) -DM, which leverages data augmentation and language model-driven feature encoding to predict HbA1c trends using easily accessible patient-level variables. Our model captures complex relationships among patient characteristics and enhances predictive performance through Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for synthetic data augmentation and LMs for feature embedding. By transforming patient profiles into rich latent representations, our approach enables a more comprehensive analysis of how patient-level variables correlate with HbA1c trends over time.ResultsUsing clinical data from 257 DM patients, GLM-DM achieves 70.2% accuracy of HbA1c trend prediction, outperforming classic classifiers and transformer-based models. Ablation studies confirm the effectiveness of GAN-based augmentation and LM-driven embedding. Our model achieves 68.2% prediction accuracy for Type 1 DM and 72.7% for Type 2 DM.ConclusionProposed approach learns the underlying complex function of HbA1c using clinical variables easily available at the patient visit and leveraging the power of LMs to accurately predict the trend of HbA1c in a period. The model can enhance patient advisories for daily diabetes management without the need for continuous glucose monitoring.Keywords: diabetes mellitus, blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c, language models, generative adversarial network, deep learning

Introduction

Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is a highly prevalent chronic disease that affects more than 400 million people worldwide [1]. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) reflects an individual’s degree of chronic glycemic exposure, with higher values representing more severe hyperglycemia [1]. HbA1c is associated with the risk of microvascular diabetes complications [2]. Therefore, HbA1c is a key measurement that drives clinical decision making with regard to choices for therapy and in patient education [3,4]. However, because HbA1c is typically measured every three to six months, there is a clinical need for continuous estimation of HbA1c levels to support timely interventions and optimize diabetes management.

Existing research has explored the relationship between HbA1c and glucose data. For instance, prior studies have identified a near-linear relationship between average blood glucose measurements and HbA1c levels [5–7], particularly in Type 1 diabetes, where HbA1c levels are more directly influenced by insulin therapy and frequent glucose fluctuations. In contrast, the relationship in Type 2 DM is often more complex due to factors such as insulin resistance, weight changes, and lifestyle modifications. More complex non-linear models have been employed to examine the relationship of HbA1c with the daily self-monitored blood glucose (BG) profiles, and data derived from continuous glucose monitors [8,9]. Deep learning models have also been applied to predict HbA1c, even in cases of poor glycemic control data and incomplete data [2,10]. However, the aforementioned prediction methods rely heavily on extensive BG monitoring, which can be challenging in clinical settings due to patient noncompliance or availability of glucose monitoring technology (e.g., supply chain issues or lack of affordability). This limitation complicates the accurate estimation of HbA1c trends, especially when glucose data is scarce.

With the rise of language models (LMs), there has been growing interest in their application to DM care. Flory et al. [11] investigated how the GPT-4 compares to endocrinologists in diabetes-related decision-making, finding that while GPT-4’s responses were largely consistent across runs, they diverged from endocrinologist decisions in clinically important ways. Similarly, Ye et al. [12] evaluated the quality of large language model-generated responses compared to physician responses in a rheumatology setting. They found that although AI-generated responses were rated similarly to physician responses by patients, rheumatologists considered them significantly inferior. Pavon et al. [13] underscored the challenges of integrating large language models (LLMs) in diabetes management and advocates for human-in-the-loop (HITL) systems. The study presents GPT-4 as a promising but imperfect tool for clinical decision-making, reinforcing the necessity of human oversight and cautioning against overreliance on AI for diabetes management without rigorous evaluation. While LMs show promise as a clinical decision-support tool, these studies highlight its limitations, emphasizing the necessity of human oversight and the risks of relying on AI-driven diabetes management without rigorous evaluation.

To address these challenges, we explore a reliable deep learning model leveraging the pre-trained knowledge LMs to predict HbA1c trends using easily accessible patient variables such as weight, body mass index (BMI), age, and prior HbA1c values. HbA1c could be estimated from continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data, but would depend on the patient wearing such a device and the clinician having the ability to download and display the CGM data. Thus, HbA1c provides a simpler and more routinely available measure, making it highly valuable in everyday clinical practice despite its less frequent testing (typically every 3–6 months). As the correlation between the HbA1c level and the monitored blood glucose profile has been well-studied, we are motivated by the observation that blood glucose level is (linearly or non-linearly) associated with the weight loss changes or other easily accessible patient-level variables [14]. Therefore, our model leverages widely available patient characteristics to capture these complex relationships, providing an alternative to approaches that require dense glucose monitoring data.

We introduce GANs and LM boosted Neural Network for Diabetes Mellitus (GLM-DM), a novel approach that leverages pre-trained knowledge from language models to predict HbA1c trends using demographic and metabolic data collected at the point of care. GANs [15] are a type of neural network framework where a generator and a discriminator compete, allowing realistic synthetic data to be produced. To mitigate the high cost of clinical data collection and the challenge of limited clinical data, we incorporate GANs to augment the training dataset. By learning class-level distributions for different patient types, GANs enhance our model’s generalization ability while reducing the required amount of patient data. We then utilize the pre-knowledge and representation power of LMs, such as OpenAI’s transformer-based models, to map the augmented features into an embedding space. Finally, we introduce a neural network structure to learn the relationships between the two types of embeddings, enabling the stable prediction of HbA1c trends.

Dataset and preprocessing

The dataset we used to develop the prediction model were from surveys collected from an outpatient endocrinology clinic of an academic medical center. Surveys were performed as part of a project examining social media use by DM patients [16]. Sociodemographic data collected included age, gender, race/ethnicity, marital status, financial income, educational level, and employment status. DM-specific data consisted of DM type, duration, and mode of treatment. In particular, treatment information (prior therapy) was included as a feature, covering categories such as insulin-only therapy, oral agents, non-insulin injectables, combinations of these, and no medication. Survey data was also linked to obtain information on weight, BMI, BG, lipids, and HbA1c at 3 (n = 3m), 6 (n = 6m), and 12 (n = 12m) months after the index visit (n = 0). DM classification was based on clinical history. For example, patients with a history of early age of onset DM, history of diabetic ketoacidosis, and dependency on insulin since diagnosis was considered type 1 DM, while later age of onset and use of non-insulin therapies was consistent with type 2 DM. As shown in Table 1, there were some cases whose DM was due to other etiologies (e.g., glucocorticoid use, pancreatectomy) designated as “Other”.

Detailed characteristics of patients.

For statistical modeling, these sociodemographic and metabolic data are referred to as the “basic features” for every patient, which were extracted and preprocessed. Detailed feature type and used preprocessed methods are presentated in Table 1. Specifically, to preprocess the categorical and ordinal type of data, we used one-hot encoding [17] to vectorize them. For continuous values, e.g., we use min-max scaling to normalize the data. Furthermore, we computed the change of weight and BMI in 3, 6, and 12 months after index visit compared to starting point and refer them as “trend features”. Similarly, HbA1c values at these time points were compared to baseline (HbA1c0), and their differences (e.g., HbA1c3ₘ − HbA1c0, HbA1c6ₘ − HbA1c0, HbA1c12ₘ − HbA1c0) were computed and, where applicable, incorporated as input trend features alongside weight and BMI; the corresponding HbA1c trends at each future time point were subsequently used as supervised signals (labels). This design allowed us to capture both baseline patient status and temporal dynamics of key variables.

We then applied locally linear projection [18] to extract a d dimensional basic feature vector for each patient, denoted by x1∈Rd (xu,1 represent the x1 of patient u), which is to enable our model to exploit the personalized information. Based on the assumption that there exists a relationship between the weight and BMI to BG, and that BG has a significant correlation with HbA1c, weight (BMI) and HbA1c can be bridged by some unknown function. This motivates us to predict the trend of HbA1c from the trend of weight and BMI. We computed the change of weight and BMI in 3, 6, and 12 months after index visit and refer them as “trend features” for the purposes of training models. Specifically, given a patient u and the current timestamp t, let wt(u),bt(u) be the weight and BMI of patient u at the timestamp t. To calculate the trend, the weight wt^(u) and BMI bt^(u) at a past timestamp t^ are required to be accessible. Then, we can acquire the trend wt−t^(u)=wt(u)−wt^(u);bt−t^ (u)=bt(u)−bt^(u), which take crucial roles in the input of the proposed model. We refer to xu,2={wt−t^(u),bt−t^ (u)} as the “trend” feature of patient u or x2 for brevity.

LM-driven feature embedding

To enhance feature representation, we first leverage language models to encode patient basic features into meaningful embeddings. Specifically, we apply an LM-based encoder to further transform augmented basic features into a d dimensional embedding vector, denoted by x′1∈ℝd (where x′u,1 represent the x1 of patient u). This transformation allows our model to capture personalized patterns, extract relevant medical semantics, and preserve latent relationships within patient data that may not be explicitly apparent in raw numerical values. By embedding these features into a high-dimensional space, we enable the model to better understand subtle variations in patient characteristics and enhance predictive accuracy.

Furthermore, to maximize the expressive power of trend features, we extend the LM-based encoding to transform trend-related variables as well. Specifically, we input the raw trend features x2 into the same LM-based encoder, generating a semantically rich embedding vector, denoted as x′2∈ℝd, which is aligned with the embedded basic features. This additional representation step allows the model to capture time-dependent variations in patient health trajectories, ensuring that trend features are mapped into the same latent space as static patient attributes.

By representing patient data in this way, we uncover subtle patterns within basic and trend features respectively and uncover hidden correlations between static and dynamic patient attributes. Importantly, our approach does not rely on LMs for direct final prediction but instead utilizes their pre-trained knowledge to enrich feature representations. This prevents over-reliance on LM-generated outputs while ensuring that the downstream HbA1c trend prediction model, which operates independently of the LM and receives a more robust, semantically meaningful input. This distinction allows for greater interpretability, adaptability to new patient data, and improved predictive accuracy, even in cases where LM-generated decisions might otherwise be unreliable.

Results

Patient data characteristics

The detailed characteristics of 257 patients and feature preprocessing methods are presented in Table 1. There are 60 patients with Type1 DM, 190 patients with Type 2 DM and 7 patients whose DM type was not specified. The mean age was 62 years, most were men, and Caucasian. The average time since DM diagnosis was 17 years. The majority were also on insulin therapy. About 75% had some higher education, and almost two-thirds were employed. The above variables form the “basic” features. The average weight and BMI of all patients was 84 kg and 31.2 kg/m2. We also had patients’ weight, BMI and HbA1c across a 12-month span, such that we can compute the “trend” feature and the HbA1c trend as labels for each set of training samples.

Experimental setup

To show the effectiveness of the proposed model, we chose 4 classic classification models and 2 transformer-based methods for comparison: (1) the Random Forest [21], a bagging ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates their outputs to improve robustness and reduce overfitting; (2) XGBoost [22], boosting-based ensemble method that iteratively refines decision trees, making it a strong performer in tabular data classification tasks; (3) Support-Vector Machines (SVM) Classifier [22] which attempts to find an optimal hyperplane to maximize class separability, making it effective in high-dimensional spaces; and (4) Ridge Classifier [23], a linear regression with L2-regularization; (5) FT-Transformer [24] is an adaptation of Transformer architecture, leveraging attention mechanisms to capture complex feature dependencies.; and (6) TP-BERTa [25] is a specifically pre-trained language model for tabular data. As these models cannot learn the unknown linear relationship between the basic feature and trend feature, we choose the following formula as the input for the baselines: x1⊗θ1⋅x2, where θ1=[1,1]. This formulation ensures that both static and trend-based patient features contribute to the prediction process.

In our implementation, we use the OpenAI’s pre-trained language model, text-embedding-3-small to generate feature representations. Both the proposed model and baselines used identical inputs and labels, trained and evaluated 10 times using cross-validation, and we reported the mean of accuracy with the standard deviation in Results Section.

Modeling results

Table 2 shows that our model (with GAN) outperforms baselines with a notable average accuracy of 70.2%±1.57%. First, our deep learning architecture automatically learns the complex, non-linear relationships between basic and trend features—an essential advantage over traditional classification models. Second, the GAN-based data augmentation mitigates overfitting and enhances generalization by synthesizing diverse patient representations. Additionally, LMs provide rich semantic embeddings, capturing latent feature interactions and improving the overall predictive power of our model. Among all the methods, Gradient Boosting performed the second-best, demonstrating its effectiveness as a strong classifier in general tasks. However, its accuracy remains significantly lower than our proposed approach, highlighting the limitations of conventional techniques on specific domains. The SVM and Ridge classifiers can be thought of as linear-based classification model, which failed to capture the non-linear relation between patient features and HbA1c trend. Random Forest classifier is a sum of decision trees, of which performance is usually worse than Gradient Boosting.

Our approach achieves 68.2% accuracy for HAb1c trend prediction on Type 1 patients and 72.7% accuracy on Type 2 DM patients, respectively. These results indicate that the model can capture type-specific relationships, such as the stronger influence of weight and BMI changes in Type 2 DM, and non-linear relationship between basic features and trend features, leading to more accurate predictions.

The ablation study results, summarized in Table 3, clearly illustrate the contribution of each component in the GLM-DM model. The baseline version of GLM-DM, which excludes both the GAN and LM components, achieves a testing accuracy of 63.1% with a standard deviation of ±6.61%. This indicates that GAN can facilitate the generalization ability of neural network model. Finally, the full GLM-DM model, which includes both GAN and LM components, reaches the highest testing accuracy of 70.2% with a lower standard deviation of ±0.57%. These results demonstrate that both GAN and LM significantly enhance the model’s performance, with the combination of both yielding the best predictive accuracy and stability.

Discussion

In this paper, we present a method for predicting HbA1c trends using easily accessible patient-level data, reducing reliance on continuous blood glucose (BG) monitoring. Our approach leverages the pre-trained knowledge and semantic understanding of LMs to enhance feature representation. Rather than using LMs for direct decision-making, we employ them as a representation learning tool, extracting meaningful embeddings that capture complex relationships between patient attributes. These patient-level variables, including demographic information, weight, and BMI, are significantly easier to obtain than continuous BG measurements. By utilizing LM-encoded embeddings, our model enhances feature representation, allowing for a more accurate prediction of HbA1c trends. This could help better inform patients on their weight management that could affect future metabolic control and reduce the risks of serious DM-related complications. Since a patient’s demographic information is typically static (with the exception of increasing age), the input to this model is primarily weight or BMI over a given period. The model can then predict changes in HbA1c trends (increase, steady, or decrease) with a manually set threshold, such as γ = 0.5. If the predicted trend is “steady,” it indicates that the HbA1c level is stable, with a change in HbA1c of less than 0.5. Therefore, no further BG or HbA1c testing is necessary for that patient. This would be particularly clinically helpful if this data is lacking at the point of care. Conversely, if the predicted trend is “increase,” it suggests that the patient’s HbA1c index has increased by at least 0.5 compared to the last test. The patient should then be vigilant about their diet and exercise habits and is recommended to schedule a clinical visit if our model continues to predict an “increase.” In many cases, when the predicted trend is “decrease,” this would provide positive feedback that recent treatment or lifestyle changes have been effective for the patient. Therefore, based on our model’s predictions using easily accessible weight or BMI, patients can be more aware of their HbA1c status and make more informed decisions, potentially reducing the need for unnecessary BG or HbA1c testing.

The experiment results show that the proposed model can accurately predict the trend of HbA1c on our dataset (up to 70.2% accuracy). Although the dataset is relatively small, our technique can help alleviate this challenge. By utilizing GANs to learn the class-level conditional distribution of patients, we can generate synthetic data that enhances model generalization. This prevents the predictive model from overfitting or memorizing individual data points, allowing it to capture the overall trend more effectively. Although GANs help reduce the dependency on dataset size, acquiring more real data remains the most direct way to enhance the performance of classification models.

Another important factor is how to exploit the basic features and trend features of patients. The basic features are usually the profiles of patients, which could be exploited by the models to obtain patient-specific knowledge. The trend features include weight, BMI, and more features that can be considered, such as blood pressure, and time of physical activities. These features all have potential connections with the change of HbA1c, aiming to explore in the future. However, based on our experiments, it is difficult to predict the HbA1c trend exclusively based on either the basic features or trend features, as shown in Figures 2 and 3. By analyzing the correlation between these two types of features, our model successfully learns their relationship, combining them to enhance HbA1c trend prediction. When combining both basic and trend features together to predict the trend of HbA1c, as visualized in Figure 3(b), the newly obtained embeddings (features) are easier to classify. Therefore, we believe HbA1c and trend features are non-linearly correlated, and thus we use the deep neural network to learn the non-linear function from HAb1c to variables including weight and BMI change, given that the neural network has been proven to be the universal approximator [26]. Combining basic features with weight and BMI changes enables accurate HbA1c trend predictions.

A key aspect of our approach is how LMs are integrated into the framework. Instead of relying on LMs for direct decision-making, we use them as a representation learning tool to extract semantically rich embeddings from patient features. Pre-trained LMs, such as OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small, encode vast linguistic and medical knowledge, allowing the model to better capture feature dependencies and contextual relationships. However, while LMs enhance feature representation, they are not responsible for generating final predictions. This prevents over-reliance on LMs, ensuring that decision-making is rooted in structured predictive modeling rather than AI heuristics. By treating LMs as a ladder for feature extraction rather than an autonomous decision-maker, we improve the robustness, interpretability, and clinical reliability of our model.

This study has several limitations. First, our model predicts HbA1c trends rather than exact values, which limits its direct clinical interpretability. Extending the framework to forecast precise HbA1c values would require larger datasets, richer input features, and more sophisticated models, and such approaches must be rigorously evaluated before clinical deployment. Second, although language models help encode patient information into useful representations, they were not used for direct diabetes management or clinical decision-making in this work, and future research should carefully explore their role with appropriate human-in-the-loop supervision.

A third limitation relates to biases that affect HbA1c results. For instance, recent blood trasfusions, or hemolytic disorders with high red cell turnover can result in low HbA1c values, and the model proposed here would not be valid in those scenarios. However, the patients included here were stable, ambulatory patients. Fourth, our models inlcuded therapies at the time of the visit, rather than therapeutic changes made over time (a much more realistic clinical scenario). Additional studies that incorporate the more dynamic features of therapeutic changes could be tested. Finally, further studies are needed to determine how to operationalize this method in real-world settings, such as integrating real-time predictions into patient care to guide education and behavioral interventions.

Conclusion

In this paper, we study the problem of predicting the HbA1c trend in a period based on easily accessible patient-level data, such as the personal profile features and the change in body index, eliminating the need for continuous blood glucose monitoring. To achieve this, we formulated basic features and trend features as inputs and developed a neural network-based model to learn their complex relationships for HbA1c trend prediction. To mitigate limited clinical data availability, we integrated GANs to model class-level distributions and synthesize realistic patient data, improving the model’s generalization ability. Additionally, we leveraged LMs for feature representation, utilizing their pre-trained knowledge and semantic understanding to enhance embeddings without relying on them for direct decision-making. By employing LMs as a representation learning tool rather than a decision-maker, we ensure more reliable predictions while avoiding over-dependence on language models. Our experiments demonstrate that this approach outperforms existing classification models in accuracy and robustness. The proposed model can serve as a valuable tool for diabetes management, helping patients better understand the impact of weight management on metabolic control and reducing the risk of severe diabetes-related complications.

Future work

Our current model predicts HbA1c trends rather than specific values, a limitation we aim to address by developing a method to directly forecast the HbA1c index from accessible features. This work is more challenging because due to the need for precise predictions (specific numbers for the HbA1c) based on limited information, requiring larger data sets, refined input features, and greater accuracy. Accordingly, the model will be more complicated and should be evaluated extensively and professionally to implement in the real world. Despite these challenges, our success in trend prediction offers hope for accurately forecasting the HbA1c index. Although LMs help encode patient information into rich representations, we do not use them for direct diabetes management or final clinical decision-making. This distinction is crucial in ensuring that the model captures complex relationships within patient data, avoiding over-reliance on LMs in high-stakes healthcare applications. To enhance model’s capacity and generalizability, we recognize the importance of leveraging LMs for direct decision-making output. A promising future work to address this challenge is through human-in-the-loop supervision. This can be achieved through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which aligns model-generated predictions with expert-validated outcomes. What’s more, future work is needed to better determine how to “operationalize” this approach, i.e., applying this method in real-time at the patient point-of-care to educate them on how changes in weight may impact their metabolic control.
