Management of Acute Diarrhea in Children after Implementing the Thai Clinical Practice Guideline
Authors: Sittichoke Prachuapthunyachart, Voranush Chongsrisawat, Siriluck Jennuvat, Thitima Ngoenmak, Alisara Damrongmanee, Phisek Yimyaem, Busara Charoenwat, Hansa Sriphongphankul, Punnapatch Piriyanon, Nopaorn Phavichitr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAcute diarrhea in children is a common clinical problem, although practice management can vary. This study evaluates the practice of pediatricians in the management of acute diarrhea in children after implementing the 2019 Thai Clinical Practice Guideline.MethodsOnline questionnaires were sent to Thai pediatricians (general pediatricians or other pediatric subspecialists [GPs] and pediatric gastroenterologists [PGs]) from November 2022 to April 2023. This online survey collected anonymous demographic data and practice-related data.ResultsA total of 210 pediatricians (179 GPs and 31 PGs) participated in the survey. PGs administered intravenous fluids less often than GPs (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.12; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.04–0.38; p<0.001) but added potassium after patients began to urinate more than GPs (aOR, 4.76; 95% CI, 1.85–12.27; p<0.001). PGs preferred to use reduced-osmolality oral rehydration salts (ORS) more than GPs (aOR, 9.81; 95% CI, 2.63–36.60; p<0.001). Both GPs and PGs agreed to continue breastfeeding (88.1%). However, fewer PGs recommended switching to lactose-free formula for formula-fed patients (aOR, 0.36; 95% CI, 0.14–0.92; p=0.04). PGs were more likely to have experiences using adjunctive therapy, such as racecadotril and diosmectite (aOR, 7.86; 95% CI, 1.69–36.52; p=0.009 and aOR, 15.42; 95% CI, 2.90–33.63; p<0.001, consecutively). Domperidone was mostly used for patients with the first vomiting episode (52.4%).ConclusionThis survey provides insight into the practices of Thai pediatricians for the management of acute diarrhea in children. Variations in the use of oral antibiotics, antiemetics, adjunctive drugs, electrolyte supplementation, and feeding were observed, which may not align with the guideline.

INTRODUCTION

Acute diarrhea in children is a major public health problem and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children worldwide, including Thailand. According to the World Health Organization data, diarrhea is the third leading cause of death in children under 5 years old. It is responsible for killing around 443,832 children under 5 and an additional 50,851 children aged 5 to 9 years each year [1]. Findings from the Global Burden of Disease Study indicated that diarrheal diseases were among the leading causes of death. However, there has been a decreasing trend from 1990 to 2021, with the ranking shifting from 5th to 14th. The age-standardized rate per 100,000 declined from 60.6 to 15.4 during this period [2]. Similarly, the decreasing trend of epidemiology has been observed in Thailand over the 10 years from 2013 to 2022. The incidence has reduced from 1,756.48 to 1,001.72 per 100,000, respectively, with no reported deaths in 2022. Despite the decreasing trend of acute diarrhea, the highest-prevalence age groups were 0–4 years old (3,929.48 per 100,000), followed by 5–9 years old (1,419.30), and over 65 years old (1,177.37), respectively. The epidemiology report underscored the burden of diarrheal illness in young children, especially in the 0–4-year-old age group, which held the highest records over the past 5 years. January was the month with the highest incidence report. The cluster with the highest number of patients was in a school affected by norovirus and rotavirus. Schools were the location where most clusters occurred, accounting for 5 of 8 events. Sources of illness included contaminated water, ice, and food that was cooked or prepared a long time in advance. Norovirus was reported as the most common pathogen [3].

Several clinical practice guidelines have been developed to help guide physicians in managing acute diarrhea in children [4567]. In 2019, the Thai Society of Pediatric Gastroenterology and Hepatology implemented the 2019 Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) for Acute Diarrhea in Children [8]. This CPG is intended to be used as a reference for the management of children with acute diarrhea who are older than 1 month, have normal immunity, and do not have severe malnutrition (Supplementary Table 1). The CPG provides recommendations in the following areas: definition, assessment of dehydration, laboratory testing, management of dehydration, appropriate feeding, adjunctive therapy, antiemetics, and antibiotics. However, pediatricians may still have a variety of practices depending on their place of work, environmental factors, and access to the guidelines. Therefore, this study would help to assess the practices of pediatricians after the publication of the CPG. The study aimed to assess the practices of pediatricians on acute diarrhea in children and to identify factors that may affect the practices of Thai pediatricians for further development of care for this group of patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was a questionnaire-based cross-sectional survey approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (approval No. 0373/65). The target population of this study was general pediatricians or other pediatric subspecialists (GPs) and pediatric gastroenterologists (PGs) in Thailand who took care of children with acute diarrhea. To recruit participants, questionnaires and promotional posters were sent to pediatricians with available contact information via a nationwide database. Informed consent from participants was obtained via the online questionnaire, which included the contact information of the principal investigator for questions. By completing the questionnaire and returning it, participants consented to the anonymous and voluntary collection of data. Their practice data were collected anonymously and divided into five sections: (1) participant information (anonymous), (2) diagnosis and laboratory tests, (3) inpatient care and fluid management, (4) dietary modifications and medications, (5) knowledge of the CPG and recommendations. The participant information section included the following items: type of pediatric specialty, age, years of experience as a pediatrician, region of practice, type of practice setting, and number of beds in the practice setting.

Data were collected from Google Forms and extracted. STATA software (version 17; StataCorp) was used for statistical analysis. Mean±standard deviation, and frequencies and percentages are used to describe characteristics. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher exact tests. Continuous variables were compared using a t-test. Logistic regression analysis was used to determine the association of variables. The results are expressed as odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values. Differences with p<0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

From November 2022 to April 2023, 1,826 invitations were sent via e-mail blasts and a social group application. A total of 210 pediatricians (179 GPs, 31 PGs) completed the questionnaire, resulting in an 11.5% response rate. The mean ages of both groups were not significantly different. However, PGs generally had more experience, with the majority practicing for 11–20 years, whereas most GPs had 5 years of practice or less (p<0.001). PGs were also more likely to practice in Bangkok and work in larger hospital settings (p<0.05) (Table 1).

The assessment of the degree of dehydration signs was largely similar between groups. High pulse rates or weak pulses and hypotension were mainly used as the surrogate markers to evaluate mild-to-moderate and severe dehydration (28.6% and 33.3%, respectively). Most pediatricians ordered stool examinations upon patient admission, but fewer than half did so for outpatients. Stool culture was performed in more than 50% of practices by 44.3% of respondents. However, most pediatricians chose not to test stools for viral pathogens, gastrointestinal panel polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and Clostridioides difficile toxin assay. Unavailability of tests in the practice settings was the most common limitation for GPs. Although 44.1% of GPs chose to send stool studies for pathogen identification when symptoms did not improve within 3 days, GPs’ decisions were split equally between day 3 and day 7 of symptoms. PGs chose to perform more investigations for stool pathogens than GPs in the following situations: fever, underlying chronic illness or immunocompromised host, infants younger than 6 months, no improvement in 7 days, history of travel to an endemic area, disease control during an outbreak, and living in an area endemic for protozoa or parasites (Table 2). Most pediatricians (76.5% GPs, 74.2% PGs) agreed to send a complete blood count in every hospitalized patient. Although 64.8% of GPs believed that complete blood count might be helpful to differentiate some causative agents, 38.7% of PGs concurred with this idea. The decision to do a blood culture was mostly congruent between GPs and PGs. Blood cultures were primarily collected in patients with underlying chronic illness or risk of severe symptoms (67.1%), high-grade fever (69.5%), mucous bloody stools with high-grade fever (84.3%), severe symptoms such as somnolence, hypotension, or shock (89.5%), and those receiving intravenous antibiotics (54.3%). PGs decided to request blood culture more often than GPs for patients who had underlying chronic illness or risk of severe symptoms (aOR, 3.19; 95% CI, 1.08–9.42; p=0.035).

Indications for hospitalization went in the same direction. Most pediatricians admitted patients when they had the age lower than 3 months old (83.8%), high-graded fever (79.5%), severe dehydration, or shock (98.6%), decreased urine output (82.4%), decreased oral intake (87.1%), little oral rehydration salts (ORS) intake (51.9%), profuse watery diarrhea (72.4%), severe vomiting (83.8%), alteration of consciousness (94.8%), or underlying chronic illness (71.9%). Although both standard World Health Organization oral rehydration salts (WHO-ORS) and reduced-osmolality ORS (RO-ORS) were commonly recommended by pediatricians, PGs showed a strong preference for RO-ORS compared to GPs (aOR, 9.81; p=0.001). Most pediatricians chose to give intravenous fluids to patients with severe vomiting with limited oral intake, moderate dehydration, or severe dehydration or shock. However, PGs were less likely than GPs to give intravenous fluids for moderate dehydration (aOR, 0.12; 95% CI, 0.04–0.38; p<0.001). The majority agreed on giving an isotonic solution to patients with shock (97.1%) and giving intravenous fluid in the first phase of therapy without giving bicarbonate salts (72.9%). During the maintenance phase, most pediatricians administered 5% dextrose in half-strength normal saline solution to younger children >1 month old and 5% dextrose in normal saline solution in older children with adjustment according to serum sodium level (43.8%). More PGs than GPs added potassium to intravenous fluid during the maintenance phase after patients urinated (aOR, 4.76; p=0.002). By contrast, more GPs chose to monitor serum potassium levels and added potassium when levels were low (Table 3).

In breast-fed infants with diarrhea, both GPs and PGs agreed to continue breastfeeding (88.1%). Recommendations regarding diet for older children with diarrhea who took formula or cow’s milk along with an age-appropriate diet were otherwise inconsistent. Fewer PGs recommended switching to lactose-free formula (aOR, 0.36; p=0.04), while more PGs advocated for early feeding within 4–6 hours (aOR, 3.34; p=0.001) and avoiding sugary beverages (aOR,10.10; p<0.001) (Table 3).

Adjunctive treatments, including racecadotril, diosmectite, and probiotics/synbiotics, were used more commonly by PGs than GPs. Most usage limitations were due to high cost and no universal coverage. Zinc was given to patients older than 6 months with suspected zinc deficiency or malnutrition in 35.7% of the respondents. The two most used antiemetic agents were ondansetron and domperidone, 42.4% vs. 52.4% as the first vomiting episode and 69.0% vs. 14.8% as the subsequent episodes, respectively. Most pediatricians (87.6%) prescribed antibiotics on fewer than half of the occasions. Most common indications for empirical antibiotic treatment include diarrhea with blood and mucus accompanied by fever, suspected sepsis or severe symptoms, infants younger than 3 months with bloody stools, immunocompromised patients, and suspected Vibrio cholerae infection. The most common oral empirical antibiotic prescriptions were cephalosporins (51.0%), followed by fluoroquinolones (31.0%) and azithromycin (10.0%), whereas cephalosporins (95.2%) were the most common intravenous empirical antibiotic therapy (Table 4).

Most GPs (75.4%) and PGs (100.0%) were aware of the 2019 Thai Clinical Practice Guideline (CPG) for Acute Diarrhea in Children, published by the Thai Society of Pediatric Gastroenterology and Hepatology [8]. The most common challenges in following the guideline faced by both GPs and PGs included drug or adjuvant administration (43.8%), laboratory investigation (31.0%), and antibiotic prescription (27.6%). Analysis of the GP cohort based on practice duration revealed that awareness of the 2019 Thai CPG was significantly higher among those with less than 5 years of experience compared to those with 5 or more years (93% vs. 64%, p<0.001). DISCUSSION

The present survey investigated the practice of pediatricians across the entire country for the management of acute diarrhea in children, categorized into GPs and PGs. Demographic data revealed differences between the groups, including the duration of their work experience, regions of practice, and number of hospital beds, which may explain the differences in practice, at least in part.

Assessment of dehydration is essential but may be challenging. Three standard clinical evaluation methods are the WHO scale, the clinical dehydration scale (CDS), and the Gorelick scale. A systematic review reported that the CDS can help assess moderate-to-severe dehydration in high-income settings, but that the WHO and Gorelick scales are not helpful [9]. However, measuring capillary refill time using a novel noninvasive technology at the bedside, along with temperature and body mass index, can categorize dehydration accurately and may be used in low-resource settings [10]. The Thai CPG suggests using three components to evaluate for dehydration, including skin turgor, capillary refill time, and respiratory pattern. In the current study, the respondents assessed dehydration with some misinterpretation, using high pulse rates or weak pulses for mild-to-moderate dehydration instead of severe dehydration. A previous study in Thailand showed that the severity of dehydration was not documented in 60% of cases [11]. This deficit highlights the importance of improvement in the documentation of dehydration. The severity of dehydration should be assessed in all cases of acute diarrhea, regardless of whether hospitalization is required, because it could help to ensure that patients receive the appropriate level of care. Most respondents preferred to have laboratory tests conducted in a hospital setting rather than an outpatient setting. General pediatricians were more convinced to use complete blood count to identify causative agents, which might be misleading for the treatment. Other indications from the respondents to do more laboratory testing appeared appropriate. The unavailability and excessive costs were the limitations for some advanced stool testing, such as viral pathogen and gastrointestinal panel PCR. Despite the limitation, unnecessary laboratory testing appears to be a matter of concern, as has also been described in previous studies. A study from multiple institutions in Italy found that up to 57.5% of all children with acute diarrhea were hospitalized unnecessarily, and the most common area of noncompliance in practice was laboratory testing (35.8%) [12]. Similarly, a survey from Korea indicated laboratory testing for pathogens was more common for hospitalized patients [13]. By contrast, the previous study in Thailand, which evaluated knowledge and clinical practice for the treatment of acute diarrhea in children, found that laboratory tests were performed in less than 20% of cases. Such a finding could be due to the low hospitalization rate, with fewer than 10% of cases [11]. Despite the rising trend of laboratory testing, investigation for causative pathogens should still be considered in all cases of acute diarrhea in children with severe symptoms or risk factors for complications. This investigation can help identify patients who may benefit from antibiotic therapy and proper management.

The present study showed that most pediatricians conformed to the CPG in the indication for patient admission and initial fluid management. Most pediatricians decided to admit patients who had severe dehydration, alteration of consciousness, decreased oral intake, and decreased urine output. The findings are consistent with those from a study in Korea, which reported that severe dehydration was the most common indication for hospitalization, and the initial treatment was intravenous fluids [13]. The present study found that PGs chose to add potassium in intravenous fluid during the maintenance phase after patients’ urination more than GPs (74.2% vs. 40.8%, p=0.002). By contrast, more GPs planned to monitor serum potassium and corrected it when the level was low. In addition, PGs significantly followed the recommendations in terms of using RO-ORS, early feeding within 4–6 hours after initiating rehydration therapy, and not changing the formula to a lactose-free formula more than GPs (aOR, 9.81, 3.34, 0.36, respectively). By contrast, GPs preferred using standard WHO-ORS for rehydration therapy and delayed food intake. These differences likely stem from the universal awareness of the 2019 CPG among the PG group (100.0% vs. 75.4% in GPs) and a transitional confusion regarding ORS formulations. While RO-ORS is now the predominant formulation available nationwide, many practitioners remain accustomed to standard WHO-ORS protocols from earlier training. In addition, some practitioners may be unaware that the ORS they are currently using is the RO-ORS formulation, even though the original WHO-ORS formula has been phased out and is no longer available. While the differences in fluid and electrolyte management between GPs and PGs may appear subtle in a survey setting, they are of paramount importance in clinical practice. The transition from standard WHO-ORS to RO-ORS, which was favored significantly more by PGs (84% vs. 48%), is not merely a change in formulation but a critical step in reducing treatment failure. RO-ORS has been shown to decrease stool output and the need for unscheduled intravenous fluids compared to older, higher-osmolality solutions.

Furthermore, the management of potassium supplementation highlights a significant disparity in safety protocols. PGs were nearly twice as likely as GPs to add potassium to maintenance intravenous fluids once urination was confirmed (74.2% vs. 40.8%). This proactive approach reflects a strict adherence to the pathophysiology of acute diarrhea, where significant potassium loss is common. By contrast, the wait-and-see approach of monitoring serum levels before correction, favored by many GPs, may delay treatment for hypokalemia. This may lead to complications such as paralytic ileus or cardiac arrhythmias in vulnerable children. Therefore, these subtle deviations from the guideline represent missed opportunities for optimizing recovery and preventing preventable iatrogenic complications during hospitalization. Practice on the dietary recommendations aligned with a previous multicenter study in Italy, which found dietary change as the second most common noncompliant area of management [12]. A survey study in Korea also showed that general pediatricians used medications and recommended dietary changes slightly more than gastroenterologists [13]. Such findings highlight the necessity to evaluate the reasons for noncompliance with recommendations for dietary change, especially by nonpediatric gastroenterologists.

PGs prescribed adjunctive therapies, including racecadotril, diosmectite, and probiotics/synbiotics, significantly more often than GPs. A possible explanation is that most PGs are based in the capital city and large hospitals, which may provide them with more opportunities to conduct investigations for stool pathogens and prescribe adjunctive therapy. A previous survey found that probiotic use was more prevalent among older pediatricians and those working in the private sector [14]. Strains and duration of treatment remain an essential topic. The European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition recommended the use of various probiotic strains, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, Saccharomyces boulardii, and L. reuteri in children with acute diarrhea, based on evidence with a low to very low certainty and a weak grade of recommendation [15]. By contrast, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis showed a low certainty of evidence for the effect of probiotics on clinical cure and duration of diarrhea in children. There was significant diversity in the genus, species, dosages, and duration of treatment in the trial, and administration with high levels of heterogeneity [16]. Therefore, the cost-effectiveness of probiotics as well as other adjunctive therapies should be a matter of concern.

Most of the PGs and GPs did not prescribe antibiotics routinely in acute childhood diarrhea. They followed the CPG for antibiotic usage in acute diarrhea, which advocates for the usefulness of the guideline implementation. Antibiotic use rationale appeared to be reasonable in other previous studies as well, although a survey in Italy indicated 9.2% prescribed non-recommended antibiotics [12]. Owing to the high rate of cephalosporin resistance in Thailand, the guideline recommended fluoroquinolones as the first-choice empirical antibiotics for outpatients. However, in this survey, the most prescribed antibiotics were cephalosporins, followed by fluoroquinolones, azithromycin, and cotrimoxazole. This may be due to historical safety concerns regarding fluoroquinolone use in children, the unavailability of specific syrup formulations, or a lack of awareness regarding current local resistance patterns and the 2019 guideline recommendations. The European guideline reported that ondansetron is an effective antiemetic in children with acute gastroenteritis [4]. According to the Thai CPG, a single dose of ondansetron may be effective for combating vomiting in acute diarrhea in children older than 6 months [8]. However, domperidone appeared to be the most prescribed antiemetic for the first vomiting episode in this study (52.4%), followed by ondansetron (42.4%). Ondansetron prescribing rate rose to 69.0% for the next vomiting episodes. Domperidone use was common, which is consistent with a previous study from a single center in Thailand and a survey in Korea [1113]. This is probably due to the availability of its syrup form. However, its routine use has not been supported in the guideline because of the low level of evidence and potential QT interval prolongation, causing cardiac arrhythmia [17]. Conversely, ondansetron was used less frequently during initial vomiting episodes. This may be attributed to the unavailability of an oral syrup formulation in Thailand, despite evidence supporting its efficacy in pediatric acute gastroenteritis.

Previous research on the clinical practice of pediatricians in the treatment of acute diarrhea in children has shown that following clinical practice guidelines may improve clinical outcomes, reduce economic losses from unnecessary testing, and reduce the risk of complications [1819]. However, previous studies from both developed and developing countries have shown that a lot of pediatricians do not follow clinical practice guidelines [192021]. Increasing awareness of clinical practice guidelines should be implemented. In the present survey, most GPs and all PGs were aware of the published CPG, although noncompliance in some areas was noted. Educational programs for pediatricians and other healthcare providers could help to ensure that they are aware of the latest evidence-based recommendations for the treatment of acute diarrhea. Judicious use of laboratory investigations and the rationale of antibiotic prescription should be recognized. Regarding nutrition, pediatricians should promote early feeding. Children with acute diarrhea should be encouraged to continue or resume feeding as soon as possible. Adequate nutritional intake will help to prevent malnutrition and other complications. In breast-fed infants, breastfeeding should be encouraged. It provides crucial immune protection and can help to reduce the risk of acute diarrhea. Changing to a lactose-free formula should be reserved only for children who develop secondary lactase deficiency from viral gastroenteritis. Notably, the significantly higher awareness of the 2019 CPG among early-career GPs (93% vs. 64% in experienced GPs) did not translate into higher adherence rates for RO-ORS or early feeding protocols. This discrepancy suggests that adherence may be influenced more by institutional protocols and the localized availability of specific medications rather than academic knowledge alone. Consequently, educational programs should not only target individual knowledge but also address systemic barriers to ensure the latest evidence-based recommendations are practical and accessible in daily clinical practice.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the overall response rate was 11.5%, which introduces a risk of selection bias and potentially limits the generalizability of these findings to the broader pediatric community in Thailand. Geographically, a significant portion of respondents, particularly pediatric gastroenterologists, were based in Bangkok, with very low representation from other regions such as the South. Furthermore, most participants practiced in public tertiary or university hospitals, suggesting the results may not fully capture practice patterns in primary care or non-hospital-based settings. Second, as this was a survey-based study, the data rely on self-reported practices rather than objective chart reviews; therefore, responses may reflect perceived adherence to guidelines rather than actual clinical behavior. Finally, the relatively small sample size of pediatric gastroenterologists (n=31) may lead to statistical instability in certain multivariate analyses, and these specific comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

Although many practices, particularly in patient assessment, laboratory testing, and general management, align with the 2019 Thai Clinical Practice Guideline for Acute Diarrhea in Children, variations exist in the use of oral antibiotics, antiemetics, adjunctive medications, types of ORS, potassium supplementation, early feeding, and switching to lactose-free formula. These deviations from the guidelines were significantly different between GPs and PGs.
