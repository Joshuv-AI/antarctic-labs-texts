Industrial energy use and oil dependence as conditional drivers of carbon emissions in Saudi Arabia — evidence from quantile regression
Authors: Uzma Khan, Sahar Abdo Mohamed Elsayed, Aarif Mohammad Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSaudi Arabia’s per capita carbon emissions have risen persistently since the 1970s, driven by energy-intensive industrialisation and a structural reliance on oil. Existing studies have largely treated energy consumption as a direct, additive driver of emissions, overlooking how its environmental impact depends on the industrial structure that absorbs it. This study proposes a conditional emissions framework and tests it on Saudi annual data for 1970–2024, combining an autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) model with an explicit energy–manufacturing interaction term, quantile regression across τ = 0.1–0.9, and Toda–Yamamoto causality analysis. In the long-run ARDL specification, the standalone coefficient on energy consumption is negative; however, the implied marginal effect at the sample-mean manufacturing share is positive (≈ 0.43), and rises monotonically with the manufacturing share of GDP, confirming that energy becomes emissions-intensive only within industrial production (interaction coefficient = 2.49, p < 0.01). The quantile regression results refine rather than overturn this picture: the energy–manufacturing interaction is most evident in the lower-emission regimes and attenuates toward the upper tail. At the same time, oil dependence exerts a consistent positive effect across most of the distribution. A 1984 structural break, linked to the oil-price collapse and the policy pivot toward subsidised industrial energy, is significant across all emission regimes, providing empirical support for carbon lock-in theory. These findings imply that effective decarbonisation under Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 requires restructuring industrial energy use rather than simply reducing its aggregate volume.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53731-w.Keywords: Carbon emissions, Industrialisation, Energy consumption, Oil dependence, Quantile regression, Structural breaks, Carbon lock-in, Conditional emissions frameworkSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Saudi Arabia’s economic transformation since the 1970s has been accompanied by a sustained rise in per capita carbon emissions, driven largely by energy-intensive growth, industrial expansion, and persistent reliance on oil revenues1,2. As the Kingdom advances its Vision 2030 agenda — aimed at diversifying the economic base while improving environmental sustainability — identifying the structural determinants of emissions has become increasingly important3–5. The post-1984 industrial pivot, supported by heavily subsidised domestic energy prices, established the institutional foundations of the energy-intensive industrial complex that continues to shape the Kingdom’s emissions trajectory today6,7.

A substantial body of empirical literature confirms a long-run relationship between economic activity, energy consumption, and carbon emissions in Saudi Arabia. ARDL-based studies consistently document cointegration and causal linkages among these variables2,4,8,9. More recent contributions extend this framework by incorporating non-linearities and asymmetries, demonstrating that positive and negative shocks to economic growth and oil-sector dynamics exert differential effects on emissions1,10,11. Structural-break analyses similarly highlight the importance of oil-market fluctuations and policy transitions in reshaping emissions dynamics over time12,13.

Despite these advances, the existing literature remains anchored in a simplifying assumption: that energy consumption exerts a uniform and direct effect on carbon emissions. Most empirical models rely on aggregate indicators and mean-based estimation techniques, implicitly treating energy as an inherently emissions-generating input2–4,8,9. This assumption is restrictive in three respects. First, it overlooks distributional heterogeneity, failing to examine whether the determinants of emissions differ across low- and high-emission regimes. Second, it neglects the structural interaction between energy use and industrialisation, obscuring how the allocation of energy within production systems shapes environmental outcomes. Third, it under-specifies oil dependence as a structural mechanism, focusing primarily on direct effects rather than on its role within the broader energy–industry nexus.

These limitations are particularly relevant in the Saudi context, where industrial development has historically been underwritten by subsidised energy and oil-derived revenues. The mid-1980s collapse in global oil prices marked a structural transition from revenue-led expansion to energy-intensive industrialisation. This shift likely reinforced long-term carbon dependence by embedding energy-intensive production processes within the industrial sector6. The implications of that transition for the energy–emissions relationship remain insufficiently explored.

This study addresses these gaps by reconceptualising the energy–emissions relationship as conditional rather than intrinsic. It posits that energy consumption does not inherently increase emissions; rather, its environmental impact depends on how it is utilised within the industrial structure and the extent to which this process is reinforced by oil dependence. To operationalise this framework, the study integrates interaction modelling, structural-break analysis, and quantile regression within a unified empirical strategy. An interaction term between energy consumption and industrialisation is introduced to capture their joint effect on emissions; a structural break associated with the mid-1980s oil-price collapse is incorporated to account for regime shifts; and quantile regression is used to uncover heterogeneity in the emissions distribution, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of state-dependent dynamics.

This study makes four distinct contributions to the literature, each addressing a gap that prior Saudi-focused studies have not resolved. First, it is the first study on Saudi Arabia to model energy consumption as a conditional rather than direct driver of emissions, operationalised through an explicit interaction term between energy supply and manufacturing value added. Existing ARDL-based studies4,8,9 include energy as an additive regressor, implicitly assuming a fixed marginal effect across all structural contexts. The present study relaxes this assumption and shows that the marginal emissions elasticity of energy rises with the manufacturing share of GDP — a structurally conditioned mechanism absent from prior specifications.

Second, it provides the first quantile regression analysis of carbon-emission drivers in Saudi Arabia, revealing heterogeneity across low- and high-emission regimes that mean-based ARDL estimates inherently conceal. While Moustafa and Alomran11 and Mahmood et al.1 introduce asymmetric effects via non-linear ARDL, they remain anchored to conditional-mean estimation. They cannot identify regime-dependent effects across the emissions distribution.

Third, it integrates oil dependence as an explicit structural amplifier within an interaction framework — rather than merely as an additional covariate — and demonstrates via Toda–Yamamoto causality that oil rents and emissions form a mutually reinforcing cycle. Mahmood et al.10 examine oil-rent asymmetries but do not jointly model the energy–industry–oil-rent nexus within a single unified specification.

Fourth, by explicitly incorporating the 1984 structural break into both the long-run cointegration framework and the distributional analysis, the study connects an identifiable macroeconomic turning point — the oil-price collapse and the subsequent pivot to subsidised energy-for-industrialisation — to the carbon lock-in trajectory, providing a historically grounded, mechanism-based account with direct policy relevance for Vision 20305,14–16.

The novelty of the study, relative to the existing Saudi literature, is threefold. Methodologically, it is the first to combine interaction-based cointegration modelling with quantile regression in this context. Conceptually, it introduces the conditional emissions framework as a theoretical organising device — distinct from the EKC hypothesis and additive growth–emissions models — that explicitly positions industrialisation as a moderating variable rather than an independent determinant. Empirically, by showing that the pre-1984 structural-break dummy is significant and positive across all quantiles from τ = 0.1 to 0.9, it provides the first econometric evidence linking Saudi Arabia’s post-oil-shock industrial strategy to a durable, cross-regime carbon lock-in trajectory.

Literature review

Theoretical framework: resource dependence, carbon lock-in, and conditional emissions

This study is grounded in the intersection of the resource-dependence framework17,18 and the concept of carbon lock-in19,20. Carbon lock-in describes the self-reinforcing dynamic by which infrastructure investments, institutional arrangements, and pricing mechanisms entrench carbon-intensive production pathways and create persistent barriers to transition. In resource-dependent economies, this dynamic is amplified: subsidised energy prices and oil-derived fiscal capacity simultaneously support energy-intensive manufacturing and weaken the relative price signal that would otherwise discipline emissions. The conditional emissions framework developed here operationalises this insight by treating the marginal emissions impact of energy as a function of industrial structure rather than as a constant — a perspective absent from additive specifications and one that, as the empirical sections will show, materially changes how the Saudi emissions trajectory should be interpreted.

In resource-dependent economies, abundant fossil-fuel revenues influence economic structures by promoting energy-intensive production systems and discouraging the adoption of energy-efficient alternatives21,22. Over time, this generates structural dependence not only on oil revenues but also on fossil energy as a core input into industrial activity. Carbon lock-in emerges when these structural conditions become self-reinforcing through institutional arrangements, infrastructure investments, and pricing mechanisms that favour carbon-intensive production pathways.

Building on this foundation, the conditional emissions framework specifies that the impact of energy consumption on emissions depends on its interaction with industrial structure and on the level of oil dependence. Within this framework, energy consumption represents the scale input, industrialisation determines the allocation of energy within production, and oil dependence acts as a structural amplifier. Energy consumption does not exert a uniform marginal effect on emissions; instead, its impact varies depending on the structural context in which it is utilised. When energy expansion occurs in less carbon-intensive sectors or is accompanied by efficiency improvements, its effect on emissions may be limited. When energy is absorbed into manufacturing under conditions of resource dependence, it becomes a significant driver of emissions.

The conditional emissions framework also speaks to the literature on rebound effects in industrial energy use23,24. When efficiency improvements lower the effective price of energy services within manufacturing, output may expand sufficiently to offset part — or in extreme cases, all — of the engineering-level energy savings. In a resource-dependent economy with subsidised industrial energy prices, this rebound channel is reinforced by the absence of a price signal that would otherwise discipline expansion. The conditional structure adopted here is therefore consistent with rebound dynamics: the marginal emissions impact of energy is not a property of energy use per se, but of the institutional and pricing context that determines whether efficiency gains are reinvested in output. This perspective complements the carbon lock-in argument by clarifying why aggregate energy-reduction policies may be insufficient.

Economic growth and emissions dynamics

Empirical studies consistently find a positive relationship between economic growth and carbon emissions in Saudi Arabia. ARDL-based analyses confirm long-run cointegration between GDP and emissions, with positive short-run elasticities2–4,8,9. Evidence regarding the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) remains mixed: while some studies identify partial or short-run EKC patterns3,5, others reject its validity over longer horizons1,11. Cross-country evidence from G7 economies suggests that urban and industrial development jointly shape environmental quality through channels that operate beyond aggregate GDP25. This finding motivates a closer look at structural composition rather than aggregate scale.

Industrialisation and urbanisation

Industrialisation is widely recognised as a key driver of emissions in Saudi Arabia. Empirical evidence indicates that increases in industrial activity raise emissions, with asymmetric effects observed between expansion and contraction phases1. Urbanisation similarly contributes to emissions growth, although its impact varies across policy and planning frameworks26. Despite these findings, industrialisation is typically modelled as an independent determinant. Limited attention has been given to its interaction with energy consumption, particularly in shaping emissions intensity across different stages of development. The G7 evidence in Katircioglu25 supports the broader claim that urban-industrial transformation is a structural rather than additive driver of environmental outcomes.

Energy consumption and emissions

Energy consumption remains a central determinant of emissions, particularly in economies dominated by fossil fuels. Empirical estimates suggest that increases in energy use are associated with significant rises in carbon emissions2,4,9,14. Existing studies, however, predominantly rely on additive models, which do not capture the conditional nature of energy use within production systems. Beyond the demand for energy itself, financial-system efficiency has been shown to shape the trajectory of renewable-energy adoption across OECD economies27, reinforcing the broader theoretical point that energy outcomes are mediated by structural conditions rather than determined solely by aggregate quantities.

Oil dependence and structural effects

Oil dependence plays a critical role in shaping emissions dynamics through both direct and indirect channels. Empirical evidence indicates that increases in oil-income shares have strong positive effects on emissions, often exhibiting asymmetry1,10. These effects operate through production structures, investment patterns, and energy-pricing mechanisms. In line with Sodeyfi and Katircioglu28, who establish a long-run interaction among business conditions, industrial production, and crude-oil prices, oil-dependent economies exhibit production structures tightly coupled to oil-market dynamics. Building on this evidence, Katircioglu et al.29 demonstrate the value of treating oil-price changes as a moderator rather than an additive determinant. This methodological insight motivates the interaction-based specification adopted in this study.

Recent contributions in the energy-economics literature emphasise that institutional capacity and geopolitical risk shape the trajectory of energy transitions in non-linear ways, particularly in resource-dependent economies30. This literature, which increasingly relies on panel quantile methods to capture distributional heterogeneity, is methodologically aligned with the present study and reinforces the case for treating oil dependence as a structural amplifier rather than a simple covariate. Saudi Arabia’s emissions trajectory cannot be separated from its position as a swing producer in global oil markets and from the institutional architecture that channels oil revenues into industrial subsidisation.

Structural breaks and regime shifts

Structural breaks associated with oil-price shocks and policy reforms significantly influence emissions dynamics. Studies that incorporate structural change highlight the importance of accounting for regime shifts when modelling the energy–growth–emissions nexus12,13. For Saudi Arabia specifically, the mid-1980s oil-price collapse and the subsequent pivot toward subsidised industrial energy represent a particularly consequential regime shift, the empirical signature of which is captured by the structural-break dummy in the present study.

Research gap and positioning

The existing literature establishes strong relationships among economic growth, energy consumption, industrialisation, and emissions, while also recognising non-linearities and structural breaks. Three key gaps remain. First, limited attention has been paid to interactions between energy consumption and industrial structure. Second, oil dependence is rarely integrated as a structural mechanism within this interaction. Third, distributional heterogeneity across emission regimes remains underexplored. This study addresses these gaps by integrating interaction modelling, structural break analysis, and quantile regression within a unified framework, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of emissions dynamics in a resource-dependent economy.

Methodology

This study employs a multi-stage econometric framework to investigate the drivers of per capita carbon emissions in Saudi Arabia from 1970 to 2024. The analysis progresses from establishing basic data properties to modelling asymmetric effects and causal dynamics, ensuring robustness at each stage.

Data and preliminary analysis

The study uses annual time-series data for Saudi Arabia spanning 1970–2024. The dependent variable is the natural logarithm of carbon emissions per capita (LNCP). The independent variables are: the log of manufacturing value added as a percentage of GDP (LNM); the log of energy supply per capita (LNEC); the log of the interaction term between manufacturing and energy supply (LNINT), included to capture the synergistic effect of industrial activity and energy use; and the log of oil rents as a percentage of GDP (LNO). In this log-log specification, the coefficient on LNINT measures how the elasticity of emissions with respect to energy supply varies with the manufacturing share of GDP. A dummy variable (D01) is incorporated to account for a significant structural break, taking the value 1 for 1970–1983 and 0 thereafter, as identified by the subsequent testing.

Multicollinearity considerations and identification. The simultaneous inclusion of LNEC, LNM and their interaction LNINT introduces predictable multicollinearity among regressors, as is standard with multiplicative interaction specifications. We rely on three mitigating considerations rather than dropping any variable. First, the ARDL framework’s distributed-lag structure absorbs a portion of the contemporaneous collinearity between levels, which is the principal reason ARDL is treated as the inferential benchmark. Second, the central inferences of the paper concern joint, conditional effects (the marginal effect ∂LNCP/∂LNEC = β_LNEC + β_LNINT × LNM) rather than the standalone β_LNEC coefficient, and the inferential validity of joint marginal effects is robust to collinearity even when individual t-statistics are inflated. Third, the structural-break dummy D01 and the oil-dependence variable LNO are not part of the interaction set and remain stable in sign, magnitude and significance across all four estimators (ARDL, FMOLS, DOLS, CCR), which is the most credible robustness signal the data permit. We acknowledge as a methodological limitation that we do not present numerical Variance Inflation Factors or a mean-centred re-specification of the interaction term in this revision; both would be valuable next steps and are noted in Sect. 5.

Both ADF and PP tests are reported to ensure robustness to potential serial correlation and heteroskedasticity in the errors.

Prior to model estimation, the time-series properties of the data were examined. The integration order was determined using the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) and Phillips–Perron (PP) unit root tests31,32. All variables are integrated of order one, I(1), satisfying a key precondition for the ARDL approach (Table 2).

Benchmark long-run specification and interpretation strategy

While multiple cointegration estimators are reported (FMOLS, DOLS, CCR), the ARDL framework is treated as the primary inferential benchmark. Three considerations motivate this choice. First, ARDL explicitly models both short-run dynamics and long-run equilibrium within a unified error-correction framework. Second, it accommodates mixed integration orders, I(0) and I(1). Third, and most importantly for this study, ARDL’s lag structure is more robust to multicollinearity arising from interaction terms, as the distributed-lag specification helps absorb some of the collinearity among regressors.

The FMOLS, DOLS, and CCR estimators are therefore employed strictly as robustness checks to assess the stability of long-run relationships under alternative treatments of endogeneity and serial correlation. Given the presence of the interaction term, these single equation cointegration estimators are known to be sensitive to multicollinearity36. Differences in coefficient magnitude or sign across estimators are interpreted as reflecting this sensitivity rather than as evidence against cointegration itself. The consistency of the structural-break dummy (D01) and oil-dependence variable (LNO) across all estimators provides confidence in the core findings.

Toda–Yamamoto causality test

To examine the predictive direction and precedence between variables, the Toda–Yamamoto modified Granger non-causality test was applied38. This method is advantageous because it does not require pre-testing for cointegration and is valid regardless of the variables’ integration order. A VAR(p) model was estimated, where the optimal lag p was selected, and d_max (the maximum order of integration) extra lags were added. Causality was tested using a Wald test on the first p lags of the variables. This test identifies whether past values of one variable help predict another, establishing directional linkages within the system.

Diagnostic and stability tests

The reliability of the ARDL–ECM estimates was verified through standard diagnostic tests, including the Breusch–Godfrey LM test for serial correlation, the Breusch–Pagan–Godfrey test for heteroskedasticity, and the Jarque–Bera test for normality of residuals. The stability of the long-run parameters was assessed using the Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) and CUSUM of Squares tests39. All models satisfied these diagnostic checks, confirming the validity of the inferences drawn.

Methodological positioning

The empirical strategy follows a clear methodological hierarchy. The ARDL bounds-testing framework serves as the baseline approach for identifying long-run cointegration and short-run dynamics among carbon emissions and their economic drivers. The FMOLS, DOLS, and CCR estimators are employed solely as robustness checks. The quantile regression framework is the study’s main empirical contribution, as it captures heterogeneity and regime-dependent effects across the emissions distribution. Finally, the Toda–Yamamoto causality analysis plays a supporting role, clarifying the direction of influence among variables rather than serving as the primary inferential tool.

Endogeneity and identification strategy

A potential concern in modelling the energy–emissions relationship is endogeneity arising from reverse causality and omitted variables. The ARDL framework partially addresses endogeneity through its distributed-lag structure, which weakens contemporaneous feedback between energy consumption, industrial activity and emissions. The Toda–Yamamoto causality test offers a complementary directional inference. Together, these support a structural reading of the long-run estimates, but neither is a substitute for an instrumental-variable identification strategy. We therefore interpret the long-run coefficients as conditional associations within an internally consistent econometric system, not as strict causal parameters.

A natural extension is to instrument energy consumption and oil dependence using exogenous global oil-market shocks (e.g., post-1973 OPEC supply decisions, the 1986 oil-price collapse, the 2014 shale-driven price decline), with refinery capacity and pre-determined energy-policy variables as additional instruments. We identify this IV extension as the highest-priority follow-on for sharpening causal identification, and discuss it further in Sect. 5.

Marginal-effect interpretation of the energy coefficient

Before turning to the individual coefficients, it is important to clarify how the negative LNEC coefficient (β_LNEC = − 8.33) should be interpreted in the presence of the interaction term LNINT. In the log-log specification, the marginal effect of energy supply on emissions is not given by β_LNEC alone but by ∂LNCP/∂LNEC = β_LNEC + β_LNINT × LNM. At the sample-mean manufacturing share (LNM ≈ 3.52), this evaluates to − 8.33 + 2.49 × 3.52 ≈ 0.43, indicating that, at typical industrialisation levels, energy supply has a positive marginal effect on emissions, with an elasticity of approximately 0.43. The marginal effect is statistically indistinguishable from zero at very low manufacturing shares, becomes positive and significant at the sample mean, and continues to rise at elevated manufacturing levels. The negative standalone coefficient, therefore, reflects the conditional structure of the model — energy use becomes emissions-intensive only as it is absorbed into industrial production — and should not be read as a direct claim that more energy reduces emissions.

Evaluated across the empirically observed range of LNM, the conditional marginal effect of energy supply on emissions is small and statistically weak at the 25th percentile of manufacturing share, positive and significant at the median, and rises further at the 75th percentile. This monotone gradient is the substantive content of the conditional emissions framework: energy supply becomes more emissions-intensive precisely as the economy industrialises, and the long-run estimates are entirely consistent with this reading once the algebra of the interaction model is taken into account.

Coefficient-by-coefficient discussion

With this clarification in place, the remaining long-run coefficients can be read straightforwardly. Manufacturing value added (LNM), entered as a standalone term, is statistically insignificant: the analogous interpretation is that the marginal effect of LNM on emissions also depends on the level of LNEC, and the standalone coefficient should not be interpreted as the total effect of industrialisation. Oil dependence (LNO) carries a positive sign but does not reach significance at the 10% level in the long-run ARDL specification; its consistent significance in the quantile regression (Sect. 4.5) and in the causality analysis (Sect. 4.6) provides stronger evidence for the structural-amplifier role of oil dependence in the Saudi context.

The structural-break dummy (D01) is positive and significant. Note on dummy coding: D01 = 1 for the pre-break period (1970–1983) and D01 = 0 from 1984 onward. The estimated positive coefficient (0.518), therefore, indicates that, conditional on energy supply, manufacturing share, the energy–manufacturing interaction, and oil dependence, the pre-1984 period is associated with systematically higher per capita emissions than the post-break regime. This may seem counterintuitive given the substantive interpretation of post-1984 industrialisation as a driver of carbon lock-in. It is not, because the post-break period is precisely the regime in which the LNINT and LNO channels become quantitatively important; once these structural channels are absorbed into the model, the residual effect attributable to the pre-break period is the higher emissions intensity associated with the early oil-revenue-driven expansion that occurred before energy-for-industrialisation policies took hold. The persistence of D01 across all quantiles (Sect. 4.5) reinforces this reading: the early regime leaves an emissions imprint detectable across the full conditional distribution.

This finding departs from the conventional literature, which typically identifies energy consumption as a direct, additive driver of emissions. The absence of a positive standalone energy effect — once the interaction is taken into account — suggests that aggregate energy use does not inherently translate into higher emissions unless mediated through production structures.

Interaction effects: conditional role of energy consumption

To capture the conditional nature of the energy–emissions relationship, the model incorporates an interaction term between energy consumption and industrialisation. The coefficient on the interaction term is positive (2.49) and statistically significant at the 1% level, indicating that the environmental impact of energy consumption depends critically on its allocation within the industrial sector. As shown in Sect. 4.1, the marginal effect of energy on emissions is ∂LNCP/∂LNEC = β_LNEC + β_LNINT × LNM, which is weak or insignificant at low levels of industrialisation and progressively stronger as industrialisation increases.

Economically, this reflects a structural mechanism whereby energy consumption becomes emissions-intensive only when absorbed into manufacturing and heavy industry. In the Saudi context, this is consistent with the expansion of energy-intensive industrial activities supported by subsidised energy prices and oil revenues. The result provides empirical support for the conditional emissions framework proposed in this study, while the regime-dependent reading developed in Sect. 4.5 refines the framework by showing that this conditional amplification is not uniform across the emissions distribution.

Short-run dynamics and adjustment mechanism

The error correction term (ECT) is negative and statistically significant at the 1% level, confirming the presence of a stable adjustment mechanism toward long-run equilibrium. The magnitude of the ECT (− 0.770) suggests a relatively rapid speed of adjustment: short-run deviations from equilibrium are corrected within a reasonable horizon, with approximately 77% of the disequilibrium absorbed within one year.

Short-run coefficients (Table 6) show that fluctuations in industrial activity and oil dependence have immediate effects on emissions, whereas the impact of energy consumption remains conditional and less pronounced when read through the marginal-effect lens. This pattern reinforces the argument that structural factors dominate the short-term dynamics of emissions.

This alignment between short-run adjustments and long-run equilibrium confirms that emissions dynamics are structurally anchored rather than driven by temporary fluctuations, reinforcing the conditional emissions framework.

Robustness of long-run estimates

Alternative estimators (FMOLS, DOLS, CCR) were examined as robustness checks (see Supplementary Table A1). Due to their sensitivity to multicollinearity in the presence of an interaction term, point estimates varied; however, the qualitative finding that energy and industry jointly determine emissions remained robust, as did the consistent significance of the structural-break dummy D01 and oil dependence LNO across all four estimators. This consistency is the most credible robustness signal the data permit and justifies the focus on the ARDL specification for inferential purposes.

Distributional heterogeneity: quantile regression results

Quantile regression estimates reveal substantial heterogeneity in the drivers of carbon emissions across the conditional distribution of LNCP (Table 7). The discussion that follows reads the standalone LNEC and LNINT coefficients in their conditional form, consistent with the marginal-effect interpretation in Sect. 4.1.

The standalone coefficient on energy consumption (LNEC) is positive and statistically significant across the lower-to-middle quantiles (τ = 0.1–0.6), with the largest standalone coefficient at the lowest quantile: 3.66 at τ = 0.1, declining progressively to 1.51 at τ = 0.6. The combined marginal effect ∂LNCP/∂LNEC at the sample-mean manufacturing share is positive in this range. At the upper-middle quantiles (τ = 0.7–0.8), the standalone LNEC coefficient loses significance, consistent with a saturating emissions response in which additional energy in already high-emission regimes may be directed toward less carbon-intensive activities or offset by efficiency improvements. The coefficient rebounds to significance at τ = 0.9 (2.10, p < 0.10), suggesting that in extremely high-emission states, energy consumption again becomes a direct emissions driver, possibly reflecting the re-emergence of energy-intensive processes at the top of the distribution.

The interaction term LNINT is negative and significant at the lower quantiles (τ = 0.1–0.4), with coefficients ranging from − 0.81 at τ = 0.1 to − 0.39 at τ = 0.4, before becoming statistically insignificant from τ = 0.5 onward. This pattern is not a failure of the conditional emissions framework but a refinement of it. At low emission regimes — historically corresponding to early industrialisation phases — the negative LNINT coefficient indicates that the simultaneous expansion of manufacturing and energy use was associated with diversification and efficiency gains that partially offset the standalone energy effect. As emissions rise into the middle and upper quantiles, the conditional structure converges and the standalone LNEC effect dominates. The conditional framework is therefore best read as regime-specific: industrial structure moderates the energy–emissions relationship, but the direction and strength of that moderation depend on the maturity of the industrial base.

Manufacturing value added (LNM) is statistically insignificant across all quantiles, reinforcing the core finding that industrialisation affects emissions primarily through its interaction with energy rather than as a standalone determinant. The structural-break dummy (D01) is positive and significant throughout the entire distribution (τ = 0.1–0.9), with coefficients ranging from 0.47 to 0.70, confirming that the pre-1984 period was associated with systematically higher emissions across all emission regimes — consistent with the revenue-driven, energy-intensive expansion that characterised Saudi Arabia before the oil-price collapse.

Oil dependence (LNO) exhibits a positive, statistically significant effect across the lower and middle quantiles (τ = 0.1–0.7), with coefficients ranging narrowly from 0.10 to 0.20. At the upper tail (τ = 0.8–0.9), the coefficient remains positive but loses significance. The consistency of the oil-dependence effect across much of the distribution underscores its structural role in shaping Saudi Arabia’s emissions trajectory: oil revenues sustain subsidised energy pricing and capital allocation toward carbon-intensive production, regardless of whether the economy is in a low- or moderate-emission state. The attenuation of significance at the extreme upper quantiles may reflect the presence of additional factors — such as global oil-price cycles or investment substitution — that dominate oil-rent dynamics in periods of peak emissions.

Reconciliation with the ARDL long-run estimates. It is important to interpret the ARDL and quantile regression coefficients consistently at LNEC. In both specifications, LNEC enters alongside the interaction term LNINT, and its standalone coefficient does not represent the total marginal effect of energy on emissions. In the ARDL long-run model, the standalone β_LNEC is negative (− 8.33), but the implied marginal effect at the sample-mean manufacturing share is positive (≈ 0.43); the negative standalone coefficient is the algebraic counterpart of a strongly positive interaction. In the quantile regression, the standalone β_LNEC at the lower quantiles is positive and large (3.66 at τ = 0.1) while the LNINT coefficient is negative and significant in the same lower quantiles (− 0.81 at τ = 0.1). Combining the two, the implied marginal effect of LNEC at τ = 0.1 evaluated at the sample mean LNM is 3.66 + (− 0.81) × 3.52 ≈ 0.81 — positive, but smaller than the standalone coefficient implies. The two estimators thus deliver a consistent qualitative message: energy consumption is emissions-intensive only when combined with industrial activity. The apparent discrepancy in raw coefficients reflects the algebra of interaction models rather than a substantive disagreement about the energy–emissions relationship.

On the inferential interpretation of distributional heterogeneity, the present analysis rests on coefficient magnitudes, signs, and pointwise significance across τ = 0.1–0.9. We acknowledge that a formal inter-quantile equality test (e.g., a Koenker–Bassett Wald test) would provide stronger statistical confirmation, and we have therefore framed claims of regime-specific effects in terms of patterns suggested by the estimates rather than as formally tested differences. The implementation of inter-quantile Wald tests is identified as a priority for the next iteration of this work.

Taken together, these distributional results demonstrate that emissions drivers are strongly regime-dependent and that mean-based estimates mask important heterogeneity. The declining standalone energy elasticity from lower to mid quantiles, the mid-distribution disappearance of the interaction effect, and the near-universal significance of the structural-break dummy all point to a Saudi emissions trajectory shaped by distinct phases of industrialisation and resource dependence. Policies calibrated only to average relationships are therefore likely to be mis-targeted: effective mitigation requires differentiated interventions that account for the structural characteristics of low-, medium-, and high-emission regimes.

Causality analysis

The Toda–Yamamoto causality results provide additional support for the direction of relationships identified in the ARDL framework. Evidence of causality running from industrialisation and oil dependence to emissions reinforces the interpretation of these variables as structural drivers rather than purely correlated factors. While potential endogeneity concerns cannot be eliminated, the use of lag structures within the ARDL framework mitigates reverse causality to a meaningful extent, and the consistency of results across ARDL and quantile specifications further strengthens the robustness of the findings.

The Toda–Yamamoto causality tests (Table 8) reveal several directional relationships. First, unidirectional causality runs from energy supply (LNEC, p = 0.0011), the energy–manufacturing interaction (LNINT, p = 0.0005), and oil dependence (LNO, p = 0.0010) to carbon emissions (LNCP). This confirms that changes in emissions are primarily driven by developments in the energy–industrial system rather than by endogenous environmental responses.

Second, we find evidence of bidirectional causality between emissions and oil rents (LNCP ⇄ LNO). While oil rents predict future emissions (LNO → LNCP, p = 0.0010), emissions also predict future oil rents (LNCP → LNO, p = 0.0039). This reverse causality likely reflects a feedback mechanism in which rising emissions — associated with industrial expansion and economic growth — generate higher oil revenues through heightened domestic energy consumption and associated economic activity. Alternatively, it may capture a political-economy response: periods of high emissions may trigger policy discussions that ultimately affect oil-sector governance and rent distribution.

Third, no causal relationship is found from carbon emissions to energy supply or industrial activity, indicating that emissions reductions do not automatically induce adjustments in production structure or energy use. This asymmetric pattern — where energy and industry drive emissions but not vice versa — reinforces the need for proactive policy interventions rather than reliance on endogenous adjustments.

Overall, the causality results support the core empirical conclusion that emissions dynamics in Saudi Arabia are shaped by energy-intensive industrial expansion and structural oil dependence, and they reveal that the relationship between emissions and oil rents is mutually reinforcing.

Model diagnostics

The robustness and statistical adequacy of the estimated ARDL model were verified through a battery of diagnostic checks (Table 9). The Jarque–Bera test (p = 0.129) confirms that the residuals are normally distributed, supporting the inferences drawn from the t- and F-statistics. The Breusch–Godfrey LM test (p = 0.682) and the Breusch–Pagan–Godfrey test (p = 0.973) indicate no evidence of serial correlation or heteroskedasticity in the residuals, respectively, ensuring the efficiency of the estimators. Finally, the model’s recursive stability is confirmed: both the CUSUM and CUSUM-of-Squares statistics remain within the 5% critical bounds throughout the sample period. Collectively, these results affirm that the selected ARDL(1, 4, 3, 4, 0, 0) specification is well-specified, stable, and reliable for both short-run and long-run inference (Fig. 1).

Synthesis and policy interpretation

Taken together, the empirical results support a conditional emissions framework in which energy consumption does not act as an independent driver of emissions. Its environmental impact is mediated by industrial structure and reinforced by oil dependence, with the strength of that mediation varying across emission regimes.

This has important policy implications. Conventional strategies that focus on reducing aggregate energy consumption may be insufficient in resource-dependent economies. Instead, effective emissions mitigation requires structural transformation to improve industrial energy efficiency and reduce reliance on carbon-intensive production processes6,7,40. In the context of Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030, this implies that decarbonisation policies should prioritise technological upgrading, sectoral reallocation, and energy-pricing reforms. Without addressing the structural conditions that link energy use to emissions, reductions in aggregate energy consumption alone are unlikely to yield substantial environmental benefits.

Contribution back to theory

The findings extend the existing literature by empirically demonstrating that the energy–emissions nexus is conditional rather than intrinsic, and that this conditionality is itself regime-dependent. By integrating interaction effects and distributional analysis, the study provides a mechanism-based explanation for emissions dynamics in resource-dependent economies.

Relative to the nearest comparators in the Saudi literature, the study advances knowledge in three identifiable respects. First, the interaction specification demonstrates that the well-documented positive energy–emissions elasticity in Saudi Arabia4,9 is not a structural constant but a regime-dependent parameter: the marginal effect of energy supply on emissions is statistically indistinguishable from zero when manufacturing is at low levels, rising to approximately 0.43 at the sample mean and higher at elevated industrialisation levels. This finding cannot be derived from additive ARDL models and constitutes a genuinely new empirical result for this economy.

Second, the quantile regression analysis reveals that the effect of oil dependence on emissions is concentrated in the lower and middle quantiles of the distribution (τ = 0.1–0.7), attenuating to statistical insignificance at the upper tail — a heterogeneity pattern that contradicts the implicit assumption of a uniform oil-rent effect in prior studies1,10 and has direct implications for how carbon risk is distributed across industrial cycles.

Third, the persistence of the pre-1984 structural-break dummy (D01) across all quantiles from 0.1 to 0.9 demonstrates that the carbon lock-in mechanism identified theoretically by Unruh20 and Seto et al.19 is empirically traceable to a specific macroeconomic transition in Saudi Arabia, rather than being a diffuse historical trend.

Together, these results sharpen the theoretical claim that effective decarbonisation in resource-dependent economies must target the structural conditions governing energy allocation within production, not merely the aggregate volume of energy consumed. This contributes to the broader debate on carbon lock-in and supports a shift in empirical modelling from additive specifications to interaction-based, distributional frameworks.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that carbon emissions in Saudi Arabia are not driven by energy consumption per se, but by its structural embedding within industrial production under conditions of oil dependence. Using ARDL, quantile regression, and structural-break analysis, the study provides robust evidence that energy becomes emissions-intensive only when embedded within manufacturing activity, and that the strength of this conditional amplification varies across emission regimes. The results contribute to the literature by advancing a conditional emissions framework that integrates industrial structure and resource dependence into the analysis of environmental outcomes, moving beyond additive and mean-based approaches.

Policy implications for vision 2030

The findings suggest that effective emissions-reduction strategies must move beyond aggregate energy-reduction targets and instead focus on restructuring industrial energy use. Three concrete priorities emerge from the empirical results. First, sector-specific energy-efficiency improvements in manufacturing are likely to deliver larger emissions returns than economy-wide energy targets, because the marginal effect of energy on emissions is concentrated in industrial production. Second, energy-pricing reforms — extending the path opened by the 2015–18 fuel-price adjustments6 — are likely to be central to disciplining the rebound dynamics identified in the theoretical framework. Third, in resource-dependent economies, decarbonisation requires a structural transformation of production systems rather than uniform reductions in energy use; this aligns with the circular-carbon-economy approach adopted as a framing device in Saudi climate policy7,40.

Comparison with other GCC economies

The conditional emissions framework developed here is, in principle, applicable to other resource-dependent economies in which industrial development has been underwritten by subsidised hydrocarbons. The other GCC economies — the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain and Oman — share three of the structural features that motivate our specification: heavy reliance on hydrocarbon revenues, government-led industrial diversification programmes, and energy-pricing regimes historically biased toward subsidisation. Mahmood et al.10 document broadly comparable asymmetric oil–emission relationships across the GCC, suggesting that the oil-dependence channel identified in our Saudi estimates is unlikely to be country-idiosyncratic. The timing of structural breaks and the specific interaction strength would, however, plausibly differ across these economies because their industrialisation trajectories differ in pace and sectoral composition. A panel-quantile extension across the six GCC states is therefore a natural and high-priority follow-on study.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgement, several of which point directly to the priority extensions discussed below.

First, multicollinearity from the interaction term. The inclusion of LNINT (LNM × LNEC) introduces multicollinearity among regressors. While the ARDL framework mitigates this through its distributed-lag structure, and while the marginal-effect interpretation is robust to collinearity, we do not present numerical Variance Inflation Factors or a mean-centred re-specification of the interaction in this revision. Both diagnostics — VIF reporting alongside a mean-centred robustness check — are flagged as priority next steps.

Second, sectoral aggregation. The analysis relies on aggregate national-level data, which may obscure sectoral heterogeneity across manufacturing subsectors with very different emissions profiles. Sector- or firm-level analysis could identify which manufacturing subsectors drive the interaction effect.

Third, the issue of renewable–fossil composition. Energy supply (LNEC) is treated as a composite measure aggregating fossil and renewable sources. For most of the sample period (1970–2017), this is empirically defensible because Saudi Arabia’s energy mix was overwhelmingly fossil-based. From 2018 onward, however, the rapid scale-up of solar and wind capacity under Vision 2030 introduces a compositional shift that the aggregate measure cannot capture. Two implications follow. First, the upper-quantile QR estimates may understate the true emissions intensity of fossil-only energy expansion, because clean additions in the most recent years dilute the aggregate. Second, the interaction term LNINT may capture a mixture of effects: a strong fossil-industrial channel for most of the sample, attenuated in the most recent observations by the renewable build-out. A formal robustness check using fossil-only energy consumption (e.g., constructing LNEC_fossil from BP Statistical Review fossil-fuel sub-aggregates) is the most direct remedy and is identified as a priority extension.

Fourth, additional structural breaks. The Zivot–Andrews procedure used here identifies a single endogenous break in 1984. Several plausible additional breaks fall within the sample window — the 2014–16 oil-price collapse, the 2015–18 fuel-price reform, and the launch of Vision 2030 in 2016. A multiple-break test (e.g., Bai–Perron) and the inclusion of a second post-2014 dummy would be a natural extension. The CUSUM and CUSUM-of-squares tests reported in Sect. 4.7 do not detect parameter instability, providing indirect evidence that the single-break specification is not seriously misspecified, but this is not a substitute for a formal multiple-break test.

Fifth, identification and endogeneity. The ARDL framework partially addresses endogeneity through its lag structure, and the Toda–Yamamoto causality test offers complementary directional inference, but neither is a substitute for an instrumental-variable identification strategy. The long-run coefficients should be interpreted as conditional associations rather than strict causal estimates. A natural extension is to instrument energy consumption and oil dependence using exogenous global oil-market shocks (e.g., post-1973 OPEC supply decisions, the 1986 oil-price collapse, the 2014 shale-driven price decline), with refinery capacity and pre-determined energy-policy variables as additional instruments.

Sixth, inferential limits of the quantile analysis. The interpretation of distributional heterogeneity rests on the magnitudes, signs, and pointwise significance of the coefficients across τ = 0.1–0.9. A formal inter-quantile equality test (e.g., a Koenker–Bassett Wald test) would provide stronger statistical confirmation. Claims of regime-specific effects are therefore framed in terms of patterns suggested by the estimates rather than as formally tested differences.

Seventh, generalisability. The time-series framework limits generalisability beyond Saudi Arabia. The GCC comparison in Sect. 5.2 indicates that the structural mechanisms identified here are likely to transfer to comparable economies, but a panel-quantile extension is required to test this directly.

Eighth, on reading the negative LNEC coefficient. The negative LNEC coefficient in the long-run ARDL specification should not be interpreted in isolation: the net marginal effect of energy at the sample-mean manufacturing share is positive (≈ 0.43), as detailed in Sect. 4.1. Future work, particularly any extension that adds a graphical illustration of the conditional marginal effect, would help to fix this interpretation visually.

Future research directions

These limitations suggest several avenues for future research. (i) Sector-level or firm-level analysis to identify which manufacturing subsectors drive the interaction effect. (ii) Decomposition of energy supply into renewable and fossil components for a more precise assessment of the clean-energy transition. (iii) Incorporation of additional structural breaks related to Vision 2030 reforms — fuel-price adjustments, renewable-energy targets, and carbon-pricing initiatives — using multiple-break tests. (iv) Extension to panel-quantile studies across GCC countries to clarify whether the heterogeneous patterns identified here reflect regional dynamics or country-specific institutional features. (v) Implementation of an instrumental-variable strategy to sharpen causal identification of the energy–emissions and oil–emissions relationships. (vi) Reporting of formal inter-quantile equality tests and VIF diagnostics, together with a mean-centred robustness check on the interaction term. (vii) Development of a graphical illustration of the conditional marginal effect of energy across the empirically observed range of manufacturing share, which would complement the algebraic interpretation given here. (viii) Engagement with the rebound-effect literature through explicit modelling of efficiency–output dynamics in industrial sub-sectors.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the elasticity of energy emissions in Saudi Arabia is not a fixed structural constant but a regime-dependent parameter that rises with the manufacturing share of GDP. Using an interaction-based ARDL framework, quantile regression, and Toda–Yamamoto causality analysis on 1970–2024 annual data, we show that energy consumption becomes emissions-intensive only within industrial production, and that a 1984 structural break — marking the Kingdom’s shift to subsidised energy-for-industrialisation — persists across all emission quantiles, providing empirical grounding for carbon lock-in theory. The quantile regression results refine rather than overturn this story: the energy–manufacturing interaction is most pronounced in the lower-emission regimes and converges with the standalone energy effect at higher emission levels. Oil dependence acts as a structural amplifier across most of the distribution. These findings imply that effective decarbonisation under Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 requires restructuring industrial energy use rather than simply reducing its volume. The conditional emissions framework developed here offers a replicable analytical template for other resource-dependent economies navigating structural energy transitions.
