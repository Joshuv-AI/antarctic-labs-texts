An Energy-Preserving Domain of Dependence Stabilization for the Linear Wave Equation on Cut-Cell Meshes
Gunnar Birke, Christian Engwer, Sandra May, Louis Petri, Hendrik Ranocha

Abstract
We present an energy-preserving (either energy-conservative or energy-dissipative) domain of dependence stabilization method for the linear wave equation on cut-cell meshes. Our scheme is based on a standard discontinuous Galerkin discretization in space and an explicit (strong stability preserving) Runge Kutta method in time. Tailored stabilization terms allow for selecting the time step length based on the size of the background cells rather than the small cut cells by propagating information across small cut cells. The stabilization terms preserve the energy stability or energy conservation property of the underlying discontinuous Galerkin space discretization. Numerical results display the high accuracy and stability properties of our scheme.



1
Introduction

Cut-cell meshes have been the focus of considerable research over the years due to their easy generation compared to more traditional meshing methods. Objects or interfaces are placed inside a so-called background mesh, often Cartesian, and their intersections with elements from the background mesh are removed. Although this process can be implemented very efficiently, it gives up nearly any control over the shape and sizes of mesh elements, resulting potentially in arbitrarily small cut cells. In the context of solving first-order hyperbolic conservation laws, this leads to the
small cell problem
: severe and practically infeasible time step size restrictions when explicit time stepping methods are employed.

Over the last few decades a variety of different approaches have emerged to handle the
small cell problem
. Perhaps the conceptually easiest method is so called cell merging/agglomeration where small cut cells are merged with their bigger neighbors, forming a new grid without small cut cells; see, e.g.,
[
4
,
29
]
and also
[
28
,
25
]
for more recent developments.
Although cell merging is conceptually simple, a general implementation is very involved and requires working directly on the mesh.

The alternative line of attack is a suitable modification of the numerical scheme in the neighborhood of small cut cells. Here, several solution approaches for solving hyperbolic conservation laws have been developed in the context of finite volume (FV) and discontinuous Galerkin (DG) schemes. Examples for FV schemes are the
h-box
-method
[
6
,
5
]
, flux redistribution
[
12
,
13
]
, dimensionally split methods
[
22
,
20
]
, state redistribution (SRD)
[
7
,
19
]
, and mixed explicit-implicit methods
[
23
]
. To our knowledge only few approaches exist for higher-order DG finite element methods discretizations. Specifically we want to mention the extension of SRD to DG
[
19
]
, the Domain of Dependence (DoD) stabilization
[
16
,
24
]
and adaptations of the ghost penalty stabilization hyperbolic problems
[
18
,
32
]
,
originally developed for elliptic equations
[
11
]
.

Despite this active development, the
small cell problem
still poses a significant challenge w.r.t. the aim of explicit, higher-order methods with provable stability and conservative guarantees. Regarding the analysis of higher-order cut-cell methods for the wave equation, we are aware of two approaches in the context of DG schemes. Sticko and Kreiss
[
32
,
33
]
proposed a ghost penalty type stabilization for the second-order formulation and proved energy stability. Chan and co-authors
[
41
]
obtained results for the SRD method applied to the wave equation in first-order formulation. Both approaches rely on a stabilization that is not based on physical properties of the PDE operator; as a consequence the authors are able to prove energy stability, but energy conservation is violated.

Our approach for designing an energy-stable and energy-conservative scheme
that solves the
small cell problem

is based on
the DoD method, first introduced in
[
16
]
for the linear advection equation.
The idea is to augment a standard DG scheme by spatial stabilization terms on small cut cells to ensure stability of the time discretization under reasonable time step sizes.
The design of the stabilization terms depends on the problem that needs to be solved.
The DoD stabilization was extended to nonlinear equations in 1d in
[
24
]
, and as
a first-order version
to linear systems in 2d in
[
9
]
.
Both works are not straightforward to generalize;
[
24
]
makes significant use of the 1d structure, whereas the construction in
[
9
]
is only feasible for for piecewise constant polynomials. Regarding the analysis of the DoD method, fully discrete stability results were obtained for the linear advection equation; in 2d for piecewise constant polynomials
[
8
]
and in 1d for arbitrary order polynomials
[
26
]
. Using the stability proof,
[
8
]
further derives an a-priori error estimate in 2d. Additionally in
[
27
]
a SBP property was shown for the DoD stabilization, again in 1d and for the linear advection equation. This work uses a splitting between central and dissipative fluxes and we will apply a very similar idea here.

The main contribution of this paper is a generalization of the original DoD stabilization
[
16
]
which allows us to derive cut-cell stabilizations for the linear wave equation, including an appropriate handling of reflecting boundary conditions.
The generalized construction is based on a distinction between central fluxes and dissipative fluxes, which are handled separately. To stabilize high-order discretizations, we introduce the concept of, as we call them,
propagation forms
, generalizing the initial extension of fluxes. These propagation forms need to fulfill a particular integration-by-parts style structure. Using this abstract property, we can prove energy stability and even derive an energy-conservative semi-discretization in space, by controlling the added dissipation. A technical challenge was the handling of reflecting boundary conditions, which is again simplified by the distinction of central fluxes and dissipative fluxes and the construction of suitable propagation forms.

This article is structured as follows: In Section
2
we introduce the linear wave equation as our model equation as well as the standard DG scheme on which our stabilized scheme is based upon.
Section
3
contains the description of the generalized DoD stabilization itself and the particular construction for the wave equation.
Our main theoretical result is presented in Section
4
where we prove that our stabilized scheme is energy-stable/conservative, i.e., it preserves the energy stability/conservation property of the underlying DG scheme.
In Section
5
we present numerical results, displaying the accuracy and long term stability properties of our scheme. We also provide a short discussion on numerical precision issues.

General setting

We consider the acoustic wave equation written in its first-order form. Let

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad u(x,t)=\begin{pmatrix}p(x,t)\\
v_{1}(x,t)\\
v_{2}(x,t)\end{pmatrix},\quad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

(1)

be the state vector with pressure

v=(v_{1},v_{2})^{T}
. In the following we will drop the explicit dependence on

and

. Let

\displaystyle f(u)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}(u)\\
f_{2}(u)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}u\\
A_{2}u\end{pmatrix},\quad A_{1}={\footnotesize\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{~\,}c@{~\,}c@{}}0\hfil\penalty\ &c\hfil\penalty\ &0\\
c\hfil\penalty\ &0\hfil\penalty\ &0\\
0\hfil\penalty\ &0\hfil\penalty\ &0\end{array}\right)},\;A_{2}={\footnotesize\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{~\,}c@{~\,}c@{}}0\hfil\penalty\ &0\hfil\penalty\ &c\\

be the flux function with

a constant denoting the speed of sound. Given a unit vector

the flux in direction

is given as
f_{n}=f\cdot n=n_{1}f_{1}+n_{2}f_{2}
and can be computed as

with

A_{n}=A\cdot n=n_{1}A_{1}+n_{2}A_{2}.

(8)

We point out that

is symmetric and has two nonzero eigenvalues
c
and

.

The wave equation, written as a first-order system of hyperbolic conservation laws, takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\nabla\cdot f(u)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

(9a)

\displaystyle v\cdot n

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(9b)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=u_{0}

\displaystyle\text{on }\Omega\text{ at }t=0,

where

2.1
Base discretization

To discretize the domain
\Omega
, we first start with a rectangular domain
\widehat{\Omega}\supset\Omega
and a so-called background mesh
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{h}
of
\widehat{\Omega}
, a structured Cartesian mesh
of mesh size
h
,
such that
\bigcup_{\widehat{E}\in\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\overline{\widehat{E}}=\overline{\widehat{\Omega}}
.

With this we define a cut-cell mesh on
\Omega
consisting of elements

\mathcal{M}_{h}=\{E=\widehat{E}\cap\Omega:\widehat{E}\in\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{h}\},

and internal and boundary faces given by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{\gamma=\bar{E}_{1}\cap\bar{E}_{2}:|\gamma|>0,E_{1},E_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{h}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{\gamma=\bar{E}\cap\partial\Omega:E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{h}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}\cup\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h},

where
|\gamma|
denotes the 1D volume (length).
Given an element
E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}
we denote its sets of
internal, boundary, and all faces by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}(E)=\{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}:\gamma\cap\partial E\neq\emptyset\},\quad\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)=\{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}:\gamma\cap\partial E\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{h}(E)=\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}(E)\cup\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E).

We further define for each element
E
the index-set
\mathbb{I}(E)=\{1,\ldots,|\Gamma_{h}(E)|\}
, consecutively numbering all faces, such that any
i\in\mathbb{I}(E)
identifies exactly one face
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma_{h}(E)
. If
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}(E)
is an internal face we denote by
E_{i}
the corresponding neighbor element. If
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)
and there is no physical neighbor at this face, we will abuse notation by still writing
E_{i}
and using this as an index variable later. Formally we can think of
E_{i}
as a ghost cell which shares the face
\gamma_{i}
with
E
.

For each internal face
\gamma=\bar{E}_{1}\cap\bar{E}_{2}\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}
we fix a unique left-sided cell
E_{\gamma}^{L}\in\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
. The corresponding right-sided cell will be denoted by
E_{\gamma}^{R}
.
The unit normal vector
n_{\gamma}
on
\gamma
is uniquely defined to point from left to right, i.e.,

n_{\gamma}=n_{E_{\gamma}^{L}}|_{\gamma}.

Given a face
\gamma=\bar{E}_{\gamma}^{L}\cap\bar{E}_{\gamma}^{R}\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}
and a function
w
defined on
E_{\gamma}^{L}\cup E_{\gamma}^{R}
such that both
w|_{E_{\gamma}^{L}}
and
w|_{E_{\gamma}^{R}}
have well-defined traces on
\gamma
, we define left and right values of
w
as

w_{\gamma}^{L}=\operatorname{tr}_{\gamma}(w|_{E_{\gamma}^{L}}),\;w_{\gamma}^{R}=\operatorname{tr}_{\gamma}(w|_{E_{\gamma}^{R}}),

and averages and jumps

\left\{\mskip-5.0mu\{{w}\right\}\mskip-5.0mu\}_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{2}(w^{L}_{\gamma}+w^{R}_{\gamma}),\quad\llbracket w\rrbracket_{\gamma}=w^{L}_{\gamma}-w^{R}_{\gamma}.

In the DG discretization, the reflecting boundary condition
v\cdot n=0
is not imposed strongly; instead, it is implemented in a weak sense by constructing suitable numerical fluxes on the boundary. To do so we define the mirroring operator.

Definition 1

(Mirroring operator)
.

We introduce a mirroring operator that represents a suitable notion of a reflected state on the boundary, see also
[
42
]
. In the course of the paper we will further need to mirror points, as well as states. By abuse of notation we will denote all these operations by
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
.

Let a cell
E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}
and a boundary face
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)
be given.
For a state
u=(p,v)^{T}
we define the state mirrored at
\gamma
component wise as

\mathfrak{M}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\begin{pmatrix}p\\
v\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}p\\
v-2(v\cdot n)n\end{pmatrix}.

(10)

This definition is consistent, as for the exact solution with
v\cdot n=0
the reflected velocity simply changes the sign of the normal component. The pressure should be continuous under reflection, which is also reflected in the definition. If an approximate
\tilde{v}
does not fulfill the boundary condition the operator introduces a jump such that the average
\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{v}+\mathfrak{M}_{n}(\tilde{v}))=0
and the jump will later show up as a penalty, weakly enforcing the boundary condition.

We further introduce an extension of this definition to the whole element
E
, allowing to mirror a complete vector-valued polynomial function
u\in\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}
instead of just a single pointwise state. We first denote the orthogonal projection of
x\in E
onto
\gamma
as
x^{\perp}
and
n_{\gamma}^{\perp}
as the unit outer normal in
x^{\perp}
.
With this we define the generalized mirroring operator

\mathfrak{M}_{n}:\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}\to\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3},\quad\bigg(x\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}p(x)\\
v(x)\end{pmatrix}\bigg)\mapsto\bigg(x\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}p(x)\\
v(x)-2(v(x^{\perp})\cdot n)n\end{pmatrix}\bigg),

(11)

which allows to evaluate
\mathfrak{M}_{n}(u)(x)\penalty\ \forall x\in E
. We note that this definition coincides on
\gamma
with the definition of mirroring the state (
10
).

Remark 1

(Skew symmetry)
.

Note that
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
is skew-symmetric on
\gamma
:

\langle A_{n}\mathfrak{M}_{n}(u),w\rangle=-\langle A_{n}u,\mathfrak{M}_{n}(w)\rangle.

(12)

This is an important property of as it leads to energy conservation on reflecting boundaries.

We can now state the standard DG discretization in space that will later be stabilized appropriately. As a function space we choose

\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}\coloneqq\{v_{h}\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}\;\big|\;\forall E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}:(v_{h})_{E}\coloneqq(v_{h})_{|E}\in P^{r}(E)^{3}\},

with
P^{r}(E)
being the space of polynomials of degree
r
on
E
.
On this space we consider discretizations that can be formulated as a bilinear operator
a_{h}:\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}\times\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
using central fluxes and an additional diffusive stabilization operator
s_{h}:\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}\times\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}a_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})=&-\sum_{E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}\int_{E}f(u_{h})\cdot\nabla w_{h}+\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}}\int_{\gamma}\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\big[f_{n}(u_{\gamma}^{L})+f_{n}(u_{\gamma}^{R})\big],\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\rangle\\
&+\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}}\int_{\gamma}\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\big[f_{n}(u_{h})+f_{n}(\mathfrak{M}_{n}(u_{h}))\big],w_{h}\rangle\end{split}

(13)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the Euclidean scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}s_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})=&\phantom{\>+}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}}\int_{\gamma}\langle S_{n}(u^{L}_{\gamma},u^{R}_{\gamma}),\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\rangle\\
&+\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}}\int_{\gamma}\langle S_{n}(u_{h},\mathfrak{M}_{n}(u_{h})),w_{h}\rangle\end{split}

(14)

where

S_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}

with unit vector
n
is a dissipative operator. Here
u^{L}_{\gamma}
and
u^{R}_{\gamma}
denote the state in the left or right neighbor, as defined above.
Note that throughout the paper we will often consider two arbitrary states, not directly connected to a specific face. In this case we use the general notation of
u^{\mu}
and
u^{\nu}
for these two states, to indicate that they do not belong to a mesh face.

We assume the diffusive operator
S_{n}
to be linear, i.e.
S_{n}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu})=S_{n}^{M}(u^{\mu}-u^{\nu})
with
S_{n}^{M}
being positive semi-definite. A common choice for the wave-equation is the Lax-Friedrichs dissipation

S_{n}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu})=\frac{\rho(A_{n})}{2}(u^{\mu}-u^{\nu})

(15)

with
\rho(A_{n})
being the spectral radius of the matrix
A_{n}
defined in (
8
), which is equal to the speed of sound
c
as introduced in (
2
).

The complete base semi-discrete scheme reads as follows: Find
u_{h}(t)\in\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}
such that

\int_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\langle\partial_{t}u_{h}(t),w_{h}\rangle+a_{h}(u_{h}(t),w_{h})+s_{h}(u_{h}(t),w_{h})=0\quad\forall w_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}.

(16)

The scheme is then completed by applying an explicit Runge-Kutta (RK) scheme in time.

2.2
Surface forms and numerical fluxes

We can formulate the scheme in terms of numerical fluxes
\mathcal{H}_{n}
. Setting

\mathcal{H}_{n}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu})=\frac{1}{2}\big[f_{n}(u^{\mu})+f_{n}(u^{\nu})\big]+S_{n}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu})

the discretization (
16
) can be written as

\int_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\langle\partial_{t}u_{h}(t),w_{h}\rangle+a^{\text{num}}_{h}(u_{h}(t),w_{h})=0\quad\forall w_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}

with

\displaystyle a^{\text{num}}_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}\int_{E}f(u_{h})\cdot\nabla w_{h}+\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}}\int_{\gamma}\langle\mathcal{H}_{n}(u^{L}_{\gamma},u^{R}_{\gamma}),\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\rangle

\displaystyle\>\phantom{-}\>+\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}}\int_{\gamma}\langle\mathcal{H}_{n}(u_{h},\mathfrak{M}_{n}(u_{h})),w_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})+s_{h}(u_{h},w_{h}).

Note that with the choice (
15
), this simply corresponds to using the Lax-Friedrichs flux in a standard DG formulation.

Although this is the common form when working with hyperbolic conservation laws, splitting the numerical flux into central and dissipative parts will significantly simplify our presentation due to their different nature. To see this and for later purposes, we introduce the notation of surface forms
b_{\gamma}:L^{2}(\gamma)^{3}\times L^{2}(\gamma)^{3}\times L^{2}(\gamma)^{3}\to\mathbb{R}

b_{\gamma}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)=\int_{\gamma}\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\big[f_{n}(u^{\mu})+f_{n}(u^{\nu})\big],w\rangle,

(17)

where
\gamma\in\Gamma_{h}
is a mesh face.
On the one hand we have the identity

\displaystyle a_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})=

\displaystyle-\sum_{E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}\left(\int_{E}f(u_{h})\cdot\nabla w_{h}+\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}(E)}b_{\gamma}(u_{\gamma}^{L},u_{\gamma}^{R},w_{h}|_{E})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)}b_{\gamma}(u_{h},\mathfrak{M}_{n}(u_{h}),w_{h})\right)

which connects these surface forms to the space discretization. On the other hand we have the following statement.

Lemma 1
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}
be a mesh element,
u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w\in\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}
vector-valued polynomial functions. Then it holds

\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{h}(E)}b_{\gamma}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)=\int_{E}\tfrac{1}{2}[f(u^{\mu})+f(u^{\nu})]\cdot\nabla w+\int_{E}\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\cdot[f(u^{\mu})+f(u^{\nu})],w\rangle.

(18)

Proof.

By integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{h}(E)}\int_{\gamma}\langle\tfrac{1}{2}f_{n}(u^{\mu}),w\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle\tfrac{1}{2}f_{k}(u^{\mu}),\partial_{k}w\rangle+\int_{E}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{k}f_{k}(u^{\mu}),w\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\tfrac{1}{2}f(u^{\mu})\cdot\nabla w+\int_{E}\tfrac{1}{2}\langle\nabla\cdot f(u^{\mu}),w\rangle

and similarly for
u^{\nu}
. Adding both equations together yields the result.
∎

So for the central flux, we have an integration by parts formula that can also be seen as a balance of fluxes. It is this balance of fluxes that we will exploit to construct our method. Constructing a similar balance for dissipative fluxes is highly non-trivial and does not seem to provide any benefit. Splitting our numerical fluxes in central and dissipative parts thus seems natural.

3
Domain of Dependence Stabilization

In order to overcome the small cell problem and allow explicit time stepping methods with a CFL condition that is independent of the cut-cell size, we now introduce a generalization of the original DoD discretization, as it was described in
[
16
]
.

As in
[
16
]
we add stabilization terms on small cut cells to overcome these time step restrictions. Whether a cell is small is decided based on its volume size relative to its background cell.

Definition 2

(Cell classification)
.

We denote the volume fraction of an element
E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}
by

\alpha_{E}=\frac{|E|}{|\widehat{E}|},

where
\widehat{E}
is the corresponding background cell. We then define
the set of stabilized cut cells
as

\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}=\{E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}\;|\;\alpha_{E}<\alpha\}

(19)

with
\alpha\in(0,1)
being the threshold for volume fractions of cut cells that will be stabilized.

Typically not all cut cells need stabilization and often it is advisable to lower the time step size slightly to allow for a slightly smaller
\alpha
. In section
5
we will give a concrete choice for our chosen test cases.

In the following we will make a few assumptions on the set
\mathcal{I}
of small cut cells.

(i)

For any small cut-cell
E\in\mathcal{I}
, there is no
i\in\mathbb{I}(E)
such that
E_{i}\in\mathcal{I}
or in other words, small cut cells do not have small neighbors.

(ii)

For any small cut-cell
E\in\mathcal{I}
, if there is a face
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)
, its outer normal vector
n_{\gamma}
is constant. In other words,
\gamma
is a subset of a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(iii)

There is a constant
\rho>0
such that for any
E\in\mathcal{I}
it holds that
h_{E}^{d}\leq\rho|E|
where
h_{E}
is the diameter of the cell
E
.

Remark 2
.

Assumption
(i)
is the same as in
[
16
]
, where as condition
(iii)

limits the amount of anisotropy in the cut-cell mesh (at least for small cells).
The later is only currently needed due to technical limitations of our implementation. Assumption
(ii)
is a restriction on the geometry, which relates to the handling of reflecting boundary conditions.

The general framework that we present here does
in principal
not require the conditions
(ii)
and
(iii)
. It is our concrete construction and implementation that we used for our numerical results in section
5
which is limited in this regard. Condition
(i)
is more fundamental and will require an extension of our general framework that can handle patches of small cut cells.

Given these assumptions, we will now construct the DoD stabilized semi-discrete scheme. It takes the form: Find
u_{h}(t)\in\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}
such that for all
w_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}

\int_{\Omega}\langle d_{t}u_{h}(t),w_{h}\rangle+a_{h}(u_{h}(t),w_{h})+J^{a}_{h}(u_{h}(t),w_{h})+s_{h}(u_{h}(t),w_{h})+J^{s}_{h}(u_{h}(t),w_{h})=0.

(20)

The stabilization terms
J^{a}_{h}
and
J^{s}_{h}
contain element stabilization terms for all cells
E\in\mathcal{I}
that are considered small and are given as

\displaystyle J^{a}_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{I}}J^{0,E}_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})+\sum_{E\in\mathcal{I}}J^{1,E}_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})

(21a)

\displaystyle J^{s}_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{I}}J^{s,E}_{h}(u_{h},w_{h}).

(21b)

Before giving the definitions of
J_{h}^{0,E}
,
J_{h}^{1,E}
and
J^{s,E}_{h}

[
16
]

Definition 3

(Extension operator)
.

We follow the definition proposed in

.
Given a discrete function
w_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}
and a cell

Definition 4

(Reflected extension operator)
.

Let
w_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}

an element and

(22)

To unify the application of the different extension operators, we introduce additional notation. Let
E\in\mathcal{I}
be a small cut cell with
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma_{h}(E)
and
\gamma_{j}\in\Gamma_{h}(E)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{ij}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}&\quad\text{if }\mathcal{E}=E\text{ or }\mathcal{E}=E_{k},k\in\{i,j\}\text{ and }\gamma_{k}\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}\\

(23)

the extension operator between
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}
that selects either of the previously defined extension operators based on whether we are extending from an internal or external boundary. Note that we abuse notation here by writing by writing
\mathcal{E}=E_{i}
even if

for

. Note also that we have suppressed the dependence on

In figure
2
we displayed an example for the linear transport equation with a flux direction parallel to the cut interface. In this case only interactions between two different faces have to be considered, due to a no-flow condition on the cut cell, see also
[

. But here we are now considering the linear wave equation. The behavior of its characteristics is very different and resembles a cone-like structure in space-time. Then interactions between all faces with their respective neighbor elements have to be taken into account to properly extend the domain of dependence. To do so, we introduce the following concept:

Definition 5

(Propagation forms)
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{I}
be a small cell. We introduce a set of surface and volume propagation forms as functionals

\displaystyle p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle:(P^{r}(E))^{3}\times(P^{r}(E))^{3}\times(P^{r}(E))^{3}\to\mathbb{R}

and

\displaystyle p_{V}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle:(P^{r}(E))^{3}\times(P^{r}(E))^{3}\times(P^{r}(E))^{3}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle p_{V}^{E,*}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle:(P^{r}(E))^{3}\times(P^{r}(E))^{3}\times(P^{r}(E))^{3}\to\mathbb{R},

where
p_{V}^{E,*}
should mimic a kind of an adjoint operator to
p_{V}^{E}
. These forms are required to be symmetric in the first two arguments and linear in the third. Furthermore they have to satisfy for all vector-valued polynomial functions
u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w\in\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}
the following properties:

1.

Let
i,j\in\mathbb{I}(E)
. Then it holds

p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)+p_{ji}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)=p_{V}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)+p_{V}^{E,*}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w).

(24a)

2.

Let
j\in\mathbb{I}(E)
. Then it holds

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j}p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)=b_{\gamma_{j}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

(24b)

with
b_{\gamma_{j}}
from (
17
).

Remark 3
.

Equation (
24a
) can be seen as a variation of the integration by parts property, compare with (
18
) which has a similar structure, i.e. a balance between surface and volume integral terms. Equation (
24b
) is essentially a consistency property. The idea is that all fluxes coming from neighbor elements and going into a particular cell will add up to the standard DG flux from the base scheme. Similar ideas appeared already in
[
9
]
but in a more concrete shape based on products of flux matrices. The additional freedom that we gain from working with abstract forms is a key step in constructing a high-order method at small boundary cells.

The propagation forms are the key concept that enables us to extend the DoD method to the wave equation and to prove energy conservation, however we have only given an abstract definition. The properties (
24a
) and (
24b
) define a linear system which is underdetermined. A particular example extending the concept of a central flux is the following:

Example 1

(Central propagation forms)
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{I}
and
i,j\in\mathbb{I}(E)
. Let
K=|\mathbb{I}(E)|
be the number of faces of
E
. The central (surface) propagation form on
E
from
E_{i}
to
E_{j}
is given by

p^{E,Z}_{ij}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)=\tfrac{1}{K-1}b_{\gamma_{j}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)-\tfrac{K-2}{K(K-1)}b_{\gamma_{i}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)+\tfrac{1}{K(K-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{I}(E)\\
k\neq i,j\end{subarray}}b_{\gamma_{k}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w).

(25)

The central volume propagation forms on
E
are given by

\begin{split}p_{V}^{E,Z}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)&=\tfrac{2}{K(K-1)}\int_{E}\tfrac{1}{2}\big[f_{k}(u^{\mu})+f_{k}(u^{\nu})\big]\cdot\nabla w,\\
p_{V}^{E,Z,*}(w^{\mu},w^{\nu},u)&=\tfrac{2}{K(K-1)}\int_{E}\nabla\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\big[f(w^{\mu})+f(w^{\nu})\big]u.\end{split}

(26)

These constitute a set of surface and volume propagation forms on
E
, i.e. they fulfill the properties (
24a
) and (
24b
):

1.

We have

\displaystyle p_{ij}^{E,Z}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)+p_{ji}^{E,Z}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2}{K(K-1)}\biggl(b_{\gamma_{i}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)+b_{\gamma_{j}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{I}(E)\\
k\neq i,j\end{subarray}}b_{\gamma_{k}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)\biggr)

\displaystyle=p_{V}^{E,Z}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)+p_{V}^{E,Z,*}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

where the last equation follows from (
18
) and (
26
). This establishes (
24a
).

2.

By definition and using
K=\mathbb{I}(E)
we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j}p_{ij}^{E,Z}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq: central surface propagation form}}}{=}\>\phantom{+}\>\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j}\tfrac{1}{K-1}b_{\gamma_{j}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)-\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j}\tfrac{K-2}{K(K-1)}b_{\gamma_{i}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}{}+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j}\tfrac{1}{K(K-1)}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{I}(E),k\neq i,j}b_{\gamma_{k}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle=\>\phantom{+}\>b_{\gamma_{j}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)-\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j}\tfrac{K-2}{K(K-1)}b_{\gamma_{i}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}{}+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j}\tfrac{1}{K(K-1)}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{I}(E),k\neq i,j}b_{\gamma_{k}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle=b_{\gamma_{j}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w).

For the last step note that any
b_{\gamma_{i}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)
,
i\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j
, is exactly
K-2
times contained in the sum

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j}\tfrac{1}{K(K-1)}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{I}(E),k\neq i,j}b_{\gamma_{k}}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w).

Thus (
24b
) does hold as well.

The propagation forms allow us to define in what direction information flows but they do not specify the precise amount. For this we introduce the capacity of a small cell and its stabilization parameter:

Definition 6

(Cell capacity)
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{I}
be a small cell and
r
be the polynomial degree used in the definition of
\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}

is defined as

\mathfrak{c}_{E}(\Delta t)=\frac{1}{2r+1}\frac{|E|}{\Delta tc\max_{i\in\mathbb{I}(E)}|\gamma_{i}|}

(27)

and its stabilization parameter as
\eta_{E}=\eta_{E}(\Delta t)=1-\mathfrak{c}_{E}
.

Now we are ready to give the definitions of
J_{h}^{0,E}

J_{h}^{1,E}
and

in (

). The stabilization of a small cut-cell

is based on a direct coupling of neighboring cells, effectively tunneling information through the small cut cell.

Definition 7

(Cell stabilization terms)
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{I}
be a small cut cell. The cell-wise stabilization terms

,

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}J^{0,E}_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})&=\>\phantom{-}\>\eta_{E}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{I}(E)^{2},\,i<j}J^{0,E}_{h,ij}(u_{h},w_{h})\\

(28a)

\displaystyle J^{1,E}_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle=\>\phantom{-}\>\eta_{E}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{I}(E)^{2},\,i<j}J^{1,E}_{h,ij}(u_{h},w_{h}),

(28b)

\displaystyle\begin{split}J^{s,E}_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})&=\>\phantom{-}\>\eta_{E}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{I}(E)^{2},\,i<j}J^{s,E}_{ij}(u_{h},w_{h})\\

&\mathrel{\phantom{=}}{}-\eta_{E}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)}\int_{\gamma}\langle S_{n}(u_{h},\mathfrak{M}_{n}(u_{h}),w_{h}\rangle.\end{split}

(28c)

For the surface contributions
J_{h,ij}^{0,E}

1.

(29)

If one of the two face is a reflecting boundary face, the stabilization needs to be defined separately. W.l.o.g we assume that

to the dissipative stabilization is

(32)

Remark 4

(Structure of the stabilization terms)
.

We want to make a few remarks on the structure of the stabilization terms and comparisons with prior formulations.

1.

The structure of the volume stabilization term (
31
) and that of
J^{1}_{h}
in
[
24
]
are essentially identical, with two important differences. The obvious one is the use of the volume propagation forms and the weighting in front of the integral term (which already appeared in the central volume propagation forms).
This is necessary to properly consider the relationships between multiple neighbor elements of the stabilized cut cell.
The second difference is
that we only use the central flux in (
31
), respectively in our volume propagation forms, while in
[
24
]
the term
J^{1}_{j}
contained arbitrary numerical fluxes that in general could contain dissipative fluxes.

Having no dissipative fluxes in the volume term has several important benefits.
First, it allows us to construct an energy-conservative formulation of the DoD method (or even a formulation with the SBP property
[
27
]
).
Secondly, in our experience the construction of a consistent extension of dissipative surface fluxes into the volume of the cut cell is very delicate, while adding dissipation purely via surface terms, as in (
32
), is straightforward.
Hence, the splitting between central and dissipative fluxes allows for a much simpler extension of our method to multiple space dimensions.

2.

The inclusion of a separate dissipation term like (
32
) depending on extended polynomial functions was already done in
[
9
]
where the amount of dissipation was based on eigendecompositions of the occuring operators. Here the construction is much simpler, due to the separation of central and dissipative terms.

3.

In (
28
) the sums go over all index pairs
(i,j)\in\mathbb{I}(E)
such that
i<j
but since
J^{0,E}_{ij}
contains the propagation form for both index pairs
(i,j)
and
(j,i)
we have an extended flux in both directions. What we do not have, though, is a flux for the index pair
(i,i)
, that is there is no recurring flux in the formulation.

4.

Even though the neighbor dissipation term (
32
) is dissipative, the term (
28c
) is not. When proving energy stability we must consider
s_{h}+J^{s}_{h}
, which is indeed dissipative, as we will prove in section
4
.

4
Energy conservation and stability

To prove statements about energy preservation, we need two additional properties. They are related to the concept of flux potential and entropy-conservative fluxes in the theory of entropy-conservative/stable schemes developed in
[
36
,
38
]
.

Definition 8

(Energy-preserving propagation forms)
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{I}
be a small cut cell and consider a set of propagation forms consisting of
p_{ij}^{E}
with
i,j\in\mathbb{I}(E)
,
i\neq j
,
p_{V}^{E}
and
p_{V}^{E,*}
. We call this set of propagation forms energy preserving if it satisfies the following properties:

1.

Let
K=|\mathbb{I}(E)|
be the number of faces of
E
. Then for all
w_{h}\in\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}
it must hold that

\frac{2}{K(K-1)}\int_{E}f(w_{h})\cdot\nabla w_{h}=\frac{1}{2}p_{ij}^{E}(w_{h},w_{h},w_{h})+\frac{1}{2}p_{ji}^{E}(w_{h},w_{h},w_{h}).

(33)

2.

Let
u^{\mu},u^{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}
. Then it must hold that

p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},u^{\mu}-u^{\nu})=\frac{1}{2}p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\mu},u^{\mu})-\frac{1}{2}p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\nu},u^{\nu},u^{\nu}).

(34)

As an example we will show that the central propagation forms that we defined in
1
are energy preserving:

Proposition 1
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{I}
be a small cut cell. The set of central propagation forms on
E
defined in example
1
is energy preserving.

Proof.

1.

Let
w_{h}\in\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{E}f(w_{h})\cdot\nabla w_{h}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{E}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle f_{k}(w_{h}),\partial_{k}w_{h}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{E}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle f_{k}(w_{h}),\partial_{k}w_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{E}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle f_{k}(w_{h}),\partial_{k}w_{h}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{E}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle w_{h},f_{k}(\partial_{k}w_{h})\rangle

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{E}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle f_{k}(w_{h}),\partial_{k}w_{h}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{E}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle w_{h},\partial_{k}f_{k}(w_{h})\rangle

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{E}f(w_{h})\cdot\nabla w_{h}+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{E}[\nabla\cdot f(w_{h})]w_{h}

where the second equality is due to the symmetry of the linear operator
f_{k}
and the third equality is due to
f_{k}
being linear and constant in space. Multiplying with
\frac{2}{K(K-1)}
and using (
26
) and (
24a
) we get

\displaystyle\tfrac{2}{K(K-1)}\int_{E}f(w_{h})\cdot\nabla w_{h}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{2}{K(K-1)}\bigg[\>\phantom{+}\>\int_{E}\tfrac{1}{2}[f(w_{h})+f(w_{h})]\cdot\nabla w_{h}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\hphantom{\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{2}{K(K-1)}\bigg[}+\int_{E}\tfrac{1}{2}[\nabla\cdot f(w_{h})+\nabla\cdot f(w_{h})]w_{h}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big[p_{V}^{E}(w_{h},w_{h},w_{h})+p_{V}^{E,*}(w_{h},w_{h},w_{h})\big]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big[p_{ij}^{E}(w_{h},w_{h},w_{h})+p_{ji}^{E}(w_{h},w_{h},w_{h})\big].

2.

The form
p_{ij}^{E}
is linear in the third argument. Furthermore by the definition of
p_{ij}^{E}
and the central flux as well as the symmetry of
A_{n}
(which implies
\langle A_{n}u^{\mu},u^{\nu}\rangle=\langle u^{\mu},A_{n}u^{\nu}\rangle)
we have

\displaystyle p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\mu},w)+\tfrac{1}{2}p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\nu},u^{\nu},w)

(35)

\displaystyle p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\mu},u^{\nu})

\displaystyle=p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\nu},u^{\nu},u^{\mu})

(36)

for all
w\in\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}
and all
u^{\mu},u^{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}
. Thus

\displaystyle p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},u^{\mu}-u^{\nu})

\displaystyle=p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},u^{\mu})-p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\nu},u^{\nu})

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq: central propagation form splitting}}}{=}\tfrac{1}{2}\big[p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\mu},u^{\mu})+p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\nu},u^{\nu},u^{\mu})\big]-\tfrac{1}{2}\big[p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\mu},u^{\nu})+p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\nu},u^{\nu},u^{\nu})\big]

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq: central propagation form symmetry}}}{=}\tfrac{1}{2}p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\mu},u^{\mu},u^{\mu})-\tfrac{1}{2}p_{ij}^{E}(u^{\nu},u^{\nu},u^{\nu}).

∎

At the domain boundary additional complications arise and we need to take into account the reflecting wall boundary condition. For the base scheme (
16
) energy conservation follows from the skew-symmetry of the reflecting wall boundary condition (
12
) and we need to pose a similar condition for our propagation forms:

Definition 9

(Reflecting propagation forms)
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{I}
be a small cut cell and consider a set of propagation forms consisting of
p_{ij}^{E}
with
i,j\in\mathbb{I}(E),i\neq j
,
p_{V}^{E}
, and
p_{V}^{E,*}
. We call this set of propagation forms a set of reflecting propagation forms if for any
i\in\mathbb{I}(E)
, such that

p_{ji}^{E}(u^{\mu},\mathcal{L}_{E_{j}}^{\gamma_{i}}(u^{\nu}),w)=-p_{ji}^{E}(\mathcal{L}_{E_{j}}^{\gamma_{i}}(u^{\mu}),u^{\nu},\mathcal{L}_{E_{j}}^{\gamma_{i}}(w)),\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{I}(E),\;j\neq i,

(37)

and all
u^{\mu},u^{\nu},w\in\mathcal{P}^{r}(E)^{3}
.

Again this is only an abstract definition. Given assumption
(ii)
we can construct a set of reflecting propagation forms which essentially amounts to adding an element of the kernel of the linear system posed by (

Example 2

(Reflecting central propagation forms)
.

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}{}-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathcal{E}\in\{E_{i},E_{j}\}}\tfrac{1}{2}p_{ij}^{E}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{ij}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{ij}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{E}}^{ij}(u_{h}))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}{}+\tfrac{1}{2}p_{ij}^{E}(\mathcal{L}_{E_{j}}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E_{j}}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E_{j}}(u_{h}))

\displaystyle=0.

If there is no face
\gamma_{i}
with
i\in\mathbb{I}(E)
and
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)
we are done because in this case we have

J^{0,E}_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})+J^{1,E}_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})=\eta_{E}\big(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{I}(E)^{2},\,i<j}T_{ij}^{1}+T_{ij}^{2}+T_{ij}^{3}+T_{ij}^{4}-T_{ij}^{4}+T_{ij}^{V,1}+T_{ij}^{V,2}\big)=0.

Otherwise if there is a face (by assumption single)
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)
we still have to handle

\displaystyle J^{0,E}_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})+J^{1,E}_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})

\displaystyle=\eta_{E}\big(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{I}(E)^{2},\,i<j}T_{ij}^{1}+T_{ij}^{2}+T_{ij}^{3}+T_{ij}^{4}-T_{ij}^{4}+T_{ij}^{V,1}+T_{ij}^{V,2}\big)

\displaystyle=\eta\sum_{j\in\mathbb{I}(E),j\neq i}\big[p_{ji}^{E}(\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}^{\gamma_{i}}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\hphantom{\eta\sum_{j\in\mathbb{I}(E),j\neq i}\big[}-\tfrac{1}{2}p_{ji}^{E}(\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}))\big].

Using property (
24b
) of definition
5
we have

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\eta\sum_{j\in\mathbb{I}(E),j\neq i}\big[p_{ji}^{E}(\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}^{\gamma_{i}}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}))-\tfrac{1}{2}p_{ji}^{E}(\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}))\big]

\displaystyle=\eta\big[b_{\gamma_{i}}(\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}^{\gamma_{i}}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}))-\tfrac{1}{2}b_{\gamma_{i}}(\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}))\big]

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\eqref{eq: boundary reflection skew symmetry}}}{=}\eta\big[\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\gamma_{i}}\langle A_{n}u_{h},u_{h}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}b_{\gamma_{i}}(\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}),\mathcal{L}_{E}(u_{h}))\big]=0

and thus the proof is complete.
∎

The above proposition ensures energy conservation for the central stabilization term. If
S_{n}=0
, this will already be enough to show energy conservation. However, if we want to add dissipation, we would like to have that the dissipative stabilization term
J^{s}_{h}
is non-negative to have an energy dissipative scheme. Unfortunately this is not the case, so we need to look for a different strategy. The trick will be to compensate non-dissipative terms from
J^{s}_{h}
with terms from
s_{h}
, ensuring that their sum is dissipative. This strategy was already used in
[
24
]
and
[
34
]
, although in these works there was no explicit splitting between conservative and dissipative terms.

Lemma 2
.

Let
w_{h}\in\mathcal{V}^{r}_{h}
be a discrete function. Then

s_{h}(w_{h},w_{h})+J^{s}_{h}(w_{h},w_{h})\geq 0.

(41)

Proof.

By definition
s_{h}(w_{h},w_{h})\geq 0
since
S_{n}^{M}
is positive semi-definite. Considering

\displaystyle J^{s,E}_{h}(w_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle=\>\phantom{-}\>\eta_{E}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{I}(E)^{2},\,i<j}J^{s,E}_{ij}(w_{h},w_{h})-\eta_{E}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}(E)}\int_{\gamma}\langle S_{n}(w^{L}_{\gamma},w^{R}_{\gamma}),\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}{}-\eta_{E}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)}\int_{\gamma}\langle S_{n}(w_{h},\mathfrak{M}_{n}(w_{h})),w_{h}\rangle

we note that the first sum can be bounded from below since
J^{s,E}_{ij}(w_{h},w_{h})\geq 0
, which follows from (
32
) and again that
S_{n}^{M}
is positive semi-definite. We are thus left with the sum

-\sum_{E\in\mathcal{I}}\bigg[\eta_{E}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}(E)}\int_{\gamma}\langle S_{n}(w^{L}_{\gamma},w^{R}_{\gamma}),\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\rangle+\eta_{E}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)}\int_{\gamma}\langle S_{n}(w_{h},\mathfrak{M}_{n}(w_{h})),w_{h}\rangle\bigg]

which are indeed negative semi-definite. Since small cells do not have small neighbors, any face
\gamma
in the above sum appears only once. Furthermore, the integrand
\langle S_{n}(w^{L}_{\gamma},w^{R}_{\gamma}),\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\rangle
is precisely the same as in (
14
). Since
\eta_{E}\in[0,1]
we get that

s_{h}(w_{h},w_{h})-\sum_{E\in\mathcal{I}}\bigg[\eta_{E}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{i}}_{h}(E)}\int_{\gamma}\langle S_{n}(w^{L}_{\gamma},w^{R}_{\gamma}),\llbracket w_{h}\rrbracket\rangle+\eta_{E}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\text{b}}_{h}(E)}\int_{\gamma}\langle S_{n}(w_{h},\mathfrak{M}_{n}(w_{h})),w_{h}\rangle\bigg]\geq 0

which finishes the proof.
∎

Theorem 1
.

We have for the approximate solution
u_{h}(t)
and for any
T>0
that

\frac{1}{2}||u_{h}(T)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{2}||u_{h}(0)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(42)

with equality if
S_{n}=0
, i.e. if there is no dissipation.

Proof.

Testing with
u_{h}(t)
in (
20
) gives

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}||u_{h}(t)||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+a_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))+J^{a}_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))+s_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))+J^{s}_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))=0.

Integration over
(0,T)
yields

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}||u_{h}(T)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&=\frac{1}{2}||u_{h}(0)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\int_{0}^{T}\>\phantom{+}\>a_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))+J^{a}_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))\\
&\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\hphantom{\frac{1}{2}||u_{h}(0)||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\int_{0}^{T}}+s_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))+J^{s}_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t)).\end{split}

It is a known result that
a_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))
vanishes and due to proposition
2
the central stabilization term
J^{a}_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))
also vanishes. We are thus left with the dissipative terms

s_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))+J^{s}_{h}(u_{h}(t),u_{h}(t))

which we know to be non-negative from lemma
2
. If
S_{n}
is zero, they vanish and we have equality.
∎

We present numerical results to demonstrate the stability and accuracy properties of our scheme.

5.1
Implementation

We implement our numerical tests using the PDE framework DUNE
[
1
,
3
,
2
,
14
,
15
]
. The cut-cell construction builds upon the TPMC
[
17
]
library and parallel execution of test cases uses GNU Parallel
[
40
]
.
For reproducibility the complete implementation with the main dependencies and build instructions can be found under
[

]
.

For numerical simulations we need to pick a time step size and a threshold for the small cell classification (
19
). As already mentioned our goal is to solve the
small cell problem
, i.e., we want to choose a time step size based on background cells even in the presence of arbitrarily small cut cells. Let
h
denote the face length of a cartesian background element. A typical CFL condition for a DG scheme solving the wave equation would be

\Delta t\leq\frac{1}{2r+1}\frac{h}{c}

(43)

where
c
is the speed of sound. Using this CFL condition would essentially require us to stabilize every cut cell that is only a little bit smaller than background cells, meaning a value of
\alpha
close to
1
in (
19
). Instead we relax (
43
) by a constant factor
\frac{1}{4}
to get a CFL condition

\Delta t\leq\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{2r+1}\frac{h}{c}

(44)

which still depends only on the background cell but does not require stabilization on large cut cells. For
\alpha
in (
19
) we choose
\alpha=\frac{1}{10}
which in combination with (
44
) has worked out well in our numerical tests.

For the cell stabilization terms introduced in definition
7
we need to pick a set of propagation forms. We will always use the reflecting set of propagation forms defined in example
2
which has all the properties we need for energy preservation and we will verify numerically that this is indeed the case.

To integrate the semidiscrete approximations in time, we use the following explicit strong-stability preserving (SSP) Runge-Kutta methods:

•

SSPRK(2,2): The two-stage, second-order method of Heun
[
31
]

•

SSPRK(3,3): The three-stage, third-order method of Shu and Osher
[
31
]

•

SSPRK(10,4): The ten-stage, fourth-order method of Ketcheson
[
21
]

Results on fully-discrete stability for linear problems for these methods have been obtained, e.g., in
[
37
,
30
,
35
,
39
]
.

5.2
Error measures

We are interested in the behavior of the error of the discrete solution under mesh refinement of the background mesh, measured under various norms.
A natural choice for linear hyperbolic systems is the
L^{2}
norm

\left\|{e}\right\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\sum_{E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}\int_{E}{e}^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(45)

As is often done for hyperbolic PDEs on cut-cell meshes we further investigate the error in the
L^{\infty}
-norm

\left\|e\right\|_{\infty}=\sup\limits_{E\in{\mathcal{M}_{h}}}\left\|e\right\|_{E,\infty}\approx\max\limits_{E\in{\mathcal{M}_{h}}}\max\limits_{(x,\omega)\in\text{QR}(E)}|e(x)|

(46)

which we approximate via a pointwise evaluation at the points of high-order quadrature rules
QR(E)
on the mesh elements.
Due to their difference in size, it is to be expected that tiny cut cells induce numerical round-off errors that will be visible in the
L^{\infty}
-norm. To explore this issue, we introduce the filtered mesh

{\mathcal{M}_{h}}_{\!,\rho}:=\left\{E\in\mathcal{M}_{h}:\alpha_{E}>\rho\right\},

(47)

and on this filtered mesh the filtered
L^{\infty}
-norm

\left\|e\right\|_{\infty,\rho}=\sup\limits_{E\in{\mathcal{M}_{h}}_{\!,\rho}}\left\|e\right\|_{E,\infty}\approx\max\limits_{E\in{\mathcal{M}_{h}}_{\!,\rho}}\max\limits_{(x,\omega)\in\text{QR}(E)}|e(x)|

(48)

which computes an approximation of the pointwise error on all cells with a cell volume fraction larger than
\rho>0
, i.e. it skips small cells during computation of the error. We want to point out again that the mesh
{\mathcal{M}_{h}}_{\!,\rho}
is only used to evaluate
\left\|e\right\|_{\infty,\rho}
. We never skip very small cells when computing a discrete solution.

The components of the exact solutions in our test cases will be denoted by
p
,
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, as above. Their discrete counterparts will be denoted by
p_{h}
,
v_{h,1}
and
v_{h,2}
.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have presented a generalized construction of a DoD stabilization for cut-cell DG discretizations to support linear systems of first order hyperbolic PDEs, in particular the linear wave equation. The construction is based on propagation forms that mimic the transport across small cut cells and fulfill an integration-by-parts type consistency. With this we proved energy preservation of the method, which is also backed by our numerical tests. The method shows optimal convergence and is robust in long-time simulations. The generalized construction explicitly distinguishes between central fluxes and diffusive stabilization and by this is able to control the energy error and even construct a fully energy conservative discretization.

In future work we will extend this method to non-linear settings, in particular the Euler equations. Also the abstract construction via propagation forms should ease the path to a robust stabilization in 3D.
