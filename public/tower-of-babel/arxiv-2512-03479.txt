Towards Object-centric Understanding for Instructional Videos
Wenliang Guo, Yu Kong

Abstract
Understanding procedural activities is crucial for developing future assistive AI that can reason about complex real-world tasks. Existing action-centric methods struggle with the flexibility of real procedures, where step order varies depending on object states. In this work, we propose to shift the focus to an object-centric paradigm by regarding actions as mechanisms that drive state transitions. To advance this direction, we introduce Object-IVQA , a long-form instructional video benchmark with 107 videos and 514 open-ended question–answer pairs annotated with temporally grounded evidence. The benchmark evaluates four dimensions of object-centric reasoning, including state evolution, precondition verification, counterfactual reasoning and mistake recognition. We further propose an agent framework that orchestrates object-centric planning, perception, analysis and generation tools, enabling explicit evidence retrieval and multi-hop reasoning across disjoint segments. Experiments show that existing large vision–language models struggle in object-level recognition and reasoning, whereas our framework achieves substantially improvement.

Human life consists of procedural activities across diverse domains, such as housework
34
, assembly task
31
, and surgery
1
. Successfully performing these tasks requires planning, recognizing relevant objects, and reasoning about how actions alter object states. Future assistive AI systems are expected to exhibit comparable capabilities, enabling them to support humans or even autonomously execute complex tasks. To this end, AI needs to first understand the underlying procedure, which raises a fundamental question: What governs the progression of procedural activities?

Most existing approaches attempt to address this question from an action-centric perspective, focusing on modeling action transitions
3
, constructing action graphs
15
, or implicitly learning temporal structures
45
;
27
. While effective for tasks such as action segmentation
45
and next-step prediction
3
;
27
, these methods often struggle to generalize to real-world scenarios due to the complexity and variability of procedural workflows. In practice, procedural tasks involve action sequences governed by strict preconditions and causal dependencies, yet they also allow flexibility that certain steps may be optional, interchangeable, or executable in different orders without affecting the final outcome. This duality exposes a core limitation of action-centric approaches: they fail to robustly adapt to the dynamic and diverse real-world cases.

In this work, we propose to address this question from a different perspective: the progression of procedural activities is fundamentally driven by object dynamics, i.e., changes in object state, while actions serve primarily as mechanisms that induce those state transitions. Consequently, the execution of any action must be conditioned on the current state of the relevant object. For instance, if a banana has already been peeled, the peeling action should be skipped. This implies that the underlying structure of procedural tasks is causally organized around object states. Such a perspective shifts procedural understanding from an action-centric paradigm to an object-centric one.

For advancing object-centric procedural understanding, two key challenges must be addressed. First, existing datasets fail to capture how object states evolve throughout a procedure.
32
introduced an instructional video dataset for object state recognition, but its annotations are limited to coarse states (background and initial/final), restricting its value for broader downstream tasks. More recently,
38
proposed a large-scale object-centric dataset targeting general-domain videos, but it lacks the diverse and fine-grained state transitions that appears in procedural contexts.

To mitigate data scarcity, we build an automated video collection and annotation pipeline, followed by manual refinement. Aiming for more natural and flexible human–computer interaction, we formulate object-centric procedural understanding as an open-ended video question-answering (video QA) task
14
;
6
in natural language, as illustrated in Figure
1
. Leveraging existing instructional datasets
29
;
19
;
34
, we introduce
Object-IVQA
, a long-form object-centric instructional VQA benchmark comprising 107 videos (8.9 minutes on average) and 514 question–answer pairs annotated with supporting temporal evidence. The questions span four dimensions—state evolution, precondition verification, counterfactual reasoning, and mistake recognition, which enables a comprehensive evaluation of the latest large vision–language models (LVLMs) in object understanding and revealing their limitations in temporal and causal reasoning.

The second challenge in object-centric procedural understanding stems from the temporal sparsity and discontinuity of object interactions. Manipulations of the same object often occur across non-consecutive video segments, as commonly seen in real-world workflows, for instance, a banana may be prepared early but stirred later. This requires multi-hop reasoning over temporally disjoint segments. To tackle this challenge, we introduce an agent framework
37
. The framework consists of four agents responsible for planning, video processing, video analyzing, and text generation. Each agent is equipped with multiple callable tools (e.g., object detection, tracking, captioning). Specifically, we employ LLM-as-planner
12
;
8
to first generate a tool-use plan conditioned on the question. The remaining agents then execute this plan to extract supporting temporal evidence and produce the final answer. By modularizing the reasoning process, our framework can explicitly retrieve and link non-contiguous segments involving the same object, enabling the system to uncover the spatial and temporal causal dependencies underlying action–object interactions. In summary, our contributions are threefold:

•

We introduce and formalize object-centric procedural understanding as a video QA task that requires recognizing object states and reasoning about their dynamics.

•

We present
Object-IVQA
, a dataset of 514 question–answer pairs grounded in 107 long instructional videos, and benchmark both closed- and open-source LVLMs, revealing their limitations in object-level understanding.

•

We propose an agent framework for multi-hop reasoning, employing an LLM-as-planner to dynamically invoke a predefined object-centric toolbox of agents.

Related Work

Procedural Understanding.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in understanding procedural activities. Instructional videos, which comprise action sequences aimed at achieving specific goals such as cooking or assembly, serve as ideal resources to study procedural understanding. Early efforts introduced large-scale datasets by annotating instructional videos with step-wise action labels and temporal segments
34
;
26
;
46
, thereby facilitating research in areas, such as action segmentation
45
, grounding
7
, anticipation
11
, and mistake detection
19
;
18
;
29
;
31
. To understand procedural activities, traditional approaches have primarily focused on modeling interdependencies between actions mainly through temporal modeling using Transformers
45
;
27
or by constructing procedural graphs
3
;
15
. However, these methods have largely concentrated on transitions between actions, while paying limited attention to the underlying
object dynamics
which is a central focus of this work.

Object Understanding.

Some recent work
40
;
38
for video understanding have shifted from scene-level semantics to fine-grained object modeling for capturing dynamics and state transitions. In instructional video domain, where goal-directed human–object interactions define the procedural structure,
32
introduced self-supervised learning of state-modifying actions, followed by
33
who proposed a unified framework for jointly learning multiple object–action pairs.
44
further enabled multi-object state change prediction conditioned on language. Despite this progress, existing methods still rely on task-specific models. Whether general-purpose LVLMs can support object-centric reasoning in instructional videos remains unexplored. To address this, we benchmark state-of-the-art LVLMs using video QA as a proxy task, and further propose an agent framework to tackle this challenge.

Video Question-Answering.

Video QA evaluates a model’s ability to reason over temporal visual content and produce grounded answers. Early datasets
16
;
43
focused on short and trimmed clips emphasizing event-level perception, while later work expanded to long-form videos requiring multi-step
25
and causal reasoning across temporal spans
5
. In the instructional video domain, ProMQA series
14
;
13
introduced multimodal QA benchmarks aligned with structured knowledge of human–object interactions to support goal-directed comprehension. Beyond single-hop reasoning,
6
explored multi-hop temporal reasoning by linking evidence across distant video intervals. Building on these advances, our proposed benchmark extends instructional video QA to fine-grained and multi-hop reasoning centering on object dynamics. It unifies temporal grounding and causal understanding by requiring models not only to recognize and reason over evolving object states, but also to localize supporting visual evidence across temporally dispersed segments.

Agent framework for video understanding.

Recent advances in video understanding have enabled agent-based frameworks
37
;
42
;
17
to coordinate multiple specialized models within a unified system, allowing them to jointly address complex video perception and reasoning tasks. Such modular frameworks typically decompose a task into executable steps and dynamically invoke different agents or tools. Inspired by this paradigm, we develop an agent framework tailored for instructional videos. By translating video QA queries into modular operation sequences, the framework adaptively assembles toolchains to perform object-centric analysis and multi-hop reasoning.

Object-centric Video Benchmark

In this section, we introduce an object-centric instructional video question answering (
Object-IVQA
) dataset designed to evaluate large vision–language models (LVLMs) on fine-grained recognition and multi-hop temporal reasoning. To ensure the reliability of the constructed data, we develop a five-stage semi-automatic data preparation pipeline, as illustrated in Figure

, which systematically integrates video selection, dense caption generation, question–answer construction, rule-based filtering, and manual refinement. By combining LVLM-based generation with human verification and correction, this pipeline enables scalable dataset expansion while maintaining high data quality.

3.1.1
Stage 1: Video Collection

Aiming to evaluate how LVLMs understand action-triggered object dynamics over time, we collect videos with rich human–object interactions from three instructional video datasets: COIN
34
, CaptainCook4D
29
, and EgoPER
19
datasets. CaptainCook4D contains 384 egocentric videos covering 24 recipes, EgoPER consists of 385 egocentric cooking videos spanning 5 recipe categories, and COIN is a large-scale dataset with 11,827 third-person videos from 180 tasks. From COIN dataset, we only select videos in cooking scenarios, as cooking involves structured procedures suitable for analyzing temporal reasoning.

We establish several criteria for video selection. First, since long-form video understanding remains a major challenge for LVLMs and naturally supports multi-hop reasoning, we retain only videos longer than 6 minutes from CaptainCook4D and EgoPER, and those longer than 3 minutes from COIN because COIN videos are generally much shorter. Second, to ensure diversity in activities and procedures, we filter out highly redundant samples within each activity by measuring the similarity of action sequences across video samples using edit distance and cosine similarity of textual embeddings. Please refer supplementary material for details. After applying these criteria, we obtain a collection of 107 videos with an average duration of
535.0\pm 267.6
seconds from 52 different procedural activities. It contains both egocentric and exocentric videos rather than the single-view setting adopted in previous work
6
;
5
, allowing a more comprehensive assessment of the LVLM from different points of view.

3.1.2
Stage 2&3: Captioning and QA Generation

To generate object-centric QA pairs, we first prompt Qwen3-VL
4
to produce dense frame-level captions for each video, which textually capture object states, actions, and human–object interactions. We then integrate several multimodal information to construct prompt for GPT-5: a) captions, which provide fine-grained scene descriptions, b) step annotations, which offer full procedural context, c) sparsely sampled video frames that supply video context, d) task descriptions, which introduce the overall goal by indicating a specific QA type. The resulting QA pairs contain not only questions and answers but also temporally grounded evidence spans that indicate the specific video segments supporting the corresponding answer.

Furthermore, as shown in Figure
3
, by using different task descriptions as prompt, the generated QAs contains four distinct types that examine a model’s understanding of object dynamics from multiple perspectives, including:

•

Object preparation
: questions that ask which preceding actions cause the manipulated object to undergo specific state transitions before a given action;

•

State evolution
: questions that investigate how the state of the same object changes between two actions, as influenced by other relevant manipulations;

•

Counterfactual situation
: questions that pose “what if” scenarios and require answers grounded in the object’s actual state appearing in videos;

•

Mistake recognition
: questions that ask to recognize mistakes during the procedure, and explain them based on object-state evidence.

3.1.3
Stage 4&5: Data Quality Control

Even though GPT-5 with carefully designed prompts generates high-quality QA pairs empirically, we apply a multi-stage validation pipeline to further ensure data reliability. First, we use vision–language model (VLM) to measure semantic similarity between QA pairs and remove highly similar ones, thereby reducing redundancy. Next, since we observed some questions can be answered purely through commonsense reasoning, we query a large language model (LLM) given question but without providing the video. Then we score the answers (will be described in Section 4), and the questions with high scores are discarded. We also remove
Mistake
-type QA pairs with repeated error patterns to preserve diversity. Finally, all QA pairs are manually refined to ensure that answers are strictly grounded in the video, temporal spans are accurately annotated, and language is clear and logically consistent. Through Stage 4 and 5, each QA pair is ensured to be precise, unambiguous and temporally supported, and aligned with the objects and actions depicted in the corresponding video.

3.2
Object-centric Instructional QA

Object-IVQA
benchmark dataset consists of 107 long-term videos and 514 QA pairs, covering cooking activities that require complex multi-hop temporal reasoning for object dynamics. We provide a comprehensive analysis below.

Video Statistics.

Videos are sourced from three instructional datasets: CaptainCook4D (58.3%), COIN (36.1%), and EgoPER (5.6%). This composition introduces diversity in egocentric and exocentric viewpoints, task complexity, and environmental settings. As shown in Figure
4(a)
, video durations range from 180 to 900 seconds, with an average of 536.0 seconds. More than 75% of the videos exceed six minutes. This long-duration characteristic naturally creates temporal discontinuities and provides rich contextual information for evaluating long-range multi-hop reasoning over disjoint object interactions.

Activity Distribution
.

Object-IVQA
spans 52 cooking activities, each consisting of multiple instances. Compared with other procedural activities such as household tasks, cooking involves more structured and causally linked object-state transitions, making it a suitable testbed for assessing object understanding. As shown in Figure
4(b)
, the most frequent activities include “making coffee,” “preparing spicy tuna avocado rolls,” and “making cucumber-yogurt sauce.”, which involves complex and fine-grained human-object interactions. The variety of activities introduces substantial differences in object manipulation patterns, visual conditions, and task structure to make
Object-IVQA
a comprehensive benchmark.

QA Type Distribution.

Our dataset contains 514 QA pairs spanning four types, as shown in Figures
3
and
4(c)
. Preparation reasoning (36.8%) and mistake recognition (31.7%) constitute the majority of QAs, requiring models to trace object states back to earlier causal steps and to identify deviations from the correct procedure. Counterfactual reasoning (16.9%) and object-state evolution reasoning (14.6%) further enrich the temporal and causal dependencies required for a comprehensive evaluation.

Error Distribution.

51 videos (47.7%) in our dataset contain procedural mistakes, resulting in diverse error instances across activities. We categorize them into six types, with their sample counts summarized in Figure
4(d)
. Procedural mistakes are typically sparse, short-lived, and embedded within complex visual scenes, making them difficult to detect. This places strong demands on models to understand procedural context in order to infer how an observed action diverges from the correct one.

In instructional videos, manipulations on the same object often occur with temporal gaps. As illustrated in Figure
3
, background frames or unrelated action segments (shown in gray) may intervene between the segments relevant to a given question, creating temporal discontinuities that require multi-hop reasoning over long video horizons. To address this challenge, we propose an object-centric agent framework that dynamically invokes specialized agents to process, analyze, and reason over videos, ultimately generating answers grounded in inferred evidence. In this section, we will first present an overview of our agent framework, then introduce the function and implementation of each agent, respectively.

4.1
Framework Overview

As shown in Figure
5(a)
, the framework consists of four agents: a Planning Agent, a Video-Processing Agent, a Video-Analyzing Agent, and a Text-Generation Agent. The three video-related agents are each equipped with multiple callable tools, enabling rich and flexible functionality. The LLM-based planning agent first determines the information needed to answer the question, queries the toolboxes of all video agents, and constructs an ordered sequence of tool calls to extract perceptual evidence and analyze object states. The final answer is obtained by executing these calls in sequence. By leveraging our customized agents for object-centric understanding, the framework not only enables efficient object localization and description but also identifies and selects key video segments through the analyzing agent, thereby supporting robust, object-centric multi-hop reasoning.

4.2
Video-Processing Agent

The Video-Processing Agent converts unstructured video streams into temporally organized units for subsequent reasoning. It is implemented using a set of built-in Python functions and external libraries (e.g.,
Decord

1
1

1

An open-source video processing library available on GitHub:
https://github.com/dmlc/decord
.
). Specifically,
Video_Load
tool initializes the input by reading the video file and recording the frame rate, duration, and resolution.
Frame_Sample
tool performs frame sampling and returns a time-indexed collection of frames. Each frame is annotated with a precise timestamp, ensuring that evidence can be accurately localized in time during later stages.
Frame_Trim
tool supports queries involving causal or sequential relations (e.g., before or after a reference interval) and filters the retrieved frames based on temporal relationships. Together, these tools transform continuous video into timestamped structured evidence segments, building the foundation for object-centric reasoning.

4.3
Video-Analyzing Agent

The Video-Processing Agent extracts fine-grained semantic cues that identify action context and track how objects evolve over time.
Frame_Retrieve
tool, built on EVAL-02 CLIP
10
, retrieves frames containing the question-relevant object predicted by the Planning Agent, allowing for the localization of an approximate temporal window in which the answer resides.
Obj_Det
tool applies Grounding DINO
22
for open-vocabulary text-based referring detection, returning bounding boxes, and confidence scores of objects, which are essential for tracking object dynamics and state transitions.
Action_Rec
tool employs a multimodal LVLM Qwen3-VL
41
to generate temporally aligned descriptions of object-involved actions, enabling the agent to determine when an object is manipulated and in what manner. By adaptively combining these tools, the framework is able to construct rich multimodal object-centric representations that explicitly link each object to the action events it participates in along with their timing and location, thereby establishing the foundations for object-centric procedural understanding.

4.4
Text-generation Agent

Extensive previous research
37
;
42
;
17
demonstrates that text integration enhances the ability of LVLMs to interpret visual cues. Building upon this insight, the Text-generation Agent transforms images and videos into descriptive language, enabling object-centered process comprehension and facilitating open-ended answer generation.
Img_Caption
tool produces fine-grained, object-focused captions using a BLIP-based captioner
20
, enriching raw visual inputs with explicit descriptions of object appearance, attributes, and contextual cues. This compensates for the limited structured information in pixel-only representations.
Context_Sum
tool, implemented with Llama-3.1
9
, fuses action descriptions, captions, and detection results, and summarizes to an interpretable textual context of how objects evolve over time triggered by actions. Based on the context summary, sampled frames and the question,
Answer_Gen
tool implemented based on Qwen3-VL
41
performs multi-modal reasoning and finally produces the answer along with the supporting time evidence.

4.5
Multi-agent Collaboration

Each agent contributes complementary semantic and contextual information to address object-centric video QA. Figure
5
(b) shows a concrete example of multi-agent collaboration. The process begins with a tool-usage plan generated dynamically by the Planning Agent, which interprets the question and synthesizes a program specifying what evidence to retrieve and in what order. This program encodes object-centric constraints (e.g., focusing on “butter”), temporal logic (e.g., frames preceding the melting event), and the sequence of perception and reasoning operations.

Next, the Video-Processing and Video-Analyzing Agents work together to reduce the long video into a set of temporally and semantically aligned segments. Low-level operations, such as loading, sampling and retrieving reference timestamps, establish the temporal structure, while higher-level perception modules further refine the search to object-relevant intervals. Through this collaboration, the agents progressively convert the raw video into object-centric, temporally localized evidence units.

The Text-Generation Agent integrates the multimodal outputs from previous steps, including detected objects, action cues, and selected timestamps, and produces the final open-ended answer in natural language. Because each intermediate result is explicitly generated, exchanged across agents, and referenced during the final reasoning step, the overall workflow provides transparent, step-by-step reasoning with clear answer traceability. This dynamic collaborative manner enables the system to track object states across discontinuous video segments and ground answers in precise temporal evidence efficiently.

5
Experiments

In this section, we benchmark the latest Large Vision-language model (LVLM) capabilities of fine-grained recognition and multi-hop reasoning on
Object-IVQA
dataset. We also evaluate our proposed agent framework and observe it outperforms even the best LVLMs not only on the quality of answers but also the accuracy of supporting evidence.

5.1
Evaluation Metrics

LLM-based Answer Evaluation.

To comprehensively evaluate model performance, we adopt an LLM-as-judge paradigm following previous works
21
;
24
. Specifically, we use GPT-5-mini
28
as an evaluator to perform semantic comparisons conditioned on the questions between model-generated answers and ground-truth annotations. The evaluator assigns scores along four complementary dimensions that jointly assess factual correctness, descriptive completeness, narrative coherence, and temporal reasoning:

•

Contextual Integration (CI) measures whether the answer faithfully reflects the factual video content while avoiding hallucinations, omissions, or misinterpretations.

•

Detail Orientation (DO) evaluates the inclusion and correctness of key fine-grained elements required for a complete and accurate description.

•

Contextual Understanding (CU) assesses the alignment of the answer with the overall narrative structure, causal flow, and contextual dependencies present in the video.

•

Temporal Understanding (TU) focuses on the correctness of temporal logic, including event ordering, state transitions, and causal relations.

Each dimension is scored on an integer scale from 0 to 5, where 5 denotes a fully correct answer and 0 indicates a completely incorrect one. The final score for each answer is the average across all four dimensions. Leveraging the strong reasoning capabilities of LLMs, this rubric ensures an fine-grained and reliable quantitative evaluation.

Evidence Localization.

To measure the alignment between the predicted and ground-truth time spans of the evidence, we calculate their mean Intersection-over-union (mIoU) . Given

Agent framework.

Despite calling smaller tool models, our agent framework achieves answer quality comparable to or even surpassing commercial LVLMs. More importantly, it also provides stronger evidence localization. This suggests that for object-centric procedural understanding, a well-designed modular architecture and reasoning workflow are more crucial than model scale itself. Even without large parameter counts, our framework leverages an explicit perceive–analyze–generate pipeline to provide robust grounding and more stable multi-hop reasoning.

6
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we shift procedural video understanding from an action-centric perspective to an object-centric paradigm. We introduce Object-IVQA, a benchmark spanning four complementary reasoning types that jointly evaluate a model’s ability to recognize object states, infer causal dependencies, and identify procedural mistakes. To address the temporal sparsity and discontinuity of object interactions, we propose an agent framework that decomposes video QA task into planning, processing, analysis, and text generation, enabling explicit multi-hop reasoning over disjoint segments. Experiments show that state-of-the-art LVLMs still struggle with fine-grained object reasoning and temporal causal inference, whereas our proposed agent framework substantially improves both answer quality and evidence localization.

Despite this progress, object-centric procedural understanding in complex real-world workflows still remains an open challenge. One promising direction is incorporating self-supervised objectives for object-state learning, such as masked-state modeling
27
which encourages models to recover missing state representation. Another direction is constructing symbolic graphs of object-state inspired by action graphs
3
;
30
, or temporal-logic formulations
39
to impose interpretable structure and stronger procedural constraints. Finally, our agent-based design also suggests opportunities for richer agentic workflows, including self-reflection loops
37
, improved planning strategies, and more expressive toolsets for object-centric perception and reasoning. These directions may help advance AI systems to better understand the inherent nature of procedural activities in the future.
