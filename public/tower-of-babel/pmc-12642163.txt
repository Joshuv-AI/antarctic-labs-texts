Coronary aneurysms and dissections in a patient with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease: a case report

Background

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is the most common hereditary renal disorder, with an estimated prevalence of 1 case per 2500 individuals. Its course is characterized by the progressive development and expansion of multiple cysts throughout the renal parenchyma, which may or may not lead to loss of renal function as the disease advances [1]. This monogenic disease is primarily caused by mutations leading to loss of function in the PKD1 and PKD2 genes, located on the short arm of chromosome 16, which encode polycystin-1 and polycystin-2 proteins, respectively. Mutations in the PKD1 gene account for approximately 85% of cases, while loss of function in the PKD2 gene accounts for the remainder [2].

ADPKD, being a multisystemic pathology, exhibits various renal and extrarenal manifestations. The renal manifestations are numerous and variable, presenting in different forms. Flank pain, caused by the compression of adjacent structures by the cysts and by capsule enlargement, is common. In addition to this symptom, compressive manifestations, such as early satiety and recurrent vomiting, may arise due to stomach compression. Other possible symptoms include hematuria (due to cyst rupture), nocturia, polyuria, hypertension, nephrolithiasis, reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR), renal insufficiency, and renal failure [3]. Another feature of ADPKD is the gradual increase in kidney volume, associated with a stable or gradually declining GFR.

Extrarenal manifestations are also common, most frequently involving hepatic and pancreatic cysts, intracranial aneurysms, abdominal hernias, and cardiac valve lesions[1, 2]. Although less common, aneurysms and dissections in other locations, such as coronary arteries, have also been documented; these are the focus of this case report [2].

There remains a limited understanding of the pathophysiology of vascular complications related to ADPKD in humans. However, evidence supports a key role of polycystins in vasculature integrity. Genetic studies in mice have demonstrated high PKD1 expression throughout the cardiovascular system, particularly in the circle of Willis. Furthermore, both polycystin-1 and polycystin-2 are expressed in endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle, where they are essential for vascular integrity. These findings suggest a possible relationship between PC1 and PC2 deficiency and vascular fragility [4]. Intracranial aneurysms (ICAs) thus represent the most feared complication associated with ADPKD, with a prevalence of approximately 8–12% in asymptomatic individuals. Rupture is a potentially fatal complication, accounting for about 6% of all deaths in patients with ADPKD. Screening methods are therefore used to detect aneurysms before rupture.

However, ICAs are not the only aneurysms that occur more frequently in these patients. The incidence of ADPKD-related coronary aneurysms remains uncertain, with most available data derived from small studies and individual case reports [5], further justifying the need for this study. The literature indicates that cardiovascular conditions are the leading cause of mortality in these patients, even after appropriate clinical treatment, with approximately half of deaths attributed to ischemic heart disease [2]. Given the impact of these manifestations, healthcare professionals need to be aware of these associations, conduct targeted diagnostic evaluations, and implement effective therapeutic strategies. A proactive and integrated approach is crucial to improving clinical outcomes and the quality of life of patients with ADPKD.

Case presentation

The patient was a Caucasian male in his 50s, weighing 80 kg and measuring 179 cm in height, diagnosed with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (Figs. 1 and 2). He had systemic arterial hypertension as a comorbidity, managed with a beta blocker (BB) and an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI). Clinical follow-up revealed preserved GFR while on 10 mg/day of prednisone, mycophenolate mofetil 1 g/day, and sirolimus 2 mg/day, taken regularly. He had been previously diagnosed with polycystic kidney disease and underwent kidney transplantation in 2004 from a related living donor without pre-transplant nephrectomy. Postoperatively, he developed hemolytic-uremic syndrome, requiring discontinuation of calcineurin inhibitor. In early 2020, 3 months later, he presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset acute chest pain. Initial evaluation revealed rhythmic, normal heart sounds with no murmurs, edema, or lower limb induration. Vital signs were stable. Coronary CT angiography showed no coronary calcifications but revealed aneurysmal dilations in the proximal and middle segments of the right coronary artery and the anterior descending artery. A significant mural thrombus was identified without calcifications, along with moderate luminal narrowing in the mid-anterior descending artery. Serial lab tests revealed elevated troponin levels, increasing from 0.68 ng/mL to 2.02 ng/mL within 2 hours. The surgical intervention involved resection of aneurysmal segments of the right coronary artery (proximal and middle thirds) and anterior descending artery (proximal and middle thirds), with coronary artery bypass grafting using the internal mammary artery and saphenous vein grafts to the diagonal branch, marginal branch, and right coronary artery. Post-surgery, he was transferred to the ICU under deep sedation (RASS-5), requiring norepinephrine (0.3 mcg/kg/minute) and epinephrine (0.05 mcg/kg/minute) infusions. Despite initial stability, he developed hypovolemia and electrical instability necessitating a temporary epicardial pacemaker. The patient’s condition deteriorated, leading to his death 2 days post-surgery.

Discussion

In this study, we reviewed, reported, and analyzed the case of a man in his late 50s with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) who had previously undergone a kidney transplant and presented with multiple aneurysms, thrombi, and coronary dissection. As previously mentioned, ADPKD is the most common hereditary renal condition, with an estimated prevalence of 1 case per 2500 individuals. Its primary characteristic is the development and expansion of multiple cysts throughout the renal parenchyma, which may or may not be associated with organ dysfunction [1].

It is currently known that the disease is predominantly caused by loss-of-function mutations in the PKD1 and PKD2 genes, located on the short arm of chromosome 16. These genes encode polycystin-1 and polycystin-2 proteins, respectively. These proteins are transient receptor potential calcium channel regulators located on the primary cilia of renal tubular cuboidal cells [2]. Loss of either protein is directly linked to decreased intracellular calcium levels, which in turn leads to increased intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) concentration. This activates proliferation and secretion pathways in renal tubular cells, thereby contributing to cyst formation [1, 6].

Correlating the literature with this case, imaging studies suggest that the patient had a functional deficiency of polycystins, despite the absence of detailed genetic screening. This patient exhibited renal manifestations, including progressive kidney enlargement, reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and renal insufficiency, which led to a kidney transplant in 2004.

ADPKD is a multisystemic pathology, meaning it can present various extrarenal manifestations beyond renal alterations. Patients with ADPKD frequently present with extrarenal conditions, including hepatic and pancreatic cysts, intracranial aneurysms, abdominal hernias, and cardiac valve lesions (1;2). Less commonly, aneurysms and dissections in other locations, such as coronary arteries, have also been documented, making these manifestations the focus of this study [2].

While knowledge of the vascular complications of ADPKD in humans remains limited, various studies have highlighted the functional role of polycystins in vascular health. Genetic studies in rats revealed widespread PKD1 expression in the cardiovascular system, particularly in the circle of Willis. Additionally, polycystin-1 and polycystin-2 are expressed in endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle, critical to vascular integrity. These findings suggest a possible relationship between polycystin deficiency and vascular fragility [4].

Intracranial aneurysms (ICAs) are the most feared complication associated with ADPKD [7], with a prevalence of approximately 8–12% in asymptomatic individuals. In patients with ADPKD with a positive family history of ICAs, the incidence increases to 16–23%. Aneurysm rupture is associated with high mortality and morbidity, presenting with symptoms such as sudden and severe headaches (thunderclap headaches) and nonspecific neurological symptoms [4].

Abnormalities in other vascular sites, such as aortic aneurysms, coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs), and cervicothoracic dissections, also occur more frequently in patients with ADPKD [8]. The association between ADPKD and CAAs, the focus of this study, is rare and has only been documented 16 times in the literature.

A systematic review by Neves et al. (2016), involving 23 patients with ADPKD with coronary manifestations, found that 16 had CAAs. It was further concluded that CAAs related to ADPKD are more prevalent in male individuals, with a mean age at diagnosis of 44 years. Among these 16 patients, 6 were asymptomatic, 4 experienced acute myocardial infarction (AMI), and 3 presented with angina. In most cases, percutaneous intervention with stent placement was the preferred therapeutic approach for symptom relief and the prevention of ischemic or hemorrhagic events. However, in cases of coronary dissection, percutaneous interventions had poor success rates [2].

Data from Neves et al. also indicate that asymptomatic CAA occurrences ranged from 20% to 40% of the cases analyzed in their study, emphasizing the importance of further investigations to prevent ischemic or hemorrhagic myocardial events [9].

In this case, we observed several extrarenal cardiovascular manifestations, including hypertension, moderate mitral insufficiency, and atrial and ventricular extrasystoles, along with conditions rarely described in international literature: coronary aneurysms and dissections. As highlighted by Neves et al., cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in patients with ADPKD, with more than half of cases attributed to ischemic heart disease, which surpasses end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in prevalence.

This case, together with existing evidence, shows that coronary aneurysms and dissections are significant causes of AMI and mortality in patients with this hereditary condition. The clinical course of CAA is riddled with complications, including thrombosis, thromboembolism, arteriovenous fistula formation, vasospasm, and rupture [6]. Other studies have reported high AMI prevalence, complicated aneurysms, and postoperative complications associated with deaths in patients with ADPKD [2].

Vascular wall involvement, which causes aneurysms, often occurs in the subarachnoid space. However, vascular abnormalities can be observed in other regions, as evidenced by the increased incidence of aortic aneurysms. CAAs in patients with ADPKD remain underreported [10]. The incidence of CAAs related to ADPKD is still unclear, with most available information derived from small studies and individual case reports [5], justifying the relevance of this study.

This case warrants visibility due to its rarity and severity, as well as the extensive information provided by the patient’s family. The literature does not yet well establish the association between coronary artery complications and ADPKD, and robust evidence confirming these conditions as hereditary consequences is lacking. We report this case to expand the available evidence and contribute to the literature on this subject, aiming to enable new diagnostic methods and therapeutic approaches for similar patients in the future.

Conclusion

Mutations in the PKD1 and PKD2 genes may be associated with arteriopathies in intracerebral arteries and may also predispose to abnormalities in coronary arteries. It is important to emphasize that atherosclerosis is not the only cause of angina and myocardial infarction (MI), conditions that present high morbidity and mortality rates. The reviewed literature and the reported case highlight the role of aneurysms and coronary dissections as contributing factors to mortality from cardiovascular events. Furthermore, it underscores the need for further studies and analyses to assess the relationship between coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) and autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, as well as to investigate potential risk factors and interventions for similar cases in patients with this hereditary condition.
