Risk factors for avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bin Dong, Feihu Li, Chenjing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAvascular necrosis (AVN) is a severe complication following pediatric femoral neck fractures (PFNFs). Identifying risk factors for AVN is critical for guiding timely treatment and follow-up. However, prior studies report inconsistent associations. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to identify risk factors associated with AVN after PFNFs.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library through April 30, 2025. Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the association between potential risk factors and AVN. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, meta-regression, and subgroup analyses were performed.ResultsThirty-four studies comprising 1332 pediatric patients (1340 fractures) were included. Older age (≥ 12 years; RR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.09–1.82), Delbet type I/II fractures (RR = 1.96, 95% CI 1.60–2.39), initial displacement (RR = 2.98, 95% CI 2.04–4.35), and poor reduction quality (RR = 2.43, 95% CI 1.46–4.05) were significantly associated with increased AVN risk. Gender, injury mechanism, time to reduction, and reduction method showed no overall association. Meta-regression identified follow-up duration as a significant moderator. Subgroup analyses of long-term follow-up (≥ 5 years) revealed that delayed reduction increased AVN risk (RR = 2.63, 95% CI 1.35–5.11), while closed reduction and internal fixation (CRIF) reduced risk compared to open reduction (RR = 0.40, 95% CI 0.24–0.65). Subgroup differences between long-term and short-term follow-up were both statistical significant.ConclusionsOlder age, Delbet type I/II classification, initial displacement, and poor reduction quality are significant predictors of AVN following PFNFs. Early reduction and CRIF may provide superior long-term outcomes. Further high-quality prospective studies with extended follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06423-6.Keywords: Avascular necrosis, Pediatric femoral neck fractures, Risk factors, Meta-analysis

Background

Pediatric femoral neck fractures (PFNFs) are rare, comprising less than 1% of all pediatric fractures [1, 2]. Despite their low incidence, these injuries carry significant clinical importance due to their association with serious complications, particularly avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head [3, 4]. AVN is the most concerning sequela of PFNFs, with reported incidences ranging from 0 to 64%, influenced by factors such as fracture type, patient age, and timing of intervention [5, 6]. A recent meta-analysis estimated the pooled incidence of AVN to be approximately 22% [7]. Given that the pediatric femoral head relies primarily on the medial femoral circumflex artery for blood supply, trauma and surgical intervention may compromise vascular integrity, resulting in ischemic necrosis [8].

The clinical consequences of AVN in children are often severe and irreversible. AVN can lead to femoral head collapse, joint incongruity, chronic pain, leg length discrepancy, early-onset osteoarthritis, and the eventual need for hip arthroplasty at a young age [9]. These long-term outcomes significantly impair physical function and impose psychological and socioeconomic burdens on affected children and their families. Identifying risk factors for AVN is essential for guiding timely clinical decisions and optimizing long-term management strategies.

Although numerous studies have investigated potential risk factors for AVN following PFNFs, results have been inconsistent. Frequently studied variables include patient age, Delbet fracture classification, fracture displacement, timing of surgical intervention, and method of reduction and fixation [7, 10–12]. However, these meta-analyses only focused on some of these risk factors and neglected the impact of follow-up duration on risk factor-outcome associations. In a large retrospective cohort, Wang et al. identified both age and initial displacement as independent risk factors for AVN [13]. While delayed surgery (beyond 24 h) has traditionally been considered a risk factor, recent studies have questioned this association, reporting no significant correlation in some cohorts [14, 15]. Similarly, although closed reduction is often preferred to preserve blood supply, evidence suggests that open reduction may be necessary in certain cases to restore proper alignment and potentially reduce AVN risk [5, 16]. Nevertheless, many previous studies suffer from small sample sizes and limited statistical power, and no consensus currently exists regarding optimal treatment timing, reduction method, injury mechanism, or patient age in relation to AVN risk following PFNFs.

Given these uncertainties and limitations of previous meta-analyses, a comprehensive meta-analysis, with a broader inclusion of risk factors, more recent studies and follow-up–stratified analyses, is warranted to systematically evaluate the associations between clinical and surgical factors and the development of AVN in pediatric femoral neck fractures. Such analysis may offer evidence-based guidance for early risk stratification and individualized treatment approaches aimed at preserving the native hip joint and improving long-term outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Literature search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline [17]. A comprehensive literature search was performed across the PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases from their inception to April 30, 2025. The search strategy combined MeSH terms and their variations. Detailed search terms for each database are provided in Additional file 1: Table S1. The search was limited to studies published in English. In addition, the reference lists of relevant reviews and included studies were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. We did not perform forward citation tracking.

In case of overlapping or duplicate populations reporting the same risk factor, the study with the largest sample size was selected for the analysis of this risk factor. Two authors (FL and CL) independently screened for eligibility. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated to assess inter-reviewer agreement in a subset (n = 100) of articles at title/abstract screening stage and in all studies at full-text reviewing stage. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third author (BD).

Data extraction and quality assessment of included studies

Baseline information extracted from each included study included: first author, publication year, country, sample size, gender distribution, age, fracture type, mechanism of injury, initial fracture displacement, time to reduction, reduction method, quality of reduction, other potential risk factors, and follow-up duration. Additionally, the number of AVN cases and corresponding risk factor data for AVN and non-AVN groups were collected. Fracture type was defined according to the Delbet classification: type I (transepiphyseal), type II ((transcervical), type III (cervicotrochanteric), and type IV (intertrochanteric) [18]. A pre-piloted data extraction sheet prepared in Excel was tested on a subset of studies, and all data were extracted into the standardized Excel spreadsheets by two reviewers. Data extraction was independently conducted by two reviewers (FL and CL), with disagreements resolved by discussion with a third author (BD).

The quality of case–control and retrospective cohort studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which scores studies on a scale of 0–9 across 8 domains [19]. Studies scoring ≥ 6 were considered high-quality while those scoring < 6 were deemed low-quality. The quality of case-series studies was assessed by the Institute of Health Economics (IHE) checklist, a 20-criterion quality appraisal checklist with a maximum of 20 points [20]. Quality assessment were independently conducted by two reviewers (FL and CL), with disagreements resolved by discussion with a third author (BD).

Statistical analysis

Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I2 statistic and Q test. I2 values < 25%, 25–50%, and > 50% were considered to indicate low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. A random-effects model was used for pooled analyses when I2 > 50% or the P-value of the Q test was < 0.10. Otherwise, a fixed-effects model was applied. Continuous variables were dichotomized for analysis. Patient age at injury was categorized as < 12 years or ≥ 12 years, based on the cutoff used by Wang et al. [13]. Time to reduction was also categorized into early and delayed using specific timepoint thresholds (12, 24, or 48 h). Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to evaluate the strength of association between each risk factor and AVN. Meta-regression analysis was performed when 10 or more studies were available to explore whether baseline characteristics influenced the association between risk factors and AVN. Subgroup analyses were also conducted based on mean follow-up duration (< 5 years, ≥ 5 years). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results. Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s test. When publication bias was suspected, the trim-and-fill method was applied to evaluate its potential impact. Certainty of evidence was assessed by the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation [21]. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of studies included in the meta-analysis

The comprehensive literature search identified 1698 articles, of which 907 unique article remained after removing duplicates (Fig. 1). Following title and abstract screening, 841 irrelevant articles were excluded, and 32 full-text articles were further excluded based on eligibility criteria (Additional file 1: Table S2). The kappa coefficient was 0.819 at title/abstract screening stage and 0.909 at full-text reviewing stage, indicating a high level of inter-reviewer agreement. Ultimately, 34 studies involving 1332 pediatric patients (1340 fractured hips) were included in the meta-analysis [4, 5, 13–16, 22–49]. Sample sizes ranged from 8 to 241. The included studies were conducted across various countries: the United States (n = 9), China (n = 6), India (n = 6), Turkey (n = 6), Austria (n = 1), Canada (n = 1), Saudi Arabia (n = 1), Serbia (n = 1), Egypt (n = 1), Germany (n = 1), and the United Kingdom (n = 1). Five studies were published before 2000, 11 between 2000 and 2010, and 18 after 2010. There were 20 case–control studies and 14 case-series studies [4, 22–24, 28, 31–34, 36, 37, 40, 45, 46].

Flowchart of literature search and selection

Across all studies, the number of AVN cases ranged from 1 to 59, which summed up to 372. The incidence of AVN varied from 5.6 to 64.4%. The incidence ranged from 5.6% to 52.9% in case-series studies and from 11.4 to 64.4% in case–control studies, without significant difference between these two study types. According to the Delbet classification, the distribution of fracture types was as follows: type I (n = 60), type II (n = 700), type III (n = 454), and type IV (n = 125). Mean follow-up durations ranged from 1 to 17 years. Eight potential risk factors were analyzed for their association with AVN: gender, age, Delbet classification, initial displacement, injury mechanism, time to reduction, reduction method, and reduction quality. All included case–control studies were deemed high quality based on NOS assessment. The mean IHE checklist point for case-series studies was 10.6 (range: 8–14), suggesting a fair methodological quality. Gender

Twenty-two studies involving 646 fractures evaluated the association between gender and AVN risk. No heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%, P = 0.714), allowing the use of a fixed-effects model. The risk of AVN did not differ significantly between male and female patients (RR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.61–1.03, Fig. 2).

Forest plot of meta-analysis of association between gender and risk of avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures

Age

Seventeen studies (660 fractures) assessed the impact of age on AVN risk. Pooled analysis using a fixed-effects model indicated that patients aged ≥ 12 years had a significantly higher risk of AVN than those aged < 12 years (RR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.09–1.82, Fig. 3).

Forest plot of age associated with avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures

Delbet classification

A total of 31 studies (1224 fractures) compared AVN risk between Delbet type I/II and type III/IV fractures. Type I/II fractures were associated with a significantly higher risk of AVN (RR = 1.96, 95% CI 1.60–2.39, I2 = 0, Fig. 4). Further comparison between Delbet type II and III fractures was performed using data from 28 studies (802 fractures). Type II fractures carried a significantly higher AVN risk than type III fractures (RR = 1.73, 95% CI 1.38–2.18, Additional file 2: Fig. S1).

Forest plot of Delbet classification associated with avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures

Initial displacement status

Eighteen studies involving 738 fractures analyzed the association between initial displacement and AVN. A fixed-effects model was used (I2 = 29.5%, P = 0.116). Displaced fractures had a significantly increased risk of AVN compared to non-displaced ones (RR = 2.98, 95% CI 2.04–4.35, Fig. 5).

Forest plot of initial displacement status associated with avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures

Injury mechanism

Fifteen studies (440 fractures) assessed the impact of injury mechanism. Mechanisms were categorized as high-energy (e.g., motor vehicle accidents, falls from height) or low-energy (e.g., sports injuries, falls during daily activities). No significant difference in AVN risk was found between the two groups (RR = 1.40, 95% CI 0.97–2.02, Fig. 6).

Forest plot of injury mechanism associated with avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures

Time to reduction

Time to reduction was analyzed using three cutoffs: 12 h (n = 2), 24 h (n = 11), and 48 h (n = 2). Overall, pooled analysis with a fixed-effects model found no significant difference in AVN risk between delayed and early reduction (RR = 1.19, 95% CI 0.82–1.72, Fig. 7).

Forest plot of time to reduction associated with avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures

Reduction method

Twenty-five studies compared closed reduction and internal fixation (CRIF, n = 529) to open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF, n = 423). A random-effects model was employed for meta-analysis (I2 = 30.1%, P = 0.079). No significant difference in AVN risk was found between the two methods (RR = 0.82, 95% CI 0.58–1.15, Fig. 8).

Forest plot of reduction method associated with avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures. CRIF: closed reduction and internal fixation; ORIF: open reduction and internal fixation

Reduction quality

Ten studies assessed the association between reduction quality and AVN, using either anatomical versus non-anatomical classification or the Haidukewych criteria (excellent/good/fair/poor) [50]. Meta-analysis with a random-effect model (I2 = 52.3%, P = 0.020) showed that poorer reduction quality was significantly associated with increased AVN risk (RR = 2.43, 95% CI 1.46–4.05, Fig. 9).

Forest plot of reduction quality associated with avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach indicated that pooled results for gender, age, injury mechanism, and time to reduction lacked robustness. Exclusion of Wang W et al. rendered the association between age and AVN non-significant (RR = 1.25, 95% CI 0.91–1.71, Additional file 2: Fig. S2) [13]. Conversely, excluding Chaudhary et al. or Togrul et al. resulted in a significant association between gender and AVN risk (Additional file 2: Fig. S3) [29, 46]. Omitting Spence et al. also changed the results for injury mechanism and time to reduction from non-significant to significant (Additional file 2: Figs. S4 and S5) [43].

Meta-regression and subgroup analysis

Meta-regression was conducted to identify moderators of risk factor–AVN associations, including publication year, sample size, mean age, follow-up duration, and proportions of male patients, Delbet type I/II fractures, displaced fractures, CRIF procedures, high-energy injuries, and early reduction (Additional file 1: Table S3. Publication year was negatively associated with the effect size for Delbet classification (P = 0.031). Follow-up duration emerged as a significant moderator for gender (P = 0.046, Additional file 2: Fig. S6), time to reduction (P = 0.047, Additional file 2: Fig. S7), and reduction method (P = 0.001, Fig. 10).

Meta-regression of mean follow-up duration modulating the association of reduction method with avascular necrosis in pediatric femoral neck fractures. RR: risk ratio

Studies were divided into short-term (< 5 years) and long-term (≥ 5 years) follow-up (Additional file 1: Table S4). In the long-term subgroup, delayed reduction significantly increased AVN risk (RR = 2.63, 95% CI 1.35–5.11, Additional file 2: Fig. S8), and CRIF was associated with lower AVN risk compared to ORIF (RR = 0.40, 95% CI 0.24–0.65, Fig. 11). These effects were not observed in short-term follow-up. The subgroup differences were statistically significant for both factors (P = 0.007 and P < 0.001, respectively). No significant subgroup effects was observed for gender (P = 0.175).

Forest plot of subgroup analysis of reduction method associated with avascular necrosis stratified by mean follow-up duration. CRIF: closed reduction and internal fixation; ORIF: open reduction and internal fixation

Publication bias

Potential publication bias was suggested by Egger’s test for Delbet classification, initial displacement, and reduction quality (P < 0.05, Additional file 1: Table S5). Trim-and-fill analysis showed that the results remained significant for Delbet classification (RR = 1.60, 95% CI 1.32–1.94, Additional file 2: Fig. S9) and reduction quality (RR = 2.05, 95% CI 1.22–3.42, Additional file 2: Fig. S10). However, the effect size for initial displacement was reduced from RR = 2.98 to RR = 1.76 (95% CI 1.24–2.51) after trim-and-fill analysis (Additional file 2: Fig. S11).

Certainty of evidence

The evidence starts at low certainty since all included studies were observational studies. The final judgement was low certainty of evidence for gender, age, Delbet classification (II vs. III), injury mechanism, and time to reduction, and very low certainty of evidence for Delbet classification (I/II vs. III/IV), initial displacement, reduction method, and reduction quality (Additional file 1: Table S6).

Discussion

This comprehensive meta-analysis synthesized data from 34 studies involving 1332 pediatric patients with 1340 femoral neck fractures to evaluate risk factors for AVN following PFNFs (Table 2). Our findings indicate that older age (≥ 12 years), Delbet type I/II fractures, initial displacement, and poor reduction quality are significantly associated with increased AVN risk. In contrast, gender, injury mechanism (high- vs. low-energy), time to reduction (delayed vs. early), and reduction method (CRIF vs. ORIF) were not consistently associated with AVN. Notably, subgroup and meta-regression analyses revealed that follow-up duration significantly moderated several risk factor-outcome associations. In long-term follow-up (≥ 5 years), early reduction and CRIF resulted in significantly reduced risk of AVN.

Age has long been postulated as a potential risk factor for AVN, although prior studies have yielded inconsistent results [13, 43]. Our pooled analysis demonstrated that patients aged ≥ 12 years had a 40% higher risk of AVN compared to younger children. This increased risk may be attributed to age-related changes in the vascular anatomy of the proximal femur. With increasing age, the transphyseal vessels regress, and the femoral head becomes primarily reliant on the lateral epiphyseal artery, which is more vulnerable to injury during fracture or reduction [8, 37, 51]. Furthermore, older children tend to sustain higher-energy trauma due to increased activity levels and body mass, leading to more severe fracture patterns. These findings are consistent with prior meta-analyses reporting higher AVN rates in adolescents than in younger children [7, 11]. However, sensitivity analysis suggested that the association between age and AVN risk was heavily influenced by the inclusion of a single large study by Wang W et al. [13], limiting the robustness of this conclusion.

Delbet fracture classification emerged as a strong predictor of AVN. Type I and II fractures demonstrated significantly higher AVN rates (45% and 32%, respectively) compared to type III and IV fractures (17% and 12%, respectively) [7]. Our meta-analysis found that pediatrics with type I/II factures had nearly two-fold risk of developing AVNs to those with type III/IV fractures. This pattern reflects the proximity of type I and II fractures to the femoral head and their greater likelihood of disrupting its blood supply [52]. Our subgroup analysis further confirmed that type II fractures carried a higher AVN risk than type III fractures, supporting the importance of anatomic location in AVN pathogenesis. These results are in line with prior meta-analyses identifying fracture type as a key determinant of AVN risk [7, 11, 52].

Initial displacement was also identified as a significant risk factor, with displaced fractures associated with nearly a threefold increase in AVN risk compared to non-displaced fractures. Displacement likely reflects more severe trauma and mechanical disruption to both bone and surrounding vasculature [5]. Additionally, attempts at realignment during reduction may further compromise blood flow to the femoral head. Our findings reinforce prior studies that have emphasized the clinical importance of fracture displacement as a predictor of AVN [5, 13, 22].

The impact of timing to reduction on AVN risk remains controversial. Several earlier studies suggested that delayed reduction increases AVN risk due to prolonged vascular compromise [5, 27]. Feng W et al. identified time from injury to surgery as an independent risk factor for AVN in a retrospective analysis of 45 patients [5]. Using 12 h as cutoff, Bukva et al. found a significantly higher rates of AVN in delayed-treated fractures compared with early-treated fractures (66.7% vs. 18.7%, P = 0.014) [27]. However, more recent observational studies and larger meta-analyses have not found a significant association [7, 11, 13, 30, 40, 52]. One explanation for this discrepancy is that more severe injuries, which are intrinsically more likely to develop AVN, are often prioritized for urgent treatment, thereby confounding the effect of timing. Although our overall analysis did not reveal a significant association between time to reduction and AVN, meta-regression demonstrated a positive correlation between follow-up duration and effect size. Subgroup analysis confirmed that the negative impact of delayed reduction became more apparent in long-term follow-up (≥ 5 years), suggesting a latent progression of ischemic injury.

The influence of reduction method (CRIF vs. ORIF) on AVN risk is similarly debated. While some studies report lower AVN rates with CRIF [26], others have found the opposite, particularly in displaced or delayed-treated fractures, where ORIF may achieve better anatomical alignment [16, 44, 53]. Our meta-analysis found no significant difference in AVN risk between the two methods overall, consistent with prior meta-analyses [7, 11, 54, 55]. However, meta-regression showed a significant linear relationship between follow-up duration and the effect size for reduction method. Subgroup analysis revealed that CRIF was associated with significantly lower AVN risk compared to ORIF in the long-term follow-up (≥ 5 years) subgroup, although results should be interpreted with caution due to potential confounding. Specifically, the choice of open reduction may reflect more complex or severe fracture patterns that are inherently more prone to AVN.

Our study revealed that initial displacement, poor reduction quality and Delbet type I/II were among the top risk factors for AVNs, increasing the incidences by 2–3 times. For high-risk patient presenting displaced type I/II fractures, timely surgery and proper decision of reduction and fixation method seem to be crucial factors to achieve better reduction quality and reduce AVN occurrence. Despite controversial roles in ANV occurrence, our meta-analysis revealed comparable incidences of early versus delayed reduction and CRIF versus ORIF in the early period of follow-up but suggested a protective role of early reduction and CRIF in long-term follow-up. Each of the two protective factors were associated with more than 50% of reduction in AVN incidences when extending the follow-up to over 5 years. These findings also highlighted the need of long-term monitor of AVN occurrence.

Compared with previous meta-analyses [7, 11, 12, 52, 54–56], our study offers several key advantages. First, we included a broader and more recent dataset encompassing 34 studies from diverse geographic regions. Second, we analyzed a more comprehensive range of risk factors, including reduction quality and injury mechanism, which have been underexplored in previous reviews. Third, we performed meta-regression and subgroup analyses to explore the moderating effects of follow-up duration, offering more nuanced insights into the time-dependent nature of AVN development. Finally, our sensitivity analyses assessed the influence of individual studies, improving the robustness of our findings.

Nonetheless, this study has the following limitations. (1) Our study was not prospectively registered, leading to increased risk of selective reporting. (2) Non-English articles were excluded from our study, which potentially caused language bias. (3) Egger’s test makes sense only if there is a reasonable amount of dispersion in the sample sizes and a reasonable number of studies. Yet, most studies included in this meta-analysis had sample sizes and the numbers of included studies for several outcomes were relatively small. Consequently, the results of Egger’s test for publication bias assessment may not be reliable. (4) Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis suggested the lack of robustness for gender, age, injury mechanism, and time to reduction. The significant or non-significant associations between these risk factors and AVNs should be more cautiously interpreted. (5) Most included studies were retrospective in design, which introduces potential selection bias, inconsistent data collection, and unmeasured confounding. (6) Diagnostic standards of AVNs and definitions of key variables such as displacement, reduction quality, and injury mechanism varied across studies, which introduce possible heterogeneity and limiting the generalizability of pooled estimates. (7) Due to reliance on univariate analyses, we could not determine independent risk factors after adjusting for covariates.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis identified several key risk factors associated with AVN following PFNFs, including older age (≥ 12 years), Delbet type I/II fractures, initial displacement, and poor reduction quality. While overall associations with time to reduction and reduction method were not statistically significant, long-term follow-up data suggest significant associations between early reduction and CRIF with reduced AVN risk. These findings underscore the predictive value of several risk factors in AVNs development in pediatric femoral neck fractures. Clinicians should be especially attentive in high-risk cases to optimize outcomes and preserve native hip joint function. Further prospective studies with standardized methodologies, larger sample sizes, and extended follow-up are warranted to confirm and refine these risk predictors.
